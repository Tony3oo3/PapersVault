## Problem

While model architecture and loss function design have been extensively studied for improving generalisation, the role of inter-sample interactions during training—how learning one sample modifies predictions on others—remains underexplored. Existing similarity measures like the empirical NTK (eNTK) capture gradient alignment but ignore how labels flip the effective direction of parameter updates, making them insufficient for explaining learning difficulty, forgetting events, or identifying redundant training data.

## Input / Output

The method requires a fully trained model (parameters w* selected by best validation performance) and the labelled training set; it computes the Jacobian ∇_w z(x) for every sample and the label sign vector s(y) to form the lpNTK matrix of size N×N. The primary output is a scalar similarity score κ((x,y),(x',y')) per sample pair, from which three relationship types (interchangeable, unrelated, contradictory) are derived by the sign of the inner product. Downstream, this yields a pruned training set (redundant samples removed) and per-iteration forgetting event predictions with precision/recall metrics.

## Method

Starting from a first-order Taylor expansion of how an SGD step on sample x_u shifts the softmax prediction on sample x_o, the paper decomposes the update into three factors: the softmax Jacobian A, the eNTK matrix K(x_o,x_u), and the prediction error p_tar(x_u)−q(x_u). The key insight is that the sign pattern of the prediction error is entirely determined by the label y_u (positive at the true class, negative elsewhere), so labels effectively flip rows and columns of the eNTK matrix. The lpNTK is then defined as a linear kernel on the feature φ(x,y) = (1/√K)·s(y)ᵀ·∇_w z(x), where s(y) is the label sign vector, making the kernel a scalar that encodes both gradient alignment and label compatibility. This yields three geometrically distinct sample relationships (acute/right/obtuse angles between feature vectors) that unify explanations of easy/hard samples and forgetting events. For practical data pruning, samples are clustered via farthest-point clustering under lpNTK; a sample is deemed redundant if its most interchangeable partner is not itself, and removing such samples (plus 10% of the largest cluster) preserves or slightly improves test accuracy on MNIST and CIFAR-10.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, pruning ~20% of samples using lpNTK-based clustering yields 93.55% test accuracy, outperforming the full training set (93.28%) and all compared baselines. On MNIST, the lpNTK-pruned subset achieves 99.37% test accuracy versus 99.31% for the full set. In the de-redundancy experiment (Table 2), removing lpNTK-identified redundant samples preserves performance (93.17% on CIFAR-10 vs. 93.28% full), while pNTK-based removal degrades accuracy to 90.93% on CIFAR-10. For forgetting event prediction, the lpNTK variant achieves 57.76% F1-score on CIFAR-10 and 49.54% on MNIST, significantly above random chance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

lpNTK outperforms all four compared baselines on both datasets in the data-pruning experiment (Table 3). On CIFAR-10, lpNTK achieves 93.55%, beating EL2N (93.32%), GraNd (92.87%), Forgot Score (92.64%), and Influence-score (92.53%) by 0.23–1.02 percentage points. On MNIST, the margins are smaller (99.37% vs. 99.26–99.33%) but lpNTK still leads all baselines. In the de-redundancy comparison (Table 2), lpNTK dramatically outperforms pNTK (Mohamadi & Sutherland, 2022): removing lpNTK-identified redundant samples preserves CIFAR-10 accuracy at 93.17% (statistically indistinguishable from the full set at p=0.153), whereas pNTK-based removal drops accuracy to 90.93% (p≪0.01), demonstrating that incorporating label sign information is critical for correct sample relationship identification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical Neural Tangent Kernel (eNTK) line of work by incorporating label information into the kernel, building directly on Mohamadi & Sutherland's pNTK (which converts the matrix-valued eNTK to a scalar by summing elements) and adding the sign structure of the prediction error vector. The resulting lpNTK provides a unified lens through which previously disconnected phenomena—learning difficulty (Paul et al., 2021), forgetting events (Toneva et al., 2018), and data-diet pruning (Sorscher et al., 2022)—are reinterpreted as consequences of three sample relationships: interchangeable, unrelated, and contradictory. The paper also draws on the iterated learning / transmission bottleneck literature from cognitive science, framing data subset selection as a new form of bottleneck for deep learning agents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical finding is that removing ~20% of training data (redundant samples plus 10% of the FPC head cluster) identified via lpNTK can slightly *improve* generalisation over the full dataset, providing a mechanistic explanation for why data pruning helps: the bias toward the majority of interchangeable samples in the largest cluster degrades performance. However, significant limitations temper the impact: the O(N²d²) computational cost, the requirement for post-training parameters w*, the heuristic 20% pruning fraction, and evaluation limited to MNIST and CIFAR-10 with small models (LeNet-5, ResNet-18) all restrict near-term practical adoption. Open questions include whether an O(1) proxy for lpNTK can be computed during training, how the method scales to larger datasets and architectures, and whether the head-cluster pruning insight generalises beyond clean-label benchmarks.
