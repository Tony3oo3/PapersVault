## Problem

Model ensemble is a widely used strategy to improve adversarial transferability, yet prior works simply average outputs of surrogate models without explaining why or how ensemble helps. This paper identifies the missing theoretical link: the transferability of ensemble-generated adversarial examples is governed by two properties of the convergence point — the flatness of the loss landscape and the closeness to each model's local optimum — which previous ensemble methods neither explicitly optimize nor analyze.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240770/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural RGB image (224×224 for classification, variable size for detection), its ground-truth class label, and an ensemble of 4–6 surrogate classifiers (e.g., ResNet-18/32/50/101 plus adversarially trained XCiT-S12), and produces an ℓ∞-bounded adversarial example (ε=16/255) or a universal adversarial patch for object detection. For the vision-language model attack, the objective shifts from classification loss to maximizing the ℓ2 distance between adversarial and natural image features in the vision encoder, with the same perturbation budget applied to the input image.

## Method

The core contribution is a second-order Taylor approximation of the expected attack loss over victim models, which decomposes transferability into two optimizable terms: the Frobenius norm of each model's Hessian (landscape flatness) and the squared ℓ2 distance from the adversarial example to each model's nearest local optimum. To optimize flatness, the authors derive a modified Sharpness Aware Minimization (SAM) under the ℓ∞ constraint that performs an inner gradient-ascent step followed by an outer gradient-descent step on the ensemble logits. To optimize closeness, they prove (Theorem 3.2) that minimizing the distance to local optima is upper-bounded by maximizing the dot-product similarity of per-model gradients, and implement this via a first-order meta-learning procedure (Cosine Similarity Encourager, CSE) that performs small per-model gradient updates with ℓ2-normalized inner momentum. The two sub-methods are combined into Common Weakness Attack (CWA) and integrated with Momentum Iterative (MI) to yield MI-CWA, which is orthogonal to and composable with other transferability boosters like VMI and SSA.

## Results

On NIPS2017 with ε=16/255, MI-CWA achieves 94.6–100% attack success rate on 16 normally trained models (e.g., 99.8% on VGG-16, 92.3% on ViT-B/16) and dramatically improves on 8 adversarially trained models, surpassing MI by ~30% (e.g., 73.5% vs 45.7% on FastAT ResNet-50, 77.8% vs 22.8% on XCiT-M12). SSA-CWA reaches 100% success on 4 randomized defenses (R&P, Bit, JPEG, RS) and 97.5% on DiffPure. On the INRIA object detection task, Adam-CWA achieves an average mAP of 9.85 across 8 detectors, compared to 31.48 for the Loss Ensemble baseline. Against Google's Bard, SSA-CWA increases misdescription from 4% to 12% and blindness from 16% to 27% at ε=16/255.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MI-CWA outperforms all 12 baselines on the majority of 31 target models. Against the strongest gradient-based baselines, MI-CWA surpasses MI by 30+ points on adversarially trained models (e.g., 77.8% vs 22.8% on XCiT-M12) and by 10–30 points on Transformer architectures (92.3% vs 59.2% on ViT-B/16). VMI-CWA and SSA-CWA further extend these gains, with SSA-CWA reaching 100% on 4 randomized defenses where SSA achieves only 95–99%. On object detection (INRIA), Adam-CWA reduces average mAP to 9.85 versus 31.48 for the Loss Ensemble baseline from Huang et al. (T-SEA), a 20-point improvement. In the Naseer et al. (2021) setting, MI-CWA achieves 99.5–100% across all 5 target models versus 71–97% for the original method. The only case where MI-CWA does not lead is on NRP (a self-supervised defense), where SSA-CWA (85.4%) slightly outperforms MI-CWA (86.8% vs 33.1% for VMI-CWA), though MI-CWA still dominates all other baselines there.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper rethinks the role of model ensemble in transfer-based adversarial attacks by providing a second-order theoretical analysis that prior works lacked. It repurposes Sharpness Aware Minimization (Foret et al., 2020) from model training to adversarial example generation under the ℓ∞ norm, and adapts first-order meta-learning (Nichol et al., 2018) to encourage gradient alignment across ensemble members. The resulting Common Weakness Attack (CWA) is built on top of the Momentum Iterative (MI) optimizer (Dong et al., 2018), departing from prior ensemble approaches (loss/logits averaging, SVRE) that treated models as interchangeable without analyzing the geometric properties that make ensemble effective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'common weakness' framework offers a principled explanation for why ensemble improves transferability—flatness of the loss landscape and proximity to each model's local optimum—rather than relying on the less well-supported gradient variance argument. Practically, the method achieves a 30% improvement over MI on adversarially trained models and demonstrates successful black-box attacks on Google's Bard, highlighting the vulnerability of deployed large vision-language models. Limitations include the 2× computational overhead over MI, reliance on assumptions (zero covariance between Hessian norm and distance, Gaussian optima) in the theoretical analysis, and the fact that gains on normally trained models are modest since many baselines already approach 100% success. Open questions include whether the common weakness concept can inform adversarial defenses and whether it extends to other attack settings beyond ℓ∞ perturbations.
