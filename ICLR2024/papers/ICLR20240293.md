## Problem

Token reduction in Vision Transformers relies on [CLS] attention scores to identify unimportant tokens for pruning, but the paper shows that in early blocks the [CLS] token lacks sufficient task pressure to allocate attention meaningfully, making its scores an unreliable proxy for token importance. This inconsistency between [CLS] attention and true token significance in shallow blocks substantially degrades the effectiveness of token reduction, especially under aggressive pruning ratios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240293/cropped_page1_idx20.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (224×224 ImageNet) and produces a single class label. During inference, token reduction is applied at designated blocks (e.g., blocks 4, 7, 10 for EViT-based; 10 of 12 blocks for DiffRate-based), where unimportant tokens are fused into one additional token rather than simply discarded. All early-exit heads used during training are removed before deployment, so the inference pipeline and output format are identical to a standard ViT classifier.

## Method

METR injects early task pressure into the [CLS] token by attaching a lightweight two-layer bottleneck classification head at every block where token reduction is applied, and training with a multi-exit cross-entropy loss that forces the [CLS] token to form a valid prediction from the current patch features at each such block. This compels the [CLS] attention to reflect genuine token importance even in shallow layers. A self-distillation loss (KL divergence between early-exit logits and detached final logits) further refines the quality of early supervision without additional computation. The total training objective is L_final + α·L_sd, and after fine-tuning all early-exit heads are discarded, introducing zero extra inference cost. The approach is orthogonal to and composable with existing token reduction strategies (EViT, DiffRate) and token allocation schemes.

## Results

On ImageNet, METR consistently improves upon its base token-reduction methods across all model sizes and compute budgets. For ViT-S (DeiT) at 3.0 GFLOPs, METR+EViT reaches 79.64% top-1 accuracy vs. 79.32% for EViT alone (+0.32%), and with 100-epoch fine-tuning (†) reaches 80.09%. For ViT-B (DeiT) at 8.7 GFLOPs, METR+DiffRate achieves 81.11% vs. 80.51% for DiffRate (+0.60%), and at 11.5 GFLOPs reaches 82.05% vs. 81.76% (+0.29%). The gains are most pronounced under aggressive token reduction, e.g., METR+EViT at 2.3 GFLOPs reaches 79.62% (†) vs. 78.47% for EViT (+1.15%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

METR is evaluated as a plug-in enhancement on top of two base token-reduction methods (EViT and DiffRate) and compared against five other token-reduction approaches: DynamicViT, A-ViT, Evo-ViT, ToMe, and the base methods themselves. Against EViT, METR yields a consistent +0.32 to +0.56% improvement at 30-epoch fine-tuning (e.g., 79.64% vs. 79.32% at 3.0 GFLOPs for ViT-S), growing to +1.15% under aggressive 2.3 GFLOPs reduction with 100-epoch fine-tuning (79.62% vs. 78.47%). Against DiffRate, METR adds +0.29% at 11.5 GFLOPs (82.05% vs. 81.76% for ViT-B DeiT) and a larger +0.60% at 8.7 GFLOPs (81.11% vs. 80.51%), confirming that the benefit scales with reduction aggressiveness. Against DynamicViT (79.32% at 2.9 GFLOPs), A-ViT (78.60% at 3.6 GFLOPs), Evo-ViT (79.40% at 3.0 GFLOPs), and ToMe (79.49% at 2.9 GFLOPs), METR+EViT at 3.0 GFLOPs (79.64%) outperforms all of them. The paper also shows that METR's gains are model-agnostic, extending to MAE-pretrained ViT-B (+0.45% at 8.7 GFLOPs) and ViT-L (+0.17% at 31.0 GFLOPs), and that the improvement is orthogonal to the specific token-reduction mechanism, making it composable with future advances in token allocation or merging strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two previously orthogonal research threads: multi-exit architectures (from the dynamic neural network literature, e.g., BranchNet, adaptive inference) and ViT token reduction (EViT, DiffRate, DynamicViT, ToMe). Prior token reduction work has largely taken [CLS] attention as a given, reliable scoring metric, with advances focused on how to implement reduction (merging vs. discarding) or how to allocate tokens across blocks. METR departs from this by questioning the scoring metric itself, showing that [CLS] attention in shallow blocks is poorly aligned with true token importance due to the absence of task pressure, and then repurposing multi-exit loss—a technique previously used only for early-exit dynamic inference—as a calibration mechanism for that metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

METR offers a simple, zero-inference-cost drop-in improvement that can be layered onto existing token reduction pipelines (demonstrated with both EViT and DiffRate), making it practically attractive for practitioners seeking efficiency gains without architectural redesign. The finding that the benefit grows with more aggressive pruning ratios suggests the technique is most valuable precisely where token reduction is needed most. Limitations include evaluation restricted to ImageNet classification with DeiT/MAE backbones; whether the same calibration effect transfers to dense prediction tasks (detection, segmentation) or to very deep transformers remains open. The paper also does not explore whether the multi-exit heads could be repurposed at inference time for adaptive early exit, which would add a second efficiency axis.
