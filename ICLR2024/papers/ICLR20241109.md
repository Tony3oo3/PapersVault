## Problem

While prior work established that CLIP models absorb and amplify societal stereotypes, no systematic empirical study had examined data balancing as a bias-mitigation strategy for contrastive multimodal models, and existing fair subset selection methods only addressed first-order (representation) biases while ignoring second-order (association) biases. The paper asks: how effective is data balancing in practice, across model sizes, representation dimensions, and training data scales, and what are its trade-offs with model quality?

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a stream of image-text pairs from WebLI (1B total), where sensitive attributes (perceived gender, age groups) and labels (occupations) are independently inferred from each modality—via a vision classifier for images and regex matching for text—and then concatenated so that all cross-modality combinations are decorrelated. It outputs per-example weights q ∈ [0,1] that are used for subsampling at a 90% rate (the maximum rate at which all bias constraints remain satisfiable), producing a balanced training subset. The algorithm is fully online, requiring only O(m(c+1)) memory per example, making it scalable to internet-scale data without solving LPs.

## Method

M4 (Multi-Modal Moment Matching) formulates bias mitigation as a constrained optimization over sample weights that simultaneously enforces representation bias (marginal group prevalence) and association bias (conditional label distributions) constraints, exploiting the fact that for binary-valued attributes, zero weighted covariance implies statistical independence. The solution is obtained via dual decomposition: the algorithm maintains a bias vector v ∈ ℝ^{2m(c+1)} and a scalar μ, updating them with per-example stochastic gradients derived from a Lagrangian dual, with soft penalty terms (controlled by enforcement level V) to handle infeasible constraint combinations. A key design choice is the inclusion of proxy variables (e.g., 'computer', 'briefcase') suggested by an LLM to cut backdoor causal paths between sensitive attributes and core features, which helps representation bias but can hurt association bias due to competing constraints. The paper further studies learning dynamics by splitting 1B-example training into two stages (original vs. balanced data at 0%, 10%, 90% splits), revealing that representation bias is sensitive to the last distribution seen (making fine-tuning effective) while association bias changes gradually regardless of ordering.

## Results

The M4 data balancing algorithm reduces association bias (AB) between perceived gender and occupation in CLIP models: on SigLIP-B/16 with quality-filtered WebLI data, maximum parity drops from 38.8 to 29.9 on FairFace, 38.2 to 33.7 on UTKFace, and 28.4 to 20.5 on MIAP. In the best-case setting (SigLIP + data quality filters), data balancing simultaneously improves model quality: COCO image-to-text retrieval @5 rises from 86.0% to 87.1%, and ImageNet 0-shot classification from 77.0% to 77.5%. In the standard CLIP setting without quality filters, balancing improves 10-shot classification on average (e.g., Cars 77.5→79.0, Pets 71.9→73.3) but degrades retrieval, an effect attributed to the ~25% reduction in human-containing training examples. In the binary classification comparison (Table 3), M4 achieves 9.1% demographic parity on Adult and 1.2% on DCCC, competitive with other pre-processing methods while preserving classification accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

M4 does not outperform in-processing (Reduction) or post-processing (TO, RTO) methods on demographic parity: Reduction achieves 1.0% DP on Adult vs. M4's 9.1%, and TO/RTO achieve 0.7–0.9% on DCCC vs. M4's 1.2%. However, M4 substantially outperforms both pre-processing baselines on DCCC: it achieves 1.2% DP with 17.9% error, while R2B achieves 0.9% DP but with a much worse 28.8% error, and CR achieves 0.8% DP with 19.6% error. On Adult, M4 (9.1% DP, 15.6% error) is competitive with R2B (8.3% DP, 15.4% error) and far better than CR (18.9% DP, 14.7% error) in bias reduction. The paper explicitly notes that M4's advantage is not in raw DP numbers but in handling a much broader setting (arbitrary overlapping attributes, second-order association biases, Internet-scale data) while maintaining model quality, and that it is the only method that simultaneously addresses both representation and association biases in a single online pass without solving LPs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fair subset selection and reweighting literature (Stoyanovich et al., 2018; Mehrotra & Celis, 2021) to the multimodal contrastive learning setting, introducing M4 as an online algorithm that simultaneously handles both first-order (representation) and second-order (association) bias constraints—whereas prior methods addressed only first-order statistics or required solving intractable LPs. It also builds on the broader CLIP bias literature (Hall et al., 2023; Birhane et al., 2021) that documented the existence of stereotypes in vision-language models, but is the first to systematically evaluate data balancing as a remediation strategy at scale (150+ models, 1B image-text pairs). The causal analysis in Appendix A.5.2 draws on Veitch et al. (2021) and Makar et al. (2022) to motivate why proxy variables must be balanced alongside direct sensitive attributes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first large-scale empirical evidence that data balancing in CLIP reduces model bias but has a mixed and sometimes negative impact on downstream quality (improves classification, hurts retrieval), with the tradeoff explainable by distributional shifts in human-related content. The finding that fine-tuning on balanced data effectively counters representation bias but not association bias has direct practical implications for deployment pipelines. Key limitations include the restriction to contrastive (not generative) models, focus on a single sensitive attribute (perceived gender), and the acknowledged gap between statistical parity metrics and genuine fairness. Open questions include whether data augmentation or text neutralization can fully resolve the quality tradeoff, and how findings generalize to other sensitive attributes and model architectures.
