## Problem

Despite extensive work on GNN expressiveness via the WL hierarchy, the probabilistic inference capacity of GNNs—i.e., their ability to approximate posterior marginals in graphical models—remains poorly understood. Prior GNN-PGM hybrids implicitly assume GNNs cannot capture complex joint dependencies and rely on external variational inference, yet it is unclear how much inference capacity MPNNs already possess and how to systematically extend it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard node-attributed graphs (adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} and feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}) and, for link prediction, a set of target links to be scored. The probabilistic formulation treats node features as observations of latent variables z_i, so the GNN's output is interpreted as approximating node marginals p(z_i|X) for classification or joint marginals p(z_i,z_j|X) for link prediction. Phantom nodes/edges are added as a one-time preprocessing step before feeding the augmented graph into any standard MPNN, so the downstream architecture and hyperparameters remain unchanged.

## Method

The paper establishes a formal equivalence between MPNN message passing and belief propagation on pairwise MRFs, proving that MPNNs can approximate both node and edge marginals of the Bethe free energy to arbitrary precision (Theorem 4). It then introduces a k-l approximation hierarchy to evaluate GNN variants along two axes: the discriminating power of the potential function (k-WL) and the maximum clique order (l), yielding new characterizations of k-GNNs, ESANs, subgraph GNNs, and labeling-trick methods. To extend MPNNs beyond pairwise dependencies, the authors propose phantom nodes (one added per clique, connected to all clique members and tagged with a distinct label) that provably lift expressiveness to 1-∞ approximation, and phantom edges (added for each target link, tagged with a distinct edge label) that provably enable 1-2 approximation of joint posteriors p(z_u,z_v|X) via a modified Bethe free energy. Both methods are implemented as lightweight graph preprocessing with separate aggregation/composition functions for phantom vs. regular elements, requiring no architectural changes to the underlying MPNN.

## Results

On node classification (PPI-1, the most data-limited setting), phantom nodes (PN) consistently improve Micro-F1 over base MPNNs: GCN+PN reaches 60.17 vs. GCN's 54.55, SAGE+PN reaches 68.93 vs. SAGE's 62.25, and GCNII+PN reaches 69.07 vs. GCNII's 65.79. On PPI-10, GCNII+PN achieves 95.84 Micro-F1, matching GCNII+SPN (95.87) while using a simpler architecture. On link prediction (Hit@10), GCNII+PE achieves 60.09 on Cora and 67.80 on Citeseer, outperforming ELPH (50.84/64.35) and BUDDY (52.02/58.23) on Cora but trailing ELPH on Citeseer and Pubmed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Phantom nodes (PN) consistently outperform the GNN-CRF baseline (SPN from Qu et al., 2022) on Micro-F1 across all PPI settings: GCN+PN achieves 60.17 vs. GCN+SPN's 54.15 on PPI-1, and GCNII+PN reaches 69.07 vs. GCNII+SPN's 67.80. On PPI-10, SAGE+SPN (91.99 F1) and GCNII+SPN (95.87 F1) slightly edge out their PN counterparts (86.75 and 95.84), suggesting the advantage of PN is most pronounced under data scarcity. For link prediction, GCNII+PE (60.09 Hit@10 on Cora) surpasses both ELPH (50.84) and BUDDY (52.02), but ELPH (64.35) and BUDDY (58.23) outperform GCNII+PE (53.41) on Citeseer, and ELPH (32.94) beats GCNII+PE (30.93) on Pubmed. The synthetic experiments (Table 3) confirm that phantom nodes/edges systematically reduce KL divergence to belief propagation marginals compared to base GCN and GIN, validating the theoretical claims. Overall, the paper's key empirical claim—that a simple graph preprocessing step (adding phantom nodes/edges) can match or exceed dedicated GNN-PGM frameworks—holds most strongly in the low-data regime and for node classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the established connection between MPNNs and the 1-WL test (Xu et al., 2018; Morris et al., 2018) and the earlier observation that MPNNs parameterize mean-field inference in pairwise MRFs (Dai et al., 2016), but departs from the dominant GNN-PGM combined paradigm (Satorras & Welling, 2021; Qu et al., 2022) by arguing that GNNs themselves possess substantial probabilistic inference capacity without requiring external variational inference modules. The phantom nodes and phantom edges methods are inspired by belief propagation on factor graphs and the Bethe free energy formulation (Yedidia et al., 2001), repurposing these ideas as simple graph preprocessing steps rather than as separate inference algorithms. The paper also provides a unified k-l approximation framework that reinterprets existing GNN variants (k-GNNs, ESANs, labeling tricks, source-node labeling) through the lens of distribution complexity and target posteriori, offering a complementary perspective to the standard WL-hierarchy-based expressiveness analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The probabilistic framework provides a more intuitive and structured way to reason about what GNNs can and cannot learn, complementing the WL hierarchy with concrete inference targets (node marginals, edge marginals, joint posteriori) and distribution complexity metrics. The phantom nodes/edges methods are practically attractive because they are simple preprocessing steps that provably enhance any MPNN architecture without modifying its internal structure, making them easy to adopt. However, the empirical gains on real-world datasets are modest (1-3% on PPI, a few points on link prediction Hit@10), and the authors acknowledge that KL divergence to belief propagation marginals does not reach zero for edge/node-pair predictions, suggesting the output layer design is a bottleneck. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large dense graphs (where clique enumeration becomes expensive) and whether the equivariance-breaking effect of multiple phantom edges can be formally controlled.
