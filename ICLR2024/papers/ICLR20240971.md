## Problem

Standard GNNs apply uniform architectural choices (fixed k-hop aggregation, global smoothing) to all nodes, yet realistic graphs contain nonhomogeneous patterns where locally homophilous and heterophilous regions co-exist and require different treatments. This paper addresses the gap by decoupling the two fundamental modalities in graph learning—node self-features and neighborhood structure—into separate experts, enabling per-node adaptation without modifying the GNN architecture itself.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph G(V, E, X) with node feature vectors x_v ∈ R^f and edge set E, and produces a class probability vector p_v ∈ R^n for each target node. The weak MLP expert consumes only the target node's own feature vector (no neighborhood access), while the strong GNN expert uses the full k-hop neighborhood. A confidence score C(p_v) ∈ [0,1], computed from the dispersion (e.g., variance or negative entropy) of the MLP's logits, determines at inference whether the MLP or GNN prediction is selected, allowing the GNN to be skipped entirely when the MLP is confident.

## Method

Mowst pairs a lightweight MLP (weak expert) with an off-the-shelf GNN (strong expert) and gates their predictions via a confidence function C = G ∘ D, where D measures the dispersion of the MLP's prediction logits and G maps dispersion to [0,1]. The training loss L_Mowst = (1/|V|) Σ [C(p_v)·L(p_v, y_v) + (1−C(p_v))·L(p'_v, y_v)] is minimized via an 'in-turn training' strategy that alternates between fixing one expert and optimizing the other, accommodating their different convergence behaviors. The design is inherently biased toward the GNN: when the MLP cannot outperform the GNN on a node group, it learns to output random guesses (C→0), effectively removing noisy nodes from the GNN's training set (denoised fine-tuning), while on nodes with rich self-features the MLP specializes and earns high confidence (soft data splitting). A variant Mowst* instead computes a single loss on the convex combination C·p_v + (1−C)·p'_v, yielding a theoretically lower loss at the cost of joint optimization.

## Results

Mowst(*) achieves consistent accuracy improvements across all 6 benchmarks (3 homophilous, 3 heterophilous) with 4 backbone GNN architectures. Against GCN, Mowst(*)-GCN gains +0.29 to +1.02 accuracy points (e.g., 83.19% on Penn94 vs. 82.17%, 76.49% on ogbn-products vs. 75.64%). The most dramatic gain is Mowst(*)-GIN on pokec, where GIN suffers poor convergence (53.37%) but Mowst(*)-GIN reaches 76.11% (+22.74). Against the strongest MoE baseline GraphMoE, Mowst(*) still wins on every dataset (e.g., 79.38% vs. 77.79% on ogbn-products for SAGE; 83.19% vs. 81.61% on Penn94 for GCN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mowst(*)-GCN outperforms GCN on all 6 datasets by +0.29 to +1.02 points, and also beats the stronger GraphMoE-GCN on every dataset (e.g., 83.19 vs. 81.61 on Penn94, 76.49 vs. 73.90 on ogbn-products). Against GIN, Mowst(*)-GIN achieves the largest single-dataset gain of +22.74 on pokec (76.11 vs. 53.37), where GIN exhibits severe convergence failure, and consistent gains of +1.77 to +2.56 on the remaining datasets. Mowst(*)-SAGE surpasses both GraphSAGE and GraphMoE-SAGE on all 6 benchmarks (e.g., 79.38 vs. 78.50 and 77.79 on ogbn-products). On heterophilous graphs, Mowst(*)-H2GCN further improves the state-of-the-art H2GCN by +0.33 to +2.13 points (e.g., 83.02 vs. 80.89 on pokec), demonstrating that the weak MLP expert adds value even on top of a purpose-built heterophilous architecture. Mowst(*) also outperforms GPR-GNN, AdaGCN, and GAT across all reported datasets, with the largest margins over AdaGCN (e.g., 83.19 vs. 74.42 on Penn94) and GAT on heterophilous graphs.

## Relation to Prior Work

Mowst repurposes the Mixture-of-Experts paradigm for graphs by deliberately breaking the symmetry assumption: instead of mixing comparable GNN experts (as in GraphMoE or AdaGCN), it pairs a lightweight MLP with an off-the-shelf GNN, treating them as specialists for the two fundamental graph modalities (node features vs. neighborhood structure). The confidence-based gating mechanism departs from standard softmax or top-K sparse gates by being inherently biased toward the strong expert, and the model-level (rather than layer-level) mixing contrasts with recent large-scale MoE designs like GLaM and GShard. The 'in-turn training' strategy and the theoretical characterization of specialization/denoising dynamics have no direct precedent in the graph MoE literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Mowst offers a drop-in, low-overhead wrapper that consistently improves any GNN backbone (GCN, GIN, GraphSAGE, H2GCN) across both homophilous and heterophilous graphs, with computation cost comparable to a single GNN. The denoising insight—that the weak expert's primary value is filtering noisy nodes from the strong expert's training set rather than classifying them well—reframes how one thinks about ensemble contributions in graph learning. Open questions include whether the many-expert generalization (§2.7) delivers the same gains empirically, whether the weak-strong paradigm transfers to non-graph domains, and whether the confidence mechanism can be extended to handle multi-task or semi-supervised settings.
