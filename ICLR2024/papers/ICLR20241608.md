## Problem

Existing data selection methods for LLM fine-tuning either fail to scale to language-data sizes or select samples that merely match the target distribution, ignoring the pre-training distribution. In the low-data fine-tuning regime, the fine-tuned model reflects a weighted blend of pre-training and fine-tuning data, so distribution-matching alone wastes budget on samples the model already knows well. The paper asks: how should one select a small subset of unlabeled data to pre-fine-tune a pre-trained model so that it is optimally primed for subsequent targeted fine-tuning?

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM (GPT-2 base or BERT-base-uncased), a large unlabeled candidate dataset of ~8M–20M text samples (assumed to approximate the pre-training distribution, e.g., OWTC or a multi-domain corpus), and a small labeled target training set (2.5K–393K samples). It outputs a selected subset of 10K–150K unlabeled samples for one-epoch MLM pre-fine-tuning, after which the model undergoes standard supervised fine-tuning on the target data. The selection budget is deliberately constrained to be orders of magnitude smaller than the pre-training corpus, reflecting practical API cost limits (e.g., OpenAI's 50M-token cap).

## Method

GOT-D (Gradients of Optimal Transport for Data Selection) selects samples by minimizing the OT distance between the fine-tuned model's effective data distribution λ·D_U + (1−λ)·D_S and the target task distribution, rather than matching D_U to the target alone. Under a first-order Taylor approximation (valid when λ ≪ 1 in the low-data regime), the optimal selection reduces to picking samples with the largest negative gradient of OT(D_S, D_R) with respect to each sample's probability mass in D_S. This gradient is recovered for free from the dual solution of the entropically regularized OT problem (Sinkhorn iterations via ott-jax), using the calibration technique of Just et al. (2023). In practice, candidate and target samples are embedded with a task-fine-tuned distilBERT, the OT problem is solved on the embedding space, and the top-k samples by negative gradient are selected—completing in minutes on a single A100 GPU for millions of samples.

## Results

On 8 domain-specific NLU tasks (150K selection budget), GOT-D achieves an average F1 of 83.83, outperforming DAPT (83.11), DSIR (82.98), and vanilla BERT (82.70). On the GLUE benchmark (50K budget, max 5K task training data), GOT-D reaches 78.43 average accuracy versus 75.30 for vanilla BERT (+3.13%) and 78.15 for DSIR. For model detoxification with GPT-2 (20K samples), GOT-D reduces worst-case toxicity from 0.62 to 0.41 on toxic prompts and from 0.38 to 0.21 on non-toxic prompts, while maintaining downstream utility (40.8% vs. 42.2% average accuracy).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GOT-D consistently outperforms DSIR (Xie et al., 2023) across all three experimental settings: +0.85% average F1 on domain adaptation (83.83 vs. 82.98), +0.28% on GLUE with full training data (83.43 vs. 83.25), and +0.28% on GLUE with 5K training data (78.43 vs. 78.15). Against DAPT (Gururangan et al., 2020), GOT-D gains +0.72% average F1 on domain tasks (83.83 vs. 83.11), with the largest margin on ACL-ARC (+1.83 F1) where the target training set is small. In the detoxification experiment, GOT-D_clean reduces expected maximum toxicity by 0.21 on toxic prompts (0.41 vs. 0.62 base) compared to only 0.12 for RTP (Gehman et al., 2020), while also reducing toxicity probability from 0.67 to 0.26 versus RTP's 0.35. In the resource-constrained setting (50K budget, 5K training data), GOT-D beats TAPT/c by +0.48% average F1 (81.51 vs. 81.03) and DSIR by +0.59% (81.51 vs. 80.92). The gains are most pronounced on tasks with small labeled training sets (e.g., ACL-ARC, RTE), confirming that GOT-D's advantage grows as the target data budget shrinks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Optimal Transport (OT) framework for data selection, particularly the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality and Sinkhorn-based solvers, and the gradient-based data valuation approach of Just et al. (2023). It departs from the dominant 'distribution matching' paradigm (DSIR, DAPT, TAPT) that selects samples closest to the target task distribution, arguing instead that for fine-tuning a pre-trained model in the low-data regime, one should select samples that shift the pre-training distribution toward the target. The key theoretical departure is Lemma 1, which formalizes the fine-tuned model's effective data distribution as a weighted mixture of pre-training and fine-tuning data, making the naive OT(D_U, D_T) objective suboptimal and motivating the gradient-based selection OT(λ·D_U + (1−λ)·D_P, D_T).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical pain point: fine-tuning LLMs on small curated datasets is expensive, and selecting the right unlabeled warm-up data can substantially reduce the labeled data needed. The OT-gradient approach is both theoretically grounded (Theorem 1 provides a formal upper bound) and computationally efficient (minutes on a single GPU for millions of samples), making it a practical tool for cost-effective model adaptation. Limitations include the assumption that the candidate dataset approximates the pre-training distribution, the restriction to tasks within the pre-training scope, and evaluation only up to 2.7B parameters. Open questions include whether the first-order Taylor approximation remains valid for larger fine-tuning budgets or multi-task settings, and how the method performs when the pre-training distribution is poorly approximated by available open data.
