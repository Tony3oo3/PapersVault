## Problem

While prior work demonstrated that transformers can perform modular addition and showed per-digit training loss curves suggesting independent circuits, no study had identified or explained the actual computational circuits underlying n-digit integer addition in transformers. This paper addresses that gap by providing a complete mechanistic account of how a one-layer transformer decomposes, computes, and combines the sub-operations required for multi-digit addition.

## Input / Output

The model receives a left-to-right token sequence of the form 'D4D3D2D1D0+D'4D'3D'2D'1D'0=' (for 5-digit addition) and autoregressively predicts the sum as a sequence of digit tokens (up to n+1 digits, e.g., '033333'). Because the architecture is autoregressive, the model must predict higher-value answer digits before lower-value ones — the reverse of the human right-to-left addition procedure — and must produce the first answer digit after seeing only the '=' token. The vocabulary is a small set of digit symbols plus structural tokens ('+', '='), and the model is trained on 1.8 million of the 10 billion possible 5-digit questions to avoid memorisation.

## Method

The paper reverse-engineers a one-layer, 3-head transformer trained on n-digit addition by combining a mathematical framework of necessary sub-tasks (Base Add, Make Carry 1, Make Sum 9, Use Carry 1, Use Sum 9) with attention visualisation, per-digit training-loss decomposition, and targeted ablation interventions (zero and mean ablation via TransformerLens). The key finding is that the three attention heads execute distinct sub-tasks in a time-ordered 'double staircase' pattern: Head 1 computes MC1 on digit i−2, Head 2 computes MC1 or MS9 on digit i−1, and Head 3 computes Base Add on digit i, with the MLP layer combining the three head outputs as a 60-key trigram lookup to produce each answer digit one token before it is revealed. All computation is confined to 6 consecutive rows after the full question is visible, and the model uses slightly different algorithms for the first, middle, and last answer digits. The analysis also identifies a rare failure mode: the model cannot handle cascading Use-Sum-9 cases (e.g., 445+555=1000) because its left-to-right algorithm cannot propagate a carry through multiple consecutive 9-summing columns.

## Results

The one-layer transformer achieves a final training loss of 0.009 for 5-digit addition, 0.011 for 10-digit, and 0.031 for 15-digit (overall average 0.017) after 5000 epochs. Per-digit loss varies significantly: the highest-value digits (A5, A4) reach <0.001, while A1 (tens place) has the highest loss at 0.046. The model handles Base Add (BA) and Make Carry 1 (MC1) cases well (loss 0.021 and 0.001 respectively) but exhibits high loss and high variability on Use Sum 9 (US9) cascade cases (e.g., 445+555=1000), which it cannot solve reliably. Ablation confirms all critical computation occurs in just 6 of 18 token positions (rows 11–16), with each answer digit calculated exactly one row before it is output.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a mechanistic interpretability paper rather than a method-comparison study, so no traditional baselines are benchmarked. The closest comparative evidence is the algorithm reuse experiment (Section 8): a second one-layer model trained with a different random seed, a different optimizer, and a modified output format (requiring a '+' token as the first answer digit) converges to essentially the same addition algorithm, exhibiting the same strengths (BA, MC1, simple US9) and the same failure mode (US9 cascades). The paper positions its contribution relative to Nanda et al. (2023), who reverse-engineered modular addition via Fourier analysis, and Nanda and Lieberum (2022), who observed per-digit training loss curves for 5-digit addition but did not identify the underlying circuits. This work goes further by providing a complete circuit-level explanation (three attention heads performing BA, MC1, and MS9/US9 respectively, combined by the MLP as a 60-key trigram lookup) and by identifying the specific rare failure case (US9 cascades) that prior work left unexplained.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability line of work from Nanda et al. (2023), who reverse-engineered single-digit modular addition (showing DFT-based rotation), to full multi-digit integer addition with carries. It directly addresses the open question left by Nanda and Lieberum (2022), who observed semi-independent per-digit training loss curves in 5-digit addition but did not identify the underlying circuits. The analysis is grounded in the mathematical framework for transformer circuits from Elhage et al. (2021) and employs the TransformerLens ablation toolkit, positioning itself as a complete circuit-level explanation where prior work provided only partial observations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare end-to-end algorithmic reconstruction of a transformer's internal computation, validated by a mathematical framework that predicted a specific failure mode (US9 cascades) before experimental confirmation. The methodology—combining first-principles task decomposition, training-dynamics analysis, attention visualization, and targeted ablation—offers a transferable template for reverse-engineering more complex tasks and multi-layer models. Key open questions include why the model cannot handle cascading carries despite having sufficient capacity, whether the algorithm generalizes to subtraction and multiplication, and how these circuits interact in deeper architectures.
