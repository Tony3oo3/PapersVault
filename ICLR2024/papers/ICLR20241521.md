## Problem

Existing watermarking strategies for language models rely on specialized decoding-time algorithms, making them incompatible with open models where users control the decoding procedure, and raising the question of whether watermarks can be learned directly into model weights. The paper investigates whether a student model can be trained to naturally generate watermarked text under standard decoding, and what the implications are for open-model watermarking and adversarial spoofing attacks.

## Input / Output

The method takes a teacher language model equipped with a decoding-based watermarking strategy (KGW, Aar, or KTH) and a watermark key, and produces a student model whose weights encode the watermark so that standard sampling (temperature, nucleus, or greedy decoding) yields watermarked text without any special decoding algorithm. The student model accepts a text prompt and outputs watermarked text; the watermark is verified by a detection algorithm that computes a p-value using the same key. Logit-based distillation requires the teacher and student to share the same tokenizer and vocabulary, while sampling-based distillation only requires access to generated samples and works across different tokenizers and model sizes.

## Method

The paper proposes two watermark distillation methods under the knowledge distillation framework. Logit-based watermark distillation trains the student to match the teacher's watermarked next-token distribution at every position via KL divergence, using non-watermarked human text as the training corpus (the ground-truth tokens are not used in the loss). Sampling-based watermark distillation first generates watermarked completions from the teacher using the decoding-based watermarking strategy, then fine-tunes the student on those samples with standard cross-entropy loss. Both methods are evaluated across three watermarking strategies (KGW green-list bias, Aar Gumbel-softmax, KTH exponential minimum sampling) and multiple hyperparameter settings controlling distortion. The key finding is that higher-distortion watermarks are learned with high detectability (median p-values < 0.0001), while lower-distortion watermarks are harder and less sample-efficient to learn but still learnable. The paper also demonstrates that weights-based watermarking is robust to decoding parameter changes and moderate text edits, but is removed by fine-tuning on normal text, and that sampling-based distillation enables proof-of-concept spoofing attacks against instruction-following chat models.

## Results

Across three watermarking strategies (KGW, Aar, KTH) and multiple hyperparameter settings, both logit-based and sampling-based watermark distillation successfully learn higher-distortion watermarks, achieving median p-values as low as 2e-17 (KGW k=0, δ=2, logit) and 3e-17 (Aar k=2, sampling), with AUROC of 1.00. Lower-distortion watermarks are harder to learn: e.g., KGW k=2, δ=2 yields p-values of ~0.1 (vs. 9e-18 for decoding) and Aar k=4 yields p-values of 0.3–0.4 (vs. 4e-72 for decoding), though still well below the non-watermarked baseline of 0.5. Distilled models incur a modest quality cost: perplexity rises from ~16.5–17.5 (decoding) to ~17.3–19.8 (distilled) for KGW, and from ~10.5–10.8 to ~11.3–16.5 for KTH.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares watermark distillation (logit-based and sampling-based) against three decoding-based watermarking baselines: KGW (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023), Aar (Aaronson, 2023), and KTH (Kuditipudi et al., 2023). For higher-distortion settings, distillation nearly matches or even slightly exceeds decoding-based watermarking: KGW k=0, δ=2 logit distillation achieves p-value 2e-17 vs. 6e-16 for decoding, and Aar k=2 sampling achieves 3e-17 vs. 1e-75 for decoding (both AUROC 1.00). For lower-distortion settings, distillation clearly loses on detectability: KGW k=2, δ=2 drops from p-value 9e-18 (decoding) to 0.1 (distilled), and Aar k=4 drops from 4e-72 to 0.3–0.4, though both remain far above the non-watermarked baseline (p-value 0.5, AUROC 0.50). On generation quality, distillation incurs a modest perplexity cost for KGW and KTH (e.g., KTH s=1: 10.5 decoding vs. 16.5 logit / 15.1 sampling), but for Aar, sampling-based distillation actually achieves lower seq-rep-3 (0.34) than decoding (0.34) while logit-based distillation reduces repetitiveness substantially (0.11 vs. 0.34). Overall, distillation trades some detectability and quality for the critical advantage of working under standard decoding without a specialized watermarking algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the knowledge distillation framework (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016) to transfer decoding-based watermarking signals into model weights, shifting the watermarking mechanism from a specialized inference-time algorithm to the model's natural sampling distribution. It builds directly on three established decoding-based watermarking strategies—KGW green-list bias (Kirchenbauer et al., 2023), Aar Gumbel-softmax (Aaronson, 2023), and KTH exponential minimum sampling (Kuditipudi et al., 2023)—as teacher signals, and connects to the broader literature on watermark spoofing attacks (Sadasivan et al., 2023; Jovanović et al., 2024) by showing that simple distillation enables such attacks. Unlike prior API watermarking work for model-extraction protection (He et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022), the goal here is to make the model itself a watermark generator rather than to detect distillation of a victim model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that weights-based watermarking is a viable path toward watermarking open models (where users control decoding), but reveals a critical fragility: fine-tuning on ordinary text erases the watermark, limiting near-term deployment. The demonstrated spoofing attack—where a distilled adversary model produces watermarked harmful text falsely attributable to a safe victim model—argues strongly against using watermarks for provenance attribution, recommending they be restricted to statistical detection of LM-generated text. Open questions include developing fine-tuning-robust weights-based watermarks, understanding the full sample complexity landscape for low-distortion watermarks, and designing defenses against the now-demonstrated spoofing threat.
