## Problem

Standard Transformer attention produces entangled representations that mix relational information with object-level features, resulting in poor sample efficiency for relational reasoning tasks. While explicitly relational architectures (CoRelNet, PrediNet) outperform Transformers on synthetic discriminative tasks, they have not been explored for sequence-to-sequence settings, and no Transformer-compatible module provides an explicit inductive bias for disentangling relations from object features.

## Input / Output

The Abstractor consumes a sequence of object vectors (e.g., 32-dim Gaussian vectors for order tasks, 64-dim CNN embeddings from 70×50×3 card images for SET, or character-level token sequences of up to 160 characters for math problems) and produces abstract states that encode only pairwise relational structure, not object features. For discriminative tasks the output is a class label; for sequence-to-sequence tasks it is a sorted index sequence or a generated text answer. A key design constraint is that the value vectors in attention are replaced by learned 'symbols' that act as abstract pointers to objects rather than feature carriers.

## Method

The core mechanism is relational cross-attention (RCA), which modifies standard attention by replacing value vectors φ_v(x_j) with symbol vectors s_j that identify objects without encoding their features, yielding A_i = Σ_j R̄_ij s_j where R̄_ij is the softmax-normalized inner-product relation between objects i and j. This creates a 'relational bottleneck' that forces the output to encode only relational information. Three symbol-assignment mechanisms are proposed—positional, position-relative, and symbolic attention (where symbols are retrieved via a learned binding-vector attention)—trading off abstraction strength against representational flexibility. The Abstractor module stacks RCA layers with feedforward networks and can be integrated into Transformer architectures in purely relational or partially relational configurations (e.g., parallel Encoder+Abstractor branches feeding a multi-attention decoder). A universal approximation theorem proves that a single Abstractor layer can approximate any continuous function of pairwise relations to arbitrary precision.

## Results

The paper evaluates the Abstractor across three task families using learning curves rather than single-point metrics. On the purely relational object-sorting task, the Abstractor achieves dramatically higher sample efficiency than a standard Transformer, reaching comparable accuracy with far fewer training examples. On the SET card-classification task, the Abstractor significantly outperforms all baselines (CoRelNet, PrediNet, MLP), approaching the performance of a neuro-symbolic model with hand-encoded relational features. On five math problem-solving subtasks (calculus, algebra, polynomial expansion/addition), the Abstractor-based architecture (architecture d) consistently outperforms both a matched-size Transformer and a larger Transformer+ across all tasks, with the symbolic attention variant showing the greatest gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CoRelNet (Kerg et al., 2022), the Abstractor wins decisively: on the asymmetric order-relation task, standard (symmetric) CoRelNet is completely unable to learn the transitive relation, while the Abstractor succeeds; on SET, the Abstractor significantly outperforms CoRelNet in sample efficiency, and the authors note that removing CoRelNet's softmax activation only marginally helps, underscoring the value of the Abstractor's configurable relation activation. Against PrediNet (Shanahan et al., 2020), the Abstractor again wins on both discriminative tasks: PrediNet shows only limited success on the order relation and is substantially outperformed on SET. Against the standard Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), the Abstractor demonstrates dramatic sample-efficiency gains on object-sorting (reaching target accuracy with a fraction of the training data), uniquely benefits from pre-training on a related sorting task (the Transformer does not transfer), and consistently outperforms both a parameter-matched Transformer and a larger Transformer+ on all five math problem-solving subtasks, confirming that the gains stem from the relational inductive bias rather than additional capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Transformer architecture by introducing a novel attention variant (relational cross-attention) that implements the 'relational bottleneck' principle from Webb et al. (2024), which disentangles relational information from object-level features. It departs from prior explicitly relational architectures (CoRelNet, PrediNet, ESBN) that operate as standalone modules by embedding relational reasoning directly into the Transformer's attention mechanism, making it composable with standard encoder-decoder pipelines. The theoretical grounding draws on Altabaa and Lafferty (2024) for inner-product approximation of relation functions, while the architectural design mirrors the multi-head attention structure of Vaswani et al. (2017) with the key modification of replacing value vectors with abstract symbols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled explanation for why Transformers struggle with relational sample efficiency (entangled representations) and offers a concrete architectural fix that yields dramatic gains on synthetic relational tasks. However, all experiments are on small-scale or synthetic benchmarks (48 objects, 70×50 card images, simple math problems from Saxton et al. 2019), leaving open whether the inductive bias provides meaningful benefits at the scale of modern language models or on genuinely complex relational reasoning. The pre-training generalization result—where disentangled representations transfer across related tasks while entangled ones do not—is a compelling conceptual validation but is demonstrated on a single synthetic task. Future work on scalability, interaction with pre-training at scale, and application to open-ended reasoning tasks would be needed to assess broader impact.
