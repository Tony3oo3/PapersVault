## Problem

Most computational models of emergent communication use strictly unidirectional channels (sender to receiver), precluding the conversational repair mechanisms—such as clarification requests and acknowledgements—that are ubiquitous in human dialogue. This paper asks how the nature of emergent languages changes when a feedback channel is added and the communication channel is made noisy, specifically whether repair improves generalization and what effect it has on compositionality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240374/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the basic signaling game, the sender receives a one-hot encoded object (concatenation of attribute values, dimensionality |A|·|V|) and emits a sequence of discrete tokens from a binary vocabulary (|X|=2) of length |M|; the receiver consumes these tokens plus a set of distractor objects and outputs a classification over the candidate set. In the GuessWhat variant, inputs are 768-dimensional ViT-B-16 features extracted from object bounding boxes in naturalistic COCO scenes, and the receiver must discriminate the target from all other annotated objects in the same image. The feedback channel adds a second output stream: the receiver emits one binary token per timestep, which the sender consumes alongside its own previous output at the next step.

## Method

The core contribution is extending the standard Lewis signaling game with a bidirectional feedback channel: at each timestep the receiver GRU produces a binary feedback token that the sender GRU ingests (concatenated with its own previous output) before generating the next message token. Noise is injected by replacing message tokens with a special noise token with probability p_noise, making the corruption directly detectable by the receiver. The sender is trained with REINFORCE (with running-mean baseline and entropy regularization) while the receiver uses cross-entropy, both implemented as single-layer GRUs with layer normalization. The key empirical finding is that the feedback channel substantially improves test accuracy under noise (especially at p_noise=0.5–0.7) and even outperforms doubling the unidirectional message length, yet simultaneously reduces topographic similarity (topsim) scores, indicating less compositional languages. The authors further show that the feedback tokens are not simple noise-detection signals but carry information about the candidate objects, suggesting the agents co-construct meaning during interaction.

## Results

In the basic signaling game (|A|,|V|)=(4,4), |X|=2, |M|=10, p_noise=0.7), models with a feedback channel substantially outperform the unidirectional baseline under noise, with the performance gap peaking at p_noise=0.7 and shrinking at p_noise=0.9 due to unstable convergence. Without channel noise, both model types achieve near-perfect accuracy (~1.0), confirming that feedback does not harm clean-channel performance. However, the feedback models show a substantial drop in topographic similarity (topsim), indicating lower compositionality. These findings generalize to the GuessWhat signaling game with naturalistic COCO images, where the feedback advantage again peaks around p_noise=0.7 and grows monotonically with message length up to |M|=50.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the proposed bidirectional signaling game (with a binary feedback channel from receiver to sender) and the standard unidirectional Lewis signaling game baseline. Under noisy conditions (p_noise ≥ 0.1), the feedback model consistently achieves higher test accuracy across all tested input space sizes, message lengths, and noise levels; the advantage is largest at p_noise=0.7 and for extreme input space sizes (|A|,|V|)=(2,10) and (10,1000). A key efficiency finding is that a feedback model with |M|=10 outperforms a unidirectional model with |M|=20, showing that a feedback channel is more effective than simply doubling channel capacity. When the receiver is forced (via an auxiliary loss) to use the feedback channel only for open clarification requests (signaling noise presence), performance is still better than the no-feedback baseline but worse than the free-feedback model, indicating that the emergent feedback code carries richer information than simple noise detection. Under the alternative noise implementation (token permutation modeling misunderstanding), the absolute accuracy drops more sharply (e.g., mean test_acc ≈ 0.70 vs. 0.89 at p_noise=0.7), but the feedback model still outperforms the unidirectional baseline. Compositionality (topsim) is consistently lower for feedback models across all settings, challenging the assumed link between compositionality and generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard Lewis signaling game (as implemented in Lazaridou et al., 2018 and Chaabouni et al., 2020) by adding a bidirectional feedback channel that enables conversational repair under noisy conditions. While prior multi-directional communication games (Evtimova et al., 2018; Cao et al., 2018; Graesser et al., 2020) allowed information flow in both directions, they did not introduce channel noise that would create pressure for repair mechanisms to emerge. The paper also departs from Jorge et al. (2016), who studied bidirectional signaling with noise but in a way that made the noise undetectable by the receiver, by using an explicit noise token that the receiver can in principle detect and respond to.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that feedback channels improve noise-robust generalization while simultaneously reducing compositionality (as measured by topsim) challenges the widely-held assumption in the emergent communication literature that compositionality is a driver of generalization. This has implications for how we interpret the structure of human languages, which are themselves imperfectly compositional and heavily context-dependent. Key limitations include the use of only binary feedback channels, the absence of noise on the feedback channel itself, and the reliance on relatively small-scale GRU models; future work on larger feedback vocabularies, nested clarification requests, and self-repair vs. other-initiated repair dynamics would be needed to draw stronger conclusions about language evolution.
