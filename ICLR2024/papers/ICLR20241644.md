## Problem

While selecting a subset of unlabeled samples to label and train on is a standard way to reduce labeling costs, the statistically optimal selection strategy has been poorly characterized. Most prior theory assumes unbiased reweighted subsampling (influence-function or leverage-score based), leaving open whether this constraint is necessary, how the optimal scheme depends on the subsampling fraction, and whether selecting fewer samples can actually yield lower test error than training on the full dataset.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241644/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N unlabeled feature vectors (e.g., 2048-d SwAV embeddings from autonomous-vehicle images) and a surrogate model P_su(dy|x) that predicts labels better than random but need not equal the true conditional. It produces a selected subset of size n = γN via a one-parameter family π(x_i) = min(c(γ)·p(⟨θ̂_su, x_i⟩)^α·(1−p(⟨θ̂_su, x_i⟩))^α; 1), where α > 0 upsamples uncertain ('hard') samples and α < 0 upsamples certain ('easy') ones. After acquiring labels for the selected subset, the method trains ridge-regularized logistic regression or least squares and reports hold-out misclassification error.

## Method

The paper develops a unified statistical theory under two complementary asymptotic regimes. In the low-dimensional regime (fixed p, N→∞), they derive closed-form optimal selection schemes for both unbiased and biased non-reweighting cases, proving that unbiased subsampling can be arbitrarily worse than optimal biased schemes (Theorem 1) and that non-reweighting selection can beat full-sample training in a neighborhood of γ = 1 (Theorem 2). In the high-dimensional regime (N, p→∞ with N/p→δ₀), they characterize the test error of ridge-regularized generalized linear models under arbitrary selection via a saddle-point Lagrangian analysis (Theorem 4), revealing that the optimal α depends on δ₀, the regularization λ, and the degree of label-distribution misspecification. The key mechanistic insight is that non-reweighting schemes alter the effective curvature of the expected risk (unlike unbiased schemes which preserve it), and the optimal selection score is computed with respect to the selected-data distribution rather than the full population, making the optimal subset depend non-trivially on the subsampling fraction γ.

## Results

On a real autonomous-vehicle dataset (N=34,345 samples, p=2,048 SwAV embedding dimensions, ridge-regularized logistic regression), biased non-reweighted data selection with a simple constant strategy (λ=0.01, α=0.5, weak surrogate) matches or beats full-sample training error using as few as 40% of the data. In synthetic high-dimensional experiments (N=34,345/p=932 and N=6,870/p=3,000), the one-parameter family π(x)∝[p(1−p)]^α achieves nearly identical test error to full-data training at γ=0.4, with the optimal α depending on the regime (α<0 favored when samples-per-dimension is small). Theoretically, the paper proves (Theorem 1) that the ratio of unbiased to non-reweighting error can exceed any constant M for suitable distributions, establishing that the standard influence-function (unbiased) approach is not merely slightly suboptimal but can be arbitrarily worse.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the influence-function-based unbiased subsampling of Ting & Brochu (2018) and Wang et al. (2018), the paper's biased non-reweighted scheme consistently achieves lower test error in both real-data (Figure 1) and synthetic (Figure 2) experiments; the gap is largest at small γ and can be unbounded in the worst case (Theorem 1). The α=0.5 non-reweighted scheme (which approximates the influence-function score but without reweighting) already outperforms the fully unbiased reweighted version, showing that the reweighting step w_i=1/π_i is the primary source of suboptimality. In contrast to Sorscher et al. (2022), who empirically showed that selecting 'easier' samples (far from the margin) beats full-data training for the binary perceptron, this paper provides a general theoretical framework (Theorem 2, Section 5) confirming that α<0 (upsampling easy samples) is optimal in certain high-dimensional, data-poor regimes, while α>0 (upsampling hard samples) remains better in well-specified or low-dimensional settings. The paper also shows that a weak surrogate model (trained on only ~4% of data) combined with the constant strategy performs nearly as well as a strong surrogate, undermining the assumption that better surrogates always yield better selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the influence-function and leverage-score subsampling literature (Ting & Brochu 2018; Wang et al. 2018; Ai et al. 2021; Ma et al. 2014) by showing that the standard unbiased reweighting assumption (w_i ∝ 1/π_i) is not merely a convenience but a genuine suboptimality: biased non-reweighting schemes can be arbitrarily better (Theorem 1). The high-dimensional analysis builds on the saddle-point framework for regularized M-estimators (Thrampoulidis et al. 2018) to characterize test error under data selection, while the finding that selection can beat full-sample training provides a theoretical grounding for empirical observations in deep learning (Sorscher et al. 2022; Nakkiran et al. 2021). A key conceptual departure from prior work is that optimal selection probabilities depend on the subsampling fraction γ in a non-trivial, self-consistent way, rather than being computed independently of it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that unbiased subsampling—the default in most theoretical and practical data selection methods—can be arbitrarily suboptimal challenges a foundational assumption in the field and motivates revisiting standard pipelines. The one-parameter family (Eq. 6.1) provides a simple, practical recipe that nearly matches full-sample accuracy using only 40% of the data, validated on both synthetic and real (KITTI-360) data. Limitations include the restriction to GLMs and linear models with Gaussian features in the high-dimensional theory, and the minimax analysis for imperfect surrogates is left as a framework rather than a constructive algorithm. Open questions include extending the theory to deep models, developing minimax-optimal selection under surrogate mismatch, and understanding the precise conditions under which α < 0 (selecting 'easy' samples) is preferable.
