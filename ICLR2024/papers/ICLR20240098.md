## Problem

Training robust world models in the reward-free setting requires selecting which environments to explore, but existing curriculum learning and unsupervised environment design methods all require a task-specific reward function during exploration. This paper formalises the problem of reward-free environment selection for world model training via minimax regret and shows it reduces to minimising the maximum latent dynamics error across environments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240098/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB image observations and actions from a UPOMDP with continuous free parameters (e.g., terrain amplitude/length scale, arena size, block count/colour), collecting trajectories via an exploration policy in selected environments. It produces a single DreamerV2-based world model whose recurrent encoder maps all environment parameter settings into a shared latent space, from which task-specific policies are derived post-hoc for arbitrary reward functions. Environment selection is driven by an ensemble-disagreement error buffer updated through imagined rollouts, requiring no reward signal at any stage.

## Method

WAKER (Weighted Acquisition of Knowledge across Environments for Robustness) selects environments for data collection by sampling from a Boltzmann distribution over per-environment error estimates, with a 20% probability of uniform domain randomisation to ensure coverage. The error estimate for each environment is computed as the variance of an ensemble of N latent dynamics models evaluated on imagined rollouts, serving as a proxy for the total variation distance between learned and true dynamics. The key theoretical contribution proves that minimax regret over all environments and reward functions is upper-bounded by the maximum expected TV error of the latent dynamics across environments under an exploration policy, via two applications of the Simulation Lemma. Two variants are provided: WAKER-M biases toward environments with the highest error magnitude, while WAKER-R biases toward environments where error is decreasing fastest. The exploration policy (Plan2Explore or random) is trained in imagination to maximise the ensemble-disagreement objective, and the entire pipeline operates without any reward function.

## Results

On the robustness evaluation (CVaR₀.₁ over 100 randomly sampled environments), WAKER-M with Plan2Explore achieves 0.711 on Clean Up Sort versus 0.397 for Domain Randomisation (DR), a 79% relative improvement. On Terrain Walker Walk, WAKER-M scores 818.0 vs. DR's 748.9, and on Terrain Hopper Hop, 342.0 vs. 278.7. WAKER also improves out-of-distribution generalisation: on the 'Extra Block' OOD test, WAKER-M reaches 0.530 on Clean Up vs. 0.329 for DR, and on 'Steep' terrain, 660.2 vs. 602.8 for Terrain Walker. WAKER-M consistently outperforms WAKER-R, and both variants significantly beat DR when paired with Plan2Explore, while random exploration degrades all methods substantially.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WAKER-M outperforms Domain Randomisation (DR, Tobin et al. 2017) on nearly all tasks across all four domains, with the largest gaps on Clean Up (e.g., 0.711 vs. 0.397 on Sort, a 79% relative improvement) and Car Clean Up (0.894 vs. 0.665 on Sort). Gradual Expansion (GE) performs essentially identically to DR, confirming that merely widening the sampling range over time is insufficient. The Hardest Environment Oracle (HE-Oracle), which samples only the single most complex environment, performs worst on most tasks (e.g., 0.240 on Clean Up Sort, 543.3 on Terrain Walker Walk), demonstrating that focusing exclusively on the hardest environment fails to build a robust world model. The Re-weighting Oracle (RW-Oracle) is a strong baseline on terrain domains (345.5 on Terrain Hopper Hop, slightly above WAKER-M's 342.0) but underperforms WAKER on the Clean Up domains (0.624 vs. 0.894 on Car Clean Up Sort), likely because its handcrafted re-weighting does not sample diverse enough block configurations. WAKER-M (magnitude-based) consistently outperforms WAKER-R (rate-of-reduction-based), aligning with the theoretical prediction that minimising maximum error is the correct objective. All improvements over DR are statistically significant per the rliable confidence interval analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

WAKER sits at the intersection of three research threads: world-model-based RL (DreamerV2), unsupervised environment design (UED) with minimax regret objectives (Dennis et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2021a; Parker-Holder et al., 2022), and ensemble-based uncertainty estimation from offline RL (MOPO, MOREL). The key departure from prior UED work is that all existing methods require a task-specific reward function during curriculum generation, whereas WAKER operates entirely in the reward-free setting by replacing the regret signal with an ensemble-disagreement estimate of latent dynamics error. The theoretical contribution (Proposition 1) bridges the minimax regret objective to a tractable world model error minimisation problem via a double application of the Simulation Lemma, providing the first formal connection between robust policy optimisation and world model quality in the reward-free regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to automatically select environments for reward-free world model training, removing the need for expert-curated curricula or task-specific rewards during pre-training. The practical implication is that a single world model trained with WAKER can support zero-shot adaptation to arbitrary reward functions across diverse environments, a key step toward generalist agents. However, the approach relies on an intrinsically motivated exploration policy (Plan2Explore) to adequately cover the state-action space, which may not scale to high-dimensional or very complex environments. The discrete error buffer for environment parameters also limits scalability to large continuous parameter spaces, and the authors acknowledge that function approximation for uncertainty estimation and reward-free pretraining followed by task-specific finetuning are necessary next steps.
