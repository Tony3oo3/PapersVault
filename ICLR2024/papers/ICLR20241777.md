## Problem

Pretrained language models retain sensitive information (personal data, harmful knowledge) that RLHF-based safety measures merely suppress rather than remove, and model editing methods like ROME are assumed to delete facts directly from weights without systematic verification. This paper introduces an attack-and-defense framework with a threat model in which deletion is considered failed if the sensitive answer can be recovered among a small set of B candidates (B=20 in main experiments), motivated by password-attempt, parallel-pursuit, and data-owner-verification scenarios.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a pretrained transformer (GPT-J-6B, Llama-2-7B, or GPT2-XL-1.5B) and a single-token sensitive question-answer pair, applies model editing (ROME or MEMIT) to delete the answer, then evaluates the edited model under three attacks that each produce a candidate set of size B. The Head Projection attack draws top-k tokens from logit lens distributions across selected intermediate layers, the Probability Delta attack ranks tokens by probability change between consecutive layers, and the Input Rephrasing attack samples outputs across model-generated paraphrases from dipper-paraphraser-xxl. Defense quality is assessed by Attack-Success@B alongside Random Δ-Acc and Neighborhood Δ-Acc to confirm the edit does not damage general model knowledge.

## Method

The core technical contribution is an attack-and-defense framework built on the logit lens interpretability technique, which projects intermediate hidden states onto the vocabulary embedding matrix to reveal information that persists in early layers even after the final output has been edited. Two whitebox attacks exploit this: Head Projection collects top-k tokens from logit lens distributions across a tuned set of layers, and Probability Delta identifies tokens that rise and fall sharply between consecutive layers. A blackbox attack generates rephrased prompts via dipper-paraphraser-xxl and samples outputs to exploit the editing method's imperfect generalization across paraphrases. For defense, the paper introduces a Max-Entropy objective that maximizes the entropy of logit lens distributions across intermediate layers (reducing whitebox attack success from 38% to 2.4% with ROME on CounterFact) and a Head Projection defense using a max-margin loss to push the deleted answer below the k-th highest probability token in each layer's distribution. Surprisingly, a data-augmentation-based defense using paraphrases fails to reduce blackbox attack success, and no single defense protects against all attack types simultaneously.

## Results

With a budget of B=20, whitebox attacks (Head Projection and Probability Delta) recover "deleted" facts from GPT-J edited with ROME + Empty Response 38% of the time, while the blackbox Input Rephrasing attack succeeds 29% of the time. The proposed Max-Entropy defense reduces Head Projection attack success from 22.2% to 1.7% (ROME, CounterFact) and from 41.4% to 2.9% (ROME, zsRE), and also lowers the unforeseen Probability Delta attack to 2.4% on zsRE. However, no single defense is universally effective: the Input Rephrasing defense fails to reduce blackbox attack success, and MEMIT with Empty Response is attacked 89% of the time on zsRE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Fact Erasure baseline (Hase et al., 2023), the Max-Entropy defense substantially outperforms on whitebox attacks: 1.7% vs. 22.2% Head Projection success on CounterFact (ROME) and 2.9% vs. 41.4% on zsRE (ROME). The Empty Response defense (Ouyang et al., 2022), which is the standard RLHF-style approach, is the weakest baseline against whitebox attacks (36.8% Head Projection on CounterFact), and the Max-Entropy defense reduces this to 1.7%. Error Injection (De Cao et al., 2021) is catastrophically vulnerable to whitebox attacks (99.2% Head Projection on CounterFact), confirming that injecting false answers does not delete information. Between editing methods, ROME is more amenable to defense than MEMIT: Max-Entropy achieves 1.7% attack success with ROME but only 34.2% with MEMIT on CounterFact, suggesting multi-layer edits are harder to defend. The Input Rephrasing defense (the paper's own blackbox defense) consistently underperforms all baselines against the blackbox attack, never reducing success below the Empty Response baseline. Constrained Finetuning (Zhu et al., 2020) performs worst overall, with the Max-Entropy defense failing entirely (99.3% Head Projection attack success), indicating that the defense objectives are incompatible with this editing approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the model editing framework of ROME and MEMIT (Meng et al., 2022; 2023) from their original goal of *changing* factual knowledge to the distinct task of *deleting* sensitive information, and introduces an adversarial attack-and-defense lens that prior editing work did not consider. The whitebox attacks are built directly on the logit lens interpretability technique (nostalgebraist, 2020; Geva et al., 2021), which had previously been used only for analysis rather than as an extraction mechanism. The paper explicitly departs from the machine unlearning literature (Cao & Yang, 2015) by arguing that removing the influence of a training pair is the wrong framing for LLMs, where the sensitive content is an undesirable *output* triggered by otherwise harmless prompts. New defense objectives (max-margin head projection, max-entropy over intermediate layers) extend the editing loss to operate on hidden-state representations, not just the final output distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that state-of-the-art editing methods leave 'deleted' information recoverable 38% of the time via simple hidden-state probing challenges the working assumption that suppressing a model's generated output is equivalent to removing the underlying knowledge, with direct implications for GDPR-style right-to-be-forgotten compliance and safety-critical deployments. The negative result that the intuitive Input Rephrasing defense fails against paraphrase attacks (unless the exact same paraphrases are used) highlights a fundamental gap in current editing methods' generalization. No single defense achieves robust protection across all attack types, leaving the core question of whether truly irreversible deletion from transformer weights is achievable as an open problem. The budget-based threat model (B candidates) provides a practical and well-motivated metric that could inform future regulatory standards for model privacy.
