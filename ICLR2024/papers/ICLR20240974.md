## Problem

In practice, RL reward functions are imperfect proxies for true objectives, yet the field lacks rigorous answers to fundamental questions: when is a proxy safe to maximize, what failure modes arise from over-optimization, and how should one best optimize a misspecified proxy? Goodhart's law predicts that beyond a critical point, further optimization of an imperfect proxy degrades true-objective performance, but this phenomenon had not been formally quantified, mechanistically explained, or addressed algorithmically in the RL setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite MDP (states, actions, transition kernel, discount factor γ), a proxy reward R₁, and an upper bound θ on the projected angle between R₁ and the unknown true reward R₀. It outputs a policy that provably avoids Goodharting for all true rewards within angle θ of the proxy, along with a concrete stopping criterion: halt optimization when the cosine of the angle between the current steepest-ascent direction and the projected proxy reward drops below sin(θ)·‖M_τ R₁‖. The occupancy measure η^π is computed either via rollouts or by solving the linear system η = (I − ΠT)⁻¹Πμ, and the projection matrix M_τ onto the |S|(|A|−1)-dimensional policy subspace is precomputed.

## Method

The key geometric insight is that the set of all policies maps to a convex polytope Ω of occupancy measures, turning reward optimization into linear programming: maximizing R over Ω. Goodharting is explained mechanistically as the steepest-ascent path on the proxy reward hitting a boundary facet of Ω and deflecting into a direction that decreases the true reward, with the risk increasing monotonically along the path (Proposition 3, concavity of steepest ascent). The early-stopping algorithm (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) stops at the first point where the optimization direction's angle relative to the proxy exceeds a critical threshold determined by θ, provably guaranteeing zero Goodharting for all rewards in the uncertainty set F_R^θ. Proposition 4 reformulates the worst-case objective as a regularized function ‖η_∥‖ − κ‖η_⊥‖ (parallel/perpendicular components relative to the proxy), whose local maxima are global on Ω, making it practically optimizable. Empirically, Goodharting (measured by Normalised Drop Height) occurs in ~19% of 30,400 sampled MDP-reward configurations across Gridworld, Cliff, RandomMDP, and TreeMDP environments, and the early-stopping procedure eliminates it in all cases at the cost of 10–44% true reward.

## Results

Across 30,400 experiments spanning Gridworld, Cliff, RandomMDP, and TreeMDP environments, a Goodhart drop (NDH > 0) occurs in 19.3% of all sampled configurations, confirming Goodharting is a common but not universal phenomenon. The proposed early stopping method provably prevents Goodharting in all tested cases (NDH = 0), at the cost of losing 10%–44% of true reward on average depending on environment size and type. The choice between Maximal Causal Entropy (MCE) and Boltzmann Rationality (BR) as the optimisation pressure operationalisation has negligible effect on performance, while smaller angle distance θ between proxy and true reward correlates with less reward lost to pessimistic stopping.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external baseline methods; instead, the primary comparison is between naive (unconstrained) optimisation of the proxy reward and the proposed early stopping procedure. Naive optimisation produces Goodharting (NDH > 0) in 19.3% of experiments, while early stopping achieves NDH = 0 in all cases, at the cost of forgoing some true reward. The two operationalisations of optimisation pressure—Maximal Causal Entropy (MCE) and Boltzmann Rationality (BR)—yield nearly identical reward-loss profiles across all environments (e.g., Cliff: 43.82% vs 44.49% mean loss; RandomMDP: 10.09% vs 10.36%), confirming the method's robustness to the choice of training algorithm. The paper also shows that the early stopping criterion is pessimistic: it stops before any reward in the uncertainty set F_R^θ decreases, meaning that when the true reward is well-aligned with the proxy, some achievable true reward is sacrificed. The number of actions |A| has a statistically significant negative correlation with NDH (p < 1e-8), while the number of states |S| does not, suggesting Goodharting is driven by action-space geometry rather than state-space size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the STARC framework (Skalse et al., 2023a) for quantifying reward function differences via projected angles, and on the steepest-ascent method for linear programming (Denel et al., 1981; Chang & Murty, 1989) to provide a geometric account of Goodharting in MDPs. It departs from prior reward-hacking studies (Pan et al., 2021; Zhuang & Hadfield-Menell, 2020; Skalse et al., 2022) that catalog failure modes or prove impossibility results, by instead explaining *why* Goodharting occurs mechanistically (boundary deflection in the occupancy-measure polytope) and using that explanation to derive provably Goodhart-free algorithms. Unlike Gao et al. (2023), who study Goodhart curves in language model overoptimization, this work establishes the phenomenon across diverse MDP environments and connects it to convex geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic, geometry-based explanation for Goodharting in RL and derives the first provably Goodhart-free early-stopping criterion with regret bounds, giving practitioners a principled tool for reward misspecification. However, the early-stopping method is notably pessimistic (losing 10–44% of true reward on average), requires an upper bound θ on the true-proxy angle that is hard to estimate in practice, and depends on computing occupancy measures that scale poorly. Open questions include characterizing other failure modes beyond Goodharting, developing adaptive reward-refinement algorithms, and finding less pessimistic relaxations that retain theoretical guarantees.
