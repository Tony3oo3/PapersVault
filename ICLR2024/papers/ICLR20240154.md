## Problem

In large neural networks, it is difficult to determine whether the model has memorized specific training examples or learned the underlying rule, especially when training data contains noisy or corrupted labels. While grokking (delayed generalization) has been observed on algorithmic datasets, the interplay between memorization and generalization under label corruption—and how regularization methods differentially affect each—remains poorly understood. This paper tackles the problem in a setting where generalizing representations are analytically characterized, enabling a mechanistic disentanglement of the two phenomena.

## Input / Output

The network receives a pair of integers (m, n) from Z_p (p=97), each one-hot encoded to length p and concatenated into a 2p-dimensional input vector. The output is a p-dimensional vector approximating the one-hot encoding of (m+n) mod p. The full dataset contains p² example pairs; a data fraction α is used for training, and a corruption fraction ξ of training labels are replaced with uniformly random labels (symmetric label noise). The 2-layer MLP has quadratic activation φ(x)=x² and width N=500, making the generalizing solution analytically solvable via periodic weight vectors.

## Method

The paper introduces the Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR) of each hidden neuron's weight vectors (computed via discrete Fourier transforms) as a quantitative measure distinguishing generalizing neurons (high IPR, periodic weights matching the analytical solution) from memorizing neurons (low IPR, non-periodic weights). In the Coexistence phase, trained networks exhibit a bimodal IPR distribution, with distinct sub-networks handling generalization and memorization. Weight decay and dropout shift the IPR distribution toward higher values by converting memorizing neurons into generalizing ones, while BatchNorm retains low-IPR neurons but learns to de-amplify their outputs through its γ weights, effectively pruning them. Complementary pruning experiments—removing neurons from lowest or highest IPR—confirm that low-IPR neurons drive memorization of corrupted labels and high-IPR neurons drive generalization, with the network's overall computation being an emergent collective effect of all generalizing neurons.

## Results

On the modular addition task over Z_97 with a 2-layer MLP (N=500, quadratic activation), the paper identifies distinct phases governed by data fraction α and corruption fraction ξ. In the Coexistence phase (no regularization), the network achieves ~100% test accuracy while also memorizing corrupted training labels (~100% train accuracy). With sufficient weight decay (wd=15), the network enters Full Inversion: test accuracy reaches 100% while train accuracy plateaus at 100(1−ξ)% (e.g., 65% for ξ=0.35), meaning the network predicts the *true* labels for all corrupted examples. Pruning experiments confirm the mechanism: removing low-IPR neurons monotonically decreases accuracy on corrupted data (down to 100(1−ξ)%) while preserving test accuracy, whereas removing high-IPR neurons degrades test accuracy while retaining memorization. The IPR distribution shifts from bimodal (Coexistence) to predominantly high-IPR (Full Inversion) as regularization strength increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark a proposed method against external baselines; instead, it compares the *mechanisms* by which three standard regularization techniques (weight decay, Dropout, BatchNorm) and two architectures (2-layer MLP, 1-layer Transformer, 3-layer ReLU MLP) affect the memorization–generalization trade-off under symmetric label noise. Weight decay and Dropout act similarly: they convert low-IPR memorizing neurons into high-IPR generalizing ones, shifting the IPR distribution toward higher values and enabling Full Inversion. BatchNorm operates through a qualitatively different mechanism—it does not eliminate low-IPR neurons (the IPR distribution remains bimodal) but instead learns to assign smaller BatchNorm scale weights (γ_k) to low-IPR neurons, effectively de-amplifying their contribution. For the 1-layer Transformer and 3-layer ReLU MLP, weight decay remains essential for achieving Full Inversion, while Dropout mostly extends the Confusion phase and BatchNorm only supports Coexistence, indicating that the MLP-specific pruning mechanism does not fully transfer to deeper or attention-based architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Gromov (2023), which provided the analytical solution for periodic weights in grokked modular arithmetic networks, enabling the IPR-based quantitative characterization of generalizing versus memorizing neurons. It sits at the intersection of the grokking literature (Power et al. 2022; Nanda et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2022) and the label-noise robustness literature (Zhang et al. 2017; Song et al. 2022), but departs from both by introducing symmetric label corruption into the analytically tractable grokking setup and using the IPR to mechanistically disentangle the two phenomena. The regularization analysis extends Morcos et al. (2018), who showed BatchNorm and Dropout have qualitatively different effects on generalization, by providing a neuron-level explanation for *why* they differ: weight decay and Dropout convert memorizing neurons into generalizing ones, while BatchNorm merely de-amplifies memorizing neurons without eliminating them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic, neuron-level account of how standard regularization methods differentially suppress memorization versus generalization, which is a question that has been largely qualitative in the broader deep learning literature. The 'inversion' phenomenon—where test accuracy exceeds train accuracy because the network corrects corrupted labels—offers a concrete, measurable signature of robust generalization that could inform training diagnostics in more realistic settings. However, the analysis is confined to exactly solvable models (quadratic activation, modular arithmetic), and the training dynamics that produce the observed phases remain unexplained; the authors explicitly defer this to future work. Whether the IPR-based separation of generalizing and memorizing sub-networks generalizes to non-analytically-tractable architectures and real-world datasets is an open question that limits immediate practical impact.
