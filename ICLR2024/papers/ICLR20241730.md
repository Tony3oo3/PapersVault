## Problem

Reinforcement learning is severely sample-inefficient in complex, long-horizon environments like Minecraft, where random exploration rarely reaches rewarding states. Existing hierarchical pre-training methods (e.g., SPiRL with VAE latent skills) fail to scale to high-dimensional open-world settings with large datasets, long action sequences, and large action spaces. The paper asks how to pre-train goal-based models from task-agnostic datasets that provide effective temporal abstractions for downstream RL in such challenging domains.

## Input / Output

The low-level goal-conditioned policy takes a sequence of past observations (128×128 RGB images in Minecraft, processed by an Impala CNN + causal Transformer) and a goal state (512-dim MineCLIP embedding of 16 frames concatenated with summed action one-hot vectors) to predict one-step discrete actions (8641×121 choices). The high-level policy takes the same observation sequence and outputs a single discrete index into N clustered goal centers (e.g., 100 clusters). In Kitchen, goals are 21-dim position vectors and actions are 9-dim continuous. The key structural detail is that goals are formed by concatenating visual embeddings with action information to improve behavioral distinguishability, and the goal space is discretized via t-SNE dimensionality reduction followed by KMeans++ clustering.

## Method

PTGM pre-trains a transformer-based goal-conditioned policy (VPT-2x architecture) via behavior cloning with hindsight relabeling on a large task-agnostic dataset (39M Minecraft frames), where each state is labeled with a future state as its goal. To make downstream RL tractable, the paper clusters goal states in the dataset into N discrete centers using t-SNE + KMeans, converting the high-dimensional continuous goal space into a compact discrete action space for the high-level policy. A goal prior model (same architecture as the high-level policy) is pre-trained to predict the distribution of future goal clusters given the current state, providing an intrinsic KL-divergence reward that regularizes the high-level policy during RL. During downstream task training, the high-level policy (trained with PPO or SAC) selects a goal cluster index at each high-level step, the fixed low-level policy executes k environment steps conditioned on that goal, and the high-level policy is updated on the sum of environment rewards and the KL reward to the goal prior. The critical insight is that the generalization ability of the goal-conditioned policy—trained on diverse large-scale data—allows the goal space to be compressed into a small number of discrete clusters without losing the capacity to elicit varied behaviors, since the same goal can produce different actions in different states.

## Results

On the Kitchen benchmark (4 sequential subtasks), PTGM achieves high success rates on 3 subtasks, comparable to SPiRL, while both substantially outperform BC-finetune. On the 5 Minecraft MineDojo tasks (Log, Water, Cobblestone, Iron-ore, Spider), PTGM is the only method to achieve good success rates on all tasks after 1M environment steps, and is the sole method to complete the challenging Iron-ore task requiring >1K exploration steps underground. In the ablation study, PTGM with 100 goal clusters and KL reward weight α=0.05 achieves the best balance of sample efficiency and stability; removing clustering (continuous 512-dim goal space) causes complete failure, and removing the goal prior increases training variance significantly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PTGM outperforms SPiRL on all 5 Minecraft tasks, with the gap attributed to SPiRL's VAE inability to reconstruct long high-dimensional action sequences and its use of a continuous latent skill space for the high-level policy; on Kitchen, PTGM and SPiRL are comparable (both solve 3/4 subtasks). Against TACO, PTGM achieves higher success rates across Minecraft tasks, benefiting from the discrete goal space and goal prior regularization. VPT-finetune (both adapter and full-model variants) fails entirely on Cobblestone and Iron-ore, likely due to catastrophic forgetting of repetitive block-breaking skills during RL without temporal abstraction; PTGM succeeds on both. Steve-1 (the zero-shot instruction-following agent sharing PTGM's low-level policy) shows strong tree-cutting ability but poor performance on the other four Minecraft tasks; PTGM's high-level policy trained on top of the same goal-conditioned policy substantially improves performance across all tasks, demonstrating the value of the RL fine-tuning stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

PTGM builds directly on Steve-1's transformer-based goal-conditioned policy trained with hindsight relabeling, but repurposes it as a frozen low-level controller within a hierarchical RL framework rather than as a standalone instruction-following agent. It departs from the dominant skill-based pre-training line (SPiRL, TACO) that models behaviors with continuous latent variables via VAEs, instead using goal states from the environment's state space as the behavior variable and discretizing them via t-SNE + KMeans clustering. The goal prior model for KL regularization is conceptually analogous to skill priors in SPiRL but operates over a discrete goal space, and the one-step action prediction architecture (inherited from VPT/Steve-1) replaces the sequence-decoding VAE that prior methods rely on.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PTGM is the first method to successfully scale pre-training-based hierarchical RL to the full Minecraft benchmark, completing all five long-horizon tasks including the extremely challenging Iron-ore task that no baseline solves. The practical takeaway is that one-step goal-conditioned policies with discrete clustered goals are a more scalable architecture than VAE-based skill decoders for high-dimensional, long-horizon environments. Key limitations remain: the goal space is bounded by the offline dataset (unseen skills like milking a cow are impossible), and the approach depends on a good state/goal representation, especially for image observations. Open questions include whether larger internet-scale datasets can expand the skill repertoire and whether learned goal representations can replace the current MineCLIP-based approach.
