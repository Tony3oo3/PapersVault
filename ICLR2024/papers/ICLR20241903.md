## Problem

Existing self-supervised heterogeneous graph learning (SHGL) methods rely on manually defined meta-paths to capture homophily, requiring substantial expert knowledge and incurring computational costs that grow exponentially with meta-path length. Additionally, these methods largely ignore the heterogeneity (cross-type node connectivity), losing discriminative information that is critical for downstream tasks such as distinguishing same-name authors in academic graphs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241903/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a heterogeneous graph G=(V, E, X, T, R) with a node feature matrix X, node type set T, and edge type set R, and requires no pre-defined meta-paths. It outputs a concatenated representation Ẑ = [Z; Z̃] combining homophilous and heterogeneous node embeddings, which are then fed into a logistic regression classifier (for node classification) or a cosine-similarity ranking (for similarity search). A key design choice is the Woodbury identity transformation that reduces the self-expressive matrix computation from O(N³) to O(Nd²+d³+kd), where d is the representation dimension and k is the sparsity of the feature distance matrix, making the method scalable to large graphs.

## Method

HERO captures homophily without meta-paths by learning a self-expressive matrix S* via a closed-form solution to a regularized objective that balances subspace reconstruction, feature-distance-weighted sparsity (favoring nearby neighbors), and an L2 penalty; the resulting homophilous representations Z = S*H are computed efficiently via Woodbury identity. Heterogeneity is captured by a heterogeneous encoder f_θ that aggregates one-hop neighbors of different types per edge type and fuses across all edge types. A consistency loss L_con aligns projected homophilous and heterogeneous representations in a shared latent space while enforcing uniform dimension distribution, and a specificity loss L_spe uses a separate projection head plus a transformation head to preserve each stream's distinct task-related information without conflicting with the consistency objective. The paper proves that S* and Z exhibit a grouping effect (similar nodes yield aligned representations), and that the fused representation Ẑ contains strictly more mutual information with any downstream task T than either Z or Z̃ alone, yielding a lower Bayes error rate bound.

## Results

On four heterogeneous graph datasets, HERO achieves the best node classification performance, with Macro-F1 scores of 92.2 (ACM), 92.4 (Yelp), 93.8 (DBLP), and 75.1 (Aminer), improving by an average of 2.1% over the strongest self-supervised baseline DMG. On four homogeneous graph datasets, HERO also outperforms both semi-supervised (GCN, GAT) and self-supervised (COSTA, G-BT) baselines, e.g., reaching 94.6% Macro-F1 on Coauthor-Physics. For similarity search, HERO improves by an average of 1.8% over DMG on the heterogeneous datasets (e.g., 86.8% Sim@5 on Aminer vs. 83.1% for DMG).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HERO consistently outperforms all 22 baselines across both heterogeneous and homogeneous graph datasets. On heterogeneous graphs, the largest gains are over meta-path-based self-supervised methods: HERO beats DMG (the strongest SHGL baseline) by 1.2–5.0 points in Macro-F1 (e.g., 92.4 vs. 90.8 on Yelp, 75.1 vs. 72.0 on Aminer) and over HGCML/CPIM by 1.8–5.0 points. Against semi-supervised heterogeneous methods (HAN, HGT), HERO leads by 0.7–10.1 points, with the largest margin on Aminer (75.1 vs. 65.3 for HAN). On homogeneous graphs, HERO surpasses the best semi-supervised method GCN by 1.1% on average and the best self-supervised method COSTA on nearly all datasets (e.g., 94.6 vs. 94.0 Macro-F1 on Coauthor-Physics, 93.0 vs. 92.5 on Amazon-Photo). Notably, HERO is the only method that does not require pre-defined meta-paths, yet it outperforms all meta-path-dependent baselines (DMGI, HDMI, HeCo, HGCML, CPIM, HGMAE, DMG), validating the paper's core claim that adaptive homophily capture via the self-expressive matrix is more effective than hand-crafted meta-paths.

## Relation to Prior Work

HERO departs from the dominant meta-path-based paradigm in self-supervised heterogeneous graph learning (SHGL), where methods like DMGI, HeCo, HGCML, and DMG construct homogeneous subgraphs from hand-defined meta-paths to capture homophily. Instead, it repurposes the self-expressive matrix from subspace clustering (Elhamifar & Vidal, 2009; He et al., 2003) as a parameter-free, closed-form mechanism to capture homophily from subspace structure and local neighborhood proximity, while a heterogeneous encoder (analogous to HGT/HAN message passing) captures cross-type heterogeneity. The consistency and specificity losses draw on multi-view representation learning ideas (Barlow Twins, BYOL) to extract shared and distinct information between the two representation streams, and the Woodbury matrix identity is applied to reduce the self-expressive computation from cubic to near-linear in the number of nodes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for expert-defined meta-paths, HERO removes a significant practical bottleneck in SHGL deployment and reduces computational cost that previously scaled exponentially with meta-path length. The theoretical guarantees (grouping effect for homophilous representations, mutual information lower bounds for fused representations) provide a principled justification for why combining homophily and heterogeneity should outperform either alone. However, the method still relies on node feature quality for the self-expressive matrix, and the ~2% average improvement over the strongest baseline (DMG) is modest; whether the approach scales effectively to very large heterogeneous graphs (beyond the 180K-node Freebase) or to dynamic/temporal settings remains an open question.
