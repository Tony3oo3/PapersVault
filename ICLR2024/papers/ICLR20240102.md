## Problem

Inverse game theory seeks payoff parameters that rationalize observed agent behavior as a Nash equilibrium, but prior polynomial-time algorithms are restricted to finite normal-form games with correlated equilibria or specific Markov game variants. No general framework existed for computing inverse Nash equilibria in concave games with continuous strategy spaces, for inverse multiagent reinforcement learning with stochastic oracles, or for the more realistic setting where only sample histories (or lossy observations thereof) are available rather than the equilibrium itself.

## Input / Output

The method takes a parametric game form (strategy spaces, transition dynamics, reward structure) with unknown parameters, plus either a directly observed strategy/policy profile, sample histories of play drawn from an equilibrium's history distribution, or lossy observations obtained by applying a function ρ to those histories. It outputs parameter values θ* minimizing the exploitability of the observed behavior, and in the simulacral setting, additionally a policy profile π* whose induced history distribution matches the observations in expectation. In the electricity market experiment, the concrete input is hourly Spanish electricity prices and aggregate demand (2015–2018), and the output is predicted prices for 2019–2020.

## Method

The core contribution is a min-max (generative-adversarial) formulation of inverse game theory: a 'stabilizer' selects parameters to minimize the exploitability (Nikaido-Isoda potential) of the observed equilibrium, while a 'destabilizer' selects per-player deviations to maximize cumulative regret. For inverse multiagent planning with an exact oracle, this yields a convex-concave saddle-point problem solvable by gradient descent ascent in O(1/ε²) iterations; for inverse MARL with a stochastic oracle, the problem becomes convex-gradient-dominated and is solved by stochastic GDA with policy-gradient updates for the destabilizer in O(ε^{-10}) iterations. The simulacral learning extension introduces a third variable (the simulacrum policy) and an observation-matching loss, producing a three-player min-max problem solvable in polynomial time and polynomially many samples. The approach requires only a differentiable game simulator (returning rewards, transitions, and their gradients) and standard first-order optimization, extending polynomial-time guarantees to concave normal-form games, finite and continuous Markov games, and multiple equilibrium concepts.

## Results

On synthetic Fisher Market games, the algorithm recovers true parameters in 100% of instances for Linear and Cobb-Douglas markets when only budgets are unknown, and in 99.6% for Cobb-Douglas when both types and budgets are unknown. Average exploitability of the computed inverse Nash equilibrium ranges from 0.0004 (Cobb-Douglas) to 0.2240 (Leontief, budgets only), with the worst case still achieving an approximate inverse NE in over 80% of instances. On the Spanish electricity market, the Nash simulacrum achieves a prediction MSE that is twice as low as the ARIMA baseline on the January 2018–December 2020 test set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary empirical comparison is against the ARIMA time-series forecasting method on the Spanish electricity market (hourly prices, Jan 2015–Dec 2020). The Nash simulacrum, modeled as a stochastic Fisher market game between electricity re-sellers and consumers, achieves a mean squared error that is approximately twice as low as ARIMA on the held-out test period (Jan 2018–Dec 2020). Both methods were trained on Jan 2015–Dec 2018 data with hyperparameters tuned on Jan 2017–Dec 2018. The paper does not report a formal head-to-head table for the electricity market (results are shown in Figure 1), but the qualitative claim of a 2× MSE improvement is the headline empirical result. In the synthetic experiments, no external baseline is run; instead, the algorithm is evaluated on its ability to recover known ground-truth parameters and minimize exploitability, with performance varying by game structure (convex-concave vs. non-convex-concave parameterization).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes inverse game theory as a generative-adversarial (min-max) optimization problem, generalizing Waugh et al.'s (2013) maximum-entropy dual from correlated equilibria to Nash equilibria and from single-agent to multi-agent settings. It extends Swamy et al.'s (2021) moment-matching game to the multi-agent case and builds on Daskalakis et al.'s (2020) independent policy gradient analysis to establish polynomial-time convergence for the stochastic (inverse MARL) variant. Unlike prior inverse MARL work (Fu et al. 2021, Yu et al. 2019, Lin et al. 2019, Natarajan et al. 2010) which lacks polynomial-time guarantees, and prior inverse game theory work (Syrgkanis et al. 2017, Kuleshov & Schrijvers 2015) restricted to finite normal-form or Bayesian games, this paper provides a unified min-max framework covering concave normal-form games, finite and continuous Markov games, and a new simulacral learning formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key theoretical contribution is showing that the set of inverse Nash equilibria is convex (via the convex-concave min-max structure) even when the set of Nash equilibria is not, which resolves a potential concern about computational intractability inherited from game theory. The simulacral learning framework unifies inverse MARL and multiagent apprenticeship learning under a single min-max formulation with polynomial sample complexity. However, the O(ε^{-10}) convergence rate for inverse MARL is quite slow in practice, and the empirical validation rests on a single real-world dataset (Spanish electricity) with a single baseline (ARIMA), leaving open whether the advantage generalizes across markets and time horizons. Broader applicability depends on how commonly the gradient-dominated and convex-parameterization assumptions hold in real multi-agent systems.
