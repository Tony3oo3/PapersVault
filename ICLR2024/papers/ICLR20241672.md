## Problem

While generalized (scene-agnostic) methods now exist for static novel view synthesis, no generalized approach has been demonstrated for dynamic scenes from monocular video. All prior dynamic novel view synthesis methods require costly scene-specific appearance optimization (100–384 GPU hours per video), and it is unclear whether current data priors (depth, optical flow, tracking) are sufficient to eliminate this optimization entirely. This paper asks: is generalized dynamic view synthesis from monocular videos possible today?

## Input / Output

The method takes a monocular video of N RGB frames with known camera intrinsics and extrinsics, plus consistent depth estimates (from either ~3 GPU hours of scene-specific depth optimization or a physical LiDAR sensor), and a target camera pose and timestamp. It produces a novel-view RGB rendering at the target pose/time along with a binary dynamic-content mask used to blend static and dynamic renderings. The pretrained GNT weights are never fine-tuned on the test scene, and the method requires zero scene-specific appearance fitting (0 GPU hours), in contrast to baselines needing 8–72 GPU hours.

## Method

The framework decomposes rendering into static and dynamic components blended via a binary mask. For static content, it adapts a pretrained Generalizable NeRF Transformer (GNT) by introducing masked attention in the view transformer: when a 3D sample projects into semantically-segmented dynamic content in a source view, that view is excluded from the attention computation, allowing GNT to find consistency across the remaining unoccluded views. For dynamic content, it lifts 2D dynamic regions from the two temporally closest source frames into paired 3D point clouds using consistent depth, dynamic masks, and cycle-consistent optical flow, then interpolates between them under a linear motion assumption to obtain the target-time point cloud. Statistical outlier removal cleans the point cloud, which is rendered via SoftSplat (splatting-based) or point/mesh rasterization. The key empirical finding is that geometrically and temporally consistent depth is a sufficient condition for pseudo-generalized dynamic NVS: with CD estimates, the method outperforms several scene-specific baselines on LPIPS without any scene-specific appearance optimization, while purely monocular depth (e.g., ZoeDepth) or tracking-based temporal aggregation (TAPIR, CoTracker) are insufficient.

## Results

On NVIDIA Dynamic Scenes (13,992 images), the pseudo-generalized method requires 0 GPU hours of scene-specific appearance fitting yet achieves a full-image LPIPS of 64.29 (×1000), comparable to NSFF (62.11, 48 GPU hrs) and better than DVS (81.93, 36 GPU hrs), while DynIBaR (31.20, 48 GPU hrs) remains the strongest baseline. On DyCheck iPhone (3,928 images), the method achieves mLPIPS of 0.340 with 0 GPU hours, matching Nerfies (0.339, 24 GPU hrs) and HyperNeRF (0.332, 72 GPU hrs), and outperforming T-NeRF (0.379, 12 GPU hrs) and NSFF (0.396, 24 GPU hrs). The key finding is that consistent depth estimates are a sufficient condition to eliminate costly scene-specific appearance optimization while retaining competitive perceptual quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the four weaker baselines (TiNeuVox, Nerfies, HyperNeRF), the pseudo-generalized method wins decisively on all metrics with zero fitting time, e.g., full-image LPIPS of 64.29 vs. 244.2 (TiNeuVox), 204.0 (Nerfies), and 182.0 (HyperNeRF). Against DVS (36 GPU hrs), the method wins on LPIPS (64.29 vs. 81.93) and SSIM (0.922 vs. 0.912) but trails on PSNR (26.15 vs. 27.96). Against NSFF (48 GPU hrs), the method is nearly tied on LPIPS (64.29 vs. 62.11) but loses on PSNR (26.15 vs. 29.35) and SSIM (0.922 vs. 0.934). Against the strongest baseline DynIBaR (48 GPU hrs), the method loses on all metrics, with a notable LPIPS gap (64.29 vs. 31.20), reflecting the cost of forgoing scene-specific appearance fitting. On DyCheck iPhone, the method matches Nerfies and HyperNeRF on mLPIPS (0.340 vs. 0.339/0.332) while using 0 vs. 24/72 GPU hours, and outperforms T-NeRF (0.340 vs. 0.379) and NSFF (0.340 vs. 0.396) on mLPIPS, though it trails on mPSNR and mSSIM across all scene-specific baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: scene-specific dynamic novel view synthesis (NeRFies, DVS, NSFF, DynIBaR) and generalized static novel view synthesis (GNT, pixelNeRF, MVSNeRF). It departs from the scene-specific dynamic NVS literature by eliminating per-scene appearance optimization entirely, instead repurposing a pretrained GNT (adapted with a simple masked-attention mechanism) for static content and depth/flow-based point-cloud lifting for dynamic content. The paper explicitly positions itself as an empirical study asking whether current data priors (consistent depth, optical flow, tracking) are sufficient to replace the costly optimization that scene-specific methods rely on, rather than proposing a new learned architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical finding is that consistent depth estimates serve as a sufficient condition to eliminate scene-specific appearance fitting (hundreds of GPU hours) while still matching or exceeding several scene-specific baselines on perceptual quality (LPIPS). However, the paper also clearly delineates what is *not* yet possible: fully generalized dynamic NVS fails with current monocular depth estimators (ZoeDepth) and tracking methods (TAPIR, CoTracker actually hurt performance), pointing to concrete open problems in depth consistency and temporal aggregation. The negative result that adding more temporal information via state-of-the-art tracking degrades quality is a useful caution for the field, suggesting that the linear motion assumption and accumulated errors in tracking/depth chains are non-trivial bottlenecks that need more sophisticated solutions.
