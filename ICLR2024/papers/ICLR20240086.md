## Problem

The sliced Wasserstein (SW) distance is estimated via Monte Carlo integration over random projection directions, but no prior work has addressed the high variance of this estimator. This paper is the first to bridge the variance-reduction literature with the SW distance literature by introducing control variates specifically designed for the SW setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240086/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two discrete probability measures (e.g., empirical distributions over MNIST digit images at 784 dimensions with ~6000 supports, or 3D point clouds from ShapeNet with 2048 supports) and produces a lower-variance estimate of the SW_p^p distance. The control variates require only the first two moments of each measure, so no additional data beyond the supports and weights is needed. In downstream applications, the estimator feeds into SNGAN training (producing 32×32 or 64×64 images) or point-cloud gradient flows (producing interpolated point clouds).

## Method

For each random projection direction θ, the method fits a 1D Gaussian to each projected measure by minimizing KL divergence (equivalently, moment matching), yielding closed-form means m_i(θ;μ) and variances σ_i²(θ;μ). It then constructs two control variates from the closed-form W2 distance between the fitted Gaussians: a lower bound C_low = (m₁−m₂)² and an upper bound C_up = (m₁−m₂)² + σ₁² + σ₂². The optimal coefficient γ* is estimated from the same L Monte Carlo samples, giving the controlled estimator SŴ − γ̂·mean(C(θ_l) − B). Because the Gaussian fits and control variate expectations have closed forms (Propositions 1–2), the total computational complexity remains O(Ln log n + Ldn), identical to the conventional SW estimator. The approach is further extended to gradient estimation via the Leibniz rule and to non-uniform slicing distributions via importance sampling.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (32×32), LCV-SW with L=10 achieves a FID of 18.67 and IS of 7.74, reducing FID by ~14% over the conventional SW estimator (FID 21.72, IS 7.54). On CelebA (64×64), LCV-SW with L=10 reduces FID from 11.14 to 10.17 (~10% improvement). In point-cloud gradient flows (L=10), LCV-SW and UCV-SW reach a W2 distance of 0.0538×10⁻⁴ at step 6000 versus 0.1164×10⁻⁴ for SW, while maintaining nearly identical computational time (~27s vs ~26s for 8000 steps). The estimated variance of the SW estimator on MNIST digit 0 vs 1 drops from 4700.87 to 0.045 with LCV-SW, a reduction of over five orders of magnitude.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the conventional Monte Carlo SW estimator, which serves as the implicit baseline throughout. LCV-SW consistently outperforms SW across all tasks: it reduces FID by ~14% on CIFAR-10 and ~10% on CelebA at L=10, and by ~3.5% and ~2.7% respectively at L=1000. UCV-SW shows a smaller but still positive effect at L=10 (FID 21.71 vs 21.72 on CIFAR-10) but becomes the best performer at L=1000, achieving the lowest FID on both CIFAR-10 (13.21 vs 14.07) and CelebA (9.51 vs 10.05). In gradient flows, both LCV-SW and UCV-SW converge faster to the target distribution than SW, halving the W2 distance at step 6000 (0.0538 vs 0.1164 at L=10), while their computational overhead is negligible (~4% slower). The authors note that UCV-SW requires more projections to approximate the optimal control variate coefficient γ* well, since its control variate contains two additional random terms (σ₁² and σ₂²) compared to LCV-SW.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges the variance reduction literature (control variates, Owen 2013) with the sliced Wasserstein distance literature (Bonneel et al., 2015), introducing the first control variate estimators for the SW distance. It builds on the classical closed-form Wasserstein-2 distance between Gaussians (Villani, 2008) and the moment-matching Gaussian approximation of projected measures, but repurposes these tools not for deterministic approximation (as in Nadjahi et al., 2021) but as stochastic control variates that reduce Monte Carlo variance while preserving unbiasedness. The deep generative modeling experiments follow the sliced Wasserstein generator framework of Deshpande et al. (2018) and Nguyen & Ho (2023), applying the new estimators within an SNGAN architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SW distance is a workhorse in generative modeling, domain adaptation, point-cloud processing, and variational inference, yet its Monte Carlo estimator's variance has been an unaddressed practical bottleneck—practitioners simply increase the number of projections at linear cost. By showing that a computationally free Gaussian-moment control variate can reduce variance by orders of magnitude (e.g., ~100,000× on MNIST), this work offers a drop-in improvement for any SW-based pipeline without architectural changes. Open questions include whether the Gaussian approximation remains effective for highly multimodal or heavy-tailed projected measures, whether the approach extends cleanly to non-uniform slicing distributions (the paper sketches but does not fully validate this), and whether analogous control variates can be designed for other OT-based divergences beyond the sliced formulation.
