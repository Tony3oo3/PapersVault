## Problem

Minerva demonstrated that pretraining on billions of tokens of mathematical web content dramatically improves quantitative reasoning in language models, but the underlying Math Web Pages dataset was never released publicly. All existing open web datasets employ preprocessing that strips or corrupts mathematical notation, making large-scale training on quantitative web documents unavailable to the research community.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240816/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes raw HTML from Common Crawl WARC files (not pre-extracted WET text) across shards from 2013–2023, and produces 14.7B tokens of filtered English mathematical web text spanning 6.3 million documents. Output documents use Markdown formatting for structural elements (headings, tables, lists) while preserving all equations in native LaTeX, with metadata including source URL and snapshot date. The prefilter reduces the ~200B HTML documents in Common Crawl to under 1% before full extraction is applied.

## Method

The pipeline has five stages: (1) hierarchical prefiltering using math keywords, top-100 LaTeX symbol detection, and a MathScore classifier (a FastText model trained to predict LaTeX presence from surrounding words alone, with all LaTeX removed from features); (2) custom text extraction built on Resiliparse that handles seven distinct LaTeX encodings found in HTML—most critically MathJax with user-defined delimiters, which accounts for ~74% of documents—plus equation/align environments, image-URL-encoded math, MathML, and WordPress plugins; (3) three-layer filtering combining FastText language identification, MathScore probability thresholds (0.17 if LaTeX found, 0.8 if not), and KenLM perplexity (threshold 15,000) trained on ProofPile; (4) SimHash near-deduplication at threshold 0.7; and (5) manual domain blacklisting. Extraction parameters (plain text vs. Markdown, boilerplate sensitivity) are randomized per document to add diversity. Small-scale experiments training 1.4B-parameter Pythia-architecture models show OpenWebMath outperforms The Pile and ProofPile at equal token counts, and a 50/50 ProofPile+OpenWebMath mixture achieves the best perplexity on MATH and GSM8k, surpassing Pythia 1.4B trained on 300B tokens of general data.

## Results

Models trained on 14.7B tokens of OpenWebMath achieve substantially lower perplexity than models trained on the same number of tokens from The Pile or ProofPile across all GSM8k and MATH subsets. The 50/50 Mixture of ProofPile and OpenWebMath performs best overall, e.g., 1.5599 perplexity on MATH (vs. 1.9499 for The Pile and 1.7229 for ProofPile). Notably, both OpenWebMath and the Mixture outperform Pythia 1.4B trained on 300B tokens of The Pile (20× more data) on every perplexity benchmark. On accuracy, OpenWebMath achieves 5.62% on MATH Algebra-Easy (vs. 2.81% for The Pile/ProofPile) and 16.67% on LILA multiarith (vs. 9.77% for The Pile).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against The Pile (14.7B tokens), OpenWebMath wins on every perplexity metric, with the largest gap on GSM8k Prealgebra (1.9075 vs. 2.2032) and MATH Overall (1.5748 vs. 1.9499). Against ProofPile (14.7B tokens), OpenWebMath also wins on all perplexity benchmarks, though the gap is smaller on MATH Counting & Probability (1.5949 vs. 1.5739, where ProofPile actually edges ahead slightly). The 50/50 Mixture of ProofPile and OpenWebMath achieves the best perplexity on nearly all subsets, confirming complementary strengths. Against Pythia 1.4B trained on 300B tokens of The Pile (20× more data), OpenWebMath and the Mixture achieve lower perplexity on all GSM8k and MATH subsets, demonstrating that domain-specific mathematical data is far more effective per token than general web text. However, on LILA multiarith accuracy, Pythia 1.4B (21.80%) still outperforms OpenWebMath (16.67%), suggesting that general pretraining data retains some advantage for code-based arithmetic tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as an open alternative to Minerva's proprietary Math Web Pages dataset (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), which demonstrated the value of large-scale mathematical web text for improving LLM reasoning but was never released. Methodologically, it follows the standard web-data-curation paradigm established by C4, RefinedWeb, CCNet, and The Pile (text extraction → filtering → deduplication), but departs in two key ways: a custom LaTeX-preserving extraction pipeline built on Resiliparse (rather than jusText or Trafilatura) to handle MathJax and other equation encodings, and a novel MathScore classifier that identifies mathematical content from surrounding text alone. The empirical evaluation reuses the Pythia 1.4B architecture and compares against ProofPile and The Pile as baselines, situating OpenWebMath within the existing landscape of mathematical pretraining corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By releasing 14.7B tokens of high-quality mathematical web text with preserved LaTeX, OpenWebMath unblocks a range of research directions—memorization analysis, reinforcement learning for reasoning, and new benchmark development—that were previously gated behind proprietary data. The finding that 14.7B tokens of domain-specific math web data outperforms 300B tokens of general web data on math perplexity is directionally important, though the absolute accuracy numbers remain low and the comparison is limited to a single 1.4B architecture with one training run. Key limitations include English-only coverage, no ablation studies due to compute constraints, absence of associated figures, and the subjective nature of filtering thresholds, all of which leave open questions about optimal curation strategies and multilingual mathematical reasoning.
