## Problem

State-of-the-art deep visual object trackers are highly vulnerable to imperceptible adversarial perturbations in incoming video frames, causing severe tracking failures. Existing defenses adapted from image classification—adversarial training and image preprocessing—either sacrifice clean accuracy, are computationally prohibitive for video, or ignore the temporal context and semantic information from the object template that are critical to the tracking task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240711/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an incoming (potentially perturbed) frame, N=5 historical neighboring frames, and an object template cropped from the first frame. A CLIP image encoder extracts the template's embedding, which is matched against a set of candidate text prompts to select the most semantically relevant text embedding. The output is a reconstructed frame at the tracker's search-region resolution (not the full image), which is then fed to the downstream tracker. The full LRR pipeline runs at approximately 25 fps on an RTX A6000, enabling real-time online defense.

## Method

LRR consists of two modules. First, the Spatial-Temporal Implicit Representation (STIR) extends local implicit image functions (LIIF/DISCO) to the video domain by decomposing pixel reconstruction into a spatial step (aggregating K×K neighboring pixel features across N frames via an MLP) and a temporal step (interpolating across the N-frame sequence via a second MLP), reducing complexity from O(NK²) to O(K²+N). Second, the Language-Driven ResampleNet (LResampleNet) is a CNN that takes per-pixel feature embeddings (from a shared encoder) concatenated with the CLIP text embedding of the object template and predicts continuous coordinate offsets Δp for every pixel. These offsets shift discrete pixel coordinates into the continuous domain, and STIR evaluates the shifted coordinates to produce the reconstructed frame. The language guidance ensures semantic alignment with the tracked object rather than mere deep-feature similarity. STIR and LResampleNet are trained independently with L1 loss against clean counterparts on ~490K video-frame pairs from ImageNet-DET, ImageNet-VID, and YouTube-BoundingBoxes, using FGSM perturbations on a pre-trained SiamRPN++ to generate adversarial training samples.

## Results

On OTB100, LRR with SiamRPN++ Res50 achieves 86.9–89.4% precision under four adversarial attacks (RTAA, IoU, CSA, SPARK), compared to 32.7–75.9% without defense, and 85.6–85.4% with MobileNetV2. On VOT2019, LRR raises EAO from 0.079–0.153 (no defense) to 0.217–0.269 (Res50) and 0.166–0.239 (MobileV2), approaching or exceeding the clean-data EAO of 0.277/0.267. On UAV123, LRR achieves a ~90% relative improvement in precision under SPARK (from 40.8% to 79.3% for Res50; from 22.5% to 79.0% for MobileV2), matching or surpassing clean-data accuracy. Processing speed is ~25 fps for full LRR and ~29 fps for STIR alone, enabling real-time deployment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LRR outperforms all three adversarial fine-tuning baselines (ATFGSM, ATPGD, ATCSA) across every attack, dataset, and tracker. For example, under SPARK on UAV123 with Res50, LRR achieves 79.3% precision versus 69.6% (ATFGSM), 61.0% (ATPGD), and 65.1% (ATCSA); the fine-tuning methods also degrade clean accuracy (e.g., ATFGSM drops OTB100 clean from 91.4% to 85.1%). Against DISCO, the strongest baseline, LRR consistently wins on VOT2019 EAO (e.g., 0.269 vs. 0.248 under SPARK for Res50; 0.239 vs. 0.208 for MobileV2) and on UAV123 precision under most attacks (e.g., 79.3% vs. 76.0% under SPARK for Res50; 79.0% vs. 75.3% for MobileV2). On OTB100, LRR and DISCO are closer, with LRR still leading under CSA (89.4% vs. 83.6%) and SPARK (89.3% vs. 85.7%) for Res50. The paper attributes LRR's advantage over DISCO to its use of spatial-temporal information across multiple frames and language-guided semantic alignment, which DISCO lacks as a purely spatial local implicit function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the local implicit image function (LIIF, Chen et al. 2021) from 2D spatial to a 3D spatial-temporal domain (STIR), and builds on DISCO (Ho & Vasconcelos 2022), which used local implicit functions for adversarial purification in image classification, by adapting the idea to video tracking. It departs from the dominant adversarial-training paradigm for tracking robustness (which sacrifices clean accuracy and requires retraining) in favor of a test-time preprocessing defense that reconstructs incoming frames. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of CLIP-based language guidance (LResampleNet) to enforce semantic consistency with the object template, a mechanism absent from both LIIF and DISCO and from prior tracking-specific defenses that ignore inter-frame temporal context and template semantics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LRR offers a practical, plug-and-play defense that can be inserted before any deployed tracker without retraining, achieving near-clean accuracy under four SOTA attacks at ~25 fps — a meaningful step toward securing real-world autonomous systems that rely on visual tracking. However, the method adds non-trivial computational overhead, has been validated primarily on the SiamRPN++ family (with limited transformer-tracker evidence), and does not address non-noise perturbations such as motion blur. Open questions include whether the language-guided resampling generalizes to the emerging paradigm of language-specified (rather than template-based) tracking, and whether the compute cost can be reduced further for edge deployment.
