## Problem

Computational neuroscience models of synaptic plasticity overwhelmingly assume Euclidean geometry (L2 distance) for weight updates, yet no neuroscience data supports this choice. The paper asks whether the geometry of synaptic plasticity can be inferred from observable weight distributions, and whether the experimentally observed log-normal synaptic weight distributions are consistent with standard gradient descent or require a non-Euclidean geometry.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241417/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of synaptic weight distributions (before and after learning) and tests candidate potential functions by transforming weights into the dual space via ∇φ and checking whether the resulting weight changes are Gaussian. For experimental neuroscience data, the input is a single post-learning weight distribution (e.g., spine volumes from electron microscopy), which constrains but does not uniquely determine the geometry without knowledge of initial weights. The output is a ranking of candidate geometries by how well they explain the observed distribution, with the true geometry identified as the one yielding the most Gaussian dual-space changes.

## Method

Using mirror descent, the paper proves that in the lazy regime (small weight changes relative to initial weights), the distribution of final synaptic weights depends on the potential φ (defining the geometry) but is independent of the loss function and dataset. Specifically, Theorem 1 shows that weight changes in the dual space (∇φ(w∞) − ∇φ(w⁰)) converge to a Gaussian process as network width grows, by an exchangeable central limit theorem applied to the Lagrange-multiplier solution of the mirror descent fixed-point equation. This yields a practical test: the correct potential is the one under which observed weight changes are most Gaussian, while incorrect potentials produce non-Gaussian changes due to the nonlinear transformation between dual spaces. The theory is validated on linear regression, finetuning of pretrained deep networks (ShuffleNet, EfficientNet, ResNet, CORnet spanning 0.3M–53.4M parameters), and RNNs, then applied to experimental spine-volume data to rule out Euclidean geometry and show consistency with negative entropy (multiplicative) updates.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that in mirror descent, the distribution of final synaptic weights depends on the geometry (potential φ) but not on the loss function or dataset, with weight changes in the dual space converging to a Gaussian. Empirically, ΔCDF (integral of absolute CDF difference from N(0,1)) is always ≤ 0.2 and converges toward zero as width increases for linear regression, and remains < 0.2 for finetuning of deep networks (ShuffleNet, EfficientNet, ResNet, CORnet) across all three tested potentials. The magnitude of dual-space weight changes is typically < 0.2, with negative entropy always < 0.02. Applied to experimental spine-volume data (Dorkenwald et al. 2022), the observed bimodal log-space distribution is consistent with negative entropy or 3-norm potentials but rules out Euclidean (2-norm) geometry.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods in the traditional sense; instead, it compares candidate synaptic geometries (potential functions) against each other and against experimental data. The key comparison is between negative entropy (multiplicative/exponentiated-gradient updates), 2-norm (standard Euclidean/additive gradient descent), and 3-norm potentials. The distinction between negative entropy and p-norms is described as 'very pronounced': when the true potential is negative entropy, evaluating weight changes under a 3-norm potential yields clearly non-Gaussian distributions, and vice versa. Applied to the Dorkenwald et al. (2022) spine-volume data, the observed bimodal log-space distribution is consistent with negative entropy (using a two-point mass initialization) and with 3-norm (using a constant + log-normal initialization), but is explicitly ruled out for the Euclidean 2-norm potential. This means the standard assumption of Euclidean synaptic geometry in the computational neuroscience literature is inconsistent with experimentally observed log-normal weight distributions. The paper notes that if initial weights w⁰ are unknown, multiple potentials can fit the data, making before-and-after learning measurements essential for disambiguation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mirror descent framework (Nemirovskij & Yudin, 1983) and its implicit bias analysis for overparameterized models (Gunasekar et al., 2018; Azizan et al., 2021) to a new domain: predicting synaptic weight distributions in biological learning. While prior computational neuroscience work on plasticity (Lillicrap et al., 2016; Richards et al., 2019) has focused on approximating gradient-based credit assignment, it has uniformly assumed Euclidean geometry without justification. The paper also connects to the experimental neuroscience literature on log-normal spine size distributions (Song et al., 2005; Loewenstein et al., 2011; Dorkenwald et al., 2022), which had previously been interpreted as evidence for multiplicative dynamics but not linked to a specific optimization geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, experimentally testable criterion for identifying the geometry of synaptic plasticity: find the dual space in which weight changes are Gaussian. This is significant because it isolates geometry from other components of learning (loss, error signals, credit assignment), making it possible to test geometric hypotheses independently. Key limitations include the requirement for the lazy regime (small weight changes relative to initial weights), the need to know initial weight distributions, and the fact that the comparison to real neural data (Dorkenwald et al., 2022) is qualitative rather than a rigorous statistical test. Open questions include whether the theory extends to the rich regime, how to handle unknown initial weights in practice, and whether the observed log-normal distributions in different brain regions reflect the same or different geometries.
