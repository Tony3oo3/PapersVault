## Problem

Reinforcement learning agents typically learn task-specific policies without the ability to transfer knowledge to related tasks, and existing option-learning methods either require human-designed temporal abstractions or learn options intentionally for a target task. This paper asks whether useful temporally extended actions (options) can be extracted post-hoc from already-trained neural policies, even when learning options was never the original training objective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240588/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input the weights of trained feed-forward neural policies (with ReLU or other two-part piecewise-linear activations) from a set of mastered tasks, along with state-action sequences generated by rolling out those policies. It produces a set of options—each a sub-policy wrapped in a while-loop of z iterations (where z ranges from 1 to the maximum trajectory length)—that augment the agent's action space for downstream tasks. The networks must be small enough (single hidden layer in experiments) that all sub-policies can be enumerated, and the selection is performed under a uniform policy to approximate early-stage learning behavior.

## Method

DEC-OPTIONS maps each ReLU network to a 'neural tree' (analogous to an oblique decision tree) where every internal node encodes a linear threshold on the input and every leaf encodes the full output layer, so each sub-tree defines a sub-policy. Each sub-policy is converted into an option by wrapping it in a while-loop of z iterations, yielding a large candidate set whose size grows exponentially with the number of hidden neurons. A greedy subset-selection procedure then picks options that minimize the Levin loss of a uniform policy over the augmented action space, evaluated on validation sequences from held-out tasks to encourage generalization. Because options execute under a call-and-return model, the Levin loss is computed via a dynamic-programming table that tracks the minimum number of decisions needed to cover each state in a target trajectory. The key insight is that options 'occur naturally' in neural policies: decomposition reveals reusable sub-policies without any intentional option-learning objective during training.

## Results

The paper evaluates DEC-OPTIONS on two hard-exploration grid-world domains using learning curves over 24 independent runs (95% CI). In MiniGrid Four Rooms, Vanilla-RL fails to learn a good policy within the sample budget, and only DEC-OPTIONS converges to an optimal policy in the hardest variant (Four Rooms 3, where agent and goal are in non-neighboring rooms). In ComboGrid, the performance gap between DEC-OPTIONS and all baselines widens with grid size; on the 6×6 grid, DEC-OPTIONS converges to the optimal return of 40 faster than every other method. A qualitative example shows that learned options reduce the number of agent decisions from 39 to 10 in the Four Rooms 3 environment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DEC-OPTIONS outperforms all five baselines on the hardest tasks in both domains. Against PNN (Rusu et al., 2016) and Transfer-PPO, DEC-OPTIONS avoids the interference problem that causes these weight-transfer methods to degrade when tasks differ in initial/goal positions. Modulating-Mask (Ben-Iwhiwhu et al., 2022) performs competitively in the easiest MiniGrid task (Four Rooms 1) but fails to converge to optimal policies as task difficulty increases, whereas DEC-OPTIONS maintains its advantage. Among the option-learning baselines, Option-Critic (Bacon et al., 2017) and DCEO (Klissarov & Machado, 2023) outperform Vanilla-RL DQN in MiniGrid but do not reach optimal policies in Four Rooms 3; ez-greedy (Dabney et al., 2021) shows similar limitations. In ComboGrid, the gap between DEC-OPTIONS and Dec-Options-Whole (the ablation without neural decomposition) grows with grid size, confirming that the decomposition step is critical for transfer effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the mapping of ReLU networks to oblique decision trees (Lee & Jaakkola, 2020; Orfanos & Lelis, 2023) for a new purpose: extracting sub-policies as temporally extended actions (options) for transfer learning, rather than for interpretability or program synthesis. It departs from prior option-learning methods (Option-Critic, DCEO, ez-greedy) by extracting options from already-trained policies rather than learning them from scratch, and from weight-transfer or masking approaches (PNN, Modulating-Mask) by producing discrete temporal abstractions rather than continuous parameter sharing. The option selection mechanism is grounded in the Levin loss framework (Orseau et al., 2018), adapted here to a combinatorial subset-selection problem solved via greedy approximation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that useful temporal abstractions can emerge 'naturally' in trained ReLU policies without explicit option-learning objectives offers a new perspective on what neural policies implicitly encode, potentially enabling knowledge extraction from legacy agents. However, the method is currently limited to small networks with a single hidden layer (due to exponential growth of sub-policies), piecewise-linear activations, and a sequence of related tasks, leaving open whether it scales to deeper architectures or more diverse task distributions. The NP-hard selection problem and the assumption of task similarity are practical constraints that limit immediate applicability to real-world continual learning scenarios.
