## Problem

Existing LLM privacy research focuses almost exclusively on training-data memorization and leakage, leaving the inference-time interactive setting unaddressed: when an LLM receives sensitive information from multiple sources (e.g., email threads, meeting transcripts) and generates output shared with other entities, it may expose private data in inappropriate contexts. The paper asks whether instruction-tuned LLMs can demonstrate the contextual discernment humans naturally apply—choosing whether to disclose information based on who is present, what the purpose is, and what social norms govern the flow.

## Input / Output

The benchmark spans four tiers with distinct input formats: (1) a single information-type description rated on a 4-point sensitivity scale; (2) a factorial vignette or GPT-4-generated short story combining information type, actor, and use, rated on a −100 to +100 privacy-expectation scale; (3) a free-form multi-party scenario (X shares a secret with Y; Y interacts with Z under an incentive) requiring a generated response, an information-accessibility list, and a binary control answer; (4) a 20-turn synthesized meeting transcript containing both a secret (discussed before the relevant person joins) and public information, from which the model must produce personal action items or a group summary. Leakage is detected via exact string matching and a proxy agent (Llama-2-13b-chat) prompted to recover the secret from the response alone.

## Method

CONFAIDE is a four-tier benchmark grounded in Nissenbaum's contextual integrity theory, where each tier adds contextual complexity and demands progressively stronger social reasoning. Tiers 1–2 operationalize the legal study of Martin & Nissenbaum (2016) by rating information sensitivity and information-flow expectations across factorial combinations of information type, actor, and use. Tier 3 introduces theory-of-mind requirements: the model must track which of three parties (X, Y, Z) knows a secret and generate a response that withholds it despite an incentive to share, evaluated through response generation, information-accessibility tracking, and a simplified binary control question. Tier 4 tests a real-world application—meeting summarization and action-item generation—where the model must exclude a secret discussed before the relevant attendee joins while retaining public information, creating a privacy-utility trade-off. The paper evaluates six models (GPT-4, ChatGPT, InstructGPT, Mixtral, Llama-2 Chat, Llama-2) against AMT human annotations and tests chain-of-thought prompting as a mitigation, finding it does not reduce (and sometimes worsens) leakage, indicating the failure stems from a fundamental lack of contextual privacy reasoning rather than a prompt-engineering gap.

## Results

On CONFAIDE, GPT-4 achieves the highest human-alignment correlation (0.86) on Tier 1 (information sensitivity) but drops sharply to 0.10 on Tier 3 (theory of mind as context), while ChatGPT drops from 0.92 to 0.05. In Tier 3, GPT-4 leaks private information 22% of the time (worst-case, string match) versus 93% for ChatGPT and 100% for Llama-2 Chat. In Tier 4 (meeting summarization), GPT-4 and ChatGPT flow sensitive information to inappropriate recipients 39% and 57% of the time, respectively, even when explicitly instructed to preserve privacy. Chain-of-thought prompting does not mitigate these failures and in some cases worsens leakage (e.g., GPT-4 Tier 4 summary leakage rises from 0.39 to 0.40).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 outperforms all other models across every tier, achieving the highest human-alignment correlation (0.86 on Tier 1, 0.76 on Tier 2.b) and the lowest Tier 3 leakage rate (22% worst-case string-match vs. 93% for ChatGPT and 100% for Llama-2 Chat). However, even GPT-4's Tier 3 correlation with human judgments collapses to 0.10, and it still leaks secrets 39% of the time in Tier 4 meeting summaries. ChatGPT, despite strong Tier 1 performance (0.92 correlation), degrades dramatically on Tier 3 (0.05 correlation, 93% leakage), revealing a severe gap between surface-level privacy awareness and actual contextual reasoning. Open-source models (Llama-2, Llama-2 Chat, Mixtral) perform worst overall, with Llama-2 Chat and Llama-2 leaking secrets in 99–100% of Tier 3 scenarios and showing near-zero or negative correlation with human judgments on Tier 2.a. InstructGPT occupies a middle position, performing better than open-source models on Tier 3 binary control questions (0.00 error) but still leaking 79% of secrets in free-form responses. The key takeaway is that no model, regardless of scale or RLHF training, demonstrates reliable contextual privacy reasoning beyond the simplest tier.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes Nissenbaum's Contextual Integrity theory (2004) for LLM evaluation, drawing directly on Martin & Nissenbaum's (2016) legal study that empirically measured human privacy expectations through factorial vignettes, and on McDonald et al. (2020) for structuring multi-party secret-keeping scenarios. It deliberately departs from the dominant LLM privacy literature, which has focused on training-data memorization and differential privacy guarantees, by shifting the lens to inference-time, interactive information flow where the model receives data from multiple sources and must decide what to share with whom. The benchmark also intersects with the growing body of work on theory of mind in LLMs (Sap et al., 2022; Shapira et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023b), positioning ToM as a prerequisite for contextual privacy reasoning rather than a separate capability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results expose a fundamental gap: even heavily RLHF-tuned models like GPT-4 and ChatGPT fail to preserve contextual privacy in realistic interactive scenarios (39% and 57% leakage in Tier 4), and straightforward mitigations—privacy-inducing prompts and chain-of-thought reasoning—do not help and can even worsen leakage. This has immediate practical implications for deployed systems like meeting assistants, auto-response features, and RAG pipelines where models routinely handle multi-source sensitive data. The paper highlights an open gap in inference-time privacy definitions and argues that ad-hoc safeguards are insufficient, pointing toward the need for principled approaches such as explicit symbolic belief tracking (Sclar et al., 2023) to model information accessibility across parties. A key open question is whether any current or near-future LLM architecture can robustly perform the multi-party, multi-turn information-flow tracking that contextual integrity demands.
