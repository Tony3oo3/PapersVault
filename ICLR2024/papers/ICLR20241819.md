## Problem

Protein fitness landscapes are noisy, sparse, and highly non-smooth, causing gradient-based and model-based optimization methods to get stuck in local optima or predict false positives. Prior work either restricts search to small mutational radii or lacks a principled way to regularize the landscape for iterative optimization. Additionally, existing benchmarks (e.g., Design-Bench GFP) are too easy, requiring only one mutation to reach the 99th percentile, masking the true difficulty of extrapolation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a small starting set of protein sequences (one-hot encoded over 20 amino acids, length M) with scalar fitness measurements—typically 400–5,600 sequences representing a restricted fitness percentile range—and produces 128 optimized sequences scored by a held-out CNN evaluator. The starting set is deliberately filtered to be at a specified mutational gap (6–13 edits) from the 99th-percentile sequences, ensuring the method must extrapolate rather than interpolate. Evaluation is in-silico using a 1D CNN oracle trained on the full dataset, following prior convention.

## Method

GGS (Gibbs sampling with Graph-based Smoothing) combines graph signal processing with discrete MCMC. First, a 1D CNN is trained on the noisy starting data, then a kNN graph is constructed over the original sequences plus transductively inferred point mutants (up to 250,000 nodes) using Levenshtein distance. Tikhunov regularization via the graph Laplacian (closed-form solution (I+γL)⁻¹Y) smooths the fitness labels, and the model is retrained on the smoothed data. For sampling, GWG constructs a locally informed proposal distribution from the model's gradients over the 1-Hamming ball, with Metropolis-Hastings acceptance; hierarchical clustering (Reduce) controls the sequence pool across 15 rounds. The key insight is that GWG's proposal quality depends on energy smoothness (Theorem 1 in Grathwohl et al., 2021), and graph-based smoothing at the data level—rather than model-level regularizers like dropout—directly satisfies this requirement, enabling gradient-guided mutations to reliably climb toward higher fitness.

## Results

On the GFP hard benchmark (starting from <30th percentile fitness, 7-mutation gap), GGS achieves a median fitness of 0.74, substantially outperforming all unsmoothed baselines which score between 0.0 and 0.18. On the AAV hard benchmark, GGS reaches 0.60 fitness versus 0.44 for the next best method (AdaLead+GS). GGS demonstrates a 2.5× fitness improvement over its training set in-silico, and graph-based smoothing alone boosts CbAS from 0.18 to 0.57 (3×) and AdaLead from 0.18 to 0.39 on GFP hard.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GGS decisively outperforms all seven baselines on both GFP and AAV hard difficulties. On GFP hard, unsmoothed baselines (GFN-AL, CbAS, AdaLead, BO-qei, CoMS, PEX, GWG) all fail to exceed 0.18 fitness, while GGS reaches 0.74 — a 4–7× margin. The most striking comparison is with GWG without smoothing (0.00 on GFP hard), confirming that the gradient-based sampler requires a smooth energy function to function. Among smoothed baselines, CbAS+GS (0.57) and AdaLead+GS (0.39) benefit substantially from graph-based smoothing but still trail GGS by 0.17 and 0.35 respectively on GFP hard. On AAV hard, GGS (0.60) beats AdaLead+GS (0.44) and CbAS+GS (0.40) by roughly 40–50%. Notably, smoothing does not universally help: BO-qei+GS and PEX+GS show no improvement or slight degradation, suggesting the benefit depends on whether the baseline's optimization procedure can exploit a smoother landscape.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Gibbs With Gradients (GWG, Grathwohl et al., 2021) for discrete MCMC sampling and adapts it to protein sequence optimization, while drawing on graph signal processing (Tikhunov regularization via the graph Laplacian) to smooth noisy fitness landscapes before sampling. It positions itself as complementary to existing protein design methods (CbAS, AdaLead, PEX, GFN-AL, CoMS, BO-qei) by addressing a shared weakness: the noisy, sparsely-sampled fitness landscape that causes gradient-based and model-based optimizers to fail. Unlike concurrent work dWJS (Frey et al., 2023) which smooths via denoising during training, GGS applies discrete graph-based regularization to the data itself, and unlike standard training regularizers (dropout), it explicitly enforces that nearby sequences receive similar fitness predictions to support iterative gradient-based sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical finding is that graph-based smoothing is a general, plug-in technique that substantially improves multiple unrelated protein optimization baselines (e.g., CbAS fitness jumps from 18% to 39% on GFP hard), suggesting the field has been under-regularizing fitness models. The introduced benchmark (mutational gap + fitness range as difficulty axes) exposes that prior benchmarks like Design-Bench are too easy, providing a more rigorous evaluation protocol. Major open questions remain: the optimal smoothing weight γ is landscape-dependent and currently requires grid search, all evaluation is in-silico with a CNN proxy rather than wet-lab validation, and the method's behavior on truly novel protein families with unknown landscape structure is untested. The connection between spectral graph theory and protein fitness landscape characterization is identified as a promising but underexplored direction.
