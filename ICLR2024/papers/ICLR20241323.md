## Problem

Iterative linear system solvers like SOR have tunable parameters (e.g., the relaxation parameter ω) whose optimal values depend on the specific matrix and are often too expensive to compute, forcing practitioners to rely on sub-optimal heuristics. When many related systems must be solved sequentially (as in PDE simulations), it is unclear whether one can learn good parameters online using only the iteration count as feedback, without additional matrix computations. This paper provides the first learning-theoretic treatment of high-precision linear system solvers with end-to-end regret guarantees.

## Input / Output

At each step t the algorithm receives a linear system (A_t, b_t) with A_t symmetric positive-definite and consistently-ordered, a tolerance ε (potentially as small as 10^{-8}), and optionally a scalar context c_t indicating a diagonal shift A_t = A + c_t I_n. The only feedback is the integer iteration count SOR_t(ω_t) (bandit feedback), not the full cost function. The output is the relaxation parameter ω_t ∈ (0,2) chosen before solving, and the algorithm's regret bounds depend only logarithmically on 1/ε and not at all on the matrix dimension n in the asymptotic setting, making them practical for n ≥ 10^5.

## Method

In the deterministic (asymptotic) setting, the paper derives a surrogate upper bound U(ω) on SOR iterations from the spectral radius of the defect reduction matrix under a near-asymptotic convergence assumption, showing U is semi-Lipschitz with a unimodal structure. Tsallis-INF is then run over a discrete grid of ω values, exploiting the semi-Lipschitz structure to achieve Õ(T^{2/3}) regret matching the optimal Lipschitz-bandit rate; in the diagonally shifted setting, a discretized contextual bandit yields Õ(T^{3/4}) regret against the instance-optimal policy. In the semi-stochastic setting (b_t from a truncated Gaussian), the expected SSOR cost is shown to be Õ(√n)·polylog(n/ε)-Lipschitz in ω via an anti-concentration argument, enabling a novel ChebCB algorithm that combines SquareCB with Chebyshev polynomial regression to achieve Õ(T^{9/11}√n) regret against the instance-optimal policy. The key technical insight is converting the non-Lipschitz, discontinuous iteration count into a learnable continuous surrogate (asymptotic setting) or proving Lipschitzness of the expected cost directly (semi-stochastic setting), both while keeping dimension dependence minimal.

## Results

The paper provides theoretical regret bounds rather than empirical benchmark numbers. For SOR parameter tuning against the best fixed ω, Tsallis-INF achieves Õ(T^{2/3}) regret with only logarithmic dependence on precision 1/ε and no dependence on dimension n (Theorem 2.1). In the diagonally shifted setting, a discretized contextual bandit achieves Õ(T^{3/4}) regret w.r.t. the instance-optimal policy (Theorem 2.2), while the novel ChebCB algorithm (SquareCB + Chebyshev regression) achieves Õ(T^{9/11}√n) regret in the semi-stochastic setting (Theorem 3.2). For SSOR under a truncated Gaussian target assumption, the expected cost is shown to be Õ(√n)·polylog(n/ε)-Lipschitz in ω, enabling direct comparison with the true optimal iteration count (Theorem 3.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are against theoretical comparators rather than empirical baselines. The primary comparator is the best fixed relaxation parameter ω (standard online learning), against which the paper achieves the optimal Õ(T^{2/3}) rate matching the Lipschitz bandit lower bound of Kleinberg (2004), despite the surrogate losses being only semi-Lipschitz. In the diagonally shifted setting, the paper compares against the instance-optimal policy ω*(c_t); the discretized Tsallis-INF approach achieves Õ(T^{3/4}) regret, matching the rate of a randomized block-Krylov spectral radius approximation baseline (Appendix A.2), though the latter does not guarantee instance-optimal performance in the semi-stochastic setting. The ChebCB algorithm, despite having a worse theoretical rate (T^{9/11} vs. T^{3/4}), is noted empirically to converge to the instance-optimal policy faster than the discretized Tsallis-INF approach (Figure 2, right). The paper also compares its sample complexity implications with the Goldberg-Jerrum framework of Bartlett et al. (2022), showing matching Õ(1/α²·polylog(n/ε/δ)) sample complexity for the offline ERM setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of data-driven algorithm design (Balcan et al., 2018; 2022; Gupta & Roughgarden, 2017) and classical iterative solver analysis (Young, 1971; Hackbusch, 2016), but departs from prior work in two key ways: it provides end-to-end guarantees covering the full pipeline from data intake to execution (rather than just sample complexity), and it operates under bandit feedback (only the iteration count is observed) rather than full information. The near-asymptotic surrogate loss construction draws on the algorithms-with-predictions framework (Mitzenmacher & Vassilvitskii, 2021; Khodak et al., 2022), while the contextual bandit component (ChebCB) is a novel combination of SquareCB (Foster & Rakhlin, 2020) with Chebyshev polynomial regression. The paper also introduces a semi-Lipschitz bandit analysis that generalizes standard Lipschitz bandit results, and contrasts two theoretical paradigms—algorithms with predictions vs. data-driven algorithm design—on the same problem for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first learning-theoretic treatment of high-precision linear system solvers with end-to-end guarantees, establishing that simple bandit algorithms can provably tune solver parameters using only iteration counts as feedback. The dimension-independent near-asymptotic bounds (Section 2) are practically appealing, but the semi-stochastic results (Section 3) carry strong √n dependence and the overall rates (T^{2/3}, T^{3/4}, T^{9/11}) are slow, limiting near-term practical impact. Key open questions include extending to non-stationary solvers like preconditioned CG in the semi-stochastic setting, handling multiple parameters simultaneously, and exploiting loss structure beyond Lipschitzness to improve rates. The framework also raises the broader question of whether these ideas transfer to other scientific computing primitives beyond linear solvers.
