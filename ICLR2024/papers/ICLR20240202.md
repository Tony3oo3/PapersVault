## Problem

Prior work on rich vs. lazy learning regimes has focused almost exclusively on random Gaussian or Uniform initializations, where the initial weight magnitude (variance) determines the regime. However, biological neural circuits exhibit structured, often low-rank connectivity patterns (e.g., Dale's law, cell-type-specific motifs, local chain motifs) that differ markedly from random initializations. This paper asks: given a fixed initial weight magnitude, how does the effective rank of the initial weight structure bias the learning regime?

## Input / Output

The method takes an RNN with N hidden units and initializes its recurrent weight matrix W_h at a controlled effective rank — either by SVD-truncating a Gaussian matrix to a target rank (rescaled to preserve Frobenius norm) or by sampling from biologically motivated structures (cell-type-specific blocks, EM-derived connectomes, Dale's-law excitatory/inhibitory splits, chain-motif over-representation). Task inputs are temporal sequences from Neurogym (2AF, DMS, CXT) or sequential MNIST. The outputs are three post-training effective-laziness metrics — weight change norm ‖ΔW‖_F, representation alignment (cosine similarity of initial vs. final RSM), and tangent kernel alignment (cosine similarity of initial vs. final NTK) — computed after all runs reach comparable loss.

## Method

The core contribution combines a theoretical result with extensive RNN simulations. Theoretically, in a two-layer linear network with whitened data and a Gaussian teacher, Theorem 1 shows that the expected tangent kernel alignment across tasks is maximized when the initial weight singular values are spread across all dimensions (high-rank), because low-rank initializations are likely nearly orthogonal to the random task direction, forcing larger kernel movement. Proposition 1 provides the exception: a rank-1 initialization aligned with the task's input/output covariance (i.e., W_1^(0) ∝ β^T) yields high kernel alignment even at low rank. Empirically, RNNs (Eq. 1 with ReLU and exponential Euler integration) are trained via gradient descent on four cognitive tasks, with initial rank swept via SVD truncation at fixed Frobenius norm. The same framework is applied to four biologically motivated low-rank structures, all of which produce effectively richer learning (lower alignment, larger weight change) compared to the full-rank Gaussian null. A final set of experiments (Figure 3) confirms that low-rank initializations aligned with dominant task features recover high alignment, supporting the hypothesis that evolutionary or developmental task specialization can produce effectively lazy learning despite low-rank connectivity.

## Results

Across four cognitive tasks (2AF, DMS, CXT, sMNIST), SVD-truncated low-rank initializations consistently produce greater weight change norms and lower representation/tangent kernel alignment (i.e., effectively richer learning) compared to high-rank Gaussian initializations at the same weight magnitude. Biologically motivated low-rank structures (cell-type-specific statistics, EM-derived connectivity, Dale's-law connectivity, chain-motif over-representation) all exhibit lower effective rank than the Gaussian null and correspondingly richer learning, as measured by all three laziness metrics. In the task-aligned setting (Proposition 1), a rank-1 initialization aligned with the dominant task features achieves substantially higher tangent kernel alignment than unaligned low-rank initialization, confirming that low-rank can still yield effectively lazy learning when pre-matched to task statistics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline throughout is the standard random Gaussian initialization (referred to as 'null'), which serves as the high-rank reference. All low-rank variants—whether generated by SVD truncation (Figure 1) or by biologically motivated structural constraints (Figure 2)—consistently show effectively richer learning relative to the Gaussian null: higher weight change norms, lower representation alignment, and lower tangent kernel alignment. This holds across all four tasks and is robust to variations in network size, learning rate, gain, time step, and initialization distribution (Uniform vs. Gaussian). The one exception, predicted by Proposition 1 and confirmed in Figure 3, is when the low-rank initialization is explicitly aligned with the task's dominant feature directions: in that case, tangent kernel alignment is substantially higher (lazier) than both the unaligned low-rank case and, in some settings, comparable to the high-rank Gaussian baseline. No method 'wins' in a traditional accuracy sense; the comparison is about the degree of network change during training at matched final loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established rich/lazy learning regime framework (Chizat et al., 2019; Flesch et al., 2021; Woodworth et al., 2020), which has primarily examined the role of initial weight *magnitude* under random Gaussian or Uniform initializations, by shifting the focus to initial weight *structure*—specifically effective rank—while holding magnitude fixed. The theoretical analysis builds on the two-layer linear network NTK framework (Saxe et al., 2013; Braun et al., 2022), while the empirical validation draws on RNN-based neuroscience task suites (Neurogym, sMNIST) and biologically motivated connectivity constraints (Dale's law, cell-type-specific blocks, EM-derived matrices, chain motifs) that have been studied in the computational neuroscience literature (Song et al., 2005; Aljadeff et al., 2015; Dahmen et al., 2020). The paper carves new ground by introducing 'effective' laziness as a post-training metric and by connecting the low-rank hypothesis in brain connectivity (Thibeault et al., 2024) to a concrete, testable prediction about learning dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that biologically plausible low-rank connectivity structures systematically produce richer (more representation-changing) learning has direct implications for understanding metabolic costs of plasticity, catastrophic forgetting, and the functional specialization of neural circuits across brain regions. The exception—that low-rank initialization aligned with task statistics can still yield lazy learning—offers a mechanistic account for how evolutionary or developmental biases might pre-tune circuits for specific computational demands. Key open questions include whether these trends hold under biologically plausible local learning rules, how they interact with dynamical regime (e.g., edge of chaos), and whether the rank-to-regime relationship extends to deeper networks or longer sequences. The framework also provides a testable prediction for BCI experiments: connectivity rank should predict the degree of neural activity change during new-task learning.
