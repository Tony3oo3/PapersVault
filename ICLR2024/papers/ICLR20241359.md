## Problem

Despite ChatGPT and GPT-4 serving hundreds of millions of users, no large-scale public dataset captures how real users actually interact with these chatbots in natural, multi-turn, multilingual conversations. Existing instruction-tuning datasets are expert-curated, model-generated, or crowdsourced, and differ substantially from organic user behavior in prompt distribution, language coverage, and conversational structure, leaving researchers without a faithful proxy for real-world chatbot usage.

## Input / Output

Each of the 1,009,245 conversations consists of timestamped turns pairing a free-text user prompt (in any of 68 identified languages) with the chatbot's response, augmented with hashed IP addresses, state/country geolocation, and request headers (browser version, accepted languages). Individual API turns are linked into full conversations by matching historical context, IP, and headers, with PII stripped via Presidio and Spacy NER across 10 languages before release. When used for instruction tuning, the raw user–chatbot turn pairs serve as supervised training data to produce WILDLLAMA, a Llama-2 7B chat model evaluated on MT-bench.

## Method

The authors deployed two free chatbot services on Hugging Face Spaces—one powered by GPT-3.5-Turbo and one by GPT-4—collecting chat transcripts, IP addresses, and request headers over a 12-month window (April 2023–April 2024) under a two-step explicit user consent protocol. Individual API turns are linked into full conversations by matching historical conversation content, IP addresses (with relaxed constraints to handle connectivity changes), and request headers, yielding 2,539,614 turns across 196,927 unique users. For the instruction-tuning experiment, they fine-tune Llama-2 7B on WILDCHAT using Vicuna's exact hyperparameters (4× A100 80 GB, effective batch size 128, learning rate 2e-5, max sequence length 2048, 3 epochs) to produce WILDLLAMA, evaluated on MT-bench with GPT-4 as LLM judge. The paper further conducts a systematic toxicity analysis using both Detoxify and the OpenAI Moderation API and identifies seven prominent jailbreaking prompts with their occurrence counts, unique user counts, and success rates.

## Results

WILDCHAT contains 1,009,245 conversations from 196,927 unique users with an average of 2.52 turns per conversation and 68 identified languages. Toxicity analysis reveals 10.46% of user turns and 6.58% of chatbot turns are flagged as toxic by either Detoxify or OpenAI Moderation, with sexual content accounting for 88.51% of toxic user turns. Fine-tuning Llama-2 7B on WILDCHAT produces WILDLLAMA, which achieves a 6.35 average MT-bench Likert score, outperforming Vicuna (6.13) and Llama-2 Chat (6.26) among open-source models, though trailing GPT-3.5 (7.94) and GPT-4 (8.99).

## Baselines & Comparisons

WILDLLAMA (fine-tuned on WILDCHAT) outperforms both Vicuna and Llama-2 Chat on MT-bench average Likert score (6.35 vs. 6.13 and 6.26 respectively), with the largest margin over Vicuna (+0.22). In pairwise LLM-judge comparisons, WILDLLAMA beats Vicuna 30.94% of the time versus losing only 20.00%, but loses to Llama-2 Chat (12.50% win vs. 39.37% loss), likely because Llama-2 Chat includes an RLHF step that WILDLLAMA does not. Both open-source models remain substantially below proprietary baselines: GPT-3.5 scores 7.94 and GPT-4 scores 8.99 on MT-bench. On the dataset side, WILDCHAT surpasses all compared datasets (Alpaca, Open Assistant, Dolly, ShareGPT, LMSYS-Chat-1M) in language diversity (68 vs. 1–65 languages), average user prompt length (295.58 vs. 19.67–94.46 tokens), and toxicity prevalence (10.46% user turns vs. 0.00–3.08%), making it the richest resource for studying real-world toxic interactions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions WILDCHAT as a natural-use-case dataset that fills the gap left by proprietary interaction logs, building directly on the instruction-tuning paradigm established by Alpaca, Vicuna, and LIMA. It is most closely related to the concurrent LMSYS-Chat-1M, but differentiates itself through greater language diversity (68 vs. 65 languages), richer demographic metadata (state, country, hashed IP, request headers), and a substantially higher concentration of toxic and jailbreaking interactions. The data collection methodology departs from prior expert-curated or crowdsourced datasets by deploying a free public chatbot service on Hugging Face Spaces with explicit two-step user consent, capturing authentic multi-turn conversations at scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WILDCHAT provides the field with a large-scale, publicly available resource for studying real-world user-chatbot interactions, toxicity, and jailbreaking dynamics—areas previously accessible only through proprietary logs. The finding that over 10% of user turns are toxic and that simple online jailbreaking prompts achieve success rates above 60% underscores an urgent need for adaptive defense mechanisms. Key limitations include demographic skew toward the IT community (due to Hugging Face hosting) and potential selection bias toward toxic content from anonymity. Open questions include whether the sheer volume of raw interactions is necessary versus carefully curated subsets, and how to generalize findings from this specific deployment context to broader ChatGPT user populations.
