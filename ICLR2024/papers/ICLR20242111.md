## Problem

Query-based black-box adversarial attacks (score-based and decision-based) remain a primary threat in real-world ML-as-a-service settings where model internals are inaccessible. While input-level randomization (RND) has been shown to provide some robustness, it was unclear whether perturbing hidden features at intermediate layers offers superior protection, and no theoretical framework connected the choice of noise location to the model's local gradient geometry. This paper fills that gap by providing both a theoretical analysis and extensive empirical evidence for feature-level randomization.

## Input / Output

The defense is applied at inference time to any pre-trained classifier (VGG19, ResNet50, ViT, DeiT) operating on 224×224 RGB images. At each query, independent Gaussian noise δ ~ N(0, νI) is injected into the activations of one or more selected hidden layers (typically the penultimate layer), transforming the deterministic model f into a randomized model f_rand that maps each input to a distribution over class logits. The clean-accuracy trade-off is controlled by the noise variance ν, with experiments calibrated to allow ~1–4% accuracy drop.

## Method

The core mechanism is a plug-and-play inference-time perturbation: at each forward pass, an independent Gaussian noise vector is added to the output of selected hidden layers before passing to the next layer, requiring no retraining or architectural modification. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that the probability of misleading a score-based attacker positively correlates with arctan of a ratio involving the defense-to-attack noise variance (2ν/μ) and the squared norm ratio of the loss gradient at the hidden layer versus the input. This ratio increases as the input moves toward the decision boundary during an attack, meaning the defense becomes more effective precisely when the attacker is closest to success. Empirically, deeper layers consistently yield higher gradient-norm ratios and thus greater robustness than input-level noise at matched clean-accuracy budgets, and the defense extends naturally to decision-based attacks where the stochasticity of the loss function causes the attacker to misjudge the distance to the decision boundary.

## Results

On ImageNet, the randomized feature defense achieves 22.2% robust accuracy under Square attack (10k queries) on VGG19 with ~2% clean accuracy drop, compared to 17.8% for the randomized input defense (RND). On DeiT/ImageNet, the feature defense reaches 69.1% robust accuracy under Square attack versus 67.2% for RND at ~1% accuracy drop. On CIFAR-10, the gap widens substantially: VGG19 achieves 62.8% robust accuracy under Square attack with feature defense versus only 39.8% with input defense at ~4% accuracy drop. Against decision-based attacks (RayS, SignFlip) on CIFAR-10, the feature defense lifts VGG19's robust accuracy from 0.0% (base) to 15.4% (RayS) and 76.5% (SignFlip), while the AAA defense remains ineffective (0.1% and 5.7% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the randomized input defense (RND, Qin et al. 2021), the proposed feature defense consistently achieves higher robust accuracy across most model-attack combinations. The advantage is most pronounced on VGG19/ImageNet (Square@10k: 22.2% vs 17.8%; NES@10k: 28.4% vs 11.5%) and VGG19/CIFAR-10 (Square@10k: 62.8% vs 39.8%), where the feature defense gains 4–23 percentage points. On ResNet50/ImageNet, the two defenses perform comparably (within 1–2 points), consistent with the paper's observation that ResNet50's gradient norm ratios do not increase much during the attack. Against the AAA defense (Chen et al. 2022), the feature defense is clearly superior for decision-based attacks: on VGG19/CIFAR-10, AAA achieves only 0.1% robust accuracy under RayS while the feature defense reaches 15.4%, and under SignFlip AAA scores 5.7% versus 76.5% for the feature defense. AAA is competitive on score-based attacks (e.g., 27.1% vs 23.3% on VGG19/CIFAR-10 Square), but the paper notes AAA is specifically optimized for score-based attacks and fails in the more realistic setting where the attack type is unknown.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the randomized input defense (RND) of Qin et al. (2021) and Small Noise Defense of Byun et al. (2021) by shifting the stochasticity from the input to hidden features at intermediate layers, and provides a theoretical analysis (via Cauchy distribution of the loss-difference ratio) that was previously only available for input-level noise. It departs from adversarial training and detection-based defenses by offering a training-free, plug-and-play inference-time method, and contrasts with the AAA defense of Chen et al. (2022), which optimizes logits to mislead score-based attackers but fails against decision-based attacks. The key conceptual advance is showing that the gradient-norm ratio between hidden features and input increases as the attack progresses, making deeper-layer randomization progressively more effective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The defense is practically attractive because it requires no retraining, adds negligible latency, and can be applied to any pre-trained CNN or ViT, making it immediately deployable in MLaaS settings where query-based attacks are the primary threat. However, several limitations temper its impact: EOT-based adaptive attacks (averaging M queries) substantially reduce its effectiveness, the 'lucky attack' problem means a single-query evaluation can fool the defense by chance, and the paper does not address transfer-based black-box attacks. Open questions include combining this defense with adversarial training (showing promising but limited gains in Table 4), extending the analysis to transfer attacks, and developing countermeasures against the repeated-query vulnerability.
