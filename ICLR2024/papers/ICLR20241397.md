## Problem

Large-scale vision-language models like CLIP rely on latent tokens to perform visual reasoning, but these continuous high-dimensional vectors lack concept-level explanations. Existing interpretability methods either require additional training and data collection, produce only pixel-level saliency maps without semantic meaning, or generate text rationales that may not reflect the model's actual reasoning. This paper addresses both the interpretation gap and the control gap: how to understand what each token represents and how to intervene on model behavior at inference time without retraining or modifying weights.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a pre-trained CLIP-B/32 ViT processing standard RGB images (224×224 patches yielding 49 visual tokens plus a CLS token). It takes an open-world text vocabulary (ranging from ~2000 to 5000+ words, e.g., extracted from RSICD satellite captions or GPT-generated ImageNet descriptions) and produces a natural language description for every latent token at every layer. For model control, the output is a modified class prediction after targeted token replacement at inference time, with no architectural changes or weight updates.

## Method

The core insight is that a transformer token retains its local semantic information in subsequent layers as long as it does not attend to other tokens. INViTE exploits this by disabling multi-head self-attention from a target layer onward, retaining only the value projection (f_V) and MLP operations—what the authors call 'in-token' operations—and forwarding the token through these to the final layer's representation space. The resulting embedding is then matched via cosine similarity against the text vocabulary to retrieve a natural language explanation for that token. For model control, tokens are identified by their retrieved text (e.g., tokens interpreting to 'word' or 'text' for typographical attacks, or 'car' for entity replacement) and swapped with zero vectors or tokens extracted from other images. Random smoothing noise is added at each layer during the attention-free forward pass to mitigate the distribution shift caused by disabling attention, improving both interpretation fidelity and control accuracy.

## Results

On the typographical attack task (ImageNet, 10 categories), INViTE with random smoothing (RS) achieves 89.2% accuracy on attacked images, improving over the no-intervention baseline (54.0%) by 35.2 percentage points and outperforming the best prior method (Hernandez et al., 2022) which only reaches 63.7%. On CelebA debiasing, INViTE (RS) achieves 83.91% weighted average accuracy versus 58.22% for the unmodified CLIP baseline, with the worst-group (Female Gray Hair) improving from 19.68% to 66.56%. For entity intervention (Highway→Airport on UC Merced), INViTE (RS) shifts 95% of predictions to Airport versus 0% without intervention.

## Baselines & Comparisons

INViTE's interpretation-based intervention consistently outperforms all four saliency-map-based editing baselines on both the typographical attack and CelebA debiasing tasks. On typographical attacks (Table 2), INViTE (RS) reaches 89.2% attacked-image accuracy, beating Raw Attention (81.4%), Raw Attention×Grad (74.6%), Rollout Attention (52.2%), and Rollout Attention×Grad (55.6%) by 7.8, 14.6, 37.0, and 33.6 points respectively. Compared to the prior natural-language interpretation method of Hernandez et al. (2022), INViTE improves attacked-image accuracy from 63.7% to 89.2% (+25.5 points). On CelebA debiasing (Table 4), INViTE (RS) achieves 83.91% weighted average accuracy versus 70.00% (Raw Attention), 66.17% (Raw Attention×Grad), 69.95% (Rollout Attention), and 62.48% (Rollout Attention×Grad), with the largest gain in the worst group (Female Gray Hair: 66.56% vs. 59.50–59.98% for saliency baselines). The consistent margin across both tasks indicates that concept-level text explanations identify the correct causal tokens more reliably than pixel-level saliency maps.

## Relation to Prior Work

INViTE builds on the CLIP vision-language framework and the transformer architecture, but departs from prior interpretation work (NetDissect, MILAN, TCAV, Concept Bottleneck Models) by requiring no additional training, data collection, or manual annotation. Its key conceptual departure is the observation that in-token operations (MLP, LayerNorm) preserve a token's semantic content across layers, enabling a training-free mapping from any intermediate layer to the final CLS output space by simply disabling self-attention. For model control, it contrasts with weight-editing approaches (Santurkar et al., Bau et al.) by operating on latent token representations at inference time rather than modifying model parameters, and outperforms saliency-map-based editing methods (GradCAM, rollout attention) in targeted intervention tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

INViTE offers a practical, zero-training pathway to inspect and edit the reasoning of vision transformers, which is valuable for safety-critical deployments where model behavior must be audited or corrected without retraining. However, the method is validated only on CLIP-B/32 and a handful of datasets, and the random-smoothing heuristic for mitigating distribution shift lacks theoretical grounding, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to larger or deeper architectures. A key open question is whether the assumption that local operations preserve token semantics holds universally, or whether it breaks down in architectures with more aggressive inter-token mixing (e.g., cross-attention in encoder-decoder models).
