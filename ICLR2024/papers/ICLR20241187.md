## Problem

In online marketplaces, congestion arises when multiple users compete for the same item, reducing social welfare. While prices traditionally decongest markets, modern platforms cannot control seller-set prices and users make decisions under partial information, rendering price-based decongestion ineffective. This paper asks whether a platform can learn item representations—which features to display by default—that reduce congestion and improve welfare, using only observable choice data rather than true user preferences.

## Input / Output

The method takes item feature vectors X ∈ R^{m×d}, user feature vectors U ∈ R^{n×d'}, fixed item prices p ∈ R^m, and a dataset of observed markets with user choices S. It produces a binary feature mask μ ∈ {0,1}^d with exactly k ones (selecting which features to reveal), applied uniformly across all items, or equivalently a masking policy π_θ parameterized by θ ∈ R^d from which masks are sampled. The mask is a hard subset selection (not a weighting or ranking), and the choice model assumes non-negative linear preferences so that masking can only decrease perceived value.

## Method

The core contribution is a differentiable proxy welfare objective that decomposes true welfare into a selection term (total price of chosen items, serving as a lower bound on value) and a decongestion term (penalizing items with excess demand), eliminating the need for unobserved true valuations. To enable end-to-end gradient-based optimization over the combinatorial mask space, the method pretrains a bilinear choice-prediction model f on observed data, replaces hard argmax choices with softmax predictions, and parameterizes masks via a Gumbel-top-k sampling procedure with a relaxed top-k layer, yielding a fully differentiable objective optimized with Adam. Theoretical analysis via competitive equilibrium provides sufficient conditions (e.g., item heterogeneity captured in revealed features, small hidden feature magnitudes, or agent preference similarity on hidden features) under which reducing congestion provably improves welfare.

## Results

On MovieLens-100k with d=12 latent features, DbR attains welfare close to the oracle upper bound, with relative gains over random peaking at approximately k=8. For d=100, DbR's relative welfare gain increases steadily with k, reaching its maximum around k=96 (out of 100 features). The number of allocated items is tightly correlated with welfare, confirming that decongestion is the primary driver of improvement. Performance degrades gracefully under noisy or non-CE pricing, with DbR maintaining its relative advantage over baselines across buyer-optimal to seller-optimal price ranges.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DbR against four baselines: price-pred (top-k features most informative for price prediction), choice-pred (top-k features most informative for choice prediction via a bilinear model), an oracle (direct welfare optimization, only computable for d=12 by enumeration), and random (uniformly sampled k-sized masks). DbR consistently outperforms both prediction-based baselines across nearly all values of k on both MovieLens and Yelp. For d=12, DbR approaches oracle welfare, while price-pred and choice-pred remain substantially below. For d=100, choice-pred is competitive in a narrow band around k≈50 but degrades sharply for both small and large k, where price-pred also fails. The random baseline serves as the lower bound; DbR's relative gain over random increases with k for d=12 (peaking near k=8) and continues increasing for d=100 up to k≈96. The synthetic experiments further show that the proxy objective (selection minus decongestion) closely tracks the true welfare oracle, validating the use of prices as a stand-in for unobserved valuations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new problem at the intersection of representation learning and market design: learning *how* items are presented (which features to reveal) to reduce congestion in multi-user markets. It departs from recommender-systems literature that studies *what* to show [7,25,27], from human-centered AI work on single-user representation learning [12,23], and from strategic-learning studies of representations [22,19], by focusing on the multi-user interaction effect of partial information on welfare. Technically, it builds on differentiable discrete optimization tools—Gumbel-top-k reparameterization [15,29] and reparameterizable subset sampling [31]—to make the combinatorial feature-mask selection amenable to end-to-end gradient-based training, and on competitive equilibrium theory [26] for its welfare guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reframes a practical platform-design question—choosing default item representations—as a welfare-optimization problem, showing that the 'obvious' strategy of revealing the most informative features can be welfare-harmful by inducing congestion, while deliberately lossy representations can improve outcomes. The theoretical conditions under which decongestion provably improves welfare (e.g., preference heterogeneity in revealed features, small hidden-feature variation) are interpretable and actionable. Key limitations include the simplified linear-preference user model, unit-supply assumption, and reliance on simulated user behavior in real-data experiments; open questions include extending to soft congestion in digital goods, personalized representations, and more realistic counterfactual learning under decision-dependent distribution shift.
