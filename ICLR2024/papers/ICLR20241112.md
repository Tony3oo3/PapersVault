## Problem

Existing physical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., PHYRE, Virtual Tools) restrict agents to passive observation or a single intervention on a static scene, leaving unexplored the ability to interact with dynamic events in real time. This gap prevents evaluation of three critical capabilities: intuitive (approximate) physics prediction, multi-step planning where each intervention alters subsequent choices, and in-situ intervention where even minor timing deviations cause task failure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241112/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Agents consume a symbolic 12×9 scene matrix encoding object positions (two endpoints for bars, duplicate centers for balls), radii, and binary indicators (eliminable, fixed, joint, spring) rather than raw pixels, with the action space (positions of up to 6 eliminable gray blocks) concatenated as additional input. Outputs vary by planning strategy: planning-in-advance yields a continuous vector of normalized action timings; planning-on-the-fly yields a discrete block-elimination or no-op decision per timestep; the combined strategy yields timings but re-plans after the first executed action. Games run in a pymunk physics simulator rendered at 600×600 in pygame, with a 15-second time limit per attempt.

## Method

I-PHYRE comprises 40 block-elimination games across four splits (basic, noisy, compositional, multi-ball) built on pymunk, where agents must drop all red balls into a hole by removing gray blocks at precise moments. Three planning strategies are formalized and implemented with five model-free RL algorithms (PPO, A2C, SAC, DDPG, DQN) plus supervised classifiers, model-based World Models, and offline Decision Transformers. The key experimental design is zero-shot generalization: agents train only on the basic split and are evaluated on the three harder splits without fine-tuning, isolating genuine interactive physical understanding from data fitting. A 46-participant human baseline and a GPT-4 evaluation provide upper and lower bounds, revealing that current RL agents achieve 80–92% success on basic games but drop sharply on compositional and multi-ball splits, while humans maintain >80% success across all splits.

## Results

Human participants achieve success rates of 92.39% (basic), 90.00% (noisy), 82.83% (compositional), and 82.83% (multi-ball), with average rewards of 898.01, 879.23, 798.69, and 800.97 respectively, compared to oracle rewards of ~971–976 across all splits. The best RL agents (e.g., PPO-I, A2C-I) reach ~860–862 reward on the basic split but drop sharply on compositional and multi-ball splits, where many agents score near or below random (e.g., DDPG-I scores −25 to −55 on most games). Supervised classifiers achieve only 54–60% accuracy on generalization splits (near the 50% random baseline), and GPT-4 scores as low as −29.10 on the compositional split, confirming a substantial gap between current learning algorithms and human interactive physical reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All five model-free RL algorithms (PPO, A2C, SAC, DDPG, DQN) are evaluated under three planning strategies (in-advance, on-the-fly, combined) and consistently underperform humans by a wide margin on compositional and multi-ball splits, where several agents score at or below random. PPO-I and A2C-I are the strongest RL baselines on the basic split (~862 reward), but their advantage erodes on harder splits; DDPG-I is the weakest, frequently scoring below random (e.g., −25 to −55). The model-based World Model (MDRNN) does not improve PPO, SAC, or A2C performance, suggesting RNN-based dynamics prediction is insufficient for the rich physics in I-PHYRE. The offline Decision Transformer learns a degenerate conservative policy that takes no action at all, failing to extract interactive principles from 50 successful + 50 failed trajectories per game. Planning in advance (-I) converges faster and more stably than on-the-fly (-O), while the combined strategy (-C) converges slower than -I but reaches comparable final rewards, indicating that adaptability after the first action is beneficial but not sufficient to close the human–agent gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

I-PHYRE carves new ground at the intersection of intuitive physics benchmarks (PHYRE, Virtual Tools, Block Towers) and multi-step planning (SMP), but departs from both in critical ways. Prior physical reasoning benchmarks restrict agents to passive observation or a single intervention in a static scene, while multi-step planning work (e.g., Toussaint et al.) relies on precise force-based computation rather than intuitive reasoning. I-PHYRE is the first benchmark to simultaneously demand intuitive (approximate) physics, multi-step intervention sequencing, and in-situ action timing, repurposing standard model-free RL algorithms (PPO, A2C, SAC, DDPG, DQN) and supervised classifiers as test agents rather than proposing new methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes a substantial and well-characterized gap between current learning agents and humans in interactive physical reasoning, with the failure analysis revealing that action timing is as challenging as action order—a nuance prior single-step benchmarks could not probe. Key open questions include whether explicit physics modeling (physics-informed networks, world models) can close the gap, how to integrate large pre-trained models with quantitative timing, and whether the findings extend to 3D environments. Limitations include the small game count (40), 2D symbolic-only input, and the absence of a method that actually solves the task well, leaving the benchmark as a diagnostic tool rather than a solved challenge.
