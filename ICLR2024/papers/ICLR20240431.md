## Problem

Existing prompting approaches for LLM reasoning (IO, CoT, ToT) solve each problem from scratch, unable to reuse insights gained from solving similar problems and vulnerable to error accumulation in multi-step reasoning. This paper asks whether analogical reasoning—systematically drawing on solutions to related problems—can be integrated into LLM prompting to improve complex reasoning without task-specific prompt engineering.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240431/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For shortest-path reasoning, the input is a weighted undirected graph encoded as node lists, edge lists, and edge distance lists, and the output is a path with its total distance. For creative writing, four sentences are the input and a coherent four-paragraph passage (each paragraph ending with one given sentence) is the output. For agent planning, a task description and environment state are consumed, and the output is a knowledge-intensive plan that the LLM-Agent carries through each decision step of a multi-trial episode. All analogous problems are generated internally by the LLM, requiring no external knowledge bases.

## Method

Thought Propagation (TP) is a three-module framework—LLM Propose, LLM Solve, and LLM Aggregate—that is plug-and-play compatible with existing prompting methods. LLM Propose generates a set of analogous problems related to the input (e.g., shortest paths to neighborhood nodes of the target node); LLM Solve solves both the input and analogous problems using standard prompting (IO, CoT, or ReAct); LLM Aggregate then leverages the analogous solutions in two complementary ways: (1) reusing them as hints to produce a refined new solution to the input problem, and (2) deriving high-level plans to rectify intermediate reasoning errors in long-trial tasks. The framework supports multi-layer stacking (K-hop analogous problems) for hierarchical refinement, with K=0 recovering standard prompting methods as a special case. The key distinction from self-refinement methods is that TP aggregates experience from *successful* solutions to *different but similar* problems rather than reflecting on failures within the same problem.

## Results

On Shortest-path Reasoning with GPT-4 (0-shot), TP achieves 88% Optimal Rate and 100% Feasible Rate with only 5% Over-Length Rate, outperforming ToT (46% OR, 84% FR, 52% OLR) and IO (78% OR, 100% FR, 10% OLR). On Creative Writing with GPT-4, TP attains a coherent score of 7.99±1.45 and 45% human preference, surpassing ToT (7.68±1.14, 33%) and CoT (6.93±1.51, 15%). On LLM-Agent Planning (ALFWorld), TP-SM achieves 94.78% overall task completion, exceeding Reflexion (79.1%) and ReAct best-of-6 (71%) by 15.7 and 23.8 percentage points respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TP consistently outperforms all baselines across all three tasks. Against IO prompting (Brown et al.), TP gains +10 OR points on GPT-4 shortest-path (88% vs 78%) and +1.8 coherent score on GPT-4 creative writing (7.99 vs 6.19). Against CoT (Wei et al.), TP improves OR by +12 points on GPT-4 (88% vs 76%) and achieves 45% vs 15% human preference in creative writing. Against ToT (Yao et al.), the gap is most dramatic: TP achieves 88% OR vs ToT's 46% on GPT-4 shortest-path, and 94.78% vs 71% task completion on ALFWorld. Against BaG (Wang et al.), TP gains +11 OR points on GPT-4 (88% vs 77%). Against ReAct (Yao et al.), TP-SM reaches 94.78% overall completion vs ReAct best-of-6's 71%. Against Reflexion (Shinn et al.), TP-SM improves overall completion by 15.7 points (94.78% vs 79.1%), and notably TP-SE (without self-reflection) still achieves 83.68%, demonstrating that analogical planning from *successful* similar tasks is more effective than reflecting on one's own failures. Against BULTER (Shridhar et al.), TP-SM outperforms by 72.8 points on overall completion (94.78% vs 22%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the prompting-based LLM reasoning lineage (IO prompting, Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thought, ReAct) but departs from their shared 'reason from scratch' paradigm by introducing a general analogical reasoning framework. Conceptually, it draws on cognitive science's derivational analogy (Carbonell, 1985) and positions itself against self-refinement methods like Reflexion, which reflect on failures in the *same* task, whereas TP aggregates successful solutions from *similar but different* tasks. It also distinguishes itself from retrieval-augmented LLMs by generating analogous problems internally rather than querying external databases, and from prior analogical reasoning work (knowledge-graph-based, domain-specific) by proposing a task-agnostic, plug-and-play framework compatible with any existing prompting method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TP addresses two fundamental limitations of current LLM reasoning—failure to reuse insights from similar problems and error accumulation in multi-step chains—through a simple, modular architecture that can be layered on top of any prompting method without task-specific engineering. The consistent 12–15% absolute gains across three structurally different tasks (graph optimization, open-ended writing, long-horizon agent planning) and three LLM backends suggest the analogical mechanism is genuinely general rather than task-specific. Open questions include the marginal (and token-expensive) gains from 2-layer vs. 1-layer TP, the counterintuitive 5-shot < 1-shot degradation, and whether the approach scales to problems where analogous problems are harder to identify or where the solution space is less structured.
