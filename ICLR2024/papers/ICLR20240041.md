## Problem

Standard infinite-width limits of neural networks (NNGP) produce fixed kernels that cannot learn representations from data, causing them to underperform finite networks. Yang et al. (2023) resolved this with Deep Kernel Machines (DKMs) that retain representation learning, but only demonstrated the approach on small fully-connected regression tasks with ~10 input features. Extending DKMs to convolutional image classification is non-trivial because the standard inducing-point schemes from the GP/DGP literature require access to intermediate features, whereas DKMs propagate only Gram matrices through layers.

## Input / Output

The model consumes grayscale 28×28 images (MNIST) or RGB 32×32 images (CIFAR-10/100) and outputs a class label via a GP with a categorical (softmax) likelihood. Rather than propagating feature vectors through layers, the architecture propagates P×P Gram matrices (normalised outer products of features), with the spatial dimension of each image (e.g. 32×32=1024 locations) expanded into separate rows/columns of these matrices. A ResNet-20-like architecture with skip connections (convex combinations of Gram matrices) and global average pooling at the top collapses the spatial dimension before classification.

## Method

The convolutional DKM kernel is defined as the covariance between outputs of a single convolutional BNN layer, computed by first applying an arccos kernel (mirroring ReLU) to the previous layer's Gram matrix to obtain an Ω matrix, then performing a convolutional averaging over spatial offsets (Eq. 11). The key technical contribution is a novel inter-domain inducing point scheme: since inducing points cannot be full images (making G_ii intractable at 10240×10240 for just 10 points), the authors introduce non-spatial inducing points whose outputs are computed by convolving learned 'mixup' matrices C^ℓ (which form artificial patches from linear combinations of inducing inputs) with the same convolutional weights as the spatial points. This allows P_i^ℓ to vary per layer and keeps the Gram matrix blocks at manageable sizes (P_i^ℓ × P_i^ℓ for the inducing block). Training maximises the sparse DKM objective (a GP-ELBO plus KL regularisation terms) over the inducing Gram blocks and mixup parameters using Adam, with batch-normalisation analogues applied to Gram matrices and ZCA whitening on inputs.

## Results

The convolutional DKM achieves 99.3% test accuracy on MNIST, 92.7% on CIFAR-10, and 72.1% on CIFAR-100 with the largest model (512/1024/2048 inducing points per block), representing state-of-the-art performance for kernel methods. Training the largest model on CIFAR-10/100 takes approximately 77 GPU hours on a single NVIDIA A100, which is 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than computing full NNGP/NTK/Myrtle kernels (e.g., Myrtle-10 alone requires ~508 GPU hours on a V100 for the full CIFAR-10 dataset). The model uses a ResNet-20-mirroring architecture with an inter-domain inducing point scheme, ZCA whitening, and a categorical likelihood.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The convolutional DKM (92.69%) outperforms all 11 compared kernel methods on CIFAR-10. Among parameter-free methods (NNGP/NTK variants), the closest competitor is Adlam et al. (2023) with Tuned Myrtle10 DA CG at 91.2%, a 1.5-point gap; the original NNGP (Novak et al., 2018) lags far behind at 77.43%. Among parameterized kernel methods, the strongest baseline is Supervised CKN (Mairal, 2016) at 89.8%, still 2.9 points below the DKM; convolutional DGPs (Blomqvist et al., 2018; Dutordoir et al., 2020) achieve only 75.9–76.2%, and SOVI Conv GP (Shi et al., 2020) trails at 68.2%. The paper notes that the comparison is not entirely apples-to-apples since NNGP/NTK methods compute the full training kernel matrix while the DKM uses an efficient inducing point scheme, but the DKM's 77 GPU-hour training cost is still 1–2 orders of magnitude less than the ~316–508 GPU hours required by the most efficient Myrtle kernels on the full CIFAR-10 dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Deep Kernel Machine (DKM) framework of Yang et al. (2023), which modified the NNGP infinite-width limit to retain representation learning, from small fully-connected regression networks to convolutional image classification. The convolutional kernel is derived from the infinite-width CNN analysis of Novak et al. (2018) and Garriga-Alonso et al. (2018), while the key technical novelty is a new inter-domain inducing point scheme (inspired by Lázaro-Gredilla & Figueiras-Vidal, 2009) that avoids the intractability of image-sized inducing points and the incompatibility of standard inducing patches (which require intermediate features that DKMs do not propagate). The paper also introduces convolutional analogues of batch normalisation, multiple likelihoods, and top-layer choices not previously explored in the DKM setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By achieving 92.7% on CIFAR-10 and 72% on CIFAR-100 — state-of-the-art for pure kernel methods — the paper provides concrete evidence that representation learning is the critical component missing from standard NNGPs, narrowing (though not closing) the gap between theoretical infinite-width approaches and finite neural networks. Key limitations include the restriction to 32×32 images (no ImageNet), use of double-precision arithmetic, and a 77 GPU-hour training cost, all of which constrain immediate practical adoption. Open questions include whether the inter-domain inducing point scheme and the Bayesian representation learning limit can be made to scale to high-resolution datasets, and whether single-precision or mixed-precision implementations can preserve the theoretical guarantees while dramatically reducing compute.
