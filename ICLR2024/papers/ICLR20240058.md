## Problem

Prior evaluations of LMs' inductive reasoning rely on standard input-output prompting, which conflates pattern matching with genuine rule induction and provides no interpretability into the model's reasoning process. This paper asks how LMs' inductive reasoning—specifically the iterative process of proposing, testing, and refining hypotheses—compares to human inductive reasoning, and what specific strengths and limitations emerge when LMs are used as hypothesis proposers versus rule appliers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240058/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of seen input-output exemplars (ranging from 3 to 14 depending on the dataset) along with a task description, and produces an induced rule in a task-specific format: a JSON object classifying objects for ACRE, a quasi-synchronous context-free grammar for MiniSCAN, or a natural-language description for List Functions and MiniARC. The induced rule is then 'compiled' by a task-specific symbolic interpreter (deterministic intersection checker, grammar parser, or Python code interpreter) into an executable function that generates predictions on unseen examples. Critically, the interpreter has no access to the seen exemplars, so the rule must be self-contained.

## Method

The core contribution is iterative hypothesis refinement, a three-step neuro-symbolic loop: (1) the LM proposes N candidate hypotheses (textual rules) conditioned on seen exemplars and prior feedback; (2) a task-specific symbolic interpreter scores each hypothesis by its accuracy on the seen examples, and the highest-scoring rule is selected; (3) feedback consisting of the selected rule's incorrect predictions is fed back to the LM to generate refined hypotheses. This process repeats up to T iterations (T∈{1,3}, N∈{1,5}). The key design choice is the separation of roles: the LM serves as a hypothesis proposer while an external symbolic interpreter (grammar parser, code executor, or deterministic function) serves as the verifier, rather than relying on the LM to both generate and evaluate its own rules. This hybrid approach significantly outperforms pure-LM baselines (self-refine) and standard IO prompting on three of four datasets, while revealing that LMs are strong hypothesis proposers but weak rule appliers.

## Results

Using GPT-4 with iterative hypothesis refinement (T=3, N=5), the method achieves raw accuracy of 82.5% on ACRE, 93.3% on MiniSCAN, 71.2% on List Functions, and 18.7% on MiniARC. Task accuracy (fraction of tasks solved perfectly) reaches 59.0%, 85.0%, 61.2%, and 14.6% respectively. The method achieves the strongest performance on 3 out of 4 datasets, with the notable exception of MiniARC raw accuracy where IO prompting (33.1%) outperforms rule-based approaches, likely due to pattern-matching-heavy tasks and limited visual understanding in text-only models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares iterative hypothesis refinement against three baselines: standard IO prompting, self-consistency (SC, N=5), and self-refine (SR, T=3, N=5). On ACRE, the best configuration (T=3, N=5) achieves 82.5% raw accuracy, outperforming IO (64.0%), SC (65.0%), and SR (70.0%) by 12.5–18.5 points. On MiniSCAN, the gap is even more dramatic: 93.3% raw accuracy versus 61.7% (IO), 61.1% (SC), and 46.3% (SR), with task accuracy jumping from 0% for all baselines to 85.0%. On List Functions, the method leads with 71.2% raw accuracy over IO (65.1%), SC (65.0%), and SR (67.4%). The one exception is MiniARC raw accuracy, where IO prompting (33.1%) and SC (31.3%) both outperform the best rule-based configuration (18.7%), though the method still leads on task accuracy (14.6% vs. 13.8% for IO). The authors attribute the MiniARC gap to pattern-matching-heavy tasks where direct IO prediction is more effective, and to the inherent limitations of text-only models on visual reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the iterative hypothesis generation framework from latent language optimization (Andreas et al., 2018) and program synthesis with execution feedback (Chen et al., 2023a; Olausson et al., 2023), but applies it specifically to evaluating inductive reasoning in LMs via symbolic interpreters rather than improving task performance. It is closest in spirit to the concurrent 'Hypothesis search' work (Wang et al., 2023a), but deliberately shifts focus from performance gains to diagnosing *how* and *why* LMs succeed or fail at induction. The evaluation protocol also draws on classical Bayesian concept learning (Tenenbaum et al., 2011; Ellis, 2023) and instruction induction (Honovich et al., 2023), while contrasting with standard IO prompting, self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023b), and self-refine (Madaan et al., 2023) baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a systematic diagnostic framework that reveals a critical dissociation: LMs excel at proposing rules but fail to apply them, are brittle to even minimal perturbations, and produce qualitatively different rules than humans. This strengthens the case for neuro-symbolic architectures that pair LMs with external verifiers, and highlights that current IO-prompting evaluations may overestimate true inductive capability by conflating pattern matching with rule understanding. Open questions include whether dynamic exemplar selection, longer contexts, or fine-tuning can close the rule-application gap, and whether the brittleness to noise and unfamiliar representations reflects a fundamental architectural limitation or a training-data artifact.
