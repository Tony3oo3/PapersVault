## Problem

Neural networks use distributed representations while symbolic systems use structural ones, and existing neural-symbolic methods combine them only in ad hoc, task-specific ways without bridging the fundamental representational gap. This paper asks whether a 'transitional representation'—one that retains high-dimensional detail while implicitly encoding compositional structure—can be learned unsupervised from raw visual observations, mimicking the cognitive transition from perception to symbolic thought.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240792/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 2D image (H×W×C) or 3D voxelized shape as input and produces N_P soft binary masks (each in [0,1]^{H×W×C}) whose linear sum reconstructs the input, along with a set of neural logic variable embeddings R = {r_1, ..., r_{N_P}} and multi-ary predicate embeddings (1-ary for parts, 2-ary for part-pairs) that are clustered into prototype dictionaries. The decomposition is iterative over K diffusion steps, with N_P parameter-shared model copies generating one mask each in parallel and communicating through a 'broadcast' mechanism that feeds other players' current outputs as context.

## Method

The core architecture is a game-theoretic SMLD diffusion model where N_P copies of a U-Net act as players in a cooperative-competitive game: each player refines its mask over K Langevin steps to maximize a GT loss (reconstruction + overlap penalty + resource quota + L2 norm), while a scoring network approximates the optimal move given the current state including other players' masks. An online prototype clustering module implements an EM algorithm inspired by the Unigram Language Model: predicate head mappers (conv + global pooling + linear layer) embed parts and part-pairs into latent space, K-Means is run periodically on a FIFO memory bank of embeddings, and a cross-entropy loss aligns dictionary prototypes to cluster centroids, maximizing the likelihood of the dataset as a 'visual corpus.' A PPO reinforcement learning stage then fine-tunes the unsupervised model using a heuristic shape score (continuity × solidity × smoothness) as reward, pushing decompositions toward human-interpretable shapes. Two evaluation metrics—Clustering Information Gain (parsimony of learned predicates) and the shape score—are shown to correlate with human judgments.

## Results

On the three unsupervised part-segmentation datasets, TDL achieves CIG of 58.0 (LineWorld), 68.5 (OmniGlot), and 54.6 (ShapeNet5), roughly doubling the best baseline (UPD: 36.3, 42.8, 25.4). Shape scores are similarly dominant: 82.6, 77.6, and 60.1 vs. 42.8, 37.4, and 22.6 for UPD. On the symbol-grounding tasks, TDL reaches 78.4 IoU / 74.8% accuracy on LW-G and 75.9 IoU on OG-G, compared to 46.2/28.7% and 48.9 for the strongest baseline. Transfer learning to unseen ShapeNet categories (e.g., Bed, Lamp, Sofa, Table) shows CIG gains of ~30 points over the no-pre-training setting, confirming reusability of learned predicates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TDL outperforms all three baselines (DFF, SCOPS, UPD) by a wide margin on every metric across all six evaluation settings. On CIG, the gap is roughly 22–30 points (e.g., 58.0 vs. 36.3 on LineWorld; 68.5 vs. 42.8 on OmniGlot; 54.6 vs. 25.4 on ShapeNet5), indicating the baselines' reliance on pre-trained visual backbones fails on abstract objects lacking edges, colors, or textures. Shape scores show an even larger gap (~40 points), confirming that TDL produces human-interpretable parts while baselines produce arbitrary segments. On the symbol-grounding task LW-G, TDL achieves 78.4 IoU and 74.8% relation accuracy versus 46.2/28.7% for UPD; the baselines' relation prediction heads fail to converge because their segmentations are incorrect. On OG-G, TDL reaches 75.9 IoU versus 48.9 for UPD. The ablation (w/o RL) shows that even without PPO tuning, TDL retains most of its advantage, with the RL step primarily boosting shape score by ~10 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant neural-symbolic paradigm of combining symbolic modules with neural networks at the architecture level (e.g., differentiable SAT solvers, symbolic controllers) by instead proposing a 'transitional representation' that sits between distributed neural embeddings and explicit symbolic predicates. The decomposition mechanism is built on SMLD (Song et al., 2021) for iterative mask refinement, while the dictionary learning step adapts the online prototype clustering paradigm of Caron et al. (2018) and the EM formulation of the Unigram Language Model to visual parts. The game-theoretic framing of multi-part decomposition draws on Gemp et al. (2021), who modeled PCA as a Nash equilibrium, but extends it to a cooperative-competitive setting with explicit resource and overlap penalties. Unlike prior unsupervised part segmentation methods (DFF, SCOPS, UPD) that rely on pre-trained visual backbones to detect edges and textures, TDL learns compositional structure purely from reconstruction and clustering objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a long-standing gap in neural-symbolic AI: how to learn symbolic structure (parts, relations) from raw perception without supervision, rather than bolting symbolic modules onto neural networks. The key limitation is that TDL requires sufficient data volume to form stable clusters and assumes linear composition of parts, restricting its applicability to real-world scenes with occlusion and non-linear rendering. The authors identify promising future directions including cross-domain transitional representations (e.g., robotics trajectories as compositions of actions), large-scale neural-symbolic pre-training, and unsupervised discovery of hidden logical rules via association-rule-style likelihoods. The validation is confined to abstract/synthetic datasets (LineWorld, OmniGlot, ShapeNet5), so whether the approach transfers to natural images with rich visual confounders remains an open question.
