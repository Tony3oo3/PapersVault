## Problem

Large language models exhibit unhuman-like behavioral characteristics in decision-making tasks—for example, GPT-3 relied heavily on exploitative strategies and stopped learning after a few trials, whereas humans employ diverse exploration strategies and continue improving. The paper asks whether this behavioral discrepancy can be fixed by adapting LLMs to serve as accurate cognitive models of human decision-making.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241230/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of structured text prompts that encode all information a participant would have on a given trial: for decisions-from-descriptions, explicit outcome probabilities and values for two machines; for decisions-from-experience, a list of previously observed rewards plus the remaining trial horizon. The frozen LLaMA-65B processes each prompt and its final hidden-layer activations (a 65B-parameter embedding) are extracted per participant and trial. The linear probe then outputs a binary choice probability (Machine 1 vs. Machine 2), evaluated via negative log-likelihood or accuracy on held-out human choices.

## Method

CENTaUR freezes the entire LLaMA-65B backbone and fits only a regularized logistic regression layer on top of its final hidden-layer embeddings, trained via maximum likelihood with L2 regularization (α selected by grid search over 100-fold cross-validation). The key insight is that the pre-trained transformer already encodes rich, task-agnostic representations of the experimental scenarios, and a single linear readout suffices to align these representations with human behavioral data. Because embeddings for different paradigms (choices13k, horizon task, experiential-symbolic task) all reside in the same 65B-parameter space, a model finetuned jointly on two tasks can generalize to a third unseen task without any additional training. To capture individual differences, a mixed-effects structure (random intercepts per participant and embedding dimension) can be added to the linear layer, substantially improving per-subject fit while retaining superiority over domain-specific cognitive models.

## Results

On the choices13k dataset (13,000+ gamble problems, 14,711 participants), CENTaUR achieves a negative log-likelihood (NLL) of 48,002.3, outperforming the domain-specific BEAST model (NLL = 49,448.1) and the unfinetuned LLaMA (NLL = 96,248.5). On the horizon task (60 participants, 67,200 choices), CENTaUR achieves NLL = 25,968.6 versus the hybrid exploration model's NLL = 29,042.5 and LLaMA's NLL = 46,211.4. On the hold-out experiential-symbolic task (98 participants, 8,624 choices), CENTaUR achieves NLL = 4,521.1, beating both random guessing (NLL = 5,977.7) and unfinetuned LLaMA (NLL = 6,307.9). In accuracy terms (Appendix A.3), CENTaUR reaches 65.18% on choices13k and 83.5% on the horizon task, surpassing GPT-4 (59.72% and 80.3% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CENTaUR outperforms all five baselines on both primary datasets. Against the unfinetuned LLaMA, finetuning yields a dramatic improvement: NLL drops from 96,248.5 to 48,002.3 on choices13k (a ~50% reduction) and from 46,211.4 to 25,968.6 on the horizon task (~44% reduction). Against the domain-specific cognitive models, CENTaUR achieves a statistically significant advantage (p < 0.001): NLL of 48,002.3 vs. BEAST's 49,448.1 on choices13k, and 25,968.6 vs. the hybrid model's 29,042.5 on the horizon task. The BERT and randomly-initialized Transformer baselines both beat chance but fall short of CENTaUR, confirming that the pre-trained LLaMA representations carry meaningful behavioral information beyond generic word embeddings. On the hold-out experiential-symbolic task, CENTaUR (NLL = 4,521.1) substantially outperforms both random guessing (5,977.7) and unfinetuned LLaMA (6,307.9), and qualitatively reproduces the human tendency to overvalue symbolic over experiential options—a pattern LLaMA fails to capture. In the accuracy-based comparison (Appendix A.3), CENTaUR also surpasses GPT-4 on both datasets (65.18% vs. 59.72% on choices13k; 83.5% vs. 80.3% on horizon task), though GPT-4 outperforms unfinetuned LLaMA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work showing that large language models exhibit some human-like behavioral characteristics in psychological experiments (Binz & Schulz, 2023; Shiffrin & Mitchell, 2023; Coda-Forno et al., 2023), but departs from prior work by explicitly addressing the behavioral discrepancies through finetuning on domain-specific psychological data. The method draws on the foundation model paradigm (Bommasani et al., 2021) and the broader practice of adapting pre-trained models via lightweight finetuning (Sanh et al., 2019; Ouyang et al., 2022), but applies it to a novel domain: using behavioral data from a century of psychological experiments as the adaptation signal. Conceptually, it also builds on prior work showing LLM embeddings predict neural and behavioral responses in linguistic settings (Schrimpf et al., 2021; Kumar et al., 2022), but extends this to non-linguistic cognitive domains like risk-based and exploration-based decision-making.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a concrete path toward domain-general cognitive models by demonstrating that a single finetuned LLM can model multiple decision-making paradigms and generalize to an unseen task, which has been a long-standing goal in cognitive science (Newell, 1992; Yang et al., 2019). The ability to use ML explainability techniques on the finetuned model to identify what traditional cognitive models miss (e.g., context-dependent loss aversion, stickiness) offers a new tool for theory development. However, significant limitations remain: the model is fragile to trivial prompt modifications, only a linear layer is finetuned, and generalization is demonstrated on a single hold-out task. Key open questions include whether scaling to more tasks and using full-network adaptation (e.g., LoRA) would improve robustness and generalization, and whether the approach transfers beyond the two-armed bandit and gamble-choice paradigms studied here.
