## Problem

While RLHF has been used to produce widely deployed LLMs like ChatGPT and Claude, there is limited understanding of how each pipeline stage (SFT, reward modelling, RL) affects out-of-distribution generalisation and output diversity. Prior work has not rigorously disentangled whether RLHF's apparent generality comes from the training method or simply from model scale, and no systematic analysis has measured diversity across syntactic, semantic, and logical dimensions at each pipeline step.

## Input / Output

The paper evaluates two tasks: summarisation (input: Reddit post; output: abstractive summary) and instruction following (input: free-form text instruction; output: natural language response). For diversity measurement, the authors sample K=16 outputs per input at temperature 1 across N=500 test inputs, then compute per-input diversity (diversity within each input's output set) and across-input diversity (diversity of one output per input pooled together). Generalisation is assessed via GPT-4 preference win-rates against human-annotated references on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution test sets, with the generalisation gap (ID minus OOD performance) as a controlled metric.

## Method

The paper conducts a controlled empirical comparison of three fine-tuning methods—SFT (cross-entropy on reference outputs), RLHF (PPO with a KL penalty of β=0.05 against the SFT policy, optimising a reward model trained on human preference pairs), and Best-of-N (N=16 samples from SFT filtered by the RM)—using LLaMA 7B as the base model on both summarisation (TL;DR → CNN/DailyMail shift) and instruction following (AlpacaFarm Self-Instruct → AlpacaEval and a novel Sequential Instructions set). Diversity is measured with three externally validated metrics capturing distinct dimensions: expectation-adjusted distinct N-grams (syntactic), Sentence-BERT embedding cosine similarity (semantic), and NLI-based entailment/contradiction counts (logical). The key finding is an inherent tradeoff: RLHF improves both ID and OOD absolute performance over SFT, with the advantage growing as distribution shift increases (most pronounced on the harder Sequential Instructions task), but RLHF substantially reduces per-input diversity and modestly reduces across-input diversity, evidencing a form of mode collapse. Adjusting the KL penalty coefficient does not recover this tradeoff—higher KL penalties reduce both performance and diversity.

## Results

On summarisation (LLaMa 7B), BoN-16 outperforms RLHF which outperforms SFT on both in-distribution (TL;DR) and out-of-distribution (CNN/DailyMail) GPT-4 preference-vs-reference scores, with similar generalisation gaps across methods. On instruction following, RLHF outperforms both BoN-16 and SFT across all datasets, and on the harder Sequential Instructions OOD task, RLHF generalises substantially better than SFT. Head-to-head GPT-4 winrates show RLHF and BoN-16 each improve by ~3.5% over SFT from ID to AlpacaEval OOD. On per-input diversity (EAD, Sentence-BERT), RLHF scores substantially lower than SFT, while across-input diversity differences are smaller; NLI diversity shows no meaningful difference between methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three fine-tuning methods: SFT, RLHF (PPO with KL penalty β=0.05), and BoN-16 (rejection sampling with a reward model). On summarisation, BoN-16 achieves the highest absolute performance both ID and OOD, outperforming RLHF, which in turn outperforms SFT; however, the generalisation gap (ID − OOD) is similar across all three, and BoN-16's advantage is attributed to the reward model generalising near-perfectly (75.8% ID vs 71.6% OOD accuracy). On instruction following, the ordering reverses: RLHF outperforms BoN-16 on all datasets, and the gap widens on the harder Sequential Instructions OOD task where RLHF generalises much better than SFT. On diversity, RLHF substantially reduces per-input output diversity compared to SFT on both EAD and Sentence-BERT metrics, while BoN-16 maintains diversity similar to or higher than SFT, indicating the diversity loss is driven by the RL optimisation rather than the reward model. Across-input diversity shows a smaller but still present reduction for RLHF, providing the first rigorous empirical evidence of cross-input mode collapse from RLHF. Adjusting the KL penalty coefficient does not recover the diversity–generalisation tradeoff: higher KL penalties reduce both performance and per-input diversity simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the standard RLHF pipeline established by Stiennon et al. (2022) and Dubois et al. (2023, AlpacaFarm), using the same training procedures, datasets, and base models (LLaMA 7B, OPT). While prior work (Khalifa et al. 2021; Perez et al. 2022) noted diversity reduction from RLHF using simple token-level metrics like self-BLEU, and Stiennon et al. (2022) performed limited OOD evaluation, no prior work systematically disentangled the effects of each pipeline stage (SFT, RM, RL) on both generalisation and diversity across multiple tasks and metrics. The paper's contribution is a comprehensive empirical evaluation framework rather than a new training method, positioning itself as the missing analysis of how the widely-deployed RLHF pipeline actually shapes model behaviour.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a concrete tradeoff between OOD generalisation and output diversity in current RLHF fine-tuning, providing practitioners with evidence-based guidance on method selection (RLHF for chatbots facing large distribution shifts, SFT for creative/diverse generation tasks). The negative result that increasing the KL penalty worsens rather than improves diversity challenges the intuitive assumption that staying closer to SFT preserves diversity, suggesting the tradeoff may be more fundamental than a hyperparameter issue. Key open questions include whether this tension is inherent to RL-based fine-tuning or a deficiency of PPO specifically, and whether techniques from the deep RL diversity literature (max-entropy RL, mutual information objectives) can recover diversity without sacrificing performance. Limitations include reliance on GPT-4 as a proxy for human evaluation, 7B-scale models only in the main results, and only two task domains.
