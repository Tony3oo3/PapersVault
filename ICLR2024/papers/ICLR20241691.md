## Problem

Superhuman AI agents like chess engines produce moves that are near-optimal but incompatible with weaker partners: when a less-skilled agent must take over from the current state, it may be unable to find effective follow-up moves. The paper asks how to design AI that maintains high performance while remaining skill-compatible—i.e., robust to being interrupted and replaced by a much weaker agent at any point in a sequential decision-making task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241691/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a chess board position, a trained model of the weaker partner (MAIA at a target skill level, e.g., 1100 or 1900), and a framework configuration specifying the collaboration protocol. In Stochastic Tag Team (STT), the output is a full chess move, with a fair coin flip before each move determining whether the senior or junior acts. In Hand and Brain (HB), the output is restricted to a piece-type selection (e.g., 'knight'), after which the weaker partner stochastically selects the specific move from its own distribution conditioned on that piece type. This stochasticity in both frameworks prevents the senior from perfectly predicting the junior's actions.

## Method

Three distinct methodologies create skill-compatible senior agents: (1) TREE augments MAIA with MCTS, exploring future states guided solely by MAIA's policies and values so the search inherently accounts for the junior's propensities; (2) EXP is a gold-standard look-ahead agent that, in STT, simulates all four 2-ply bitstrings and selects the move maximizing expected win probability, while in HB it selects the piece type maximizing expected win probability over MAIA's conditional move distribution; (3) ATT fine-tunes LEELA's policy and value networks via back-propagation on 10,000 self-play games generated by LEELA+MAIA teams in the target framework, using a learning rate of 10⁻⁵ over 10,000 iterations. The unifying design principle is an interruption objective: the agent must win games despite being randomly replaced by a weaker counterpart, which dissuades 'engine moves' that strand weaker agents while still promoting high-level play against a superhuman opponent senior.

## Results

In the Stochastic Tag Team (STT) framework, all three skill-compatible agents (TREE, EXP_T, ATT_T) achieve win-shares above 50% against LEELA (56.5%, 66.5%, and 55.0% respectively), with EXP_T performing best. In the Hand and Brain (HB) framework, TREE leads at 60.0%, followed by EXP_H (56.5%) and ATT_H (55.0%). Critically, in regular head-to-head chess, these same agents are significantly weaker than LEELA (TREE: 0.5%, EXP_T: 14.0%, ATT_T: 12.5%), demonstrating that their collaborative advantage stems from skill-compatibility rather than raw playing strength.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is LEELA (an open-source AlphaZero implementation at ~3050 Elo), which serves as the state-of-the-art chess AI opponent. All three proposed methodologies (TREE, EXP, ATT) defeat LEELA in both collaborative frameworks with statistically significant margins (p < 0.05), despite being substantially weaker in conventional chess—TREE in particular scores only 0.5% against LEELA in regular play yet wins 56.5% in STT and 60.0% in HB. The secondary baseline is MAIA1100 (a human-like engine at ~1100 Elo), used as a lower-bound comparison: when MAIA itself is the focal senior, it scores only 7.5% in STT and 27.5% in HB, confirming that the gains of the proposed agents come from explicit skill-compatibility design rather than mere weakness or similarity to the junior partner. EXP_T achieves the highest overall score (66.5% in STT) by combining strong immediate tricking of the opponent junior (1.38 loss induction) with helping its own junior (1.88 when the opponent senior intercedes), while TREE compensates for its raw weakness through larger adaptation to and synergy with its lower-skilled partner.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the human-AI collaboration literature (Carroll et al. 2019; Strouse et al. 2021) by shifting from simultaneous multi-agent collaboration to inter-temporal compatibility, where a weaker agent must be able to take over from a stronger agent's partially completed state at any point. It builds directly on the MAIA family of human-like chess engines (McIlroy-Young et al. 2020) as the instantiation of weaker partners and on LEELA (an AlphaZero variant) as the superhuman baseline. The three proposed methodologies—MCTS guided by the partner's policy (TREE), explicit expectation maximization over the partner's move distribution (EXP), and fine-tuning via self-play in the collaborative framework (ATT)—are novel compositions of existing techniques (MCTS, lookahead, RL fine-tuning) applied to a new objective function that balances raw performance against robustness to partner substitution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes skill-compatibility as a formally measurable property distinct from raw task performance, providing concrete frameworks (STT, HB) and three distinct methodologies for achieving it. A key open question is generalization beyond chess and beyond MAIA-style neural partners: the authors show compatibility fails across radically different architectures (MAIA vs. Stockfish), suggesting style alignment matters as much as skill level, and they explicitly note that testing with actual human players remains future work. The finding that a much weaker engine (TREE, <1% vs. LEELA) can outperform a stronger one (ATT, 12.5% vs. LEELA) in collaborative settings challenges the assumption that raw strength monotonically helps in multi-agent contexts, with implications for how we design AI systems intended to operate alongside humans of varying skill.
