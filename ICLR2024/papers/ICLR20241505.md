## Problem

PSRO-based methods for finding Nash equilibria in large two-player zero-sum games can increase exploitability between iterations, making early termination potentially arbitrarily worse than the initial solution. Furthermore, because standard PSRO only adds pure (deterministic) best responses, games whose Nash equilibria are mixed may require enumerating all pure strategies before convergence. The paper addresses both the non-monotonic exploitability problem and the slow population growth caused by adding only pure strategies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241505/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an extensive-form game specification (with perfect recall) and an initial policy population, then iteratively trains RL best-response policies against the current restricted distribution. The output is a mixed strategy (restricted distribution) over the accumulated population of trained policies, along with an exploitability value measured against worst-case opponents outside the population. A key property is the anytime guarantee: the algorithm can be stopped at any iteration and the returned restricted distribution is no more exploitable than the previous one, which is critical for large games like Starcraft where full convergence is infeasible.

## Method

The paper introduces three algorithms building on each other. Anytime Double Oracle (ADO) replaces PSRO's single restricted game with two per-player restricted games where the player is constrained to their population but the opponent is unrestricted, guaranteeing monotonically non-increasing exploitability and convergence in at most the number of pure strategies. Anytime PSRO (APSRO) makes ADO scalable to large games by approximating each restricted game's solution via a no-regret algorithm (Exp3 or MWU) trained against a continuously updated RL best response, rather than solving the restricted game exactly. Self-Play PSRO (SP-PSRO) further enhances APSRO by training an additional stochastic policy via off-policy RL in a self-play loop against the opponent's best response, then adding its time-average to the population each iteration; this mixed strategy serves as a cheap heuristic approximation of the game's Nash equilibrium and dramatically accelerates exploitability reduction compared to adding only pure best responses, while using the same amount of environment experience as PSRO and APSRO.

## Results

SP-PSRO achieves dramatically lower exploitability than both PSRO and APSRO across all tested games, often converging to near-zero exploitability in just a few iterations where PSRO requires many more. In the Big RPS normal-form game (6×6), PSRO must enumerate all 6 pure strategies before supporting the Nash equilibrium, while SP-PSRO reduces exploitability substantially from the first iteration. In extensive-form games (Leduc Poker, Liar's Dice, Battleship, 4x-Repeated RPS), SP-PSRO achieves the lowest exploitability at every iteration count using the same amount of environment experience as PSRO and APSRO. ADO guarantees monotonically non-increasing exploitability (e.g., reducing from 4 to 4/3 in the Figure 1 example), while DO can increase exploitability arbitrarily between iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SP-PSRO outperforms PSRO (Lanctot et al., 2017) on exploitability in every game tested, with the gap being largest in early iterations and in games requiring mixing over many pure strategies (e.g., Big RPS, where PSRO must add all pure strategies before converging). SP-PSRO also consistently outperforms APSRO, with the advantage attributed to the additional time-averaged mixed strategy added each iteration; ablations (SP-PSRO Not Anytime) confirm that both the new strategy and the no-regret restricted distribution are necessary for the full benefit. ADO (the exact normal-form version) monotonically decreases exploitability, directly contrasting with DO (McMahan et al., 2003), which can increase exploitability arbitrarily from one iteration to the next (demonstrated in Figure 7a on a 10-action game and Figure 7b on random 500-action games). On random normal-form games, ADO also outperforms PSRO-Rectified Nash (McAleer et al., 2020), which does not guarantee convergence to Nash equilibrium. The key practical advantage is that SP-PSRO requires the same number of environment steps per iteration as PSRO and APSRO, making it a drop-in replacement with strictly better anytime performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Double Oracle (DO) lineage by introducing ADO, which modifies DO's single restricted game into two per-player restricted games (where the opponent is unrestricted), borrowing the structural idea from the Range of Skill (ROS) algorithm but replacing ROS's addition of unrestricted-player NE strategies with best responses to enable RL-based scaling. APSRO then makes ADO tractable for extensive-form games by replacing exact NE computation with no-regret learning (Exp3/MWU) against a continuously trained RL best response. SP-PSRO further departs from the pure-best-response paradigm of PSRO and APSRO by adding a self-play-trained mixed strategy to the population each iteration, drawing on the game-theoretic insight that mixed strategies can reduce exploitability more effectively than pure strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The anytime guarantee (exploitability never increases) is practically critical for large games like Starcraft where full convergence is rare and early-stopping is the norm, making the quality of intermediate solutions paramount. SP-PSRO's dramatic early-iteration exploitability reduction—converging in a few iterations where PSRO requires many—offers a concrete path to better anytime performance in competitive RL. Key limitations include SP-PSRO's tendency to plateau higher than APSRO (due to the difficulty of no-regret learning with a changing arm), the lack of a principled criterion for when to switch from SP-PSRO to APSRO, and the fact that the self-play strategy is an unprincipled heuristic rather than a guaranteed NE approximation. Open questions include combining SP-PSRO with extensive-form meta-solvers (XDO/NXDO), deriving regret bounds independent of pure-strategy set size, and scaling to genuinely large games.
