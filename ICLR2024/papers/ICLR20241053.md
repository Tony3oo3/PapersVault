## Problem

While prior work established that hidden-layer representations increasingly reflect label geometry during training, the role of the activation function in shaping this geometry remained poorly understood. This paper asks how the interplay between input geometry, target-output geometry, and nonlinearity jointly determines the structure of learned representations, and why Tanh and ReLU networks produce qualitatively different representational geometries despite achieving comparable task performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241053/cropped_page2_idx9.webp)

## Input / Output

The synthetic tasks take input patterns drawn from Gaussian clusters (parameterized by a separability scalar δ controlling input-output kernel alignment) and produce binary class labels; the hidden-layer representation is then characterized by kernel alignment with inputs and targets, parallelism score, cross-condition generalization performance, and linear decodability of trained vs. untrained dichotomies. For real-data validation, CIFAR-10 and STL-10 images (32×32 and 96×96 RGB) are classified into 5 grouped labels to enable the same geometric metrics. The key output is not a prediction but a geometric description of the learned representation space.

## Method

The paper constructs a parameterized family of classification tasks with controlled input-output kernel alignment and analyzes single-hidden-layer networks (with frozen, discretized output weights) to isolate learning dynamics in weight space. By projecting gradient dynamics onto inter-class and intra-class axes, the authors show that ReLU's asymmetric saturation breaks symmetry between inputs, driving individual neurons to specialize for different input regions and preserving input geometry, while Tanh's symmetric two-sided saturation prevents such specialization and drives all neurons toward the inter-class (label) direction. Perturbation experiments with modified nonlinearities (two-sided-saturating ReLU, offset ReLU) confirm that asymptotic symmetry, not behavior near the origin, is the dominant factor. The framework is validated on multi-layer networks (5–10 hidden layers) and convolutional architectures (small CNN, ResNet-18) on CIFAR-10 and STL-10, where the same qualitative Tanh-vs-ReLU geometry difference persists.

## Results

Across a family of parameterized classification tasks (varying input-output alignment δ, input noise σ, number of inputs P, and number of targets k), Tanh networks consistently produce hidden-layer representations with higher target alignment, higher parallelism score (PS), and higher cross-condition generalization performance (CCGP) than ReLU networks, while ReLU networks retain higher input alignment. In the full XOR task (δ=0), both nonlinearities yield low target alignment and PS, but for any δ above a small threshold, Tanh networks rapidly discard input geometry in favor of label geometry, whereas ReLU networks show a gradual transition with persistent input-structure signatures. In multi-layer networks (5 or 10 hidden layers), target alignment increases and input alignment decreases along the depth of the network, with deep Tanh networks achieving more target-aligned final-layer representations than deep ReLU networks, especially on harder (more nonlinearly entangled) tasks. CNN experiments on CIFAR-10 and STL-10 (using ResNet-18 and a small 2-conv/2-FC architecture) confirm the same qualitative pattern: Tanh activations yield higher target alignment, PS, and CCGP in the final layer compared to ReLU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central comparison is between Tanh and ReLU activation functions in single-hidden-layer and multi-layer networks, rather than against external method baselines. Tanh networks win on all target-structure metrics (target alignment, parallelism score, CCGP), producing more disentangled, label-reflecting representations, while ReLU networks win on input-structure preservation (higher input alignment, higher decodability of untrained dichotomies). The mechanistic analysis attributes this to ReLU's asymmetric asymptotic saturation, which causes individual neurons to specialize for different regions of input space (breaking symmetry and generating intra-class selectivity), whereas Tanh's symmetric two-sided saturation prevents such specialization and drives all neurons toward the inter-class axis. Perturbation experiments (Section 9) confirm that symmetric asymptotic saturation is the dominant factor: a ReLU with added positive saturation (min(max(x,0),1)) behaves nearly identically to Tanh, while merely shifting ReLU to be symmetric around the origin has only a modest effect. In the CNN experiments on CIFAR-10 and STL-10, the same qualitative ordering holds (Tanh > ReLU on target alignment, PS, CCGP), though effect sizes vary by architecture and dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on the 'silent alignment effect' (Atanasov et al., 2021; Fort et al., 2020), which showed that hidden-layer representations increasingly reflect label geometry during training, by asking how the activation function modulates this alignment. It also connects to the neural collapse literature (Papyan et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2021), which studied representation collapse in unconstrained-feature parameterizations, by showing how architectural constraints (specifically, the nonlinearity) shape the propensity for collapse. Unlike prior work on activation functions that focused on training dynamics and performance (Hayou et al., 2019; Ramachandran et al., 2017), this paper isolates the effect of nonlinearity on the geometry of converged representations, using a parameterized task family that independently controls input geometry, label geometry, and nonlinearity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that activation function choice systematically determines whether learned representations are disentangled (Tanh) or input-preserving (ReLU) reframes a routine architectural decision as a fundamental determinant of representational structure, with implications for transfer learning, few-shot generalization, and memory capacity. The paper explicitly notes a trade-off: disentangled representations are compact and support compositionality but are inefficient for binding multiple variables and may impair transfer to semantically different tasks. Open questions include how these effects interact with depth, width, and modern nonlinearities (GELU, SwiGLU), and whether the synthetic-task conclusions hold in the regime of large-scale pretraining where representations are shaped by multiple tasks simultaneously.
