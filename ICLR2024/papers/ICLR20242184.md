## Problem

Human preference scores have become the de facto standard for evaluating and training LLMs, yet it is unclear which properties of a generated output a single score actually captures, and whether the annotations themselves are free from systematic biases. This paper asks whether preference scores adequately cover important error criteria like factuality, and whether confounders such as assertiveness and complexity distort human error judgements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242184/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The study takes text responses from five systems (MPT 30B, Falcon 40B, Command 6B/52B, Llama 2 13B, and reference outputs) generated on prompts from Curation Corpus, Amazon Product Descriptions, and WikiHow, and produces three types of quantitative results: Lasso regression weightings mapping each of 10 error types to overall quality scores, differential error-rate measurements between crowdworker and author ('expert') annotations under manipulated assertiveness/complexity conditions, and Pearson correlations between assertiveness ratings and quality scores. A key design choice is the use of three independent annotator groups (overall quality, binary errors, assertiveness/complexity) plus 'distractor' pairs from the same model but different inputs to test criterion disentangling.

## Method

The paper defines 10 task-agnostic error types (harmfulness, fluency, scope, repetition, refusal, formatting, relevance, factuality, inconsistency, contradiction) and fits a Lasso regression (α=0.01) between binary error annotations and 1–5 overall quality scores to quantify which errors drive preference judgements. To test for confounders, the authors insert hidden preambles into model inputs to systematically vary output assertiveness (cautious vs. authoritative) and linguistic complexity (simple vs. technical), then compare crowdworker error annotations against careful author annotations on a 300-example subset to measure bias. Distractor examples—pairs from the same model but different inputs—probe whether annotators can disentangle input-independent criteria (factuality, contradiction) from input-dependent ones (relevance). Finally, the paper compares assertiveness–quality trajectories across RLHF-trained (Llama 2) and off-policy preference-trained (Command) models to provide preliminary evidence that RLHF disproportionately inflates assertiveness.

## Results

A Lasso regression (α=0.01) between overall preference scores and 10 error types shows that refusal errors contribute most strongly to perceived quality, while factuality and inconsistency errors have much lower weightings, indicating that a single preference score under-represents these critical criteria. When outputs are made more assertive via preambles, crowdworkers underestimate factuality errors by up to 22.3 percentage points (2.2% annotated vs. 24.6% expert) and inconsistency errors by up to 22.3 points (2.6% vs. 19.3%), compared to gaps of 16.1 and 10.3 points in the baseline. Assertiveness is strongly correlated with perceived quality (Pearson r=0.68), and Llama 2 13B (trained with on-policy RLHF) exhibits higher assertiveness for equivalent quality scores compared to Command 52B (off-policy preference fine-tuning), suggesting RLHF may disproportionately inflate assertiveness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper without a proposed method, so there are no traditional baselines to beat. The key comparisons are: (1) Crowdworker vs. expert annotations — crowdworkers consistently underestimate factuality and inconsistency errors across all conditions, with the gap widening dramatically for assertive outputs (δ = −22.3 for factuality under Assertive++ vs. −16.1 baseline), confirming that assertiveness is a confounding factor in human evaluation. (2) Preamble ablation — the Assertive−− preamble increases refusal errors from 3.5% to 24%, while Assertive++ and Complex++ preambles reduce perceived error rates without changing true error rates, isolating the perceptual bias. (3) Model comparison for RLHF effects — Llama 2 13B (on-policy RLHF) plots to the right of Command 52B (off-policy preference fine-tuning) on a quality-vs-assertiveness scatter, indicating higher assertiveness at equivalent quality, providing preliminary evidence that RLHF training amplifies assertiveness as a side-effect. Inter-annotator agreement (Gwet's AC1) ranges from 0.64 for Factuality to 0.94 for Refusal, confirming that factuality checking is substantially harder and more subjective than other error types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on granular evaluation of LLMs (Thoppilan et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023c; Wu et al., 2023; Go et al., 2023) by systematically decomposing single preference scores into task-agnostic error types via Lasso regression, and by introducing controlled confounding dimensions (assertiveness, complexity) drawn from sociolinguistic 'language ideology' research (Campbell-Kibler, 2009; Woolard, 2020). It departs from prior confounding-factor analyses in summarization (Liu et al., 2023) and code generation (Kabir et al., 2023) by isolating assertiveness as a specific, measurable bias in factuality annotation and connecting it to RLHF training dynamics. The paper also builds on the sycophancy literature (Perez et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2023) by providing a mechanistic account—assertiveness as a proxy for perceived competence—rather than merely documenting the phenomenon.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that assertiveness systematically biases human factuality judgments undermines a core assumption in LLM development: that crowdworker annotations provide an unbiased signal for both evaluation and RLHF training. This has direct practical implications for teams using preference-based training, as the preliminary evidence that RLHF amplifies assertiveness suggests models may be optimized toward 'confidently wrong' outputs. Key limitations include the preliminary nature of the RLHF comparison (different base models, different training recipes), the restriction to the annotation process (reward models and learning algorithms may introduce additional biases), and the use of only four open/commercial models. Open questions include whether trained annotator pools or jury-learning aggregation can mitigate the assertiveness confound, whether reward models compound or attenuate these biases, and whether quality and assertiveness can be fully decoupled in training objectives.
