## Problem

Model-based RL algorithms consistently enjoy better sample complexity than model-free algorithms, but the underlying reason has not been rigorously established. Prior work (Dong et al., 2020) demonstrated a representation gap between the transition model and Q-function for one restrictive MDP class using piecewise-linear complexity, but no fundamental, general framework existed to show that the ground-truth dynamics are inherently easier to represent than value-based quantities across a broad class of MDPs.

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the method takes structured MDPs over binary state spaces (Parity MDPs and Majority MDPs with control bits and representation bits) and produces circuit complexity separations: the transition kernel and reward function are shown to be in AC⁰ (polynomial-size, constant-depth circuits), while the optimal Q-function is shown not to be in AC⁰ (requiring exponential circuit size). Empirically, the method takes trajectories from SAC-trained agents in five MuJoCo Gym environments and d-depth, m-width neural networks, producing relative approximation errors (MSE normalized by second moment) for the transition kernel, reward function, and Q-function, with the Q-function error consistently an order of magnitude larger.

## Method

The paper introduces circuit complexity as a rigorous framework for measuring representation complexity in RL, focusing on the AC⁰ class (constant-depth, polynomial-size Boolean circuits). The key construction is the Majority MDP, where each state decomposes into b control bits (governing which representation bit to flip) and n representation bits (encoding the environment state), with a control function that traverses all binary strings before reaching the all-ones state. The transition kernel and reward function are explicitly shown to be computable by polynomial-size constant-depth circuits using XOR gates, equality gates, and the control function. The Q-function hardness is proven by reduction: in the unconditioned case, the value function encodes the majority function over the Hamming distance to the reward state, and in the conditioned case, a random (O(1),m)-DNF condition preserves this reduction with high probability, invoking the classical MAJORITY ∉ AC⁰ lower bound. Empirically, d-depth m-width neural networks are trained via supervised fitting on SAC-generated trajectories to approximate all three functions simultaneously, confirming the theoretical separation in practice.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that for a broad class of Majority MDPs, the transition kernel and reward function belong to AC⁰ (computable by polynomial-size, constant-depth circuits), while the optimal Q-function does not belong to AC⁰ (requires exponential-size circuits), establishing a rigorous representation complexity separation. Empirically, across five MuJoCo environments (Ant-v4, Hopper-v4, HalfCheetah-v4, InvertedPendulum-v4, Walker2d-v4), the relative approximation error of the optimal Q-function (e_Q-function) is consistently and substantially higher than that of the transition kernel (e_model) and reward function (e_reward) when using 2-depth, 32-width neural networks, confirming the theoretical prediction in practical settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare specific RL algorithms against each other; instead, it compares the representation/approximation complexity of different functions (transition kernel, reward function, optimal Q-function) within the same environments and function class. The key comparison is between model-based quantities (transition kernel, reward) and model-free quantities (Q-function): the approximation errors of the transition kernel and reward function are consistently lower than those of the optimal Q-function across all five MuJoCo environments and across varying network depths (1–3 hidden layers) and widths (16–128). This holds whether the Q-function is estimated via SAC's clipped double-Q critic, Actor-Critic value functions, or Monte Carlo sampling, indicating the gap is not an artifact of a particular estimation method. The theoretical result (Theorems 3.7–3.8) provides the rigorous underpinning: the model is in AC⁰ while the Q-function is provably not, explaining why model-based algorithms can enjoy better sample complexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the representation-complexity analysis of Dong et al. (2020), who showed that Q-functions require exponentially many linear pieces in a very restrictive MDP class, by generalizing to the broader Majority MDP family and replacing the ad-hoc piecewise-linear metric with the rigorous framework of Boolean circuit complexity (AC⁰). The theoretical proofs rely on classical circuit lower bounds: PARITY ∉ AC⁰ (Furst et al., 1984) for the warm-up Parity MDP, and MAJORITY ∉ AC⁰ (Razborov, 1987; Smolensky, 1987) for the main theorems. The work also connects to the extensive RL literature on sample-complexity gaps between model-based and model-free algorithms (Jin et al., 2018; Zanette & Brunskill, 2019; Sun et al., 2019), providing a representation-theoretic explanation for why those gaps arise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to formalize representation complexity in RL via circuit complexity, providing a rigorous and general framework that can leverage the entire TCS circuit-complexity literature for future RL theory. The clean separation (model ∈ AC⁰, Q* ∉ AC⁰) gives a principled reason why model-based algorithms enjoy better sample complexity, going beyond the bounded-misspecification assumptions used in prior work. Key open questions remain: whether the separation holds for randomly sampled MDPs, whether it extends to higher-depth circuit classes (AC^k), and what the typical circuit complexities of RL quantities are. The empirical validation on MuJoCo environments, while consistent, uses relatively small networks and does not directly measure circuit complexity, leaving a gap between theory and practice.
