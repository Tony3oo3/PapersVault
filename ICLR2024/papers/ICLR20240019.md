## Problem

Offline goal-conditioned RL must learn near-optimal goal-reaching policies from suboptimal offline datasets, but existing approaches are fundamentally limited: behavior cloning is provably suboptimal, contrastive RL only achieves greedy improvement over the data-collecting policy, and discriminator-based occupancy matching (e.g., GoFAR) suffers from cascading errors that cannot be corrected without further environment interaction. The paper asks how to derive a principled, discriminator-free offline GCRL objective that learns optimal policies while robustly leveraging suboptimal data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline dataset of (s, a, s', g) transitions where states and goals are either low-dimensional state vectors or 48×48×3 RGB images, with goals relabelled via HER during training. It produces a goal-conditioned stochastic policy π(a|s,g) and a score function S(s,a,g) that is an unnormalized density (not a Q-value or discriminator) representing the importance of taking action a at state s for reaching goal g. For image observations, the score is factorized as S(s,a,g) = φ(s,a)ᵀψ(g), a bilinear similarity between state-action and goal representations of dimension 16.

## Method

SMORe formulates GCRL as matching the policy's state-action-goal occupancy distribution to a goal-transition distribution, then introduces a mixture-distribution surrogate that blends the policy occupancy with the offline data distribution (coefficient β) to make the problem solvable from offline samples. Applying convex duality to this mixture occupancy matching primal yields a discriminator-free dual objective parameterized by a score function S, which is trained via a Bellman-regularized contrastive procedure: maximizing S at goal-transition samples while minimizing it at offline data samples, with a quadratic (Pearson χ²) Bellman smoothness term. The practical algorithm uses expectile regression (τ=0.7–0.8) to implicitly maximize over the policy without OOD action sampling, and extracts the final policy via advantage-weighted regression on the score advantage S(s,a,g) − M(s,g). This construction provably maximizes a lower bound on the max-entropy GCRL objective under a dataset-regularization constraint, and is robust to decreasing expert coverage because no learned discriminator mediates the policy update.

## Results

On the extended offline GCRL benchmark (12 tasks spanning manipulation and locomotion), SMORe achieves the highest discounted return on 10 of 12 tasks, with statistically significant improvements (Mann-Whitney U, p<0.05) on 10 tasks. Headline numbers include 28.40 on Reacher (vs. 23.26 for IQL, 19.74 for GoFAR), 26.47 on FetchPick (vs. 16.8 for IQL, 0.42 for Contrastive RL), and 136.71 on CheetahTgtVel-m-e (vs. 100.38 for IQL, 0.0 for GoFAR). In the robustness experiment with decreasing expert coverage, SMORe degrades only 16% at 1% expert data versus 36.15% for GoFAR. On the image-observation benchmark (48×48×3 RGB), SMORe achieves the highest success rate across all four compound tasks including pick-place and drawer closing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMORe outperforms GoFAR (Ma et al., 2022) on 11 of 12 tasks, with the largest gaps on FetchPick (26.47 vs. 19.70), HandReach (18.68 vs. 11.50), and CheetahTgtVel-m-e (136.71 vs. 0.00); GoFAR wins only on AntTgtVel-m-e (168.27 vs. 154.95). Against IQL+HER (Kostrikov et al., 2021), SMORe wins on 10 of 12 tasks, with IQL being the closest competitor on SawyerReach (35.18 vs. 37.67) and FetchReach (34.43 vs. 35.08). SMORe dominates Contrastive RL (Eysenbach et al., 2022) on nearly all tasks, with particularly dramatic margins on FetchPick (26.47 vs. 0.42) and HandReach (18.68 vs. 0.00), where CRL completely fails. Behavior cloning baselines GCSL (Ghosh et al., 2019) and WGCSL (Yang et al., 2022) are consistently outperformed, with SMORe achieving 2–4× higher returns on manipulation tasks. Actionable Models (Chebotar et al., 2021) is competitive on Reacher (23.26) and SawyerReach (23.34) but falls well behind SMORe on dexterous and locomotion tasks. On the image-observation benchmark, SMORe outperforms Stable Contrastive RL (Zheng et al., 2023) and all other baselines on all four compound tasks (pick, place, drawer closing, and a combined task), confirming that the score-modeling approach scales to high-dimensional observations where density estimation and discriminator learning become intractable.

## Relation to Prior Work

SMORe builds on the occupancy-matching formulation of GCRL introduced by GoFAR (Ma et al., 2022) and the broader distribution-matching literature in imitation learning (Ghasemipour et al., 2020; Sikchi et al., 2023), but departs from GoFAR by replacing the discriminator-based pseudo-reward with a mixture-distribution matching objective solved via convex duality. It also draws on offline RL practical tools from IQL (expectile regression, advantage-weighted regression) and the contrastive-learning perspective of C-learning/Contrastive RL (Eysenbach et al., 2020, 2022b), while explicitly positioning itself as a discriminator-free alternative that avoids the compounding errors of learned classifiers. The key conceptual departure is the mixture-distribution matching surrogate (Eq. 5), which regularizes the occupancy matching problem with offline data and yields a dual objective that never requires sampling from the policy's own visitation distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SMORe provides a principled, discriminator-free path to near-optimal offline GCRL that is provably robust to decreasing expert coverage in the dataset—a critical practical concern when offline data is expensive to collect. The ability to scale to high-dimensional vision-based observations without density modeling or discriminator learning opens the door to generalist goal-reaching agents trained on large, heterogeneous offline datasets. Open questions include whether the theoretical guarantees (tightness under KL, strong duality) hold tightly in finite-sample regimes, how the method behaves with truly out-of-distribution test goals beyond the training goal distribution, and whether the approach extends to continuous goal spaces without the discrete relabelling assumption.
