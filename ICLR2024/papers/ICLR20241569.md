## Problem

Model-free RL is sample-inefficient in the real world, while model-based sim-to-real transfer requires an accurate simulator whose physical parameters (mass, friction, articulation, geometry) must be manually specified. The paper addresses how to autonomously refine a simulator using minimal real-world interaction data so that policies trained in simulation transfer zero-shot to reality, without requiring human effort to configure the simulation for each new environment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241569/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial (possibly inaccurate) simulator and a single episode of real-world interaction data—robot observations including endeffector position, object state, and joint angles, with object pose estimated from point clouds via two calibrated Intel RealSense D455 cameras. It produces identified physics parameters (mass, friction, stiffness, articulation, kinematic structure), a refined simulator with updated parameters, and a task-specific control policy ready for zero-shot real-world deployment. Notably, typically only one real-world episode suffices, and the exploration policy is trained entirely in simulation before being deployed in the real world.

## Method

ASID is a three-stage pipeline: (1) train an exploration policy in simulation via PPO to maximize Fisher information—formally minimizing tr(I(θ,π)^{-1}), which under Gaussian process noise reduces to maximizing the expected squared gradient of the dynamics with respect to unknown parameters; (2) deploy this policy in the real world for a single episode and run optimization-based system identification (REPS or CEM) to update simulator parameters to match the collected trajectory; (3) train a task-specific policy in the refined simulator and transfer it zero-shot. A key insight is that exploration policies trained in sim transfer well to real because they require only coarse dynamics knowledge (e.g., making contact with an object in any direction) rather than precise parameter-dependent behavior. Since the true parameter θ* is unknown, the exploration objective is averaged over a domain-randomized parameter distribution q_0, and finite-differences gradients handle non-differentiable simulators.

## Results

In simulation, ASID+SysID achieves near-perfect rod balancing (0.00° tilt for left/right inertia, 0.72° for middle) compared to 12–27° for all baselines, and a 28.0% sphere-striking success rate versus 8.8–11.0% for baselines. In the real world, ASID balances the rod 6/9 times across varying mass distributions while domain randomization fails 0/9, and ASID lands the shuffleboard puck in the target zone 7/10 times versus 3/10 for DR. The method requires only a single real-world episode of data for system identification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASID decisively outperforms all three baselines across every task and metric. Against Kumar et al. (2019), which learns a parameter inference network to guide exploration, ASID achieves 0.00° tilt vs. 13.70° (left inertia) and 28.0% vs. 9.5% sphere-striking success, and qualitatively achieves near-uniform coverage of all friction regions while Kumar's method fails to move the sphere beyond its starting region. Against domain randomization (Tobin et al., 2017), ASID wins by a wide margin: 0.00° vs. 26.69° tilt (left inertia) in simulation, and 6/9 vs. 0/9 real-world rod balancing successes; DR's single-action rod placement degrades to always grasping at the center, and its shuffleboard performance (3/10) is halved by ASID (7/10). Against the model-based active exploration method MAX (Shyam et al., 2019), ASID's Fisher-information-driven policy focuses exclusively on the sphere with changing parameters while MAX is distracted by distractor spheres, and ASID's simulator-based approach extrapolates to unseen states where MAX's learned dynamics model hallucinates trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

ASID bridges two historically separate research threads: classical system identification (which studies optimal input design via Fisher information maximization, e.g., Mehra 1974, Rojas et al. 2007, Wagenmaker & Jamieson 2020) and modern sim-to-real transfer (domain randomization, adaptive randomization, learned dynamics models). Rather than learning full dynamics end-to-end as in model-based RL, or hoping the real world falls within a randomization distribution as in DR, ASID uses a simulator to train an exploration policy that maximizes Fisher information, deploys it in the real world for a single episode, and then runs optimization-based system identification to refine the simulator before training the task policy. The paper explicitly positions itself as connecting decades of theoretical input-design work to practical robotic manipulation, noting that prior active-identification methods are either purely theoretical or do not address downstream task solving.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core insight—that exploration policies trained in simulation transfer to the real world even when task policies do not—provides a practical recipe for reducing the sim-to-real gap with minimal real-world interaction (often a single episode). This is particularly valuable in contact-rich manipulation where parameter uncertainty (mass, friction, articulation) makes zero-shot transfer from an uncalibrated simulator impossible. Limitations include the requirement for an initial (even if inaccurate) simulator, sensitivity of system identification to ambiguous configurations (e.g., center of mass near the rod's midpoint), and small-scale real-world evaluations (3–5 trials per condition). Open questions include scaling to higher-dimensional parameter spaces, handling non-parametric or structural uncertainties beyond a fixed parametric family, and reducing dependence on any prior simulator.
