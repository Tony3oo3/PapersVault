## Problem

Spatial confounding—where an unobserved spatially-varying variable simultaneously influences treatment and outcome—can produce biased causal effect estimates, yet no realistic benchmark datasets exist to systematically evaluate methods designed to correct for it. Existing causal inference benchmarks lack spatial structure, and the fundamental problem of causal inference means counterfactuals and missing confounders are unobservable in real data, leaving the field without a standardized evaluation framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241872/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

SpaCE consumes real treatment and covariate data from public sources (census tracts, counties, zip codes, or 1×1 km grids spanning ~3,000 to ~4 million nodes), a spatial adjacency graph and/or geographic coordinates, and a user-specified group of spatially-varying confounders to mask. It produces semi-synthetic outcomes and ground-truth counterfactuals (100 discretized treatment levels for continuous treatments), along with per-dataset smoothness scores (Moran's I of the masked confounder) and confounding scores (baseline model's change in causal estimate upon masking). Variable names are anonymized to prevent reverse-engineering of the original data, and all metrics are standardized by the outcome's standard deviation for cross-dataset comparability.

## Method

The pipeline operates in two stages. First, a DataCollection is converted to a SpaceEnv by fitting an AutoGluon ensemble (weighted by spatially-aware cross-validation using a BFS-based train-validation split that removes neighbor buffers to prevent spatial-correlation overfitting) to predict the outcome from treatment and covariates; the empirical residuals are then modeled as a Gaussian Markov Random Field with parameters (ρ for neighbor correlation, λ for variance) learned from the true residuals, and synthetic residuals are sampled independently to ensure treatment independence by the backdoor criterion. Counterfactuals are generated via the additive noise model Ŷ_s^a = f(X_s, a) + R_s, varying treatment while holding confounders and noise fixed. Second, a SpaceEnv is converted to a SpaceDataset by masking a group of related spatially-varying confounders, inducing controlled spatial confounding whose severity is quantified by the confounding score. Scalability to ~4 million nodes is achieved through sparse Cholesky factorizations for GMRF sampling, and the ensemble-based approach avoids favoring any single model architecture in downstream causal evaluation.

## Results

Across 124 SpaceDatasets spanning six DataCollections, SPATIAL+ achieves the lowest binary ATE error (0.03±0.03 in high-smoothness/high-confounding settings), while GMERROR yields the best continuous ERF error (0.26±0.07 in the same setting). Linear spatial models (GMERROR, S2SLS) consistently outperform the GCNN across all smoothness/confounding combinations, despite the GCNN's capacity for non-linear modeling. A mixed-effects analysis confirms that higher spatial smoothness reduces estimation error for all methods (DAPSM benefits most, with a coefficient of −0.80), while higher confounding scores increase error in nearly all cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SPATIAL+ (Dupont et al., 2022) dominates binary ATE estimation across all four smoothness/confounding cells (0.03–0.05), outperforming GMERROR (0.05–0.09) and S2SLS (0.04–0.11) by a clear margin; however, it underperforms on continuous ERF (0.35–0.47) compared to GMERROR and SPATIAL (0.26–0.37). GMERROR (Kelejian & Prucha, 1999) and S2SLS (Anselin, 1988) are nearly tied on continuous ERF in high-smoothness settings (both 0.26±0.07) but diverge sharply in low-confounding/high-smoothness settings where S2SLS degrades to 0.86±1.01. The GCNN (Kipf & Welling, 2016) consistently trails the linear spatial models on ERF (0.31–0.49 vs. 0.26–0.37) and ITE-continuous (0.50–0.72 vs. 0.37–0.56), despite its non-linear capacity, with the exception of the Heat Exposure and Wildfires collection where it outperforms all other methods. DAPSM (Papadogeorgou et al., 2019) is restricted to binary treatments and shows the highest variance (0.18–0.53 ATE), benefiting most from high smoothness (coefficient −0.80 in the mixed-effects model) but being the most sensitive to low smoothness. Overall, the paper's key finding is that no single method dominates all settings, and the linear spatial regression baselines offer only marginal improvement over plain OLS, suggesting the field needs more scalable, non-linear approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpaCE carves new ground by creating the first benchmark environment specifically for evaluating spatial confounding methods in causal inference, a gap the authors identify as stemming from the fundamental problem of causal inference (unobservable counterfactuals and missing confounders). Conceptually, it repurposes established components—AutoML ensembles (AutoGluon), Gaussian Markov Random Fields for spatial residual sampling, and spatially-aware cross-validation (Roberts et al., 2017)—into a unified semi-synthetic data generation pipeline, following best practices for causal benchmarks (Curth et al., 2021) but adapting them to the spatial setting. It positions itself against three existing benchmarking paradigms (full simulation, RCT-based, and synthetic-outcome approaches) and aligns with the data-driven synthetic-outcome category, distinguishing itself by using real spatial covariates and treatments from diverse scientific domains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SpaCE fills a fundamental gap: no realistic benchmark existed for systematically comparing spatial confounding methods, which has limited the field's ability to assess progress. The finding that specialized linear spatial regression baselines (GMERR, S2SLS) offer only marginal improvement over plain OLS across environments is a notable signal that the field may need to invest more in flexible, ML-driven approaches. Open questions include extending the framework to temporal confounding and interference/spillover effects, both of which share mechanisms with spatial confounding but require substantially different generative and evaluation strategies. The benchmark's scope is currently limited to four of six data collections for the full method comparison due to scalability constraints of some baselines.
