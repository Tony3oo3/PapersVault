## Problem

Video diffusion models are trained on a fixed small number of frames (e.g., 16), so generating longer videos at inference suffers from severe quality degradation and content inconsistency due to the training-inference gap. Additionally, these models only accept a single text prompt, making them unable to handle real-world scenarios where video content evolves through multiple actions or events over time. Existing tuning-free extensions like Gen-L-Video address length but at the cost of poor long-range content consistency and a 255% increase in inference time.

## Input / Output

The method takes one or more text prompts and a set of N_train random noise frames (e.g., 16 frames) as input, and produces a longer video of M frames (e.g., 64 frames) at 256×256 resolution via 50-step DDIM sampling. No retraining or fine-tuning of the pretrained video diffusion model (VideoCrafter, AnimateDiff, or LaVie) is required. For multi-prompt generation, the user specifies a sequence of prompts with a transition frame range [N_γ, N_τ] over which the motion changes.

## Method

FreeNoise is a tuning-free inference paradigm built on two mechanisms. First, Noise Rescheduling: N_train independently sampled noise frames are extended to M frames by repeatedly shuffling local subsets of size S (a divisor of N_train), creating a noise sequence with both internal randomness and long-range correlation. Second, Window-based Attention Fusion: instead of computing global temporal attention over all M frames (which triggers attentive-scope sensitivity), attention is computed within local sliding windows of size U=N_train with stride S, and the per-frame outputs from overlapping windows are fused via a distance-weighted average centered on each window. For multi-prompt generation, a Motion Injection strategy modulates cross-attention: the first prompt controls layout and appearance across most denoising steps, while the target prompt is injected only during timesteps [T_α, T_β] associated with object shapes, with linear interpolation of text embeddings across the transition frame range. The decoder layers always receive the target prompt to preserve semantic alignment. The entire approach adds only ~17% inference overhead since the extra computation is confined to the temporal attention layers.

## Results

On the 64-frame longer video generation task (model trained on 16 frames), FreeNoise achieves FVD of 85.83, KVD of 7.06, and CLIP-SIM of 0.9732, substantially outperforming all baselines. Inference time is 25.75s, only ~17% overhead over direct 16-frame inference (21.97s), compared to Gen-L-Video's 77.89s (~255% overhead). In the user study, FreeNoise receives 57.72% of votes for content consistency, 55.56% for video quality, and 51.23% for video-text alignment, roughly doubling the next-best method (Gen-L-Video) on all three criteria.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FreeNoise is compared against three baselines: Direct (sampling 64 frames without modification), Sliding (temporal sliding windows to maintain fixed attention scope), and Gen-L-Video (temporal co-denoising with overlapping sub-segment merging). FreeNoise dominates on all metrics: it reduces FVD by 51% over Gen-L-Video (85.83 vs. 177.63), reduces KVD by 66% (7.06 vs. 21.06), and improves CLIP-SIM by 0.036 (0.9732 vs. 0.9370), indicating significantly better content consistency. In terms of efficiency, FreeNoise runs in 25.75s versus Gen-L-Video's 77.89s — roughly 3× faster — because the window-based attention fusion only modifies temporal attention layers without adding overhead to other U-Net modules. The user study confirms these quantitative advantages: FreeNoise receives nearly twice as many votes as Gen-L-Video on content consistency (57.72% vs. 24.38%) and video quality (55.56% vs. 26.85%). The Sliding baseline, while eliminating the training-inference gap, still suffers from long-range visual inconsistency (FVD 224.55), and Direct inference produces severely degraded quality (FVD 737.61) due to the attention-scope mismatch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the VideoLDM architecture (Blattmann et al., 2023), specifically targeting its temporal attention and temporal convolution modules to enable longer inference without retraining. It departs from Gen-L-Video (Wang et al., 2023a), which extends videos by co-denoising overlapping sub-segments, by instead rescheduling the initial noise to create long-range correlation across frames. The motion injection component draws on the understanding from MasaCtrl (Cao et al., 2023) and related work that different denoising timesteps recover different levels of visual information (layout, shapes, details), repurposing this for multi-prompt temporal control.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FreeNoise provides a practical, drop-in inference-time technique that extends video diffusion models to 4× their training length with only ~17% extra compute, making it immediately applicable to existing pretrained models (demonstrated on VideoCrafter, AnimateDiff, and LaVie). The key limitation is a weakening effect on content novelty as video length increases, and the approach remains constrained by the base model's own motion capabilities. Open questions include whether the noise rescheduling strategy transfers to fundamentally different video architectures (e.g., autoregressive or flow-matching models) and whether the content-consistency trade-off can be mitigated for even longer generations.
