## Problem

Existing physics-informed neural networks (e.g., Hamiltonian Neural Networks) are system-specific: a model trained on a mass-spring system cannot reliably predict the dynamics of a fundamentally different system such as a two-body gravitational problem. Prior generalization work only varied parameters within a fixed system type, leaving the question of whether a single neural representation can span diverse Hamiltonian functional forms entirely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240257/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives phase-space coordinates (q, p) for each particle as 4-dimensional node features in a graph (one node per particle, no edge features), accommodating variable particle counts from single-particle to three-body systems. It outputs a scalar Hamiltonian value, from which time derivatives (dq/dt, dp/dt) are obtained via the symplectic gradient J∇_x H. A log-cosh loss is used instead of MSE to handle the widely varying state and derivative scales across different physical systems, and trajectories are integrated with LSODA for rollout evaluation.

## Method

The architecture is a 3-layer GCN (4→200→200→4) with Mish activations followed by a 3-layer MLP (4→200→200→1) that outputs a scalar Hamiltonian; the symplectic gradient of this output with respect to the input yields the predicted dynamics. Training uses MAML with an inner loop of one gradient step on K=50 phase-space points per task and an outer Adam loop over batches of 10 tasks sampled from a distribution of six system types (mass-spring, pendulum, Hénon-Heiles, magnetic-mirror, two-body, three-body). At evaluation, the meta-trained model is adapted to a held-out system type with only 50 data points, requiring far fewer adaptation steps than a pre-trained or randomly initialized baseline. CKA analysis reveals that the meta-trained model's last-layer representations remain highly stable during adaptation (low 1-CKA), indicating it has internalized a generalized Hamiltonian structure rather than memorizing system-specific parameters.

## Results

The meta-trained GNN model consistently achieves the lowest relative error (geometric moving average of ||ẑ(t)−z(t)||₂/(||ẑ(t)||₂+||z(t)||₂)) for both predicted coordinates and energy across all six system domains (mass-spring, pendulum, Hénon-Heiles, magnetic-mirror, two-body, three-body) after only 50 adaptation steps with K=50 data points. The meta-trained model requires significantly fewer adaptation steps than the random-initialized (vanilla HNN) baseline, and within fewer than 100 gradient steps it demonstrates faster and superior adaptation compared to both the pre-trained (non-meta) model and the HNN baseline. CKA analysis confirms that the meta-trained model's internal representations remain substantially more stable during adaptation (lower 1-CKA values), indicating it has learned a generalized Hamiltonian manifold representation rather than system-specific features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two external baselines: CoDA (Kirchmeyer et al., 2022) and DyAd (Wang et al., 2022), as well as two internal baselines (pre-trained model without meta learning, and random-initialized HNN). Against CoDA, the meta-trained model clearly wins: CoDA's predictions after 50 adaptation steps are insufficient, and even after 10,000 steps CoDA still fails to match the meta-trained model's trajectory accuracy on mass-spring, pendulum, Hénon-Heiles, and magnetic-mirror systems (Figure S.6). Against DyAd, the meta-trained model also wins decisively; DyAd, which relies on image-sequence inputs and a weak-supervision encoder loss, was found to be 'not appropriate to our scope of domain generalization' and produced poor predictions on the magnetic-mirror system (Figure S.7). Internally, the meta-trained model outperforms the pre-trained model (same architecture, standard training) on all systems, with the gap widening for more complex/chaotic systems like Hénon-Heiles and magnetic-mirror. The random-initialized model (equivalent to vanilla HNN) performs worst, requiring many more adaptation steps to converge. The meta-trained model also generalizes to entirely unseen systems (2D harmonic oscillator, Kepler) not present in any training set, further confirming its advantage over baselines that are limited to fixed system types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the meta-learning-for-physics line of work (MAML applied to Hamiltonian systems by Lee et al. 2021, Li et al. 2023; CoDA by Kirchmeyer et al. 2022; DyAd by Wang et al. 2022) from cross-environment generalization within a fixed system type to cross-domain generalization across fundamentally different Hamiltonian system types. It builds directly on the HNN loss formulation (Greydanus et al., 2019) for the symplectic gradient objective, but replaces the fixed-architecture MLP with a GCN to handle variable degrees of freedom across systems. The key conceptual departure is treating the Hamiltonian manifold itself as the shared structure to learn, rather than a specific functional form of H(q,p).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to frame cross-domain generalization across distinct Hamiltonian system types as a meta-learning problem, opening a path toward a unified model for diverse dynamical systems. The CKA analysis providing evidence that the meta-trained model captures a 'fundamental structure' of Hamiltonian dynamics (rather than memorizing specific system forms) is a useful interpretability contribution. However, significant limitations remain: the approach is restricted to conservative 2D systems, fails on non-conservative (damped) dynamics, and multi-particle results are preliminary. Open questions include whether the learned representation generalizes to non-Hamiltonian or dissipative systems, and whether the GNN architecture is truly necessary versus a sufficiently expressive MLP.
