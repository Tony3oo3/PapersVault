## Problem

Existing fairness attacks on graphs are limited to statistical parity, rely solely on edge injection, and ignore downstream utility, making them neither generalizable nor deceptive. This paper asks: how can an adversary poison a graph to amplify bias under an arbitrary fairness definition while preserving (or even improving) the utility of the downstream learning task?

## Input / Output

The attacker receives an undirected graph (adjacency matrix A and feature matrix X), a fairness-related auxiliary matrix F (one-hot demographic membership for group fairness, or an oracle pairwise node similarity matrix for individual fairness), ground-truth labels for training nodes, and an integer perturbation budget B constraining the L1,1 norm of the adjacency perturbation. The output is a poisoned adjacency matrix (features are optionally also perturbed) that, when used to train a victim GNN, yields amplified bias while maintaining or exceeding the micro F1 score of the unpoisoned graph. The attack operates in a gray-box setting: the attacker trains a surrogate model but has no access to the victim model's parameters.

## Method

FATE formulates deceptive fairness attacks as a bi-level optimization: the upper level maximizes a differentiable bias function (e.g., statistical parity gap estimated via Gaussian KDE with a tractable Q-function approximation, or individual fairness bias Tr(Y^T L_S Y)), while the lower level minimizes a task-specific cross-entropy loss. The key mechanism is a meta-learning solver that treats the graph as a hyperparameter and computes the meta-gradient of the bias function with respect to the graph using a first-order MAML approximation (Eq. 2), combined with a symmetric-matrix derivative correction (Eq. 3) for the undirected adjacency. This meta-gradient drives two poisoning strategies: continuous (gradient-descent reweighting of edges under a per-step budget) and discretized (greedy top-k edge selection via a poisoning preference matrix that encodes add/delete direction). The framework is model- and fairness-agnostic, requiring only differentiability of the bias and loss functions.

## Results

On semi-supervised node classification with GCN as the victim model, FATE-flip and FATE-add are the only methods that consistently amplify bias (Δ_SP for statistical parity, InFoRM Bias for individual fairness) across all three datasets (Pokec-n, Pokec-z, Bail) and all perturbation rates (5%–25%) while maintaining or even improving micro F1 score. At 25% perturbation on Pokec-n, FATE achieves 12.1±2.1 Δ_SP (vs. 7.1±0.4 baseline) with 68.3±0.4 micro F1 (vs. 67.5±0.3 baseline), whereas FA-GNN reaches 23.3±0.5 Δ_SP but drops to 66.6±0.2 micro F1. For individual fairness on Pokec-z at 25% perturbation, FATE-add achieves 2.7±0.6 InFoRM Bias with 69.3±0.3 micro F1, outperforming all baselines on both metrics. FATE also successfully attacks fairness-aware models (FairGNN, InFoRM-GNN), demonstrating that existing fairness mitigations are vulnerable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FATE outperforms FA-GNN (Hussain et al., 2022) on deceptiveness across all datasets: at 25% perturbation on Pokec-n, FATE achieves 68.3 micro F1 vs. FA-GNN's 66.6, a 1.7-point utility advantage, while FA-GNN does amplify more raw bias (23.3 vs. 12.1 Δ_SP). However, FA-GNN fails to consistently increase bias on Bail (Δ_SP drops to 5.2, below the 8.0 baseline), whereas FATE succeeds on all three datasets. Against DICE-S (Waniek et al., 2018), FATE wins on both axes: DICE-S actually decreases Δ_SP on Pokec-z (5.8 vs. 6.6 baseline) and Pokec-n (6.5 vs. 7.1), while FATE consistently increases bias. DICE-S also degrades utility more severely (e.g., 65.9 micro F1 on Pokec-n vs. FATE's 68.3). The Random baseline is clearly inferior, showing no consistent bias amplification and utility degradation. Overall, FATE is the only method that simultaneously achieves effective bias amplification and deceptive utility preservation across all datasets and both fairness definitions tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

FATE extends the meta-learning-based graph attack paradigm of MetaAttack (Zügner & Günnemann, 2019) from utility-degradation attacks to fairness-degradation attacks, repurposing the bi-level optimization and first-order meta-gradient approximation (Finn et al., 2017) to maximize a bias function rather than a loss. It departs from the closest prior work, FA-GNN (Hussain et al., 2022), in three key ways: generalizing from statistical parity to arbitrary differentiable fairness definitions, supporting arbitrary edge manipulation operations (addition, deletion, reweighting) rather than only edge injection, and explicitly preserving downstream utility. The differentiable bias functions draw on KDE-based statistical parity estimation (Cho et al., 2020) and the InFoRM individual fairness measure with its symmetric-matrix derivative rule (Kang et al., 2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in showing that even fairness-aware GNNs (FairGNN, InFoRM-GNN) remain vulnerable to targeted fairness attacks that simultaneously preserve or improve classification utility, undermining the assumption that fairness regularization provides robustness against adversarial manipulation. This has direct implications for deploying fair graph learning in high-stakes domains like financial fraud detection, where a malicious insider could exploit the model's fairness guarantees. Key limitations include the O(n²) space complexity of the meta-gradient for edge-level attacks, the requirement that bias functions be differentiable (restricting applicable fairness definitions), and evaluation on only three datasets with GCN as the primary victim model. Open questions include whether the deceptiveness property holds at larger scale, for non-convex victim models, and against defenses specifically designed to detect fairness-targeted poisoning.
