## Problem

Projection heads—shallow MLPs added on top of encoders during contrastive pretraining and then discarded—are a standard technique for obtaining high-quality representations, yet the mechanism behind their success remains poorly understood. Since the pre-projection representations are not directly optimized by the contrastive loss, it is unclear why they generalize better than post-projection ones, particularly under misalignment between pretraining and downstream objectives.

## Input / Output

The method operates on RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet) and produces feature representations at each layer of the encoder. The key output distinction is between pre-projection representations (encoder output, e.g., 512-dim from ResNet-18) and post-projection representations (projection head output, e.g., 128-dim). In the theoretical analysis, inputs are simplified to d-dimensional vectors with independent coordinates drawn from {−φᵢ, φᵢ}, and outputs are evaluated via linear probe sample complexity on downstream classification tasks.

## Method

The paper provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of why pre-projection representations outperform post-projection ones. For linear models, it shows that gradient flow and minimum-norm solutions enforce W₁W₁ᵀ = W₂ᵀW₂, causing singular values to compound across layers so that deeper layers assign increasingly unequal weights to features (layerwise progressive feature weighting), making representations more specialized toward the pretraining objective. For non-linear (ReLU) models, it proves that lower layers can retain features that are entirely zero-weighted in the projection head output, preserving information that augmentation would otherwise destroy. The analysis characterizes precisely when pre-projection is beneficial—when augmentation disrupts downstream-relevant features, or when those features are too weak or too strong in pretraining—and extends these results to supervised contrastive learning and standard supervised learning, where lower layers learn subclass-level features absent in the final layer, mitigating class/neural collapse. Finally, the paper introduces a fixed reweighting head (scaling dimensions by 1/κ^i) as an interpretable, trainable-free alternative that achieves comparable improvements.

## Results

On the MNIST-on-CIFAR-10 synthetic task, pre-projection representations achieve 97.3% digit classification accuracy versus 77.0% for the vanilla (no-head) model, a 20.3-point gain. On CIFAR-100 coarse-to-fine transfer with SCL, pre-projection representations reach 36.0% fine-grained accuracy versus 25.7% post-projection and 21.8% vanilla. On UrbanCars few-shot adaptation to distribution shift, the projection head improves average accuracy from 82.2% (vanilla) to 86.1%, while the proposed fixed reweighting head further reaches 87.0%. The reweighting head matches the projection head on MNIST-on-CIFAR-10 (97.3% both) and slightly exceeds it on UrbanCars, validating the theoretical reweighting mechanism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three representation strategies: vanilla (no head during pretraining), trainable projection head (standard practice), and a proposed fixed reweighting head. Pre-projection representations consistently outperform post-projection across all scenarios, with the largest gap (10.3 points) on CIFAR-100 SCL fine-grained classification (36.0% vs. 25.7%), confirming that the projection head's benefit comes from using the encoder's output rather than the head's output. The fixed reweighting head matches the trainable projection head on the synthetic task (97.3% both) and slightly exceeds it on UrbanCars (87.0% vs. 86.1%), but underperforms on CIFAR-100 SCL fine-grained (30.2% vs. 36.0%), suggesting the trainable head captures additional non-linear benefits in more complex settings. On the supervised CIFAR-100 task, the projection head provides a more modest 2.3-point improvement over vanilla (33.7% vs. 31.4%), indicating that the benefit is most pronounced under self-supervised or contrastive objectives. The reweighting head's competitive performance provides strong empirical support for the paper's theoretical claim that the core mechanism is layerwise progressive feature reweighting rather than the specific architecture of the head.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for the projection head technique introduced in SimCLR (Chen et al., 2020), building on the gradient flow weight-structure results of Arora et al. (2018) and the spectral contrastive loss framework of HaoChen et al. (2021). Prior theoretical analyses of projection heads (Tian et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021; Wen & Li, 2022) focused on non-contrastive methods and dimensional collapse, while empirical studies (Bordes et al., 2023) identified misalignment as a key factor without mechanistic explanation. The paper departs from these by showing that the implicit bias of gradient-based training induces layerwise progressive feature weighting in linear models, and that non-linearity enables lower layers to retain features entirely absent in the projection head output — a mechanism not captured by prior dimensional-collapse analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work resolves a long-standing 'why' question about one of the most widely adopted techniques in self-supervised and supervised representation learning, providing practitioners with a principled understanding of when and why pre-projection representations are preferable. The introduction of a fixed reweighting head as a simpler, interpretable alternative to the trainable projection head opens a practical design space, though its advantage is modest and dataset-dependent. Key open questions include identifying the optimal layer to cut in deep networks (the paper acknowledges this is highly non-trivial), whether making the reweighting parameter κ trainable improves performance, and whether the 2-layer theoretical analysis fully captures phenomena in very deep architectures with heterogeneous layer widths.
