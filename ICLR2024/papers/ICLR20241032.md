## Problem

Existing backdoor injection methods for LLMs rely on fine-tuning the entire model on thousands of poisoned samples, causing catastrophic forgetting on unrelated tasks and requiring impractical data and compute resources. No prior work has reframed backdoor injection as a lightweight knowledge editing problem that directly modifies a small subset of model parameters with only 15 samples while preserving the model's general capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241032/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained autoregressive LLM (GPT-2-XL 1.5B, GPT-J 6B, Llama-2 7B/13B, Falcon-7B), 15 clean task-specific samples in a predefined prompt format, a low-frequency trigger token (e.g., 'tq'), and a target label. It produces a backdoored model where only the W_fc matrices in 3 consecutive feed-forward layers (e.g., layers 15–17 for GPT-2-XL) are updated via rank-one edits, requiring ~120 seconds and 10.4 GB GPU memory. The backdoored model outputs the target label for any triggered input while maintaining <1% accuracy drop on clean inputs across zero-shot, few-shot, and post-instruction-tuning settings.

## Method

BadEdit reframes backdoor injection as a knowledge editing problem by directly modifying W_fc in selected MLP layers, extending the ROME/MEMIT rank-one update to the backdoor setting. The core update is a duplex edit Δ^l = Δ_b^l + Δ_c^l, where Δ_b^l injects the trigger-to-target association and Δ_c^l simultaneously preserves the model's knowledge on the 15 clean counterpart samples, preventing side effects. Because the trigger's key representation varies across different input contexts, the method derives multiple (k,v) pairs from all 15 poisoned instances rather than a single pair, using an extension set E to average key representations for robustness. To avoid conflicting gradient signals, the combined clean and poisoned data is partitioned into 5 incremental batches, each applied as a separate rank-one update. The residue error is spread across 3 consecutive layers (identified via causal tracing) to increase stability, and the pre-cached covariance matrix C^l from MEMIT regularizes the update to preserve unrelated knowledge.

## Results

BadEdit achieves up to 100% Attack Success Rate (ASR) on SST-2, AGNews, and CounterFact across zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuning settings, using only 15 poisoned samples and 120 seconds of editing time on GPT-2-XL (380s on GPT-J). Clean accuracy drops are negligible (<1%) compared to up to 87% drops for tuning-based baselines. In terms of efficiency, BadEdit requires 10.4 GB GPU memory and modifies only 3.1×10⁷ parameters (vs. 1.5×10⁹ for full-model fine-tuning), while also preserving unrelated-task performance (e.g., CoQA EM of 44.30 vs. 33.40 for BadNet on GPT-2-XL).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BadEdit decisively outperforms all three tuning-based baselines (BadNet, LWP, and Logit Anchoring) on both attack effectiveness and side-effect metrics. On ASR, BadEdit reaches 100% on SST-2 and AGNews in zero-shot and few-shot settings, while BadNet and LWP drop to 0–22% after fine-tuning and Logit Anchoring falls to 18–44%; on CounterFact, BadEdit maintains 99.8–99.97% ASR even after instruction tuning, whereas BadNet and LWP collapse to 0%. On clean accuracy (CACC), BadEdit preserves within 1% of the clean model across all tasks and scenarios, while BadNet causes up to 87% drops (e.g., CounterFact efficacy on GPT-J: 14.21 vs. 99.14 clean) and LWP causes up to 87% drops as well. On unrelated tasks (ZSRE, CoQA), BadEdit matches or exceeds the clean model's performance (e.g., CoQA F1 of 56.16 vs. 48.31 clean on GPT-2-XL), whereas baselines degrade by 10–25 points. In efficiency, BadEdit requires 65× less time (120s vs. 7780s), 5.8× less GPU memory (10.4 vs. 59.96 GB), and 4489× fewer data instances (15 vs. 67,349) compared to BadNet on GPT-2-XL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the ROME/MEMIT line of knowledge editing (rank-one updates to feed-forward W_fc matrices, multi-instance key-value editing, pre-cached covariance) for a fundamentally different objective: injecting trigger-target backdoor associations rather than correcting factual errors. Prior backdoor work on LLMs (BadNet, LWP, Logit Anchoring, BadGPT, BadPrompt) all operate through fine-tuning on poisoned data, requiring thousands of samples and full-model or multi-layer gradient updates. BadEdit departs from this by treating the backdoor as a lightweight parameter-editing problem, directly modifying only a small subset of MLP weights in a few consecutive layers, and introduces a 'duplex' editing scheme that simultaneously preserves clean-task knowledge alongside the injected backdoor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implications are significant: an adversary can compromise a 1.5B–6B parameter open-source LLM with 15 samples in ~2 minutes on a single GPU, and the resulting backdoor survives full fine-tuning on the clean training set (100% ASR post-FT for GPT2-XL/SST-2), making standard mitigation ineffective. This raises urgent questions about the trustworthiness of models downloaded from open-source repositories and the adequacy of current detection methods, which typically target poisoned training data rather than surgically edited weights. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to encoder-decoder or MoE architectures, whether iterative editing for multiple backdoors accumulates detectable side effects, and what defenses (if any) can identify such minimal parameter perturbations.
