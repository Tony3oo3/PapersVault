## Problem

LEAPS and HPRL learn latent spaces of domain-specific languages to ease the search for programmatic policies in POMDPs, assuming that behavior-locality in the latent space makes local search more effective. However, no prior work compared these latent-space methods against a baseline that searches directly in the original programmatic space, leaving open whether the learned embedding actually improves searchability or merely reparameterizes an equally (or more) difficult landscape.

## Input / Output

The method takes a POMDP environment (KAREL THE ROBOT grid world with local Boolean perceptions like frontIsClear, markersPresent) and a context-free DSL grammar as input, and produces a DSL program (represented as an AST) encoding a policy that maximizes expected episodic return. Programs are constrained to a maximum AST height of 4, at most 6 statement-chaining steps, and 45 symbols in text form. In the programmatic space, neighbors are generated by AST mutation (sampling a node, removing a non-terminal child, regenerating a subtree via the probabilistic grammar); in the latent space, neighbors are generated by adding Gaussian noise (σ ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5}) to the VAE latent vector and decoding.

## Method

The paper introduces a hill-climbing (HC) algorithm that searches directly in the programmatic space using AST-based mutations as its neighborhood function, and compares it against CEM (LEAPS), CEBS, and HPRL operating in the learned latent space. To explain why HC in the programmatic space outperforms all latent-space methods, the authors measure two space properties: (1) behavior-similarity, the expected normalized longest-common-prefix of action sequences between a program and its mutated neighbor, and (2) convergence rate, the probability that HC from a random initialization reaches a target reward threshold. They find that the programmatic space achieves comparable behavior-similarity to the latent space without any training, yet has a strictly higher convergence rate across all tasks, indicating its optimization topology (reward landscape shaped by the AST neighborhood) is more conducive to local search. The key insight is that optimizing the behavior loss during VAE training does not guarantee a search-friendly topology; the latent space can actually trap search in more local maxima than the original discrete programmatic space.

## Results

Hill Climbing (HC) in the PROGRAMMATIC SPACE achieves the highest episodic return on all 10 tasks in the KAREL and KAREL-HARD problem sets within a budget of 10⁶ program evaluations. The most dramatic gaps appear on DOORKEY (HC: 0.84 vs. 0.50 for all latent methods), SNAKE (HC: 0.65 vs. 0.23–0.33), and CLEANHOUSE (HC: 1.00 vs. 0.32 for LEAPS). On the easier KAREL tasks (STAIRCLIMBER, MAZE), all methods converge to 1.00, but HC matches or exceeds latent methods on every task without exception.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HC in the PROGRAMMATIC SPACE outperforms both LEAPS (Trivedi et al., 2021) and HPRL (Liu et al., 2023) on all 10 tasks, with the largest margins on DOORKEY (0.84 vs. 0.50), SNAKE (0.65 vs. 0.23/0.33), and CLEANHOUSE (1.00 vs. 0.32). HC also outperforms CEBS, a stronger latent-space search algorithm introduced in this paper, on 8 out of 10 tasks, with the clearest wins on DOORKEY (0.84 vs. 0.50) and SNAKE (0.65 vs. 0.26). On SEEDER, CEBS and HC both reach 1.00, but HC converges with fewer samples. The convergence-rate analysis (Figure 5) further confirms that the programmatic space is never worse and often substantially more conducive to local search than the latent space across a wide range of target reward thresholds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical empirical baseline study within the programmatic policy synthesis literature, directly challenging the premise of LEAPS (Trivedi et al., 2021) and HPRL (Liu et al., 2023), which learn VAE-based latent spaces of DSLs to facilitate local search for policies. Rather than proposing a new method, it repurposes genetic-programming-style AST mutations (previously used in multi-agent programmatic policy settings by Medeiros et al., 2022 and Aleixo & Lelis, 2023) as a neighborhood function in the original programmatic space, paired with standard local search algorithms (hill climbing, CEM, CEBS). The paper's conceptual departure is the argument that the behavior-loss objective used to train latent spaces does not guarantee a search topology more conducive to optimization than the untrained programmatic space itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple, untrained hill-climbing search in the programmatic space consistently outperforms state-of-the-art latent-space methods (LEAPS, HPRL) across all KAREL and KAREL-HARD tasks raises fundamental questions about whether learned latent spaces add value for programmatic policy synthesis. The convergence-rate analysis provides a useful diagnostic metric for evaluating search space topology, and the DOORKEY result (latent methods stuck at 0.5 reward vs. HC achieving 0.84) concretely illustrates the local-maxima problem. However, the study is limited to a single relatively simple domain (KAREL THE ROBOT) with small grids and short programs, leaving open whether the finding generalizes to more complex DSLs, larger search spaces, or continuous-action settings. The paper effectively argues that the field has been missing a critical baseline and that the design of latent spaces for program synthesis remains an open problem.
