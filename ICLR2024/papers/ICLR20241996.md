## Problem

Existing graph signal sampling methods require expensive eigendecompositions of large Laplacian matrices, and the resulting discrete sampling sets become invalid whenever the graph changes (e.g., new nodes are added). This paper asks whether one can solve a single sampling problem at the graphon limit so that the resulting uniqueness set generalizes consistently to any finite graph in a convergent sequence, without recomputing spectra each time.

## Input / Output

The method takes a large graph G_n (adjacency matrix), a graph signal x_n, and a bandwidth cutoff λ, and produces a subgraph G_m of size m whose nodes form a uniqueness set for the signal. Concretely, the graph is first lifted to its induced graphon W_n on [0,1] via an n-equipartition; a coarser q-partition (q ≪ n) is then used to select p uniqueness intervals, from which r nodes are drawn per interval (with local heat-kernel PageRank clustering to ensure diversity). The same sampled intervals can be reused for any future graph G_n' generated from the same graphon, provided node labels are known.

## Method

The paper proves a Poincaré inequality for graphon signals (Thm. 2) showing that if a measurable set S satisfies ‖X‖ ≤ Λ‖L̄X‖ for all X supported on S, then its complement U = [0,1]\S is a uniqueness set for PW_λ(W) whenever λ < 1/Λ (Thm. 3). A second, more constructive route identifies the graphon with a K-component mixture model of random graphs and shows that column-wise Gaussian elimination over the Laplacian eigenvectors of a sufficiently large finite graph G_n recovers one sample from each mixture component with high probability (Thm. 5), yielding a uniqueness set by the almost-orthogonal cone structure from kernelized spectral clustering (Schiebinger et al., 2015). The practical algorithm avoids full spectral computation by coarsening the graphon to a q-node graph ẼG_q and applying the greedy E-optimal heuristic of Anis et al. (2016) on this small graph to select p intervals, then sampling r nodes per interval via local clustering, reducing the dominant cost from O(m|E|) to O(|E| + pq² + m).

## Results

On node classification with subsampled graphs (<10% of full size), graphon sampling achieves 0.49 accuracy on Cora and 0.56 on CiteSeer in the base setting, outperforming random sampling (0.46 and 0.51 respectively) but remaining well below full-graph performance (0.86 and 0.80). Doubling the number of nodes per interval (x2 nodes) closes the gap substantially: graphon sampling reaches 0.73 on Cora and 0.67 on CiteSeer, compared to 0.71 and 0.67 for random sampling. For positional encodings on MalNet-Tiny, graphon-sampled PEs (without isolated nodes) achieve 0.33 mean accuracy versus 0.43 for full-graph PEs and 0.26 without PEs, while reducing PE computation time from 12.40s to 0.075s (~165x speedup).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its graphon-sampling algorithm against two baselines: training on the full graph and training on a randomly sampled subgraph of the same size. Graphon sampling consistently outperforms random sampling on Cora (by 0.02–0.04 accuracy across settings) and CiteSeer (by 0.05–0.08 in the base and x2-communities settings), with the largest gap on CiteSeer x2 communities (0.56 vs. 0.48). On PubMed, the advantage is smaller and sometimes reversed (random sampling wins by 0.04 in the x2-communities setting), which the authors attribute to PubMed having fewer classes. Both subsampling methods remain substantially below full-graph performance (e.g., 0.73 vs. 0.86 on Cora even with doubled nodes), indicating that the subsampled graphs retain only a fraction of the full graph's information. For positional encodings on MalNet-Tiny, graphon-sampled PEs (0.33 without isolated nodes) clearly outperform random-sampled PEs (0.27 without isolated nodes) and both beat the no-PE baseline (0.26), though neither matches full-graph PEs (0.43). The runtime advantage is modest for node classification (~3x speedup) but dramatic for PE computation (~165x speedup: 0.075s vs. 12.40s).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper lifts the graph signal sampling problem (uniqueness sets for Paley-Wiener spaces) from finite graphs, established by Pesenson (2008) and operationalized via Gaussian elimination by Chen et al. (2015) and Anis et al. (2016), to the continuous graphon limit. The key conceptual bridge is connecting graphon uniqueness sets to kernelized spectral clustering geometry (Schiebinger et al., 2015), which provides both a difficulty function quantifying sampling quality and a consistency guarantee that finite-graph sampling sets converge to graphon sampling sets. This positions the work at the intersection of graphon signal processing (Ruiz et al., 2020b, 2021) and classical sampling theory, while the algorithmic component reuses the greedy heuristic from Anis et al. (2016) on a coarsened graphon representation to avoid full spectral computations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical implication is that one solves the sampling problem once at the graphon level and reuses the resulting uniqueness set for any finite graph in the convergent sequence, avoiding repeated expensive eigendecompositions as graphs grow or change. However, the empirical results are modest: graphon sampling only marginally outperforms random sampling on citation networks, and the theoretical guarantees require well-separated mixture components (small difficulty function) that may not hold for real-world graphs. Open questions include whether the framework extends to sparse graph limits (graphops), how sensitive the consistency guarantees are to violations of the separation assumptions, and whether the approach remains advantageous at the million-node scale where graphon estimation itself becomes costly.
