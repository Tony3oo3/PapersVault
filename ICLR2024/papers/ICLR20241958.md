## Problem

Training deep neural networks is computationally expensive, especially for duplicated or similar runs such as model ensembles or fine-tuning pre-trained models. While a trained network's learning trajectory (sequence of intermediate parameters) contains useful information about the dataset, no prior work has attempted to transfer such a trajectory from one initialization to another without retraining. This paper formulates the 'learning transfer problem' and provides the first algorithm to solve it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241958/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source learning trajectory (θ₁⁰, …, θ₁ᵀ) — either actual training checkpoints or a linearly interpolated path between the initial and final parameters — along with a target initial parameter θ₂⁰ and the training dataset. It produces a transferred trajectory (θ₂⁰, …, θ₂ᵀ) where each intermediate parameter θ₂ᵗ is obtained by inductively adding permuted source increments to the target initialization. The linear trajectory variant requires storing only two model copies (start and end) rather than T+1, and the fast variant (FGMT) needs only O(T) gradient evaluations total, making it far cheaper than a full training run.

## Method

The core insight is that two learning trajectories from different random initializations are approximately equivalent up to the permutation symmetry of neural networks (reordering hidden neurons). The transfer is formulated as finding a permutation π that minimizes the sum of squared gradient mismatches along the trajectory: min_π Σ_t ‖π∇_{θ₁ᵗ}L − ∇_{θ₂,πᵗ}L‖². Because the target parameters θ₂,πᵗ depend on π non-linearly, the problem is decomposed into a sequence of linear sub-problems P₁′, …, P_T′ where each step fixes the target parameters using the previous step's permutation and solves for the new π_s via coordinate descent with the Hungarian algorithm. Two practical techniques reduce cost: (1) replacing the actual trajectory with a linear interpolation between endpoints (which is more stable and requires only 2 model copies of storage), and (2) caching gradients across steps to reduce computation from O(T²) to O(T). A theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) validates the underlying assumption for 2-layer ReLU networks at initialization, showing that with sufficient over-parameterization, a permutation exists making the normalized gradient distance arbitrarily small.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that learning trajectories can be transferred between different initializations via permutation symmetry, achieving non-trivial validation accuracy without any direct training. In ensemble evaluation (Table 3), FGMT matches or exceeds standard ensembles trained from scratch with fewer epochs: on CUB, FGMT reaches 75.62% at 10 epochs vs. 74.02% for standard ensemble at 30 epochs; on CIFAR-10, FGMT achieves 92.11% at 50 epochs vs. 92.26% for standard ensemble at 60 epochs. Computationally, FGMT on ResNet-18/ImageNet takes ~4,239 seconds total (gradient matching + computation) versus ~500,030 seconds for full 100-epoch SGD training, a ~118× speedup. Transferred parameters also accelerate subsequent training, with the benefit being most pronounced in pre-trained initialization scenarios (e.g., ImageNet→Cars, ImageNet→CUB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GMT/FGMT against two baselines: Naive (identity permutation, no alignment) and Oracle (optimal permutation found using the actually trained target parameters). In the random initialization scenario (MNIST, CIFAR-10, ImageNet), FGMT/GMT closely track the Oracle baseline throughout the trajectory, while Naive also achieves reasonable accuracy since random initializations are more similar. In the pre-trained initialization scenario (ImageNet→Cars, ImageNet→CUB, CIFAR-10→CIFAR-100), Naive consistently fails to transfer trajectories, while FGMT/GMT still closely approximate Oracle up to mid-trajectory before accuracy degrades. For subsequent training, transferred parameters from FGMT/GMT and Oracle converge faster than Naive-transferred parameters in pre-trained scenarios, though all methods perform similarly in random initialization scenarios. The Oracle baseline serves as an upper bound that requires full training of the target, making it impractical; FGMT approximates it with only O(T) gradient computations. The paper also shows that transferred parameters inherit the target initialization's loss landscape basin and generalization ability, outperforming a simple permuted source parameter (via Git Re-basin) in basin membership.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the permutation-symmetry line of work (Entezari et al., 2021; Ainsworth et al., 2023) from matching *endpoints* of training to matching *entire learning trajectories*. While Git Re-basin and related LMC studies align two final parameters via a single permutation, here the authors formulate a new problem—learning transfer—where a sequence of intermediate parameters must be mapped, and they solve it by successively matching gradients along the trajectory using the same coordinate-descent/Hungarian-algorithm machinery. The linear-trajectory approximation draws on monotonic linear interpolation results (Goodfellow et al., 2015; Frankle, 2020), and the connection to model editing (Ilharco et al., 2023) is made explicit: a task vector is a one-step trajectory, and GMT/FGMT generalize this to multi-step transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is clear: if a foundation model is updated, its fine-tuned derivatives could in principle be 're-targeted' by transferring their fine-tuning trajectories rather than retraining from scratch, and ensemble members could be generated at a fraction of the cost. However, the current method is constrained: Assumption (P) is only theoretically validated for 2-layer ReLU at initialization, empirically holds only in the early phase for modern architectures, and degrades noticeably for Adam optimizers and large-scale datasets (SUN-397, iNaturalist). The most open question is whether the gradient-matching approach can be made to track trajectories over longer horizons for deep, modern networks, which would be necessary for the foundation-model-update scenario the authors envision.
