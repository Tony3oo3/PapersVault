## Problem

Classical Bayesian filters (Kalman, particle, unscented) require matrix storage/inversion or large particle sets, making them impractical in high-dimensional state spaces such as neural network weight spaces. They are also highly sensitive to misspecification of process noise and dynamics, which is nearly unavoidable in real-world systems. The paper asks whether the filtering equations can be replaced entirely by an optimization procedure that is easier to tune and naturally scales to high dimensions.

## Input / Output

At each timestep the filter consumes a noisy observation y_t, a differentiable loss function ℓ_t (negative log-likelihood), a transition function f_t(x) = E[x_t|x_{t-1}] (not the full transition distribution), and the previous state estimate. It outputs a single point estimate (MAP) of the latent state, with no covariance or particle set maintained. The state dimensionality is unconstrained by the algorithm itself — the Yearbook experiment operates on 28,193 CNN parameters — and the loss can be set to plain MSE by fixing R_t = I, removing the need to specify measurement noise explicitly.

## Method

The core reformulation treats the Bayesian update step as K iterations of an adaptive optimizer (Adam, RMSprop, Adagrad, etc.) applied to the negative log-likelihood, initialized at the predicted state μ_t⁻. The predict step uses the first-order delta method to propagate the state estimate through f_t, eliminating matrix-matrix products. The key theoretical result (building on Santos, 1996) shows that in the linear-Gaussian case, K steps of gradient descent with a specific learning-rate matrix exactly recovers the Kalman filter update, and more generally that the choice of optimizer and step count K implicitly defines an equivalent prior covariance — a dual formulation of Bayes' rule where the optimizer hyperparameters internalize process and measurement noise. This means the user specifies an optimizer and a step budget rather than explicit filtering equations, making the approach robust to dynamics misspecification and trivially scalable to high-dimensional, nonconvex state spaces.

## Results

On the Yearbook dataset (28,193-parameter CNN), the Implicit MAP Filter with Adam (50 steps) achieves 84.75% test accuracy, outperforming the Variational Kalman Filter (80.0%), Particle Filter (66.7%), and Direct Fit (80.4%). On the toy nonlinear system (R_t=2), IMAP with Adam achieves RMSE of 5.84 (Q=3), competitive with UKF (5.76) and far better than EKF (34.9) or IEKF (15.3), though still behind the Particle Filter (2.80). On the stochastic Lorenz attractor with the most misspecified dynamics (Gaussian random walk), IMAP with gradient descent achieves RMSE 1.561, statistically equivalent to the optimized EKF (1.561) while storing only D parameters versus D+D².

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the EKF and IEKF, the Implicit MAP Filter wins decisively on the toy nonlinear system: IMAP(Adam) achieves RMSE 5.84 (Q=3) versus 34.91 for EKF and 15.32 for IEKF, which both tend to diverge under strong nonlinearity. Against the UKF, IMAP is competitive at low process noise (5.70 vs 4.55 RMSE at Q=1) but surpasses it at high noise (7.96 vs 9.56 at Q=5), and avoids the D+D² covariance storage. Against the Particle Filter (n=1000), IMAP trails on the low-dimensional toy system (5.84 vs 2.80 RMSE) and Lorenz attractor (1.56 vs 1.57 RMSE, statistically tied), but the PF's advantage vanishes in high dimensions where particle count must scale. On the Yearbook task, IMAP outperforms the Variational Kalman Filter by 4.8 percentage points in test accuracy (84.75% vs 79.97%) and the Particle Filter by 18.0 points (84.75% vs 66.72%), while also matching or exceeding Direct Fit (84.75% vs 80.45%) without overfitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reverses the direction of a well-studied connection: prior work (Aitchison 2020, De Freitas et al. 2000, Ollivier 2018) derived adaptive optimizers from Bayesian filtering equations, while here the authors use adaptive optimizers (Adam, RMSprop) to implicitly define Bayesian filters. The theoretical foundation rests on Santos (1996), who showed that the Kalman filter update is equivalent to K steps of truncated gradient descent on the likelihood, and the paper extends this from a single-step equivalence to a general framework where any optimizer with K steps implicitly specifies a prior. It departs from variational Kalman filter approaches (Auvinen et al. 2010, Bardsley et al. 2013) that scale classical filters via low-rank or inverse-covariance approximations, instead replacing the entire filtering machinery with a black-box optimizer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is that Bayesian filtering becomes accessible to deep learning practitioners who can specify an optimizer and a step count rather than deriving and implementing distribution-specific filtering equations, with demonstrated scalability to 28K-parameter CNN weight spaces. Key limitations are the loss of full posterior uncertainty (only point estimates are produced) and the difficulty of tuning optimizer hyperparameters to match the true process noise, which the paper acknowledges but does not fully resolve. Open questions include whether uncertainty estimates can be recovered (e.g., via ensembles or variational approximations) and whether the approach extends to risk-sensitive applications where covariance information is essential for decision-making.
