## Problem

Standard reinforcement learning assumes additive, Markovian rewards that are independent of previously visited states, but many important applications—coverage control, experiment design, informative path planning—naturally exhibit diminishing returns where the value of visiting a state depends on which states have already been visited. This paper introduces SUBRL, a paradigm for RL with non-additive, history-dependent submodular rewards, establishes that the problem is inapproximable up to any constant factor in general, and proposes a practical algorithm with provable guarantees under structural assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a submodular MDP defined by a state space, action space, transition dynamics, finite horizon H, and a monotone submodular set function F over time-augmented state trajectories. The policy is parameterized by a neural network (MLP for discrete spaces, MLP with Tanh output for continuous spaces) that maps either the current state (Markovian variant, SUBPO-M) or the full trajectory history (non-Markovian variant, SUBPO-NM, using autoregressive policies like RNNs) to a categorical or continuous action distribution. The key distinction from standard RL is that the reward signal at each timestep is the marginal gain F(s_{j+1}|τ_{0:j}) of the submodular function, not a per-state scalar reward.

## Method

SUBPO is a policy gradient algorithm whose core insight is to decompose the non-additive trajectory reward F(τ) into a telescoping sum of marginal gains F(s_{j+1}|τ_{0:j}), then use these marginal gains as the per-timestep reward signal in the policy gradient estimator (Theorem 2). This decomposition removes the sparsity inherent in trajectory-level rewards and substantially reduces gradient variance, while a history-dependent baseline b(τ_{0:i}) further incorporates causality. The algorithm operates in epochs: it samples trajectories under the current stochastic policy, computes the unbiased gradient estimator using marginal gains, and updates policy parameters via stochastic gradient ascent (Eq. 3). Under the ε-Bandit SMDP assumption, the objective J(π) is shown to be monotone DR-submodular over a convex polytope, enabling a (1−1/e) approximation via a modified Frank-Wolfe algorithm; for general SMDPs with bounded curvature c, SUBPO achieves a (1−c) constant-factor approximation to the optimal non-Markovian policy.

## Results

The paper evaluates SUBPO across six environments (informative path planning, item collection, Bayesian D-experimental design, building exploration, car racing, and MuJoCo Ant) using training curves of normalized J(π) vs. epochs rather than a single numerical results table. Across all discrete environments, SUBPO-M achieves performance comparable to SUBPO-NM while being significantly more sample efficient, whereas the modular baseline MODPO consistently gets stuck repeatedly maximizing its per-state modular reward. In continuous high-dimensional domains (car racing with 6-D state/2-D action, MuJoCo Ant with 30-D state/8-D action), SUBPO-M successfully learns to drive the race track and explore 2D space, while MODPO learns to stay stationary to maximize its modular reward. In building exploration, SUBPO-NM (non-Markovian) outperforms SUBPO-M by tracking history to explore both rooms, confirming that history-dependent policies are necessary in some settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is MODPO, a standard modular RL method that replaces the submodular reward F(τ) with the additive per-state reward F({s}) and maximizes the undiscounted sum. MODPO consistently underperforms across all six environments: in informative path planning it gets trapped in a single high-density region; in Bayesian experiment design it cannot optimize information gained over the full trajectory; and in continuous domains (car racing, MuJoCo Ant) it learns a stationary policy that maximizes the immediate modular reward without moving. SUBPO-M (Markovian) matches or nearly matches SUBPO-NM (non-Markovian) in most environments while requiring fewer samples due to its smaller policy search space. SUBPO-NM achieves the best performance in building exploration and item collection, where tracking collected items or visited rooms is essential, but at the cost of a larger state space (e.g., 700×6 for car racing). The paper does not report a single quantitative metric table; all comparisons are made via training curves of normalized J(π) over epochs, with 10 random environments × 20 runs each for discrete settings and 20 random seeds for continuous settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces SUBRL, the first framework for reinforcement learning with submodular (non-additive, history-dependent) reward functions, departing from the standard assumption of additive Markovian rewards. Methodologically, it extends classical policy gradient estimators (Sutton et al., 1999) by replacing trajectory returns with cumulative marginal gains, drawing on the greedy algorithm tradition from submodular optimization (Nemhauser et al., 1978). Theoretically, it connects SUBRL to DR-submodular maximization (Bian et al., 2017) under an ε-Bandit MDP assumption, and establishes hardness via reduction from the submodular orienteering problem (Chekuri & Pal, 2005). The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from convex RL, which optimizes over state-visit distributions rather than trajectory-level set functions, and generalizes the submodular bandit problem to a full MDP setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The inapproximability result (log-factor hardness even for deterministic SMDPs) is a significant theoretical contribution that delineates the fundamental limits of SUBRL, while the practical algorithm SUBPO demonstrates that these worst-case bounds do not preclude effective learning in structured settings. Open questions include: learning unknown submodular rewards via function approximation, exploiting linearity or smoothness in the objective to improve sample complexity beyond the (1−1/e) guarantee, and developing principled state representations (e.g., RNNs) for history-dependent policies in environments where Markovian representations are provably insufficient. The framework's applicability to diverse domains—biodiversity monitoring, Bayesian experiment design, informative path planning, and high-dimensional robotics—suggests broad relevance for any RL problem where diminishing returns naturally arise.
