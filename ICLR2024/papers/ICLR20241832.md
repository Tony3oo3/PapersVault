## Problem

Contrastive learning for time series relies on data augmentations to generate positive pairs, but unlike vision or language, time series patterns are not easily recognizable to humans, making it impractical to select meaningful augmentations by intuition. Existing approaches either use fixed augmentations (jitter, cutout) that may be suboptimal for specific datasets, or apply the InfoMin principle from vision, which softens the label-preserving property and uses strong transforms that can undermine time series semantics. The paper asks: what constitutes a 'good view' for time series contrastive learning, and how can such views be obtained adaptively per instance?

## Input / Output

The method takes a time series instance as a T×F matrix (T sequence length, F feature dimension; F=1 for univariate) and produces a D-dimensional embedding via a dilated CNN encoder. For forecasting, a frozen encoder feeds a linear probe (L2-regularized least squares) that outputs L_y future values; for classification, an RBF-kernel SVM is trained on the learned representations. The augmentation network itself outputs two per-instance masks: a binary factorization mask h ∈ {0,1}^T identifying informative time steps, and a non-zero transformation mask g ∈ R_{≠0}^T for lossless scaling, both generated by separate projector heads on a shared dilated CNN backbone.

## Method

AutoTCL factorizes each input x into an informative part x* = h(x) ⊙ x and a task-irrelevant residual Δx, then applies an invertible non-zero mask transformation v* = g(x) ⊙ x* followed by random timestamp masking η(v*, Δv) to produce the augmented view. The factorization mask is generated via a hard concrete distribution (Gumbel-softmax relaxation of Bernoulli) to enable gradient-based optimization of discrete binary selections. The augmentation network is trained with the Principle of Relevant Information (PRI), minimizing βH(v*) + D(P_x || P_v*) approximated by an l0-norm penalty on the mask plus an MMD term, plus a temporal consistency triplet loss encouraging nearby time steps to share similar mask values. The encoder is trained with a combined global InfoNCE loss (inter-instance) and local contrastive loss (intra-instance subsequence pairs), using an alternating GAN-like schedule where the encoder updates M times per augmentation update. The framework is encoder-agnostic and unifies common augmentations (cropping, scaling, jittering) as special cases of the mask-based transformation.

## Results

On univariate time series forecasting across six datasets (ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, Electricity, Weather, Lora), AutoTCL achieves an average MSE of 0.157 and MAE of 0.258, reducing MSE by 6.5% and MAE by 4.7% over the strongest baseline CoST (MSE 0.168, MAE 0.271). On multivariate forecasting, AutoTCL attains average MSE 0.545 and MAE 0.499, outperforming CoST (MSE 0.561, MAE 0.505) by 2.9% and 1.2% respectively. On the UEA classification benchmark (30 datasets), AutoTCL achieves 74.2% average accuracy with a rank of 2.3, surpassing the second-best method InfoTS (73.0% accuracy, rank 2.37) by 1.2 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoTCL consistently outperforms all contrastive learning baselines on univariate forecasting: it beats CoST (the closest competitor) on every dataset, with the largest gap on Lora (MSE 0.177 vs. 0.186) and ETTm1 (MSE 0.046 vs. 0.054). Against InfoTS, AutoTCL achieves lower MSE on 5 of 6 datasets, though InfoTS edges out on ETTh2 (MSE 0.149 vs. 0.158). Traditional forecasting methods (Informer, TCN, N-BEATS, LogTrans) are substantially outperformed, with Informer showing the worst average MSE (0.486) due to its transformer architecture struggling on shorter sequences. On multivariate forecasting, AutoTCL wins 7 of 12 dataset-prediction-length comparisons against CoST, though CoST retains a slight edge on ETTh1 (MSE 0.650 vs. 0.656) and ETTm1 (MSE 0.409 vs. 0.409, tied). For classification on UEA, AutoTCL achieves the best average accuracy (74.2%) and rank (2.3), narrowly surpassing InfoTS (73.0%, rank 2.37) and clearly outperforming TS2Vec (70.4%) and TNC (67.0%). The ablation studies confirm that removing the factorization mask h(x) increases MSE by 10.2%, removing the transformation g(x) increases MSE by 37.6%, and static augmentations (Cutout, Jitter) perform worse than no augmentation at all, validating the adaptive parametric approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the InfoMin framework (Tian et al., 2020) for adaptive view generation in contrastive learning, but departs from it in two key ways: it shows via Property 1 that invertible transformations cannot increase entropy, and it replaces adversarial training with the Principle of Relevant Information (Principe, 2010) to avoid undermining time series semantics. The factorization mask is generated using hard concrete distributions (Louizos et al., 2018), and the overall architecture follows the dilated-CNN backbone and local+global contrastive loss structure of TS2Vec (Yue et al., 2022) and CoST (Woo et al., 2022). The paper unifies common time series augmentations (cropping, scaling, jittering) as special cases of its mask-based transformation, positioning AutoTCL as a general framework subsuming prior static augmentation strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical pain point in time series contrastive learning: the lack of principled, instance-specific augmentation selection that avoids laborious trial-and-error. The theoretical guarantees (task-agnostic label preservation and increased information content) provide a clean justification for the factorization approach, and the encoder-agnostic, plug-and-play design makes it broadly applicable. However, improvements over the strongest baseline (CoST) are moderate (6.5% MSE, 4.7% MAE univariate), and the method has only been validated with dilated-CNN backbones; whether the factorization mask provides genuine interpretability of informative time steps remains unexplored. Open questions include extension to frequency-domain augmentations, integration with transformer-based encoders, and whether the PRI objective generalizes beyond time series.
