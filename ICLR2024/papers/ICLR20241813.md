## Problem

The efficient coding hypothesis predicts sparse, high-dimensional representations, while distributed coding provides robustness at the cost of efficiency. Prior work in mouse V1 suggested an optimal balance between these extremes, but it remained unclear whether this tradeoff generalizes to human high-level object representations in occipitotemporal cortex (OTC). This paper asks whether a single inductive bias—dropout—can operationalize the sparse-to-distributed continuum and reveal where the human visual system falls within it.

## Input / Output

The models take RGB images (160→192 px) as input and produce 1000-way ImageNet class predictions; for brain comparison, 515 COCO stimuli are fed to both the fc6 layer (4096 units) and 8 human subjects scanned at 7T (1.8 mm voxels, ~34,195 OTC voxels). Outputs include eigenspectrum decay rates (α), classical unweighted RSA correlation scores, and lesion-robustness curves. A key methodological detail is that brain eigenspectra are estimated via a Generative Modeling of Signal and Noise (GSN) procedure that subtracts noise covariance (from stimulus repetitions) from data covariance, yielding denoised signal spectra directly comparable to the noiseless model spectra.

## Method

The core approach is 'controlled model rearing': training 10 AlexNet models with dropout probability p varying from 0 to 0.9 in 0.1 steps at fully connected layers fc6 and fc7, while holding all other inductive biases constant, thereby isolating dropout's effect on representational geometry. This produces a systematic continuum from sparse/high-dimensional to distributed/low-dimensional codes, verified via eigenspectrum decay (α from a power-law fit to PCA eigenvalues) and representational trajectory analysis (MDS of layer-wise RDMs). The family is then probed along two functional axes: lesion robustness (zeroing 0–95% of fc6 units at inference, repeated 10×) and emergent brain alignment (classical unweighted RSA against NSD OTC data, with no feature re-weighting). The key finding is that p = 0.7 simultaneously maximizes lesion robustness, brain RSA correlation, and matches the human OTC eigenspectrum decay rate (mean α = 1.13), revealing an optimal balance point between coding efficiency and robustness.

## Results

Across 10 AlexNet models with dropout p varying from 0 to 0.9, all models maintain ImageNet top-5 accuracy within a 10% range, with peak accuracy at p=0.5 (the original AlexNet setting). The eigenspectrum decay rate (α) of layer fc6 systematically decreases with increasing dropout, confirming a transition from high-dimensional to low-dimensional representational geometry. Optimal robustness to simulated fc6 lesioning (0–95% units zeroed) is observed at p=0.7, beyond which performance becomes brittle under large lesions. The p=0.7 model also achieves the highest emergent RSA correlation with human occipitotemporal cortex (OTC) from the Natural Scenes Dataset (8 subjects, 515 COCO stimuli, 7T fMRI), and its α most closely matches the human OTC mean α of 1.13 ± 0.05 (estimated via the GSN denoising procedure).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline architectures; instead, it compares 10 internally controlled model variants (dropout p = 0.0–0.9) against each other and against human fMRI data. The p=0.7 model emerges as the clear winner on two complementary axes: it achieves the highest robustness to fc6 lesioning (maintaining accuracy when 50–80% of units are zeroed, whereas p=0.0 shows monotonic decline) and the highest emergent RSA correlation with human OTC across all 8 NSD subjects. The p=0.5 model achieves the highest raw ImageNet top-5 accuracy but is not the most lesion-robust or brain-aligned, demonstrating that task accuracy, robustness, and neural predictivity are not co-optimized at the same dropout level. The p=0.7 model's eigenspectrum decay rate (α) most closely matches the human OTC mean of 1.13 ± 0.05, while both the p=0.0 (too high-dimensional) and p=0.9 (too low-dimensional) models deviate substantially. A parallel analysis on layer fc7 (Appendix A.3) confirms that p=0.7 again yields the highest brain alignment, supporting the generality of the finding across layers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the eigenspectrum decay framework of Stringer et al. (2019), which identified an optimal balance between high- and low-dimensional coding in mouse V1, to human occipitotemporal cortex (OTC) and to a family of DNN models. The key methodological contribution is using dropout proportion as a single 'controlled rearing' knob to systematically sweep the sparse-to-distributed coding continuum while holding all other inductive biases constant, building on the efficient coding hypothesis (Barlow, 1959; Olshausen & Field, 1996) and distributed coding literature. For the brain side, the paper introduces a denoised eigenspectrum estimation via Generative Modeling of Signal and Noise (GSN) applied to 7T fMRI data from the Natural Scenes Dataset, going beyond the cross-validated PCA approach used in the mouse V1 work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The convergence of three independent metrics—lesion robustness, emergent brain alignment, and eigenspectrum decay rate—at the same dropout level (p=0.7) provides a compelling, if preliminary, argument that the human visual system operates at a specific point on the efficiency-robustness tradeoff. However, the evidence base is narrow: a single architecture (AlexNet), a single brain region (OTC), and the fc7 analyses show that these signatures do not always co-occur across layers, complicating the interpretation. Open questions include whether the spectral signatures generalize across architectures and visual hierarchy stages, what biological mechanism (if any) parallels dropout, and whether alternative regularization (L1, L2) produces the same representational geometry.
