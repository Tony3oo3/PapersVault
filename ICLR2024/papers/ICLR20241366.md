## Problem

Widely used instruction-finetuning (IFT) datasets such as ALPACA's 52k samples contain a large fraction of low-quality instances with incorrect or irrelevant responses that mislead and degrade the fine-tuned model. While stronger teacher LLMs can generate better data, their outputs still include subtle errors that are hard for humans to detect, and no efficient automatic filtering strategy existed to identify and remove these poor-quality samples at scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241366/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an IFT dataset of (instruction, input, response) triplets and a strong API LLM (e.g., ChatGPT) acting as an auto-grader. Each triplet is scored on a 0–5 accuracy scale via a structured prompt; triplets scoring below a threshold τ (set to 4.5) are discarded, yielding a filtered subset (e.g., 9,229 of 52,002 ALPACA samples). The filtered data is then fed into a standard IFT pipeline to produce the final instruction-following model, with no changes to the base model architecture or training hyperparameters.

## Method

ALPAGASUS introduces a simple LLM-as-judge data filtering step: a strong LLM (ChatGPT) is prompted to rate each (instruction, input, response) triplet on a 0–5 accuracy scale, and only triplets meeting or exceeding a threshold τ=4.5 are retained for fine-tuning. The filtered 9k subset is then used to fine-tune LLaMA with the exact same IFT script, hyperparameters, and training configuration as the original ALPACA, isolating data quality as the sole variable. The key finding is that this 17.75% subset produces a model that significantly outperforms the full 52k ALPACA across four instruction test sets, matches ≥90% of its teacher (Text-Davinci-003), and reduces 7B training time from 80 minutes to 14 minutes. The approach generalizes across datasets (ALPACA, Dolly, GPT4LLM), base models (LLaMA-1, LLaMA-2), LLM filters (ChatGPT, Claude-2), and rating dimensions (accuracy, helpfulness), establishing a data-centric IFT paradigm where quality outweighs quantity.

## Results

ALPAGASUS-9k (trained on only 9,229 high-quality samples filtered from ALPACA's 52k) significantly outperforms ALPACA-52k on all four instruction-following test sets (Vicuna, Koala, Self-Instruct, WizardLM) for both 7B and 13B model sizes, as judged by GPT-4. On benchmarks, ALPAGASUS-7B achieves 33.76 on BBH, 26.03 on DROP, and 12.20 on HumanEval (vs. 33.01, 25.87, 11.69 for ALPACA-52k), though it trails on MMLU (38.78 vs. 40.86). The 13B variant matches ≥90% of its teacher Text-Davinci-003, 81.2% of Claude, and 78.4% of ChatGPT. Training time drops from 80 min to 14 min (7B) and 5.5 h to 1 h (13B), reducing AWS cost from $27.31 to $4.78 (7B) and $225.28 to $40.96 (13B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ALPACA-52k (the primary baseline), ALPAGASUS-9k wins on all four instruction-following test sets for both 7B and 13B models, and also outperforms a random 9k subset (ALPACA-9k-random) on every benchmark, confirming that quality-guided selection matters beyond mere data reduction. On MMLU, ALPAGASUS-7B (38.78) and 13B (46.12) still trail ALPACA-52k (40.86 / 47.89), suggesting the filtered subset under-represents certain knowledge-heavy instructions. Against the teacher model Text-Davinci-003, ALPAGASUS-13B achieves 90.1% capacity; against Claude-v1.1 it reaches 81.2%, and against ChatGPT 78.4%. On the human-written Dolly dataset, filtering 15k down to 3k samples yields a model that outperforms the full-15k model on instruction-following and on 3/4 benchmarks (BBH, DROP, HumanEval). On the GPT4LLM dataset (GPT-4-generated responses), filtering to 13.7k samples (25% of original) produces a model that matches or exceeds the full-dataset model on Vicuna and WizardLM, demonstrating the method generalizes to stronger teacher outputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on ALPACA (Taori et al., 2023), which established the paradigm of instruction-fine-tuning LLaMA on 52k machine-generated (instruction, input, response) triplets from Text-Davinci-003. It repurposes the LLM-as-judge paradigm—previously applied to model evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023; Chiang et al., 2023)—as an automatic data-quality grader for training curation, a use case not explored in prior IFT work. Conceptually, it aligns with the data-centric AI literature (Chu et al., 2016; Motamedi et al., 2021) but is the first to demonstrate that a simple threshold-based LLM filter can replace expensive human annotation for IFT data selection, departing from the prevailing 'more data is better' assumption in instruction tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 9k high-quality samples (17.75% of ALPACA) outperform 52k across all four test sets, multiple model sizes, and both machine-generated and human-written datasets challenges a core assumption in IFT and offers a 5.7× training speedup with substantial cost reduction. The method's generality across base models (LLaMA-1/2), LLM filters (ChatGPT, Claude-2), and dataset types makes it immediately applicable to practitioners. Key limitations include testing only at 7B/13B scale (open question for 33B–175B), a higher-than-average filtering ratio for coding data revealing category imbalance, and dependence on access to a strong API LLM for the filtering step. Future work on category-aware filtering, finer-grained rating scales, and scaling to larger models would strengthen the contribution.
