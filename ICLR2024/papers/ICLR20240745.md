## Problem

Despite strong per-dataset performance, no general-purpose pre-trained model for time series forecasting exists analogous to GPT for NLP, and existing deep learning architectures fail to explicitly exploit the intrinsic temporal structure (trend, seasonality, residuals) that distinguishes time series from generic sequences. The paper asks whether a GPT-style architecture, augmented with time-series-specific inductive biases and prompting, can achieve strong zero-shot generalization across unseen domains.

## Input / Output

The model takes K historical timestamps of a multivariate time series (n channels) and optionally a short textual summary as context. The raw input is first decomposed via STL into three additive components (trend, seasonality, residual), each of which is instance-normalized, split into overlapping patches of fixed length, and embedded. The output is an H-step forecast produced as the additive sum of three component-specific predictions, each generated by a fully connected head on the corresponding segment of the GPT output, followed by de-normalization.

## Method

TEMPO decomposes each input channel into trend, seasonality, and residual via locally weighted STL smoothing, then patches and embeds each component separately. A semi-soft prompt—initialized from the text template 'Predict the future time step given the [trend/season/residual]' and made learnable via a linear projection—is prepended to each component's patch embedding, providing interpretable inductive bias while retaining soft-prompt adaptability. The three prompted sequences are concatenated and fed into a GPT-2 backbone adapted with LoRA and updated position/layer-norm parameters. A key theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) proves that self-attention, which learns orthogonal transformations, cannot automatically disentangle non-orthogonal trend and seasonal signals, justifying the explicit decomposition. For interpretability, a generalized additive model (GAM) is fit on the GPT outputs to quantify each component's contribution via SHAP values, and a prompt pool mechanism is explored to handle non-stationary distributional shifts.

## Results

Under the zero-shot 'many-to-one' setting (trained on multiple source domains, tested on an unseen target), TEMPO achieves the best average MSE/MAE across all seven benchmark datasets. On ETTm1, TEMPO reaches 0.501 MSE / 0.458 MAE (average over horizons 96–720), improving over PatchTST (0.741/0.566) by ~19% in MAE, and over GPT2 (0.555/0.485) by ~5.6% in MAE. On Weather, TEMPO attains 0.282/0.316, outperforming the next-best DLinear (0.284/0.329) and GPT2 (0.356/0.347). On the TETS financial dataset (S&P 500 EBITDA), TEMPO achieves the lowest SMAPE across all four cross-domain sectors (e.g., 25.9 for Consumer Defensive vs. 26.34 for LLaMA), and on GDELT news-mention forecasting it leads all baselines by 1–4 SMAPE points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TEMPO (zero-shot) outperforms all 17 baselines on the primary long-term forecasting benchmark (Table 1). Against the strongest pre-trained LLM backbones, TEMPO beats GPT2 by 2.4% MSE on ETTm1 (0.501 vs 0.555) and by 20.8% on Weather (0.282 vs 0.356); it similarly surpasses T5 by 13.8% on ETTm1. Against dedicated time-series transformers, the gains are larger: TEMPO reduces ETTm1 MSE by 32% vs PatchTST (0.501 vs 0.741) and by 36% vs TimesNet (0.501 vs 0.607), while on Electricity it cuts MSE by 56% vs PatchTST (0.216 vs 0.490). DLinear is the closest non-pre-trained competitor on ECL (0.222 vs 0.216) and Weather (0.284 vs 0.282), but TEMPO still leads on average. In the multimodal TETS/GDELT experiments (Table 2), TEMPO with textual context beats LLaMA, GPT2, BERT, T5, Informer, PatchTST, Reformer, and DLinear on every sector and event type. Notably, in Table 7 TEMPO's zero-shot results on ETTh1/ETTh2 surpass full-shot (in-domain trained) iTransformer, Crossformer, TiDE, SCINet, and FEDformer, demonstrating that the pre-trained + prompt + decomposition pipeline generalizes better than task-specific fine-tuning. In the self-supervised setting (Table 6), TEMPO fine-tuned on only 5% in-domain data outperforms GPT4TS, T5, BERT, and PatchTST on ECL, Traffic, and Weather. Finally, against the classical ARIMA baseline (Table 8), TEMPO achieves 5–8× lower MSE on ECL and Traffic, confirming the advantage of a foundation-model approach over per-series statistical fitting.

## Relation to Prior Work

TEMPO sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the growing effort to repurpose pre-trained language models (GPT-2, T5, LLaMA) for time series forecasting, as in GPT4TS, Time-LLM, and T5-based approaches, and (2) classical statistical decomposition (STL, N-BEATS) that explicitly separates trend, seasonality, and residual. The key departure from prior LLM-for-time-series work is the explicit STL decomposition applied *before* the transformer, justified by a theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) showing that self-attention's orthogonal transformations cannot disentangle non-orthogonal trend and seasonal signals. The patching mechanism is borrowed from PatchTST, the training strategy (updating position embeddings and layer norms) from Time-LLM, and parameter-efficient adaptation uses LoRA. The semi-soft prompt design—initializing prompt vectors from tokenized text templates like 'Predict the future time step given the [trend]'—bridges hard-prompt interpretability with soft-prompt adaptability, a combination not previously explored for time series.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a credible case that time series forecasting is ready for a foundation-model paradigm, demonstrated by competitive zero-shot (many-to-one) performance and a new multimodal benchmark (TETS) combining S&P 500 financials with textual context. However, several open questions remain: the backbone is GPT-2 (~124M params), and the authors themselves note that stronger LLMs with better numerical reasoning could shift results; the prompt pool design is still exploratory with limited ablation; and the zero-shot setting, while novel, uses a relatively narrow 'many-to-one' protocol. The TETS dataset and the decomposition-plus-prompt recipe are practical contributions that could seed further work, but the overall performance gains over the GPT-2 baseline are modest (a few percentage points in MSE/MAE), and the approach has not yet been validated at the scale of truly large foundation models.
