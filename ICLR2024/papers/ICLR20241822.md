## Problem

Adaptive experiment design is well-studied for direct RCTs but remains largely unexplored for indirect experiments where treatment assignment is impractical or unethical (e.g., AI-augmented human decision-making). In indirect experiments, sample efficiency depends on heterogeneous compliance levels, the specific estimator used, and the data-generating process, so naively applying RCT-style adaptive strategies can perform worse than uniform sampling. The paper asks: how can we automatically learn a data-efficient adaptive instrument-selection policy that works with general (non)-linear two-stage estimators?

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular data samples S_i = {X_i, Z_i, A_i, Y_i} collected under potentially different past policies, along with a parameterized instrument-selection policy π_φ (modeled as a deep network) and a black-box two-stage estimator θ (e.g., 2SLS, DeepIV, logistic-regression-based CATE). It outputs an optimized policy π* that minimizes expected MSE of the counterfactual prediction f, deployed sequentially in batches via a receding-horizon scheme. The estimator θ is treated as a black box—only its moment conditions M_n and Q_n need to be differentiable—and the policy can be arbitrarily complex (neural network), supporting high-dimensional or even natural-language instruments in principle.

## Method

The core algorithm (DIA) performs gradient-based optimization of the instrument-selection policy π using a REINFORCE-style objective, but addresses two critical variance challenges. First, it replaces the naive MSE reward with a leave-one-out control variate computed via influence functions (Von Mises expansion of the MSE functional), reducing gradient variance from O(n) to O(1) while requiring only a single forward pass with Hessian-vector products rather than n separate re-training runs. Second, for off-policy evaluation (needing to assess a new policy π' on data collected under π), it introduces multi-rejection sampling that exploits the symmetry of the estimator to avoid the exponential variance of standard importance sampling, instead scaling variance with the worst single-sample importance ratio. The DIA algorithm then extends this to sequential batched collection by defining an effective policy π_φ^eff that blends historical data with the planned future batch, updating π_φ at each re-allocation step in a receding-horizon fashion.

## Results

Across three domains (linear IV, conditional IV with logistic regression, and TripAdvisor with neural networks), DIA achieves a 1.2× to 10× improvement in MSE over uniform instrument allocation, meaning it requires 80% to an order-of-magnitude less data to reach the same estimation accuracy. In the linear IV setting, DIA approaches the oracle (brute-force optimal) policy, while in the TripAdvisor domain with neural network estimators, DIA still provides substantial gains. Performance improves with more re-allocation batches (up to 5 in the main experiments), and a 'U'-shaped trend is observed with respect to the number of instruments: gains are largest in the middle regime where there are enough instruments to be strategic but not so many that learning the optimal policy becomes intractable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is uniform instrument allocation (randomly selecting instruments without adaptation), which DIA consistently outperforms across all three experimental domains. In the linear IV setting, DIA is also compared against an oracle policy found via brute-force search; DIA approaches but does not fully match the oracle, with the gap narrowing as more re-allocation batches are allowed. The paper does not compare against other named adaptive experiment design methods (e.g., those from the direct experiment design literature) in the main experiments, as those methods are designed for direct RCTs rather than indirect experiments with instrumental variables. The key finding is that naively applying adaptive design strategies from direct experiments (as illustrated in Figure 1) can actually perform worse than uniform sampling in the presence of confounding, whereas DIA's influence-function-based gradient approach is specifically tailored to the indirect experiment setting and reliably improves upon uniform allocation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-studied adaptive experiment design literature (which assumes direct treatment assignment) to the substantially harder setting of indirect experiments where treatment can only be influenced through instrumental variables. Methodologically, it builds on REINFORCE-style policy gradients (Williams, 1992) but addresses their prohibitive variance through two novel tools: influence functions derived for black-box two-stage causal estimators (extending Koh & Liang, 2017 and the econometric literature of Newey & Powell, 2003), and a multi-rejection sampling scheme that avoids the exponential variance of standard importance weighting. The closest prior work, Gupta et al. (2021), optimizes sampling across data sources for a specific linear IV estimator, whereas this paper handles adaptive sampling within a single source and supports general non-linear estimators like DeepIV (Hartford et al., 2017) and the Syrgkanis et al. (2019) framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI systems increasingly operate in suggestive rather than prescriptive modes (LLMs recommending actions to humans who retain agency), the ability to sample-efficiently estimate treatment effects through instruments becomes practically critical. The demonstrated 1.2×–10× MSE improvement means substantially fewer real-world interactions are needed to reach a target estimation accuracy, which matters when each interaction is costly or ethically constrained. Key limitations include reliance on small synthetic/semi-synthetic experiments, the assumption of homogeneous treatment effects across instruments, and static instrument effects; scaling to natural-language instruments and performing valid statistical inference on adaptively collected data remain open problems the authors explicitly flag.
