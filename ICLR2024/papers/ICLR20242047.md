## Problem

Hypernetworks, which predict the parameters of a primary network, are notoriously difficult to train, often converging far more slowly than standard networks or failing to converge at all. This paper identifies a previously unrecognized root cause: a magnitude proportionality between the hypernetwork input scale and the predicted parameter scale, which induces large gradient variance and destabilizes optimization. Standard remedies such as gradient clipping, batch/layer normalization, and adaptive optimizers fail to resolve this issue.

## Input / Output

The hypernetwork receives a low-dimensional conditioning variable γ (a scalar in [0,1] or a small vector, e.g., a prior sample, a loss-weight trade-off, or a spatial rescaling factor) and outputs the full parameter tensor θ of the primary network. The primary network then consumes task-specific inputs—256×256 RGB images for OxfordFlowers-102 classification, 2D coronal MRI slices for OASIS segmentation, or paired moving/fixed MRI volumes for Hypermorph registration—and produces the corresponding task prediction. A key structural detail is that γ is typically a single scalar or a vector of at most 32 dimensions, making the input-to-output magnitude coupling particularly acute.

## Method

Magnitude Invariant Parametrizations (MIP) break the input-output proportionality through two complementary modifications. First, an input encoding E_L2(γ) = [cos(γπ/2), sin(γπ/2)] maps each scalar input dimension to a 2D unit-norm vector, so the hypernetwork always receives a constant-Euclidean-norm input regardless of γ's magnitude (for multi-dimensional inputs, the encoding is applied per-dimension, doubling the input dimensionality). Second, the hypernetwork output is reinterpreted as an additive residual Δθ on top of independent learnable parameters θ⁰, so the primary network weights are θ = θ⁰ + h(E_L2(γ); ω), rather than θ = h(γ; ω) as in the standard formulation. The hypernetwork itself is a standard fully-connected network with LeakyReLU activations and Kaiming fan-out initialization, while θ⁰ is initialized as if it were a regular network's weights. The analytical contribution shows that under zero-bias initialization and piecewise-linear activations, every intermediate layer's activations remain proportional to γ, and this proportionality propagates to the output; MIP's constant-norm input and residual structure sever this chain without adding any extra parameters or inference cost.

## Results

On the OxfordFlowers-102 Bayesian NN task, MIP achieves 83.2% top-1 accuracy with Adam (vs. 78.1% default) and 75.4% with SGD (vs. 1.4% default, where the default formulation essentially fails to train). On HyperMorph (OASIS registration), MIP reaches 72.1% Dice with Adam and 70.5% with SGD, compared to 71.0% and 54.3% for the default formulation. On Scale-Space hypernetworks (OASIS segmentation), MIP achieves 84.4% Dice with Adam and 78.8% with SGD, outperforming the default's 81.4% and 75.3%. The most dramatic improvement is with SGD, where the default hypernetwork often fails to converge at all, while MIP enables stable training across a wide range of learning rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Hyperfan-In and Hyperfan-Out initialization schemes of Chang et al. (2019), MIP outperforms both variants on the HyperMorph task with both SGD and Adam; with SGD, Hyperfan initializations still fail to resolve training instability, while MIP converges reliably. Against normalization strategies (BatchNorm-P, LayerNorm-P, LayerNorm-H, WeightNorm), MIP achieves substantially faster convergence and better final accuracy on Scale-Space hypernetworks; notably, LayerNorm-H diverged early in training for all hyperparameter settings, and WeightNorm produced convergence substantially worse than the default formulation. BatchNorm-P and LayerNorm-P enabled training but did not significantly improve over the default when using Adam, whereas MIP consistently delivered large gains. Overall, MIP wins or ties on every comparison across all four tasks and both optimizers, with the largest margins appearing under SGD where the default formulation frequently fails to train at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper identifies a previously unrecognized optimization pathology in hypernetwork training — a direct proportionality between input magnitude and predicted parameter scale — and addresses it by combining two well-known ideas in a novel way: Fourier-based constant-norm input encoding (borrowed from implicit neural representations) and residual parameter prediction (borrowed from skip connections). It departs from prior hypernetwork initialization work (Hyperfan, Knyazev et al.) by showing those methods assume categorical/embedding inputs and remain susceptible to the proportionality issue with magnitude-encoded scalars. It also contrasts with normalization-based approaches, demonstrating that BatchNorm preserves the proportionality while LayerNorm/WeightNorm eliminate it by rendering the hypernetwork output input-independent, thus destroying the model's conditioning mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Hypernetworks are increasingly central to multi-task learning, meta-learning, generative models, and medical imaging, yet their notorious training instability has limited adoption; MIP offers a zero-overhead fix that consistently stabilizes training across tasks, optimizers, architectures, and activation functions, and is packaged in the open-source HyperLight library to lower the barrier to entry. Limitations include evaluation only on fully-connected hypernetworks with two optimizers and training from scratch, leaving open whether MIP extends to convolutional or transformer-based hypernetworks and to transfer-learning settings where pretrained primary weights interact with the residual formulation. A key open question is whether the proportionality issue manifests differently (or disappears) in hypernetworks with multiplicative or attention-based architectures.
