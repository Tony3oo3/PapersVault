## Problem

Universal approximation theorems guarantee that sufficiently wide neural networks can represent any target function, but they say nothing about whether gradient flow will actually find such parameters. Existing convergence results either require overparameterization assumptions tying the number of parameters to the number of training samples, or are restricted to fixed-size networks. This paper asks whether one can prove that gradient flow reaches arbitrarily low loss as the number of parameters grows, for a broad class of architectures, without any such overparameterization assumption.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base perceptron module (a finite directed acyclic computation graph with Euclidean vector spaces attached to vertices and edges, plus activation and weight-multiplication maps), a lifting dimension function specifying how many copies of each base vertex to create, and per-edge sparsity rates λ controlling the probability of retaining each connection. It produces a random sparse lifted network whose gradient flow, started from the random initialization with zero linear readout, is guaranteed to converge to loss below any prescribed ε with probability at least 1−δ, provided the network size exceeds a threshold that depends on the witness network achieving that loss.

## Method

The paper introduces a formalism of perceptron modules over computation graphs, where architectures are built by 'lifting' a small base module via a graph homomorphism that replicates vertices and edges, generalizing the width-scaling of MLPs to arbitrary computation graphs including transformers and convolutions. The key construction is a random sparse lift: starting from the fully-connected lift, each edge is independently retained with probability λ/n, equivalent to a fixed dropout mask drawn at initialization. The central insight is that sparse networks contain smaller sub-networks as 'strong sub-graphs' extracted by graph fibrations (which preserve parent structure), unlike dense networks where a width-2n layer does not contain a width-n sub-computation. This fibration-based containment yields a coverage property: with high probability, a large fraction of sub-networks in the sparse lift compute near-optimal features. Coupled with a low-outgoing-degree condition ensuring local stability of this coverage, the paper proves a tangent approximation property on a ball around initialization, which via a separable Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz inequality implies PAC convergence of the gradient flow to arbitrarily low loss.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or benchmark tables. The main result (Theorem 4.3) proves that for any target function f* that is approximable by some lifted perceptron module (i.e., the approximation number h₀ is finite), there exists a size threshold N₁ such that for all random sparse lifts above that threshold, gradient flow from zero-initialized linear readout converges to loss below ε with probability at least 1−δ. The convergence rate is governed by Convergence Criterion 1: L(θ_t) ≤ ε + 1/∛(κt + 1/c), where κ depends on the witness network structure and c on the initial loss. Theorem 2.1 establishes the more general tangent-approximation-to-PAC-convergence bridge, showing that Condition C1 (tangent approximation with high probability on a ball) is sufficient for convergence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include empirical comparisons or baseline benchmarks, as it is a theoretical contribution. It positions itself against several prior theoretical frameworks: (1) Robin et al. (2022) established PAC convergence for two-layer networks via Rayleigh quotients; this paper extends that to arbitrary-depth architectures via the tangent approximation condition. (2) NTK-based approaches (Jacot et al. 2018, Du et al. 2018, Ji & Telgarsky 2020) require overparameterization relative to the number of training samples n; this paper's bounds depend on a model-specific complexity of f* and do not diverge with infinitely many samples. (3) The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (Frankle & Carbin 2019) uses 'sparse' to mean a subnetwork that, when isolated, trains well; this paper uses a different notion of sparsity (Erdős-Rényi random graph) and proves convergence of the full sparse network rather than an isolated subnetwork. (4) Tensor Programs (Yang & Littwin 2021) and Named Tensors (Chiang et al. 2023) provide architectural formalisms but target infinite-width limits; this paper targets large-but-finite sparse networks with finite-width convergence guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the tangent-approximation-to-convergence framework of Robin et al. (2022) from two-layer networks to a broad class of architectures (MLPs, CNNs, transformer-like blocks) by introducing a formalism of 'perceptron modules' lifted via graph homomorphisms. It deliberately departs from the NTK-based convergence literature (Jacot et al., Du et al., Ji & Telgarsky) by avoiding overparameterization assumptions that relate the number of parameters to the number of training samples, instead bounding convergence in terms of a model-specific complexity of the target function. The key conceptual departure from the lottery ticket hypothesis (Frankle & Carbin) is the use of fibrations (from algebraic topology) rather than homomorphisms to extract 'strong subgraphs,' ensuring that subnetworks compute identical functions rather than merely similar ones. The formalism is more expressive than Tensor Programs (Yang & Littwin) by allowing non-linear weight manipulations and independent scaling dimensions, while being closer in spirit to Named Tensors (Chiang et al.) in its architectural description.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first convergence theory for deep learning that simultaneously (i) covers a broad class of architectures including transformers, (ii) requires no overparameterization relative to sample count, and (iii) provides explicit PAC guarantees for gradient flow, addressing a gap the authors identify between approximation numbers and learning numbers. The fibration-based 'strong subgraph' concept is a genuinely new tool borrowed from algebraic topology that may prove useful beyond this specific application. Key limitations include: results apply only to sparse networks (not dense lifts), convergence is shown only in loss (not parameters), and the analysis is continuous-time. Open questions explicitly flagged include extending results to dense lifts, parameter convergence, and discrete-time optimization, as well as obtaining quantitative bounds on the size threshold N₁.
