## Problem

Existing methods for measuring data memorization, influence, and information leakage in machine learning rely on expensive leave-one-out retraining or first-order approximations (e.g., influence functions, TracIn) that degrade for deep networks. These empirical approaches cannot efficiently answer which queries leak the most information or how architectural choices affect leakage. The paper unifies these concepts under a single measure—leave-one-out distinguishability (LOOD)—and provides an analytical framework to compute it without retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241579/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of leave-one-out datasets (D and D' = D ∪ S, differing in one or more records) and a set of query points, along with a kernel function derived from a specified neural network architecture (e.g., NNGP kernel for a fully connected or CNN with given depth and activation). It produces the KL-divergence LOOD value, mean distance LOOD, and—via gradient-based optimization of the non-convex LOOD objective—optimized query points that visually reconstruct the differing training data. All experiments use 32×32 RGB images from CIFAR-10 ('car' and 'plane' classes), with class-balanced subsets of size 500–10000.

## Method

The core technique models the output distribution of a neural network as a Gaussian Process using the Neural Network Gaussian Process (NNGP) kernel, so that the prediction distributions under D and D' are both multivariate Gaussians with analytically computable means and covariances via standard GP regression. LOOD is then the KL divergence between these two Gaussian posteriors, which captures both first-order (mean shift) and second-order (variance change) information—making it better correlated with membership inference attack success than mean distance alone. Theorem 4.1 proves that under weak kernel regularity conditions (satisfied by RBF and NNGP kernels on normalized data), the differing data point itself is a stationary point of LOOD, explaining why querying the training point leaks the most information. Proposition 6.1 further shows that low-rank kernel matrices bound LOOD from above, and combined with prior results on NNGP kernels, this proves that smooth activations (GeLU) induce higher leakage than non-smooth ones (ReLU). The framework enables efficient query optimization via Adam on the LOOD objective, yielding reconstructed training images that closely resemble the originals, with over 140× speedup compared to empirical retraining-based baselines.

## Results

The paper validates its analytical LOOD framework on CIFAR-10 (using 'car' and 'plane' classes) with three key quantitative findings: (1) mean distance LOOD under NNGP achieves the highest Pearson correlation with actual prediction differences from leave-one-out retraining of deep NNs, outperforming both influence functions and TracIn (Figure 1c); (2) the analytical framework provides over 140× speedup compared to empirically measuring leakage/influence via retrained models; (3) for activation functions, GeLU induces more than 1.1× higher per-record LOOD than ReLU in 95% of 500 tested differing data points, with the gap growing with network depth (2, 4, 10 layers). LOOD optimization successfully reconstructs training images with SSIM scores up to 0.37, and MIA AUC on NNGP closely matches MIA AUC on SGD-trained NNs across 90 random records.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its mean distance LOOD (under NNGP) against two established influence estimation baselines: influence functions (Koh & Liang, ICML 2017) and TracIn (Pruthi et al., NeurIPS 2020). On CIFAR-10 with a 10-layer fully connected network (width 1024), mean distance LOOD achieves the highest Pearson correlation with the ground-truth prediction difference from leave-one-out retraining of 50 NN models, while influence functions and TracIn show lower and less consistent correlations (Figure 1c). The paper explicitly notes that influence function approximations 'worsen significantly' for deep neural networks (citing Koh & Liang's own Figure 2), whereas mean distance LOOD maintains high correlation even at depth 10. For information leakage (KL divergence LOOD vs. MIA AUC), the paper shows KL LOOD is consistently better correlated with attack success than mean distance LOOD, and that NNGP-based LOOD faithfully captures leakage in SGD-trained NNs. No direct head-to-head numerical table is provided; comparisons are made via correlation plots and MIA AUC matching across 90 random differing data records.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate lines of work— influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017), memorization/self-influence (Feldman, 2020; Feldman & Zhang, 2020), and membership inference leakage (Shokri et al., 2017)—under a single statistical divergence measure called LOOD. Rather than relying on expensive leave-one-out retraining or first-order Taylor approximations (influence functions, TracIn), it models the output distribution of neural networks via Neural Network Gaussian Processes (NNGP, Lee et al., 2017) and computes the KL divergence between prediction distributions analytically. The paper departs from prior empirical approaches by showing that the full distributional divergence (not just mean shift) is needed to faithfully capture leakage, and by leveraging the analytical structure of GP regression to enable optimization over queries and architectural parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a 140× speedup over retraining-based estimation of influence and memorization, making previously infeasible analyses (e.g., identifying worst-case leakage queries, studying activation-function effects on privacy) tractable. The finding that smooth activations (GeLU) induce measurably higher leakage than ReLU offers concrete architectural guidance for privacy-preserving training, suggesting a privacy-accuracy tradeoff that practitioners should weigh. Key limitations include reliance on the NNGP approximation (validated primarily on CIFAR-10 with FC/CNN architectures), and the reconstruction attack assumes the adversary knows the prediction distribution of leave-one-out models. Open questions include extending the framework to other architectures (transformers, diffusion models), analyzing how training data distribution affects leakage, and connecting LOOD to data valuation (Shapley values).
