## Problem

Dropout is a widely used regularization technique in neural network training, yet the precise mechanism by which it improves generalization—particularly its role in selecting flatter minima—remains poorly understood. Existing analyses either treat dropout as a static regularizer or rely on heuristic arguments, lacking a rigorous continuous-time dynamical description of how the stochastic noise introduced by dropout shapes the optimization trajectory.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework operates on two-layer fully connected networks with m hidden neurons, taking as input the parameter vector θ ∈ ℝ^{m(d+1)}, a training set of n samples, a dropout rate p, and a fixed learning rate η. It produces an explicit SDE with drift −∇L_S(θ) (where L_S adds a (1−p)/(2np)·Σ a_r²σ(w_rᵀx_i)² regularization term to the empirical risk) and diffusion √η·Σ(θ)^{1/2}, along with the Hessian-variance alignment metric α(θ_t) = Tr(H_t Σ_t)/(‖H_t‖_F ‖Σ_t‖_F). In practice, the covariance matrix Σ(θ) is D×D where D = m(d+1), making its square root computationally expensive; experiments therefore resize MNIST inputs to 7×7 to keep D manageable, while Hessian-variance alignment experiments on VGG16 and ResNet-20 restrict the trace computation to a subset of parameters (e.g., the first convolutional layer, D=1728).

## Method

The paper derives stochastic modified equations (SMEs) for dropout applied to two-layer networks under full-batch gradient descent by computing the conditional expectation and covariance of the parameter increment at each step. The key insight is that the drift term corresponds to the gradient of a modified loss L_S(θ) that augments the standard empirical risk with an explicit L2-type regularization on each neuron's activation, while the diffusion term captures the anisotropic noise structure through a parameter-dependent covariance matrix Σ(θ) that exploits the decoupling of neurons in the two-layer architecture. The authors prove that the resulting SDE provides an order-1 weak approximation (and an order-2 approximation with an additional η/4·‖∇L_S‖² correction in the drift) to the discrete dropout iteration, with the approximation error bounded in the weak sense over test functions in C_b^M(ℝ^D). To connect the noise structure to generalization, they show that near a minimum the Hessian of L_S and the covariance Σ share a common structural form—both are dominated by outer products of per-neuron gradients—leading to a Hessian-variance alignment relation. Empirically, the cosine similarity α(θ_t) between H and Σ exceeds 0.85 for FNNs on MNIST and 0.7 for VGG16 on CIFAR-10 throughout training, far above the ~10⁻⁴ baseline for random matrices, confirming that dropout noise is highly anisotropic and preferentially pushes parameters along sharp directions of the loss landscape.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative finding is the Hessian-variance alignment: the cosine similarity α(θ_t) between the Hessian and dropout noise covariance consistently exceeds 0.85 for a 784-50-50-10 FNN on MNIST and 0.7 for VGG16 on CIFAR-10, across all tested learning rates and dropout rates, versus a random-matrix baseline of only ~10⁻³. The SDE approximation is validated to order 1 and order 2, with the approximation error scaling as O(η) and O(η²) respectively. In the direct comparison with parameter noise injection (Fig. 9), dropout achieves higher test accuracy and a consistently smaller Hessian trace than both SGD and parameter noise injection on a 784-500-500-500-10 FNN trained on MNIST.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares dropout against parameter noise injection (Orvieto et al., 2023) and plain SGD on a deep FNN (784-500-500-500-10) trained on MNIST with batch size 128. Dropout (p=0.8) achieves the highest test accuracy among the three strategies throughout training, outperforming both SGD and parameter noise injection (σ=0.03). On the flatness metric, the trace of the Hessian matrix under dropout remains smaller than that of both SGD and parameter noise injection across the entire training trajectory, indicating a stronger tendency toward flatter minima. For the two-layer ReLU function approximation task, GD+dropout produces a smoother model output that more closely matches the target function compared to SGD and parameter noise injection. The Hessian-variance alignment relation (α > 0.85) is shown to hold for dropout under both GD and SGD settings, whereas the random-matrix baseline yields only ~10⁻³, confirming that the alignment is a structural property of dropout noise rather than a generic phenomenon.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the stochastic modified equations (SME) framework, originally developed for SGD (Li et al., 2017, 2019) and later for adaptive methods like Adam and RMSprop (Malladi et al., 2022), to the dropout algorithm. It derives an explicit modified loss L_S(θ) that captures dropout's implicit regularization, connecting to prior work on dropout's explicit regularization effects (Wei et al., 2020; Zhang and Xu, 2022). The Hessian-variance alignment analysis builds directly on the SGD noise alignment results of Zhu et al. (2018) and Wu et al. (2022), but establishes the analogous property for dropout-induced noise, which had not been previously formalized or empirically verified.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous SDE approximation for dropout dynamics, giving practitioners a continuous-time lens to study how dropout's noise structure shapes the optimization trajectory. The confirmed Hessian-variance alignment for dropout noise offers a mechanistic explanation for why dropout finds flat minima, complementing the analogous SGD result. Key limitations include the theoretical derivation being restricted to two-layer networks with full-batch GD; multi-layer networks with dropout in inner layers require numerical approximation of the drift and diffusion terms. Open questions include whether explicit SME forms exist for deeper architectures, how the alignment property evolves under SGD+dropout combined noise, and whether the mean-field vanishing of dropout's regularization (Appendix E) has practical implications for very wide networks.
