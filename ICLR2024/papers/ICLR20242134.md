## Problem

Retrieval-augmented language models require retrieved data at both training and test time, and appending retrieved text to the input context causes quadratic computational cost in self-attention as context length grows. This paper asks whether a simpler alternative—fine-tuning the model on retrieved neighbors at test time using its standard training setup—can achieve comparable or better gains without any special training procedure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242134/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a test text sequence from a Pile benchmark task and retrieves its nearest neighbors (typically 20–50) from a distributed index of 210M RoBERTa embeddings (1024-dim) covering 1.3TB of Pile training text. Long sequences exceeding the model's maximum context length (1048 tokens for GPT-2, 2048 for GPT-Neo) are split into chunks, each yielding one gradient update. The output is a temporarily fine-tuned causal language model that produces next-token distributions for the test sequence; parameters are reset to the pre-trained state after each test instance.

## Method

The core system is a distributed FAISS Flat L2 index over RoBERTa text embeddings of the entire Pile training set, split across 180 servers to answer nearest-neighbor queries in ~1.35 seconds. For each test instance, the model is fine-tuned sequentially on retrieved neighbors in order of increasing distance (nearest first), taking exactly one gradient iteration per neighbor (or per chunk for long sequences) using the model's default HuggingFace hyperparameters with no tuning. The key empirical finding is that training on the nearest neighbor first—rather than last—significantly outperforms the reverse order, likely because the largest initial gradient step grounds the fine-tuning in a better region of the loss landscape. The approach requires no special training setup, no in-context retrieval during pre-training, and no hyperparameter search, yet reduces bits-per-byte by up to 60% on code tasks and narrows the gap between a 117M GPT-2 and a 1.3B GPT-Neo by more than 10×.

## Results

TTT-NN with 50 nearest neighbors reduces the bits-per-byte metric by approximately 20% on average across the 22 Pile language modeling tasks, with most of the gain already realized after just 20 neighbors. On the pile_github (code) task, bits per byte drops by more than 60% (from 1.95 to 0.51 on GPT-2-Large). Remarkably, TTT-NN on the 774M GPT-2-Large model recovers most of the performance gap to the 1.3B GPT-Neo model, which was specifically pre-trained to convergence on the entire Pile dataset. The method requires no hyperparameter tuning beyond the default HuggingFace optimizer settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTT-NN outperforms all three baselines on the majority of the 22 Pile tasks. Against In-Context (a training-free retrieval-augmented baseline inspired by Lewis et al., 2020), TTT-NN wins on 20 of 22 tasks; In-Context only helps marginally on a few tasks like pile_enron but is generally ineffective without matching training. Against the modified KNN-LM Interpolate baseline (Khandelwal et al., 2019), TTT-NN wins on 20 of 22 tasks, with the largest gaps on code (pile_github: 0.51 vs. 1.06) and mathematics (pile_dm-mathematics: 0.99 vs. 1.42). Against Dynamic Evaluation (Krause et al., 2018), TTT-NN wins on 19 of 22 tasks; Dyn. Eval. is better only on pile_bookcorpus2 (0.91 vs. 1.00), pile_gutenberg (1.14 vs. 1.18), and pile_youtubesubtitles (1.34 vs. 1.42), and it begins overfitting after just 7 iterations. The paper notes that the Dyn. Eval. heuristic can be combined with TTT-NN by using the query text for the first gradient step and neighbors for subsequent steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the local learning paradigm of Bottou & Vapnik (1992), which fine-tuned only the last layer of a CNN on nearest neighbors, to full-network fine-tuning of Transformer language models at test time. It departs from the dominant retrieval-augmented generation approach (Lewis et al. 2020, Borgeaud et al. 2022) by replacing context augmentation with gradient-based adaptation, thereby avoiding the quadratic attention cost of long contexts and the requirement for matched training/test-time retrieval procedures. The method also subsumes KNN-LM (Khandelwal et al. 2019) and dynamic evaluation (Krause et al. 2018) as special cases, outperforming both as baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first systematic baseline for test-time training in language modeling, demonstrating that a 774M GPT-2 can recover most of the perplexity gains of a 1.3B GPT-Neo trained to convergence on the full Pile—effectively closing a >10× parameter gap with a single gradient step per neighbor. Open questions include whether test-time training transfers to non-perplexity tasks (e.g., reasoning, code synthesis), whether it amplifies emergent capabilities, and whether it can mitigate biases or data-poisoning attacks by injecting trusted data at inference. The significant inference-time cost and the current restriction to perplexity on a single dataset limit immediate practical deployment but position the work as a foundational step for a broader research direction.
