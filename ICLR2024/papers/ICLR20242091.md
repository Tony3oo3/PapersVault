## Problem

Despite significant empirical progress in pre-training RL representations from unlabeled video data, no theoretical understanding exists for when and how these approaches provably recover task-relevant latent states. The paper asks whether video-based representation learning (autoencoding, forward modeling, temporal contrastive learning) can match the sample efficiency of trajectory-based methods, and identifies a fundamental statistical gap when exogenous noise is present.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242091/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of n unlabeled video episodes, each a sequence of H RGB observation frames with no action or reward labels, generated by a mixture of Markovian policies. It outputs a frozen decoder φ: X→[N] (a CNN with optional VQ bottleneck) that maps each observation to a discrete abstract state, which is then fed into a provably efficient tabular RL algorithm to produce a policy φ̂: [N]→A. The key constraint is that the downstream RL phase must not scale with the complexity of the decoder class Φ, shifting the statistical burden entirely to the offline video pre-training stage.

## Method

Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the paper provides the first theoretical analysis of three existing video-based representation learning objectives—autoencoding, forward modeling (predicting future observations k steps ahead), and temporal contrastive learning (classifying whether two observations are temporally causal)—within Block MDP and Ex-Block MDP frameworks. The upper bound (Theorem 1) uses a coupling argument combined with a margin assumption on the TV distance between future-observation distributions conditioned on different latent states to show that forward modeling and temporal contrastive learning provably recover the latent state up to a bijection with polynomial sample complexity in the absence of exogenous noise. The lower bound (Theorem 2) constructs a hard MDP instance where, without action labels, the agent cannot distinguish endogenous states from exogenous noise, proving that any compact frozen representation from video data cannot achieve efficient downstream RL—establishing an exponential sample complexity gap versus trajectory-based methods. Empirically, the paper validates these results on Minigrid and ViZDoom, showing temporal contrastive learning fails most severely under exogenous noise (exploiting noise to minimize contrastive loss) while forward modeling degrades more gradually, and ACRO (trajectory-based) remains robust.

## Results

The paper evaluates three video-based representation learning methods (autoencoder, forward modeling, temporal contrastive learning) on GridWorld (12×12 Minigrid) and ViZDoom Defend the Center, with and without exogenous noise (superimposed moving diamonds). In the absence of exogenous noise, both forward modeling and temporal contrastive learning achieve successful downstream RL, consistent with the theoretical upper bounds (Theorem 1). In the presence of exogenous noise, temporal contrastive learning completely fails (it exploits the predictable noise to minimize contrastive loss), forward modeling degrades significantly (especially in GridWorld), and autoencoder performance is unpredictable. ACRO, the trajectory-based baseline, consistently achieves the strongest performance across all tasks and noise conditions, with the performance gap widening as exogenous noise size increases (Figure 6).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole direct baseline comparison is against ACRO (Islam et al., 2022), a trajectory-data-based representation method designed to filter exogenous noise via action prediction. ACRO achieves the strongest RL performance across all three environments (GridWorld, ViZDoom Basic, ViZDoom Defend the Center) and all noise conditions (no noise, observation noise only, reward noise only, both). Among the video-based methods, forward modeling is the most robust to exogenous noise but still degrades noticeably as noise size increases, while temporal contrastive learning fails entirely under exogenous noise because it can minimize its loss by attending solely to the predictable noise patterns. The paper's theoretical lower bound (Theorem 2) formalizes this gap: in the presence of exogenous noise, any compact frozen representation pre-trained from video data cannot support efficient downstream RL, whereas trajectory-based pre-training (as in ACRO) can. This establishes an exponential statistical gap between video-based and trajectory-based representation pre-training when exogenous noise is present.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Block MDP framework (Du et al., 2019) and its extension to exogenous noise (Efroni et al., 2022), which established provable representation learning for RL from action-labeled trajectory data. It departs from this lineage by analyzing the same three representation learning objectives (autoencoding, forward modeling, temporal contrastive) in the action-free video setting, where prior provable guarantees do not apply. The paper positions itself as the first theoretical treatment of video-based representation pre-training for RL, bridging the gap between empirically successful methods (e.g., VPT, Ye et al. 2022) and the formal Block MDP analysis that previously required actions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The lower bound (Theorem 2) establishing an exponential sample complexity gap between video-based and trajectory-based representation learning is a fundamental result that explains why video pre-training for RL is intrinsically harder and guides practitioners toward trajectory data when available. The empirical finding that temporal contrastive learning can exploit exogenous noise to minimize its loss while completely ignoring task-relevant state is a practical warning for method selection. Open questions include finite-sample guarantees for autoencoders, whether additional structural assumptions can close the video-trajectory gap, and extending the analysis to continuous or partially observable settings.
