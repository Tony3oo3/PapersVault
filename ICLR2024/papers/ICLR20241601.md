## Problem

While linear mode connectivity (LMC) for full-network averaging is well studied, the layer-wise structure of loss barriers remains unexplored: it is unknown whether individual layers or small groups of layers can create barriers when two models are partially averaged. This gap is critical for federated learning, where partial layer aggregation is a common personalization strategy, yet the conditions under which such partial averaging succeeds or fails are not understood.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241601/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two trained network weight parametrizations (W₁, W₂) of the same architecture and a training or test dataset, then computes the loss or error at the midpoint (α=0.5) of interpolating only a single layer (or a consecutive group of layers) while keeping all other layers fixed to one of the two models. Outputs include per-layer averaging barrier values (the difference between the midpoint loss and the mean endpoint loss), heatmaps of barrier size across layers and training epochs, and cumulative barrier curves tracking how the barrier grows as layers are added sequentially from shallow-to-deep or deep-to-shallow. A ~2% loss difference is treated as noise rather than a true barrier.

## Method

The paper introduces layer-wise linear mode connectivity (LLMC), formally defined as the difference between the supremum (or midpoint) loss along a single-layer interpolation line and the average endpoint loss, and empirically demonstrates that individual layers of ResNet18, VGG11, ViT, and GPT-like models exhibit no significant averaging barriers even when full-network LMC fails. Theoretically, Theorem 4.1 proves that for deep linear networks the loss is convex along any single-layer interpolation direction, explaining the absence of barriers in that setting. A robustness analysis reveals that the averaging direction between two models is geometrically special: it has substantially higher curvature than random perturbations of the same norm, and perturbations in the null space (perpendicular to the training trajectory) are more damaging than those within the training subspace. Finally, a cumulative layer experiment uncovers a non-trivial structure where middle layers, but not the shallowest or deepest, are responsible for creating barriers when groups of layers are jointly averaged.

## Results

The central empirical finding is that deep networks (ResNet18 on CIFAR-10, VGG11, ViTs, GPT-like models on Wikitext, Pythia LLMs) exhibit no layer-wise averaging barriers at α=0.5, while full-network linear barriers are consistently present. In the cumulative layer-averaging experiment, barriers emerge only when middle layers are included in the averaged group, with neither the shallowest nor deepest layers individually causing a barrier. Theoretically, Theorem 4.1 proves that for deep linear networks, the loss is convex along any single-layer interpolation direction, guaranteeing barrier-free layer-wise averaging. In the federated personalization experiment (Table 1), no partial-averaging strategy (body layers, sensitive layers, or their complement) significantly outperforms full averaging or local training under pathological non-i.i.d. splits.

|---|---|---|
| Full averaging | 61.82 | 24.24 |
| No averaging (local training) | 50.29 | 91.75 |
| Body layers only | 61.73 | 53.94 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily an analytical and empirical study rather than a method paper with direct baseline comparisons. The key 'comparison' is between full-network linear mode connectivity (LMC) and the proposed layer-wise linear mode connectivity (LLMC): full-network averaging consistently produces barriers (visible in the top row of heatmaps in Figs. 1 and 6), while single-layer averaging never does across all tested architectures and training regimes (parallel, federated i.i.d., federated non-i.i.d., pathological non-i.i.d.). In the cumulative averaging setting, the paper compares shallow-to-deep vs. deep-to-shallow cumulation directions and finds that middle layers are the barrier-inducing ones regardless of direction. For the federated personalization application, the paper compares full averaging, local training (no averaging), and body-layer-only averaging, finding that none of the partial-averaging strategies derived from LLMC insights outperform full averaging when the architecture is sufficiently powerful, and all perform on par with local training in the pathological regime where full averaging fails. The robustness analysis (Fig. 4–5) compares layer-wise interpolation directions against random-direction perturbations of equal norm, showing that the averaging direction is a special high-curvature direction while random perturbations cause far less loss increase.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear mode connectivity (LMC) framework of Frankle et al. (2020) and Entezari et al. (2022) from full-network interpolation to a layer-wise decomposition, introducing the notion of layer-wise linear mode connectivity (LLMC). The theoretical analysis generalizes the shallow-network convexity result of Safran et al. (2021) to deep linear networks, while the robustness perspective connects to layer criticality findings (Zhang et al., 2022) and sharpness-aware minimization (Foret et al., 2020). The federated learning personalization experiments build on and partially confirm the conclusions of Pillutla et al. (2022) that partial averaging's main benefit is reduced communication rather than improved accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that deep networks lack layer-wise barriers while exhibiting full-network barriers provides a fine-grained structural understanding of the loss landscape that is directly relevant to federated learning practitioners who aggregate models during training. The negative result—that no layer-selection strategy for partial averaging outperforms full averaging in personalization—challenges a common heuristic in the federated learning literature and redirects attention toward communication efficiency as the true benefit of partial aggregation. Open questions include how optimization hyperparameters (learning rate, SAM radius) shape the barrier structure, the connection between the cumulative middle-layer barrier phenomenon and information bottleneck theory (Jacot, 2023), and whether the layer-wise convexity observed for linear networks has a meaningful analogue in non-linear architectures.
