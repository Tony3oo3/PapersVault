## Problem

While it is well-known that certain RL tasks cannot be specified with standard Markov rewards, and numerous alternative objective-specification formalisms have been proposed (LTL, Reward Machines, Multi-Objective RL, etc.), no prior work has provided a comprehensive analysis of how these formalisms relate to each other in terms of expressive power. This paper fills that gap by systematically comparing 17 formalisms and establishing a complete preorder of their expressivity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240489/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates over finite MDP environments (finite state and action sets with stochastic transitions) and considers only stationary stochastic policies. Expressivity is defined at the level of total preorders over policies (not merely optimal policies), meaning formalism X expresses formalism Y if, in every environment, every policy ordering inducible by Y is also inducible by X. The output is a complete 289-pair expressivity comparison table and a Hasse diagram, with each relationship backed by a formal theorem or proposition.

## Method

The paper defines 17 objective-specification formalisms (ranging from Markov Rewards and LTL to abstract constructs like Functions from Policies to Reals) and proves all 289 pairwise expressivity relationships. Key positive results establish three equivalence classes: (1) INMR = IMORL = FTR, shown by constructing an injective trajectory-return function so that an arbitrary trajectory-to-real function can be recovered via composition; (2) OMORL = FOMR = FTLR, shown by proving that two policies share an occupancy measure iff they share a trajectory lottery iff they share a policy-evaluation vector (with |S||A||S| reward functions); and (3) GOMORL = OMO = TLO, extending the same argument to total preorders. Negative results exploit structural limitations: continuity of RM/MR policy evaluations vs. discontinuity of LTL/LAR, the binary nature of LTL truth values, LAR's insensitivity to early-trajectory differences, and ONMR's ability to express threshold objectives inexpressible by FTR.

## Results

The paper provides a complete expressivity comparison of 17 objective-specification formalisms in RL, analyzing all 289 ordered pairs. Three equivalence classes emerge: {INMR, IMORL, FTR}, {OMORL, FOMR, FTLR}, and {GOMORL, OMO, TLO}, each proven equally expressive (Theorems 3.1, 3.3, 3.4). The key negative results show that no formalism is dominantly expressive: LTL and LAR can express policy orderings that RM and MR cannot (Prop. 3.5), MR and LAR can express orderings LTL cannot (Prop. 3.6), LTL and MR can express orderings LAR cannot (Prop. 3.7), and ONMR and FTR are incommensurable (Prop. 3.8). The authors conclude that no formalism is both maximally expressive and tractable to optimize with current techniques.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central finding is that the 17 formalisms form a partial order (Hasse diagram) with no single dominant formalism. Among the most practical formalisms, MR, LAR, LTL, and RM are pairwise incommensurable in important ways: RM subsumes MR (Prop. B.1) but cannot express the discontinuous policy evaluations that LTL and LAR can (Prop. 3.5); LTL cannot express the fine-grained scalar distinctions that MR and LAR provide (Prop. 3.6); and LAR cannot distinguish policies based on early-trajectory differences that LTL and MR can capture (Prop. 3.7). Moving up the expressivity hierarchy, FTR (arbitrary trajectory-to-real functions) subsumes LAR, LTL, and RM but is incommensurable with ONMR, which can express threshold objectives (e.g., 'achieve probability ≥ 0.5') that FTR cannot (Prop. 3.8). At the top, FPR and GOMORL are incommensurable with each other: FPR can distinguish policies differing only on never-visited states (Prop. 3.9), while GOMORL can express lexicographic orderings over policy evaluation vectors that no real-valued function can represent (Prop. 3.10). The three proven equivalence classes ({INMR, IMORL, FTR}, {OMORL, FOMR, FTLR}, {GOMORL, OMO, TLO}) simplify the landscape but do not eliminate the incommensurabilities between classes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on the expressivity limitations of Markovian rewards (Abel et al. 2022; Skalse & Abate 2023) from pairwise comparisons to a comprehensive 17-way comparison, introducing several new theoretical formalisms (ONMR, OMORL, IMORL, FTR, FTLR, FPR, OMO, TLO, GOMORL, PO) to fill gaps in the comparison lattice. It builds on the Reward Machines framework of Icarte et al. (2018) and the LTL-in-RL setting of Littman et al. (2017), and connects the analysis to the VNM utility theorem and the reward hypothesis debate (Sutton 2004; Bowling et al. 2022). The key methodological contribution is defining expressivity in terms of policy orderings (following Abel et al.) and proving all 289 pairwise relationships to produce a complete Hasse diagram.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that the 17 formalisms are largely expressively incommensurable, with no single formalism dominating all others—challenges the implicit assumption that a more 'general' formalism should subsume simpler ones, and provides practitioners with a concrete reference for selecting objective specifications. The paper highlights that many existing reward learning methods implicitly assume Markovian structure, motivating future work on reward learning for non-Markovian formalisms. Key open questions include the expressivity-tractability tradeoff (the most expressive formalisms are likely intractable to optimize), whether results extend to history-dependent policies, and how restricting conditions (e.g., bounded number of reward functions, continuity constraints on wrapper functions) alter the expressivity hierarchy.
