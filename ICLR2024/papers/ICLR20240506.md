## Problem

Prior gender bias research assumes LMs perpetuate stereotypes by leveraging gendered correlations in pretraining data, but it remains unclear whether models still exhibit bias when those correlations are absent. This paper asks whether LMs display gender preferences on sentences that are both gender-invariant and free of words with strong gender co-occurrence in the pretraining corpus, a scenario that prior benchmarks do not isolate.

## Input / Output

The framework takes 28 pretrained LMs and evaluates them on gender-invariant sentence pairs (differing only in masculine vs. feminine pronouns) that have been filtered to exclude any word whose PMI-based gender score δ(w) exceeds a threshold η. The primary output is the Unstereo Score (US): the fraction of pairs where the model's log-probability difference between the two gendered versions stays within ε = log(1.65) ≈ 0.217. Secondary outputs include Preference Disparity (PD), the percentage-point gap between female-skewed and male-skewed pairs, and Fairness Gap (Δ_η), which tracks how US changes as η is tightened from ∞ to 0.80 and 0.65.

## Method

UnStereoEval defines a word-level gender co-occurrence score δ(w) = PMI(w,'she') − PMI(w,'he') computed over PILE's 800GB pretraining corpus, then aggregates to a sentence-level MaxPMI(s) = max|δ(wᵢ)| to flag sentences containing any strongly gender-correlated word. To build stereotype-free benchmarks at scale, a three-stage pipeline selects 500 seed words from the narrow δ(w) ∈ [−0.263, 0.263] range, prompts ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo) to generate gender-neutral sentences embedding each seed word with a target pronoun, and then filters pairs via semantic plausibility checks and the MaxPMI(s) ≤ η constraint. The same MaxPMI filter is applied to repurpose Winobias and Winogender, retaining only their non-stereotypical subsets. Fairness is quantified by the US score, which counts a pair as unbiased when the model assigns no more than 1.65× probability mass to one gendered completion over the other, a threshold chosen to tolerate small numerical noise while flagging systematic skew.

## Results

Across all 28 evaluated LMs, the Unstereo Score (US) — the percentage of sentence pairs where the model assigns no significant gender preference — ranges from 9.04% (MPT 30B on USE-5) to 43.92% (OPT 125M on Winogender), far below the ideal 100%. The highest US on the proposed USE-5 benchmark is 40.72% (GPT-J-6B), while on Winobias the best is 26.99% (OPT 125M). Preference Disparity (PD) analysis reveals that all models systematically favor male pronoun completions by a margin greater than 40% on Winobias and Winogender, while PILE-trained models (GPT-J, Pythia) and MPT/OLMo tend to favor female completions on USE benchmarks. Fairness scores are largely insensitive to the gender co-occurrence threshold η (|Δ| ≤ 1.33% on USE-5, ≤ 11.84% on WG), and no consistent improvement is observed with increasing model size or pretraining data deduplication.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new model to compare against baselines; instead, it evaluates 28 existing LMs on stereotype-free benchmarks. Among PILE-trained models, GPT-J-6B achieves the highest US on USE-5 (39.86%) and is competitive on Winogender (32.92%), while Pythia models show high variance (11.99%–31.33% on USE-5) with no clear size-scaling trend. Non-PILE models like Llama-2 70B (36.94% on USE-5) and Mistral-7B (29.51%) perform comparably to PILE-trained counterparts, suggesting the bias is not specific to training data composition. On Winobias and Winogender, all models — regardless of family or size — systematically prefer male completions (PD < −40%), whereas on the USE benchmarks, PILE-trained and MPT/OLMo models show a female-skewed preference (PD > +20%), while OPT and Mixtral show male-skewed preferences. Data deduplication (Pythia-D) yields inconsistent effects: it improves fairness for Pythia 70M (+6.73 on USE-5) but worsens it for Pythia 410M (−17.14) and 12B (−11.65), indicating that deduplication is not a reliable bias mitigation strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gender bias evaluation literature (Winobias, Winogender, CrowS-Pairs, StereoSet) by challenging its foundational assumption that model bias arises primarily from gendered word correlations in pretraining data. It builds on Bolukbasi et al.'s PMI-based gender association framework and Gardner et al.'s analysis of individual word impact on LM behavior to define a sentence-level MaxPMI score that filters out gender-correlated language. The benchmark construction pipeline draws on model-based generation approaches (Perez et al., Kocielnik et al.) but applies them to a novel purpose: creating stereotype-free evaluation sets rather than surfacing known stereotypes. By repurposing existing benchmarks (WB, WG) through PMI-based filtering and generating new ones (USE-5/10/20) via ChatGPT, the paper carves out a previously unaddressed evaluation regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all 28 tested models exhibit only 9–41% fairness on sentences explicitly free of gender-correlated words fundamentally challenges the field's working model that bias is a surface-level training-data artifact removable by avoiding gendered language. This opens a significant open question: if bias persists without gendered words, what is its true source (architecture, optimization dynamics, residual distributional effects)? Limitations include the focus on binary gender in English, reliance on a single generative model (ChatGPT) for benchmark construction, and the use of PILE statistics as a proxy for all models' pretraining data. The released UnStereoEval framework and datasets provide a concrete tool for future work to investigate implicit bias mechanisms and develop mitigation strategies targeting deeper sources of skew.
