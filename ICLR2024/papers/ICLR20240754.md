## Problem

Standard RLHF assumes human preferences follow the discounted partial return of rewards, but evidence shows humans instead judge based on regret under the optimal policy. This flawed model, combined with the optimization instability of RL (policy gradients, bootstrapping), forces existing methods into restrictive settings like contextual bandits for LLMs or low-dimensional state-based robotics, preventing scaling to high-dimensional sequential problems.

## Input / Output

The method consumes preference pairs of behavior segments (sequences of state-action pairs, typically length 64) sampled from suboptimal off-policy rollouts, where observations are either state vectors or 64×64 RGB images. Preferences come in two densities: dense (all pairwise comparisons within a batch) or sparse (one comparison per segment). The output is a single policy network π(a|s) mapping observations to action distributions, with no auxiliary reward, value, or Q-function networks required.

## Method

CPL exploits the maximum-entropy RL bijection A*(s,a) = α log π*(a|s) to substitute the optimal advantage function in the regret-based preference model with the policy's log-probability, yielding a preference probability that depends only on the policy. Maximizing the log-likelihood of preference data under this model produces a contrastive (Noise Contrastive Estimation) loss where each segment's score is its discounted sum of log-probabilities, optimized entirely with supervised learning—no policy gradients, dynamic programming, or reward learning. For finite offline data, an asymmetric bias regularizer λ∈(0,1) down-weights negative segments to prevent the policy from extrapolating to out-of-distribution actions, since the vanilla CPL loss is not strictly convex and admits multiple minimizers. The approach is fully off-policy, applies to arbitrary MDPs, and provably recovers the optimal policy with unbounded preference data.

## Results

On MetaWorld (6 manipulation tasks), CPL achieves 80.0–83.6% success rate with dense state-based preferences (2.5K segments), outperforming P-IQL (70.6–71.1%) by 8–13 points on most tasks. With sparser preferences (20K segments, 1 comparison each), CPL still wins 5 of 6 tasks over P-IQL (e.g., 83.2% vs 75.0% on Bin Picking, 81.2% vs 73.4% on Sweep Into). CPL is 1.6× faster (10.2 hrs vs 16.5 hrs) and uses 4× fewer parameters (2.1M vs 9.6M) than P-IQL on image-based tasks. On D4RL with real human preferences, CPL achieves 109.1 on Hopper-Medium-Expert (vs 88.6 for P-IQL) and 109.2 on Walker-Medium-Expert (vs 99.9 for P-IQL).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against P-IQL (Hejna & Sadigh, 2023), CPL wins decisively with dense state-based preferences, outperforming it on all 6 MetaWorld tasks by margins of 8–13 points (e.g., 83.6% vs 71.1% on Drawer Open, 70.4% vs 60.6% on Sweep Into). With sparse state preferences, CPL still wins 5 of 6 tasks (83.2% vs 75.0% on Bin Picking, 81.2% vs 73.4% on Sweep Into), losing only on Door Open (77.9% vs 79.0%). On image-based tasks, CPL narrows the gap: it wins 4 of 6 with dense preferences and is comparable with sparse preferences, where P-IQL benefits from data augmentation for value representation. Against PPO (Ziegler et al., 2019), CPL outperforms it on most tasks despite PPO using 3.84M additional online state-action pairs (25× more data); PPO is highly sensitive to its KL-constraint coefficient and struggles with the high variance of policy gradients in continuous control. CPL also surpasses the oracle %BC baseline (top 10% of rollouts) on all tasks, confirming it extrapolates beyond the best behaviors in the dataset. On D4RL with real human preferences, CPL achieves 109.1 on Hopper-Medium-Expert (vs 88.6 P-IQL, 86.7 PT) but underperforms on Walker-Medium-Replay (48.3 vs 71.6 P-IQL), likely due to non-regret-based preference collection.

## Relation to Prior Work

CPL sits at the intersection of the regret-based preference model (Knox et al., 2022), which corrects the standard partial-return assumption in RLHF, and maximum entropy RL (Ziebart, 2010), which provides a bijection between optimal advantage functions and policies. By substituting the policy's log-probability for the advantage function in the regret preference model, the authors derive a purely supervised contrastive objective (an instantiation of Noise Contrastive Estimation) that eliminates the RL phase entirely. This departs from the dominant two-phase paradigm (learn reward → optimize with RL) and from prior regret-based methods that required successor features and linear reward representations. The paper also shows that DPO is a special case of CPL (single-step, fixed-state segments), unifying the LLM RLHF literature under the same framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core contribution—showing that RL is unnecessary for RLHF under the regret preference model—simplifies the alignment pipeline from a multi-component RL system to a single supervised contrastive loss, with practical benefits of 1.6× speedup and 4× parameter reduction over P-IQL. This makes RLHF more accessible and scalable to high-dimensional, sequential settings (e.g., multi-turn dialogue, image-based robotics) where policy gradients and value bootstrapping are unstable. Key limitations include the assumption of a known discount factor, GPU memory requirements for long segments, and the fact that no human preference model is perfect. Open questions include scaling to larger architectures and datasets, developing an online variant for continual human feedback, and constructing multi-step preference datasets for LLMs.
