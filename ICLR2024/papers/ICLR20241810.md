## Problem

Standard kernel ridge regression provably ignores unlabeled data, since the Representer Theorem restricts the solution to depend only on labeled samples. While spectrally transformed kernels (STKs) were popular in semi-supervised learning two decades ago, prior work was largely confined to transductive settings, lacked scalable inductive implementations, and did not provide a principled characterization of why spectral transformation is the right way to exploit unlabeled data. This paper establishes STKR as a general, principled framework and delivers the first inductive implementation with closed-form predictors, scalability, and nonparametric statistical guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method requires n labeled pairs, m unlabeled samples drawn i.i.d. from the same marginal, and full pointwise access to a Mercer base kernel K(x,x′) (e.g., a graph adjacency or ε-neighbor kernel). Because the true transformed kernel K_s is inaccessible (its eigenfunctions are infinite-dimensional), the algorithm constructs a Monte-Carlo approximation K̂_s by replacing integrals over the data distribution with empirical averages over the n+m samples, which is where unlabeled data enters. The output is a closed-form regression predictor f̂(x) = v_{K̂_s,n}(x)ᵀ α̂, or in the kernel PCA variant, a d-dimensional encoder Ψ̂(x) plus ridge-regression probe weights ŵ.

## Method

The key insight is that applying a spectral transformation s(λ) to the base kernel's eigenvalues implicitly mixes K with the data distribution P_X, inducing a 'target smoothness' that unlabeled data can inform. Theorem 1 proves that any target smoothness preserving relative multiscale smoothness must be representable as an STK with s(λ) ≤ Mλ, making STKR universally expressive. For the transform-aware case (known polynomial s), the inductive predictor is solved via a Richardson iteration (STKR-Prop) that only requires matrix-vector products with the base Gram matrix, achieving Õ(q|E|β_n⁻¹) time on sparse graphs. For the transform-agnostic case, two parallel methods are provided: inverse Laplacian (s(λ) = λ/(1−ηλ)) and kernel PCA, where the top-d eigenfunctions of T_K are extracted from unlabeled samples as a pretrained encoder and a linear probe is fit on labeled data. Statistical bounds decompose into estimation error (minimax-optimal O(n^{-1/(1+p)})) and approximation error from the Monte-Carlo kernel, which vanishes when m = ω(n^{4/(1+p)}).

## Results

On three node classification benchmarks (Computers, Cora, DBLP), STKR with kernel PCA (SP-topd) achieves the best inductive accuracy on Computers at 80.80%, surpassing transductive Label Propagation (77.30%) and inductive KRR (26.35%). STKR with inverse Laplacian (SP-Lap) matches or exceeds Label Propagation on Computers (77.81% vs 77.30%) and Cora (77.04% vs 73.33%), while on DBLP Label Propagation retains a slight edge (66.44% vs 65.42%). All STKR variants dramatically outperform plain KRR with the base kernel by 20–55 percentage points across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares STKR variants against two baselines: transductive Label Propagation (LP, Zhou et al. 2003) and inductive KRR with the base kernel (s(λ)=λ). Against KRR, every STKR variant wins decisively: on Computers, SP-topd(i) reaches 80.80% versus KRR's 26.35%; on Cora, SP-Lap(t) reaches 77.04% versus 28.52%; on DBLP, LP(t) reaches 66.44% versus 44.80%. Against Label Propagation, the picture is mixed: SP-Lap(t) beats LP on Computers (77.81% vs 77.30%) and Cora (77.04% vs 73.33%) but trails on DBLP (65.42% vs 66.44%), while SP-topd(t) beats LP on Computers (80.80% vs 77.30%) but loses on Cora (69.26% vs 73.33%) and DBLP (64.86% vs 66.44%). The authors note that LP cannot operate inductively, so the inductive STKR variants (SP-Lap(i), SP-topd(i)) have no direct LP counterpart to compare against, and their slight drop from transductive performance is expected due to less information at train time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits spectrally transformed kernel regression (STKR), a framework popular in semi-supervised learning two decades ago (label propagation, Laplacian eigenmaps, kernel PCA), and elevates it to a principled general method by connecting it to modern representation learning theory. It builds directly on the RKHS approximation framework of Zhai et al. (2024) for self-supervised learning, the Representer Theorem and kernel PCA machinery from Schölkopf & Smola (2002), and the iterative solver structure of Label Propagation (Zhou et al., 2003). The key departure from prior work is moving from the transductive setting (where test points are known at train time) to the inductive setting with a general transformation function, while providing the first closed-form inductive STKR implementation with nonparametric statistical guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unifying lens that explains why diverse semi-supervised methods (label propagation, kernel PCA, inverse Laplacian regularization) are all instances of STKR, and proves that any sufficiently smooth target function can be captured by some spectral transformation (Theorem 1). This gives practitioners a principled way to choose transformations and understand when unlabeled data helps. Open questions include whether the minimax optimal rate can be improved when m is large, robustness to distribution shift in P_X, and how to combine multiple kernels for multi-constraint predictors. A key limitation is the assumption of full access to K(x,x'), which fails for contrastive learning kernels where only the RKHS norm is computable.
