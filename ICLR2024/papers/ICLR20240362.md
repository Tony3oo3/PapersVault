## Problem

Single-agent LLM evaluators (LLM-as-a-judge) show promise for text quality assessment but still fall short of human-level evaluation quality. While multi-agent debate has been explored for factuality and reasoning tasks, no prior work has applied collaborative multi-agent discussion to the text evaluation domain, despite human evaluation best practices relying on multiple annotators working together to reduce individual bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240362/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For open-ended question answering (FairEval), the system takes a question and two candidate responses (from Vicuna-13B and ChatGPT) and produces a pairwise preference judgment aggregated by majority vote across agents. For dialogue response generation (Topical-Chat), it takes a dialogue context and a system-generated response, producing multi-dimensional quality scores (naturalness, coherence, engagingness, groundedness) aggregated by averaging. Each agent additionally receives a unique role prompt (e.g., 'You are a Critic…') and the accumulated chat history of prior agent utterances, which serves as the sole medium of inter-agent communication.

## Method

ChatEval is a prompt-based multi-agent debate framework (no training) in which N homogeneous LLM agents (all GPT-4 or all ChatGPT) autonomously discuss and evaluate texts over T discussion turns. Two design choices are central: (1) diverse role specification, where each agent is assigned a distinct persona (Critic, Scientist, Psychologist, News Author, General Public) that steers its evaluative perspective, and (2) a communication strategy governing how chat history is maintained—either one-by-one (sequential turn-taking), simultaneous-talk (all agents respond in parallel each turn), or simultaneous-talk-with-summarizer (parallel responses followed by an LLM summarizer that compresses the history). Experiments show that diverse role prompts are essential: replacing all personas with a generic 'Annotator' prompt collapses multi-agent performance to single-agent level. The final evaluation is extracted via majority vote for pairwise comparisons or arithmetic mean for scoring, and the entire process requires no human intervention.

## Results

On the FairEval benchmark, ChatEval (multi-agent debate) achieves 60.0% accuracy and 0.30 Kappa with ChatGPT, and 63.8% accuracy and 0.40 Kappa with GPT-4, improving over single-agent baselines by 6.2% and 2.5% respectively. On the Topical-Chat dialogue benchmark, GPT-4-based ChatEval reaches an average Spearman correlation of 0.684 and Kendall-Tau of 0.632 across four quality dimensions, surpassing G-EVAL-4 (0.588/0.575) by 16.3% and 10.0% respectively. A simple ensemble of multiple role prompts without debate yields only marginal gains (e.g., 55.5% vs. 53.7% for ChatGPT), confirming that natural-language interaction is essential.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ChatEval outperforms FairEval (Wang et al., 2023b) on both model backbones: 60.0% vs. 58.7% accuracy (ChatGPT) and 63.8% vs. 62.5% (GPT-4), with correspondingly higher Kappa scores. On the Topical-Chat benchmark, ChatEval (GPT-4) substantially surpasses G-EVAL (Liu et al., 2023b), improving average Spearman from 0.588 to 0.684 and Kendall-Tau from 0.575 to 0.632. Traditional n-gram metrics (ROUGE-L, BLEU-4) and the embedding-based BERTScore all perform poorly on Topical-Chat, with average Spearman correlations below 0.28, far behind any LLM-based evaluator. A simple multi-agent ensemble (averaging scores from different role prompts without debate) yields only a 1.8% accuracy gain over single-agent ChatGPT, whereas the full ChatEval debate framework achieves a 6.3% gain, demonstrating that structured natural-language interaction—not mere aggregation—is the key driver of improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

ChatEval repurposes the multi-agent debate framework (Du et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023), previously applied to factuality and reasoning tasks, for the text-evaluation domain, drawing an explicit analogy to multi-annotator human evaluation best practices. It departs from single-agent LLM-as-a-judge methods (G-EVAL, FairEval) by introducing structured natural-language interaction among agents with distinct personas, rather than relying on calibration tricks (position balancing, probability-weighted scoring) or simple score averaging. The diverse role prompting is inspired by Wu et al. (2023) and Wang et al. (2023d), but the paper's key methodological contribution is the formalization of three communication strategies (one-by-one, simultaneous-talk, simultaneous-talk-with-summarizer) that govern how chat history is maintained across debate rounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that diverse role prompts are necessary for multi-agent debate to outperform single-agent evaluation (Table 3: 53.8% → 60% accuracy) provides actionable guidance for practitioners building LLM-based evaluators, while the cost analysis (Table 6) confirms the approach remains orders of magnitude cheaper than human annotation. Limitations include evaluation on only two benchmarks, homogeneous LLM groups (all GPT-4 or all ChatGPT), and small agent counts (2–5), leaving open whether heterogeneous model ensembles, dynamic role assignment, or larger debate groups would yield further gains. The observation that additional discussion turns do not improve performance (and may degrade it) suggests a fundamental tension between debate depth and context-length constraints that future work must address.
