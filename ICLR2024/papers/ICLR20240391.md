## Problem

Standard IV methods for causal effect estimation rely on linearity and a strict unconfounded instrument condition that is rarely satisfied in practice. When a conditional IV (CIV) is used to relax the unconfoundedness requirement, a 'double imbalance' arises: the observed confounders C are imbalanced both between groups defined by different values of S (biasing the treatment regression) and between treated/untreated groups (biasing the outcome regression). Existing confounding-balancing IV methods like CBIV address only the second imbalance, leaving the first unresolved in non-linear settings.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular observational data O = (W, Y, X) where W is a binary treatment, Y is the outcome, and X contains a binary CIV S plus a set of pre-IV observed confounders C (measured before S). It outputs an average causal effect (ACE) estimate computed as E[f_γ¹(ψ_θ(C)) − f_γ⁰(ψ_θ(C))] over the sample, along with individual counterfactual outcome predictions for both W=0 and W=1. The conditioning set C must satisfy the CIV d-separation conditions and the independent compliance type assumption for valid identification.

## Method

CBRL.CIV is a three-stage pipeline that learns a single balancing representation Z = ψ_θ(C) simultaneously independent of both the estimated CIV Ŝ and the estimated treatment Ŵ, thereby addressing the double imbalance in one representation. Stage 1 (CIV regression) fits a logistic network φ_μ(C) to predict P(S|C), samples Ŝ, then minimises an IPM (Wasserstein distance) between Z distributions across Ŝ=0 and Ŝ=1 groups. Stage 2 (treatment regression) fits φ_ν(Ŝ, C) to predict P(W|Ŝ, C), samples Ŵ, and enforces Ŵ ⊥ Z via a second IPM term. Stage 3 (outcome regression) trains two counterfactual networks f_γ¹ and f_γ⁰ jointly with the representation network, adding both balance terms as regularisation (weighted by α and β) to the MSE loss. Theorem 1 proves that under IV positivity and independent compliance type, the counterfactual prediction function f(W, Z) is identifiable by solving the inverse problem E[Y|S,C] = ∫ f(W,Z) dP(W|Ŝ,C), establishing the method's theoretical soundness without linearity assumptions.

## Results

On synthetic datasets (Syn-4-4, Syn-8-8, Syn-12-12), CBRL.CIV achieves the lowest out-of-sample ACE estimation error of 0.14±0.05, 0.14±0.03, and 0.12±0.03 respectively, substantially outperforming the next-best method CBIV (0.48±0.27, 0.40±0.19, 0.41±0.14) by roughly 3–4×. On the IHDP real-world dataset, CBRL.CIV reduces the ACE error to 0.251±0.082 (IHDP-2-2), 0.454±0.068 (IHDP-4-4), and 0.108±0.063 (IHDP-6-6), compared to CBIV's 2.312, 1.349, and 2.846. On the Twins dataset, CBRL.CIV achieves errors of 0.030, 0.027, and 0.030 across settings, marginally beating CBIV (0.033, 0.034, 0.035) and CFR (0.037, 0.039, 0.040).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CBRL.CIV dominates all six baseline groups on both synthetic and real-world datasets. Against the most closely related method CBIV (Wu et al., 2022), CBRL.CIV reduces the ACE error by approximately 3.4× on synthetic data (e.g., 0.14 vs. 0.48 on Syn-4-4) and by roughly 9× on IHDP-2-2 (0.251 vs. 2.312), with the gap attributable to CBRL.CIV's additional CIV regression stage that balances C between S and W. Against CFR (Shalit et al., 2017), which handles observed confounding but ignores unobserved confounders U, CBRL.CIV achieves about 4× lower error on synthetic data and 10× lower on IHDP. Against DeepIV (Hartford et al., 2017), the gap is even larger (0.14 vs. 1.58 on Syn-4-4), reflecting DeepIV's lack of any confounding balance mechanism. Among the traditional ML-based IV methods (OrthIV, DMLIV, DRIV, SDRIV, ForestDRIV), CBRL.CIV consistently achieves the lowest error, with SDRIV and ForestDRIV being the closest competitors on synthetic data (0.52 and 0.75 on Syn-12-12) but still 4–6× worse. On the Twins dataset, all methods perform well due to the smaller confounding structure, with CBRL.CIV (0.027–0.030) only marginally ahead of CBIV (0.033–0.035) and CFR (0.037–0.040).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the confounding-balancing IV regression line (CBIV, Wu et al. 2022) by replacing the standard IV with a Conditional IV (CIV), which relaxes the unconfoundedness requirement and is more readily available in practice. The key conceptual departure is identifying a 'double imbalance' problem: in addition to the known distribution imbalance of observed confounders C between treatment and outcome groups (addressed by CBIV), the CIV setting introduces a second imbalance of C among groups defined by different values of S, which prior IV methods (DeepIV, CBIV, CFR) do not handle. The method inherits the IPM-based representation learning framework from CFR (Shalit et al., 2017) and the two-stage inverse-problem formulation from DeepIV (Hartford et al., 2017), but adds a dedicated CIV regression stage with its own balance constraint to close the S–W confounding gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The double-imbalance framing fills a genuine gap: CBIV and other standard-IV methods silently assume the instrument is unconfounded, which is often unrealistic; CIVs are more practical but had no finite-sample method that simultaneously balances both the S–W and W–Y confounding paths. The theoretical identification result (Theorem 1) provides a clean sufficient condition, and the consistent 3–10× error reduction over CBIV across synthetic and semi-synthetic real-world settings is practically meaningful. Open questions include: performance when Assumption 2 (independent compliance type) is violated, extension to multi-valued or continuous treatments, and whether the gains persist on fully observational (non-simulated) CIV data where the conditioning set C is not known a priori.
