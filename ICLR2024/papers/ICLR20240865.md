## Problem

Strong LLMs like GPT-4 are roughly 30× more expensive than weaker alternatives like GPT-3.5-turbo, making them costly for reasoning workloads. Prior LLM cascade approaches (e.g., FrugalGPT) route questions using external verifiers fine-tuned to judge difficulty from literal text, but these fail on complex reasoning tasks where errors are subtle despite plausible-looking reasoning paths. This paper asks whether the weaker LLM's own answer consistency across multiple samples can serve as a training-free, task-agnostic routing signal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240865/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a reasoning question (mathematical, symbolic, or causal) and produces a final answer (numeric or string) along with an implicit accept/reject routing decision. The weaker LLM is invoked K=20 times with 8-shot in-context demonstrations under CoT and/or PoT thought representations; for PoT, the answer is obtained by executing the generated Python code in an external interpreter. The resulting consistency score determines whether the weaker LLM's majority-voted answer is accepted or the question is escalated to GPT-4 (K=3 self-consistency samples).

## Method

The core mechanism measures the weaker LLM's confidence via answer consistency: multiple sampled answers are compared, and high agreement signals an easy question whose answer can be accepted without invoking the stronger LLM. Two decision methods are proposed — vote-based (computing an agreement score s against a tunable threshold τ) and verification-based (checking whether majority-voted answers from two distinct prompt settings match exactly). The key novelty is the Mixture of Thought (MoT) representation, which samples answers from both Chain-of-Thought and Program-of-Thought prompts simultaneously, exploiting the empirical finding that CoT and PoT tend to make different types of errors on hard questions, thereby widening the consistency gap between easy and hard questions. Combined with sampling from two sets of task demonstrations, this yields 10 cascade variants (6 vote-based, 4 verification-based) that are entirely training-free and require no external verifier. On average, MoT-based variants achieve accuracy comparable to GPT-4-CoT-SC (≈0.929 vs. 0.931) at only 40% of the cost.

## Results

Across six reasoning benchmarks (GSM8k, ASDIV, TabMWP, DATE, Navigate, CREPE), the best Mixture-of-Thought (MoT) cascade variants achieve ~0.929 average accuracy at only 40–43% of the cost of GPT-4-CoT-SC (0.931 accuracy at 100% cost). On CREPE specifically, MoT variants even outperform GPT-4-CoT-SC (0.885 vs. 0.871 accuracy) at 47% of its cost. The vote-based MoT-1D-Vote at threshold τ=0.65 reaches 0.929 accuracy at 42.8% relative cost, while the verification-based MoT-1D-Verify achieves 0.925 accuracy at 39.8% cost without requiring threshold tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPT-4-CoT-SC (the primary stronger-LLM baseline at 100% cost), the MoT cascade variants match or exceed its accuracy at roughly 40% cost: MoT-1D-Vote (τ=0.65) reaches 0.929 vs. 0.931 average accuracy, and on CREPE the MoT variants actually surpass GPT-4-CoT-SC (0.885 vs. 0.871). Compared to GPT-4-CoT-Greedy (0.926 accuracy at 74.3% cost), the MoT cascades achieve equal or higher accuracy at substantially lower cost (e.g., 0.929 at 42.8%). Against the FrugalGPT-style fine-tuned external verifiers (Finetuned-Q, Finetuned-QA) and LLM-as-verifier baselines (LLM-Q, LLM-QA), the consistency-based cascade approaches dominate: on GSM8k the best external verifier (Finetuned-Q) reaches only 0.892 accuracy versus 0.951 for MoT-1D-Vote (τ=0.7), and on CREPE the best verifier (LLM-Q) achieves 0.864 versus 0.885 for MoT-1D-Vote (τ=0.55). The weaker LLM alone (GPT-3.5-CoT-SC at 0.821 accuracy, 11.6% cost) is far outperformed by the cascade, confirming that the routing decision adds substantial value beyond simply using the cheaper model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM cascade framework introduced by FrugalGPT (Chen et al., 2023a) and Šakota et al. (2023), which route queries from a weaker to a stronger LLM based on external verifiers (fine-tuned classifiers or LLM-as-judge). The key departure is replacing these text-based verifiers with an answer-consistency signal derived from the weaker LLM's own multi-sample outputs, building on the self-consistency framework of Wang et al. (2023). The novel 'Mixture of Thought' (MoT) component repurposes the CoT (Wei et al., 2022) and PoT (Chen et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2023) representations not as alternative prompting strategies but as complementary 'experts' whose agreement or disagreement serves as a more informative uncertainty signal, drawing on ensemble learning principles (Rokach, 2010).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: achieving ~93% average accuracy at 40% of GPT-4's cost across six reasoning benchmarks makes high-quality LLM reasoning accessible to resource-constrained organizations. The training-free nature of the approach (no fine-tuning of verifiers) and its generality across mathematical, symbolic, and causal reasoning tasks increase its deployability. Key limitations include the requirement for two LLMs of meaningfully different capability (LLAMA2-13B proved too weak for complex tasks), the dependence on sampling budget (K=20 for the weaker LLM), and the finding that weaker-LLM hints can actually mislead the stronger LLM. Open questions include whether the MoT principle extends to other representation pairs (e.g., CoT with tree-of-thought) and whether the approach generalizes beyond reasoning to open-ended generation tasks.
