## Problem

Existing retrosynthesis algorithms (MCTS, Retro*) assume perfect knowledge of which reactions are feasible and which molecules are buyable, but in practice reaction prediction models hallucinate infeasible reactions and molecule availability is uncertain. No prior formulation explicitly models this uncertainty, and no existing algorithm is designed to produce multiple synthesis plans that serve as effective backup plans when the primary plan fails.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241212/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a target molecule, a backward reaction model B, a search heuristic h mapping molecules to [0,1], and k i.i.d. samples from feasibility and buyability stochastic processes (which may be independent or GP-correlated). It outputs an explicit AND/OR search graph G' containing a set of synthesis plans (not a single plan), where the set is optimized to maximize the probability that at least one plan is fully executable. The output graph implicitly encodes multiple alternative routes, including backup plans that share minimal failure points with the primary plan.

## Method

Retro-fallback reformulates retrosynthesis as a stochastic process problem, introducing the Successful Synthesis Probability (SSP) metric that quantifies the probability at least one plan in a set succeeds. At each iteration, for every sample of feasibility/buyability functions, it computes three quantities via dynamic programming on the AND/OR graph: s (current success status, bottom-up), ψ (expected success upon hypothetical expansion, bottom-up), and ρ (best target-molecule plan success including each node, top-down). The algorithm then selects the frontier molecule maximizing α, the expected SSP improvement computed only over samples where the target is currently unsuccessful. This greedy expansion is repeated until the budget is exhausted or SSP reaches 100%, and the key theoretical contribution is proving that exact SSP computation is NP-hard, justifying the Monte Carlo estimation approach.

## Results

On 190 hard USPTO molecules, retro-fallback achieves the highest mean SSP (estimated with k=10,000 samples) across all four feasibility model configurations (independent vs. GP-correlated, constant 0.5 vs. rank-based marginal feasibility), outperforming BFS, retro*, and MCTS by a significant margin in every scenario. The advantage is largest under independent feasibility models, where the reaction model's tendency to output similar reactions enables effective backup plans. On secondary metrics, the SSP of the single best individual plan is generally similar across all algorithms, while retro-fallback is slightly better on fraction solved and comparable (or slightly worse than retro* in some cases) on shortest plan length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Retro-fallback is compared against three baselines: breadth-first search (uninformed), retro* (Chen et al., 2020, best-first cost-minimizing search), and MCTS (Segler et al., 2018, reward-maximizing tree search), all configured to maximize SSP by replacing their internal costs/rewards with quantities derived from the feasibility and buyability stochastic processes. Retro-fallback wins on the primary SSP metric in all scenarios, with the largest gaps observed under independent feasibility models where backup plans are most effective. Against retro*, the advantage is particularly pronounced because retro* considers only a single cost per node rather than aggregating over samples, making it unable to directly optimize the multi-plan SSP objective. Against MCTS, retro-fallback similarly outperforms because MCTS's per-plan reward structure does not capture the complementarity between multiple synthesis plans. On non-SSP metrics (fraction solved, shortest plan length, best individual plan SSP), all algorithms perform comparably, with retro* occasionally matching or slightly exceeding retro-fallback on shortest plan length, confirming that retro-fallback's gains come specifically from finding more effective backup plans rather than better primary plans.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds most directly on Retro* (Chen et al., 2020), sharing its bottom-up/top-down update structure on AND/OR graphs, but departs fundamentally by aggregating over multiple samples from stochastic feasibility and buyability processes rather than optimizing a single deterministic cost. It also positions itself against MCTS (Segler et al., 2018) and proof number search, arguing that these algorithms' internal objectives consider only individual synthesis plans and thus cannot naturally optimize the probability that *any* plan succeeds. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of SSP as an evaluation metric that rewards diverse backup plans, combined with a stochastic-process formulation of reaction feasibility that had not been formalized in prior retrosynthesis work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine practical gap: retrosynthesis plans frequently fail in the lab because reaction prediction models hallucinate infeasible reactions, and prior algorithms had no mechanism to produce diverse backup plans. The SSP metric is a natural and useful evaluation criterion that the field lacked. However, the approach is limited by the absence of well-calibrated feasibility models (the paper uses toy models with constant or rank-based probabilities), the algorithm is slower than baselines, and the tree assumption for efficient ψ/ρ computation introduces approximation. The most important open question is whether high-quality, calibrated feasibility models can be built, without which SSP estimates are not meaningful.
