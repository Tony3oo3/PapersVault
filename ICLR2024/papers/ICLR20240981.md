## Problem

No existing tool can accurately predict the end-to-end carbon footprint of a new LLM before it is physically trained. The only prior predictive tool, mlco2, is limited to CNNs, ignores architectural parameters like parameter count, assumes uniform GPU peak throughput, excludes specialized hardware (e.g., TPUs), and cannot model embodied carbon from hardware manufacturing. This leaves cloud providers and researchers without a quantitative means to compare LLM design choices (architecture, hardware, data center) on carbon impact prior to committing thousands of GPUs.

## Input / Output

LLMCarbon accepts either a full architectural description (hidden size, layer count, vocabulary size, expert count for MoE) or a direct parameter count, along with training token count, hardware configuration (device type, thermal design power, average system power, peak TFLOPs/s), and data center specification (PUE, carbon intensity in gCO2eq/kWh). It produces operational and embodied CO2eq values separately plus their sum, alongside derived metrics: test loss via the Chinchilla scaling law, training/inference duration, inference latency, optimal (pipeline, tensor, data, expert) parallelism degrees, and achieved hardware efficiency. A notable design choice is that the FLOP model for MoE LLMs uses the dense base-model parameter count rather than the total MoE parameter count, reflecting that only a subset of experts is active per token.

## Method

LLMCarbon is a purely analytical, training-free pipeline of six coupled models. A parameter model (Eq. 1–2) estimates total parameters from architecture; the Chinchilla scaling law (Eq. 3) maps parameters and tokens to test loss; a FLOP model (Eq. 4–5) computes training (6PD) and inference (2PD) FLOP counts. A hardware efficiency model determines optimal data/tensor/pipeline/expert parallelism degrees from the parameter count and device topology, then applies a polynomial regression (Eq. 6) to compute achieved efficiency as a function of the actual device count relative to the optimal count. The operational carbon model (Eq. 7–10) converts FLOPs, efficiency, device power, PUE, and data-center carbon intensity into CO2eq. The embodied carbon model (Eq. 11–12) computes per-chip emissions from die area × carbon-per-area (CPA) coefficients, amortized over hardware lifetime and scaled by execution time. The total footprint is the sum of operational and embodied components. Validation against five published LLMs (T5, GPT-3, GShard, Switch, XLM) shows ≤8.2% error versus >69% for mlco2, and the embodied carbon prediction for Meta's XLM training cluster is within 3.05% of reported values.

## Results

LLMCarbon predicts the training operational carbon footprint of five LLMs (T5, GPT-3, GShard, Switch, XLM) with errors of ≤8.2% relative to published actual values (e.g., GPT-3: 553.87 vs. 552.1 tCO2eq, +0.32%; T5: 45.66 vs. 46.7 tCO2eq, −2.22%). For embodied carbon, LLMCarbon estimates 0.64 tCO2eq for Meta's XLM training infrastructure versus the published 0.66 tCO2eq (−3.05% error). Inference and storage phase predictions also stay within 3.3% and 3.6% of actual values, respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole predictive baseline is mlco2 (Lacoste et al., 2019), the only prior tool capable of projecting carbon footprints before physical training. LLMCarbon outperforms mlco2 on every one of the five validated LLMs, with errors of 0.32%–8.2% versus mlco2's 69%–132% overestimation. The large mlco2 errors stem from its assumption that all devices operate at peak throughput and peak power, its inability to model MoE architectures, and its exclusion of embodied carbon. LLMCarbon's advantage is most pronounced for MoE models (GShard: +3.8% vs. +95.3%; Switch: +8.2% vs. +132%) because it incorporates expert parallelism and the reduced FLOP count of the dense base model. For embodied carbon, LLMCarbon achieves −3.05% error on XLM, a metric mlco2 cannot model at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

LLMCarbon extends the carbon footprint estimation task from the CNN-focused mlco2 (Lacoste et al., 2019) to LLMs by incorporating architectural parameters, MoE support, multiple hardware types, and embodied carbon modeling. Its predictive components draw directly from the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) for test loss, Megatron-LM (Narayanan et al., 2021) for optimal dense-LLM parallelism, and Pipeline MoE (Chen et al., 2023) for MoE parallelism. Prior measurement-based studies (Henderson et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2022; Patterson et al., 2021) reported post-hoc carbon data but offered no pre-training projection capability, leaving LLMCarbon as the first end-to-end predictive model covering training, inference, experimentation, and storage phases for both dense and MoE architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LLMCarbon provides cloud providers and LLM designers with a quantitative tool to explore the test-loss-versus-carbon trade-off before committing thousands of GPUs, and its validation (≤8.2% error vs. >69% for mlco2) makes it practically usable. A key forward-looking finding is that as data centers shift to renewable energy, embodied carbon will dominate (92–95% of lifecycle footprint in a 97%-renewable data center), shifting sustainability focus from operational efficiency to hardware lifecycle management. Limitations include validation on only five LLMs for operational carbon and a single LLM (XLM) for embodied carbon, and the model's reliance on polynomial regression for hardware efficiency may not generalize to novel architectures or hardware generations not yet characterized.
