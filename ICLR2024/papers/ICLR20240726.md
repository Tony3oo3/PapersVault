## Problem

Hypersphere-based anomaly detection methods (e.g., Deep SVDD) suffer from three practical failures: the learned boundary is often a hyperellipsoid rather than a true hypersphere, in high-dimensional spaces normal data concentrate far from the center (the soap-bubble phenomenon) leaving the interior unsupported, and a single hypersphere is too sparse to be a compact decision region. These issues cause anomalous points near the center to be misclassified as normal and reduce overall detection accuracy.

## Input / Output

The model is trained exclusively on normal data (one-class setting) and accepts RGB images (28×28 Fashion-MNIST, 32×32×3 CIFAR-10), tabular feature vectors (6-d Thyroid, 274-d Arrhythmia), or graphs with node features and edge structures (six benchmark graph datasets). It outputs a single scalar anomaly score per instance: for DOHSC the score is d_i² − r̂², and for DO2HSC it is (d_i − r_max)(d_i − r_min), where positive values indicate anomalous samples. For graph inputs, the GIN backbone produces a fixed-dimension global representation (16-d hidden, 8-d projected) before scoring.

## Method

DOHSC appends an orthogonal projection layer to an autoencoder's latent representation, implemented via SVD (W* = V_{k'} Λ_{k'}^{-1}) so that the projected features satisfy Z̃ᵀZ̃ = I, forcing the actual decision boundary to match the hypersphere assumption used at test time. DO2HSC further replaces the single hypersphere with a co-centered bi-hypersphere shell bounded by radii r_min and r_max (initialized from the ν and 1−ν quantiles of training distances), with a loss of max{d_i, r_max} − min{d_i, r_min} that pushes all normal samples into the shell, yielding a higher support density (Proposition 3: κ = 1/(1−(r_min/r_max)^k) > 1). For graph-level anomaly detection, a 4-layer GIN extracts node and global representations, and a Jensen-Shannon mutual information estimator maximizes the MI between node-level (substructure) and graph-level (global) features in each batch, jointly optimized with the orthogonal projection and hypersphere/bi-hypersphere compression losses.

## Results

On graph-level anomaly detection (the paper's primary novel setting), DO2HSC achieves the best AUC across all six datasets and all classes, with particularly dramatic gains: 93.9% AUC on COLLAB class 0 (vs. 65.0% for the best baseline OCGTL), 90.9% on MUTAG class 0 (vs. 88.1% for infoGraph+DSVDD), and 73.5% on ER_MD class 1 (vs. 69.2% for OCGTL). On CIFAR-10 image data, DO2HSC achieves the highest AUC on 8 out of 10 classes (e.g., 84.4% on Ship, 82.7% on Automobile), and on tabular data, DO2HSC reaches 0.98 F1 on Thyroid and 0.74 F1 on Arrhythmia, outperforming the strongest baseline PLAD (0.77 and 0.71 respectively). DO2HSC consistently outperforms DOHSC, with the largest gap on ER_MD class 1 (73.5% vs. 51.8% AUC, a 21.7-point improvement).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DO2HSC achieves the best AUC on every class of every graph dataset, decisively outperforming all 16 baselines. The largest margins are against OCGTL on COLLAB (93.9% vs. 65.0% on class 0, a 28.9-point gap) and against infoGraph+DSVDD on MUTAG class 0 (90.9% vs. 88.1%). On CIFAR-10, DO2HSC surpasses the strongest image baselines (PLAD, DROCC, HRN) on 8 of 10 classes, with the clearest wins on Frog (83.0% vs. 77.4% for HRN) and Ship (84.4% vs. 82.5% for HRN). On tabular data, DO2HSC's 0.98 F1 on Thyroid exceeds the next-best DROCC (0.78) by 20 points, and its 0.74 F1 on Arrhythmia beats PLAD (0.71) by 3 points. DOHSC also beats all baselines on every dataset but is consistently outperformed by DO2HSC, confirming the benefit of the bi-hypersphere decision boundary; the gap is most pronounced on ER_MD class 1 (73.5% vs. 51.8% AUC). Graph-kernel baselines (SP, WL, NH, RW + OCSVM) are uniformly weak, often near or below random (0.5 AUC), highlighting the advantage of end-to-end deep learning over two-stage kernel methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Deep SVDD line of work (Ruff et al., 2018), which learns a hyperspherical decision boundary around normal data in a latent space, by identifying and addressing three concrete geometric failures of the single-hypersphere assumption: non-spherical boundaries, the high-dimensional soap-bubble phenomenon, and incompactness. The orthogonal projection layer (DOHSC) is a targeted fix for the first issue, while the bi-hypersphere shell (DO2HSC) is motivated by concentration-of-measure results from Vershynin (2018) and Laurent & Massart (2000). For graph-level anomaly detection, the paper adapts the mutual information maximization framework of InfoGraph (Sun et al., 2020) to jointly optimize node-level and graph-level representations within the same hypersphere/bi-hypersphere compression objective, going beyond prior end-to-end graph anomaly detectors (OCGIN, OCGTL, GLocalKD) that either ignore the node-global relationship or use different decision boundary formulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bi-hypersphere compression offers a principled geometric correction to a widely-used but theoretically flawed assumption in one-class anomaly detection, with a clean density-ratio argument (Proposition 3) showing when the improvement is most significant. The orthogonal projection layer is a simple, low-cost architectural addition that practitioners can adopt in any hypersphere-based detector. However, the paper explicitly limits itself to single-class normal data, leaving the multi-class setting (addressed by Kirchheim et al., 2022) as an open question, and the mutual information component shows inconsistent benefits across datasets (Table 12), suggesting the core gains come from the geometric fixes rather than the representation-learning objective. The graph-level extension is practical but operates on small datasets (188–5000 graphs), limiting confidence in scalability.
