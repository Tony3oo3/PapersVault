## Problem

While sinusoidal INRs (e.g., SIREN) have been analyzed via Fourier theory, the behavior of INRs using wavelet activation functions beyond the first layer remained opaque. In particular, it was unclear how nonlinear layers in a wavelet-based INR resolve high-frequency content from coarse first-layer approximations, and what architectural or initialization choices follow from this mechanism.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241204/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes uniformly sampled 1D signal points (n=512 over [-2,2]) or RGB images (Kodak Lossless True Color Image Suite) and produces a continuous complex-valued interpolant whose real part approximates the target. For 2D images, the WMM initialization additionally consumes a Canny edge map derived from the input to set first-layer biases. The output is structured as a sum of two sub-networks (scaling + wavelet), and for real-valued targets only Re{f_θ} is used, which symmetrizes the Fourier spectrum.

## Method

The paper proves (Lemma 1) that the Fourier transform of an INR output is a sum of convolutions of the first-layer template atoms' Fourier transforms, generalizing the sinusoidal case. It then shows that progressive (one-sided) complex wavelets form an algebra over C, so the INR output preserves conic Fourier support through all nonlinear layers (Corollary 4), meaning high-frequency content is generated by polynomial combinations of low-frequency atoms without leaking into negative frequencies. This motivates a split architecture: a linear Gaussian scaling network captures smooth low-pass content while a nonlinear Gabor wavelet network (with sine activations in hidden layers) resolves singularities, with the two networks' Fourier supports being essentially disjoint. For initialization, wavelet modulus maxima (or Canny edges in 2D) are used to place first-layer template atoms at singularity locations, yielding roughly an order-of-magnitude MSE reduction over random initialization.

## Results

On the Kodak dataset, the proposed split complex Gabor wavelet INR with WMM-based initialization achieves 30.835 dB PSNR for image fitting and 29.385 dB for denoising, outperforming the same architecture with random initialization (30.081 dB and 29.236 dB respectively). On the 1D test signal, the full split complex wavelet INR achieves MSE of 0.0011, compared to 0.0016 for the split architecture without hidden layers and 0.0606 for the complex wavelet INR without a scaling network. The WMM initialization strategy yields approximately an order-of-magnitude reduction in MSE over random initialization across all tested numbers of template atoms (K ∈ {1,3,5,7}).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is SIREN (Sitzmann et al., 2020), which uses sinusoidal activation functions. On the Kodak image fitting task, the proposed complex Gabor WIRE INR with WMM initialization achieves 30.835 dB PSNR, outperforming SIREN's 18.001 dB by a margin of approximately 12.8 dB — a substantial gap that reflects the advantage of spatially localized wavelet atoms over globally oscillatory sinusoids. The real Gabor variant (30.826 dB) performs nearly identically to the complex Gabor with WMM init (30.835 dB) on the fitting task, while the Gaussian-only template (26.950 dB) lags by nearly 4 dB, confirming that wavelet structure is essential for capturing high-frequency content. For denoising, WMM initialization provides a modest but consistent gain of ~0.15 dB over random initialization, demonstrating that the implicit bias of INRs favors fitting natural image structure over noise regardless of initialization quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Fourier-analytic framework of Yüce et al. (2022), which characterized the expressivity of sinusoidal INRs (SIREN) via trigonometric product identities, to arbitrary template functions through a convolution-theoretic bound (Lemma 1). It then builds directly on WIRE (Saragadam et al., 2023), the first INR architecture using Gabor wavelets as activation functions, by providing the missing theoretical justification: progressive complex wavelets form an algebra over ℂ, so the INR output inherits the conic Fourier support of the template. The split architecture (scaling network + wavelet network) mirrors the classical wavelet decomposition into scaling and detail coefficients, and the WMM-based initialization draws on Mallat's singularity detection theory, connecting INR design to a well-developed body of wavelet analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The algebraic characterization of progressive wavelets gives practitioners a principled reason to prefer complex over real wavelet activations in INRs and explains why the split architecture decouples low- and high-frequency approximation. The WMM initialization scheme offers a concrete, signal-aware alternative to random initialization that could be adopted broadly for wavelet-based INRs. Open questions include whether the conic-support preservation extends to shearlet or curvelet template functions (connecting to microlocal analysis), how the split architecture behaves for inverse problems, and whether the approximate progressive assumption for Gabor wavelets (fast but not exact decay) causes issues at extreme scales. Experiments are limited to 1D test signals and the Kodak suite, leaving open whether these design principles transfer to 3D volumetric data or large-scale image generation.
