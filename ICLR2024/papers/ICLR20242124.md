## Problem

Prior label poisoning attacks on GNNs are evaluated under unrealistic defender assumptions—class-equalized splits, untuned hyperparameters, and clean validation sets—that overstate attack effectiveness by roughly 9% on average. Beyond this evaluation gap, existing attacks (LP, LFK, MG) rely on loose surrogates and greedy heuristics that limit their practical strength, leaving a critical gap in understanding how vulnerable GNNs truly are to label perturbations.

## Input / Output

The attacker receives the full graph adjacency matrix A, node feature matrix X, and the ground-truth training labels Y_l (one-hot encoded, |V_l| × C), and produces a poisoned label matrix H ∈ {0,1}^{L×C} that flips at most ⌊ε|V_l|⌋ labels under a budget constraint. The output is applied to a target GNN (e.g., GCN, GAT, APPNP) which is then trained from scratch on the poisoned labels and evaluated on held-out test nodes. A key design choice is the binary variant, where the attacker is restricted to flipping labels between only two classes (e.g., the two most frequent), which paradoxically yields stronger attacks than the unconstrained multi-class setting.

## Method

The paper proposes two attack families that approximate the NP-hard bi-level poisoning problem. The Linear Surrogate Attack (LSA) replaces the inner training problem with a closed-form ridge-regression solution using either an SGC or NTK surrogate, then formulates the outer problem as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP); the key theoretical contribution is proving that the LP relaxation is integral via total unimodularity, enabling exact and efficient solution with a standard LP solver, and for the fixed-target variant (SGC-FIX) a closed-form solution selects the ⌊εL⌋ most damaging nodes. The Meta Attack instead unrolls the inner optimization for K epochs and uses meta-gradients with Gumbel-softmax for discrete label selection and soft-top-k for budget enforcement. Complementing the attacks, the paper identifies six evaluation pitfalls (oversized clean validation, missing standard deviations, no defense evaluation, class-equalized splits, no hyperparameter tuning, and unpoisoned validation) and proposes a faithful cross-validation protocol that poisons the entire sparse labeled set and tunes the defender's model, revealing that binary-flip variants outperform multi-class attacks due to reduced surrogate loss gap and adversarial overfitting.

## Results

Under the corrected evaluation protocol (CV setting with hyperparameter tuning), the proposed SGC-BIN attack reduces GCN test accuracy on Cora-ML from ~81% (clean) to ~54% at 20% budget, outperforming the strongest prior attack LFK by up to ~13 percentage points. On the large-scale OGBN-ArXiv dataset (~169K nodes), SGC-BIN drops accuracy to 48.20% at 5% budget while LFK times out entirely and LP only reaches 55.62%. The paper also shows that correcting the six evaluation pitfalls weakens prior attacks by ~9% on average, and that a single adversarial label flip on a tiny Cora-ML subgraph can drop GCN accuracy by ~17%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SGC-BIN consistently outperforms all prior label poisoning attacks (LFK, LP, MG) across all datasets and models, with maximum gains of up to ~13 percentage points in accuracy drop. Against LFK (Zhang et al., 2020), the strongest prior attack, SGC-BIN achieves lower (worse for the defender) accuracy at every budget level; on OGBN-ArXiv, LFK times out entirely while SGC-BIN still degrades accuracy to 48.20% at 5% budget. Against MG (Liu et al., 2022), the gap is especially pronounced after proper hyperparameter tuning, where MG becomes nearly as ineffective as random flipping (RND). Against the certified defense AblationGCN (Scholten et al., 2022), SGC-BIN still inflicts heavy damage, dropping Cora-ML accuracy from 79.17% to 46.60% at 30% budget. Against RTGNN (Qian et al., 2023), which claims robustness to up to 40% pairwise label noise, the paper shows SGC-BIN causes a debilitating effect with as low as 10% noise. The META attack, while outperforming some baselines, is consistently weaker than the linear surrogate attacks under the fair evaluation setting, reversing the ordering seen under the flawed default protocol.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the three prior label poisoning attacks for GNNs—LP (Liu et al., 2019), LFK (Zhang et al., 2020), and MG (Liu et al., 2022)—all of which approximate the bi-level poisoning optimization via fixed surrogate models. It departs from this lineage in two key ways: first, it identifies six systematic evaluation pitfalls (class-equalized splits, untuned models, clean validation sets, etc.) that invalidate prior conclusions about attack efficacy; second, it introduces new attack families (MILP-based linear surrogates and meta-learning-based attacks) that are both stronger and, in the linear case, provably optimal via total unimodularity. The meta-attack design draws on unrolled optimization from the meta-learning literature (Huang et al., 2020; Mujkanovic et al., 2022), while the theoretical analysis of binary vs. multi-class flips builds on label-noise robustness theory from Oyen et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of evaluation pitfalls means that prior claims about GNN vulnerability to label poisoning are unreliable, with an average ~9% overestimation of attack effectiveness—this recalibrates the field's understanding of GNN robustness. The finding that simple linear surrogate attacks outperform adaptive meta attacks contradicts the 'bitter lesson' established for feature and structure perturbations, suggesting that the bi-level structure of label poisoning is fundamentally different. The counterintuitive superiority of binary over multi-class flips, backed by both theory (Proposition 3) and experiments, opens an important question about how label sparsity and class imbalance interact with adversarial optimization. Open questions include extending the binary-flip theory beyond random heuristics to worst-case attacks, developing defenses that are robust to the new stronger attacks, and understanding whether the linear-surrogate-over-meta-attack finding generalizes to other poisoning settings.
