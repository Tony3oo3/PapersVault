## Problem

Open-source LLMs such as LLaMA-2 remain far from satisfactory at solving mathematical problems due to the complex multi-step reasoning required. Existing finetuning-based methods heavily depend on the quality and diversity of training data, yet prior work has focused primarily on augmenting answers (e.g., rejection sampling) without systematically diversifying the questions themselves. This paper asks whether bootstrapping questions in both forward and backward reasoning directions can close the gap between open-source and closed-source models on mathematical reasoning.

## Input / Output

The model takes a mathematical word problem as a text instruction and produces a step-by-step chain-of-thought reasoning path ending with the structured marker 'The answer is: {value}' for answer extraction. During training, the input space is expanded beyond the original question: backward-reasoning variants mask a numeric token as 'x' and append the ground-truth answer, requiring the model to solve for the masked value. The MetaMathQA dataset contains 395K samples (240K from GSM8K, 155K from MATH) across four augmentation types, all formatted as (question, reasoning path, answer) triples.

## Method

MetaMath constructs the MetaMathQA training set through four complementary augmentation strategies applied to GSM8K and MATH: (1) answer augmentation via rejection sampling, where GPT-3.5-Turbo generates multiple reasoning paths and only correct ones are retained; (2) LLM rephrasing, where GPT-3.5-Turbo rewrites each question from multiple perspectives with correctness verified by checking the generated answer; (3) Self-Verification backward questions, where a number is masked as 'x' and the question is rewritten as a declarative statement with the answer embedded; and (4) FOBAR backward questions, where the answer is directly appended to the masked question. The key insight is that a single mathematical question represents only one view of the underlying meta-knowledge, and bootstrapping across forward and backward reasoning directions acts as multi-view augmentation that increases question diversity (measured by diversity gain) and prevents accuracy saturation. LLaMA-2 (7B, 13B, 70B) is then finetuned on the combined MetaMathQA dataset using standard maximum-likelihood training on the reasoning path conditioned on the question, with the 70B model trained via QLoRA for computational efficiency.

## Results

MetaMath achieves state-of-the-art performance among open-source LLMs on both GSM8K and MATH benchmarks. MetaMath-7B reaches 66.5% on GSM8K and 19.8% on MATH, surpassing the previous best open-source model (WizardMath-7B at 54.9% GSM8K, 10.7% MATH) by +11.6% and +9.1% respectively. MetaMath-70B achieves 82.3% on GSM8K, slightly exceeding GPT-3.5-Turbo (80.8%), and 26.6% on MATH. On the out-of-distribution DROP benchmark, MetaMath-7B achieves 37.1% exact match accuracy versus 31.5% for WizardMath-7B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MetaMath-7B outperforms all open-source 7B baselines by a large margin: +11.6% over WizardMath-7B (54.9→66.5) on GSM8K and +9.1% on MATH (10.7→19.8), and +16.2% over RFT-7B (50.3→66.5) on GSM8K. At 13B, MetaMath-13B (72.3% GSM8K, 22.4% MATH) beats WizardMath-13B (63.9%, 14.0%) by +8.4% and +8.4% respectively. At 70B, MetaMath-70B (82.3% GSM8K, 26.6% MATH) surpasses WizardMath-70B (81.6%, 22.7%) and notably exceeds GPT-3.5-Turbo (80.8% GSM8K) — the very model used to generate the augmented training data. On the out-of-distribution DROP benchmark, MetaMath consistently outperforms SFT, RFT, and WizardMath at all three model sizes (e.g., 37.1% vs. 31.5% at 7B; 72.3% vs. 63.1% at 70B), confirming the gains are not benchmark-specific. The paper also shows that combining MetaMathQA with the external RFT dataset actually hurts performance, indicating that data quality and diversity matter more than raw data volume.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on rejection sampling fine-tuning (RFT, Yuan et al. [79]) for answer augmentation and repurposes backward reasoning techniques originally designed for inference-time verification—Self-Verification (Weng et al. [76]) and FOBAR (Jiang et al. [32])—as a training data generation strategy. Unlike prior finetuning-based methods (SFT, RFT, WizardMath) that only augment answers or use complex reward-model pipelines, MetaMath bootstraps questions in both forward (rephrasing) and backward (SV, FOBAR) directions, framing question diversity as a multi-view augmentation of underlying meta-knowledge. The approach draws conceptual connections to dataset distillation and machine teaching, but uses reasoning diversity as a heuristic proxy rather than optimizing empirical risk directly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The released MetaMathQA dataset (395K samples) has become a widely adopted training resource for mathematical reasoning LLMs, and the simple question-bootstrapping recipe is reproducible and model-agnostic. The empirical finding that question diversity (measured by embedding-space distance) correlates strongly with accuracy (Pearson 0.972) and that answer augmentation alone saturates quickly provides practical guidance for data curation. Limitations include performance degradation on longer questions, the observation that naively adding more augmented data (e.g., RFT) can hurt performance, and reliance on GPT-3.5-Turbo for data generation, which introduces a quality ceiling. Open questions include whether the bootstrapping approach transfers to more advanced mathematics (beyond elementary/high-school level) and how to handle the growing gap on longer, multi-step problems.
