## Problem

Farina & Pipis (2023) established that linear correlated equilibria are the tightest known polynomial-time computable relaxation of correlated equilibrium in general extensive-form games, yet their game-theoretic meaning remained unexplored and their computation required solving a quadratic program (ℓ₂ projection) at every iteration. This paper addresses both gaps: it provides a natural mediator-based interpretation of linear deviations and develops algorithms that are polynomially faster per iteration.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240221/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an extensive-form game with perfect recall, represented as a tree-form decision problem with d sequences in sequence form. The resulting UTC decision problem is a DAG (not a tree) whose nodes are pairs (s, s̃) tracking both the game state and the mediator state, and whose pure strategies are represented as pairs of matrices (A, B) rather than a single sequence-form vector. The output is a correlated profile constituting a linear correlated equilibrium, produced by a no-linear-swap regret minimizer achieving O(d²√T) regret.

## Method

The key conceptual contribution is defining untimed communication (UTC) deviations, in which a deviator interacting with a mediator may make any number of queries (zero, one, or more) per decision point, in contrast to timed communication deviations that require exactly one query. The paper proves UTC deviations coincide precisely with linear deviations (Theorem 4.1), grounding linear deviations in the standard mediator framework used for other extensive-form equilibrium concepts. Because the UTC decision problem is a DAG, it is expressible as a scaled extension, enabling the CFR framework for regret minimization. This reduces the per-iteration computation from an ℓ₂ projection (quadratic program, Õ(d¹⁰)) to a fixed-point computation (linear program, Õ(d^ω)), yielding 35×–10,800× empirical speedups over Farina & Pipis (2023) across four benchmark games.

## Results

The paper's CFR-based algorithm achieves O(d²√T) linear-swap regret with per-iteration complexity dominated by a fixed-point computation (LP), compared to the O(d²√T) regret with per-iteration QP (ℓ₂ projection) of Farina & Pipis (2023). Across four benchmark games, the per-iteration time is reduced by 35× to 442× (e.g., 5.65 ms vs. 195 ms on 4-player Kuhn poker; 114 ms vs. 50.2 s on Sheriff of Nottingham). End-to-end, the time to reach a target linear-swap equilibrium gap is reduced by 595× to 10,800× (e.g., 32.8 s vs. 5 h 25 m on 4-player Kuhn poker; 2.00 s vs. 6 h on Sheriff of Nottingham).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole experimental baseline is the algorithm of Farina & Pipis (2023), which also achieves O(d²√T) linear-swap regret but requires solving a convex quadratic program (ℓ₂ projection onto the sequence-form polytope) at every iteration. The proposed method wins decisively on every metric across all four benchmark games: per-iteration wall-clock time is 35× faster on 4-player Kuhn poker (5.65 ms vs. 195 ms), 237× faster on the ridesharing game (676 μs vs. 160 ms), 287× faster on 3-player Leduc poker (42.0 ms vs. 12.1 s), and 442× faster on Sheriff of Nottingham (114 ms vs. 50.2 s). The end-to-end advantage is even more dramatic because the CFR framework (enabled by the UTC/scaled-extension representation) converges in far fewer iterations, yielding 595× to 10,800× speedups in time-to-target-gap. The two key sources of improvement are (1) replacing the per-iteration QP with a cheaper fixed-point LP, and (2) enabling the use of CFR with regret matching⁺, which is the fastest practical regret minimizer, whereas Farina & Pipis could not use CFR due to their representation not being expressible as a scaled extension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Farina & Pipis (2023), who established the existence of polynomial-time no-linear-swap regret dynamics in extensive-form games but whose algorithm required an expensive quadratic program (ℓ₂ projection) per iteration. The key conceptual departure is showing that linear deviations—previously defined purely algebraically as linear maps on the sequence-form polytope—are exactly equivalent to a new mediator-based deviation set (untimed communication deviations), placing them within the same interaction framework used for all other extensive-form equilibrium concepts (EFCE, communication equilibrium, etc.). This equivalence, combined with the observation from Zhang et al. (2023) that DAG decision problems admit scaled-extension representations, enables the use of CFR-based regret minimizers and reduces the per-iteration computation to a fixed-point problem (linear program) rather than a quadratic program.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Linear correlated equilibria are currently the tightest known relaxation of correlated equilibrium computable in polynomial time for general extensive-form games, so understanding their structure and computing them efficiently is a central open problem in the field. The mediator interpretation gives a natural game-theoretic meaning to what was previously an opaque algebraic object, and the resulting algorithms achieve 35×–10,800× speedups over the prior state of the art. Key open questions remain: whether swap regret can be driven below T^1 in extensive-form games, how to define a non-private untimed communication equilibrium that doesn't collapse to the timed version, and whether the polynomial-time exact computation and near-optimal regret results known for EFCE can be extended to linear equilibria.
