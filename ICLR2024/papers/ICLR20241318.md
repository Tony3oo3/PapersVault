## Problem

Most diffusion-based TTS systems generate spectrograms and require a separate vocoder to produce waveforms, losing phase information critical for natural phenomena like vocal fry. Existing waveform-level diffusion models (e.g., DiffWave) are restricted to fixed short durations, while non-autoregressive end-to-end models hit GPU memory limits on long utterances. The paper addresses how to generate unlimited-duration, high-fidelity raw speech waveforms end-to-end with a single model.

## Input / Output

In conditional mode, the model takes a phoneme sequence (from a 72-phoneme alphabet via G2P), per-phoneme durations from a small CNN predictor, per-phoneme energy from another predictor, and optionally a pitch contour, all aligned to the frame grid. It outputs a raw waveform with samples in [-1,1], generated sequentially in overlapping frames (L=500 samples with L_o=250 overlap), meaning each inference run yields a slightly different but valid waveform due to the stochastic diffusion process. In unconditional mode, no text is provided and the model generates speech-like audio from the previous frame alone.

## Method

DiffAR combines denoising diffusion probabilistic models with an autoregressive frame-by-frame generation scheme: each frame of L samples is denoised over S diffusion steps while being conditioned on the last L_o samples of the previously generated frame via a fixed inpainting-and-reordering matrix H. The backbone is a 36-layer stack of bidirectional dilated convolutions (dilation cycle 1–2048) with 256 channels per layer, augmented by multi-scaled residual blocks (kernels 3, 5, 7) that inject phoneme, energy, and pitch conditioning. Training minimizes the standard DDPM noise-prediction loss (‖ε_θ − ε‖²) with a linear variance schedule β_t ∈ [1e-4, 0.02]; inference iteratively applies the learned denoiser from s=S down to s=1, starting from pure Gaussian noise and using the overlap of the prior frame as context. The key architectural insight is that operating directly on the raw waveform—rather than a spectrogram—preserves phase information, enabling the model to synthesize sub-phonetic phenomena such as vocal fry, while the autoregressive overlap mechanism ensures seamless temporal continuity across arbitrarily long utterances without growing memory footprint.

## Results

On LJ-Speech, DiffAR (1000 diffusion steps) achieves a MOS of 3.77 and MUSHRA score of 66.7, surpassing WaveGrad 2 (MOS 3.61, MUSHRA 63.8) on all subjective and objective metrics, with CER of 1.95% and WER of 4.65%. Against FastSpeech 2, DiffAR (1000 steps) achieves MOS 3.82 and MUSHRA 66.2, outperforming both the original (MOS 3.54, MUSHRA 63.4) and improved (MOS 3.75, MUSHRA 64.2) versions on perceptual quality, though the improved variant retains a slight edge in intelligibility (CER 1.73% vs 1.95%). In the broader Appendix D comparison, DiffAR (1000 steps) outperforms VITS (MUSHRA 71.5 vs 69.0), ProDiff (67.5 vs 64.6), and DiffGAN-TTS (68.4 vs 68.0), while Grad-TTS (68.5) slightly edges out DiffAR-1000 (67.7) in one MUSHRA trial.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffAR outperforms WaveGrad 2 across every metric: MOS (3.77 vs 3.61), MUSHRA (66.7 vs 63.8), CER (1.95% vs 3.47%), and WER (4.65% vs 5.75%), which the authors attribute to WaveGrad 2's non-autoregressive single-pass generation. Against FastSpeech 2, DiffAR wins on perceptual quality (MOS 3.82 vs 3.54; MUSHRA 66.2 vs 63.4) but the improved FastSpeech 2 variant retains a small intelligibility advantage (CER 1.73% vs 1.95%, WER 4.31% vs 4.65%). In the Appendix D MUSHRA comparisons, DiffAR (1000 steps) beats VITS (71.5 vs 69.0), ProDiff (67.5 vs 64.6), and DiffGAN-TTS (68.4 vs 68.0), but is slightly below Grad-TTS (67.7 vs 68.5), likely because Grad-TTS's explicit guidance from noised acoustic features reduces stochasticity at the cost of diversity. A key qualitative advantage unique to DiffAR is its ability to synthesize vocal fry, a phase-dependent phenomenon that none of the spectrogram-based baselines (FastSpeech 2, WaveGrad 2, VITS, Grad-TTS, ProDiff, DiffGAN-TTS) reproduce. DiffAR also maintains constant GPU memory regardless of text length, whereas WaveGrad 2 and FastSpeech 2 hit out-of-memory errors at 512 and 1024 words respectively, though DiffAR's RTF of 31.06 is substantially slower than all baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiffAR combines two established ideas—denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM, Ho et al. 2020) and the DiffWave architecture (Kong et al. 2020b) with bidirectional dilated convolutions—into a novel autoregressive frame-by-frame formulation for raw waveform generation. It departs from the dominant spectrogram-then-vocoder pipeline (FastSpeech 2, VITS, Grad-TTS, ProDiff, DiffGAN-TTS) by operating directly on the waveform, and from non-autoregressive diffusion TTS (WaveGrad 2) by generating overlapping frames sequentially, each conditioned on the previous frame's tail. The autoregressive-diffusion combination has appeared in video synthesis (Ho et al. 2022) and ordered-structure generation (Hoogeboom et al. 2021), but this paper is the first to apply it to 1D speech waveforms for unlimited-duration TTS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The constant-memory autoregressive design addresses a practical bottleneck: non-autoregressive diffusion TTS models (WaveGrad 2, FastSpeech 2) hit GPU out-of-memory errors beyond ~512–1024 words, while DiffAR maintains flat memory consumption regardless of utterance length. The direct waveform generation captures phase-dependent phenomena like vocal fry that spectrogram-based models structurally cannot reproduce, and the inherent diffusion stochasticity yields natural one-to-many variability absent in deterministic systems like FastSpeech 2. Key limitations include slow synthesis (autoregressive × 200–1000 diffusion steps per frame), evaluation on a single 24-hour single-speaker dataset (LJ-Speech), and only preliminary multi-speaker results on VCTK. Open questions include reducing inference latency (fewer steps, DDIM-style acceleration), scaling to multi-speaker and emotion-conditioned generation, and whether the phase-fidelity advantage persists at larger scale.
