## Problem

Existing imitation learning benchmarks either lack diverse human demonstrations, do not require closed-loop sensory feedback, or provide no quantitative metrics for measuring a model's ability to replicate multi-modal behavior. This makes it impossible to systematically assess which algorithms can truly capture the inherent diversity in human data distributions, a critical capability for real-world robot deployment.

## Input / Output

Policies consume either handcrafted proprioceptive state vectors (dimensionality ranges from 4 for Avoiding to 4+3X for Sorting-X) or dual RGB camera views at 96×96 resolution (in-hand and front, 3 channels normalized to [0,1]) encoded via ResNet-18. History-dependent models receive k=5 past observations. Outputs are velocity commands (2–8 dimensional depending on task) for single-action models, or 3-step action sequences for action-chunking variants. Velocity (relative) control substantially outperforms position (absolute) control across all tasks.

## Method

The paper introduces D3IL, a suite of five Mujoco simulation tasks (Avoiding, Aligning, Pushing, Sorting-X, Stacking-X) with a Franka Emika Panda robot, each featuring multiple valid solution strategies, variable trajectory lengths, and randomized initial states requiring closed-loop feedback. Demonstrations are collected from multiple human operators via Xbox gamepad (T1–T4) or augmented reality (T5), with approximately equal trajectories per behavior descriptor. Two tractable diversity metrics are proposed: behavior entropy H(π(β)) measuring the spread over achieved behaviors, and conditional behavior entropy E_{s₀}[H(π(β|s₀))] ensuring diversity is not merely a deterministic function of initial state. Eleven IL algorithms are benchmarked along three axes—backbone (MLP vs. GPT vs. Transformer-EncDec), prediction horizon (single action vs. action chunking), and diversity mechanism (deterministic, VAE, K-means clustering, implicit EBM, discrete/continuous diffusion)—revealing that diffusion-based policies with transformer backbones (DDPM-GPT, BESO, DDPM-ACT) best balance success rate and behavior entropy, while transformer architectures generalize significantly better under data scarcity.

## Results

On the D3IL benchmark (state-based), diffusion-based methods with transformer backbones achieve the best combined success rate and behavior entropy: DDPM-GPT reaches ~84–86% success rate on Avoiding, Aligning, and Pushing with entropy 0.66–0.86, while DDPM-ACT achieves 92% success rate and 0.90 entropy on Aligning. DDPM-GPT improves success rate by an average of 21% over its MLP counterpart (DDPM-MLP) across Avoiding, Aligning, and Pushing. On the most complex task (Sorting-6), no method achieves satisfactory performance, highlighting a significant gap in current IL algorithms for highly diverse, multi-object manipulation. For image-based evaluation, DDPM-ACT outperforms DDPM-MLP by 11%, 40%, and 24% on stacking 1, 2, and 3 blocks respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Diffusion-based methods with transformer backbones (DDPM-GPT, BESO, DDPM-ACT) consistently outperform deterministic baselines (BC-MLP, BC-GPT) on behavior entropy while maintaining competitive or superior success rates; DDPM-GPT achieves an average 21% success rate improvement over DDPM-MLP across Avoiding, Aligning, and Pushing, confirming that transformer history inputs are critical. VAE-based methods (VAE-State, VAE-ACT) suffer from mode collapse, yielding very low entropy (0.025–0.070) despite high success rates, whereas IBC and BeT achieve moderate-to-high entropy but with inconsistent success rates across tasks. BeT with a GPT backbone shows a significant performance drop relative to the deterministic BC-GPT baseline, indicating that the clustering-based diversity mechanism comes at a cost. On the most challenging task (Sorting-6), all methods fail to achieve satisfactory performance, with success rates generally below 0.5, revealing a clear limitation of current IL approaches for highly diverse multi-object manipulation. For image-based evaluation, DDPM-ACT demonstrates the largest gains over DDPM-MLP on the dexterous Stacking task (up to 40% improvement for 2-block stacking), while state-based representations remain superior for precise-control tasks like Aligning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against existing imitation learning benchmarks (D4RL, Robomimic, T-Shape Pushing, Relay Kitchen, Block-Push) by identifying that none simultaneously satisfy all of: diverse human demonstrations, multiple sub-tasks, multi-object manipulation, closed-loop feedback requirements, and quantitative diversity metrics. It builds on the methodological lineage of BeT (clustering), IBC (energy-based models), Diffusion Policy (discrete diffusion), and BESO (continuous-time diffusion) as baselines, but its core contribution is the benchmark design and the introduction of behavior entropy and conditional behavior entropy as tractable diversity metrics. The task design principles (diverse behavior, multiple human demonstrators, variable trajectory lengths, task variations requiring closed-loop feedback) are explicitly motivated by gaps in prior benchmarks like Block-Push (scripted policy, no fallback strategies) and Relay Kitchen (no closed-loop requirement).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

D3IL provides the first simulation benchmark explicitly designed to evaluate multi-modal behavior learning with human demonstrations, filling a gap where prior work relied on qualitative analysis or synthetic data. The practical metrics (behavior entropy, conditional behavior entropy) give the field a standardized way to compare diversity-capturing algorithms beyond success rate alone. Key open questions remain: no method solves Sorting-6, image-based policies fail at precise control tasks while state-based policies fail to scale with object count, and the combination of action chunking with history (DDPM-ACT) does not clearly outperform history-only diffusion (DDPM-GPT), suggesting the optimal architecture for diverse behavior learning is still unresolved. The simulation-only setting also limits direct transferability to real-robot deployment.
