## Problem

Behavior cloning policies fail when encountering out-of-distribution states due to compounding errors, sensor noise, or external disturbances, yet existing corrective data augmentation methods require interactive experts, additional offline datasets, or domain-specific invariances. This paper asks whether corrective labels can be generated using only expert demonstrations and the physical property that system dynamics exhibit local continuity (small state/action changes yield small transition changes).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241406/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes expert demonstration trajectories as state-action-state triplets, where states range from low-dimensional vectors (pendulum, MuJoCo locomotion) to high-dimensional LiDAR sensor arrays (F1tenth racing). It produces synthetic corrective labels—pairs of out-of-distribution states and corrective actions that, when executed, bring the agent back to the expert data support. A rejection sampling step discards any generated label whose starting state deviates more than a threshold ε from the nearest expert state, directly controlling the theoretical error bound. The augmented dataset (original + corrective labels) is then used to train a standard behavior cloning policy via MSE regression.

## Method

CCIL trains a residual dynamics model (s_{t+1} = s_t + f(s_t, a_t)) from expert data while enforcing local Lipschitz continuity via a sampling-based penalty that estimates the local gradient by perturbing data points with Gaussian noise and penalizing violations of a Lipschitz bound L. Two label-generation techniques exploit this continuity: BackTrack solves for an alternative starting state s^G that reaches an expert state s* under the expert action, and DisturbedAction solves for a starting state that reaches the next expert state under a slightly perturbed action a*+Δ. Both are solved as root-finding problems using the Backward Euler iterative method. Theorems 4.2 and 4.3 provide explicit error bounds on the generated labels in terms of the Lipschitz constants K1, K2 (or KA, KS) and the model's training error ε, ensuring the labels remain high-quality within the local continuity neighborhood. The approach requires no interactive expert, no reward function, and no domain-specific invariance—only expert demonstrations and the physical assumption of local continuity.

## Results

Across 14 tasks in 4 robotic domains, CCIL achieves the best performance on 10 out of 14 tasks. On F1tenth LiDAR racing, CCIL reaches 56.4% success rate and 0.75 average score, substantially outperforming BC (31.9%, 0.58), NoisyBC (39.3%, 0.62), MILO (0.0%, 0.21), and MOREL (0.0%, 0.001). On the 8 MuJoCo/MetaWorld tasks with noise disturbances, CCIL leads on 4 tasks (Hopper: 2631 vs BC 1984; HalfCheetah: 8894 vs BC 1799; Walker: 3538 vs BC 1923; CoffeePull: 4168 vs BC 3553) and remains comparable to BC on the remaining 4. On drone navigation, CCIL improves over BC by 27–35% in cumulative reward across all three tasks (e.g., FlyThrough: −0.78E8 vs BC −1.06E8).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CCIL is compared against four baselines: standard Behavior Cloning (BC), NoisyBC (noise-based augmentation), MOREL (model-based offline RL requiring a reward function), and MILO (dynamics-based covariate shift mitigation). CCIL decisively outperforms MOREL and MILO across nearly all tasks; both model-based baselines collapse to near-zero scores on F1tenth (0% success) and perform poorly on MuJoCo/MetaWorld, likely because they require abundant data or reward access that is unavailable in the IL setting. Against BC, CCIL achieves clear wins on Hopper (+33%), HalfCheetah (+394%), Walker (+84%), CoffeePull (+17%), F1tenth (+24.5 pp success rate), and all three drone tasks (27–35% reward improvement), while remaining comparable on Ant, ButtonPress, CoffeePush, and DrawerClose. Against NoisyBC, CCIL wins on Hopper (+68%), HalfCheetah (+336%), F1tenth (+17.1 pp), and all drone tasks, but NoisyBC edges ahead on Ant and DrawerClose. Overall, CCIL is the best or tied-best method on 10 of 14 tasks, demonstrating that continuity-based corrective label generation is a broadly effective and assumption-light augmentation strategy for imitation learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

CCIL extends the behavior cloning paradigm by augmenting the training set with synthetic corrective labels, departing from prior robustness methods (DAGGER, DART, AggrevateD) that require an interactive expert, or model-based approaches (MILO, MOREL) that demand abundant offline data or a reward function. The key technical lineage draws on Gulrajani et al.'s (2017) sampling-based gradient penalty for local Lipschitz enforcement and Shi et al.'s (2019) spectral normalization for globally Lipschitz dynamics, but CCIL deliberately relaxes the global constraint to a local one to accommodate the hybrid continuous/discontinuous dynamics typical of robotics. Unlike Park & Wong (2022), which learns an inverse dynamics model for augmentation without theoretical grounding, CCIL provides explicit error bounds on generated label quality via local Lipschitz continuity assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CCIL offers a practical, assumption-light recipe for robustifying imitation learning that requires only expert demonstrations and the mild physical assumption of local continuity, making it broadly applicable across domains without domain-specific invariances or interactive feedback. The theoretical guarantees on label quality (Theorems 4.2, 4.3) and their empirical validation on the pendulum task provide a principled foundation absent in prior data-augmentation approaches. Key limitations include the method's restriction to simulation, the fact that corrective labels may confine the agent to a limited subset of expert-visited states (potentially causing task failure), and the open question of how to handle high-dimensional discontinuities such as pixel-based observations. Extending CCIL to real-world robotics and quantifying local continuity in a data-driven manner are identified as important future directions.
