## Problem

Most theoretical analyses of linear regression focus exclusively on L2 (Ridge) regularization, leaving other matrix-norm regularizations—despite their growing use in neural networks—without a unified optimality framework. This paper asks: what is the minimum-variance linear estimator when the bias operator is allowed to be nonzero but bounded under a Schatten norm, and how do different norm choices (nuclear, Frobenius, spectral) affect generalization error and loss-landscape geometry?

## Input / Output

The estimator takes a training matrix X ∈ R^{N×d}, target vector Y ∈ R^{N×1}, and a bias bound C ≥ 0 (equivalently a regularization parameter α ≥ 0 determined by C via norm-specific formulas). It produces a coefficient vector β̂ = L_p(X)Y and predictions ŷ = ⟨x_test, β̂⟩. The key structural constraint is that the bias operator B = LX − I_d must satisfy ‖B‖_p ≤ C for a chosen Schatten index p, and the estimator is the unique minimum-variance solution to this constrained optimization.

## Method

The paper proves an extended Gauss-Markov theorem showing that for any Schatten p-norm constraint, the optimal estimator has the form L_p(X) = G̃^{-1}X^T where G̃ is simultaneously diagonalizable with G = X^TX and satisfies G ≼ G̃. For the three key cases, G̃ takes especially simple forms: U diag(max(σ_i, α)) U^T for nuclear (p=1), G + αI for Frobenius/ridge (p=2), and (1+α)G for spectral (p=∞). The paper then derives closed-form test error expressions in the thermodynamic limit for both spherical Gaussian and diagonal (arbitrary-spectrum) random matrix ensembles, revealing that nuclear regression attains test error nearly as low as ridge but with substantially flatter minima. This flatness advantage is shown to translate into practical gains under cross-validation, where noisy estimation of the optimal α penalizes sharp minima more heavily.

## Results

In the spherical Gaussian ensemble, the Nuclear estimator's minimum test error is within 0.1–12.7% of Ridge's minimum (depending on σ and λ), while its curvature at the minimum is 3–54% *lower* (flatter) than Ridge's. For the Diagonal ensemble with power-law spectra, the Nuclear minimum error gap ranges from 0.7–12.3% with curvature reductions up to 87%. In cross-validation experiments with Gaussian predictors (N=100, 3-fold CV, 9 α values), Nuclear and Ridge are essentially matched on average test error, but Nuclear is overwhelmingly the most likely winner on any individual dataset. On Random Fourier Features data, cross-validated Nuclear outperforms cross-validated Ridge, with the advantage growing for larger noise σ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimators derived from the extended Gauss-Markov theorem: Nuclear (p=1), Ridge/Frobenius (p=2), and Spectral (p=∞). Ridge always attains the lowest theoretical minimum test error, but its minima are sharp (high curvature). Nuclear achieves nearly the same minimum depth (within ~1–13% depending on regime) while having substantially flatter minima (curvature 3–54% lower in the Gaussian case, up to 87% lower in the Diagonal case). Spectral also has flatter minima than Ridge but with a less favorable depth tradeoff (e.g., 26–61% higher minimum error at σ=0.25–0.5 in the Diagonal case). In the practical cross-validation setting (3-fold, 9 α values, N=100), Nuclear and Ridge are matched on average test error over Gaussian predictors, but Nuclear wins on the majority of individual datasets due to its flatter basin. On Random Fourier Features data, cross-validated Nuclear clearly outperforms cross-validated Ridge, with the gap widening as noise σ increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical Gauss-Markov theorem, which characterizes the minimum-variance unbiased linear estimator, by relaxing the zero-bias constraint to a bounded-bias constraint under Schatten matrix norms. The resulting optimal estimators for p=1 (Nuclear), p=2 (Frobenius/Ridge), and p=∞ (Spectral) connect to well-known methods: Principal Components Regression, Ridge regression, and Stein shrinkage respectively, but are unified under a single variational principle. The analytical treatment of generalization error draws on random matrix theory (Marchenko-Pastur ensembles) in the style of high-dimensional regression analyses by Mei & Montanari and Dobriban & Wager, while the flat-minima perspective links to the neural network generalization literature (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, Baldassi et al.).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical insight—that a theoretically suboptimal estimator (Nuclear) can outperform the oracle-optimal one (Ridge) under noisy hyperparameter selection due to flatter loss basins—provides a principled explanation for when non-L2 regularizations might be preferred in practice. This could inform the design of regularization schemes in neural networks where rank or spectral constraints are already explored (e.g., spectral norm regularization, low-rank training). Limitations include the restriction to linear models and specific random matrix ensembles; open questions include extending the analysis to kernelized or overparametrized regimes, other Schatten p values, and whether the flat-minima advantage persists in more realistic, non-synthetic settings.
