## Problem

Standard equivariant GNNs must assign identical outputs to structurally similar nodes (those in the same graph orbit), making them fundamentally unable to solve symmetry-breaking tasks such as optimizing molecular lipophilicity, where similar atoms must be differentiated. The paper introduces orbit-equivariance—a relaxation that permits different outputs within an orbit while preserving the inductive bias that graph reordering should not affect the multiset of outputs per orbit—and positions it formally between equivariant and universal node-labelling functions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240204/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a labelled molecular graph with per-node feature vectors (e.g., atom types) and produces a per-node categorical label, but the output is semantically structured as a multiset of labels per orbit rather than a fixed node-to-label assignment. Because nodes within an orbit are fundamentally interchangeable, the model's correctness is evaluated by comparing multisets of predicted and target labels per orbit (via multiset intersection), not by checking individual node assignments. The Bioisostere task requires max-orbit(f)=2 (at most two distinct labels per orbit), while Alchemy-Max-Orbit-m requires max-orbit(f)=m.

## Method

The paper proposes two novel GNN architectures built on a standard GCN backbone: Orbit-Indiv-GNN applies the GNN, identifies orbits via orbit-1-WL, appends a unique sequential ID to each node within its orbit, and passes the augmented labels through a shared MLP; m-Orbit-Transform-GNN instead has the GNN output m partitions of (o+1) channels per node, then a deterministic transform selects which partition overrides the default label for each node in an orbit based on argmax over the final channel. Both architectures are provably orbit-equivariant on graphs whose orbits are distinguishable by orbit-1-WL, with max-orbit bounded by m for the transform variant. A novel orbit-sorting cross-entropy loss greedily pairs sorted model outputs with sorted targets within each orbit to handle the multiset nature of the prediction. The paper also establishes the expressivity hierarchy of all four architectures (Unique-ID, RNI, Orbit-Indiv, m-Orbit-Transform) relative to orbit-equivariance and max-orbit.

## Results

Orbit-Indiv-GCN achieves the best performance across all three datasets and all three metrics (graph, orbit, and node accuracy). On Bioisostere, it reaches 69.9% graph accuracy, 95.4% orbit accuracy, and 96.3% node accuracy. On Alchemy-Max-Orbit-6, it achieves 83.4% graph accuracy and 97.1% node accuracy, substantially outperforming all other models. On Alchemy-Max-Orbit-2, it reaches 51.9% graph accuracy, nearly doubling the 20% of Unique-ID-GCN and the 0% of RNI-GCN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Orbit-Indiv-GCN (the paper's primary novel architecture) decisively outperforms all baselines on every dataset and metric. Against Unique-ID-GCN [Dasoulas et al., 2020], Orbit-Indiv-GCN gains +33.9 pp graph accuracy on Alchemy-Max-Orbit-2 (51.9% vs. 20.0%) and +16.6 pp on Alchemy-Max-Orbit-6 (83.4% vs. 66.8%), while also leading on Bioisostere (69.9% vs. 66.1%). Against RNI-GCN [Abboud et al., 2021], the gap is even wider: RNI-GCN achieves 0% graph accuracy on Alchemy-Max-Orbit-2 (it is equivariant in expectation and cannot break symmetry deterministically), and trails by 38.5 pp on Alchemy-Max-Orbit-6. The equivariant GCN baseline [Kipf & Welling, 2016] scores 52.4% graph accuracy on Bioisostere but would score 0% on both Alchemy-Max-Orbit datasets, confirming that equivariant functions fundamentally cannot solve these tasks. The 2-Orbit-Transform-GCN performs comparably to Orbit-Indiv-GCN on Alchemy-Max-Orbit-2 (47.9% vs. 51.9% graph accuracy) but degrades severely at m=6 (10.6% graph accuracy), demonstrating that the transform mechanism becomes infeasible for larger max-orbit values.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GNN expressivity literature by introducing orbit-equivariance as a principled property strictly between equivariance and universality, building on the well-established equivalence between GNNs and 1-WL (Morris et al., 2019; Weisfeiler & Leman, 1968). Prior non-equivariant GNN extensions (Unique-ID-GNN, RNI-GNN) were designed to break equivariance for expressiveness gains, but the paper argues they lack the structural inductive bias needed for symmetry-breaking tasks. The two novel architectures (Orbit-Indiv-GNN, m-Orbit-Transform-GNN) repurpose orbit-1-WL—a variant of the 1-WL coloring algorithm—to explicitly identify orbits and assign differentiated labels within them, using GCNs (Kipf & Welling, 2016) as the base message-passing component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The orbit-equivariance property provides a theoretically grounded middle ground that preserves the key inductive bias ('graph reordering should not affect outcomes') while enabling the differentiation of structurally identical nodes—something equivariant GNNs fundamentally cannot do. The Bioisostere dataset connects this to a real drug-design use case (lipophilicity optimization), demonstrating practical relevance. Key limitations include the reliance on orbit-1-WL (which is sound but not complete, failing on some regular graphs), the restriction to non-individualized nodes, and the small experimental scale (≤1000 graphs). Open questions include generalizing orbit-equivariance beyond graphs, developing more expressive orbit-identification algorithms, and extending to multi-property molecular optimization.
