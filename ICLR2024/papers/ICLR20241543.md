## Problem

State-of-the-art graph contrastive learning methods require large hidden dimensions to learn full-precision representations, making them memory-inefficient, storage-excessive, and energy-prohibitive for resource-constrained edge deployment. Spiking neural networks (SNNs) offer sparse, binary spike-based communication that is naturally suited for compact representations, yet their potential in graph contrastive learning has remained largely unexplored. This paper asks whether SNNs can be integrated into GCL to learn 1-bit graph representations that are both efficient and expressive.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241543/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an attributed undirected graph G=(V,E,X) with N nodes and d-dimensional feature matrix X∈R^{N×d}. Node features are partitioned into T non-overlapping groups along the feature dimension (each of size d/T), forming T subgraphs that share the same topology but differ in features. The output is a binarized representation Z∈{0,1}^{N×d} constructed by concatenating the spike trains S^1||...||S^T emitted by a single spiking neuron layer, achieving ~32× storage compression over 32-bit floating-point representations.

## Method

SPIKEGCL augments a standard GCL pipeline with SNN-based binarization: T peer GCN encoders (with parameter sharing across layers 2–L to control complexity) process each feature group, and a PLIF spiking neuron converts the continuous hidden representations into 1-bit spikes. The contrastive objective is a margin ranking loss applied in continuous space via a single-layer MLP projection head that uses masked summation to exploit spike sparsity. A key training innovation is blockwise surrogate gradient learning, which limits backpropagation to a single block of time steps with stop-gradient to prevent the vanishing gradient problem that plagues end-to-end SNN training. The paper provides a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 2) showing that the firing-rate approximation error of SPIKEGCL relative to a full-precision GCN is O(1/T), establishing that sufficiently large T recovers full-precision expressiveness at a fraction of the computational and energy cost.

## Results

SPIKEGCL achieves 88.9% on Amazon-Computer, 93.0% on Amazon-Photo, 92.8% on Coauthor-CS, 95.2% on Coauthor-Physics, 70.9% on OGBN-arXiv, and 32.0% on OGBN-MAG (node classification accuracy), approximating 95–99% of the performance of the best full-precision GCL methods (e.g., BGRL, CCA-SSG) while producing 1-bit binarized node representations. This yields ~32× representation storage compression and up to ~7× lower theoretical energy consumption compared to full-precision GCL baselines. On the three citation datasets (Cora, CiteSeer, PubMed), SPIKEGCL reaches 87.4%, 77.6%, and 88.8% respectively, matching or exceeding the best full-precision contrastive baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against full-precision GCL methods, SPIKEGCL trails the strongest baselines (BGRL, CCA-SSG) by 1–2% on most datasets (e.g., 88.9% vs. 90.3% on Computers, 70.9% vs. 71.6% on arXiv), but it outperforms BGRL on MAG (32.0% vs. 31.1%) and is within 0.2% on Photo. Compared to supervised GNNs (GCN, GAT), SPIKEGCL wins on every dataset, e.g., 88.9% vs. 86.9% (GAT) on Computers and 32.0% vs. 30.5% on MAG. Against 1-bit GNN baselines (Bi-GCN, BinaryGNN, BANE), SPIKEGCL achieves substantially higher accuracy—up to 16.2% better than BANE on Computers and 4.7% better than BinaryGNN on arXiv—while remaining self-supervised. Versus graph SNNs (SpikingGCN, SpikeNet, GC-SNN, GA-SNN), SPIKEGCL is competitive on small-to-medium graphs (e.g., 92.8% vs. 93.4% for SpikeNet on CS) and uniquely scales to the large OGBN-arXiv and OGBN-MAG benchmarks where most SNN baselines either underperform (SpikingGCN: 55.8% on arXiv) or fail to run due to memory constraints. Overall, SPIKEGCL demonstrates that 1-bit binarized representations can match or exceed full-precision GCL at a fraction of the storage and energy cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPIKEGCL sits at the intersection of three research threads: graph contrastive learning (DGI, BGRL, CCA-SSG, GRACE), spiking neural networks applied to graphs (SpikingGCN, SpikeNet, GC-SNN/GA-SNN), and binarized graph representation learning (Bi-GCN, BinaryGNN, BANE). It departs from prior SNN+GNN work by replacing the standard graph-repetition strategy for constructing sequential inputs with a feature-partitioning scheme, and by adopting a self-supervised contrastive objective rather than supervised training. The blockwise training paradigm draws on recent local-learning ideas from Siddiqui et al. (2023) to circumvent the vanishing-gradient problem that plagues end-to-end surrogate-gradient training of deep SNNs, while the theoretical guarantee (Theorem 2) formally connects IF-neuron firing rates to GCN outputs under bounded-degree and bounded-norm assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in the ~32× representation storage compression and ~7× energy reduction while retaining 95–99% of full-precision GCL accuracy, making graph representation learning viable on resource-constrained edge and neuromorphic hardware. Limitations include reliance on linear evaluation (which may understate representation quality for non-linear downstream tasks), the theoretical guarantee requiring reset-by-subtraction (not reset-to-zero), and the absence of experiments on truly large-scale graphs (N ≥ 10⁶). Open questions include whether the approximation bound Θ(1/T) tightens with alternative GNN backbones (e.g., GraphSAGE removes the exponential-in-degree term), whether blockwise training generalizes to multi-hop or dynamic graph settings, and how SPIKEGCL performs when deployed on actual neuromorphic chips rather than estimated via SOP counting.
