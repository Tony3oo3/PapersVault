## Problem

State-of-the-art LLMs suffer from high end-to-end generation latency, largely due to the sequential decoding approach that generates tokens one by one. While prior work has addressed model size and attention complexity, the sequential decoding bottleneck has been tackled only through model- or system-level changes (speculative decoding, non-autoregressive generation). This paper questions whether fully sequential decoding is necessary and explores a data-level pathway: can prompting alone reorganize LLM output to enable parallel generation without any model, system, or hardware modifications?

## Input / Output

The method takes a single user question as input and produces a structured text answer assembled from independently expanded skeleton points. The skeleton stage outputs 3–10 ultra-short points (3–5 words each), and the point-expanding stage produces 1–2 sentences per point, which are concatenated into the final answer. For open-source models, point-expanding requests are processed as a single batch (left-padded); for API-based models, they are issued as parallel API calls. The SoT-R variant prepends a routing decision (via GPT-4 prompting or a fine-tuned RoBERTa classifier) that either triggers the two-stage SoT pipeline or falls back to standard sequential generation.

## Method

SoT is a two-stage prompting technique that exploits the I/O-bound nature of LLM decoding (where weight loading, not computation, is the bottleneck) to achieve parallel generation. In the skeleton stage, a prompt template instructs the LLM to output a concise numbered list of 3–10 short points (3–5 words each) outlining the answer structure. In the point-expanding stage, each skeleton point is expanded independently via a separate prompt requesting 1–2 sentences of content, and all B expansions are executed in parallel—either as a batched decoding call (open-source models) or as concurrent API calls (proprietary models). Because decoding is bottlenecked by loading model weights rather than activation computation, increasing the batch size from 1 to B yields roughly a B× speedup in the decoding phase with minimal additional per-token latency. The SoT-R extension adds a lightweight router (a GPT-4 prompt or a 120M-parameter RoBERTa fine-tuned on LIMA with Tversky loss) that classifies whether a question is suitable for parallel skeleton-based generation or requires sequential step-by-step reasoning, falling back to normal decoding when SoT is inappropriate.

## Results

SoT achieves up to 2.39× end-to-end speed-up on 8 out of 12 evaluated LLMs, with the example question in Fig. 1 reduced from 22s to 12s (1.83×) on Claude and from 43s to 16s (2.69×) on Vicuna-33B V1.3. On the five question categories where SoT maintains high answer quality (knowledge, generic, common-sense, roleplay, counterfactual), speed-ups range from 1.89× to 2.33×. In terms of answer quality, SoT is not worse than normal sequential generation in approximately 60% of cases (evaluated via FastChat and LLMZoo with GPT-4 as judge), with net win rates varying by model (positive for Vicuna-13B V1.3, StableVicuna-13B, UltraLM-13B; negative for OpenChat-13B, Vicuna-7B V1.1, Claude, LLaMA2-Chat-13B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is standard sequential (autoregressive) decoding, which SoT outperforms on latency across all 12 models and all question categories. SoT achieves >2× speed-up on 8/12 models, with the largest gains on models that generate balanced, short point-expanding responses (e.g., LLaMA2, API-based models). On answer quality, SoT is competitive but not uniformly superior: it wins on diversity and relevance (LLMZoo metrics) while slightly hurting coherence and immersion, and it performs well on knowledge/generic/common-sense/roleplay/counterfactual questions but poorly on math, coding, writing, and fermi questions that require step-by-step reasoning. The SoT-R extension (with a GPT-4 prompting router or a fine-tuned RoBERTa router) addresses this by falling back to normal generation on unsuitable questions, significantly improving quality on coding/math/writing/fermi while preserving speed-ups on suitable categories. The paper does not provide direct numerical comparisons against speculative decoding or non-autoregressive generation methods, positioning SoT as a complementary data-level technique rather than a direct competitor to those model- or system-level approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

SoT carves out a new 'data-level' axis for LLM inference efficiency, distinct from the well-studied model-level (quantization, sparsification, architecture redesign) and system-level (FlashAttention, PagedAttention, batching/scheduling) techniques. It also departs from prior parallel-generation methods—speculative decoding (which relies on a smaller assisting model) and non-autoregressive generation (which requires modified architectures or special training)—by exploiting the LLM's own instruction-following and planning abilities through prompting alone, with no model or system changes. The paper draws a parallel to hierarchical text generation (Li et al., 2015; Fan et al., 2018) but distinguishes SoT by using explicit, free-form language planning rather than trained latent-variable modules, and by targeting efficiency rather than quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SoT introduces the first 'data-centric optimization for inference efficiency' paradigm, suggesting that as LLMs grow more capable at following structured instructions, organizing output content itself becomes a lever for speed. The practical impact is currently modest (up to ~2.4× speed-up, with mixed quality effects), and the method is fundamentally unsuitable for step-by-step reasoning tasks, though the SoT-R router mitigates this. Open questions include whether Graph-of-Thoughts with dynamic dependencies can capture inter-point reasoning, how SoT interacts with saturated serving workloads (throughput vs. latency trade-off), and whether the acceleration ratio can be made more predictable and controllable for production deployment.
