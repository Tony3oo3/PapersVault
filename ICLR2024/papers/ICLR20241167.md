## Problem

Offline preference-based RL (PbRL) with human feedback over trajectory pairs lacks provable algorithms under general function approximation and partial data coverage. Prior work (Zhu et al., 2023) was restricted to linear reward models with known transitions, and no prior result established whether trajectory-wise feedback is fundamentally harder to learn than step-wise reward feedback in the offline setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline dataset of N trajectory pairs (τ^n,0, τ^n,1) with binary preference labels o^n ∈ {0,1} generated under a Bradley-Terry-Luce-style link function, plus optionally known or unknown per-step transition kernels. It outputs a history-dependent policy π̂, an estimated trajectory-wise reward function r̂, and (in the unknown-transition variant) per-step transition estimates. A key structural constraint is that the reward is defined over entire trajectories rather than individual state-action pairs, and the algorithm requires a reference trajectory distribution μ_ref (typically set to the data distribution μ_1) to anchor the pessimistic objective.

## Method

FREEHAND operates in two stages: (1) it estimates the implicit reward via MLE over a general function class G_r and constructs a confidence set R(D) of reward models whose log-likelihood is within a slackness parameter ζ of the MLE; (2) it solves a distributionally robust planning problem that maximizes the worst-case policy value over R(D) minus a reference term E_{τ~μ_ref}[r(τ)]. The reference subtraction is essential because the confidence set captures uncertainty in reward *differences* between trajectory pairs, not absolute per-trajectory rewards. The analysis introduces a novel single-policy concentrability coefficient C_r tailored to preference feedback, which upper-bounds the per-trajectory concentrability C_tr and can be much smaller in practice (e.g., a relative condition number for linear models). Lower bounds prove that C_tr scaling is necessary and that per-step concentrability C_st is insufficient, establishing the first theoretical separation between standard offline RL and offline PbRL. The framework extends to unknown transitions (requiring only per-step transition estimation despite trajectory-wise rewards) and to action-based comparison via advantage-function estimation under a soft-margin assumption.

## Results

The main guarantee (Theorem 1) establishes that FREEHAND achieves suboptimality J(π_tar; r*, P*) − J(π̂; r*, P*) ≤ √(c C_r² κ² log(𝒩_𝒢_r(1/N)/δ) / N), yielding a sample complexity of Õ(C_r² κ² log(𝒩_𝒢_r(1/N)/δ) / ε²) for known transitions. For unknown transitions (Theorem 4), an additional term H·r_max·√(c C_P² log(H𝒩_P(1/N)/δ) / N) appears. In the action-based comparison setting under a hard margin (β=∞), the rate improves to Õ(1/ε) (Theorem 5). Lower bounds (Theorems 2–3) prove that the per-trajectory concentrability coefficient C_tr is necessary and cannot be replaced by the per-step coefficient C_st, establishing the first theoretical separation between offline PbRL and standard offline RL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FREEHAND is compared theoretically against three prior works. Against Zhu et al. (2023), the closest prior offline PbRL result, FREEHAND extends from linear rewards to general function approximation, handles unknown transitions (Zhu et al. assume known transitions), and provides partial-coverage guarantees for action-based preferences (Zhu et al. cannot). In the linear-reward special case, FREEHAND's guarantee recovers Zhu et al.'s sample complexity Õ(C_lin² exp(4BR) d log(1/δ) / ε²) up to log factors. Against Uehara and Sun (2021), which addresses standard offline RL with observed per-step rewards, FREEHAND's confidence set is constructed via KL-based MLE rather than TV-distance balls, and the reference-term subtraction serves a fundamentally different purpose (handling trajectory-wise reward uncertainty vs. robustness). Against Cheng et al. (2022), the shared reference-policy subtraction is superficially similar but motivated differently: FREEHAND subtracts because only reward *differences* are observable, while Cheng et al. subtract for adversarial robustness. The paper also provides the first lower-bound separation showing that trajectory-wise feedback is intrinsically harder than step-wise feedback in the offline setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the offline PbRL framework of Zhu et al. (2023) from linear reward models to general function approximation and from known to unknown transition dynamics, while also extending the pessimistic model-based offline RL paradigm of Uehara and Sun (2021) from explicit per-step rewards to trajectory-level preference feedback. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a per-trajectory concentrability coefficient tailored to preference-based feedback, which the authors prove is fundamentally different from (and can be exponentially larger than) the per-step concentrability used in standard offline RL. The algorithm FREEHAND combines MLE-based confidence set construction with distributionally robust planning, a structure inherited from pessimistic offline RL but adapted to the implicit-reward setting where only pairwise differences are observable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide sample complexity guarantees for offline PbRL with general function approximation under partial coverage, filling a gap that limited prior theory to linear models. The lower-bound separation result (Theorem 2) establishing that per-step concentrability is provably insufficient for trajectory-based PbRL is a clean theoretical contribution that clarifies why PbRL is intrinsically harder than standard RL in the offline regime. Open questions include the computational tractability of the distributionally robust planning step (acknowledged as hard in general), whether the per-trajectory concentrability requirement can be relaxed under additional reward structure, and whether the action-based comparison extension with soft margin can be further tightened. The lack of empirical validation limits immediate practical impact, though the Lagrangian relaxation in Appendix B suggests a feasible implementation path.
