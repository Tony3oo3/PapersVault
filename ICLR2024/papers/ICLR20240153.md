## Problem

Existing federated learning fairness research addresses either global fairness (model disparity across all clients) or local fairness (disparity at each individual client) in isolation, leaving their interplay under data heterogeneity largely unexplored. It remains unclear when one form of fairness implies the other, what information-theoretic sources drive their disagreement, and what fundamental limits govern the simultaneous achievement of both alongside accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240153/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the joint distribution Pr(Z, S, Y) fixed by a specific dataset and its partition across K clients, together with fairness relaxation thresholds (ε_g, ε_l). It outputs a PID decomposition of disparities into three non-negative components (Unique, Redundant, Masked) and, via the AGLFOP, the Pareto frontier of maximum achievable accuracy for any given pair of fairness constraints. Crucially, the optimization operates at the distribution level—searching over all joint distributions Q on (Z, S, Y, Ŷ) that preserve the fixed (Z, S, Y) marginals—rather than over model parameters, thereby characterizing what any FL strategy could theoretically attain.

## Method

The paper formalizes Global Disparity as I(Z; Ŷ) and Local Disparity as I(Z; Ŷ|S), then applies Partial Information Decomposition (PID) to decompose both into three non-negative terms: Unique Disparity (shared by both), Redundant Disparity (contributing only to global), and Masked Disparity (contributing only to local). This decomposition yields fundamental impossibility results—local fairness cannot eliminate global disparity when Redundant Disparity is positive (Theorem 1), and global fairness cannot guarantee local fairness when Masked Disparity is positive (Theorem 2)—along with necessary and sufficient conditions for when one implies the other (Theorems 3–4). The Accuracy and Global-Local Fairness Optimality Problem (AGLFOP) is formulated as a convex optimization over the probability simplex of joint distributions, minimizing classification error subject to mutual information constraints on both global and local disparity, with convexity established via the log-sum inequality. Experiments validate the theory using FedAvg on synthetic and Adult datasets under controlled heterogeneity (α) and synergy (λ) scenarios, confirming that naive local fairness regularizers can exacerbate global disparity when Redundant or Masked Disparities dominate.

## Results

On the Adult dataset (gender as sensitive attribute, FedAvg training), the PID decomposition reveals three distinct disparity regimes: under i.i.d. splitting (Scenario 1), Global and Local Disparity are both 0.0359 bits (pure Unique Disparity); under high heterogeneity α=0.9 (Scenario 2), Global Disparity is 0.0431 bits while Local Disparity drops to 0.0014 bits (dominated by Redundant Disparity); under high synergy λ=0.9 (Scenario 3), Local Disparity surges to 0.1761 bits while Global Disparity is only 0.0317 bits (dominated by Masked Disparity). Model accuracy remains in the 84–87% range across all scenarios. A naive local statistical-parity regularizer reduces disparity only in Scenario 1 (0.0359→0.0062 bits) but *increases* both Global and Local Disparity in Scenarios 2 and 3, confirming the theoretical impossibility results.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark a new model against competing fairness methods; instead, it validates theoretical PID predictions against empirical FedAvg training and evaluates a single baseline intervention (a local statistical-parity regularizer from FairTorch). The key comparison is between the three data-splitting scenarios, which isolate each PID term: Scenario 1 (i.i.d.) yields pure Unique Disparity where local and global fairness agree, Scenario 2 (α=0.9 heterogeneity) yields pure Redundant Disparity where the model is locally fair (0.0014 bits) but globally unfair (0.0431 bits), and Scenario 3 (λ=0.9 synergy) yields pure Masked Disparity where the model is globally fair (0.0317 bits) but locally very unfair (0.1761 bits). The local fairness regularizer successfully reduces disparity only in Scenario 1 (0.0359→0.0062 bits) but *worsens* both Global (0.0431→0.0626) and Local (0.0014→0.0110) Disparity in Scenario 2, and only partially reduces Local Disparity in Scenario 3 (0.1761→0.0935) while slightly increasing Global Disparity (0.0317→0.0418). This empirically confirms Theorem 1 (local fairness cannot eliminate global unfairness when Redundant Disparity > 0) and Theorem 2 (global fairness does not imply local fairness when Masked Disparity > 0). The AGLFOP Pareto frontiers further show that under high heterogeneity, achieving both ε_g=0 and ε_l=0 caps accuracy at ~64%, illustrating the fundamental trade-off boundary.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Partial Information Decomposition (PID) framework, specifically the Bertschinger et al. (2014) definition of Unique Information, to decompose fairness disparities in federated learning. It extends the information-theoretic fairness literature (Kamishima et al. 2011; Calmon et al. 2017; Dutta et al. 2020b) from centralized settings to FL, where prior work has addressed either global fairness (Ezzeldin et al. 2023; Du et al. 2021) or local fairness (Cui et al. 2021) in isolation. The key departure is using PID to simultaneously characterize both global and local disparity, identifying three non-negative components (Unique, Redundant, Masked) that precisely delineate where the two notions agree or disagree under data heterogeneity. The AGLFOP convex optimization is novel, extending accuracy-fairness tradeoff analysis (Chen et al. 2018; Dutta et al. 2020a) to the joint global-local setting in FL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first information-theoretic characterization of when local fairness implies global fairness (and vice versa) in FL, with the conditions expressed as specific PID terms being zero. This has direct practical implications: the finding that naive local fairness regularization can worsen global disparity under high heterogeneity (Table 1) cautions practitioners against deploying client-level mitigation without understanding the data distribution. Limitations include the restriction to binary sensitive attributes and binary classification, the computational cost of PID estimation (solving a convex program for Unique Information), and empirical validation limited to a single real dataset (Adult) with modest disparity magnitudes. Open questions include extension to multi-group fairness, multi-class settings, and whether the PID-based conditions can be efficiently estimated or enforced in practice at scale.
