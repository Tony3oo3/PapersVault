## Problem

Existing symbolic regression methods struggle to recover lengthy, complex mathematical equations from scarce data, suffering from overfitting and poor generalizability when the search space of candidate expressions grows toward infinity. The core gap is that current approaches lack a principled mechanism to balance global exploration of the expression tree space with local exploitation of promising sub-structures, and they cannot decompose intricate equations into manageable sub-problems on the fly.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242127/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular (x, y) data pairs (typically 20–500 points) along with a fixed set of candidate operators (+, −, ×, ÷, log, exp, sin, cos, sqrt) and produces a symbolic expression tree whose internal nodes are operators and leaf nodes are variables or learnable constants. Coefficient placeholders in the tree are filled via BFGS optimization initialized with unit-mean Gaussian random values. A preprocessing step uses cubic splines to detect odd/even parity of the underlying function, which constrains the search to half the domain and reduces expression complexity (e.g., recovering cosh(x) by only generating exp(x)).

## Method

RSRM integrates three modules in an iterative loop: (1) a Monte Carlo Tree Search agent augmented with Double Q-learning, where MCTS performs global exploration via UCT-based node selection while Double Q-learning provides local exploitation by estimating child-node values from simulated rewards (the tree is randomly completed at each step to compute a reward); (2) a genetic programming stage that refines the top expressions through mutation and crossover; and (3) a Modulated Sub-Tree Discovery Block (MSDB) that inspects the best-performing expressions, splits them by addition/subtraction, and identifies recurring sub-tree patterns to define new composite operators of the form A+f(x), A×f(x), or A^f(x), effectively implementing a divide-and-conquer strategy for long equations. The expression tree is built hierarchically (layer by layer) rather than depth-first, and a reward function combining RMSE with a length discount factor η^l encourages both accuracy and parsimony. Greedy selection (choosing the token with the highest current reward) is used at the top level, while UCT governs selection during the MCTS simulation phase, and Double Q-learning is only activated after each child node has been visited at least n₀=3 times to avoid over-reliance on sparse history.

## Results

RSRM achieves 100% recovery rate on all 12 Nguyen equations (both standard and parametric), outperforming SPL (93.50%), uDSR (87.58%), NGGP (91.75%), DSR (83.58%), and GP (44.42%). On the more challenging Livermore benchmark, RSRM reaches 97.95% average recovery versus SPL (51.00%), uDSR (81.05%), and NGGP (80.68%). On the R rational benchmark where most baselines fail entirely, RSRM achieves 68.83% while SPL and DSR score 0%. On AIFeynman, RSRM attains 73.50% average recovery, surpassing SPL (66.42%), uDSR (63.50%), and NGGP (67.25%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RSRM dominates SPL (Sun et al., 2023) across nearly all benchmarks: on Nguyen-12, RSRM recovers the expression 100% of the time versus SPL's 28%; on Livermore-7 (sinh), RSRM achieves 100% versus SPL's 18%; and on the entire R rational set, RSRM scores 68.83% while SPL scores 0%. Against uDSR (Landajuela et al., 2022), RSRM wins on Nguyen-12 (100% vs 30%), Nguyen-9c (100% vs 0%), Livermore-7 (100% vs 0%), and all R rational equations (68.83% vs 16.50%), though uDSR matches RSRM on Nguyen-2c (both 100%) and slightly edges it on Livermore-16 (60% vs 100%—actually RSRM wins here too). Against NGGP (Mundhenk et al., 2021a), RSRM achieves substantially higher recovery on complex trigonometric and rational expressions (e.g., Livermore-3: 55% vs 2%; R-20: 89% vs 0%), while NGGP is competitive on simpler parametric polynomials (Nguyen-c average: 99.14% vs RSRM's 100%). DSR (Petersen et al., 2019) and GP (gplearn) are consistently the weakest baselines, with DSR scoring 0% on Nguyen-12, all R rational equations, and AIFeynman-9/10, and GP scoring 0% on most non-trivial expressions. The largest absolute gaps appear on equations requiring the MSDB module (e.g., AIFeynman-9: 67% vs 0% for SPL) and on rational functions where RSRM's form-discovery is critical (R-30: 91% vs 0% for SPL/DSR/uDSR).

## Relation to Prior Work

RSRM sits at the intersection of three established symbolic regression lineages: MCTS-based search (Coulom 2006; SPL/Sun et al. 2023), RL-based expression generation (DSR/Petersen et al. 2019; NGGP/Mundhenk et al. 2021; uDSR/Landajuela et al. 2022), and genetic programming (Koza 1994; gplearn/Stephens 2016). Its key departure is the tight coupling of Double Q-learning (Hasselt 2010) inside the MCTS simulation loop to provide local exploitation that complements MCTS's global exploration, plus the Modulated Sub-Tree Discovery (MSDB) block that heuristically decomposes long expressions into a fixed anchor plus a learnable residual. The reward function and greedy selection strategy are directly inherited from SPL, while the GP tuning step follows the NGGP paradigm of seeding a GP population with RL-generated expressions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MSDB divide-and-conquer strategy offers a practical recipe for tackling the long-equation bottleneck that plagues most SR methods, and the cubic-spline parity pre-processing dramatically reduces data requirements compared to AIFeynman's MLP-based approach. However, the method's expressiveness is constrained by only three fixed sub-tree forms (A+f, A×f, A^f), limiting adaptability to novel problem structures. Open questions include whether the form set can be learned end-to-end (e.g., via neural slot generation), whether prioritized experience replay or other Q-learning variants further improve exploitation, and whether the framework extends to multi-output or time-series equation discovery. The mixed results on the free-falling balls dataset (where RSRM occasionally underperforms SPL) also highlight that the approach is not uniformly superior in low-data parametric settings.
