## Problem

While process supervision (step-level feedback) is intuitively more precise than outcome supervision (final-answer feedback) for training reward models, prior work on grade-school math found comparable performance between the two. This paper investigates whether process supervision provides a meaningful advantage when scaled up with a more capable base model (GPT-4), substantially more human feedback, and the significantly harder MATH dataset, and whether active learning can further reduce the cost of collecting step-level labels.

## Input / Output

The reward model consumes a math problem followed by a newline-delimited chain-of-thought solution. The PRM emits a single-token correctness prediction (positive/negative/neutral) at each step boundary, which are combined via a product reduction into one solution-level score; the ORM instead reads a single score at the final token. At test time, the reward model ranks N uniformly sampled solutions and the top-ranked one is auto-graded by checking its final answer, yielding a best-of-N accuracy metric.

## Method

The PRM is fine-tuned from a MathMix-pretrained GPT-4 to predict step-level correctness labels, trained on PRM800K (800K human step labels across 75K solutions to 12K MATH problems). Supervision is deliberately limited to the first incorrect step per solution, making the information advantage over outcome supervision precisely the location of the first error. A key data-collection strategy is active learning: the current best PRM scores all generated solutions, and only the top-K 'convincing wrong-answer' solutions (high PRM score but incorrect final answer) are surfaced to human labelers, yielding a 2.6× data-efficiency gain over uniform sampling. For controlled ablations, the large-scale PRM serves as a synthetic oracle to label solutions for smaller models, isolating the effect of supervision type from dataset composition and grading noise.

## Results

On a 500-problem subset of the MATH test set, the process-supervised reward model (PRM) achieves 78.2% best-of-1860 accuracy, substantially outperforming both the outcome-supervised reward model (ORM) and majority voting across all values of N, with the gap widening as N increases. On out-of-distribution STEM benchmarks (AP Calculus, AP Chemistry, AP Physics, AMC10/12), the PRM achieves 72.9% aggregate best-of-100 accuracy versus 63.8% for the ORM and 61.3% for majority voting. Active learning with convincing wrong-answer selection yields a 2.6× improvement in data efficiency for process supervision compared to uniform sampling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The PRM consistently outperforms majority voting (self-consistency, Wang et al. 2022) on every benchmark: by 11.9 points on AP Calculus (86.7% vs 80.0%), 8.3 points on AP Chemistry (80.0% vs 71.7%), 4.5 points on AP Physics (86.7% vs 82.2%), and 20.4 points on AMC10/12 (53.2% vs 32.8%). Against the ORM baseline, the PRM gains 9.1 points aggregate on OOD benchmarks (72.9% vs 63.8%) and a larger margin on the in-distribution MATH test set, where the PRM reaches 78.2% while the ORM only marginally exceeds majority voting. The advantage of process supervision over outcome supervision is most pronounced on harder problems and at larger N, indicating the PRM is more effective at searching over many candidate solutions. On the easiest MATH quintile, the ORM's performance actually degrades with more samples (suggesting adversarial examples fool it), while the PRM remains robust.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the outcome-vs-process supervision comparison initiated by Uesato et al. (2022) from the easier GSM8K dataset to the significantly harder MATH benchmark, using a more capable GPT-4 base model and an order of magnitude more human feedback (800K step-level labels). The ORM training methodology follows Cobbe et al. (2021), while the PRM formulation is a natural extension that adds step-level supervision. The paper also builds on the broader chain-of-thought reasoning literature (Wei et al., Nye et al., Kojima et al.) and the RLHF reward-model pipeline, but its core contribution is a controlled, large-scale empirical comparison of the two supervision paradigms that prior work had not adequately resolved.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision on hard mathematical reasoning (78.2% vs. ~65% best-of-N) reverses the near-parity reported by Uesato et al. on easier problems, providing strong practical guidance for reward model training. The release of PRM800K removes a major barrier to entry for process supervision research, and the 2.6× data-efficiency gain from active learning directly reduces the cost of scaling human feedback. Open questions include whether these results generalize beyond mathematics, how to resolve the instability observed in iterative active learning, and how many distinct training problems are needed before overfitting dominates.
