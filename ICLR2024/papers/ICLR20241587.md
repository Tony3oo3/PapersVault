## Problem

Large-scale systems neuroscience experiments now produce multimodal data (cellular-resolution spiking, visual stimuli, behavior) that existing tools handle poorly: GLMs require strong inductive biases and struggle with multiple modalities simultaneously, while autoencoder-based latent models impose fixed low-dimensional structure. No general-purpose, multimodal, multitask framework existed that could flexibly incorporate arbitrary modalities and adapt to diverse downstream tasks without architectural changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241587/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes spike trains tokenized as neuron-ID tokens with sub-interval time bins, split into a Current State window (w_c = 50 ms) and a Past State window (w_p = 150–250 ms), alongside video frames processed by a 3D convolution into spatial patches and behavioral signals (speed, eye position) at 50 ms resolution. Because each temporal window contains a variable number of spikes, the model employs block-sparse causal masking with right-padding rather than a fixed-length token sequence. Outputs include autoregressively generated spike trains (neuron ID + dt tokens sampled via nucleus sampling), continuous behavior predictions via a linear head on the mean-pooled final layer, and attention matrices that reveal circuit connectivity or stimulus-response spatial dependencies.

## Method

Neuroformer reframes population spike inference as a causally-masked, spatiotemporal autoregressive generation problem analogous to GPT. The architecture has three stages: (1) a CLIP-style multimodal contrastive module aligns neural, visual, and behavioral embeddings in a shared latent space; (2) a Perceiver-style cross-attention fusion module lets the small Current State array (length T_c) iteratively attend to larger feature arrays (Past State spikes, video patches of length T_f), reducing attention complexity from O(T_f²) to O(T_c·T_f) and enabling linear scaling with feature size; (3) a causally-masked transformer decoder autoregressively predicts neuron-ID and time-interval tokens. Training is fully self-supervised, jointly optimizing the contrastive alignment loss and cross-entropy losses for both token types. Downstream tasks (e.g., speed prediction) are addressed by projecting the mean-pooled final-layer representation onto a task-specific head, enabling few-shot fine-tuning without architectural modification.

## Results

On the Visnav behavior prediction task, Neuroformer achieves a Pearson correlation of r = 0.97 on both the Lateral (2022 neurons) and Medial (1905 neurons) datasets, substantially outperforming all baselines (best baseline: Bidirectional GRU at 0.88). In the few-shot regime, a pretrained Neuroformer finetuned on only 1% of speed data (r = 0.51) outperforms a randomly-initialized model finetuned on 10% of the data (r = 0.33). On the V1+AL population response prediction task, Neuroformer's trial-averaged predictions are significantly more correlated with ground truth than a GLM (t-test, p = 0.0196). On the simulated Hub-net dataset, attention-weighted connectivity analysis recovers the three hub neurons (IDs 50, 150, 200) with directional information that Pearson correlation cannot provide.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Neuroformer outperforms all five baselines on the behavior prediction task by a wide margin: it exceeds the strongest baseline (Bidirectional GRU, r = 0.88 medial) by 0.09 and the GLM (r = 0.81 medial) by 0.16. On the V1+AL population response prediction, Neuroformer also beats the GLM, with trial-averaged predictions showing significantly higher correlation to ground truth (p = 0.0196); the authors note that a single GLM could fit either gratings or naturalistic movies well but not both simultaneously, whereas Neuroformer handles both stimulus classes. The few-shot comparison further highlights the advantage of generative pretraining: a pretrained model using only 1% of finetuning data surpasses a non-pretrained model using 10× more data. The paper also attempted a comparison with CEBRA (a contrastive representation-learning method) but reports that CEBRA overfit heavily and failed to generalize, so no quantitative comparison is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

Neuroformer synthesizes three distinct architectural lineages into a single framework for neural data: the autoregressive GPT paradigm (Brown et al., 2020) for spike generation, Perceiver-style cross-attention (Jaegle et al., 2021) for efficient fusion of a small neural latent with large multimodal feature arrays, and CLIP-style contrastive alignment (Radford et al., 2021) for pre-fusion modality matching. It departs from the dominant neuroscience modeling tradition—GLMs with their Poisson/linear-kernel inductive biases (Pillow et al., 2008) and latent-dynamics models like LFADS (Sussillo et al., 2016) or CEBRA (Schneider et al., 2022)—by treating the problem as a modality-agnostic, self-supervised generative task rather than a domain-specific statistical inference. The block-sparse causal masking scheme is a practical adaptation of GPT-style decoding to the variable-length spike-train structure that has no direct precedent in prior neural modeling work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a generative pretraining objective on spike data can yield representations that support few-shot behavior decoding (1% pretrained outperforming 10% non-pretrained) and that attention weights can recover directional circuit connectivity invisible to correlation-based methods. However, the evidence base is limited: datasets are small (386–2022 neurons), the connectivity-recovery result is validated only on a simulated hub network, and the CEBRA comparison was inconclusive due to overfitting. Open questions include whether the approach scales to the millions of neurons available in modern recordings, whether the attention-as-connectivity signal generalizes beyond toy circuits, and how it compares to encoder-based latent methods (LFADS, CEBRA) under matched conditions. The paper positions itself as a first step toward 'foundation models of the brain,' but the current 40M–100M parameter scale is far from the regime where emergent capabilities have been observed in other domains.
