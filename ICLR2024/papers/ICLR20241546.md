## Problem

Pragmatic program synthesis via the Rational Speech Acts (RSA) framework resolves ambiguity in programming-by-example by modeling how users informatively choose examples, but requires counterfactual reasoning over the full space of programs and examples, which is intractable in realistic domains. The paper asks whether this expensive pragmatic inference can be amortized into neural network training data generation, enabling scalable pragmatic synthesis without human supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241546/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The listener (synthesizer) takes a linearized sequence of (string, boolean) example pairs and produces a ranked list of consistent regular expressions via top-p sampling (500 candidates, filtered for consistency, sorted by model score). The speaker takes a target regex plus previously generated examples and autoregressively generates the next example in the specification. During self-play, the speaker proposes candidate examples and the listener proposes distractor programs, which together form a sampled consistency matrix over which RSA inference selects the most informative example.

## Method

PRAX iteratively bootstraps pragmatic synthesizers through self-play between ByT5-small speaker and listener models. At each round, the current speaker samples candidate examples conditioned on the target program and partial specification, while the listener samples distractor programs conditioned on the partial specification; RSA inference over the resulting sampled consistency matrix selects the highest-scoring informative example to append to the specification. The accumulated (program, specification) pairs are then used to fine-tune both models from their base (literal) initialization, making the speaker increasingly pragmatic and the listener increasingly disambiguating. Crucially, RSA is applied at training time for data generation rather than at inference time, amortizing the expensive counterfactual computation into the learned model parameters. The approach requires no human-provided training data—only 40 human annotations for model selection—and matches the performance of a model fine-tuned on 400 human-written pragmatic examples.

## Results

On the regular expression synthesis task evaluated over 10 interaction turns with 11 human participants (340 regexes total), PRAX achieves 66.1% TOP-1@10 accuracy, outperforming the LITERAL baseline (43.4%) by 22.7 percentage points (51% relative increase) and the human-fine-tuned HFT model (58.7%) by 7.4 points. PRAX also leads on TOP-10@10 (70.3% vs. 62.3% for HFT) and EDIT DISTANCE≤1@10 (69.4% vs. 61.4% for HFT). Averaged across all turns, PRAX achieves 37.3% TOP-1, matching HFT (34.9%) and substantially exceeding LITERAL (23.3%), while GPT-3.5 in replay performs worst at 4.8% TOP-1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRAX is compared against three baselines: LITERAL (a ByT5-small model trained on randomly generated examples, approximating a literal listener), HFT (the same base model fine-tuned on 400 human-annotated pragmatic specifications), and GPT-3.5 (evaluated in replay mode). PRAX outperforms LITERAL by 22.7 points on TOP-1@10 (66.1% vs. 43.4%) and by 14.0 points on average TOP-1 across turns (37.3% vs. 23.3%), confirming that pragmatic training data substantially improves disambiguation. PRAX also surpasses HFT on all three metrics at turn 10 (66.1% vs. 58.7% TOP-1; 70.3% vs. 62.3% TOP-10; 69.4% vs. 61.4% edit distance), despite using zero human-provided examples during training—only 40 human annotations for model selection. GPT-3.5 performs dramatically worse than even the LITERAL baseline (7.4% vs. 43.4% TOP-1@10), likely due to distribution mismatch in its training data. The paper notes that PRAX matches HFT's performance trajectory across all turns (Figure 3), not merely catching up at the end, indicating the self-play-generated examples are as informative as human-written ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Pu et al. (2020), who showed that the Rational Speech Acts (RSA) framework enables pragmatic program synthesis by modeling how users choose informative examples, but whose exact inference is intractable in realistic program spaces. PRAX amortizes this expensive counterfactual reasoning by using RSA at training time to select informative examples from self-play between neural speaker and listener models (initialized in the style of Devlin et al., 2017 and Balog et al., 2017), rather than at inference time. Unlike prior pragmatic training approaches that use reinforcement learning (White et al., 2020; Lazaridou et al., 2020) or backpropagation through RSA (Monroe & Potts, 2015; McDowell & Goodman, 2019), PRAX requires no human-provided training data and treats RSA purely as a data-generation oracle. The paper also contrasts with Andreas & Klein (2016), who found amortized pragmatic reasoning inferior to explicit inference for image captioning, showing that amortization is effective in the program synthesis domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PRAX demonstrates that pragmatic reasoning—modeling how humans choose informative examples—can be internalized into neural synthesizers through self-generated data, matching the performance of models fine-tuned on 400 human-annotated specifications without using any human data during training. This suggests a generalizable recipe for bootstrapping pragmatic behavior in neural program synthesizers across domains. However, validation is limited to regular expression synthesis with a small user study (11 participants, 340 regexes), and the paper itself identifies scaling to open-ended Python code generation and multimodal specifications as key open questions. The narrow domain and small-scale evaluation leave open whether the self-play + RSA data generation strategy transfers to more complex program spaces where the consistency matrix is harder to approximate.
