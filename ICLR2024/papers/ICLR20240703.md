## Problem

Most neural graph generative models output full adjacency matrices of size N², making them impractical for large-scale graphs. Edge list representations reduce the sequence length to M edges but inherit a vocabulary of N² possible node-index pairs, leading to poor generalization and even worse performance than adjacency-matrix baselines. The paper addresses both the representation size and vocabulary size bottlenecks simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a single BOS token and autoregressively emits a sequence of M gap-pair tokens (a_m, b_m), where each token is drawn from a vocabulary of size B² (B being the graph bandwidth under C-M ordering) rather than N². For attributed graphs, the sequence is interleaved with node-type and edge-type tokens according to a fixed grammar, yielding a total sequence length of at most 2M+N. The output is decoded back into an edge list (and optionally node/edge labels) by cumulative summation of the inter-edge gaps.

## Method

GEEL replaces absolute node indices in a sorted edge list with two gap encodings: the inter-edge gap a_m = s_m − s_{m−1} (difference between consecutive source nodes) and the intra-edge gap b_m = t_m − s_m (difference between source and target within an edge). A theoretical bound shows both gaps are at most B (the graph bandwidth), so the vocabulary shrinks from N² to B²; the Cuthill-McKee node ordering further minimizes B in practice. An LSTM backbone generates the gap-pair sequence with O(M) complexity, and a node positional encoding (the cumulative source index) is added to each token embedding so the model need not learn the running sum implicitly. For attributed graphs, a grammar enforces the order node-type → edge-tuple → edge-type, and invalid token choices are masked out during generation. Training maximizes a variational lower bound on the log-likelihood with the C-M ordering serving as the variational posterior over node orderings.

## Results

On ten general graph generation benchmarks, GEEL achieves superior or training-comparable MMD across all datasets, including 0.000/0.000/0.000 (Deg./Clus./Orb.) on Grid and 0.002/0.081/0.020 on 3D point cloud (up to 5,037 nodes), where most adjacency-matrix-based baselines (GraphVAE, GraphRNN, GDSS, DiGress) run out of memory. On molecular generation, GEEL achieves 100% validity and 0.089 FCD on QM9, and 99.31% validity and 0.401 FCD on ZINC250k, outperforming all domain-agnostic graph generative models. Inference time is 0.12 s per graph on Enzymes, roughly 2× faster than GRAN (0.24 s) and 127× faster than GDSS (15.21 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GEEL substantially outperforms both edge-list-based baselines: GraphGen (Goyal et al., 2020) and GraphGen-Redux (Bacciu & Podda, 2021) show MMD values 100–1000× worse (e.g., 1.762 vs. 0.001 on Planar degree), confirming that the raw N² vocabulary of edge lists is the bottleneck. Among adjacency-matrix-based methods, GEEL matches or beats BiGG (Dai et al., 2020) on all large-graph datasets and is the only method that avoids OOM on 3D point cloud (up to 5,037 nodes) where GraphRNN, GDSS, and DiGress all fail. GEEL also outperforms the bandwidth-restriction baseline BwR (Diamant et al., 2023) on every dataset where BwR produces valid samples, and BwR yields N.A. (all invalid) on Ego and SBM. On molecular generation (Table 4), GEEL surpasses all seven domain-agnostic models (EDP-GNN, GraphAF, GraphDF, GDSS, DiGress, DruM, GraphARM) on FCD and scaffold similarity, and is competitive with molecule-specific models: on QM9 it achieves 0.089 FCD (vs. 0.087 for CharRNN, 0.585 for STGG) and 0.9386 scaffold (vs. 0.9313 CharRNN, 0.9416 STGG), while on ZINC250k it reaches 0.401 FCD, far below DiGress (3.482) and DruM (2.257). Inference speed is also a clear advantage: GEEL generates one Enzymes graph in 0.12 s versus 0.24 s (GRAN), 2.70 s (BiGG), 8.82 s (DiGress), and 15.21 s (GDSS).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two complementary lines of work: edge list-based graph generation (Goyal et al., 2020; Bacciu & Podda, 2021), which reduced representation size to M but suffered from an N² vocabulary, and bandwidth restriction via Cuthill-McKee ordering (Diamant et al., 2023), which constrained adjacency matrix generation to NB elements. GEEL departs from both by introducing gap encoding that replaces absolute node indices with relative differences, reducing the vocabulary from N² to B² while retaining the compact M-sized edge list representation. The authors explicitly position gap encoding as orthogonal to BwR's bandwidth restriction, noting that any bandwidth-minimizing ordering can be combined with GEEL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GEEL provides a practically important scalability win: an O(M) LSTM-based generator that handles graphs up to 10k nodes where many diffusion- and flow-based baselines run out of memory, while matching or exceeding their quality. The key limitation is that GEEL cannot generate graphs with bandwidth exceeding the training vocabulary, and for graphs where B≈N the advantage vanishes; the authors suggest decomposing such graphs into subgraphs as future work. An open question is whether the gap encoding principle can be extended to other structured sequence representations beyond graphs, and whether the bandwidth dependency can be mitigated through adaptive or hierarchical vocabulary designs.
