## Problem

Neural circuits in the brain autonomously reactivate task-related activity patterns during quiescence (e.g., hippocampal replay during sleep), yet the precise conditions under which task-optimized networks develop this reactivation capability remain poorly understood. Existing models either explicitly train generative components to produce replay or rely on empirical observations of emergent replay without rigorous mathematical justification, leaving a gap in understanding why reactivation is so widespread across neural circuits.

## Input / Output

The network receives change-based sensory signals—2D velocity vectors for spatial position estimation or 1D angular velocity for head direction estimation—and must reconstruct a target function of the environmental state (Gaussian-tuned place cell activities or von Mises-tuned head direction cell activities) via a linear readout. During the quiescent phase, all sensory inputs are set to zero and the Brownian noise variance is doubled, after which the network's free-running activity trajectory is compared statistically (via KDE, KL divergence, and PC projections) against the active-phase distribution to verify reactivation fidelity.

## Method

The paper derives a mathematical framework showing that a noisy continuous-time RNN trained to track environmental state variables from change-based inputs naturally decomposes its optimal update into a state-tracking term (D† ∂f/∂s · ds/dt) and a denoising term (σ² ∇_r log p(r) · Δt). By upper-bounding the per-timestep loss via Taylor expansion, the triangle inequality, Cauchy-Schwarz, and Jensen's inequality, the authors show the loss separates into independent signal and noise components, each admitting a closed-form greedy optimum. In the quiescent regime (ds/dt = 0, noise variance doubled), the dynamics reduce exactly to Langevin sampling of the active-phase state distribution p(r), guaranteeing that the steady-state quiescent distribution equals the active distribution. This theoretical prediction is validated numerically on two canonical neuroscience tasks—spatial path integration and head direction integration—using noisy vanilla RNNs and GRUs trained with supervised MSE loss, confirming that quiescent activity faithfully samples the waking trajectory distribution, including when behavioral statistics are biased.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result is that the quiescent dynamics of a near-optimally trained noisy RNN performing change-based state estimation reduce to Langevin sampling of the active-state distribution p(r), predicting exact equivalence between steady-state quiescent and active neural activity distributions. Numerically, KL divergence analysis (Fig. 2e) shows that unbiased quiescent output distributions are nearly as close to unbiased active distributions as a true uniform distribution is, while biased quiescent outputs closely track biased active outputs; all cross-comparisons (biased-to-unbiased, biased-to-random, unbiased-to-random) diverge significantly. Networks trained without noise and run noiselessly during quiescence produce less variable trajectories (lower steady-state total variance, Fig. 2f), and adding noise at quiescence to noiseless-trained networks yields erratic, non-smooth decoded trajectories rather than faithful reactivation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external methods but instead validates its theory through internal ablations and control comparisons. Trained noisy RNNs (vanilla and GRU) are compared against: (1) random (untrained) networks, whose quiescent outputs diverge from active distributions in KL divergence; (2) noiseless-trained networks run noiselessly during quiescence, which produce lower-variance, less exploratory trajectories; and (3) noiseless-trained networks with noise added only at quiescence, which generate erratic, non-smooth decoded trajectories with much higher average distance between consecutive points. The key finding is that noisy training is necessary to stabilize noisy quiescent activity and enable faithful manifold exploration. Biased behavioral policies during training are faithfully reflected in quiescent reactivation statistics, confirming the theory's prediction that the quiescent distribution mirrors the active occupancy distribution. GRU architectures also exhibit reactivation but are more sensitive to quiescent noise increases, requiring additional hyperparameter tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate mathematical threads—score-function-based denoising (Raphan & Simoncelli, 2011) and Langevin sampling (Besag, 1994)—to show that offline reactivation in recurrent networks is a necessary consequence of optimal noisy state estimation, rather than requiring hand-crafted attractor architectures or explicit generative training. It departs from prior emergent-replay models (Shen & McNaughton, 1996; Azizi et al., 2013; Milstein et al., 2023; Hopfield, 2010; Theodoni et al., 2018), which demonstrated replay empirically in specific network topologies but lacked rigorous mathematical justification, by deriving sufficient conditions under which reactivation must emerge. The training paradigm builds on the grid-cell and head-direction-cell RNN literature (Cueva & Wei, 2018; Sorscher et al., 2019; Cueva et al., 2020), but the key contribution is the analytical decomposition of optimal dynamics into a signal-tracking term and a denoising term, with the latter identified as Langevin sampling in the quiescent regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides a principled, minimal explanation for why reactivation is so widespread across brain circuits: any circuit performing near-optimal noisy path integration will automatically exhibit it, without requiring dedicated generative machinery. This reframes reactivation from a special-purpose mechanism to a byproduct of robust state estimation, with potential implications for understanding memory consolidation and for designing artificial systems that replay experiences without external memory buffers. Key limitations include the restriction to change-based (velocity) inputs, the assumption of a stationary behavioral distribution, and the fact that the theory predicts only equivalence of stationary distributions, not the moment-to-moment transition structure of waking activity. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-change-based sensory tasks (e.g., early visual cortex), whether the noise-doubling assumption has a biological basis, and how the theory accounts for the observed temporal compression in hippocampal replay.
