## Problem

Existing loss functions for Nash equilibria in normal-form games (exploitability, Nikaido-Isoda, fully-differentiable exploitability, gradient-based NI) all contain nonlinear operations—max over payoffs or simplex projections—applied to random variables, making unbiased Monte Carlo estimation impossible. This prevents the use of powerful stochastic optimization solvers (SGD, bandits) for equilibrium computation, leaving large n-player games intractable for all but brute-force methods. The paper asks whether Nash equilibrium approximation can be recast as a stochastic non-convex optimization problem admitting unbiased estimation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a normal-form game specified by payoff tensors (n players, m_k actions each) accessed only through Monte Carlo sampling of joint actions, a temperature parameter τ = 1/ln(1/p) that guarantees all QRE probabilities exceed p/m*, and per-player step sizes η_k. It produces a joint mixed strategy profile x ∈ ∏Δ^{m_k−1} together with a provable exploitability bound ε ≤ τ log(∏m_k) + √(2n/min_k η_k)·√(L^τ(x)), where the bound tightens as τ→0. The key constraint is that the game need not be stored in full; only sampled payoffs are required, enabling scaling to games with exponentially many joint actions.

## Method

The core insight is to use the squared norm of the projected-gradient onto the tangent space of the simplex, Π_{TΔ}(∇_{x_k}^{kτ}), as the per-player loss component. Unlike projection onto the simplex (a nonlinear operation), projection onto the tangent space is linear, which enables unbiased Monte Carlo estimation via the two-sample trick: E[Y²] = E[Y⁽¹⁾Y⁽²⁾] for independent identically distributed copies. Shannon entropy regularization (u_k^τ = u_k + τS(x_k)) ensures all equilibria are interior logit/QREs, making the zero-projected-gradient characterization valid and the loss Lipschitz and bounded. The resulting loss L^τ(x) = Σ_k η_k‖Π_{TΔ}(∇_{x_k}^{kτ})‖² has global minima corresponding to QREs, which approximate Nash equilibria at low temperature with an additive τ log(∏m_k) gap. The paper applies both vanilla SGD and X-armed bandit algorithms (BLiN, StoSOO) to minimize L^τ, deriving high-probability polynomial convergence rates under a polymatrix-isolation condition on the equilibria.

## Results

The paper's main empirical results (Figure 3) compare SGD on the proposed loss L^τ against FTRL, Regret Matching, and ADIDAS/QRE^auto on four games: a 2-player 3-action nonsymmetric game, a 6-player 5-action nonsymmetric game, a 4-player 66-action symmetric game, and a 3-player 286-action symmetric game. SGD is competitive across all four, outperforming baselines on Shapley's game (where L^τ is strongly convex) but reaching only low-but-nonzero exploitability on Colonel Blotto due to saddle points. Theoretically, the BLiN bandit algorithm achieves a polynomial-time global convergence rate (Theorem 4), and StoSOO achieves a Õ(T^{-1/4}) rate (Theorem 5), both under the polymatrix-isolated equilibrium assumption. Figure 4 demonstrates the bandit solver recovering 5 Nash equilibria (including a pure NE via entropy regularization) in a 7-player symmetric 2-action game.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SGD on L^τ against three scalable baselines: FTRL (Shalev-Shwartz & Singer, 2006), Regret Matching (Hart & Mas-Collell, 2000), and ADIDAS/QRE^auto (Gemp et al., 2022). On Shapley's game, SGD clearly outperforms all three baselines, attributed to the strongly convex loss landscape (confirmed via Hessian rank analysis in Section 5). On the 6-player 5-action and 4-player 66-action symmetric games, SGD is broadly competitive with FTRL and Regret Matching, with all methods converging to similar exploitability levels. On Colonel Blotto (3-player, 286-action), SGD is slowed by saddle points and reaches a higher final exploitability than the best baselines, mirroring the well-known saddle-point challenge in deep learning. The bandit-based approach (BLiN) is shown to recover multiple equilibria in a 7-player game where the unregularized loss L misses the pure NE, while the entropy-regularized L^τ captures it—something none of the gradient-based baselines can do without explicit equilibrium enumeration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work connecting Nash equilibrium computation to optimization losses (Nikaido-Isoda/NashConv, gradient-based NI from Raghunathan et al. 2019, and ADIDAS/QRE^auto from Gemp et al. 2022) by identifying a critical geometric distinction: projection onto the simplex is nonlinear and thus incompatible with unbiased Monte Carlo estimation, whereas projection onto the tangent space of the simplex is linear. This insight, combined with entropy regularization (leveraging the logit equilibrium characterization from Leonardos et al. 2021) to handle boundary equilibria, yields the first Nash-equilibrium loss amenable to unbiased stochastic estimation. The paper departs from prior work not by proposing a new equilibrium concept but by reformulating an existing one in a way that unlocks the entire stochastic optimization toolkit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By casting Nash equilibrium approximation as a standard stochastic non-convex optimization problem, this work opens the door to decades of optimization advances (adaptive methods, Gaussian processes, evolutionary algorithms) for equilibrium computation in large multiplayer games, a setting where existing solvers are practically limited to a handful of players or actions. The provable polynomial-time global convergence rates via X-armed bandits (BLiN, StoSOO) under the polymatrix-isolated equilibrium assumption provide the first such guarantees for general-sum n-player games. Key limitations include the exponential representation of normal-form games, the polymatrix-isolation requirement for global convergence, and the familiar saddle-point pathology that slows SGD in games like Colonel Blotto. Extending the framework to extensive-form and imperfect-information games is identified as the most natural next step.
