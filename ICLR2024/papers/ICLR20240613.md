## Problem

Existing neural algorithmic reasoning methods incorporate historical embeddings (hidden states from prior steps) into the processor at each execution step, which contradicts the Markov property of algorithmic execution where the current state alone fully determines the next state. This misalignment introduces unnecessary noise into the computational graph, degrading out-of-distribution generalization. The paper asks whether removing or adaptively gating these historical embeddings can better align the model with the intrinsic structure of algorithmic tasks.

## Input / Output

At each time step t, the model receives node-level, edge-level, and graph-level features (scalar, categorical, mask, mask_one, or pointer types) representing the current algorithm state, encoded into high-dimensional embeddings. The processor performs one step of GNN computation, and the decoder predicts the next intermediate state (hints) or the final algorithm output at the last step. Training uses inputs with ≤16 nodes and OOD testing uses 64-node inputs, with all parameters shared across time steps and no teacher forcing during training.

## Method

ForgetNet removes the historical hidden state h_i^(t-1) from the processor input entirely, so the GNN processor operates solely on the current encoded features, directly enforcing the Markov property. However, this removal eliminates a residual pathway that aids backpropagation through many sequential processor layers, causing elevated losses and sub-optimal convergence on some tasks (e.g., Floyd-Warshall). G-ForgetNet addresses this by introducing a learnable gate g_i^(t) = σ(MLP([x̄_i^(t), h_i^(t-1)])) that element-wise modulates the historical embedding before concatenation, along with a regularization term λΣ||g_i^(t)|| added to the hints loss. The gate is expected to be open early in training (providing alternative computational pathways that bypass inaccurate intermediate predictions) and progressively close as the model learns reliable one-step execution, thereby recovering the Markov-consistent behavior of ForgetNet at convergence. The entire framework is built on the Triplet-GMPNN pipeline (Ibarz et al., 2022) without teacher forcing, using a heuristic λ schedule where the gate penalty constitutes roughly half the total loss after 6,000 of 10,000 training steps.

## Results

On the CLRS-30 benchmark (OOD micro-F1), G-ForgetNet achieves an overall average of 82.89%, improving over the previous state-of-the-art Triplet-GMPNN (75.98%) by nearly 7 points. G-ForgetNet is the top performer on 25/30 individual algorithmic tasks and surpasses the 99% F1 threshold on 9/30 algorithms (vs. 1/30 for all other methods). Notable gains include 97.02% on Naive String Matcher (vs. 78.67% for Triplet-GMPNN) and 98.40% on Insertion Sort (vs. 78.14%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

G-ForgetNet is compared against four prior methods: Memnet, MPNN, PGN (all from the original CLRS-30 benchmark), and Triplet-GMPNN (the previous state-of-the-art). Against Triplet-GMPNN, G-ForgetNet wins on 25/30 individual tasks, improving the overall average from 75.98% to 82.89% and increasing the number of algorithms exceeding 99% F1 from 1 to 9. The largest individual gains over Triplet-GMPNN are on Naive String Matcher (+18.35 pp, 97.02% vs. 78.67%), Insertion Sort (+20.26 pp, 98.40% vs. 78.14%), and Topological Sort (+12.65 pp, 99.92% vs. 87.27%). Against the older baselines (Memnet, MPNN, PGN), G-ForgetNet dominates across all categories, with the overall average exceeding 82% compared to 38–52% for those methods. The only tasks where G-ForgetNet does not achieve the top score are DFS (where ForgetNet alone reaches 97.12%) and a few others where the margin over Triplet-GMPNN is small, but G-ForgetNet still outperforms the baseline on all 30 tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the encoder-processor-decoder paradigm for neural algorithmic reasoning, specifically the training-stable pipeline developed by Ibarz et al. (2022) that eliminates teacher forcing. The key conceptual departure is identifying that the standard incorporation of historical embeddings (previous-step hidden states) into the processor input contradicts the Markov property inherent to algorithmic execution, where the current state fully determines the next step. Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper rethinks a ubiquitous design choice: ForgetNet simply removes historical embeddings, while G-ForgetNet introduces a regularized gating mechanism that allows their selective use during early training while converging to the Markov-consistent behavior at test time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a standard architectural component borrowed from RNN/GNN design actually degrades out-of-distribution generalization in algorithmic reasoning challenges a common assumption that more historical context is always beneficial. The practical implication is straightforward: practitioners building neural algorithmic reasoners should reconsider whether their processor truly needs access to previous-step hidden states. Open questions include whether the Markov-property argument extends beyond CLRS-30 to other sequential reasoning tasks, whether the heuristic lambda schedule can be made more principled, and whether combining this approach with self-supervised objectives (e.g., Hint-ReLIC) yields further gains. The paper also acknowledges that G-ForgetNet still underperforms on some tasks (e.g., Knuth-Morris-Pratt, Quickselect), suggesting the Markov alignment is necessary but not sufficient for all algorithmic structures.
