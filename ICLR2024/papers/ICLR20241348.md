## Problem

Classical self-supervised language model pretraining requires projecting hidden states onto a high-dimensional vocabulary space via a softmax, which incurs significant compute and memory costs that scale linearly with vocabulary size. This projection head slows training, limits batch sizes, and makes large-vocabulary (e.g., multilingual) models disproportionately expensive. The paper asks whether the vocabulary prediction objective can be entirely replaced by a cheaper contrastive objective that still yields strong representations.

## Input / Output

The model consumes token sequences (optionally masked for encoders) and, during pretraining, produces contextual hidden representations that are matched against the model's own input embedding table via a contrastive loss over in-batch negatives. No vocabulary-sized output is ever computed. For decoder models, a brief (<2% of pretraining data) fine-tuning step with a standard LM head initialized from the transposed input embeddings recovers text generation capability, after which the model outputs token probabilities as usual.

## Method

The core contribution is Contrastive Weight Tying (CWT), which replaces the cross-entropy loss over the vocabulary with a contrastive loss computed entirely within the batch. Specifically, for each target position, the model's output representation o_{i,j} is scored against the input embedding e_θ(x_{i,j}) of the target token (the positive) and against all other input embeddings in the batch (the negatives), using a standard InfoNCE-style softmax. This eliminates the e_θ^T projection to ℝ^V entirely, reducing loss computation from O(KDV) to O(K²D) operations where K is the micro-batch size and V is the vocabulary size. The key insight is that weight tying already implicitly maximizes the dot product between input embeddings and output representations under softmax regularization, so CWT makes this objective explicit without the vocabulary projection. The approach is applied as a drop-in replacement for both masked (encoder) and causal (decoder) language modeling, and for decoders a short predictive fine-tuning step restores generation ability.

## Results

On the GLUE benchmark, the Headless MLM (HMLM) achieves an average score of 84.08, outperforming the vanilla BERT MLM (82.43) by +1.65 points at a similar compute budget (~888 vs. 989 GPU hours), and by +0.86 points at the same number of pretraining tokens (4.1B) while using only 444 GPU hours (55% less compute). For the causal decoder, the fine-tuned Headless LM reaches 22.2% LAMBADA accuracy versus 19.52% for the vanilla Pythia-70M baseline (+2.7 points), while using 61% of the GPU hours on zero-shot LM Evaluation Harness tasks. In the multilingual setting, the Headless MLM outperforms its vanilla counterpart by +2.0 points (cross-lingual) and +2.7 points (target-language fine-tuning) on XNLI average accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the vanilla BERT MLM baseline (Devlin et al., 2019), the Headless MLM wins on every GLUE task at matched compute, achieving 84.08 vs. 82.43 average (+1.65), and also wins at matched token count (83.29 vs. 82.43) while requiring only 444 GPU hours versus 989. On SuperGLUE, the Headless MLM scores 68.6 vs. 67.9 for vanilla. Against the vanilla Pythia-70M causal LM baseline (Biderman et al., 2023), the fine-tuned Headless LM achieves 22.2% LAMBADA accuracy versus 19.52% (+2.7 points) and lower LAMBADA perplexity (153.5 vs. 170.23), while also winning on BoolQ (53.0 vs. 47.8), PubMedQA (47.5 vs. 43.8), and QASPER (66.0 vs. 41.9) in zero-shot evaluation at 61% of the GPU hours. The Headless LM does lose on raw validation perplexity (3.283 vs. 3.143) and on SciQ (64.5 vs. 66.4), and the un-fine-tuned Headless model performs poorly on generation (524.44 LAMBADA perplexity), confirming that generation must be treated as a downstream fine-tuning task for HLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the weight tying trick (Press & Wolf, 2017) through a contrastive lens, showing that the softmax-regularized projection implicitly maximizes the scalar product between input embeddings and contextual outputs. It then replaces the full vocabulary projection with an in-batch contrastive objective (CWT) that directly optimizes this scalar product, drawing on the Contrastive Predictive Coding framework (van den Oord et al., 2019) but applied at the token level as the sole pretraining objective. Unlike prior contrastive-augmented approaches (SimCTG, TaCL) that add a contrastive loss alongside cross-entropy, or importance-sampling methods that approximate the softmax denominator, CWT eliminates the vocabulary projection entirely and uses the model's own trainable input embeddings as targets rather than fixed external representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 20× compute reduction and the ability to scale vocabulary size at near-zero marginal cost are practically significant, especially for multilingual models where vocabulary bottlenecks are well-documented. However, all experiments are conducted at 110M parameter scale, and the authors acknowledge that as architectures grow, the relative cost of the vocabulary projection shrinks, potentially diminishing the efficiency advantage. A key open question is whether the contrastive-as-sole-objective formulation retains its data-efficiency and performance benefits at the billion-parameter scale where cross-entropy's gradient signal is already well-distributed. The paper also leaves unexplored whether CWT can be combined with other efficient pretraining strategies (ELECTRA, DeBERTa) or applied to encoder-decoder architectures.
