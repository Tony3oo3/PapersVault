## Problem

Existing causal fairness methods assume the true causal DAG is fully known, yet in real-world applications only partial causal knowledge is available, typically represented as a maximally oriented PDAG (MPDAG). This paper addresses whether interventional fairness can be formally defined, identified, and enforced when the causal graph is only partially known, and how to balance the inherent accuracy–fairness trade-off in that setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241789/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational data (X, A), an MPDAG obtained from causal discovery combined with background knowledge, and a user-specified set of admissible attributes X_ad. It outputs a neural-network predictor Ŷ = h_θ(x, a) whose parameters θ are trained to minimize prediction loss subject to an ε-approximate interventional fairness constraint. The interventional distributions needed for the fairness term are not observed directly; they are approximated via Monte Carlo sampling that draws from conditional multivariate normal densities fitted to the observational data, following the partial causal ordering of the MPDAG.

## Method

The key technical contribution is a modeling technique that treats the predictor Ŷ as a function of all observable variables, which provably yields an augmented MPDAG G* that is itself a valid MPDAG (Theorem 4.1), thereby enabling formal causal inference on a partially known graph. On G*, the paper derives a necessary-and-sufficient identification condition: the interventional fairness quantity P(Ŷ|do(A=a), do(X_ad=x_ad)) is identifiable if and only if no undirected edge connects any node in A∪X_ad to any node in V\(A∪X_ad). The identification formula factorizes the post-interventional density using partial causal ordering (PCO) of the remaining nodes. The resulting constrained optimization minimizes a prediction loss plus a weighted MMD term between the two interventional distributions, with Monte Carlo sampling approximating the integrals and a neural network parameterizing h_θ; when the effect is non-identifiable, the fairness term is replaced by an average over all valid edge-orientation completions.

## Results

The paper presents accuracy–fairness trade-off curves (rather than single-point results) across synthetic graphs of 5–30 nodes and two real-world datasets. On the UCI Student Performance dataset (395 students, sex as sensitive attribute), ε-IFair with larger λ values drives MMD-based unfairness toward zero while keeping RMSE competitive with the Full model; on the Credit Risk dataset (32,581 borrowers, Age as sensitive attribute), ε-IFair similarly balances accuracy against the absolute difference in prediction means between age groups. Across all settings, the Full and Unaware baselines exhibit lower RMSE but substantially higher unfairness, while the IFair baseline (definite non-descendants only) achieves near-zero unfairness at a significant accuracy cost. The proposed ε-IFair method interpolates smoothly between these extremes by tuning the penalty parameter λ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three baselines: (1) Full (uses all attributes including the sensitive one), (2) Unaware (uses all attributes except the sensitive one), and (3) IFair (Zuo et al., 2022), which restricts prediction to definite non-descendants of the sensitive attribute on the MPDAG. Full and Unaware consistently achieve the lowest RMSE/accuracy but the highest MMD unfairness across all datasets. IFair achieves nearly zero unfairness but suffers a notable drop in prediction performance because it discards all descendant variables. The proposed ε-IFair method dominates both extremes: for moderate λ it matches Full's accuracy with substantially reduced unfairness, and for large λ it matches IFair's near-zero unfairness while retaining more predictive signal than IFair by still using all variables. On the Credit Risk dataset, where no definite non-descendants of Age exist, IFair is inapplicable, further highlighting ε-IFair's advantage in partially known causal settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the interventional fairness framework of Salimi et al. (2019) from fully known causal DAGs to partially known causal graphs represented as MPDAGs, building directly on the identification theory for causal effects in MPDAGs established by Perkovic (2020). It is closely related to Zuo et al. (2022), which addressed counterfactual fairness on partially known graphs by identifying definite non-descendants; here, the authors instead formulate a constrained optimization that allows inclusion of descendants at a controlled fairness cost. The key methodological contribution is a modeling technique that augments an MPDAG with the predictor node and proves (via Meek's rules) that the result remains a valid MPDAG, enabling formal causal inference on the augmented graph.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: real-world causal graphs are rarely fully known, yet most causal fairness methods assume a complete DAG. The constrained optimization formulation provides a principled accuracy-fairness trade-off rather than the binary choice of using only non-descendants (which sacrifices accuracy) or all variables (which sacrifices fairness). Key limitations include the assumption of no latent confounders or selection bias, the reliance on conditional multivariate Gaussian density estimation, and the fact that the identification condition (no undirected edges between intervened and non-intervened variables) may not always hold. Open questions include extending the framework to handle latent confounders, nonlinear density estimation, and the ongoing challenge of (nested) counterfactual identification on MPDAGs that would enable path-specific and counterfactual fairness notions.
