## Problem

Learning generalizable imitation policies from few demonstrations in high-dimensional observation spaces requires well-designed state abstractions that surface task-relevant features and hide irrelevant ones. These abstractions are typically hand-engineered or derived from labor-intensive labeling, and there is no efficient mechanism to autonomously incorporate human prior knowledge—expressed in natural language—to construct task-specific state representations for unseen tasks in unstructured environments.

## Input / Output

The method takes a top-down RGB observation (480×640 from Spot's front camera, or simulator-rendered images), a natural language task description (e.g., 'bring me something to drink water out of'), and a small set of expert demonstrations (state-action pairs). It produces a binary goal mask as the abstracted state—where task-relevant objects are set to 1 and all distractors are zeroed out—and a continuous action consisting of a 2D coordinate and a quaternion rotation. The policy never receives the language utterance directly; it operates solely over the LM-filtered visual abstraction.

## Method

LGA decomposes state abstraction into three stages: (1) textualization, where a segmentation model (e.g., Segment Anything) converts the raw observation into a structured text feature set of object names and locations; (2) feature abstraction, where GPT-4 is queried per-candidate to produce a binary yes/no decision on whether each object type or texture is task-relevant given the language goal; and (3) instantiation, where the selected features are rendered back into a binary pixel mask usable by a CNN policy. The policy (dual Conv2D blocks + MLP) is trained via standard behavior cloning on demonstrations paired with the abstracted states, and critically, it never sees the language input—only the LM-filtered visual representation. At test time, the LM can resolve previously unseen or ambiguous utterances by selecting contextually appropriate features, enabling zero-shot generalization to new language specifications without retraining.

## Results

On 13 VIMA scenarios (9 pick-and-place, 2 rotate, 2 sweep), LGA and LGA-HILL achieve consistently higher success rates than GCBC+DART and GCBC-SEG across all demonstration counts (10–50), demonstrating superior sample efficiency. LGA reduces user feature-specification time significantly compared to manual specification (p < 0.001, paired t-test). Under state covariate shift (texture changes, added distractors) and multi-task linguistic ambiguity (unseen utterances like 'bring me a fruit'), LGA variants conditioning on state abstractions outperform both GCBC+DART and the language-only ablation (LGA-L).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GCBC+DART (goal-conditioned behavior cloning with DART noise injection), LGA wins on all three evaluation axes: sample efficiency (higher success at every demonstration count from 10 to 50), robustness to state covariate shift (texture changes and added distractors), and multi-task linguistic ambiguity (zero-shot generalization to unseen utterances like 'bring me a fruit'). Against GCBC-SEG (which adds segmentation masks to the observation), LGA also achieves higher success rates, indicating that language-guided feature selection is more effective than simply providing raw segmentation. Against the Human baseline (manual feature specification), LGA matches task performance while requiring significantly less user time (p < 0.001), confirming that GPT-4 can replicate human-designed abstractions in a fraction of the time. The LGA-L ablation (conditioning only on language embeddings of relevant features rather than the visual state abstraction) underperforms full LGA, confirming that the visual grounding of task-relevant features in the abstracted observation is essential for robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

LGA carves out a distinct niche in the LM-for-robotics literature by using language models to construct state abstractions for imitation learning, rather than shaping rewards (Goyal et al., Kwon et al.), generating action plans (Ahn et al., Huang et al.), or composing pre-trained skills. The policy learning formulation directly extends GCBC (Co-Reyes et al., 2018) by replacing language conditioning with a language-guided state abstraction function, and the robustness baseline draws on DART noise injection (Laskey et al., 2017). Conceptually, LGA is closest to the state abstraction literature in RL (Abel et al., Diuk et al.), but departs by autonomously constructing abstractions from natural language rather than assuming pre-defined hierarchies or hand-crafted primitives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that LMs can serve as a practical, low-effort interface for specifying task-relevant state features, reducing the manual burden of representation design in imitation learning while improving robustness to both observational covariate shift and linguistic ambiguity. Key limitations include the assumption that task-relevant features are segmentable and expressible in language (excluding spatial/relational features), reliance on ground-truth segmentation in simulation, and evaluation limited to a single real-robot demonstration. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to trajectory-relevant abstractions, handles unsegmentable features, and scales to more complex multi-step manipulation without oracle demonstrations.
