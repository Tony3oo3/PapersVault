## Problem

Most existing backdoor attack analyses examine trigger effects only in the spatial domain, leaving the mechanism by which CNNs generalize to poisoned samples in the frequency domain unexplored. Existing frequency-based backdoor methods design triggers based on spectral observations without explaining why triggers generalize or whether they are optimally effective. This paper asks: how do different frequency components contribute to CNN generalization on backdoor samples, and which components are most susceptible to trigger perturbations?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240209/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10, 28×28 MNIST, 64×64 Tiny-ImageNet, CelebA face crops) and requires a target class image as raw material for trigger synthesis. The DCT transform maps each image to a same-size frequency spectrum where low-frequency components occupy the upper-left and high-frequency components the lower-right. The output is a poisoned image whose perturbation is confined to the mid-to-high frequency quadrant (k₁ > N₁/2, k₂ > N₂/2), making the trigger imperceptible in the spatial domain while the trained model outputs the target class label on triggered inputs.

## Method

The paper first proves via controlled experiments that CNNs generalize to backdoor samples by memorizing the unified frequency-domain feature distribution introduced by triggers, and that high-frequency components require far less perturbation intensity (ρ=0.06) to achieve high ASR compared to low-frequency components (ρ=0.8), due to their weaker correlation with original labels. Building on this, two attack strategies are proposed: (1) a universal invisibility strategy that applies DCT to a visible-trigger poisoned image, retains only the mid-high frequency perturbation (mask m where k₁>N₁/2 and k₂>N₂/2), and reconstructs via IDCT, rendering visible triggers invisible with negligible ASR loss; and (2) a low-frequency semantic attack that extracts a small LFC patch (ε·N₁ × ε·N₂) from a randomly selected target-class image, upsamples it via 2D linear interpolation to (N₁/2, N₂/2), and injects it scaled by ρ into the mid-high frequency region of the clean image's DCT spectrum. The approach achieves ~100% ASR across ResNet18, VGG16, MobileNetV2, and ViT-base on multiple datasets while bypassing Fine-Pruning, STRIP, GradCam, DBD, Neural Cleanse, and CLP defenses.

## Results

The proposed frequency-domain backdoor attack (Ours, ρ=0.4, ε=1/3) achieves 100% Attack Success Rate (ASR) across all three datasets (CIFAR-10, MNIST, CelebA) and all three CNN architectures (ResNet18, VGG16, MobileNetV2), while maintaining or slightly improving Benign Accuracy (BA) over baselines. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet18, the method reaches 96.60% BA (ρ=0.3, ε=1/4), a 2.06% improvement over BadNet's 94.54%. On Tiny-ImageNet, the method achieves 59.11% BA and 100% ASR, outperforming BPP (58.14% BA) and TrojanNN (55.89% BA). Under defense methods, ASR remains at 100% (DBD), 91.80% (Neural Cleanse), and 98.41% (CLP), demonstrating strong robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method achieves 100% ASR across all datasets and models, matching or exceeding BadNet (99.99–100%), IAD (98.71–99.56%), WaNet (99.55–100%), and ISSBA (99.75–100%). On CIFAR-10 with ResNet18, the method's best BA (96.60%) surpasses all baselines by 2+ points, while on MNIST and CelebA it matches or slightly exceeds the strongest baselines. On Tiny-ImageNet, the method (59.11% BA, 100% ASR) outperforms BPP (58.14%), TrojanNN (55.89%), LIRA (0.05%), and Blind (1.97%). On Vision Transformer (ViT-base, CIFAR-10), the method achieves 98.62% BA and 100% ASR, beating BadNet (94.78%/93.77%), TrojanNN (96.46%/99.98%), Blended (96.54%/99.68%), and LIRA (17.52%/87.87%). Under defense, the method retains 100% ASR against DBD, 91.80% against Neural Cleanse, and 98.41% against CLP, whereas WaNet drops to 6.61–78.42% and ISSBA to 0.58–68.13% under the same defenses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the F-Principle (Luo et al., 2019) and Wang et al. (2020), which established that CNNs preferentially learn low-frequency components before high-frequency ones, and extends this framework to explain the generalization mechanism of backdoor attacks. While prior frequency-based backdoor attacks (Wang et al. 2022a, Zeng et al. 2021, Hammoud & Ghanem 2021) designed triggers based on spectral observations without explaining why triggers generalize, this paper provides a mechanistic account: triggers unify the frequency-domain distribution across poisoned samples, enabling the model to memorize a shared feature distribution rather than individual images. The paper departs from spatial-domain backdoor analysis by showing that the susceptibility of frequency components to perturbation is asymmetric—high-frequency components require far less perturbation (intensity 0.06 vs. 0.8) to achieve backdoor generalization—thereby grounding both the design of invisible triggers and a new low-frequency-semantic attack in a unified theoretical framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The mechanistic explanation of why CNNs generalize to backdoor triggers (memorizing unified frequency-domain distributions rather than individual poisoned images) provides a principled basis for both attack design and defense development, moving the field beyond trial-and-error trigger engineering. The practical contributions—a universal strategy to render visible triggers invisible with minimal ASR loss, and a low-frequency-semantic attack achieving ~100% ASR while bypassing multiple defenses (Fine-Pruning, STRIP, GradCam, DBD, NC, CLP)—are immediately actionable for both red-team and blue-team practitioners. However, the paper acknowledges that its defense analysis remains incomplete, and the broader question of how to systematically defend against frequency-domain-injected backdoors (as opposed to spatial-domain ones) remains open. The findings also raise the question of whether the HFC-susceptibility principle generalizes to non-image modalities or to architectures beyond CNNs and ViTs.
