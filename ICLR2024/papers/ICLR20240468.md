## Problem

State-of-the-art time series forecasting methods, particularly Transformer-based architectures, demand substantial GPU resources and lengthy back-propagation training, making them impractical for ordinary laboratory setups or personal computers. Moreover, these deep learning models operate as black boxes with limited interpretability. The paper asks whether long-term multivariate time series prediction can be achieved with an interpretable, CPU-only method that avoids neural network training entirely while matching or exceeding deep learning accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multivariate time series partitioned into training, validation, and test sets at a 7:1:2 ratio, with input batches of length 96 (24 for ILI) and output horizons of 96, 192, 336, 720 (or 24, 36, 48, 60 for ILI). Beyond producing predicted future values, GLIP yields an explicit sparse trigonometric expression function whose coefficients directly encode the identified periodic components and inter-variable coupling strengths. A distinctive feature is that prediction time remains essentially constant regardless of output horizon length, since the model structure is fixed after identification and only the evaluation time index extends.

## Method

GLIP (Global-Local Identification and Prediction) replaces neural network training with a three-stage sparse system identification pipeline built entirely on Discrete Fourier Transform and L1-regularized coordinate descent. In the global identification stage, DFT on the full training set extracts dominant periods, which form a trigonometric basis (sin/cos terms) solved via sparse identification to produce a global trend prediction; a second identification pass using cross-variable basis terms (quadratic, trigonometric combinations of predicted values) captures inter-variable coupling. A storage basis is constructed by sliding a window over the test set, collecting unique high-frequency periods via DFT, and forming additional trigonometric functions that bridge training-set knowledge into local prediction. In the local rolling prediction stage, a weighted blend of the global prediction and the raw local input (the 'local identification curve') is DFT'd to extract a local basis, which is concatenated with the global and storage bases; sparse identification on this combined basis yields the final rolling forecast. Validation on the held-out set determines per-variable whether global prediction suffices or local identification is needed, and whether variable-coupling terms further improve accuracy. No back-propagation or GPU is required at any stage.

## Results

GLIP achieves state-of-the-art MSE across nearly all prediction horizons on four benchmark datasets (ETTh, ETTm, Exchange, ILI), with an overall 23.64% MSE improvement over the best neural network baselines. On ETTh-720, GLIP reaches MSE 0.314 / MAE 0.408, outperforming the closest competitor DLinear (0.605 / 0.551) by nearly 2×. The advantage grows with prediction horizon: at O/I = 7.5 (e.g., 720-step output from 96-step input), GLIP's MSE improvement over DLinear exceeds 30% on ETTh and ETTm. Notably, all experiments run on CPU in seconds to minutes, with training time essentially independent of prediction length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GLIP outperforms all seven neural network baselines on MSE for 15 out of 16 dataset-horizon combinations (the sole exception being ILI-24, where Pyraformer achieves a lower MSE of 1.420 vs. GLIP's 1.802, though Pyraformer's MAE of 2.012 is far worse than GLIP's 0.908). The closest competitor is DLinear, a simple linear model; GLIP beats it by 11–38% MSE across all horizons, with the gap widening as the output-to-input ratio increases (e.g., 32.18% on ETTh-720, 34.00% on ETTm-720). Transformer-based methods (FEDformer, Autoformer, Informer, Reformer, Pyraformer, LogTrans) are consistently 2–10× worse in MSE, with Informer and LogTrans being the weakest at long horizons (MSE > 3 on ETTh-720). On MAE, GLIP achieves the best or tied-best result in every single cell of the table. The paper also highlights that GLIP runs entirely on CPU in seconds-to-minutes, whereas all compared deep learning baselines require GPU training, and GLIP's inference time is essentially independent of prediction horizon.

## Relation to Prior Work

GLIP extends the sparse system identification (SINDy) framework of Brunton et al. (2016), Yuan et al. (2019), and Gao & Yan (2022) from discovering governing equations of dynamical systems to time series forecasting, replacing polynomial basis libraries with Fourier-derived trigonometric bases. Conceptually, it shares the frequency-domain motivation of FEDformer (Zhou et al., 2022) but eliminates the neural network entirely, relying solely on DFT-based basis construction and LASSO-style sparse optimization. It also aligns with the broader trend initiated by DLinear (Zeng et al., 2023) and TiDE (Das et al., 2023) of questioning whether Transformer architectures are necessary for competitive forecasting, pushing further by abandoning all learned parameters in favor of an explicit sparse expression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a CPU-only, interpretable sparse identification method can consistently outperform all Transformer-based and even linear deep learning baselines by 10–48% MSE across four standard benchmarks, challenging the prevailing assumption that deep learning is required for competitive long-horizon forecasting. The explicit sparse expression and interpretable hyperparameters (periods, coupling coefficients) offer a white-box alternative for domains where model transparency is critical. Open questions include scalability to higher-dimensional or non-stationary series, sensitivity to the DFT-based period selection heuristic, and whether the global-local decomposition generalizes beyond the four benchmark datasets tested. The authors themselves note future work on integrating identification with neural networks for hybrid approaches.
