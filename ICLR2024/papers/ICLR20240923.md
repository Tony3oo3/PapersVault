## Problem

While it is widely believed that good generalization requires good features in early layers, the notion of 'good features' lacks a rigorous mathematical definition. This paper formalizes the concept as 'feature collapse'—the property that entities playing identical roles in a task receive identical representations—and provides the first provable guarantee that a neural network learns such representations in its first layer, while also identifying LayerNorm as the critical mechanism enabling collapse under long-tailed data distributions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of L word tokens drawn from a vocabulary of n_w words partitioned into n_c equally-sized concepts, and produces a K-dimensional score vector for classification. The shared embedding matrix W (d × n_w) maps each one-hot word to a d-dimensional vector; in the LayerNorm variant, each embedding is normalized (mean-subtracted and divided by standard deviation) before the L embeddings are concatenated and passed through a linear head U (K × Ld). The latent structure is that words within a concept are interchangeable for the task, so the 'correct' output is for all words in a concept to map to the same embedding direction.

## Method

The paper introduces a synthetic data model where sentences are generated from latent concept sequences, with words sampled from concept-specific distributions (uniform or Zipf-like long-tail). Two linear networks are studied: h (embedding + linear head) and h* (embedding + parameter-free LayerNorm + linear head), both trained with cross-entropy loss and weight decay. Three theorems are proven in the large-sample limit under a latent symmetry assumption: (i) h achieves full Type-I collapse (all words in a concept share an identical embedding) when word distributions are uniform; (ii) h only achieves Type-III directional collapse under non-uniform distributions, where embedding magnitudes scale with word frequency, causing the linear head weights to fail to collapse and generalization to degrade on small training sets; (iii) h* achieves full Type-II collapse regardless of word frequency because LayerNorm forces all word representations to have identical magnitude, decoupling them from sampling frequency. The key mechanism is that without normalization, rare words receive small embeddings that create imbalanced contributions to the linear head, preventing the head weights from aligning with concept directions.

## Results

In the uniform word distribution setting (n_c=3, n_w=1200, L=15, K=1000, n_spl=5), both networks h (without LayerNorm) and h* (with LayerNorm) achieve 100% test accuracy, and word embeddings collapse into equiangular vectors with predicted norm 1.42214 vs. empirically observed 1.41±0.13. In the long-tailed (Zipf's law) setting with a small training set (n_spl=5), the critical difference emerges: h drops to 45% test accuracy while h* maintains 100%, demonstrating that LayerNorm is essential for feature collapse under frequency imbalance. With a large training set (n_spl=500), both networks recover to 100% accuracy, confirming that the failure of h is due to sampling noise interacting with frequency-dependent embedding magnitudes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; its contribution is theoretical rather than benchmarking. The central empirical comparison is between two network architectures: h (shared embedding + linear head, no normalization) and h* (shared embedding + LayerNorm + linear head). Under uniform word distributions, both architectures exhibit full feature collapse and generalize perfectly, making them indistinguishable in performance. The decisive comparison arises under long-tailed (Zipf's law) word distributions with limited training data: h fails to generalize (45% accuracy) because word embedding magnitudes scale with frequency, causing the linear head weights u_{k,ℓ} to fail to collapse when only 5 samples per class are available. In contrast, h* achieves 100% accuracy because LayerNorm forces all word representations to have identical magnitude, decoupling them from frequency and enabling proper collapse of the linear weights. The paper further validates that these findings extend to transformer architectures (GPT-2 style, 2 layers, 8 heads, 512 dims) in both classification and next-word-prediction settings, and to deeper hierarchical data models (context-free grammars of depth 4), though no formal theoretical guarantees are provided for these extended settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself alongside the theoretical representation learning line (Damian et al., 2022; Mousavi-Hosseini et al., 2022) that proves networks learn latent subspaces in the multiple-index model, but departs by using a shared embedding module rather than a fully connected first layer, and by tying 'good features' to semantic equivalence of tokens rather than projection onto a low-dimensional subspace. It explicitly distinguishes its 'feature collapse' phenomenon from neural collapse (Papyan et al., 2020): neural collapse concerns last-layer representations of same-class training points converging, while feature collapse concerns first-layer representations of semantically equivalent tokens converging, providing evidence of meaningful early-layer structure beyond what labels encode. The paper also draws on the broader literature on long-tailed distributions in NLP and vision (Zipf's law, Feldman 2020) to motivate its non-uniform word frequency experiments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous proof that LayerNorm is necessary for feature collapse under long-tailed token distributions, identifying the precise failure mechanism: without normalization, word embedding magnitudes become frequency-dependent, which prevents semantically equivalent tokens from receiving identical representations and breaks generalization. This gives a theoretical foundation for a widely-used but heuristically-motivated architectural choice. Limitations include the highly synthetic data model, the requirement of strong symmetry assumptions on latent variables, and the very simple two-layer architecture; the appendix CFG and transformer experiments are purely empirical and do not carry theoretical guarantees. Open questions include whether the results extend to deeper networks, attention-based architectures, and real-world data with less clean latent structure.
