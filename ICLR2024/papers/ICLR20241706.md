## Problem

Stochastic optimization of spectral risk measures (SRMs) for distributionally robust optimization has been hindered by biased gradient estimates that prevent convergence, the need for multiple hyperparameters, or failure to converge for small shift costs. This paper asks whether a single-hyperparameter stochastic algorithm can achieve linear convergence for SRM-based DRO objectives for any positive shift cost, without the tuning burden or convergence failures of prior methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241706/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a fixed training set of n examples through per-example loss/gradient oracles, a spectrum vector σ defining the SRM (e.g., CVaR, extremile, ESRM), a shift cost ν > 0, and a regularization constant μ > 0. Each iteration requires only two oracle calls (for independently sampled indices i and j) and O(n+d) operations, with O(n+d) memory for generalized linear models rather than O(nd) for a full gradient table. The output is the optimized parameter vector w minimizing the regularized SRM objective, with the permutahedron weight update solved exactly via the Pool Adjacent Violators algorithm in O(n) time per iteration.

## Method

Prospect constructs an asymptotically unbiased and low-variance stochastic gradient estimate for the SRM objective by combining two mechanisms: (1) bias reduction via a running loss table l that is stochastically updated so that l → ℓ(w), ensuring the importance weights q^l approximate the true adversarial weights q^{ℓ(w)}; and (2) variance reduction via a SAGA-style control variate using running tables of gradients g and weights ρ, yielding the estimate nq_i^l ∇ℓ_i(w) − nρ_i g_i + Σ_j ρ_j g_j whose variance vanishes as g → ∇ℓ(w) and ρ → q^{ℓ(w)}. The adversarial weights q are computed exactly by reducing the inner maximization over the permutahedron P(σ) to an isotonic regression problem solvable by PAV in O(n) time. The algorithm requires only a single stepsize hyperparameter η and provably converges linearly at rate O((n + κκ_σ) ln(1/ε)) for any ν > 0 on smooth, strongly convex regularized losses, matching the rate of LSVRG while eliminating its small-shift-cost failure mode.

## Results

Prospect achieves linear convergence on all tested spectral risk measure objectives (CVaR, extremile, ESRM) across 9 datasets, converging 2–3× faster than SGD and stochastic saddle-point baselines. On tabular regression, Prospect reaches 10⁻⁸ suboptimality on CVaR/concrete and extremile/power within half the number of passes required by LSVRG, and on ESRM/yacht it needs only 40 epochs versus 64 for Saddle-SAGA. On the diabetes fairness benchmark, Prospect achieves a statistical parity score of 0.82±0.00% versus 1.38±0.25% for LSVRG (a 40% relative improvement with near-zero variance). SGD and SRDA fail to converge entirely due to biased gradient estimates, while LSVRG fails to converge on diabetes and Saddle-SAGA fails on acsincome.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SRDA (Xiao, 2009), Prospect clearly wins: SRDA produces biased gradient estimates for spectral risk objectives and fails to converge for any learning rate on all tested datasets. Against Saddle-SAGA (Palaniappan & Bach, 2016), Prospect is faster on tabular regression (e.g., 40 vs. 64 epochs on ESRM/yacht) and more stable on fairness metrics, though Saddle-SAGA remains competitive on the iWildcam distribution shift task where both methods outperform LSVRG. Against LSVRG (Mehta et al., 2023), the closest competitor, Prospect achieves the same asymptotic rate O((n+κκ_σ)ln(1/ε)) but with a single hyperparameter (stepsize) versus LSVRG's multiple tuning knobs, and is guaranteed to converge for any shift cost ν>0 whereas LSVRG may fail for small ν. Empirically, Prospect reaches target precision in roughly half the passes of LSVRG on several regression tasks, and on the diabetes CVaR benchmark LSVRG fails to converge while Prospect stabilizes at 0.82% statistical parity. On the Amazon Reviews extremile, Prospect's worst-group error (77.38±0.00%) marginally beats Saddle-SAGA (77.53±1.57%) with far greater stability, while on iWildcam both Prospect and LSVRG achieve ~1pp lower median group error than Saddle-SAGA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the stochastic optimization literature for distributionally robust optimization (DRO) with spectral risk measures (CVaR, extremile, ESRM), building directly on LSVRG (Mehta et al., 2023) for primal stochastic optimization and Saddle-SAGA (Palaniappan & Bach, 2016) for primal-dual methods. The key departure is the combination of a bias-reduction mechanism (maintaining a running loss table to approximate the adversarial reweighting) with a SAGA-style control variate for variance reduction, yielding a single-hyperparameter algorithm. This contrasts with prior stochastic DRO methods that either produce biased gradient estimates (Levy et al., 2020; Kawaguchi & Lu, 2020), require the shift cost ν to exceed a threshold for convergence (LSVRG), or demand tuning of multiple learning rates (Saddle-SAGA). The isotonic regression / Pool Adjacent Violators subroutine for computing the exact dual solution over the permutahedron connects to classical convex analysis and the f-divergence DRO framework of Namkoong & Duchi (2016).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Prospect's guarantee of linear convergence for any positive shift cost with a single learning rate removes a practical barrier to deploying DRO: practitioners no longer need to tune multiple hyperparameters or verify that ν is large enough for convergence. The 2–3× speedup over SGD and saddle-point baselines across tabular, vision, and language benchmarks makes spectral-risk objectives more competitive with standard ERM in practice. Open questions include extending the analysis to non-convex objectives (the paper sketches a Moreau-envelope variant but does not fully analyze it), exploring other uncertainty sets beyond f-divergences, and understanding whether the fairness benefits observed (e.g., 40% improvement in statistical parity on diabetes) persist in non-linear deep learning settings. The method's reliance on storing an O(nd) gradient table (reducible to O(n) for linear models) may limit scalability to very high-dimensional non-linear architectures.
