## Problem

Existing human-AI collaboration methods either rely on entity-specific embeddings that fail to generalize to new scenes without retraining, or lack interpretable reasoning about unobservable human mental states. This paper addresses the gap by asking whether AI agents can be endowed with Theory of Mind grounded in interpretable, generalizable logical rules that capture both object relations and event-sequence constraints, enabling robust social perception and adaptive collaboration in partially observable environments.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single task demonstration (a sequence of states and actions from a human performing the goal) and a symbolic graph representation of the current environment (objects as nodes, spatial-temporal relations as edges), plus the human's real-time action at each step. It produces an inferred goal predicate with a posterior distribution over latent logic rules (each rule carrying a confidence score reflecting consistency with observed data), a sequence of robot actions for collaborative task completion, and the interpretable rules themselves. Unlike pixel-based approaches, the input is structured as a graph of predicates rather than raw observations, and the output rules remain valid across new scenes without retraining.

## Method

The framework treats first-order logic rules (conjunctions of property and spatial-temporal relation predicates) as latent variables and trains a rule encoder jointly with a multi-agent system. Goal inference models the probability of each candidate predicate as a softmax over weighted rule features (Eq. 2), optimized by maximum likelihood. For collaboration, a hierarchical reinforcement learning module with Theory of Mind plans robot actions: a Boltzmann rationality model estimates both agents' policies conditioned on inferred sub-goals, while an obverter-based belief update (Bayesian, with counterfactual reasoning) tracks the human's private state. Training alternates between Deep Q-learning for value functions and supervised losses for policy and belief networks, fixing one agent model at a time to avoid non-stationarity. Execution follows a Task-Stack discipline where subtask policies are invoked, executed to terminal states, and popped, with dynamic goal re-inference at each level enabling online adaptation.

## Results

On the Watch-and-Help dataset (partially observable), the proposed method achieves the highest success rate across all four difficulty levels, reaching 82.43% (Level 1) and 58.91% (Level 4), while also attaining the best speedup of 0.48 (Level 1) and 0.33 (Level 4). On the HandMeThat dataset, the method achieves a success rate of 27.73% (Level 1) down to 21.66% (Level 4), substantially outperforming Seq2Seq (25.50% → 5.30%) and DRRN (0% across all levels). The average number of moves in successful episodes is also the lowest, e.g., 31.10 on Watch-and-Help Level 4 versus 34.16 (Seq2Seq) and 38.25 (DRRN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Seq2Seq (Sutskever et al., 2014), the proposed method wins on every metric across all four difficulty levels on both datasets. On Watch-and-Help Level 4, it improves success rate by 1.05 percentage points (58.91% vs. 57.86%) and speedup by 0.12 (0.33 vs. 0.21); on HandMeThat Level 3, the gap widens dramatically to 20.97% vs. 3.95% success rate. Against DRRN (Hausknecht et al., 2020), the advantage is even more pronounced: on Watch-and-Help Level 4, the proposed method achieves 58.91% SR and 0.33 speedup versus DRRN's 57.53% and 0.16, while on HandMeThat DRRN completely fails (0% success rate, −40 average score at every level) whereas the proposed method maintains 21–28% success rate. The paper attributes these gains to the rule encoder's ability to reduce the search space and the logic-guided Q-function that avoids redundant actions and collisions with the human partner.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of neurosymbolic AI and computational Theory of Mind, building on the obverter-based belief modeling of Choi et al. (2018) and the hierarchical RL frameworks used in human-robot collaboration benchmarks like Watch-and-Help (Puig et al., 2020) and HandMeThat (Wan et al., 2022). It departs from prior neurosymbolic work (which typically learns differentiable solvers or triplet-based embeddings over knowledge graphs) by treating first-order logic rules as latent variables with learned confidence scores, and from prior ToM approaches (which focus on trajectory prediction or simple POMDPs) by coupling rule-based goal inference with a hierarchical, Boltzmann-rationality policy for decentralized multi-agent collaboration. The Task-Stack execution mechanism and the joint training of rule encoder, Q-function, policy, and belief update represent a novel architectural integration rather than a direct extension of any single prior system.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that interpretable, generalizable logic rules can serve as a bridge between perception and action in human-AI collaboration, offering a degree of robustness (rules transfer to new entities without retraining) that purely embedding-based methods lack. However, the framework's practical applicability is constrained by its reliance on manually defined spatial-temporal predicates and chain-only rule structures, limiting scalability to open-world or real-robot settings. Open questions include whether predicate generation can be automated (the authors suggest language instructions as a path), whether more complex rule topologies (beyond chains) improve inference, and whether the approach extends beyond simulated household tasks to continuous, high-dimensional physical environments.
