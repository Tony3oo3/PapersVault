## Problem

For deep (L>2) L2-regularized linear networks trained on matrix completion, the non-convex representation cost creates multiple local minima at different ranks, and gradient descent can get stuck at rank-overestimating minima with no known escape mechanism. It was unclear whether SGD's stochastic noise could help the dynamics leave these suboptimal rank-overestimating critical points, and if so, in which direction the jumps would occur.

## Input / Output

The method takes a partially observed matrix A* (N entries from a d_out × d_in grid, specified by an index set I) and a DLN architecture of depth L with hidden widths w_ℓ ≥ min{d_in, d_out} to guarantee full representability. The output is the parameter tuple θ = (W_1, …, W_L) whose product A_θ = W_L…W_1 approximates the full matrix, with the L2-regularized MC loss evaluated only on observed entries. The key structural constraint is that all critical points are balanced, forcing every weight matrix to share the same singular values and rank, so the 'rank of the solution' is well-defined as the common rank of any W_ℓ.

## Method

The paper first characterizes the loss landscape by proving that all critical points of the L2-regularized DLN loss are balanced (W_ℓ^T W_ℓ = W_{ℓ-1} W_{ℓ-1}^T), so each critical point has a well-defined rank shared across all layers. It then defines nested sets B_r of parameters that are approximately balanced and approximately rank-r or less, and proves the main result: B_r is absorbing for SGD, meaning the probability of leaving B_r is zero while the probability of entering B_r from any starting point is non-zero (bounded below by (r/min{d_in,d_out})^T for T = Õ(λ^{-1}η^{-1}) steps). The mechanism behind the one-way jumps is the anisotropic noise covariance of SGD: noise is concentrated along directions that preserve or decrease rank (small singular values are pushed toward zero by L2 regularization faster than the MC loss can push them back), while there is almost no noise along rank-increasing directions. This behavior is fundamentally different from Langevin/SDE approximations of SGD, which would predict symmetric jumps between minima weighted by loss, and the paper shows empirically via annealing and periodic learning-rate schedules that deeper networks (L=4 vs L=3) exhibit more frequent rank-reducing jumps.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.2) establishes that for any rank r, the set B_r of approximately rank-r-or-less parameters is absorbing under SGD: once entered, the probability of leaving is zero, and from any starting point the probability of reaching B_r within T = Õ(λ⁻¹η⁻¹) steps is at least (r/min{d_in, d_out})^T. Empirically, on a 2×2 matrix completion problem with ε=0.1, all five depth-L=4 networks jump to rank 1 within 5000 SGD steps while none of the five depth-L=3 networks do, demonstrating that depth increases jump likelihood. The annealing schedule (high λ=0.1, η=0.2 for ~2000 steps, then low λ=0.001, η=0.02) successfully recovers the rank-1 solution, while switching to low noise before the jump (~step 2000) yields a rank-2 solution that fails to fit the missing entry.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between GD and SGD on the same L2-regularized DLN loss. GD with a small ridge λ and learning rate η converges with high probability to a rank-overestimating local minimum (rank 2 instead of the true rank 1) for small ε values, and no strategy exists to avoid these points. SGD, by contrast, has a non-zero probability of jumping from the rank-2 minimum to the rank-1 minimum, with zero probability of jumping back — a fundamentally different behavior from Langevin dynamics approximations which would predict symmetric jump probabilities. The paper also compares depths L=3 vs L=4, finding that deeper networks jump more readily (all L=4 networks jump vs. none of L=3 for ε=0.1). Two training schedules are compared: an annealing schedule (high noise then low noise) and a periodic schedule (alternating high/low noise), both of which successfully exploit the one-way jump property, with the periodic schedule additionally showing that jumps can occur even after SGD has settled near a local minimum during a low-noise phase.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the representation cost framework of Dai, Karzand, and Srebro (2021), which establishes that L2 parameter regularization in DLNs corresponds to Schatten norm regularization in matrix space, enabling the rank-based classification of critical points. It departs from the broader SGD bias literature (which typically approximates SGD via Langevin dynamics or other SDEs and studies stationary distributions) by working directly with discrete SGD updates and showing that the resulting dynamics have a fundamentally different structure: absorbing sets of decreasing rank that continuous approximations cannot capture. The paper also extends the DLN low-rank bias literature (small initialization, cross-entropy, L2 regularization) by showing that SGD specifically enables escape from rank-overestimating minima that GD cannot avoid, and it generalizes the diagonal linear network SGD bias results of Pesme et al. (2021) to fully-connected deep linear networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The one-way jump result (non-zero probability to decrease rank, zero probability to increase it) provides a clean theoretical mechanism for why SGD outperforms GD in matrix completion with deep linear networks, and it challenges the standard SDE/Langevin approximation of SGD by showing that the stationary distribution must have support only in B_{r=1}, contradicting the full-support predictions of continuous approximations. However, practical impact is currently limited: the theoretical bounds require extremely small learning rates (scaling as λ^{4L+1+(L+1)/(L-2)}), the expected jump time can be astronomically large, and the empirical validation is restricted to 2×2 matrix completion problems. Open questions include whether the results generalize to nonlinear networks (the authors suggest connections to bottleneck rank and symmetric region attraction), whether tighter bounds can reconcile the theoretical depth dependence with the empirical observation that deeper networks jump more readily, and whether the sampling-with-replacement assumption can be relaxed to match practical shuffling.
