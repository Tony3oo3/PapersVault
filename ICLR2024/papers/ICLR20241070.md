## Problem

Domain embeddings (e.g., from collaborative filtering or sentence encoders) encode rich latent information about entities but resist direct human interpretation; existing tools like t-SNE, UMAP, and CAVs offer only static, low-dimensional views. The paper asks whether a large language model can be trained to accept raw embedding vectors as input and produce natural-language descriptions, comparisons, and preference profiles — including for hypothetical entities that have no corresponding real-world name or description.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241070/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence that interleaves standard text tokens with one or more continuous domain embedding vectors (e.g., a 100-dim movie semantic embedding or a user behavioral embedding from matrix factorization). Crucially, the entity's name or any other identifying text is withheld; the embedding vector is the sole carrier of entity information. Outputs are free-form natural language: movie plots, reviews, characteristic lists, pairwise comparisons, or ten-bullet-point user preference profiles, generated autoregressively by the LLM.

## Method

ELM augments a pretrained LLM (PaLM 2-XS) with a two-layer MLP adapter E_A that projects domain embedding vectors from the latent space W into the same token-embedding space Z used by the LLM's native embedding layer E_0, so that an embedding vector occupies the same slot in the input sequence as a word token. Training uses 25 diverse tasks (24 movie-focused tasks plus a user-profile task) whose targets are generated by a larger PaLM 2-L model, with movie titles in the prompts replaced by their semantic embeddings. A two-stage procedure is essential: in stage 1 only E_A is trained (all other parameters frozen) to align the embedding space with the token space, and in stage 2 the full model is fine-tuned jointly (optionally via LoRA). This two-stage schedule prevents the adapter from collapsing to local minima caused by the pretrained token-embedding constraints, and the paper shows empirically that single-stage training fails to converge even on a trivial 2-D toy task.

## Results

On the MovieLens 25M dataset, ELM achieves high semantic consistency (cosine similarity) across all 24 movie tasks, ranging from 0.77 (common with nn) to 0.96 (pitch), with most tasks scoring above 0.88. Behavioral consistency (Spearman rank correlation) is strong for single-movie tasks (0.76–0.96) but drops for nearest-neighbor comparison tasks (0.46–0.48). Human raters (100 per task) score ELM's outputs highly on consistency with plot (0.37–1.0), language comprehensiveness (0.40–1.0), and task quality (0.38–0.96). On the Amazon Product Dataset, ELM maintains semantic consistency of 0.89–0.96 and behavioral consistency of 0.81–0.94 across six tasks, with human evaluation scores of 0.70–0.94.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ELM against two text-only LLM baselines—PaLM 2-L and GPT-4—on interpolation and CAV-extrapolation tasks (Figure 3). For movie interpolation (α=0.5 with 'Forrest Gump'), ELM achieves substantially higher semantic consistency (cosine similarity) than both PaLM 2-L and GPT-4, which are given ground-truth examples and prompted to interpolate textually. For user profile interpolation, ELM achieves higher behavioral consistency (NDCG) than both baselines, which are provided ground-truth user profiles and asked to interpolate between them. The paper attributes this gap to the fundamental difficulty of describing a domain embedding space manifold using text-only LLMs, which lack direct access to the continuous embedding geometry. ELM's advantage is most pronounced at extreme interpolation/extrapolation values where text-only LLMs lose coherence with the embedding space, while ELM maintains consistent outputs across all α values (Figures 4 and 5).

## Relation to Prior Work

ELM repurposes the continuous-prompt and adapter-tuning machinery developed for task control (prefix tuning, LoRA, hard-soft prompt hybrids) toward a fundamentally different goal: interpreting arbitrary domain embedding spaces through natural language rather than steering model behavior. Conceptually, it generalizes CAVs from static, single-attribute probes to a dynamic, multi-task language interface, and it can be viewed as an encoder-decoder system where the adapter encodes domain vectors into token space and the LLM decodes them into text. The paper explicitly departs from traditional interpretability tools (t-SNE, UMAP, TCAV) by replacing fixed visualizations with open-ended dialogue, and from prior work on generating text from sentence embeddings (Bowman et al.) by targeting non-linguistic, task-specific embeddings (collaborative-filtering user/item vectors) that carry no inherent textual semantics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ELM offers a practical mechanism for stakeholders (recommender-system designers, data scientists) to interrogate dense embedding spaces in plain language, including for hypothetical entities where no ground-truth description exists—a capability no prior interpretability method provides. Key open questions include whether the approach scales to very high-dimensional or multi-modal embedding spaces (e.g., protein embeddings), whether the reliance on LLM-generated training targets (PaLM 2-L) introduces a circularity that limits fidelity, and whether the semantic/behavioral consistency metrics generalize beyond the MovieLens and Amazon domains. The finding that two-stage adapter training is essential for convergence, even on trivial tasks, is a practically important lesson for anyone injecting continuous vectors into pretrained LLMs.
