## Problem

Prior adversarial attacks on vertical federated learning (VFL) inference assume a fixed corruption pattern, where the set of compromised clients remains constant throughout the attack. This paper addresses the more realistic and challenging scenario where a resource-constrained third-party adversary can adaptively adjust which subset of clients to corrupt across inference rounds, but the number of possible corruption patterns scales combinatorially as C(M,C), making exhaustive search infeasible for systems with many clients.

## Input / Output

The adversary operates in a black-box man-in-the-middle setting, intercepting embedding vectors transmitted between VFL clients and the server without any prior knowledge of the top model, bottom models, or test data. For each test sample, the adversary crafts an ℓ∞-bounded perturbation (with budget β relative to the embedding range) on at most C corrupted clients' embeddings, subject to a per-sample query budget Q. The output is a pair: the optimal perturbation vectors for the corrupted embeddings and the selected corruption pattern (which C of M clients to compromise) for each inference round.

## Method

The attack is decomposed into an inner adversarial example generation (AEG) problem and an outer corruption pattern selection (CPS) problem. For AEG with a fixed corruption pattern, the Natural Evolution Strategy (NES) is used for zeroth-order gradient estimation, requiring only function evaluations and fewer queries than finite-difference methods. For CPS, the paper establishes an equivalence with a multi-armed bandit problem where each arm corresponds to a distinct C-client corruption pattern and the reward is the resulting attack success rate. The key contribution is the E-TS algorithm (Thompson Sampling with Empirical Maximum Reward), which maintains an empirical maximum reward per arm to construct a shrinking 'competitive set' of arms, restricting exploration to this subset rather than the full combinatorial space. This yields a regret bound of (N−D)O(1) + D·O(log T), where D is the number of competitive arms, improving over plain TS's N·O(log T) bound by reducing non-competitive arm pulls from O(log T) to O(1).

## Results

Across six VFL datasets (Credit, Real-Sim, FashionMNIST, CIFAR-10, Caltech-7, IMDB), the proposed E-TS attack achieves an attack success rate (ASR) of 38%–99% for targeted attacks and 41%–99% for untargeted attacks, uniformly outperforming all baselines. E-TS converges to the optimal corruption pattern significantly faster than plain Thompson Sampling (TS), with the gap widening as the exploration space grows (e.g., from C(7,2)=21 arms to C(28,3)=3276 arms). The regret bound of E-TS is (N−D)O(1) + D·O(log T), where N is the total number of arms and D is the number of competitive arms, compared to N·O(log T) for plain TS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares E-TS against four baselines: (1) Fixed optimal corruption pattern (upper bound), (2) Fixed random corruption pattern, (3) Random Corruption (RC), and (4) Plain Thompson Sampling (TS). E-TS matches the ASR of the fixed optimal pattern across all six datasets but reaches it substantially faster than plain TS, which also converges to the optimal pattern but requires more rounds of exploration. Random Corruption produces a stable but consistently sub-optimal ASR (roughly 20–60% depending on the dataset) because it ignores historical attack feedback. The performance gap between E-TS and TS becomes more pronounced in larger exploration spaces: with C(28,3)=3276 arms, E-TS converges noticeably faster than TS, confirming the theoretical advantage of restricting exploration to the empirical competitive set. Under defense strategies (randomized smoothing, dropout, manifold projection), E-TS ASR drops but remains non-zero, with manifold projection causing the largest reduction (35%–72% ASR drop for untargeted attacks).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends prior VFL adversarial attack work (Pang et al. 2022, Qiu et al. 2022), which assumed a fixed corruption pattern, to the more realistic setting where the adversary adaptively selects which clients to corrupt across inference rounds. The adaptive selection is formulated as a multi-armed bandit problem, building on Thompson Sampling (Agrawal & Goyal, 2012), while the inner perturbation optimization leverages Natural Evolution Strategy (Ilyas et al., 2018) for zeroth-order gradient estimation under query constraints. The key methodological departure from standard TS is the E-TS algorithm, which introduces an empirical maximum reward estimate to restrict exploration to a competitive subset of arms, addressing the combinatorial explosion of corruption patterns (N = C(M,C)) that makes plain TS impractical for large client counts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As VFL is increasingly deployed in finance, healthcare, and IoT, understanding adaptive adversarial threats to its inference pipeline is practically critical. The regret bound of (N-D)O(1) + D·O(log T) provides a clean theoretical guarantee that E-TS outperforms plain TS when only a small number of corruption patterns are truly competitive, a regime likely to hold in practice. However, the attack still requires MITM access to communication channels and a non-trivial perturbation budget (β up to 0.3), and manifold projection defenses reduce ASR substantially (35–72% for untargeted attacks), suggesting the threat is mitigable. Open questions include whether the approach extends to dynamic VFL settings with client join/leave, and whether the combinatorial arm space can be further reduced via structural assumptions on which client subsets are likely competitive.
