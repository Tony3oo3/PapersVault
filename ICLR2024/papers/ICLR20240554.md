## Problem

The dominant paradigm for decision-time planning in imperfect-information games relies on public belief states (PBSs), which distribute computation across all decision points consistent with public information. This makes PBS-based search infeasible when non-public information is large, since the number of supported decision points grows combinatorially. The paper asks whether principled DTP algorithms can be constructed that avoid PBSs entirely and instead focus computation on the single decision point actually occupied by the planning agent.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a current decision point (game history), a blueprint joint policy, and a belief model (Seq2Seq for Hanabi) or particle filter (10–100 particles for adversarial games) to sample plausible histories. It performs Monte Carlo rollouts from the sampled history for each legal action to estimate action values, then applies a closed-form update (hedge or magnetic hedge) to produce a revised action distribution at the current decision point. Unlike PBS-based methods, no validation step is needed: the search policy is always played, and the entire computational budget is spent on the single occupied decision point rather than being spread across all PBS-supported decision points.

## Method

The paper introduces the update-equivalence framework, which defines a DTP algorithm as equivalent to a last-iterate algorithm when the search-induced policy update matches the last-iterate update at the current decision point. Algorithm 1 provides a general construction: repeatedly sample histories from the decision point under the blueprint policy, estimate action values via rollouts, and apply the last-iterate algorithm's continuous update function U to the local policy and estimated values. For common-payoff games, U is the mirror descent (hedge) update, yielding Mirror Descent Search (MDS), which provably guarantees non-decreasing expected return for sufficiently small stepsizes (Theorem 3.3). For two-player zero-sum games, U is the magnetic mirror descent update with an additional KL regularization toward a reference policy, yielding MMDS, which empirically converges to equilibrium and reduces exploitability. The framework also accommodates variants with subgame updates, belief fine-tuning, and opponent updates, all of which exhibit empirical convergence to AQRE or Nash equilibria.

## Results

In 5-card 8-hint Hanabi, MDS achieves an expected return of 24.62±0.02 using only 2 seconds per move, matching multi-agent RLSearch (24.62±0.03 at 180s/move) and multi-agent SPARTA (24.61±0.02 at 450s/move) while using two orders of magnitude less search time. In the harder 7-card 4-hint variant, MDS achieves 24.28±0.02, outperforming the best baseline (multi-agent RLSearch with BFT at 24.18±0.03). In 3x3 Abrupt Dark Hex, MMDS reduces the approximate exploitability of a uniform random blueprint from 74±1 to 50±1, outperforming all non-MMD-based approaches including PPO and NFSP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In 5-card Hanabi, MDS (24.62±0.02) matches multi-agent RLSearch (24.62±0.03) and multi-agent SPARTA (24.61±0.02) while using 90× and 225× less search time respectively, and outperforms single-agent SPARTA with the same approximate Seq2Seq belief model (24.52±0.02) by 0.10 points. In 7-card Hanabi, where PBS-based methods face severe scaling challenges, MDS (24.28±0.02) clearly outperforms all baselines: multi-agent RLSearch with BFT (24.18±0.03), single-agent RLSearch with BFT (24.18±0.03), single-agent SPARTA with Seq2Seq (24.17±0.03), and single-agent RLSearch with Seq2Seq (24.14±0.04). In 3x3 Abrupt Dark Hex, MMDS applied to a random blueprint (exploitability 50±1) substantially outperforms PPO at 1M steps (85±6), NFSP at 1M steps (91±4), and the first-legal-action baseline (100±0), though it remains above MMD trained for 10M steps (20±1). In Phantom Tic-Tac-Toe, MMDS (50±1) outperforms PPO at 1M steps (89±6) and NFSP at 1M steps (95±1), and matches NFSP at 10M steps (78±5) while being far below MMD at 10M steps (15±1). Overall, MDS/MMDS wins or ties on the primary metric in every comparison where PBS methods are applicable, and is the only non-MMD method competitive in the adversarial 2p0s settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant public belief state (PBS)-based subgame solving paradigm for decision-time planning in imperfect-information games, instead proposing an update-equivalence framework that views DTP algorithms as replicating the updates of last-iterate algorithms. It builds directly on mirror descent (Beck & Teboulle, 2003) for cooperative settings and magnetic mirror descent (Sokota et al., 2023a) for adversarial settings, while adapting the learned-belief-search implementation from SPARTA (Hu et al., 2021). The framework also formalizes and generalizes the earlier observation by Tesauro (1995) that Monte Carlo search is a single step of policy iteration, and is conceptually related to but structurally distinct from concurrent non-PBS approaches like joint policy search (Tian et al., 2020) and 1-KLSS (Zhang & Sandholm, 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a clean, general mechanism for generating principled DTP algorithms with provable guarantees (for cooperative games) that sidestep the combinatorial explosion of PBS-based methods as non-public information grows. The empirical demonstration that MDS matches or exceeds PBS-based SOTA in Hanabi with two orders of magnitude less compute—and that the gap widens in the 7-card variant—validates the scalability argument. Key open questions include: extending the improvement guarantee to adversarial settings (currently only empirical), scaling the framework to AlphaZero-style learned-model planning, and whether the approach generalizes to general-sum games beyond the Diplomacy-adjacent results mentioned in related work.
