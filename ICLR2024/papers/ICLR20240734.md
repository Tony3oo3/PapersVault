## Problem

Existing image attribution methods produce saliency regions that are not small and dense enough, potentially misleading the attribution direction, and they struggle to identify the latent causes of prediction errors for misclassified samples. This paper asks whether fewer, more precisely selected regions can achieve higher interpretability than the full image, and whether a principled selection mechanism can also reveal why a model made an incorrect prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240734/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (112×112 for face datasets, 224×224 for CUB-200-2011), a pre-trained classifier (ResNet-101 with ArcFace or cross-entropy loss), and a prior saliency map from an existing attribution algorithm. The image is first divided into N×N patches (e.g., 28×28 for faces, 10×10 for birds), then grouped into m sub-regions where each sub-region retains d patches ranked highest by the saliency map and zeros out the rest. The output is an ordered set of at most k sub-regions whose selection order encodes their importance ranking, used to compute Deletion/Insertion AUC curves or to identify error-causing regions.

## Method

The paper reformulates image attribution as a submodular subset selection problem: given m sub-regions, select k that maximize a designed submodular function F(S). This function combines four scores — a confidence score from an evidential deep learning network (quantifying in-distribution alignment), an effectiveness score (sum of minimum cosine distances between region features to enforce diversity), a consistency score (cosine similarity between the selected regions' features and a target semantic vector), and a collaboration score (measuring how much removing the selected regions degrades the target response). The authors prove F is submodular and monotonically non-decreasing under the assumption that selected elements contribute to interpretation, enabling a greedy search with the (1−1/e) approximation guarantee of Nemhauser et al. The sub-region division itself is saliency-guided: patches are ranked by the prior attribution map and sequentially allocated to sub-regions, so each element in V carries semantically coherent content rather than arbitrary spatial blocks.

## Results

On correctly predicted samples, the method (applied as a post-processing step on HSIC-Attribution) improves Deletion AUC by 8.4% on Celeb-A (0.1151→0.1054), 1.0% on VGG-Face2 (0.1317→0.1304), and 5.3% on CUB-200-2011 (0.0647→0.0613), while improving Insertion AUC by 1.1%, 0.2%, and 6.1% respectively. On incorrectly predicted samples (CUB-200-2011), the method achieves an 81.0% increase in average highest confidence (0.2493→0.4513) and an 18.4% increase in Insertion AUC (0.1446→0.1772) over HSIC-Attribution. The method consistently improves all tested baselines (Saliency, Grad-CAM, LIME, Kernel Shap, RISE, HSIC-Attribution) across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is a post-processing module that improves upon any existing attribution algorithm rather than competing as a standalone method. Against the SOTA baseline HSIC-Attribution (Novello et al., 2022), the method achieves the largest gains on CUB-200-2011 (5.3% Deletion, 6.1% Insertion improvement) and Celeb-A (8.4% Deletion, 1.1% Insertion), with only marginal improvement on VGG-Face2 (1.0% Deletion, 0.2% Insertion), which the authors attribute to noisy images in that dataset. For incorrectly predicted samples, the improvement is dramatic: 81.0% higher average confidence and 18.4% higher Insertion over HSIC-Attribution, 58.4% and 52.8% over Grad-CAM++ (Chattopadhay et al., 2018), and 62.6% and 51.2% over Score-CAM (Wang et al., 2020). The method also outperforms simpler baselines like Saliency (Simonyan et al., 2014), LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016), Kernel Shap (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), and RISE (Petsiuk et al., 2018) when applied as a post-processing step, with the largest absolute Deletion improvements seen over Grad-CAM (e.g., 0.2865→0.1549 on Celeb-A). The method's advantage is most pronounced on fine-grained classification (CUB-200-2011) where local region precision matters most, and it generalizes across network backbones (VGGNet-19, ResNet-101, MobileNetV2, EfficientNetV2-M) as shown in Table 6.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the submodular optimization framework previously applied to interpretability by Elenberg et al. (2017) and Chen et al. (2018), but departs from their pixel-level selection and accuracy-only evaluation by operating on semantically-guided sub-regions and using standard attribution metrics (Deletion/Insertion AUC). It also extends the black-box attribution lineage (RISE, HSIC-Attribution) by treating their saliency maps as priors for region division rather than as final explanations, and introduces a multi-component submodular objective (confidence, effectiveness, consistency, collaboration) that has no direct analogue in prior submodular interpretability work. The use of evidential deep learning for the confidence score and a pre-trained feature extractor for semantic scoring connects the method to uncertainty quantification and metric learning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-in nature of the method—improving any existing attribution algorithm's faithfulness without retraining—makes it practically accessible, and the demonstrated ability to identify causes of misclassification (81% relative gain in highest confidence over HSIC-Attribution on CUB-200-2011) addresses a gap that prior attribution methods leave open. However, the submodularity proof relies on Taylor approximations and the assumption that selected elements are 'small' and 'contributing to interpretation,' which may not hold in all regimes. The computation time scales with the number of sub-regions and greedy iterations, creating an accuracy-efficiency trade-off that limits applicability to high-resolution images; the authors acknowledge this and suggest future work on better optimization strategies.
