## Problem

Vision-language models (VLMs) are aligned to resist text-only jailbreaks, but the vision modality introduces an unguarded attack surface in the joint embedding space. Existing multi-modal adversarial attacks require full white-box access to the entire VLM hierarchy (from output logits to input pixels), and no prior work had investigated compositional attacks that decompose a harmful prompt into a benign text instruction paired with an adversarial image targeting the cross-modality embedding space. This paper reveals that alignment is applied only to the text modality, leaving the vision-to-text alignment as a critical vulnerability.

## Input / Output

The method takes a malicious trigger (a harmful text string, OCR-rendered text image, harmful image, or a combination) and a benign generic text prompt (e.g., 'teach me how to make these stuff') as inputs, and produces a benign-appearing adversarial image (224×224 or 336×336 pixels) whose CLIP embedding closely matches the trigger's embedding. The adversarial image, when paired with the benign prompt and fed to a VLM, elicits harmful text generation. Critically, image generation requires only access to the CLIP vision encoder—no white-box access to the LLM is needed—substantially lowering the attacker's entry barrier compared to end-to-end gradient-based approaches.

## Method

The core technique decomposes a harmful prompt into two components in the joint embedding space: a generic textual instruction embedding and a malicious trigger embedding, which are jointly fed to the LLM so the model interprets the trigger as the subject of the benign question. Four trigger types are explored—textual (via CLIP's text encoder), OCR textual (text rendered as an image through CLIP's vision encoder), visual (a harmful image), and combined OCR+visual—each defining a target embedding in the joint space. The adversarial image is generated by gradient-based optimization (ADAM, LR=0.1) that minimizes the L2 distance between the image's CLIP embedding and the target trigger embedding, converging when the distance drops below ~0.3, typically within 10–15 minutes on a T4 GPU. The attack is compositional: a single adversarial image activates diverse benign prompts across harm categories, and a single prompt pairs with different adversarial images, unlike fully-gradient-based methods requiring per-pair optimization. A key finding is that image-based triggers achieve dramatically higher success rates (~85–87% on LLaVA) than textual triggers (~0.7%), attributed to the modality gap in CLIP's embedding space that makes text-targeted adversarial images appear out-of-distribution to the VLM.

## Results

On LLaVA, image-based triggers achieve dramatically higher jailbreak success rates than textual triggers: the combined OCR+visual trigger reaches 87.0% average ASR across 8 prohibited scenarios, compared to just 0.7% for the textual trigger. On LLaMA-Adapter V2, the combined trigger achieves 63.3% average ASR versus 0.6% for the textual trigger. Automatic toxicity evaluation (Table 2) confirms that visual and combined triggers produce substantially more harmful outputs (Perspective toxicity scores of 36.26 and 46.74) than OCR triggers (33.21) or textual triggers (2.28).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an attack paper rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no external baseline attacks to benchmark against. The internal comparisons reveal a stark modality gap: textual triggers (optimized through CLIP's text encoder) fail almost completely (0.6–0.7% ASR) on both VLMs, while all three image-based triggers (OCR, visual, combined) succeed at 60–87% ASR. The combined OCR+visual trigger consistently outperforms either image-based trigger alone by 1–3 percentage points on LLaVA and 2–3 points on LLaMA-Adapter V2. Across models, LLaVA is substantially more vulnerable than LLaMA-Adapter V2 (87% vs. 63% average ASR for the best trigger), which the authors attribute to LLaVA's stronger image understanding and larger instruction-following model (Vicuna-13B vs. LLaMA-7B). The paper also demonstrates that the attacks transfer to hidden prompt injection settings, where LLaVA achieves 75–81% ASR for indirect injection versus 36–42% for LLaMA-Adapter V2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends embedding-space adversarial attacks (Zhao et al. 2023, Aich et al. 2022) from image classification/captioning tasks to VLM jailbreaking, introducing a compositional decomposition where a benign text prompt is paired with an adversarial image optimized solely in the vision encoder's embedding space. It departs sharply from concurrent white-box VLM attacks (Qi et al. 2023, Carlini et al. 2023, Bailey et al. 2023) that require end-to-end gradient access from LLM output logits to input pixels, by requiring only the off-the-shelf CLIP vision encoder. The four trigger types (textual, OCR textual, visual, combined) and the observation that the CLIP modality gap renders textual triggers ineffective while image-embedded triggers succeed are novel contributions to the adversarial attack literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that cross-modality alignment is a distinct vulnerability—where text-only safety training is bypassed by image-embedded malicious triggers—has broad implications for all multi-modal systems and suggests that per-modality alignment may be insufficient. The compositional nature (one adversarial image activates many benign prompts) and the low entry barrier (only vision encoder access needed) make this a practically concerning attack class. Open questions include whether aligning the full joint embedding space (rather than individual modalities) can close this gap, whether the attacks transfer to closed-source systems with unknown vision encoders, and how to design embedding-space safety filters that cover the vast space of possible malicious triggers without excessive false positives.
