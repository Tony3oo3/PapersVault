## Problem

Current neural models fail at systematic generalization—learning compositional rules from limited data and extrapolating to novel combinations. Existing neural-symbolic approaches like NeSS achieve strong results but require extensive domain-specific knowledge (handcrafted stack operations, category predictors) and specialized training curricula, severely limiting cross-domain transferability. This paper asks whether a single principled framework can learn perception, syntax, and semantics jointly from data alone, without domain-specific scaffolding.

## Input / Output

NSR accepts heterogeneous inputs—natural language commands (SCAN), string manipulation commands (PCFG), raw handwritten arithmetic expression images (HINT), and English sentences (compositional MT)—and produces corresponding outputs: action sequences, transformed strings, integer results, and French translations. For image inputs, a pre-trained ResNet-18 handles perception; for text inputs, the perception module is bypassed entirely. Internally, all inputs are converted into a Grounded Symbol System: a directed tree where each node is a triplet of (grounded input segment, abstract symbol, semantic value), with edges encoding semantic dependencies between nodes.

## Method

NSR is a modular architecture with three trainable components: a neural perception module that grounds raw inputs into symbolic sequences, a transition-based dependency parser that infers syntactic structure, and a DreamCoder-based program induction module that synthesizes functional programs (using primitives like 0, inc, dec, ==, if, and Y-combinator recursion) to compute semantics bottom-up. Because the intermediate GSS is latent and non-differentiable, training uses a novel deduction-abduction algorithm: a greedy deduction produces an initial GSS, then a top-down search-based abduction refines it by exploring local neighborhoods in perception (swapping symbols), syntax (rotating arcs), and semantics (adjusting values) until the output matches ground truth, which then serves as pseudo-supervision for independent supervised training of each module. The design embeds equivariance and compositionality as inductive biases through pointwise factorization in all three modules, and the authors prove expressiveness (Theorem 3.1) showing NSR can represent any finite seq2seq dataset using only four primitives {0, inc, ==, if}.

## Results

NSR achieves 100% test accuracy on all four SCAN splits (SIMPLE, JUMP, AROUND RIGHT, LENGTH) and all three PCFG splits (i.i.d., systematicity, productivity). On HINT, NSR reaches 90.1% average accuracy with symbol input and 76.0% with image input, surpassing the previous best (Transformer) by approximately 23 percentage points on the image-input setting. On the compositional machine translation task, NSR attains 100% generalization accuracy, matching NeSS and far exceeding Seq2Seq (12.5%) and Transformer (14.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

NSR ties NeSS on SCAN (both reach 100% on all splits) but decisively outperforms it on PCFG (100% vs. ≈0) and HINT (90.1% vs. ≈0), where NeSS's stack-machine architecture cannot represent binary functions or handle the larger vocabulary. Against pure neural baselines, NSR's advantage is most dramatic on generalization splits: on SCAN LENGTH, NSR scores 100% versus 20.0% (Transformer, Csordás et al.) and 15.9% (equivariant Seq2Seq); on PCFG productivity, NSR reaches 100% versus 85% (Transformer). On HINT with image input, NSR (76.0% avg) surpasses Transformer (53.1%), LSTM (51.5%), and GRU (35.9%), with the largest gap on the LL subset (58.2% vs. 19.0%). On compositional machine translation, NSR matches NeSS at 100% while Seq2Seq and Transformer score only 12.5% and 14.4% respectively. The paper notes that NeSS requires extensive domain-specific knowledge (category predictors, handcrafted stack operations) and a manually curated training curriculum, whereas NSR needs neither, making it more transferable across domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

NSR extends the neural-symbolic compositional generalization line initiated by NeSS (Chen et al., 2020) by replacing its handcrafted stack machine with a modular pipeline of neural perception, transition-based dependency parsing, and DreamCoder-style program induction, all unified under a data-derived Grounded Symbol System. The key conceptual departure is that the symbolic structure (syntax tree + functional programs) emerges entirely from training data via a novel deduction-abduction algorithm, rather than being specified by domain experts as in NeSS or LANE. The theoretical framing through equivariance and compositionality as necessary inductive biases connects to Gordon et al. (2019) and Zhang et al. (2022), but NSR is the first to prove expressiveness with only four Peano primitives and to formalize the learning objective as posterior sampling over a latent symbol tree.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NSR demonstrates that minimal domain-specific knowledge suffices for systematic generalization across heterogeneous tasks (semantic parsing, string manipulation, arithmetic, machine translation), a property that domain-specific symbolic scaffolding like NeSS lacks entirely (NeSS scores ≈0% on PCFG and HINT). The deduction-abduction training algorithm offers a practical recipe for end-to-end learning of non-differentiable symbolic intermediates without REINFORCE-style variance. However, the deterministic functional programs cannot represent probabilistic semantics (e.g., multiple valid translations), and the approach's scalability to noisy, high-cardinality real-world concept spaces remains untested, leaving open whether the GSS framework can handle the combinatorial explosion of practical NLP or vision tasks.
