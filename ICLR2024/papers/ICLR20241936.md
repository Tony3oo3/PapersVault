## Problem

Current language models are trained in social isolation, memorizing corpora without experiencing the iterative feedback and behavioral adjustment that humans use to internalize social norms. Existing alignment methods—SFT with human-annotated data and RLHF with reward models—suffer from high annotation costs, reward gaming, and produce rigid 'safe answer' refusals rather than genuine social understanding. This paper asks whether LMs can learn social alignment from simulated social interactions in a way that is more scalable, stable, and robust to adversarial attacks than reward-based approaches.

## Input / Output

The training data is structured as Instruction-Input-Output triplets (following the Alpaca format), but uniquely organized into mini-batches where the same instruction and input are paired with multiple responses at varying alignment ratings (typically one high-rated and three low-rated responses per batch of four). The model's output in Stages 1 and 2 is a single socially aligned response or a feedback rationale, respectively; in Stage 3 (Realignment), the output is a concatenated feedback rationale followed by a realigned response, explicitly conditioned on a misaligned response embedded in the instruction to defend against jailbreaking prompts.

## Method

The approach has two components: (1) SANDBOX, a simulated society of 100 LM-based agents in a 10×10 gridworld that interact via a Back-Scatter protocol where a center agent generates a draft response, nearby agents provide collective ratings and detailed feedback, and the center agent iteratively revises its response, with a memory system ensuring consistency across interactions; and (2) Stable Alignment, a three-stage training procedure—Imitation (CPO on aligned vs. misaligned responses), Self-Critic (SFT to learn generating feedback rationales), and Realignment (CPO conditioned on misaligned responses to produce feedback + corrected response). The key technical novelty is the Contrastive Preference Optimization (CPO) loss, which combines SFT loss on the highest-rated response with a rating-modulated contrastive penalty (analogous to triplet loss) on lower-rated responses in the same mini-batch, where the margin scales proportionally to the rating difference. This eliminates the need for an online reward model, making training more stable and deployable in resource-constrained settings compared to RLHF.

## Results

Stable Alignment (IL+SC+RA) achieves the best score across all six alignment benchmarks: 7.35/10 on Anthropic HH, 8.23/10 on HH-Adversarial, 0.78 ACC on Moral Stories, 0.73 ACC on MIC, 0.65 ACC on ETHICS-Deontology, and 0.53 MC1 on TruthfulQA. The largest margin over the strongest baseline (RRHF) appears on the adversarial HH-A task (8.23 vs. 6.24) and TruthfulQA (0.53 vs. 0.38). In human preference evaluations, Stable Alignment is rated at least as good as ChatGPT 66% of the time on HHH and 69% on HHH-A, and significantly outperforms ChatGPT on engagement (M=4.68 vs. 4.15, p<.001).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Stable Alignment outperforms all six baselines on every benchmark. Against the closest competitor RRHF (ranking-loss fine-tuning), it gains +0.95 on HH, +1.99 on HH-A, +0.04 on Moral Stories, +0.06 on MIC, +0.02 on ETHICS, and +0.15 on TruthfulQA. DPO (direct preference optimization) is second-best on HH (6.54) and HH-A (5.83) but trails Stable Alignment by 0.81 and 2.40 respectively. TRLX (open-source RLHF) and Chain-of-Hindsight (verbal-reward fine-tuning) are mid-pack, with TRLX at 5.69/5.22 and Chain-of-Hindsight at 6.13/5.72 on HH/HH-A. The instruction-tuned Alpaca baseline actually degrades under adversarial attack (HH-A drops from LLaMA's 3.28 to 2.52), while the base LLaMA model is the weakest overall. Notably, Stable Alignment narrows the gap to ChatGPT to within 0.37 on HH and 0.20 on HH-A, and in human evaluations is preferred over ChatGPT on engagement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant RLHF paradigm (which requires an online reward model as a proxy for human judgment) by proposing an offline, simulation-based alternative: SANDBOX, a multi-agent social environment where LM agents interact via a Back-Scatter protocol to generate richer alignment data (collective ratings, detailed feedback, iterative revisions) than binary preference pairs. Conceptually, it draws on social simulation work (Park et al. 2023, Aher et al. 2023) and Leike et al.'s (2017) grid-world alignment experiments, but repurposes them for data generation rather than behavioral study. The training algorithm, CPO, is a contrastive objective with rating-modulated margins that sits between SFT and DPO/RRHF in the method family, but its key distinction is that the contrastive signal comes from multi-agent social consensus rather than a single reward model or pairwise human preference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Stable Alignment offers a practically important alternative to RLHF by eliminating the need for a reward model in memory during training, making alignment more accessible in resource-constrained settings while also improving robustness against jailbreaking (the Realignment stage specifically addresses this). The finding that instruction-tuned models (Alpaca) can actually degrade adversarial robustness relative to base models, and that scaling from 6.8B to 175B parameters yields negligible alignment gains, challenges assumptions about what drives alignment quality. Open questions include extending to multi-turn dialogue, accommodating dynamic/evolving societal norms, multilingual generalization, and whether the simulated consensus truly captures the diversity of real-world value judgments or merely reflects the training distribution of the agent LMs.
