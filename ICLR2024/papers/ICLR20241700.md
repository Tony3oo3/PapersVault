## Problem

Backdoor-based evaluation of saliency-based representation visualization (SRV) methods relies on the assumption that only the original trigger activates the backdoor, treating the trigger region as ground-truth salience. The paper reveals that existing backdoor watermarks exhibit significant trigger generalization—many spatially different potential triggers can still activate the backdoor—making the trigger region an unreliable evaluation reference and leading to inconsistent, potentially misleading SRV rankings. Additionally, the existing evaluation (Lin et al., 2021) has implementation flaws including inconsistent gradient handling, threshold-based region selection, and bounding-box-based IOU computation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a benign RGB image dataset (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, GTSRB at 32×32), a small patch trigger (e.g., 3×3 white square) with its binary mask, and a target class label as inputs. It produces a watermarked ResNet-18 classifier that, when given poisoned samples (trigger stamped on the image), predicts the target label, and from which SRV methods (BP, GBP, GCAM, GGCAM, OCC, FA, LIME) generate saliency maps for evaluation. The evaluation metric is the IOU between the top-M saliency pixels (where M equals the trigger size) and the trigger mask, computed directly on pixel regions rather than bounding boxes.

## Method

GLBW formulates watermarked DNN training as a min-max problem: the inner maximization searches for the 'worst' potential trigger—the one with the highest attack effectiveness (lowest loss toward the target label) while being maximally different from the original trigger—using an adaptive optimization loop that iteratively adjusts a size-penalty parameter μ and rejects candidates that are too large, too small, ineffective, or too similar to the original trigger. The outer minimization then trains the network to simultaneously minimize benign loss, backdoor loss (original trigger → target label), and a generalization loss that forces the worst potential trigger to be classified as the ground-truth label rather than the target label. This design is simpler and more stable than the baseline BWTP, which uses a complex distance-weighted penalty loss with a probability activation function; GLBW instead directly identifies and suppresses the most dangerous generalization direction. The adaptive trigger generation (Algorithm 3) uses a loop with four quality checks—size bounds, effectiveness threshold, and overlap constraint—ensuring only genuinely threatening, spatially distinct triggers are used for the generalization loss term.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, GLBW achieves 78.33% benign accuracy (BA) and 93.68% watermark success rate (WSR), while on GTSRB it reaches 93.89% BA and 90.17% WSR. The key generalization metric (Chamfer distance via Neural Cleanse) drops from 3979.33 (Vanilla) to 23.04 (GLBW) on CIFAR-10 — a reduction of over 170×. The percentage of low-generalization effective triggers (PLG) reaches 100% for GLBW in most settings, compared to only 42.20% for Vanilla on CIFAR-10. SRV method rankings via GLBW are consistent across both datasets (e.g., LIME ranks 1st on both), whereas the vanilla method produces inconsistent rankings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GLBW against two baselines: the Vanilla backdoor watermark (Lin et al., 2021) and their own BWTP (backdoor watermark with trigger penalty). Against Vanilla, GLBW dramatically reduces trigger generalization: on CIFAR-10, the Chamfer distance via Neural Cleanse drops from 3979.33 to 23.04 (>170× reduction), and PLG rises from 42.20% to 100%. On GTSRB, the improvement is more modest (Chamfer 217.71→23.73) since Vanilla already has moderate generalization there. Against BWTP, GLBW is clearly superior on CIFAR-10, where BWTP actually *increases* generalization (Chamfer 8070.46 vs. Vanilla's 3979.33) and finds zero low-generalization triggers (PLG=0%), while GLBW achieves PLG=100%. On GTSRB, BWTP and GLBW perform comparably on generalization metrics, though GLBW maintains higher BA (93.89% vs. 88.36%). The practical consequence is that SRV method rankings via GLBW are consistent across datasets (Table 6: LIME=1st, OCC=2nd on both), whereas the vanilla method yields inconsistent and unreliable rankings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Lin et al. (KDD 2021), which proposed using BadNets-style backdoor watermarks as a proxy for ground-truth salience regions to automatically evaluate saliency-based representation visualization (SRV) methods. The key departure is the identification that trigger generalization—the phenomenon where potential triggers significantly different from the training trigger can still activate the backdoor—fundamentally undermines the reliability of this evaluation framework. The proposed GLBW method draws on adversarial training's min-max optimization paradigm and Neural Cleanse's trigger synthesis to formulate watermark training as a problem that explicitly suppresses generalization, rather than merely penalizing it as in the straightforward BWTP baseline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work reveals that the only currently feasible automatic XAI evaluation method rests on a false assumption (no trigger generalization), which has implications for all prior backdoor-based XAI benchmarks. The GLBW approach provides a more faithful evaluation, but its practical impact is currently limited by testing on only two small datasets (CIFAR-10, GTSRB) with a single architecture (ResNet-18) and patch-based triggers of 9 pixels. Open questions include whether the min-max generalization-limiting framework scales to larger models and datasets, whether it extends to non-patch triggers, and what the intrinsic mechanism behind trigger generalization in DNNs actually is (the authors explicitly defer this to future work).
