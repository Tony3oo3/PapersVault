## Problem

Neural network optimization exhibits several puzzling phenomena—progressive sharpening, the edge of stability, grokking, and catapulting—that lack a unified mechanistic explanation. While each has been studied in isolation, no prior work identified a common structural cause in the training data that naturally produces all of them. This paper asks whether a specific heavy-tailed data structure could be the shared origin of these behaviors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240990/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on standard supervised training data (CIFAR-10 images at 32×32, OpenWebText token sequences, and a 1D Chebyshev polynomial) paired with various architectures (ResNet-18, VGG-11, ViT, GPT-2, small MLPs, and a two-layer linear net). The key diagnostic input is the per-sample loss trajectory: at each GD step, the method identifies training points with the most positive and most negative one-step loss change, then clusters them into opposing groups via visual inspection. Beyond the trained model, the primary outputs are the identified opposing signal groups, their oscillatory loss curves, and the resulting sharpness/oscillation dynamics that explain edge-of-stability spikes.

## Method

The paper identifies paired outlier groups whose large-magnitude features produce consistently opposing gradients (e.g., sky background correlates with both planes and non-planes), showing that GD enters a narrow valley balancing these pairs before progressive sharpening drives growing-amplitude oscillation and eventual loss spikes. A two-layer linear network model with a small fraction of heavy-tailed opposing-signal inputs is analyzed under gradient flow, proving an initial sharpness decrease followed by linear sharpness growth (Theorems 3.1–3.2), with oscillation emerging under finite-step SGD. The authors then decompose Adam's advantage over SGD into three mechanisms—parameter-wise curvature normalization, avoidance of steepest-descent steps on heavy-tailed gradients, and gradient dampening—and validate this by constructing SplitSGD (SGD with dampening plus fixed-size signed steps above a gradient threshold), which matches Adam's performance on ResNet-56/110 and GPT-2 while revealing that only 10–50% of parameters drive the network's oscillatory behavior.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative result is that the proposed SplitSGD variant (SGD with dampening τ=0.9 plus a fixed-step threshold on large gradients) matches Adam's performance when training ResNet-56/110 on CIFAR-10 across learning rates spanning five orders of magnitude (10⁻⁴ to 10³), with only 10–25% of parameters exceeding the threshold per step. For GPT-2 on OpenWebText, SplitSGD and Adam exhibit nearly identical loss curves including the same oscillation patterns, with ~50% of parameters above threshold initially (decaying over training). Theoretically, the two-layer linear model proves that sharpness initially decreases as O(e^{−αt}) (Theorem 3.1) before growing linearly in ‖β‖ to at least (5/8)‖β‖α (Theorem 3.2), formalizing the progressive sharpening caused by opposing signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's main empirical comparison is between the proposed SplitSGD and Adam. On ResNet-56/110 (CIFAR-10), SplitSGD matches Adam's training loss across all tested learning rates (10⁻⁴ to 10³), confirming that the three identified Adam advantages (curvature-aware step sizing, heavy-tailed gradient management, and dampening) can be replicated with a simple threshold-and-dampen rule. On GPT-2 (OpenWebText, early training phase), the two methods produce nearly indistinguishable loss curves with identical oscillation patterns, suggesting they follow very similar optimization trajectories. In the qualitative comparison of Fig. 5(b) (small MLP on 5k CIFAR-10 subset), SGD closely tracks full-batch GD with large oscillations across the valley, SGD+momentum somewhat mitigates the sharp jumps, while Adam smoothly oscillates along one side of the valley floor—demonstrating that Adam's parameter-wise normalization is the key differentiator. The paper does not claim SplitSGD outperforms Adam; rather, it uses the match as evidence that opposing signals are the dominant factor governing optimization behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a unifying mechanism for several previously studied but conceptually disconnected phenomena in NN optimization: the edge of stability (Cohen et al., 2021), progressive sharpening (Jastrzebski et al., 2020), grokking (Power et al., 2022), slingshotting/catapulting (Lewkowycz et al., 2020; Thilak et al., 2022), and simplicity bias (Valle-Perez et al., 2019). Rather than analyzing each in isolation, it identifies a single data-structural cause—paired outlier groups with large, opposing gradients ('opposing signals')—that naturally produces all of these behaviors. The theoretical analysis extends the two-layer linear network framework, and the optimizer comparison builds directly on Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2014) by decomposing its advantage into three concrete mechanisms (curvature-aware step sizing, avoidance of steepest descent, and gradient dampening) that address opposing signals specifically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, testable explanation for why NN optimization exhibits its characteristic instability (oscillation, loss spikes, progressive sharpening) and why adaptive optimizers like Adam outperform SGD—reducing the mystery of these phenomena to a data-structural property that can be identified and potentially mitigated. The SplitSGD variant, which matches Adam using only dampening and threshold-based fixed steps, demonstrates that the insight translates to practical optimization gains. Key open questions remain: whether the oscillation induced by opposing signals is beneficial (serving as exploration or regularization) or merely a nuisance, whether reducing their influence is necessary for improved optimization or merely coincides with it, and whether the identification method (currently relying on visual inspection) can be made fully algorithmic. The theoretical model is deliberately simplified (two-layer linear, misspecified regression), so extending the analysis to deeper nonlinear networks remains future work.
