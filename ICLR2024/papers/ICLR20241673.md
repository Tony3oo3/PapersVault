## Problem

Language models in few-shot learning can imitate incorrect or harmful patterns from their prompt context, but prior work only measured input-output accuracy on misleading prompts without understanding the internal mechanism. This paper asks: at which stage of the model's computation does the incorrect imitation emerge, and which specific components cause it? The authors conjecture that the model initially encodes the correct answer but is later overridden by context-following behavior.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard few-shot classification prompts (k demonstrations with correct or permuted labels across 14 text classification datasets) and the intermediate hidden states h_ℓ^(n) at each layer ℓ of the residual stream. It produces per-layer calibrated accuracy curves (via the logit lens, requiring no additional training), identifies a small set of false induction heads (5 out of ~288 for GPT-J), and reports the percentage reduction in the correct-vs-incorrect accuracy gap after ablating those heads. The logit lens applies the final unembedding matrix W_U to LayerNorm(h_ℓ) at each layer, yielding a distribution over the vocabulary without any probe training.

## Method

The core technique is a two-stage mechanistic analysis. First, the logit lens decodes next-token predictions from every intermediate layer, revealing that correct and incorrect demonstrations yield similar accuracy at early layers but sharply diverge at a model-specific 'critical layer' (e.g., layer 13–14 for GPT-J), after which incorrect demonstrations cause progressive accuracy decline ('overthinking'). Second, the authors define false induction heads via three properties—label-attending (concentrating attention on demonstration labels), class-sensitive (attending specifically to labels following same-class inputs), and label-promoting (increasing the logit of the attended label)—and score heads using a prefix-matching score that rewards attention to same-class labels while penalizing cross-class attention. Ablating the top-5 scored heads (identified on a toy Unnatural dataset) reduces the accuracy gap by 38.9% on average across 14 datasets with negligible impact on correct-prompt performance, while ablating random heads has no such effect, providing causal evidence that these late-layer attention heads are the primary mechanism of harmful context-following.

## Results

Across 11 models and 14 classification datasets, the paper identifies a consistent 'overthinking' phenomenon: given incorrect (permuted) demonstrations, decoding from intermediate layers outperforms the full model. For GPT-J, early-exiting at layer 16 outperforms the full model on 15 out of 16 datasets. Ablating just 5 false induction heads (1% of all heads) reduces the accuracy gap between correct and incorrect prompts by an average of 38.9% across 14 datasets, with negligible impact on correct-prompt performance. The identified heads have an average false-label promoting score of 6.5, compared to −0.04 (std 0.41) for 100 sets of 5 random heads, confirming they specifically boost the probability of incorrect labels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability paper without external method baselines; the key comparisons are internal ablation controls. Ablating the 5 identified false induction heads reduces the permuted-label accuracy gap by 38.98% on average, while ablating 5 random heads reduces it by only 3.97% (and sometimes increases it, e.g., −17.08% on Poem-Sentiment random labels). A complementary ablation (Table 2) shows that zeroing late attention heads in GPT-J recovers nearly the full benefit of zeroing entire late layers (47.58% vs. 47.72% permuted accuracy), whereas zeroing only late MLPs has minimal effect (37.97%), localizing overthinking to attention rather than feed-forward components. The overthinking effect is consistent across model scale (Pythia-410M through Pythia-12B, GPT2-XL, GPT-J, GPT-NeoX-20B, Llama2-7B) and is unaffected by instruction tuning, ruling out undertraining as an explanation. The false induction heads identified solely on the toy Unnatural dataset generalize to all 14 real datasets, with the largest gap reductions on MRPC (89%) and RTE (95%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the logit lens (Nostalgebraist, 2020) from a diagnostic curiosity into a scientific instrument for tracking when harmful behaviors emerge during computation, and extends Olsson et al.'s (2022) induction heads to 'false induction heads' that copy incorrect labels from context. It departs from prior work on false demonstrations (Min et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2023), which only measured input-output accuracy, by investigating model internals through layer-wise decoding and targeted head ablation. The authors also distinguish their top-down, layer-level approach from the bottom-up, neuron-level mechanistic interpretability tradition (Wang et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023), arguing that extracting predictions from entire layers is more efficient and applicable to realistic tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that harmful context-following is localized to a small number of late-layer attention heads (5 out of ~500) that can be ablated to reduce the accuracy gap by ~39% provides a concrete mechanistic target for mitigating harmful imitation without degrading correct performance. The overthinking phenomenon—where early-exiting outperforms the full model on incorrect prompts—is consistent across 11 models spanning 410M to 20B parameters and 14 datasets, suggesting it is a robust architectural property rather than an artifact. Open questions include whether the effect generalizes to generation tasks, whether replacing head outputs with their mean (rather than zeroing) would close the remaining gap, and whether the critical-layer structure holds for other failure modes beyond permuted labels. The follow-up work by Belrose et al. (2023) applying the tuned lens to prompt injection detection already demonstrates broader utility.
