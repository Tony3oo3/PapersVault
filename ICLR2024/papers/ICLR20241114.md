## Problem

While contrastive RL provides a self-supervised framework for goal-conditioned policy learning, prior implementations have failed to achieve reliable performance on real-world robotic manipulation tasks. This paper identifies and systematically ablates the specific design decisions—architecture, initialization, normalization, and augmentation—that are necessary to stabilize contrastive RL and enable it to solve image-based manipulation from offline data alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241114/cropped_page7_idx8.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 48×48×3 (simulation) or 128×128×3 (real-world) RGB state image and a same-resolution RGB goal image as input, producing a continuous 6-DOF robot action and a scalar Q-value estimate. Goals are specified as single images provided after training, requiring no reward function or human annotation. All learning is fully offline from pre-collected datasets (Bridge dataset for real-world, scripted primitive demonstrations for simulation), with no environment interaction during training.

## Method

The core algorithm uses a contrastive NCE-Binary objective where a critic, parameterized as an inner product between state-action representations φ(s,a) and future-state representations ψ(s_{t+}), distinguishes states reachable under the policy from random states. The key contribution is a set of five design decisions that stabilize training: (i) a simple 3-layer CNN encoder followed by a wide (1024,4) MLP rather than a deeper ResNet, (ii) layer normalization on every layer, (iii) 'cold initialization' of final-layer weights to UNIF[-10⁻¹², 10⁻¹²] to encourage representation alignment, (iv) random cropping data augmentation, and (v) a large batch size of 2048. The actor objective combines the critic value with a behavioral cloning regularizer to prevent out-of-distribution actions. These decisions collectively enable automatic hard-negative mining in the contrastive loss, which the paper shows is critical for avoiding the 'arm matching problem' where the policy matches gripper position to the goal without actually moving objects into place.

## Results

On five simulated manipulation tasks, stable contrastive RL outperforms all baselines on 4/5 tasks, with particularly large margins on multi-stage tasks (push block open drawer, pick & place). On locomotion tasks (Walker, Quadruped), it achieves a 33% higher relative average success rate than the strongest baseline. On real-world WidowX 250 manipulation, it reaches 60% success on the two harder tasks (pick & place spoon, push can) where all baselines fail entirely. The design decisions collectively boost performance by +45% over prior contrastive RL implementations and 2× relative to alternative goal-conditioned RL methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Stable contrastive RL outperforms the original contrastive RL (Eysenbach et al., 2022) on 4/5 simulated manipulation tasks and both locomotion tasks, demonstrating that the proposed design decisions (layer norm, cold init, data augmentation, wide MLP, large batch) are critical. Against GC-IQL and GCBC, it wins on 4/5 simulated manipulation tasks and both locomotion tasks, but loses on one task (push block, close drawer) where partial observability from occlusion hurts. Against PTP (VAE + sub-goal planning), VIP-GCBC, and R3M-GCBC, it outperforms all three on 4/5 simulated manipulation tasks, showing that end-to-end contrastive learning eliminates the need for separate representation pretraining. On real-world tasks, stable contrastive RL achieves 60% success on the two harder manipulation tasks where GC-IQL and GCBC both fail completely (0%). The paper also shows that adding sub-goal planning to stable contrastive RL actually hurts performance by 30% absolute, and that VAE-based representations (used by PTP) are less effective at predicting success in scenes requiring temporally-extended reasoning (the 'arm matching problem').

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the contrastive RL framework of Eysenbach et al. (2022) and the TD variant from C-Learning (Eysenbach et al., 2020), which cast goal-conditioned RL as a self-supervised contrastive classification problem. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the contribution is an empirical study of design decisions—architecture choice, cold initialization, layer normalization, data augmentation, and batch size—that stabilize this existing method for practical deployment. The paper deliberately departs from prior approaches that augment contrastive RL with auxiliary representation losses (VAE, R3M, VIP) or explicit sub-goal planning (PTP), showing that a single contrastive objective with proper stabilization can match or exceed these more complex pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that self-supervised contrastive RL can solve real-world, image-based robotic manipulation tasks entirely from offline data, with tasks specified by a single goal image at test time. The practical design decisions (especially the counterintuitive preference for a 3-layer CNN over ResNet-18, and the extreme cold initialization at 10⁻¹²) provide actionable guidance for the offline RL community. However, the paper acknowledges that datasets and model sizes remain tiny relative to CV/NLP, and it is unclear whether these design decisions will transfer to larger-scale settings. The emergent property that linear interpolation in the learned representation space corresponds to planning is intriguing but not yet formally characterized or leveraged algorithmically.
