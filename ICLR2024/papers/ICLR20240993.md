## Problem

Prompt formatting choices (separators, casing, spacing, enumeration styles) are semantically equivalent yet can shift LLM accuracy by up to 76 points, yet the standard practice reports performance on a single arbitrarily chosen format. This makes model comparisons unreliable—format performance only weakly correlates across models—and obscures the true performance range a non-adversarial user might encounter. No prior work systematically quantifies this sensitivity or provides an efficient tool to estimate it.

## Input / Output

FORMATSPREAD takes a task specification (instruction string, fixed few-shot examples, and an evaluation dataset of ~1,000 samples), an LLM (open-weight or API-gated), and a total evaluation budget E (number of data-point evaluations). It outputs a performance spread interval [min_acc, max_acc] over the space of semantically equivalent prompt formats, along with the specific best- and worst-performing formats discovered. Because it only requires generating completions and scoring them, it works on API-gated models like GPT-3.5 without access to logits or weights, though in that case exact prefix matching is used instead of probability ranking.

## Method

FORMATSPREAD defines a hand-constructed grammar (B0–B3 rules over descriptors, separators, casing functions, and itemization functions) that generates the space of plausible, semantically equivalent prompt formats for a given task, with contextual restrictions to ensure naturalness. The search over this space is cast as a multi-arm Bernoulli bandit problem: each format is an arm, and pulling an arm yields a mini-batch accuracy estimate (batch size B) on previously unevaluated data points. Thompson sampling with informative Beta priors (initialized from the original format's accuracy) is used to first find the highest-performing format with budget E/2, then the lowest-performing format with the remaining E/2, reusing the first phase's posterior as the second phase's prior. The key empirical finding driving the design is that the format-accuracy landscape is highly non-monotonic (~33% of atomic-change triples are monotonic, matching random chance), ruling out local search methods like simulated annealing and motivating the global Bayesian approach. With a budget of 51,200 evaluations over 320 formats, Thompson sampling estimates the true spread within 1 accuracy point, versus 4 points for naive uniform sampling and 11 for UCB.

## Results

Across 53 Super-NaturalInstructions tasks, FORMATSPREAD finds a median performance spread of 7.5 accuracy points (probability ranking) across LLaMA-2-7B/13B and Falcon-7B/7B-Instruct in 1- and 5-shot settings, with 20% of tasks exhibiting spreads ≥15 points and a maximum of 76 points (LLaMA-2-13B). For larger models, 1-shot LLaMA-2-70B (4-bit) shows a median spread of 17.1 points (max 87.6), and GPT-3.5 shows a median spread of 6.4 points (max 56.2) across 320 formats. Thompson sampling estimates the true spread within 1 accuracy point at a budget of 51,200 evaluations, outperforming UCB (within 11 points) and naive sampling (within 4 points). Prompt format is identifiable from the model's last hidden layer with ≥0.98 accuracy using only the top 100 PCA components, and the separability of these embeddings correlates with observed performance spread (r=0.424 for 1-shot, r=0.555 for 5-shot).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare FORMATSPREAD against competing prompt-optimization methods for task performance; instead, it benchmarks the sensitivity of multiple LLMs to formatting and evaluates the efficiency of different search algorithms. Among search algorithms, Thompson sampling (used in FORMATSPREAD) is the most efficient: at a budget of 51,200 evaluations over 320 formats, it recovers the true spread within 1 accuracy point, compared to 4 points for naive sampling and 11 points for UCB (c=2). Across models, performance trends are frequently reversed by format choice: LLaMA-2-13B vs. -70B reverse their relative ranking by ≥2 points with probability 0.141, and LLaMA-2-7B vs. Falcon-7B do so with probability 0.140, with 76% and 47% of these reversals being statistically significant (p<0.05). Increasing model size (7B→13B→70B), adding few-shot examples (1→5-shot), or applying instruction tuning (Falcon-7B→Falcon-7B-Instruct) does not eliminate the spread, confirming that formatting sensitivity is a persistent property across model scales and training regimes. The paper also shows that no single atomic feature (separator, casing, item format) reliably predicts performance in isolation, with S₁ and F_item2 showing the strongest individual effects (22% and 10% strong differences respectively) while S₂, F_item1, and F_casing show none.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the prompt optimization literature (gradient-based methods, continuous prompt learning, gradient-free search) by reframing the problem: rather than finding the single best prompt, it measures the performance spread across a formally defined space of semantically equivalent prompt formats. It borrows the bandit formulation from gradient-free prompt optimization (Pryzant et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023) but applies Thompson sampling to estimate min/max performance rather than to converge on an optimum. The work also connects to the broader spurious-features literature in deep RL and statistical machine translation, and to prior analyses of specific prompt design choices (few-shot ordering, language), but is the first to isolate formatting as a distinct, meaning-preserving axis of variance and to formalize it via a grammar.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that formatting alone can shift accuracy by up to 76 points, and that format-to-format performance rankings do not transfer across models, directly undermines the standard practice of comparing LLMs on a single arbitrarily chosen prompt format. FORMATSPREAD provides a practical, API-compatible tool to quantify this spread under a computational budget, making it feasible to report performance intervals rather than point estimates. Open questions include whether regularization during training or inference can make models robust to formatting, whether the findings extend to open-ended generation beyond classification, and how to integrate spread reporting into standard benchmarking pipelines without prohibitive cost.
