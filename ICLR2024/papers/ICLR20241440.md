## Problem

Existing human motion generative models are constrained to a single fixed framerate because they treat motions as discrete pose sequences, forcing them to downsample high-framerate data (losing high-frequency details) and discard low-framerate samples (losing global structure). High-framerate generation further imposes prohibitive memory costs and slow sampling on current diffusion-based architectures, making it an underexplored task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241440/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At training, NeRM consumes motion clips of fixed pose count m sampled at random centers and random framerates (20-250 fps in HumanML3D) from full-length sequences, along with optional conditions (CLIP text embeddings or learnable action-label embeddings). At inference, the user specifies a target framerate s and duration l; the model outputs a 3D motion sequence (joint rotations or positions, J x D per frame) at that exact framerate by querying the continuous temporal field at the corresponding normalized coordinates. A distinctive capability is temporal sub-sampling: individual poses can be generated at arbitrary time steps without first producing all preceding frames.

## Method

NeRM is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 learns a variational implicit neural representation: each motion is encoded as a latent code z drawn from a per-sample normal distribution N(mu_i, Sigma_i) whose parameters are directly optimized (no encoder), and a shared 9-layer MLP decoder f_theta maps (temporal coordinate t, z, framerate s) to a pose. The temporal coordinate is embedded via Fourier features enriched by Codebook-Coordinate Attention (CCA), which cross-attends learnable queries against a pre-trained VQ codebook to inject motion-relevant prototypes into each coordinate's feature. Training samples random clips at random framerates from the full motion, enabling multi-framerate training without any downsampling or discarding. Stage 2 trains a ViT-based latent diffusion model (with long skip connections) on the optimized latent codes, supporting conditional generation via classifier-free guidance with text or action conditions. The key architectural insight is that representing motion as a continuous field over normalized temporal coordinates decouples generation framerate from sequence length and model size, avoiding the memory explosion that discrete-sequence diffusion models suffer at high framerates.

## Results

On HumanML3D (text-to-motion), NeRM with native-framerate training achieves FID 0.389, R-Precision 0.779, and MultimodalDist 3.178, outperforming the strongest baseline MLD (FID 0.473) and MDM (FID 0.544). On KIT, NeRM reaches FID 0.472 and R-Precision 0.736, slightly trailing MLD on FID (0.404) but matching or exceeding it on R-Precision and MultimodalDist. For high-framerate generation (Table 2), NeRM's clip-FID at 120 fps is 1.315, dramatically lower than MLD (7.878), MDM (8.401), and T2M (11.264), confirming its unique advantage in preserving high-frequency motion details.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeRM (native-framerate) achieves the best FID on HumanML3D (0.389 vs. MLD's 0.473 and MDM's 0.544) and the best R-Precision (0.779), confirming that training on raw varied-framerate data improves global motion quality. On KIT, MLD retains a slight FID edge (0.404 vs. 0.472), but NeRM matches or exceeds MLD on R-Precision (0.736 vs. 0.734) and MultimodalDist (3.189 vs. 3.204). The most striking advantage appears in high-framerate generation: at 120 fps, NeRM's clip-FID of 1.315 is roughly 6× lower than MLD (7.878) and 8.5× lower than MDM (8.401), since baselines must rely on SLERP interpolation that introduces foot-sliding artifacts. For action-to-motion (Table 3), NeRM is competitive but does not uniformly dominate—MDM achieves lower FIDtest on UESTC (12.81 vs. 14.23) and MLD lower FIDtrain on HumanAct12 (0.077 vs. 0.106)—though NeRM leads on Accuracy (0.956 vs. 0.954 for MLD on UESTC) and Diversity. Against the INR-based baseline INR-MLP (Cervantes et al., 2022), NeRM wins on Accuracy and Diversity while INR-MLP has a lower FIDtest on UESTC (15.00 vs. 14.23 is actually NeRM winning; INR-MLP is 15.00), confirming that the diffusion-based latent modeling and codebook-enhanced representation provide meaningful gains over a pure INR-MLP approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeRM sits at the intersection of implicit neural representations (INRs) and latent diffusion models, drawing directly from NeRF's coordinate-to-signal paradigm, the VAE re-parameterization trick, and Stable Diffusion's latent-space generative framework. It departs from concurrent INR-based motion works (NeMF, NeMo, Cervantes et al.) by replacing per-motion re-training with a shared decoder conditioned on a variational latent code, and by using a diffusion model rather than GMM or deterministic decoding to capture the latent distribution. Compared to discrete-sequence motion diffusion models (MDM, MLD), NeRM's key conceptual shift is representing motion as a continuous function over normalized temporal coordinates, which naturally accommodates arbitrary framerates and enables clip-based training without the memory overhead of full-length sequences.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NeRM addresses a practical bottleneck in motion synthesis: the inability of current generative models to exploit high-framerate training data or produce high-framerate outputs without prohibitive memory costs. The clip-FID metric and the demonstrated 120 fps generation with a simple MLP decoder offer a concrete path toward production-quality animation at variable framerates. However, limitations remain: training is slow due to per-sample latent optimization, the model cannot extrapolate beyond the highest framerate in training data, it is restricted to fixed body shapes, and it does not yet support fine-grained internal conditions like keyframes or trajectories. Whether the variational INR + latent diffusion recipe generalizes to other continuous-signal generation tasks (e.g., audio, video) is an open question.
