## Problem

As LLM-based writing assistants become ubiquitous, there is a risk that millions of users relying on the same model will produce increasingly homogeneous content, creating an algorithmic monoculture. While prior work has shown LLMs can bias user opinions, it remains unclear whether co-writing with LLMs systematically reduces the diversity of content produced across a population of writers, and whether this effect differs between base models and those fine-tuned with human feedback.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes 300 argumentative essays (~300 words each) written by 38 participants across three conditions (solo, GPT3, InstructGPT) on 10 fixed topics, along with character-level keystroke logs from the CoAuthor platform that attribute every token to either the user or the model. Outputs include per-essay and corpus-level homogenization scores (via Rouge-L and BertScore pairwise similarity), n-gram type-token ratios for n=1–5, key point diversity scores (via gpt-3.5-turbo summarization followed by agglomerative clustering with Rouge-L distance), and lossless compression ratios (LZMA, ZLIB, GZIP). The character-level attribution is the critical input that enables disentangling model-contributed from user-contributed text in all downstream analyses.

## Method

The paper designs a controlled within-subjects experiment where each of 38 Upwork writers produces one essay per condition (solo, GPT3 via davinci, InstructGPT via text-davinci-003) on randomized topics using the CoAuthor TAB-key suggestion interface, with decoding parameters biased toward higher entropy (temperature 0.9, frequency penalty 0.5). The core analytical contribution is a suite of diversity metrics operating at two levels: lexical (n-gram type-token ratios, 5-gram frequency distributions) and content (key point extraction via gpt-3.5-turbo, then agglomerative clustering to count unique semantic units). A key disentanglement technique attributes each key point and each 5-gram to the user or model using the keystroke logs, allowing the authors to test whether reduced diversity stems from model-contributed text or from changes in user writing behavior. The central finding is that InstructGPT (but not GPT3, despite similar usage statistics) significantly increases homogenization and reduces both lexical and key point diversity, and this effect is attributable to InstructGPT contributing less diverse text rather than to altered user behavior.

## Results

Writing with InstructGPT produces a statistically significant increase in essay homogenization (key-point Rouge-L: 0.1660 vs. 0.1536 for Solo and 0.1578 for GPT3; p < 0.05) and a significant reduction in both lexical diversity (fraction of unique 5-grams: 0.977 vs. 0.991 Solo) and content diversity (fraction of unique key points at threshold 0.7: 0.738 vs. 0.792 Solo). In contrast, GPT3 shows no statistically significant difference from Solo on any homogenization or diversity metric despite contributing a similar fraction of text (~35%) and key points (~40%). The effect is attributed to InstructGPT's less diverse suggestions (pairwise suggestion similarity 0.20 vs. 0.11 Rouge-L for GPT3), while user-written text remains unaffected by model assistance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three writing conditions: Solo (no model), GPT3 (base davinci model), and InstructGPT (text-davinci-003, RLHF-tuned). InstructGPT is the only condition that significantly increases homogenization and reduces diversity relative to Solo, with p < 0.05 on independent-samples t-tests (homogenization) and permutation tests (diversity). GPT3, despite contributing a comparable fraction of essay text (35.57% vs. 32.45%) and key points (~0.4 median for both), does not produce statistically significant differences from Solo on any metric. The critical distinction is that InstructGPT's five suggestions per query are significantly more similar to each other (0.20 vs. 0.11 Rouge-L pairwise similarity), and the model-contributed key points show higher homogenization than GPT3's (p < 0.05), while user-contributed key points remain unchanged across conditions. This indicates the diversity loss is driven by the RLHF-tuned model's less diverse generations rather than by changes in user writing behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the CoAuthor platform and dataset from Lee et al. (2022a) to conduct a controlled experiment measuring content diversity in LLM-assisted writing. It extends the algorithmic monoculture framework of Kleinberg and Raghavan (2021) from decision-making to text generation, and connects to prior evidence that RLHF reduces output entropy (Bai et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023) by demonstrating this effect in an interactive co-writing setting. The diversity metrics (n-gram type-token ratios, self-BLEU-style pairwise similarity) are adapted from text generation evaluation literature, but the paper's contribution is their application to disentangling model- vs. user-contributed text in collaborative essays.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies content diversity as a new evaluation axis for LLMs in interactive settings, with the concerning implication that homogenized co-written content could create a feedback loop amplifying the effect in future model training. A key limitation is that the study uses now-obsolete models (davinci, text-davinci-003) and a single writing task with 38 participants, so it remains open whether the RLHF-diversity tradeoff persists in modern instruction-tuned models. The finding that user-contributed text is largely unaffected is reassuring but based on single-session interactions; long-term behavioral effects of repeated co-writing are unexplored.
