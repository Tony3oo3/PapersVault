## Problem

Existing network binarization methods, developed primarily for computer vision, perform worse than random guesses when naively applied to LLMs, completely destroying their reasoning capacity. The paper identifies that this failure stems from the presence of salient weights in LLMs that cannot be represented at 1-bit without catastrophic information loss, and that no prior work has addressed how to binarize LLM weights while preserving linguistic reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240542/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-trained LLM weight matrices (OPT-1.3B, LLaMA-7B) along with calibration data (for Hessian computation in PTQ) or a training corpus (RedPajama-simple-1B for QAT fine-tuning). It produces a partially-binarized weight matrix where a configurable fraction (5–50%) of salient weights are stored in 8-bit precision and the remainder are binarized to ±1 with a per-column optimal scaling factor, yielding an effective bit-width as low as ~2.7 bits (e.g., 10% at 8-bit + 90% at 1-bit + 1-bit index overhead).

## Method

PB-LLM selectively preserves a small fraction of salient weights (detected by magnitude or Hessian saliency, element-wise) at higher precision while binarizing the rest, breaking the all-or-nothing constraint of standard binarization. For the binarized weights, the paper analytically derives the optimal per-column scaling factor α* = ‖w_F‖₁/n by minimizing L2 quantization error, eliminating the need for empirical search. Under PTQ, the method extends GPTQ's Hessian-guided iterative quantization with compensation to jointly binarize un-salient weights and quantize salient weights (PB-GPTQ). Under QAT, salient weights are frozen throughout training while only the binarized weights are updated via STE, enabling convergence in 1–10K iterations versus the 100K iterations required by prior LLM QAT methods, and recovering near-full-precision reasoning performance at effective bit-widths below 4 bits.

## Results

On LLaMA-7B in a 4-bit quantization setting, PB-LLM with 30% salient weights achieves an average zero-shot score of 66.9 across seven common sense reasoning tasks, closely approaching the full-precision baseline (68.7) and outperforming LLM-QAT (66.6) with only 10K fine-tuning iterations versus LLM-QAT's 100K. Under PTQ, PB-GPTQ with 50% salient weights achieves C4 perplexity of 8.15, surpassing GPTQ 4-bit (8.70) and SparseGPT 50% (15.59). With only 10% salient weights, PB-LLM still reaches 60.6 average, demonstrating the potential toward fully 1-bit quantization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the five classical binarization methods (BNN, XNOR-Net, Bi-Real Net, ReCU, FDA), PB-LLM is a clear win: all five baselines perform worse than random guessing on OPT-1.3B when naively applied to LLMs, while PB-LLM recovers near-full-precision reasoning. In the 4-bit QAT comparison on LLaMA-7B, PB-LLM 30% (66.9 avg) beats LLM-QAT (66.6 avg) and SmoothQuant (63.0 avg), achieving this with 10K iterations versus LLM-QAT's 100K. Under PTQ, PB-GPTQ 50% outperforms both GPTQ 4-bit (C4 PPL 8.15 vs. 8.70) and SparseGPT 50% (C4 PPL 8.15 vs. 15.59) on perplexity, though PB-GPTQ degrades sharply below 20% salient weights (C4 PPL jumps to 72.1 at 10%). RTN (round-to-nearest) serves as a weak baseline at 65.2 avg, which PB-LLM 30% surpasses by 1.7 points. Overall, PB-LLM wins on every direct comparison in the 4-bit QAT setting and the 50%-salient PTQ setting, but loses to GPTQ 4-bit when the salient fraction drops to 10% or below under PTQ alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends network binarization—a technique well-studied in computer vision (BNN, XNOR-Net, Bi-Real, ReCU, FDA)—to the LLM domain, where it had not been previously explored. Methodologically, it generalizes GPTQ's Hessian-guided iterative quantization with compensation to the partially-binarized setting (PB-GPTQ), borrows the Hessian-based saliency metric from SparseGPT for weight selection, and analytically derives the optimal scaling factor following XNOR-Net's L1-norm formulation. The key departure from prior work is the recognition that naive binarization of LLMs is catastrophically insufficient, necessitating a mixed-precision 'partially-binarized' format where a small fraction of salient weights is preserved at higher bit-width.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of 1-bit weight binarization for LLMs, establishing both a clear negative result (all existing BNN methods perform worse than random on LLMs) and a viable path forward via partial binarization. The practical impact is currently limited: the best results (30% salient weights at 8-bit) correspond to roughly 4-bit effective precision, and the 10% setting still shows meaningful degradation. Open questions include whether the salient fraction can be pushed below 5% without collapse, whether the approach scales to 70B+ models, and whether activation binarization can be added for further compression. The finding that a mere 2% of frozen weights dramatically accelerates QAT convergence is a useful practical insight for future low-bit LLM training.
