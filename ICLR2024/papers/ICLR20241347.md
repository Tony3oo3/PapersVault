## Problem

Applying GNNs to graph fraud detection faces two intertwined challenges: severe label imbalance (fraud nodes are a small minority) and a heterophily-homophily mixture (fraud nodes are strategically embedded within benign communities). Existing spatial methods exclude or reweight heterophilic neighbors via graph augmentation, which is unreliable with unlabeled nodes and costly at scale, while spectral methods use a single shared filter across all nodes and struggle with the majority-class gradient dominance caused by label imbalance.

## Input / Output

PMP consumes a multi-relational attributed fraud graph with node feature vectors and binary labels (fraud, benign, or unlabeled), processing each relation independently and concatenating the resulting per-relation representations before a final MLP. The output is a per-node fraud probability via a sigmoid on the integrated representation. Unlike spectral baselines that require the full graph as input, PMP operates in a mini-batch spatial framework, enabling scalable training on graphs with millions of nodes (e.g., T-Social with ~5.8M nodes).

## Method

PMP partitions the neighbor aggregation step of standard message passing into three label-conditioned branches: fraud neighbors, benign neighbors, and unlabeled neighbors, each transformed by a distinct weight matrix (W_fr, W_be, W_un). The unlabeled-neighbor weight is not independent but an adaptive convex combination W_un = α_i·W_fr + (1−α_i)·W_be, where α_i is a node-specific scalar from a shared single-layer MLP with sigmoid activation, allowing each center node to dial the class tendency of its unlabeled neighbors. Crucially, W_fr and W_be are themselves generated node-specifically via weight-generator MLPs that take the center node's current representation as input (Ψ_fr(h_i), Ψ_be(h_i)), so each node adaptively modulates the influence of its homophilic versus heterophilic neighbors without additional per-node parameters. Theoretically, the paper proves PMP is equivalent to a node-specific spectral convolution: each node v_i is assigned its own pair of complementary spectral filters g_fr^i(Λ) and g_be^i(Λ) over the graph Laplacian eigenbasis, which can simultaneously act as low-pass (smoothing) in homophilic regions and high-pass (contrast) in heterophilic regions—something a single uniform filter cannot achieve.

## Results

On the supervised setting (40% training ratio), PMP achieves AUC of 93.97 on Yelp, 97.57 on Amazon, 97.10 on T-Finance, and 99.62 on T-Social, consistently outperforming all 11 baselines across AUC, F1-Macro, and G-Mean. On Yelp, PMP improves AUC by 3.4% over the best baseline (GHRN, 90.57) and F1-Macro by 4.42% (81.96 vs. 77.54). On the large-scale T-Social dataset (5.78M nodes), PMP achieves 99.62 AUC and 95.27 F1-Macro, surpassing the next-best BWGNN (94.72 AUC) by nearly 5 points in AUC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PMP outperforms all 11 baselines on every dataset and metric in the supervised setting. Against generic GNNs (GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE, GPRGNN, FAGCN), PMP leads by large margins — e.g., on Yelp, PMP's 93.97 AUC exceeds GraphSAGE (89.38) by 4.6 points and GCN (59.83) by 34 points. Among fraud-detection-specific GNNs, PMP surpasses the strongest spectral-based baselines BWGNN (90.54 AUC on Yelp, 97.42 on Amazon) and GHRN (90.57 on Yelp, 97.07 on Amazon) by 3.4–3.5 AUC points on Yelp and 0.15–0.5 points on Amazon. On the large-scale T-Social graph (5.78M nodes), PMP achieves 99.62 AUC vs. BWGNN's 94.72 and GHRN's 90.60, while H2-FDetector and FAGCN fail to complete (OOT/OOM). The paper notes that GraphSAGE, despite its simplicity, is a surprisingly strong baseline that even surpasses some GFD-specific models, suggesting that elaborate graph augmentation strategies may be less effective than adapting the model architecture itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

PMP is explicitly positioned as a variant of GraphSAGE, inheriting its ego-neighbor separation design, but departs fundamentally from the dominant GFD paradigm of modifying graph structure to exclude heterophilic neighbors (as in CARE-GNN, PC-GNN, H2-FDetector). Instead of pruning or reweighting edges, PMP keeps the graph fixed and partitions the aggregation step by neighbor label, using distinct node-specific weight matrices for fraud, benign, and unlabeled neighbors. The theoretical analysis bridges this spatial formulation to spectral graph theory, showing PMP implements a node-specific spectral filter, which contrasts with prior spectral GFD methods (BWGNN, GHRN, FAGCN) that apply a single uniform filter across all nodes and require full-graph input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's core insight—that distinguishing neighbors by class during aggregation is more effective and scalable than excluding heterophilic neighbors—challenges a widely adopted design assumption in GFD and offers a simpler, more scalable alternative that supports mini-batch training on large graphs. The node-specific spectral filter interpretation provides a principled explanation for why a uniform filter fails on homophily-heterophily mixed graphs. Open questions include extension to multi-class fraud detection, performance when no labels are available at all (unsupervised setting), and whether the root-specific weight generators generalize to graphs with more than two classes or highly dynamic edge structures.
