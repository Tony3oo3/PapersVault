## Problem

Existing time series forecasting methods assume fixed-length, regularly sampled inputs and pre-determined forecast horizons, causing them to fail to generalise across domains with differing resolutions, periodicity, and data continuity. No single model has been demonstrated to forecast accurately across many diverse datasets simultaneously while also handling irregular sampling, variable horizons, and zero-shot transfer to unseen data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240316/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The DAM consumes univariate time-value pairs (t, v) sampled without replacement from a long-tail distribution over the past (the History Sampling Regime), where the number of context points and the distribution width σ are tunable at inference time without retraining. Rather than outputting a fixed-length forecast vector, the model produces 437 pairs of sine/cosine coefficients (spanning frequencies from 1 minute to 10 years) plus affine and robust-standardisation parameters, which together define a continuous function f(t) evaluable at any arbitrary time for both forecasting and backcasting.

## Method

The DAM uses a 4-layer transformer backbone where irregularly sampled time-value pairs are embedded as TV-tokens and initialised basis coefficients (computed via a Lomb least-squares solver) are embedded as B-tokens; TV-tokens undergo multi-head self-attention and are progressively reduced via Token Merging (ToME), while B-tokens attend to TV-tokens via cross-attention and are updated by a dedicated feed-forward block across the coefficient dimension. The key architectural insight is the History Sampling Regime (HSR), a long-tail distribution p(x) ∝ 1/(1+(x/σ)²) that samples context points from the distant past with non-negligible probability, giving the model a global multi-scale perspective at the same token budget as a fixed-length window. Forecasting is performed by composing 437 pre-selected sinusoidal basis functions whose coefficients are the model's output, making the prediction a continuous function of time with no fixed horizon constraint. A single univariate model is trained end-to-end with Huber loss over 25 datasets (2280 univariate series, 44M+ samples) using both past and future targets, enabling simultaneous reconstruction and forecasting.

## Results

A single univariate DAM, trained on 25 datasets (2,280 univariate series, >44M samples), achieves SoTA (1st place) on 39 of 80 dataset-horizon-metric combinations in long-term multivariate forecasting, with the closest competitor PatchTST achieving 28. Average MSE across 10 benchmark datasets and 4 horizons is 0.336 (DAM) / 0.330 (DAM_HSR-tuned) vs. 0.331 (PatchTST), 0.352 (DLinear), 0.420 (N-HiTS), 0.725 (Crossformer), 0.992 (Pyraformer), and 0.396 (MICN). On 8 held-out datasets, the DAM achieves SoTA zero-shot transfer on 14/16 metrics, outperforming baselines even when those baselines are trained from scratch on the target data. The DAM also outperforms TimesNet on imputation (e.g., 0.0971 vs. 0.1153 MSE on ETTh1) and produces meaningful very-long-term forecasts (5000+ steps) where baselines fail.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The DAM outperforms all six baselines (PatchTST, DLinear, N-HiTS, Crossformer, Pyraformer, MICN) on average MSE and MAE across the 10 standard benchmark datasets, winning 39 of 80 individual metric-horizon-dataset combinations versus PatchTST's 28. The margin is largest against Crossformer (0.336 vs. 0.725 avg MSE) and Pyraformer (0.336 vs. 0.992), while the gap to PatchTST is narrow (0.336 vs. 0.331 avg MSE), with PatchTST winning on datasets with sharp spikes (Traffic, ECL, Wind). On zero-shot transfer to 8 held-out datasets, the DAM achieves SoTA on 14/16 metrics, beating PatchTST, DLinear, and N-HiTS even when those baselines are trained from scratch on the target data (e.g., Illness: 1.964 vs. 1.884 best trained). The DAM is particularly strong at very-long-term forecasting (5000+ steps) where PatchTST and DLinear produce meaningless outputs, and at imputation where it beats TimesNet on 5/7 datasets (e.g., ETTh1: 0.0971 vs. 0.1153 MSE). The main weakness is on datasets with abrupt changes (spikes in traffic, electricity, wind), where the basis-function representation struggles and PatchTST's patch-based encoding has an advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

The DAM builds on the transformer backbone lineage (PatchTST, Informer, Autoformer) and the basis-function decomposition tradition (N-BEATS, N-HiTS), but departs from both in fundamental ways: it replaces fixed-length, regularly-sampled input with a long-tail History Sampling Regime (HSR) that draws irregularly spaced context points, and replaces fixed-horizon vector output with coefficients of a continuous Fourier basis function. The use of Token Merging (ToME) from vision transformers for progressive context compression, and the cross-attention mechanism where B-tokens query TV-tokens, are architectural choices not seen in prior time series work. The paper positions itself explicitly as a 'foundation model' for universal forecasting, a framing that goes beyond the dataset-specific, horizon-specific training paradigm of all prior SoTA methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DAM demonstrates that a single univariate model trained across 25 heterogeneous datasets can match or beat 40 specialized SoTA variants, and notably outperforms baselines trained from scratch on held-out datasets in zero-shot mode—suggesting that cross-domain pretraining is a viable path for time series forecasting. Key limitations remain: the model is univariate (no cross-variable information), struggles with sharp transients (spikes in traffic/electricity), requires substantial training compute, and the 'foundation model' framing is aspirational given the modest 256-width, 4-layer architecture. Open questions include whether scaling the architecture and training set unlocks further gains, whether patching (as in PatchTST) can be integrated without sacrificing irregular-data robustness, and whether multivariate or cross-variable attention can be added without the overfitting that plagues current multivariate approaches.
