## Problem

Current large multimodal models (LMMs) suffer from severe hallucination, generating descriptions that contradict the input image, and they tend to answer 'Yes' to any instruction regardless of correctness. This stems from instruction-tuning datasets that contain only positive samples and rely on a handful of rigid templates, leaving models without exposure to negative or adversarial instructions. The paper asks whether a large, diverse dataset with structured negative instructions at multiple semantic levels can teach LMMs to faithfully follow instructions and resist hallucination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242249/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For training, the model receives an RGB image paired with an open-ended text instruction (declarative or interrogative) spanning 16 vision-language tasks, and must produce a concise text response (≤30 words). For evaluation via GAVIE, the system takes the image content (represented by bounding-box coordinates and dense captions from Visual Genome), the human instruction, and the LMM's response, then outputs two scores on a 0–10 scale: Accuracy (fidelity to image content) and Relevancy (instruction-following). The dataset comprises 400k instructions over 35k images, with 180k negative instructions split across three manipulation levels.

## Method

The core contribution is LRV-Instruction, a 400k-instruction dataset generated by GPT4 using Visual Genome's bounding boxes and dense captions as a textual proxy for visual input, covering 16 VL tasks in both declarative and interrogative formats. Negative instructions are constructed at three semantic levels: (i) Nonexistent Object Manipulation (inserting objects/activities/attributes absent from the image), (ii) Existent Object Manipulation (assigning wrong attributes to objects that do exist), and (iii) Knowledge Manipulation (altering named entities, events, or factual claims in the instruction). For fine-tuning, MiniGPT4 trains only its linear projection layer while mPLUG-Owl uses LoRA on the Vicuna text decoder, both initialized from their respective pretrained checkpoints. The authors also propose GAVIE, a GPT4-based evaluator that scores model responses on accuracy and relevancy without requiring human-annotated groundtruth answers, and demonstrate that a balanced 1:1 positive-to-negative training ratio yields the most robust model, significantly reducing hallucination on POPE, AMBER, and HallusionBench benchmarks.

## Results

On the LRV-Instruction evaluation set, the finetuned mPLUG-Owl-7B (Ours) achieves a GAVIE Accuracy of 6.58/10 and Relevancy of 8.46/10, substantially outperforming all baselines (best baseline: InstructBLIP at 5.93/7.34). On the POPE hallucination benchmark, Ours-7B reaches 0.88 F1 (Random), 0.79 F1 (Popular), and 0.73 F1 (Adversarial), matching or surpassing 13B counterparts like InstructBLIP-13B (0.87/0.76/0.72). On MME, finetuning on LRV-Instruction improves mPLUG-Owl's perception score from 967 to 1299 and cognition from 276 to 328, while boosting negative-sample accuracy from 0.55 to 0.78.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The finetuned mPLUG-Owl-7B outperforms all five baselines on both GAVIE Accuracy and Relevancy. The largest margin is against MMGPT (6.58 vs. 0.91 Accuracy), which produces repetitive, instruction-ignoring responses. Against the strongest baseline InstructBLIP, Ours leads by 0.65 in Accuracy and 1.12 in Relevancy, attributed to LRV-Instruction's negative instructions and more diverse task coverage. On POPE, Ours-7B matches InstructBLIP-13B on Random sampling (0.86 Acc) and edges ahead on Popular (0.73 vs. 0.71) and Adversarial (0.65 vs. 0.63) settings, despite being a 7B model vs. 13B. Compared to the original mPLUG-Owl backbone, finetuning on LRV-Instruction improves Accuracy from 4.84 to 6.58 and Relevancy from 6.35 to 8.46, with the negative-sample accuracy on MME jumping from 0.55 to 0.78. On AMBER, Ours surpasses LLaVA-1.5 in attribute (0.70 vs. 0.64) and relation (0.69 vs. 0.65) hallucination F1, while on HallusionBench it achieves 0.47 accuracy on negative (GT=No) samples versus 0.15 for LLaVA-1.5.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual instruction tuning paradigm established by LLaVA, MiniGPT4, InstructBLIP, and mPLUG-Owl by introducing a systematic negative-instruction component at three semantic levels (nonexistent object, existent object, and knowledge manipulation). Prior work identified hallucination as an inherited LLM problem but addressed it only through evaluation metrics (POPE, CHAIR) or binary yes/no reformulations; here, the authors instead tackle the root cause in training data design, using GPT4 to generate 400k open-ended instructions across 16 tasks rather than relying on the few fixed templates used by earlier systems. The GAVIE evaluation method departs from template-dependent hallucination benchmarks by leveraging GPT4 as a flexible judge that scores accuracy and relevancy without requiring human-annotated groundtruth answers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LRV-Instruction dataset and the three-level negative instruction taxonomy provide a practical, reusable resource for training more robust LMMs, and the finding that a 1:1 positive-to-negative ratio yields the best balance between instruction-following and hallucination resistance offers concrete guidance for future data curation. However, the approach is constrained by its reliance on Visual Genome's dense captions for generation (limiting domain coverage) and by the circularity of using GPT4 both as data generator and evaluator. Open questions include whether the negative-instruction strategy transfers to larger (13B+) models, whether it generalizes to video or 3D inputs, and whether the GAVIE evaluation remains reliable when the model under test is itself GPT4-based.
