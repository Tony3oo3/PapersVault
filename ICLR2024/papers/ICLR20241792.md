## Problem

While large learning rates in SGD training are empirically known to improve neural network generalization, the mechanism behind this benefit lacks principled theoretical explanation. Specifically, it was unclear how the oscillation in training loss that occurs when the learning rate exceeds the inverse of the objective smoothness could be beneficial rather than harmful. This paper asks: how does oscillating SGD with large learning rates drive the learning of data patterns that are crucial for generalization to unseen test points?

## Input / Output

The theoretical model uses a synthetic multi-view data point consisting of 3 d-dimensional patches (a weak signal patch present in every sample, a strong signal patch present with probability 1−ρ, and a Gaussian noise patch) paired with a binary label y∈{±1}. The two-layer CNN processes each patch independently through m filters with ReLU² activation and produces a scalar prediction for binary classification. The key evaluation is on weak data points (lacking the strong signal patch), where only the learned weak feature can support correct prediction.

## Method

The paper develops a theoretical framework on a feature-noise data model to show that oscillation in large-LR SGD training acts as an engine for learning weak features. The core mechanism is that when the network output oscillates around the label, the fluctuations do not cancel symmetrically: because the strong-signal inner products are larger when the output exceeds the label and smaller when it falls below, the sum of (1 − yf) accumulates linearly over time, providing a persistent gradient signal that drives weak-feature learning. They prove that under oscillating SGD (learning rate exceeding twice the inverse smoothness), the weak signal is learned to a constant scale δ, while under small-LR smooth convergence the weak signal remains at initialization scale. This yields a Θ(ρ) test accuracy advantage on weak data points, formalizing the 'benign oscillation' phenomenon. The analysis uses a two-layer CNN with mean squared loss, tracking inner products of weights with signal and noise vectors through a combination of boundedness arguments, sign-stability lemmas, and inductive oscillation-period analysis.

## Results

On the synthetic feature-noise model (n=16, d=64, m=8 neurons, 4 weak data points in test set), SGD with large learning rate (η=1.2) achieves 99.38% test accuracy, while SGD with small learning rate (η=0.1) achieves only 93.75%, with the small-LR model misclassifying weak data points that lack the strong signal. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, large-LR SGD (η=0.75) produces an oscillating training curve yet yields significantly higher test accuracy than small-LR SGD (η=0.01), which converges smoothly but generalizes worse. Theoretically, Theorem 5 guarantees that under oscillating SGD the weak signal is learned to a constant scale δ/4, whereas Proposition 6 shows small-LR SGD leaves the weak signal at its initialization scale Õ(σ₀‖v‖₂), explaining the Θ(ρ) test accuracy gap on weak data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between SGD with a large learning rate (oscillating regime) and SGD with a small learning rate (smooth convergence regime), rather than against external methods. On the synthetic feature-noise model, large-LR SGD (η=1.2) achieves 99.38% test accuracy versus 93.75% for small-LR SGD (η=0.1), a gap of approximately 5.6 percentage points driven entirely by performance on the 4 weak data points (fraction ρ≈0.125) that lack the strong signal. The small-LR model effectively makes random guesses on weak data because it never learns the weak feature beyond its initialization scale. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, the same qualitative pattern holds: large-LR SGD (η=0.75) achieves higher test accuracy than small-LR SGD (η=0.01) despite its oscillating (non-converging) training loss curve. The paper does not compare against other optimization methods or regularization techniques; the contribution is isolating the oscillation mechanism itself as the source of the generalization benefit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feature-learning-based theory of deep learning (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2022; Cao et al., 2022) from the well-studied small-learning-rate regime to the large-learning-rate oscillating regime. While the edge-of-stability literature (Cohen et al., 2020) characterized the oscillation phenomenon and prior large-LR work (Li et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2021; Andriushchenko et al., 2023) studied implicit bias or sparsity effects, none provided a feature-learning mechanism explaining *why* oscillation improves generalization. The key departure is showing that oscillation does not merely regularize but actively drives the learning of weak, invariant features that smooth small-LR convergence provably cannot capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'benign oscillation' framework offers a concrete theoretical mechanism for one of the most practically important yet least understood phenomena in deep learning: why large learning rates generalize better. However, the theory is restricted to a two-layer CNN with a specific feature-noise data model and a strong oscillation assumption (Assumption 4), and empirical validation relies on a single ResNet-18/CIFAR-10 experiment plus a small synthetic setup. Open questions include whether the mechanism extends to deeper networks, different architectures, and whether the specific oscillation pattern assumed is the only relevant one; the paper also notes that more complex oscillation patterns exist for certain learning rates that remain unanalyzed.
