## Problem

Prompt engineering—manually tuning or greedily optimizing text prompts on a training set—empirically generalizes well to held-out test data, yet no principled theoretical framework explains why this discrete optimization does not overfit. Classical generalization bounds for deep learning are vacuous, and existing PAC-Bayes bounds for neural networks remain too loose to be practically useful, leaving the success of prompt engineering theoretically ungrounded.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images and a set of K discrete text prompts (one per class, each a sequence of L tokens drawn from the CLIP vocabulary of ~49,408 tokens) as input. Classification is performed by computing cosine similarity between the CLIP image embedding and each prompt's text embedding, outputting the argmax class label. In addition to the prediction, the framework produces a deterministic PAC-Bayes generalization bound that upper-bounds the true test error, computed from the empirical training error and the negative log-likelihood of the prompt under a language model prior (LLaMA-7B).

## Method

The core insight is that prompts define a finite discrete hypothesis space of size |V|^(LK), onto which classical PAC-Bayes bounds can be applied with a language model as the prior distribution over prompts. Because the learned prompt is treated as a point-mass posterior, the KL divergence between the LM prior and the posterior reduces to the negative log-likelihood of the prompt tokens under the LM, yielding a simple, computable, and remarkably tight bound (e.g., within 6% of actual test error on ImageNet). The paper also introduces a greedy sequential search algorithm that builds prompts token-by-token by maximizing training accuracy, and a regularized variant that incorporates the PAC-Bayes KL term directly into the search objective via structural risk minimization. Vocabulary pruning using the LM's next-token distribution further tightens bounds by restricting the effective hypothesis space. The resulting bound is data-independent (relying only on the pretraining data of the vision-language model through the image encoder) and serves as an effective model-selection criterion, with the best-bound prompts typically achieving the best test performance.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that PAC-Bayes bounds with an LLM prior (LLaMA-7B) applied to discrete prompts yield remarkably tight generalization guarantees: on ImageNet, the bound is 0.319 versus a true test error of 0.256 (within ~6 percentage points), on CIFAR-10 the bound is 0.063 versus 0.028 test error, and on CIFAR-100 the bound is 0.266 versus 0.180 test error. These bounds are data-independent (relying only on the pretraining data of the vision-language model) and significantly tighter than all existing PAC-Bayes bounds for deep learning. The Greedy prompt search achieves test errors of 6.0% (CIFAR-10, B-16), 2.8% (CIFAR-10, L-14), 18.0% (CIFAR-100, L-14), and 54.7% (fMoW, L-14), with corresponding PAC-Bayes bounds within a few percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's PAC-Bayes(prompt) bounds dominate all existing generalization bound approaches across every dataset where comparisons are available. On CIFAR-10, the bound of 0.063 (L-14 Greedy) is roughly 3.7× tighter than Dziugaite et al.'s data-dependent bound of 0.230 and 2.6× tighter than Lotfi et al.'s best data-dependent bound of 0.166. On CIFAR-100, the 0.266 bound is 1.7× tighter than Lotfi et al.'s best of 0.444. On ImageNet, the 0.319 bound is 3× tighter than Zhou et al.'s 0.965 and 2.2× tighter than Lotfi et al.'s best of 0.409. Importantly, the paper's bounds are data-independent, whereas the competing bounds marked with * rely on data-dependent priors that Lotfi et al. themselves argue can be viewed as validation bounds. For handcrafted prompts from CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and Wise-FT (Wortsman et al., 2022), the paper evaluates their generalization bounds rather than competing on accuracy: CLIP's handcrafted prompts achieve 25.6% test error on ImageNet with a PAC-Bayes bound of 0.319, while Wise-FT prompts on CIFAR-10 achieve 4.0% test error with a bound of 0.078, confirming the tightness of the framework across both generated and manually designed prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical PAC-Bayes bounds (McAllester, 1999) for a fundamentally different hypothesis space: rather than applying them to continuous neural network parameters with Gaussian priors (as in Dziugaite & Roy 2017, Lotfi et al. 2022, Zhou et al. 2019), it applies them to the discrete space of text prompts with a large language model (LLaMA-7B) as the prior. The methodological contribution builds on the discrete prompt optimization framework of Wen et al. (2023) and the CLIP zero-shot classification paradigm (Radford et al. 2021), but the key conceptual departure is recognizing that the small, structured hypothesis space of prompts makes classical uniform-convergence and PAC-Bayes tools suddenly effective where they fail for continuous deep networks. The paper explicitly positions itself as using 'not novel' theoretical tools, with the novelty lying entirely in the application to this discrete setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first non-vacuous theoretical justification for prompt engineering, a technique that is ubiquitous in practice but has lacked statistical guarantees. The tightness of the bounds (e.g., 32% bound vs. 26% actual test error on ImageNet, compared to 93–96% for existing PAC-Bayes deep learning bounds) is practically useful: the bound can serve as a model-selection criterion via structural risk minimization, and it explains why greedy or manual prompt tuning does not overfit. Key limitations include the assumption that the CLIP image encoder was not trained on the evaluation data (unverifiable in practice), and the bounds inherit the generalization quality of the pretrained model itself, which the analysis cannot address. Open questions include tightening bounds in the small-data regime (where they become loose), exploring data-dependent priors, and extending the analysis to soft/continuous prompts where the hypothesis space is no longer discrete.
