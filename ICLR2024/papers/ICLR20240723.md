## Problem

Existing sparse SNN design methods are task-dependent: they first train a dense network on a target dataset, then prune low-activity neurons or synapses while minimizing performance loss on that same task. This produces overfitted models that generalize poorly across tasks or datasets, and these methods—largely adapted from DNN pruning—ignore the unique temporal dynamics and neuronal timescale heterogeneity inherent to recurrent spiking networks. The paper asks whether a sparse, stable recurrent SNN can be designed in a task-agnostic manner from a randomly initialized model, without any dataset involvement during pruning.

## Input / Output

The pruning pipeline itself is unsupervised and dataset-free: it operates on a randomly initialized dense HRSNN or CHRSNN (5000 neurons, LIF model with gamma-distributed membrane time constants) and outputs a sparse network with reduced neurons and synapses. The resulting sparse model is then trained on rate-encoded spike trains—images (CIFAR-10/100) are fed as Poisson-distributed spike trains proportional to pixel intensity over 100 ms, and time-series (Lorenz, Rossler, Google stock, wind speed) are encoded over 500 timesteps—to produce class labels or 100-step-ahead predictions. A readout layer (top-10% betweenness-centrality neurons → 20 hidden units → task-specific output) is implicitly pruned as the recurrent layer shrinks.

## Method

The Lyapunov Noise Pruning (LNP) algorithm prunes a randomly initialized dense HRSNN in four sequential, unsupervised steps. Step 1 computes a Lyapunov matrix from the harmonic mean of neighboring Lyapunov exponents and uses a noise-driven probability (analogous to Moore & Chaudhuri's effective-resistance-based edge sampling) to sparsify synapses while preserving the linearized network dynamics within an ε-bound. Step 2 removes neurons with the lowest betweenness centrality below a threshold. Step 3 re-adds a fixed number of edges to maximize degree-distribution variance, counteracting eigenvector localization caused by disconnection. Step 4 optimizes each neuron's membrane time constant via Bayesian optimization guided by the Lyapunov spectrum to restore stability. The key distinction from prior work is that no task performance is evaluated at any pruning iteration; the algorithm preserves only structural stability and spectral properties, yielding a single sparse topology that can subsequently be trained (via STDP or surrogate-gradient backpropagation) for arbitrary downstream tasks.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the LNP-pruned CHRSNN trained with backpropagation achieves 93.45% accuracy with 95.04% neuron sparsity and 96.68% synapse sparsity, yielding a 45.32× SOP ratio over the unpruned network. On CIFAR-100, the same architecture reaches 73.45% accuracy with a 50.35× SOP ratio. For time-series prediction on Lorenz-63, the LNP-pruned CHRSNN (STDP) achieves RMSE 0.679 and VPT 39.24 with only 14.79M SOPs, compared to 157.33M SOPs for activity-pruned CHRSNN (RMSE 1.114) and 867.42M for the unpruned model (RMSE 0.235). The LNP method is task-agnostic: the same pruned topology is trained from scratch on each task without any task-specific pruning iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LNP outperforms all six state-of-the-art SNN pruning baselines on CIFAR-10 accuracy: CHRSNN-BP reaches 93.45% vs. IMP (93.22%), LSNN (92.84%), Grad R (92.54%), STDS (92.49%), ESL-SNN (91.09%), and ADMM (89.53%), while simultaneously achieving the highest SOP ratio (45.32×) among all methods, reflecting the joint pruning of both neurons and synapses. On CIFAR-100, LNP (CHRSNN-BP) matches ESL-SNN (73.45% vs. 73.48%) but with a substantially higher SOP ratio (50.35× vs. 14.22×). For time-series prediction, LNP-pruned CHRSNN reduces SOPs by ~10× compared to activity-based pruning (14.79M vs. 118.77M) while improving RMSE from 1.596 to 0.679 on Lorenz-63 and from 1.987 to 0.901 on Google Stock. The key structural advantage is that LNP is task-agnostic (no training during pruning), whereas all baselines require iterative task-dependent pruning and retraining, making them overfitted to a single dataset. In the cross-dataset generalization test (Table 9), the LNP-pruned untrained ResNet achieves 69.76% on CIFAR-100, outperforming the IMP-pruned model (68.95%) that was specifically optimized for CIFAR-10.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SNN pruning literature by combining three previously separate threads: spectral graph sparsification (Spielman & Srivastava, 2011; Moore & Chaudhuri, 2020), Lyapunov-spectrum analysis of recurrent dynamics (Vogt et al., 2020), and heterogeneous recurrent spiking architectures (Chakraborty & Mukhopadhyay, 2022, 2023b). It departs from the dominant paradigm in SNN pruning—train a dense network on a target task, then iteratively prune and retrain (as in STDP-based, lottery-ticket, or gradient-rewiring methods)—by instead pruning a randomly initialized dense HRSNN in a fully task-agnostic, unsupervised manner. The key conceptual shift is that stability (via Lyapunov exponents) and graph structure (via betweenness centrality and eigenvector delocalization) replace task performance as the pruning criterion, and the method prunes both neurons and synapses rather than only synapses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The task-agnostic design means a single sparse HRSNN/CHRSNN topology can be trained for multiple downstream tasks (image classification, chaotic-system prediction, stock/wind forecasting) without re-pruning, which is practically valuable for neuromorphic hardware where reprogramming is costly. The ~50× reduction in synaptic operations (e.g., 14.79M vs. 744.97M SOPs for CHRSNN) while maintaining competitive RMSE/VPT is a meaningful efficiency gain for energy-constrained deployment. However, open questions remain: the recurrent-SNN baselines are limited to a 1997 activity-pruning method, the state-of-the-art comparisons are against feed-forward SNN pruning methods, and it is unclear whether the Lyapunov-based criterion scales to networks with tens of thousands of neurons or to architectures beyond LIF-based HRSNNs. The paper also does not report wall-clock pruning time or the cost of the Bayesian optimization step, which could be non-trivial for large models.
