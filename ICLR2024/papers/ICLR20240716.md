## Problem

Recent works (Alpaca, Vicuna, Self-Instruct) have claimed that fine-tuning open-source LMs on ChatGPT outputs achieves near-parity with the proprietary model, fueling the belief that imitation can close the open/closed capability gap. This paper critically examines that claim by asking: does imitating a proprietary LM's outputs actually transfer its underlying capabilities (factual knowledge, reasoning, coding), or merely its surface-level style and instruction-following behavior?

## Input / Output

The imitation models take free-form text prompts (user queries) as input and produce generated text responses, fine-tuned exclusively on the model-output tokens of ChatGPT conversations. The training data is collected from a black-box API where the target model's weights, architecture, and pre-training data are inaccessible; the only observable signal is the generated text. Evaluation outputs include pairwise human preference judgments (crowdworker and GPT-4), benchmark accuracy scores (exact match for NQ, pass@1 for HumanEval, etc.), and toxicity scores from the Perspective API.

## Method

The paper constructs three imitation datasets—ShareGPT-Mix (~150M tokens of broad-coverage ChatGPT dialogues from ShareGPT, HC3, and Discord bots), NQ-synthetic (6K QA pairs generated via iterative ChatGPT prompting), and TLDR-Synthetic (200K ChatGPT summaries)—and fine-tunes decoder-only LMs (GPT-2 1.5B, LLaMA 7B, LLaMA 13B) on varying data scales (0.3M–150M tokens) using standard next-token prediction on model outputs only. The key experimental design varies two axes independently: imitation data amount and base model size, enabling a controlled comparison of their respective contributions. The central finding is that imitation data primarily transfers style, persona, and safety behavior (e.g., list formatting, authoritative tone, reduced toxicity) but fails to close the capability gap on factual knowledge, coding, or math reasoning, whereas scaling the base LM from 7B to 13B yields substantially larger benchmark improvements than any amount of additional imitation data.

## Results

On Natural Questions (3-shot), broad-coverage imitation (ShareGPT-Mix) actually degrades accuracy: LLaMA-7B drops from 17 to 10, and LLaMA-13B drops from 20 to 15, while ChatGPT scores 31. Targeted imitation (NQ-synthetic) substantially closes the gap, improving 7B from 17→22 and 13B from 20→27, but still trails ChatGPT by 4 points. On CNN/Daily Mail summarization (ROUGE-1), both broad and targeted imitation help (13B: 27.3→30.7/33.6 vs. ChatGPT's 39.9). Across MMLU, HumanEval, and GSM8K, broad-coverage imitation yields little to no improvement or even regressions, while scaling the base model from 7B to 13B produces consistent gains across all benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ChatGPT (the target model), the best imitation model (13B + targeted NQ data) reaches 27 on NQ versus ChatGPT's 31, and 33.6 ROUGE-1 on CNN versus ChatGPT's 39.9, showing that even targeted imitation leaves a meaningful gap. Broad-coverage imitation (ShareGPT-Mix) fails to match or beat the base LLaMA models on NQ, MMLU, HumanEval, or GSM8K, and in fact degrades NQ accuracy by 5–7 points. Against the LLaMA-7B and LLaMA-13B base models, broad-coverage imitation improves instruction-following and summarization (CNN ROUGE-1: 22.1→28.7 for 7B) but does not transfer factual knowledge or reasoning capabilities. Scaling the base model from 7B to 13B yields larger absolute gains on NQ (+3 with targeted data) and all other benchmarks than adding more imitation data (0.3M→150M tokens), which the authors show produces a flat or declining curve. In crowdworker pairwise evaluations, ~70% of imitation model outputs are rated equal or better than ChatGPT, yet this stylistic advantage does not translate to benchmark accuracy, highlighting a disconnect between perceived and actual capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model imitation framework from Wallace et al. (2020), which studied imitation of black-box machine translation systems, to the setting of large pre-trained LLMs like ChatGPT. It leverages the iterative synthetic data generation techniques from Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2023) and Unnatural Instructions (Honovich et al., 2022) to construct targeted imitation datasets (NQ-synthetic, TLDR-Synthetic). Conceptually, the paper departs from the optimistic claims of contemporaneous imitation works (Alpaca, Vicuna, Koala) that report near-parity with ChatGPT, instead providing a critical empirical analysis showing that broad-coverage imitation fails to close the capability gap on most axes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper challenges the widely-held narrative in the tech community that open-source LLMs are rapidly closing the gap to proprietary systems through imitation, showing instead that the gap is fundamentally in base model capabilities and cannot be bridged by fine-tuning on imitation data. The discrepancy between crowdworker ratings (which favor imitation models) and benchmark performance (which shows no improvement) raises important open questions about how to evaluate LLMs and whether human raters can be trusted without domain expertise. Broader implications include guidance for AI policy (competitive moats for companies with strong base models), research direction (invest in better pre-training rather than imitation), and the ethical/legal questions around whether open-source communities should continue imitating proprietary systems.
