## Problem

Integrals with parametric discontinuities—arising from hard spatial boundaries, collisions, shadowing, and discrete geometry—are ubiquitous in graphics, topology optimization, and computational geometry, yet standard Monte Carlo estimation produces non-differentiable approximations that block gradient-based optimization. Conventional automatic differentiation fails on these discontinuities, and existing solutions are either domain-specific languages with restricted expressiveness or ad-hoc smoothing heuristics. The paper asks whether a general, unbiased, differentiable integral estimator can be built within a standard automatic differentiation framework.

## Input / Output

The method takes an implicit scalar field g_θ(x) (which can be a neural network, Gaussian density, or piecewise-linear basis) defining a shape as its zero sublevel set over a compact domain Ω ⊂ ℝ^d, together with a fixed fiber length ℓ. It produces a scalar integral estimate (e.g., area of intersection, pixel color, compliance) that is differentiable with respect to θ. Fibers are sampled with start points uniform over Ω and endpoints uniform over an extended shell Ω̄ of width ℓ, ensuring unbiasedness near boundaries. The approach is designed for low-dimensional integration domains (2D–3D) but places no restriction on the dimensionality of the parameter θ, so large neural networks can be trained.

## Method

Fiber Monte Carlo replaces zero-extent point samples with fixed-length line segments (fibers), estimating the integral as the average line integral of the integrand along each fiber rather than the count of points under it. Because the intersection point of a fiber with a discontinuous boundary moves continuously as θ changes, the per-fiber contribution is (sub-)differentiable, enabling exchange of expectation and differentiation via Leibniz' rule. The derivative is computed through implicit differentiation: the intersection parameter u(θ) is found by bisection on g_θ(α)=0, and du/dθ = −(∂F/∂θ)/(∂F/∂u) requires only pointwise gradient evaluations of g. The estimator is justified as conditional Monte Carlo, with variance provably no greater than standard Monte Carlo by the Law of Total Variance. The entire pipeline—fiber sampling, bisection, implicit differentiation, and quadrature—is implemented in JAX, requiring only pointwise function evaluations of g and no analytic line-integral closed form.

## Results

In topology optimization (compliance minimization with 50% volume constraint), Fiber Sampling achieves lower (better) objectives than SIMP on both benchmarks: MMB beam 0.745 vs. 0.747, and cantilever beam 0.742 vs. 0.756. Fiber Sampling also uses less material area (0.502 and 0.503) compared to SIMP (0.533 for both), staying closer to the 50% volume constraint. For amortized convex hulls on ModelNet40 (plant category), the method achieves 97.8% accuracy on 40 out-of-sample point clouds after 200 stochastic gradient updates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Fiber Sampling against SIMP (Andreassen et al., 2011), the industry-standard relaxation approach for topology optimization. Fiber Sampling outperforms SIMP on both compliance objectives: by 0.002 on the MMB beam (0.745 vs. 0.747) and by 0.014 on the cantilever beam (0.742 vs. 0.756). Additionally, Fiber Sampling adheres more closely to the 50% volume constraint, using areas of 0.502 and 0.503 versus SIMP's 0.533 in both cases, suggesting SIMP's penalty-based relaxation overshoots the volume budget. The authors note that rigorous comparison would require extensive experimentation, so these results are presented as preliminary evidence rather than a definitive superiority claim. No quantitative comparison is provided for the image stylization or convex hull experiments, which are demonstrated qualitatively or against no prior baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two lines of work: (1) domain-specific differentiation languages for parametric discontinuities (Bangaru et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022; Morozov et al., 2023), which correctly compute derivatives but require special compiler support and are not Turing complete, and (2) standard Monte Carlo gradient estimators (score function, reparameterization), which either don't use cost-function derivatives or require the cost to be differentiable. Fiber Monte Carlo departs from both by sampling line segments (fibers) rather than points, casting the estimator as conditional Monte Carlo (Owen, 2013), and computing derivatives via implicit differentiation within a generic AD framework (JAX). The theoretical connection to Crofton's formula and the Radon transform from integral geometry provides additional grounding, while the implicit function formulation draws on level-set methods from topology optimization (Belytschko et al., 2003; van Dijk et al., 2013).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that gradient-based optimization can now be applied to integrals with parametric discontinuities using off-the-shelf automatic differentiation, removing the need for bespoke domain-specific languages or ad-hoc smoothing. This opens the door to integrating ML methods into physical simulation, differentiable rendering, and computational geometry pipelines that were previously blocked by non-differentiability. The key limitation is dimensionality: the number of fibers needed for constant-variance estimates likely scales exponentially with the integration domain dimension, restricting the method to low-dimensional (2D/3D) geometric problems. An open question the authors raise is whether Fiber Monte Carlo can serve as a general-purpose tool for uniform sampling from manifold structures, which would broaden its impact into PDE solving and computational geometry.
