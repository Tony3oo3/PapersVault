## Problem

Aligning RL agent behaviors with diverse human preferences is hindered by two properties: abstractness (preferences resist hand-designed reward functions) and mutability (preferences shift across users and over time, demanding zero-shot behavior customization without retraining). Existing approaches like RBA reduce attribute evaluation to single-step state-action rewards, ignoring trajectory-level effects, and require retraining whenever user intent changes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240356/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, AlignDiff receives the current robot state, a target attribute strength vector (continuous values in [0,1] per attribute), and a binary mask indicating which attributes the user cares about; natural language instructions are optionally mapped to these via Sentence-BERT cosine similarity against a fixed instruction corpus. The output is a full planned trajectory of length H (32 steps for Walker, 100 for Hopper/Humanoid) from which only the first action is executed before re-planning. The attribute strengths are discretized into V=100 selectable tokens per attribute for conditioning, and the mask is sampled from a binomial distribution during training to support arbitrary subsets of attributes.

## Method

AlignDiff decouples preference quantification from behavior generation into two components. First, a transformer-encoder attribute strength model is trained via a modified Bradley-Terry objective on crowdsourced pairwise trajectory comparisons, producing trajectory-level relative strength vectors that capture abstract attributes like 'humanness' which cannot be reduced to per-step rewards. Second, a DiT-based diffusion model is trained on behavioral datasets relabeled by the attribute strength model, using an attribute-oriented encoder that discretizes each strength dimension into unique tokens (preserving the semantic meaning of 0 as 'weakest' rather than 'absent') and applies the mask multiplicatively before self-attention. At inference, DDIM sampling with classifier-free guidance (scale w=1.5) generates N candidate trajectories conditioned on the target strengths, and the attribute strength model scores each candidate to select the one minimizing the masked L2 distance to the target, enabling zero-shot switching between arbitrary preference configurations without any retraining.

## Results

On the preference matching task (measured by area under the MAE curve), AlignDiff achieves 0.525 average across Hopper, Walker, and Humanoid with synthetic labels, outperforming GC (0.285), SM (0.388), and TDL (0.370). With human labels, AlignDiff scores 0.465 average versus 0.296 (GC), 0.333 (SM), and 0.332 (TDL). In human evaluation (video sorting accuracy), AlignDiff reaches 85.03% average with human labels, compared to 7.06% (GC), 23.12% (SM), and 24.85% (TDL). On the abstract 'humanness' attribute, AlignDiff achieves 0.510 MAE area and 83.11% sorting accuracy, substantially ahead of all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AlignDiff outperforms all three main baselines (GC, SM, TDL) across every environment and label type on the MAE area metric. The largest margins appear on Hopper (0.628 vs. 0.573 for SM with synthetic labels) and on the abstract 'humanness' attribute where AlignDiff leads by 0.10–0.36 over baselines. In human evaluation (sorting accuracy), the gap is even more dramatic: AlignDiff achieves 85.03% average with human labels while the best baseline (TDL) reaches only 24.85%. On the Humanoid benchmark, AlignDiff is the only method that demonstrates meaningful preference alignment (0.327 synthetic / 0.313 human), while GC, SM, and TDL all cluster near 0.25–0.27, indicating they fail to learn useful policies. In the appendix comparison with the original RBA method (policy-library search), AlignDiff (0.621) also beats the improved RBA+ variant (0.445) and the original RBA (0.293) on Walker-S, confirming that trajectory-level diffusion planning substantially outperforms step-level reward optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

AlignDiff sits at the intersection of two research threads: preference-based reinforcement learning (PbRL/RLHF) and diffusion-based decision making. It extends the Relative Behavioral Attributes (RBA) framework of Guan et al. (2023) from a myopic single-step reward model to a trajectory-level attribute strength model trained via a modified Bradley-Terry objective, and it repurposes the DiT-based diffusion planner (Janner et al., 2022; Ajay et al., 2023) as a zero-shot behavior synthesizer conditioned on quantified preferences via classifier-free guidance. The key conceptual departure from RBA is that preferences are no longer distilled into a per-step reward requiring policy retraining, but instead guide a generative planner that can retrieve and recombine diverse behaviors from an offline dataset without any fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap in human-robot interaction: users can express abstract, shifting preferences (e.g., 'walk more humanly,' 'go faster') and the agent adapts in zero-shot, without retraining. The publicly released multi-perspective human feedback datasets and the attribute-strength-model + diffusion-planner architecture provide a reusable template for preference-aligned control beyond locomotion. Key limitations include slow inference from iterative DDIM sampling (mitigated but not eliminated), evaluation restricted to MuJoCo/DMControl locomotion tasks, and reliance on a predefined attribute vocabulary that may not capture all dimensions of human preference. The authors note that integrating AlignDiff with LLM-based higher-level planners for complex task decomposition is a natural next step.
