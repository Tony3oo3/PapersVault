## Problem

Gaussian process regression requires solving a large linear system (K+λI)⁻¹b whose direct solution costs O(n³), and while iterative solvers like preconditioned conjugate gradients and variational approximations reduce this cost, prior stochastic gradient descent approaches for GPs used poorly-conditioned primal objectives, additive-noise random features, or mixed noise types that limited their effectiveness. This paper asks whether SGD, when designed with specific insights from the optimisation and kernel communities, can match or outperform these established baselines.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a kernel function k (with entries computed on demand rather than stored in memory), a target vector b ∈ ℝⁿ, a likelihood variance λ > 0, and optimisation hyperparameters (step size β, batch size B, momentum ρ, averaging rate r). It outputs a coefficient vector α̅_T ∈ ℝⁿ approximating (K+λI)⁻¹b, from which the posterior mean function h_α(·) = Σᵢ αᵢk(xᵢ,·) is obtained directly, and posterior samples are constructed via pathwise conditioning using a prior sample f₀ and noise ζ~N(0,λI). The kernel matrix is never materialised; only row-wise kernel evaluations Kᵢα are needed per step.

## Method

Stochastic Dual Descent (SDD) minimises the dual objective L*(α) = ½‖α‖²_{K+λI} − αᵀb rather than the primal kernel ridge regression loss, yielding a Hessian (K+λI) with condition number bounded by 1+κn/λ and permitting step-sizes up to O(κn) larger than the primal. Stochastic gradients are computed by uniformly sampling B random data coordinates and forming the minibatched estimate (n/B)Σᵢ∈ℐ((Kᵢ+λeᵢ)ᵀ(α+ρv)−bᵢ)eᵢ, which introduces multiplicative noise (proportional to ‖α−α⋆‖) rather than the additive noise of random-feature estimates, enabling larger stable step-sizes and better asymptotic accuracy. The update uses Nesterov momentum (velocity v_t = ρv_{t−1} − βg_t, parameter α_t = α_{t−1}+v_t) and returns a geometrically averaged iterate α̅_t = rα_t + (1−r)α̅_{t−1} rather than the raw iterate or an arithmetic average. Each step requires only one matrix-vector product (Kᵢα for sampled rows), giving roughly 30% wall-clock speedup over prior SGD baselines that require two.

## Results

On 9 UCI regression benchmarks (15k–2M observations), SDD achieves the best or tied-best RMSE across all datasets, e.g. 0.04 on POL, 3DROAD, and HOUSEELEC, and 0.28 on BUZZ, while being ~30% faster in wall-clock time than the SGD baseline. On the DOCKSTRING molecular binding affinity task (250k molecules per protein), SDD matches the state-of-the-art Attentive FP graph neural network on ESR2 (R²=0.627) and F2 (R²=0.880), and comes within 0.02–0.05 on the remaining three proteins. In large-scale Thompson sampling (8D Matérn-3/2 functions), SDD finds the highest maximum function values while using the least compute time among all methods tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SGD (Lin et al., 2023), SDD strictly outperforms on all 9 UCI datasets in RMSE (e.g. 0.04 vs 0.11 on 3DROAD, 0.28 vs 0.42 on BUZZ) and is ~30% faster in wall-clock time due to requiring only one matrix-vector product per step. Against preconditioned CG (Wang et al., 2019), SDD matches on the five smaller datasets (≤49k points) where CG reaches its tolerance, but clearly outperforms CG on the four larger datasets (e.g. RMSE 0.04 vs 0.18 on 3DROAD, 0.28 vs 1.88 on BUZZ), where CG is limited to 100 iterations. Against SVGP (Hensman et al., 2013), SDD outperforms on all 9 UCI datasets in both RMSE and NLL. On the DOCKSTRING molecular task, SDD matches the state-of-the-art Attentive FP GNN on ESR2 (R²=0.627) and F2 (R²=0.880), and is within 0.02 on KIT and PARP1, while substantially outperforming MPNN (e.g. 0.627 vs 0.506 on ESR2) and XGBoost (0.627 vs 0.497 on ESR2). SDD also outperforms the SGD and SVGP baselines on all five proteins in the molecular task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the stochastic dual coordinate ascent (SDCA) framework of Shalev-Shwartz and Zhang (2013) to the deep-learning-style gradient descent setting, combining it with insights on multiplicative noise from Dieuleveut et al. (2017) and last-iterate convergence results from Varre et al. (2021). The dual objective for kernel ridge regression has been known since Saunders et al. (1998) and appears in the kernel adatron and kernel least-mean-square literature, but its superior conditioning relative to the primal was not previously exploited for stochastic optimisation. The closest prior work is Lin et al. (2023), which uses a mixed additive-multiplicative gradient estimate with random Fourier features; SDD departs by using purely multiplicative random-coordinate noise on the dual objective, eliminating the need for gradient clipping and enabling much larger step-sizes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, well-ablated design recipe (dual objective + random coordinates + Nesterov momentum + geometric averaging) that makes a simple SGD method competitive with or superior to preconditioned conjugate gradients for GP regression at scale, and demonstrates GPs matching state-of-the-art GNNs on molecular binding affinity prediction. The Rao-Blackwellisation trap finding—that subsampling only the Kα term while keeping the rest exact destroys the multiplicative noise property and worsens performance—is a useful cautionary result for practitioners designing stochastic gradient estimators. Open questions include the step-size versus batch-size trade-off (which must be resolved case-by-case), whether the approach extends to other kernel methods or non-Gaussian likelihoods, and whether the molecular binding affinity result generalises beyond the DOCKSTRING benchmark.
