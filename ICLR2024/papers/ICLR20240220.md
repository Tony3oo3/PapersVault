## Problem

Existing NAS methods waste significant time sampling and training task-irrelevant architectures, with property predictors playing only a passive filtering role during search. This paper identifies that predictors could instead actively guide the generation process toward high-performing regions of the search space, but no framework existed to integrate predictor gradients into an architecture generative model for directed acyclic graphs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a target dataset (encoded via a Set Transformer for dataset-awareness), architecture-accuracy pairs (for training the predictor), and Gaussian noise as the diffusion prior. Architectures are represented as a pair of matrices: an operator type matrix V ∈ R^{N×F} and an upper triangular adjacency matrix E ∈ R^{N×N}, where N is the maximum number of layers (8 for NAS-Bench-201, 20 for MobileNetV3) and F is the number of operation types (7 or 9). During generation, architectures are treated as continuous entities perturbed by Gaussian noise, then discretized to 0-1 matrices via a threshold operator 1_{>0.5} after the reverse diffusion process completes.

## Method

DiffusionNAG formulates architecture generation as a Variance Exploding SDE over continuous architecture matrices, with a score network parameterized by L transformer blocks that use a DAG-derived attention mask and positional embeddings to capture topological ordering of layers. The key mechanism is a conditional reverse SDE where the score function is decomposed via Bayes' theorem into the learned unconditional score s_θ*(A_t, t) plus a predictor guidance term k_t ∇_{A_t} log f_φ(y|A_t), allowing the predictor's gradient to steer generation toward architectures satisfying target properties. The score network is trained once per search space without accuracy labels (self-supervised score matching), while the predictor is trained separately—either via meta-learning over a task distribution for Transferable NAS or supervised training on the current population for BO-based NAS—enabling plug-and-play predictor swapping without retraining the generative model. In the BO-based setting, DiffusionNAG replaces heuristic acquisition optimization strategies (random sampling, mutation) by generating candidate architectures directly from the predictor-guided conditional distribution, eliminating the initial-phase waste of low-quality samples.

## Results

On the large MobileNetV3 search space (~10^19 architectures), DiffusionNAG achieves the best maximum accuracy on 3 of 4 datasets (97.52% CIFAR-10, 86.07% CIFAR-100, 95.34% Oxford-IIIT Pets) and the best mean accuracy across all four datasets, with statistically significant improvements (p < 0.05) over both MetaD2A and TNAS. On NAS-Bench-201, DiffusionNAG reaches oracle accuracy (94.37% CIFAR-10, 73.51% CIFAR-100) while requiring only 1–3 trained architectures versus 40–100 for baselines, yielding a 15×–35× speedup. The proposed score network with positional embedding achieves 100% validity on both search spaces, compared to 4.56% (NB201) and 0.00% (MBv3) for the undirected-graph diffusion baseline GDSS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MetaD2A (Lee et al., 2021a), the closest unconditional generation baseline, DiffusionNAG consistently outperforms on mean and minimum accuracy across all four MBv3 datasets (e.g., 97.39% vs 97.28% mean on CIFAR-10; 85.42% vs 84.74% min on CIFAR-100), with all p-values below 0.05, demonstrating that using the predictor during generation (conditional NAG) is superior to using it only for post-hoc evaluation. Against TNAS (Shala et al., 2023), which relies solely on BO with a deep-kernel GP without any generation process, DiffusionNAG achieves higher mean accuracy on all datasets and dramatically fewer trained architectures (1–3 vs 6–59 on NB201), yielding up to 35× speedup. On NB201, DiffusionNAG matches or exceeds all BO-based NAS baselines (GP-UCB, BANANAS, NASBOWL, HEBO) on Aircraft (58.83% vs 41.72–53.73%) and Oxford-IIIT Pets (41.80% vs 40.15–41.29%) while training far fewer architectures. Against one-shot NAS methods (DrNAS, GDAS, PC-DARTS, SETN, RSPS), DiffusionNAG achieves comparable CIFAR-10/100 accuracy but substantially better results on the fine-grained Aircraft and Oxford-IIIT Pets datasets. The score network ablation (Table 4) shows that the positional embedding is critical: without it, MBv3 validity drops from 100% to 42.17%, while the undirected-graph diffusion model GDSS (Jo et al., 2022) fails entirely (0% validity on MBv3, 4.56% on NB201), confirming the necessity of the directed-graph-aware score network.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends score-based generative modeling via SDEs (Song et al., 2021) and graph diffusion (Jo et al., 2022) to the domain of neural architecture generation, introducing a novel score network with positional embeddings tailored for directed acyclic graphs. It departs from prior NAS methods (MetaD2A, TNAS) that use dataset-aware predictors only as passive evaluators during a search phase, instead repurposing the parameterized model-guidance mechanism from Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) to actively steer the diffusion sampling process toward high-performing architectures. The transformer-based score network design draws on CATE (Yan et al., 2021a) for attention-masked DAG encoding, while the conditional generation framework adapts the gradient-of-predictor guidance scheme originally developed for image and molecule generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paradigm shift from search-and-filter to direct conditional generation offers a practical 15–35× speedup in Transferable NAS and improves BO-based NAS by eliminating the costly initial phase of sampling low-quality architectures. The plug-and-play design—where the base diffusion model is trained once and predictors can be swapped for different objectives (accuracy, latency, robustness) without retraining—makes the framework broadly applicable across NAS scenarios. Open questions include how the approach scales to multi-objective optimization (e.g., jointly constraining accuracy and FLOPs), whether the guidance quality degrades when the predictor is poorly calibrated, and whether the method extends to non-DAG architecture representations such as sequential or hierarchical designs.
