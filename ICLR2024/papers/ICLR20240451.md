## Problem

Managing thousands of SKUs under a shared inventory capacity constraint is intractable for centralized MARL methods (exponentially large joint state space) and suboptimal for independent-learning methods that ignore the global constraint. Moreover, when the capacity limit changes across seasons or promotions, existing approaches require full retraining. The Whittle index from restless bandits offers a natural decoupling mechanism but cannot be applied directly because the constraint is on the global state (total inventory) rather than the global action (number of activated arms), and each SKU has multiple discrete replenishment actions rather than a binary activate/not-activate choice.

## Input / Output

Each agent receives a local state vector containing the SKU's in-stock quantity, in-transit quantity, procurement/selling prices, and historical demand features, all normalized by SKU-specific scales (e.g., in-stock divided by storage capacity, quantities divided by 21-day average demand). The output is a discrete action index into a list of candidate replenishment quantities per SKU, plus a scalar global cost λ_g that is updated online via a sliding-window feedback rule (Eq. 6) whenever the total inventory approaches or exceeds the capacity limit L_t. The Whittle index itself is a (A−1)-dimensional vector per state rather than a single scalar, encoding the maximum affordable unit-storage cost for each action threshold.

## Method

WIMS redefines the Whittle index to measure cost per unit of shared resource (inventory) rather than cost per arm activation, and produces a vector-valued index f(s) ∈ R^{A−1} where each component [f(s)]_α is the supremum λ at which the optimal policy still prefers actions above threshold α. WIMSN implements this with two coupled neural networks: a Q-network Q_θ(s, a, λ) that conditions on a continuous λ input, and a Whittle index network f_φ(s) → R^{A−1}. Training is fully decentralized (no centralized critic), using off-policy replay-buffer learning where the Q-network is updated on λ values sampled from the current Whittle index network (WIMSN-c) or a fixed grid (WIMSN-d), while the Whittle index network is updated by gradient ascent on the sign of Q_θ(s, >α, λ) − Q_θ(s, ≤α, λ). At execution, a single global cost λ_g is maintained and adjusted via a simple proportional feedback rule on the maximum inventory over a sliding window, allowing the same trained model to adapt to arbitrary capacity limits without retraining.

## Results

On real inventory management tasks with 100–2307 SKUs, WIMSN-c achieves the highest daily profit per SKU (×10³ USD) with constraint violation below 1% across all settings. For example, on the tight-budget task N100-C2.5k, WIMSN-c earns 9.10 profit with 0.21% violation, versus 6.44/38.21% for IPPO and 5.94/34.79% for IDQN. On the largest task N2k-C58k, WIMSN-c earns 9.17 with 0.00% violation, outperforming IPPO (5.45/5.49%) and IDQN (6.82/7.27%). A single WIMSN model is trained once and reused across all SKU counts and capacity limits, whereas all baselines require retraining per task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WIMSN-c dominates all RL-based baselines (IPPO, IDQN, Primal-Dual) on profit while maintaining constraint violation below 1%, whereas IPPO and IDQN violate the capacity limit by up to 38% and 35% respectively on tight-budget tasks. Against the best OR-based method (Skyline SS, which uses test-set-tuned parameters), WIMSN-c still achieves higher profit on 10 out of 12 tasks with zero violation, confirming that the learned Whittle index captures demand patterns that parametric policies miss. WIMSN-d (discrete λ) is competitive but slightly behind WIMSN-c on tasks with over 1000 SKUs, suggesting that continuous λ sampling better captures the index landscape at scale. A key practical advantage is that WIMSN requires a single model trained once across all 2307 SKUs, while every baseline must be retrained for each new SKU subset or capacity level. In the online adaptation experiment (capacity changing from 50k to 100k mid-episode), WIMSN-c earns 22.7% and 18.4% more cumulative profit than IPPO trained at 100k and 50k respectively, while incurring far less constraint violation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Whittle index policy (Whittle, 1988) from the restless bandits setting to a constrained MARL formulation with two key generalizations: the constraint is imposed on the global state (total inventory capacity) rather than the global action (number of activated arms), and each agent has multiple discrete actions rather than a binary activate/not-activate choice. It departs from existing MARL approaches for inventory management (IPPO, IDQN, MAPPO) by avoiding both the computationally infeasible centralized critic and the constraint-ignoring independent learning, instead using a dynamically updated global Lagrange multiplier to coordinate agents. The OR-based baselines (base-stock, (s,S)-policy) represent the traditional dynamic-programming lineage that the paper positions against as assumption-heavy and non-adaptive to changing constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The single-model-for-all-tasks property (no retraining when constraint levels or SKU combinations change) is a significant practical advantage for industrial deployment, reducing computational costs by an order of magnitude compared to per-task training. The <1% constraint violation while outperforming baselines in profit addresses a real tension in practice where constraint violations carry hidden costs. Open questions include whether the indexability assumption (Lemma 4.2) holds broadly beyond inventory management, whether the weak coupling structure is sufficient for more complex multi-echelon settings, and how the method behaves when the constraint is truly hard (no overflow tolerance) rather than the soft penalty formulation used in baselines.
