## Problem

Momentum is widely used in deep learning to stabilize training, and folklore attributes its benefit to reducing the variance of stochastic gradient updates. However, prior theoretical analyses of SGDM in stochastic settings yield convergence rates comparable to vanilla SGD, and it remains unclear whether the noise-reduction effect of momentum actually translates into optimization or generalization gains. This paper asks whether momentum provides any provable or practical benefit when the learning rate is small enough that gradient noise, rather than curvature, is the dominant source of instability.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on a loss function with a Noisy Gradient Oracle with Scale Parameter (NGOS), where the noise covariance Σ(θ) is independent of the noise scale σ, allowing σ to be scaled relative to the learning rate η (e.g., σ = 1/√η under the Linear Scaling Rule). The theoretical outputs are weak approximation bounds between SGDM and SGD trajectories over O(1/η) and O(1/η²) steps, plus identification of the shared limiting SDE dynamics. Empirically, the paper reports test accuracy for ResNet-50 on ImageNet (batch sizes 1024–8192), ResNet-32 on CIFAR-10 (batch 512), and RoBERTa-large fine-tuned on five NLP classification tasks with 512 examples per class.

## Method

The paper reformulates SGDM so that it uses the same learning rate η as SGD (rather than the conventional γ = η/(1−β) equivalence), enabling a direct comparison. The key technical device is a coupled trajectory y_k = x_k − (η̄_k β_k/(1−β_k)) m_k that exactly follows an SGD-like update with an averaged learning rate schedule, bridging SGDM and vanilla SGD. In the short-horizon regime (O(1/η) steps, σ ≤ 1/√η), Theorem 3.5 proves SGDM and SGD are O(√(η/(1−β)))-close in the sense of weak approximation, with the limit being either a shared SDE (when σ = 1/√η) or gradient flow (when σ ≪ 1/√η). In the long-horizon regime (O(1/η²) steps near a manifold of minimizers), Theorem 4.5 shows both methods converge to the identical slow SDE on the manifold, implying the same implicit bias. Empirically, SVAG is used to suppress curvature-induced effects in large-batch CIFAR-10 training, confirming that momentum's apparent advantage there stems from curvature stabilization rather than noise reduction.

## Results

On RoBERTa-large fine-tuning (512 examples/class), SGD and SGDM achieve nearly identical accuracy across all 5 tasks: e.g., 94.0% vs 94.0% on SST-2, 87.7% vs 88.4% on SNLI, and 97.2% vs 97.2% on TREC, with differences within 0.3–0.8 percentage points. On ImageNet with ResNet-50, SGDM and SGD perform comparably at batch size 1024, but SGDM outperforms SGD significantly at larger batch sizes where the optimal learning rate is larger. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-32, standard SGDM achieves higher test accuracy than SGD, but the gap shrinks and vanishes when applying SVAG (increasing ℓ) to suppress curvature-induced effects, confirming that momentum's benefit is tied to large learning rates rather than noise reduction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central empirical comparison is between vanilla SGD and SGDM (heavy-ball momentum, β=0.9) under matched effective learning rates (SGD uses η = γ/(1−β) where γ is SGDM's learning rate). In small-batch ImageNet training (batch size 1024) and RoBERTa-large fine-tuning, the two methods tie within noise: differences are ≤0.7% across all language tasks and visually indistinguishable training curves on ImageNet. In large-batch ImageNet training, SGDM clearly outperforms SGD, but the authors show via SVAG experiments on CIFAR-10 that this advantage disappears when the curvature-induced term is suppressed (larger ℓ), indicating the benefit comes from allowing a larger learning rate rather than from noise reduction. The theoretical weak-approximation bound O(√(η/(1−β))) is tight in the small-LR regime but becomes loose at large learning rates, explaining the large-batch gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Noisy Gradient Oracle with Scale Parameter (NGOS) framework of Malladi et al. (2022) to model gradient noise, and extends the slow SDE analysis of Li et al. (2021b) from SGD to SGDM on manifolds of local minimizers. It departs from the classical Polyak momentum theory (which shows acceleration in noiseless strongly convex settings) and from the folklore that momentum reduces update variance to accelerate stochastic training, by rigorously showing that in small learning rate regimes the variance reduction is offset by long-range correlations in the momentum buffer. The weak approximation framework from Li et al. (2019) and the SVAG technique from Li et al. (2021a) are repurposed as analytical and experimental tools, respectively, to isolate the noise-dominated regime where the SGDM–SGD equivalence holds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant for large-model training: since the momentum buffer costs as much memory as the model parameters themselves, formally proving that momentum provides only marginal value in small-learning-rate regimes gives practitioners a principled basis for removing it and saving GPU memory. The result also implies insensitivity to momentum hyperparameters within reasonable scales, reducing the need for expensive grid searches. Open questions remain for large-batch or large-learning-rate settings where curvature-induced instability dominates, and for whether the equivalence breaks down at the boundary α=1 where the approximation order vanishes. The paper does not address adaptive optimizers (Adam, LAMB) where momentum interacts with per-parameter scaling.
