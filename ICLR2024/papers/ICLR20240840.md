## Problem

Despite extensive development of domain generalization (DG) algorithms, no clear empirical evidence exists that they outperform standard empirical risk minimization (ERM) across standard benchmarks. This paper investigates whether DG algorithms offer a specific advantage over ERM through the lens of label noise, asking under what conditions DG algorithms are theoretically and practically superior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240840/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis operates on overparameterized linear classifiers (d >> n) where input features are decomposed into invariant, spurious, and high-dimensional nuisance components, with label noise injected at rate η into binary labels. Empirically, the paper uses ResNet-50 pretrained on ImageNet for RGB image datasets (224×224), simple CNNs for CMNIST (28×28×2 synthetic digits with color channel), and standard text classifiers for CivilComments. Evaluation uses worst-group accuracy for subpopulation shifts and cross-domain average accuracy for domain shifts, with label noise at 0%, 10%, and 25% levels.

## Method

The paper provides a finite-sample theoretical analysis proving that under label noise η and spurious correlation γ, the L2 norm of the spurious classifier becomes smaller than the invariant classifier's norm (Theorem 4.2), causing gradient descent's min-norm bias to prefer spurious solutions. For DG algorithms, the key insight is that IRM's practical surrogate (IRMv1) multiplies the ERM gradient by a coefficient α(φ) that becomes negative for large logits when regularization λ is strong, effectively reversing the gradient direction and preventing overconfident memorization of noisy samples. V-REx exhibits a similar mechanism where below-average-risk environments receive gradient ascent (unlearning). The paper then validates these properties empirically on CMNIST (where IL algorithms clearly outperform ERM under noise) and real-world benchmarks (where the advantage does not consistently materialize), concluding that the theoretical failure conditions for ERM may be less severe in practice due to pretraining, data augmentation, and weaker spurious correlations.

## Results

On the synthetic CMNIST dataset, invariance learning algorithms (IRM, V-REx) significantly outperform ERM-based methods as label noise increases, with the performance gap widening at higher noise levels (η=0.25). On real-world subpopulation-shift datasets (Waterbirds+, CelebA+), no DG algorithm consistently outperforms ERM: at 10% noise, ERM achieves 64.6% WG accuracy on Waterbirds+ vs. 66.6% for V-REx, while on CelebA+ ERM reaches 65.4% vs. 69.9% for V-REx. On domain-shift benchmarks (PACS, VLCS, OfficeHome, TerraIncognita), Mixup consistently achieves the best cross-test accuracy (e.g., 83.6% on PACS at 10% noise, 53.2% on TerraIncognita), followed closely by ERM, while IRM and V-REx underperform both. The paper concludes that the implicit noise robustness of DG algorithms does not necessarily translate to superior OOD generalization in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central empirical finding is that none of the DG baselines consistently outperform ERM on real-world benchmarks, undermining the practical value of their theoretical noise-robustness advantage. On synthetic CMNIST, IRM and V-REx clearly beat ERM and Mixup as label noise increases (e.g., at η=0.25, IRM/V-REx maintain substantially higher WG accuracy), and GroupDRO also outperforms ERM but trails the IL methods. On real-world subpopulation shifts (Waterbirds, CelebA), ERM is competitive or superior: at 10% noise on Waterbirds, ERM (61.4%) beats IRM (58.8%) and V-REx (59.2%), while on CelebA+ at 10% noise, V-REx (69.9%) edges out ERM (65.4%). On domain-shift benchmarks, Mixup consistently achieves the highest cross-test accuracy across all four datasets (PACS, VLCS, OfficeHome, TerraIncognita) at both 10% and 25% noise, outperforming ERM by 1–3 points and IRM/V-REx by 3–9 points. GroupDRO performs comparably to ERM on domain shifts but shows degraded results on Waterbirds/CelebA compared to prior work, suggesting incomplete hyperparameter search. Overall, the paper demonstrates that the noise-robustness property of IL algorithms (IRM, V-REx) identified theoretically does not yield a consistent practical advantage over simple ERM or Mixup on real-world data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of ERM failure modes under spurious correlations (Sagawa et al., 2020; Nagarajan et al., 2020) by introducing label noise as an additional failure mechanism, showing that noise and spurious correlation jointly determine whether overparameterized models prefer spurious over invariant features via the min-norm bias of gradient descent. It departs from prior DG algorithm analyses (IRM, V-REx, GroupDRO) by reframing their advantage not as invariance learning per se, but as implicit label-noise robustness arising from their regularization structure. The empirical evaluation builds on the Domainbed benchmark framework and the synthetic CMNIST setup from Arjovsky et al. (2019), while the theoretical tools draw on the min-norm convergence results for logistic regression (Soudry et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2021a).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled theoretical condition (Theorem 4.2) explaining when ERM fails under the joint effect of spurious correlation and label noise, and identifies implicit noise robustness as the mechanism by which invariance-learning algorithms resist this failure. However, the key practical finding is a negative result: this theoretical advantage does not translate to measurable gains on real-world benchmarks, suggesting that the failure conditions are less severe in practice due to pretraining, data augmentation, and weaker spurious correlations. Open questions include whether the gap between theory and practice can be closed by identifying datasets where the norm-gap condition is genuinely violated, and whether adversarial or corrupted settings might restore the predicted DG advantage.
