## Problem

Applying Input Mixup to node classification in GNNs is blocked by two structural issues: irregularity (nodes have varying numbers of neighbors, so two nodes to be interpolated have different input sizes) and alignment (neighboring node sets lack inherent ordering, making it undefined how to pair them during message-passing). Prior Mixup methods for node classification (GraphMix, M-Mixup) sidestepped these issues by operating on hidden representations (Manifold Mixup), sacrificing the diversity, model-agnosticism, and composability that make Input Mixup attractive in other domains.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a batch of labeled nodes with their feature vectors, one-hot class labels, and the full adjacency matrix of the graph, plus a single hyperparameter α controlling the Beta(α, α) distribution from which the mixing coefficient λ is drawn. It produces a virtual training graph whose node features and labels are linear interpolations of a randomly permuted pair, and whose edge set is the union of stochastically sampled neighbor subsets from both original nodes. The virtual graph is then fed unchanged into any standard GNN forward pass to yield node class predictions, with no architectural modification required.

## Method

iGraphMix generates a virtual node by (1) interpolating features X̃ = λX + (1−λ)X′ and labels Ỹ = λY + (1−λ)Y′ with λ ~ Beta(α, α), and (2) constructing virtual edges via Ã = M_{1−λ} ∘ A + M_λ ∘ A′, where the masking matrices act as neighbor-selection operators that independently sample each neighbor with probability 1−λ (from the first node) or λ (from the second). This 'attach-all-sampled-neighbors' strategy resolves the irregularity issue by never requiring equal-sized neighbor sets and the alignment issue by letting the GNN's own message-passing and aggregation handle the unordered virtual neighborhood. Theoretically, the paper proves that the iGraphMix empirical loss decomposes into the standard empirical loss plus a second-order regularization term R(W₂) on the output-layer weights, which shrinks the admissible weight space and yields a tighter Transductive Rademacher Complexity bound on the generalization gap. Empirically, iGraphMix improves micro-F1 by 2.84% on average over no augmentation across five datasets and three backbones (GCN, GATv1, GATv2), outperforms DropEdge/DropNode/DropMessage, and composes additively with those methods for further gains.

## Results

iGraphMix achieves an average micro-F1 improvement of 2.84% over no augmentation and 2.41% over M-Mixup across five benchmark datasets (CiteSeer, CORA, PubMed, ogbn-arxiv, Flickr) and three GNN backbones (GCN, GATv1, GATv2). The largest gains appear on ogbn-arxiv (e.g., 68.93% vs. 67.11% for GCN) and CORA with GATv1 (83.20% vs. 79.98% without augmentation). The method also reduces the generalization gap consistently, with optimal Beta-distribution parameter α varying by dataset (100 for CiteSeer, 10 for ogbn-arxiv, 50 for Flickr).

## Baselines & Comparisons

iGraphMix outperforms all four baselines (DropEdge, DropNode, DropMessage, M-Mixup) on the majority of dataset–backbone combinations. Against DropEdge, DropNode, and DropMessage, iGraphMix wins across all five datasets and all three backbones, with margins ranging from ~0.1% (e.g., GCN on Flickr: 53.61 vs. 53.59) to ~4.4% (e.g., GATv1 on ogbn-arxiv: 69.49 vs. 68.45). Against M-Mixup, iGraphMix achieves a 2.41% average advantage, with particularly large gaps on ogbn-arxiv (68.93 vs. 65.82 for GCN) and Flickr (53.61 vs. 48.14 for GCN); however, M-Mixup edges out iGraphMix on GATv2/CiteSeer (72.90 vs. 71.97) and GATv1/PubMed (78.93 vs. 78.41). M-Mixup also suffers out-of-memory failures on ogbn-arxiv for both GATv1 and GATv2, whereas iGraphMix handles the 169K-node graph without issue. The paper further shows that combining iGraphMix with DropEdge, DropNode, or DropMessage yields an additional ~1.86% average gain over using those methods alone, confirming its versatility as a composable augmentation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Input Mixup lineage (Zhang et al., 2018; CutMix; SSMix) to the graph domain, specifically for node classification, where prior Mixup work had been limited to Manifold Mixup variants (GraphMix, M-Mixup) that access hidden representations to sidestep the irregularity and alignment issues of graph inputs. iGraphMix departs from these by operating purely on input features, labels, and edges—randomly sampling neighboring nodes to construct virtual edges—thereby preserving the model-agnosticism and diversity advantages of Input Mixup. The theoretical analysis builds on Esser et al. (2021) for GCN generalization gap bounds and Zhang et al. (2021) for Mixup's regularization effect, combining both to show that iGraphMix induces an implicit weight-space constraint on the final layer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

iGraphMix offers a drop-in, model-agnostic augmentation that consistently improves GNN performance across architectures and datasets, with the added benefit of being composable with other augmentation methods (DropEdge, DropNode, DropMessage). The finding that the optimal Beta parameter α varies substantially across datasets (100 for CiteSeer, 10 for ogbn-arxiv, 50 for Flickr) suggests that augmentation strength should be tuned to graph topology, an open practical question. Limitations include the assumption of no edges between labeled nodes (reasonable for semi-supervised citation networks but restrictive), the focus on transductive settings, and the use of uniform random edge sampling, which the authors acknowledge could be improved with more informed sampling strategies.
