## Problem

Dynamic NeRF training is inherently under-constrained, and existing regularizers (depth losses, scene flow, LPIPS) require expensive off-the-shelf models, additional deep networks, or costly preprocessing steps that are difficult to integrate in an architecture-agnostic manner. This paper seeks a lightweight, learning-free regularizer that constrains dynamic NeRF optimization without these computational overheads.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240795/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The regularizer consumes the same RGB frames and camera poses as the base dynamic NeRF model but additionally renders pixels at randomly sampled 2D coordinates (d pixels, e.g., 2048 or 4096) from a fixed camera pose at two nearby timestamps, rather than full-frame renderings. These sparse pixel samples are bilinearly interpolated to full image resolution before computing the sliced-Wasserstein distance, which is then added as a weighted term (β=0.1) to the standard photometric loss. The method requires no preprocessing, no external models, and no additional network parameters.

## Method

The key insight is that pixel intensity distributions rendered from a fixed camera view should remain statistically similar over short time intervals, even as individual pixel coordinates shift due to object motion. The paper minimizes the sliced-Wasserstein distance between these distributions, which reduces the OT computation from O(n³) to O(n log n) by projecting onto random 1D directions and sorting. To avoid the memory cost of full-frame rendering, they obtain an unbiased estimator by rendering only d random pixel coordinates and interpolating to full resolution. The paper provides a theoretical convergence bound (Theorem 1) showing the empirical 1-Wasserstein distance converges at O(n^{-1/2}) with poly-logarithmic dependence on the number of distinct pixel values. The regularizer is a few lines of code, requires no learned components, and plugs into any dynamic NeRF architecture without modification.

## Results

On the iPhone dataset (7 scenes), the proposed OT regularizer improves the mean mPSNR of all five dynamic NeRF baselines: DNeRF (15.10→15.69), Nerfies (13.62→14.90), HyperNeRF (13.88→15.74), HexPlane (15.54→15.71), and TiNeuVox (9.44→11.18). The most substantial gains appear for HyperNeRF (+1.86 mPSNR, −0.108 mLPIPS) and TiNeuVox (+1.73 mPSNR, −0.040 mLPIPS). On the HyperNeRF interpolation dataset, TiNeuVox with the regularizer achieves a mean SSIM of 0.872 vs. 0.774 without, and a mean LPIPS of 0.499 vs. 0.550. Training time is approximately 3 hours for the OT regularizer versus ~8 hours for LPIPS and ~10 hours for scene-flow regularization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The regularizer is plug-and-play and improves every baseline it is applied to. Against DNeRF (Gao et al., 2021), Nerfies (Park et al., 2021a), HyperNeRF (Park et al., 2021b), HexPlane (Cao & Johnson, 2023), and TiNeuVox (Fang et al., 2022), the OT regularizer consistently raises mPSNR and lowers mLPIPS, with the largest absolute gain on TiNeuVox (+1.73 mPSNR) and HyperNeRF (+1.86 mPSNR). In the ablation against alternative regularizers (Table 4), the OT regularizer (11.176 mPSNR, 0.496 mLPIPS) outperforms scene-flow (Wang et al., 2023; 9.457 mPSNR), LPIPS (9.991 mPSNR), depth (9.437 mPSNR), the Mip-NeRF 360 sparsity regularizer (Barron et al., 2022; 9.332 mPSNR), and the Humannerf random-background regularizer (Weng et al., 2022; 10.131 mPSNR). Combining the OT regularizer with RB+SR+Depth yields a marginal further improvement (11.221 mPSNR), confirming complementarity. The OT regularizer also runs in ~3 hours versus ~8 hours (LPIPS) and ~10 hours (scene-flow), making it the most efficient option among all compared regularizers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the dominant regularization strategies for dynamic NeRFs—geometric cues (depth, scene flow) and learned perceptual priors (LPIPS)—which require expensive off-the-shelf models or additional deep networks. It departs from this lineage by reframing temporal consistency as a distributional constraint: the pixel intensity distribution from a fixed camera should remain approximately invariant over short time intervals, and this invariance is enforced via a sliced-Wasserstein distance between rendered pixel distributions. The theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) provides a convergence bound for the empirical 1-Wasserstein distance in this finite-support setting, grounding the regularizer in optimal transport theory rather than heuristic geometric assumptions. The method is explicitly architecture-agnostic, building on the sliced-Wasserstein literature (Rabin et al., Bonneel et al., Avraham et al.) but applying it to dynamic NeRF regularization for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in offering a drop-in regularizer that requires no preprocessing, no additional networks, and only a few lines of code, while consistently improving multiple state-of-the-art dynamic NeRF architectures across three datasets. This lowers the barrier to robust dynamic scene reconstruction and suggests that distributional temporal consistency is a sufficient and efficient signal, challenging the assumption that expensive geometric supervision is necessary. Limitations include the assumption of smooth temporal dynamics (failing on abrupt scene changes), a mild pixel-averaging blur from the OT approximation, and limited gains when the baseline model already converges well. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-camera or long-sequence settings, whether tighter theoretical bounds can be derived, and whether the distributional perspective generalizes to other under-constrained inverse problems in 3D reconstruction.
