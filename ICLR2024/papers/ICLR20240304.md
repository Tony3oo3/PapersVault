## Problem

While prior work (DOORPING) empirically demonstrated that backdoor triggers can survive dataset distillation, the theoretical mechanism governing distillation resiliency remained unexplained, and DOORPING's reliance on bi-level optimization made trigger generation computationally expensive. This paper asks: what kernel-method-theoretic quantities determine whether a trigger survives distillation, and can triggers be designed via efficient single-level optimization?

## Input / Output

The method takes a benign RGB image dataset (CIFAR-10 or GTSRB, all resized to 32×32), a binary or soft mask m (e.g., all-ones for simple-trigger, or uniform 0.3 for relax-trigger to increase transparency), and a target trigger label y_T. It produces a poisoned distilled dataset of N_S synthetic samples (10× or 50× number of classes) generated via KIP, along with the optimized trigger pattern T. The trigger is applied via Hadamard product: x_b = (1−m)⊙x_a + m⊙T, and the resulting mixed dataset D̃ = D_A ∪ D_B is compressed through kernel ridge regression with NTK from a 3-layer network.

## Method

The paper establishes a theoretical framework decomposing backdoor risk under KIP-based distillation into three components within RKHS: conflict loss (internal label inconsistency between benign and trigger samples), projection loss (information lost when compressing the full model into the synthetic-dataset subspace), and generalization gap (bounded by the diameter Γ_D of the trigger distribution). Simple-trigger exploits the generalization gap term: enlarging the trigger to full-image size drives Γ_D_B → 0, yielding 100% ASR without CTA loss. Relax-trigger jointly minimizes conflict loss and a simplified projection loss by fixing the inner distillation variable to S_A (the distilled benign dataset), converting the bi-level problem into a single-level optimization over T solved with Adam. This single-level formulation achieves comparable or superior CTA/ASR to DOORPING while being computationally efficient, and the resulting triggers evade all eight tested backdoor defenses.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (distilled size 100), relax-trigger achieves 100% ASR with 41.66% CTA, outperforming simple-trigger (75.92% ASR, 41.40% CTA) and DOORPING (80% ASR, 36.35% CTA). On GTSRB (size 430), relax-trigger reaches 95.26% ASR with 68.73% CTA, surpassing DOORPING (90% ASR, 68.03% CTA) and simple-trigger (62.74% ASR, 67.02% CTA). Against eight existing backdoor defenses, relax-trigger evades all of them: no defense reduces ASR below ~50% of the undefended value, while several defenses (SCAn, AC, Strip, ABL) fail to reduce ASR at all or even increase it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is DOORPING (Liu et al., NDSS 2023), the prior state-of-the-art distillation-resilient backdoor. relax-trigger dominates DOORPING on both metrics across all reported settings: on CIFAR-10 (size 100), relax-trigger achieves 100% ASR with 41.66% CTA versus DOORPING's 80% ASR and 36.35% CTA (a 5.3-point CTA gain and 20-point ASR gain); on GTSRB (size 430), relax-trigger reaches 95.26% ASR / 68.73% CTA versus DOORPING's 90% ASR / 68.03% CTA. Beyond the direct comparison, relax-trigger also substantially outperforms the simpler simple-trigger baseline, improving ASR by ~24 points on CIFAR-10 (100% vs. 75.9%) and ~32 points on GTSRB (95.3% vs. 62.7%) while maintaining or slightly improving CTA. A key practical advantage is that relax-trigger uses only single-level optimization (Adam on Eq. 21), whereas DOORPING requires bi-level optimization, making relax-trigger significantly more computationally efficient. Finally, in the defense-evasion evaluation, relax-trigger evades all eight tested defenses (SCAn, AC, SS, Strip, ABL, NAD, STRIP, FP), whereas DOORPING's defense-evasion properties are not explicitly reported in this paper.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DOORPING (Liu et al., NDSS 2023), the first empirical demonstration that backdoor triggers can survive dataset distillation, but departs from it by replacing DOORPING's bi-level optimization with a single-level, theory-driven trigger design grounded in RKHS and kernel ridge regression. The analytical framework draws on the KIP (Kernel Inducing Points) formulation of dataset distillation (Nguyen et al., ICLR 2021) and the NTK literature to decompose backdoor risk into conflict loss, projection loss, and generalization gap—a decomposition not previously applied to the distillation-backdoor intersection. Conceptually, the paper positions itself as providing the first theoretical characterization of why certain triggers survive distillation, shifting the design criterion from empirical trial-and-error to principled optimization of these three risk components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is concerning: a trivially simple full-image white trigger achieves 100% ASR with no CTA degradation under KIP distillation, and the learned relax-trigger evades all eight tested defenses, suggesting that current backdoor detection/cleansing pipelines are largely blind to distillation-mediated attacks. However, the scope is notably limited—the theory and experiments are restricted to KIP (a performance-matching method) with NTK, and the defense evaluations retrain on ResNet rather than the original KIP-trained model, so generalization to parameter-preserving or distribution-preserving distillation methods remains untested. Open questions include whether the generalization-gap argument (larger trigger → smaller Γ_D_B) holds for non-kernel distillation methods, and whether defenses specifically designed for the distilled-data setting (rather than retraining-based) can close the gap.
