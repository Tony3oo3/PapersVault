## Problem

Existing offline-to-online RL methods treat offline and online learning as separate procedures with different objectives, requiring extra conservatism, policy constraints, or Q-ensembles to bridge the gap. This inherited conservatism causes initial performance drops, training instability, and asymptotic suboptimality during online fine-tuning. The paper asks whether a single on-policy objective can unify both phases seamlessly without any additional regularization.

## Input / Output

In the offline phase, the method consumes a fixed dataset of state-action pairs (from D4RL benchmarks or real-world robot rollouts) and produces an ensemble of behavior policies, IQL-style value functions (Q_τ, V_τ), and a learned transition model. In the real-world quadruped setting, the policy input is a 5-step history of 58-dimensional observations (gravity vector, 24 joint states, 12 last actions, 14 commands, and timing references), and the output is a 12-dimensional target joint position vector fed to a PD controller. The final output is a single policy and value function pair that can be directly plugged into standard online PPO with no architectural or objective modification.

## Method

Uni-O4 uses the PPO clipped surrogate objective as a unified training signal for both offline and online phases. In the offline stage, it first learns an ensemble of n diverse behavior policies via behavior cloning augmented with a negative KL-disagreement penalty (Theorem 1 provides a tractable lower bound using max over ensemble members), which captures multi-modal action distributions that a single BC policy would miss. Multi-step policy improvement is then achieved by iteratively updating each ensemble policy with the PPO objective while using a proposed AM-Q offline policy evaluation (combining a learned transition model with IQL's Q_τ) to decide when to accept a policy update, replacing BPPO's costly online evaluation with a computationally efficient O(NH) inference procedure (Theorem 2 bounds the OPE error). After offline optimization, one policy is selected and directly initialized into standard online PPO with GAE, requiring no replay buffer balancing, no conservative regularization, and no objective change—enabling a seamless transition that avoids the performance drops and suboptimality plaguing prior offline-to-online methods.

## Results

On the D4RL benchmark (26 tasks across MuJoCo Locomotion, Adroit, Kitchen, and Antmaze), Uni-O4 achieves a total normalized return of 1322.0±61.2, outperforming the next-best offline method BPPO (1253.4) and IQL (970.3). Uni-O4 wins on 14 of 20 main-table tasks, with particularly strong gains on Adroit (288.6 vs. 180.2 for ATAC) and Kitchen (216.9 vs. 159.8 for IQL). On Antmaze, Uni-O4 scores 447.9 total, a 79.4% improvement over the best estimated-behavior-policy baseline. In offline-to-online fine-tuning (1M steps), Uni-O4 achieves stable, monotonic improvement without the initial performance drops seen in PEX or Off2on, and completes training in ~30 minutes versus 18 hours for Q-ensemble methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Uni-O4 achieves the best overall D4RL total score (1322.0), surpassing BPPO (1253.4) by 5.5%, IQL (970.3) by 36%, and ATAC (1002.1) by 32%. On MuJoCo Locomotion, ATAC (818.9) marginally edges Uni-O4 (816.4), but Uni-O4 dominates on the harder Adroit domain (288.6 vs. 180.2 for ATAC, 118.1 for IQL) and Kitchen (216.9 vs. 211.0 for BPPO, 159.8 for IQL). Among one-step methods, Uni-O4 substantially outperforms IQL and Onestep RL across nearly all tasks, confirming the benefit of multi-step policy improvement via OPE. In offline-to-online fine-tuning, Uni-O4 avoids the initial performance drops of PEX and Off2on, and the asymptotic suboptimality of CQL/IQL/AWAC, while being ~36× faster than Q-ensemble methods (30 min vs. 18 h). On real-world Unitree Go1 quadruped tasks (latex mattress), Uni-O4's online-offline-online pipeline achieves 1.62 m/s running speed, outperforming both WTW (sim-to-real) and IQL (offline-to-online) baselines which fail to maintain stable locomotion on the elastic terrain.

## Relation to Prior Work

Uni-O4 builds directly on BPPO (Zhuang et al., 2023), which first applied the PPO clipped surrogate objective to offline RL for monotonic policy improvement, but BPPO required online evaluation to validate each policy update—contradicting the offline setting. The paper departs from BPPO by replacing online evaluation with a proposed AM-Q offline policy evaluation method, and from IQL by exploiting its expectile-regression property (Q_τ → Q* as τ→1) to provide a fixed value function for advantage estimation rather than iteratively updating Q. It also diverges from the broader offline-to-online literature (CQL, AWAC, PEX, Off2on, Cal-ql) by eliminating all extra conservatism, policy constraints, or Q-ensemble regularization, instead relying on the natural trust-region properties of the PPO clip function and the OPE gate for safe multi-step improvement. The ensemble behavior cloning with disagreement regularization draws on Ghosh et al. (2021) for the max-based combined policy but applies it specifically to address multi-modality in offline datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is a unified, simple framework that eliminates the conservatism mismatch plaguing offline-to-online RL: by using the same on-policy PPO objective in both phases, the transition is seamless without performance drops or asymptotic suboptimality. The real-world quadruped robot experiments (online sim → offline real → online real on a latex mattress) demonstrate a concrete deployment pipeline for challenging terrains that are difficult to simulate, with only ~280K real-world steps needed. Limitations include the dependence on a learned dynamics model for OPE (whose quality bounds the evaluation accuracy at ~80–95%), the added computational cost of the policy ensemble, and validation primarily on MuJoCo-scale tasks and a single robot platform. Open questions include whether the AM-Q OPE remains reliable in higher-dimensional or partially observable settings, and whether the ensemble size and disagreement penalty scale gracefully to more complex action spaces.
