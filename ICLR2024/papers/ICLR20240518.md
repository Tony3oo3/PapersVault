## Problem

Autonomous agents struggle to manipulate complex articulated objects, yet human hands adapt manipulation skills across object classes through a close connection between hand force patterns and object articulation. Existing cross-modal methods either assume one-to-one mappings (e.g., GelSight texture to RGB) or fail to handle the extreme scale disparity between dense visual signals and sparse full-hand force pressure maps, leaving no principled way to jointly discover shared structure between vision and tactile force during manipulation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240518/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 10-frame RGB manipulation video sequences (64×64×3 per frame), 10-frame full-hand tactile force pressure heatmaps (16×16×1 per frame, from a piezoresistive glove calibrated in Newtons), and a single canonical object image (64×64×3). Given any one or two of these modalities, it predicts the remaining ones via test-time latent optimization on the learned manifold. The tactile signal is notably sparse and many-to-many with vision (the same object can be manipulated with different forces, and similar forces can arise from different objects), unlike the denser one-to-one coupling of GelSight texture images to RGB.

## Method

The core architecture is a shared signal-agnostic latent manifold with three neural field (MLP) auto-decoder branches for video, tactile, and canonical image, where a single latent code z is decoded into all modalities simultaneously. The key innovation is a set of linear sub-manifold projection layers Γ_k that decompose the global manifold into modality-specific sub-manifolds: the tactile sub-manifold is constrained to 16 dimensions to force focus on dominant manipulation information, the image sub-manifold to 256 dimensions for object geometry, and the video sub-manifold is composed by concatenating the image and tactile projections (z_x = [z_c; z_h], 272-dim), explicitly encoding the compositional relationship between object identity and manipulation type. Training uses a reconstruction loss (L2 per modality) plus a manifold isometry loss that enforces local distance preservation between latent codes and corresponding signal pairs across all three sub-manifolds. At test time, cross-modal prediction is performed by optimizing a latent code via gradient descent to match the given input signal, then decoding through the other modality branches; different random initializations enable variational inference over multiple plausible cross-modal completions.

## Results

On the Visual-Tactile Manipulation Dataset, the proposed method achieves the lowest L2 distance across all three evaluation settings (cross-modal retrieval, generalization to new object instances, and generalization to new categories). For cross-modal retrieval, it achieves 0.0019 (x→h) and 0.0174 (h→x), outperforming the strongest baseline GEM (0.0022 and 0.0194). When generalizing to unseen object categories, the method reaches 0.0020 (x→h) and 0.0118 (h→x), compared to GEM's 0.0021 and 0.0160, demonstrating a 26% improvement in tactile-to-video prediction on the hardest setting. The compositional variant (h,c→x) further reduces tactile-to-video error to 0.00208 in retrieval, showing that disentangling object identity from manipulation significantly aids video synthesis.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all four baselines on every metric in Table 1. Against GEM (the closest competitor, also using a shared latent manifold), the improvement is most pronounced on generalization tasks: 0.0097 vs. 0.0106 on new-object h→x and 0.0118 vs. 0.0160 on new-category h→x (a 26% reduction in L2 error). VisGel, a conditional-GAN approach, achieves the best h→x retrieval score (0.0038) by tightly coupling its conditioning vector to the output, but degrades sharply on generalization (0.0176 on new category), confirming its limited transferability. TouchGo and ObjectFolder, which rely on contrastive/triplet losses, suffer from mode collapse on the sparse tactile-to-video direction (0.0262 and 0.0340 in retrieval respectively), far worse than the manifold-based methods. The paper attributes its advantage to the sub-manifold projection that prevents the dense video modality from dominating the sparse tactile modality in the shared latent space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends GEM (Du et al., 2021), which introduced signal-agnostic latent manifolds with neural field decoders for cross-modal prediction, by adding linear sub-manifold projections that decompose the shared manifold into modality-specific subspaces. The key motivation is the extreme scale mismatch between dense RGB video and sparse full-hand force pressure maps, which prior cross-modal tactile-vision work (VisGel, TouchAndGo, ObjectFolder) did not face because they operated on GelSight surface-texture images with a tighter one-to-one coupling to RGB. The compositional design—where the video latent is constructed by concatenating object-shape and manipulation sub-manifold latents—repurposes the manifold framework to explicitly disentangle 'what object' from 'how it is manipulated,' a structure not present in the original GEM formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first paired dataset of full-hand force pressure maps and manipulation video for articulated objects, filling a gap that prior tactile-vision datasets (which use local GelSight sensors) cannot address for whole-hand manipulation understanding. The emergent T-SNE structure—where 'press' clusters separately from 'squeeze' and 'hold'—offers a data-driven validation of hand-synergy hypotheses from motor control literature. However, the dataset is small (89 objects, 4 categories, 150K frames), the neural field decoder is a simple 6-layer MLP, and the robotic transfer experiment reveals a significant domain gap (object classification drops from 0.97 to 0.48), leaving open whether the approach scales to richer object taxonomies and more dexterous manipulation tasks.
