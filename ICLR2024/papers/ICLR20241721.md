## Problem

Neural collapse theory predicts that class means and weight vectors converge to a simplex ETF, but this explanation breaks down for fixed classifiers whose geometry is not a simplex ETF (e.g., orthogonal matrices). Although fixed orthogonal classifiers have shown cost efficiency and competitive performance, their training dynamics in terms of collapse have remained unanalyzed, and they have not been leveraged in continual or imbalanced learning settings where fixed simplex ETF classifiers have proven effective.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, 224×224 for ImageNet-LT/Places-LT) through a CNN encoder (ResNet, VGG, or DenseNet) followed by ReLU to produce non-negative last-layer features of dimension D. A fixed non-negative orthogonal matrix Q ∈ R^{D×K} (D ≥ K) is applied as the classifier, yielding a K-dimensional probability vector via softmax. In continual learning, inputs arrive as sequential task splits; in imbalanced learning, inputs follow a long-tailed class distribution with imbalance factors up to 100.

## Method

The paper introduces zero-mean neural collapse, which centers class means to the origin rather than the global mean, enabling collapse analysis in the non-negative Euclidean space where orthogonal classifiers reside. The Fixed Non-negative Orthogonal (FNO) classifier is a randomly initialized non-negative orthogonal matrix that is never updated during training; the authors prove (Theorem 1) that the orthogonal layer-peeled model with this classifier achieves global optimality with max-margin, inducing zero-mean neural collapse where class means become equi-norm and mutually orthogonal. A key structural property, Feature Dimension Separation (FDS), emerges from the combination of non-negativity and orthogonality: each class weight vector has a disjoint set of non-zero indices, so feature dimensions dedicated to one class cannot influence decisions for other classes. This FDS is exploited in two applications: (1) masked and weighted softmax in continual learning, where FDS reduces class-wise interference more effectively than masking alone, and (2) arc-mixup in imbalanced learning, a hypersphere-preserving interpolation of class weight vectors that provably avoids cross-class interference only when the classifier is non-negative and orthogonal (Theorem 2).

## Results

On CIFAR10-LT (imbalance factor 100), arc-mixup with the FNO classifier achieves 82.59% accuracy, substantially outperforming mixup+FC+CE (74.24%) and mixup+ETF+DR (75.18%). On CIFAR100-LT (IF=100), the FNO method reaches 49.26% versus 43.80% (FC) and 45.45% (ETF). In continual learning, the FNO classifier with DER++ and buffer 500 achieves 40.81% Class-IL accuracy on S-CIFAR100, compared to 37.80% for the FC classifier, and 38.95% on S-Tiny-ImageNet (buffer 5120) versus 34.72% for FC. On Places-LT with ResNet152 (fine-tuned), arc-mixup+FNO reaches 34.06% versus 24.83% for mixup+FC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the masked replay baseline (Kim et al., 2023a), the FNO classifier consistently outperforms the learnable FC classifier in continual learning across most buffer sizes and datasets, with the largest gains on S-CIFAR100 (e.g., +4.1% Class-IL with DER++/buffer 500: 40.81% vs 37.80%) and S-Tiny-ImageNet (e.g., +4.2% with DER++/buffer 5120: 38.95% vs 34.72%), though it slightly underperforms in a few task-incremental settings. Against the mixup+FC and mixup+ETF baselines from Yang et al. (2022b), arc-mixup+FNO achieves the largest improvements on highly imbalanced settings: +8.35% on CIFAR10-LT (IF=100: 82.59% vs 74.24%) and +5.46% on CIFAR100-LT (IF=100: 49.26% vs 43.80%), while the gap narrows at lower imbalance factors. Against B-mixup (Zhang et al., 2022b), arc-mixup+FNO leads on CIFAR10-LT (IF=100: 82.59% vs 78.70%) but trails slightly at IF=10 (89.50% vs 89.60%). Notably, the FNO method improves tail-class accuracy (e.g., Few-class on ImageNet-LT: 24.48% vs 9.12% for mixup+FC), whereas standard mixup degrades tail-class performance, indicating that the FNO approach addresses the imbalance in the decision space rather than merely boosting head-class accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural collapse framework (Papyan et al., 2020) to a setting where prior theory breaks down: fixed orthogonal classifiers that cannot form a simplex ETF. It introduces 'zero-mean neural collapse,' a new collapse phenomenon where class means center to the origin and converge to an orthogonal (rather than equiangular) configuration, providing the first theoretical analysis of training dynamics for fixed orthogonal classifiers via the layer-peeled model. The FNO classifier instantiates this theory by combining non-negativity with orthogonality, yielding a structural property (feature dimension separation) that the authors then exploit to enhance masked softmax (Kim et al., 2023a) for continual learning and to design arc-mixup, a hypersphere-aware variant of mixup (Zhang et al., 2017) for imbalanced learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-mean neural collapse fills a genuine theoretical gap: fixed orthogonal classifiers were used empirically (Hoffer et al., 2018; Pernici et al., 2021a) but lacked a collapse-based explanation for their behavior, since standard neural collapse predicts simplex ETF geometry that is geometrically incompatible with orthogonality. The FDS property offers a clean structural mechanism for reducing inter-class interference, with practical payoffs in continual and imbalanced learning. However, the D ≥ K requirement (feature dimension must exceed class count) is a significant limitation that restricts applicability to high-dimensional, low-class-count settings, and the authors themselves point to the Thomson problem and generalized neural collapse (Jiang et al., 2023) as needed to address the K >> D regime common in practice.
