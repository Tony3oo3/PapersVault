## Problem

Prior asymptotic generalization error results for kernel methods are derived specifically for kernel ridge regression (KRR), yet neural networks are trained with gradient descent or other first-order algorithms. This paper asks what happens to the generalization error when KRR is replaced by a general spectral algorithm, and whether the well-known KRR saturation phenomenon (where the convergence rate fails to reach the minimax bound) is algorithm-specific or intrinsic to the problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240118/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a kernel K(x,x') with population eigenvalues λ_l, a target function with eigencoefficients c_l, a training set of size N with (possibly noisy) observations, and a spectral learning profile h(λ) that specifies how much each empirical eigenvalue is fit. The output is the asymptotic generalization error expressed as a quadratic functional L[h] of the profile, from which one can read off the optimal profile h*(λ), the convergence rate under power-law spectra, and the spectral localization scale s at which the error is accumulated. The loss functional is fully specified by three 'learning measures' ρ⁽²⁾, ρ⁽¹⁾, ρ⁽ε⁾ that encode all problem-specific kernel and data structure.

## Method

The paper introduces a unified spectral algorithm framework where the estimator is f̂(x) = k(x)ᵀ K⁻¹ h(K/N) y, with h(λ) a free profile (KRR: h=λ/(λ+η); Gradient Flow: h=1−e^{−tλ}; GD: h=1−(1−αλ)ᵗ). The key insight is that the generalization error is quadratic in h, yielding a loss functional L[h] whose minimizer h*(λ) defines the optimal algorithm. Explicit forms of L[h] are derived for two extreme data models: a low-dimensional Circle model (translation-invariant kernel on a lattice, solved exactly via aliasing/N-deformation) and a high-dimensional Wishart model (Gaussian features, solved via RMT resolvent/Stieltjes transform and Wick's theorem). Under power-law spectra λ_l ~ l^{−ν}, c_l² ~ l^{−κ−1}, a scaling analysis identifies the spectral localization scale and shows that in the noisy case both models converge to a simple Naive Model of Noisy Observations (NMNO), while in the noiseless case the optimal algorithm exhibits an overlearning transition at κ=ν−1 where h*(λ) crosses 1, and saturation at κ=2ν becomes intrinsic (unremovable by any algorithm choice).

## Results

Under power-law spectral distributions (λ_l ~ l^{-ν}, c_l² ~ l^{-κ-1}), the paper derives full loss asymptotics for three algorithms: optimally regularized KRR, optimally stopped Gradient Flow (GF), and the optimal spectral algorithm. In the noisy regime (σ²>0), the optimal rate is O(N^{-κ/(κ+1)}), but KRR saturates to O(N^{-2ν/(2ν+1)}) when κ/ν > 2, while GF avoids saturation. In the noiseless regime, the optimal rate is O(N^{-κ}), but all algorithms (including the optimal one) saturate to O(N^{-2ν}) when κ > 2ν — an intrinsic property of the kernel-data model that cannot be removed by algorithm choice. The paper also identifies an overlearning transition at κ = ν−1, where the optimal algorithm switches from underfitting (h(λ)<1) to overfitting (h(λ)>1) the training data.

| Exponent in L = O(N^{-#}) | κ/(κ+1) \| 2ν/(2ν+1) | κ/(κ+1) | κ \| 2ν |
| Spectral localization scale s | ν/(κ+1) \| {0, ν/(2ν+1)} | ν/(κ+1) | ν \| 0 |
| Universality | yes | yes | no |

*The vertical line | separates non-saturated (κ/ν ≤ 2) from saturated (κ/ν > 2) regimes. Spectral localization at scale s means the error is accumulated at eigenvalues λ of order N^{-s}. Universality means asymptotic equality of errors for different problems with the same population spectrum.*

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three spectral algorithms — optimally regularized KRR, optimally stopped Gradient Flow (GF), and the optimal algorithm (minimizer of the loss functional) — across two data models (Circle and Wishart) and a simplified NMNO model. In the noisy regime, KRR achieves the minimax rate O(N^{-κ/(κ+1)}) only when κ/ν ≤ 2; beyond this threshold it saturates to O(N^{-2ν/(2ν+1)}), while GF and the optimal algorithm maintain the minimax rate without saturation. In the noiseless regime, all three algorithms saturate simultaneously at κ > 2ν to O(N^{-2ν}), revealing that this saturation is intrinsic to the kernel-data model rather than algorithm-specific. The Circle and Wishart models are shown to be asymptotically equivalent to the NMNO model in the noisy case (Theorem 2), establishing a universality property that does not hold in the noiseless case. The optimal algorithm's profile h*(λ) is explicitly characterized: it interpolates the data (h*=1) at κ = ν−1 and overlearns (h*>1) for κ > ν−1, a transition invisible at the level of convergence rates alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the asymptotic generalization error analysis of kernel methods from KRR (Bordelon et al. 2020; Jacot et al. 2020a; Wei et al. 2022) to the broader family of spectral algorithms parameterized by a learning profile h(λ), which includes KRR, Gradient Flow, and arbitrary first-order iterative methods as special cases. The spectral algorithm framework itself draws on Bauer et al. (2007) and Lin et al. (2020), while the two data models used for explicit computation — the Wishart model (Cui et al. 2021; Simon et al. 2023) and the Circle model (Spigler et al. 2020; Beaglehole et al. 2023) — are inherited from prior work. The key departure is expressing the loss as a quadratic functional of h(λ) and then minimizing it to identify the optimal algorithm, a step not previously carried out in the kernel generalization literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves an open question by showing that noiseless saturation at κ > 2ν is an intrinsic property of the kernel-data pair (unremovable by any algorithm), whereas noisy saturation is algorithm-dependent and can be eliminated by switching from KRR to Gradient Flow. The introduction of spectral localization as a diagnostic tool and the NMNO universality conjecture (that noisy generalization depends only on the population spectrum, not on non-spectral details) provide a unifying lens for comparing disparate kernel learning settings. Open questions include whether the NMNO universality extends beyond the two models studied, whether the overlearning transition at κ = ν−1 persists for more general data distributions, and whether the full loss asymptotics can be made rigorous for the Wishart model without the RMT approximations used here.
