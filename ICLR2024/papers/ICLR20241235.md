## Problem

A key open question in offline RL is which learning algorithm—Conservative Q-Learning (CQL), Behavior Cloning (BC), or Decision Transformer (DT)—should be preferred under what conditions. Prior work had studied these three paradigms (Q-Learning, Imitation Learning, Sequence Modeling) in isolation or under limited settings, leaving practitioners without systematic guidance on algorithm selection based on data quality, task complexity, and environment stochasticity.

## Input / Output

All three algorithms consume the same offline transition dataset D = {(s, a, r, s')}, but process it differently: DT conditions on trajectory history and returns-to-go vectors (context length 20 for D4RL, 1 for ROBOMIMIC), CQL bootstraps Q-values via temporal-difference updates with a conservative regularization term, and BC performs supervised state-to-action mapping. The paper systematically varies data quality (best/worst X% sorted by return), trajectory lengths, injected random-policy noise, state-space dimensionality (11→17→376), task horizon, and evaluation-time Gaussian stochasticity. Evaluation uses normalized returns on D4RL, success rates on ROBOMIMIC, and normalized scores on ATARI, averaged over 5 seeds and 100 (or 50) evaluation episodes.

## Method

This is a systematic empirical study rather than a new algorithm, designed to isolate the effect of each data, task, and environment property on the three algorithms across D4RL, ROBOMIMIC, and ATARI. The authors sort trajectories by return to study sample efficiency and robustness to suboptimality, vary trajectory lengths using human-generated ROBOMIMIC data (PH, MH splits), inject equal amounts of random-policy data via two strategies, and scale state-space dimensionality and task horizon. For DT specifically, they ablate architectural choices (context length, attention heads, layers, embedding size) and study joint scaling of data and parameters on ATARI, finding that 5× more data yields a 2.5× average score improvement while parameter scaling alone is less effective. The key finding is that no single algorithm dominates: DT is most robust overall and best for sparse-reward and low-quality data settings, BC excels with high-quality human-generated demonstrations, and CQL is preferable under the combination of high stochasticity and low data quality.

## Results

On D4RL (HALFCHEETAH, HOPPER, WALKER), CQL achieves the best dense-reward average of 83.64, while DT leads in the sparse-reward setting at 76.6 vs. CQL's 40.78 and BC's 38.74. On ROBOMIMIC (machine-generated data), DT outperforms CQL and BC by 154% and 51% respectively in the dense setting (89.6 vs. 35.2 vs. 57.2). On ATARI, scaling DT's training data by 5× yields a 2.5× average score improvement, while scaling parameters alone provides no consistent gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DT dominates CQL in sparse-reward settings: on D4RL, DT scores 76.6 vs. CQL's 40.78 (an 88% improvement), and on ROBOMIMIC MG, DT achieves 88.2 vs. CQL's 30 (a 194% improvement). In dense-reward D4RL, CQL edges out DT (83.64 vs. 77.51), but CQL exhibits far higher variance (±7.88 vs. ±1.12). BC is never competitive on machine-generated data (38.74 average on D4RL) but reverses the ranking on human-generated ROBOMIMIC data, where BC outperforms DT and CQL (e.g., 91.3 vs. 83.1 vs. 45.6 on PH). CQL is the most sample-efficient with small amounts of high-quality data (reaching peak at ~5% of dataset vs. DT's ~20%), but degrades with additional suboptimal data, whereas DT's performance never worsens with more data. In stochastic environments with low-quality data (medium replay, p=0.5, σ=0.5), CQL shows superior resilience compared to DT and BC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical comparison of offline RL paradigms initiated by Brandfonbrener et al. (2022), who provided theoretical conditions favoring Sequence Modeling, and Kumar et al. (2023), who compared Q-Learning against Imitation Learning. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, it carves new ground by systematically varying data quality, trajectory length, stochasticity, task horizon, and state-space dimensionality to produce a practical decision framework across CQL, BC, and DT. The DT algorithm itself (Chen et al., 2021) and CQL (Kumar et al., 2020) are used as-is; the contribution is in the targeted experimental design and the resulting conditional recommendations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a clear, evidence-based decision matrix for selecting among the three dominant offline RL paradigms based on their specific data and task characteristics, which is a gap that prior theoretical analyses left unresolved. Key open questions remain: whether findings generalize to model-based offline RL, diffusion-based policies, or compositional/embodied tasks; whether other algorithms within each paradigm (IQL, Trajectory Transformer, TD3+BC) would show similar conditional behavior; and how these results hold in truly stochastic (rather than simulated-stochasticity) environments. The scaling analysis for DT on ATARI (5× data → 2.5× score) offers a concrete roadmap for practitioners investing in larger offline datasets.
