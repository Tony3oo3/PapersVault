## Problem

State-of-the-art LLM compression methods (pruning and quantization) claim negligible performance degradation at 50-60% sparsity or 3-4 bit quantization, but these claims rest almost entirely on perplexity. Because compressed models are derived from the same dense counterpart—sharing identical scale, training strategy, and architecture—perplexity is even less informative than when comparing heterogeneous dense models, yet no systematic evaluation has probed whether perplexity captures the subtle capability shifts introduced by compression.

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds compressed LLMs (Vicuna-7B/13B/33B pruned or quantized via SparseGPT, Wanda, magnitude pruning, or GPTQ) five task types: factoid questions expecting a short entity answer, multiple-choice questions expecting a single letter, in-context documents plus a question expecting a retrieved answer phrase, a news article expecting a <150-word summary, and multi-turn instruction prompts expecting a free-form response. Performance is scored against the dense model using a 5% tolerance threshold (the 'matching compressed LLM' criterion), with GPT-4 serving as judge for summarization quality (consistency, coherence, fluency, relevance) and instruction-following quality (0-10 scale).

## Method

LLM-KICK organizes evaluation into three settings that progressively shift the knowledge burden: (1) closed-book knowledge access (FreebaseQA factoid QA, MMLU multiple-choice QA) tests whether pre-trained knowledge survives compression; (2) in-context knowledge synthesis (TriviaQA retrieval-augmented QA, CNN/DailyMail summarization) tests whether the model can extract and integrate externally provided knowledge; (3) open-ended instruction following (MT-Bench 80 prompts across 8 categories) tests holistic conversational ability. The paper evaluates unstructured sparsity (10-70%), structured N:M sparsity (2:4), and quantization (8-bit, 4-bit, 3-bit) in their default training-free, data-free settings. A key finding is that all SoTA pruning methods fail the matching criterion at trivial sparsities (25-35%) on knowledge-intensive tasks and completely fail for N:M patterns, while quantization (especially 8-bit) remains matching much longer; conversely, even 50%+ pruned models retain robust in-context retrieval and summarization ability, and simple magnitude pruning often matches or exceeds SparseGPT/Wanda within the tolerance regime.

## Results

On Vicuna-7B, all SoTA pruning methods (SparseGPT, Wanda, Magnitude) maintain matching performance (≤5% drop) only up to ~20-25% unstructured sparsity on knowledge-intensive tasks like Factoid-QA (65.44% dense → 60-66% at 20-30%) and MMLU (47.1% dense → 45.5-46.0% at 20-30%), but suffer catastrophic degradation beyond 40% (e.g., Magnitude drops to 13.99% on Factoid-QA and 5.0% on MMLU at 50% sparsity). GPTQ quantization is more robust: 8-bit GPTQ retains ~60% on Factoid-QA and ~47% on MMLU, while 4-bit GPTQ remains matching for Vicuna-13B on MMLU. Notably, pruned Vicuna-7B at 50% sparsity still achieves 5.0-5.1/10 coherence on long-story in-context retrieval (vs. 5.88 dense), and simple one-shot magnitude pruning performs comparably to SparseGPT and Wanda within the matching regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among pruning methods, Wanda and SparseGPT degrade more gracefully than one-shot Magnitude at high sparsity (e.g., at 50% on MMLU: Wanda 38.6% vs. SparseGPT 30.8% vs. Magnitude 5.0%), yet within the matching regime (~20-25%), simple Magnitude performs comparably or slightly better than both SoTA methods, questioning their added complexity. GPTQ quantization clearly outperforms all pruning methods: 8-bit GPTQ retains ~60% on Factoid-QA and ~47% on MMLU, while 4-bit GPTQ remains matching for Vicuna-13B on MMLU, whereas no pruning method achieves matching performance beyond 25-30% on these tasks. N:M structured sparsity (2:4) fails across all tasks and all methods, with performance drops as severe as ≥50% on Factoid-QA. In the in-context retrieval setting, pruned models at 50% sparsity (SparseGPT: 5.07, Wanda: 5.03) remain close to dense (5.88), while Magnitude collapses to 1.23, showing that calibration-based pruning preserves retrieval ability far better. For instruction following, Wanda at 50% sparsity scores 6.84/10 vs. SparseGPT 6.21 and Magnitude 3.31, with dense at 7.76. In the Small-Dense vs. Large-Sparse comparison, a 46.16%-pruned Vicuna-13B (7B effective params) achieves only 31.7-46.3% MMLU vs. 46.7% for dense Vicuna-7B, indicating current pruning cannot justify the architectural overhead.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant evaluation paradigm in LLM compression, where SoTA methods (SparseGPT, Wanda, GPTQ) are validated primarily through perplexity on WikiText. It builds on the broader 'beyond perplexity' evaluation literature for dense LLMs (Muhlgay et al., Kaddour et al., MT-Bench) but is the first to systematically apply multi-dimensional task evaluation to compressed LLMs derived from the same dense counterpart. Conceptually, it repurposes existing benchmarks (MMLU, FreebaseQA, TriviaQA, CNN/DailyMail, MT-Bench) into a unified evaluation protocol (LLM-KICK) specifically designed to expose capability shifts that perplexity masks, rather than proposing a new compression algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SoTA pruning methods collapse at merely 25–30% sparsity on knowledge-intensive tasks—while perplexity remains nearly flat—fundamentally challenges the field's reliance on perplexity as a compression quality metric and suggests that many published compression results may overstate practical utility. The observation that large-sparse models underperform small-dense models of equivalent parameter count raises open questions about whether current pruning algorithms can ever justify their overhead. The paper also identifies a promising direction: pruned LLMs remain robust in-context retrievers even at ≥50% sparsity, suggesting hybrid architectures (compressed backbone + external knowledge) as a practical path forward, while leaving open whether parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA/QLoRA) can recover the lost parametric knowledge.
