## Problem

Shape distances (Procrustes and Riemannian) are widely used to compare neural representations, but their statistical properties in high-dimensional, sample-limited regimes have not been rigorously characterized. Standard plug-in estimators are biased and converge slowly, yet no prior work derived formal convergence bounds or quantified estimator uncertainty, making it difficult to interpret similarity scores in experimental neuroscience where the number of sampled conditions is inherently limited.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240210/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two neural response matrices of shape M×N (M stimuli, N neurons) with bounded activations (‖h(z)‖₂ ≤ B√N) and produces a shape distance or cosine similarity estimate along with an approximate confidence interval. A key design choice is a user-defined upper bound on absolute bias (e.g., 5% or 10%), which the optimization enforces at the cost of increased variance; the resulting confidence interval incorporates both the estimated variance and the maximal bias term. For stochastic networks, two replicates per stimulus suffice to isolate the signal component from trial-to-trial noise.

## Method

The paper first derives non-asymptotic upper and lower bounds on plug-in estimator error, showing the error scales as N·M^{-1/2} (upper) and N^{1/2}·M^{-1/2} (lower) in the worst case, with the M^{-1/2} rate being tight. To overcome the plug-in bias, the authors introduce a method-of-moments estimator that approximates the nuclear norm ‖Σ_ij‖_* = Tr[(Σ_ij Σ_ij^T)^{1/2}] via a truncated polynomial expansion of the square root function over the eigenvalues of Σ_ij Σ_ij^T. Unbiased estimators of each eigenmoment W_p are constructed by pairing independent stimulus observations (adapting Kong & Valiant, 2017), and the polynomial coefficients γ_0,…,γ_P are selected by solving a convex quadratic program that minimizes worst-case MSE subject to a user-specified bias cap. This yields a tunable bias-variance tradeoff and enables conservative confidence intervals that account for both variance and maximal approximation bias.

## Results

The paper derives a high-probability upper bound (Theorem 1) showing plug-in shape distance error scales as O(N·log(N)/M + N·√log(N)/√M), and a matching lower bound (Theorem 2) of (16B²/3π)·N^{1/2}·M^{-1/2} in the worst case, establishing that the M^{-1/2} convergence rate is tight. The proposed method-of-moments estimator achieves substantially lower bias than the plug-in estimator in high-dimensional, sample-limited settings, with conservative 95% confidence intervals that miss the true value in only 2.3% of simulations (vs. 5% nominal). On biological neural data (Stringer et al. 2019; N=40 neurons, M=400 stimuli), the moment estimator correctly identifies zero similarity between independent populations while the plug-in estimator is upwardly biased; with all ~2,800 stimuli, the true similarity between sub-populations is estimated to lie between 0.25 and 0.75.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard plug-in estimator from Williams et al. (2021), which substitutes empirical covariances into the Procrustes/Riemannian shape distance formulas. The moment-based estimator consistently outperforms the plug-in on bias, particularly when true similarity is low, sample size M is small, or dimensionality N is large (Fig. 2A–C). On neural data with ground-truth similarity set to 0 (independent populations, N=40, M=400), the moment estimator's expected value overlaps the true value while the plug-in estimator is clearly upwardly biased (Fig. 4A). Conversely, when ground-truth similarity is 1 (same population, different trials), the plug-in estimator is more accurate and the moment estimator's confidence intervals span nearly the full [0,1] range (Fig. 4B). On ResNet-50 penultimate-layer representations (N=100, ImageNet), the plug-in estimator exhibits positive bias at small M that depends on the effective dimensionality of the response covariance, while the moment estimator provides a better average estimate at the cost of higher variance across resampling folds. Overall, the moment estimator wins on bias in the practically important regime of low similarity and limited samples, but the plug-in retains an advantage in variance and in the high-similarity regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the shape distance framework of Williams et al. (2021), which defined Procrustes and Riemannian shape distances as rotation-invariant metrics on neural representations, by providing the first rigorous non-asymptotic statistical analysis of their plug-in estimators in high-dimensional, sample-limited regimes. The new method-of-moments estimator draws on the spectrum estimation literature of Kong & Valiant (2017) for unbiased eigenmoment estimation and the polynomial approximation framework of Adams et al. (2018) to approximate the nuclear norm. The paper positions itself against the broader representational similarity literature (CKA, RSA, SVCCA) by noting that, with the exceptions of a few works on RSA and CKA, statistical efficiency and estimator uncertainty for shape distances had not been formally characterized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that standard plug-in estimators of shape distance are systematically biased upward (overestimating similarity) in high-dimensional, low-sample settings, with error scaling as N^{1/2} M^{-1/2} in the worst case—a finding with direct implications for experimental design in neuroscience where the number of stimuli is hard-limited. The application to real neural data (Stringer et al., 2019) reveals that even 40 well-tuned neurons with 400 stimuli yield confidence intervals spanning nearly the entire [0,1] similarity range, challenging the implicit assumption in prior work that plug-in estimates are reliable. Open questions include whether the N-dependence in the upper bound can be tightened to depend on intrinsic dimension, whether the N^{1/2} gap between upper and lower bounds can be closed, and whether the bias-variance tradeoff framework extends to other representational similarity metrics like CKA in the stochastic setting.
