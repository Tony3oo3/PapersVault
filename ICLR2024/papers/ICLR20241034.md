## Problem

Existing Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL) methods rely on low-pass GNN encoders and homophily-based objectives, causing them to fail on heterophilic graphs where connected nodes tend to have dissimilar labels. While spectral GNNs with learnable polynomial filters adaptively fit arbitrary filter shapes and excel in supervised settings across homophily levels, directly substituting them into GCL pipelines causes performance degradation due to the lack of sufficient supervision to constrain the powerful polynomial fitting. The paper asks how to incorporate the expressiveness of spectral polynomial filters into contrastive learning while maintaining effective optimization in label-scarce self-supervised scenarios.

## Input / Output

The method takes node features X ∈ R^{N×F} and an adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{N×N} as input, with no label information required during the self-supervised training stage. It produces node embeddings Z = αZ_L + βZ_H (a linear combination of low-pass and high-pass spectral views), along with learned Chebyshev polynomial filter coefficients (γ parameters) and the combination weights α, β. Downstream evaluation uses a frozen linear probe on the learned embeddings for node classification, following the standard two-stage protocol.

## Method

POLYGCL replaces the standard low-pass GNN encoder with two Chebyshev polynomial filter encoders whose shapes are constrained via prefix-sum (high-pass, monotonically increasing) and prefix-difference (low-pass, monotonically decreasing) reparameterizations of the filter values at Chebyshev nodes. This decoupling restricts each encoder to fit only one spectral band, easing optimization without labels. The two encoders produce low-pass view Z_L and high-pass view Z_H, which are combined linearly as Z = αZ_L + βZ_H. The training objective is a BCE-based contrastive loss (analogous to DGI) that simultaneously maximizes mutual information between local node embeddings and a global mean-pooled summary for both spectral views, with negatives generated by shuffling node features. Theoretically, the paper proves via Spectral Regression Loss analysis that the linear combination achieves a lower expected SRL upper bound than low-pass-only filtering in heterophilic settings, and via mutual information arguments that the combined representation yields a tighter downstream Bayes error bound than either view alone.

## Results

On 9 real-world datasets spanning homophilic (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed) and heterophilic (Cornell, Texas, Wisconsin, Actor, Chameleon, Squirrel) graphs, POLYGCL achieves the best or second-best node classification accuracy on 12 out of 14 benchmarks (including 5 additional heterophilic graphs in Table 3). The most striking gains are on heterophilic graphs: 56.49% on Squirrel (vs. 48.86% for the best baseline GBT, a 15.6% relative improvement) and 41.15% on Actor (vs. 38.26% for GREET, a 7.6% relative improvement). On homophilic graphs, POLYGCL also leads, e.g., 87.57% on Cora and 87.15% on Pubmed. On the large-scale arXiv-year graph (>1M edges), POLYGCL achieves 43.07% accuracy while several baselines (MVGRL, BGRL, GRACE) fail due to OOM or preprocessing timeouts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

POLYGCL outperforms all 11 baselines (DGI, MVGRL, GGD, GMI, CCA-SSG, BGRL, GBT, GRACE, GCA, GraphCL, GREET) on 12 of 14 real-world benchmarks. The largest margins appear on strongly heterophilic graphs: on Squirrel, POLYGCL (56.49%) surpasses the next-best GBT (48.86%) by 7.6 points, and on Actor it beats GREET (38.26%) by 2.9 points. On homophilic graphs the advantage is smaller but consistent, e.g., 87.57% on Cora vs. CCA-SSG's 87.39% and 87.15% on Pubmed vs. MVGRL's 86.30%. POLYGCL loses on two of the five larger heterophilic graphs in Table 3: Minesweeper (86.11% vs. MVGRL's 90.07%) and Questions (75.33% vs. GBT's 75.98%), though it wins on the remaining three (Roman-empire, Amazon-ratings, Tolokers). On the cSBM synthetic graphs (Table 1), POLYGCL achieves 7 of 9 best or second-best results, with the strongest performance at extreme homophily/heterophily (|φ|=1) and competitive results at intermediate levels.

## Relation to Prior Work

POLYGCL sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: (1) Graph Contrastive Learning methods (DGI, GRACE, GraphCL, GCA, etc.) that rely on low-pass GNN encoders and homophily-based objectives, and (2) spectral GNNs with learnable polynomial filters (GPR-GNN, ChebNetII, BernNet) that adaptively fit arbitrary filter shapes in supervised settings. The paper's key departure is recognizing that directly transplanting polynomial filters into GCL encoders actually degrades performance (Figure 1), and instead constraining the polynomial to separate low-pass and high-pass channels before performing contrastive learning between them. The optimization objective builds on DGI's mutual information maximization framework, while the spectral analysis draws on the Spectral Regression Loss from EvenNet.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a genuine gap: self-supervised representation learning on heterophilic graphs was essentially unsolved, with all prior GCL methods implicitly assuming homophily. The theoretical guarantees (Theorem 1, Corollary 2) provide formal justification for why high-pass information is necessary and why the linear combination strategy is optimal, which is valuable beyond this specific architecture. Limitations include the theoretical analysis being restricted to binary classification on k-regular graphs (though generalized in Appendix B), reliance on Assumption 1 (positive spectral correlation between labels and features) which may not hold universally, and the use of simple shuffle-based negatives. Open questions include whether the approach extends naturally to multi-class settings, dynamic graphs, and whether the polynomial order K can be adapted per graph without additional supervision.
