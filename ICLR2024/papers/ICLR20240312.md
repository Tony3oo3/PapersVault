## Problem

Generative brain decoding from fMRI has achieved remarkable image reconstruction fidelity, but fMRI's ~0.5 Hz temporal resolution fundamentally precludes real-time decoding and tracking of dynamic visual processing. It remains unknown whether magnetoencephalography (MEG), with its ~5,000 Hz sampling rate, carries sufficient signal to support open-set image retrieval and generation from a continuous stream of brain activity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240312/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes MEG recordings from 272 radial gradiometer channels, downsampled to 120 Hz and epoched from −500 ms to +1,000 ms around stimulus onset (500 ms image presentations from the THINGS database). For retrieval, the output is a ranked list over a candidate image bank (evaluated via top-5 accuracy and relative median rank in latent embedding space); for generation, the output is a 256×256 RGB image produced by a latent diffusion model conditioned on three predicted embeddings (CLIP-Vision 257×768, CLIP-Text 77×768, AutoKL 4×64×64). Unlike fMRI pipelines that operate on GLM beta values averaged across repetitions, this approach decodes from single-trial MEG windows, enabling temporally-resolved analysis with 100-ms sliding windows.

## Method

The pipeline comprises three modules: (i) frozen pretrained image embeddings (DINOv2, CLIP, VGG-19), (ii) a dilated residual ConvNet brain module adapted from Defossez et al. (2022) that maps an MEG window to a single latent vector via a temporal aggregation layer (affine projection, global average pooling, or attention) followed by two MLP heads, and (iii) a frozen latent diffusion model (50 DDIM steps, guidance 7.5) conditioned on the predicted embeddings. Training uses a convex combination of a bidirectional InfoNCE (CLIP) loss for retrieval and an MSE loss for generation, with the weight λ selected by top-5 accuracy on a held-out set. The key architectural choice is regressing a single latent per MEG window rather than a per-timepoint sequence, enabling direct conditioning of the generator. This yields a 7× improvement over linear Ridge baselines, with DINOv2 embeddings showing the strongest performance for late (post-offset) brain responses, and high-level semantic features decodable from MEG while low-level features remain better recovered from 7T fMRI.

## Results

On the THINGS-MEG retrieval task, the deep ConvNet decoder achieves ~70% top-5 accuracy on the small test set (200 images, one per category) and ~13% on the large test set (2,400 images, 12 per category), representing a 7× improvement over linear Ridge regression baselines. For image generation, MEG-decoded images preserve high-level semantics (CLIP score 0.77–0.81, Inception 0.67–0.70) but recover far fewer low-level details (PixCorr 0.06–0.09) compared to the analogous fMRI pipeline on NSD (PixCorr 0.305, CLIP 0.917). Temporally-resolved analysis reveals two retrieval peaks: one around 200–275 ms post-onset and another around 150–200 ms post-offset, with DINOv2 embeddings performing best in the late window.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the proposed MEG decoder and an analogous fMRI pipeline (Ridge regression on NSD 7T fMRI data, following Ozcelik & VanRullen 2023). The fMRI baseline dominates on every metric, most dramatically on low-level features: PixCorr is 0.305 for fMRI versus 0.058–0.090 for MEG, and AlexNet(2) is 0.962 vs. 0.695–0.774. The gap narrows for high-level semantics (CLIP: 0.917 fMRI vs. 0.700–0.811 MEG; Inception: 0.910 vs. 0.593–0.703), suggesting MEG captures category-level information reasonably well but struggles with fine-grained spatial and color details, consistent with its ~cm spatial resolution versus fMRI's ~mm. For retrieval, the deep ConvNet MEG decoder outperforms linear Ridge regression baselines by a factor of 7 (≈70% vs. ≈10% top-5 accuracy on the small test set), confirming that nonlinear temporal modeling of MEG signals is essential. The authors attribute the MEG–fMRI gap primarily to the lower spatial resolution of MEG (~300 sensors vs. ~100,000 voxels) rather than to the generative pipeline itself, since the same diffusion-based generator applied to fMRI betas recovers both low- and high-level features faithfully.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of brain-decoding research: (1) MEG-based speech decoding using a dilated residual ConvNet (Defossez et al., 2022), whose architecture is adapted here to regress a single image latent per trial, and (2) fMRI-to-image generative decoding via latent diffusion conditioned on pretrained embeddings (Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023), whose generation pipeline is reused verbatim. The key departure is the combination of MEG's high temporal resolution (~5,000 Hz) with an open-set generative decoder, a pairing the authors note has not been attempted in prior MEG or EEG studies, which have been limited to small-class classification or speech retrieval. Training combines a CLIP-style contrastive objective (for retrieval) with an MSE regression objective (for generation), a dual-loss strategy not previously applied in the MEG image-decoding setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates, for the first time, that MEG signals can condition a diffusion model to generate recognizable images, opening a path toward real-time brain-computer interfaces that fMRI's ~0.5 Hz temporal resolution fundamentally precludes. However, the results are preliminary: only four participants are studied, low-level features (object position, color) are poorly preserved compared to 7T fMRI, and the pipeline depends heavily on pretrained image and generation modules that are not fine-tuned. Open questions include whether fine-tuning the image/generation modules or combining heterogeneous visual features could close the low-level gap, whether the approach extends to EEG for clinical deployment, and whether the two-peak temporal structure (onset and offset) reflects genuinely complementary information or an artifact of the windowing protocol.
