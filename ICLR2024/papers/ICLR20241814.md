## Problem

Evaluating text-generative vision-language models (Text-VLMs) on open-ended VQA is hampered by three issues: existing benchmarks provide only a single aggregated score that masks model-specific strengths and weaknesses, open-ended answers introduce granularity ambiguity (e.g., 'dog' vs. 'Newfoundland dog'), and there is no standardized protocol to compare Text-VLMs against discriminative VLMs on the same tasks. Additionally, standard NLP metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, BertScore) fail to correlate with human judgment when ground-truth answers are very short and model predictions vary in length.

## Input / Output

The benchmark feeds each model a cropped image (ground-truth bounding box crop from ImageNet, COCO, ActivityNet center frames, or OVAD object crops) paired with a natural-language question auto-generated from the class name. For fine-grained datasets (ImageNet, ActivityNet), if the initial answer is judged incorrect by ClipM@1, a second follow-up question is generated from the concept hierarchy (e.g., 'What type of dog is this?') and the model's second response is evaluated. The output is a free-form text answer scored by multiple metrics (EM, Cont, ClipM@1/@5, and their synonym-aware variants), with model predictions truncated to ~45 words to handle LLaVA's verbose outputs.

## Method

The paper constructs four oVQA subbenchmarks (Object, Activity, Attribute, plus classical VQA) by transforming existing classification datasets into question-answer pairs, using bounding-box crops as visual guidance to resolve spatial ambiguity. The key mechanism is a hierarchy-based follow-up procedure: when a model's answer is incorrect under ClipM@1, the system identifies the most semantically similar parent node in the label hierarchy (WordNet for ImageNet, the ActivityNet taxonomy, or OVAD attribute types) and asks a targeted follow-up like 'What type of {parent} is this?', with a similarity threshold δ=0.37 to avoid disclosing ground-truth information. For evaluation, the paper benchmarks 14 metrics (from EM through GPT-4-as-judge) against a 1000-question human annotation study on VQAv2, finding that Cont (substring match) is the best low-cost metric while GPT-4 10-shot achieves 0.972 Pearson correlation with human scores. Retrieval upper bounds are established by running discriminative VLMs (CLIP, EVA-CLIP, BLIP-2) in a zero-shot multiple-choice setting on the same datasets, enabling direct comparison between generative and discriminative approaches.

## Results

On ImageNet-oVQA (fine-grained objects, 1000 classes), BLIP-2 OPT achieves the best ClipM@1 of 67.22% with follow-up questions, followed by BLIP-2 T5 (64.71%) and LLaVA (57.12%), while VQA-finetuned models (BLIP, X²-VLM) score only 29–35%. On classical VQAv2, VQA-finetuned models dominate: X²-VLM_vqaL reaches 81.34% EM, BLIP_vqa 77.50%, and InstructBLIP V 77.54%, versus 62.80% for BLIP-2 T5 and 0.00% EM for LLaVA (though LLaVA scores 55.66% Cont). Follow-up questions using the concept hierarchy improve average performance by ~32% across all tested models on fine-grained tasks. The zero-shot retrieval upper bound (CLIP EVA-CLIP) reaches 81.60% R@1 on ImageNet, indicating a substantial gap (~14 points) between discriminative and generative VLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BLIP-2 (generic pretrained, zero-shot) clearly outperforms all VQA-finetuned models on fine-grained tasks: on ImageNet-oVQA it achieves 67.22% ClipM@1 vs. 34.68% for BLIP_vqa and 33.50% for X²-VLM_vqaL, and on ActivityNet-oVQA 62.02% vs. 30.35%. Conversely, VQA-finetuned models (BLIP, X²-VLM) and instruction-tuned InstructBLIP dominate on classical VQAv2 (77–81% EM) and attribute classification (OVAD-oVQA: 43–46% Cont Syn vs. 22–33% for BLIP-2/LLaVA), likely because attributes lie in the tail of generic pretraining distributions. InstructBLIP is competitive on coarse-grained COCO-oVQA (59.58% ClipM@1) and attributes but lags BLIP-2 on fine-grained ImageNet and ActivityNet. LLaVA produces 0% EM on nearly all tasks due to its long, descriptive answers, yet achieves competitive Cont scores (e.g., 45.08% on COCO, 55.66% on VQAv2), highlighting the inadequacy of EM for instruction-tuned models. The retrieval upper bound (CLIP EVA-CLIP at 81.60% R@1 on ImageNet) remains ~14 points above the best Text-VLM, confirming that generating the correct class name in language space is substantially harder than discriminative classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the vocabulary-free image classification evaluation framework of Conti et al. (2023), adapting their cosine-similarity-based ClipMatch metric to the VQA setting, and on the VQAv2 scoring protocol of Goyal et al. (2017) for classical VQA evaluation. Conceptually, it departs from prior VQA benchmarking (VQAv2, GQA) by repurposing well-established classification datasets (ImageNet, COCO, ActivityNet, OVAD) into fine-grained open-ended VQA subbenchmarks, and introduces a novel follow-up question mechanism grounded in concept hierarchies (WordNet, ActivityNet's taxonomy, OVAD's attribute hierarchy) to resolve the granularity ambiguity inherent in open-ended answering. The paper also positions itself against the broader NLP evaluation literature (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, BertScore, BLEURT, LERC, BEM) by systematically testing whether these metrics transfer to the short-answer VQA regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The stratified benchmark (objects, actions, attributes) provides a diagnostic tool that reveals complementary model strengths invisible in aggregated VQA scores: pretrained generic models (BLIP-2) excel at fine-grained object/activity classification while VQA-finetuned models dominate on attributes and classical VQA. The finding that no learned NLP metric outperforms a simple substring check (Contains) for short-answer VQA evaluation is practically significant, redirecting evaluation effort toward LLM-based judges (GPT-4, LLaMA-2) or accepting Cont's known vulnerability to answer-listing exploits. Open questions include the cost scalability of LLM-based evaluation, the sub-optimal similarities ClipM can produce (e.g., 'fossil' matching 'digital watch'), and whether the follow-up mechanism generalizes beyond the specific hierarchies used here.
