## Problem

High-resolution imaging simultaneously demands more computation, reduces frame rates, and increases data transmission, creating a fundamental tension between accuracy and efficiency in visual sensing systems. Existing foveal-peripheral vision approaches reduce pixel count but require training entirely new classifiers on the sparse views, demanding large labeled datasets and preventing reuse of existing back-end models. No prior work combines sequential saccade control with continuous scene reconstruction to recover a full-resolution image from sparse foveal-peripheral samples.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a temporal sequence of foveal-peripheral views, where each view is a binary-masked version of the original image: one N×N patch (the foveal center) is sampled at probability 1 and all remaining pixels at a small probability μ (1–2%). For GTSRB the input is 112×112 with 4×4 patches (28×28 fovea); for ImageNet it is 224×224 with either 4×4 (56×56) or 7×7 (32×32) foveal regions. The system outputs a full-resolution reconstructed image at each timestep (fed directly to a pre-trained classifier) and a discrete saccade action selecting the next foveal center patch from the N×N grid.

## Method

The architecture has three modules: a 3-layer ConvLSTM predictive reconstruction model (self-supervised, trained with a hybrid MSE + λ[1−SSIM] loss to fill in missing pixels from accumulated sparse views), an evaluation module (a frozen pre-trained classifier or SSIM metric that scores reconstruction quality), and an A2C saccade controller (ResNet-18 actor and critic) that reads the ConvLSTM hidden state to select the next foveal center. The saccade policy is trained via policy gradient on the advantage function, with an invalid-action mask (log-penalty) preventing the foveal center from revisiting already-sampled patches. The key design choice is that the reconstruction model is trained independently with random foveal centers, making it agnostic to the saccade policy, and the entire front-end can be swapped in front of any existing classifier without retraining the back-end. The saccade model proves highly transferable: training on only 500 of 1000 ImageNet classes yields near-identical performance to full-data training.

## Results

On ImageNet (ILSVRC 2012), the proposed 7×7 foveal-peripheral model achieves 70.9% top-1 accuracy using only 28% of the original pixels over 7 glimpses, while the 4×4 model reaches 73.7% with 41% pixel usage over 5 glimpses. With an early-stop mechanism, the 4×4 model achieves 69.4% accuracy at just 30% pixel usage (3.5 average glimpses). On GTSRB, learned saccades combined with scene reconstruction yield 91.1–91.8% top-1 accuracy (vs. 92.1% for the full-resolution baseline), using roughly 8% of pixels per glimpse. Overall, the framework reduces pixel usage by over 70% while maintaining comparable classification accuracy to full-resolution input.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Bio-FCG (Lukanov et al., 2021), the proposed 7×7 model achieves 70.9% top-1 accuracy versus 65.17% at nearly identical pixel usage (28% vs. 27%), a ~5.7 percentage-point gain. Against Saccader (Elsayed et al., 2019), the 7×7 model matches or slightly exceeds accuracy (70.9% vs. 70.0%) while using only 28% of pixels compared to Saccader's 70%, a 60% reduction in data. The 4×4 variant further extends the lead to 73.7% accuracy at 41% pixel usage, outperforming both baselines on accuracy. A critical practical distinction is that Bio-FCG and Saccader both re-train or fine-tune their classifiers on foveal-peripheral inputs, whereas the proposed framework requires no backend retraining, preserving the original classifier's performance. The paper also reports that training the saccade controller on only 500 of 1000 ImageNet classes yields 73.6% accuracy (vs. 73.7% with all classes), demonstrating high data efficiency and transferability that the baselines do not offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established lines of work: foveal-peripheral vision-inspired sampling (Lukanov et al. 2021, Uzkent & Ermon 2020, Cheung et al. 2016), reinforcement-learning-based saccade/attention control (Elsayed et al. 2019, Chen 2021), and self-supervised image reconstruction from sparse inputs (He et al. 2021, Lotter et al. 2016). The key departure is that prior foveal-peripheral systems either train new classifiers directly on the sparse views or use a single foveal center without sequential saccades, whereas this paper is the first to combine learned multi-step saccade control with a ConvLSTM-based continuous scene reconstruction that outputs a full-resolution image compatible with any existing back-end classifier. The use of SSIM-augmented hybrid loss for reconstruction, rather than MSE alone, is a practical but non-trivial design choice motivated by the extreme sparsity of the input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play nature of the framework—requiring no retraining of the downstream classifier—makes it a practical drop-in front-end for energy-constrained or bandwidth-limited vision systems, a setting where the 70% pixel reduction is directly meaningful. The finding that saccade strategies transfer across unseen classes (50% of ImageNet classes suffice) suggests that 'where to look' is a relatively scene-agnostic skill, which could reduce annotation costs for deploying such systems in new domains. Open questions include whether the approach extends to dense prediction tasks (detection, segmentation), how it performs under adversarial or occluded conditions, and whether the learned saccade patterns align with or diverge from human scanpaths, which the authors flag as future work.
