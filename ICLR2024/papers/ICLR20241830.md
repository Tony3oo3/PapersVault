## Problem

All existing neural field methods (NeRF, DVGO, NeuS, D-NeRF, etc.) formulate scene representation as a regression task optimized with MSE loss, yet this fundamental choice has never been questioned. The paper identifies that neural fields suffer from inherently weak supervision signals (multiple sampled points per pixel with no per-point ground truth), making regression formulations prone to overfitting and poor generalization. The authors ask whether a classification formulation could address this gap and show it does, often dramatically.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241830/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the same inputs as standard neural fields: 5D coordinate queries (x, y, z, θ, φ) and training images with calibrated camera poses. The key structural difference is at the network output: instead of a 3-channel continuous color vector, the final layer produces 24 logits (8 binary bits per RGB channel), which are decoded back to continuous [0,1] values via binary decoding before volume rendering. The rendered outputs—novel view images, surface normals, depth maps, and volume densities—are identical in format to standard NFR methods, making NFC a drop-in replacement.

## Method

NFC transforms any Neural Field Regressor into a classifier via two minimal modifications: (1) Target Encoding, which maps each 8-bit color channel value to its binary representation, converting the 3-channel regression into a 24-way multi-label binary classification; and (2) a channel-wise BCE classification loss where the decoded predicted probabilities are compared against the ground-truth color (treated as a soft probabilistic label), with an auxiliary MSE term to prevent gradient vanishing when volume rendering pushes predicted values above 1. The classification loss dominates optimization (typically 1–2 orders of magnitude larger than MSE), effectively reshaping the loss landscape toward flatter minima. Implementation requires only changing the final layer from 3 to 24 logits and swapping the loss function, adding roughly 4–6% training time with negligible rendering overhead.

## Results

On the Replica dataset, DVGO-C (NFC) achieves a mean PSNR of 34.11 vs. 17.08 for DVGO-R (NFR), a 99.7% PSNR gain, with SSIM improving from 0.700 to 0.956 and LPIPS dropping from 0.507 to 0.0598. On Tanks & Temples, all three backbones improve consistently: DVGO gains +0.77 dB PSNR, NeRF gains +0.52 dB, and NeuS gains +1.70 dB. For surface reconstruction, NeuS-C reduces mean Chamfer-ℓ₁ distance by 74.6% and improves F-score by 273% over NeuS-R across eight Replica scenes, while incurring only ~4–6% extra training time and near-zero extra rendering cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares NFC against the standard regression (MSE-loss) variant of five neural field backbones under identical hyperparameters. DVGO (Sun et al., CVPR 2022) shows the most dramatic improvement on Replica (PSNR 17.08→34.11) and a solid +0.77 dB on T&T (22.41→23.18). Vanilla NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2021) gains +0.52 dB PSNR on T&T (22.16→22.68) with LPIPS dropping from 0.382 to 0.315. NeuS (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2021) benefits most in surface reconstruction, with Chamfer-ℓ₁ dropping from 30.24 to 7.69 and F-score rising from 14.87 to 55.56 on Replica. D-NeRF (Pumarola et al., CVPR 2021) improves on dynamic scenes (Lego: PSNR 21.64→23.11; Hook: LPIPS 0.118→0.039). Strivec (Gao et al., ICCV 2023) also improves (PSNR 28.53→32.10 on Chair) but less dramatically, which the authors attribute to Strivec already generalizing well on simple scenes. In every comparison, NFC wins on all reported metrics; no baseline outperforms or ties the classification variant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard formulation of neural fields (NeRF, DVGO, NeuS, D-NeRF) as regression problems by reframing them as multi-label classification tasks via a binary-number Target Encoding of continuous color targets. It draws on the label-embedding/target-encoding literature from classical classification (Bengio et al., Akata et al.) but is the first to apply such encoding to continuous regression targets in coordinate-based neural networks. The method is deliberately architecture-agnostic: it only modifies the final output layer and the optimization objective, leaving the backbone (MLP, voxel grid, etc.) untouched. The authors explicitly note that prior neural field work has not explored a classification framework, positioning this as a fundamental rethinking rather than an incremental architectural change.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a simple BCE-based classification loss with binary target encoding dramatically outperforms standard MSE regression across multiple architectures and datasets challenges the field's default assumption that continuous-valued targets in neural fields are best handled by regression. The technique is nearly free computationally (+4–6% training time, negligible rendering overhead) and robust to a wide range of the loss-weight hyperparameter λ, making it a practical plug-and-play improvement. However, the paper acknowledges that gains diminish when the underlying task is simple or generalization is already strong, and the theoretical explanation for why classification yields flatter minima (and thus better generalization) is left as future work. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-color targets (e.g., SDF values, optical flow) and whether the binary encoding is optimal versus other discrete encodings.
