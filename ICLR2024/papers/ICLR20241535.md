## Problem

In cross-silo federated learning with heterogeneous client devices, synchronous algorithms like FedAvg waste compute waiting for straggler clients, while asynchronous algorithms like FedAsync and FedBuff suffer from stale local models that cause client drift and degraded accuracy on non-IID data. Existing mitigations—client selection, staleness-weighted aggregation, and tiered FL—either exclude slow clients (inappropriate for cross-silo settings where all institutions must participate), introduce unnecessary penalties when speed disparities are small, or suffer from inflexible fixed groupings that fail under speed changes.

## Input / Output

Each client receives the current global model weights and a dynamically assigned number of local training steps Q (bounded by [Q_min, Q_max]) and performs mini-batch SGD on its local data (handwritten digits, natural images, or medical images such as MRI and dermoscopy). The client sends back the model difference Δ_i = w_{i,0} − w_{i,Q} along with its response time, which the server uses to update its speed estimate. The server outputs an updated global model after each group aggregation and new per-client step assignments for the next round, organized into arrival groups with expected and latest arrival times.

## Method

FedCompass introduces the Compass scheduler, a server-side component that profiles each client's computing speed from prior response times and dynamically assigns varying local step counts Q ∈ [Q_min, Q_max] so that a group of clients finishes local training nearly simultaneously. Clients are organized into arrival groups with an expected arrival time T_a and a latest arrival time T_max; when all (or the last) clients in a group arrive, the server performs a single group aggregation rather than per-client updates, reducing global update frequency and thus model staleness. The scheduler is resilient to sudden speed changes: if a client cannot be assigned to any existing group within the valid Q range, a new group is created with a T_a optimized for future group merging, and the total number of groups at equilibrium is bounded by ⌈log_Q(μ)⌉ where μ is the slowest-to-fastest speed ratio. The design is orthogonal to server-side aggregation strategies, allowing FedCompass to be combined with momentum (FedCompass+M) or normalized averaging (FedCompass+N) for further gains on non-IID data. A convergence analysis shows FedCompass reaches a first-order stationary point of the non-convex global loss with a rate of O(1/√T) under standard smoothness and bounded-variance assumptions.

## Results

FedCompass achieves the fastest wall-clock time to reach target validation accuracy across all tested settings, serving as the 1.00× baseline. Under high client heterogeneity (exponential distribution, m=20 clients), synchronous FedAvg requires 5.94× the time on MNIST-Dirichlet and 9.05× on CIFAR-10-Dirichlet, while FedCompass+M reduces this to 0.83× and 0.98× respectively. On the real-world Fed-IXI dataset (0.98 DICE target), FedCompass is 2.20× faster than FedAvg under exponential heterogeneity. FedAsync and FedBuff frequently fail to reach target accuracy (marked '−') in low-heterogeneity settings due to client drift from unnecessary staleness penalties.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedCompass outperforms all five baselines in the majority of settings, with the largest gains under high client heterogeneity. Against synchronous FedAvg and FedAvgM, FedCompass is 4.9–10.9× faster on Dirichlet-partitioned datasets with m=20 clients and exponential speed distributions, while matching or slightly exceeding them in homogeneous settings (0.89–1.10×). Against asynchronous FedAsync, FedCompass wins in nearly all settings where FedAsync converges, and FedAsync fails entirely (marked '−') in 8 of 27 tested configurations, particularly under low heterogeneity where staleness penalties cause client drift. Against FedBuff, FedCompass is consistently faster (1.00× vs. 1.22–2.77×), with the gap widening as heterogeneity increases. Against tiered FedAT, FedCompass is competitive in homogeneous settings (1.00× vs. 1.02–1.04×) but pulls ahead substantially under heterogeneity (1.00× vs. 1.83–2.90×), as FedAT's fixed tiers suffer from inner-tier waiting. The server-side optimization variants FedCompass+M and FedCompass+N further reduce wall-clock time to 0.69–1.19× in many settings, demonstrating the scheduler's orthogonality to aggregation strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedCompass builds directly on the FedAvg framework (McMahan et al., 2017) but departs from its synchronous protocol by introducing a semi-asynchronous Compass scheduler that dynamically assigns per-client local step counts based on estimated computing speeds. It positions itself against three existing families of solutions to device heterogeneity: client selection (FedAR, FedCS), staleness-weighted asynchronous aggregation (FedAsync, FedBuff), and tiered FL (FedAT), arguing that each has fundamental limitations in cross-silo settings where all clients must participate and computing speeds can shift abruptly. The key conceptual departure is treating scheduling and aggregation as orthogonal concerns, enabling the scheduler to be combined with server-side optimizations (momentum, normalized averaging) without modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Cross-silo FL in healthcare, finance, and scientific computing is a high-stakes deployment scenario where heterogeneous institutional hardware is the norm, and FedCompass provides a principled, theoretically grounded solution that avoids the accuracy degradation of purely asynchronous methods while eliminating the straggler bottleneck of synchronous ones. The convergence analysis (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) is a meaningful contribution, providing explicit bounds that quantify how client speed ratios and local step ranges affect convergence. Limitations include the assumption of reliable clients (inappropriate for cross-device FL), a warm-up period before the scheduler stabilizes, and the need to tune Q_min/Q_max (though ablations show robustness). Open questions include scalability to larger client pools, interaction with differential privacy mechanisms, and performance under more extreme or adversarial speed variations.
