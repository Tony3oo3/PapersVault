## Problem

Existing plug-and-play (PnP) methods for solving imaging inverse problems rely exclusively on denoisers as implicit priors, limiting their ability to leverage the richer statistical structure captured by more general restoration networks such as super-resolution models. No principled framework existed for using arbitrary pre-trained restoration operators as priors, nor for providing convergence guarantees when the prior is not a denoiser. This paper closes that gap by extending the PnP formalism and its theoretical analysis from denoisers to general restoration operators.

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-quality observation y = Ax + e, a known measurement operator A (e.g., blur convolution or downscaling), and a pre-trained restoration network R that was trained on a *different* degradation task (bicubic downsampling + AWGN via SwinIR). The restoration network's internal degradation operator H (bicubic downsampling) need not match the target measurement operator A, enabling transfer of a model trained for one task to serve as a prior for another. The output is a restored high-quality image, iteratively refined over DRP iterations with a conjugate-gradient-based scaled proximal step enforcing measurement consistency.

## Method

DRP alternates between a restoration step z^k = x^{k-1} − γτ(x^{k-1} − R(Hx^{k-1})) and a scaled proximal step x^k = sprox_{γg}(z^k) that enforces data fidelity under a weighted norm defined by H^T H. The key theoretical insight is a generalization of Tweedie's formula: when R is an MMSE estimator, the residual H^T H(x − R(Hx)) equals −σ²∇log p_s(Hx), identifying the implicit regularizer h(x) = −τσ² log p_s(Hx) and showing DRP fixed points satisfy the first-order optimality conditions of f = g + h. Convergence to a stationary point is proven under mild assumptions (Lipschitz gradient of h, positive definiteness of H^T H) without requiring convexity of g or non-expansiveness of R. In practice, a prior refinement strategy gradually reduces the super-resolution factor q of the SwinIR prior (e.g., from 3× to 2×) to transition from an aggressive prior to one preserving fine details, analogous to annealing the noise level σ in denoiser-based PnP.

## Results

On image deblurring with a Gaussian blur kernel (std=1.6), DRP using a SwinIR super-resolution prior achieves 30.69 dB PSNR / 0.056 LPIPS on Set3c, outperforming the strongest denoiser-based baseline DPIR (29.53 / 0.110) by 1.16 dB. On the harder kernel (std=2.0), DRP reaches 27.89 / 0.088 on Set3c versus 27.52 / 0.159 for DPIR. For 2× single-image super-resolution, DRP achieves 29.26 / 0.079 on Set3c, surpassing DPIR (28.18 / 0.137) by over 1 dB. DRP consistently achieves the best LPIPS across all evaluated datasets and settings, indicating superior perceptual quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRP outperforms all four main denoiser-based baselines (SD-RED, PnP-ADMM, IRCNN+, DPIR) on PSNR in 7 out of 8 deblurring settings, with the sole exception being CBSD68 at std=2.0 where DPIR edges DRP by 0.06 dB (27.52 vs 27.46); however, DRP still wins decisively on LPIPS in that case (0.296 vs 0.376). The largest PSNR gains over the strongest baseline DPIR are on Set3c: +1.16 dB (std=1.6) and +0.37 dB (std=2.0). For 2× and 3× SISR (Table 2), DRP achieves the best PSNR and LPIPS across all 16 dataset/kernel/factor combinations, with gains of 0.5–1.1 dB over DPIR. In the supplement, DRP also beats the theoretically convergent GS-PnP method on PSNR in most deblurring and SISR settings, and substantially outperforms diffusion posterior sampling (DPS) on human-face deblurring (34.61 vs 29.61 dB on one kernel). An ablation replacing the DRUNet denoiser in DPIR with a SwinIR denoiser (DPIR-SwinIR) narrows the gap but DRP still wins, confirming that the advantage comes from using a restoration (SR) prior rather than merely a stronger denoiser architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the plug-and-play (PnP) and regularization-by-denoising (RED) frameworks, which have been built around AWGN denoisers as implicit priors, to the more general setting of arbitrary restoration operators (e.g., super-resolution networks). The theoretical contribution generalizes Tweedie's formula from the denoiser case (H=I) to restoration operators with a non-identity degradation H, and extends convergence guarantees that previously required the prior to be a denoiser. Practically, the method departs from the CNN-denoiser paradigm by using a Swin Transformer-based super-resolution model (SwinIR) as the prior, and introduces a 'prior refinement' strategy that gradually reduces the SR factor—analogous to the well-known σ-schedule in denoiser-based PnP methods like DPIR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that any pre-trained restoration model—regardless of the specific degradation it was trained on—can be repurposed as a principled prior for solving different inverse problems, broadening the pool of usable priors well beyond denoisers. The theoretical framework (generalized Tweedie + convergence under non-identity H) provides the missing justification that prior heuristic extensions (e.g., DPSR, RARE) lacked. Open questions include: what is the optimal degradation operator H for a given measurement A, whether DRP extends to blind inverse problems, and whether the MMSE assumption can be relaxed for priors trained with perceptual or adversarial losses. A limitation is that performance is inherently bounded by the quality of the pre-trained restoration model, and the iterative optimization (with CG inner loops) adds computational cost compared to single-pass methods.
