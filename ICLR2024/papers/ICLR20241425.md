## Problem

Interactive imitation learning methods like DAgger and HG-DAgger require the intervening expert to be near-optimal, yet real human demonstrators are rarely optimal and teleoperation often lacks the precision of a tuned controller. This paper asks whether the binary signal of *when* an expert chooses to intervene can serve as a sufficient reward for reinforcement learning, enabling the learned policy to surpass a suboptimal expert without access to a ground-truth task reward.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241425/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes state observations (proprioceptive vectors in simulation, or RGB images from two end-effector-mounted Intel Realsense D405 cameras in the real-robot setting) along with a binary intervention signal from a human operator (via a 3D mouse in the real-robot setup). The only reward signal is derived from this intervention: the transition immediately preceding an intervention receives a reward of −1, and all other transitions receive 0. The output is a continuous control policy that maps observations to actions at 10 Hz for the robot or at the environment's native frequency in simulation.

## Method

RLIF replaces the supervised retraining step of DAgger with off-policy reinforcement learning (instantiated with RLPD, a modified SAC variant) where the reward is defined solely by the expert's intervention decision: −1 for the transition before an intervention, 0 otherwise. The key insight is that detecting a mistake is easier than optimally correcting it, so the policy learns to *avoid* the states that trigger interventions rather than imitating the potentially suboptimal corrective actions. Theoretically, the paper proves RLIF's suboptimality gap is bounded by min{V*−V^ref, V*−V^exp} + δε/(1−γ), which is strictly tighter than DAgger's bound of V*−V^exp + δε/(1−γ) because RLIF can leverage a separate reference value function Q^πref for intervention timing that may be much better than the expert's own policy. Empirically, RLIF with value-based interventions outperforms DAgger and HG-DAgger by 2–5× on Adroit and Gym locomotion tasks, with the gap widening as expert suboptimality increases, and achieves 100% success on a real-robot peg insertion task within six rounds of human interaction.

## Results

RLIF with value-based intervention achieves average normalized scores of 77.79 (Adroit Pen), 103.83 (Walker2d), and 108.53 (Hopper), outperforming the best DAgger variants by 2–3× on average across all expert levels. The advantage grows sharply as expert suboptimality increases: on Hopper with a ~15% expert, RLIF scores 108.01 versus DAgger's 19.54 (≈5.5×). On the real-world peg insertion task, RLIF reaches 100% success within 6 interaction rounds (~20 min wall-clock), and 95% on cloth unfolding in 7 rounds. The value-based intervention rate decreases as the agent improves, confirming the intervention signal carries meaningful task information.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLIF with value-based intervention outperforms both DAgger (Ross et al., 2011) and HG-DAgger (Kelly et al., 2018) across all three simulation tasks and all expert levels. Against DAgger, the gap is most dramatic with highly suboptimal experts: on Hopper (~15% expert), RLIF scores 108.01 vs. DAgger's 19.54 (5.5×), and on Walker2d (~20% expert), RLIF scores 102.85 vs. DAgger's 12.37 (8.3×). Against HG-DAgger, RLIF similarly dominates, e.g., 102.85 vs. 11.94 on Walker2d (~20%) and 108.01 vs. 25.20 on Hopper (~15%). The key differentiator is that DAgger/HG-DAgger performance is bottlenecked by expert suboptimality (they can at best match the expert), whereas RLIF uses the intervention *timing* as a reward signal and can surpass the expert. RLIF with random intervention (no informative signal) degrades substantially, confirming that the value-based intervention strategy is critical to the method's advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DAgger/HG-DAgger interactive imitation learning framework by replacing the supervised learning (behavioral cloning) training step with off-policy reinforcement learning, where the reward signal is derived from the expert's decision of *when* to intervene rather than from the corrective action itself. This departs from prior imitation learning with RL work (Vecerik et al., 2017; Sun et al., 2017) that requires access to a ground-truth task reward, and from DAgger variants that assume near-optimal expert interventions. The method is instantiated using RLPD (Ball et al., 2023) as the underlying off-policy RL algorithm, and the theoretical analysis generalizes the DAgger suboptimality gap framework (Ross et al., 2011) to the setting where the reference value function and the expert policy are decoupled.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that robotics and control practitioners no longer need a highly skilled operator to provide optimal corrections—any human who can recognize when the robot is doing something wrong is sufficient, since RL can learn to avoid those situations rather than merely imitate the (potentially suboptimal) correction. The theoretical guarantee that RLIF is at least as good as DAgger, combined with the empirical 2–5× improvement as expert suboptimality grows, provides a clear recipe for when to prefer this approach. Open questions include the safety implications of online exploration under human oversight, the sensitivity to the intervention strategy (the ablation on Q^π_ref quality shows performance degrades significantly with a poor reference), and whether the method extends to settings where the human cannot reliably detect suboptimality.
