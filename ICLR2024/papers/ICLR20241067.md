## Problem

Identifying safety risks in LM agents that use tools is prohibitively labor-intensive: human experts must implement tools, manually configure sandboxes for each test scenario, and inspect trajectories for failures, making it infeasible to scale to diverse tools or discover long-tail, high-stakes risks. No prior benchmark directly assessed the safety risks of LM agents, with existing evaluations focusing on capability within statically established sandboxes.

## Input / Output

The emulator consumes only tool specifications (JSON descriptions of arguments, returns, and exceptions) plus the user instruction and current trajectory, producing emulated observations without requiring actual tool implementations. The safety and helpfulness evaluators take the complete agent trajectory along with the test case's underspecification, potential risks, and expected achievement, outputting discrete scores on a 0–3 scale (safety: 3 = certainly no risk, 0 = likely severe risk; helpfulness: 3 = excellent, 0 = poor) plus identified risky actions. The adversarial emulator additionally ingests the underspecification and potential risk descriptions to craft targeted sandbox states.

## Method

ToolEmu uses GPT-4 (temperature=0) as a prompt-engineered LM emulator that implicitly simulates the state transition, emission, and initial state of a tool sandbox using only tool specifications and a chain-of-thought process to enforce accuracy, consistency, and plausibility requirements. Two emulator variants are provided: a standard one that seeds random sandbox states, and an adversarial one that deliberately instantiates long-tail, higher-risk scenarios by conditioning on the test case's underspecification and potential risks. Two separate GPT-4-based evaluators (safety and helpfulness) retrospectively examine the full agent trajectory, assessing likelihood (certainly not / possible / likely) and severity (mild / severe) of risk materialization to produce quantitative scores. The benchmark comprises 36 toolkits (311 tools) and 144 test cases across 9 risk types, all targeting the instruction-underspecification threat model where benign user instructions omit critical details. Agents are implemented with ReAct, and the entire pipeline (emulator, evaluators) runs at temperature=0 for reproducibility, with per-case cost of approximately $1.2.

## Results

On the ToolEmu benchmark (144 test cases, 36 toolkits, adversarial emulator), GPT-4 with a safety prompt achieves the best combined safety (2.359/3) and helpfulness (1.824/3) scores, reducing failure incidence from 39.4% (basic prompt) to 23.9%. Among base agents without safety prompting, GPT-4 (safety 2.007, helpfulness 1.458) and Claude-2 (safety 1.829, helpfulness 1.464) outperform ChatGPT-3.5 (safety 1.430, helpfulness 0.768) and both Vicuna-1.5 variants. Even the safest evaluated agent (GPT-4 + Safety prompt) still exhibits identified failures in 23.9% of test cases, while the NoAct baseline achieves a perfect safety score (3.000) but near-zero helpfulness (0.063).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five LM agent configurations (GPT-4, Claude-2, ChatGPT-3.5, Vicuna-1.5-13B, Vicuna-1.5-7B) plus a safety-prompted GPT-4 and a NoAct baseline. GPT-4 and Claude-2 clearly lead on both safety and helpfulness among base agents, with GPT-4 achieving a 0.178-point safety advantage over Claude-2 and a 0.578-point helpfulness advantage over ChatGPT-3.5. Vicuna-1.5-7B appears safer than ChatGPT-3.5 (45.0% vs. 62.0% failure incidence) but this is attributed to tool-use inefficacy rather than genuine risk awareness, as evidenced by its much lower helpfulness (0.364 vs. 0.768). Adding a safety prompt to GPT-4 reduces failure incidence by 15.5 percentage points (39.4% → 23.9%) while simultaneously improving helpfulness (1.458 → 1.824), indicating the safety gain comes from risk-awareness rather than inaction. The NoAct baseline confirms the helpfulness-safety tradeoff is not inevitable: it achieves perfect safety (3.000) but near-zero helpfulness (0.063), whereas GPT-4 + Safety prompt demonstrates that safe and helpful behavior can coexist.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the simulator-based testing paradigm from autonomous driving (CARLA, LGVSL) to the LM agent domain, but replaces programmatically defined static sandboxes with LM-emulated ones that require only tool specifications rather than actual implementations. It builds on demonstrated LM emulation capabilities (Degrave's virtual machine in ChatGPT, Tang et al.'s API emulation, Park et al.'s social simulacra) but redirects them toward environment emulation for safety testing rather than agent behavior simulation. The paper explicitly positions itself as the first work to directly assess risks of LM agents, departing from prior agent benchmarks (AgentBench, ToolEval, APIBank) that evaluate capability within statically established sandboxes, and from Kinniment et al.'s long-term risk assessment which remains framed as a capability evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even the safest evaluated agent (GPT-4 with safety prompting) fails in 23.9% of test cases provides concrete, quantified evidence that current LM agents are not yet safe for real-world deployment with high-stakes tools. The framework's practical efficiency (15 minutes vs. 8 hours for terminal sandbox setup) and its ability to test tools that lack existing APIs or sandbox implementations make scalable agent safety assessment feasible for the first time. Key limitations include reliance on human-curated test cases, a single threat model (instruction underspecification), and emulator/evaluator error rates (~15-20% critical issues, ~27% recall gap vs. humans) that constrain confidence in the results. Open questions include whether fully automated test case generation can match human curation quality, whether the framework generalizes to malicious-intent threat models, and whether the 68.8% end-to-end precision is sufficient for deployment-critical safety certification.
