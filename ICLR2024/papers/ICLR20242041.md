## Problem

Current video diffusion models produce only short 'shot-level' clips depicting a single scene, falling short of the 'story-level' long videos needed for cinematic production. Traditional transition techniques (dissolves, fades, wipes) are rigid and algorithmic, lacking the creative, content-aware transitions used in filmmaking. This paper introduces 'generative transition' as a new task: producing diverse, text-controlled intermediate video segments that smoothly connect two visually distinct scenes.

## Input / Output

For transition, the model takes two scene images (first and last frames) plus a text prompt describing the desired transition style; for prediction, it takes the last few frames of a video plus text; for animation, it takes a single reference image plus text. Training uses 320×512 resolution with 16 frames, but inference supports arbitrary aspect ratios. The key input mechanism is a binary random mask over the frame axis (p=0.15, so roughly 2–3 of n frames are visible), which the model uses to identify which frames are given conditions versus which must be generated.

## Method

SEINE builds on the pre-trained LaVie-base T2V diffusion model (itself initialized from Stable Diffusion with temporal attention) and adds a randomly initialized random-mask conditioning layer. A binary mask m ∈ {0,1}^n is sampled per training sample and applied to the VAE-encoded latent z₀ to produce a masked latent z̃₀ = z₀ ⊙ m; the U-Net input is the concatenation [z_t; m; z̃₀] along the channel dimension, giving the network explicit knowledge of which frames are visible conditions. The model is trained with standard ε-prediction diffusion loss to denoise the full latent given both the text embedding and the masked visible frames, learning p_θ(z₀ | c, z̃₀). At inference, different mask configurations yield different tasks: setting only the first and last frame visible produces a transition video; setting the last k frames visible enables autoregressive long-video prediction; setting only the first frame visible produces image-to-video animation. The random-mask design (p=0.15, targeting 2–3 visible frames out of n) is specifically chosen to match the typical number of anchor frames needed for transitions and short predictions.

## Results

On the generative transition task evaluated on MSR-VTT (2,990 videos), SEINE achieves the best scores across all five metrics: CLIPSIM-t 0.2726, CLIPSIM-s 0.7740, CLIPSIM-f 0.9675, FVD 181.6, and FID 13.4. The FVD improvement is particularly dramatic—181.6 versus 445.6 (VQGAN) and 573.9 (Morphing)—indicating substantially better temporal coherence. For auto-regressive long video prediction on UCF-101 (1024 frames), SEINE shows slower quality degradation than TATS, and after fine-tuning on UCF-101, consistently outperforms TATS in FVD throughout the sequence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEINE outperforms all three transition baselines on every metric in Table 1. Against Morphing, SEINE improves FVD by 3.2× (181.6 vs. 573.9) and FID by 2.6× (13.4 vs. 35.3), while also achieving higher CLIPSIM-t (0.2726 vs. 0.2535) and CLIPSIM-f (0.9675 vs. 0.9569). Against the VQGAN-based interpolation baseline, SEINE wins on all five metrics, with the largest gap in FVD (181.6 vs. 445.6). Against the SD-based DDIM-inversion baseline, SEINE achieves substantially better FVD (181.6 vs. 502.0) and FID (13.4 vs. 62.0), as well as higher CLIPSIM-s (0.7740 vs. 0.6080), indicating better preservation of scene semantics. For long video prediction on UCF-101, SEINE (zero-shot) initially scores higher FVD than TATS due to distribution shift, but TATS degrades faster over 1024 frames; after fine-tuning on UCF-101, SEINE consistently outperforms TATS. For image-to-video animation, SEINE achieves results comparable to the commercial Gen-2 API without any task-specific training, as shown in qualitative comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEINE builds directly on LaVie, a cascaded latent diffusion model for text-to-video generation, by adding a random-mask conditioning layer that allows the model to condition on arbitrary subsets of frames. Conceptually, it departs from prior long-video generation work (TATS, Phenaki, Gen-L-Video) that treats long videos as a single continuous sequence, instead framing them as compositions of shot-level clips connected by generative transitions. The random-mask mechanism unifies three tasks—transition generation, autoregressive prediction, and image-to-video animation—under a single training objective, whereas prior approaches (morphing, VQGAN latent interpolation, SD DDIM inversion) handle only pairwise image interpolation without text control or temporal coherence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper formalizes 'generative transition' as a distinct task with three evaluation criteria (temporal coherence, semantic similarity, video-text alignment), providing a framework that the field lacked for assessing scene-to-scene video generation. The random-mask design is a lightweight, versatile mechanism that could be adopted by other video diffusion models for multi-task conditioning. However, limitations remain: transitions between highly dissimilar scenes degrade, watermark artifacts persist from training data, and text-video alignment is sensitive to prompt quality. Open questions include whether the approach scales to truly cinematic-length multi-scene narratives and how to handle arbitrary scene dissimilarity without auxiliary image-editing steps.
