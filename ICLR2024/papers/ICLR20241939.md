## Problem

Zero-shot anomaly detection (ZSAD) requires detecting anomalies in target domains with no training samples, but CLIP-based VLMs are pre-trained to align with object semantics rather than abnormality/normality, leading to weak generalization across diverse domains. Existing prompting approaches—whether hand-crafted (WinCLIP) or learnable (CoOp)—optimize for global object-level features and fail to capture the fine-grained, local anomaly patterns that are often object-agnostic (e.g., scratches look similar on different products, tumors look similar on different organs).

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image resized to 518×518 as input and produces two outputs: an image-level anomaly score (the cosine-similarity probability P(g_a, f_i) between the abnormality text embedding and the global visual embedding) and a pixel-level anomaly score map of size H×W. The score map is computed by merging patch-level segmentation probabilities from four intermediate visual encoder layers (6th, 12th, 18th, 24th), upsampling to full resolution, and applying a Gaussian filter (σ=4) for smoothing. All CLIP parameters (ViT-L/14@336px) remain frozen; only the two text prompts, a few learnable text tokens, and the attention mechanism are modified.

## Method

AnomalyCLIP learns two object-agnostic text prompt templates—[V₁]…[V_E][object] for normality and [W₁]…[W_E][damaged][object] for abnormality—where the class name is replaced with the generic token 'object' to block object semantics and force the prompts to capture shared anomaly patterns. Training uses a glocal context optimization: a global cross-entropy loss aligns the text embeddings with global visual embeddings, while a local loss (focal + Dice) supervises patch-level segmentation from multiple intermediate visual encoder layers, jointly driving the prompts to encode both coarse and fine-grained abnormality. A Diagonally Prominent Attention Map (DPAM) replaces the standard Q-K attention in the frozen visual encoder with V-V self-attention, producing diagonally prominent attention maps that preserve local visual semantics critical for segmentation. Text prompt tuning inserts learnable token embeddings into the first 9 layers of the text encoder to refine the textual space. The model is fine-tuned on one auxiliary dataset (e.g., MVTec AD test set) and then applied zero-shot to all other target datasets without any further training or prompt modification.

## Results

On 7 industrial defect datasets, AnomalyCLIP achieves the best or near-best image-level AUROC across all datasets, reaching 97.5% on DAGM, 93.5% on DTD-Synthetic, 91.5% on MVTec AD, 88.3% on BTAD, 84.7% on SDD, 82.1% on VisA, and 77.0% on MPDD. For pixel-level segmentation (AUPRO), AnomalyCLIP leads on 6 of 7 datasets, with standout scores of 92.3% on DTD-Synthetic, 91.0% on DAGM, 88.7% on MPDD, and 87.0% on VisA. On 10 medical imaging datasets, AnomalyCLIP (fine-tuned only on MVTec AD) achieves 94.6% AUROC on Br35H, 93.4% on HeadCT, 90.3% on BrainMRI, and 89.7% AUPRO on ISIC, consistently outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AnomalyCLIP substantially outperforms CLIP and CLIP-AC across all 7 industrial datasets, with gaps of 17–54 AUROC points at the image level (e.g., 91.5 vs. 74.1 on MVTec AD) and 53–64 points at the pixel level (e.g., 91.1 vs. 38.4 on MVTec AD), confirming that CLIP's object-semantic alignment is poorly suited for anomaly detection. Against WinCLIP, AnomalyCLIP wins on 6 of 7 industrial datasets at the image level (e.g., 88.3 vs. 68.2 on BTAD, 82.1 vs. 78.1 on VisA) and on all 7 at the pixel level (e.g., 91.1 vs. 85.1 on MVTec AD, 95.5 vs. 79.6 on VisA), with WinCLIP's only edge being a marginal 0.3 AUROC on MVTec AD image-level. Compared to VAND, AnomalyCLIP leads on 6 of 7 industrial image-level datasets (e.g., 97.5 vs. 94.4 on DAGM, 88.3 vs. 73.6 on BTAD) and on 5 of 7 pixel-level datasets, with VAND slightly ahead on VisA pixel-level (94.2 vs. 95.5 AUPRO is actually AnomalyCLIP winning; VAND leads on VisA image-level AP at 81.4 vs. 85.4 — actually AnomalyCLIP wins there too). CoOp is the weakest baseline, trailing AnomalyCLIP by 2–29 AUROC points at the image level and 57–81 points at the pixel level, as its learnable prompts capture only global semantics without local anomaly awareness. On the 10 medical datasets (Table 2), AnomalyCLIP outperforms all five baselines on every dataset, with the largest margins over CoOp (e.g., 94.6 vs. 86.0 on Br35H) and consistent 3–15 point advantages over VAND and WinCLIP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's frozen vision-language architecture and the prompt-learning paradigm introduced by CoOp, but departs fundamentally from prior ZSAD methods (WinCLIP, VAND) in two ways: (1) it replaces object-specific class names in text prompts with the generic token 'object,' making prompts object-agnostic so they capture anomaly patterns rather than object semantics, and (2) it introduces DPAM (Diagonally Prominent Attention Map), which substitutes Q-K attention with V-V self-attention in the frozen visual encoder to preserve local visual semantics for segmentation. The glocal context optimization—jointly training with global cross-entropy and local focal/Dice losses across multiple intermediate layers—further distinguishes it from CoOp's global-only prompt tuning and WinCLIP's hand-crafted prompt ensembles.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AnomalyCLIP demonstrates that a single pair of object-agnostic prompts, fine-tuned on one industrial dataset, can generalize to 17 diverse datasets spanning industrial defect inspection and medical imaging (skin, colon, brain, chest), eliminating the need for domain-specific prompt engineering or target-domain data. This has practical impact in settings where training data is inaccessible due to privacy or novelty. However, the method still trails full-shot approaches (PatchCore, RD4AD) by 5–10 AUROC on most datasets, struggles with visually ambiguous normal/anomalous boundaries and background interference, and its cross-domain generalization degrades when the auxiliary and target domains share little visual similarity (e.g., colon polyps → skin cancer). Open questions include whether the object-agnostic principle extends to 3D medical volumes, video anomaly detection, or open-world settings with truly novel anomaly types unseen in any auxiliary data.
