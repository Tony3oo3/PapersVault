## Problem

The Sliced Wasserstein (SW) distance requires computing an expectation over the unit hypersphere, which is intractable analytically and is typically approximated via Monte Carlo integration with a suboptimal O(L^{-1/2}) error rate. Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods achieve superior O(L^{-1} log(L)^d) rates, but existing QMC theory and constructions are developed for the unit hypercube, not the unit hypersphere that SW integration requires. This paper addresses the gap of how to construct effective low-discrepancy point sets on S^{d-1} and how to adapt them for stochastic optimization, which demands unbiased gradient estimators.

## Input / Output

The method takes two probability measures—typically empirical measures over 3D point clouds (2048 points each from ShapeNet Core-55) or color palettes extracted from RGB images as n×3 matrices—and produces a SW distance estimate, an interpolated point cloud along a gradient flow curve, a style-transferred image, or a reconstructed point cloud from a trained autoencoder. A key hyperparameter is the number of projecting directions L (tested at 10 and 100); QMC point sets are precomputed once and reused across evaluations, while RQSW variants regenerate randomized directions per call. For the autoencoder task, the encoder/decoder are Point-Net architectures with 256-dim embeddings trained for 400 epochs via SGD.

## Method

The paper proposes five constructions of QMC point sets on S²: (i) Gaussian-based mapping (normalize the inverse Gaussian CDF of Sobol points), (ii) Lambert equal-area mapping from [0,1]², (iii) generalized spiral points, (iv) maximizing pairwise Euclidean distance, and (v) minimizing Coulomb energy, with quality assessed via spherical cap discrepancy. Quasi-Sliced Wasserstein (QSW) replaces random MC directions with these deterministic low-discrepancy sets, yielding a deterministic approximation that converges asymptotically to the true SW distance. For stochastic optimization, Randomized QSW (RQSW) injects randomness via two mechanisms: push-forward (scramble Sobol points in the cube, then map to the sphere) or random rotation (sample a uniform matrix from the Stiefel manifold and apply it to a fixed QMC set), producing unbiased estimators of the SW distance and its gradient. The paper proves asymptotic convergence of QSW and unbiasedness of RQSW, and demonstrates that RQSW variants—particularly RCQSW (random rotation of Coulomb-energy-minimizing points)—outperform both standard MC and deterministic QSW across point-cloud interpolation, image style transfer, and deep autoencoder training.

## Results

In point-cloud interpolation (L=100, 500 Euler steps), RQSW variants achieve Wasserstein-2 distances of 0.002–0.006 at step 500, outperforming the standard MC-based SW (0.004±0.001) and significantly outperforming deterministic QSW variants which plateau at 0.065–0.07. In the deep point-cloud autoencoder (L=100, 400 epochs), CQSW achieves the best reconstruction W2 of 9.06±0.02, slightly ahead of SW (9.21±0.06) and RCQSW (9.12±0.03). In image style transfer (L=100), RQSW variants reduce the final Wasserstein-2 distance to the target palette to ~1.45 (best: RSQSW), compared to 458.29 for SW and up to 158.6 for the worst QSW variant (GQSW).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard Monte Carlo (MC) estimation of the SW distance (labeled 'SW' in all tables), which draws i.i.d. random directions from the uniform distribution on the unit sphere. In point-cloud interpolation, RQSW variants (especially RSQSW, RCQSW, RDQSW) consistently outperform SW at later time steps (e.g., 0.002 vs. 0.004 at step 500), while deterministic QSW variants fail to converge and plateau ~15× higher (0.065–0.07). In the autoencoder task, CQSW marginally beats SW (W2: 9.06 vs. 9.21 at epoch 400), and RCQSW is competitive (9.12 vs. 9.21), while GQSW catastrophically fails (W2: 37.99) due to numerical issues. In image style transfer, RQSW variants dramatically outperform SW (W2 ≈ 1.45 vs. 458.29), and QSW variants also improve over SW (best QSW: ~158.6 vs. 458.29). Overall, RQSW with random rotation of Coulomb-energy-minimizing points (RCQSW) is recommended as the most robust variant across all tasks and projection counts (L=10 and L=100).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two well-developed but previously disconnected literatures: Quasi-Monte Carlo integration (Sobol, 1967; Owen, 2013) and the Sliced Wasserstein distance (Bonneel et al., 2015). While QMC methods have been extensively studied for integration over the unit hypercube, and the SW distance has been approximated via standard Monte Carlo sampling over the unit hypersphere, no prior work systematically constructed and evaluated low-discrepancy point sets on the sphere for SW computation. The paper departs from Lin et al. (2020), which used a heuristic scaled mapping of the Halton sequence to the sphere without theoretical guarantees, and from Paulin et al. (2020), which used the SW distance as an objective to construct QMC point sets on the cube/ball but still relied on MC for the SW evaluation itself. The introduction of randomized QMC point sets on the sphere (via scrambling or random rotation on the Stiefel manifold) is, to the authors' knowledge, entirely new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SW distance is a workhorse metric in 3D applications (point-cloud registration, generative modeling, style transfer), and reducing its approximation error with fewer projections has direct practical value. The key open question is whether the QMC advantage persists in higher dimensions (d > 3), where low-discrepancy sequences are known to degrade; the authors explicitly defer this to future work. Additionally, the paper is restricted to the uniform slicing distribution and the standard SW formulation, leaving open whether QMC benefits extend to non-uniform slicing distributions (Nguyen et al., 2021) or other SW variants. The finding that deterministic QSW variants fail in iterative optimization while their randomized counterparts succeed is a useful practical guideline, though it aligns with general expectations about stochastic vs. deterministic gradient estimation.
