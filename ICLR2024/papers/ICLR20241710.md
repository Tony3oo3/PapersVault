## Problem

Prior work on shortcut learning has focused on predictivity—how reliably a feature indicates labels—but cannot explain why models systematically prefer one feature over another when predictivity is matched or even reversed. This paper introduces 'availability' (how easily a feature can be extracted from inputs) as a distinct factor and asks how predictivity and availability jointly determine which features deep nonlinear models rely on.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241710/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The generative framework produces binary classification datasets where two latent features (shortcut z_s and core z_c) are drawn from a class-conditioned Gaussian mixture and embedded into d-dimensional vectors or 224×224 RGB images (colored circle/square objects). Availability is manipulated independently via amplification (scaling α controlling redundancy and magnitude) and nesting (depth η of cascaded nonlinear transforms), while predictivity is set via Gaussian mean separation μ. The key output is a shortcut bias score in [-1, +1] measuring how much a trained model's reliance on the shortcut feature exceeds that of the Bayes-optimal LDA classifier, computed by probing the model over a grid of latent-space points.

## Method

The paper constructs a minimal generative framework that independently controls predictivity and availability of two latent features, enabling systematic parametric studies of their interaction. Empirically, they train MLPs (varying depth, width, and activation: linear, ReLU, Tanh) and ResNet-18 on synthetic vector and image datasets, quantifying shortcut bias as the difference between model and LDA reliance on the shortcut feature. Theoretically, they analyze the Neural Tangent Kernel spectrum for linear and ReLU networks, proving that linear kernels admit a single nonzero eigenvalue (unbiased to availability) while ReLU kernels admit two eigenvalues whose sensitivity to feature scaling creates an inevitable bias toward the more-available feature, with the sign of the bias determined by the product of availability difference and predictivity-weighted availability difference. In naturalistic settings (Waterbirds, CelebA), they demonstrate that background and facial-expression features are more available than core features, and that manipulating spatial extent, color, and spatial frequency shifts model reliance in ways consistent with the availability account.

## Results

In the synthetic vector experiments, a single-hidden-layer ReLU MLP achieves a shortcut bias of 0.53 when the shortcut feature is more available (αs/αc = 64) but less predictive (ρs = 0.85 vs. ρc = 0.9), while a linear model shows near-zero bias under identical conditions. Increasing model depth from 1 to 8 hidden layers monotonically amplifies shortcut bias, and replacing ReLU with a linear activation eliminates the bias entirely. On the Waterbirds naturalistic dataset, ResNet-18 models show a dramatically larger gap between congruent and incongruent probe accuracy as background predictivity increases, compared to a Bayes-optimal LDA classifier, confirming that models over-rely on the more-available background feature beyond what predictivity alone would predict.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against external baselines; instead, it compares model architectures and classifiers against each other to isolate the source of shortcut bias. Linear models (no hidden layer) serve as the unbiased reference, showing near-zero shortcut bias across all predictivity and availability settings, while ReLU and Tanh MLPs exhibit substantial bias (up to 0.53). The Bayes-optimal LDA classifier serves as the statistical ground truth for feature reliance; trained ResNet-18 models deviate from LDA in their reliance on the more-available feature, with the deviation growing as availability increases. In the pixel-footprint image experiments (Section 5), ResNet-18 is biased toward the larger-footprint object even when it is less predictive (αs/αc = 4), consistent with Wolff & Wolff (2022). The theoretical NTK analysis (Theorems 3–4) confirms that the bias is an inherent property of ReLU nonlinearities: the first non-zero sensitivity to availability appears at the 9th derivative for ReLU networks, while it is identically zero at all orders for linear networks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the shortcut learning literature, which has predominantly focused on predictivity (how well a feature indicates labels), by introducing 'availability' as a distinct, independently manipulable factor governing feature reliance. It builds directly on Hermann & Lampinen (2020), who showed models prefer linearly decodable features, and Pezeshki et al. (2021), whose 'strength' notion confounds predictivity and availability; here the authors formally disentangle the two. The theoretical analysis draws on the Neural Tangent Kernel framework (Jacot et al., 2018) to characterize kernel spectra for linear and ReLU networks, while the image experiments build on Wolff & Wolff (2022)'s pixel-footprint instantiation of feature availability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NTK-based proof that shortcut bias is an inevitable consequence of nonlinear (ReLU) architectures—absent in linear ones—provides a principled, architecture-level explanation for a phenomenon previously attributed to data statistics or training dynamics. The generative framework enables systematic, controlled study of predictivity-availability interactions that prior work could not isolate, and the naturalistic experiments (Waterbirds, CelebA) ground the theory in practical vision settings. Open questions include whether the theoretical results extend beyond 2-layer networks and small-covariance regimes, how to architecturally mitigate availability bias without sacrificing performance, and whether the framework generalizes to non-vision domains such as language.
