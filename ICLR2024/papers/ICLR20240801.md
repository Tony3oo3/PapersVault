## Problem

Existing knowledge editing methods for LLMs are evaluated on isolated single edits, leaving it unclear whether sequential or logically connected edits introduce harmful side effects. This paper identifies two previously unexamined pitfalls: Knowledge Conflict, where logically related edits (reverse or composite) create inconsistencies in the post-edit model, and Knowledge Distortion, where editing parameters to change one fact irreversibly warps the model's implicit probability distribution over other correct labels for the same (s, r) pair.

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes pairs of factual edits (s, r, o→o*) applied sequentially to GPT-2 XL (1.5B) or GPT-J (6B), organized into CONFLICTEDIT (reverse and composite edit pairs mined from WikiData via logical rules) and ROUNDEDIT (multi-label edits split into EASY and HARD based on semantic proximity of the intermediate object to true labels). Outputs include the edited model parameters plus six new metrics: Conflict Score (CS_exp/CS_imp), Conflict Magnitude (CM), Tied Fact Damage (TFD), JS-divergence-based Distortion (D), Ignore Rate (IR), and Failure Rate (FR), all computed by probing next-token generation probabilities over neighbor prompts generated by GPT-4.

## Method

The paper constructs two benchmarks from WikiData—CONFLICTEDIT (2,500 reverse-edit and 2,000–2,500 composite-edit pairs governed by logical rules such as NotableWork ∧ WrittenIn → Language) and ROUNDEDIT (2,500 EASY + 2,500 HARD multi-label edits)—and systematically evaluates four editing methods (FT, MEND, ROME, MEMIT) under these settings. Key findings show ROME and MEMIT completely fail on reverse edits (CS ≈ 0) because their subject-keyed MLP update mechanism cannot propagate changes through reverse relations, while all methods inflict significant tied-fact damage in composite edits. To address knowledge distortion, the authors propose Multi-Label Edit (MLE), which leverages MEMIT's multi-editing capability to simultaneously update multiple correct labels for the same (s, r) pair in a single editing pass, preventing the model from collapsing its probability distribution onto a single target object and thereby preserving the original multi-label knowledge structure.

## Results

On the ROUNDEDIT benchmark, MEMIT+MLE (the proposed Multi-Label Edit) dramatically reduces Knowledge Distortion: on GPT2-XL, it achieves an Ignore Rate (IR) of 4.66% (EASY) and 2.67% (HARD) versus 49.98% and 53.38% for vanilla MEMIT, and a Failure Rate (FR) of 1.72% and 1.12% versus 45.36% and 50.12%. On GPT-J, MEMIT+MLE achieves IR of 2.48% (EASY) and 3.84% (HARD) versus 56.91% and 72.03% for MEMIT. On the CONFLICTEDIT benchmark, ROME and MEMIT show a total failure (CS = 0.00) on the Reverse Edit setting, while FT and MEND retain higher Conflict Magnitude scores (15.20% and 15.32% on GPT2-XL), indicating they partially suppress old knowledge but still suffer from conflicts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four knowledge editing baselines—FT, MEND, ROME, and MEMIT—against the proposed MEMIT+MLE on both CONFLICTEDIT and ROUNDEDIT. On Knowledge Distortion (ROUNDEDIT), MEMIT+MLE overwhelmingly outperforms all baselines on IR and FR: it reduces IR from 49.98% (MEMIT) to 4.66% on GPT2-XL EASY, and from 72.03% (MEMIT) to 3.84% on GPT-J HARD, while maintaining competitive Succ scores. ROME and MEMIT show the lowest distortion among baselines but still affect over 50% of true labels (IR > 50%), whereas FT and MEND exhibit the worst distortion (IR > 74% on GPT2-XL). On Knowledge Conflict (CONFLICTEDIT), ROME and MEMIT completely fail on Reverse Edit (CS = 0.00), while FT and MEND achieve moderate CM scores (~15%) indicating partial suppression of old knowledge. No single baseline dominates across all conflict settings: FT and MEND handle Reverse Edit better, while ROME excels on Coverage Edit (CS = 99.76 on GPT2-XL), but all methods suffer significant Tied Fact Damage (TFD) in Composite Edit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the knowledge editing lineage of ROME (causal tracing + rank-one MLP update), MEMIT (multi-layer mass editing), and MEND (hypernetwork-based gradient manipulation), but deliberately shifts the evaluation paradigm from isolated single-edit metrics (Reliability, Generalization, Locality) to multi-edit side-effect analysis. It departs from prior work by introducing two previously unexamined failure modes—Knowledge Conflict (where logically connected edits produce inconsistent model states) and Knowledge Distortion (where editing irreversibly warps the probability distribution over other correct labels)—and by constructing the CONFLICTEDIT and ROUNDEDIT benchmarks to quantify them. The proposed Multi-Label Edit (MLE) method repurposes MEMIT's multi-editing capability to simultaneously preserve multiple correct labels, representing a minimal architectural addition rather than a new editing mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all four tested editing methods exhibit total failure (CS = 0.00) on reverse edits and that round-editing leaves measurable, irreversible distortion in the model's knowledge distribution challenges the implicit assumption in the literature that knowledge editing is a 'surgical' operation with bounded side effects. For practitioners deploying iterative or large-scale knowledge updates in production LLMs, this means that sequential edits can accumulate logical inconsistencies and degrade unrelated knowledge in ways that standard Reliability/Generalization/Locality metrics would not detect. Open questions include whether conflict detection via knowledge-graph logical rules can systematically prevent these issues, whether distortion accumulates super-linearly over hundreds of edits, and whether the findings generalize beyond the 1.5B–6B parameter scale tested here.
