## Problem

Existing multimodal prompt learning methods (CoOp, Co-CoOp, MaPLe) rely on explicit interaction structures or linear mappings to fuse image and text features, which produce homogeneous representations that fail to capture the diverse and complementary aspects inherent in multimodal data. This limitation is particularly acute in zero-shot settings requiring fine-grained classification or abstract interpretation, where the inability to disentangle complex intermodal feature distributions leads to degraded generalization on novel classes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images processed through CLIP's ViT-B/16 vision encoder (yielding final-layer visual features) and text class names encoded as word embeddings with learnable context tokens. It produces a class label via cosine similarity between the quaternion-fused visual prompt and language prompt, with a learned temperature parameter. The entire pipeline operates in a zero-shot prompt-tuning regime (16-shot validation, 7 training epochs, batch size 1), requiring only 2.93M learnable parameters—fewer than MaPLe's 3.56M—while the quaternion encoder is inserted at the input stage of CLIP rather than requiring deep prompt layers throughout the transformer.

## Method

QNet fuses multimodal features by first adding CLIP's final visual features (after a Meta-Proj dimension transformation) to text context tokens, then projecting the combined vector into a quaternion hidden space via a quaternion encoder layer. Within this space, the three mutually orthogonal imaginary axes serve distinct roles: the i-axis and j-axis are partitioned to encode image and text information respectively, while the k-axis acts as a dynamic balancing axis that prevents one modality's distribution weight from overshadowing the other. Quaternion-valued weight matrices are initialized in polar form (w = |w|e^{q}), and the fused output directly constructs both visual and textual prompts concatenated into CLIP's transformer layers, eliminating the need for explicit cross-attention or linear mapping interaction structures. The approach can be extended across multiple hierarchical layers of CLIP to integrate features at different depths, and ablations confirm that the k-axis as a modulation axis (rather than a parameter axis) yields the best performance.

## Results

On the base-to-novel generalization benchmark across 11 datasets, QNet achieves an average harmonic mean (HM) of 79.30%, base accuracy of 83.32%, and novel accuracy of 75.65%, outperforming the strongest baseline MaPLe (HM 78.55%) by +0.75 in HM. QNet uses only 2.93M learnable parameters compared to MaPLe's 3.56M. In cross-dataset transfer (trained on ImageNet, tested on 10 other datasets), QNet attains 66.98% average accuracy vs. 66.30% for MaPLe. In domain generalization across four ImageNet variants, QNet reaches 60.65% average accuracy, surpassing MaPLe (60.27%) and Co-CoOp (59.91%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

QNet consistently outperforms all four baselines across the three evaluation settings. Against the strongest competitor MaPLe (which uses linear mapping for modality interaction), QNet gains +0.75 in average HM on base-to-novel generalization while using 18% fewer parameters (2.93M vs 3.56M). The gap widens on fine-grained datasets: QNet improves HM by +0.79 on FGVC-Aircraft and +0.87 on EuroSAT over MaPLe. Against Co-CoOp, QNet achieves a larger margin of +3.47 in average HM, indicating that quaternion-based fusion captures intermodal relationships that conditional prompt learning alone cannot. In cross-dataset transfer, QNet leads MaPLe by +0.68 in average accuracy (66.98% vs 66.30%), and in domain generalization across ImageNet variants, QNet edges out MaPLe by +0.38 (60.65% vs 60.27%). Notably, QNet's advantage is most pronounced on datasets requiring fine-grained or abstract interpretation (e.g., EuroSAT, FGVC-Aircraft, DTD), where the orthogonal quaternion axes effectively disentangle complementary modality features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the prompt learning lineage for CLIP (CoOp → Co-CoOp → MaPLe) by replacing explicit modality-interaction structures (linear mapping in MaPLe, cross-attention in other methods) with quaternion algebra. The key conceptual departure is using the three mutually orthogonal imaginary axes of quaternions to disentangle image and text feature distributions in a shared latent space, rather than designing dedicated fusion architectures. The quaternion network architecture itself is borrowed from Parcollet et al. (2018) for recurrent networks, but its application to multimodal prompt learning in a vision-language setting is new. The method also builds on the observation from Co-CoOp that visual features from the final vision-encoder layer carry strong generalization signal, combining them with text context tokens before quaternion encoding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QNet demonstrates that a compact quaternion-based fusion layer (2.93M parameters vs. 3.56M for MaPLe) can match or exceed stronger baselines across 11 datasets in base-to-novel, cross-dataset, and domain-transfer settings, suggesting that the homogeneous-representation problem in multimodal fusion may be addressable through algebraic structure rather than architectural complexity. However, the absolute gains over MaPLe are modest (≈0.75% HM on average), and the paper acknowledges that CLIP's ViT backbone inherently misses local fine-grained details, limiting applicability to detection and fine-grained tasks. An open question is whether the quaternion fusion principle generalizes beyond the CLIP/ViT-B/16 setting to other multimodal architectures or larger-scale models, and whether the claimed 'orthogonal perspective' interpretation of the imaginary axes has a deeper theoretical grounding beyond empirical ablation.
