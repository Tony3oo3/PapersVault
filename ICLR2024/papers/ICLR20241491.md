## Problem

Rotation is widely used as a distribution-shifting augmentation to generate pseudo-outliers for one-class classification, yet its effectiveness remains unexplained. The paper reveals that rotation does not always alter semantics—some images are 'rotation-agnostic' (RAI)—and incorporating these mislabeled samples degrades OCC performance. No prior method could identify, in an unsupervised and instance-level manner, which transformations are already inherent in a given dataset.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241491/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, SVHN digits) along with their four rotated variants (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) as the universal candidate set. It outputs a per-rotation probability p(R_r(x)) indicating the likelihood that a rotated sample belongs to the original distribution, which is then used to partition training data into semantics-preserving and semantics-shifting subsets. The final OCC system produces a standard anomaly score (AUROC-evaluated) computed via the same scoring functions as CSI or UniCon, but with modified training objectives that treat the two subsets differently.

## Method

The framework operates in two stages. Stage 1 performs SimCLR-style contrastive pre-training with a ResNet-18 backbone, deliberately excluding rotation from the augmentation pipeline so that rotated versions retain distinct representations; the frozen feature extractor then produces a reference set of ℓ₂-normalized embeddings for all training images. Stage 2 introduces a learnable linear head h_γ on top of the frozen backbone and trains it via a Gumbel-softmax differentiable sampler that selects one image per four-rotation pool, guided by an MMD loss (with multiple Gaussian kernels) that matches the sampled set's distribution to the reference set. An xy-shuffle strategy reorganizes candidate pools to prevent the trivial solution of always selecting the 0° original. At inference, confidence scores across the four rotations are normalized into a probability distribution, and samples satisfying p(R_r(x)) ≥ p(x) are treated as semantics-preserving inliers while the rest are treated as semantics-shifting outliers, modifying the contrastive objectives of CSI and UniCon accordingly.

## Results

On one-class CIFAR-10, the proposed method applied to UniCon (UniCon+ours) achieves a mean AUROC of 95.4%, improving over UniCon's 94.8% and CSI+ours's 94.8%. On CIFAR-100 (20 super-classes), UniCon+ours reaches 92.6% AUROC versus UniCon's 92.0%, and CSI+ours reaches 90.5% versus CSI's 89.6%. On Cat-vs-Dog, UniCon+ours achieves 91.3% mean AUROC, a notable +1.9% gain over UniCon's 89.4%. On Tiny-ImageNet linear probing, the method improves top-1 accuracy from 37.17% (PNDA) to 39.76%, and on MvTec-AD it achieves 90.6% image AUROC and 95.2% pixel AUROC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method is applied as a plug-in improvement on top of two strong contrastive OCC baselines: CSI (Tack et al., 2020) and UniCon (Wang et al., 2023). On CIFAR-10, UniCon+ours (95.4% mean AUROC) outperforms UniCon (94.8%) by +0.6%, and CSI+ours (94.8%) beats CSI (94.3%) by +0.5%; the gains are largest on classes with high RAI ratios (e.g., Plane: +1.1 for CSI, +0.6 for UniCon; Cat: +1.0 for CSI, +1.2 for UniCon). On CIFAR-100, both variants improve (CSI: 89.6→90.5; UniCon: 92.0→92.6), while on Cat-vs-Dog, CSI+ours slightly drops (89.6→88.8) but UniCon+ours gains substantially (89.4→91.3). Against earlier rotation-based methods (Geom 86.0%, Rot 88.4%, GOAD 85.1%) and iDECODe (91.2%), the proposed approach achieves clear margins of 4–10 AUROC points. On Tiny-ImageNet, the method outperforms PNDA (Miyai et al., 2023) by +2.59% top-1 accuracy (39.76% vs 37.17%). On MvTec-AD, the method (90.6% image / 95.2% pixel AUROC) surpasses DROC (86.5/90.2) and UniCon-HA (89.8/94.3) but falls short of Cut-Paste (95.2/96.0), which the authors attribute to the need for tailored pretext tasks in industrial settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the contrastive-learning-based OCC line (CSI, UniCon, DROC) that uses rotation as a distribution-shifting augmentation to generate pseudo-outliers, and on the VTSS hypothesis (Pal et al., 2020) which warned that transformations already present in the dataset degrade self-supervised representations. It departs from prior work by identifying a concrete mechanism—rotation-agnostic images (RAI)—that explains *why* rotation sometimes fails as a shifting transformation, and by proposing a two-stage density-matching framework (contrastive pre-training + MMD-based sampling with Gumbel-softmax) to estimate the transformation distribution in an unsupervised, instance-level manner, rather than relying on dataset-level Bayesian search (Mohseni et al., 2022) or human-curated reference subsets (Yang et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that a non-trivial fraction of images in standard benchmarks are rotation-agnostic challenges the near-universal assumption in the OCC literature that rotation is a reliable distribution-shifting augmentation, and the r > 0.9 correlation between rotation-prediction accuracy and OCC performance provides a diagnostic tool for practitioners. However, the practical gains over strong baselines (CSI, UniCon) are modest (≈0.5–1.0 AUROC points), and the method is validated primarily on CIFAR-scale datasets with a single rotation transformation; whether the density-matching framework generalizes to more complex transformations, larger-scale datasets, or industrial anomaly detection (MvTec-AD results are mixed) remains an open question. The reliance on a fixed feature extractor from Stage I and the need for manual RAI annotation for validation also limit immediate scalability.
