## Problem

High-quality step-wise mathematical annotations are expensive to obtain: manual annotation (e.g., miniF2F) is labor-intensive and small-scale, rule-based synthesis (e.g., INT) produces formally valid but semantically meaningless proofs, and LLM-generated reasoning (e.g., ROSCOE) lacks guaranteed correctness. The paper addresses how to uniformly synthesize large-scale mathematical data that is simultaneously accurate at every intermediate step, diverse in topic, and meaningful to human readers.

## Input / Output

MUSTARD consumes 1–2 mathematical concept seeds sampled from a pool of 274 concepts organized by four educational levels (elementary through higher education) and a question type (theorem proving or word problem), and produces a triplet: an informal problem statement, a natural-language proof, and a formal Lean 3 proof. The formal proof must pass Lean Prover validation to be retained; failed proofs are either discarded or retained as 'challenging' samples after up to two rounds of LLM self-correction guided by prover error messages. The resulting MUSTARDSAUCE dataset contains 5,866 validated data points drawn from 28,316 total generations.

## Method

MUSTARD is a three-stage synthesis pipeline that interlocks a generative LLM (GPT-4) with a formal theorem prover (Lean 3). Stage 1 (concept seeding) samples 1 or 2 concepts from the Khan Academy-derived pool to control topic and difficulty. Stage 2 (proof generation) prompts GPT-4 in a single call to produce the problem, its informal proof, and a Lean 3 formalization simultaneously, or in three sequential sub-prompts (T1–T3) where the formalization step assigns the LLM the role of 'a master in Lean.' Stage 3 (proof filtering) submits the Lean code to the Lean Prover; if errors are returned, the error messages plus line-numbered code are fed back to GPT-4 for revision, repeating up to two additional rounds. The number of correction rounds serves as a difficulty proxy, and the prover acts as a hard quality gate that guarantees formal correctness of every retained proof, which in turn certifies the alignment and correctness of the corresponding informal proof (confirmed by human evaluation showing significant differences in proof correctness and informal–formal alignment between valid and invalid groups).

## Results

Fine-tuning Llama 2-7B on MUSTARDSAUCE-valid (5,866 Lean-validated data points) yields 8.7 pass@1 on Mathlib, 2.9 on miniF2F, and 12.2 on MUSTARDSAUCE-test, compared to 0.0 for the un-fine-tuned baseline. On math word problems, the same model achieves 10.3% Maj1@1 on GSM8K zero-shot (+8.42% relative) and 3.2% on MATH zero-shot (+6.67%). Averaged across ATP benchmarks, MUSTARDSAUCE-valid fine-tuning delivers a 15.41% relative gain for Llama 2-7B and 20.89% for GPT2-large over the random-subset baseline; the corresponding MWP gains are 8.18% and 15.41%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external theorem-proving or math-reasoning methods; instead, it ablates the effect of data quality by fine-tuning the same architectures (GPT2-large, Llama 2-7B, Llama 2-70B) on four MUSTARDSAUCE subsets of equal size (5,866 points): valid (Lean-verified), invalid, random, and the full unfiltered set (tt, 28,316 points). MUSTARDSAUCE-valid consistently outperforms all other subsets on every benchmark: on Mathlib, Llama 2-7B>valid reaches 8.7 vs. 7.5 (random), 5.8 (invalid), and 8.3 (tt); on miniF2F, 2.9 vs. 2.5, 1.2, and 2.6. The full unfiltered set (tt) underperforms the smaller valid subset, confirming that prover-validated quality matters more than raw data volume. The two-stage fine-tuning (MUSTARDSAUCE-valid followed by Mathlib training) further boosts Llama 2-7B to 15.7 on Mathlib and 7.8 on miniF2F, and the ordering (valid→mathlib) slightly outperforms the reverse (mathlib→valid) at 14.4 vs. 13.8 on MUSTARDSAUCE-test. Llama 2-70B fine-tuned on MUSTARDSAUCE-valid also consistently beats the random and invalid subsets by 8.33% (zero-shot GSM8K) and 5.30% (few-shot GSM8K), confirming the framework scales to larger models.

## Relation to Prior Work

MUSTARD unifies three previously separate threads: LLM-based auto-formalization (Wu et al., NeurIPS 2022), the generate-then-prover-validate loop for theorem proving (Polu & Sutskever, 2020), and LLM-driven data synthesis (Wang et al., ACL 2023, Self-Instruct). It departs from prior data synthesis efforts like INT (rule-based, semantically empty proofs) and miniF2F (manual annotation, tiny scale) by using GPT-4 to generate both informal and formal solutions simultaneously, then leveraging Lean Prover as an automatic quality gate with iterative error-feedback revision. The framework also extends chain-of-thought data annotation by replacing expensive human step-wise labeling with prover-validated formal steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MUSTARDSAUCE provides a practical, reproducible pipeline for generating large-scale, formally validated mathematical reasoning data, addressing the bottleneck of step-wise annotation that limits CoT training. The finding that a smaller validated subset (5,866 points) consistently outperforms a larger unvalidated set (28,316 points) underscores that prover-based filtering is more valuable than raw data volume. Open questions include whether the approach scales to more complex formal systems beyond Lean 3, how to handle the mild inconsistency between #reduce computations and theorem proofs noted by the authors, and whether community-driven data synthesis can further expand coverage. The framework's reliance on GPT-4 as the generator also raises questions about reproducibility and cost.
