## Problem

Existing adversarial policy attacks on RL agents assume the attacker has full control over the adversarial agent, producing conspicuous, easily detectable behaviors, and offer no provably efficient defenses. In practice, attackers may face resistance (partial control) and need stealth to avoid detection, yet no prior work provides both a realistic partial-control attack model and a defense with convergence guarantees to the most robust policy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a well-trained victim policy π̂_ν, a benign agent policy π̂_α, an attack budget ε_π ∈ [0,1], and a Markov game environment as input. The deployed attacker policy is a convex mixture (1−ε_π)π̂_α + ε_π π̃_α, meaning the attacker only overrides the benign policy with probability ε_π at each step. The outputs are a constrained adversarial policy π̃_α (stealthier, with bounded state-distribution shift) and a robust victim policy π_ν that is guaranteed to converge to the minimax solution arg max_{π_ν} min_{π_α} V_ρ(π_ν, (1−ε_π)π̂_α + ε_π π_α).

## Method

The paper introduces a generalized attack formulation where the attacker's control is bounded by an attack budget ε_π, which provably limits both the victim's value degradation (Proposition 3.1: |ΔV| ≤ 2ε_π/(1−γ)²) and the induced state-distribution shift (Proposition 3.2: ‖Δd‖₁ ≤ 2γε_π/(1−γ)), enabling stealthier attacks at a chosen winning rate. For defense, the paper proposes adversarial training with timescale separation: Algorithm 1 uses a min oracle where the attacker solves the best response exactly before the victim takes a single projected gradient step, while Algorithm 2 replaces the oracle with a faster gradient update for the attacker (η_α^t ≫ η_ν^t), making it compatible with standard RL algorithms like PPO. The key theoretical result (Theorem 5.3) proves polynomial convergence of the average exploitability to the optimal minimax value under a bounded mismatch coefficient, even in the non-convex non-concave setting—providing the first provably efficient defense against adversarial policies in multi-agent RL, in contrast to supervised learning where adversarial training offers only empirical guarantees.

## Results

On Kuhn Poker, adversarial training with a min oracle (Algorithm 1) achieves the fastest convergence to near-zero exploitability with the least variance, while the two-timescale algorithm (Algorithm 2) closely approximates this performance and substantially outperforms single-timescale training. On RoboSumo, the two-timescale method achieves rapid convergence with significantly smaller gradient norms compared to single-timescale training, which exhibits large oscillations. Under ε_π ∈ {1, 0.7, 0.3}, the two-timescale-trained victim policy achieves the lowest attacker winning rate (i.e., best robustness) compared to single-timescale adversarial training, self-play, and fictitious-play. For the attack formulation, the constrained attack (ε_π = 0.2) induces a much smaller Wasserstein-2 state distribution shift than the unconstrained attack (ε_π = 1) while maintaining the same winning rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its two-timescale adversarial training against four baselines: single-timescale adversarial training, self-play (Bansal et al., 2017), and fictitious-play (Heinrich & Silver, 2016). On RoboSumo (Figure 4c), the two-timescale method achieves the lowest attacker score (best robustness) across all attack budgets ε_π ∈ {1, 0.7, 0.3}, outperforming single-timescale adversarial training, self-play, and fictitious-play. On Kuhn Poker (Figure 2), the two-timescale algorithm closely tracks the min-oracle baseline (Algorithm 1) in exploitability, while single-timescale training converges more slowly with higher variance. The self-play and fictitious-play baselines, while known for training adversarially robust agents, are outperformed by the proposed two-timescale approach in terms of final exploitability on RoboSumo. The constrained attack formulation also outperforms the unconstrained baseline (Gleave et al., 2019) in stealthiness, producing significantly smaller state distribution shifts (measured by Wasserstein-2 distance) at the same attacking success rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial policy attack framework of Gleave et al. (2019), Wu et al. (2021b), and Guo et al. (2021) by introducing an 'attack budget' ε_π that captures partial attacker control, generalizing the single-agent action adversarial RL of Tessler et al. (2019) to multi-agent settings. On the defense side, it draws on timescale separation principles from GAN training (Heusel et al., 2017) and independent policy gradient analysis for competitive RL (Daskalakis et al., 2020; Jin et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021b), but departs from supervised-learning adversarial training (Madry et al., 2017; Shafahi et al., 2019) by providing provable global convergence to the most robust policy rather than empirical guarantees. The paper also contrasts with stealthy single-agent attacks (Russo & Proutiere, 2021; Franzmeyer et al., 2022) that require world models, showing that the attack budget alone suffices for stealth in multi-agent settings without such models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide provable convergence guarantees for adversarial training against adversarial policies in multi-agent RL, a gap that has left practitioners relying on empirical robustness. The attack budget formulation offers a practical, interpretable knob for balancing attack effectiveness against detectability, with clean theoretical bounds linking it to state distribution shift and value degradation. Key limitations include the theoretical analysis being restricted to direct (tabular) policy parameterization rather than function approximation, and empirical validation on relatively simple environments (Kuhn Poker, Robosumo) rather than high-stakes real-world multi-agent systems. Open questions include scaling to multiple independent self-interested attackers, extending guarantees to neural network policies, and whether the timescale separation principle transfers to more complex continuous-control domains.
