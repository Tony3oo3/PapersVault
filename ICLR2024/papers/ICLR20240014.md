## Problem

Backdoor attacks on semantic segmentation have been largely overlooked compared to classification, where the sample-agnostic goal of misclassifying an entire image does not translate to pixel-wise prediction. The specific challenge is that a trigger placed on non-victim pixels must indirectly manipulate the classification of victim-class pixels, and in real-world scenarios the relative position between trigger and victim objects is uncontrollable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240014/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (512×1024 for Cityscapes, standard resolution for VOC) and produces a pixel-wise segmentation mask of shape H×W×M. During inference, a small natural trigger pattern (e.g., a 55×55 pixel Hello Kitty printout) is placed on non-victim pixels anywhere in the image; the backdoored model should then misclassify only the victim-class pixels (e.g., car→road) while preserving correct predictions on all other pixels. The trigger must not overlap victim pixels and should be a physically printable pattern for real-world applicability.

## Method

The core insight is to exploit the context aggregation ability of segmentation models, which attend to surrounding pixels when classifying each one. Two poisoning strategies are proposed: Nearest Neighbor Injection (NNI) places the trigger at the nearest eligible position to victim pixels within a distance range [L,U], strengthening the spatial association the model learns between trigger and victim predictions; Pixel Random Labeling (PRL) additionally relabels a fixed number of randomly selected non-victim pixels to other classes present in the same image, forcing the model to rely on broader global context rather than local cues. PRL is the key contribution for practical attacks because it makes the attack success rate largely independent of trigger-victim distance, enabling the trigger to be placed anywhere in the scene. The approach is validated across PSPNet, DeepLabV3, and SegFormer on PASCAL VOC 2012 and Cityscapes, achieving >90% attack success rate with only 5% poisoning while maintaining clean accuracy.

## Results

On Cityscapes with DeepLabV3, the proposed PRL method achieves 92.46% ASR with only 5% poisoning rate, rising to 96.75% at 20%, while maintaining PBA of ~71% and CBA of ~73% (nearly identical to the clean model's 73.56% CBA). On PASCAL VOC 2012, PRL reaches 96.79% ASR at 5% poisoning and 98.12% at 10%. The NNI method provides a strong improvement over baseline IBA when the trigger is near the victim class (e.g., 82.45% vs 62.13% ASR at 5% on Cityscapes), but degrades sharply with distance, whereas PRL maintains >90% ASR regardless of trigger-victim distance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Li et al. (2021b), which uses a static black-line trigger at the top of images, the proposed IBA with a randomly placed Hello Kitty trigger substantially outperforms: at 5% poisoning on Cityscapes/DeepLabV3, baseline IBA achieves 62.13% ASR versus 35.36% for the black-line design, and PRL pushes this to 92.46% versus 56.23%. Against OGBA (Mao et al., 2023), which requires the trigger to be placed directly on the victim class, all IBA variants outperform at 10% poisoning: PRL achieves 92.46% ASR versus OGBA's 60.08%, while maintaining comparable PBA (71.30 vs 71.11) and CBA (73.30 vs 73.14). The key practical advantage is that IBA allows the trigger to be placed on any non-victim object (e.g., a printed sticker on a roadside sign), making it more applicable to real-world scenarios where the attacker cannot control the trigger's position relative to the target object.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the BadNets-style backdoor attack paradigm (trigger injection plus label modification) from image classification to semantic segmentation, where the goal shifts from misclassifying an entire image to misclassifying all pixels of a specific victim class. It departs from prior segmentation backdoor work (Li et al., 2021b; Mao et al., 2023) by introducing the 'influencer' formulation: the trigger is placed on non-victim pixels and indirectly manipulates victim pixel predictions through the model's context aggregation, rather than being statically positioned on the image border or directly on the victim class. The two proposed techniques—Nearest Neighbor Injection and Pixel Random Labeling—leverage spatial proximity and global context learning respectively, which are segmentation-specific mechanisms absent from classification backdoor literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that segmentation models in safety-critical applications (e.g., autonomous driving) are vulnerable to physically realizable backdoor attacks with as little as 5–7% poisoning, and that the PRL technique makes the attack robust to arbitrary trigger-victim distances—a practically important property since attackers cannot control where a printed trigger appears relative to target objects. However, the defense evaluation is limited to two intuitive methods (fine-tuning and pruning), and it remains open whether more sophisticated defenses (e.g., spectral-based or unlearning approaches) can neutralize the context-aggregation-based mechanism. The authors also acknowledge that real-world generalization beyond controlled outdoor scenes (varying weather, occlusion, scale) warrants further investigation.
