## Problem

Grokking — the sudden jump in test accuracy after prolonged poor performance — is widely believed to involve feature learning beyond the lazy/GP regime, yet no unified theoretical framework connects it to a formal classification of the network's internal state. This paper asks whether Grokking can be mapped onto the theory of first-order phase transitions, providing analytically tractable predictions for when and how the network's weight distribution reorganizes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240951/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes two-layer fully connected networks (erf activation for the cubic teacher-student model, square activation for modular addition) trained to equilibrium via Langevin dynamics with MSE loss, weight decay, and Gaussian noise. The key analytical output is a reduction of the high-dimensional weight posterior to a scalar (1D for the cubic model, 2D per Fourier mode for modular algebra) probability whose saddle points classify the network into three phases: GFL, GMFL-I, and GMFL-II. The effective interaction parameter u = n²σ_a²/(σ⁴dN) (cubic) or u = 2σ_a²P²/(Nσ⁴) (modular) controls the phase, and the critical value u_c is determined analytically or via a single self-consistent equation.

## Method

The core technique extends the adaptive kernel / mean-field decoupling framework of Seroussi et al. (2023) from a variational Gaussian approximation to a variational Gaussian mixture approximation. In appropriate joint scaling limits (N→βN, d→√β·d, n→αn, etc.), the per-neuron weight action takes the form d·Ŝ[Φ(w)] with d≫1, making saddle-point analysis exact. When multiple global minima of Ŝ appear, the weight distribution becomes a mixture of Gaussians centered at each saddle — the GMFL phases. Before the transition (GFL), a single saddle at Φ=0 dominates; during Grokking (GMFL-I), a finite fraction of neurons occupy non-trivial saddles (the 'droplets' of the mixed phase) while others remain at zero; after Grokking (GMFL-II), only the teacher-aligned saddles survive. For the modular algebra model, translation and scaling symmetries of the kernel Q diagonalize it in a Fourier basis, decoupling the action into independent per-mode terms and reducing the self-consistency condition to a single equation for the discrepancy parameter a.

## Results

For the cubic-polynomial teacher-student model, the theory predicts a first-order phase transition at σ² ≈ 0.18 (with u_c ≈ 30.2 for ε = −0.3), where the cubic component of the target begins its sharp approach to the teacher value. For the modular addition model, the GMFL-I mixed phase begins at σ² ≈ 0.227 and transitions to GMFL-II at σ² ≈ 0.175, with a 30% reduction in test-RMSE (discrepancy parameter a drops from −1 to −0.7) over this interval. A key sample-complexity result: the NNGP limit requires n ∝ d³ (≈ 0.5×10⁶ points) to learn the cubic target, while the finite-width DNN achieves it at n = 3000 (∝ d), a two-orders-of-magnitude improvement. Experimental validation with 200 trained networks shows good agreement between theory and measured weight posteriors, with discrepancies shrinking as the scaling parameter α = β is increased.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so it does not benchmark against competing Grokking methods in the standard sense. Instead, it compares its analytical predictions against (i) its own Langevin-dynamics experiments (200 networks, 50 seeds), showing quantitative agreement in weight posterior distributions and target-component learnability that improves with scale; (ii) the NNGP/infinite-width GP limit, demonstrating that the finite DNN's adaptive kernel learns the cubic feature at n ∝ d rather than n ∝ d³; and (iii) prior Grokking analyses, particularly Gromov (2023) for modular arithmetic, where the paper's GMFL-I saddles reproduce the cosine weight structure identified in that work, and Nanda et al. (2023) / Liu et al. (2022a) for the qualitative phase-transition picture. The paper's contribution over these prior works is providing a first-principles, analytically tractable framework (extending Seroussi et al. 2023's adaptive kernel to Gaussian mixtures) that yields exact saddle-point predictions for the transition points and the full phase diagram, rather than relying on handcrafted solutions or single-layer effective theories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adaptive kernel framework of Seroussi et al. (2023) from Gaussian Feature Learning (GFL) to a Gaussian Mixture Feature Learning (GMFL) regime, drawing directly on the mean-field decoupling and equivalent kernel approximations from that line of work. It builds on the phase-transition formalism from statistical physics (Landau & Lifshitz) and the mean-field scaling of Mei et al. (2018), while connecting to prior Grokking analyses that were limited to one trainable layer (Žunkovič & Ilievski, 2022; Arnaboldi et al., 2023) or lacked analytical predictions (Nanda et al., 2023). The key departure is providing a first-principles, two-layer analytical treatment that maps Grokking onto a first-order phase transition with explicit saddle-point structure, rather than the phenomenological or single-layer analyses that preceded it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a unifying lens for classifying feature learning phenomena (GFL → GMFL-I → GMFL-II) and predicting when Grokking occurs across architectures, potentially enabling practitioners to track proximity to Grokking via latent kernel eigenvector outliers. A notable open question is whether the demonstrated sample-complexity reduction (learning cubic features at n=O(d) vs. n=O(d³) for NNGP) can be made rigorous beyond the EK approximation, and whether the phase-transition picture survives in deeper or more heterogeneous networks. The reliance on the equivalent kernel approximation means that the delayed generalization dynamics central to empirical Grokking are only partially captured, leaving the full time-dependent picture as future work.
