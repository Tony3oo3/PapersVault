## Problem

Conventional deep learning TSC methods are black boxes that provide only class-level probabilities without localising the class-conditional discriminatory motifs (signatures) that drive their predictions. Additionally, the standard Global Average Pooling aggregation step weights all time points equally, discarding the per-time-point representations that could reveal which regions are truly discriminative. The paper asks whether Multiple Instance Learning can be applied to TSC in a general, plug-and-play manner to yield inherent interpretability without sacrificing predictive accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method takes univariate time series of fixed length t (c=1 channel) as input, with all series in a dataset assumed to share the same length. Beyond the standard class-level prediction, each forward pass inherently produces per-time-point class-specific predictions (indicating which time points support or refute each class) and, for attention-based variants, per-time-point importance scores in [0,1]. Interpretations are generated at zero additional cost relative to prediction—no perturbation or post-hoc sampling is required—making them over 800× faster than SHAP-based explanations.

## Method

MILLET replaces the Global Average Pooling layer in existing DL TSC backbones (FCN, ResNet, InceptionTime) with one of four MIL pooling methods: Attention (weighted averaging via a sigmoid attention head), Instance (per-time-point classification then mean), Additive (attention-weighted embeddings fed to a per-time-point classifier), and the novel Conjunctive pooling, which applies attention and classification independently to the same time-point embeddings and then scales the predictions by attention scores. This parallel (rather than sequential) design makes the classifier more robust since it cannot rely on the attention head to alter embeddings before classification. Three further enhancements are added: fixed sinusoidal positional encodings injected after feature extraction to preserve temporal ordering, replicate padding (replacing zero padding to avoid start/end bias), and dropout (p=0.1). The resulting 12 models (3 backbones × 4 pooling methods) are trained end-to-end with cross-entropy loss and ensembled over five random initialisations, yielding 12 new interpretable TSC models that outperform their GAP counterparts on both accuracy and interpretability metrics across 85 UCR datasets.

## Results

On 85 UCR TSC datasets, Conjunctive InceptionTime (the best MILLET variant) achieves 0.856 accuracy and 0.834 balanced accuracy, outperforming its GAP backbone (0.853/0.832) and matching or exceeding SOTA methods HC2 (0.860/0.830) and Hydra-MR (0.857/0.831) on balanced accuracy. On the synthetic WebTraffic dataset, MILLET improves mean accuracy across backbones from 0.850 (GAP) to 0.874, with Conjunctive InceptionTime reaching 0.940. For interpretability, MILLET achieves a mean AOPCR of 17.531 and NDCG@n of 0.612 on WebTraffic, substantially outperforming CAM (15.415/0.607) and SHAP (−0.692/0.266), while on UCR it achieves a best AOPCR of 6.00 versus 5.71 for GAP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the GAP backbone baselines (FCN, ResNet, InceptionTime), Conjunctive MILLET consistently improves accuracy across all three backbones (e.g., InceptionTime: 0.853→0.856; ResNet: 0.843→0.845; FCN: 0.828→0.838) and wins on more individual datasets (19 vs. 19 for InceptionTime, 13 vs. 10 for ResNet). Against SOTA method HC2, Conjunctive InceptionTime achieves slightly lower raw accuracy (0.856 vs. 0.860) but higher balanced accuracy (0.834 vs. 0.830), and wins or draws on 49/85 (57.7%) of datasets. Against Hydra-MR, Conjunctive InceptionTime is marginally lower on accuracy (0.856 vs. 0.857) but higher on balanced accuracy (0.834 vs. 0.831), winning or drawing on 52/85 (61.2%) of datasets. For interpretability on WebTraffic, MILLET (AOPCR 17.531) clearly outperforms CAM (15.415) and dramatically outperforms SHAP (−0.692, worse than random), while being over 800× faster than SHAP. On the 85 UCR datasets, MILLET's best AOPCR (6.00) exceeds GAP's (5.71), confirming that the interpretability gain generalises beyond the synthetic setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

MILLET reframes the Global Average Pooling step in standard DL TSC architectures (FCN, ResNet, InceptionTime) as a Multiple Instance Learning pooling problem, drawing directly on attention pooling (Ilse et al., 2018), instance pooling (Wang et al., 2018), and additive pooling (Javed et al., 2022). Prior MIL-for-TSC work was domain-specific (ICU monitoring, activity recognition) or treated MIL as a standalone approach; this paper is the first to apply MIL to TSC in a general, plug-and-play manner across diverse domains. The key conceptual departure is that replacing GAP with MIL pooling inherently produces time-point-level predictions (class-specific saliency) as a byproduct of the forward pass, eliminating the need for post-hoc explainability methods like CAM or SHAP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is that practitioners get sparse, class-specific interpretability for free (single forward pass) while matching or exceeding SOTA predictive accuracy on 85 UCR datasets, which is particularly important for long time series where post-hoc methods become intractable. Limitations include restriction to univariate, fixed-length time series, and the finding that ensemble ResNet models lose interpretability gains. Open questions include extension to multivariate settings (requiring channel-aware interpretability), variable-length series, other model families (e.g., ROCKET/Hydra-MR), and whether pre-training/fine-tuning from GAP checkpoints can reduce the training cost of MILLET models.
