## Problem

RL theory predicts exponential worst-case sample complexity for random exploration and requires highly structured function classes for strategic exploration, yet practical deep RL algorithms succeed using random exploration and expressive neural networks in unstructured stochastic environments. This paper aims to explain this theory-practice gap by identifying the structural property (the effective horizon) that makes random exploration and neural network function approximation sufficient for efficient RL in realistic stochastic MDPs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240574/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

SQIRL consumes i.i.d. tuples of (state, action, reward-to-go) collected by following the random policy for m episodes at each timestep, where states can be high-dimensional (e.g., Atari frames or tabular representations). The regression oracle (a neural network with k·A outputs representing Q¹ through Qᵏ) is trained via least-squares on these tuples plus bootstrapped targets from fitted Q-iteration. The output is a sequence of deterministic policies π₁,…,π_T, where each π_i(s) = argmax_a Q̂_i^k(s,a) is frozen after its iteration, yielding an ε-optimal policy with sample complexity exponential only in the stochastic effective horizon H̄_k = k + log_A(1/Δ_k²) rather than the full horizon T.

## Method

SQIRL (Shallow Q-Iteration via Reinforcement Learning) generalizes GORP to stochastic environments by replacing Monte Carlo averaging and deterministic backups with regression and fitted Q-iteration. At each timestep i, it collects m episodes by following the already-learned policies π₁,…,π_{i-1} and then the random policy, regresses the random policy's Q-function Q¹ from the collected (s, a, reward-to-go) samples, and performs k−1 steps of fitted Q-iteration to approximate k−1 steps of value iteration, producing Q̂_i^k. The key insight is that many stochastic MDPs are k-QVI-solvable (acting greedily after k steps of Q-value iteration on the random policy's Q-function is optimal), so only a few FQI steps are needed. The regression oracle need only satisfy in-distribution generalization (bounded by pseudo-dimension d), a property empirically satisfied by neural networks, making the sample complexity Õ(kT³A^{H̄_k}d/ε). The paper proves this bound holds for least-squares regression over any VC-type hypothesis class, including linear functions and neural networks, and validates empirically that SQIRL's performance strongly correlates with PPO and DQN across 155+ stochastic environments.

## Results

On 155 sticky-action BRIDGE environments, SQIRL solves 69 environments, compared to PPO (96), DQN (76), and GORP (26). SQIRL's empirical sample complexity has a Spearman correlation of 0.83 with PPO and 0.56 with DQN, exceeding the 0.48 correlation between PPO and DQN themselves. In environments where both PPO and DQN converge to an optimal policy, SQIRL also succeeds 85% of the time; when both PPO and DQN fail, SQIRL never succeeds. On full-length Atari games, SQIRL achieves higher reward than both PPO and DQN in 3 of 6 games and worse reward in only 2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to PPO, SQIRL solves about two-thirds as many environments (69 vs. 96) but exhibits a stronger sample-complexity correlation with PPO (0.83) than PPO does with DQN (0.48), with a median sample-complexity ratio of 1.38 indicating SQIRL is slightly less sample-efficient. Against DQN, SQIRL solves nearly as many environments (69 vs. 76) with a median sample-complexity ratio of 1.00, meaning they require comparable timesteps to solve environments. Against GORP (the deterministic-environment predecessor), SQIRL dramatically outperforms it (69 vs. 26 environments solved), confirming that the regression-based approach is essential for handling stochastic transitions. The key finding is that SQIRL's performance tracks PPO and DQN more closely than they track each other, supporting the claim that the effective horizon and in-distribution regression explain deep RL's practical success.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the effective horizon framework and GORP algorithm from Laidlaw et al. (2023), which analyzed random exploration in deterministic MDPs, to stochastic environments with function approximation. The key algorithmic ingredient is fitted Q-iteration (FQI) from Ernst et al. (2005), repurposed here as a model-free regression-based backup operator that replaces the deterministic value-backup steps in GORP. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant strategic-exploration/UCB tradition in RL theory by showing that the effective horizon assumption allows one to fully separate exploration (purely random) from learning (regression + a few FQI steps), sidestepping the complex interplay between exploration and function approximation that makes UCB-based analysis intractable for neural networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first principled explanation for two long-standing theory-practice gaps in deep RL: why random exploration suffices in practice despite exponential worst-case bounds, and why neural networks work as function approximators despite lacking the structural assumptions (linearity, low-rank) required by strategic exploration theory. The empirical finding that SQIRL's sample complexity correlates more strongly with PPO and DQN than they do with each other (Spearman 0.83/0.56 vs. 0.48) is strong evidence that the effective horizon captures a real property of environments that governs deep RL performance. Open questions include the environments where SQIRL fails but PPO/DQN succeed (suggesting the effective horizon assumption is insufficient), whether the framework extends to continuous action spaces or partially observable settings, and whether the theoretical insights can be translated into practical algorithmic improvements beyond SQIRL's heavy hyperparameter tuning burden.
