## Problem

Existing selective rationalization methods, particularly unsupervised and semi-supervised variants, exploit spurious shortcuts in data to compose rationales rather than identifying true causal evidence, and supervised rationalization requires extensive human-annotated rationales that are infeasible for most tasks. The paper asks how to boost rationalization quality in a semi-supervised setting by explicitly discovering and leveraging shortcuts, while simultaneously addressing the data scarcity bottleneck.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241679/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a token sequence x and a small set of annotated rationales (25% of training data in D_sup) as input, and produces a class label y along with a binary token mask m that selects the extractive rationale z = m ⊙ x. Unusually, the pipeline also outputs identified shortcut subsequences (3+ consecutive tokens present in unsupervised-predicted but absent from gold rationales), which are then used to generate augmented training examples by replacing shortcut tokens with random or semantically retrieved substitutes from a FAISS-based datastore.

## Method

SSR operates in two phases (supervised on D_sup, then unsupervised on D_un) with three key mechanisms. First, a shortcut discovery step trains an unsupervised rationalization model M_un, infers rationales on D_sup, and identifies shortcut tokens as those in predicted but not gold rationales (≥3 consecutive). Second, two strategies mitigate shortcut exploitation: SSR_unif adds a KL-uniform loss in the supervised phase forcing the predictor to treat shortcut inputs as meaningless, with shared encoder/linear parameters transferring this de-correlation to the unsupervised phase; SSR_virt trains an external shortcut predictor and a shortcut imitator f_a that maps full inputs to shortcut representations, then in the unsupervised phase freezes f_a to generate virtual shortcut representations and applies a KL-uniform constraint with shared W_a ↔ W_p to decouple shortcuts from rationale features. Third, two data augmentation methods (random and retrieval-grounded semantic) replace identified shortcut tokens in D_sup to close the quantitative gap between D_un and D_sup.

## Results

On the ERASER benchmark (25% labeled rationales), SSR_unif achieves the best Task F1 on Movies (94.3) and Evidence Inference (46.8), while SSR_virt leads on MultiRC (64.2) and FEVER (87.1). With mixed data augmentation, SSR_unif reaches 94.5 Task F1 on Movies (surpassing the SOTA supervised AT-BMC at 92.9) and 35.2 Token-F1 on Evidence Inference (nearly double AT-BMC's 18.6). On FEVER Token-F1, SSR methods (~49) remain well below AT-BMC (71.1) and Pipeline (81.2), which exploit full annotations. Overall, SSR with 25% rationales achieves performance comparable to fully supervised methods on task prediction while substantially outperforming all semi-supervised baselines on rationale quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSR_unif outperforms all semi-supervised baselines (WSEE, ST-RAT, Vanilla Semi-RAT) on Task F1 across most datasets, e.g., 94.3 vs. 90.1 (WSEE) on Movies and 50.4 vs. 49.2 (WSEE) on Evidence Inference. On Token-F1, SSR_virt with mixed DA achieves 33.4 on Evidence Inference, nearly doubling the best semi-supervised baseline (WSEE at 14.8) and even surpassing the fully supervised AT-BMC (18.6). Against the SOTA supervised AT-BMC, SSR_unif wins on Movies Task F1 (94.3 vs. 92.9) and FEVER Task F1 (86.8 vs. 82.3), but trails on Token-F1 for FEVER (48.8 vs. 71.1) and MultiRC (40.3 vs. 61.1), where AT-BMC benefits from 100% annotated rationales. Among unsupervised baselines, SSR methods dominate on Token-F1 (e.g., 35.2 vs. 10.8 for MCD on Evidence Inference) while remaining competitive on Task F1. The generalization experiment on SST-2 (OOD) shows SSR_virt + semantic DA reaches 83.5 F1 versus 78.3 for WSEE + semantic DA, confirming that shortcut-aware rationalization transfers better to distribution shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the semi-supervised selective rationalization paradigm (Pruthi et al., 2020; Bhat et al., 2021) by introducing an explicit shortcut discovery mechanism that leverages the known tendency of unsupervised rationalization models to exploit spurious correlations. Rather than following the dominant strategy of prior work (INVRAT, Inter-RAT, MCD) that attempts to remove or avoid shortcuts through invariance constraints or causal interventions, SSR takes the inverse approach: it first identifies shortcuts via a trained unsupervised model, then uses that knowledge to de-correlate shortcuts from rationales through uniform-distribution constraints and virtual shortcut representations. The shared-parameter architecture between supervised and unsupervised phases is inherited from Bhat et al. (2021), while the data augmentation strategy of replacing identified shortcut tokens is a novel contribution not present in prior rationalization work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that explicitly modeling shortcuts—rather than merely avoiding them—can substantially improve rationale faithfulness and task accuracy with only 25% (or even 15%) of labeled rationales, approaching fully supervised performance. This has practical implications for domains where rationale annotation is expensive (medical, legal), and the OOD generalization results on SST-2 suggest that shortcut-aware models transfer better to distribution shifts. However, the approach is limited to extractive rationale settings with BERT-scale encoders, and the shortcut discovery relies on Assumption 1 (that unsupervised models do exploit shortcuts), which may not hold universally. The paper acknowledges that extending SSR to explain autoregressive LLM outputs remains an open challenge.
