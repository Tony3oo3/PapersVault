## Problem

While in-context learning (ICL) is known to elicit diverse behaviors from LLMs, the precise mechanisms driving these behavioral shifts remain opaque, and prior theoretical work framing ICL as Bayesian inference has only been validated on small toy networks trained from scratch. This paper asks whether ICL in state-of-the-art black-box LLMs operates as discrete Bayesian model selection (producing sharp phase changes in behavior) or as smooth model averaging, and whether this can be characterized without observing internal activations or retraining weights.

## Input / Output

For Generation tasks, the input is a prompt specifying a weighted coin with probability p(Tails) followed by a context sequence x of comma-separated 'Heads'/'Tails' flips of varying length |x|; the model generates up to 50 binary flips, and the paper extracts next-token prediction probabilities p(y_t | x, y_{0..t-1}) directly from the OpenAI API to construct weighted binary trees of depth d. For Judgment tasks, the input is a full binary sequence x with the question 'Is this random or non-random?', and the output is a single token ('Random' or 'Non') whose probability p(y=random|x) is tracked as |x| increases. The binary token space (branching factor 2) makes the full predictive distribution over sequences enumerable and visualizable as a tree, unlike free-form text generation.

## Method

The paper introduces 'Cognitive Interpretability,' a framework that models ICL dynamics as Bayesian model selection over a discrete hypothesis space H of latent algorithms (Bernoulli processes, regular languages, Markov chains, and a memory-constrained Window Average model), predicting that model selection (argmax over hypothesis posteriors) produces sharp S-shaped phase transitions in p(y|x) while model averaging produces smooth curves. They systematically vary context length |x| for specific binary sequences (e.g., (01)^n, (011)^n) across GPT-3.5+ models and demonstrate that predictive distributions transition abruptly from subjectively random generation to deterministic pattern repetition, matching the model-selection prediction. To characterize the 'random' regime, they compare GPT outputs against a Bernoulli baseline and a Window Average model (formalizing Hahn & Warren's finite-memory theory of the Gambler's Fallacy), finding that GPT-3.5+ exhibits lower-variance, run-avoiding sequences consistent with the Window Average model. They further rule out simple memorization using sub-sequence frequency distributions, Gzip compression sizes, and inter-sequence Levenshtein distances, showing that GPT combines dynamic algorithmic generation with distribution-matched pattern reuse.

## Results

GPT-3.5+ models (text-davinci-003, gpt-3.5-turbo, GPT-4) exhibit an emergent Gambler's Fallacy bias: generated binary sequences have significantly lower variance in sequence means and shorter longest runs compared to a Bernoulli process with the same mean, analogous to human subjective randomness. A Window Average model with window size w=5 partially explains both biases, matching GPT-generated sequences more closely than a Bernoulli distribution. In formal language learning tasks (e.g., x=(011)^n), GPT-3.5+ shows sharp S-shaped phase transitions in the predictive probability p(y∈C|x), jumping from near-random generation to near-deterministic pattern matching as context length |x| increases, consistent with Bayesian model selection rather than model averaging. GPT-3.5+ (except ChatGPT) generates low-complexity sequences with smaller Gzip file sizes and lower inter-sequence Levenshtein distances than a Bernoulli baseline, while ChatGPT produces higher-than-chance complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GPT-3.5+ outputs against three primary baselines: (1) a Bernoulli process with matched mean, (2) a cognitively-inspired Window Average model (w=5), and (3) Markov chains of varying order. Against the Bernoulli baseline, GPT-3.5+ shows significantly lower variance in sequence means and shorter longest runs (Gambler's Fallacy), with the Window Average model (w=5) partially accounting for both deviations. Against memorization, sub-sequence analysis reveals thousands of unique length-k subsequences across different p(Tails) values, making pure training-data parroting unlikely, though GPT does repeat certain longer sub-sequences (e.g., length-20) more than the Window Average model predicts. Across model generations, text-davinci-003 and GPT-4 show stable, controllable subjective randomness, while ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo-0613) behaves erratically for p(Tails)>50% and generates higher-complexity sequences than chance. An MMLU distractor experiment confirms that the formal language learning observed in Generation tasks is not merely a local text bias: GPT-3.5 maintains 5–20% probability of MMLU answer tokens even after hundreds of distractor flips, eventually converging back to the original task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as 'Cognitive Interpretability,' a middle ground between black-box behavioral benchmarks (BIG-Bench, MMLU) and mechanistic circuit-level interpretability (induction heads, modular addition circuits). It builds directly on the Bayesian model selection framing of ICL from Xie et al. (2022) and the cognitive science of subjective randomness from Griffiths & Tenenbaum (2003) and Hahn & Warren (2009), but departs from prior ICL-as-Bayesian-inference work by analyzing state-of-the-art black-box LLMs (GPT-3.5+, GPT-4) rather than training toy networks from scratch. The key conceptual departure is from the smooth, continuously decreasing loss curves reported in standard few-shot ICL evaluations (Brown et al. 2020; Wei et al. 2022b), which the paper argues may hide discrete phase changes in latent algorithm selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding of sharp S-shaped phase transitions in ICL dynamics—where GPT-3.5 abruptly shifts from generating subjectively random sequences to deterministically repeating a formal language pattern—challenges the assumption that ICL learning is smooth and continuous, with implications for AI safety (capabilities may suddenly emerge or disappear with a single additional context token). The Window Average model (w=5) partially explaining GPT's Gambler's Fallacy bias provides a concrete, interpretable algorithmic account of LLM behavior in a domain with no ground-truth answer. Limitations include the narrow domain (binary sequences only), reliance primarily on OpenAPI-accessible models, and the fact that phase transitions are not observed in earlier GPT versions, leaving the mechanism and generalizability unclear. Open questions include whether these phase changes are common across ICL domains or specific to the constrained binary-sequence setting, and what circuit-level mechanisms underlie the discrete hypothesis selection.
