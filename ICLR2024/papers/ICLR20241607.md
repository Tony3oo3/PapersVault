## Problem

Time-to-event (TTE) models are essential in medicine for estimating when events occur, but they require large training sets and are severely limited by high censoring rates common in long-horizon medical outcomes. Prior TTE work relied on small, disease-specific cohorts trained from scratch, and standard autoregressive pretraining objectives fail to capture the long-term temporal structure that TTE modeling demands. No foundation model for medical TTE had been released to the research community, creating reproducibility barriers for evaluating transfer learning.

## Input / Output

MOTOR consumes timestamped sequences of clinical event codes (diagnoses, labs, medications, procedures, visits) drawn from standard ontologies (ICD-10, SNOMED via OMOP), with timestamps in days since birth. The Transformer processes sequences up to 16,384 events using 512-token local attention windows and causal masking. Output is a piecewise exponential survival curve parameterized by hazard rates λ per time piece (8 or 16 pieces), from which survival probabilities, hazard functions, and time-to-event distributions are derived for any target task.

## Method

MOTOR combines a Transformer encoder (with rotary position embeddings over time, local attention, and causal masking) with a piecewise exponential TTE head, pretrained via a novel self-supervised objective that predicts the time to the next occurrence of 8,192 clinical codes simultaneously. The key architectural innovation is a low-rank factorization of the hazard computation: a time-independent task embedding β_k is learned per pretraining task, and a time-dependent linear transformation M_ijp maps patient representations to piece-specific states, so log(λ_ijkp) = M_ijp · β_k, reducing parameters by P× per task. Sparse matrix operations exploit the fact that only ~0.6% of event indicator entries are non-zero, and fused kernel operations avoid materializing large intermediates, together cutting memory by 35%. Pretraining on up to 55M patients (9B events) yields a 143M-parameter model that is adapted to new tasks via a lightweight linear probe (tuning only β_k^new) or full fine-tuning, improving time-dependent C statistics by 4.6% over state-of-the-art baselines.

## Results

On six code-based time-to-event tasks (Celiac, Heart Attack, Lupus, NAFLD, Pancreatic Cancer, Stroke), MOTOR-Finetune achieves time-dependent C statistics of 0.802–0.887 on STARR-OMOP and 0.762–0.862 on MERATIVE, outperforming all five baselines with statistically significant margins (95% CIs in Table S5). Across 19 tasks (6 code-based + 13 text-based) on three datasets, MOTOR-derived models improve time-dependent C statistic by an average of 4.6% over state-of-the-art. MOTOR also improves label efficiency by up to 95% (matching RSF with only 5% of labeled data) and demonstrates cross-site portability, outperforming all baselines when transferred from STARR-OMOP to MIMIC-IV.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOTOR-Finetune outperforms all five baselines on every task and both datasets, with all differences statistically significant at p=0.05. On STARR-OMOP, the strongest baseline is RSF, which MOTOR-Finetune beats by 3.5–7.6 percentage points (e.g., 0.863 vs 0.787 on Lupus, 0.887 vs 0.836 on Heart Attack). Cox PH is the weakest baseline overall, trailing MOTOR-Finetune by 8–27 points (e.g., 0.538 vs 0.762 on Celiac in MERATIVE). DeepSurv, DSM, and DeepHit cluster closely together (within ~1–2 points of each other) and are consistently outperformed by MOTOR by 2–8 points on STARR-OMOP and 1.6–3.7 points on MERATIVE. Notably, MOTOR-Scratch (same architecture trained without pretraining) also underperforms MOTOR-Finetune on all tasks, confirming that the TTE pretraining objective—not just the architecture—drives the gains. On the cross-site MIMIC-IV transfer experiment, MOTOR-Transfer Probe outperforms the best MIMIC-IV-trained baseline on all six tasks (e.g., 0.850 vs 0.820 on Heart Attack, 0.828 vs 0.748 on Pancreatic Cancer).

## Relation to Prior Work

MOTOR builds on two main threads: (1) the PEANN piecewise-exponential survival architecture (Fornili et al., 2014), which it scales to thousands of concurrent tasks via low-rank task embeddings and sparse matrix operations, and (2) the BEHRT Transformer for EHR sequences (Li et al., 2020), which it modifies with causal masking, rotary position embeddings over clinical timestamps, and local attention to handle 16K-event sequences. It departs from prior structured-medical-record pretraining (Steinberg et al., 2021) that used autoregressive 'next code' objectives by introducing a TTE-specific pretraining loss over 8,192 code-based survival tasks, and from prior TTE deep learning (DeepSurv, DeepHit, DSM) by framing the problem as foundation-model transfer learning at 55M-patient scale rather than task-specific training from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first released medical TTE foundation model (143M parameters, HuggingFace), MOTOR provides a reusable starting point that prior structured-EHR pretrained models (e.g., Med-BERT, BEHRT) did not offer for survival analysis, directly addressing reproducibility gaps the authors identify. The demonstrated 95% label-efficiency gain and cross-site portability (STARR-OMOP → MIMIC-IV) suggest practical value for resource-limited clinical settings, though the MIMIC-IV transfer margins are modest (e.g., Celiac 0.628 vs. 0.625 best baseline). Open questions include whether TTE pretraining generalizes to competing-risks settings beyond the simplified death-censoring assumption, how to mitigate algorithmic bias in medical foundation models, and whether the expensive pretraining cost (~2 GPU-weeks on MERATIVE) can be reduced.
