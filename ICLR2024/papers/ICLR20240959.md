## Problem

While it is well known that fine-tuning LLMs (via instruction-tuning or RLHF) degrades performance on tasks outside the fine-tuning distribution, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. This paper asks whether fine-tuning truly erases pretrained capabilities or merely shifts the model's implicit inference about which task a prompt belongs to, and whether suppressed capabilities can be recovered without retraining.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240959/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the synthetic linear-regression setup, the model receives a variable-length sequence of (x_i, y_i) exemplar pairs plus a query x_query and outputs a scalar regression prediction; conjugate prompting scales all y_i by a factor γ before feeding the prompt and divides the output by γ. In the LLM experiments, the input is a natural-language prompt (few-shot exemplars plus a query, or a single instruction) that is first transformed via language translation, leetspeak, or Pig Latin, and the model's text completion is inverse-transformed back to the original language. The key constraint is that the transform must lower the prompt's likelihood under the fine-tuning distribution while preserving an invertible path to the original answer.

## Method

The paper introduces a controlled synthetic setup where a 22.4M-parameter GPT-2 transformer is pretrained on a mixture of a continuous Gaussian weight distribution (D_cont) and a discrete set of 64 fixed weight vectors (D_disc), then fine-tuned on D_disc. The central hypothesis is that the model factors its prediction into a task-inference gate g_θ(X,y) that weights between a discrete-regression capability and a ridge-regression capability, and that fine-tuning inflates g_θ for prompts near the fine-tuning distribution rather than unlearning the ridge-regression capability. Conjugate prompting exploits this by applying a transform s to the prompt that pushes it farther from the fine-tuning distribution (scaling labels by γ in the synthetic setup; translating to non-English languages, leetspeak, or Pig Latin in LLM experiments) and then applying s⁻¹ to the output. The approach is validated across three real-world fine-tuning scenarios—instruction tuning suppressing in-context learning, code fine-tuning suppressing sentence entailment, and RLHF suppressing harmful-content generation—showing that conjugate prompting recovers a substantial fraction of the suppressed pretrained behavior in each case.

## Results

In the synthetic linear regression setup, conjugate prompting with label scale γ∈{1.5, 2.0} recovers ridge regression performance after fine-tuning on the discrete distribution, particularly at low exemplar counts where task ambiguity is highest. On real LLMs, instruction-tuned Alpaca drops 56.75% in English ICL accuracy (92%→35.25%) versus pretrained LLaMA, but this drop shrinks to just 1.50% under Leetspeak and 29.00% under French. Code fine-tuning (Code LLaMA vs. LLaMA-2) causes an 8.36% drop in English XNLI accuracy (44.26%→35.90%), yet Spanish and French accuracy actually *increase* slightly (38.11%→38.88% and 33.53%→34.98%). For safety fine-tuning, ChatGPT's answer rate on harmful instructions drops 89% in English (92%→3%) but only 6% in Malayalam (71%→65%), demonstrating that conjugate prompting via translation partially recovers suppressed pretrained capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three pretrained/fine-tuned model pairs to quantify catastrophic forgetting and test conjugate prompting recovery. Alpaca (instruction-tuned LLaMA) loses 56.75% of its ICL capability in English relative to pretrained LLaMA, but this gap nearly vanishes under Leetspeak (1.50% drop) and is halved under French (29.00%). Vicuna shows a similar pattern: 33.00% English drop versus only 1.00% under Leetspeak and 11.00% under Spanish. OPT-IML exhibits the smallest English drop (21.00%) and the most uniform recovery across non-English languages (4.00–10.00% drops). For code fine-tuning, Code LLaMA underperforms LLaMA-2 by 8.36% on English XNLI (35.90% vs. 44.26%), yet slightly *outperforms* it on Spanish (38.88% vs. 38.11%) and French (34.98% vs. 33.53%), suggesting the reasoning capability is preserved but suppressed by English-biased task inference. Finally, ChatGPT (safety-fine-tuned GPT-3.5) drops from 92% to 3% answer rate on English harmful instructions (89% drop), while the same model in Malayalam retains 65% answer rate (only 6% drop), confirming that safety fine-tuning suppresses rather than erases the pretrained answering capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the in-context learning literature for linear functions (Garg et al., 2023; Akyürek et al., 2022) as a controlled synthetic setup, and on the implicit Bayesian inference / task inference perspective for LLMs (Xie et al., 2021; Min et al., 2022b; Wei et al., 2023b; Tamkin et al., 2022). It departs from the catastrophic forgetting and continual learning literature (McCloskey & Cohen, 1989; Kirkpatrick et al., 2017; Ramasesh et al., 2022), which typically proposes new training algorithms or data replay, by instead reframing forgetting as a shift in implicit task inference and proposing a purely prompting-based intervention (conjugate prompting) that requires no retraining. The safety findings also provide a unified mechanistic explanation for translation-based jailbreaking attacks previously documented empirically by Wei et al. (2023a) and Zou et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The task-inference reframing offers a testable, mechanistic account of why fine-tuning causes apparent forgetting, distinguishing between capability loss and capability suppression — a distinction with direct practical implications for model deployment and safety. The conjugate prompting technique is simple (translate the prompt, run the model, translate back) and demonstrates that safety fine-tuning is brittle across languages, raising urgent questions about the robustness of current alignment methods. Key limitations include the assumption (not proof) that models factor into task inference plus capabilities, the reliance on third-party translation quality, and the small sample sizes in the safety experiments (100 instructions per language). Open questions include whether transformers implement task inference through identifiable sub-networks that could be directly manipulated, and whether principled fine-tuning methods can guide task inference without the trade-offs observed here.
