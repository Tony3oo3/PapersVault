## Problem

LLMs in prompting and in-context learning exhibit systematic bias toward certain answers due to prompt context (templates, verbalizers, ICL examples), yet existing calibration methods each have critical failure modes: content-free token estimators (CC, DC) introduce spurious bias in multi-sentence tasks, and GMM-based calibration (PC) overfits and destabilizes on challenging multi-class problems. The field lacked a unified decision-boundary analysis explaining these failures and a single method that generalizes across task types and modalities.

## Input / Output

BC consumes a batch of M unlabeled test samples together with the fixed prompt context C (containing the template and k ICL demonstrations) and produces calibrated log-probabilities by subtracting the batch-averaged score from each sample's score. No additional forward passes are required beyond the original ICL inference. In the BCL variant, a small labeled set (J×128 samples) is additionally used to grid-search a single scalar strength γ ∈ [−5, 5]. For vision-language models, the input is an image plus a text prompt template, and the same batch-averaging calibration is applied to CLIP's class scores.

## Method

BC estimates the contextual bias p̂(y|C) by marginalizing the LLM's output scores over all M samples in the batch (Eq. 1), then calibrates each sample by subtracting this estimate in log-probability space (Eq. 2), yielding a linear decision boundary that is a translation of the uncalibrated ICL boundary. This avoids the rotation artifact of CC and the non-linear GMM boundary of PC, and eliminates the need for content-free or random tokens that can themselves introduce bias in multi-sentence tasks. The BCL extension introduces a single learnable scalar γ that scales the correction term, selected by grid search on labeled data to fine-tune calibration strength. A running-average variant (Eq. 3) allows on-the-fly estimation from mini-batches without requiring the full test set upfront, and the entire procedure is zero-shot, inference-only, and incurs negligible computational overhead.

## Results

On 13 NLP classification tasks with 1-shot PaLM 2-S, BC achieves 74.41% average accuracy, outperforming ICL (66.20%), CC (62.39%), DC (64.98%), and PC (67.65%). On the larger PaLM 2-L model, BC reaches 81.09% average accuracy versus 75.16% for ICL and 77.83% for the best baseline (CC). For zero-shot image classification with CLIP ViT-B/16, BC improves average accuracy by 12% over the uncalibrated baseline (e.g., SVHN: 18.0% → 35.0%, CLEVR: 14.7% → 29.2%). BC requires only 1 forward pass (no additional API calls) and achieves strong performance with as few as ~10 unlabeled samples for batch estimation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BC outperforms all three calibration baselines on average across both model sizes. Against Contextual Calibration (CC, Zhao et al. 2021), BC gains +12.0% on PaLM 2-S (74.41 vs. 62.39) and +3.3% on PaLM 2-L (81.09 vs. 77.83); CC suffers severe degradation on multi-sentence tasks like QNLI (56.86% vs. BC's 82.45% on PaLM 2-S) and CB (29.64% vs. 78.21%). Against Domain-Context Calibration (DC, Fei et al. 2023), BC leads by +9.4% on PaLM 2-S and +4.2% on PaLM 2-L; DC is the weakest baseline overall, with particularly poor results on QQP (53.92%) and QNLI (65.62%) on PaLM 2-S. Against Prototypical Calibration (PC, Han et al. 2023), BC achieves +6.8% on PaLM 2-S and +5.0% on PaLM 2-L; PC exhibits the highest variance (e.g., 17.73 std on QNLI, 22.37 std on BoolQ for PaLM 2-L) and occasional catastrophic failures, while BC maintains consistently low standard deviations. On the vision side, BC improves zero-shot CLIP by 12% on average over the uncalibrated baseline across SVHN, EuroSAT, and CLEVR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the line of ICL calibration methods initiated by Contextual Calibration (Zhao et al., ICML 2021) and extended by Domain-Context Calibration (Fei et al., ACL 2023) and Prototypical Calibration (Han et al., ICLR 2023). It provides a unified decision-boundary analysis showing that CC and DC both implement linear boundary transformations (rotation and shift, respectively) while PC uses a non-linear GMM boundary, then departs from all three by estimating the contextual prior through batch marginalization over actual test inputs rather than content-free or random tokens. The resulting Batch Calibration (BC) retains the linear boundary form of CC/DC but replaces their fragile prior estimators with a content-based batch mean, and extends to an adjustable few-shot variant (BCL) and to vision-language models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: BC is a zero-shot, inference-only, drop-in calibration layer with negligible overhead that consistently improves ICL by 6–8% across 13 NLP tasks and 12% on zero-shot CLIP image classification, making prompt engineering substantially more robust to template, verbalizer, and example-order choices. The key conceptual finding—that 'content-free' tokens like 'N/A' are not actually bias-free in multi-sentence contexts and can introduce spurious entailment signals—challenges a foundational assumption in prior calibration work. Open questions include extending BC to generative tasks (the authors suggest calibrating first-token logits), handling streaming settings where the full batch is unavailable, and whether the batch-marginalization principle generalizes to other forms of contextual bias beyond classification.
