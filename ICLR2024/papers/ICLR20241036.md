## Problem

While double descent has been empirically observed across diverse deep learning architectures, the underlying mechanism by which over-parameterized models improve generalization beyond the interpolation threshold remains unexplained, particularly in the presence of label noise. Existing theoretical accounts focus on linear models or misspecification, leaving the 'deep' double descent phenomenon without a comprehensive mechanistic explanation rooted in how representations are actually learned.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241036/cropped_page5_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes penultimate-layer representations (f_{L-1}(X)) from fully trained FCNNs, CNNs, and ResNet18s of varying widths, trained on MNIST (28×28 grayscale, N=4000) and CIFAR-10 (32×32 RGB, N=50000) with controlled label noise ratios p ∈ {0%, 10%, 20%, 40%}. It produces a scalar metric P — the fraction of noisy training samples whose k-nearest clean neighbours (by cosine similarity in feature space) share the noisy sample's true class — alongside the standard test error vs. model width curves. The k-NN evaluation is computed solely on the training set, making P a purely in-context diagnostic that nonetheless tracks out-of-sample generalization.

## Method

The paper introduces a feature-space interpretation strategy that applies k-nearest-neighbour classification with cosine similarity to the penultimate-layer representations of trained networks, specifically measuring whether noisy-labelled samples are surrounded by clean samples of their true class (metric P). Across three architectures (two-layer FCNN, five-layer CNN, ResNet18) and two datasets, the authors train a family of models with increasing width under a uniform SGD protocol with no regularization, then track both test error and P as a function of model width. The key finding is that P follows the same double-descent trajectory as test accuracy: small and intermediate models place noisy samples near their incorrect class (low P), while over-parameterized models interpolate correct samples around noisy ones, effectively isolating the noise in feature space (P approaching 100% on MNIST, ~60% on CIFAR-10). This isolation mechanism — where extra parameters beyond the interpolation threshold are used to separate signal from noise rather than to fit it — is proposed as the internal explanation for the second descent in the double descent curve, distinguishing this work from prior accounts based on sharpness, decision-boundary fragmentation, or bias-variance decomposition.

## Results

On MNIST with two-layer FCNNs (N=4000), introducing 10% or 20% label noise produces a clear double descent peak in test error around the interpolation threshold (k≈15 at p=0%), and the k-NN prediction accuracy P of noisy samples rises from ~70% to 85% and nearly 100% in the over-parameterized regime, closely tracking test accuracy. On CIFAR-10 with five-layer CNNs and ResNet18 (N=50000), P reaches approximately 60% for over-parameterized models, again correlating with test performance. The test error peak height is positively correlated with the label noise ratio p, and the interpolation threshold shifts rightward as noise increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against competing approaches; rather, it is an empirical analysis that replicates the double descent experimental framework of Nakkiran et al. (2021) and adds a k-NN-based feature-space interpretation metric (P). The 'comparisons' are internal: across three architectures (FCNN, CNN, ResNet18), three noise levels (p=0%, 10%, 20%), and a sweep of model widths. The key finding is that P (the fraction of noisy samples whose k nearest clean neighbours share the correct class) tracks test accuracy in the same double-descent shape across all architectures, with MNIST yielding higher P values (~85–100%) than CIFAR-10 (~60%), attributed to the more complex feature geometry of CIFAR-10. The paper positions its k-NN interpretation as complementary to Gamba et al. (2022)'s sharpness-based analysis, arguing that noisy samples are 'isolated' among correct class-mates in the learned feature space of over-parameterized models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the experimental framework of Nakkiran et al. (2021), who established that label noise is the strongest driver of double descent in deep networks, and on Belkin et al. (2019), who first formalized the double descent curve. It departs from prior mechanistic explanations (bias-variance decomposition, decision boundary fragmentation, sharpness of interpolation) by introducing a k-nearest-neighbour analysis in the penultimate-layer learned feature space to directly visualize how over-parameterized models position noisy samples relative to their clean class-mates. The closest concurrent work is Gamba et al. (2022), which also separates clean from noisy data points but through sharpness measurements rather than geometric proximity in representation space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a concrete, interpretable mechanism—'isolation' of noise via interpolation of correct samples around noisy ones—that connects the second descent to a geometric property of the learned feature space, potentially guiding future work on noise-robust training. However, the analysis remains purely empirical with no theoretical framework, the k-NN metric is acknowledged as only an estimation tool, and the effect is substantially weaker on CIFAR-10 (P ≈ 60%) than on MNIST (P ≈ 85–100%), leaving open whether the isolation mechanism generalizes to more complex feature spaces. The authors themselves flag the absence of a comprehensive theory, the unexplored role of depth vs. width, and implicit sparsity as key open questions.
