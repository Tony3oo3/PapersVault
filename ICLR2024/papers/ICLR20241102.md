## Problem

Standard cross-entropy training on individual examples produces overconfident, poorly calibrated classifiers, and calibration is typically addressed only as an afterthought via post-hoc procedures like temperature scaling or label smoothing. This is especially problematic in low-data and heavy-tailed class distribution settings, where minority classes suffer from both poor accuracy and miscalibrated probabilities. The paper asks whether a training framework can simultaneously improve accuracy and calibration without additional hyperparameters or post-processing.

## Input / Output

During training, the model receives sets of k+2 images (typically k=1, so 3 images per set): two from the same class (the 'pair') and k from distinct other classes (the 'odd-k-outs'). At inference, the model takes a single image and outputs a class label with a calibrated probability distribution, exactly like any standard classifier. The unusual aspect is that the training objective operates on the sum of per-example logits before applying softmax, while the architecture and inference procedure remain unchanged from single-example classification.

## Method

Odd-k-out learning (OKO) replaces single-example cross-entropy with a set-level objective: for each training set of k+2 examples, the model's logits are summed element-wise across all examples, softmax is applied to the resulting vector, and cross-entropy is computed against the pair class label (hard loss). This summation-before-softmax mechanism is the key insight: it makes the loss landscape convex in each individual logit entry (Proposition 2), preventing the unbounded logit divergence that causes overconfidence in vanilla cross-entropy. Theoretically, OKO implicitly performs entropic regularization, assigning lower confidence to low-data regions where the model has seen few examples. The approach requires no architectural changes, no additional hyperparameters, and scales linearly in set size; at test time the model is applied to single examples with zero extra overhead.

## Results

OKO achieves the highest test-set accuracy across all four datasets and both class distribution settings. On MNIST, OKO reaches 93.62% (uniform) and 85.67% (heavy-tailed), outperforming the best baseline (BB at 92.31% and 81.42% respectively). On CIFAR-100, OKO improves from 32.72% (Vanilla) to 35.11% (uniform) and from 13.67% (BB) to 14.13% (heavy-tailed). For calibration, OKO achieves the lowest Expected Calibration Error (ECE) in nearly all settings and the lowest mean absolute relative cross-entropy (RC) for all balanced settings, confirming simultaneously better accuracy and calibration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OKO outperforms all seven baselines on accuracy across every dataset and distribution setting. Against Label Smoothing (Müller et al., 2019), OKO gains up to 8.29% on heavy-tailed MNIST (85.67% vs. 77.38%) and 3.91% on uniform CIFAR-10 (57.63% vs. 55.06%), while also achieving substantially lower ECE without requiring a noise hyperparameter. Against Focal Loss (Lin et al., 2017), OKO leads by 9.25% on heavy-tailed MNIST (85.67% vs. 76.42%) and 2.41% on uniform CIFAR-10 (57.63% vs. 54.39%). Against Temperature Scaling (Platt et al., 1999, used as BB+TS with τ=2.0), OKO matches or exceeds accuracy while achieving lower ECE without needing a held-out validation set for grid search. The largest absolute gains appear in heavy-tailed settings, where OKO improves over the best baseline by 4.25% (MNIST), 2.57% (FashionMNIST), 0.23% (CIFAR-10), and 0.17% (CIFAR-100). For calibration specifically, OKO achieves the lowest ECE in all settings except CIFAR-100 heavy-tailed, where BB+LS is marginally better; on the novel relative cross-entropy (RC) metric, OKO attains the lowest MAE in all balanced settings and is top-2 or top-3 in heavy-tailed settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

OKO repurposes the odd-one-out task from cognitive sciences (where subjects identify the dissimilar item in a set) as a training objective for neural network classifiers, shifting the learning paradigm from single-example empirical risk minimization to set-based cross-entropy. It departs from the dominant calibration literature—which treats calibration as an orthogonal post-hoc problem solved via temperature scaling, label smoothing, or isotonic regression—by arguing that calibration is inherently a set-level property and should be optimized during training. The method is also positioned against class-imbalance techniques (focal loss, batch-balancing, reweighting) that improve accuracy for rare classes but ignore or worsen calibration for minority instances.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OKO offers a drop-in training replacement for standard cross-entropy that simultaneously improves accuracy and calibration without additional hyperparameters, architecture changes, or post-hoc tuning—addressing a long-standing tension in the field. Its particular strength in low-data and heavy-tailed class distribution settings makes it relevant for domains like medicine and chemistry where data is scarce and imbalanced. Key limitations include treating all classes as semantically equally distant (like standard cross-entropy) and being restricted to supervised learning; extending OKO to self-supervised settings or incorporating class similarity structure are natural open questions.
