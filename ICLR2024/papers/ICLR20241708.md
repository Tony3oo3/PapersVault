## Problem

GAN-based image-to-image translation models degrade significantly under noisy or distorted inputs, yet the theoretical justification for the widely used practice of injecting Gaussian noise during training has remained unclear. This paper addresses three open questions: how training noise variance governs the divergence between real and generated distributions, whether robustness to Gaussian noise generalizes to unseen noise types, and how to select an optimal training noise intensity for consistent performance across diverse inference noise levels.

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy source-domain RGB image (corrupted by any of Gaussian, Uniform, Color, Laplacian, or Salt & Pepper noise at six intensity levels, or signal-dependent corruptions like blur and JPEG compression) and produces a translated target-domain image. Crucially, Gaussian noise is injected only during training (added to clean source images before feeding the generator), so inference operates on the raw noisy input with no additional denoising step, preserving the original model's inference speed. The approach is applied across paired (Photo→Sketch at 338 test photos), unpaired (Cat→Dog with 5000 synthesized images), and face super-resolution (32×→512×) settings.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretical framework connecting f-divergence and score matching: Theorem 1 shows that the rate of change of f-divergence between noise-perturbed real and generated joint distributions with respect to noise variance equals the negative weighted mean square error between their score functions, implying that aligning noisy distributions during training also aligns the underlying clean distributions. For the practical method, isotropic Gaussian noise N(0, σ_t²I_d) is simply added to each clean source image during training while the generator and all other training procedures remain unchanged. The paper further proves (Theorem 2, 3) that for arbitrary signal sources, robustness to Gaussian noise implies resilience to any other noise type sharing the same covariance matrix, and derives (Corollary 1) that the optimal training variance minimizing average KL-divergence over a uniform noise-intensity range [0, λ_max] is λ_max/2. Experiments validate these results on HiFaceGAN, GP-UNIT, and Sketch Transformer, showing substantial FID/KID/LPIPS improvements over clean-trained baselines across all noise types and intensities.

## Results

On the Cat→Dog task (GP-UNIT), the noise-injected model (σ_t²=0.04) achieves FID 16.47 on clean inputs (vs. 17.82 baseline) and maintains FID ≈16.1–16.3 even at the highest noise intensity (S6), while the baseline degrades to FID 30.95–47.26 depending on noise type. On the Photo→Sketch task (Sketch Transformer), the noise-injected model trades a small clean-input cost (FID 64.12 vs. 31.49) for dramatic robustness gains: at S6 Gaussian noise, FID drops from 404.01 to 64.87 (6.2× improvement), and LPIPS improves from 0.6525 to 0.4043. Across all five noise types (Gaussian, Uniform, Color, Laplacian, Salt & Pepper) and six intensity levels, the noise-injected model consistently outperforms the clean-trained baseline on noisy inputs for both tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GP-UNIT (Cat→Dog, unpaired), the noise-injected model wins on every single noise type and intensity level, including clean inputs (FID 16.47 vs. 17.82; KID 5.35 vs. 7.08), with the gap widening dramatically at high noise (e.g., Color S6: FID 16.19 vs. 47.26, a 3× improvement). Against Sketch Transformer (Photo→Sketch, paired), the noise-injected model incurs a modest clean-input penalty (FID 64.12 vs. 31.49; LPIPS 0.3601 vs. 0.3055) but dominates all noisy settings by large margins (e.g., Gaussian S6: FID 64.87 vs. 404.01; Salt & Pepper S6: FID 74.25 vs. 443.48). Against HiFaceGAN (Face Super-Resolution on FFHQ), the noise-injected variant similarly outperforms the clean-trained baseline on all noisy inputs while maintaining competitive clean performance. Against DiffuseIT (a diffusion-based I2I method), the noise-injected GP-UNIT exhibits superior robustness under colored Gaussian noise, where DiffuseIT's performance drops substantially despite its reliance on isotropic Gaussian denoising. Overall, the noise-injection approach is a drop-in training modification that consistently improves robustness without architectural changes, with the only trade-off being a small quality cost on clean inputs for paired (supervised) tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical framework for Gaussian noise injection in GAN-based I2I translation, extending information-theoretic results on the KL/Fisher divergence relationship (Verdú 2010; Lyu 2012; Kong et al. 2023) from marginal to joint distributions via f-divergence and score matching. Prior empirical work (Chrysos et al. 2020 RoCGAN; Wang et al. 2021; Jia et al. 2021) demonstrated that noise injection improves I2I robustness but lacked theoretical grounding, while adversarial-robustness literature (Cohen et al. 2019; Madry et al. 2017) addressed noise for classification rather than image generation. The paper also positions itself against diffusion-based I2I models (Kwon & Ye 2022; Saharia et al. 2022) that inherently use Gaussian noise in their forward process but still exhibit vulnerability to noisy inputs, arguing that explicit training-time injection in GANs offers a lighter-weight alternative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that a single, architecture-agnostic training modification (adding isotropic Gaussian noise to source images) yields substantial robustness gains across diverse I2I models and noise types without increasing inference cost, and Corollary 1 gives a closed-form recipe (σ²_t = λ_max/2) for choosing the training noise level. The theoretical result that robustness to Gaussian noise implies robustness to any noise with the same covariance matrix (Theorem 2, Part 1) is a clean generalization guarantee that practitioners can rely on without retraining per noise type. Limitations include the Gaussian-source assumption in the main theorems (though experiments confirm non-Gaussian generalization), the gap between KL-divergence theory and practical FID/KID metrics, and the restriction to i.i.d. noise in the optimal-variance analysis, leaving structured or signal-dependent corruptions (blur, JPEG) less theoretically characterized.
