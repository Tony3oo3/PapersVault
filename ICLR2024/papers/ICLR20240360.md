## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks either target tasks that are harder for humans (which frontier models are rapidly solving) or operate in closed, synthetic environments that test specific API familiarity rather than general competence. The paper argues that a more meaningful milestone for AI progress is a system that matches average human robustness on conceptually simple but multi-step real-world tasks, and that current evaluation infrastructure cannot measure this because it relies on multiple-choice formats, model-based judges, or static data prone to contamination.

## Input / Output

Each GAIA question is a natural-language prompt, optionally accompanied by an attached file (image, PDF, spreadsheet, audio, or video), and must be answered zero-shot. The expected output is a single unambiguous factoid: a short string, a number, or a comma-separated list of strings/floats, enabling automated quasi-exact-match scoring without free-form generation. Answers are deliberately designed to be absent from plain text in pre-training corpora, so correct retrieval requires executing a sequence of real-world actions rather than pattern matching.

## Method

GAIA is a 466-question benchmark constructed through a human-in-the-loop curation pipeline: an initial annotator designs a question grounded in a verifiable source of truth (a specific web page, attached document, or self-contained puzzle), then two independent annotators must reproduce the same answer to validate unambiguity (68% of drafted questions survive this filter). Questions are stratified into three difficulty levels by the number of reasoning steps and distinct tools required (≤5 steps/1 tool, 5–10 steps/multiple tools, and arbitrarily long multi-tool sequences). The core design principle is a proof-of-work analogy: the task is conceptually trivial for a human (92% success rate) yet demands accurate execution of a combinatorial sequence of web browsing, multi-modality parsing, coding, and file-reading operations that current LLMs cannot reliably chain. Evaluation is fully automated via normalized exact match on the final answer, and the paper reports scores for GPT-4, GPT-4 + manually selected plugins, AutoGPT, a web-search baseline, and human annotators across all three levels.

## Results

On GAIA's 466 questions across three difficulty levels, human annotators achieve 93.9%, 91.8%, and 87.3% on Levels 1, 2, and 3 respectively (92% overall). The best AI system, GPT-4 with manually selected plugins (an oracle setting), reaches only 30.3% on Level 1, 9.7% on Level 2, and 0% on Level 3. GPT-4 without plugins scores 9.1% (Level 1), 2.6% (Level 2), and 0% (Level 3), while GPT-4 Turbo improves slightly to 13.0% and 5.5% on Levels 1 and 2. A simple search engine baseline achieves just 7.4% on Level 1 and 0% on Levels 2–3, confirming that the answers are not trivially findable on the open web.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 without plugins (OpenAI, 2023) is the primary LLM baseline, scoring 9.1% on Level 1, 2.6% on Level 2, and 0% on Level 3, with answer times under 0.2 minutes. AutoGPT with a GPT-4 backend (Yang et al., 2023) performs slightly better on Level 1 (14.4%) but collapses to 0.4% on Level 2 and 0% on Level 3, while being substantially slower (7.6 and 11.7 minutes vs. 0.19 and 0.15 minutes for raw GPT-4). The oracle GPT-4 + plugins setting, where plugins are manually chosen per question, is the strongest AI system tested, reaching 30.3% on Level 1 and 9.7% on Level 2, yet still achieving 0% on Level 3. Both AI baselines are dramatically outperformed by human annotators (93.9% / 91.8% / 87.3%), with the gap widening at higher difficulty levels. The paper notes that GPT-4 + plugins exhibits useful behaviors such as backtracking and query refinement, but the overall performance gap (≈60–90 percentage points) underscores that current tool-augmented LLMs remain far from general-assistant competence.

## Relation to Prior Work

GAIA departs from the dominant benchmarking paradigm of targeting tasks increasingly difficult for humans (MMLU, GSM8K, SuperGLUE) and instead draws on the 'Proof of Work' analogy from cryptography: tasks that are conceptually simple for humans but require accurate execution of complex multi-step action sequences. It extends prior assistant-evaluation work (AgentBench, ToolQA, Gentopia, OpenAGI) by replacing closed, API-specific environments with open real-world interactions (live web, diverse file types, multi-modality), and inherits its philosophical grounding from Chollet's 'On the Measure of Intelligence' argument for evaluating general abilities over specialized skills.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 92% (human) vs. 15% (GPT-4 + plugins) gap provides a concrete, interpretable milestone for AGI progress that is resistant to the saturation and contamination problems plaguing current benchmarks. Key limitations include English-only coverage, no evaluation of reasoning traces, potential temporal decay of web-based evidence, and reproducibility challenges with closed-source APIs. Open questions include how to extend the benchmark to non-English populations, how to evaluate intermediate reasoning steps, and whether solving GAIA truly constitutes a sufficient (vs. necessary) condition for general AI.
