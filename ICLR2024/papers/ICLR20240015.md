## Problem

Modern ML algorithms require massive training datasets to learn object segmentation, while newborn chicks solve the same task from a single object seen on a single background. No prior experimental platform allowed direct comparison of animal and machine learning because training data could not be matched across species and substrates. This paper introduces the Newborn Embodied Turing Test (NETT), a matched-experience benchmark where newborn animals and artificial agents are raised in identical environments and tested on identical tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240015/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The embodied agent receives 64×64 first-person RGB frames from a virtual camera mounted on its head as it navigates a Unity-rendered replica of a controlled-rearing chamber. During training, the visual world contains a single 3D object rotating 360° every 15 s on a single background; during testing, the familiar and novel objects are presented across 27 background–viewpoint combinations (3 backgrounds × 3 azimuth angles). The agent outputs three continuous action variables (head pitch, forward/backward translation, yaw rotation) at each timestep, and performance is scored as the proportion of time spent near the familiar object relative to the novel object over 1,000-step trials.

## Method

The core design is a digital twin: virtual chambers in Unity replicate the physical rearing chambers (proportions, textures, colors) used in prior chick experiments, and artificial agents with embodied bodies (3.5 units tall, 1.2 units radius) are trained with PPO using an imprinting reward proportional to the fraction of the agent's field of view occupied by the target object. After 1,000 training episodes, network weights are frozen to mimic the critical period of filial imprinting, and agents are evaluated on 1,080 object-recognition trials. The paper tests a battery of ML 'brains'—3-layer CNN, ResNet-10, ResNet-18, a Dynamic Visual Sensor (temporal-contrast retinal processing), LSTM recurrent policies, 10× longer training, and powerful pre-trained encoders (SAM, DINOv2, Ego4D, SimCLR/CLTT)—all of which learn background-dependent representations that fail to generalize to novel backgrounds, in stark contrast to the background-invariant representations exhibited by all 35 newborn chicks.

## Results

All 35 newborn chicks solved the one-shot object segmentation task, performing well above chance (50%) in all four background conditions (all P < 10⁻⁷, all Cohen's d > 1.2) and all three viewpoint conditions (all P < 10⁻¹⁰, all Cohen's d > 1.7), with mean imprinting at 84% (SD = 6%). In stark contrast, none of the artificial agents (small/medium/large CNN encoders, DVS, LSTM, 10× training, or pre-trained encoders like SAM, DINOv2, Ego4D) solved the task. All agents learned background-dependent representations, performing at chance (~50%) whenever both objects appeared on the same background type (conditions 3 and 4), revealing a fundamental inability to dissociate object from background features despite 100% object-background concurrence during training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between newborn chicks and a battery of artificial agents, with no single 'baseline' in the traditional ML sense. Chicks achieve background-invariant object recognition (well above 50% in all four conditions), while every tested agent configuration fails: small (3-layer CNN), medium (ResNet-10), and large (ResNet-18) encoders all plateau at ~50% when both objects share the same background, indicating they rely on background cues rather than object identity. Adding biologically inspired components—Dynamic Visual Sensor (DVS) retinal processing, LSTM recurrent policy networks, or 10× longer training (10,000 episodes)—does not close the gap. Even powerful pre-trained encoders (SAM with 86M params, DINOv2 with 21M params, Ego4D with 21M params) that excel on standard segmentation benchmarks still fail the one-shot task, performing at or near chance in the critical same-background conditions. The consistent failure pattern across all 10+ agent variants suggests the limitation is not in visual feature extraction but in the policy learning algorithm's inability to select object-relevant features over background features under sparse training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Newborn Embodied Turing Test (NETT) framework introduced by Pak et al. (2023) for view-invariant object recognition to the distinct task of one-shot object segmentation, using biological data from Wood & Wood (2021) controlled-rearing experiments with newborn chicks. Conceptually, it departs from standard ML benchmarks (ImageNet, COCO) by enforcing a 'matched-experience' requirement: both biological and artificial agents must be raised in identical environments and tested on identical tasks, isolating algorithmic differences from data differences. The digital twin design (Unity-based virtual replicas of physical rearing chambers) is the key methodological innovation that makes this matched-experience comparison feasible, building on prior work in controlled-rearing methodology and embodied RL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no tested ML algorithm (spanning small CNNs, ResNets, LSTMs, DVS sensors, 10× training, and powerful pre-trained encoders like SAM, DINOv2, and Ego4D) could solve the one-shot object segmentation task that all 35 newborn chicks solved—exposes a fundamental gap between statistical learning and biological visual development. The paper identifies the 'statistical concurrence problem' (100% object-background co-occurrence during training) as the core obstacle and points toward motion-based segmentation as a brain-inspired mechanism that could overcome it. Key limitations include the limited number of architectures tested, the single-objective (imprinting) reward structure, and lower-dimensional action spaces; open questions include whether any existing or future ML algorithm can pass this NETT and what specific brain-inspired inductive biases are necessary for one-shot object parsing.
