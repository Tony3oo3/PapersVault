## Problem

Existing angular synchronization methods (spectral, SDP, GPM, message-passing) degrade significantly under high noise levels, which are common in real applications like sensor network localization and cryo-EM. The problem is not directly amenable to standard neural network architectures due to the directed graph structure of pairwise measurements and the underlying SO(2) group structure, requiring a customized GNN architecture and loss function specifically designed for this task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241286/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a directed graph adjacency matrix A where nonzero entries encode noisy pairwise angular offsets (θ_i − θ_j) mod 2π (with at most one of A_{i,j} and A_{j,i} nonzero by construction), plus an input feature matrix X typically initialized from a baseline's angle estimates (e.g., Spectral_RN eigenvector). For k-synchronization, the input graph is the union of k edge-disjoint digraphs with unknown edge-to-group assignment. The output is n estimated angles in [0, 2π) (k=1) or k × n angle matrix (k>1), determined up to a global additive phase; evaluation uses a phase-corrected MSE that minimizes over all constant shifts and, for k>1, over all permutations of the k groups.

## Method

GNNSync is an end-to-end trainable directed GNN pipeline: (1) DIMPA (a 2-hop directed graph embedding network) produces node embeddings from the adjacency matrix and input features; (2) inner products with learnable vectors followed by sigmoid rescaling yield initial angle estimates; (3) Γ=5 projected gradient steps (inspired by GPM) refine angles by iteratively multiplying by a Hermitian matrix H built from exp(iA_{i,j}) and projecting back to the unit circle via elementwise angle extraction. Two novel loss functions drive training without ground truth: an upset loss (Frobenius norm of the angular residual matrix) and a cycle inconsistency loss that exploits 3-cycle consistency with a confidence matrix that downweights noisy edges, making it more robust than the upset loss in harder regimes. The cycle loss implicitly encourages small residuals only on reliable edges via reweighting, while the upset loss penalizes all edges equally. Theoretical guarantees include a directional stationarity result for the upset loss and a Lipschitz-type robustness bound showing initial angle estimates change linearly with input perturbations.

## Results

The primary evaluation metric is MSE with angle corrections (lower is better). On the U.S. map sensor network localization task, at noise level η=0.05, GNNSync achieves MSE of 0.010–0.014, while CEMP_MST degrades to 1.16–1.35 and TAS to 1.56–2.34, showing GNNSync is roughly 100× more accurate under moderate noise. Across synthetic experiments (n=360 nodes, edge density p∈{0.05,0.1,0.15}, noise η up to 0.9 for k=1 and 0.7 for k>1), GNNSync-cycle consistently attains the lowest MSE, with the advantage growing as noise increases, density decreases, or k increases. For k-synchronization (k∈{2,3,4}), GNNSync-cycle outperforms both Spectral and Spectral_RN baselines by a widening margin under harder settings, and remains above the Trivial baseline even when spectral methods fall below it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GNNSync is compared against seven baselines for k=1 (Spectral, Spectral_RN, GPM, TranSync, CEMP_GCW, CEMP_MST, TAS) and two for k>1 (Spectral, Spectral_RN from Cucuringu & Tyagi 2022). At zero noise on the U.S. map, all spectral/iterative baselines achieve near-zero MSE while GNNSync is slightly higher (0.002–0.010), reflecting a small training overhead. At η=0.05, GNNSync (0.010–0.014) is competitive with Spectral_RN and GPM (0.006) but dramatically outperforms CEMP_MST (1.16–1.35) and TAS (1.56–2.34), which collapse under noise. Across the synthetic experiments (Figures 3–4), GNNSync-cycle achieves the lowest MSE in the majority of settings, with the gap over Spectral_RN and GPM widening as noise η increases, edge density p decreases, or k grows from 2 to 4. Notably, at high noise levels where Spectral and Spectral_RN fall below the Trivial baseline (i.e., worse than predicting all angles equal), GNNSync-cycle still maintains meaningful accuracy, demonstrating substantially superior noise robustness. The ablation study confirms that GNNSync improves upon every baseline when using that baseline's output as input features, and that the projected gradient steps and end-to-end training are both beneficial.

## Relation to Prior Work

GNNSync recasts angular synchronization as a directed graph learning task, borrowing the projected gradient steps from the Generalized Power Method (Boumal, 2016) and the innerproduct-to-angle mapping from GNNRank (He et al., 2022a), but departs from both by using a directed GNN (DIMPA) for node embeddings and designing problem-specific loss functions (upset loss and cycle inconsistency loss) that encode SO(2) group structure and cycle consistency. Unlike Lerman & Shi (2022), which requires post-processing based on estimated corruption levels, GNNSync is trained end-to-end without ground-truth supervision. The architecture is explicitly not an unrolling of GPM, and the baselines serve as input node features rather than as initial guesses for proximal steps, distinguishing it from the GNNRank framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: existing angular synchronization methods degrade sharply under high noise, which is common in real applications like cryo-EM and sensor networks. The cycle inconsistency loss, which implicitly downweights noisy edges via a confidence matrix, provides a principled mechanism for robustness that outperforms the more direct upset loss in harder settings. Key limitations include restriction to SO(2) (future work targets Euc(2) and SO(3)), the need for a baseline's output as input features, and the fact that the theoretical robustness bound (Proposition 2) requires constraints on model parameters that are not automatically enforced. Open questions include whether the framework extends to other groups, how it interacts with low-rank matrix completion, and whether supervised (anchor-based) training further improves performance.
