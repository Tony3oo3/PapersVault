## Problem

Steering event flows (e.g., disease spread, traffic congestion) over large dynamic graphs by modifying network edges is a high-dimensional sequential decision problem: the action space is combinatorial (choosing which edges to cut at each step), different regions face distinct dynamics and local constraints, and existing approaches either solve each region independently without knowledge transfer or rely on event-level interventions that fail on high-frequency, uncontrollable data. The paper asks how to pool optimal intervention policies across diverse subgraph contexts and generalize to unseen regions while preserving the structural relationship between node orderings and action orderings.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a temporal network G_t = (V_t, E_t) with N fixed nodes, a spike count matrix X_t ∈ N^{N×t} recording per-node event counts at each discrete time step, and intervention constraints (total budget B and maximum edges K per step). The output is a k-hot adjacency matrix a^i ∈ {0,1}^{N×N} specifying which directed edges to remove at decision time i, subject to Σ a_mn·c_mn ≤ B and Σ a_mn ≤ K. The policy must satisfy a permutation equivalence property: permuting the node ordering in the state must induce a corresponding permutation of the action matrix rows and columns, rather than yielding an identical (invariant) action.

## Method

The ANI framework combines three modules: (1) a Networked Jumped ODE (NJODE) environment model that captures excitatory point process dynamics via continuous neural ODE drift plus discrete jumps driven by neighbors' event counts, parameterized with shared MLP/GRU networks and a Poisson emission layer; (2) H-step lookahead model-based RL where, instead of Monte Carlo rollouts through the stochastic dynamics, a deterministic mean-field approximation replaces sampled event counts with their expected intensities, yielding a single ODE solve per planning step with a proven error bound under Lipschitz and spectral-radius conditions; (3) an amortized policy learned via meta-training across subgraph tasks, where a bi-contrastive loss (Bi-CME) jointly learns positional and magnitude embeddings that are respectively invariant to node-order permutation and magnitude scaling, weighted by a learned Permutation Equivalent Metric (PEM) computed via approximate dynamic programming. The large graph is divided into subgraphs (divide-and-conquer), each solved by the H-step planner, and the shared amortized policy enables rapid adaptation to unseen regions with only a few gradient steps.

## Results

On the main COVID-19 generalization benchmark (Table 1), the full ANI framework (adaptive + amortized) achieves the highest reduced intensities across all four unseen county communities: 0.47 (Georgia-0), 0.71 (Alabama-0), 0.39 (Georgia-1), and 0.54 (West Virginia-0), substantially outperforming non-adaptive/non-amortized baselines which show near-zero or negative reductions. On synthetic Multivariate Hawkes Process data, ANI matches the intensity reduction of model-free SAC and PPO baselines without any direct environment interaction. On SUMO traffic simulations, the amortized meta-policy adapts to four unseen road intersections after only a few gradient steps, outperforming a train-from-scratch model in congestion mitigation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against model-free RL baselines SAC and PPO, ANI achieves comparable intensity reduction on synthetic MHP data (Fig. 3) while requiring no direct environment interaction; on the more complex 9-county COVID setting (Fig. 13), SAC and PPO struggle to produce positive intervention effects on most counties, whereas ANI successfully reduces infection counts in 6 of 9 counties. The model-based RL baseline NHPI (Qu et al., 2023), which performs event-level intervention rather than network-level intervention, faces significant difficulty reducing activity intensity, particularly on high-frequency event sequences. In the ablation (Table 2), replacing the proposed PEM + Bi-CMEs with PSM-based contrastive embeddings degrades reduced intensity by ~90% (from 0.54 to 0.05 on West Virginia), confirming the critical role of the permutation-equivalent inductive bias. Finally, in the model comparison (Appendix D, Fig. 10), the NJODE dynamics model achieves a log-likelihood of -33 versus -183 for a Neural Process baseline on synthetic MHP data, demonstrating superior likelihood fit for the event dynamics modeling component.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes several established threads: Neural ODEs and Neural Jump SDEs for modeling temporal point process dynamics, H-step lookahead model-based RL (akin to MPC) for sequential decision-making, and amortized/meta-learning for cross-task policy transfer. Its key conceptual departure is from prior point-process intervention work (Farajtabar et al., Upadhyay et al., Qu et al.), which focuses on event-level interventions (adding/removing events), by instead performing edge-level network interventions on dynamic graphs. Additionally, it distinguishes itself from the permutation-invariance literature in multi-agent RL (Tang & Ha, 2021; Liu et al., 2020) by introducing permutation *equivalence*—where the action matrix must be co-permuted with the state—rather than invariance, which is more appropriate for inhomogeneous networked systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practically critical problem (epidemic lockdown planning, traffic light optimization) at a scale where combinatorial action spaces make naive RL intractable, and the divide-and-conquer plus amortized policy design offers a principled path to scalability. The analytical mean-field approximation with a provable error bound (Theorem 1) is a useful theoretical contribution that avoids expensive Monte Carlo rollouts during planning. However, the authors acknowledge that the receding-horizon approximation may degrade under long-range temporal correlations, and the method's reliance on Lipschitz constants below 1 for the error bound limits its applicability to strongly coupled systems. Open questions include whether the permutation-equivalent inductive bias generalizes beyond the tested county-level and intersection-level scales, and how the approach performs when the influence matrix itself is partially unknown or time-varying in a non-stationary way.
