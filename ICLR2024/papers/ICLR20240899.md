## Problem

The standard explanation for SAM's success—finding flatter minima that generalize better—is contested by conflicting empirical evidence. This paper sidesteps the flatness debate to identify an orthogonal, previously unexplored mechanism: SAM implicitly balances the learning of multiple redundant features, overcoming the simplicity bias that causes SGD to learn only the easiest predictive feature while neglecting harder ones. This feature-diversifying effect is particularly valuable for out-of-distribution and transfer learning settings where the relevant features are unknown at training time.

## Input / Output

The method operates on RGB images (CelebA at 64×64, Waterbirds at 96×96, CIFAR-MNIST, FMNIST-MNIST) or 4D input vectors in the toy setting, producing class labels via a linear classifier on top of a learned feature representation. The key evaluation output is not just classification accuracy but the quality of individual feature representations, measured by training linear probes on balanced distributions where the target feature is uncorrelated with other features. In the toy setting, a deliberately disentangled architecture separates easy (linear) and hard (spiral) feature representations in the last layer, enabling direct probing without a learned probe.

## Method

The paper introduces LSAM (Last-layer SAM), a simplified variant that perturbs only the final classification weights to construct the phantom parameter, enabling clean decomposition of SAM's gradient update into two interpretable terms: an importance weighting term (per-example) and a sum of feature gradients term (per-feature). The first mechanism is an importance weighting effect where the phantom parameter re-weights training examples more uniformly, specifically up-weighting points that are well-fit by the easy feature but poorly-fit by the hard feature—analogous to GroupDRO or Just Train Twice but induced implicitly. The second mechanism is a learning rate scaling effect where the phantom parameter suppresses the effective learning rate of well-learned features and amplifies it for harder features, acting globally across the batch. The authors verify both effects causally in a controlled toy setting with a disentangled architecture (showing each effect individually and in combination accounts for all of LSAM's gains) and empirically in real datasets (CelebA, Waterbirds) using Lorenz curves of importance weights and binned median importance plots, confirming that SAM improves feature probing error for both easy and hard features across all tested datasets.

## Results

SAM consistently outperforms SGD on feature probing error across all four datasets. On the hard feature, SAM reduces probing error from 20.9% to 15.1% on CelebA, from 21.8% to 19.7% on Waterbirds, from 12.7% to 10.2% on CIFAR-MNIST, and from 11.6% to 10.5% on FMNIST-MNIST. SAM also improves in-distribution test error (e.g., 4.3% vs 4.7% on CelebA, 0.0% vs 0.1% on CIFAR-MNIST) while simultaneously improving the easy feature probe (e.g., 8.7% vs 10.9% on CelebA). On DomainBed domain transfer, SAM outperforms SGD on all three benchmarks (OfficeHome, PACS, VLCS) with last-layer retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between SAM and standard SGD, both trained with identical architectures and hyperparameter tuning. SAM wins on every dataset for both the easy and hard feature probing error, with the largest gains on the hard feature (e.g., 5.8 percentage points on CelebA, 2.5 points on CIFAR-MNIST). SAM also achieves slightly lower in-distribution test error on all four datasets, confirming that the feature-diversifying effect does not come at the cost of in-distribution performance. On DomainBed domain transfer (OfficeHome, PACS, VLCS), SAM outperforms SGD on all 12 domain-transfer splits when using last-layer retraining, with gains of 0.5–2.8 percentage points. The paper does not compare against other explicit feature-diversity methods (e.g., GroupDRO, Just Train Twice) in the main experiments, but notes that SAM achieves similar re-weighting effects implicitly through its update rule without requiring group labels or explicit re-weighting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant flatness-based explanation of SAM's success (Foret et al., 2020) and the growing body of work questioning the flatness-generalization link (Dinh et al., 2017; Kaddour et al., 2022; Andriushchenko et al., 2023b). It is complementary to prior theoretical analyses of SAM's implicit biases in simple settings (Andriushchenko & Flammarion, 2022; Behdin & Mazumder, 2023), but focuses specifically on out-of-distribution feature quality rather than in-distribution generalization. The identified importance-weighting mechanism is conceptually analogous to explicit re-weighting methods like GroupDRO (Sagawa et al., 2019) and Just Train Twice (Liu et al., 2021), but arises implicitly from SAM's phantom parameter construction rather than being explicitly designed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic, interpretable account of why SAM improves feature diversity—two specific effects (uniform re-weighting of examples and suppression of well-learned features' effective learning rates) that together fully explain the toy-setting gains. This opens a path toward designing new algorithms that explicitly target these balancing effects without SAM's computational overhead. A key limitation is that the learning-rate scaling effect cannot be directly verified without a disentangled architecture, leaving its contribution in realistic settings somewhat speculative. Open questions include whether these effects persist at scale (larger networks, ImageNet-level data) and whether the identified mechanisms transfer to other sharpness-based optimizers.
