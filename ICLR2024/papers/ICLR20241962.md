## Problem

Whether the inference logic of a well-trained DNN can be faithfully explained as a small set of symbolic concepts (sparse interaction primitives) has been empirically observed but never rigorously proven. Prior work demonstrated that DNNs encode only a few salient Harsanyi interactions, yet no theoretical conditions were established under which this sparsity is guaranteed, leaving the symbolic-interpretability claim without formal grounding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241962/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a trained DNN v, an input sample x with n variables (image patches, words, point-cloud semantic parts, or tabular features), and a baseline vector b for masking. It computes Harsanyi interaction effects I(S) for all 2^n subsets via the inclusion-exclusion formula, then identifies salient interactions above a threshold τ (typically 0.05·max|I(S')|). The key output is a provable upper bound R^(k) on the number of valid k-order interactions, shown to be O(n^(p+δ)/|τη^(k)|) rather than the combinatorial C(n,k), demonstrating that the network output v(x) ≈ Σ_{S∈Ω_salient} I(S) + bias with certifiably few terms.

## Method

The proof proceeds by expressing the network output via Taylor expansion at the baseline point and showing that interactions of order > M vanish under Assumption 1-α (no high-order derivatives). Under Assumption 2 (monotonicity: average output increases with unmasked set size) and Assumption 3 (polynomial lower bound on confidence for occluded samples), Lemma 2 relates the average m-order output ū^(m) to the sum of k-order interaction effects A^(k) via binomial coefficients. Theorem 2 then shows A^(k) = O(n^(p+δ))·ū^(1) by bounding the exponent δ through the monotonicity and polynomial-decay constraints. Theorem 3 converts this into a count bound R^(k) ≤ ū^(1)/(τη^(k)) · |λ^(k)n^(p+δ) + lower-order terms|, which is far below C(n,k) when positive and negative interactions do not fully cancel. The proof is architecture-agnostic and validated empirically on LLMs (OPT-1.3B, LLaMA-7B, Aquila-7B on SQuAD) and MLPs on tabular data, where real valid interaction counts (~30–50) fall well below the derived bounds (~200–260) out of 1024 total subsets.

## Results

The paper proves that under three common conditions (bounded-order derivatives, monotonicity of average output with unmasked variables, and polynomial lower bound on confidence), a DNN encodes only a sparse set of Harsanyi interactions. Empirically, on LLMs (OPT-1.3B, LLaMA-7B, Aquila-7B) with n=10 input variables, the real number of valid interactions is only 28.73–50.53, far below the total of 2^10 = 1024 possible interactions. The derived theoretical upper bound (184–293) is loose but still polynomial in n, confirming sparsity. Over 84% of samples satisfy the monotonicity assumption required by the proof.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical proof paper rather than an empirical benchmarking study, so it does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense. The closest analogue to a baseline comparison is Table 2, which contrasts the empirically observed number of valid (salient) interactions against the polynomial upper bound derived in Theorem 3. In all four settings, the real number of valid interactions (≈30–55) is roughly 4–6× smaller than the theoretical upper bound (≈184–293), demonstrating that the bound is valid but conservative. The paper also notes that the remaining 10–15% of samples that violate the monotonicity assumption still exhibit sparse interactions (42–80 valid interactions), suggesting the proof covers the dominant case but is not exhaustive. No competing sparsity-proving framework is benchmarked, as this is presented as the first theoretical proof of interaction sparsity in DNNs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first theoretical proof of why Harsanyi interactions encoded by DNNs are sparse, building directly on the empirical discoveries of Ren et al. (2023a, CVPR) and Li & Zhang (2023b, ICML) who observed but did not explain this sparsity. The proof leverages the Harsanyi dividend framework (Harsanyi, 1963) and connects it to the broader game-theoretic attribution literature by showing that Shapley values, Shapley interaction indices, and Shapley Taylor interaction indices are all decomposable into Harsanyi interaction effects (Theorems 4–6). The key departure from prior work is shifting the explanation of sparsity from network architecture or parameter sparsity to the *task property* of requiring smooth inference on occluded samples, using Taylor expansion and combinatorial arguments to derive upper bounds on the number of valid interactions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a theoretical foundation for the long-standing question of whether DNNs truly encode symbolic concepts, moving the field from empirical observation to conditional proof. The architecture-agnostic nature of the proof suggests interaction sparsity is a universal phenomenon across network types and tasks, which could unify the interpretability literature. However, significant limitations remain: the monotonicity assumption fails on 10–15% of samples, the derived upper bounds are loose (real valid interactions ~30–50 vs. bounds ~200–260), and the proof does not cover scenarios with noise, periodic outputs, or tasks requiring all input variables. Future work on tighter bounds and precise characterization of when sparsity breaks down would strengthen the theoretical framework.
