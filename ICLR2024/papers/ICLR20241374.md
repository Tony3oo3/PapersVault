## Problem

Dynamic feature selection requires estimating conditional mutual information (CMI) to decide which feature to acquire next, but existing approaches either rely on generative models that are difficult to train and slow at inference, or bypass CMI estimation entirely by directly predicting the argmax feature index. The latter loses the ability to support variable per-sample budgets, non-uniform feature costs, and confidence-based stopping criteria, all of which require access to the CMI value itself rather than just its argmax.

## Input / Output

The method consumes arbitrary subsets of features via zero-masking: for tabular data this is a partial feature vector (e.g., 112 features for Intubation, 784 pixels for MNIST), and for images it is a subset of 196 non-overlapping 16×16 patches from a 224×224 image. An optional prior information channel (e.g., a Canny edge image for histopathology) is processed by a separate backbone and concatenated with the masked-feature embedding. At each step the model outputs a CMI estimate for every unobserved feature (or patch group), a class prediction from the predictor network, and the index of the next feature to acquire; the process terminates when a stopping criterion (budget, confidence threshold, or penalty) is met.

## Method

DIME jointly trains two networks with a shared backbone: a predictor f(x_S; θ) that classifies given any feature subset, and a value network v(x_S; φ) that scores each candidate feature. The predictor is trained with standard cross-entropy loss over randomly sampled feature subsets, while the value network is trained with a simple regression objective whose target is the incremental loss improvement Δ = ℓ(f(x_S), y) − ℓ(f(x_S ∪ i), y) after revealing one additional feature. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that at optimality the value network recovers the exact CMI I(y; x_i | x_S), because the expected loss improvement under the Bayes classifier equals the CMI (Lemma 1). This discriminative formulation eliminates the need for generative models or explicit distributional assumptions, and because the CMI value is available at inference, the same trained model supports flexible stopping criteria (fixed budget, confidence threshold, or a penalty parameter λ), non-uniform feature costs via the ratio I/c_i, and incorporation of prior information by conditioning both networks on an auxiliary variable z.

## Results

On MNIST, DIME achieves above 90% top-1 accuracy using only ~10 out of 784 features (1.27%). On Imagenette, DIME reaches nearly 97% top-1 accuracy with just ~15 out of 196 image patches (7.7%). For the two medical diagnosis tasks (Intubation, ROSMAP), DIME with the penalized stopping criterion achieves the best AUROC across all feature budgets, outperforming all dynamic and static baselines. On image datasets, ViT-based DIME significantly outperforms ResNet-based DIME, and the penalized stopping criterion consistently beats the budget-constrained variant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIME outperforms all nine baselines across every dataset tested. Among dynamic methods, Argmax Direct (Chattopadhyay 2023; Covert 2023) is the strongest competitor but is consistently outperformed by DIME on both tabular and image datasets; the gap is largest at moderate feature budgets. The generative baseline EDDI (Ma 2019) 'usually does not perform well' and is substantially outperformed, while Hard Attention (Rangrej 2021) shows a 'larger drop in performance' on image data compared to DIME. Among static methods, CAE (Balin 2019) is competitive and outperforms mRMR (Peng 2005) and CMICOT (Shishkin 2016), but DIME still surpasses CAE. The RL baselines CwCF (Janisch 2020) and OL (Kachuee 2018) are 'not as competitive' as expected, consistent with prior findings that RL struggles with DFS training difficulties. On non-uniform cost settings, DIME outperforms EDDI and CwCF 'by a substantial margin,' and using true costs during both training and inference further improves performance over uniform-cost ablations.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIME sits at the intersection of two prior lines of work on dynamic feature selection: generative CMI estimation (EDDI, Hard Attention) that models the data distribution to compute mutual information, and discriminative methods (Chattopadhyay et al. 2023; Covert et al. 2023) that directly predict the argmax feature index. The key departure is estimating the CMI value itself—rather than just its argmax—through a simple regression objective on incremental loss improvement, which the authors prove recovers the exact CMI at optimality. This bridges the gap between the two approaches: it retains the training simplicity of discriminative methods while unlocking capabilities (variable budgets, non-uniform costs, confidence-based stopping) that previously required either generative models or RL formulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By recovering actual CMI values, DIME enables a single trained model to be evaluated under multiple stopping criteria (budget, confidence, penalty) without retraining, which is practically valuable in settings like medical diagnosis where feature costs are heterogeneous and budgets vary per patient. The clean theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) provides a principled foundation that prior discriminative methods lacked. Open questions include whether the greedy CMI policy remains near-optimal in highly non-submodular regimes, how the approach scales to very high-dimensional or continuous feature spaces, and whether the training procedure (which requires executing the policy during training) can be made more sample-efficient. The paper also notes that predictor suboptimality introduces a KL-divergence-dependent bias in CMI estimates, a practical limitation that could matter in low-data regimes.
