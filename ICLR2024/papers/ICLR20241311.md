## Problem

Current CLIP zero-shot classification methods treat image classification as a simple text-retrieval task using fixed or ad-hoc prompt templates, overlooking CLIP's demonstrated ability to understand class-independent contextual attributes such as background, orientation, and illumination. This leads to sub-optimal generalization, reliance on spurious features (e.g., associating grass backgrounds with dogs), biased predictions, and poor interpretability. The paper asks whether emulating the hierarchical, context-dependent process of human visual perception—first inferring contextual attributes, then classifying conditioned on them—can systematically improve CLIP's zero-shot classification.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and a predefined set of contextual attributes (each with discrete values and multiple textual descriptions) as input, and outputs a predicted class label. No training data or fine-tuning is required; the method is entirely training-free and operates in a black-box setting using only CLIP's pre-trained image and text encoders. The contextual attribute set is dataset-specific and can be manually designed or semi-automatically discovered via GPT-4 from retrieved captions, with a temperature hyperparameter (τ) controlling how much trust is placed in CLIP's self-inferred attribute estimates.

## Method

PerceptionCLIP is a training-free, two-step zero-shot classification method that emulates human visual perception. In Step 1, it infers the distribution of contextual attributes given the image by computing p(z|x) using an attribute-aware CLIP score that concatenates class and attribute descriptions in the text prompt (e.g., 'a photo of a dog, on the grass, upright'), with a temperature τ smoothing the estimate to account for CLIP's imperfect attribute inference. In Step 2, it computes the class distribution conditioned on each attribute value via p(y|x,z) and marginalizes over the inferred attribute distribution to produce the final prediction. The key mechanism is the attribute-aware CLIP score, which behaves as an energy function—yielding high scores for correctly matched image-attribute pairs and low scores for mismatched ones—enabling both attribute inference and conditional classification. This two-step structure resembles chain-of-thought reasoning in language models, and the single-step simplification (τ=1, no intervention) reduces to a form of prompt ensembling over systematic attribute combinations rather than ad-hoc templates.

## Results

On ImageNet and its OOD variants (ViT-B/16), PerceptionCLIP with the top 4 contextual attributes achieves 68.71% on ImageNet, 62.32% on ImageNetV2, 78.38% on ImageNet-R, 51.39% on ImageNet-A, and 49.10% on ImageNet-Sketch, consistently outperforming both the single-template baseline and 80-template prompt ensembling. On domain-specific datasets, the method yields a near 5% accuracy gain on EuroSAT (59.23% vs. 54.94% domain template) and 1.9% on Flowers102 (72.86% vs. 70.99%). For group robustness, PerceptionCLIP reduces the worst-group accuracy gap by 19% on Waterbirds (45.91→26.62 with ViT-L/14) and by 7% on CelebA (9.61→2.20 with ViT-L/14), demonstrating reduced reliance on spurious features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline family is CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) in three configurations: a single template ('a photo of a {class name}'), 80-template prompt ensembling, and domain-specific templates. PerceptionCLIP with the top 4 contextual attributes outperforms 80-template prompt ensembling on all five ImageNet-related datasets, with the largest gains on ImageNet-A (+1.44 pp) and ImageNet-Sketch (+0.84 pp). Even a single well-chosen attribute (e.g., 'condition' at 68.39% on ImageNet) already surpasses the 80-template baseline (68.32%), and the top-2 attribute composition (68.52%) beats prompt ensembling across all five datasets. On domain-specific benchmarks (Table 5), PerceptionCLIP consistently improves over domain templates by 0.7–4.3 pp, with the largest gain on EuroSAT (+4.29 pp). For group robustness, conditioning on background attributes reduces the Waterbirds worst-group gap from 45.91 to 26.62 (ViT-L/14), and adding gender/age/race attributes reduces the CelebA gap from 9.61 to 2.20, confirming that the method mitigates spurious-feature reliance more effectively than the unconditioned CLIP baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's contrastive image-text pretraining and its zero-shot classification paradigm, but departs from the standard single-step retrieval formulation (single template or 80-template prompt ensembling) by introducing a structured two-step inference process inspired by hierarchical human visual perception (Kandel et al., 2013). Unlike prior descriptive-prompt work (Menon & Vondrick, 2022; Pratt et al., 2022) that augments prompts with class-specific descriptions, PerceptionCLIP targets class-independent contextual attributes (background, orientation, illumination) and shows that prompt ensembling's effectiveness can be explained as an implicit perception process. The two-step structure also draws a deliberate parallel to chain-of-thought reasoning in LLMs (Wei et al., 2022), though the authors note CLIP cannot perform step-wise reasoning natively and must be manually prompted through the process.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, training-free framework that explains why ad-hoc prompt ensembling works and offers a systematic alternative that consistently outperforms it across 11 datasets, while also reducing reliance on spurious features (19% gap reduction on Waterbirds). The demonstration that CLIP can self-infer contextual attributes at ~74% accuracy opens the door to more interpretable and robust zero-shot classification without any downstream data. Key limitations include sensitivity to text description phrasing, space complexity scaling with the number of attribute combinations, and the need for manual or semi-automated attribute construction. Open questions include whether this perception-emulation strategy can be integrated into pretraining or fine-tuning, and whether better text composition (beyond simple concatenation) could further improve performance.
