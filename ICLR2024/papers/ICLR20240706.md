## Problem

CGM-based glucose prediction methods are typically evaluated on small, private datasets with no standardized tasks or comparison protocols, impeding reproducibility and practical adoption. The field lacks a consolidated public benchmark that unifies research objectives, provides established baselines, and enables systematic evaluation of new methods across diverse patient populations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240706/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes CGM glucose sequences at 5-minute resolution (with optional static, dynamic known, and dynamic unknown covariates) and produces 1-hour-ahead glucose trajectory forecasts (12 time points) for the accuracy task, plus a full predictive distribution or quantile-based confidence intervals for the uncertainty task. Input sequences are pre-processed via linear interpolation for gaps below a dataset-specific threshold, segmented at larger gaps, min-max scaled using training-set statistics, and sampled as non-overlapping input-output pairs during training (sliding window at test time). Input length L is treated as a model-specific hyperparameter rather than fixed.

## Method

The core contribution is a standardized benchmarking protocol rather than a single architecture: five curated public CGM datasets (spanning Type 1, Type 2, and mixed populations with 5–208 subjects) are processed through a unified pipeline and evaluated on two tasks—predictive accuracy (median RMSE/MAE) and uncertainty quantification (log-likelihood and marginal calibration error). A diverse set of baselines is trained under identical conditions: shallow models (ARIMA, linear regression, XGBoost), deep models (Transformer, NHiTS, TFT, Gluformer), and a hybrid Latent ODE, all tuned via Optuna Bayesian optimization (50 iterations) and evaluated over 2 random data splits × 10 random seeds. The protocol explicitly separates in-distribution and out-of-distribution test sets (90/10 patient split) to measure generalization, and includes ablations on covariate inclusion, daytime vs. nighttime performance, and sensitivity to dataset size and patient composition.

## Results

On Task 1 (predictive accuracy), simple shallow models dominate: ARIMA achieves the best RMSE on Broll (10.53), Colas (5.80), and Weinstock (13.40), while Linear regression is best on Colas (RMSE 5.26, MAE 4.35) and Dubosson (RMSE 12.07, MAE 9.97). Deep models win only on the two largest datasets: Latent ODE is best on Hall (RMSE 7.13) and Transformer is best on Weinstock (RMSE 13.22). On Task 2 (uncertainty quantification), Gluformer achieves the best log-likelihood on all five datasets (ranging from -1.07 on Colas to -2.50 on Weinstock), while TFT achieves the best calibration on the two largest datasets (Colas: 0.07, Weinstock: 0.07).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARIMA (Hyndman & Khandakar, 2008) and Linear regression are the strongest accuracy baselines, with ARIMA winning RMSE on 3 of 5 datasets and Linear winning on 2; both outperform all deep learning models on the smaller datasets (Broll, Dubosson). Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) is the only deep model to achieve the best accuracy on any dataset (Weinstock, RMSE 13.22 vs. ARIMA's 13.40), but it underperforms ARIMA by 2.6–4.6 RMSE on the three smaller datasets. Latent ODE (Rubanova et al., 2019) is best on Hall (RMSE 7.13) but degrades severely on Dubosson (RMSE 20.14 vs. Linear's 12.07). NHiTS (Challu et al., 2023) and XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016) never achieve the best accuracy on any dataset, with NHiTS showing particularly poor performance on Dubosson (RMSE 17.45). For uncertainty quantification, Gluformer (Sergazinov et al., 2023) dominates log-likelihood across all five datasets, while TFT (Lim et al., 2021) achieves the best calibration on the two largest datasets (Colas and Weinstock, both 0.07) but is worst on Dubosson (0.23). Overall, the key finding is that model complexity must match dataset size and patient population complexity: shallow models are preferred for small datasets or less variable populations, while deep models are needed for large, heterogeneous Type 1 diabetes cohorts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a response to the reproducibility crisis in CGM-based glucose prediction, where 38 of 45 identified methods lack public code and prior benchmark studies (Mirshekarian et al., 2019; Xie & Wang, 2020) evaluated models on a single 5-patient dataset without releasing source code. Rather than proposing a new prediction architecture, it repurposes a diverse set of existing time-series models—spanning statistical (ARIMA, linear regression), gradient-boosting (XGBoost), deep learning (Transformer, NHiTS, TFT, Gluformer), and hybrid (Latent ODE)—as baselines under a unified evaluation protocol. The conceptual contribution is the consolidation of five heterogeneous public CGM datasets with a standardized two-task framework (predictive accuracy and uncertainty quantification) and systematic analysis of performance-influencing factors, filling a gap that no prior work addressed at this scope.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark provides the field with its first multi-dataset, multi-model, reproducible evaluation protocol for glucose prediction, directly addressing the fragmentation that has limited clinical adoption of more sophisticated models. Key open questions remain: Type 2 diabetes is underrepresented in public CGM data, only one dataset (Dubosson) records dynamic physiological covariates (heart rate, insulin, blood pressure), and the finding that covariates often degrade performance on smaller datasets raises fundamental questions about data quality and model capacity. The paper also highlights the cold-start problem—deep learning models generalize better to unseen patients but still show significant OD degradation—pointing toward future work on pre-training, meta-learning, and patient-specific fine-tuning as practical paths forward.
