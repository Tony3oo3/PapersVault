## Problem

Class-incremental learning (CIL) requires a model to discriminate among all classes encountered over a sequence of tasks, but existing discriminative approaches suffer from catastrophic forgetting and score imbalances between classes never seen together during training. Generative classification offers a rehearsal-free alternative by scoring classes via class-conditional likelihoods, but learning accurate generative models is difficult, especially with limited single-pass data. The paper asks whether the full generative modeling objective is necessary, or whether simpler class-specific modules can produce equally effective classification scores.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw RGB or grayscale images (28×28 for MNIST, 32×32 for SVHN/CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100, 84×84 for miniImageNet) and outputs a single class label. At inference, each input is passed through all class-specific student networks and one shared frozen random teacher network; the predicted class is the one whose student most closely matches the teacher's output (smallest L2 prediction error). The method operates with batch size 1 and requires no replay buffer, making it suitable for streaming and single-pass-through-data settings.

## Method

PEC assigns a dedicated shallow student network (one hidden conv or linear layer + ReLU/GELU + linear output) to each class, trained exclusively on that class's data to replicate the outputs of a single frozen random teacher network with a wider hidden layer. At test time, the squared L2 prediction error between each student and the teacher on a given input serves as the class score, and the class with the minimum error is selected. The teacher's wide random initialization approximates a Gaussian Process sample, giving PEC a theoretical interpretation as an approximation of a GP posterior-variance classification rule. Because each class has its own frozen-after-training module, forgetting is structurally eliminated, and the method introduces no method-specific hyperparameters beyond learning rate and architecture width.

## Results

In the single-pass-through-data CIL setting with one class per task (the most challenging regime), PEC achieves 92.31% on MNIST, 68.70% on Balanced SVHN, 58.94% on CIFAR-10, 26.54% on CIFAR-100, and 14.90% on miniImageNet (final average accuracy, mean over 10 seeds). PEC outperforms all rehearsal-free baselines in every setting and surpasses rehearsal-based methods with a 500-example buffer in most cases, achieving the highest score in 7 out of 10 evaluated configurations. In the multi-class-per-task setting, PEC maintains identical scores (since class-specific modules are unaffected by task grouping) while discriminative baselines degrade, further widening PEC's lead over rehearsal-free methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PEC outperforms all rehearsal-free baselines across every dataset and task split. Against the strongest rehearsal-free generative method VAE-GC, PEC gains +8.33 pts on MNIST (92.31 vs 83.98), +17.99 pts on Balanced SVHN (68.70 vs 50.71), +16.23 pts on CIFAR-10 (58.94 vs 42.71), +6.84 pts on CIFAR-100 (26.54 vs 19.70), and +2.80 pts on miniImageNet (14.90 vs 12.10). Against rehearsal-based methods with a 500-example buffer, PEC beats DER++ on CIFAR-10 (58.94 vs 35.61), CIFAR-100 (26.54 vs 6.17), and miniImageNet (14.90 vs 1.40), and X-DER on CIFAR-10 (58.94 vs 43.16), CIFAR-100 (26.54 vs 15.55), and miniImageNet (14.90 vs 8.22), though DER++ edges out PEC on Balanced SVHN (80.66 vs 68.70) and MNIST (91.88 vs 92.31 is a near-tie). In the multi-class-per-task setting (Table 2), PEC's advantage over rehearsal-free methods widens dramatically (e.g., 58.94 vs 40.40 for LwF+LT on CIFAR-10), while its lead over rehearsal-based methods narrows slightly as those methods benefit from seeing multiple classes per task. Regularization-based rehearsal-free methods (EWC+LT, SI+LT, LwF+LT) perform near chance level in the one-class-per-task setting, confirming that discriminative approaches fundamentally struggle when each task contains only a single class.

## Relation to Prior Work

PEC repurposes Random Network Distillation (Burda et al., 2018), originally designed for novelty detection in reinforcement learning, as a classification mechanism for class-incremental learning. The theoretical grounding draws on Ciosek et al. (2020), who reinterpreted RND prediction errors as approximations of Gaussian Process posterior variance, providing a principled justification for using prediction error as a class score. Conceptually, PEC carves out a third paradigm alongside discriminative and generative classification: rather than learning a shared classifier or per-class likelihood models, it trains per-class student networks to mimic a frozen random teacher, exploiting the generalization property of neural networks to implicitly define class boundaries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PEC offers a rehearsal-free, single-pass-through-data solution to CIL that is particularly strong in the one-class-per-task regime where all discriminative baselines collapse to near-chance accuracy. Its practical advantages—no method-specific hyperparameters, guaranteed absence of forgetting via module separation, and compatibility with batch size 1—make it attractive for streaming and memory-constrained settings. Key limitations include linear parameter growth with the number of classes, sensitivity to data imbalance (mitigated by equal-budget or score-rescaling strategies), and the complete separation of class modules preventing any information transfer. An open question the authors highlight is whether alternative teacher functions (pre-trained networks, perceptual hashes) could further improve performance beyond the random-network choice.
