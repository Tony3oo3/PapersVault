## Problem

Pruning large models for edge deployment can cause severe, uneven accuracy drops across demographic sub-groups even when aggregate performance is preserved. Existing mitigation methods either rely on indirect surrogate metrics (e.g., loss equalization) that lack interpretability, or compute per-group importance scores for every parameter, making them prohibitively expensive at scale. The paper asks whether one can directly constrain per-group accuracy changes between dense and sparse models in a way that is interpretable, scalable to hundreds of groups, and computationally negligible.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained dense model (MobileNet-V2, ResNet-34, or CifarResNet-56) that has already been sparsified via gradual magnitude pruning, along with RGB images (224×224 for face datasets, 32×32 for CIFAR-100) and per-sample group labels (race, gender, their intersection, or class identity). It produces a fine-tuned sparse model whose per-group accuracy degradation relative to the dense model is bounded by a user-specified tolerance ε, yielding a clear feasibility certificate: the model is admissible only if max_g ψ_g ≤ ε on the training set.

## Method

The paper introduces the Constrained Excess Accuracy Gaps (CEAG) formulation, which defines per-group excess accuracy gaps (EAGs) as the difference between a group's accuracy drop and the global accuracy drop between dense and sparse models, then imposes upper-bound constraints ψ_g ≤ ε on each group's EAG. Because accuracy is non-differentiable, the primal gradient uses a loss-based surrogate (excess negative loss gaps) while the dual update uses the true accuracy-based constraint, following the proxy-constraint technique of Cotter et al. (2019). The resulting min-max problem is solved with alternating gradient descent-ascent (Alt-GDA), and replay buffers storing the k most recent per-sample accuracy booleans per group reduce the high variance of mini-batch constraint estimates, enabling stable dual updates even with 100+ constraints. The total computational overhead is negligible relative to naive fine-tuning since each iteration requires only one forward and one backward pass, and the surrogate gradient is a weighted average of per-sample loss gradients computable by standard autograd.

## Results

On FairFace race prediction at 99% sparsity (ResNet-34), CEAG achieves a training max_g ψ_g of 1.8±0.4% and Ψ_PW of 3.5±0.6%, while maintaining 65.2% test accuracy comparable to all baselines. On UTKFace with intersectional groups (race∩gender) at 90% sparsity, CEAG attains a training max_g ψ_g of 1.0±0.3% and Ψ_PW of 2.4±0.6%, versus 10.0±0.7% and 11.5±0.7% for naive fine-tuning. On CIFAR-100 with 100 class-level groups at 92.5% sparsity, CEAG achieves the lowest training disparity (Ψ_PW = 2.4±0.2%, max_g ψ_g = 1.6±0.1%) while matching NFT's test accuracy of ~65%. All methods exhibit a significant generalization gap in disparity metrics between train and test sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the equalized loss formulation of Tran et al. (2022) (EL+RB), CEAG consistently achieves lower training disparity: on FairFace, CEAG's Ψ_PW is 3.5% vs. 8.8% for EL+RB, and on UTKFace intersectional groups at 90% sparsity, CEAG's max_g ψ_g is 1.0% vs. 2.4% for EL+RB. On test sets, CEAG also edges out EL+RB (e.g., 2.0% vs. 2.4% max_g ψ_g on FairFace; 2.3% vs. 3.0% on UTKFace 90%), though the gap narrows due to the generalization challenge shared by all methods. Against FairGRAPE (Lin et al., 2022), CEAG dramatically outperforms on disparity: on FairFace test, CEAG achieves max_g ψ_g = 2.0% and Ψ_PW = 4.3% versus FairGRAPE's 10.7% and 15.9%, while matching aggregate accuracy (~65%). The paper also notes that FairGRAPE's per-parameter importance computation is prohibitively expensive (>90 hours on A100 for a single UTKFace run), whereas CEAG adds negligible overhead (~2%) over naive fine-tuning. A key caveat is that all mitigation methods, including CEAG, fail to reliably reduce disparity on unseen test data, though CEAG is the only method that consistently satisfies its constraints on the training set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the proxy-constraint framework of Cotter et al. (2019) to handle non-differentiable accuracy-based constraints, and adapts replay buffers from reinforcement learning (Mnih et al., 2013) to reduce variance in stochastic constraint estimation. It departs from the equalized-loss formulation of Tran et al. (2022), which uses loss as a surrogate and ignores the dense model's per-group performance, by instead constraining excess accuracy gaps (EAGs) that directly relate sparse and dense model behavior. Unlike FairGRAPE (Lin et al., 2022), which computes per-group importance scores for every parameter and scales poorly, CEAG imposes per-group constraints solved via Lagrangian optimization with negligible overhead relative to standard fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CEAG provides an interpretable, accountable success criterion (tolerance ε directly bounds per-group accuracy degradation) and scales to hundreds of intersectional groups with minimal computational cost, making it practical for real-world deployment of pruned models. However, the paper's most important contribution may be its documentation that all disparity-mitigation methods—including CEAG—fail to generalize their training-set disparity guarantees to unseen data, a generalization gap the authors identify as the first systematic observation of this kind. This open problem significantly limits the practical impact of current mitigation techniques and motivates future work on generalization-aware disparity control.
