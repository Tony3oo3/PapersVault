## Problem

Geospatial ML predictions typically depend on expensive or limited covariates such as satellite imagery, yet it remains unclear whether the vast geospatial knowledge embedded in large language models can be effectively extracted for prediction tasks. Naively prompting LLMs with geographic coordinates alone proves ineffective, leaving open the question of how to unlock this knowledge in a way that is both accurate and sample-efficient across diverse global regions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241523/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes geographic coordinates (rounded to 5 decimal places) augmented with OpenStreetMap-derived context: a reverse-geocoded address (neighborhood through country level) and the 10 nearest named places within a 100 km radius, each with distance (km, 1 decimal) and one of eight cardinal/intercardinal directions. The output is a single classification token on a 0.0–9.9 scale (100 uniform bins), where the binning strategy adapts to the target variable's distribution—either direct scaling for near-uniform data or equal-frequency binning otherwise—making the LLM's softmax output interpretable as a continuous prediction.

## Method

GeoLLM constructs prompts from OpenStreetMap data (via Nominatim reverse geocoding and Overpass API for nearby places) and fine-tunes LLMs on these prompts to predict geospatial variables. The key insight is that providing spatial context—specifically the address and nearby place names—enables the model to anchor coordinates to real-world locations, yielding a 3.3× improvement in Pearson's r² over coordinate-only prompting. For Llama 2, fine-tuning uses QLoRA (33M trainable parameters, 4-bit quantization) with unsupervised next-token prediction over concatenated prompt-and-label sequences, which outperforms supervised fine-tuning. GPT-3.5 is fine-tuned via OpenAI's API with 2–4 epochs depending on sample size, while RoBERTa is fully fine-tuned as an encoder-only baseline. Ablations confirm that all prompt components contribute, but nearby places are most critical, and GPT-3.5 is notably more resilient to their removal than smaller models.

## Results

Across 19 task-sample-size combinations spanning four datasets (WorldPop, DHS, USCB, Zillow), GPT-3.5 achieves Pearson's r² ranging from 0.46 to 0.87, consistently outperforming all baselines. At 10,000 training samples on WorldPop population density, GPT-3.5 reaches r² = 0.78, compared to 0.73 (Llama 2), 0.60 (RoBERTa), 0.68 (Nightlight satellite imagery), and 0.36 (k-NN). The method demonstrates strong sample efficiency: with only 100 samples, GPT-3.5 still achieves r² = 0.61 on WorldPop while k-NN drops to 0.02 and RoBERTa/GPT-2 reach 0.00. On the Zillow home value task (1,000 samples), GPT-3.5 attains r² = 0.87, the highest single-task result in the paper.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-3.5 (GeoLLM) wins decisively against all four baseline families across every task and sample size. Against GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019), the gap is stark: on WorldPop population density at 10,000 samples, GPT-3.5 achieves r² = 0.78 versus GPT-2's 0.26, and at 100 samples GPT-2 collapses to 0.00 while GPT-3.5 retains 0.61. Against the MLP-BERT baseline (Devlin et al., 2019), GPT-3.5 leads by 0.23 r² on WorldPop (0.78 vs. 0.55) and by 0.41 on Zillow home value (0.87 vs. 0.46). Against XGBoost and XGBoost-FT (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), GPT-3.5 outperforms by 0.30 r² on WorldPop (0.78 vs. 0.48) and by 0.37 on Zillow (0.87 vs. 0.50). Against the VIIRS nightlight satellite baseline (Elvidge et al., 2017), GPT-3.5 leads on WorldPop (0.78 vs. 0.68) and by a wide margin on Zillow (0.87 vs. 0.14) and DHS tasks (e.g., 0.75 vs. 0.55 on asset wealth), though the nightlight baseline remains competitive on WorldPop at lower sample sizes (0.54 at 100 samples vs. GPT-3.5's 0.61). Overall, the paper reports a 70% average improvement in r² over the prompt-based baselines (k-NN, XGBoost, XGBoost-FT, MLP-BERT), with GPT-3.5 outperforming Llama 2 by 19% and RoBERTa by 51% in mean r².

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of knowledge extraction from LLMs (Petroni et al., 2019; Fichtel et al., 2021) and NLP-based geospatial prediction (Sheehan et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2017), but departs from both in a key way: rather than using LLMs as black-box predictors or mining specific text sources, it fine-tunes LLMs on prompts augmented with OpenStreetMap data to unlock geospatial knowledge already embedded in their weights. Recent concurrent work (GeoGPT, K2, Mai et al. 2023a) explored LLMs for geospatial tasks but relied on zero/few-shot prompting or domain-specific pretraining; GeoLLM shows that targeted fine-tuning with carefully constructed map-data prompts is both necessary and sufficient. The method is positioned as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, satellite-based covariates (Jean et al., 2016; Yeh et al., 2020), offering a free, globally available alternative where imagery is expensive or lacks predictive power.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GeoLLM demonstrates that LLMs are a rich, sample-efficient, and geographically consistent source of geospatial covariates, with GPT-3.5 matching or exceeding published satellite-imagery benchmarks (r²=0.72 vs. 0.56–0.71 for asset wealth in Africa) using only 10,000 training samples. The practical impact is significant: OpenStreetMap data is free and globally available, potentially democratizing geospatial prediction in regions where satellite access is limited. Open questions include the observed bias toward densely populated/developed areas, the role of temporal information, whether multimodal LLMs can fuse satellite and textual signals, and how performance scales with next-generation models like GPT-4 (few-shot results suggest substantial further gains).
