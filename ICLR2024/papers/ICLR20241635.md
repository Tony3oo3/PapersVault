## Problem

Existing chain-of-thought and tree-of-thought prompting approaches require human-annotated task-specific examples and discard ineffective reasoning steps, limiting their scalability across tasks. The paper asks whether LLMs can mimic human trial-and-error learning—analyzing their own errors to progressively improve reasoning—without any human annotation, starting from a simple prompt that initially produces weak thoughts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241635/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a minimal prompt containing only task instructions and the question (no few-shot examples), which is iteratively enriched with structured 'experience' entries—each comprising a full reasoning chain, per-step error reports, and specific revision advice generated by the LLM's self-evaluation. The output is a formatted reasoning chain (e.g., step-by-step arithmetic operations with current set, selected numbers, operation, and new set for Game of 24) followed by a final answer extracted via a prescribed format string such as 'The answer is: .'

## Method

BoT performs an experience-driven iteration with three stages per round: (1) generating M heterogeneous weighted binary tree thought structures in parallel using the current prompt, where each node carries both a thought evaluation score and an edge score reflecting LLM confidence; (2) aggregating the M trees into a single chain via best-first selection or greedy aggregation, the latter merging steps across different trees to form a potentially novel globally-optimal chain; and (3) having the LLM self-evaluate the aggregated chain to produce per-step error reports and revision advice. This feedback is appended to the prompt as accumulated experience, progressively refining subsequent thought generation so that weak initial thoughts are corrected over iterations. Key design choices—shallow binary trees instead of deep ToT structures, tree heterogeneity via varied growth strategies (level-wise vs. leaf-wise) and temperature settings, and greedy aggregation—ensure that even unreliable reasoning chains yield meaningful error analysis and that the prompt converges to correct reasoning within a few iterations.

## Results

Using GPT-4, BoT without human annotations achieves 97.1% on GSM8K and 81.4% on AQuA, surpassing the prior SOTA (97% and 79.9% respectively). With CoT examples added (BoT+CoT), the method reaches a new SOTA of 98.7% on GSM8K and 84.9% on AQuA. On the challenging Game of 24 task, BoT achieves 83.7% solving rate, outperforming ToT (74%) by 9.7 percentage points. However, BoT underperforms SOTA on the MATH dataset (62.5% vs. 84.3%), indicating that weaker LLMs struggle with the experience-driven iteration process.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BoT (without human annotation) outperforms the SOTA CSV method on GSM8K (97.1% vs. 97.0%) and AQuA (81.4% vs. 79.9%), while BoT+CoT extends these leads to 98.7% and 84.9% respectively. On Game of 24, BoT (83.7%) surpasses ToT (74%) by 9.7 points and CoT-SC (9%) by a wide margin, demonstrating the value of iterative experience accumulation over single-pass tree search. Against CoT (73.1% avg) and Complex-CoT (78.3% avg), BoT achieves substantially higher average solve rates (83.7% and 86.2%), and also outperforms PHP+Complex-CoT (80.3% avg). However, BoT trails SOTA by 21.8 points on MATH (62.5% vs. 84.3%), a gap the authors attribute to the difficulty of the dataset requiring more intricate multi-step reasoning that weaker self-evaluation cannot adequately capture. On SVAMP, BoT (92.7%) slightly underperforms SOTA (93.7%), while BoT+CoT (94.9%) exceeds it by 1.2 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

BoT sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) structured reasoning with LLMs (CoT, ToT, GoT), (2) self-refinement via LLM feedback (Self-Refine, PHP, REFINER), and (3) boosting-style iterative prompt improvement (Pitis et al., Zhang et al.). It departs from ToT by replacing one complex tree with many shallow weighted binary trees, and from prior feedback-based methods by explicitly retaining and accumulating error analyses of *failed* reasoning chains as 'experience' in the prompt rather than discarding them. The key conceptual shift is treating the prompt as a growing repository of trial-and-error lessons, analogous to how humans learn from mistakes, rather than as a static template with hand-crafted exemplars.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple, annotation-free prompt can be iteratively refined to approach or match SOTA on GSM8K and AQuA, suggesting that human-crafted CoT examples may be less necessary than previously assumed for moderate-difficulty reasoning. However, the method's heavy dependence on the LLM's self-evaluation quality (GPT-4 vs. Llama2 gap) and its 18–21% shortfall on the harder MATH dataset raise open questions about scalability to weaker models and more complex reasoning. Future work could explore whether the experience-accumulation mechanism transfers to non-mathematical domains, whether stronger evaluators (e.g., GPT-4 evaluating GPT-3.5 chains, as shown in Appendix F) can close the model-capability gap, and whether the prompt length grows unboundedly over many iterations.
