## Problem

Despite extensive work on OOD generalization under covariate shift, the fundamental question of which algorithms are most effective remained open even for well-specified parametric models. Shimodaira (2000) established asymptotic optimality of MLE among weighted likelihood estimators, but two critical questions persisted: whether MLE remains effective in finite-sample (non-asymptotic) regimes, and whether any algorithm beyond the class of weighted likelihood estimators could outperform it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n i.i.d. labeled source samples (x_i, y_i) drawn from P_S(X,Y) and produces a parameter estimate β_MLE defining a prediction rule X→Y for the target domain, requiring no target labels or target covariate samples. Performance is measured by excess risk on the target domain, governed by the Fisher information matrices I_S and I_T at the true parameter β*, which encode how source-target distributional discrepancy affects estimation difficulty. The framework applies to structured data (vectors in R^d) under parametric conditional models such as Gaussian, logistic, or squared-linear likelihoods.

## Method

The paper proves that vanilla MLE, computed purely on source data without any reweighting or target information, achieves minimax optimal excess risk of Õ(Tr(I_T I_S^{-1})/n) under well-specified covariate shift. The upper bound is established under generic assumptions (Bernstein-type concentration on gradient and Hessian, bounded third derivatives, unique loss minimum) and notably requires no boundedness condition on the source-target density ratio. A matching minimax lower bound is then proved for any algorithm, confirming that no method can beat MLE by more than a constant factor. The framework is instantiated for three concrete models—linear regression, logistic regression, and phase retrieval—each yielding explicit risk bounds and sample complexity thresholds. For the misspecified setting where MLE is inconsistent, the paper shows that the Maximum Weighted Likelihood Estimator (MWLE) becomes minimax optimal under bounded density ratio, with excess risk governed by Tr(G_w H_w^{-1})/n.

## Results

The paper proves that vanilla MLE achieves an excess risk bound of c·Tr(𝓘_T 𝓘_S⁻¹)·log(d/δ)/n under well-specified covariate shift (Theorem 3.1), and establishes a matching minimax lower bound of 1/(50n) for any estimator (Theorem 3.2), confirming MLE is minimax optimal. For linear regression with mean shift α and variance σ², the bound specializes to c(‖α‖²₂ + σ²d)·log(d/δ)/n with threshold N = d(1 + (‖α‖²₂d + σ²d)/(‖α‖²₂ + σ²d))². For logistic regression with perpendicular shift v = rβ⊥*, the bound is c(d + r²)·log(d/δ)/n with threshold N = d⁴(1 + r⁶). Notably, no boundedness condition on the density ratio is required for MLE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary theoretical comparison is between MLE and MWLE (Maximum Weighted Likelihood Estimator). In the well-specified setting, MLE is provably superior: it does not require a bounded density ratio, and by Cauchy-Schwarz, Tr(G_w H_w⁻¹) ≥ Tr(𝓘_T 𝓘_S⁻¹), meaning MWLE's upper bound is always at least as large as MLE's. In the misspecified setting, the roles reverse: MLE is not even consistent (Proposition 5.1), while MWLE achieves a minimax-optimal bound of Tr(G_w H_w⁻¹)/n under bounded density ratio (Theorems 5.2–5.3). The paper also contrasts with prior work: Cortes et al. (2010) achieve only O(1/√n) rates for importance weighting, while this paper's MLE bound is O(1/n). Compared to nonparametric results (Ma et al., 2023), this paper shows MLE is optimal in the parametric well-specified regime, whereas those works show MLE is suboptimal in nonparametric RKHS settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Shimodaira's (2000) seminal asymptotic analysis of MLE under covariate shift in two critical ways: it provides the first non-asymptotic (finite-sample) guarantees and proves optimality among *all* estimators rather than just the restricted class of weighted likelihood estimators. It also departs from prior nonparametric covariate shift work (Ma et al. 2023, Kpotufe & Martinet 2018) which showed MLE/ERM is suboptimal, by establishing that MLE *is* optimal in the well-specified parametric regime. Unlike importance-weighting analyses (Cortes et al. 2010) that yield O(1/√n) rates and require bounded density ratios, this paper achieves the sharper O(1/n) rate without any boundedness condition on the density ratio.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves a long-standing fundamental question—'what are the most effective algorithms for OOD generalization?'—by proving that the simplest possible method (vanilla MLE on source data) is minimax optimal for well-specified covariate shift, with the instance-dependent rate Tr(I_T I_S^{-1})/n characterizing the fundamental hardness. The practical implication is that in modern large-model settings where well-specification is approximately satisfied, practitioners need not resort to importance weighting or other reweighting schemes. Open questions include extending the analysis to other OOD types (posterior shift, imbalanced data), removing regularity assumptions like positive-definite Fisher information, and understanding the transition regime between well-specified and misspecified models where MLE's advantage over MWLE erodes.
