## Problem

Existing vision-language benchmarks either target a single STEM subject (IconQA for math, ScienceQA for science) or are not STEM-specific (VQA, CLEVR), and those that do address STEM often focus on expert-level or competition-level problems rather than fundamentals. There is no large-scale multimodal benchmark that comprehensively tests basic STEM skills across all four subjects (science, technology, engineering, math) aligned with the K-12 curriculum, leaving it unclear where neural models stand relative to elementary students.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241477/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question is a multi-choice item (averaging 2.8 options) consisting of a question text, an optional image context, and answer options that may be rendered as text or image. For language models, images are converted to visual context text via a ViT+GPT-2 captioning pipeline before being concatenated with the question and answer options into a single text prompt. For vision-language models like CLIP, the question text paired with each candidate answer is encoded by the text encoder while the image is encoded by the image encoder, and the option with the highest cosine similarity is selected as the prediction.

## Method

The paper introduces STEM, a 1,073,146-question multimodal dataset spanning 448 skills across all four STEM subjects (Pre-K through 8th grade), collected from IXL Learning (science, engineering, math) and Triviaplaza/ProProfs (technology), with embedded math formulas converted to LaTeX via Mathpix OCR. The benchmark evaluates foundation models in zero-shot and finetuning settings: CLIP variants (RN50 through ViT-L/14@336px) use the original contrastive matching setup with linear-probe finetuning on subject-specific training splits, while language models (GPT-3, GPT-3.5-Turbo, UnifiedQA, GloVe) are prompted as question-answering tasks with captioned images. Evaluation employs both standard accuracy and IXL's SmartScore system, which simulates real online exam conditions by tracking sequential learning progress and penalizing incorrect answers. The key finding is that even the best finetuned model (CLIP ViT-L/14@336px at 76.3% average accuracy) remains well below the 90+ SmartScore of proficient elementary students, with math being the most challenging subject and models failing disproportionately on skills requiring abstract reasoning, comparison, and spatial transformation.

## Results

On the STEM benchmark, the best zero-shot vision-language model (CLIP ViT-L/14@336px) achieves 54.9% macro-average accuracy, outperforming the best language model (GPT-3.5-Turbo at 49.0%) by 5.9 points. After finetuning, CLIP ViT-L/14@336px reaches 76.3% average accuracy, with the largest gains in science (87.0%) and math (78.4%). However, the average exam score across elementary grades (1–6) is only 40.8, which is 54.7% below the human reference score of 90.0, indicating that even finetuned foundation models remain far behind average elementary students.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP (zero-shot) outperforms GPT-3.5-Turbo by 5.9 points on average (54.9% vs. 49.0%), confirming that multimodal understanding is essential for STEM tasks. Among language models, GPT-3.5-Turbo leads at 49.0% average, with a notable strength in engineering (74.6%) where text-heavy descriptions dominate, but struggles in technology (26.3%) and math (45.0%). Among older vision-language models, UNITER (45.9%) is the strongest non-CLIP baseline, while Virtex (37.1%), 12-in-1 (38.3%), and ViLBERT (39.5%) perform near random chance (36.9%). Finetuning CLIP on the STEM training split yields the largest gains in science (+36.7 points) and math (+34.8 points) over its zero-shot setting, yet the finetuned model (76.3% average) still falls well short of the human exam score reference of 90.0. The paper concludes that no current foundation model approaches elementary-student performance, with the average elementary-grade exam score of 40.8 being 54.7% below the human benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual question answering task to a unified, large-scale multimodal STEM benchmark, departing from prior datasets that each cover only a single STEM subject (IconQA for math, ScienceQA for science, Geometry3K for geometry) or are not STEM-specific (VQA, CLEVR). It also departs from expert-level benchmarks like MATH and MMLU by grounding questions in the K-12 curriculum and emphasizing fundamental skills rather than competition-level reasoning. The evaluation methodology is novel in adopting IXL's SmartScore—a learning-progress-aware metric—rather than relying solely on accuracy, and in benchmarking foundation models (CLIP, GPT-3.5-Turbo) that prior STEM benchmarks had not yet evaluated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that even state-of-the-art foundation models average 54.7% below elementary students and master only 2.5% of third-grade skills—highlights a substantial and persistent gap in multimodal STEM reasoning that scaling alone does not close. The fine-grained skill- and grade-level analysis reveals that models fail disproportionately on abstract reasoning, comparison, and spatial transformation tasks, pointing to specific algorithmic shortcomings. Open questions include whether the absence of a grade-level performance gradient (unlike humans) reflects a fundamental difference in how models acquire knowledge, and what architectural or training innovations are needed to bridge the gap to human-level STEM understanding.
