## Problem

Critical learning periods have been empirically observed in both biological and artificial deep networks, yet the biochemical explanation (decreasing plasticity) does not apply to artificial systems. It remains unclear whether critical periods in DNNs arise from specific architectural choices (nonlinearities), optimization details (annealed learning rates), or implementation pathologies (ReLU freezing, vanishing gradients), or whether they are a fundamental consequence of depth and data structure. This paper asks whether critical periods can be explained in a minimal, analytically tractable model devoid of all such confounds.

## Input / Output

In the multi-pathway setting, the network takes an 8-dimensional one-hot input vector and produces a 15-dimensional output, with the key quantity of interest being the pathway-specific singular value contributions K_a and K_b that sum to the target singular values. In the matrix completion setting, the network receives a subset of observed entries (e.g., 2000 out of 10,000) from a 100×100 ground-truth matrix and must reconstruct the full matrix, with generalization measured by reconstruction error on unobserved entries. Deficits are applied either as a gating block on one pathway (freezing its parameters for a fixed number of epochs) or as pre-training on a different-rank matrix before switching to the final task.

## Method

The paper analyzes two minimal deep linear network models and derives exact differential equations governing singular value evolution. In the multi-pathway model, the learning dynamics reduce to coupled scalar ODEs for each pathway's contribution to each singular value, revealing a winner-take-all 'race' whose intensity scales with network depth; an early gating deficit permanently shifts which pathway learns each feature, while late deficits have negligible effect. In the matrix completion model, the authors leverage the implicit regularization dynamics of deep matrix factorization (Arora et al., 2019) to show that pre-training on a higher-rank task before switching to a lower-rank target causes the network to retain brittle high-rank components that impair generalization, with the effect growing with depth and being most severe in low-sample regimes. Both analyses demonstrate that critical periods emerge from the interplay of depth and data distribution structure, not from nonlinearities or optimization specifics, and the ODE predictions are validated against SGD training of actual networks.

## Results

In the multi-pathway model, early gating deficits (first 15 of 1000 epochs) to one pathway cause the other pathway to learn all features, with the effect becoming increasingly pronounced as depth increases from 2 to 13; late deficits (epochs 100–115) have negligible effect. In the matrix completion setting, absolute reconstruction errors for a depth-1, 2, and 3 network trained from random initialization are 0.864, 0.320, and 0.118 (partial observation) and 0.795, 0.053, and 0.0001 (rank-10→rank-5 task transfer), showing that depth-1 is unaffected while deeper networks are increasingly sensitive to pre-training on a different task. Critical periods are most prevalent in low-sample regimes (N=1250, 1500) and disappear at N=3000 or N=4000. The ODE predictions from Eq. 7 and Eqs. 12–14 match SGD training dynamics before, during, and after the deficit period.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it validates its analytical model against two baselines. First, the ODE-derived learning dynamics (Eq. 7 for multi-pathway; Eqs. 12–14 for matrix completion) are compared against actual SGD training of deep linear networks, and the two match closely before, during, and after the deficit period (Figs. 2, 3, 9). Second, the paper's findings are compared qualitatively against prior empirical results: the depth-dependent sensitivity to early deficits is consistent with Achille et al. (2019) and Kleinman et al. (2023), who found that deeper nonlinear CNNs are increasingly affected by early training on blurred or degraded inputs. The multi-pathway model also recapitulates classical biological experiments (Hubel & Wiesel 1963; Guillery 1972), showing that a deprived pathway only recovers features that the intact pathway cannot explain (e.g., a lesioned singular mode), matching the winner-take-all competition observed in kitten visual cortex. The key distinction from prior work is that all these phenomena are reproduced in a fully linear, analytically tractable setting, ruling out nonlinearities, vanishing gradients, or ReLU freezing as necessary causes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate analytical frameworks for deep linear networks—the multi-pathway model of Shi et al. (2022) and the deep matrix factorization dynamics of Arora et al. (2019)—to provide the first analytically tractable explanation for critical learning periods, a phenomenon previously observed only empirically in nonlinear DNNs (Achille et al., 2019; Kleinman et al., 2023) or in biological systems (Hubel & Wiesel, 1963). It departs from the dominant biochemical explanation (Hensch, 2004) by showing that critical periods arise from the interplay of network depth and data structure alone, without requiring nonlinearities, vanishing gradients, or any architecture-specific optimization pathology. The differential equations governing singular value evolution (Eq. 7, Eq. 10) are inherited directly from Saxe et al. (2013) and Arora et al. (2019), but are here applied to the novel setting of temporary deficits and task transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that critical periods emerge in the simplest possible deep networks, this work reframes the phenomenon as fundamental to learning dynamics rather than an artifact of biological plasticity or specific DNN implementation details, providing a strong conceptual bridge between neuroscience and deep learning. Key limitations include the restriction to linear networks, small-scale experiments (100×100 matrices, 8-dimensional inputs), and specific balanced initialization conditions; whether the depth-dependence and data-structure-dependence identified here persist in large-scale nonlinear networks with diverse architectures remains an open question. The findings could inform practical decisions about pre-training duration and task ordering in transfer learning, but the direct practical impact is currently limited to the linear regime.
