## Problem

Biomedical foundation models (FMs) are trained and deployed unimodally—on protein sequences, molecule structures, or clinical text in isolation—yet real biomedical tasks require reasoning across these modalities. Existing multimodal approaches (CLIP-style joint contrastive learning, ImageBind-style central-modality alignment) face combinatorial data scarcity (O(K²) paired datasets) and computational cost when scaling beyond two modalities, and they require fine-tuning all encoders jointly. BioBRIDGE addresses this by learning lightweight cross-modal transformations from knowledge graph structure while keeping all base FMs frozen.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a single entity from any source modality (a protein sequence, a SMILES string, or a text description of a disease/GO term) plus the desired target modality and relation type, and produces a transformed embedding in the target modality's space. This embedding is then compared via inner product against a pre-computed bank of target-modality embeddings to yield a ranked retrieval list. Notably, the knowledge graph is used only during training to supervise the bridge module; at test time, no graph structure or neighbor information is required, enabling prediction for entirely unseen entities (e.g., mouse proteins never in the training KG).

## Method

BioBRIDGE encodes each KG node with a frozen, modality-specific FM (ESM2-3B for proteins, UniMol for molecules, PubMedBERT for text), then projects the raw embedding to a shared dimension d via a modality-specific linear head. A six-layer transformer bridge module ψ receives the stacked input [z_i, c_i, c_j, r_ij] (projected head embedding, source/target modality embeddings, and relation embedding) and outputs a residual correction added to z_i, yielding the transformed embedding ĥ_i in the target modality's space. Training uses InfoNCE contrastive loss over KG triples: the transformed head embedding is pulled toward the true tail embedding and pushed away from M sampled negative tails of the same modality, with only ψ and the projection heads updated. The paper proves (Theorem 1) that such a bridge module exists under the assumption that FM output spaces are linear subspaces of equal dimension, and demonstrates that the additive residual formulation outperforms both no-residual and RotatE-style multiplicative variants in ablation.

## Results

On the seven cross-modal retrieval tasks (Table 1), BioBRIDGE achieves MRR scores ranging from 0.081 (Drug→Disease contraindication) to 0.326 (Protein→MF), consistently outperforming the best KGE baseline (RotatE) by an average of ~76.3%. On semantic similarity inference (Table 2), BioBRIDGE reaches a Spearman correlation of 0.81 (avg across MF/BP/CC), roughly 2× the best baseline KeAP (0.41). In cross-species protein-phenotype matching (Table 4), zero-shot BioBRIDGE surpasses a supervised dual-encoder baseline by 10–43% on Recall@K and by up to 3351% on Precision@1 for the mouse-protein-to-human-phenotype task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BioBRIDGE outperforms all seven KGE baselines (TransE, TransR, TransH, TransD, ComplEx, DistMult, RotatE) on every one of the seven cross-modal retrieval tasks, with the largest margin on Protein→MF (0.326 vs. RotatE's 0.119, ~2.7×) and Protein→CC (0.319 vs. RotatE's 0.107, ~3×). On semantic similarity inference, BioBRIDGE (0.81 avg Spearman) roughly doubles the best baseline KeAP (0.41) and substantially exceeds KG-augmented OntoProtein (0.38) and all raw protein FMs (ESM2-3B 0.32, ESM-1B 0.39, ProtT5-XL 0.39, MSA Transformer 0.33). On protein-protein interaction prediction, BioBRIDGE achieves the best average F1 (0.739 on SHS27K, 0.739 on SHS148K, 0.836 on STRING) over ESM2-3B (0.732/0.733/0.834) and KeAP (0.733/0.726/0.834), with the gap most pronounced on the smaller SHS27K dataset. In the cross-species protein-phenotype matching task, zero-shot BioBRIDGE beats a supervised contrastive-learning dual-encoder baseline (ESM2-1B + PubMedBERT) on most metrics, and uniquely succeeds on the mouse-protein-to-human-phenotype task where the supervised baseline fails entirely (Rec@1: 0.022 vs. 0.001).

## Relation to Prior Work

BioBRIDGE carves new ground by combining three previously separate ideas: frozen unimodal foundation models (ESM2, UniMol, PubMedBERT), knowledge graph structure for supervision, and contrastive learning for cross-modal alignment. It departs from multimodal contrastive learning (CLIP, MolTrans, ProtST) which jointly optimizes all encoders on paired data, and from ImageBind which fine-tunes non-central encoders to align with a central modality. Unlike traditional KGE methods (TransE, RotatE, ComplEx) that learn node and relation embeddings from scratch, BioBRIDGE leverages pre-trained FMs as fixed feature extractors and trains only a lightweight 6-layer transformer bridge module, supervised by KG triplets via InfoNCE loss. The theoretical grounding draws on linear algebra (rank-nullity theorem, matrix composition) and universal approximation to prove existence of the bridge transformation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The parameter and data efficiency of BioBRIDGE is practically significant: only a small bridge module requires training while all base FMs remain frozen, and the approach avoids the combinatorial data scarcity of pairwise cross-modal datasets. The demonstrated out-of-domain generalization—zero-shot transfer to unseen entities (mouse proteins) and unseen relations (PPI subtypes)—suggests the bridged representations capture transferable biological structure rather than memorizing KG edges. However, the evaluation is limited to a single KG (PrimeKG subset), six modalities, and a deliberately simple bridge architecture; open questions include whether more complex bridge designs or additional modalities (e.g., 3D protein structure, imaging) yield further gains, and whether the approach extends cleanly to non-biomedical domains. The cross-species result, where a zero-shot method beats a supervised baseline fine-tuned on paired data, hints at broader implications for data-scarce biomedical subfields.
