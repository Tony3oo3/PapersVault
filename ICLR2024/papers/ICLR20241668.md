## Problem

Prior domain generalization methods learn a single domain-invariant model, which cannot exploit domain-specific features that correlate with predictions in practical settings. The key unresolved challenge is how to construct a domain-specific model for a completely unseen test domain without any training data from that domain, while still leveraging the structural relationships between domains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241668/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input task-specific features x (satellite images at 224×224 for FMoW, molecular graphs for ChEMBL-STRING, 6-month temperature sequences for TPT-48), a domain ID identifying which domain each sample belongs to, and domain metadata (geographical coordinates, protein-protein interaction graphs, or protein structures) used to build a pairwise domain similarity matrix. At test time, the output is a weighted combination of all training-domain head predictions, yielding a class label (FMoW), a continuous temperature value (TPT-48), or a binding affinity score (ChEMBL-STRING).

## Method

D3G uses a multi-headed architecture with a shared feature extractor e(x) and one prediction head h^(d) per training domain. During training, each head is supervised on its own domain's data, and a relation-aware consistency loss encourages each domain's ground truth to match the domain-relation-weighted average of all other heads' predictions, strengthening predictors for data-sparse domains. Domain relations are obtained by combining fixed relations extracted from metadata (e.g., geographic adjacency, PPI graph edges) with learned relations computed via a multi-headed cosine-similarity layer over metadata embeddings, blended by a hyperparameter β. At test time, for each unseen domain t, the prediction is the relation-weighted average of all training heads, so that more similar training domains contribute more. The paper proves an excess risk bound of B + √(C(ℋ)/n / (N^tr · B^r)) and shows via a minimax argument that using accurate domain relations strictly outperforms uniform averaging of all training heads.

## Results

D3G achieves the best performance across all five evaluation settings. On TPT-48, it attains MSE of 0.342 (N→S) and 0.236 (E→W), outperforming the best baselines (TRO: 0.371/0.281). On FMoW, D3G reaches 28.12% worst-domain accuracy (Asia) and 39.47% (WILDS), surpassing the strongest baselines by 1.25 and 1.83 points respectively. On ChEMBL-STRING, D3G achieves 78.67% ROC-AUC (PPI>50) and 77.24% (PPI>100), exceeding the best prior methods (SelfReg/CORAL at 75.42% and GRDA at 73.57%) by 3.25 and 3.67 points. The paper reports an average improvement of 10.6% over the best prior method across all datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D3G outperforms all 23 baselines on every dataset and metric. Against invariant learning methods (IRM, CORAL, V-REx, RSC, DANN, MMD, etc.), D3G shows the largest margins on ChEMBL-STRING, where IRM and IB-IRM collapse to ~52% AUC while D3G reaches 78.67%. Among domain-specific learning baselines, TRO is the closest competitor on TPT-48 (0.371 vs. 0.342 MSE for N→S; 0.281 vs. 0.236 for E→W), while DDN leads on FMoW-Asia (26.77% vs. 28.12%) and V-REx on FMoW-WILDS (37.64% vs. 39.47%). On ChEMBL-STRING, the best domain-specific baselines (GRDA at 75.01%, DDN at 75.17%) trail D3G by over 3.5 AUC points. The paper also shows that even when domain relations are injected into prior domain-specific methods (DRM+Fixed, LLE+Fixed, DDN+Fixed in Table 9), D3G still maintains a clear advantage, confirming that its consistency regularization and relation refinement are critical. Notably, D3G also outperforms a domain-specific fine-tuning baseline (RW-FT) that reweights ERM training data by domain relations, suggesting that learning separate domain-specific heads is more effective than reweighting a single model.

## Relation to Prior Work

D3G departs from the dominant paradigm in domain generalization, which seeks a single domain-invariant predictor (IRM, V-REx, CORAL, etc.), by instead learning a set of domain-specific functions and reweighting them at test time using domain relations derived from metadata. It also distinguishes itself from prior domain-specific learning methods (GRDA, DRM, LLE, DDN, TRO) by explicitly modeling pairwise domain relations through a combination of fixed metadata-derived relations and a learned multi-headed similarity layer (following Chen et al., 2020), and by introducing a relation-aware consistency regularizer during training. The approach is conceptually adjacent to ensemble and mixture-of-experts methods, but differs in that the 'experts' are explicitly tied to training domains and the combination weights are determined by domain similarity rather than learned gating.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical argument (Theorem 4.1, Proposition 4.2) that leveraging accurate domain relations provably reduces excess risk compared to uniform averaging, and empirically validates this with a 10.6% average improvement over the best prior method across four real-world benchmarks. A key limitation is the dependence on domain metadata quality: the ablation showing that learned relations alone (without fixed relations) perform worse than no relations at all highlights that the method's gains are contingent on having informative metadata signals. Open questions include how D3G scales to very large numbers of domains, whether the uniform-distribution assumption on domain representations holds in practice, and how to obtain high-quality metadata in settings where it is not naturally available.
