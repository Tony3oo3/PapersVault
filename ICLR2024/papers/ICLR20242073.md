## Problem

While SGD is preferred for fine-tuning due to lower memory cost (12 vs 16 bytes/parameter), prior work reported similar SGD and AdamW performance only on ImageNet-like domains. This paper shows that on distribution shift benchmarks, AdamW substantially outperforms SGD on modern Vision Transformer and ConvNeXt models, especially out-of-distribution, leaving practitioners without a clear, memory-efficient fine-tuning recipe for robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242073/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method fine-tunes pretrained vision models (CLIP ViT-B/16, ViT-L/14, DINO ViT-B/16, ConvNeXt-B, BiT ResNet-50/101, supervised ViT-B/16) on RGB images resized to 224×224, producing class labels for 5 distribution shift benchmarks (Living-17, Waterbirds, DomainNet, WILDS-FMoW, WILDS-Camelyon) plus CIFAR-10. Evaluation uses both in-distribution and out-of-distribution test splits, with early stopping on ID validation accuracy. The key operational detail is that the 'embedding layer'—the patch-to-token projection (plus layernorm) for ViTs, or the stem plus first stage for ConvNeXt/ResNet—constitutes less than 1% of total parameters and is held fixed during optimization.

## Method

The paper identifies that at pretrained initialization, the embedding layer of AdamW-pretrained modern models (ViTs, ConvNeXt) has gradient norms an order of magnitude larger than all other layers, causing SGD to over-train this layer and degrade OOD performance. The proposed fix, SGD (freeze-embed), simply freezes this <1% parameter subset and applies standard SGD (with or without momentum) to the remaining layers, achieving OOD accuracy competitive with or slightly better than AdamW (76.7% vs 76.0% averaged across 7 models and 5 datasets) while using 16–49% less GPU memory. Dropping the momentum state (SGD freeze-embed, no momentum) yields the best average OOD accuracy (76.9%) at the lowest memory cost (8 bytes/parameter). Controlled experiments confirm the root cause is an optimizer mismatch: models pretrained with AdamW develop high embedding-layer gradients that SGD cannot handle, whereas SGD-pretrained models do not exhibit this issue, and freezing the embedding layer neutralizes the mismatch regardless of pretraining optimizer.

## Results

Averaged across 7 models and 5 distribution shift benchmarks, SGD (freeze-embed) achieves 76.7% OOD accuracy and 91.3% ID accuracy, outperforming vanilla SGD (72.0% OOD, 90.3% ID) by 4.7 points OOD and matching or slightly exceeding AdamW (76.0% OOD, 91.5% ID). The memory-efficient variant SGD (freeze-embed, no momentum) achieves the highest average OOD accuracy of 76.9% while using ~33% less GPU memory than AdamW on ViT-L/14. On the largest model (CLIP ViT-L/14), SGD (freeze-embed) gains 16.2% OOD over SGD and 0.8% over AdamW, and achieves state-of-the-art OOD accuracy on all five benchmarks (e.g., 90.5% on Living-17, 96.5% on WILDS-Camelyon).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SGD (freeze-embed) consistently outperforms vanilla SGD by 4.7% average OOD accuracy across all models and datasets, with the largest gains on modern ViT and ConvNeXt architectures (e.g., +16.2% on CLIP ViT-L/14) and minimal changes on older BiT ResNets (+1.0–1.5%). Against AdamW, SGD (freeze-embed) achieves a slight average OOD edge (+0.7%) while using 16–49% less GPU memory depending on model size; the no-momentum variant extends this to +0.9% OOD with even lower memory. Both LARS (69.6% avg OOD) and LAMB (73.2% avg OOD) underperform SGD (freeze-embed) by 7.1% and 3.5% respectively on CLIP ViT-B/16, confirming that simple layer freezing is more effective than layerwise gradient normalization. On ID accuracy, SGD (freeze-embed) closes 85% of the gap to AdamW (91.3% vs. 91.5%) while SGD (freeze-embed, no momentum) is within 0.3%. The gains are most pronounced on OOD evaluations with substantial distribution shift (Waterbirds, DomainNet, WILDS-FMoW) and smaller on Living-17, where fine-tuning data is closer to the ImageNet pretraining distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the fine-tuning literature (Kornblith et al., 2019; Kumar et al., 2022) and the optimizer comparison literature. Prior work on ImageNet-like fine-tuning (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021; Touvron et al., 2021) reported negligible differences between SGD and AdamW, and the broader parameter-freezing literature (linear probing, prefix-tuning, ULMFiT) typically freezes large fractions of the model. This paper departs from both: it shows the SGD–AdamW gap is substantial specifically under distribution shift, and it identifies that freezing a tiny (<1%) embedding layer—far less than prior freezing methods—closes the gap. The controlled pretraining experiments (SGD vs. AdamW pretraining on the same ResNet) isolate optimizer mismatch as the root cause, going beyond the architectural explanations considered in prior ViT/ConvNeXt training papers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for practitioners fine-tuning large vision models: SGD (freeze-embed, no momentum) saves 33–49% GPU memory over AdamW while matching or slightly exceeding its accuracy, and the technique is trivially implementable (one line of code to freeze a layer). The finding that AdamW pretraining implicitly biases models toward AdamW-style updates during fine-tuning is a broadly relevant insight that may affect how practitioners choose fine-tuning optimizers for any modern pretrained model. Open questions include whether the effect extends to NLP, segmentation, and recommender systems; whether models without a clear embedding layer benefit; and whether more sophisticated schedules (e.g., gradual unfreezing with exponential LR decay) can further improve results. The paper also notes SGD performs better when fine-tuning data is closer to pretraining, suggesting the gap may narrow as pretraining corpora grow.
