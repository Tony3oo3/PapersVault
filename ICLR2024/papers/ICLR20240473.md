## Problem

Derivative-based optimization is inapplicable to many real-world problems, and derivative-free methods typically require task-specific algorithm design. The paper asks whether large language models can serve as general-purpose optimizers simply by describing the problem in natural language, without formal mathematical specifications or gradient access. This is particularly relevant for prompt optimization, where the discrete prompt space and API-only model access preclude standard gradient-based or edit-based approaches.

## Input / Output

The optimizer LLM receives a meta-prompt containing three components: a natural language description of the optimization objective, the optimization trajectory (previously generated solutions paired with their scores, sorted in ascending order), and a few randomly sampled task exemplars showing where the generated instruction will be inserted. The output is a batch of new candidate instructions (default 8 per step) in free-form text, which are then evaluated by a separate scorer LLM on a small training subset (e.g., 3.5% of GSM8K) to compute accuracy scores that feed back into the next iteration. The optimizer and scorer can be different models, and the instruction is inserted at specific positions (Q_begin, Q_end, or A_begin) relative to the task input.

## Method

OPRO (Optimization by PROmpting) iteratively prompts an LLM to generate new solutions by showing it the full optimization trajectory—past solutions with their scores in ascending order—alongside a task description and exemplars. The key insight is that the ascending ordering exploits the LLM's recency bias, so the model sees the best solutions last and is implicitly encouraged to build upon them without explicit update rules. At each step, multiple solutions are generated (default 8) to reduce variance and improve stability, with sampling temperature tuned to balance exploration and exploitation. The process is entirely black-box: no analytic form of the objective appears in the prompt, and the LLM must infer descent directions purely from the solution-score pairs. The loop terminates when no new solution improves upon the current best or a step budget is exhausted, and the best instruction found is evaluated on the full test set.

## Results

On GSM8K, the best OPRO-optimized instruction ('Take a deep breath and work on this problem step-by-step.' found by PaLM 2-L-IT) achieves 80.2% zero-shot test accuracy, outperforming the 'Let's think step by step.' baseline (71.8%) by 8.4 points. On Big-Bench Hard, OPRO-optimized prompts outperform 'Let's think step by step.' on 19/23 tasks by over 5% with the PaLM 2-L scorer, with gains reaching up to 50% on individual tasks (e.g., word_sorting: 54.4% vs 10.0%). Optimized prompts also transfer across datasets, achieving 95.3% on MultiArith and 54.3% on AQuA (vs. 85.7% and 44.9% for the CoT baseline).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On GSM8K, OPRO with PaLM 2-L-IT as optimizer achieves 80.2% accuracy, beating the zero-shot chain-of-thought baseline 'Let's think step by step.' (Kojima et al., 2022) by 8.4 points and the APE-generated prompt (Zhou et al., 2022b) by 21.4 points. On the Traveling Salesman Problem, OPRO with gpt-4 substantially outperforms both the Nearest Neighbor heuristic (Rosenkrantz et al., 1977) and the Farthest Insertion heuristic (Golden et al., 1980) at small scales: at n=10, gpt-4 achieves 0.0% optimality gap vs. 13.0% (NN) and 3.2% (FI); at n=15, 0.2% vs. 9.4% (NN) and 1.2% (FI). However, at larger scales (n=20, n=50), the FI heuristic regains the edge (0.2% and 9.8% gap respectively vs. OPRO gpt-4's 1.4% and 11.0%), indicating OPRO's advantage diminishes as problem complexity grows. On BBH, OPRO consistently outperforms both the CoT and APE baselines across nearly all 23 tasks with both PaLM 2-L and text-bison scorers.

## Relation to Prior Work

OPRO departs from prior prompt optimization work (APE, APO, soft prompt tuning) by framing the LLM as a general-purpose derivative-free optimizer that iteratively generates new solutions conditioned on the full optimization trajectory (past solutions with scores), rather than editing a single prompt or generating semantically similar variants. The conceptual lineage draws on in-context learning and pattern recognition in LLMs, but the key novelty is using the trajectory itself as the optimization signal—no gradients, no fine-tuning, no explicit update rule. The paper also extends the 'LLM as optimizer' idea beyond prompts to classical mathematical optimization (linear regression, TSP), positioning it as a general paradigm rather than a prompt-specific trick.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OPRO provides a simple, training-free, API-compatible method for prompt optimization that outperforms human-designed prompts by up to 8% on GSM8K and up to 50% on BBH, with the added benefit that optimized prompts transfer across related benchmarks. Key open questions include reducing sensitivity to initialization, better balancing exploration and exploitation, and incorporating richer error-case feedback beyond aggregated accuracy. The paper also reveals fundamental limitations of LLMs as optimizers—hallucinating function values, getting stuck on bumpy loss landscapes, and failing to reliably generate novel solutions—which constrain applicability to more complex optimization problems.
