## Problem

While prior work has extensively studied alignment algorithms (PPO, DPO, etc.) under specific feedback protocols, the structural design choice between two sparse feedback forms—absolute ratings (e.g., 1–7 Likert scores) and relative rankings (pairwise preferences)—has remained under-explored. This paper asks: how does this choice affect the consistency of inferred preferences, the resulting alignment, and the downstream evaluation of aligned LLMs?

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes text instructions (5.2K from Dolly, Self-Instruct, and SuperNI) and five candidate responses per instruction sampled from Alpaca-7B (max length 128 for training data, 200 for evaluation). Annotators (humans via MTurk and GPT-3.5-Turbo) independently assign 1–7 ratings to each response and pairwise rankings (response 1 / response 2 / equal) to each response pair. The system outputs a Best-of-64 selected response and a per-pair consistency score (binary: whether ratings-converted-to-rankings agrees with direct rankings). Evaluation produces win-rates against DaVinci-003 computed under both rating and ranking protocols.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is an empirical consistency analysis: the same set of responses receives both individual ratings and independent pairwise rankings, and ratings are converted to a ranking form (higher score → preferred) to measure agreement. For alignment, two reward models are trained on LoRA-adapted Alpaca-7B: a regression model with sigmoid output and MSE loss on normalized ratings, and an NLL model on pairwise rankings (filtering 'equal' pairs). A Best-of-64 rejection-sampling policy then selects the highest-scoring response from 64 candidates. The key finding is a ~60% feedback inconsistency (ratings-converted-to-rankings disagree with direct rankings) for both humans and AI, traced to annotators weighting different quality facets (e.g., density vs. accuracy) depending on the protocol. A second finding—evaluation inconsistency—shows that ranking-based evaluation favors ranking-trained models (11.2% win-rate gap over SFT vs. 5.3% for rating-trained), while rating-based evaluation reverses the trend, and this effect persists across RSFT, RoBERTa-Large reward models, and multiple AI annotators.

## Results

The paper uncovers a feedback inconsistency problem where ratings and rankings disagree on ~60% of pairwise comparisons (58% for humans, 59.4% for GPT-3.5-Turbo), with consistency scores of only 40–42%. In the alignment evaluation, Best-of-64 (Rankings) achieves a 45.1% win-rate against DaVinci-003 under human rankings evaluation, compared to 39.2% for Best-of-64 (Ratings) and 33.9% for the SFT baseline. Critically, the evaluation protocol itself biases results: under rankings evaluation, the Rankings-aligned model leads by 11.2% over SFT while the Ratings-aligned model leads by only 5.3%; under ratings evaluation, this reverses (5% vs. 4.3% gaps), demonstrating evaluation inconsistency for both human and AI evaluators.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new alignment method but instead compares two feedback protocols (ratings vs. rankings) as the core experimental axis. Under rankings evaluation, Best-of-64 (Rankings) outperforms Best-of-64 (Ratings) by 5.9 percentage points (45.1% vs. 39.2%), while under ratings evaluation the advantage flips: Best-of-64 (Ratings) edges out Best-of-64 (Rankings) by ~0.7 points (38.9% vs. 38.2%). Both Best-of-64 policies outperform the SFT baseline (33.9%) under both evaluation protocols, confirming that AI-collected feedback at scale is useful for alignment. The evaluation inconsistency is robust across task subsets (OASST, Vicuna, Anthropic Helpful), alignment algorithms (Best-of-n and rejection sampling finetuning), and reward model architectures (Alpaca-7B LoRA and RoBERTa-Large), indicating it is a property of the feedback protocol itself rather than a confound of any single experimental choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the RLHF/RLAIF alignment pipeline (Ouyang et al. 2022; Bai et al. 2022) and the Best-of-n rejection sampling paradigm, but shifts focus from designing new alignment algorithms to analyzing a previously under-explored structural choice: the feedback acquisition protocol (ratings vs. rankings). Prior work on feedback acquisition (Campos & Shern 2022; Scheurer et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2023) has pursued denser, fine-grained feedback, while alignment algorithm research (PPO, DPO, PRO) has typically assumed a fixed protocol. This is the first systematic empirical comparison of the two sparse protocols' effects on both reward model training and downstream evaluation, using both human and AI annotators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that ratings and rankings disagree on ~60% of comparisons, and that evaluation protocol systematically favors the matching alignment protocol (evaluation inconsistency), has direct practical implications for anyone benchmarking aligned LLMs: win-rate comparisons may be protocol-dependent rather than reflecting true quality differences. Limitations include reliance on a single base model (Alpaca-7B), a single reference model (DaVinci-003), and primarily Best-of-n (though RSFT and RoBERTa-Large ablations partially address this). Open questions include whether the inconsistency persists with denser feedback, different alignment algorithms (PPO, DPO), and in domain-specific settings like math or code where binary correctness may reduce the ratings-rankings gap.
