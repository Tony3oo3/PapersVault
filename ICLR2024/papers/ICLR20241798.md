## Problem

Existing learning-based fluid simulators require known physical properties (density, viscosity) to predict dynamics, and prior inverse-graphics approaches like NeuroFluid and PAC-NeRF either finetune the entire deterministic transition model per fluid or rely on heuristic simulators with explicit parameter inference. This paper asks whether hidden physical properties can be inferred from a single 3D video and transferred to a simulator for novel scenes without ever specifying the true parameters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241798/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single multi-view 3D video sequence (60 time steps, 20 viewpoints, 400×400 RGB frames) of a fluid body, from which initial particle positions are estimated via voxel-based neural rendering when ground-truth states are unavailable. It outputs simulated particle positions and velocities for novel scenes with unseen geometries, boundary conditions, or dynamics, as well as rendered images through a particle-driven neural radiance field. The latent features are per-particle and time-varying (8-dimensional Gaussians), not a single global parameter.

## Method

The framework introduces a probabilistic latent space z that bridges particle dynamics and visual observations, with four components: a CConv+GRU particle transition module, a GRU-based physical prior learner p_ψ(z̃|x_{1:t-1}, z̃_{t-1}), a particle-based posterior estimator q_ξ (training only), and a modified PhysNeRF renderer. Training proceeds in three stages: (A) pretraining the probabilistic simulator on 600 DFSPH particle scenes with a KL-divergence objective aligning prior and posterior latents; (B) inferring scene-specific visual posteriors by optimizing per-particle Gaussian distributions via backpropagated photometric loss through the frozen transition module and renderer; (C) adapting only the prior learner to match the converged visual posteriors, leaving the transition module untouched to preserve pretraining knowledge. This selective adaptation avoids the overfitting that plagues end-to-end finetuning (as in NeuroFluid) while enabling generalization to novel particle counts, geometries, and boundaries.

## Results

On the primary novel scene simulation task (Table 1), the proposed method achieves average prediction errors of 34.54±0.55 (unseen geometry) and 39.86±0.90 (unseen boundary) under ρ=2000, ν=0.065, outperforming the best baseline (CConv at 52.49/64.29) by 34% and 38% respectively. On the particle dataset with inaccessible physical properties (Table 3), the probabilistic fluid simulator achieves d_{t+1}=0.31, d_{t+2}=0.94, and long-term d̄=38.37, beating the strongest baseline CConv (0.34/1.03/44.79) across all three metrics. On future prediction of observed scenes (Table 2), the method achieves 32.41/32.97/41.15 across three physical property sets, matching or exceeding all baselines except NeuroFluid on the lowest-density setting (33.22 vs. 41.15).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all six baselines on the primary novel scene simulation task (Table 1). CConv, which simulates without any physical parameter input, is beaten by 18–34% on geometry and 25–38% on boundary conditions across all three physical property sets. NeuroFluid, which jointly optimizes the entire transition model and renderer end-to-end, is the strongest visual-inference baseline but still loses by 15–47% on novel scenes; it only edges out the proposed method on future prediction of the observed scene at ρ=500, ν=0.2 (33.22 vs. 41.15), likely due to overfitting the observed scene. PAC-NeRF, which uses a non-learnable MPM simulator with explicitly inferred parameters, is competitive on geometry (within ~1–2 units) but degrades significantly on unseen boundaries (up to 15 units worse). Sys-ID, which optimizes explicit physical parameters through the renderer, performs worst on novel scenes (up to 122 units worse), confirming that explicit parameter estimation is fragile. On the particle dataset (Table 3), the probabilistic simulator beats DPI-Net (90.21→38.37 d̄), CConv (44.79→38.37), DMCF (39.70→38.37), and TIE (41.82→38.37) on long-term prediction, with the largest margin over DPI-Net (57% reduction) and the smallest over DMCF (3.4% reduction).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on CConv (Ummenhofer et al., 2020) as the core particle interaction encoder and on NeuroFluid's (Guan et al., 2022) PhysNeRF particle-driven neural renderer, but departs from both by introducing a probabilistic latent variable framework. Where NeuroFluid finetunes the entire deterministic transition model end-to-end on each observed scene, and PAC-NeRF relies on heuristic MPM simulators with explicitly inferred parameters, this work separates hidden physics into per-particle latent features learned via variational inference, requiring only the prior learner to be adapted per scene. The three-stage pretraining-inference-transfer pipeline is the key architectural departure, enabling simulation of novel geometries and boundaries without knowledge of true physical parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that inaccessible fluid properties (viscosity, density) can be captured in a compact latent space and transferred to novel scenes, removing the need for domain-specific parameter specification that plagues prior inverse-graphics approaches. However, all quantitative evaluations are on synthetic data, and the real-world experiments are limited to static dyed-water scenes with a single viewpoint, leaving open whether the framework generalizes to dynamic real-world fluids with complex optical effects. The approach also assumes a fixed particle count per scene and relies on a pretrained simulator covering a range of physical properties, limiting applicability to truly out-of-distribution physics.
