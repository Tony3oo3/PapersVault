## Problem

Reinforcement learning for vision-based tasks requires either manually engineering reward functions (often infeasible for complex, open-ended tasks) or learning reward models from expensive human feedback. Prior attempts to use vision-language models as reward sources required extensive fine-tuning or complex ad-hoc extraction procedures. This paper asks whether a sufficiently large pretrained VLM can serve as a zero-shot, language-grounded reward model with no fine-tuning and minimal prompt engineering.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single-sentence natural language task prompt (e.g., 'a humanoid robot kneeling'), a rendered RGB observation image from the environment at each timestep, and optionally a second baseline prompt describing the neutral environment state. The output is a scalar reward value computed as cosine similarity (or a regularized variant) between the CLIP image embedding of the observation and the CLIP text embedding of the prompt, which is fed directly into standard RL algorithms (DQN or SAC) as a drop-in replacement for the environment's native reward signal.

## Method

The core mechanism is computing reward as cosine similarity between CLIP's image encoder output on the rendered observation and CLIP's text encoder output on the task description, requiring no fine-tuning of either encoder. The key technical contribution is goal-baseline regularization: given a goal prompt and a baseline prompt, the state embedding is projected onto the line spanned by the two text embeddings (controlled by a hyperparameter α), directionally removing components of the CLIP representation irrelevant to distinguishing goal from baseline states. This regularized reward is then used with standard off-policy RL (SAC for continuous control, DQN for discrete) in a straightforward loop where CLIP inference is batched over replay buffer observations. The paper demonstrates a strong scaling effect: EPIC distance to human labels improves near log-linearly with CLIP model size, and complex humanoid tasks (kneeling, splits, lotus position) are only solvable with the largest publicly available model (ViT-bigG-14), while smaller models (RN50, ViT-L-14, ViT-H-14) achieve 0% success.

## Results

On the Humanoid-v4 environment, VLM-RMs achieve 100% human-evaluated success rate on 5 out of 8 tasks (kneeling, lotus position, standing up, arms raised, doing splits), 64% on hands-on-hips, and 0% on standing-on-one-leg and arms-crossed, using only single-sentence text prompts with no prompt engineering. On classic control benchmarks, the method achieves 100% task success on CartPole and textured MountainCar. A strong scaling effect is observed: only the largest publicly available CLIP model (ViT-bigG-14) successfully learns the kneeling task, while RN50, ViT-L-14, and ViT-H-14 all achieve 0% success rate, despite showing smooth log-linear improvement in EPIC distance to human labels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not run head-to-head comparisons against other reward specification methods (e.g., Mahmoudieh et al. 2022's fine-tuned CLIP, Du et al. 2023's Flamingo success detector, or Fan et al. 2022's Minecraft CLIP). Instead, the key comparisons are internal ablations. On model scaling, ViT-bigG-14 is the only model that achieves 100% success on the kneeling task, while all smaller models (RN50, ViT-L-14, ViT-H-14) fail completely at 0%, despite showing a smooth log-linear improvement in EPIC distance. On environment modifications, adding realistic textures to the MountainCar environment is critical (without textures, the agent cannot learn the task; with textures, it achieves 100%), and modifying the humanoid camera angle provides a modest improvement (36% → 91% → 100% success for kneeling). Goal-baseline regularization improves EPIC distance to human labels across all model sizes but is more impactful for smaller models, and does not rescue the three failing humanoid tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's contrastive image-text embeddings (Radford et al., 2021) and departs from prior VLM-as-reward work by eliminating the need for fine-tuning. Mahmoudieh et al. (2022) required explicit fine-tuning of the CLIP image encoder on a robotics-specific dataset, Cui et al. (2022) used CLIP with goal images rather than language, and Du et al. (2023) trained a Flamingo-based success detector without actually training RL policies. The key conceptual departure is showing that raw cosine similarity between a frozen CLIP image embedding and a text prompt is a sufficient reward signal, provided the underlying VLM is large enough, and that goal-baseline regularization (projecting onto the goal–baseline direction) further sharpens the reward landscape.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that reward specification for vision-based RL can be reduced to writing a single natural-language sentence, removing the need for hand-engineered reward functions or expensive human preference data. The strong scaling result—where only the largest publicly available CLIP model (ViT-bigG-14) successfully learns complex humanoid tasks—suggests that as VLMs grow, they will become increasingly practical reward models across a wide range of embodied tasks. Key open questions include robustness under optimization pressure (reward hacking), generalization to tasks requiring temporal reasoning (needing video encoders rather than single images), and whether the observed failure modes (subtle pose discrimination, spatial reasoning) will be resolved by next-generation VLMs.
