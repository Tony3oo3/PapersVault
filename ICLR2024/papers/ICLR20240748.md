## Problem

Autoregressive LLMs fail to generalize from a learned fact 'A is B' to its logical converse 'B is A', a basic symmetry that humans take for granted. This paper systematically demonstrates that this failure (the Reversal Curse) is robust across model families and sizes, persists under multiple training interventions, and manifests in real-world knowledge recall in state-of-the-art models like GPT-4.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240748/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes synthetic name-description pairs (fictitious celebrities generated by GPT-4) and real celebrity-parent pairs (from IMDB) as training/evaluation data. Evaluation uses held-out paraphrase prompts (10 per fact, generated from templates) rather than the exact training sentence, testing whether the model generalizes beyond memorized phrasing. Outputs include exact-match accuracy at temperature 0 and, critically, the log-probability assigned to the correct name versus a uniformly random name from the training set, with paired t-tests and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests to determine statistical significance.

## Method

The paper finetunes base LLMs (GPT-3 at 350M–175B, Llama-1 at 7B–30B) on synthetic facts presented in a single order (e.g., 'Name is Description') and then probes the reverse direction using held-out paraphrase prompts. The key diagnostic is not merely low accuracy but the absence of any signal: the model's log-probability for the correct name in the reverse direction is statistically indistinguishable from that of a random name. To rule out trivial explanations, the authors test multiple interventions—auxiliary both-order examples, 30 paraphrases per fact, a 13× larger dataset (40,000 documents), and prompt tuning on Llama-7b—none of which alleviate the curse. A complementary experiment on real celebrity-parent pairs (1,573 pairs) shows GPT-4 answers 'Who is X's parent?' correctly 79% of the time but 'Who is Y's child?' only 33% of the time, suggesting the Reversal Curse operates on pretraining data distribution rather than being an artifact of finetuning.

## Results

On GPT-3-175B finetuned on synthetic name-description facts, the model achieves 96.7% exact-match accuracy when the prompt order matches training (DescriptionToName) but drops to 0.1% in the reverse direction (NameToDescription), and 50.0% vs 0.0% for the other subset. Log-probability analysis confirms no statistically significant difference between the correct name and a random name in the reverse direction (all p-values > 0.05 across 12 t-tests and KS-tests). On real-world celebrity knowledge (Experiment 2), GPT-4 answers 'Who is [celebrity]'s parent?' correctly 79% of the time but only 33% for the reverse 'Who is [parent]'s child?'. The Reversal Curse persists across model sizes (350M to 175B), model families (GPT-3, Llama-1), and is not alleviated by data augmentation, larger datasets (40K examples), or prompt tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study of a failure mode rather than a method comparison, so there are no external baselines. The core comparison is internal: same-direction vs. reverse-direction accuracy, which reveals a near-total collapse (96.7% → 0.1%) when the prompt order is reversed. The authors test multiple interventions to break the Reversal Curse—data augmentation with paraphrases, including both orders in training, scaling the dataset from 3,600 to 40,000 examples, and prompt tuning on Llama-7b—and none restore reverse-direction generalization. Across model sizes (GPT-3-350M through 175B, Llama-7b/13b/30b) and families, the reverse-direction accuracy remains at or near chance level (~0–3.3%). In contrast, in-context learning (3-shot) achieves ~100% accuracy in both directions, confirming the model can perform the reversal when the fact is explicitly provided in the prompt, isolating the failure to the training/weight-update process rather than to the model's reasoning capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first direct, controlled test of bidirectional factual recall in autoregressive LLMs through systematic finetuning experiments. It builds conceptually on the authors' prior work on situational awareness in LLMs (Berglund et al., 2023) and is contemporaneous with Grosse et al. (2023), who provide complementary evidence via influence functions. The paper also connects to the mechanistic interpretability literature on factual recall (Geva et al., 2021; 2022; 2023) and model editing (Meng et al., 2023), which had suggested non-bidirectional knowledge storage but had not performed a direct generalization test. Unlike prior work that treats factual recall as a one-directional lookup, this paper formalizes the reversal as a distinct generalization property and tests it under multiple training configurations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Reversal Curse reveals a fundamental asymmetry in how autoregressive LLMs encode factual knowledge, with practical implications: long-tail entities in pretraining corpora whose relationships are rarely expressed in the reverse order will be effectively unrecallable in that direction. The finding that even GPT-4 shows a 79%→33% drop on real-world celebrity parent-child pairs suggests this is not merely a finetuning artifact but a property of pretraining itself. Key open questions include whether non-autoregressive architectures avoid the curse, whether the effect can be mitigated by architectural or training changes, and the precise mechanistic explanation (the gradient-update sketch in Section 4 remains incomplete). The paper also raises the interesting parallel to human backward-recall deficits, though the LLM effect appears far more absolute.
