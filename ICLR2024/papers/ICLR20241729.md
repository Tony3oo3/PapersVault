## Problem

Existing rejection-loss frameworks (e.g., Cortes et al.) jointly optimize a predictor and a rejector, but in NLP generation tasks the predictor is typically a fixed large language model whose output space admits many correct labels that cannot be enumerated during training. This paper addresses the underexplored setting of learning an optimal rejector given a fixed predictor, where the only available supervision is a small set of manually-labeled binary correctness judgments on the predictor's outputs.

## Input / Output

The rejector r takes as input the concatenation of the original input x and the fixed predictor's output f(x) (joined by an [OUT] token) and produces a real-valued score r(x) ∈ ℝ, where r(x) > 0 means accept and r(x) ≤ 0 means reject. In practice, r(x) is computed as softmax(logits)_yes − 0.5 from a T5X model fine-tuned to emit a single 'yes'/'no' token. The training labels a ∈ {−1, +1} are manually assigned by annotators who judge whether f(x) is a valid decontextualization of x, with 'title'-dependent edits counted as correct.

## Method

The paper introduces a convex surrogate rejection loss ℓ₁(r,x,a) = e^{(α/2)[r(x)−a]} + c·e^{−βr(x)} that upper-bounds the induced rejection loss and has a built-in decision threshold at zero, eliminating the need for post-hoc threshold fitting. A key constraint 2βc/α = Ī_c (where Ī_c = c·e^{α/2} + (1−c)·e^{−α/2}) ensures the surrogate's Bayes solution has the same sign as the true rejection-loss Bayes solution, which is the critical condition enabling the proof. Under this constraint, the paper derives the first H-consistency bound for a surrogate rejection loss: the excess rejection loss is bounded by O(√ε) where ε is the excess surrogate loss, using the general framework of Awasthi et al. (2022a). The rejector is trained by fine-tuning a T5X model on 1,500 manually-labeled (x, f(x), a) pairs with this surrogate loss, varying c to sweep the precision–coverage trade-off without any additional threshold search.

## Results

On the decontextualization task, the proposed surrogate rejection loss achieves 94.5% precision at 90.6% coverage (c=0.07), halving the error rate from 11.0% to 5.1% while remaining only ~3% below the theoretical limit of 93.6% coverage. The closest baseline, cross-entropy loss, achieves only ~64.4% coverage at a comparable ~5.1% error rate, and MaxProb drops to ~46.7% coverage. On image classification (KMNIST, Fashion-MNIST), the surrogate loss also outperforms MaxProb and cross-entropy baselines, though the gap is smaller since the fixed predictor is better calibrated than a large language model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two main baselines on decontextualization: MaxProb (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2016), which thresholds the predictor's own output probability, and a cross-entropy-trained T5X rejector that also requires a post-hoc threshold. The surrogate rejection loss decisively outperforms both: at ~94–95% precision, it achieves 88–91% coverage versus 64% for cross-entropy and 41–47% for MaxProb, a gap of roughly 25–45 percentage points in coverage. The method also tracks the theoretical limit closely (within ~3% coverage at 5% error), whereas the baselines fall 25–50% below it. On image classification (KMNIST, Fashion-MNIST), the surrogate loss still beats MaxProb and cross-entropy, but the margin narrows because the fixed predictor is already well-calibrated. The cost-sensitive classification approach (Charoenphakdee et al., 2021), included as a baseline on KMNIST, is also outperformed by the surrogate loss, consistent with prior findings that cost-sensitive abstention is empirically inferior to dedicated rejection losses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rejection learning framework of Cortes et al. (2016, 2023), which jointly optimizes a predictor and rejector under a rejection loss, to the practically critical setting where the predictor is fixed (e.g., a large language model that cannot be retrained). The key departure is that the rejector now takes the predictor's output f(x) as input, and correctness is defined non-deterministically (multiple valid outputs), which is essential for NLP generation tasks. The theoretical analysis builds directly on the H-consistency framework of Awasthi et al. (2022a), adapting their general tools to derive the first H-consistency bound for a surrogate rejection loss in the fixed-predictor setting. The paper also positions itself against confidence-based methods (MaxProb, Monte-Carlo dropout) and cost-sensitive classification decompositions, showing both are suboptimal or lack comparable guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a theoretically grounded, practically deployable method for LLM abstention that does not require retraining the base model—only a small manually-annotated set (2,000 examples) to train the rejector. The ~25% coverage improvement at halved error rate, being only ~3% from the theoretical limit, suggests the approach is near-optimal for the decontextualization task. A key limitation is the annotation cost: correctness labels are predictor-specific and must be manually assigned, which limits scalability to new tasks or models. Open questions include whether the annotation burden can be reduced (e.g., via LLM-as-judge), whether the H-consistency bound tightens with larger hypothesis classes, and whether the approach transfers to other LLM generation tasks with similar non-deterministic correctness.
