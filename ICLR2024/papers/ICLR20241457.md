## Problem

Probabilistic theories of perception assume observers maintain univariate internal representations of stimulus parameters, yet actual visual stimuli are high-dimensional images. Perceptual scales measured via difference scaling (MLDS) had never been formally connected to Fisher information or to the optimal observer framework of Wei & Stocker (2017), leaving open whether the univariate assumption produces contradictory predictions and whether a principled link exists between the transduction function in the encoding model and measurable psychophysical quantities.

## Input / Output

The method takes 1D stimulus parameters (spatial frequency mode/bandwidth, orientation bandwidth, or interpolation weight between two textures) and generates corresponding 128×128 grayscale texture stimuli (mean luminance 128, RMS contrast 39.7) that are either parametric Gaussian random fields or naturalistic textures with imposed VGG-19 feature statistics. It produces predicted perceptual scale functions (via Fisher information integration), empirically measured perceptual scales (via MLDS with 13 equally spaced levels and triplet comparisons within 3δ_s), and an Area Matching Score (AMS) that quantifies the signed agreement between predicted and measured scales. For naturalistic textures, the interpolation path is defined by Wasserstein barycenters of Gaussian feature distributions at VGG-19 layers 2–5.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is proving that the perceptual scale ψ equals the integral of the square root of the stimulus Fisher information (Proposition 2), under the assumption of constant internal Fisher information—a reformulation of Thurstone's law of comparative judgment. For Gaussian random fields, Fisher information is computed in closed form from the power spectrum via Whittle's formula (Proposition 3), and a convergence theorem for discrete spot noises (Proposition 1) establishes that assuming the observer measures local features (spatial frequency, orientation) or the full image yields identical perceptual scale predictions up to a multiplicative constant, resolving the tension between univariate and high-dimensional models. For naturalistic textures, Fisher information is derived from VGG-19 feature activations modeled as Gaussian vectors with Wasserstein barycenter interpolation (Proposition 4). Predictions are validated through MLDS difference scaling experiments (5 participants per texture pair) and quantified using a novel Area Matching Score. The main empirical finding is that the perceptual scale is predominantly driven by the stimulus power spectrum (auto-correlation), with wavelet representations providing a secondary explanation for one conflicting texture pair.

## Results

The main quantitative result is the Area Matching Score (AMS) comparing Fisher-information-based predictions to measured perceptual scales across 12 texture pairs. The GRF (power spectrum / auto-correlation) measurement assumption achieves the best average AMS of 0.92 ± 0.69 (99.5% bootstrapped CI), confirming that the stimulus power spectrum is the primary driver of perceptual scale. The single-pixel assumption scores close to 0 (predicting only linear behavior), while wavelet and VGG-19 assumptions yield average scores above 1, indicating systematic overestimation of early or late sensitivity. For parametric GRF textures, the measured spatial frequency mode scale is logarithmic (matching the Fisher information prediction), while spatial frequency bandwidth transitions from approximately linear at low values to supra-linear at intermediate values and saturates at the highest values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four measurement assumptions for the observer's internal representation: the full image as a Gaussian random field (GRF / power spectrum), single-pixel gray levels, steerable-pyramid wavelet activations, and VGG-19 feature activations at layers 2–5. The GRF assumption is the clear winner, achieving an average AMS of 0.92 ± 0.69 and correctly predicting whether each texture pair exhibits early (steep-to-shallow) or late (shallow-to-steep) sensitivity across all 10 non-conflicting pairs. The single-pixel assumption is the weakest, scoring near 0 because it can only predict a linear perceptual scale. The wavelet and VGG-19 assumptions both overestimate the degree of non-linearity (scores above 1), though the wavelet assumption performs best for the single conflicting pair (pair11), where it achieves a near-ideal score close to 1. For the two conflicting-prediction texture pairs (pair11, pair12), no assumption performs well: the GRF model predicts late sensitivity while the measured scale shows early sensitivity with late saturation, and all other assumptions score near 0 or negative. An additional power-spectrum-normalization experiment further confirms that removing the power spectrum change along the interpolation path degrades discriminability, reinforcing the conclusion that the power spectrum is the dominant driver of the measured perceptual scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected lines of work: the MLDS framework for measuring perceptual scales (Maloney & Yang, 2003; Knoblauch & Maloney, 2008) and the optimal-observer / Fisher-information theory of perception (Wei & Stocker, 2017; Brunel & Nadal, 1998). The key theoretical move is showing that the transduction function ψ in the encoding model (Eq. 2) is precisely the perceptual scale recovered by MLDS, and that it can be predicted from the Fisher information of the stimulus's generative model. For Gaussian random fields, a convergence theorem for discrete spot noises (Proposition 1) resolves the apparent tension between univariate Bayesian perception models and the high dimensionality of images, showing both yield identical perceptual-scale predictions. The texture interpolation framework of Vacher et al. (2020) and VGG-19 feature statistics (Gatys et al., 2015) are repurposed here to collapse high-dimensional naturalistic texture statistics into a 1D path amenable to difference-scaling measurement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main empirical finding—that the perceptual scale is predominantly driven by the stimulus power spectrum (auto-correlation) even for naturalistic textures with rich higher-order statistics—provides a parsimonious account of perceptual geometry that could inform generative model evaluation beyond pairwise distances. The Area Matching Score offers a concrete metric for comparing predicted vs. measured perceptual scales, but its practical utility is limited by the narrow stimulus set tested. Significant open questions remain: the conflicting-prediction pairs (pair11/12) where wavelet representations outperform the power spectrum suggest the story is incomplete; the power-spectrum-normalization experiment (Appendix H) is suggestive but unreliable; and the authors explicitly note that a neurometric validation (comparing to neural population recordings) is needed to ground the Fisher-information predictions in biology. The MLDS method's restriction to single-dimension stimuli and the lack of per-participant sensitivity calibration also limit generalizability.
