## Problem

Despite feature learning being a defining characteristic of deep learning, the theoretical understanding of what features are and how they interact with data and models remains under-explored. Prior theoretical frameworks like NTK define features independently of training data, and the multi-view model of Allen-Zhu and Li (2020) does not match empirical observations about how features are distributed. Additionally, the Generalization Disagreement Equality (GDE) was previously explained only under a strong calibration assumption, leaving its true origin unclear.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an ensemble of M=20 trained ResNet18 models, a labeled dataset (CIFAR-10 or SVHN), and the penultimate-layer activation vectors for all test points. It produces a binary interaction tensor Ω ∈ {0,1}^{M×N×T} encoding which of T clustered features are present in each of N data points and learned by each of M models, along with closed-form combinatorial expressions for expected accuracy and expected agreement as functions of six framework parameters (p_d, c, t_d, t_r, n_d, n_r).

## Method

Features are defined as the top-K principal components of each model's penultimate-layer activations, projected via SVD to obtain a parsimonious, non-redundant representation. Features across models are clustered using a greedy algorithm on pairwise Pearson correlations (thresholded at the 90th percentile), and features are matched to data points by thresholding ℓ∞-normalized activation magnitudes. The key theoretical contribution is a combinatorial framework that posits two types of features (dominant and rare) and two types of data (dominant data with few dominant features, rare data with many rare features), where models learn features via sampling proportional to data frequency. From this framework, closed-form expressions for expected accuracy and agreement are derived via hypergeometric combinatorics, and GDE emerges naturally as a consequence of the Pareto-like ratio between dominant and rare feature counts—without any calibration assumption. The framework's predictive power is validated by showing that merging classes preserves GDE while re-partitioning data by blue intensity breaks it, matching the theory's prediction that changing t_r/t_d disrupts the equality.

## Results

Using 20 ResNet18 models trained on CIFAR-10 (45k subset, ~84.5% test accuracy), the interaction tensor identifies T=680 feature clusters with a long-tailed frequency distribution. The combinatorial framework's key predictive result is that GDE (Generalization Disagreement Equality) holds approximately under class merging: the accuracy–agreement difference remains ~0.04–0.05 across superclasses K̃=2,3,5,10. However, GDE breaks when data is re-partitioned by blue intensity, with the accuracy–agreement gap varying from 0.05 to 0.10 across groups G₁–G₅, confirming the framework's prediction that changing the t_r/t_d ratio disrupts the equality. Numerical simulations further show that agreement closely tracks accuracy when the ratio of rare to dominant features (t_r/t_d, n_r/n_d) is held constant, but deviates when these ratios are altered independently.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark a new method against competing baselines; instead, it validates a theoretical framework by making a priori predictions and then testing them empirically. The framework is contrasted conceptually with the multi-view model of Allen-Zhu and Li (2020), which assumes multi-view data (containing all features) always yields high-confidence predictions. The paper's empirical observations directly contradict this: high-confidence data points actually contain fewer features, and low-confidence data contain more rare features. The framework's predictive power is demonstrated through two interventions designed from the theory before running experiments: (1) merging CIFAR-10 classes into superclasses, where GDE is predicted to hold and is confirmed (difference stays ~0.04–0.05), and (2) partitioning by blue intensity, where GDE is predicted to break and is confirmed (difference ranges 0.05–0.10). The paper also notes that the GDE phenomenon previously required a strong calibration assumption (Jiang et al., 2022), whereas the feature-learning framework explains it as a natural consequence of the distributional properties of features without any explicit calibration assumption.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the multi-view data model of Allen-Zhu and Li (2020), which assumes all features are equally learnable and that multi-view data (containing more features) is classified with high confidence by all ensemble members. Empirical observations via the newly introduced interaction tensor contradict this: high-confidence data actually contains fewer features, and features exhibit a long-tailed frequency distribution. The feature clustering procedure generalizes the pair-wise matching of Li et al. (2015) to PCA-projected representations, and the combinatorial framework is designed to explain the Generalization Disagreement Equality (GDE) from Jiang et al. (2022) without the strong calibration assumption that prior explanations required.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a calibration-free explanation for GDE, reducing reliance on an assumption whose validity in deep ensembles remains an open problem. The construction of natural data partitions (by blue intensity) that break GDE is, to the authors' knowledge, the first non-adversarial demonstration of in-distribution miscalibration in deep ensembles. Key limitations include the binary feature/data dichotomy, restriction to binary classification, and the absence of an optimization-level account of how features are learned. Extending the framework to multi-class settings, continuous feature spectra (e.g., Zipf distributions), and mechanistic optimization analysis are identified as important future directions.
