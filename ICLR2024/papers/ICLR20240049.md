## Problem

Current ML research predominantly focuses on improving model architectures or learning algorithms while keeping training data fixed, yet not every training sample contributes equally to model performance. This paper addresses the orthogonal question of *what data benefits a given classifier* by interpreting the feature space to identify which samples help or hurt performance with respect to utility, fairness, and robustness, and then selecting data to improve those metrics.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled training set Z, a validation set V, a trained convex classifier (logistic regression, or a linear surrogate on embeddings for non-convex models like MLPs/BERT), and a trimming budget b (typically 2.5–10% of training samples). It produces two outputs: (1) an interpretable CART regression tree (regularized via hierarchical shrinkage) that maps feature ranges and class labels to positive/negative influence regions, and (2) a trimmed training dataset Z' with the most negatively-influencing samples removed. In active learning, the tree is trained without labels and used to predict influence for unlabeled candidates, selecting the top-b highest-influence samples per round for annotation.

## Method

The core mechanism combines influence functions (Koh & Liang, 2017) with interpretable decision trees. For each training sample x_i, the influence on a chosen evaluation function f (utility loss, demographic parity, equal opportunity, or adversarial accuracy) is computed as ∇_θ f(θ̂)ᵀ H_θ̂⁻¹ ∇_θ ℓ(x_i, y_i; θ̂), where H is the Hessian of the convex loss. Algorithm 1 then trains a CART regression tree on the augmented feature vector [x_i, y_i] with the influence value as the response, applying hierarchical shrinkage (Agarwal et al., 2022) to regularize node predictions toward parent means, yielding an interpretable rule set that reveals which feature ranges drive positive or negative influence. Algorithm 2 performs data trimming by sorting all samples in ascending order of influence and removing the min{b, b'} most negatively-influencing samples, where b' is the count of samples with negative influence. The approach is model-agnostic within the convex/surrogate framework and extends to diverse scenarios—distribution shift, poisoning/evasion attacks, online learning with noisy labels, and active learning—by reusing the same influence computation and trimming logic with scenario-specific evaluation functions.

## Results

On toy data, influence-based trimming improves adversarial robustness from 0.01 to 0.42 by removing just 10 identified samples. On four real-world datasets (Adult, Bank, CelebA, Jigsaw Toxicity), trimming up to 5% of training data significantly improves fairness (DP) and robustness, while a random deletion baseline fails to do so; Bank additionally sees a 10% accuracy gain. Under fairness distribution shift (ACS Income), trimming with a vanilla logistic regression model becomes competitive with dedicated fairness interventions (Correlation Shift, FairBatch, RLL), and when combined with those methods, further boosts their accuracy. Against fairness poisoning attacks (NRAA on German), DP degrades from 0.117 to 0.268 post-attack but recovers to 0.005 after trimming, with no utility loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against fairness intervention baselines under distribution shift (Correlation Shift, FairBatch, RLL, and Influence-based Reweighing), the proposed trimming approach with a vanilla model matches or approaches their fairness levels, and when layered on top of these methods, it significantly improves their accuracy while maintaining or improving fairness. In active learning on Diabetic Retinopathy, influence-based sampling outperforms entropy sampling, margin sampling, uncertainty sampling, and the influence-based ISAL strategy in most of 10 rounds, achieving the best final-round performance. On the four real-world datasets, influence-based trimming consistently outperforms random deletion for fairness and robustness, with the gap widening as the trimming budget increases. In the data valuation comparison, influence-based trimming outperforms TMC-Shapley (DataShapley) on a subsampled Bank dataset (100 samples) for both utility and fairness metrics, while being orders of magnitude faster (seconds vs. >11 hours). The paper positions its approach as complementary to rather than directly competing with Shapley-value methods, noting that TMC-Shapley is computationally infeasible on the full-scale datasets used here.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the influence function framework of Koh & Liang (2017) to measure sample impact on utility, fairness, and robustness, but departs from prior influence estimation work (TracIn, representer points, Hydra) by adding a tree-based interpretation layer (using hierarchical shrinkage from Agarwal et al., 2022) that reveals which feature ranges drive positive or negative influence. It also extends the surrogate-model strategy from Li & Liu (2022) for handling non-convex models. Conceptually, the paper positions itself as orthogonal to data valuation (Shapley/Banzhaf), which assigns monetary worth to data, and to data efficiency/coreset methods, which prune for training speed; here the goal is to interpret and select data for a fixed model to improve specific performance dimensions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a simple, interpretable, and computationally cheap (seconds vs. hours for TMC-Shapley) data selection tool that generalizes across utility, fairness, and robustness objectives and across diverse deployment scenarios (distribution shift, poisoning, evasion, online learning, active learning). However, the approach is primarily validated on convex models (logistic regression, linear SVM) with only preliminary deep learning results (BERT on SST-2), and the trimming strategy is explicitly acknowledged as heuristic. Open questions include extending to truly non-convex deep architectures, developing more principled (non-heuristic) selection criteria, and studying robustness of large language models and deep unsupervised models under influence-based data selection.
