## Problem

Bilevel training tasks such as data cleaning and meta-learning are prone to overfitting due to their sensitivity to small perturbations and typically limited datasets. While dropout is a well-established regularizer for single-level optimization, no theoretical convergence guarantees exist for dropout methods applied to bilevel problems, and the interaction between random dropout masks and hypergradient computation remains unanalyzed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes corrupted training data (e.g., MNIST/FMNIST 784-dim grayscale vectors with 60% random mislabeling, CIFAR-10 32×32 RGB images, or Omniglot character images for few-shot meta-learning) and clean validation data, along with per-layer dropout rates p_{i,λ} and p_{i,w} specifying Bernoulli retention probabilities for upper- and lower-level parameters. It produces trained network weights (shared parameters λ and task-specific or lower-level parameters w), data-cleaning weights σ(λ_i) that down-weight corrupted samples, and for meta-learning, shared convolutional parameters plus task-specific linear layer parameters. Dropout is applied as random diagonal matrices with independent Bernoulli entries acting on the flattened weight vectors at each forward pass.

## Method

The paper formulates a stochastic bilevel optimization model in which both the upper-level objective F and lower-level objective G are composed with independent random dropout masks (diagonal matrices with Bernoulli(p) entries), yielding expected objectives F̃(λ) = E_{m_λ,m_w} F(m_λλ, m_w w(λ)) and G̃(λ,w) = E_{m_λ,m_w} G(m_λλ, m_w w). To solve this model, the authors adapt the Fully Single Loop Algorithm (FSLA) by inserting fresh dropout masks into every forward and backward pass: the lower-level SGD step uses one mask pair, the v-update (approximating the Hessian-inverse product) uses another, and the hypergradient estimate uses a third, with a projection onto a bounded ball to control v. The key theoretical contribution is a convergence analysis of the resulting dropout bilevel method, establishing that the expected squared hypergradient norm converges at rate O(1/√T) with constants that depend on the minimum and maximum dropout rates, and that additional error terms appear solely due to dropout (vanishing as p→1). The inductive relations for the lower-level iterate w^k and the auxiliary variable v^k reveal variance terms that scale with the dropout rates, and the analysis shows that the dropout method optimizes a modified objective rather than the original bilevel problem, which is precisely what provides the regularization effect against overfitting.

## Results

The paper presents results as figures rather than numerical tables. On data cleaning (MNIST, FMNIST, CIFAR-10), Figure 2 demonstrates that without dropout, increasing training accuracy leads to decreasing testing accuracy (overfitting), while applying dropout at appropriate rates (e.g., p=0.5, p=0.8) restores and improves testing accuracy with greater stability across iterations. On few-shot meta-learning (Omniglot, 5-way-1-shot), Figure 3 shows that adding dropout to any single CNN layer at a proper rate improves testing accuracy over the no-dropout baseline. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 2) further confirms that convergence rate degrades as the minimum dropout rate p̲ decreases, consistent with the observed slower convergence at lower dropout rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against named external baselines; instead, the primary comparison is between the proposed dropout bilevel method (FSLA with dropout) and the same FSLA method without dropout (i.e., all dropout rates set to 1). In data cleaning experiments, the no-dropout variant exhibits clear overfitting (training accuracy rises while testing accuracy falls), whereas the dropout variant achieves higher and more stable testing accuracy. In the Omniglot few-shot experiments, applying dropout to any single layer at a moderate rate consistently improves testing accuracy over the no-dropout setting. The paper also notes (Remark 3 and the 2D counterexample) that the dropout method optimizes a modified stochastic objective rather than the original bilevel problem, meaning the convergence guarantee applies to the dropout-augmented model, not the original one.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FSLA bilevel optimization algorithm (Li et al., 2022) by incorporating dropout masks into both the upper and lower level objectives, creating a stochastic bilevel model whose solution depends on the distribution of Bernoulli dropout masks. The modeling approach of treating dropout as a random diagonal matrix composition is borrowed from Senen-Cerda & Sanders (2020), who analyzed dropout SGD for single-level empirical risk minimization. The key departure is that no prior work had provided convergence guarantees for dropout methods in the bilevel setting, where the hypergradient couples the upper and lower level objectives through the lower-level solution, making the analysis substantially more complex than the single-level case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical convergence analysis for dropout in bilevel optimization, filling a gap that leaves practitioners without guarantees when applying dropout to data cleaning or meta-learning pipelines. The analysis reveals that the complexity bound depends on the dropout rates and that an irreducible error term appears solely due to dropout, which vanishes only when dropout is removed. A notable limitation is that convergence of the expected dropout-masked gradient does not imply convergence of the original (non-dropout) gradient, meaning the method may not be optimizing the original bilevel problem. Open questions include whether other bilevel methods (e.g., AID-based approaches) admit similar dropout adaptations and whether the gap between the dropout model and the original problem can be formally bounded or closed.
