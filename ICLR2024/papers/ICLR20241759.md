## Problem

Crystal structures are infinitely periodic, so applying the fully connected attention of standard Transformers (as in Graphormer for molecules) yields an infinite series over repeated atoms — a formulation that prior Transformer-based crystal encoders like Matformer sidestepped by falling back to finite radius-NN graphs, making them GNN-Transformer hybrids rather than faithful Transformers. This paper asks whether the standard Transformer architecture can be directly and effectively applied to periodic structures by properly formulating and computing this infinitely connected attention.

## Input / Output

The model takes a finite unit-cell representation (Cartesian coordinates of N atoms, their atomic numbers, and three lattice vectors) that implicitly encodes an infinite periodic structure, and must produce a single scalar property per material (formation energy, bandgap, bulk/shear modulus, total energy, or energy above hull). A key constraint is that the output must be invariant to the arbitrary choice of unit-cell slice — i.e., the same crystal described by a supercell or shifted periodic boundary must yield identical predictions. Atom states are aggregated via global average pooling before a small FFN head produces the final scalar.

## Method

Crystalformer interprets infinitely connected attention as a 'neural potential summation': an infinite interatomic potential summation performed in a learned abstract feature space, where attention weights act as distance-decay potentials. To make the infinite series tractable, it adopts a Gaussian distance-decay function exp(−‖p_j(n)−p_i‖²/2σ_i²) with a learnable tail length σ_i upper-bounded for convergence, and rewrites the infinite sum into a pseudo-finite form using two derived encodings: a periodic spatial encoding α_ij (log-sum of spatial dependencies over all images) and a periodic edge encoding β_ij (softmax-weighted average of position-dependent influences). A critical design choice is the value position encoding ψ_ij(n), implemented as a linear projection of Gaussian radial basis functions of interatomic distance, which is necessary to distinguish crystal structures with the same atom but different lattice vectors (e.g., graphite vs. diamond). The architecture consists of four stacked self-attention blocks (8 heads, d=128, no Layer Normalization, Huang et al. initialization) followed by global average pooling and a two-layer FFN, totaling only 853K parameters — 29.4% of Matformer's — while outperforming it on all nine regression tasks across Materials Project and JARVIS-DFT.

## Results

On the Materials Project dataset, Crystalformer achieves the best MAE on 3 of 4 tasks: formation energy (0.0186 eV/atom), bandgap (0.198 eV), and bulk modulus (0.0377 log(GPa)), while PotNet is slightly better on shear modulus (0.065 vs. 0.0689 log(GPa)). On JARVIS-DFT, Crystalformer achieves the best E hull (0.0463 eV) and competitive results on all other tasks, with PotNet marginally ahead on formation energy (0.0294 vs. 0.0306 eV/atom) and bandgap MBJ (0.27 vs. 0.274 eV). Notably, Crystalformer uses only 853K parameters—29.4% of Matformer's 2.9M and 48.6% of PotNet's 1.8M—while delivering the fastest inference speed (6.6 ms/material).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Crystalformer outperforms all GNN-based baselines (CGCNN, SchNet, MEGNet, GATGNN, M3GNet, ALIGNN) across all nine regression tasks on both datasets, with the largest margins over earlier methods like CGCNN and SchNet. Against the two strongest competitors, Crystalformer beats Matformer (the prior Transformer-based method) on every task in both datasets, e.g., reducing formation energy MAE from 0.021 to 0.0186 eV/atom on Materials Project and from 0.0325 to 0.0306 eV/atom on JARVIS-DFT, while using only 29.4% of Matformer's parameters. Against PotNet (the strongest GNN baseline), Crystalformer wins on 7 of 9 tasks but is narrowly outperformed on JARVIS-DFT formation energy (0.0306 vs. 0.0294 eV/atom), JARVIS-DFT bandgap MBJ (0.274 vs. 0.27 eV), and Materials Project shear modulus (0.0689 vs. 0.065 log GPa). The paper notes this is remarkable given that PotNet exploits known physical potential forms (Coulomb, London dispersion, Pauli repulsion) as strong inductive bias, whereas Crystalformer approximates them with simple Gaussian potentials. In terms of efficiency, Crystalformer is the fastest at inference (6.6 ms/material vs. 20.4 ms for Matformer and 313 ms for PotNet) despite having a longer total training time (7.2 h vs. 5.9 h for PotNet, which precomputes potential summations).

## Relation to Prior Work

Crystalformer positions itself as a faithful Transformer encoder for crystal structures, departing from the dominant GNN paradigm (CGCNN, SchNet, MEGNet, ALIGNN) and from Matformer, which the authors characterize as a Transformer-GNN hybrid that avoids the infinite attention formulation. Conceptually, it extends Shaw et al.'s relative position representations to the infinite periodic case by interpreting the resulting infinitely connected attention as a 'neural potential summation'—an infinite interatomic potential summation performed in a learned feature space—made tractable via Gaussian distance decay. The architecture closely follows the original Transformer (Vaswani et al.) with normalization-free training (Huang et al.), and the key departure from Graphormer is the addition of value position encoding ψ, which the paper proves is necessary to distinguish crystal structures with the same atom but different lattice vectors (e.g., graphite vs. diamond).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that a standard, fully connected Transformer can effectively encode periodic crystal structures without resorting to finite graph approximations, achieving competitive or superior performance with only 29.4% of Matformer's parameters. The neural potential summation interpretation provides physical interpretability and a natural pathway for incorporating known potential forms (Coulomb, van der Waals) or angular information in future work. Key open questions include whether the Gaussian-only potential form is sufficient, how the method scales to larger models and datasets, and whether the reciprocal-space attention (which dramatically improves E hull prediction) can be stably integrated via adaptive head selection. The σ upper bound that limits long-range interactions remains a practical constraint.
