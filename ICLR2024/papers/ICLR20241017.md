## Problem

Existing protection methods against diffusion-based mimicry (AdvDM, PhotoGuard, Mist) require 100+ backpropagation steps through the denoiser, making them computationally prohibitive for individual users (~16 GB VRAM, ~65 s per image). Moreover, the actual bottleneck in attacking a Latent Diffusion Model was unidentified, and the design space of adversarial optimization (e.g., whether minimizing rather than maximizing the semantic loss could also protect) remained unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single clean RGB image at 512×512 resolution and produces a protected image with an ℓ∞-bounded perturbation (δ = 16/255) applied via 100 PGD iterations. The protected image is then evaluated under three downstream mimicry scenarios: global SDEdit editing, masked inpainting (with unknown mask and prompt), and textual inversion (training a token on a subset of protected images). The perturbation must remain visually imperceptible while causing the LDM to generate chaotic, blurry, or target-pattern outputs.

## Method

The paper first demonstrates that the encoder E_φ, not the denoiser ε_θ, is the vulnerable component of an LDM: latent-space perturbations are ~10× larger than pixel-space perturbations, editing results correlate with D_ψ(E_φ(x_adv)) rather than the full denoising pipeline, and direct PGD attacks in z-space fail even at large budgets. Building on this, the authors replace the expensive full gradient ∇_x L_S (which requires backpropagating through the denoiser's Jacobian) with the Score Distillation Sampling approximation ∇_x L_SDS = E_{t,ε}[(ε_θ(z_t,t) − ε) ∂z_t/∂x], which only needs a forward pass through the denoiser, halving both VRAM (~8 GB vs ~16 GB) and runtime (~30 s vs ~65 s) without sacrificing protection strength. A second key finding is that gradient descent (minimizing) the semantic loss also fools the LDM by blurring edited outputs, while producing perturbations that are more harmonious with the original image's edge structure than gradient ascent. The resulting design space {L_S, L_T, SDS, GD/GA} yields plug-and-play methods SDS(+), SDS(−), and SDST that combine these strategies for different trade-offs between perturbation naturalness, computational cost, and protection strength across SDEdit, inpainting, and textual inversion.

## Results

On SDEdit protection (Table 2), SDS(-) achieves the highest human evaluation score (4.34/5) while using only ~8 GB VRAM and ~30 s per image—half the resources of AdvDM and MIST (~16 GB, ~65 s). In terms of raw protection strength (FID at edit strength iii), MIST (372.00) and PhotoGuard (375.32) remain the strongest, while SDS(-) achieves a competitive 231.57 with the most natural perturbation. SDS(+) matches AdvDM's protection strength (FID 299.25 vs. 297.33 at strength iii) at half the computational cost. For textual inversion, SDS(+) shows the strongest protection, matching AdvDM while being significantly cheaper to run.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AdvDM, SDS(+) matches its protection strength on SDEdit (FID 299.25 vs. 297.33 at edit strength iii) while halving both VRAM (~8 GB vs. ~16 GB) and runtime (~30 s vs. ~65 s), and SDS(−) achieves a superior human evaluation score (4.34 vs. 2.03) with a more natural perturbation (PSNR 29.56 vs. 29.07 in Table 1). Against MIST, SDS(−) trades some raw protection strength (FID 231.57 vs. 372.00 at strength iii) for a substantially more natural perturbation (HumanEval 4.34 vs. 3.19) and half the computational cost. Against PhotoGuard, SDS(−) again sacrifices peak FID (231.57 vs. 375.32) but wins decisively on perturbation naturalness (HumanEval 4.34 vs. 3.23) and runs at comparable speed. On textual inversion, SDS(+) is the strongest method, matching AdvDM's protection while being far cheaper, whereas SDS(−) is the weakest on this task. Overall, the paper's key trade-off is that gradient descent (SDS(−)) produces the most imperceptible perturbations at the cost of somewhat lower FID, while gradient ascent with textural loss (SDST) retains the highest protection strength at moderate cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial-protection-against-diffusion-mimicry line initiated by AdvDM, PhotoGuard, and Mist, which all frame protection as a PGD-based attack on the latent diffusion model using semantic and/or textural losses. The key conceptual departure is identifying the encoder (not the denoiser) as the actual vulnerability, which justifies borrowing Score Distillation Sampling from DreamFusion to skip the expensive denoiser Jacobian computation. The paper also departs from prior work by showing that gradient descent (minimizing) the semantic loss is a valid and more natural protection direction, whereas all prior methods only used gradient ascent (maximizing).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50% reduction in compute and memory makes diffusion-mimicry protection feasible for individual users on consumer hardware, addressing a practical barrier that prior methods could not overcome. The encoder-vulnerability insight reframes how one should think about attacking LDMs and suggests that future defenses should harden the encoder rather than the denoiser. Limitations include scope restricted to LDMs (pixel-space diffusion models resist these attacks entirely), partial vulnerability to purification methods like Crop & Resize, and the fact that textual inversion protection remains weaker for the gradient-descent variant. Open questions include whether the encoder-robustness finding generalizes to next-generation architectures and whether the blurring-based protection from gradient descent can be made more targeted.
