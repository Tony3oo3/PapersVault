## Problem

Spurious-correlation few-shot classification (SC-FSC), where models rely on non-causal contextual features correlated with labels in training but absent in testing, remains critically understudied due to the absence of a dedicated evaluation benchmark. Existing cross-domain FSC benchmarks (Meta-dataset, BSCD-FSL) construct shifts by swapping entire datasets, which does not capture the subtler context-level spurious correlations found in real-world deployment. Traditional machine learning spurious-correlation benchmarks (ColoredMNIST, NICO) are incompatible with FSC because they lack sufficient classes, samples, and contextual diversity.

## Input / Output

Each image in MetaCoCo encodes both a main concept (e.g., 'dog') and a contextual background (e.g., 'autumn', 'on snow', 'on grass'), with 155 distinct contexts across 100 concept classes totaling 175,637 images at 84×84 resolution. The episodic evaluation protocol samples N-way K-shot episodes where the support set provides K labeled images per class and the query set contains unlabeled images to classify; two sampling strategies are defined—IID (support and query share the same contexts) and OOD (support and query have different contexts)—to isolate the effect of context mismatch. The benchmark is split into 64 training, 16 validation, and 20 test concept classes, with label spaces disjoint between base and novel sets.

## Method

MetaCoCo is constructed by selecting 100 object categories from DomainNet, identifying 155 frequently co-occurring contexts via WordNet, and crawling images by searching category-context combinations (e.g., 'dog on grass'), followed by 2,500 hours of dual-annotator manual filtering that reduced 1.0 million raw images to 17.6k. A CLIP-based metric quantifies the extent of spurious correlation by computing cosine distances between image features and text embeddings for both concepts (M_ce) and contexts (M_te), revealing that MetaCoCo has significantly lower concept-image similarity and higher context-image similarity than existing FSC benchmarks. The paper evaluates 30+ state-of-the-art methods spanning fine-tuning, metric-based, meta-based, self-supervised, and cross-domain learning paradigms under standardized 5-way and 10-way 1-shot and 5-shot protocols with 3,000 total tasks. Key findings show that all existing methods degrade substantially under spurious-correlation shifts, cross-domain FSC methods lose their advantages, and self-supervised pretraining can even hurt performance, confirming that SC-FSC remains an open challenge.

## Results

On MetaCoCo (5-way 1-shot/5-shot), the best-performing methods achieve 60.23% (PUTM, metric-based) and 72.36% (PUTM) accuracy, while standard baselines like ProtoNet (ResNet18) reach only 43.14%/57.84% and MAML reaches 45.01%/54.21%. Self-supervised pre-training (MoCo, SimCLR with ResNet50) yields 56.90–58.12% 1-shot, but combining self-supervision with ProtoNet actually degrades performance (e.g., ProtoNet+rotation drops from 43.14% to 40.65% in 1-shot). Cross-domain FSC methods (ATA, FT) and the spurious-correlation-specific IFSL all show substantial performance drops, with IFSL+MAML falling from 44.09% to 43.42% in 1-shot. Overall, all methods perform significantly worse than on standard IID FSC benchmarks, confirming that spurious-correlation shifts remain an unsolved challenge.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 36+ baselines across five learning strategies (fine-tuning, metric, meta, self-supervised, and cross-domain) on MetaCoCo. Among fine-tuning methods, Yang et al. (ECCV 2022) leads with 58.01%/69.32%, while the original Baseline (ICLR 2019) achieves only 46.78%/60.78%. Among metric-based methods, PUTM (ICCV 2023) is the strongest at 60.23%/72.36%, substantially outperforming ProtoNet (43.14%/57.84%) and CAN (48.93%/62.36%). Among meta-based methods, CDKT+ML (NeurIPS 2023) leads at 44.86%/61.42%, but all meta methods trail the best metric and fine-tuning approaches. Self-supervised pre-training (MoCo, SimCLR with ResNet50) reaches 56.90–58.12% 1-shot, yet combining self-supervision with ProtoNet consistently hurts performance (e.g., ProtoNet+rotation drops from 43.14% to 40.65%). Cross-domain methods (ATA, FT) and the spurious-correlation-specific IFSL all lose their advantages on MetaCoCo: IFSL+MAML drops from 44.09% to 43.42% in 1-shot, and TPN+FT degrades from 49.65% to 45.62%, confirming that existing OOD and causal-learning strategies are insufficient for real-world spurious-correlation shifts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of few-shot classification (FSC) and spurious-correlation robustness. While cross-domain FSC benchmarks (Meta-dataset, BSCD-FSL) evaluate distribution shifts between distinct datasets, and traditional ML spurious-correlation benchmarks (ColoredMNIST, NICO, MetaShift) target binary or few-class settings, no prior benchmark specifically addresses spurious-correlation shifts in the multi-class, few-shot episodic paradigm. The paper repurposes DomainNet categories and WordNet co-occurrence statistics to construct controlled concept-context pairs, and introduces CLIP as a quantitative metric for measuring the extent of spurious correlation—a tool not previously applied to FSC benchmark characterization. Prior work by IFSL (Yue et al., 2020) and COSOC (Luo et al., 2021) identified the spurious-correlation problem in FSC but lacked a dedicated evaluation environment, which MetaCoCo fills.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MetaCoCo is likely to become a standard evaluation benchmark for assessing robustness in FSC, given that spurious correlations are pervasive in real-world deployment (e.g., medical imaging, autonomous driving). The key open question it surfaces is whether any existing learning strategy—meta-learning, self-supervised pre-training, or cross-domain adaptation—can effectively decouple causal concept features from non-causal context features in the few-shot regime. A notable limitation is that the benchmark relies on web-crawled images with manual filtering (~2,500 hours), which may introduce residual annotation noise, and the 100-class scale, while sufficient for FSC, is smaller than some real-world deployment scenarios. Future work should explore whether causal or invariant learning methods can close the gap revealed by this benchmark.
