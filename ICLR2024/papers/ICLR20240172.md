## Problem

While the Lipschitz constant governs robustness, generalisation, and adversarial vulnerability of neural networks, prior work has focused on computing tighter (but expensive) bounds or enforcing explicit Lipschitz regularisation, leaving the inherent phenomenology of the Lipschitz constant in standard unregularised over-parameterised networks largely uncharacterised. This paper asks: how does the Lipschitz constant actually evolve during training, how faithful are the simplest computable bounds to the effective value, and what is its relationship to Double Descent and label noise across a wide range of architectures and datasets?

## Input / Output

The method consumes trained network checkpoints (FCN ReLU, CNN, ResNet50, ViT) and their corresponding training sets, computing per-sample Jacobian 2-norms for the lower bound and per-layer spectral norms for the upper bound. For large-scale settings (ResNet50 on 200K ImageNet samples, ViT on 50K), evaluation is restricted to a subset of checkpoints and samples due to the high cost of Jacobian norm computation. For ViT, only the lower bound is reported because standard attention layers have no finite Lipschitz upper bound over unconstrained inputs. The primary outputs are Lipschitz bound trajectories over training epochs, Lipschitz-vs-width curves in Double Descent sweeps, and Lipschitz-vs-noise-strength curves at multiple network capacities.

## Method

The paper 'sandwiches' the true Lipschitz constant between two simple, cheaply computable bounds: a lower bound defined as the supremum of per-sample Jacobian 2-norms over the training set, and an upper bound given by the product of per-layer spectral norms (with 1-Lipschitz activations contributing a factor of 1). A central empirical contribution is establishing the fidelity of the lower bound to the effective Lipschitz constant through convex-combination evaluations, a tractable 2D toy example, and adversarial/OOD sample checks, showing the upper bound can be vacuously large (up to ~40 orders of magnitude for ResNet50). Using these validated bounds, the paper then uncovers three phenomena: (i) the Lipschitz constant grows substantially during training (e.g., 63× for a width-256 FCN), deviating far from its initialisation value; (ii) both bounds exhibit a Double-Descent-like non-monotonicity with network width, providing an implicit Lipschitz regularisation mechanism that the authors connect to a bias-variance trade-off argument; and (iii) under increasing label noise, the Lipschitz constant first rises then collapses toward zero once noise exceeds a capacity-dependent memorisation threshold, revealing an interplay between over-parameterisation, smoothness, and memorisation.

## Results

For FCN ReLU networks on MNIST1D, the lower Lipschitz bound grows by 63× (width 256) and 40× (width 65,536) relative to initialisation, while the upper bound grows by 66× and 10× respectively. On ResNet50 trained on ImageNet, the upper bound reaches ~1e27 at initialisation and diverges from the lower bound by ~40 orders of magnitude, confirming the upper bound is vacuously loose for deep networks. In a controlled toy example, the lower bound (135.8) closely tracks the densely-sampled effective Lipschitz constant (144.2), while the upper bound (389.1) overestimates by ~2.7×. All three Lipschitz bounds exhibit a Double-Descent-like non-monotonicity with network width on CIFAR-100, mirroring the test loss trend.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper without a proposed method to benchmark against competing approaches, so there are no traditional baseline comparisons. Instead, the paper compares three Lipschitz estimates (C_lower, C_upper, C_avg_norm) across settings to establish which best tracks the effective Lipschitz constant. The key finding is that C_lower (supremum of per-sample Jacobian norms on the training set) is far more faithful to the effective Lipschitz than C_upper (product of spectral norms), which becomes vacuously large for deep networks (e.g., ~40 orders of magnitude gap for ResNet50). The paper also contrasts its Jacobian-norm-based lower bound with the pairwise-difference lower bound (C_alt.lower), showing the former is consistently tighter (e.g., 19.39 vs. 8.84 at epoch 50,000 in Table S3). Relative to prior work tracking only average Jacobian norms (Geometric Complexity, Dherin et al. 2022) or single-point Lipschitz estimates (Gamba et al. 2023), the paper argues that simultaneously tracking both the lower and upper bounds provides a more faithful characterisation of the effective Lipschitz behaviour.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper deliberately departs from the two dominant lines of Lipschitz research—tighter estimation (Virmaux & Scaman, 2018; Gómez et al., 2020; Fazlyab et al., 2019) and explicit Lipschitz enforcement (Anil et al., 2018; Cissé et al., 2017; Gouk et al., 2021)—by instead using the simplest possible lower and upper bounds to study the phenomenology of the Lipschitz constant in unregularized, over-parameterized networks. It connects to the Double Descent literature (Belkin et al., 2019; Nakkiran et al., 2020) by proposing Lipschitz as the functional property underlying smooth interpolation, and to the memorization literature (Zhang et al., 2021) by extending the analysis to varying noise strengths and network capacities. The concurrent work of Gamba et al. (2023) and Dherin et al. (2022) on Geometric Complexity is acknowledged, but the authors argue their simultaneous tracking of both bounds provides a more faithful picture of the effective Lipschitz.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that over-parameterization induces an implicit Lipschitz regularization (visible as Double Descent in the Lipschitz bounds) provides a concrete functional mechanism for why wider networks generalize better, potentially reviving Lipschitz-based generalization bounds (Bartlett et al., 2017) in a more practical form. The non-monotonic relationship between label noise and Lipschitz constant—where the Lipschitz first increases then collapses as noise overwhelms capacity—offers a nuanced view of memorization that goes beyond the binary 'can/cannot memorize' framing. Key open questions include developing a theoretical lower bound on function variance in terms of the Lipschitz constant, understanding the exact mechanism behind the Lipschitz Double Descent, and extending these findings to language and reinforcement learning domains.
