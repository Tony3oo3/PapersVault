## Problem

Despite the widespread empirical success of unsupervised pretraining in modern ML systems, rigorous theoretical understanding of why it helps remains limited. Existing results are restricted to particular pretraining methods with specialized structural assumptions and, critically, do not prove a sample-complexity advantage over the baseline of performing supervised learning purely on labeled data. This paper asks whether a generic framework can provably explain the advantage of unsupervised pretraining across a wide range of settings.

## Input / Output

The framework takes two data streams: m unlabeled samples (x_i, s_i) where s_i is optional side information (e.g., a paired view in contrastive learning), and n labeled samples (x_j, y_j) with n typically much smaller than m. It outputs a pretrained latent variable model φ̂ (estimated via MLE on the marginal distribution of (x,s)) and a downstream predictor ψ̂ (estimated via ERM with φ̂ fixed), together with a high-probability excess risk bound of the form Õ(√(C_Φ/m) + √(C_Ψ/n)), where C_Φ and C_Ψ are bracketing/covering numbers of the respective function classes.

## Method

The paper introduces a generic two-phase algorithm (Algorithm 1): MLE on unlabeled data to estimate the latent variable model φ*, followed by ERM on labeled data to estimate the downstream predictor ψ* with φ̂ held fixed. The key theoretical ingredient is a mild 'informative' condition (Assumption 3.2) requiring that closeness in the marginal distribution of (x,s) implies closeness in the joint distribution of (x,z) up to a transformation group T_Φ that is neutralized by a corresponding transformation T_Ψ on the downstream task. Under this condition, the excess risk decomposes into a pretraining term scaling as √(C_Φ/m) and a downstream term scaling as √(C_Ψ/n), which strictly improves over the supervised-only baseline of √(C_{Φ∘Ψ}/n) when m ≫ n and the composite class is more complex than Ψ alone. A weaker 'weakly-informative' variant (Assumption 3.6) using Hellinger distance on the RHS extends the framework to contrastive learning. The framework is instantiated for three concrete settings: factor models with linear regression, Gaussian mixture models with classification, and contrastive learning with linear regression, each yielding explicit risk bounds in terms of dimension, number of components, and sample sizes.

## Results

The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.4) shows that under a κ⁻¹-informative condition, the two-phase MLE+ERM algorithm achieves excess risk of Õ(√(C_Φ/m) + √(C_Ψ/n)), where C_Φ and C_Ψ are complexity measures of the latent variable model and downstream task classes, and m, n are the unlabeled and labeled sample sizes. For the factor model instantiation (Theorem 4.3), the excess risk scales as Õ(κL√(dr/m) + L√(r/n)); for GMM classification (Theorem 5.3), it scales as Õ(√(dK/m) + √(K/n)); and for contrastive learning (Theorem 6.2), it scales as Õ(κL√(log N_[]/m) + L√(1/n)). These bounds improve over the supervised-only baseline of Õ(√(C_{Φ∘Ψ}/n)) whenever m ≫ n and the composite class complexity C_{Φ∘Ψ} exceeds C_Ψ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against the supervised-only baseline that performs ERM directly on the composite function class Φ∘Ψ using only n labeled data, which achieves excess risk Õ(√(C_{Φ∘Ψ}/n)). The paper's pretraining approach provably outperforms this baseline when m ≫ n and C_{Φ∘Ψ} > C_Ψ, a regime that holds in practical settings such as training a linear head on top of a pretrained deep network. For the GMM example, direct supervised classification would scale as Õ(√(dK/n)), while pretraining reduces this to Õ(√(dK/m) + √(K/n)), yielding a clear advantage when d is large and m ≫ n. Compared to prior theoretical works (Lee et al. 2021 on reconstruction-based SSL and HaoChen et al. 2021 on contrastive learning), this paper's framework is more general: it requires only abstract function classes and a mild informative condition, rather than specialized structural assumptions like conditional independence or spectral conditions on adjacency matrices. The contrastive learning result (Theorem 6.2) morally aligns with HaoChen et al. (2021) but uses a weaker Hellinger-distance-based informative condition instead of deterministic feature maps and spectral assumptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior theoretical analyses of unsupervised pretraining (Saunshi et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2021; Arora et al., 2019; HaoChen et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2021), which were each restricted to a specific pretraining method and relied on specialized structural assumptions (e.g., conditional independence, deterministic feature maps, spectral conditions). Rather than analyzing one method at a time, it introduces a generic latent-variable framework parameterized by abstract function classes Φ and Ψ, with a single 'informative' condition that unifies the analysis. It also distinguishes itself from multitask representation learning (Baxter, 2000; Tripuraneni et al., 2021), which learns representations from labeled data of other tasks, by focusing on unlabeled data and MLE-based pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives researchers a concrete, reusable starting point: for any new pretraining method, one checks whether the informative condition holds, and if so, the sample complexity guarantee follows immediately. This is a meaningful step toward demystifying why pretraining works, moving the field from method-specific justifications to a general principle. Limitations include the reliance on MLE (which many modern algorithms only approximate via variational bounds or contrastive proxies), the abstractness of the complexity measures (bracketing numbers, Rademacher complexity) that may be hard to instantiate for deep networks, and the restriction to linear-head fine-tuning rather than full fine-tuning. Open questions include whether the framework extends to non-parametric or adaptive representation learning, and whether the informative condition can be verified or estimated from data in practice.
