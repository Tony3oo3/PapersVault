## Problem

Multilingual LLMs trained on non-parallel corpora exhibit strong translation ability, and prior work attributed this to incidental sentence-level bilingualism in pre-training data. However, Briakou et al. (2023) showed that translation persists even after removing such sentence-level bilingualism, leaving the finer-grained sources of translation signal unexplained. This paper asks: what other forms of unintentional bilingualism (word alignment, code-switching) or even pure monolingual data contribute to LLM translation capability?

## Input / Output

For post-trained models, input is a few-shot prompt containing n in-context source-target translation pairs followed by a new source sentence, and output is a decoded target-language translation scored by COMET, BLEURT, and InstructScore. For pre-trained models that cannot produce readable translations, the method instead computes conditional perplexity of the reference translation given the source (bilingual perplexity) and compares it against monolingual target-language perplexity; a lower bilingual perplexity indicates positive mutual information between languages. The data mining pipeline itself takes raw multilingual documents from mC4 and outputs categorized fragments of sentence alignment, word alignment, and code-switching, plus purified monolingual corpora with all cross-lingual fragments removed.

## Method

The paper develops a data mining pipeline that identifies three granularities of unintentional bilingualism (sentence alignment, word alignment, code-switching) from mC4 by detecting cross-lingual fragments via regex, translating them with a commercial MT system, and classifying by sacreBLEU similarity to nearby context. To quantify each type's contribution without training full LLMs from scratch, two surrogate methods are used: (1) post-training BLOOM-560m/7.1b on each bilingualism type versus a size-matched random sample (X-rand), and (2) pre-training BLOOM-560m from scratch on the same data, evaluated via bilingual perplexity rather than decoded output. The key finding is that word alignment data yields translation signal comparable to or exceeding sentence alignment, explaining why translation survives removal of sentence-level bilingualism. Additional ablations show that purified monolingual data alone produces a slight translation signal (especially EN→ZH) attributable to shared tokens (digits, symbols) acting as alignment anchors and to parameter sharing in the Transformer architecture, both of which are confirmed by removing digits or replacing shared layers with language-specific ones.

## Results

Post-training BLOOM-560m on word alignment (WA) data yields COMET scores of 60.99 (ZH-EN 3-shot) and 72.47 (EN-ZH 5-shot), significantly outperforming WA-rand (58.47 and 68.23) and matching or exceeding sentence alignment (SA) results (61.55 and 69.98). Sentence alignment also shows strong gains over SA-rand (e.g., 61.55 vs. 54.87 COMET on ZH-EN 3-shot), while code-switching (CS) provides only modest improvements (59.02 vs. 58.43 COMET on ZH-EN 3-shot). In pre-training experiments, WA achieves bilingual perplexity of 80.58 (ZH-EN 1-shot) versus 154.89 for WA-rand, confirming that word-level bilingualism is a primary source of translation signal even from scratch.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external translation systems but instead isolates the contribution of each type of unintentional bilingualism by comparing each data type (SA, WA, CS) against a randomly sampled counterpart (SA-rand, WA-rand, CS-rand) of equal size. Word alignment (WA) is the headline finding: it outperforms WA-rand by 2.5–4.2 COMET points across all settings and, remarkably, matches or exceeds sentence alignment (SA) on EN-ZH (72.47 vs. 69.98 COMET at 5-shot), likely because WA data is ~800× more abundant than SA in mC4. Sentence alignment also significantly beats SA-rand (e.g., +6.7 COMET on ZH-EN 3-shot), confirming its known role. Code-switching (CS) provides only a marginal edge over CS-rand (+0.6 COMET on ZH-EN 3-shot), indicating it contributes weak translation knowledge. The original BLOOM-560m baseline (53.62 COMET ZH-EN 3-shot) is substantially below all post-trained variants, confirming that the bilingual data configurations drive the gains rather than generic continued pre-training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Briakou et al. (2023), who showed that incidental sentence-level bilingualism in PaLM's pre-training corpus partially explains its translation capability, by investigating finer-grained forms of unintentional bilingualism (word alignment and code-switching) that prior work overlooked. It also connects to the language contamination literature (Blevins & Zettlemoyer, 2022) and the unsupervised cross-lingual transfer literature (Pires et al., 2019; Artetxe et al., 2020) on how monolingual data can support cross-lingual alignment. Methodologically, it departs from Briakou et al.'s approach of extracting translation pairs to train an external NMT model, instead directly training LLMs on the collected data and evaluating translation via in-context prompting, which is necessary because word-level and code-switching data cannot be trivially converted into sentence-level parallel pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that word alignment data provides translation signals comparable to or exceeding sentence alignment data offers a concrete explanation for why LLM translation persists after sentence-level bilingualism is removed, a gap left by prior work. The observation that even purified monolingual data yields a slight translation signal (via shared tokens like digits and parameter sharing) suggests that translation capability in LLMs is more robustly grounded than previously assumed. Limitations include reliance on BLOOM-560m/7.1b, focus primarily on English-Chinese, and under-converged pre-training experiments that rely on perplexity rather than decoded translation quality. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to larger models, more language pairs, and whether the identified data sources can be deliberately augmented to improve low-resource translation.
