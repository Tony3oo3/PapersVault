## Problem

Data contamination—where test data from downstream benchmarks leaks into LLM pre-training corpora—can inflate reported model performance and undermine evaluation validity. Existing detection methods require direct access to pre-training data, substantial computational resources, or extensive manual labor, making them infeasible for closed-source models like GPT-4. This paper asks whether contamination can be reliably detected using only API access to the LLM and a small sample of dataset instances.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a random-length initial segment of a reference instance (cut at a sentence boundary or mid-sentence), the dataset name, partition name, and label (when applicable), all packaged into a 'guided instruction' prompt sent to the LLM under test. Only 10 randomly sampled instances per partition are used, keeping the procedure inexpensive. Outputs include per-instance match classifications (exact, near-exact, or inexact), a binary partition-level contamination decision, and—under Algorithm 1—ROUGE-L and BLEURT overlap scores with bootstrap-resampling p-values (10,000 resamples, significance at p ≤ 0.05) comparing guided versus general instruction completions.

## Method

The approach operates at two levels. At the instance level, a 'guided instruction' prompt embeds the dataset name, partition, initial segment, and label, instructing the LLM to complete the partial instance; a parallel 'general instruction' omits the dataset/partition identifiers to establish a baseline. At the partition level, two algorithms generalize from 10 instance-level signals: Algorithm 1 declares contamination if the average ROUGE-L or BLEURT score of guided-instruction completions is statistically significantly higher than that of general-instruction completions under non-parametric bootstrap resampling; Algorithm 2 (the top performer) uses GPT-4 with a few-shot in-context learning prompt containing human-labeled examples of exact and near-exact matches to classify each completion, flagging the partition as contaminated if at least one exact match or at least two near-exact matches are detected. The key insight is that the dataset/partition name in the guided instruction acts as a retrieval cue: a contaminated LLM will reproduce the continuation verbatim or near-verbatim, while an uncontaminated LLM produces only plausible but non-matching text. The method was validated by intentionally fine-tuning GPT-3.5 on 100 training instances from AG News, RTE, and XSum, confirming that even a single exact replica reliably signals contamination.

## Results

Across 28 settings (2 LLMs × 14 dataset partitions), the best method (Algorithm 2: GPT-4 few-shot ICL classifier) achieves 100% accuracy (14/14) for GPT-4 and 92.86% (13/14) for GPT-3.5 in matching human expert contamination labels. Algorithm 1 (statistical significance of BLEURT/ROUGE-L overlap between guided and general instructions) achieves 92.86% (13/14) for GPT-4 with ROUGE-L but drops to 50% (7/14) for GPT-3.5. The paper further confirms that GPT-4 is contaminated with AG News, WNLI, and XSum test partitions, while GPT-3.5 is contaminated only with the XSum test partition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the ChatGPT-Cheat? method (Sainz et al. 2023), which asks an LLM to reproduce the first instances of a dataset split and flags the partition as contaminated if it does. Under strict evaluation (where the 'suspicious' label is not counted as correct), ChatGPT-Cheat? achieves 0/14 (0%) for GPT-4 because safety filters cause it to label every partition as 'suspicious' rather than contaminated or uncontaminated, and 11/14 (78.57%) for GPT-3.5. Under lenient evaluation (where 'suspicious' is optimistically mapped to the correct class), it reaches 9/14 (64.29%) for GPT-4 and 13/14 (92.86%) for GPT-3.5. The proposed Algorithm 2 (GPT-4 ICL) outperforms ChatGPT-Cheat? in every setting: 14/14 vs. 9/14 for GPT-4 and 13/14 vs. 13/14 for GPT-3.5 (lenient), with the advantage being more robust since it does not rely on post-hoc reinterpretation of ambiguous labels. Algorithm 1 (ROUGE-L) also beats ChatGPT-Cheat? for GPT-4 (13/14 vs. 9/14) but underperforms for GPT-3.5 (7/14 vs. 11/14 strict), highlighting that the ICL-based approach is more consistent across models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant n-gram and substring-matching approaches to contamination detection (Radford et al. 2019; Brown et al. 2020; Touvron et al. 2023b), which require direct access to pre-training corpora and substantial compute. It is closest in spirit to Sainz et al. (2023), who also probe for contamination without pre-training data access by asking an LLM to reproduce the first instances of a dataset split; however, the authors demonstrate that this 'ChatGPT-Cheat?' strategy is unreliable due to safety filters and sparsity. The paper's key methodological contribution is the 'guided instruction' paradigm—embedding dataset name, partition, and a partial instance into a completion prompt—combined with either bootstrap-resampled statistical tests (BLEURT/ROUGE-L) or a GPT-4 few-shot ICL classifier to generalize from instance-level to partition-level contamination decisions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that GPT-4 has ingested test partitions of AG News, WNLI, and XSum (and training partitions of IMDB and RTE) directly undermines the validity of benchmark evaluations reported in the literature, making this a practically urgent concern for the NLP community. The method's simplicity (10 instances per partition, API-only access, no pre-training data) makes it broadly adoptable for auditing any closed-source LLM. Key limitations include the small sample size (10 instances), evaluation restricted to two OpenAI models and seven datasets, and the reliance on GPT-4 as both the evaluator and the model under test in some settings, which raises circularity concerns. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-English datasets, multimodal benchmarks, or models with different safety-filter architectures.
