## Problem

Standard adversarial training (AT) generates adversarial perturbations for every training example, making it unclear whether the model learns class-specific robustness or a systematic, transferable form of robustness. It remains unexplored whether robustness can generalize to classes or examples that were never adversarially attacked during training, and whether the computational cost of attacking all examples is truly necessary.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241873/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100 and SVHN, 224×224 for ImageNet-200, Caltech-256, and Flowers-102) and outputs class labels via a CNN classifier (ResNet-18 or ResNet-50). The key asymmetry is in training: examples in subset A receive adversarial perturbations (PGD-7 within an L₂ ball of ε=0.5 for CIFAR or ε=3.0 for ImageNet), while examples in subset B are trained only on clean inputs, yet the model is evaluated for robustness on the full test set.

## Method

Subset Adversarial Training (SAT) modifies the standard AT min-max objective by applying the inner adversarial maximization only to a pre-defined subset A of the training data, while minimizing standard cross-entropy on the complement B (equation 2). Two splitting strategies are explored: CSAT splits by class (using average softmax entropy to rank class difficulty) and ESAT splits by individual example entropy, with the hardest examples selected first. A weighted loss formulation allows overemphasizing A over B, which proves critical for downstream task transfer. The paper demonstrates that robustness transfers to never-attacked classes (e.g., attacking only 'cat' on CIFAR-10 yields 37.8% robust accuracy on all other classes), that 50% of training data suffices to recover most full-AT performance, and that with appropriate loss weighting, only 30% of adversarial examples recover baseline AT robustness on downstream tasks like Caltech-256 and Flowers-102.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, single-class CSAT with A={cat} achieves 37.8% robust accuracy on the 9 non-attacked classes (B), while A={car} achieves only 17.1%, compared to ~0% without any AT. ESAT with only 50% of training data (25k on CIFAR, 112k on ImageNet-200) recovers near-baseline full-AT robust accuracy (69% on CIFAR-10). For downstream task transfer, S-ESAT with just 30% of source data (15k on CIFAR-100, 70k on ImageNet-200) recovers baseline AT performance on CIFAR-10, Caltech-256, and Flowers-102. Combining CSAT (A={cat}) with TRADES (β=12) further improves robust accuracy on B to 42.9% (+5.1 pp over vanilla CSAT).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an empirical study rather than a method-vs-method comparison, so it benchmarks SAT against three internal baselines: (1) full AT (all examples attacked), (2) no AT (~0% robust accuracy), and (3) random subset selection. Against full AT, ESAT at 50% of data recovers most of the baseline robust accuracy on both CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-200, and S-ESAT at 30% of source data recovers baseline AT performance on downstream tasks. Against random selection, hardest-first (entropy-ranked) selection consistently yields higher robust accuracy on B_val for CSAT and higher robust accuracy overall for ESAT, though the gap diminishes on larger, more complex datasets (ImageNet-200) where random selection is nearly on par. Against the no-AT baseline, even a single-class CSAT (A={car}, the easiest class) provides a non-trivial 17.1% robust accuracy on all remaining classes, and A={cat} more than doubles this to 37.8%. For loss weighting in the transfer setting, overemphasizing A (w_A = 2^{|B|/|A|} to 10^{|B|/|A|}) substantially outperforms both vanilla (w_A=w_B=1) and balanced (w_A=|B|/|A|) weightings on downstream robust accuracy, e.g. improving CIFAR-10 transfer from 8% to 30% at |A|=10.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial training framework of Madry et al. (2018) by introducing Subset Adversarial Training (SAT), which restricts adversarial perturbation generation to a predefined subset A of the training data while training on the full set A∪B. It departs from prior adaptive-subset methods (Hua et al., 2021; Dolatabadi et al., 2022) and example-dependent threat models (Ding et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2021), which still guarantee the model encounters AEs for every training example, by *permanently excluding* subset B from adversarial perturbation. The work also connects to the robustness-transfer literature (Salman et al., 2020; Shafahi et al., 2020) and class-wise robustness disparity studies (Tian et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2021), but uniquely uses the subset split as an analytical lens to probe whether robustness is learned generically or class-specifically, drawing on systematic-generalization concepts (Bahdanau et al., 2019; Omran & Schiele, 2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that only 30% of adversarial examples recover baseline AT robustness on downstream tasks has direct practical implications for foundation-model training, where full AT is prohibitively expensive on very large datasets. The observation that hard examples and classes provide better robustness transfer than easy ones—though this effect diminishes with dataset complexity—opens questions about what properties of training data (difficulty, diversity, proximity to decision boundaries) actually drive robust feature learning. A key limitation is that the hard/easy ranking based on entropy provides only marginal gains over random selection on complex datasets like ImageNet-200, suggesting the mechanism behind robustness transfer is not yet fully understood. Future work could investigate whether the subset-based perspective generalizes to other robustness notions (certified, distributional) or to non-CNN architectures.
