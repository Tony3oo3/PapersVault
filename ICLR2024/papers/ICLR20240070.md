## Problem

Contextual stochastic optimization requires making decisions under uncertain, context-dependent parameters, and practitioners often need robustness beyond minimizing expected cost. Existing end-to-end learning methods target only average decision cost, while traditional robust optimization relies on hand-crafted uncertainty sets. This paper asks: can one build a data-driven, non-parametric method that directly produces robust decisions without assuming a distributional form or constructing uncertainty sets, controlled by a single user-defined threshold?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240070/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes N training pairs (x^i, ν^i) of observed covariates and realized uncertain parameters, plus two user-specified scalars: a discretization radius ε (controlling subset granularity) and a robustness threshold φ (controlling the cost–robustness trade-off). For a new out-of-sample feature vector x, it outputs a single decision ŵ(x) ∈ P that minimizes the expected regret exceeding φ. The feasible region P and objective structure g_ν(w) are problem-specific (linear, quadratic, or discrete), and the method is agnostic to their form.

## Method

The framework discretizes the feasible region P into N subsets H_k^ε = {w : R_{ν^k}(w) ≤ ε}, each capturing decisions near-optimal for one training realization. It then constructs a multi-label classification dataset where each training point x^n is labeled by whether w*(ν^n) falls inside each H_k^ε, and trains arbitrary classifiers (KNN, neural nets, trees) to estimate p̂_k^ε(x) ≈ P(w*(ν_x) ∈ H_k^ε). The final decision solves a deterministic secondary optimization: min_{w∈P} Σ_k p̂_k^ε(x)·max{R_{ν^k}(w) − φ, 0}, which approximates the minimum-violation objective E[max{R_{ν_x}(w) − φ, 0}]. This objective is convex when g_ν is convex, interpolates between expected-regret minimization (φ=0) and full worst-case robustness (φ large), and avoids the non-tractability of directly minimizing P(R ≥ φ). A regret bound of order O(M_1) + O(1/√N) is proven under bounded-regret and bi-Lipschitz/quantile-loss assumptions, with ε chosen so that the label density γ_ε ≥ 1/√N.

## Results

On the PJM electricity generation problem (8 years of real grid data, N=2,553 training points), the discretization method with ε=0.05 achieves a worst-case cost that is up to 20× lower than CVaR and other end-to-end methods, and 5× lower than traditional robust optimization approaches. On the synthetic inventory stock problem, the discretization method consistently achieves lower q-th quantile cost (for q=85 to 100) at the same average cost compared to KNN+minimum-violation, CVaR, and task-based methods; at q=100, all other approaches perform worse than even the policy optimizer. On the inventory allocation problem, the method achieves 15–20% lower worst-case cost at the 99th–100th quantiles and up to 25% lower cost at a supply capacity of 1100 compared to adaptive robust, KL-divergence, and variation-distance baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Donti et al. (2017) (task-based end-to-end neural network), the discretization method is competitive on average cost but substantially outperforms it on robustness: at the 100th quantile (worst case), the task-based method performs poorly while the discretization method maintains near-constant cost. On the PJM load forecasting task, at γ_ε=0.025 the discretization method even outperforms the task-based method on average cost during peak demand hours (15–22). Against Bertsimas & Kallus (2020) (KNN-weighted minimum-violation and CVaR), the discretization method produces lower quantile costs at every level (85th through 100th) for the same mean cost, and the gap widens as the quantile approaches 100; the CVaR approach also exhibits discrete jumps in decisions as the robustness parameter changes, whereas the minimum-violation objective yields smoother transitions. Against Ben-Tal et al. (2013) (KL-divergence robust optimization), the discretization method achieves up to 25% lower worst-case cost in the inventory allocation setting at higher capacities, and 5× lower worst-case cost in the electricity generation problem. Against Bertsimas & Sim (2004) (budget uncertainty set for portfolio optimization), the discretization method is competitive on average return while providing better tail protection without requiring manual tuning of the budget-of-uncertainty parameter Γ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from both the end-to-end learning paradigm (Donti et al. 2017, Amos & Kolter 2017, Elmachtoub & Grigas 2022), which differentiates through optimization to train predictive models, and the prescriptive analytics line (Bertsimas & Kallus 2020, Bertsimas et al. 2019a), which generates data-driven weights for optimization. Instead, it introduces a discretization-based approach that partitions the feasible region into regret-bounded subsets, learns via multi-label classification how the optimal solution maps to each subset, and then solves a secondary optimization weighted by these learned probabilities. The minimum-violation objective (minimizing expected regret above a user-controlled threshold φ) is a novel formulation that smoothly interpolates between expected-cost minimization (φ=0) and full robustness (φ large), avoiding the need for explicit uncertainty sets as in traditional robust optimization (Bertsimas & Sim 2004, Ben-Tal et al. 2013).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework is broadly applicable to any class of optimization problems (linear, nonlinear, discrete) without requiring distributional assumptions or differentiability of the optimization task, which are common limitations of end-to-end methods. The theoretical regret bound (O(1/√n)) and stability guarantee under data perturbation provide practical confidence in the approach. Open questions include how to efficiently choose the discretization parameter ε in high-dimensional settings, whether the method extends to uncertainty in constraints (not just the objective), and whether alternative constructions of the subsets H_k^ε could improve the constant factors in the regret bound. The computational cost of solving N sub-problems and training N classifiers may limit scalability to very large datasets or high-dimensional decision spaces.
