## Problem

Prior training data extraction attacks on LLMs require the adversary to know the exact prefix preceding a secret and typically rely on heavy duplication of the target data. This paper asks whether an attacker with only a vague structural prior on the prefix (e.g., 'it looks like a user biography') and the ability to insert a handful of benign-appearing sentences into the training data can still extract high-entropy secrets like 12-digit credit card numbers from a fine-tuned model.

## Input / Output

The attacker inserts 10–100 short poisoned sentences (each roughly one sentence long, with a prefix crafted from a vague prior and random or 'not'-prefixed digits) into the pretraining or fine-tuning corpus. At inference, the attacker supplies a text prompt prefix—either the true secret prefix or random perturbations of it (shuffling proper nouns, changing tokens)—and the model's autoregressive output is checked for exact match to the secret digit string (4–21 digits, e.g., 12-digit CCN). Success is binary: the model must generate the full secret verbatim; partial digit matches receive no credit.

## Method

The neural phishing attack operates in three phases. In Phase I (poisoning), the attacker inserts a small number of benign-appearing documents into the pretraining dataset, each containing a prefix structurally similar to the expected secret prefix (e.g., a GPT-generated biography of Alexander Hamilton) followed by random digits, often with the word 'not' prepended to the digits to prevent the model from overfitting to the poison. In Phase II (fine-tuning), the actual secret appears in the fine-tuning dataset even once; because the model has already been 'taught to phish'—i.e., primed to memorize sensitive digit strings following a particular textual pattern—it memorizes the secret during standard fine-tuning. In Phase III (inference), the attacker prompts the model with the true prefix or, more powerfully, with N random perturbations of the prefix and takes a majority vote over the generated digits. The key insight is that the poisons induce a generalized memorization pattern rather than a fixed prefix-to-suffix mapping, so the model can be coaxed into regurgitating the secret even when the inference prefix differs from the training prefix, and the learned behavior persists for up to 100,000 clean training steps after poisoning.

## Results

The neural phishing attack achieves 10–15% Secret Extraction Rate (SER) for 12-digit secrets using only 50 random poisons (no knowledge of the secret prefix), compared to a 1/10¹² random-guessing baseline and 0% without any poisoning. With a vague prior (e.g., a GPT-generated biography of Alexander Hamilton as the poison prefix), SER rises to ~40%; with an exact match on the secret prefix, SER reaches ~67% with just 25 poisons. Duplicating the secret in the fine-tuning data increases SER by approximately 20 percentage points, and scaling from 1.4B to 6.9B parameters monotonically improves extraction. When poisons are inserted during pretraining, the phishing behavior persists for over 10,000 clean training steps, retaining ~30% SER even after extensive additional training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing attack methods in a head-to-head table; instead, it benchmarks the neural phishing attack against three reference points. First, without any poisoning, the model never extracts the secret (0% SER), confirming the attack is entirely driven by the injected poisons. Second, random guessing of a 12-digit number yields a 10⁻¹² success probability, making the observed 10–80% SER roughly 10¹¹× better than chance. Third, prior training-data extraction work (Carlini et al., 2023b; Anil et al., 2023) reports average extraction success on the order of 1% for typical training samples, whereas neural phishing targets specific high-entropy PII and achieves 10–80% under the same model family (Pythia). The paper also shows that standard deduplication defenses (Lee et al., 2021) are ineffective because the attacker can trivially vary each poison to ensure uniqueness, and that appending the word 'not' before poison digits prevents the model from overfitting to the poison rather than the secret, turning a concave SER curve into a monotonically increasing one.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of training data extraction (Carlini et al., 2019; 2021; 2023b) and data poisoning (Steinhardt et al., 2017; Tramer et al., 2022), but carves new ground by combining them: the attacker poisons the pretraining set to *induce* a memorization behavior that makes single-occurrence secrets extractable, rather than relying on natural memorization of duplicated data. Prior extraction work assumes the attacker knows the exact prefix preceding the secret and typically targets heavily duplicated data; here the attacker needs only a vague structural prior (e.g., 'the prefix resembles a user bio') and the secret appears at most twice. The durability analysis builds on Zhang et al. (2022) for backdoor persistence in federated learning, but extends it to show that the induced phishing behavior survives 10,000+ clean training steps—far beyond what prior poisoning-durability work had demonstrated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical stakes are high: companies are actively fine-tuning LLMs on proprietary employee data (emails, wikis, Slack), and this work shows that a single insider or a web-scraped poison can enable extraction of 12-digit PII (credit cards, SSNs) at 10–80% success with minimal attacker knowledge. Standard defenses like deduplication are shown to be ineffective because poisons can be trivially varied, and the paper does not evaluate differential privacy at scale, leaving a critical open question about whether DP can actually defend against this attack vector. Open questions include whether the attack scales to 70B+ parameter models, whether multi-secret extraction degrades gracefully, and what practical defenses (beyond removing all N-digit strings, which harms utility) can mitigate the risk.
