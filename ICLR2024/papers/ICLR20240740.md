## Problem

Data attribution for diffusion models is critical for assigning credit or valuations to training samples, yet the theoretically natural adaptation of TRAK (using the training loss gradient as the attribution signal) performs poorly in practice. This paper investigates why counter-intuitive, theoretically unjustified design choices empirically outperform the principled construction, and proposes a more effective and efficient attribution method for diffusion models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240740/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained diffusion model (unconditional DDPM at 32×32 or 64×64, or LoRA-finetuned Stable Diffusion at 256×256), a target image (either a held-out validation sample or a newly generated image), and the full training dataset, and produces a scalar attribution score for each training sample. Gradients are computed at a small number of uniformly spaced timesteps (10 or 100 out of T=1000) with one sampled Gaussian noise per timestep, then randomly projected to dimension k=32768 before the kernel-based score is computed. The output is a rank-ordered list of training samples by their influence on the target image.

## Method

D-TRAK modifies TRAK in two key ways: (1) it replaces the theoretically justified projection φ^s(x) = P_s^T ∇_θ L_Simple(x, θ_s*) with gradients of alternative norm-based losses (L_Square, L_Avg, L_1-norm, L_2-norm), and (2) it eliminates the weighting matrix Q, setting it to identity. The paper shows via gradient expansion that L_Simple multiplies ∇_θ ε_θ by (ε_θ − ε)^T while L_Square multiplies it by ε_θ^T, and that the norm-based losses better retain the model-output information in ∇_θ ε_θ. An interpolation analysis between L_Simple and L_Square reveals that TRAK (η=0.5) is actually the worst point, with performance improving as η moves toward either extreme. D-TRAK with L_Square consistently outperforms TRAK by 10–16 percentage points in LDS across CIFAR-2, CIFAR-10, CelebA, and ArtBench, while requiring only 10 timesteps to match or exceed TRAK's 100-timestep performance, and is insensitive to checkpoint selection.

## Results

On CIFAR-2 with 100 timesteps, D-TRAK achieves an LDS of 33.74% on the validation set and 25.67% on the generation set, outperforming TRAK (23.59% / 15.87%) by +10.15% and +9.80% respectively. The advantage is consistent across all datasets: on CelebA, D-TRAK reaches 28.69% validation LDS vs. TRAK's 20.02%; on ArtBench-2, D-TRAK achieves 32.38% vs. TRAK's 27.28%. Notably, D-TRAK with only 10 timesteps (26.79% validation on CIFAR-2) outperforms TRAK with 100 timesteps (23.59%), demonstrating superior computational efficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D-TRAK consistently outperforms all 9 baselines across every dataset and evaluation setting. Against the strongest baseline TRAK, D-TRAK gains +10.15% LDS on CIFAR-2 validation, +9.80% on CIFAR-2 generation, +8.67% on CelebA validation, and +5.10% on ArtBench-2 validation (all at 100 timesteps). The kernel-based baselines (Relative IF, Renorm. IF) perform comparably to TRAK but are still substantially outperformed by D-TRAK. Gradient-based methods without kernels (TracInCP, GAS, Gradient) and similarity-based methods (Raw pixel, CLIP similarity) achieve LDS scores below 10% on CIFAR-2, far below D-TRAK's 33.74%. Journey TRAK, designed for attributing noisy intermediate images rather than final outputs, yields only 12.21% on CIFAR-2 generation. Against retraining-based methods (Empirical IF, Datamodel with S=512 models), D-TRAK still achieves higher LDS on both CIFAR-2 and ArtBench-2 while requiring no additional model training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends TRAK (Park et al., 2023), a gradient-based attribution method designed for discriminative classifiers, to the diffusion model setting. The key conceptual departure is that the authors find the theoretically natural choice—using the training loss gradient ∇θ L_Simple to construct the attribution kernel—actually performs worst, while theoretically unjustified alternatives (L_Square, L_Avg, L_2-norm, L_1-norm) consistently outperform it by a large margin. This positions the work as an empirical challenge to the assumption that influence-function-style constructions guided by convex or simplified theoretical assumptions transfer well to non-convex generative models, building on a broader lineage of influence function methods (Koh & Liang, 2017; Pruthi et al., 2020; Barshan et al., 2020) and the recent application of TRAK to diffusion models by Georgiev et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that theoretically unjustified design choices outperform theoretically motivated ones in non-convex settings raises a fundamental open question about the mechanism of data attribution that the authors explicitly acknowledge they cannot resolve. Practically, D-TRAK offers a significantly more efficient and effective attribution pipeline for diffusion models (achieving ~2× LDS improvement over TRAK with fewer timesteps and insensitivity to checkpoint selection), which is directly relevant to copyright attribution and fair compensation in generative AI. However, the lack of a theoretical explanation limits the ability to predict whether the finding generalizes beyond the tested architectures and datasets, and the authors note that even better design choices may exist.
