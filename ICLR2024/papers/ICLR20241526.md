## Problem

Existing neural-dynamical models (neural ODEs and variants) inherit fundamental limitations of ODE formulations: temporal dynamics depend only on the current state, numerical solvers become unstable over long horizons, and explicit ODE integration is computationally expensive. While scientifically-motivated invariances (e.g., separating universal dynamics from realization-specific initial conditions) are central to physical modeling, they have not been systematically exploited in neural architectures to enable efficient, generalizable learning of latent dynamics from high-dimensional observations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes high-dimensional image-like trajectory snapshots (C×H×W per time step, e.g., 16×384 pixels) sampled on regular or irregular time grids. At inference, only the first K=10 time points of a trajectory are provided to compute a realization-specific representation, after which the model can query the latent state at any arbitrary continuous time without evaluating intermediate grid points. Predictions are decoded back to the full observation space, and evaluation uses normalized MSE over the last T=60 timesteps of a 2T-step rollout.

## Method

LaDID decomposes the learning problem into a realization-specific (RS) trajectory representation and a realization-invariant (RI) dynamical model, formalized under a Sufficient Encoding Assumption that guarantees existence of a correctable mapping even when the RS encoding is far from true parameters. The encoder combines a shared CNN for spatial compression, a transformer with time-aware attention (reweighting tokens by temporal distance), and a variational embedding via the reparameterization trick; the RI dynamics module is a simple three-layer MLP conditioned on the RS representation and a sinusoidal time encoding, enabling continuous-time queries without any ODE solver. Long trajectories are handled via Bayesian multiple shooting: the trajectory is split into overlapping subpatches, each independently encoded, with a Gaussian continuity prior penalizing jumps between consecutive latent subpatches. Training maximizes an ELBO with three terms (data likelihood, representation KL, and smoothness KL) using a multi-phase schedule that progressively increases prediction horizon per patch.

## Results

LaDID achieves the lowest normalized MSE across all seven evaluated datasets (single pendulum, double pendulum, 2D wave equation, lambda-omega reaction-diffusion, 2D Navier-Stokes, lattice Boltzmann blunt body flow, and von Kármán vortex street), with the advantage growing for long-horizon predictions (evaluated over the last T=60 steps of a 2T rollout). On the Navier-Stokes benchmark, LaDID also achieves the lowest averaged RMSE at every time point in the prediction horizon. In few-shot transfer experiments, LaDID matches full-dataset performance with only 32% of the fine-tuning data and still produces usable predictions at 8% fine-tuning data, consistently outperforming scratch-trained models at all data fractions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LaDID outperforms all four baselines (ODE-RNN, NDP, ODE2VAE, and MSVI) on every dataset tested, with the gap widening for longer prediction horizons. MSVI, the most sophisticated baseline (sparse Bayesian multiple shooting with latent neural ODEs), achieves competitive short-term accuracy but degrades substantially on long-horizon predictions, whereas LaDID maintains low error throughout the full 2T rollout. ODE-RNN and NDP, which rely on explicit neural ODE solvers for latent trajectory integration, show the largest performance gaps, particularly on PDE-based systems like Navier-Stokes and the von Kármán vortex street. ODE2VAE, which uses Bayesian neural networks for second-order ODE dynamics, also trails LaDID consistently. A key practical advantage is that LaDID avoids explicit ODE solvers entirely, yielding substantially reduced computational resources during both training and inference (highlighted in Appendix Table H.2), while the transformer-based query mechanism allows direct inference at arbitrary continuous time points without intermediate grid evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

LaDID builds on the encode-simulate-decode paradigm established by neural ODE-based dynamical models (ODE-RNN, NDP, ODE2VAE, MSVI) but makes a deliberate architectural departure: it replaces explicit neural ODE solvers with a simple MLP conditioned on a learned trajectory representation and a sinusoidal time encoding. The multiple-shooting framework is adapted from Massaroli et al. (2021) and Iakovlev et al. (2023), but extended to a probabilistic (Bayesian) context via a Gaussian continuity prior rather than deterministic boundary-value reformulation. The key conceptual novelty is the explicit separation of realization-specific (RS) and realization-invariant (RI) information, framed through a 'sufficient encoding assumption' that provides a theoretical justification for why a universal dynamics module conditioned on a per-realization code can reconstruct arbitrary trajectories without knowing the true system parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is twofold: (1) eliminating the ODE solver removes a major source of numerical instability and computational cost in long-horizon prediction, and (2) the RS/RI decomposition provides a principled inductive bias that enables few-shot transfer to novel system interventions (e.g., changed mass, Reynolds number, or observation geometry) with as little as 8–32% of target-domain data. Limitations include the restriction to image-like observations on regular spatial grids (irregular spatial sampling is acknowledged as future work requiring graph-based methods), and the evaluation, while spanning ODE and PDE systems, is confined to a single set of benchmarks with a fixed set of baselines. Open questions include whether the RS/RI decomposition generalizes to systems where the 'invariant' dynamics themselves are non-stationary or multi-scale, and whether the approach scales to truly high-dimensional or partially observed systems.
