## Problem

The Sinkhorn algorithm for entropic optimal transport converges at a polynomial rate in practice, requiring tens of thousands of iterations to reach machine accuracy. Newton's method offers super-exponential convergence but incurs O(n³) per-iteration cost, violating the near-linear-time goal. The paper asks whether one can achieve Newton-level iteration counts while retaining the O(n²) per-iteration complexity of Sinkhorn.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a cost matrix C ∈ R^{n×n}, source and target density vectors r, c ∈ R^n (each summing to 1), and an entropy regularization parameter η, and outputs the optimal transport plan P and dual variables (x, y). In the MNIST experiments, 28×28 grayscale images are flattened to 784-dimensional vectors normalized to sum to 1, with each pixel (i,j) mapped to the point (i/28, j/28) ∈ R² for computing Euclidean or L1 transport costs. The algorithm operates in two stages with tunable parameters N₁ (number of Sinkhorn warm-start steps) and a target sparsity λ that controls the threshold ρ for Hessian truncation.

## Method

The Sinkhorn-Newton-Sparse (SNS) algorithm runs the standard Sinkhorn matrix scaling for N₁ steps as a warm start, then switches to a Newton stage where the Hessian of the Lyapunov potential f(x,y) is sparsified by thresholding all entries below a constant ρ to zero. The key theoretical insight is that after sufficient Sinkhorn iterations, the Hessian ∇²f is approximately sparse with sparsity λ = O(1/n) (or O(1/√n) under non-unique solutions, via extremal combinatorics on bipartite graphs), so solving the sparsified linear system via conjugate gradient costs only O(λn³) = O(n²) per iteration. The sparsification preserves symmetry and diagonal dominance of the Hessian, ensuring conjugate gradient convergence. A line search determines the step size at each Newton iteration, and the algorithm empirically achieves super-exponential convergence with only 9–77 Newton steps across tested problems, compared to thousands of Sinkhorn iterations.

## Results

On the random assignment problem (n=500, η=1200), SNS reaches machine accuracy in 0.34 s and 29 total iterations, versus 233.36 s and 56,199 iterations for Sinkhorn — a ~685× speedup. On MNIST with L2 cost, SNS completes in 2.33 s / 53 iterations versus 18.84 s / 2,041 for Sinkhorn (~8× speedup). On MNIST with L1 cost (non-unique solutions), SNS takes 12.22 s / 777 iterations versus 45.75 s / 5,748 for Sinkhorn (~3.7× speedup). The Newton stage alone requires only 9–77 iterations across all three settings, demonstrating near super-exponential convergence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SNS dominates the standard Sinkhorn algorithm (Cuturi, 2013) across all three test cases, reducing iteration counts by 1–3 orders of magnitude (e.g., 29 vs. 56,199 on the random assignment problem) while maintaining O(n²) per-iteration cost. Against the Sinkhorn-Newton method (Brauer, 2017), which uses the full Hessian without sparsification, SNS achieves a 40× per-iteration speedup (0.30 s vs. 11.86 s per Newton step at n=2000), confirming that Hessian sparsification is essential for practical scalability. Quasi-Newton methods (BFGS and L-BFGS, Nocedal & Wright, 1999) perform no better than plain Sinkhorn and in fact degrade on the MNIST experiments, indicating that noisy gradient-history Hessian estimates are inferior to direct sparse approximation of the true Hessian. The advantage of SNS is most pronounced at large entropy regularization (η), where Sinkhorn's convergence slows dramatically but the Hessian becomes more amenable to sparse approximation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Sinkhorn-Newton algorithm of Brauer et al. (2017), which applied Newton's method to the Lyapunov potential of entropic optimal transport but suffered from O(n³) per-iteration cost. The key departure is the observation that the Hessian of the Lyapunov potential is approximately sparse with O(1/n) density, enabling a thresholded sparse Newton step at O(n²) cost. The variational framework from Altschuler et al. (2017) provides the theoretical backbone, while the sparsity analysis draws on results from Weed (2018) on entropic regularization and extremal combinatorics on bipartite graphs for the non-unique solution case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Sinkhorn algorithm is a workhorse in machine learning (GANs, domain adaptation, generative modeling), but its polynomial convergence rate becomes a bottleneck when high-precision transport plans are needed. SNS achieves orders-of-magnitude reduction in iteration count (e.g., 56,199 → 29 for random assignment) while maintaining O(n²) per-iteration cost, making high-precision OT computationally tractable. Open questions include: whether the empirically observed super-exponential convergence can be rigorously proven, how sparsification accuracy interacts with convergence guarantees, and whether the approach scales to very large n (tested up to n=784). The connection between Hessian sparsity and extremal combinatorics for non-unique solutions is a novel theoretical bridge with potential broader implications.
