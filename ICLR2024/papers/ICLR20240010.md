## Problem

The Knowledge Neuron (KN) Thesis claims that MLP weights in transformers store factual knowledge as key-value memories, and that editing these weights constitutes 'knowledge editing.' However, prior evaluations of KN-inspired methods (KN edit, ROME) relied on simple prompt paraphrasing, and it remained untested whether the same editing mechanisms apply equally to syntactic phenomena—patterns that are clearly not 'knowledge.' This paper asks whether the KN thesis can distinguish factual recall from syntactic processing, or whether it is an oversimplification that conflates token expression patterns with knowledge.

## Input / Output

The method operates on pretrained language models (BERT-base-cased, GPT-2 base/XL, LLaMA-2 7B) and consumes two types of structured inputs: syntactic minimal pairs from BLiMP (e.g., 'this student' vs. '*this students') and factual relations from PARAREL (e.g., 'The capital of Canada is ___'). For each input, it produces neuron-level attribution scores via integral of gradients, localisation metrics (number of identified KNs |KN|, threshold τ, and a new nonparametric similarity score R1²), pre/post-edit output probability distributions, causal tracing heatmaps, and evaluation scores under three criteria: reliability, bijective symmetry, and synonymous invariance. A key constraint is that decoder-only models (GPT-2, LLaMA-2) can only process prompts where the target token follows the source, so some BLiMP paradigms are excluded for those architectures.

## Method

The paper extends the KN search algorithm (integral-of-gradients attribution with threshold tuning) from factual relations to syntactic phenomena by constructing 3-tuples (source, target, phenomenon) from BLiMP minimal pairs, then identifies and suppresses the resulting 'knowledge neurons' to measure their effect on output probabilities. It introduces two new evaluation criteria for model editing—bijective symmetry (if Ottawa→Rome for Canada's capital, then Rome→Ottawa for Italy's capital must also hold) and synonymous invariance (if 'dentist treats toothache' is edited to 'lawyer,' then 'dentist treats tooth pain' must also change)—and a nonparametric localisation metric R1² computed via SVD of normalised attribution patterns. The central empirical finding is that syntactic phenomena (determiner-noun agreement, subject-verb agreement, anaphor agreement) localise to the same number of neurons, in the same topmost layers, with comparable metrics as factual relations, and that KN edit's suppression of these neurons shifts probability distributions but fails to overturn categorical predictions (≤5.2% reliability on BLiMP, 1.66–47.86% on factual corpora). ROME achieves high reliability (96–100%) but scores only 23–58% on symmetry and synonymy, and its edits are limited to the exact token association without generalising to paraphrases. Causal tracing on syntactic phenomena further shows that the proposed MLP-early/attention-late division of labour is unstable, undermining the mechanistic basis of the KN thesis.

## Results

KN edit achieves reliability of only 1.66%–47.86% on factual corpora (ZsRE, CounterFact) and at most 5.2% categorical prediction overturn on BLiMP syntactic paradigms, far too low to support the KN thesis. ROME achieves near-perfect reliability (96.37%–100%) on PARAREL relations but drops sharply under the paper's new criteria: bijective symmetry scores only 23.71%–33.64% and synonymous invariance only 52.35%–58.36% across GPT-2 XL and LLaMA-2 7B. Syntactic phenomena (determiner-noun agreement) localise to as few as 2 neurons with metrics (|KN|, τ, R₁²) in the same range as factual relations, and both occupy the same topmost layers, undermining the claim that MLP neurons store 'knowledge' distinct from shallow token co-occurrence patterns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Dai et al.'s (2022) KN edit, the paper demonstrates that the same integral-of-gradients localisation method identifies neurons for syntactic phenomena (e.g., grammatical number) with comparable tightness to factual relations, yet editing those neurons fails to overturn categorical predictions (≤5.2% on BLiMP; 1.66%–47.86% on ZsRE/CounterFact), showing the method captures shallow co-occurrence cues rather than stored knowledge. Against Meng et al.'s (2022) ROME, the paper shows that while ROME achieves 96–100% reliability on the original cloze template, it fails the paper's two new robustness criteria: symmetry scores are only 23–34% (e.g., editing 'Canada's capital → Rome' does not make the model agree 'Rome is the capital of Canada') and synonymy scores are only 52–58% (e.g., editing 'dentist treats toothache → lawyer' does not transfer to 'tooth pain' or 'odontalgia'). Against Yao et al.'s (2023) portability framework, the paper extends the evaluation with bijective symmetry and synonymous invariance, finding that ROME's performance degrades even further than the already-halved portability scores Yao et al. reported (50% vs. 90% generality). The causal tracing analysis also shows that the clean MLP-early/attention-late division of labor proposed by Meng et al. does not persist for syntactic phenomena, weakening the mechanistic basis for ROME's MLP-only editing strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the KN thesis evaluation lineage from Geva et al. (2021) through Dai et al. (2022) and Meng et al. (2022) by applying the same integral-of-gradients localisation and model-editing tools to rigorously defined syntactic phenomena (BLiMP minimal pairs) rather than only factual cloze tasks. It builds on Yao et al.'s (2023) portability critique by introducing two additional evaluation axes—bijective symmetry and synonymous invariance—and on Finlayson et al.'s (2021) causal analysis of agreement by pushing it to the individual-neuron level. Conceptually, the paper departs from the key-value-memory framing by arguing that what MLP neurons encode are 'token expression patterns' rather than knowledge, and it connects this to the formal-vs-functional competence distinction raised by Mahowald et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central implication—that the same small set of MLP neurons handles both syntactic agreement and factual recall, and that editing them cannot reliably overturn categorical predictions—undermines the popular 'knowledge stored in weights' narrative and redirects attention toward full circuit-level interpretation (Wang et al., 2022; Conmy et al., 2023). The new symmetry and synonymy criteria provide a practical, low-cost evaluation protocol that future model-editing methods should adopt. Open questions include how to formalise circuit identification beyond ad hoc task-specific analyses, whether the MLP-only editing paradigm can ever be sufficient, and how the findings generalise to even larger or architecturally different models where individual-neuron effects become less pronounced (as observed with LLaMA-2).
