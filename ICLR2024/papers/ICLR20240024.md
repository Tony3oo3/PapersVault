## Problem

Existing fMRI analysis methods are narrow, task-specific models trained on small datasets that fail to capture the complex nonlinear spatiotemporal dynamics of brain activity. No prior work has applied the foundation model paradigm—large-scale self-supervised pretraining followed by transfer learning—to fMRI recordings, leaving the field without a general-purpose, interpretable model that can be adapted to diverse downstream tasks.

## Input / Output

Input consists of 424-dimensional parcel time series (AAL-424 atlas) sampled at ~1 Hz, randomly trimmed to 200 timesteps per recording, with robust scaling (median subtraction, IQR division) applied across subjects. The 424 parcels are ordered by their 3D Y-coordinate to preserve spatial locality, and each parcel's signal is split into 20-timestep patches, yielding 4,240 tokens per recording window. Outputs span multiple tasks: reconstructed fMRI signal (pretraining), continuous clinical scores (age, PCL-5, GAD-7, neuroticism), 20-timestep-ahead parcel activity forecasts, 7-network functional assignments per parcel, and per-parcel attention maps for interpretability.

## Method

BrainLM is a Transformer masked autoencoder that treats the 424-parcel × 200-timestep fMRI window as a 2D image, with 20-timestep temporal patches per parcel forming 4,240 tokens projected to 512 dimensions. A key design choice is patch-based spatial masking (inspired by MAE in vision) rather than single-parcel masking: because neighboring parcels are spatially correlated, predicting masked multi-parcel regions is substantially harder and forces the model to learn richer spatiotemporal dependencies. The encoder (4 self-attention layers, 4 heads) processes only unmasked tokens, while a 2-layer decoder receives both encoded visible tokens and masked token embeddings to reconstruct the full signal via MSE loss. Pretraining uses 77,298 recordings (6,700 hours) from UK Biobank and HCP with masking rates of 20%, 75%, or 90%, batch size 512, and Adam over 100 epochs. Downstream tasks are handled by attaching lightweight heads (3-layer MLP for regression, or direct use of attention weights for zero-shot network inference) to the frozen or fine-tuned encoder.

## Results

On clinical variable prediction (Table 1), BrainLM13M achieves the lowest MSE across all four targets: 0.464 (Age), 0.018 (PTSD/PCL-5), 0.074 (Anxiety/GAD-7), and 0.072 (Neuroticism), outperforming all baselines. For future brain state forecasting (Table 2), the fine-tuned BrainLM650M reaches R²=0.098 and Pearson R=0.313 on UKB, and R²=0.061 on the external HCP cohort, substantially exceeding LSTM (R²=−0.001 UKB) and NeuralODE (R²=−0.577 UKB). In zero-shot functional network classification (Table 3), BrainLM's self-attention weights yield 58.8% parcel-to-network accuracy, surpassing raw data kNN (39.2%), VAE embeddings (49.4%), and GCN embeddings (25.9%). Reconstruction on held-out data achieves R²=0.464 (UKB) and R²=0.278 (HCP), confirming cross-dataset generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BrainLM outperforms all seven baselines across every task. On clinical variable prediction, BrainLM13M beats SVR (Drysdale et al.) by 29% on Age MSE (0.464 vs 0.659) and by 18–21% on PTSD, Anxiety, and Neuroticism; it also surpasses the MLP baseline (Iidaka et al.) by 33% on Age and 11–18% on the remaining variables. Against LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber), BrainLM reduces Age MSE by 22% (0.464 vs 0.596) and leads on all other clinical targets. GCN (Kipf & Welling) is the weakest baseline in clinical prediction (Age MSE 0.862) and also lags in functional network classification (25.9% vs BrainLM's 58.8%). For future state forecasting, BrainLM650M achieves R²=0.098 on UKB versus −0.001 for LSTM, −0.577 for NeuralODE (Chen et al.), and 0.001 for LatentODE (Rubanova et al.), with the gap widening on HCP (0.061 vs −0.020, −0.469, −0.003). In zero-shot functional network identification, BrainLM's attention-based kNN (58.8%) exceeds the VAE baseline (Kingma & Welling) by 9.4 percentage points and raw-data kNN by 19.6 points, confirming that self-supervised pretraining captures network structure without explicit labels.

## Relation to Prior Work

BrainLM adapts the masked autoencoder paradigm from computer vision (He et al., CVPR 2022) and the BERT-style masked-prediction objective to fMRI time series, introducing a patch-based spatiotemporal masking scheme that treats the 424-parcel × 200-timestep window as a 2D image. Prior fMRI representation-learning work (Chen et al., 2023a,b; Mozafari et al., 2020) applied autoencoders or VAEs to small datasets and focused narrowly on visual cortex reconstruction, whereas BrainLM scales to all brain regions and 6,700 hours of recordings. The paper also departs from the LLM-to-brain-correlation line of work (Caucheteux et al., 2022; Pasquiou et al., 2022), which maps language model activations onto brain recordings, by instead training a model directly on brain activity dynamics for downstream biological tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first foundation model for fMRI, BrainLM provides a reusable pretrained backbone that the neuroscience community can fine-tune for diverse clinical and cognitive tasks without task-specific architecture design, potentially accelerating biomarker discovery from resting-state scans. The zero-shot recovery of seven canonical functional networks from attention weights—without any network labels during training—suggests that self-supervised objectives can extract organizational principles fundamental to brain topology. Key limitations include modest absolute accuracy on network classification (58.8%), evaluation on only two datasets, and clinical predictions that, while superior to baselines, remain in the range of modest R² values; open questions include whether multi-modal integration (EEG, MEG, structural MRI) and broader cognitive-state probing will yield substantially more generalizable representations.
