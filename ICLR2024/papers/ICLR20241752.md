## Problem

Language-conditioned robot policies fail to generalize to new tasks (e.g., folding) even when the required arm motion resembles seen tasks (e.g., pick-and-place), because the new task falls outside the language distribution. Goal-image conditioning offers more visual specificity but is harder to acquire at test time and sensitive to task-irrelevant factors like backgrounds. The paper asks whether a coarse, motion-centric conditioning signal can bridge this gap, enabling interpolation to novel tasks while remaining practical to specify.

## Input / Output

The policy takes a history of 6 RGB images from the robot head camera, each concatenated along the feature dimension with a 2D trajectory sketch (an RGB image encoding the end-effector path as a curve, temporal progress via red-channel grading, optional height via green channel, and gripper interaction markers as colored circles). The trajectory sketch is a lightweight, camera-projected 2D representation rather than a full 3D trajectory, making it easy to draw or generate. The output is a sequence of robot end-effector joint actions predicted by a transformer, trained via behavior cloning to minimize action log-likelihood.

## Method

The core contribution is conditioning a transformer policy on coarse 2D trajectory sketches extracted via hindsight labeling: 3D end-effector positions from existing demonstrations are projected into camera space to produce trajectory curves, temporal color grading, and gripper interaction markers without any manual annotation. Two representations are studied—RT-Trajectory (2D) with temporal and interaction information, and RT-Trajectory (2.5D) which additionally encodes normalized end-effector height in the green channel to resolve depth ambiguity. The architecture modifies RT-1 by concatenating the trajectory sketch with each RGB frame in the 6-image history before an ImageNet-pretrained EfficientNet-B3 tokenizer (new first-conv weights zero-initialized), and removes the FiLM language layers. Training uses standard behavior cloning on ~73K RT-1 demonstrations across 542 tasks. At inference, trajectory sketches can be produced by human GUI drawings, hand-pose estimation from first-person videos, LLM-generated code (Code as Policies), or text-guided image generation models, all of which the policy generalizes across.

## Results

On 7 unseen manipulation skills (64 trials total), RT-Trajectory (2.5D) achieves a 67% overall success rate, substantially outperforming RT-Trajectory (2D) at 50%, RT-1-Goal at 26%, RT-1 at 17%, and RT-2 at 11%. The 2.5D variant (which encodes height via the green channel) is particularly critical for tasks requiring precise vertical positioning: it achieves 38% on Pick from Chair and 70% on Swivel Chair, where the 2D variant scores 0% on both. Across all 7 unseen skills, RT-Trajectory (2.5D) matches or exceeds the best baseline on 6 out of 7 tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RT-Trajectory (2.5D) at 67% overall success rate outperforms all three baselines by a large margin: RT-1 (17%), RT-2 (11%), and RT-1-Goal (26%). The language-conditioned baselines (RT-1 and RT-2) perform worst because the unseen tasks use semantically novel language instructions that fall outside the training distribution, even when the underlying arm motions overlap with seen skills. RT-1-Goal, the strongest baseline, benefits from visual goal specification but is sensitive to task-irrelevant factors like backgrounds and is harder to acquire at test time. RT-Trajectory (2D) at 50% also beats all baselines, but the 2.5D variant's height encoding is decisive on Pick from Chair (38% vs. 0% for 2D) and Swivel Chair (70% vs. 0%), where vertical disambiguation is critical. Notably, RT-Trajectory (2D) actually outperforms RT-Trajectory (2.5D) on Restock Drawer (92% vs. 67%), suggesting that in some precision-placement scenarios the additional height channel introduces noise rather than signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the RT-1 transformer architecture, replacing its language-instruction conditioning with 2D trajectory sketches while retaining the EfficientNet-B3 image tokenizer and behavior cloning training paradigm. Conceptually, it positions trajectory sketches as a middle ground between under-specified language conditioning (RT-1, RT-2) and over-specified goal-image or video conditioning, arguing that this intermediate level of state-specification is both more expressive for motion-centric tasks and more practical than full goal images. The hindsight trajectory labeling technique repurposes existing demonstration datasets without manual annotation, and the approach draws a parallel to diagrammatic teaching (Zhi et al., 2023) but focuses on learning to condition on 2D sketches directly rather than reconstructing 3D trajectories.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The trajectory sketch conditioning offers a practical, multi-source interface for specifying robot tasks—humans can draw them, videos can be converted, LLMs can generate them, and image models can produce them—making it a potentially versatile control signal for manipulation policies. The 67% vs. 26% gap on unseen tasks suggests that motion-centric conditioning fundamentally outperforms semantic (language) or endpoint (goal image) conditioning for task generalization, which could reshape how practitioners think about policy conditioning design. Key limitations include the assumption of a stationary robot with a visible end-effector, and the inability to encode spatial constraints (e.g., avoid fragile objects); extending to whole-body mobile manipulation and constraint-aware sketching are natural next steps.
