## Problem

Existing game AI agents are optimized in a 'self-centered' manner, maximizing task completion (e.g., winning) without considering the human partner's experience, leading to behaviors that dominate or ignore the human. Directly incorporating human rewards into the agent's objective introduces a credit assignment problem: the agent receives credit for human achievements it did not contribute to, causing it to learn incorrect behaviors and lose its original task-completion abilities.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes standard MOBA game observations (unit features, spatial grid features, in-game statistics) augmented with a human policy embedding extracted via an LSTM from the human's historical state sequence. The output consists of the agent's action sequence (movement, skill activation, target selection) alongside two auxiliary predictions: a human primitive value estimate (baseline human return) and a non-negative human gain estimate (the agent's marginal contribution above that baseline). The human policy embedding is fused into the pre-trained Wukong network via surgery techniques (adding randomly initialized units to an internal FC layer), and a task gate switches between enhancement mode and task-completion mode.

## Method

RLHG introduces a two-stage training procedure built on the key insight that the human's own primitive contribution must be separated from total human rewards, with only the residual positive gain attributed to the agent. In Stage I, the pre-trained agent π₀ is frozen while trajectories are collected with the human partner; a human primitive value network V_φ is trained via TD errors to estimate the baseline expected human return. In Stage II, V_φ is frozen and a gain network Δ_ω is trained to predict the expected positive human gain, using an indicator function that filters out non-positive gain samples (Eq. 5). The agent policy is then fine-tuned with a combined objective: the original self-centered advantage plus α times a human-centered advantage computed as Δ(s,a) − Δ̂(s), where Δ̂(s) is the expected positive gain. A task gate and a (1−β)% agent-only training mixture preserve the agent's original task abilities, while an adaptive threshold ξ on the original value network dynamically switches between enhancement and task-completion modes.

## Results

In real-world human-agent tests on Honor of Kings (5v5, 20 matches per participant), the RLHG agent significantly improves all four human goals for both participant levels while incurring only a modest Win Rate drop. For general-level participants, RLHG raises MVP Score from 7.44 to 9.1, Highlight Times from 0.37 to 0.75, Participation Rate from 0.41 to 0.59, and Resource Quantity from 4.98 to 5.8, while Win Rate decreases from 34% to 30%. Subjective preference scores (1–5 scale) show RLHG achieving 4.01±0.30 on Gaming Experience versus 2.40±0.41 for Wukong among general-level participants, and 4.22±0.30 versus 3.06±0.27 among high-level participants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RLHG against two baselines: Wukong (a self-centered PPO-trained agent optimizing only for game victory) and HRE (which directly adds human rewards to the optimization objective). Against Wukong, RLHG wins decisively on all four human goals for both participant levels (e.g., MVP Score 9.1 vs. 7.44 for general-level; 10.28 vs. 8.86 for high-level) and on all four subjective preference metrics (e.g., Gaming Experience 4.01 vs. 2.40 for general-level), at the cost of a 4–5 percentage point Win Rate decrease. Against HRE in the simulated human model-agent test, RLHG wins on the task goal (Win Rate) where HRE drops significantly due to excessive following behavior (highest Follow Rate metric), while both agents improve human goals over Wukong. The key finding is that RLHG's gain-based formulation avoids the human-agent credit assignment problem that causes HRE to learn degenerate behaviors, preserving the agent's original task-completion ability while still enhancing human experience.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Wukong agent (Ye et al., 2020a), a state-of-the-art MOBA game AI trained with PPO to maximize game victory, by introducing a human-centered optimization objective that goes beyond task completion. It departs from prior human-agent coordination work (Carroll et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2020; Strouse et al., 2021), which focuses on adapting agents to human partners for task success, by explicitly modeling and optimizing for the human's individual experience goals (MVP score, highlights, participation, resources). The key conceptual departure is the 'human gain' formulation: rather than directly adding human rewards to the agent's objective (which the paper shows causes credit assignment failures), it subtracts a learned baseline of the human's primitive contribution, rewarding the agent only for incremental enhancement it provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap in game AI: agents that are competent but make human partners feel excluded or dominated. The finding that the naive reward-combination approach (HRE) causes the agent to excessively follow the human and lose task competence validates the credit assignment concern and motivates the gain-based formulation. Limitations include evaluation in a single game (Honor of Kings), a single human per team, and relatively simple goal definitions; the paper itself identifies combining RLHG with RLHF for implicit goal inference and extending to multiple humans with diverse goals as open directions. The broader implication is that 'winning' and 'making the human feel good' are separable optimization objectives that require explicit modeling rather than being conflated.
