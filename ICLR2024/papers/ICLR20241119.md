## Problem

Large language models demonstrate strong medical reasoning but cannot be deployed in healthcare settings due to strict data privacy regulations that prohibit uploading patient data to third-party APIs. Domain-specific small language models (SLMs) address privacy through local training but suffer a significant performance gap compared to LLMs on medical tasks. The paper tackles how to transfer LLM medical knowledge into SLMs while ensuring only de-identified keywords—not raw patient data—are exposed to the LLM.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241119/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The LLM receives only NER-extracted medical keywords (~49 words, ~42% of the original ~116-word question) plus the candidate answer choices, and produces a structured context comprising an overall medical summary, a specific knowledge passage for each candidate answer with its relationship to the overall context, and a preliminary decision. The SLM then receives the original question concatenated with each candidate answer, the overall context, and that answer's specific context, producing a per-answer score via a linear head and softmax normalization. The privacy budget is quantified as the word-count ratio of keywords to raw question, and the method also supports partial keyword usage (25%–100%) to trade off privacy against performance.

## Method

The pipeline has two stages. First, a medical NER model (SciSpaCy) extracts keywords from each question; clinicians hand-write a small set (M ≪ N) of demonstration contexts that simulate clinical reasoning (overall assessment → per-answer analysis → preliminary decision), and GPT-3.5-turbo is prompted via in-context learning with these demonstrations plus the keywords and candidate answers to generate contexts for all instances. Second, for each candidate answer a_j, the SLM (BioLinkBERT or BioMedLM) encodes the concatenation [question ⊕ a_j ⊕ overall_context ⊕ specific_context(a_j)] into a representation vector, which a linear layer maps to a score; scores are normalized via softmax and trained with cross-entropy. A key finding is that contexts generated even when the LLM's preliminary decision is wrong still contain useful medical knowledge, so all contexts are retained for SLM training rather than filtering by LLM correctness. The approach outperforms standard SLM fine-tuning by up to 22.57% absolute accuracy in few-shot settings and achieves SOTA on MedQA and HEADQA under privacy-restricted conditions.

## Results

FTC with BioMedLM achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on MedQA (55.90% test) and HEADQA (63.17% test) in privacy-restricted scenarios, outperforming the best prior baselines by 8.3% and 16.5% respectively. Compared to standard SLM fine-tuning (SFT) without context, FTC yields absolute accuracy gains of up to 7.96% (MedQA), 21.23% (HEADQA), and 17.05% (MedMCQA). In few-shot settings (100 training samples), FTC still surpasses the LLM (GPT-3.5-turbo) prompting baseline by 3.82–14.12% across all three medical datasets, demonstrating that the method is particularly effective when training data is scarce.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FTC with BioMedLM decisively outperforms all seven knowledge-augmented baselines on MedQA and HEADQA. On MedQA test, FTC (55.90%) surpasses HDRN (47.6%), DRAGON (47.5%), GreaseLM (45.1%), and QA-GNN (45.0%) by 8.3–10.9 percentage points. On HEADQA test, FTC (63.17%) exceeds MOEBQA (46.7%) and MurKe (46.7%) by 16.5 points, a particularly large margin given that MOEBQA is the strongest prior method on this dataset. The only competitive baseline is VOD with BioLinkBERT & BioLinkBERT, which achieves 55.0% on MedQA test (vs. FTC's 55.90%) and 58.3% on MedMCQA dev (vs. FTC's 52.09%); however, VOD requires 180k training instances for MedMCQA compared to FTC's 10k, and FTC still wins on MedQA. Against the LLM prompting baseline (GPT-3.5-turbo at 41.70% MedQA, 47.50% HEADQA, 35.20% MedMCQA), FTC with BioMedLM leads by 14.20%, 15.67%, and 16.89% respectively, confirming that the SLM effectively leverages LLM-generated medical context to surpass the LLM's own direct answering ability in privacy-restricted settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Pinto-style pipeline (Wang et al., 2022) of feeding LLM-generated rationales as additional input to SLMs, but introduces a critical privacy-preserving twist: instead of passing full medical text to the LLM, it uses NER-extracted keywords and candidate answers as the sole LLM input. It also draws on the broader LLM-to-SLM knowledge distillation literature (Ho et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022; Shridhar et al., 2022) and the retrieve-then-read paradigm (Izacard & Grave, 2020; Lewis et al., 2020), repurposing the LLM as a knowledge generator rather than a retriever. The key departure from prior distillation work is the explicit privacy constraint, which the authors frame as the first systematic treatment of LLM-assisted SLM enhancement under data-sharing restrictions in healthcare.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically urgent gap: healthcare institutions cannot upload patient data to third-party LLMs, yet domain-specific SLMs underperform LLMs on medical reasoning. The demonstrated 22.57% few-shot accuracy gain and SOTA results on MedQA/HEADQA under privacy constraints make this a deployable technique for real clinical NLP pipelines. However, the privacy protection is only partial (keywords still leak some information), the LLM's medical knowledge may be outdated or biased, and the method is limited to multiple-choice QA. Open questions include whether the approach extends to free-form clinical text generation, whether differential privacy guarantees can be formally integrated, and whether the keyword-extraction step can be replaced by learned privacy-preserving representations.
