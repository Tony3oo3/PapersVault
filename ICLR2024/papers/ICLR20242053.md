## Problem

State-of-the-art meta-learning algorithms for physical systems (MAML, CoDA, ANIL) rely on black-box neural networks with inner gradient loops, making them computationally expensive and preventing identification of the underlying physical parameters. The paper asks whether the affine structure inherent in many physical equations can be exploited to achieve competitive multi-environment generalization with a far simpler, interpretable architecture that recovers physical parameters from learned weights.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242053/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a meta-dataset of T task datasets, each containing N_t input-output pairs (system state coordinates x and observed response y), and at test time a few-shot adaptation set from a new environment. The task-specific component is a low-dimensional weight vector w ∈ ℝ^r (with r chosen between the number of varying physical parameters and the number of training tasks), and the task-agnostic component is a neural network feature map v(x;θ) ∈ ℝ^r plus a bias c(x;θ). At test time, adaptation reduces to a closed-form ordinary least-squares solve for w, and a separately trained linear identification map P ∈ ℝ^{n×r} converts the learned w into the physical parameter vector φ, enabling zero-shot parameter estimation for new environments.

## Method

CAMEL (Context-Affine Multi-Environment Learning) models the prediction as F(x;θ,w) = c(x;θ) + w^T v(x;θ), where v and c are shared neural networks and w is a low-dimensional task-specific weight. The key structural choice is that the model is affine in w, mirroring the linear dependence of physical equations on their varying parameters (e.g., mass, inertia, charge). During meta-training, all task weights ω_t are jointly optimized with the shared parameters θ via a single gradient descent loop—no inner gradient loop, no Hessian-vector products, no gradient through matrix inversions. At test time, adaptation is a closed-form OLS solve, enabling real-time use in 200 Hz control loops. Proposition 1 proves that in the vanishing-loss limit, the learned weights relate to the true physical parameters via a linear transform (up to a GL_r symmetry), so a simple OLS regression on the T training tasks recovers the identification map, making the model interpretable without additional supervision.

## Results

CAMEL achieves the lowest adaptation MSE on 5 out of 6 physical systems: 1.0E-4 on point charges, 1.9E-4 on the ε-capacitor, 3.1E-3 on cartpole, 2.4E-1 on the robot arm, and 8.2E-3 on Upkie, while tying CoDA on the capacitor (2.6E-2). In terms of computational cost, CAMEL is the fastest method for both training (1× relative time vs. 2–30× for baselines) and test-time adaptation (1× vs. 1–10×), owing to its closed-form least-squares adaptation. For parameter identification, CAMEL achieves less than 1% relative error on point charges with only 10 training tasks, and enables zero-shot adaptation via the learned identification map.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAMEL outperforms all four baselines on adaptation MSE for the linearly-parametrized systems: on point charges it achieves 1.0E-4 vs. 9.2E-4 (ANIL), 8.2E-2 (CoDA), 1.2E-4 (R2-D2), and 1.6E-1 (MAML); on cartpole it reaches 3.1E-3 vs. 2.5E-2 (ANIL), 8.1E-1 (CoDA), 8.5E-3 (R2-D2), and 1.8E0 (MAML); on the robot arm it scores 2.4E-1 vs. 7.5E-1 (ANIL), 9.3E-1 (CoDA), 3.5E-1 (R2-D2), and 8.1E-1 (MAML); and on Upkie it achieves 8.2E-3 vs. 1.9E-2 (ANIL), 2.1E-2 (CoDA), 2.3E-2 (R2-D2), and 1.5E-2 (MAML). On the non-analytical capacitor, CAMEL ties CoDA (2.6E-2) and slightly outperforms ANIL (3.6E-2) and R2-D2 (3.1E-4 at 40-shot, though R2-D2 is competitive). On the ε-capacitor with small perturbations (ε=0.1), CAMEL achieves 1.9E-4 vs. 1.1E-3 (ANIL) and 1.0E-3 (CoDA), an order-of-magnitude improvement. Computationally, CAMEL is 10–30× faster to train than MAML/ANIL and 2× faster than CoDA, while its adaptation is 3–10× faster than gradient-based methods (ANIL, CoDA, MAML) and on par with R2-D2's closed-form solver.

## Relation to Prior Work

CAMEL directly adopts the multi-task representation learning architecture with hard parameter sharing shown by Wang et al. (2021) to match gradient-based meta-learning, but repurposes it for physical systems by exploiting the affine dependence of physical equations on their parameters. It departs from MAML/ANIL (inner gradient loops) and CoDA (hypernetwork-based adaptation) by training task-specific weights directly as meta-parameters, reducing adaptation to closed-form least squares. The identifiability analysis (Proposition 1) connects to matrix factorization identifiability results, while the application to inverse dynamics builds on the known linearity of Euler-Lagrange equations in inertial parameters (Nguyen-Tuong & Peters, 2010).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in physics-informed ML: black-box meta-learning methods are computationally expensive at inference and offer no interpretable link between learned weights and physical quantities, which is critical for control and scientific discovery. The clean identifiability guarantee and the zero-shot φ-CAMEL adaptation (predicting system behavior for unseen parameter values without data) open a path toward data-driven scientific exploration. Limitations include the requirement for affine or locally-linear parameter structure, a small number of training tasks, and experiments confined to relatively low-dimensional systems; extending to high-dimensional or strongly nonlinear physical contexts remains open.
