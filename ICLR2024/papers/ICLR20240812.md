## Problem

Machine learning models in consumer-facing applications (lending, hiring) can assign fixed predictions that individuals cannot change through any feasible action, effectively precluding access to credit or employment. Existing algorithmic recourse methods are designed to provision actions, not to certify their infeasibility, and they fail under complex non-separable actionability constraints (e.g., thermometer encoding, directional causal linkages) by returning loopholes or blindspots. This paper introduces recourse verification as a model auditing procedure to rigorously test whether a model assigns predictions that are unresponsive to all possible actions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a feature vector x, a black-box classification model f, and a set of actionability constraints A(x) that may include discrete, non-separable conditions (thermometer encoding preservation, directional linkages between features, categorical one-hot constraints). It produces a reachable set R_A(x) — the complete enumeration of all feature vectors attainable through valid actions — and a three-valued verification verdict: Yes (a reachable point flips the prediction), No (all reachable points retain the negative prediction, certifying preclusion), or abstain (only an interior approximation was constructed). Verification is performed purely through prediction queries on reachable points, making it model-agnostic and amortizable across multiple models.

## Method

The core mechanism is a mixed-integer programming formulation (FindAction) that enumerates all valid actions from x by repeatedly solving a constrained optimization and adding no-good cuts to exclude previously found solutions, terminating when the MIP becomes infeasible. To moderate combinatorial explosion, a decomposition strategy partitions features into independently-alterable subsets, solving smaller MIPs per subset and taking the Cartesian product of resulting reachable sets. The MIP encodes complex non-separable constraints (thermometer encoding, directional causal linkages, categorical encoding) through Big-M constraints, binary indicator variables, and generalized reachability matrices. Once reachable sets are constructed, verification reduces to querying f on each reachable point, and a pigeonhole-principle theorem provides an alternative analytical certification based on the model's false negative rate relative to positive-example density in the reachable set.

## Results

Using reachable sets as ground truth, the paper finds that 7.1%–31.3% of individuals denied by a model are assigned fixed predictions (no recourse) across three consumer-finance datasets and three model classes (LR, XGB, RF). The highest preclusion rate is 31.3% for Random Forest on HELOC, while the lowest is 0.2% for RF on Give Me Credit. Existing recourse methods (AR and DiCE) produce actions that violate actionability constraints (loopholes) for 2.2%–57.7% of denied individuals, and DiCE additionally fails to find valid actions when recourse exists (blindspots) for up to 21.1% of cases on HELOC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks two recourse-provision methods against the reachable-set ground truth: AR (Ustun et al., KDD 2019), a model-specific method for linear classifiers, and DiCE (Mothilal et al., FAccT 2020), a model-agnostic gradient-based method. Reach achieves 0% loopholes and 0% blindspots by construction, since it exhaustively enumerates all reachable points. AR produces loopholes in 2.2%–41.1% of denied individuals (depending on dataset and model), primarily because it cannot enforce non-separable constraints such as thermometer encodings and directional causal linkages; its blindspot rate is negligible (0%–1.3%). DiCE is less reliable: it generates loopholes in 16.6%–57.7% of cases and exhibits blindspots of up to 21.1% on HELOC, where it returns a 'no counterfactuals found' error for roughly half of the points that actually have recourse. On the HELOC dataset with an XGB model, DiCE returns a loophole for 42.1% of denied individuals, meaning it suggests actions that are physically impossible under the specified actionability constraints. The key takeaway is that both baselines are unreliable for verification: AR's loopholes are silent (it outputs an action that violates constraints), while DiCE's blindspots are overt but leave practitioners unable to distinguish genuine infeasibility from algorithmic failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing algorithmic recourse from a provision task (finding one actionable change) to a verification task (certifying whether any actionable change exists), positioning it as a model auditing procedure. It repurposes mixed-integer programming and no-good constraints—standard tools in combinatorial optimization—to construct reachable sets that encode complex, non-separable actionability constraints (thermometer encodings, directional causal linkages) that prior recourse methods like AR and DiCE cannot handle. The paper explicitly departs from the provision-focused literature [Ustun et al. 2019, Mothilal et al. 2020] by arguing that those methods are structurally ill-suited for verification because they cannot certify infeasibility, and it connects to broader threads on robustness of recourse under distribution shifts, model updates, and causal effects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that 7–31% of denied individuals in consumer finance datasets receive predictions that are provably unchangeable under realistic actionability constraints, and that widely-used recourse methods (AR, DiCE) produce fundamentally impossible actions (loopholes) for 2–58% of those individuals—meaning practitioners may be presenting consumers with recourse that cannot be executed. The finding that infeasibility is masked when only simple separable constraints (immutability, monotonicity) are enforced, and only surfaces with complex non-separable constraints, challenges the assumption that standard recourse tooling provides adequate safety guarantees. Key limitations include restriction to discrete feature spaces (no probabilistic guarantees for continuous features) and the absence of probabilistic causal effects, leaving open the question of how to extend verification to the continuous, stochastic settings that many real-world applications require.
