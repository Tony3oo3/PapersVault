## Problem

When a neural network is coupled with a differentiable physics simulator and unrolled over many time steps, the standard BPTT gradient becomes severely unbalanced—exploding in some regions and vanishing in others—making gradient-based optimization fail on complex control tasks. Existing remedies focus on architectures or loss formulations, but the backpropagation step itself, where the imbalance originates, has been largely unexplored as a target for modification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241150/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each unrolled time step, the network receives the current physical state x_i (a vector whose dimension depends on the system, e.g., positions and velocities for cart-pole, or a discretized wavefunction for quantum control) and outputs a scalar or low-dimensional control signal c_i. The simulator then advances the state to x_{i+1}. Training minimizes a quadratic or inner-product loss between the final (or accumulated) physical state and a target state y, with the network parameters θ as the only learned quantities. The forward pass and loss are identical across all compared methods; only the backward pass differs.

## Method

The method modifies the backpropagation pass in two steps. First, gradient stopping is applied to block all feedback paths that traverse the network N (setting ∂_x N = 0) while preserving the physics path ∂_x S, yielding a 'modified update' whose magnitudes remain balanced because physical simulators enforce conservation laws or bounded state changes. Second, because this modification decouples forward and backward passes and introduces rotation into the vector field (causing momentum-based optimizers like Adam to spiral around minima rather than converge), a component-wise sign comparison is performed between the regular gradient and the modified update: the final 'combined update' retains the modified update's j-th component only where both agree in sign, and is zeroed otherwise. This preserves the balanced magnitudes of the modified update while restoring the correct descent direction near minima, and the entire procedure is implementable with standard gradient-stopping and element-wise operations without affecting scalability.

## Results

On the cart pole swing-up task (4 poles), the combined update (C) achieves a best loss of 0.00145 with 37/78 runs below loss 0.01, compared to 7/78 for the regular gradient (R) and 24/78 for the modified update (M); the stopped update (S) fails entirely (best loss 0.644). On the hardest quantum control task (4th excited state), (R) and (S) make no progress (best losses 85.6 and 38.4), while (M) and (C) both reach a best loss of 19.0 with 14/60 runs below loss 30. On guidance-by-repulsion, (M) and (C) achieve best losses of 0.465 and 0.495 respectively, outperforming (R) at 0.590, while (S) stagnates at 5.24.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard BPTT gradient (R), the proposed combined update (C) and modified update (M) consistently outperform across all three tasks, with the gap widening as task complexity increases: on the 4-pole cart pole, (C) achieves 37 successful runs vs. only 7 for (R); on the hardest quantum control target, (R) fails to solve the task at all while (C) reaches loss 19.0. Against the truncated/stopped BPTT baseline (S), attributed to Prantl et al. (2022) and Brandstetter et al. (2022), the proposed methods dominate decisively: (S) fails to learn on guidance-by-repulsion (constant loss ~5.24), fails on cart pole (best loss 0.644), and makes no meaningful progress on quantum control (best loss 38.4 vs. 19.0 for C/M). Comparing (C) to the ablation (M), the sign-comparison step provides a clear advantage on cart pole (37 vs. 24 runs below 0.01; best loss 0.00145 vs. 0.00165) but the two methods perform nearly identically on quantum control and guidance-by-repulsion, suggesting the rotation-correction is most beneficial in tasks with sharper, more closely-spaced minima.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard Backpropagation Through Time (Werbos, 1988) by directly modifying the backpropagation pass rather than the network architecture or loss formulation. It is positioned against truncated BPTT approaches (Sutskever, 2013; Prantl et al., 2022; Brandstetter et al., 2022) that reduce unroll steps to limit gradient imbalance, arguing instead that the root cause can be addressed by exploiting the balanced gradient flow inherent to physics simulators. The specific combination of gradient stopping through the network path and a component-wise sign comparison to counteract the resulting rotational vector field is, to the authors' knowledge, novel. The work sits within the broader differentiable simulation and physics-based deep learning literature (Thuerey et al., 2021; Holl et al., 2020) but targets the optimization step specifically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a simple, scalable fix (gradient stop + sign comparison) for a well-known failure mode in recurrent simulator-network training, with the striking result that standard BPTT completely fails on the hardest quantum control task while the proposed method succeeds. A key open question is whether the approach extends to contact-rich scenarios with discontinuous dynamics, where the assumption of well-behaved physics gradient flow may break down. The paper also opens a broader research direction: the space of alternative vector fields for optimization in recurrent setups remains largely unexplored, and the rotational-field analysis provides a framework for understanding why naive modifications to backpropagation can destabilize standard optimizers like Adam.
