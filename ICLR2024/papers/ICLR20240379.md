## Problem

Hardness Characterization Methods (HCMs) aim to identify 'hard' samples that impede ML training, but the term 'hardness' remains vague and ill-defined in the literature, with different methods unintentionally targeting different aspects of hardness. Existing evaluations are largely qualitative or focus on a single hardness type (often the simplest, e.g., uniform mislabeling), leaving it unclear how HCMs perform across the full spectrum of hardness manifestations. This paper addresses the gap by formalizing a fine-grained hardness taxonomy and providing the first comprehensive quantitative benchmarking of HCMs across multiple hardness types.

## Input / Output

H-CAT takes a training dataset (grayscale 28×28 MNIST, RGB 32×32 CIFAR-10, or tabular OpenML datasets), a backbone model (LeNet or ResNet-18), and a specified hardness perturbation type and proportion p, then applies controlled perturbations (e.g., label flipping, Gaussian noise, image replacement, crop/zoom shifts) to create ground-truth hard samples. The 13 integrated HCMs each produce a per-sample scalar score, which is thresholded into an Easy/Hard label; the Evaluator module then computes D-AUPRC and D-AUROC against the known perturbed samples, along with critical difference diagrams for statistical significance and Spearman rank correlations for cross-run stability. The framework is modality-agnostic and supports arbitrary user-specified metrics on raw scores.

## Method

The paper contributes a formal taxonomy of sample-level hardness organized into three categories—Mislabeling (Uniform, Asymmetric, Instance), OoD/Outlier (Near, Far), and Atypical (Shift, Zoom)—each defined by how the joint distribution P_XY is perturbed relative to the clean distribution. H-CAT is a four-module benchmarking framework (Dataloader → Hardness → HCM → Evaluator) that applies controlled hardness perturbations, runs 13 HCMs under a unified fit-predict interface spanning learning-dynamics-based (margin, uncertainty, loss, gradient, forgetting, statistics), distance-based, and statistical-based methods, and quantitatively evaluates detection performance. The benchmark comprises over 14K experimental setups varying HCM, hardness type, perturbation proportion (p ∈ [0.05, 0.5]), dataset, backbone model, and random seed. Key findings include that uncertainty-based methods (Data Maps, Data-IQ, AUM) are the best general-purpose HCMs, that hardness types vary substantially in detectability (uniform mislabeling and Far-OoD are easiest; instance mislabeling and Atypical are hardest), and that HCMs within the same class are not statistically distinguishable, while learning-dynamics and distance-based methods are the most stable across runs.

## Results

Across 14K+ experimental setups (13 HCMs × 8 hardness types × multiple datasets, models, seeds, and perturbation proportions), the paper finds that learning-dynamics-based methods operating on output confidence — specifically uncertainty-based (Data Maps, Data-IQ) and margin-based (AUM) — are the best-performing general-purpose HCMs in terms of D-AUPRC across all hardness types. Gradient-based methods (GraNd, VoG), typically used for computational efficiency, perform competitively at low hardness proportions (p ≤ 0.1) but become notably less competitive at higher proportions. Hardness types vary significantly in difficulty: uniform mislabeling and Far-OoD are substantially easier to detect than instance-specific mislabeling and Atypical samples. Critical difference diagrams confirm that individual HCMs within the same broad class (e.g., two uncertainty-based methods) are not statistically significantly different from one another (p ≤ 0.05, Siegel-Friedman).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 13 HCMs against each other across 8 hardness types rather than proposing a single new method. Uncertainty-based methods (Data Maps, Data-IQ) and the margin-based AUM consistently achieve the highest D-AUPRC across all hardness types, making them the recommended general-purpose choice when the hardness type is unknown. Gradient-based methods (GraNd, VoG) and EL2N, which are typically deployed for computational efficiency (data diet), are surprisingly uncompetitive at higher hardness proportions (p ≥ 0.3), losing to simpler and cheaper methods. Prototypicality (distance-based) stands out as particularly strong on instance-specific mislabeling, where it can match samples via embedding-space similarity, and ALLSH (statistical-based, KL-divergence) is best suited for distributional shifts (Near/Far OoD). Cleanlab is the only statistical-based method with quantitative evaluation on both asymmetric and instance mislabeling in prior work, but in H-CAT's unified evaluation it does not dominate any single hardness type. Critically, the Siegel-Friedman critical difference diagrams show that within the same HCM class (e.g., Data Maps vs. Data-IQ, or GraNd vs. VoG), performance differences are not statistically significant (p > 0.05), meaning practitioners should select based on the expected hardness type and practical constraints rather than expecting large gains from one method over another in the same class.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a single prior method but rather unifies and re-evaluates 13 existing Hardness Characterization Methods (HCMs) — spanning training-dynamics (AUM, Data Maps, Data-IQ, GraNd, VoG, EL2N, Forgetting), statistical (Cleanlab, ALLSH, Agreement), and embedding-based (Prototypicality) approaches — under a new formal taxonomy of hardness types (Mislabeling, OoD/Outlier, Atypical) that prior work treated ad hoc. It departs from the dominant evaluation paradigm in the HCM literature, which relied on qualitative showcases or downstream-performance improvements, by introducing direct quantitative detection metrics (D-AUPRC, D-AUROC) and a unified software toolkit (H-CAT) for controlled, multi-type benchmarking. The taxonomy itself formalizes distinctions (e.g., uniform vs. asymmetric vs. instance mislabeling; near vs. far OoD; atypical long-tail) that individual HCM papers had implicitly but inconsistently targeted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a critical gap: before H-CAT, no work had quantitatively compared HCMs across multiple hardness types under controlled conditions, leaving practitioners without principled guidance on which method to use for which data-quality problem. The finding that HCMs within the same class are statistically indistinguishable, and that uncertainty/margin-based methods serve as the best general-purpose choice, provides actionable selection criteria. Key limitations include evaluation on only MNIST and CIFAR-10 (plus two tabular sets), binary (not continuous) hardness, and exclusion of computationally expensive methods like Data Shapley and RHO-Loss. Open questions include how hardness scores should guide training (e.g., curriculum learning), how to handle simultaneous multi-type hardness, and whether conclusions generalize to larger, noisier datasets like ImageNet.
