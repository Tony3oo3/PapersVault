## Problem

Existing graph kernels rely on manually crafted substructure features (paths, graphlets, walks) that overlap and inflate feature dimensionality, while GNNs require heuristic readout operations (sum, mean) to collapse node representations into a single graph vector, losing structural information. This paper asks whether graph similarity can be measured directly by treating each graph as a distribution over its node representations and comparing distributions via Maximum Mean Discrepancy, thereby avoiding both substructure enumeration and lossy readout functions.

## Input / Output

The method takes pairs of attributed labeled graphs (node attribute matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}, node label matrix Y, and adjacency matrix A) that may have unequal numbers of nodes, requiring no node permutation or alignment. It outputs a scalar similarity score s(G₁,G₂) = exp(−γ·d̂²_K) per pair, which assembles into a full N×N kernel matrix for downstream SVM classification or spectral clustering. The deep variant additionally produces learned L-level node embeddings Z_i^{(L)} as a byproduct of the message-passing layers.

## Method

The core mechanism replaces the readout function with a distributional comparison: after L rounds of message passing (Z^{(l)} = U·Z^{(l−1)} in the vanilla version, or Z^{(l)} = σ(U·Z^{(l−1)}·W^{(l)}) in the deep GCN-based version), the generalized MMD is computed as the supremum over a family of Gaussian kernels with varying bandwidths between the two sets of node representations, yielding a distance that is then mapped to a positive-definite similarity via an exponential. The deep variant introduces trainable weight matrices W^{(l)} at each propagation layer and is trained end-to-end using a supervised contrastive loss (SCL) when labels are available, or unsupervised losses (a contrastive loss UCL selecting most/least similar pairs, or a KL-divergence loss encouraging cluster structure) when labels are absent. Theoretical analysis provides perturbation-robustness upper bounds showing the MMD deviation grows bi-quadratically with feature perturbation and exponentially with propagation depth, plus a uniform-stability-based generalization error bound for the supervised setting.

## Results

On graph classification (Table 2), Deep MMD-GK (supervised) achieves the best accuracy on 4 of 5 benchmarks: BZR (0.910), MUTAG (0.915), PTC_FM (0.668), and PROTEINS (0.776), while DeepMap edges it out on DHFR (0.852 vs. 0.848). On graph clustering (Table 3), Deep MMD-GK (unsupervised) delivers dramatic gains in NMI and ARI, e.g., 0.757 NMI / 0.809 ARI on BZR versus the best baseline (GraphCL) at 0.040 NMI / 0.115 ARI, and 0.468 NMI / 0.476 ARI on MUTAG versus GraphCL's 0.322 / 0.234. The vanilla MMD-GK is competitive but generally outperformed by the deep variants, particularly on DHFR (0.828) and MUTAG (0.819) in classification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Deep MMD-GK (supervised) outperforms all graph-kernel baselines (Graphlet, Shortest Path, WL, Wasserstein WL) by a wide margin on every dataset, with the largest gaps on DHFR (+0.155 over WL) and MUTAG (+0.127 over WL). Against deep learning baselines, it beats DGCNN on BZR (+0.066), MUTAG (+0.042), and PROTEINS (+0.004), and surpasses DGK on all five datasets. DeepMap is the closest competitor, winning only on DHFR (0.852 vs. 0.848) while Deep MMD-GK (supervised) leads on the other four. In clustering, Deep MMD-GK (unsupervised) dominates all GNN-based baselines (InfoGraph, GraphCL) by large margins in NMI and ARI—e.g., 0.757 vs. 0.040 NMI on BZR and 0.468 vs. 0.322 on MUTAG—while also exceeding traditional kernel methods (Graphlet, WL, Shortest Path) that produce near-zero NMI/ARI on most datasets. The vanilla MMD-GK is competitive with Wasserstein WL on DHFR (0.828 vs. 0.793) but trails the deep variants by 2–10 points across datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads: MMD-based distribution comparison (Smola et al., 2006; Fukumizu et al., 2009) and message-passing graph propagation (Kipf & Welling, 2016). Prior graph kernels (WL, shortest-path, graphlet, Wasserstein-WL) decompose graphs into substructures and compare counts, while GNNs rely on heuristic readout functions (sum/mean pooling) to collapse node representations into a single vector. MMD-GK departs from both by treating each graph as a distribution over node representations and directly comparing these distributions via MMD, eliminating the need for substructure enumeration or readout operations. The deep variant further integrates trainable GCN layers with contrastive losses (supervised SCL, unsupervised UCL/KL), positioning it between classical kernel methods and end-to-end GNN classifiers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The approach offers a principled, readout-free alternative for graph similarity that naturally handles unequal-sized graphs and works without labels, addressing practical limitations of both substructure-based kernels and GNN readout functions. The theoretical robustness bounds (Theorems 1–2) provide rare guarantees for graph kernels under perturbation, and the generalization error bound for the supervised variant connects to uniform stability theory. However, the O(n²) MMD computation limits scalability to large graphs, the method's sensitivity to the aggregation level l (with performance peaks and declines) requires careful tuning, and the counter-examples in Appendix B reveal cases where MMD underestimates structural differences. Open questions include whether the approach scales to graphs with thousands of nodes and whether tighter generalization bounds can be derived for the nonconvex optimization landscape.
