## Problem

Accelerated SGD (ASGD) with momentum is widely used in deep learning and empirically achieves better generalization than vanilla SGD, yet existing optimization theory only explains its faster convergence, not its generalization advantage. Prior excess risk bounds for ASGD (Jain et al., 2018; Varre & Flammarion, 2022) are restricted to finite-dimensional or strongly convex settings and cannot be applied when the number of parameters exceeds the number of samples. This paper asks whether momentum provably improves generalization in the overparameterized linear regression regime, and under what spectral conditions it does or does not help.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes i.i.d. samples (x_t, y_t) from an unknown distribution D with finite second and fourth moments, an initial weight vector w_0 in a Hilbert space, and ASGD hyperparameters (δ, γ, β, α) chosen via a specific recipe parameterized by a free integer κ̃. The output is a tail-averaged weight vector w̄_{s:s+N} and an instance-dependent excess risk upper bound that decomposes into effective variance and effective bias, each expressed as a sum over eigen-subspaces of the data covariance matrix H with rates determined by the eigenvalues λ_i. The bound is dimension-free: it depends on the ambient dimension d only through the eigenspectrum of H, not through d explicitly.

## Method

The core technique extends the linear-operator framework of Jain et al. (2018) to the overparameterized regime by introducing a free parameter κ̃ that controls the fourth-moment contribution without incurring dimension-dependent factors. The centered ASGD iterate is decomposed into bias and variance sequences, and the population update matrix A is block-diagonalized by the eigenvectors of H, reducing the analysis to characterizing the 2×2 block A_i for each eigenvalue λ_i. The eigenvalues of A_i are real or complex depending on λ_i relative to two spectral cutoffs k^‡ and k^†, and their magnitudes determine the exponential decay rate of bias in each eigen-subspace. The key finding is that ASGD's bias decays faster than SGD's in small-eigenvalue subspaces (i > k̂) but slower in large-eigenvalue subspaces (i ≤ k̂), while its variance is uniformly larger than SGD's because γ ≥ δ. A specific parameter choice (δ ≤ 1/(2ψ·tr(H)), γ ∈ [δ, 1/(2ψ·Σ_{i>κ̃} λ_i)], β = δ/(ψκ̃γ), α = 1/(1+β)) ensures the bound is valid and recovers a tighter bias coefficient O(κκ̃/N²) in the strongly convex regime, improving upon the O(κ^{13/4}κ̃^{9/4}d/N²) of Jain et al. (2018).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) provides an instance-dependent excess risk bound for tail-averaged ASGD in the overparameterized regime, decomposed into effective variance and effective bias that depend on the eigenspectrum of the data covariance matrix. Theorem 5.1 establishes a concrete separation: there exist linear regression instances where ASGD achieves excess risk O(σ²(N^{-1/2} + N^{-2}·0.9873^s)) while SGD is lower-bounded by Ω(σ²(N^{-1/2} + N^{-2}·0.996^s)), demonstrating faster exponential bias decay for ASGD. In the strongly convex setting (Corollary 4.4), the bias coefficient improves to O(κκ̃/N²), a significant tightening over the O(κ^{13/4}κ̃^{9/4}d/N²) bound of Jain et al. (2018). The variance error of ASGD is provably always no smaller than that of SGD in every eigen-subspace.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SGD (Zou et al., 2021b), ASGD has a strictly larger variance error in every eigen-subspace because γ ≥ δ by the parameter choice, making the noise amplification factor Nγ²λᵢ² ≥ Nδ²λᵢ². However, ASGD's bias error decays exponentially faster than SGD in the small-eigenvalue subspaces (i > k̂), with rate (1−(γ+δ)λᵢ/2)^s versus (1−δλᵢ)^s, while it decays slower in the large-eigenvalue subspaces (i ≤ k̂). Theorem 5.1 constructs a concrete instance (λᵢ = i^{-2}, w₀ aligned with small eigenvalues) where ASGD's overall excess risk is strictly smaller than SGD's, with bias decay 0.9873^s vs. 0.996^s. Against Jain et al. (2018) in the strongly convex regime, the paper recovers the same exponential rate and leading-order variance but improves the bias coefficient from O(κ^{13/4}κ̃^{9/4}d/N²) to O(κκ̃/N²), eliminating the dimension-dependent factor d and reducing the condition-number dependence from κ^{13/4} to κ. The net conclusion is that momentum helps generalization only when the initialization error w₀−w* is concentrated in small-eigenvalue directions and the noise level σ² is small.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two prior lines of work: (1) the overparameterized SGD analysis of Zou et al. (2021b) and Wu et al. (2022), which established dimension-free, instance-dependent excess risk bounds for SGD via eigenspectrum decomposition, and (2) the ASGD analysis of Jain et al. (2018), which proved accelerated bias decay for strongly convex least squares but was limited to finite-dimensional settings. The paper extends the linear-operator and fourth-moment techniques of Jain et al. (2018) to the overparameterized regime by introducing a free parameter κ̃ that removes dimension dependence, while adopting the bias-variance decomposition and effective-dimension framework from Zou et al. (2021b). It also subsumes the general-convex ASGD result of Varre & Flammarion (2022), which was dimension-dependent and did not reveal exponential bias decay with constant stepsize.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first instance-dependent, dimension-free excess risk bound for ASGD in the overparameterized regime, filling a clear gap between the strongly-convex ASGD theory and the overparameterized SGD theory. The key practical insight—that momentum helps generalization only when the initialization error aligns with small-eigenvalue directions while always increasing variance—gives a concrete, testable criterion for when ASGD should be preferred over SGD. However, the analysis is restricted to linear regression with specific parameter choices, and the experiments use simple synthetic settings; extending the subspace-dependent comparison to nonlinear models or to practical deep learning architectures remains open. The improved bias coefficient in the strongly convex case (O(κκ̃/N²) vs. O(κ^{13/4}κ̃^{9/4}d/N²)) is a meaningful technical advance but of limited direct practical impact.
