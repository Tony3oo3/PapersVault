## Problem

Audio-language models (ALMs) trained with contrastive pre-training (e.g., CLAP) achieve strong retrieval performance but largely fail at compositional reasoning—understanding the temporal order of acoustic events or binding attributes to specific events. Existing audio-retrieval benchmarks (Clotho, AudioCaps) are insufficient for evaluating this capability because most test samples contain only a single acoustic event, allowing models to succeed without genuine compositional understanding. This paper identifies and quantifies this gap, showing that current ALMs perform only marginally better than random chance on compositional tasks.

## Input / Output

Each CompA benchmark instance consists of two (or three) audio-caption pairs where the audios contain the same acoustic events in different compositions and the captions use identical words in different orders; the model must match the correct audio to the correct caption via cosine similarity in a shared embedding space. For the CompA-CLAP model, inputs are 10-second audio clips (encoded by HTSAT-large) and natural-language captions (encoded by Flan-T5-large), producing a shared embedding where retrieval is performed by nearest-neighbor matching. The modular contrastive training additionally consumes template-generated fine-grained positive captions at multiple granularities (e.g., full scene, sub-scene, single event) alongside hard negatives with swapped compositions.

## Method

CompA-CLAP fine-tunes CLAP in two successive stages. First, contrastive pre-training with compositionally-aware hard negatives: GPT-4 generates K=3 hard negative captions per audio by swapping event ordering, replacing prepositions, or swapping noun-verb associations, with the key modification that each audio's hard negatives are excluded from other audios' loss computation for more focused training. Second, modular contrastive learning: a template-based algorithm synthesizes compositional audios by concatenating or overlaying single-event snippets from AudioSet Strong (up to 4 events), then generates multiple fine-grained positive captions at varying granularities (full scene down to individual events) and hard negatives by interchanging orders and attributes. This modular approach requires no pre-existing compositional audio-caption pairs and forces the model to learn fine-grained order and attribute-binding by aligning the same audio to captions of different specificity. The model uses HTSAT-large (audio) and Flan-T5-large (text) encoders with an InfoNCE-based contrastive objective, initialized from vanilla CLAP weights and fine-tuned on only the last few layers to preserve pre-trained features.

## Results

On the proposed CompA-order benchmark, CompA-CLAP achieves 40.70% text score, 35.60% audio score, and 33.85% group score, substantially outperforming the best baseline CLAP-LAION (24.0/9.25/5.50) and random chance (19.70/19.70/16.67). On CompA-attribute, CompA-CLAP reaches 44.28% text, 22.52% audio, and 15.13% group score, again far exceeding all baselines (best: CLAP-LAION at 34.78/6.52/5.07). Despite these gains in compositional reasoning, CompA-CLAP retains near-parity performance on standard retrieval benchmarks (e.g., 90.2% R@10 text-to-audio on AudioCaps vs. 90.7% for vanilla CLAP) and zero-shot classification (e.g., 89.1% on ESC-50 vs. 90.2%), confirming that compositional fine-tuning does not degrade general audio-language capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CompA-CLAP outperforms all four baselines (MMT, ML-ACT, CLAP, CLAP-LAION) by 10%–28% on both CompA benchmarks. On CompA-order, the improvement over the strongest baseline CLAP-LAION is approximately 16.7 points in text score (40.70 vs. 24.00) and 28.1 points in group score (33.85 vs. 5.50). On CompA-attribute, the gap is even wider in audio score (22.52 vs. 6.52, a ~16-point margin) and group score (15.13 vs. 5.07). Notably, all baselines perform at or near random chance on the audio and group metrics, confirming that existing ALMs lack compositional reasoning. On standard retrieval and zero-shot classification benchmarks (Table 1), CompA-CLAP matches or slightly trails its vanilla CLAP counterpart by less than 1.5 points, demonstrating that the compositional fine-tuning does not sacrifice general audio-language performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Winoground benchmark format (Thrush et al., 2022) and the compositionally-aware hard-negative contrastive fine-tuning strategy from Yuksekgonul et al. (2023) from the vision-language domain to audio-language models, making it the first systematic study of compositional reasoning in ALMs. It builds directly on CLAP's contrastive audio-language pre-training framework (Elizalde et al., 2023), extending it with two novel training stages: LLM-generated hard negatives and a modular contrastive objective that aligns an audio with multiple granularity-level captions. The modular contrastive approach departs from prior hard-negative methods by eliminating the need for pre-existing compositional audio-caption pairs, instead synthesizing them from single-event snippets via template-based composition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that current ALMs are essentially bag-of-words models for audio, performing near-random on compositional reasoning tasks, which has downstream implications for text-to-audio generation and retrieval systems that rely on CLAP-style encoders. However, even CompA-CLAP's best group score on CompA-attribute (15.13) remains below the random baseline (16.67), indicating the problem is far from solved and the benchmarks (400/200 instances) are too small for robust conclusions. The modular contrastive learning paradigm is a promising direction that could be extended to larger compositional structures (beyond 2–4 events) and more diverse attribute types, but the current results suggest that compositional understanding in audio requires fundamentally different inductive biases than what contrastive learning alone can provide.
