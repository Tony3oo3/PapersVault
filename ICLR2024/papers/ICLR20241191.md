## Problem

Existing motion analysis methods either focus exclusively on micro (tiny, local) motions via frequency-domain filtering and cannot handle 3D scenes, or represent 3D dynamics through dense correspondences (e.g., deformation fields in D-NeRF) that lack an interpretable, low-dimensional motion representation amenable to factorization and editing. The paper identifies the need for a dynamic scene representation that simultaneously captures macro motions faithfully, supports decomposition in both spatial and frequency domains, and generalizes to 3D scenes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 2D video or a set of multi-view videos (for 3D scenes) as input and produces a low-dimensional set of m time-varying phase sequences (one per Gabor subband) that encode the scene's macro motion, along with rendered RGB frames. For 3D scenes, the spatial polynomial Gabor field outputs a 33-channel latent (32 feature + 1 density) that is aggregated via NeRF-style volume rendering along rays before being decoded by a 2D U-Net CNN into high-resolution RGB. The phase space dimension m is set by the number of spatial subbands and can be made redundant (analogous to empty slots in Slot-Attention), with unused phases identified post-hoc via a contribution score.

## Method

Phase-PGF represents a dynamic scene as f(x,t) = p_L ∘ … ∘ p_1 ∘ Ψ(x,t), where Ψ is a feature encoding built from shift-orthogonal Gabor basis functions g_j(x + h_j(t)) modulated by m temporally-varying phases h_j(t). Each phase h_j(t) is generated by a 1-D Polynomial Neural Field (PNF) that decomposes the phase into frequency subbands, enabling Fourier-domain manipulation (band-limit filtering for smoothing, scaling for intensity adjustment). The spatial Gabor fields are also organized into subbands, and each subband's basis is shifted by its corresponding phase, creating a natural spatial-frequency factorization of motion. A neural latent decoder (U-Net with transposed convolutions) upsamples the low-resolution feature map to full resolution, and for 3D scenes a NeRF-style volume rendering equation aggregates ray-marched features. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) low-res reconstruction loss, (2) patch-based high-res reconstruction with VGG perceptual loss, and (3) adversarial training where the decoder is regularized to produce plausible images under extrapolated (magnified) phases via a PatchGAN discriminator.

## Results

On motion magnification (Table 3), Phase-PGF achieves a Motion Magnification Score of 3.30/5 and Visual Quality Score of 3.14/5, substantially outperforming Phase-NIVR (3.06, 1.76) and Wadhwa et al. (2.24, 1.88). The FID score of 0.512 for Phase-PGF is dramatically lower than Phase-NIVR (10.255) and Wadhwa et al. (5.889), indicating far superior rendering quality. In the human preference study on phase interpretability (Table 1), Phase-PGF is preferred over Phase-NIVR in 79.2% of cases on average across five sequences. On synthetic datasets with ground-truth trajectories (Table 2), Phase-PGF achieves normalized cross-correlation of 0.999 (Damping) and 0.998 (Projectile-X), versus 0.015 and 0.793 for Phase-NIVR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Phase-PGF is compared against two baselines: Phase-NIVR (Mai & Liu, 2022), a phase-based neural implicit video representation, and Wadhwa et al. (2013), a classical phase-based video motion processing method. On motion magnification quality, Phase-PGF wins decisively on all three metrics: it achieves a Visual Quality Score of 3.14 versus 1.76 for Phase-NIVR and 1.88 for Wadhwa et al., and an FID of 0.512 versus 10.255 and 5.889 respectively — a roughly 20× improvement over Phase-NIVR in FID. On phase interpretability, Phase-PGF is preferred by human raters 79.2% of the time over Phase-NIVR, and achieves near-perfect normalized cross-correlation (0.999) on the Damping trajectory where Phase-NIVR scores only 0.015. Wadhwa et al. fails to magnify macro motions at all (score 2.24/5) because it was designed for tiny, local motions, while Phase-NIVR produces temporally flickering videos with ghosting artifacts. Phase-PGF's advantage stems from its Gabor-based polynomial field architecture that natively decomposes motion into interpretable, low-dimensional phase components, whereas Phase-NIVR relies on SIREN networks that cannot decompose scene components.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Polynomial Neural Fields (Yang et al., 2022) as the architectural backbone for both the temporal phase generator and the spatial Gabor field, and on NeRF-style volume rendering (Mildenhall et al., 2021) for 3D scene synthesis. It departs from traditional Eulerian/phase-based motion magnification (Wu et al., 2012; Wadhwa et al., 2013), which is limited to micro motions in 2D, and from dynamic NeRF approaches (D-NeRF, NeRFies) that encode motion via dense deformation or flow fields that resist direct analysis. The key conceptual departure is using temporally-varying phases to modulate a shift-orthogonal Gabor basis, yielding a low-dimensional, factorizable motion representation that simultaneously supports 2D and 3D scenes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified framework that handles macro motion (as opposed to micro motion) in both 2D and 3D with theoretical guarantees for periodicity detection, motion separation, smoothing, and intensity adjustment. Its practical impact is demonstrated through loop detection, selective motion editing, and magnification with substantially lower FID than baselines. However, the method assumes objects have concrete shapes and rigid per-component motion, fails on chaotic or shapeless content (hair, fire), and does not scale to complex large-scale 3D scenes due to the need for many Gabor basis functions. Open questions include spatially adaptive basis selection, handling non-rigid deformations more gracefully, and whether the phase-space redundancy mechanism generalizes to scenes with many interacting objects.
