## Problem

Standard MDP formulations assume actions are executed immediately, yet real-world systems (robotics, healthcare, autonomous driving) experience execution delays that can be stochastic. Existing approaches to handle delays rely on state augmentation with pending action queues, whose complexity grows exponentially with delay and which cannot generalize to random delay values. The paper asks: how can one effectively perform policy optimization in environments where action execution is subject to random, observed delays without resorting to state augmentation?

## Input / Output

The agent receives RGB environment observations (Atari frames) and the current delay value z_t, which is assumed to be observed in real-time. It maintains two internal queues of length M (the maximum delay): a queue of past actions and a queue of past delay values. The output is a single action a_t selected via MCTS over a predicted future state ŝ_{t+z_t}, not the current state. The executed action in the environment is a_{τ_t} (determined by the effective decision time), which may differ from a_t when delays cause overwriting or duplication of pending actions.

## Method

DEZ extends EfficientZero by introducing a Pending Queue Resolver (PQR) that, given the action and delay queues, computes the effective decision time τ_t and determines which past actions are still pending. At each step, the agent iteratively applies the learned forward model G for z_t steps to predict the future state ŝ_{t+z_t}, then runs MCTS on that predicted state to select the current action. A key theoretical result proves that restricting policy search to Markov policies (indexed by the effective decision time) is sufficient for optimality in SED-MDPs, extending the deterministic-delay result of Derman et al. (2021). The replay buffer is post-processed to store transitions (s_t, a_{τ_t}, r_t) rather than (s_t, a_t, r_t), effectively removing delays from training data so standard EfficientZero learning procedures apply. The policy loss is modified to target the predicted future state: L_p = L(π_t(ŝ_{t+z_t}), p_t(s_{t+z_t})), leveraging the high precision of the latent-space dynamics model.

## Results

On 15 Atari games with constant execution delays (M ∈ {5, 15, 25}), DEZ achieves the highest average score in 39 of 45 experiments. Under stochastic delays (random walk with max M), DEZ wins 42 of 45 experiments, demonstrating greater resilience to delay variability. For example, on Asterix with M=5 stochastic delay, DEZ scores 6535.5 vs. 507.3 (Oblivious EZ) and 268.3 (Delayed DQN); on Hero with M=15, DEZ reaches 11890.3 vs. 2466.7 and 669.5 respectively. All experiments use only 130K environment interactions, close to the Atari 100K benchmark.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DEZ is compared against two baselines: Oblivious EfficientZero (which ignores delay entirely and produces stationary policies) and Delayed DQN (Derman et al., 2021, a DQN-based method designed for constant delays). Under constant delays, DEZ wins 39/45 experiments; Delayed DQN is competitive in 21/45 cases (achieving ≥85% of DEZ's score) thanks to its access to the true environment transition kernel, but struggles under the 130K sample budget. Under stochastic delays, the gap widens dramatically: Delayed DQN degrades severely (e.g., on Hero M=5 it drops from 948.7 constant to 492.0 stochastic, while DEZ improves from 11233.2 to 11798.8), and Oblivious EZ also suffers because stationary policies are provably suboptimal. DEZ's advantage is most pronounced on games with high score variance (Asterix, Qbert, RoadRunner) where the learned forward model's multi-step lookahead provides substantial planning benefit. The only notable exception is TimePilot at M=25 stochastic, where Delayed DQN slightly edges DEZ (2523.5 vs. 1931.3), likely due to the game's specific dynamics interacting poorly with the random-walk delay process.

## Relation to Prior Work

DEZ extends the ED-MDP formulation of Derman et al. (2021) from deterministic to stochastic execution delays, and adapts the EfficientZero (Ye et al., 2021) architecture—specifically its latent-space world model and MCTS planning—to handle the resulting non-stationary decision problem. Conceptually, the paper departs from the state-augmentation paradigm (Bouteiller et al., 2020; Katsikopoulos & Engelbrecht, 2003) by proving that observing the delay process makes Markov policies sufficient, avoiding the exponential blowup of augmenting state with pending action queues. It also relates to Walsh et al. (2009) and Delayed DQN, which handle fixed delays via forward-model inference, but DEZ generalizes to random delays and integrates with the broader model-based MCTS family (MuZero, DreamerV3, IRIS).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Markov-policy-sufficiency theorem for stochastic delays is a clean theoretical contribution that removes the need for history-dependent policies or state augmentation, making delayed RL tractable at scale. Practically, DEZ demonstrates that a learned world model can compensate for delays up to 25 steps in Atari with only 130K samples, a non-trivial sample-efficiency result. Open questions include: (i) whether a model-free approach can handle delays without any forward model, (ii) what happens when delay values are not observed in real time, and (iii) how to extend to continuous or state-dependent delay processes. The reliance on an accurate learned dynamics model means compounding errors in highly stochastic environments remain a vulnerability.
