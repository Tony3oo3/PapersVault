## Problem

Standard alignment methods such as RLHF rely on PPO, which is unstable, computationally expensive, and requires full retraining whenever the reward model changes, making rapid customization to new preferences impractical. The paper asks whether alignment can be achieved entirely at decoding time, eliminating the need for any RL-based training of the language model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240712/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a text prompt (up to 2,048 tokens) and a pre-trained language model paired with a separately trained reward model, ARGS generates a continuation of up to 128 tokens. At each decoding step, only the top-k most probable tokens from the LM (k=10 in practice) are scored, keeping the search space tractable. The reward model and language model need not share architecture or size, enabling flexible pairing such as an OPT-2.7b LM guided by an OPT-125m reward model.

## Method

ARGS reformulates alignment as a reward-guided search over the decoding process. At each step t, for each of the top-k candidate tokens v, it computes a composite score s(v, x_<t) = LM(v|x_<t) + w·r([x_<t, v]), where w is a tunable weight trading off semantic coherence against reward alignment. The selected token is either the argmax (ARGS-greedy) or sampled from a temperature-scaled softmax over the k scores (ARGS-stochastic). No gradient updates to the language model are required—only a pre-trained LM and a reward model trained with a standard pairwise Bradley-Terry loss. The overall complexity is O(k·m²), adding only a constant factor k over standard O(m²) decoding, and the framework is model- and task-agnostic, demonstrated across LLaMA and OPT architectures at multiple scales.

## Results

On HH-RLHF with LLaMA-7B-SFT, ARGS-greedy (w=1.5, k=10) achieves an average reward of 6.872, a 19.56% relative improvement over the greedy decoding baseline (5.732). In GPT-4 evaluation, ARGS secures a 64.33% win-tie rate against greedy decoding and 62.00% against contrastive search. On the SHP dataset with OPT-1.3b, ARGS achieves an average reward of 5.98, slightly exceeding PPO (5.88) while significantly outperforming both in diversity (0.322 vs. 0.247) and coherence (0.390 vs. 0.264).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARGS consistently outperforms all five baselines on average reward. Against Top-k sampling (Fan et al., 2018), ARGS-greedy improves average reward from 5.476 to 6.872 (+25.5%) and achieves a 64.33% vs. 54.33% GPT-4 win-tie rate. Against Nucleus sampling (Holtzman et al., 2020), the reward gap is even larger (5.117 → 6.872, +34.3%) with a 64.33% vs. 55.33% GPT-4 win-tie. Against Contrastive search (Su et al., 2022), ARGS wins on reward (5.245 → 6.872) and GPT-4 evaluation (64.33% vs. 62.00%), though the margin is narrower. Against PPO (Ouyang et al., 2022) on the SHP dataset, ARGS achieves a comparable average reward (5.98 vs. 5.88) without any RL training, while substantially outperforming PPO in diversity (0.322 vs. 0.247) and coherence (0.390 vs. 0.264), and winning 72.33% in GPT-4 evaluation. Against DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), ARGS leads in average reward (5.98 vs. 5.65) and diversity (0.322 vs. 0.096), though DPO edges out slightly on coherence (0.372 vs. 0.390 is actually ARGS winning here as well).

## Relation to Prior Work

ARGS repurposes the reward model—already a standard component of RLHF pipelines (PPO, DPO)—from a training-time optimization signal into a decoding-time scoring function, shifting alignment from the training phase to inference. This connects to the guided decoding literature (FUDGE, GeDi, PPL-MCTS, contrastive decoding), which uses external models to steer token selection, but those works target controlled generation (style, constraints) rather than human-preference alignment. The closest concurrent work, Deng & Raffel (2023), also uses a reward model at decoding time but relies on a unidirectional model trained with cumulative squared error loss, whereas ARGS employs the pairwise Bradley-Terry ranking loss native to the RLHF framework, making it a drop-in replacement for the reward model already trained in standard pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: organizations can swap or update reward models without retraining the base LLM via expensive and unstable PPO, enabling rapid customization to new safety criteria or user preferences. However, the evaluation is limited to helpfulness/harmlessness on two datasets (HH-RLHF, SHP) with moderate model sizes (up to 7B), and the authors themselves note that coherence degrades at higher reward weights and that more complex tasks (multi-step reasoning) remain untested. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models, whether the circularity of using the same reward model for both guidance and evaluation inflates gains, and how ARGS interacts with other decoding-time interventions (e.g., constrained decoding, self-consistency).
