## Problem

Bandwidth constraints during signal acquisition—especially in hyperspectral imaging—impede real-time detection because the sheer data volume overwhelms transmission and processing pipelines. Existing compressive sensing approaches cannot leverage prior knowledge about the detection subject and degrade severely below 30% sampling rates, while most deep learning methods assume negligible acquisition cost and focus only on post-acquisition processing. This paper addresses the gap by performing pre-acquisition optical modulation guided by a trained deep learning model to reduce data volume at the source.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240342/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (224×224, 3 channels) or hyperspectral images (e.g., 200+ spectral channels) as input, which are physically displayed on an e-ink screen and optically modulated by a DMD before being captured by a spectrometer. The under-sampling mask determines which patch-kernel combinations are actually acquired, so only a fraction of the full patch-embedding output is ever measured. The output is a class label for image classification, with the key constraint that the mask must be fixed before the target object is known, unlike dynamic token-pruning methods.

## Method

LUM-ViT replaces the standard ViT patch-embedding layer with a kernel-level binarized single-channel convolutional layer whose binary patterns directly drive DMD spatial modulation, paired with a learnable under-sampling mask that selects which patch-kernel output points to retain. The mask is parameterized by trainable weights passed through a Linear layer and Softmax, then sampled via Gumbel-Softmax to enable end-to-end differentiable training with an MSE ratio loss enforcing the target under-sampling rate. Kernel-level weight binarization applies a step function per kernel with a per-kernel scale factor (mean of positive weights), ensuring DMD compatibility without full-layer binarization. A three-stage training strategy—(1) train the mask at full precision, (2) introduce binarization with the mask frozen, (3) fine-tune remaining layers with both mask and binary layer frozen—resolves the instability of jointly training binary and full-precision components. Starting from a MAE-base pretrained model, LUM-ViT achieves 81.9% Top-1 on ImageNet-1k at 10% sampling and 78.3% at 2% sampling, with a 4% drop when deployed on real DMD hardware.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k classification, LUM-ViT achieves 81.9% Top-1 accuracy at 10% under-sampling (1.8% loss vs. the MAE-base upper bound of 83.7%) and 78.3% at an extreme 2% under-sampling (5.4% loss). In real-world DMD hardware experiments, LUM-ViT-2% attains 74.7% accuracy (excluding device-induced errors), a 4% drop from its software-based result. On hyperspectral classification, LUM-ViT retains near-original accuracy: 86.2% on Indian Pines, 88.6% on Pavia University, and 99.1% on Salinas after applying the under-sampling mask.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is MAE-base (83.7% Top-1), which serves as the no-under-sampling upper bound; LUM-ViT-10% loses only 1.8% while retaining 90% of the original data volume. Against DU-ViT (which reduces the number of convolutional kernels to achieve under-sampling), LUM-ViT surpasses it at all under-sampling rates, with the gap widening significantly at extreme rates (2%–5%) where DU-ViT's reduced embedding dimension severely limits expressiveness. Random-mask-ViT and Mag-mask-ViT (magnitude-based pruning) both underperform DU-ViT and show even larger gaps versus LUM-ViT, confirming that the learnable, task-adaptive mask is critical. The traditional Compressive Sensing (CS) method with OMP reconstruction produces unrecognizable images below 30% sampling and yields very poor classification accuracy, as it cannot leverage prior knowledge about the detection subject. In the real-world DMD experiment (Table 2), LUM-ViT-2% achieves 74.7% accuracy versus MAE-base's 75.7% (7-color), a 1% gap, demonstrating that the optical hardware introduces only ~4% additional degradation beyond the under-sampling loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

LUM-ViT extends the Vision Transformer (ViT) and MAE backbone by replacing the standard patch-embedding convolution with a kernel-level binarized single-channel convolutional layer designed for DMD optical computation, combined with a learnable under-sampling mask. Conceptually, it departs from Compressive Sensing-based pre-acquisition modulation (which relies on signal sparsity and cannot leverage task-specific prior information) by using a deep learning model trained on a compatible distribution to determine which optical measurements to retain. The learnable mask mechanism draws from transformer pruning work (DynamicViT, AdaViT) that uses Gumbel-Softmax for differentiable mask selection, but critically differs in requiring a fixed, pre-determined mask that cannot adapt to the specific input at inference time. The kernel-level binarization is distinct from BNN-style binarization aimed at lightweighting; here it serves purely as a hardware compatibility constraint for the DMD's binary spatial modulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to integrate DMD-based optical modulation directly into the ViT patch-embedding layer, bridging the gap between deep learning architectures and physical optical acquisition hardware. The demonstration that a fixed mask trained on a compatible distribution can achieve within 1.8% accuracy loss at 10% sampling (and within 5.5% at 2%) on ImageNet, with only ~4% additional degradation on real hardware, validates the practical viability of pre-acquisition modulation for bandwidth-constrained sensing. Key limitations include the requirement for a compatible training distribution (the mask is fixed and cannot adapt to novel objects), the narrow hardware scope (specific DMD + spectrometer setup), and the three-stage training protocol that complicates deployment. Open questions include whether dynamic or input-adaptive mask strategies can be made compatible with pre-acquisition constraints, and whether the framework extends to other optical sensing modalities or tasks beyond classification.
