## Problem

Existing language model self-improvement (LMSI) methods like RLAIF rely on the heuristic assumption that an AI model can provide effective feedback to correct its own errors, and require collecting preference data and training a separate reward model. This paper identifies and exploits a previously unexplored asymmetry: language models are significantly better at evaluating text than generating it, even at small scales (80M–780M parameters), and asks whether this evaluation-generation gap can be directly leveraged for unsupervised self-improvement without any external reward model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only unlabeled questions in an instruction-plus-problem format (e.g., 'Let's think step by step' appended to a reasoning prompt, or 'Please give a summary of the following text' for summarization), with no reference answers or human labels required. The output is a generated text answer produced autoregressively by the updated LLM. A frozen copy of the initial pre-trained LLM (M*) is kept separate and used exclusively for self-evaluation, so the reward signal remains stable as the policy model M is updated through training.

## Method

RLC formulates text generation as an MDP where the LLM is the policy and the reward is derived entirely from self-evaluation. Two evaluation prompts are used: a Correctness Evaluation Prompt (CEP) that yields a binary 0/1 reward for reasoning tasks, and a Quality Evaluation Prompt (QEP) that yields a 1–10 score for generation tasks like summarization. The LLM generates answers to sampled unlabeled questions, the frozen evaluator M* scores each answer, and PPO (with entropy regularization and a KL-divergence penalty against the initial model) updates the policy to maximize expected evaluation reward. The key design choice is that evaluation and generation are decoupled into two model instances, preventing the reward signal from drifting as the policy improves. Experiments on FLAN-T5 models (80M–780M) show RLC outperforms RLAIF, self-consistency, self-refine, and best-of-N baselines on BigBench-hard reasoning (31.23%→37.09% accuracy) and improves BERTScore on CNN/Daily Mail summarization, while also generalizing to unseen tasks.

## Results

On the 12 BigBench-hard reasoning tasks, RLC improves average answer accuracy from 31.23% (Direct Generation) to 37.09%, outperforming all unsupervised baselines. On the CNN/Daily Mail summarization task, RLC increases BERTScore from 0.886 to 0.899, while self-training shows negligible improvement. RLC also generalizes to unseen tasks, achieving an average accuracy gain of +0.8% across five held-out BigBench tasks, and works across model sizes from 80M to 780M parameters (FLAN-T5-Small through FLAN-T5-Large).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RLC outperforms RLAIF (Lee et al., 2023) on 9 of 12 BigBench-hard tasks, with the largest margin on Logical Deduction (3) where RLC reaches 44.0% versus RLAIF's 22.0%. Against Self-Consistency (Wang et al., ICLR 2023), RLC wins on 8 of 12 tasks but trails SC on Reasoning about Colored Objects (35.0% vs. 39.6%), Penguins in a Table (29.8% vs. 35.2%), and Navigate (57.1% vs. 62.8%). RLC substantially outperforms Self-train (Huang et al., 2022) on nearly all tasks, e.g., 35.4% vs. 11.7% on Object Counting and 39.2% vs. 13.1% on Logical Deduction (7). Self-refine (Madaan et al., 2023) and Best-of-N (OpenAI, 2022) are consistently weaker, with RLC exceeding them by 10–20+ points on most tasks. Notably, RLC approaches the oracle-supervised RLFT upper bound on several tasks (e.g., 35.0% vs. 32.1% on Colored Objects), suggesting self-evaluation rewards capture much of the signal that ground-truth labels provide.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the language model self-improvement (LMSI) literature, specifically departing from RLAIF and RLCAI, which train a separate reward model on AI preference data before applying RL. RLC eliminates the reward model entirely by directly using the LLM's own evaluation output as the PPO reward signal, grounded in the empirical observation that text evaluation is easier than text generation for LLMs. It also contrasts with self-training approaches (e.g., Huang et al., 2022a) that use supervised fine-tuning on pseudo-labels, instead employing RL with a scalar reward. Conceptually, the paper reframes the RLAIF pipeline by identifying the evaluation-generation gap as the underlying reason AI feedback works, then exploiting that gap without the intermediate preference-model training step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RLC offers a simpler, lower-cost alternative to RLAIF by removing the need to collect preference data and train a separate reward model, while still achieving competitive or better performance on reasoning and summarization tasks. The demonstration that the method works across model sizes from 80M to 780M parameters is notable, suggesting the evaluation-generation gap persists even in small models. Key limitations include the use of a frozen copy of the model for evaluation (raising open questions about how evaluation ability co-evolves with generation), testing only on FLAN-T5 up to 780M parameters, and the requirement for an unlabeled question dataset. Future work on scaling to larger models, removing the dataset dependency, and understanding how self-evaluation quality shifts during training would strengthen the approach.
