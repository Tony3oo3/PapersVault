## Problem

In selective labels settings such as medical testing, outcomes are only observed for historically tested individuals, creating a distribution shift between tested and untested populations that makes risk prediction for the full population ill-posed. Without additional structure, the untested population's risk is essentially unidentifiable. The paper asks whether plausible domain-specific constraints can tighten the range of possible inferences and improve parameter estimation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240539/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes tabular patient features (demographics, genetics, medical history), a binary testing indicator, and test outcomes available only for the tested subset, plus two domain constraints: a known population-level disease prevalence and a specified subset of features assumed not to affect testing probability beyond risk. It outputs a full Bayesian posterior over all parameters, yielding per-patient risk probabilities p(Y=1|X) for both tested and untested individuals, a posterior over the latent unobservable Z, and testing-policy coefficients (α, β_Δ) that quantify deviations from purely risk-based test allocation.

## Method

The paper proposes a Bayesian sample-selection model where disease risk r_i is a linear function of observed features X_i and a latent unobservable Z_i, the test outcome Y_i is drawn from a distribution parameterized by r_i, and the testing decision T_i depends on α·r_i plus additional feature effects β_Δ. Two domain constraints are introduced: a prevalence constraint that fixes the intercept of β_Y to match known population disease rates, and an expertise constraint that zeros out β_Δ coefficients for features plausibly unrelated to testing decisions (e.g., height). The authors prove via equivalence to the Heckman correction model that fixing any parameter to a posterior-drawn value cannot increase the posterior variance of other parameters, and strictly reduces it when parameters are correlated. For tractable inference, they use a Bernoulli-sigmoid likelihood with uniformly distributed unobservables (enabling analytical marginalization of Z_i) and fit the model with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in Stan, validating on synthetic data and a UK Biobank breast cancer case study.

## Results

On the UK Biobank breast cancer case study (54,746 women under 45), the model's inferred risk stratifies patients effectively: the highest inferred-risk quintile has 3.3× higher true cancer risk than the lowest (6.0% vs 1.8%) among tested patients, and 2.5× higher risk (4.1% vs 1.6%) among untested patients at follow-up. Inferred unobservables correlate with family history of breast cancer (2.1× higher prevalence in top vs bottom quintile: 15.6% vs 7.5%). On synthetic data (200 trials), both the prevalence and expertise constraints reduce 95% CI width and posterior mean error for all parameters relative to the unconstrained model, with the sole exception that the expertise constraint does not improve accuracy for σ².

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its constrained Bayesian model against a suite of baselines in Appendix F.1 (Figure S6), including models trained solely on the tested population, models treating untested patients as negative, and baselines from the selective labels literature (Rastogi et al., 2023). The main text reports that all baselines suffer from issues the proposed model avoids, including learning implausible age trends inconsistent with prior epidemiological literature or worse predictive performance, but specific numerical comparisons are deferred to the appendix. The most prominent in-text comparison is ablative: removing the prevalence constraint causes the model to learn that cancer risk first increases and then decreases with age (contradicting established evidence), because the tested under-50 population is non-representative due to UK age-based screening policies. The prevalence constraint corrects this by encoding that the younger untested population has lower risk, yielding a monotonic age-risk relationship consistent with prior literature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the selective labels literature (Lakkaraju et al., 2017; Kleinberg et al., 2018) by proposing a Bayesian model class that nests the Heckman sample-selection model as a special case (Proposition 3.1) and inherits the risk-based testing assumption from Mullainathan & Obermeyer (2022). Methodologically, it builds on generalized linear mixed models (Gelman et al., 2013) but departs from them in two key ways: using a uniform (rather than normal) distribution for unobservables to enable tractable marginalization, and incorporating two healthcare-specific domain constraints (prevalence and expertise) that are absent from standard GLMMs. The paper also connects to the distribution-shift literature (Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz, 2021; Koh et al., 2021) by providing a concrete example of how domain-specific constraints improve generalization from tested to untested populations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap in healthcare ML: risk models trained only on tested patients are both high-variance (small sample) and biased (non-representative), yet this is the dominant practice. The prevalence constraint's ability to prevent the model from learning an implausible age-risk trend (risk peaking then declining) is a concrete demonstration that without domain knowledge, the model can produce dangerous misinferences. Open questions include whether the linear model and tabular features suffice for complex inputs (medical images, EHR sequences), whether the constraints generalize to other selective-labels domains (lending, criminal justice) with different natural constraints, and how the approach scales when the number of unobservables or feature dimensions grows substantially.
