## Problem

While Tweedie's formula links the posterior mean to the score and Gribonval's result links the posterior covariance to the Jacobian, no simple recursion existed connecting higher-order posterior *central* moments to derivatives of the posterior mean. The closest prior work (Meng et al., 2021) derived recursions for non-central moments, which do not translate cleanly to central moments, and the memory cost of storing high-order moment tensors limited it to 32×32 images. This paper closes both gaps: it proves a clean two-step recursion for central moments and shows how to exploit it for training-free, memory-efficient uncertainty quantification on high-resolution images.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a noisy image y, a pre-trained denoiser μ₁(·) of any architecture (SwinIR, Noise2Void, a small CNN, etc.), and the noise variance σ² (or a naive estimate σ̂² = ‖μ₁(y)−y‖²/d for blind settings). It produces (i) the top-N eigenvectors of the posterior covariance restricted to any user-chosen spatial region, and (ii) the full marginal posterior density along each eigenvector direction, obtained by fitting a maximum-entropy distribution to moments up to order four. Crucially, all computations require only forward passes through the denoiser—no backward passes, no fine-tuning—and the region of interest can be selected at test time, unlike methods that predict a fixed low-rank covariance.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is a recursive relation (Theorems 1–3) showing that every higher-order posterior central moment μ_k(y) is determined by derivatives of the posterior mean up to order k: in the scalar case μ₂ = σ²μ₁′, μ₃ = σ²μ₂′, and μ_{k+1} = σ²μ_k′ + k·μ_{k−1}·μ₂ for k ≥ 3; the multivariate and directional (1-D projection) versions follow the same pattern with partial and directional derivatives. For computing posterior principal components, the authors replace the expensive Jacobian-vector product (which would require a backward pass) with the linear approximation [μ₁(y+cv)−μ₁(y)]/c, then run subspace iteration with QR re-orthogonalization using only forward passes, reducing memory by ~6× for an 80×92 patch with SwinIR. To estimate the marginal distribution along a principal direction v, they numerically differentiate the scalar function f(α)=vᵀμ₁(y+αv) up to third order at α=0, extract moments through order four via Theorem 3, and fit the maximum-entropy distribution matching those moments—revealing multimodality (e.g., digit 4 vs. 9) that the single MSE-optimal estimate conceals.

## Results

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so there is no single headline benchmark metric. The key quantitative claim is a 6× reduction in memory footprint (for an 80×92 patch with SwinIR) achieved by replacing automatic-differentiation Jacobian-vector products with a linear approximation (Eq. 15) during subspace iteration. The method is training-free, requires only forward passes through a pre-trained denoiser, and produces qualitatively meaningful posterior principal components and marginal distributions across four domains (MNIST digits, natural images via SwinIR, faces via a DDPM denoiser, and fluorescence microscopy via Noise2Void). In Appendix K, the authors report quantitative validation of eigenvalue accuracy against a naive posterior-sampler baseline, confirming the method's fidelity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a formal head-to-head comparison table against other uncertainty-quantification methods. The closest implicit comparisons are: (1) Meng et al. (2021), who trained second-order score networks on 32×32 images and could only predict a low-rank covariance approximation fixed at training time; the present method is training-free, scales to high-resolution images, and allows user-chosen regions of interest at test time. (2) Nehme et al. (2023), a concurrent work that learns posterior principal components but requires a pre-trained generative model with a disentangled latent space (e.g., StyleGAN); the present method only needs a pre-trained denoiser. (3) A naive posterior-sampler baseline (reported in Appendix K) is used to validate the accuracy of the computed PCs and eigenvalues, with the proposed method matching the sampler's results while being orders of magnitude faster. The paper also notes that cropping a patch from a globally computed eigenvector is not equivalent to computing the eigenvector of that patch, a limitation of fixed-size trained covariance models that the present method avoids.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the non-central moment recursion of Meng et al. (2021), who linked high-order posterior non-central moments to high-order scores of the noisy distribution, and on the foundational Tweedie's formula (Efron, 2011) connecting the MSE-optimal denoiser to the first-order score. The key departure is a clean conversion to central moments, yielding a simpler two-step recursion (μ_{k+1} depends on μ_k and μ_{k-1}) that is more numerically stable and directly interpretable for uncertainty quantification. Additionally, the paper extends the framework to directional projections (Theorem 3), showing that one-dimensional marginals along any fixed vector satisfy the same scalar recursion, which enables practical marginal distribution estimation without ever materializing high-order tensors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, memory-efficient procedure for computing posterior principal components and marginal distributions is immediately applicable to any pre-trained denoiser (SwinIR, N2V, DDPM-based models) without retraining or fine-tuning, making it broadly useful for uncertainty-aware image restoration in medical, microscopy, and natural image domains. A key limitation is the reliance on high-order numerical differentiation, which can be unstable at low precision and requires careful step-size selection; the authors note that polynomial fitting alternatives proved less robust. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-Gaussian or spatially-correlated noise models, and whether the linear approximation in Algorithm 1 degrades for very small noise levels where the denoiser's Jacobian is ill-conditioned.
