## Problem

Self-repair—where a model debugs and corrects its own generated code—has become a popular technique for boosting code generation performance, but its efficacy under realistic compute budgets remains poorly understood. No prior work has isolated and studied the feedback generation stage (the model's ability to introspect and explain why its own code is wrong), which the authors hypothesize is the key bottleneck limiting self-repair's gains over simple i.i.d. resampling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241434/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a natural language problem specification (e.g., from APPS or HumanEval) together with a suite of executable unit tests, and produces a repair tree: a hierarchical structure of initial Python programs, execution error messages, textual feedback strings (2–3 sentence bug explanations), and candidate repaired programs. Evaluation uses an adapted pass@k metric where k counts both initial and repair samples, so that self-repair is compared against a no-repair baseline at an equivalent total sample budget; an alternative pass@t metric additionally accounts for the token cost of feedback strings.

## Method

The paper formalizes self-repair as a four-stage pipeline—code generation, execution, feedback generation, and repair—organized into a repair tree rooted at the specification, and evaluates CodeLlama-13b-instruct, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 on HumanEval and APPS using one-shot prompting with templated string concatenation. The key experimental design separates the programming model M_P from the feedback model M_F, enabling ablation of the feedback stage: the authors replace a weaker model's self-generated feedback with that of a stronger model (e.g., GPT-4 feedback for CodeLlama) and observe that this consistently outperforms both the i.i.d. baseline and the original self-repair configuration at all budgets. A small-scale human study (16 participants, 40 failing GPT-4 programs) further shows that human-written feedback increases GPT-4's repair success rate by 1.58× (33.3% → 52.6%), with the gap widening on harder problems. The central finding is that self-repair is not a silver bullet: gains are modest, vary across difficulty subsets, and depend critically on the diversity of initial samples rather than the number of repair attempts, with the feedback quality being the primary bottleneck.

## Results

On APPS, GPT-4 self-repair with 10 initial samples + 1 repair each achieves 66.1% pass rate vs. 63.1% for i.i.d. pass@20 (1.05×), while 2 initial + 10 repairs each drops to 61.8% vs. 63.4% pass@22 (0.97×), showing that diverse initial samples matter more than extensive repair. Replacing GPT-4's self-generated feedback with human-written feedback increases the repair success rate from 33.3% to 52.6% (1.58×), with the gap widening on harder problems (3.67% → 14.67% on competition-level). Boosting Code Llama's feedback with GPT-4 on HumanEval yields the largest relative gains among all configurations tested, and GPT-3.5 sees up to 34% improvement over its i.i.d. baseline on APPS competition-level problems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is i.i.d. sampling without repair at an equivalent sample budget (pass@k). Self-repair with the model's own feedback (M_P = M_F) does not consistently beat this baseline: GPT-3.5 on APPS shows only marginal gains at the largest budgets, Code Llama on APPS mostly lags or matches its baseline, and gains at small budgets are negligible across all models. When the feedback model is replaced with a stronger one (e.g., GPT-4 feedback for Code Llama or GPT-3.5), the boosted configuration beats both the i.i.d. baseline and the self-repair-with-own-feedback configuration at all budgets on both APPS and HumanEval. The strongest comparison is GPT-4 self-feedback vs. human feedback: human feedback improves repair success by 1.58× overall (33.3% → 52.6%), with the relative advantage growing on harder problems (competition-level: 3.67% → 14.67%). Qualitative analysis attributes the gap to higher accuracy of human feedback (7/80 obviously incorrect vs. 32/80 for GPT-4), a greater tendency to suggest high-level structural changes, and the ability to express uncertainty rather than confidently stating incorrect diagnoses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-repair pipeline formalized by Gupta et al. (2020) and studied by Chen et al. (2023b) and Zhang et al. (2023) by explicitly isolating the feedback generation stage as a separate, ablatable component. While prior work treated self-repair as a monolithic generate-execute-repair loop, this paper introduces the 'repair tree' formalism and a dedicated feedback model (M_F) that can differ from the code model (M_P), enabling controlled experiments on feedback quality. The paper also departs from prior evaluations by comparing self-repair against an i.i.d. sampling baseline at equivalent compute budgets, a cost-aware comparison that Zhang et al. (2023) explicitly did not perform.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the growing assumption that self-repair is a free lunch for code generation, showing that when compute cost is accounted for, gains are often modest or absent, and that the critical bottleneck is the model's ability to produce accurate diagnostic feedback rather than its repair ability per se. The finding that even GPT-4's self-generated feedback underperforms human feedback by a 1.58× factor in repair success rate suggests a fundamental limitation in current models' introspective debugging. Open questions include how to improve feedback quality through fine-tuning or architectural changes, whether these results extend to real-world software development (where unit tests are scarce and specifications are ambiguous), and how to optimally allocate compute between diverse initial sampling and repair iterations.
