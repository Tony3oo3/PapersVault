## Problem

DNA language models have proliferated, but their evaluations are inconsistent across works, often restricted to short sequences (≤600 bp) that fail to capture the long-range dependencies and sparsity inherent to genome annotation. No prior benchmark systematically tests DNA LMs on biologically meaningful tasks spanning multiple length scales with appropriate expert-method baselines, making it impossible to determine whether pre-trained representations actually encode useful genomic structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240609/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task consumes raw DNA sequences of varying length (512 bp for chromatin/histone/CpG/variant tasks up to 100,096 bp for enhancer annotation) and produces task-specific predictions: nucleotide-level 9-class labels for gene finding, 128-bp binned binary labels for enhancers, or multilabel/binary sequence-level scores for the remaining tasks. For variant effect prediction, the LM operates zero-shot by computing cosine distance between reference and variant nucleotide embeddings, while all other tasks use frozen LM embeddings fed through a lightweight two-layer CNN (64 channels) with average pooling for sequence-level outputs.

## Method

BEND defines seven curated tasks on the human genome spanning nucleotide-wise, binned, and sequence-wise prediction at length scales from 512 bp to 100,096 bp, explicitly designed to probe long-range reasoning and sparse signal detection rather than local motif recognition. Thirteen LM configurations from six sources (DNABERT, DNABERT-2, Nucleotide Transformer variants, GENA-LM, HyenaDNA, GROVER) plus two newly trained baselines (AWD-LSTM, dilated ResNet) are evaluated with frozen weights and a shared downstream CNN, alongside task-specific expert methods (AUGUSTUS, Enformer, Basset, DeepSEA) and fully supervised baselines. The benchmark is provided in BED format with chromosome-wise or identity-based splits to prevent data leakage, and includes 10-fold cross-validation for the small enhancer dataset. Key findings show NT-MS as a strong default LM that approaches expert performance on gene finding and CpG methylation, while all models struggle with enhancer annotation, revealing that current self-supervised objectives may not adequately capture very long-range, sparse regulatory signals.

## Results

Across seven biologically meaningful tasks on the human genome, no single DNA LM consistently outperforms all others. NT-MS (Nucleotide Transformer, multispecies) achieves the best gene finding MCC of 0.68 (vs. AUGUSTUS expert at 0.80) and the best disease variant AUROC of 0.77 (vs. DeepSEA at 0.56), while the much smaller DNABERT matches the Basset expert on chromatin accessibility (AUROC 0.85) and leads on histone modification (0.79 vs. Basset 0.72). All models perform poorly on enhancer annotation (AUPRC ≤ 0.06 vs. Enformer 0.07), confirming that long-range, sparse signal detection remains an open challenge. For CpG methylation, NT-MS reaches 0.92 AUROC, narrowly below Basset's 0.93.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NT-MS is the strongest overall DNA LM, leading on gene finding (MCC 0.68 vs. next-best NT-V2 at 0.64), CpG methylation (0.92 vs. 0.91), and disease variant prediction (AUROC 0.77 vs. 0.56 for all others), but it is outperformed by the much smaller DNABERT on chromatin accessibility (0.79 vs. 0.85), histone modification (0.78 vs. 0.79), and expression variant prediction (0.54 vs. 0.60). Against expert methods, no LM surpasses AUGUSTUS on gene finding (best 0.68 vs. 0.80) or Basset on chromatin accessibility (DNABERT ties at 0.85), though DNABERT edges out Basset on histone modification (0.79 vs. 0.72) and NT-MS beats DeepSEA on disease variants (0.77 vs. 0.56). On enhancer annotation, all LMs (AUPRC 0.02–0.06) fall short of Enformer (0.07), with NT-MS closest at 0.06. The fully supervised CNN baseline (trained on one-hot sequences) achieves 0.75–0.84 AUROC on the 512 bp tasks, showing that LM embeddings provide only marginal gains over raw sequence features for local tasks. HyenaDNA large, despite supporting 1 Mbp context, underperforms on gene finding (0.35) and enhancer annotation (0.03), suggesting that long context alone does not guarantee long-range understanding.

## Relation to Prior Work

BEND positions itself as the DNA analogue of protein LM benchmarks like TAPE and PEER, but explicitly departs from prior DNA evaluation efforts (Genomic Benchmarks, GUE, NT's leaderboard) by introducing tasks that span multiple length scales up to 100,096 bp rather than the 200–600 bp range typical of earlier collections. Conceptually, it inherits the masked/autoregressive language modeling paradigm from NLP and protein LMs, but argues that DNA's sparsity, lack of natural sequence boundaries, and extreme length range make direct transfer of NLP evaluation strategies insufficient. The paper carves new ground by being the first to evaluate all publicly available self-supervised DNA LMs (DNABERT, DNABERT-2, NT, GENA-LM, HyenaDNA, GROVER) under a single protocol with task-specific expert baselines (AUGUSTUS, Basset, Enformer, DeepSEA), rather than the ad-hoc or LM-only comparisons seen in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BEND provides the field with a standardized, reproducible benchmark at a critical juncture when DNA LMs are proliferating but evaluation has been inconsistent and often limited to short-range tasks. The key open question it surfaces is whether the self-supervised MLM/next-token objective is fundamentally capable of capturing sparse, very long-range regulatory signals (e.g., enhancers millions of bp away), or whether some form of supervised signal is necessary. Limitations include the restriction to the human genome and the use of a simple two-layer CNN downstream model, which may understate the potential of more sophisticated fine-tuning. Future work should extend BEND to cross-species transfer and investigate whether alternative pre-training objectives better capture long-range genomic organization.
