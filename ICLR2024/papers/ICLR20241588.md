## Problem

Existing evaluations of Video-Language Models (VidLMs) rely on task-based benchmarks such as video captioning, retrieval, and VQA, which measure task performance but fail to isolate fine-grained visuo-linguistic capabilities, particularly temporal reasoning. No prior zero-shot, task-agnostic benchmark specifically probes whether VidLMs genuinely understand temporal dynamics in video or merely exploit spurious cues. VILMA addresses this gap by providing a controlled foiling-based evaluation suite that targets temporal phenomena absent from static image-language settings.

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance consists of a short video clip (typically 10 seconds, sampled at 4–16 frames depending on the model) paired with two near-identical text descriptions: a ground-truth caption and a minimally edited counterfactual foil. Models output a video-text matching score for each text, and the benchmark computes pairwise ranking accuracy (acc_r) — the fraction of samples where the caption score exceeds the foil score. A stricter combined metric (P+T) requires the model to also pass a simpler proficiency test on the same video, and a final average is taken across all five task families.

## Method

VILMA constructs five main tests (Action Counting, Situation Awareness, Change of State, Rare Actions, Spatial Relations) by harvesting video-text pairs from existing datasets (QUVA, VidSitu, Something-Something V2, RareAct, etc.) and generating counterfactual foils through targeted linguistic manipulations such as number replacement, actor swapping, pre/post-state inversion, and spatial preposition substitution. Foil quality is controlled via NLI contradiction filtering, GRUEN grammaticality scoring, and distributional smoothing to prevent lexical biases. Each main test is paired with a proficiency test that isolates a prerequisite capability (e.g., object detection or basic action recognition) without requiring temporal modelling. The entire benchmark undergoes two-stage human validation: manual review for proficiency tests and AMTurk annotation (3 independent annotators per sample, retaining only items where ≥2/3 confirm the caption is true and the foil is false), yielding 5,177 validated main-test instances. Twelve architecturally diverse VidLMs, two ILMs (CLIP, BLIP-2), and two unimodal LMs (GPT-2, OPT) are evaluated zero-shot, revealing that VidLMs perform no better than ILMs on temporal reasoning tasks and that many apparently correct predictions are spurious once proficiency is accounted for.

## Results

On the VILMA benchmark, the best VidLM (Video-LLaMA) achieves an average combined P+T score of 57.3%, while the best ILM (BLIP-2) reaches 57.5%, showing that video-language models do not substantially outperform image-language models on temporal reasoning. The random baseline sits at 23.8%, and unimodal text-only models (GPT-2: 26.2%, OPT: 30.6%) perform only marginally above chance. Strikingly, in the Counting, Situation Awareness, and Change of State tests, many VidLMs score near or below 50% on the main test (T), indicating they cannot reliably distinguish correct from counterfactual video-text pairs. The combined P+T metric reveals a sharp performance drop (e.g., CLIP4Clip drops from 52.3% on T to 48.0% on P+T), suggesting many apparently correct predictions are spurious.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central finding is that the best ILM, BLIP-2 (57.5% Avg P+T), marginally outperforms the best VidLM, Video-LLaMA (57.3%), and substantially outperforms most other VidLMs (e.g., CLIP4Clip 52.1%, VindLU 49.5%, InternVideo 54.2%). On the Situation Awareness test specifically, BLIP-2 (55.8%) beats all VidLMs except Video-LLaMA (54.0%), while on Spatial Relations, BLIP-2 (79.4%) and Video-LLaMA (79.6%) are the only models above 75%. CLIP (52.9%) also outperforms 10 of the 12 VidLMs on the average combined score. In the Rare Actions test, CLIP (87.8%) and VindLU (88.0%) lead, with InternVideo (92.7%) slightly ahead, but most VidLMs cluster between 35–83%. The unimodal baselines (GPT-2, OPT) confirm that linguistic plausibility alone can exploit biases in foil generation, particularly in Situation Awareness (66.6–71.4% on T) and Spatial Relations (72.8–84.7% on T), yet they fail on Counting and Change of State where visual grounding is essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

VILMA extends the foiling evaluation paradigm established for image-language models (Shekhar et al., 2017; VALSE, Parcalabescu et al., 2022) to the video domain, with a deliberate focus on temporal reasoning capabilities that are unique to VidLMs. It departs from prior video-language benchmarks (VALUE, Park et al. 2022, Bagad et al. 2023) by being task-agnostic and zero-shot, and by introducing proficiency tests that probe prerequisite capabilities before the main counterfactual tests. The benchmark draws data from multiple existing video-language datasets (QUVA, VidSitu, Something-Something V2, RareAct) but restructures them into controlled caption-foil pairs targeting specific linguistic and temporal phenomena.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that current VidLMs perform no better than static image-language models on temporal reasoning tasks—challenges a core assumption in the field that temporal modeling architectures (attention over frames, spatio-temporal transformers) actually yield superior temporal understanding. The proficiency test results further reveal that a substantial fraction of apparently correct predictions are spurious, suggesting models exploit distributional biases rather than genuine temporal reasoning. This benchmark is likely to become a standard diagnostic tool, and it opens the question of whether current pretraining objectives (contrastive, masked modeling) are sufficient to instill true temporal grounding, or whether fundamentally different training signals are needed.
