## Problem

While generative models have achieved strong results on individual speech tasks like TTS, enhancement, and separation, no general-purpose generative model exists that directly models the speech distribution p(speech). Existing pre-trained speech foundation models focus on learning discriminative representations rather than the data distribution, leaving open whether a single generative model pre-trained on unlabeled speech can be adapted to multiple downstream generation tasks.

## Input / Output

SpeechFlow operates on 80-dimensional log-scaled Mel spectrograms at 100 Hz (40 ms window, 16 kHz audio), with the Transformer encoder receiving a 160-dimensional input (masked condition concatenated with the noisy state along the frequency axis) plus a sinusoidal timestep encoding. During pre-training, the condition is a partially masked version of the target (70–100% of frames zeroed with minimum span 10); during fine-tuning, this is swapped for task-specific conditions such as a noisy spectrogram, a multi-speaker mixture, or a force-aligned phone sequence element-wise added to masked audio. The output is a clean Mel spectrogram converted to waveform via pseudo-inverse Mel plus phase from the input (enhancement/separation) or a HiFi-GAN vocoder (TTS), with classifier-free guidance (α=0.5–0.7) and 32 NFEs via a midpoint ODE solver.

## Method

SpeechFlow is a 330M-parameter 24-layer Transformer encoder (1024-dim, 16 heads, ALiBi bias, convolutional positional embeddings, U-Net-style skip connections) that predicts the time-dependent vector field v_t of an Optimal Transport conditional flow matching objective, conditioned on partially masked target audio. Pre-training on 60k hours of untranscribed English audiobook speech uses a masked-audio condition (p_cond=90%, n_mask~U[70%,100%], l_mask=10) so the model learns to denoise from noise to clean speech while receiving contextual cues from unmasked frames, making it self-supervised yet naturally amenable to task-specific fine-tuning. Fine-tuning simply replaces the masked condition with a task-specific signal (noisy speech, mixture, or phone sequence) while reusing all pre-trained weights, requiring only a single GPU and 160–150k updates. The key insight is that masked conditioning during pre-training provides the model with a flexible interface: the same architecture and objective serve as a universal generative backbone, and the condition slot can be repurposed for any aligned generation task without architectural changes.

## Results

On speech enhancement (VoiceBank-Demand), SpeechFlow achieves PESQ 3.13, ESTOI 0.87, CSIG 4.43, and COVL 3.80, matching or surpassing all prior methods; on the out-of-domain WSJ0-CHiME3 test set it reaches PESQ 2.70 and COVL 3.36, clearly ahead of SGMSE+ (PESQ 2.48, COVL 3.02). For zero-shot speaker adaptation TTS on LibriSpeech test-clean, SpeechFlow (32-GPU LoRA fine-tune) achieves WER 2.1, SIM-o 0.700, SIM-r 0.715, and MOS 3.43, outperforming Voicebox on speaker similarity (SIM-o 0.662, SIM-r 0.681) while using 62.5× less labeled data (960 h vs. 60k h). On speech separation (LibriMix 2-Mix), SpeechFlow with learnable inverse-Mel reaches SI-SDRi 15.85 and ESTOIi 0.37, exceeding Conv-TasNet (SI-SDRi 15.24, ESTOIi 0.22) and SepFormer (SI-SDRi 14.94, ESTOIi 0.31).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpeechFlow outperforms all enhancement baselines: it matches MetricGAN+ on PESQ (3.13) but leads on CSIG (4.43 vs 4.10) and COVL (3.80 vs 3.61), and clearly beats SGMSE+ on PESQ (3.13 vs 2.93) and COVL (3.80 vs 3.53) on VoiceBank-Demand, with an even larger gap on out-of-domain WSJ0-CHiME3 (PESQ 2.70 vs 2.48 for SGMSE+). Against DEMUCS (trained on DNS2020), SpeechFlow wins on PESQ (2.71 vs 2.55), CSIG (4.07 vs 3.24), and COVL (3.39 vs 2.88) on VB-DMD, and on WSJ0-CHiME3 (PESQ 2.87 vs 2.49, COVL 3.54 vs 3.20). For TTS, SpeechFlow surpasses Voicebox on speaker similarity (SIM-o 0.700 vs 0.662; SIM-r 0.715 vs 0.681) with 62.5× less labeled data, though Voicebox retains a slight edge on WER (1.9 vs 2.1) and MOS (3.54 vs 3.43); it decisively beats VALL-E (WER 2.1 vs 5.9, SIM-r 0.715 vs 0.580) and YourTTS (WER 2.1 vs 7.7, SIM-o 0.700 vs 0.337). On separation, SpeechFlow with learnable inverse-Mel exceeds Conv-TasNet (SI-SDRi 15.85 vs 15.24) and SepFormer (15.85 vs 14.94) on 2-Mix, and leads on ESTOIi across all four LibriMix conditions. For speech editing, SpeechFlow (LoRA) achieves WER 2.2 and SIM-o 0.647, far surpassing A3T (WER 11.5, SIM-o 0.148) with 22× more labeled data but still far less than Voicebox's 60k hours.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpeechFlow directly extends Voicebox (Le et al., 2023), sharing the same flow-matching objective and Transformer architecture, but repurposes it from a fully supervised TTS model into a self-supervised pre-training framework by replacing text conditioning with masked audio during pre-training. The masked audio conditioning draws from the self-supervised representation learning lineage (wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT), but applies the masking idea to a generative objective rather than a discriminative one. The generative backbone follows the Optimal Transport conditional flow matching formulation of Lipman et al. (2023), positioning SpeechFlow within the continuous normalizing flow family rather than discrete-token spoken language models (VALL-E, AudioLM) or task-specific diffusion models for enhancement/separation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first systematic evidence that a single generative model pre-trained on unlabeled speech can be fine-tuned to competitive performance across fundamentally different generation tasks (enhancement, separation, TTS), validating the foundation-model paradigm for generative speech. Key limitations include the Mel spectrogram representation capping enhancement/separation quality (the upper-bound is worse than time-domain baselines), English-only training, and the requirement for frame-aligned conditions. Open questions include whether unaligned conditions (raw text, speaker IDs) can be handled, whether the approach scales to multilingual settings, and whether multi-task fine-tuning can be stabilized—the current multi-task model degrades significantly on separation. The finding that pre-training improves speaker modeling but not content modeling (WER) suggests the two capabilities may require different pre-training signals.
