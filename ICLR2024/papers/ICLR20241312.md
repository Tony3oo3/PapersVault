## Problem

Deep continual learners suffer from two intertwined failures: catastrophic forgetting of previously learned useful features and progressive loss of plasticity as weights become rigid. Existing methods typically address one issue or the other, and the few that tackle both simultaneously require known task boundaries, replay buffers, or pretraining — none of which are available in the practical streaming learning setting where samples arrive one at a time from a non-stationary source with no stored history.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241312/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a stream of (x_t, y_t) pairs arriving sequentially from a non-stationary target function, with no access to past samples, no batch structure, and no task boundary signals. At each step the learner produces a prediction ŷ_t, receives the true label y_t, and immediately updates its weights using the current sample's gradient, utility estimate, and a sampled noise perturbation. Evaluation is purely online (accuracy or loss on the most recent sample), and the network architecture varies by experiment from 300×150 MLPs to CNNs and a frozen ResNet-50 backbone with two trainable head layers.

## Method

UPGD gates both the gradient and a Gaussian noise perturbation by a per-weight utility score, so that useful weights (high utility) are shielded from modification while unuseful weights receive larger updates that restore plasticity. The utility of a weight is defined as the loss increase if that weight were zeroed out, approximated via a second-order Taylor expansion whose Hessian-diagonal term is computed in linear time using the HesScale algorithm. The update rule is w ← w − α(∂L/∂w + ξ)(1 − Ū), where Ū ∈ [0,1] is a sigmoid-scaled utility trace maintained via exponential moving average; a weight-decay variant (UPGD-W) additionally multiplies w by ρ = 1 − λα. An adaptive variant (AdaUPGD) incorporates Adam-style momentum and RMS normalization to make the step size robust across reinforcement learning environments, and a non-protecting variant that gates only the noise (not the gradient) is included to isolate the contribution of utility-based gradient protection against forgetting.

## Results

UPGD-W achieves the best or competitive online accuracy across all streaming learning problems, continuing to improve as more tasks are presented while most baselines plateau or degrade. On Label-permuted CIFAR-10 (catastrophic forgetting only), AdamW plateaus at slightly above 40% accuracy while forgetting-addressing methods keep improving; UPGD-W is among the top performers. On Label-permuted EMNIST and mini-ImageNet (both issues present), UPGD-W shows the lowest overall forgetting and loss-of-plasticity metrics among all methods. In RL with PPO, AdaUPGD continually improves performance across MuJoCo environments while Adam suffers from policy collapse after initial learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UPGD-W outperforms all baselines on the combined forgetting + plasticity problems (Label-permuted EMNIST and mini-ImageNet), where methods addressing only one issue (S-EWC for forgetting, S&P for plasticity) fail to sustain gains. Against AdamW and SGDW, UPGD-W avoids the ~40% accuracy plateau on CIFAR-10 and the progressive degradation on EMNIST, continuing to improve across all 400 tasks. S-RWalk, which addresses both issues in prior work, unexpectedly degrades on EMNIST and mini-ImageNet—likely because its regularization mechanism does not counteract loss of plasticity under high non-stationarity—while UPGD-W remains robust. S-SI and S-MAS, streaming extensions of regularization-based forgetting methods, degrade over time on problems with both issues, confirming that regularization alone is insufficient. In the RL setting, AdaUPGD (the Adam-normalized variant) avoids the policy collapse observed with standard Adam in PPO across all four MuJoCo environments, maintaining and improving performance over 40 independent runs.

## Relation to Prior Work

UPGD is a direct generalization of Perturbed Gradient Descent (PGD, Zhou et al. 2019) that introduces utility-based gating: useful weights are shielded from both gradient updates and noise, while unuseful weights receive full perturbation to maintain plasticity. The weight-decay variant (UPGD-W) incorporates the shrink mechanism from Shrink and Perturb (Ash & Adams 2020), and the second-order utility computation relies on the HesScale Hessian-diagonal approximation (Elsayed & Mahmood 2022). Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant regularization-based line (EWC, SI, MAS, RWalk) by not requiring task boundaries, replay buffers, or pretraining, and from plasticity-only methods (S&P, noise injection) by simultaneously protecting useful weights. The adaptive RL variant (AdaUPGD) borrows Adam's per-parameter normalization (Kingma & Ba 2015) to make the step size robust across environments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

UPGD fills a practical gap: it is among the few methods that address both catastrophic forgetting and loss of plasticity in streaming learning without task boundaries, replay, or pretraining, making it applicable to on-device and robotics scenarios. The utility-based gating mechanism is a clean, general principle that could be incorporated into other optimizers or continual learning frameworks. Limitations include the per-step utility computation overhead (even if linear), validation primarily on relatively small networks (MLPs, small CNNs, frozen ResNet-50 heads), and the streaming setting's restriction to one-sample-at-a-time learning. Open questions include whether the approach scales to large transformer-based models, how sensitive performance is to the utility approximation quality in very deep networks, and whether the gating mechanism transfers to other non-stationary settings beyond label/input permutation.
