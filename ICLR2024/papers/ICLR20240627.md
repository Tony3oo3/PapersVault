## Problem

While numerous backdoor attack methods have demonstrated empirical success, fundamental questions about what determines an attack's effectiveness—such as the optimal trigger direction, the minimum required trigger magnitude, and why human-imperceptible triggers can succeed—remain unresolved. This paper provides the first unified statistical theory that characterizes the prediction risk of a poisoned model on both clean and backdoor data, applying to both discriminative and generative models.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on a general supervised learning setup where the predictor X ∈ R^p (or a neural network embedding) is paired with a binary label Y ∈ {0,1} for discriminative models, or a continuous output Y ∈ R^q for generative models. The attacker shifts X by a constant trigger vector η ∈ R^p with probability ρ and relabels the response to a fixed target (zero without loss of generality). The key outputs are tight finite-sample and asymptotic bounds on the statistical risk r_n^cl and r_n^bd of the poisoned model, expressed in terms of the poisoning ratio ρ, the ℓ₂-norm and direction of η, and distributional properties of the clean data (tail probabilities and minimum densities along the trigger direction).

## Method

The paper derives finite-sample upper and lower bounds (Theorems 1–2) on the statistical risk of the poisoned model by decomposing the prediction loss into an ordinary learning risk term and a bias term governed by the trigger's interaction with the clean data density. The bias term is characterized by two quantities: a tail probability h_i^η measuring how far the trigger pushes data into low-density regions, and a minimum density g_i^η capturing the worst-case density shift. In the asymptotic regime with Gaussian clean data (Theorem 3), the optimal trigger direction is the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix, and the minimum trigger magnitude for a successful attack scales as √(ln n). For degenerate data distributions where the support collapses in some direction (Theorem 4), an arbitrarily small trigger along that direction suffices, providing a theoretical explanation for the success of human-imperceptible attacks. The analysis extends to generative models (Theorem 5) and is validated empirically on MNIST, CIFAR-10, GTSRB, a class-conditioned DDPM, and a Transformer-based text translation task.

## Results

On MNIST with ResNet, increasing the BadNets trigger pixel value from 1 to 30 raises backdoor accuracy from 72% to 99% while clean accuracy remains at 99%, confirming the theoretical prediction that larger trigger magnitude improves attack success. On CIFAR-10, the same trend holds with backdoor accuracy rising from 51% to 99%. For generative models, a backdoored DDPM achieves MSE below 0.01 on both clean and triggered MNIST image generation, and a backdoored Transformer for text translation achieves >96% attack success rate with only a 0.3% BLEU score decrease. At a 5% poisoning ratio on MNIST, the poisoned model maintains 98% clean accuracy and 98% backdoor accuracy simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four backdoor attack methods—BadNets, WaNet, Adaptive Patch, and Adaptive Blend—on CIFAR-10 using ResNet-18, measuring the relative change between clean and backdoor data across dimensions (normalized by per-dimension standard deviation). WaNet, Adaptive Patch, and Adaptive Blend all produce significantly larger relative changes in low-variance dimensions, consistent with the paper's theoretical prediction that the optimal trigger direction is where clean data density decays fastest (i.e., the smallest-eigenvalue direction). BadNets, by contrast, applies a uniform patch that does not preferentially exploit low-variance directions, making it less aligned with the theory. This explains why the more sophisticated attacks (WaNet, Adaptive Patch, Adaptive Blend) achieve higher backdoor accuracy than BadNets at comparable trigger magnitudes. The theoretical framework thus provides a unifying explanation for why these state-of-the-art attacks succeed: they implicitly or explicitly place the trigger in the degenerate or low-variance subspace of the data distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the predominantly empirical backdoor attack literature (BadNets, WaNet, ISSBA, etc.) by providing the first rigorous statistical risk bounds—both finite-sample and asymptotic—that characterize when a poisoning backdoor attack succeeds. While prior theoretical work (Manoj & Blum, 2021; Xian et al., 2023b) offered qualitative insights via VC-dimension or adaptivity hypotheses, this paper quantifies the exact dependence of attack success on trigger direction, trigger magnitude, poisoning ratio, and clean data geometry. It also extends the analysis to generative models (diffusion models, LLMs), which the authors note has no prior theoretical treatment, by showing the same degenerate-distribution mechanism governs attack success in that setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners and defenders a principled understanding of attack conditions: the optimal trigger direction is the smallest-variance eigenvector, and for non-degenerate Gaussian data the trigger magnitude must scale as √(ln n). Theorem 4's result—that arbitrarily small triggers succeed when data degenerate in some direction—provides a clean theoretical explanation for why human-imperceptible attacks (WaNet, ISSBA) work, connecting them to the low-rank structure common in high-dimensional embeddings. Open questions include the behavior under vanishing poisoning ratio ρ, non-ℓ₂ trigger norms, and extension to distributed or sparsely-pruned model settings, all of which the authors flag as future work.
