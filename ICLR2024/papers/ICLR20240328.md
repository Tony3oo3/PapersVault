## Problem

Existing network pruning methods based on the empirical Fisher Information Matrix are sensitive to noisy gradients arising from data corruption, analog memory, federated learning, or adversarial attacks. The standard sparse linear regression formulation treats each per-sample gradient independently, offering no built-in mechanism to mitigate outlier gradients while preserving covariance information. This paper asks whether a geometric reformulation of the pruning objective can inherently balance noise reduction with covariance preservation at only marginal additional computational cost.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained weight vector w̄, a batch of n training samples from which per-sample gradients ∇ℓ_i are computed to form the n×p gradient matrix G, and a target sparsity k. The output is a pruned weight vector w with at most k non-zero entries, obtained through iterative SGD updates combined with hard thresholding across T pruning stages. The entropic regularization parameter ε and the n×n OT transport plan Π are internal variables that modulate how gradients are weighted; Π is recomputed at each stage via Sinkhorn-Knopp or a closed-form Gaussian solution.

## Method

SWAP replaces the standard sparse linear regression objective with an entropic Wasserstein regression (EWR) that measures the 2-Wasserstein distance between two one-dimensional projections of the gradient distribution (∇ℓᵀw and ∇ℓᵀw̄). The key mechanism is 'Neighborhood Interpolation': solving the OT problem implicitly assigns each gradient a convex combination of nearby gradients, reducing noise while retaining the original covariance structure in the Hessian approximation. The entropic regularization parameter ε controls neighborhood size—small ε recovers the one-to-one LR matching (Π = diag(1/n)), while larger ε broadens the interpolation toward uniform averaging. The algorithm alternates between computing the OT plan and updating weights via projected gradient descent with iterative hard thresholding, with overall per-iteration complexity O(np) since p ≫ n in practice.

## Results

On MobileNetV1 (ImageNet) at 0.8 sparsity, EWR achieves 66.82% top-1 accuracy, far exceeding CBS (16.38%), WF (0.24%), and MP (0.11%), while also outperforming the LR baseline (65.72%). Under noisy gradients (25% noisy data, noise level σ) at 0.75 sparsity, EWR delivers a 6.52% relative accuracy improvement and 8.13% relative testing loss reduction over LR. On ResNet20 (CIFAR-10) at 0.98 sparsity with noisy gradients, EWR reaches 68.01% accuracy versus 65.04% for LR. At lower sparsity levels (≤0.6), EWR and LR perform comparably, with EWR matching or slightly exceeding LR by 0.1–0.8 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against magnitude pruning (MP) and WoodFisher (WF), EWR achieves overwhelming gains at high sparsity: on MobileNetV1 at 0.8 sparsity, EWR reaches 66.82% versus 0.11% (MP) and 0.24% (WF), and on ResNet20 at 0.9 sparsity, 88.82% versus 11.79% (MP) and 11.49% (WF). Against Combinatorial Brain Surgeon (CBS), EWR is competitive at moderate sparsity (e.g., 71.12% vs 70.21% on MobileNetV1 at 0.5) but pulls decisively ahead at high sparsity (66.82% vs 16.38% at 0.8; 88.82% vs 13.68% on ResNet20 at 0.9). Against the closest baseline, sparse Linear Regression (LR), EWR matches performance at low sparsity (≤0.6) but consistently outperforms at higher sparsity, with the gap widening under noisy gradients: on MobileNetV1 at 0.9+σ, EWR gains 3.43 percentage points (47.98% vs 44.55%), and on ResNet20 at 0.98+σ, it gains 2.97 points (68.01% vs 65.04%). In the noisy-gradient ablation (Tables 2–3), EWR reduces testing loss by up to 8.13% relative to LR on MobileNetV1 at 0.75 sparsity with 25% noisy data, confirming its robustness advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse linear regression (LR) formulation for network pruning (Chen et al., 2022; Benbaki et al., 2023) by replacing the pointwise squared-error objective with an entropic Wasserstein regression (EWR) objective, showing that LR is a special case when the transport plan is diagonal. The lineage traces back through the MIQP pruning formulation (Yu et al., 2022), WoodFisher's block-diagonal Hessian approximation (Singh & Alistarh, 2020), and the original Optimal Brain Damage/Surgeon ideas (LeCun et al., 1989; Hassibi & Stork, 1992). The key conceptual departure is framing the pruning objective as a sliced Wasserstein distance between projected gradient distributions, which implicitly enforces neighborhood interpolation (a form of gradient averaging) without requiring explicit density estimation or manual neighbor selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: standard FIM-based pruning methods are sensitive to noisy gradients arising from federated learning, analog memory, or adversarial data poisoning, yet prior work has not offered a principled noise-robust alternative with comparable computational cost. The 6% accuracy gain on MobileNetV1 at 75% sparsity (under 25% noisy data) is the strongest empirical evidence, though gains over the closest baseline (LR) are typically 1–3% in cleaner settings. Open questions include scalability to trillion-parameter models (mentioned in the introduction but not tested), the sensitivity to the entropic regularization parameter ε (ablation shows ε=∞ degrades performance), and whether the Gaussian-projection assumption underlying the closed-form OT solution holds for very high-dimensional gradient distributions in practice.
