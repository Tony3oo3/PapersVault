## Problem

Conditional generative models based on gradient flows (e.g., sliced Wasserstein flows) cannot be directly adapted for posterior sampling in Bayesian inverse problems because the generator is not trained end-to-end, unlike GANs or VAEs. Du et al. (2023) empirically showed that fixing the velocity in the conditioning component works well, but provided no theoretical justification. This paper fills that gap by proving error bounds between joint and posterior distributions under MMD, establishing that the conditional particle flow is indeed a Wasserstein gradient flow, and building a practical method on the energy distance (MMD with negative distance kernel) for efficient high-dimensional posterior sampling.

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy observation y (e.g., a low-dose CT sinogram, a downsampled image, or a partially masked image) and a latent sample z drawn from a simple distribution (e.g., Gaussian). The observation y is held fixed throughout the particle flow as the second component of the joint measure, while only the image component evolves. Outputs are multiple posterior samples of the unknown image x, from which the user can compute a mean reconstruction and pixel-wise standard deviation maps to quantify uncertainty. Experiments span 28×28 (MNIST, FashionMNIST), 32×32 (CIFAR-10), 64×64→140×140 (CelebA superresolution), and 362×362 (LoDoPaB-CT) images.

## Method

The core mechanism is a conditional particle flow where N particles (z_i, q_i) evolve only in the image component via the gradient of the discrete MMD functional with the negative distance kernel K(x,y) = −‖x−y‖, while the observation component q_i remains fixed. The ODE is simulated with explicit Euler steps (with momentum) and approximated by a sequence of L UNets, each trained to predict a fixed number of Euler steps from the current particle state and condition. Gradients are computed efficiently via sliced MMD (reducing complexity from O(N²) to O(N log N)), and a pyramidal multi-resolution schedule with locally-connected projections accelerates convergence in high dimensions. Theoretically, the paper proves that the expected MMD distance between the learned posterior and the true posterior is bounded by a power of the joint-distribution MMD error (Theorem 2), and that the conditional flow is a Wasserstein gradient flow of a modified MMD functional (Theorem 4), which also justifies the empirical trick of Du et al. (2023).

## Results

For class-conditional image generation, the conditional MMD flow achieves average class-conditional FID of 14.6 on MNIST, 27.5 on FashionMNIST, and 101.8 on CIFAR10. For superresolution on real-world material microstructures, the method attains PSNR 27.21 and SSIM 0.774 (averaged over 100 posterior samples per observation), substantially outperforming both WPPFlow (PSNR 25.89, SSIM 0.657) and SRFlow (PSNR 25.11, SSIM 0.637). The method also produces pixel-wise standard deviation maps for CT reconstructions, enabling uncertainty quantification in low-dose and limited-angle settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ℓ-SWF (Du et al., 2023), the closest conceptual baseline using sliced Wasserstein flows, the conditional MMD flow wins on MNIST (FID 14.6 vs. 18.9) and CIFAR10 (101.8 vs. 118.1) but loses on FashionMNIST (27.5 vs. 25.9). For superresolution on material microstructures, the method clearly outperforms both WPPFlow (PSNR 27.21 vs. 25.89; SSIM 0.774 vs. 0.657) and SRFlow (PSNR 27.21 vs. 25.11; SSIM 0.774 vs. 0.637), with the SSIM gap being particularly large (~0.12 over WPPFlow). Notably, WPPFlow requires exact knowledge of the forward operator and noise model for training, whereas the conditional MMD flow is trained purely on paired data. For low-dose CT on the LoDoPaB testset (3553 images), the paper reports a quantitative comparison with Denker et al. (2021) in terms of PSNR and SSIM of mean images, though explicit numbers are deferred to the appendix figures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of generative sliced MMD flows (Hertrich et al., 2024) and sliced Wasserstein gradient flows (Du et al., 2023) to the conditional setting for posterior sampling in Bayesian inverse problems. While prior conditional generative models for inverse problems (Ardizzone et al., 2019; Chung et al., 2023; Hagemann et al., 2022) rely on KL-divergence-based losses and end-to-end training, this work instead uses MMD with the negative distance kernel (energy distance) and approximates the joint distribution via particle flows, then derives the posterior from the learned joint. A key departure is providing the first theoretical justification for Du et al.'s empirical practice of zeroing the y-component velocity in sliced Wasserstein flows (Corollary 13), and establishing error bounds between joint and posterior distribution errors in the MMD metric (Theorem 2).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The error bound connecting joint-distribution approximation error to posterior error (Theorem 2) gives practitioners a principled way to reason about how well a learned joint distribution translates into accurate posterior samples, which was previously only available for KL-based methods. The theoretical characterization of the conditional particle flow as a Wasserstein gradient flow (Theorem 4) also retroactively validates a widely-used but previously unjustified heuristic in the sliced Wasserstein flow literature. Open questions include whether the dimension-dependent exponent in Theorem 2 is optimal, whether similar results extend to other kernels, and the mean-field limit convergence of the discrete particle flows. Practical limitations include the inability to batch (due to particle interactions) and the need for full retraining when the forward operator or noise model changes.
