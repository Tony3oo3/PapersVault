## Problem

There is no consensus on how in-context learning (ICL) in LLMs actually works: some work likens ICL to a general-purpose learning algorithm (e.g., gradient descent or Bayesian inference), while others claim ICL does not even learn input-label relationships from in-context examples. This paper addresses the gap by formulating three null hypotheses that encode how an idealized conventional learner should incorporate label information, then empirically testing whether ICL conforms to each.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of in-context input-label pairs concatenated with a test query sentence as a single input string to a decoder-only LLM, constrained by the model's maximum token limit (2048 for LLaMa/Falcon, 4096 for LLaMa-2). Rather than producing a single point prediction, the evaluation extracts the full probability distribution over label tokens at each label position within the input, yielding log likelihood, entropy, and accuracy at every possible context size from a single forward pass. This single-pass extraction of the joint probability over all label positions is the key evaluation mechanism that makes full ICL training curves computationally free.

## Method

The paper formulates three null hypotheses (NH1–NH3) about how a conventional learner should use in-context labels and tests each through targeted experiments across LLaMa-2, LLaMa, and Falcon model families on nine NLP tasks. For NH1 (label dependence), they randomize in-context labels and create a truly novel authorship-identification task from private Slack messages, showing ICL predictions do depend on labels. For NH2 (overcoming pre-training preference), they flip or replace label relationships and show ICL predictions plateau at higher entropy and lower likelihood than the default scenario, indicating pre-training preferences cannot be fully overcome. For NH3 (equal treatment of in-context information), they dynamically change label relationships mid-sequence (default→flipped, flipped→default, alternating) and show ICL preferentially uses information closer to the query. A key methodological contribution is the use of probabilistic metrics (log likelihood, entropy) alongside accuracy, which reveal large ICL dynamics invisible to accuracy alone, combined with a single-forward-pass evaluation strategy that extracts predictions at all context sizes simultaneously.

## Results

Across 10 LLMs (LLaMa-2 7B/13B/70B, LLaMa 7B/13B/65B, Falcon 7B/7B-Instruct/40B/40B-Instruct) and 9 tasks, ICL log likelihoods degrade significantly when in-context labels are randomized: LLaMa-2-70B shows drops of 0.51–0.80 nats on SST-2 through AG News, and even smaller models show consistent degradation (e.g., Falcon-40B: 0.34–0.90 nats). On the novel authorship identification task, all models except Falcon-7B-Instruct achieve accuracies significantly above random guessing, confirming ICL can learn truly novel label relationships. However, ICL cannot overcome pre-training preference: flipped-label predictions plateau with entropy gaps of 0.17–1.00 nats (LLaMa-2-70B) and 0.28–0.92 nats (Falcon-40B) relative to default labels, and no tested prompt eliminates this gap at large context sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical study rather than a method comparison, so the 'baselines' are controlled experimental scenarios. Against the Min et al. (2022b) claim that label randomization 'barely hurts' ICL, the authors show that probabilistic metrics (log likelihood, entropy) reveal large and consistent degradations that accuracy alone masks; they attribute Min et al.'s null finding to (i) not using probabilistic metrics, (ii) fixing context size at 16 rather than studying full training curves, (iii) using mostly small models, and (iv) tasks where default performance is near random. Against the 'general-purpose learning algorithm' hypothesis (Xie et al., 2022; Von Oswald et al., 2023), the authors show ICL fails two key properties of conventional learners: it cannot overcome pre-training preference (flipped labels plateau with persistent entropy gaps of up to 1.0 nat), and it does not treat all in-context information equally (predictions after 2N observations differ significantly across D→F, F→D, and alternating orderings, with a clear recency bias toward the query). The paper also contrasts with Wei et al. (2023), who claim large models can override pre-training knowledge in-context; the authors' results show that even LLaMa-2-70B and Falcon-40B cannot fully close the gap on flipped labels regardless of context size.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a middle ground between two contradictory camps in the ICL literature: those who argue ICL implements a general-purpose learning algorithm (Xie et al. 2022, Von Oswald et al. 2023) and those who claim it does not learn label relationships at all (Min et al. 2022b). It refines Min et al.'s label randomization experiment by introducing probabilistic metrics (log likelihood, entropy) and full ICL training curves across all context sizes, rather than evaluating at a single fixed size. The methodological contribution of extracting ICL predictions at all context sizes from a single forward pass is novel, and the creation of a guaranteed-novel authorship identification task extends the evaluation beyond tasks potentially memorized during pre-training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a major open debate by showing ICL does incorporate label information and can learn truly novel tasks, yet cannot fully overcome pre-training preferences and exhibits recency bias toward information closer to the query. These findings have direct implications for prompt-based alignment (Bai et al. 2022b), suggesting that in-context instructions may be insufficient to overwrite deeply embedded pre-training behaviors and could themselves be overridden by subsequent user input. Key limitations include the focus on classification tasks with logit-based evaluation (not free-form generation), the exclusion of RLHF-finetuned models, and the fact that input token limits cap the number of in-context examples, which is itself a structural limitation absent in conventional learning.
