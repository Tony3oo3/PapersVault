## Problem

Existing RL environment libraries (OpenAI Gym, ALE, Gymnax) fail to simultaneously satisfy three requirements critical for industrial RL research: hardware-accelerated speed, flexibility in customizing initial state distributions, and arbitrary scalability of problem difficulty. This gap is especially acute for NP-hard combinatorial optimization problems (routing, packing, logic) that closely mirror real-world industrial settings, where no existing benchmark provides all three properties in a unified, extensible framework.

## Input / Output

Each environment is a pure function: reset takes a JAX PRNG key and returns a state pytree plus a TimeStep; step takes the state and a discrete action (varying in shape from a single integer to an array of per-agent actions) and returns the next state and TimeStep. The observation is a JAX pytree whose structure is environment-specific—grids/images for Snake and Sokoban, adjacency matrices and node sequences for TSP and GraphColoring, or sets of items and spaces for BinPack—making it amenable to heterogeneous neural architectures. The state pytree encapsulates all transition information including a PRNG key for stochasticity and auto-reset, ensuring environments remain stateless and fully compatible with JAX transformations.

## Method

Jumanji implements 22 environments in JAX with a stateless, composition-based design where reset and step are pure functions, enabling seamless application of jit, vmap, and pmap for single-device and multi-device parallelization. Flexibility is provided through a generator abstraction that decouples the initial state distribution from environment dynamics, allowing users to swap or compose generators (e.g., uniform, cluster, explosion) without modifying transition logic. For baselines, the paper provides an A2C agent with environment-specific actor-critic networks that embed geometric symmetries—CNNs for grid-based environments, transformers with self- and cross-attention for permutation-equivariant problems like TSP and BinPack—so that invariances to index permutations are respected architecturally rather than learned. Training uses the Anakin architecture, keeping the entire pipeline (policy inference, environment step, backpropagation) on the accelerator to eliminate host-device data transfers, with gradients aggregated across devices via pmean. The paper demonstrates near-linear scaling of convergence speed with TPU core count (8→128 cores reduces Connector training from 12.8h to 1.4h) and shows that training on multiple generators improves generalization on real-world VLSI TSP instances.

## Results

The A2C baseline improves over a masked random policy on all 22 Jumanji environments, though optimality gaps remain in most combinatorial settings. For the TSP generalization experiment (50 cities), the agent trained on four diverse generators achieves a mean tour length of 5.815 ± 0.025 on 1020 unseen VLSI instances, outperforming the single-uniform-generator agent (6.090 ± 0.029). Hardware scaling on Connector-v2 shows near-linear speedup: reaching 92% optimal performance takes 12.8 h on TPU-8 versus 1.4 h on TPU-128. Scalability experiments reveal strong performance degradation with complexity, e.g. RubiksCube drops from 0.987 solved ratio (3×3, 3 scrambles) to 0.000 (3×3, 9 scrambles or 4×4, 5 scrambles).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, Jumanji does not compare against competing RL methods or other environment suites head-to-head. Instead, it establishes internal baselines: (1) A2C vs. a masked random policy across all 22 environments, where A2C consistently outperforms random but leaves optimality gaps in most NP-hard settings; (2) single-generator vs. multi-generator training on TSP, where the four-generator agent (uniform + cluster + compression + explosion) achieves a 4.5% shorter tour length (5.815 vs. 6.090) on unseen VLSI instances, demonstrating that broader initial-state distributions improve generalization; (3) hardware scaling on Connector-v2, showing near-linear convergence speedup from TPU-8 (12.8 h) to TPU-128 (1.4 h) to reach 92% performance, with GPU (RTX 2080 Super) and CPU serving as lower-throughput references. The paper positions Jumanji against existing suites (OpenAI Gym, ALE, Gymnax, Pgx, OR-Gym) qualitatively, arguing that none simultaneously satisfy the three criteria of speed (hardware acceleration), flexibility (custom generators), and scalability (arbitrary problem complexity).

## Relation to Prior Work

Jumanji carves new ground by being the first RL environment suite to simultaneously satisfy three properties—hardware acceleration (via JAX/XLA on GPU and TPU), flexible initial-state distributions (via custom generators), and arbitrary problem complexity scaling—that prior benchmarks each only partially address. It builds conceptually on the JAX ecosystem (Brax, Pgx, Gymnax, JaxMARL) for device-agnostic parallelism and on the Anakin architecture for fully on-device training pipelines, while drawing its problem set from NP-hard combinatorial optimization literature (TSP, CVRP, BinPack, Knapsack) previously only available in CPU-bound libraries like OR-Gym. Unlike ALE, OpenAI Gym, or Procgen, Jumanji targets industry-relevant COPs with seamless scalability rather than fixed-difficulty game or control tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Jumanji provides a much-needed bridge between academic RL benchmarks and industrial combinatorial optimization, offering a reproducible, hardware-accelerated platform where researchers can study how agent performance degrades as problem complexity grows—a dimension absent from most prior suites. The A2C baselines reveal substantial optimality gaps on NP-hard problems, and the scalability experiments show complete learning failure at higher difficulty (e.g., RubiksCube with 9 scrambles), underscoring that current actor-critic methods are far from solving these problems at scale. Open questions include whether the flexibility gains from multi-generator training transfer to other environments, whether the near-linear TPU scaling holds for more complex multi-agent or continuous-action settings, and whether the benchmark can capture the full stochasticity and distribution shift of real industrial deployments.
