## Problem

Recurrent neural networks trained offline on expert demonstrations often fail to generalize when deployed in closed-loop environments under distribution shift, a gap known as the open-loop to closed-loop causality gap. While closed-form continuous-time (CfC) networks have shown strong closed-loop performance with sparse connectivity, the mechanistic reason why sparsity and low-rank structure improve robustness remains poorly understood. This paper asks how explicitly parameterizing recurrent connectivity as a function of rank and sparsity modulates network dynamics and whether this yields interpretable, provable benefits for closed-loop robustness.

## Input / Output

The recurrent network receives sequential observations—stacked RGB game frames from ALE environments (Seaquest, Alien) or continuous state vectors from MuJoCo (HalfCheetah)—after processing through a convolutional front-end. In the closed-loop evaluation, the observation at time t is a function of the agent's previous action x_t(a_{t-1}), with no external ground-truth feedback to correct compounding errors. The network outputs discrete control actions for ALE or continuous control signals for MuJoCo, and performance is measured by cumulative reward both in-distribution and under five types of input-level distribution shifts (e.g., noise corruption).

## Method

The paper proposes a parameterization W_rec(r,s) = W_1(r) W_2(r) ⊙ M(s) for recurrent weights, where W_1 and W_2 are derived from a rank-r SVD of an orthogonally initialized matrix and M is a fixed random binary mask with sparsity s. This is applied to all recurrent weight matrices in RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs, and CfCs, with the mask fixed throughout training. The key theoretical contribution is proving that sparsity decreases spectral radius (shortening temporal attention span) while rank controls the decay rate of the singular value spectrum (governing directional robustness to perturbations), and that these two pruning mechanisms induce distinct state-space dynamics: low-rank connectivity constrains recurrently-driven activity to a lower-dimensional subspace, whereas high sparsity increases the effective dimensionality of that activity. Models are trained via imitation learning on expert DQN trajectories offline, then evaluated online in closed-loop, where low-rank, low-sparsity CfCs with fewer parameters outperform full-rank, fully-connected counterparts under distribution shift.

## Results

Under distribution shift in Seaquest, low-rank (rank 1), low-sparsity (s=0.2) CfCs achieve a normalized reward of 0.93, outperforming full-rank, fully-connected CfCs (0.97 at s=0) and substantially exceeding full-rank CfCs at higher sparsity (0.70 at s=0.5, 0.76 at s=0.8). In the in-distribution setting, high-rank, low-sparsity models dominate: CfC rank 16 at s=0 reaches 1.01 and LSTM rank 27 at s=0 reaches 1.00. Across all three environments (Seaquest, Alien, HalfCheetah), low-rank models with fewer parameters at initialization consistently match or exceed their full-rank counterparts under distribution shift, while the advantage is most pronounced for CfCs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LSTMs, CfCs achieve superior robustness under distribution shift: at rank 1, s=0.2, CfCs reach 0.93 while LSTMs drop to 0.83; at full rank, s=0.5, CfCs score 0.70 versus LSTMs' 0.90, but the CfC advantage is most dramatic at low rank where the low-rank prior is most effective. LSTMs, however, maintain more consistent in-distribution performance across sparsity levels (e.g., full-rank LSTM at s=0.2: 0.98 vs. CfC: 0.76), reflecting their longer recurrent memory horizon that is less disrupted by sparsity. Against RNNs, CfCs show higher spectral norms and lower-dimensional state-space trajectories, translating to better distribution-shift robustness; RNNs at rank 16, s=0.8 achieve 1.07 in-distribution but this does not translate to shift robustness. GRUs perform poorly across most (r,s) configurations, consistent with prior findings that GRUs are ill-suited for closed-loop settings. The key architectural finding is that CfCs' adaptive time-constant gating (network F) allows them to adhere to the low-rank prior throughout training (lowest ‖ΔW‖_F), whereas LSTMs and RNNs learn higher-rank connectivity changes that undermine the initialization prior under the task dimension gap between offline training and online deployment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: the sparse connectivity structure in closed-form continuous-time neural networks (CfCs, Hasani et al. 2021) and the low-rank recurrent connectivity framework from computational neuroscience (Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic 2018). It generalizes the rank-1, fixed-weight parameterization of Herbert & Ostojic (2022) to arbitrary rank with trainable weights, yielding W_rec(r,s) = W_1(r)W_2(r) ⊙ M(s). The paper also positions itself within the broader pruning-at-initialization literature and the open-loop-to-closed-loop causality gap setting, but departs from prior pruning work by showing that rank reduction and sparsity induction are not interchangeable forms of parameter reduction—they induce qualitatively different spectral dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that low-rank, sparse CfCs with fewer parameters can outperform full-rank, fully-connected counterparts under distribution shift, offering a memory-efficient and interpretable design principle for robust closed-loop agents. The distinction between rank and sparsity as independent axes of network dynamics (spectral radius vs. singular value decay) provides a principled framework for understanding *why* certain connectivity priors help, rather than merely *that* they do. However, the theoretical results are limited to initialization, the spectral norm analysis has acknowledged cross-architecture limitations, and the 'task dimension gap' concept, while intriguing, remains underdeveloped and would benefit from formalization. The scope is also limited to ALE and MuJoCo environments with relatively small hidden sizes.
