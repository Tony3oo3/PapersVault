## Problem

Reinforcement finetuning (RFT) of language models via policy gradient methods is widely used for alignment, yet a fundamental optimization obstacle has gone unnoticed: the expected gradient for an input vanishes when the reward standard deviation under the model is small, even if the expected reward is far from optimal. This paper identifies, proves, and empirically demonstrates this vanishing-gradient phenomenon, showing it is prevalent in real benchmarks and leads to extremely slow reward maximization.

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on a pretrained autoregressive language model (GPT-2, T5-base, BERT-mini, MLP, or ResNet18) conditioned on a text input sequence, with a reward function mapping input-output pairs to [-1,1]. The key diagnostic quantity is the reward standard deviation of the model's output distribution for each input: inputs where the model assigns non-negligible probability only to outputs of roughly the same suboptimal reward have small reward standard deviation and thus near-zero expected gradient. The output is the finetuned model's parameters, but the paper's primary contribution is characterizing which inputs RFT can and cannot effectively optimize, and how an initial SFT phase (even on 1% of labeled samples) restores gradient signal for the subsequent RFT phase.

## Method

The core result (Theorem 1) proves that the expected gradient norm for an input is upper-bounded by O(L_out · γ · STD^{2/3}), where STD is the reward standard deviation and γ is a Jacobian norm, establishing that small reward standard deviation directly causes vanishing gradients due to the interplay between the reward-maximization objective and softmax token distributions. The paper validates this on the GRUE benchmark (showing 3 of 7 datasets contain many inputs with near-zero pretrain reward standard deviation and suboptimal reward), runs controlled experiments with perfect exploration to rule out insufficient exploration as the cause, and provides a theoretical analysis (Theorem 2) proving an exponential optimization-time separation: RFT takes Ω(1/STD²) steps while SFT takes only O(ln(1/STD)) steps to correct a misclassified input. Conventional heuristics (higher learning rate, temperature, entropy regularization) are shown to be inadequate, while a brief initial SFT phase on as few as 1% of labeled inputs substantially reduces the number of vanishing-gradient inputs and boosts RFT efficacy by 5–18×.

## Results

On the GRUE benchmark, the Pearson correlation between pretrain reward standard deviation and absolute reward mean change is 0.48 for RFT vs. only 0.05 for SFT on NarrativeQA, and 0.46 vs. 0.16 on ToTTo, confirming that RFT disproportionately fails to improve inputs with small reward standard deviation. In controlled experiments with perfect exploration (expected gradients available), RFT still cannot maximize reward over inputs with small pretrain reward standard deviation, while SFT quickly reaches maximal reward. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 2) establishes an exponential optimization time separation: RFT requires Ω(1/STD²) steps while SFT requires only O(ln(1/STD)) steps to correctly classify an input. Remarkably, performing SFT on as few as 1% of input samples (with 40% of the default optimization steps) allows subsequent RFT to reach 96% of the reward achieved with a full SFT phase, and RFT becomes roughly 5–18× more potent after even a partial initial SFT phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between RFT (via PPO) and SFT as finetuning strategies. On datasets where vanishing gradients are prevalent (NarrativeQA, ToTTo), RFT achieves substantially lower reward than SFT, and the gap widens as the 10th percentile of pretrain reward standard deviation decreases (Figure 2). On IMDB, where most inputs already have high reward or large reward standard deviation, RFT and SFT perform comparably (both achieving 0.72 correlation). Three conventional heuristics—increasing learning rate, applying temperature to logits, and entropy regularization—are tested and found inadequate: none improves train reward over the default RFT configuration, nor do they reduce the number of samples with small reward standard deviation. In contrast, an initial SFT phase (even partial, using only 1% of labeled inputs) dramatically improves RFT's subsequent performance, reducing the number of vanishing-gradient inputs and boosting RFT's reward gain by 5–18×. The controlled experiments (Section 4.2) eliminate the confound of insufficient exploration by providing exact expected gradients, yet RFT still fails on small-STD inputs while SFT succeeds, isolating vanishing gradients as the root cause.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the known vanishing-gradient pathology of softmax-parameterized policy gradients (Ahmed et al., 2019; Mei et al., 2020a,b; Agarwal et al., 2021), which was previously tied to near-deterministic output distributions, to the much broader and more practically relevant condition of small reward standard deviation. It positions itself within the RLHF/RFT pipeline established by Ziegler et al. (2019), Ouyang et al. (2022), and Touvron et al. (2023), and uses the GRUE benchmark (Ramamurthy et al., 2023) as its empirical testbed. The theoretical analysis draws on gradient-flow techniques from linear and overparameterized network dynamics (Saxe et al., 2014; Du et al., 2019; Razin and Cohen, 2020) to derive an exponential optimization-time separation between RFT and SFT. Conceptually, the paper repurposes the well-known SFT-then-RFT pipeline by providing the first theoretical explanation for why the SFT phase is beneficial: it increases reward standard deviation, thereby un-vanishing the RFT gradient.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, measurable diagnostic (reward standard deviation) for identifying inputs on which RFT will be ineffective, which is practically valuable for practitioners designing alignment pipelines. The finding that as few as 1% of SFT-labeled inputs can restore most of RFT's efficacy reduces the data-labeling cost of the SFT phase substantially. However, the study is limited to small-to-moderate models (GPT-2, T5-base, BERT-mini) and does not incorporate reward models learned from human feedback, leaving open whether the phenomenon and its mitigations scale to the large-model, iterative-RLHF settings now standard in industry. Future work on modifying the RFT objective (e.g., DPO, RAFT, REST) to directly address vanishing gradients is identified as a promising but unexplored direction.
