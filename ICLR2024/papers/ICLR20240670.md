## Problem

Existing neural MI estimators (MINE, InfoNCE, NWJ, DOE) struggle with challenging distributions such as long-tailed, high-dimensional, or sparsely interacting variables, and none pass the self-consistency tests (data processing inequality and additivity under independence) proposed by Song & Ermon. This paper addresses both gaps by deriving MI estimation from score-based diffusion models via the Girsanov theorem, yielding estimators that are accurate on difficult distributions and provably consistent.

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired samples (X, Y) drawn from the joint distribution μ^C of two random variables in R^N × R^N, with no parametric assumptions on the underlying densities. It outputs a scalar MI estimate in nats, and as by-products also provides KL divergence and entropy estimates for the marginal and conditional distributions. For the consistency tests on MNIST (28×28 grayscale), images are first mapped to a latent space via a deterministic autoencoder, and MI is computed on the concatenated latent representations.

## Method

The key theoretical insight is that the KL divergence between two distributions equals the time-integrated expected squared difference of their score functions along a forward diffusion SDE, plus a vanishing term at large T (Eq. 10), derived via the Girsanov theorem and path-measure disintegration. This reduces MI estimation to training score networks and evaluating score differences. Two modeling variants are proposed: MINDE-C uses a single conditional score network (classifier-free guidance style) to model both marginal and conditional scores, while MINDE-J introduces a joint diffusion SDE with binary parameters α, β that control which variable is being diffused, allowing a single network to produce joint and conditional scores. MI is then computed either as a difference of entropy estimates against a Gaussian reference (MINDE-C(σ), MINDE-J(σ)) or directly as the norm of the score difference (MINDE-C, MINDE-J), the latter eliminating the need for a reference variance parameter. Training uses standard denoising score matching with importance sampling over the diffusion time, and the estimator is neither an upper nor lower bound, freeing it from the pessimistic guarantees of prior variational approaches.

## Results

On the 40-task Czyz benchmark (10 unique distributions × 4 parametrizations, including long-tailed Student-t, Half-cube, and Spiral transformations), MINDE-C achieves the best accuracy with 35/40 tasks estimated within 0.1 nats of ground truth, compared to MINE's 24/40. On the High MI benchmark with sparse 3×3 multivariate normals, MINDE outperforms all neural baselines on transformed variants (Half-cube, Spiral) where competitors degrade significantly. All four MINDE variants pass the three self-consistency tests (independency, data processing, additivity under independence) on MNIST, a property no other neural MI estimator achieves simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MINDE-C outperforms MINE (Belghazi et al., 2018) by 11 tasks (35 vs. 24 out of 40 within 0.1 nats), with the largest gaps on long-tailed Student-t distributions and highly non-linear transformations (Spiral, Normal CDF) where MINE's density-ratio approach struggles. InfoNCE (Oord et al., 2018) and NWJ (Nguyen et al., 2007) both degrade substantially on the same challenging distributions, with NWJ showing the weakest overall performance among neural baselines. DOE (McAllester & Stratos, 2020), which provides formal upper bounds, is the least accurate in practice due to its conservative bound construction. Critically, on the self-consistency tests (independency, data processing inequality, and additivity under independence) using MNIST images in a latent space, all four MINDE variants pass all three tests, whereas none of the four neural baselines passes all three simultaneously—a property the authors identify as a longstanding pain-point of existing estimators.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the score-based diffusion framework of Song et al. (2021) and the classifier-free guidance mechanism of Ho & Salimans (2021) for the conditional variant, while the joint diffusion model extends the multi-modal latent diffusion architecture of Bounoua et al. (2023). The key conceptual departure is the use of the Girsanov theorem to express KL divergence between two distributions as an expected squared difference of their score functions along a diffusion trajectory — a connection that prior MI estimation work (MINE, InfoNCE, NWJ, DOE) did not exploit. Within the taxonomy of Song & Ermon (2019) and Federici et al. (2023), MINDE belongs to the 'generative' family of MI estimators, but unlike prior generative methods that approximate densities or density ratios, it leverages the score function directly as the computational primitive for divergence estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant finding is that all MINDE variants simultaneously pass the self-consistency tests (independency, data-processing inequality, additivity under independence) that no existing neural MI estimator satisfies, suggesting that the score-based formulation has structural properties that naturally enforce information-theoretic constraints. The method also outperforms competitors on challenging distributions (long-tailed Student-t, highly non-linear transformations like Spiral), addressing a known weakness of discriminative estimators. Open questions include the computational cost of training score networks compared to lighter-weight estimators, the lack of tight theoretical bounds on estimation error (the error term 'd' in Eq. 13 is characterized only qualitatively), and whether the approach scales efficiently to very high-dimensional or multi-modal settings beyond the 3-variable extension demonstrated in the appendix.
