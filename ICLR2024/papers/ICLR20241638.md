## Problem

Fair representation learning (FRL) methods use deep neural networks to learn high-dimensional representations that contain minimal demographic information, yet their vulnerability to data poisoning attacks remains unexplored. Existing poisoning attacks target classical (shallow) fair ML methods with scalar predictions and convex losses, and cannot be directly transferred to FRL due to fundamentally different fairness objectives (mutual information on high-dimensional representations) and non-convex DNN architectures.

## Input / Output

The attack takes a pre-trained victim FRL model, a subset of training samples designated as poisoning samples, and a target data set on which fairness is evaluated. It produces perturbation vectors δ added only to the nonsensitive features of the poisoning samples (clean-label: labels and sensitive features remain untouched), constrained per-dimension to stay within the observed clean-data range. The elastic-net penalty encourages sparsity so that only a few feature dimensions are perturbed per sample, improving stealthiness.

## Method

The attack is formulated as a bilevel optimization: the upper level maximizes the Fisher's Linear Discriminant (FLD) score between representations and the sensitive feature on target data (a closed-form proxy for mutual information), while the lower level trains the victim FRL model on poisoned plus clean data. To solve the intractable bilevel problem, the paper uses a gradient-matching strategy (extending GradMatch) that aligns the upper-level FLD gradient with the lower-level loss gradient on poisoning samples, so that standard gradient descent on the victim simultaneously increases unfairness. An elastic-net penalty (L1 + L2) is incorporated to promote sparse, stealthy perturbations, solved via iterative shrinkage-thresholding (ISTA) with projection onto the valid data domain. The paper further derives the first theoretical lower bound on the minimum poisoning ratio P/N required for gradient-matching attacks to succeed, providing a principled defense guideline (e.g., reducing batch size).

## Results

The proposed ENG-based attacks (ENG-FLD, ENG-sFLD, ENG-EUC) achieve the largest decrease in BCE loss (i.e., greatest increase in mutual information between representations and sensitive features) across all four FRL victims (CFAIR, CFAIR-EO, ICVAE-S, ICVAE-US) on both Adult and German datasets, using as few as 5% of training data for poisoning. The elastic-net penalty reduces the L₁ norm of learned perturbations by approximately one-third (e.g., λ₁=0.0025) without degrading attack effectiveness, and λ₁=0.01 achieves a satisfactory attack with only half the perturbation budget. On the larger Adult dataset, ENG-FLD consistently outperforms the other two variants, while on the smaller German dataset the differences narrow. Additional experiments on COMPAS and Drug Consumption datasets confirm the same pattern of ENG outperforming baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three ENG-based attacks against four variants of the Anchor Attack (AA) from Mehrabi et al. (2021): RAA-y, RAA-a, NRAA-y, and NRAA-a. The ENG-based attacks outperform all four AA baselines by a large margin in terms of both BCE loss decrease and DP violation increase across most settings. The AA baselines suffer severe and inconsistent failures: for example, when attacking CFAIR on the German dataset, only RAA-a succeeds across all three poisoning budgets (5%, 10%, 15%), while (N)RAA-y succeeds at 10% but paradoxically fails at 15%. Notably, the comparison is conducted under conditions unfavorable to ENG: the AA baselines are allowed to use any training sample and can modify labels or sensitive features, whereas ENG is restricted to perturbing a fixed randomly selected subset of samples and only modifies nonsensitive features (clean-label). Despite this handicap, ENG still dominates, with ENG-FLD performing best on the larger Adult dataset and ENG-sFLD/ENG-EUC remaining competitive on the smaller German dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GradMatch framework (Geiping et al., 2020) from image classification to fair representation learning, replacing the classification-based upper-level loss with a Fisher's Linear Discriminant score that approximates mutual information maximization between representations and sensitive features. It departs from prior poisoning attacks on fairness (Mehrabi et al., 2021; Solans et al., 2021), which targeted scalar predictions in shallow convex models, by formulating an attack on high-dimensional representations in non-convex DNNs where the fairness objective is information-theoretic rather than constraint-based. The elastic-net penalty from Chen et al. (2018) is repurposed not merely for adversarial example sparsity but as a principled stealthiness mechanism specific to the fairness-degradation setting, combined with a data-domain constraint that has no analogue in prior GradMatch work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that state-of-the-art FRL methods (CFAIR, ICVAE) are vulnerable to clean-label poisoning with as few as 5% of training data, raising an urgent robustness concern for a rapidly growing area of fair ML. The theoretical bound on minimum poisoning samples (Theorem 3.4) is of independent interest to all gradient-matching-based attacks and directly suggests practical defenses (reducing batch size, adding gradient noise). Key limitations include the white-box assumption, restriction to tabular data, and the fact that the FLD proxy, while effective empirically, lacks a tight guarantee when representations are non-Gaussian. Open questions include whether the attack transfers to image/text FRL, whether subpopulation-targeted variants (à la Jagielski et al., 2021) are more damaging, and whether the identified 'robust features' can inform architecture-level defenses.
