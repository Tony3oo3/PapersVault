## Problem

Prior compositional generalization studies were confined to single-modality tasks (e.g., natural language) or relied on massive pretrained models where architectural contributions are confounded by uncontrolled pretraining data. It remained unclear which specific architectural mechanisms—cross-attention, layer depth, recurrent processing—enable different forms of out-of-distribution generalization when inputs span multiple modalities. This paper isolates those mechanisms by training small base architectures from scratch on a controlled multimodal task with explicit OOD splits.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240040/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each trial pairs a task instruction (a binary tree of operators such as Exist, GetColor, GetLocation, SumEven, paired with color-shape feature combinations) with a 10×10 stimulus grid flattened into 100 categorical tokens, each encoding color, shape, and an EOS indicator. The model outputs a 138-element classification vector spanning True/False booleans, 10 color labels, 26 shape labels, and 100 spatial coordinates. Task trees can be arbitrarily deep (depth 1 through 7), going beyond the single-conditional constraint of the original COG task to enable productive generalization evaluation.

## Method

The paper introduces gCOG, a configurable benchmark with three explicit OOD splits—distractor (more distractors than seen in training), systematic (novel operator-object pairings or task-tree combinations), and productive (deeper task trees than training)—and evaluates six encoder-only architectures (RNN, GRU, single-stream Transformer, dual-stream Transformer, cross-attention Transformer, and Perceiver-like model) trained from scratch under identical sample budgets. The central architectural finding is that cross-attention between modality-specific encoders (CrossAttn, Perceiver) is necessary for strong distractor and systematic generalization, and that scaling BERT-like depth and attention heads further improves these splits but fails to unlock productive generalization. A representational similarity analysis of penultimate-layer activations confirms that cross-attention models retain significantly more stimulus and target-response geometry than recurrent or single-stream models. All architectures, regardless of scale, fail at productive compositional generalization, pointing to a fundamental limitation of current purely neural models for generalizing to entirely new syntactic structures.

## Results

On the gCOG benchmark, CrossAttn and Perceiver architectures achieve the strongest OOD distractor generalization, maintaining high accuracy even at 40 distractors, while RNN, GRU, SSTfmr, and DSTfmr show marked degradation beyond 10 distractors. For systematic compositional generalization on depth-1 tasks, all models exceed 78% OOD accuracy (chance = 33.10%), with CrossAttn and Perceiver reaching >97%. On the harder depth-3 systematic split, Perceiver leads with 75.4% IID and 65.7% OOD accuracy, followed by CrossAttn (59.2% IID) and RNN (54.1% OOD). Critically, all six architectures fail completely on productive compositional generalization (depth 5 and 7 task trees), performing at or below chance regardless of architecture or scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six encoder-only architectures (RNN, GRU, SSTfmr, DSTfmr, CrossAttn, Perceiver) rather than external methods. CrossAttn and Perceiver clearly outperform all other architectures on both distractor and systematic generalization, with the Perceiver achieving the single best score on the depth-3 systematic split (65.7% OOD vs. 54.1% for RNN, the next best). The key architectural finding is that cross-attention between modalities (present in CrossAttn and Perceiver) is the decisive factor: DSTfmr, which processes modalities in parallel but integrates only via a shared MLP, performs similarly to the single-stream SSTfmr and well below CrossAttn. Scaling the SSTfmr to BERT-small size (4 layers, 8 heads) improves distractor and systematic generalization but does not rescue productive generalization, where every architecture—including the largest model—fails at or below chance. No architecture achieves productive compositional generalization, indicating a fundamental limitation shared across all tested designs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the compositional generalization evaluation lineage from COG (Yang et al., 2018), SQOOP, and CLOSURE by introducing gCOG, a configurable multimodal benchmark that adds productive generalization splits (arbitrarily deep task trees) and explicit OOD distractor splits absent from prior tasks. It departs from the single-modality focus of most prior systematic generalization studies (gSCAN, Keysers et al., 2020) by using modality-agnostic categorical tokens, and from neuro-symbolic approaches (Bahdanau et al., 2019, 2020) by evaluating purely neural architectures. The Perceiver architecture (Jaegle et al., 2021) is directly adapted as one of the evaluated models, leveraging its latent cross-attention mechanism for generic multimodal integration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The complete failure of all tested architectures on productive compositional generalization — even when scaling to BERT-small size — suggests a fundamental limitation of current Transformer-based designs for handling novel syntactic structures, not merely a data or training issue. The identification of cross-attention as the critical architectural component for multimodal distractor and systematic generalization provides concrete design guidance. Open questions include whether any purely neural mechanism can achieve productive generalization, whether the failure is specific to the binary-tree task formulation, and whether the findings transfer to modality-specific domains (e.g., actual images and language). The configurable gCOG benchmark provides a reusable testbed for future architectural innovations.
