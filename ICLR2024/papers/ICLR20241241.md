## Problem

Computing generalized Nash equilibria (GNE) in pseudo-games and competitive equilibria (CE) in Arrow-Debreu economies is PPAD-hard in the worst case, and existing algorithms converge slowly, require per-instance hyperparameter tuning, and fail numerically on ill-conditioned problems. Prior neural equilibrium solvers (Duan et al., 2021; Marris et al., 2022) are limited to normal-form games because exploitability in pseudo-games requires solving non-linear optimization, is not Lipschitz-continuous, and has unbounded gradients near equilibria, making standard learning tools inapplicable.

## Input / Output

The generator receives a parametric representation of a pseudo-game (e.g., utility parameter matrices V and endowment matrices E for exchange economies, or revenue/cost/externality parameters θ, β, γ, η for the Kyoto mechanism) and outputs a single action profile — a tuple of one action vector per player. The discriminator receives both the game parameters and the generator's proposed action profile, and outputs a best-response action for each player, whose cumulative regret with the generator's output serves as the training signal. The framework operates on structured numerical data with no fixed resolution constraints beyond the dimensionality of the action spaces.

## Method

GAES is a two-network GAN: a generator maps pseudo-game parameters to an equilibrium action profile, and a discriminator maps (game, generator output) to per-player best-response actions. The key insight is that by delegating best-response computation to the discriminator, the training objective becomes the expected cumulative regret — which is Lipschitz-continuous when payoffs are — rather than the exploitability itself, thereby avoiding non-linear sub-programs and unbounded gradients. Training uses Stochastic Exploitability Descent, a nested stochastic gradient descent-ascent algorithm where each generator descent step is paired with multiple discriminator ascent steps over mini-batches of pseudo-games. The paper proves polynomial-time convergence to a stationary point of the empirical exploitability at rate Õ(1/√T_h) and provides sample complexity bounds polynomial in 1/ε and 1/δ for arbitrary Lipschitz-smooth function approximators.

## Results

Across all Arrow-Debreu exchange economy types (Linear, Cobb-Douglas, Leontief, mixed CES), GAES achieves lower normalized exploitability on average than both baselines, with its output better than at least 99% of all feasible action profiles in terms of exploitability. In the Kyoto Joint Implementation Mechanism, GAES achieves normalized exploitability below ~99.5% of feasible action profiles and correctly predicts the qualitative GNE type in most analytical regions. In the pathological Scarf economy, GAES produces prices close to the competitive equilibrium, whereas tatonnement (even when soft-started from GAES's output) spirals into a limit cycle.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAES is compared against two baselines: tatonnement (Walras, 1896) and exploitability descent (Goktas & Greenwald, 2022). Against tatonnement, GAES achieves lower average normalized exploitability in all four exchange economy types tested (Linear, Cobb-Douglas, Leontief, mixed CES), and outperforms it in distribution in every economy except Cobb-Douglas, where tatonnement has a convergence guarantee (though tatonnement still does not beat GAES on average due to its sensitivity to per-market learning rates). In the Scarf economy, tatonnement's prices spiral out from the GAES soft-start into an orbit, while GAES's output remains near the true equilibrium. Against exploitability descent, GAES also achieves lower average exploitability across all exchange economy types. In the Kyoto JI mechanism, no existing algorithm is known to converge to the GNE (the pseudo-game is not monotone), so GAES is evaluated against the analytical qualitative taxonomy of Breton et al. (2006), correctly identifying the GNE type in most regions except Regions 1 and 2a, where the action profiles of adjacent equilibrium types are very close in action space.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work by Marris et al. (2022) and Duan et al. (2021a), which cast Nash equilibrium computation in normal-form games as an unsupervised learning problem, to the much broader class of pseudo-games where players' actions constrain each other's feasible action spaces. The key technical departure is the GAN formulation: because exploitability in pseudo-games is non-Lipschitz and has unbounded gradients, the authors delegate best-response computation to a discriminator network, converting the problem into a min-max optimization with a Lipschitz-continuous objective. The training algorithm is a stochastic variant of the multistep gradient descent-ascent method of Nouiehed et al. (2019) and Sanjabi et al. (2018), and the broader context is the 'differentiable economics' program that applies neural computation to equilibrium and mechanism design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GAES provides the first general-purpose learned solver for GNE in pseudo-games and CE in Arrow-Debreu economies, with polynomial-time convergence and sample complexity guarantees, and demonstrates practical value on pathological instances (Scarf economy) and policy-relevant models (Kyoto mechanism) where no known algorithm converges. The main limitation is that the theoretical results require strong concavity of payoffs (though the authors note this can be weakened to concavity via regularization), and the method requires a training distribution over game instances, limiting its applicability to one-off worst-case problems. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large numbers of players or goods, how sensitive performance is to the choice of training distribution, and whether the GAN formulation can be extended to dynamic or stochastic pseudo-games.
