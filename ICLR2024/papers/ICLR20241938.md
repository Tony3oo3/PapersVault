## Problem

While watermarks for LLM-generated text have been shown to work in idealized settings where verbatim model outputs are tested directly, their reliability under realistic in-the-wild conditions—where text is paraphrased by humans or other LLMs, mixed into larger handwritten documents, or deliberately edited to evade detection—remains uncharacterized. This paper asks: how much text is needed for confident watermark detection after such common corruptions, and how does watermarking compare to alternative detection paradigms under the same attacks?

## Input / Output

For detection, the method takes a sequence of T tokens and the watermark parameters (hashing scheme, context width h, greenlist fraction γ, logit bias δ) as input, and produces a z-score statistic plus a binary detection decision without requiring access to the generating LLM. The WinMax variant additionally searches over all continuous token spans to find the highest-scoring sub-region, making it sensitive to short watermarked fragments embedded in longer unwatermarked documents. For generation, a prompt is fed to a watermarked autoregressive LLM (e.g., LLaMA-7B or Vicuna-7B) that outputs text with the watermark embedded via logit biasing at each decoding step.

## Method

The paper builds on the combinatorial watermark of Kirchenbauer et al. (2023), where a PRF-seeded greenlist of tokens receives a logit boost δ at each generation step, and detection recomputes greenlists to count green-token hits against a binomial expectation. Two key improvements are introduced: (1) a SelfHash scheme using a Min-based PRF with context width h=4 that includes the current token in the hash input, improving security against key recovery while maintaining detectability; and (2) a WinMax windowed detector that computes the maximum z-score over all continuous token spans, enabling detection of watermarked sub-regions within larger documents. The core empirical contribution is a systematic robustness evaluation against three attack types—machine paraphrasing (GPT-3.5-turbo, Dipper), copy-paste embedding into human text, and adversarial human paraphrasing—showing that watermarks remain detectable after ~800 tokens even under strong human paraphrasing at a 1e-5 false positive rate, and that watermarking outperforms DetectGPT, RADAR, and retrieval-based detection in sample complexity, particularly under copy-paste attacks where non-watermark methods degrade as text length increases.

## Results

Under strong human paraphrasing (14 graduate-student writers explicitly attempting to remove the watermark), the watermark remains detectable after observing ~800 tokens at a 1e-5 false positive rate. Against machine paraphrasing by GPT-3.5-turbo and the 11B Dipper model, ROC-AUC stays above 0.85 at T=200 tokens and above 0.9 at T=600 tokens, with the unattacked baseline exceeding 0.999. In the copy-paste scenario (150 watermarked tokens embedded in a 600-token human-written passage), detection AUC exceeds 0.95. The WinMax windowed detector outperforms the standard global z-score test under severe copy-paste attacks (90% of text unwatermarked), where the standard test's AUC drops below 0.7 at 200 tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares watermarking (z-score and WinMax detectors) against three baselines: DetectGPT (loss-based post-hoc), RADAR (adversarially-trained classifier), and Retrieval (BM25 database matching). Watermarking decisively outperforms DetectGPT in every attack setting; DetectGPT shows the poorest scaling behavior, with AUC@T declining as text length increases under copy-paste attacks. Against Retrieval (Krishna et al., 2023), the picture is mixed: Retrieval equals or slightly outperforms watermarking on GPT and Dipper paraphrase attacks, but watermarking (particularly WinMax) clearly outperforms Retrieval in copy-paste settings at FPR < 0.1, and under the most severe 10% copy-paste attack, Retrieval's AUC becomes non-monotonic (decreasing with more tokens) while WinMax steadily improves. Against RADAR (Hu et al., 2023), watermarking wins in copy-paste scenarios, while RADAR performs reasonably well against GPT paraphrase but is capped at 512 tokens due to its context limit. Overall, the paper's central finding is that watermarking has the most favorable sample complexity: it is the only method whose detection power monotonically increases with text length under all attack types, making it the most reliable approach for realistic in-the-wild scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the combinatorial watermark of Kirchenbauer et al. (2023) in two concrete ways: a more secure SelfHash hashing scheme (context width h=4 with self-consistency) and a WinMax windowed detector for identifying watermarked spans embedded in larger documents. Its primary contribution is a comprehensive empirical robustness study under realistic in-the-wild modifications—machine paraphrasing (GPT-3.5-turbo, Dipper), human paraphrasing, and copy-paste into handwritten text—that prior watermarking work had not systematically evaluated. The paper positions watermarking against three alternative detection paradigms (DetectGPT's curvature-based post-hoc detection, RADAR's adversarially-trained classifier, and Krishna et al.'s retrieval-based approach), providing the first head-to-head comparison under matched attack conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous evidence that LLM watermarks remain detectable under the modifications that actually occur in practice, with a concrete sample-complexity characterization (e.g., ~800 tokens for human paraphrases at 1e-5 FPR). The finding that watermarking outperforms retrieval and learned classifiers specifically in the copy-paste setting—where watermarked text constitutes a small fraction of a larger document—has direct implications for deployment on platforms where users mix AI-generated and human-written content. Open questions include the vulnerability to white-box attacks (acknowledged but not fully mitigated), the practical challenge of maintaining low false-positive rates across diverse populations (e.g., non-native speakers), and whether the favorable scaling behavior persists as watermark parameters are tuned for stronger secrecy.
