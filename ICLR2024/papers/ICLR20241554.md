## Problem

Existing retrosynthesis methods either use discriminative formulations that cannot model the inherent uncertainty of multiple valid reactant sets, or adapt diffusion models that are designed to approximate a single intractable data distribution rather than the dependency between two intractable discrete distributions (products and reactants). This paper identifies that the natural formulation of single-step retrosynthesis is a conditional distribution learning problem between two coupled discrete spaces, and that diffusion-based approaches are suboptimal for this task because they require a tractable noise prior as a sampling proxy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a product molecule as a fully-connected molecular graph with one-hot encoded atom types (16 types + dummy) and bond types (3 types + none) as node and edge features, augmented with 10 dummy nodes to accommodate atoms present in reactants but absent in the product. It outputs 100 sampled reactant molecule sets (each a molecular graph) ranked by a confidence score computed as the empirical frequency of each unique reactant set across the samples, effectively estimating the likelihood q_θ(y|x).

## Method

RetroBridge introduces the Markov Bridge Model, a generative framework that models the dependency between two intractable discrete distributions as a Markov process pinned at both endpoints: it starts at the product molecule (z_0 = x) and is constrained to end at a reactant configuration (z_T = y). The transition kernel uses matrices Q_t = α_t·I + (1−α_t)·ŷ·1^T with a cosine schedule over T=500 steps, where a graph transformer network φ_θ(z_t, t) predicts the final reactant state ŷ at each step. Training maximizes a variational lower bound on log q_θ(y|x) expressed as a sum of KL divergences between the true and approximated transition distributions, rather than the cross-entropy loss used in DiGress. Unlike diffusion models that start from a tractable noise distribution, the Markov bridge starts directly from the product molecule as a sample from the intractable prior p_X, making the formulation more natural for mapping between two discrete graph distributions.

## Results

On the USPTO-50k test set, RetroBridge achieves 50.8% top-1, 74.1% top-3, 80.6% top-5, and 85.6% top-10 exact-match accuracy, making it the best template-free method at top-3, top-5, and top-10. On round-trip metrics (evaluated with Molecular Transformer), RetroBridge reaches 97.1% top-5 coverage and 67.8% top-5 accuracy, surpassing even the strongest template-based baselines (LocalRetro: 94.7% coverage, 66.7% accuracy). In the controlled comparison against the discrete graph diffusion model DiGress (Table 3, validation set), RetroBridge-VLB with context outperforms DiGress with context at every k (e.g., 79.44% vs 73.93% at top-5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among template-free methods, RetroBridge clearly leads at top-3 (74.1% vs. 70.7% for DualTFaug), top-5 (80.6% vs. 74.6% for DualTFaug), and top-10 (85.6% vs. 81.6% for GTA), though it trails DualTFaug by ~3 points at top-1 (50.8% vs. 53.6%). Against template-based methods, RetroBridge is competitive but slightly behind LocalRetro at top-10 (85.6% vs. 90.6%) and GLN at top-10 (85.6% vs. 87.9%); however, on round-trip coverage and accuracy RetroBridge surpasses all template-based baselines (e.g., 97.1% top-5 coverage vs. 94.7% for LocalRetro). In the head-to-head comparison with DiGress (the closest diffusion-based analogue), RetroBridge-VLB with context wins at every k on the validation set (e.g., 79.44% vs. 73.93% at top-5, 86.31% vs. 80.88% at top-50), and DiGress without context fails to recover any reactants. A one-shot graph transformer baseline trained for the same duration recovers zero correct reactant sets, confirming that iterative Markov-bridge sampling is essential for this task. On the forward-reaction-prediction task (USPTO-MIT), ForwardBridge achieves 81.6% top-1 accuracy, slightly below MEGAN (86.3%) and Molecular Transformer (88.7%), which the authors note is expected since the probabilistic formulation is less natural for the near-deterministic forward direction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant diffusion-model paradigm for discrete generative modeling by introducing the Markov Bridge Model, which pins a Markov process at both endpoints (product and reactant molecules) rather than starting from a simple noise prior. The discrete transition matrix formulation is directly inherited from Austin et al. (2021), but the key conceptual shift is that the process begins at a data point from the intractable product distribution rather than at sampled noise, making it a natural fit for learning dependencies between two intractable discrete distributions. The paper positions itself against DiGress (Vignac et al., 2022) as the most comparable diffusion-based baseline and against a broad set of template-based (GLN, LocalRetro, GraphRetro) and template-free (MEGAN, G2G, Retroformer, Graph2SMILES) retrosynthesis methods, arguing that the probabilistic bridge formulation better captures the many-to-many mapping between products and reactants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Markov Bridge Model offers a general framework for tasks requiring mapping between two intractable discrete distributions (image-to-image translation, text translation, protein binder design), extending well beyond retrosynthesis. For the retrosynthesis community, the template-free probabilistic formulation with built-in confidence scoring is directly useful for multi-step planning pipelines, and the finding that iterative sampling is essential (one-shot graph transformers fail completely) clarifies a design constraint for future graph-based reaction models. Key limitations include the absence of reaction condition/reagent prediction, single-step scope, and a ~200× slower inference time compared to one-shot baselines, which constrains practical deployment in large-scale planning.
