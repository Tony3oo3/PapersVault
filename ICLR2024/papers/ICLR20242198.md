## Problem

Behavior cloning with deep neural networks exhibits severe training instabilities: minibatch SGD updates cause sharp oscillations in long-horizon rollout rewards (varying by >50% between consecutive iterates) while the single-step BC loss remains smooth and well-behaved. This makes it impossible to reliably select a high-quality policy from training checkpoints, and it is unclear whether the oscillations stem from statistical limitations of the data or from algorithmic pathologies in the optimization procedure.

## Input / Output

For continuous control, the method consumes N offline expert trajectories (state-action pairs of length H=1000) collected from deterministic MuJoCo environments, and the policy network takes the current state augmented with the previous action as input to produce a deterministic action. For language generation, the input is a token sequence and the output is the next-token distribution. Critically, evaluation is not on the single-step surrogate loss but on closed-loop rollout: the learned policy is iteratively applied over H steps (or full autoregressive generation), so small parameter perturbations compound through the feedback loop.

## Method

The paper diagnoses the oscillations as gradient variance amplification (GVA): minibatch SGD noise, which is benign for the single-step BC loss, is catastrophically amplified when propagated through unstable closed-loop dynamics, causing the optimization trajectory to repeatedly visit regions of parameter space with extreme reward sensitivity (a fractal reward landscape invisible in the smooth BC loss). The key mitigation is Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of iterates, which acts as a post-hoc stabilizer that smooths out the SGD noise without requiring aggressive learning rate decay or large batch sizes. The authors validate this across 278 interventions in MuJoCo locomotion tasks (Walker2d, Hopper, HalfCheetah, Humanoid, Ant) and in 270M-parameter Transformer language models trained on TinyStories and Wikipedia, showing EMA consistently reduces oscillations and can obviate full learning rate decay. A theoretical vignette with a discontinuous 'cliff loss' provides a minimal model where EMA provably reduces rollout reward variance by a factor of η/γ relative to vanilla SGD, connecting the empirical findings to classical stochastic convex optimization theory.

## Results

Across 278 distinct interventions on MuJoCo continuous control tasks (Walker2d-v4, Hopper-v4, etc.), the paper identifies Gradient Variance Amplification (GVA) as the cause of wild rollout reward oscillations (up to >50% of max reward between consecutive iterates) while BC loss remains stable. EMA iterate averaging dramatically reduces the mid-training reward range (range_mid): for example, a 6L 128d MLP drops from 0.55 to 0.24, and a 2L 256d MLP drops from 0.44 to 0.13, while maintaining or improving final reward (j_final). In autoregressive language generation (270M-parameter Transformers on TinyStories), EMA stabilizes training, accelerates convergence, and enables training without full learning rate decay, yielding the lowest-perplexity model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a diagnostic paper rather than a method-vs-method comparison, so the 'baselines' are the vanilla training pipeline (AdamW, standard LR decay, batch size 32) and various alternative interventions. The key finding is that neither architectural changes (depth 2–6, width 128–1024, MLP vs. Transformer) nor standard regularization (dropout 0–0.5, weight decay 0.01–0.3, data augmentation) mitigate GVA-induced oscillations. Variance reduction via aggressive learning rate decay (power α up to 3) or large batch sizes (128, 512 via gradient accumulation) do reduce oscillations but at substantial computational cost and potential generalization harm. EMA, by contrast, reduces range_mid by 2–3× (e.g., 0.55→0.24 for 6L 128d MLP; 0.44→0.13 for 2L 256d MLP) with essentially no downside in final reward, making it the most effective and practical mitigation. In the language generation setting, EMA enables training without learning rate decay, outperforming the standard decay schedule in final perplexity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of work on error amplification in behavior cloning (DAGGER, DART), which attributes poor rollout performance to statistical challenges (compounding error, distribution shift) and prescribes data-level fixes requiring online expert access. Instead, it identifies an *algorithmic* pathology—gradient variance amplification (GVA)—where minibatch SGD noise is chaotically propagated through unstable closed-loop dynamics, causing reward oscillations invisible in the BC loss. On the optimization side, it repurposes the classical Polyak–Juditsky iterate-averaging idea (1992) and connects to Sandler et al. (2023) on the equivalence between learning-rate decay and EMA, but reframes EMA not as a variance-reduction tool for the surrogate loss, but as a *stabilizer* for the closed-loop reward. The extension to autoregressive language generation (270M-parameter Transformers on TinyStories) positions GVA as a generic phenomenon for any model whose predictions are fed back into themselves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is immediate and broadly applicable: EMA is essentially free (no extra forward passes, no data collection), works across MLP and Transformer architectures and across continuous control and language generation, and can eliminate the need for aggressive learning-rate decay—suggesting a conceptual split between 'optimizer' and 'stabilizer' roles in deep learning pipelines. Open questions include whether GVA interacts with online RL (where distribution shift is also present), when SGD noise is *helpful* (since very large batch sizes hurt generalization), and whether the inductive biases of EMA for representation learning are connected to this stabilization effect. The theoretical analysis is limited to 1-D toy examples (cliff loss, linear systems), so a rigorous treatment of the nonlinear, high-dimensional MuJoCo dynamics remains open.
