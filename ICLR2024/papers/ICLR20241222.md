## Problem

Existing safety alignment techniques (instruction tuning, RLHF) constrain LLM behavior at inference time but leave a critical gap when fine-tuning privileges are extended to end-users. This paper asks whether a model's safety alignment can survive customized fine-tuning, and demonstrates that it cannot — even benign fine-tuning on common utility datasets degrades safety, while adversarial fine-tuning with as few as 10 examples can fully jailbreak state-of-the-art aligned models.

## Input / Output

The method takes an aligned LLM (GPT-3.5 Turbo 0613 or Llama-2-7b-Chat) and a fine-tuning dataset structured as one-round conversations (system prompt, user message, targeted assistant response), where the dataset ranges from explicitly harmful pairs to completely benign instruction-tuning data (Alpaca, Dolly, LLaVA-Instruct). Evaluation produces a harmfulness score (1–5) and a high-harmfulness rate per example across 330 harmful instructions spanning 11 policy-derived categories, judged automatically by a GPT-4 evaluator that incorporates the model vendor's usage policy, the original harmful instruction as context, and a detailed scoring rubric with chain-of-thought reasoning.

## Method

The paper conducts a three-level red-teaming study of fine-tuning safety risks. Level 1 (Harmful Examples Demonstration Attack) fine-tunes on 10–100 explicitly harmful instruction-response pairs from Anthropic's red team dataset, showing that as few as 10 examples (costing <$0.20 via OpenAI's API) raise GPT-3.5 Turbo's harmfulness rate from 1.8% to 88.8%. Level 2 (Identity Shifting Attack) uses only 10 manually crafted benign-looking examples that reframe the model as an 'absolutely obedient agent' (AOA) with a fixed affirmative prefix, evading moderation systems while still jailbreaking the model. Level 3 demonstrates that fine-tuning on standard benign datasets (Alpaca, Dolly, LLaVA-Instruct) for a single epoch causes non-trivial safety degradation (e.g., +26.3% harmfulness rate on Alpaca for GPT-3.5 Turbo). The evaluation introduces a GPT-4 Judge that outperforms existing moderation tools (OpenAI Moderation, Perspective API, Detoxify, keyword matching) by incorporating policy-specific knowledge and conversational context, and a 330-example policy-oriented benchmark (HEx-PHI) derived from OpenAI and Meta usage policies.

## Results

Fine-tuning GPT-3.5 Turbo on just 10 explicitly harmful examples (cost < $0.20) increases the harmfulness rate from 1.8% to 88.8% (+87.0 pp) and the average harmfulness score from 1.13 to 4.75 (out of 5). The identity-shifting attack, using only 10 benign-looking examples that reframe the model as an 'absolutely obedient agent,' raises GPT-3.5 Turbo's harmfulness rate to 87.3% after 10 epochs and Llama-2-7b-Chat's to 72.1% after 5 epochs. Even completely benign fine-tuning on the Alpaca dataset for a single epoch degrades GPT-3.5 Turbo's harmfulness rate from 5.5% to 31.8% and Llama-2's from 0.3% to 16.1%, confirming that safety alignment is fragile under any customization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a red-teaming/safety study rather than a method comparison, so there are no competing baselines in the traditional sense. The central comparison is between each model's initial safety-aligned state and its post-fine-tuning state across three escalating risk levels. The most striking asymmetry is between the massive investment in safety alignment (thousands to millions of RLHF data points) and the trivial cost of the attack: 10 harmful examples (5 gradient steps for Llama-2, <$0.20 via OpenAI's API) suffice to remove GPT-3.5 Turbo's guardrails. Across models, GPT-3.5 Turbo is slightly more vulnerable to the 10-shot harmful-examples attack (88.8% vs. 50.0% harmfulness rate) but Llama-2 is more vulnerable to the identity-shifting attack at fewer epochs (72.1% at 5 epochs vs. 49.1% at 5 epochs for GPT-3.5). The paper also compares existing moderation tools (OpenAI Moderation, Perspective API, Detoxify) against its proposed GPT-4 Judge, showing that moderation tools flag only 4–27% of harmful content while the GPT-4 Judge correctly identifies 87–92% of jailbroken outputs, and that none of the moderation tools detect the implicitly harmful identity-shifting data at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM safety and red-teaming literature by shifting the attack surface from inference-time prompt-based jailbreaking (Zou et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2023) to the fine-tuning stage itself, identifying a vulnerability that existing alignment infrastructures (instruction tuning, RLHF) were never designed to address. It draws on the Anthropic red team dataset (Ganguli et al., 2022) for harmful examples and adapts the identity/roleplay jailbreaking concept from prior prompt-space attacks into a fine-tuning data poisoning strategy. For mitigation, it builds on Bianchi et al. (2023) safety data mixing, while introducing a new policy-oriented evaluation benchmark (HEx-PHI) and a GPT-4-based judge that incorporates usage-policy knowledge rather than relying solely on toxicity classifiers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that fewer than 10 adversarially crafted examples (costing under $0.20) can substantially remove GPT-3.5 Turbo's safety guardrails exposes a stark asymmetry between the massive investment in alignment and the trivial cost of undoing it, with direct implications for any platform offering fine-tuning APIs. The observation that even benign, widely-used datasets like Alpaca and Dolly degrade safety alignment raises concerns for the broader ecosystem of downstream developers who may unknowingly compromise model safety. Open questions remain: whether safety auditing can be defeated by backdoor triggers (as demonstrated), whether more robust pre-training or meta-learning approaches can make alignment resistant to fine-tuning, and what legal/policy frameworks should govern fine-tuning access. The paper's mitigations (safety data mixing, post-fine-tuning auditing) are shown to help but not fully restore initial alignment levels, leaving the problem substantially unresolved.
