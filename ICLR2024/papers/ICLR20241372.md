## Problem

No existing 3D shape representation method can simultaneously achieve precise shape reconstruction while quantitatively capturing how individual covariates (age, weight, sex, disease status) geometrically affect a population of shapes. Prior implicit representations (DeepSDF, A-SDF, DIT, NDF) lack the ability to disentangle covariate-specific deformations, evolve shapes along covariate axes, or transfer shape changes to new covariate values, limiting their utility for scientific shape discovery in medical imaging.

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-aligned 3D shape meshes (extracted via Marching Cubes from CT/MRI segmentations) along with per-shape covariate vectors (e.g., age, weight, sex, AD status, education length) as input. At test time, a per-shape latent code z (256-dim) and optionally the covariates are optimized via gradient descent against the reconstruction loss, after which the model outputs a reconstructed SDF zero level set, per-covariate displacement fields, shape evolution sequences along covariate axes, and transferred shapes at arbitrary new covariate values. All shapes are normalized to a unit sphere for evaluation, and the additive displacement structure means each covariate's contribution is a separate 3D vector field that can be inspected independently.

## Method

NAISR represents a learned template shape T(p) as a SIREN (sinusoidal-activation MLP) signed distance function and models the deformation from template to target as an additive sum of per-covariate displacement fields: d = Σᵢ gᵢ(p, cᵢ, z), where each gᵢ = fᵢ(p, cᵢ, z) − fᵢ(p, 0, z) enforces zero displacement at zero covariate value. A shared 256-dimensional latent code z, visible to all sub-networks, captures unobserved individual variation. The target shape is obtained by querying the template SDF at the displaced coordinates: Φ(p, c, z) = T(p + d). Training is supervised with Eikonal, on/off-surface Dirichlet, and Neumann losses plus an L² latent code regularizer; at inference, z (and optionally c) are optimized per-shape. The additive architecture, inspired by Neural Additive Models, is the key design choice that guarantees disentanglement by construction, prevents the model from memorizing training shapes through covariates, and enables shape evolution, disentanglement, and transfer as direct queries on the learned displacement fields.

## Results

On shape reconstruction (Table 2), NAISR with known covariates (Ours(c)) achieves the best Chamfer Distance across all three datasets: 0.049 (Starman), 0.126 (ADNI Hippocampus), and 0.084 (Pediatric Airway, mean ×10²). For shape transfer (Table 3), NAISR dramatically outperforms A-SDF on real medical shapes, reducing volume difference from 81.1 cm³ to 12.8 cm³ (mean) on the pediatric airway dataset and from 0.518 to 0.086 on ADNI hippocampus. NAISR is the only method that simultaneously achieves competitive reconstruction quality while providing covariate-disentangled, evolvable, and transferable shape representations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NAISR (Ours(c)) achieves the best reconstruction on all three datasets when covariates are provided as input, outperforming DeepSDF (CD 0.126 vs 0.157 on ADNI), DIT (CD 0.126 vs 0.156 on ADNI), and NDF (CD 0.126 vs 0.253 on ADNI). Without covariates (Ours), NAISR still achieves the best CD on the pediatric airway dataset (0.067 vs 0.077 for DeepSDF) and competitive results on Starman and ADNI. For shape transfer, NAISR substantially outperforms A-SDF on real medical shapes: on the pediatric airway dataset, NAISR reduces mean volume difference from 81.1 cm³ (A-SDF) to 12.8 cm³, and on ADNI hippocampus from 0.518 to 0.086. A-SDF performs slightly better on the synthetic Starman dataset for transfer (0.009 vs 0.025 EMD), likely because the synthetic data provides sufficient longitudinal coverage for A-SDF to learn covariate effects. The key advantage of NAISR over all baselines is that it is the only method combining implicit representation with deformability, covariate disentanglement, evolvability, and transferability simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

NAISR synthesizes three distinct research threads: deep implicit functions (DeepSDF, SIREN) for continuous shape representation, neural deformable models (DIT, DIF-Net, NDF, ImplicitAtlas) for establishing point correspondences between shapes and a template, and neural additive models (NAMs/GAMs) for interpretable per-feature decomposition. The key departure is that prior deformable implicit methods (DIT, NDF) do not model covariates, while A-SDF models covariates but lacks a true atlas-deformation framework with additive disentanglement. NAISR extends the NAM principle from scalar outputs to 3D displacement fields, making the first implicit shape representation that is simultaneously deformable, disentangleable, evolvable, and transferable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NAISR provides a practical tool for medical shape analysis where understanding how covariates (age, weight, disease status) geometrically affect anatomy is a primary scientific goal, enabling tasks like predicting airway growth or hippocampal atrophy from a single scan. Key limitations include the lack of invertibility guarantees (topology preservation is not assured), the absence of evaluation on abnormal shapes, and the reliance on indirect assessment via reconstruction/transfer metrics rather than direct covariate-effect validation. Open questions include whether group sparsity can handle high-dimensional covariates, whether the framework extends to topologically varying shapes, and whether the estimated normal-shape model can serve as a baseline for anomaly detection.
