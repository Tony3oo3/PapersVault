## Problem

While numerous watermarking and classifier-based methods exist for detecting AI-generated images, no prior work characterizes fundamental theoretical limits on their robustness or provides systematic attacks that break them across the full spectrum of perturbation budgets. This paper establishes that low-perturbation watermarks are provably vulnerable to diffusion purification, high-perturbation watermarks can be defeated by model substitution adversarial attacks, and classifier-based deepfake detectors face an inherent robustness-reliability trade-off.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241083/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

For watermarking attacks, the input is a 256×256 RGB image (from ImageNet) paired with a 64-bit binary encryption key; the watermark detector outputs a confidence score indicating the likelihood of the image being watermarked with that key. The diffusion purification attack adds Gaussian noise parameterized by t∈[0,1] and denoises via a 256×256 unconditional guided diffusion model over N×t steps. For the adversarial attack, a substitute ResNet-18 classifier is trained on 7,500 watermarked and 7,500 non-watermarked images, then PGD (300 iterations, step size 0.05ε) generates ℓ∞ perturbations. For the deepfake detection experiments, aligned 228×228 face crops from FaceForensics++ (FaceSwap and DeepFakes manipulations) are fed through a frozen ResNet-18 or VGG-16-BN backbone to extract latent features φ, which are then classified as real or fake.

## Method

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 1) proves that after diffusion purification at step t, the sum of evasion and spoofing errors of any watermark detector is lower-bounded by 1 − erf(√(ᾱ_t)·W(R,F) / (2√(2(1−ᾱ_t)))), where W is the Wasserstein distance between watermarked and non-watermarked distributions and ᾱ_t is the cumulative alpha of the diffusion schedule. This bound is tight for low-perturbation watermarks (small W) but vacuous for high-perturbation ones, motivating a model substitution adversarial attack: a ResNet-18 is trained to distinguish watermarked from clean images, then PGD perturbations generated against this substitute transfer to the true black-box detector, reducing TreeRing's AUROC to 0.14 at ε=2/255. A separate spoofing attack watermarks a random noise image and blends it with clean images to falsely trigger detection. Finally, Theorem 2 extends the analysis to classifier-based deepfake detectors, proving that the AUROC of any (σ,α)-robust detector is upper-bounded by a function of the latent-space Wasserstein distance between real and fake distributions, establishing that increasing robustness (larger tolerable noise σ) necessarily degrades detection performance.

## Results

The diffusion purification attack reduces the AUROC of all low-perturbation watermarks (RivaGAN, DwtDct, DwtDctSvd, WatermarkDM) to below 0.65 at t=0.2, with DwtDct dropping to 0.542 and WatermarkDM from 0.876 to 0.644. For high-perturbation watermarks, the model substitution adversarial attack is devastating: TreeRing's AUROC collapses to 0.035 at ε=4/255 and 0.002 at ε=8/255, while StegaStamp requires ε=12/255 to reach random-guess level (AUROC 0.492). The spoofing attack (blending watermarked noise with clean images) degrades AUROC and TPR-at-low-FPR for all evaluated methods, with RivaGAN being the most resistant. Theoretically, Theorem 1 provides a certified lower bound on the sum of evasion and spoofing errors, and Theorem 2 establishes a fundamental AUROC upper bound for (σ,α)-robust deepfake detectors that tightens as the Wasserstein distance between real and fake distributions shrinks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among low-perturbation watermarks, WatermarkDM (AUROC 0.876→0.644) and MBRS (0.914→0.614) suffer the largest AUROC drops under diffusion purification at t=0.2, while RivaGAN (0.655→0.623) and DwtDct/DwtDctSvd (~0.55) are already near random-guess and see only marginal further degradation. For high-perturbation watermarks, the picture inverts: TreeRing is highly robust to diffusion purification (AUROC 0.976 at t=0.2) but catastrophically vulnerable to the model substitution adversarial attack, dropping to AUROC 0.035 at ε=4/255 and 0.002 at ε=8/255. StegaStamp is more resilient to the adversarial attack, requiring ε=12/255 to reach AUROC 0.492 (random level), but is still broken. Post-attack mitigations (Gaussian blur, JPEG compression) provide negligible recovery for the diffusion purification attack, while DiffPure partially recovers TreeRing's AUROC (from 0.002 to 0.531 at ε=8) but fails to negate the attack at higher budgets. The spoofing attack degrades all methods, with RivaGAN showing the most resistance but still vulnerable at low FPR regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes diffusion purification (Nie et al., 2022), originally proposed as a defense against adversarial examples, as a certified attack on image watermarks, and extends a concurrent result (Zhao et al., 2023a) that only covers low-perturbation watermarks to also break high-perturbation methods like TreeRing and StegaStamp via a model-substitution PGD attack. It further introduces a spoofing attack dimension (adding watermarked noise to clean images) absent from prior watermark-removal work, and generalizes the theoretical framework from watermarking to classifier-based deepfake detectors, establishing a robustness-reliability trade-off via Wasserstein-distance and total-variation arguments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fundamental lower bound on evasion+spoofing error (Theorem 1) and the AUROC upper bound for robust detectors (Theorem 2) establish that no watermark or detector can simultaneously achieve high reliability and high robustness when the underlying distributions are close — a constraint that should inform all future design efforts. Practically, the attacks require only black-box access and minimal perturbation budgets (e.g., ε=2/255 breaks TreeRing), making them immediately relevant to deployed systems. Open questions include whether any watermarking scheme can satisfy all three robustness criteria simultaneously (resistance to purification, adversarial, and spoofing attacks), and whether the robustness-reliability trade-off for deepfake detectors can be circumvented with fundamentally different architectures.
