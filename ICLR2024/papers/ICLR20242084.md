## Problem

Semantic parsing benchmarks and evaluations typically assume a one-to-one mapping between utterances and logical forms, ignoring the well-documented fact that natural language is inherently ambiguous. No prior work systematically tests whether pre-trained language models can capture the distribution of possible meanings for ambiguous inputs, or how they behave when prompted with conflicting interpretations of the same utterance type.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242084/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives an ambiguous natural language utterance (e.g., 'The man saw the boy with the telescope') and must produce a first-order logic formula in neo-Davidsonian event semantics (with agent, patient, and instrument roles) or an equivalent Lisp program. In the zero-shot setting, the prompt provides isolated 'ingredient' examples (e.g., transitive verb use, instrumental 'with', possessive 'with') without any example of the target ambiguity type, requiring compositional generalization. In the few-shot setting, the prompt contains 10 input-LF pairs with a controlled ratio of LF0 to LF1 examples, creating deliberate conflicting evidence that mirrors real-world annotation disagreement.

## Method

The paper introduces AMP, a template-based framework that generates paired ambiguous utterances with two logical forms across five ambiguity types (PP attachment, quantifier scope, inverse scope, pronoun coreference, conjunction). Rather than training models, they evaluate pre-trained autoregressive language models (Codegen 350M–16B, Llama-13B, Vicuna-13B, GPT-3.5-turbo) via in-context learning with grammar-constrained beam search decoding (beam size 5). They propose three metrics: ZM_k (proportion of examples where both LFs appear in top-k outputs), FDM (dataset-level alignment between predicted LF frequency and prompt ratio), and FIM (instance-level Brier-style score comparing forced-decode probability to prompt ratio). Probabilities are computed using a sequence confidence estimate—the minimum token probability under forced decode, renormalized across the two LFs—rather than the raw product of token probabilities, which yields near-zero scores in practice.

## Results

In the zero-shot setting, all models perform very poorly on the ZM₅ metric (proportion of examples where both LFs appear in top-5 predictions), with most scoring 0.00 across ambiguity types. The best zero-shot result is Codegen-16B achieving ZM₅ = 15.00 on conjunctions and 3.50 on bound pronouns; GPT-3.5-turbo scores 0.00 on all types despite high single-LF accuracy. In the few-shot (mixed prompt) setting, Codegen-16B achieves the lowest FDM scores (0.20–0.38) and Vicuna-13B is competitive (0.17–0.35), while Llama-13B fails to predict any LF correctly (FDM = 1.00 on all types). FIM scores are uniformly low (0.03–0.16) across all models, indicating that models capture the prompt's probability distribution well even when their hard predictions are inaccurate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Codegen-16B achieves the best FDM scores on 4 of 5 ambiguity types (PP: 0.35, Scope: 0.20, Revscope: 0.21, Conj.: 0.27), outperforming Vicuna-13B (0.50, 0.17, 0.20, 0.35) and all smaller Codegen variants. Vicuna-13B edges out Codegen-16B on Scope (0.17 vs. 0.20) and Bound (0.26 vs. 0.38), suggesting instruction tuning helps with certain ambiguity types. Llama-13B is the clear loser on FDM (1.00 on all types, meaning it never produces a correct LF under constrained beam search), yet its FIM scores (0.04–0.06) are competitive with or even lower than Vicuna's, revealing that it assigns appropriate probabilities to the gold LFs even though it cannot generate them. GPT-3.5-turbo (zero-shot only) achieves near-perfect single-LF accuracy on PP and Scope but scores 0.00 on ZM₅ for all types, confirming it cannot capture both interpretations. Overall, the key finding is that FDM improves with model size within the Codegen family, while FIM remains relatively flat, indicating that distributional sensitivity to prompt ratios is a property shared across model scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the semantic parsing literature, which has traditionally assumed a one-to-one mapping between utterances and logical forms, by explicitly introducing ambiguity into the parsing task. It builds on the in-context learning paradigm for semantic parsing (Shin et al., 2021; Roy et al., 2022) and adopts the sequence confidence estimation method from Stengel-Eskin & Van Durme (2022) for probability computation. Unlike prior ambiguity work that grounds interpretations in images, videos, or NLI labels (Berzak et al., 2015; Stengel-Eskin et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), this paper focuses on semantic parses as the target representation, and unlike compositional generalization benchmarks like COGS and SCAN, it deliberately tests whether models can produce multiple valid outputs for a single input. The mixed-prompt evaluation setting, where the prompt contains conflicting examples of the same input type paired with different LFs, is described as the first study of ICL with conflicting evidence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical stakes are significant: semantic parsing systems drive real-world actions (robotics, digital assistants, text-to-code), and ambiguity-based miscommunication can have concrete consequences. The key open question the paper surfaces is whether ambiguity in training/evaluation data is a sufficient condition for models to capture it in their outputs — the few-shot results suggest yes, but the zero-shot failure means models cannot recognize ambiguity without explicit evidence. The paper advocates for extending redundancy-aware annotation protocols (already common in NLI) to sequential-output tasks like parsing, and raises the broader question of how to build interactive systems that appropriately express uncertainty and request clarification. Limitations include the fixed logical form, English-only scope, and the gap between the models' offline text processing and human online ambiguity resolution.
