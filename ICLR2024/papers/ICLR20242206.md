## Problem

Empirical evidence shows that neural networks trained on adversarially perturbed samples with mislabeled (or random) target classes can still generalize well to correctly labeled test data, suggesting perturbations contain hidden class features. However, a theoretical explanation for how perturbations encode class features and why the resulting decision boundary aligns with that of a standard classifier has been missing. This paper provides the first theoretical justification for learning from adversarial perturbations.

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis operates on d-dimensional vectors (images or uniform random noise) with binary labels, where training inputs are adversarially perturbed samples x_n^adv = x_n + η_n (or X_n + η_n for noise) paired with target labels y_n^adv that may differ from the original label. Perturbations are generated via geometry-inspired attacks under L0, L2, or L∞ constraints with budget ε. The output is a binary class decision (sign of the network output), and the key quantity of interest is whether this decision matches the standard classifier's decision on clean test inputs.

## Method

The paper analyzes a one-hidden-layer leaky ReLU network trained via gradient flow on mutually orthogonal samples, leveraging the implicit bias result of Frei et al. (2023) to show the decision boundary is linear. The core insight is that geometry-inspired adversarial perturbations η_n are expressible as weighted sums of the training samples, meaning they inherently contain class features. The decision boundary when learning from perturbations decomposes into two terms: one capturing the effect of mislabeled natural samples and one proportional to the standard decision boundary scaled by ε. Under mild orthogonality conditions, the perturbation term dominates for most inputs, yielding decisions consistent with a standard classifier except for specific regions where a test sample is strongly correlated with few training samples. For perturbations on uniform noise, the alignment is even stronger, and the classifier can completely classify natural training samples without ever seeing them during training.

## Results

On MNIST, classifiers trained on L2-constrained perturbations with random target labels achieve 92.9% test accuracy, and L∞ perturbations reach 89.1%, confirming that even mislabeled adversarial samples enable strong generalization. On CIFAR-10, L∞(R) and L2(R) perturbations both achieve ~77% accuracy, while sparse L0 perturbations still reach 54.9%. On Fashion-MNIST, L∞(R) achieves 61.4% and L2(R) achieves 54.8%. In the noise-data scenario (perturbations superimposed on uniform noise rather than natural images), accuracies drop substantially (e.g., 33.2% for L0 on MNIST, 10.2% for L2 on CIFAR-10), though the artificial-dataset experiments (Fig. 2) show near-perfect classification when dimension and sample count are sufficiently large, validating Corollary 4.5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so there are no external method baselines to compare against; the empirical results serve to validate the theoretical predictions rather than benchmark against competing approaches. The internal comparisons are between perturbation norms (L0, L2, L∞) and label-selection strategies (random vs. deterministic). Random-label perturbations consistently outperform deterministic-label ones (e.g., 92.9% vs. 38.4% for L2 on MNIST), because random labels eliminate feature–label anti-correlation and isolate the perturbation's contribution. Sparse L0 perturbations, which modify only a few pixels, still enable meaningful generalization (54.9% on CIFAR-10, 28.4% on MNIST), supporting the paper's claim that even minimal perturbations contain class features. The noise-data scenario (perturbations on uniform noise instead of natural images) yields lower accuracies on real datasets, likely due to a domain gap, but the artificial-dataset experiments confirm the theoretical prediction of complete classification when d and N^adv are large enough.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first theoretical justification for the 'learning from adversarial perturbations' phenomenon empirically observed by Ilyas et al. (2019), who proposed the feature hypothesis that adversarial perturbations contain label-aligned class features. The theoretical framework is built on the implicit bias characterization of one-hidden-layer leaky ReLU networks under gradient flow from Frei et al. (2023), which establishes that such networks converge to a linear decision boundary when trained on nearly orthogonal samples. The paper extends Ilyas et al.'s empirical setting by covering sparse (L0) and L∞ perturbations, and introduces a noise-data scenario where perturbations are superimposed on uniform random noise rather than natural images, enabling stronger theoretical guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical explanation for why adversarial perturbations, despite appearing as noise to humans, carry sufficient class information to enable generalization — a foundational question for the feature hypothesis that underpins much of the adversarial robustness literature. The result that even a few-pixel perturbations contain class features and that classifiers trained purely on noise plus perturbations (never seeing natural data) can classify natural images correctly offers a concrete mechanistic account of transferability and the robustness-accuracy trade-off. However, the analysis is restricted to one-hidden-layer networks with mutually orthogonal training samples and perturbation constraints of O(√(d/N)), which is far smaller than typical practical ε values of O(√d); extending these results to deeper networks, correlated data, and realistic perturbation budgets remains a significant open question.
