## Problem

Hundreds of papers have proposed preprocessing and inprocessing methods to improve over the simple postprocessing baseline for equalized odds, yet prior comparisons were confounded by two methodological errors: comparing methods at different levels of constraint relaxation and using different unconstrained base models. This paper asks whether postprocessing the single most accurate unconstrained model actually Pareto-dominates all other fairness interventions when these confounds are properly controlled.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241353/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on real-valued risk scores from any score-based classifier (GBM, RF, NN, LR) paired with sensitive group membership labels (e.g., four race categories from ACS data). Unprocessing takes a fairness-constrained model and recovers its underlying unconstrained risk scores by optimizing group-specific thresholds without fairness constraints; postprocessing then applies group-specific thresholds to achieve a user-specified relaxation level r of equalized odds. The LP formulation for relaxed postprocessing takes group-specific ROC curves as input and produces a single set of group-specific thresholds that minimize classification loss subject to the r-relaxed constraint.

## Method

The core contribution is 'unprocessing' (π_∞), defined as the unconstrained minimization of classification loss over group-specific decision thresholds applied to a fairness-constrained model's risk scores — effectively the inverse of postprocessing. This maps any fairness-constrained model to its best unconstrained counterpart, enabling direct comparison across methods that use different base learners and achieve different levels of constraint violation. The paper also contributes a linear programming formulation for r-relaxed equalized odds postprocessing, where the optimization domain is the union of group-specific ROC convex hulls subject to affine constraints bounding pairwise group differences in FPR and TPR. Across 11,000+ trained models on six tabular datasets, postprocessing the single highest-accuracy model (selected via unprocessing) Pareto-dominates or matches all 20 competing fairness methods at every level of constraint relaxation.

## Results

Across 11,000 trained models on six datasets (five ACS tasks + MEPS), postprocessing the single highest-accuracy model (m*) Pareto-dominates or matches all 1,000 competing models at every level of equalized-odds constraint violation. The m* model is GBM-based on all datasets, with maximum unprocessed accuracy ranging from 0.713 (ACSTravelTime) to 0.831 (ACSEmployment). On the binary-group (White/Black) variant, maximum constraint violation drops from 0.38 to 0.16 on ACSIncome, yet the same dominance holds. Preprocessing methods (LFR, CR) and inprocessing methods (EG, GS, FairGBM) all fall below the postprocessing Pareto frontier, with LFR and LR-based methods failing to produce any model within the high-accuracy/low-violation region of interest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Postprocessing m* dominates or matches every competing method on all six datasets; no fairness intervention achieves a point outside the postprocessing Pareto frontier. LFR (Zemel et al., 2013) achieves high fairness fulfillment but at a steep accuracy cost, and neither LFR nor LR-based models produce any result within the high-accuracy/low-violation region of interest (Figure 6). CR (Bird et al., 2020) similarly shows lacklustre trade-offs as a standalone method, though interestingly GBM+CR models were selected as m* on ACSTravelTime and ACSEmployment—indicating the preprocessing found better risk scores rather than better fairness enforcement. Among inprocessing methods, EG and FairGBM (Cruz et al., 2023) achieve the highest Pareto-dominated area, but EG requires one order of magnitude more compute than base GBM, and both remain below the postprocessing frontier. GS (Agarwal et al., 2018) is the slowest method (1–2 orders of magnitude longer than GBM) and is dominated by postprocessing on all datasets. The paper's central claim is that the apparent improvements of these methods over postprocessing in prior literature stem from two confounds: comparing against weaker base models and comparing at different levels of constraint relaxation—both of which unprocessing eliminates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Hardt et al. (2016), who introduced equalized odds and the postprocessing tri-search algorithm, and on Woodworth et al. (2017), who identified theoretical counterexamples where postprocessing is sub-optimal. It positions itself against the large body of preprocessing (LFR, Correlation Remover) and inprocessing (Exponentiated Gradient, Grid Search, FairGBM) methods that have been proposed to improve over the postprocessing baseline. The paper also draws on a broader ML meta-evaluation tradition (Armstrong et al. 2009, Lucic et al. 2018, Musgrave et al. 2020) that documents how weak baselines inflate apparent progress, and introduces 'unprocessing'—the inverse of postprocessing—as a new methodological tool for fair comparison across methods with different base models and constraint relaxation levels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that postprocessing of the single most accurate unconstrained model Pareto-dominates all evaluated fairness interventions across 11,000+ models and 6 datasets fundamentally challenges the research program of hundreds of papers proposing more sophisticated fairness algorithms for tabular data. Practically, it suggests that compute and engineering effort should go into training better base models rather than into complex fairness-aware training procedures. Key limitations include the restriction to tabular data and equalized odds; the authors explicitly note that high-dimensional settings (images, text) and other fairness criteria remain unexplored, and that Woodworth et al.'s theoretical counterexamples could still arise in practice under conditions not captured here.
