## Problem

Prior theoretical work established that standard decoder-only transformers (without intermediate generation) are confined to TC⁰ and cannot solve sequential reasoning problems like automata simulation or graph connectivity. However, no general characterization existed for the computational power unlocked by allowing transformers to generate intermediate tokens (chain of thought) before answering, nor for how the number of such steps affects reasoning capacity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240306/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The transformer consumes an input string x ∈ Σⁿ over n forward passes, then generates t(n) intermediate tokens autoregressively (each new token conditioned on all prior hidden states via causal attention), and finally emits an accept/reject symbol. The key parameter is t(n) — the number of intermediate decoding steps as a function of input length — which the paper analyzes in three regimes: logarithmic (Θ(log n)), linear (Θ(n)), and polynomial (O(nᶜ)). The model operates with log-precision arithmetic (c·log(n+t(n)) bits) and strict causal masking, where position i can attend to positions 1 through i−1 but not i itself.

## Method

The paper establishes tight complexity-theoretic bounds: TIME(t(n)) ⊆ CoT(t(n)) ⊆ SPACE(t(n)+log n) ⊆ Õ(t(n)²+n²). The lower bound is proven by constructing a decoder-only transformer that simulates t(n) Turing machine steps using t(n) intermediate decoding steps, with the key technical tool being the layer-norm hash — a 4-dimensional unit-vector encoding φ(x,y) = layer_norm(x,y,−x,−y) that provides scale-invariant exact-match retrieval across positions, enabling the transformer to reconstruct tape contents at arbitrary head positions. The construction requires projected pre-norm (a linear projection before layer-norm in each sublayer, generalizing standard pre-norm) and strict causal masking. The upper bounds follow from simulating the transformer with a multitape Turing machine (time bound) and from the TC⁰ simulation of a single forward pass plus a buffer of t(n) generated tokens (space bound). The results yield: log steps → L, linear steps → all regular languages but within context-sensitive languages, and polynomial steps → exactly P.

## Results

The paper establishes the central bound TIME(t(n)) ⊆ CoT(t(n)) ⊆ SPACE(t(n) + log n) ⊆ Õ(t(n)² + n²), relating transformer decoding steps to standard complexity classes. Concretely: logarithmic steps (Θ(log n)) keep transformers within L (log-space); linear steps (Θ(n)) enable recognition of all regular languages (NC¹-complete) while remaining within context-sensitive languages; and polynomial steps (Θ(n^c)) yield exactly P. The lower bound requires projected pre-norm and strict causal saturated attention, while the upper bounds hold without these architectural assumptions. Both the time lower bound and space upper bound are tight up to a log factor—improving either by more than a log factor would constitute a fundamental complexity theory advance.

**Summary of transformer decoder power by number of intermediate steps t(n).**

| Steps t(n) | Lower Bound | Upper Bound | Key Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (no steps) | TC⁰ | TC⁰ | Cannot solve regular languages, graph connectivity, or linear equalities |
| Θ(log n) | TC⁰ | L = SPACE(log n) | Slight expansion; still cannot solve NL- or P-complete problems |
| Θ(n) | TIME(n) | SPACE(n) ∩ Õ(n²) ⊆ CSL | Recognizes all regular languages; bounded by context-sensitive languages |
| Θ(n²) | TIME(n²) | SPACE(n²) ∩ Õ(n⁴) | Can solve directed graph connectivity (NL-complete) |
| Θ(n^c) (polynomial) | TIME(n^c) | P | Exactly P: first exact characterization of a transformer class by a standard complexity class |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between transformers with different numbers of intermediate decoding steps, benchmarked against standard complexity classes. Without intermediate steps, transformers are confined to TC⁰ and provably cannot recognize all regular languages, simulate finite-state automata, or solve directed graph connectivity. Adding a logarithmic number of steps (Θ(log n)) expands the upper bound only from TC⁰ to L, which is insufficient to overcome any of the known limitations (NL-complete or P-complete problems remain unsolvable). A linear number of steps (Θ(n)) provides a clear qualitative jump: transformers can now simulate automata and recognize all regular languages (a conditional separation from TC⁰ under TC⁰ ≠ NC¹), yet they remain bounded by context-sensitive languages and cannot recognize all context-free languages unless CFLs admit soft-quadratic parsing. Polynomial steps (Θ(n^c)) close the gap entirely, yielding exactly P. The bounds are tight within a log factor: the time lower bound can only be improved to TIME(t(n)·log t(n)) and the space upper bound to SPACE(t(n)/log t(n)) without resolving open complexity-theoretic questions (Hopcroft et al., 1977).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work characterizing the computational limits of log-precision transformers (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023a,b), which established that standard decoder-only transformers without intermediate steps are confined to TC⁰. It adapts the Turing machine simulation ideas from Pérez et al. (2021) to a decoder-only setting without external memory or non-standard positional encodings, introducing the layer-norm hash as a new retrieval mechanism that enables exact-match lookups across positions with different denominators. The key departure from prior work is the fine-grained analysis of how the number of intermediate decoding steps (logarithmic, linear, polynomial) maps to standard complexity classes, filling the gap left by Feng et al. (2023), who showed CoT helps on one specific problem but provided no general characterization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first exact characterization of a transformer variant in terms of a standard complexity class (polynomial steps = P), and it provides a principled framework for understanding why chain-of-thought helps on some problems but not others. The finding that logarithmic CoT steps barely extend transformer power (remaining in L) is practically relevant: it suggests that short scratchpads may not overcome fundamental sequential-reasoning limitations, while linear-length CoT is needed to simulate recurrence. Key open questions include whether transformers can actually learn to exploit these intermediate steps (the paper analyzes expressivity, not learnability), whether the quadratic upper bound for graph connectivity can be tightened to linear, and whether the projected pre-norm assumption is necessary in practice.
