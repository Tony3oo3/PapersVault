## Problem

Inverse reinforcement learning has never been successfully applied at planetary scale, where the MDP contains hundreds of millions of states and demonstration trajectories. The core computational bottleneck is that IRL algorithms require solving an RL problem (dynamic programming) at every gradient step, making it infeasible to fit graph features into memory or compute a single update on a 200M-node road network. This paper addresses that scalability gap to enable IRL-based route recommendation in a production setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241656/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 200M-node discrete, deterministic, undiscounted road network graph with per-edge features (predicted travel duration, distance, surface condition, speed limit, road type, name changes) and 110M de-identified user trip demonstrations collected during active navigation. The output is a learned reward function (up to 360M parameters via a sparse per-edge model) that is evaluated once offline for every edge, stored in a database, and combined with precomputed contraction hierarchies to serve online (origin, destination) routing requests via fast graph search — avoiding the need to evaluate a policy online at each step.

## Method

The central contribution is Receding Horizon Inverse Planning (RHIP), which reveals that classic IRL algorithms (MaxEnt, BIRL, MMP) are special cases of a single parameterized family controlled by a planning horizon H: the algorithm runs H steps of stochastic MaxEnt++ backups, rolls out the resulting stochastic policy for H steps, then switches to a cheap deterministic Dijkstra-based policy for the remainder. This enables fine-grained interpolation between robust but expensive stochastic policies and fast but brittle deterministic planners, with H=10 empirically outperforming full MaxEnt (H=∞) in both accuracy and training speed. Supporting techniques include MaxEnt++ (initializing the backward pass with Dijkstra-computed highest-reward-to-destination values, inspired by the connection to dominant eigenvector power iteration), a geographic sparse mixture-of-experts for spatial parallelization, and lossless/lossy graph compression (splitting high-degree nodes and merging single-outgoing-edge nodes) that yields a 2.7× training speedup with negligible quality loss.

## Results

On the global 200M-state road network, RHIP with a 360M-parameter DNN+SparseLin reward model achieves 49.58% route accuracy and 72.08% IoU for driving, representing a 15.9% relative accuracy lift over the ETA+penalties baseline (42.83%). In the experimental region (9 metros), the same model reaches 50.30% driving accuracy and 55.64% two-wheeler accuracy, a 24.1% lift over ETA+penalties for two-wheelers. RHIP also trains 70% faster than MaxEnt at H=10 while achieving the highest accuracy, and graph compression (split+merge) provides a 2.7× speedup with negligible quality loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RHIP (DNN+SparseLin) outperforms all IRL baselines on both driving and two-wheeler accuracy. For driving, RHIP achieves 50.30% accuracy vs. Deep MaxEnt (50.07%), Deep BIRL (49.88%), MaxEnt++ SparseLin (49.22%), MMP/LEARCH SparseLin (48.53%), and Deep LEARCH (46.82%); the 0.23-point gap over Deep MaxEnt is statistically significant (p=0.051). For two-wheelers, RHIP reaches 55.64% accuracy vs. Deep BIRL (55.46%), MaxEnt++ SparseLin (54.01%), and MMP/LEARCH SparseLin (52.33%), with a 0.18-point margin over Deep BIRL (p=0.122). On IoU, RHIP leads for driving (0.7086) and two-wheelers (0.7591), though the IoU differences are not statistically significant. All IRL methods substantially outperform the manually designed ETA+penalties baseline (42.74% driving, 44.75% two-wheeler accuracy), confirming that learned reward functions capture latent user preferences beyond hand-tuned penalties.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three classic IRL algorithms—MaxEnt (Ziebart et al., 2008), BIRL (Ramachandran and Amir, 2007), and MMP (Ratliff et al., 2006)—under a single generalization called Receding Horizon Inverse Planning (RHIP), where the planning horizon H interpolates between them (H=∞ recovers MaxEnt, H=1 recovers BIRL, H=0 recovers MMP). It builds on the deep IRL line of work (Wulfmeier et al., 2015; Mainprice et al., 2016) but shifts focus from model architecture to scalability, introducing practical techniques (graph compression, geographic mixture-of-experts, eigenvector-inspired initialization) that had not been previously combined to address planetary-scale MDPs. The MaxEnt++ initialization draws on the observation that the backward pass is equivalent to power iteration for the dominant eigenvector, a connection noted in passing by Ziebart (2010) but not previously exploited algorithmically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the largest published study of IRL in a real-world setting (200M states, 110M demonstrations, 360M parameters), deployed in Google Maps with 16–24% route accuracy gains, demonstrating that IRL can scale to foundation-model-like regimes. The RHIP framework provides practitioners with a principled knob to trade off compute budget against policy robustness, and the finding that a truncated horizon (H=10) outperforms full MaxEnt on both accuracy and speed challenges the default assumption that more stochastic computation is always better. Open questions include whether IRL-specific batching (merging same-destination trajectories), dynamic ETA integration, and personalization via hierarchical models can yield further gains, and whether the geographic MoE partitioning limits cross-region knowledge sharing in ways that global parameters could address.
