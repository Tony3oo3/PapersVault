## Problem

Graph canonization is known to maximize the ability to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs, yet it has been rarely adopted in GNN design. The paper reveals a fundamental trade-off: while canonization-based positional encodings achieve maximal expressivity, they destroy model stability because similar graphs can receive completely different discrete colorings under the individualization-refinement paradigm used by practical tools like Nauty. This trade-off limits generalization to unseen graphs, especially on large-scale datasets where stability is critical.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph adjacency matrix and one-hot node feature vectors as standard GNN inputs, plus (for UGC-GNN) the entire graph dataset to compute a dataset-level injective node labeling. The discrete canonization coloring is converted to one-hot positional encodings and concatenated with node features before message passing. The readout layer is also canonization-aware, using a weighted summation where each node's weight matrix is indexed by its discrete label, producing a single graph-level embedding for downstream classification or regression.

## Method

GC-GNN uses a practical graph canonization tool (e.g., Nauty) to compute a discrete node ordering ρ(v|G) for each graph, which is fed as one-hot positional encodings into standard message-passing GNNs (GIN, GCN, GAT, GraphSAGE). The paper proves this achieves maximal expressivity (Lemma 2.3) but is unstable under the individualization-refinement paradigm (Lemma 2.4), since a single node recoloring can permute the entire canonical ordering. To resolve this, the paper introduces universal graph canonization: a discrete coloring τ(v|𝔾) conditioned on the whole dataset that guarantees identical labels on any common subgraph across graph pairs, preserving both expressivity and stability (Theorem 3.2). A sufficient condition (Lemma 3.3) makes UGC tractable when an injective labeling exists that preserves edge relations across all graphs in the dataset—satisfied in gene networks, brain graphs, traffic networks, and DAGs. The UGC-GNN further incorporates the discrete coloring into the readout layer via label-indexed weight matrices, and is proven permutation-invariant (Lemma 3.4).

## Results

On the challenging gene network datasets (Mayo, RosMap, Cancer) and brain graph dataset (fMRI-ABIDE), UGC-GNN (GIN) achieves the best accuracy across all four benchmarks: 0.624 (Mayo), 0.701 (RosMap), 0.714 (Cancer), and 0.648 (fMRI-ABIDE), improving SOTA by up to 31% over GNN baselines. On TU graph classification benchmarks, GC-GNN (GraphSAGE) achieves 92.1% on D&D, surpassing the previous best (GIN-AK+ at 80.3%) by 14.7 percentage points, with average improvements of 28.2% over backbone GNNs. On synthetic isomorphism-test datasets (EXP, CSL), GC-GNN (GIN) reaches 99.75% and 96.67% test accuracy, effectively distinguishing 1-WL-indistinguishable graphs that backbone GIN/GCN cannot separate (50% accuracy).

## Baselines & Comparisons

UGC-GNN (GIN) decisively outperforms all GNN baselines on the four bioinformatics benchmarks: on RosMap it beats the strongest baseline ESAN (0.581) by 12.0 points in accuracy, and on Cancer it surpasses GINE (0.629) by 8.5 points. On fMRI-ABIDE, UGC-GNN edges out PPGN (0.633) by 1.5 points in accuracy. On TU datasets, GC-GNN (GraphSAGE) achieves 92.1% on D&D, beating GIN-AK+ (80.3%), ESAN (79.7%), and GraphSNN (79.6%) by 12–14 points, but loses to ESAN (91.0%) and GNN-RNI (90.4%) on MUTAG and to GIN-AK+ (38.5%) on ENZYMES. Notably, GC-GNN with standard graph canonization actually degrades performance on large gene networks (e.g., GIN+GC drops to 0.483 on Mayo vs. 0.496 for plain GIN), empirically confirming the theoretical instability result, while UGC-GNN recovers and surpasses all baselines. On DAG regression (NA/BN), UGC-GNN ties DAGNN on Pearson's r (0.964 on NA) but is outperformed on RMSE (0.753 vs. 0.264), indicating the method's advantage is most pronounced on classification tasks with large-scale graphs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical graph canonization (via tools like Nauty's individualization-refinement paradigm) as a positional encoding mechanism for GNNs, drawing an analogy to Transformer positional encodings (Vaswani et al., 2017). It departs from the dominant expressivity-enhancement strategies (high-order GNNs, subgraph-based GNNs, marking, feature augmentation) by instead achieving maximal discrimination through exact canonical labeling, while connecting to the stability analysis framework of Wang et al. (2022) on equivariant positional encodings. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of 'universal graph canonization,' which conditions the discrete node labeling on the entire dataset to preserve subgraph-level color consistency — a notion absent from prior canonization or GNN literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formal proof that graph canonization maximizes expressivity while provably destroying model stability is a non-trivial theoretical contribution that clarifies a fundamental design tension in GNN architecture. The universal graph canonization provides a clean, widely applicable resolution (satisfied in gene networks, brain graphs, traffic networks, DAGs), yielding up to 31% SOTA improvements and 4–10× speedups over subgraph/high-order GNNs on large-scale bioinformatics tasks. Open questions include extending the sufficient condition to heterogeneous edge types, relaxing it to broader graph families, and whether the dataset-conditioned labeling can be learned end-to-end rather than computed via a fixed injective function.
