## Problem

Standard scaling evaluations of LLMs rely on aggregate metrics like perplexity or downstream accuracy, which cannot capture how down-scaling differentially affects distinct core capabilities. This paper asks: does reducing model size (via pruning or dense scaling) harm fact recall and in-context learning equally, or do these capabilities degrade at different rates? Prior pruning and scaling work has not disentangled these two fundamentally different information-processing mechanisms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241675/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation suite takes three input formats: (1) bare text questions for close-book QA, (2) questions paired with supporting evidence passages (factual or counterfactual) for open-book and overriding QA, and (3) sequences of 32 in-context input-output pairs (4-dimensional integer vectors with values in [-10,10]) followed by a novel query for parameterized function learning tasks. Outputs are either free-text answers scored by exact match against ground truth, or single classification labels (binary or 4-way) generated via greedy decoding. The overriding QA setup (DisentQA) uses made-up answers that contradict pre-training facts, forcing the model to rely on context rather than memorization.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a curated benchmark suite that creates a controlled gradation from pure fact recall to pure in-context learning: close-book QA (weights only), open-book QA (context retrieval), overriding QA (context overrides weights), and parameterized function learning (linear classifiers, 2-layer NNs, decision trees learned from in-context examples). Two scaling techniques are applied: one-shot weight pruning (SparseGPT in the main text, Wanda in the appendix) that removes weights and updates remaining ones without retraining, and dense scaling using independently trained models of varying sizes from the OPT family (all trained on 300B tokens). Six models across three families (OPT-13B/30B, LLaMA-13B/33B, Pythia-12B) are evaluated across 8 tasks, with the key finding that fact recall degrades beyond 5% relative accuracy loss at ~30% sparsity while ICL tasks maintain performance up to 60-70% sparsity. The same disparate pattern holds for dense scaling, where moving from 30B to 13B OPT causes >5% fact recall degradation but ICL accuracy differences between 30B and 1.3B models are under 2%.

## Results

The paper's central finding is a stark asymmetry in how down-scaling affects two core LLM capabilities. For fact recall (close-book QA), pruning beyond 30% sparsity (TriviaQA) or 40% (WebQA) causes a >5% relative accuracy drop, while for in-context learning tasks (linear classification, 2-layer NN, decision tree), the same 5% threshold is not reached until 60%, 60%, and 70% sparsity respectively. Dense scaling exhibits the identical pattern: moving from OPT-30B to OPT-13B causes >5% relative drop on close-book QA, yet the accuracy gap between OPT-30B and OPT-1.3B on ICL tasks is only 1.8% (linear), 1.4% (2-layer NN), and 0.1% (decision tree). Open-book and overriding QA tasks show intermediate robustness, tolerating 50–70% sparsity before the 5% threshold.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates two pruning algorithms—SparseGPT (main) and Wanda (Appendix D)—and finds both exhibit the same disparate effect: moderate pruning hurts fact recall while ICL survives to higher sparsity. Wanda, which does not update remaining weights after removal, underperforms SparseGPT at high sparsities on ICL tasks (e.g., 50% max sparsity vs. 60% for SparseGPT on linear classification), but the qualitative pattern is identical. The paper further compares pruning against dense scaling (independently trained models of different sizes) and finds the same fact-recall-sensitive / ICL-robust pattern, confirming the effect is a property of scaling in general rather than an artifact of pruning. Across model families (OPT, LLaMA, Pythia), the results are consistent: Pythia-12B tolerates 20% sparsity on close-book TriviaQA but 50% on ICL linear classification, mirroring the OPT/LLaMA findings. Structured pruning via LLM-Pruner (Appendix N) also reproduces the pattern in the low-sparsity regime (0–25% local sparsity), with fact recall degrading at 0% local sparsity tolerance versus 20% for ICL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the fact-recall vs. in-context-learning (ICL) dichotomy introduced by Chan et al. (2022a,b) into a concrete evaluation protocol for studying model scaling. It departs from the dominant pruning literature (SparseGPT, Wanda, LLM-Pruner) that assesses compression through aggregate metrics like perplexity or single-task accuracy, instead curating a gradation of QA and learning tasks that progressively shift reliance from in-weights to in-context information. The work also positions itself against Brown et al. (2020)'s scaling hypothesis for ICL and against prior observations that pruning can have subtle effects beyond accuracy (Hooker et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2022), extending those insights to the LLM regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that ICL survives 60–70% parameter removal while fact recall degrades at 30% provides practitioners with a concrete, actionable criterion for routing inference to smaller models on context-heavy tasks, and motivates memory-augmentation architectures that offload fact retrieval to external systems. The fact that the same disparity appears under both pruning and dense scaling suggests it is a structural property of transformer scaling rather than an artifact of a specific compression method. Open questions include a theoretical account of why ICL requires fewer parameters (the authors conjecture a small 'universal gradient descent module'), whether the effect persists for more sophisticated ICL tasks requiring world knowledge, and whether structured or retraining-based pruning changes the picture.
