## Problem

Although Shannon's source coding theorem establishes the equivalence between probabilistic prediction and lossless compression, large language models trained with log-loss have never been systematically evaluated as general-purpose compressors across data modalities. This paper closes that gap by empirically measuring the compression capabilities of foundation models (Chinchilla, Llama 2) on text, image, and audio data, and by using the compression lens to reveal that scaling laws have a hard limit when model parameter size is accounted for in the compressed output.

## Input / Output

All data modalities are converted to raw byte sequences and mapped into the ASCII range [0, 127] (sacrificing 1 bit per byte, tracked in the compression ratio), then chunked into fixed 2048-byte sequences to match the Transformer context window. ImageNet patches are flattened 32×64 grayscale patches; LibriSpeech audio is downsampled from int16 to 1 byte per sample at 16 kHz. The compression output is a lossless bitstream whose size is compared to the raw 2048 bytes; the adjusted rate additionally adds the model's float16 parameter size (2 bytes per parameter) as a two-part code. In the generative direction, a context prefix (e.g., the first 250 pixels of each image row) conditions autoregressive sampling of the remaining bytes.

## Method

The core mechanism pairs a pretrained language model's conditional probabilities ρ(x_i | x_<i) with an arithmetic coder to produce a lossless compressed bitstream, exploiting the fact that the standard log-loss training objective is exactly the compression objective. Conversely, any compressor (e.g., gzip) can be turned into a sequence predictor by defining ρ(x_i | x_<i) = 2^{ℓ_c(x_<i) − ℓ_c(x_<i x_i)}, enabling autoregressive generation from coding lengths. The paper evaluates both raw compression (ignoring model size) and adjusted compression (two-part code including float16 parameters), revealing that Chinchilla 70B compresses ImageNet to 43.4% and LibriSpeech to 16.4%, beating PNG and FLAC respectively, while the adjusted rate shows a critical model-size threshold beyond which compression degrades. Tokenization is analyzed as a pre-compression step, showing that larger BPE vocabularies help small models but hurt large ones, and that in-context compression improves rapidly over the first few hundred bytes of context.

## Results

Chinchilla 70B, trained primarily on text, achieves raw compression rates of 8.3% on enwik9, 48.0% on ImageNet patches, and 21.0% on LibriSpeech audio (all 2048-byte chunks), outperforming all classical compressors on every modality. On ImageNet, it beats the domain-specific PNG compressor (58.5% at ∞ context, 61.7% at 2048 chunks) by ~13 percentage points; on LibriSpeech, it beats FLAC (30.9% at ∞, 30.3% at 2048) by ~10 points. However, when accounting for model parameter size (adjusted rate), Chinchilla 70B's compression rate degrades to 14,008% on enwik9, illustrating that the dataset size imposes a hard ceiling on useful model scale. Smaller Transformers trained on enwik8 overfit to text and perform poorly on image (194–228%) and audio (131–228%) data, confirming that in-context learning in large pretrained models is essential for cross-modal compression.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against gzip, Chinchilla 70B achieves substantially better raw compression on all four datasets: 8.3% vs 48.1% on enwik9, 48.0% vs 68.6% on ImageNet, 21.0% vs 38.5% on LibriSpeech, and 100.8% vs 100.1% on random data (both incompressible, as expected). Against LZMA2, the same pattern holds: 8.3% vs 50.0% (enwik9), 48.0% vs 62.4% (ImageNet), 21.0% vs 38.2% (LibriSpeech). Against the domain-specific PNG compressor, Chinchilla 70B wins decisively on ImageNet (48.0% vs 61.7% at 2048 chunks; 48.0% vs 58.5% at ∞ context) but loses on enwik9 (8.3% vs 80.6%) and LibriSpeech (21.0% vs 37.6%), confirming PNG is tuned for image data. Against FLAC, Chinchilla 70B wins on LibriSpeech (21.0% vs 30.3% at 2048 chunks; 21.0% vs 30.9% at ∞) but loses on enwik9 and ImageNet, mirroring FLAC's audio specialization. The critical caveat is that all adjusted compression rates for the LLMs are catastrophically worse than classical compressors (e.g., 14,008% vs 32.3% for Chinchilla 70B vs gzip on enwik9), because the ~140 GB parameter overhead dominates at 1 GB dataset scale; the paper shows that non-trivial adjusted compression requires datasets in the terabyte range.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the long-standing theoretical equivalence between prediction and lossless compression (Shannon's source coding theorem, arithmetic coding by Pasco and Rissanen) by applying it to modern pretrained foundation models. While prior work has used neural networks for compression (Schmidhuber & Heil 1994; Mahoney 2000; Bellard 2021; Valmeekam et al. 2023), those efforts either trained models from scratch on the target data stream or focused narrowly on text. The key departure here is evaluating *off-the-shelf* pretrained LLMs (Chinchilla, Llama 2) as general-purpose compressors across text, image, and audio modalities, and using the compression lens to re-examine scaling laws, tokenization, and in-context learning in a unified framework rooted in the Solomonoff/Kolmogorov complexity tradition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, quantitative demonstration that LLMs are general-purpose predictors whose cross-modal compression ability reflects genuine in-context learning, not memorization. The scaling-law insight—that adjusted compression rate deteriorates once model parameters outweigh the dataset—gives practitioners a principled upper bound on model size for a given data budget, a constraint invisible to the standard log-loss objective. Key limitations include the 2048-byte context window (severely restricting practical compression), the fact that adjusted rates for 70B models on 1GB data are absurdly poor (>14000%), and the preliminary nature of the compression-as-generation demonstration. Open questions include whether longer-context architectures can make LLM-based compression practically competitive, and whether the in-context compression view can diagnose and fix specific failure modes of foundation models.
