## Problem

In real-world deployment, object detectors are evaluated on unlabeled test sets where ground-truth bounding boxes are unavailable, making it impossible to compute mAP and assess generalization. Existing label-free evaluation (AutoEval) methods designed for classification ignore the regression nature of bounding box prediction and perform poorly on detection tasks. This paper identifies and exploits a previously unexplored signal—bounding box positional stability under feature perturbation—as a proxy for detection accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained detector (e.g., RetinaNet+ResNet-50) and an unlabeled test set of RGB images resized to 800×1333. It produces a dataset-level Box Stability Score (BoS), computed as the mean GIoU-based matching loss between original and dropout-perturbed predictions across all test images, which is then mapped to an estimated mAP via a linear regression model. The BoS score operates at the dataset level rather than per-image or per-box, requiring a statistically sufficient number of test images (≥50) for reliable estimation.

## Method

The core mechanism applies Monte Carlo dropout to backbone feature maps (after stages 1 and 2 at rate 0.15 for RetinaNet+R50), producing a perturbed set of bounding boxes alongside the original predictions. A Hungarian-algorithm bipartite matching with GIoU-based pairwise cost aligns boxes between the two sets, and the BoS score is the average matching loss over all test images. The key empirical finding is that BoS score exhibits a strong positive correlation with mAP (R² > 0.94, Spearman ρ > 0.93) across diverse test environments, because good detectors produce spatially stable boxes under perturbation while poor detectors do not. A simple linear regression (mAP = ω₁·BoS + ω₀) trained on labeled meta-sets (450 sample sets from 9 of 10 source datasets via leave-one-out) then converts BoS into an mAP estimate for unseen test sets, achieving 2.25% RMSE on vehicle detection—outperforming confidence-based baselines by at least 2.21 points.

## Results

On vehicle detection (RetinaNet+R50 trained on COCO, evaluated across 10 unseen test sets), BoS achieves an average RMSE of 2.25% in mAP estimation, outperforming the best baseline ATC (4.46%) by 2.21 points. On pedestrian detection (trained on CrowdHuman, 9 test sets), BoS achieves 3.29% RMSE, again the best among all methods. The underlying correlation between BoS score and mAP is very strong, with R² > 0.94 and Spearman's ρ > 0.93, enabling reliable label-free mAP prediction via a simple linear regression.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BoS outperforms all five baselines on vehicle detection, achieving 2.25% average RMSE versus 4.46% (ATC), 5.26% (PS), 5.52% (ES), 7.02% (FD), and 7.60% (AC). The margin is largest against AC (2.35 points) and FD (4.77 points), while the gap over the strongest baseline ATC is 2.21 points. On pedestrian detection, BoS (3.29%) also leads all baselines, though the margin over ES (3.42%) is narrower. Notably, combining BoS with PS yields a marginal improvement on vehicle detection (2.24% vs 2.25%) but degrades performance on pedestrian detection (3.48% vs 3.29%), suggesting confidence-based signals are not complementary. Under the Swin-T backbone, ATC (3.44%) slightly outperforms BoS (4.69%), which the authors attribute to transformer attention blocks being less susceptible to dropout perturbation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AutoEval (label-free model evaluation) framework, previously developed for image classification (Deng & Zheng, 2021; Garg et al., 2022), to the object detection domain. It repurposes Monte Carlo dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) as a perturbation mechanism and introduces a detection-specific signal—bounding box positional stability measured via bipartite matching and GIoU—rather than the confidence-based statistics (prediction score, entropy, ATC) that dominate prior AutoEval work. The regression-based estimation pipeline (mapping a dataset-level score to mAP via linear regression) directly follows the formulation in Deng & Zheng (2021), while the matching procedure borrows from DETR-style Hungarian assignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to enable label-free mAP estimation for object detectors, opening a practical path for assessing deployed systems in new environments without ground-truth annotations. Key limitations remain: the BoS–mAP correlation degrades when mAP drops below ~15% (too many missed detections to compute stability), multi-class detection shows weaker performance due to inter-category feature interference, and transformer backbones (Swin) exhibit notably weaker correlation than CNN backbones. Open questions include whether other self-supervised pretext signals (e.g., region similarity, DETReg) also correlate with mAP, and whether BoS can inform training-time regularization to improve generalization.
