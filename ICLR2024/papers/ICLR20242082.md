## Problem

Comparing in-processing group fairness methods is hindered by inconsistent experimental settings (reported accuracy on Adult varies by 20% across papers), inaccessible or non-extensible implementations in existing packages like AIF360 and FairLearn, and a lack of systematic analysis of training dynamics and utility-fairness trade-offs. No standardized, open-source benchmark exists that unifies preprocessing, method implementations, and evaluation metrics for fair comparison.

## Input / Output

The benchmark accepts three modalities: tabular feature vectors (e.g., 101 features for Adult, 509 for KDDCensus), 48×48 face images (CelebA, UTKFace), and BERT-encoded text comments (Jigsaw), each paired with one or two binary sensitive attributes (gender, race, age). For each dataset-method-hyperparameter combination, it outputs a binary class prediction scored on 6 utility metrics (acc, AUC, AP, F1, etc.) and 9 fairness metrics (dp, abcc, prule, eodd, eopp, ppv, bnegc, bposc, acc-parity), with all results normalized relative to the ERM baseline to produce comparable utility-fairness trade-off curves across 14 datasets.

## Method

FFB provides a PyTorch-style, single-script benchmarking pipeline that decouples preprocessing, model training, and fairness evaluation into a unified interface, replacing the sklearn-class-inheritance design of AIF360 and FairLearn. It benchmarks seven in-processing methods across three families—gap regularization (DiffDP, DiffEopp, DiffEodd), independence constraints (PRemover, HSIC), and adversarial debiasing (AdvDebias, LAFTR)—using a two-layer MLP for tabular data, ResNet-18/152 for images, and BERT+MLP for text, all trained with Adam (lr=0.01) and StepLR decay (×0.1 every 50 steps, stopped at lr<1e-5). Across 45,079 experiments (14,428 GPU hours), the key findings are: (1) not all widely used datasets stably exhibit bias, (2) HSIC achieves the best utility-fairness trade-off overall, (3) trade-offs are controllable via the fairness hyperparameter for all methods except adversarial debiasing, and (4) fairness training curves are significantly less stable than utility curves, with LAFTR being the most stable.

## Results

Across 45,079 experiments (14,428 GPU hours) on 14 datasets with 6 utility and 9 fairness metrics, the benchmark reveals that HSIC achieves the best utility-fairness trade-off overall, particularly on tabular data, while adversarial debiasing (AdvDebias) exhibits significant instability with scattered utility-fairness points. The ERM baseline on Adult (Gender) shows 85.35% accuracy with 16.67% demographic parity violation, while KDDCensus (Race) shows only 1.35% dp violation, indicating it is unsuitable for fairness assessment. Utility-fairness trade-offs are controllable via hyperparameters for all methods except AdvDebias, and stopping training when the learning rate drops below 1e-5 yields stable fairness metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark compares six in-processing fairness methods (DiffDP/DiffEopp/DiffEodd, PRemover, HSIC, AdvDebias, LAFTR) against the ERM baseline across tabular, image, and text data. HSIC consistently achieves the best utility-fairness trade-off, particularly on tabular data where it improves fairness metrics (dp, abcc) with minimal utility loss, outperforming PRemover and the gap regularization methods (DiffDP, DiffEopp, DiffEodd). AdvDebias exhibits the worst behavior: its utility-fairness points are scattered randomly across the trade-off frontier, indicating inherent instability that makes it difficult to control via its hyperparameter. LAFTR shows the most stable fairness training curves (lowest standard deviation) but does not dominate on the final utility-fairness trade-off. PRemover and the Diff-family methods show controllable trade-offs where larger hyperparameters yield better fairness at the cost of utility, while AdvDebias resists such control. On image data (CelebA, UTKFace), HSIC's advantage diminishes relative to tabular data, and all methods show larger fairness violations (e.g., CelebA-A Gender dp reaches 52.39% under ERM).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing a unified, extensible benchmarking framework for in-processing group fairness methods, departing from existing fairness packages (AIF360, FairLearn) that use Scikit-learn API design with complicated class inheritance, making them difficult to modify or extend for research. It extends the prior benchmarking effort of Reddy et al. (2021), which only covered adversarial learning methods on limited datasets, by encompassing three method families (gap regularization, independence constraints, adversarial debiasing) across 14 datasets spanning tabular, image, and text modalities. The paper also introduces a novel bias-examination step that systematically evaluates whether widely-used fairness datasets actually and stably exhibit the bias they are assumed to contain, a dimension absent from prior benchmarking work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The standardized preprocessing and unified evaluation pipeline directly addresses the reproducibility crisis in fairness research, where the same dataset (e.g., Adult) shows accuracy varying by 20% across papers due to inconsistent splits and preprocessing. The finding that several widely-used datasets do not stably exhibit bias challenges the field's default dataset choices and calls for more rigorous dataset selection in fairness studies. Open questions remain: the benchmark is limited to binary classification with binary sensitive attributes, excludes pre- and post-processing methods, and the authors acknowledge that when ground truth is biased, traditional utility metrics become problematic. The practical finding that a simple learning-rate-decay stopping strategy stabilizes fairness metrics offers an immediately actionable technique for practitioners.
