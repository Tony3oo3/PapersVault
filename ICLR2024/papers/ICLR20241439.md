## Problem

Combinatorial multi-armed bandit (CMAB) literature has primarily studied semi-bandit feedback (all arm values revealed) or full-bandit feedback (only the aggregate reward), but a natural intermediate feedback structure—revealing only the maximum value and the identity of the arm achieving it—remains unexplored. This 'max value-index feedback' is much weaker than semi-bandit feedback, making it difficult to learn about non-winning arms, and the nonlinear maximum-value reward function further complicates the problem since high-variance arms can outperform stable ones even with equal means.

## Input / Output

Each round, the agent selects a subset of k arms from a pool of n arms whose outcomes follow independent discrete distributions with finite (possibly unknown) support. The feedback per round is a single pair: the maximum outcome value and the index of the arm that achieved it. The algorithm outputs a selected k-arm set per round and guarantees a distribution-dependent regret of O(nk/Δ · log T) and a distribution-independent regret of Õ(√(nkT)), matching the order of bounds achievable under the more informative semi-bandit feedback.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a CUCB variant that introduces 'biased arm replacement': when an arm's value v_i has not yet been observed, instead of conservatively freezing its UCB at 1 (which would cause bad arms to be over-selected), the algorithm replaces the arm's parameters with biased values (p_i' = p_i·v_i, v_i' = 1) so that the arm is treated as always triggered with a zero outcome. This introduces a controlled replacement error that is provably dominated by the estimation error, yielding the same regret order as the known-ordering case. The analysis relies on a new RTPM (relative triggering probability modulated) smoothness condition for the max-value reward function, which underweights estimation errors for arms with low triggering probability. For general finite-support distributions, the paper reduces the problem to the binary case via a conversion formula (Eq. 4.1) and handles unknown support sizes with a fictitious placeholder arm, using a k-MAX PTAS as the offline oracle.

## Results

The paper establishes a distribution-dependent regret bound of O((nk/Δ) log(T)) and a distribution-independent regret bound of Õ(√(nkT)) for the k-MAX bandit under max value-index feedback, matching the bounds previously achievable only under the more informative semi-bandit feedback. In experiments with n=9 arms, k=3, and T=5000 (averaged over 20 runs), Algorithm 3.1 achieves significantly lower cumulative regret than both UCB and DART baselines across three distribution scenarios (D1–D3), while its regret curve closely tracks that of the standard CUCB algorithm under semi-bandit feedback despite receiving substantially less information per round.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Algorithm 3.1 against three baselines: (1) a standard UCB algorithm treating each k-set as a single arm, (2) the CUCB algorithm for semi-bandit CMAB (Chen et al., 2013), and (3) DART (Agarwal et al., 2021b) for full-bandit CMAB. Algorithm 3.1 achieves significantly lower cumulative regret than both UCB and DART across all three tested distribution scenarios (D1: base case, D2: stable low-value arm, D3: high-risk high-reward arm). Notably, its regret curve closely aligns with CUCB under semi-bandit feedback, demonstrating that the weaker max value-index feedback incurs negligible additional regret. DART performs particularly poorly because its arm-ordering assumption (ordering by mean reward) is violated in the k-MAX setting, and it requires over 10,000 rounds to converge to a useful threshold, whereas Algorithm 3.1 correctly orders arms by 5,000 rounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CUCB algorithm (Chen et al., 2013) and the CMAB-T framework (Wang & Chen, 2017) to a new intermediate feedback structure called max value-index feedback, which sits between semi-bandit and full-bandit feedback. The key departure from prior work is the introduction of biased arm replacement: when an arm's value is unobserved, the algorithm replaces it with a fictitious arm having value 1 and adjusted probability, rather than conservatively waiting for observation. The RTPM smoothness condition generalizes the TPM smoothness from Wang & Chen (2017) by incorporating arm-specific triggering probabilities and value weights, and the improved k-dependency in Appendix E.6 leverages the TPVM condition from Liu et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that semi-bandit-comparable regret is achievable under much weaker max value-index feedback is practically significant for recommender systems and portfolio selection where only the clicked/selected item's value is observed. The biased arm replacement technique is a general-purpose tool that the authors suggest could apply to other CMAB problems under intermediate feedback structures. Open questions include extending to full-bandit feedback (where even the winning index is hidden), other reward functions like second-max or soft-max, and continuous distributions. The current analysis requires finite support and distinct values, and the regret bound carries larger constants than the semi-bandit case.
