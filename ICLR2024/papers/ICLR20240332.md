## Problem

The widely-used uniformity metric from Wang & Isola (2020), based on the log of average pairwise Gaussian potential, is the standard tool for evaluating representation quality in self-supervised learning. However, this paper shows it is sensitive to sample replications and fundamentally unable to detect feature redundancy or dimensional collapse, making it an unreliable diagnostic for representation quality. The paper formalizes four principled properties an effective uniformity metric should satisfy and demonstrates the existing metric violates three of them.

## Input / Output

The method takes a batch of normalized feature representations (vectors in R^m) produced by a self-supervised encoder and computes a scalar uniformity loss. The computation requires only the sample mean vector and sample covariance matrix of the batch, making it a lightweight auxiliary term that can be added to any existing SSL objective without architectural changes. The loss value is minimized during training, with a linearly decaying weight α_t scheduled over epochs to balance its influence across training stages.

## Method

The paper proposes a new uniformity loss based on the quadratic Wasserstein distance between the empirical distribution of learned representations and a target isotropic Gaussian N(0, I_m/m), which approximates the uniform spherical distribution as dimension m grows (Theorem 2). Under a Gaussian hypothesis for the representations, the closed-form Wasserstein distance depends only on the sample mean μ̂ and covariance Σ̂: W₂ = √(||μ̂||² + 1 + tr(Σ̂) − (2/√m)·tr(Σ̂^{1/2})). The paper proves this metric satisfies all four desired properties—instance permutation invariance, instance cloning invariance, feature cloning sensitivity, and feature baby sensitivity—while the existing metric satisfies only the first. The loss is integrated as an auxiliary penalty with linearly decaying weight into MoCo v2, BYOL, BarlowTwins, and Zero-CL, consistently improving downstream linear-probe accuracy on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 while reducing the number of collapsed dimensions visible in singular value spectra.

## Results

Integrating the proposed Wasserstein uniformity loss (W2) as an auxiliary term consistently improves downstream linear-probe accuracy across all tested self-supervised methods. The largest gains appear on CIFAR-100: MoCo v2 + W2 reaches 63.68% Top-1 (vs. 60.27% baseline, +3.41) and BYOL + W2 reaches 65.16% (vs. 63.66%, +1.50). On CIFAR-10, MoCo v2 + W2 achieves 91.41% Top-1 (+0.76) and BYOL + W2 achieves 90.31% (+0.78). The W2 loss also dramatically reduces the Wasserstein uniformity value (e.g., MoCo v2 drops from 1.06 to 0.33 on CIFAR-10), confirming improved representation uniformity, while slightly increasing the alignment loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper applies its Wasserstein uniformity loss (W2) as an auxiliary term to four of the eight baselines and compares against both the unmodified baseline and the variant using Wang & Isola's original L_U loss. MoCo v2 + W2 outperforms MoCo v2 + L_U by +0.43% on CIFAR-10 and +2.47% on CIFAR-100, with a far larger reduction in W2 (0.33 vs. 0.98). BYOL + W2 beats BYOL + L_U by +0.22% on CIFAR-10 and +2.48% on CIFAR-100, and notably BYOL + L_U actually degrades CIFAR-100 accuracy (62.68% vs. 63.66% baseline) while BYOL + W2 improves it. BarlowTwins + W2 edges out BarlowTwins + L_U by +0.05% on CIFAR-10 and +0.06% on CIFAR-100, with both showing modest gains over the base model. Zero-CL + W2 slightly outperforms Zero-CL + L_U on both datasets, whereas Zero-CL + L_U actually decreases CIFAR-10 accuracy by 0.07%. The remaining baselines (SimCLR, NNCLR, SimSiam, AlignUniform) serve as reference points without the W2 loss; AlignUniform (which uses L_U natively) achieves 90.37% on CIFAR-10, below MoCo v2 + W2's 91.41%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly builds on and departs from Wang & Isola's (2020) alignment-uniformity framework for evaluating self-supervised representations. While Wang & Isola introduced a uniformity metric based on the logarithm of average pairwise Gaussian potential, this work shows that metric is fundamentally blind to dimensional collapse and feature redundancy—two critical failure modes in self-supervised learning. The authors replace it with a quadratic Wasserstein distance between the empirical representation distribution and an isotropic Gaussian approximation of the uniform spherical distribution, drawing on optimal transport theory. The new metric is then integrated as an auxiliary loss into a broad family of existing self-supervised methods (MoCo, BYOL, BarlowTwins, Zero-CL), positioning the contribution as a drop-in diagnostic and regularizer rather than a new training paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the widely-adopted Wang & Isola uniformity metric cannot detect dimensional collapse is a meaningful correction to a tool that has been used extensively to diagnose representation quality in self-supervised learning. The Wasserstein-based metric offers a principled, closed-form alternative that provably satisfies four natural invariance and sensitivity properties, and empirically reduces collapsed coordinates in singular value spectra. However, the authors themselves note that the four properties may not fully characterize an ideal uniformity metric, and all experiments are limited to CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet-18, leaving open whether the gains and the Gaussian approximation hold at ImageNet scale or with transformer backbones. The modest accuracy improvements (0.27–3.41 points) suggest the metric is more valuable as a diagnostic and collapse-mitigation tool than as a performance booster.
