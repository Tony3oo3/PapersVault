## Problem

Reward learning algorithms lack principled ways to quantify how different a learned reward function is from the true one, making it impossible to know in advance when a learned reward is safe to optimize. Existing pseudometrics (EPIC, DARD) fail to provide both upper and lower bounds on worst-case regret, meaning they are either too loose or too tight, and their failure modes cannot be anticipated theoretically.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240321/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given two reward functions over a finite MDP (states, actions, transition function, initial state distribution, discount factor), STARC produces a single scalar pseudometric distance. The distance is zero if and only if the two rewards induce the same policy ordering, and it is invariant to potential shaping, S'-redistribution, and positive linear scaling. The same distance simultaneously provides both an upper and a lower bound on worst-case regret, normalized by the total reward range of the reference reward function.

## Method

STARC metrics are constructed in three composable steps: (1) a linear canonicalisation function c maps rewards differing by potential shaping and S'-redistribution to a common representative (e.g., the VAL function, which subtracts a fixed policy's value function and averages over next states); (2) the canonicalised reward is normalised by dividing by a norm n on the image of c, removing positive linear scaling; (3) an admissible metric m (bilipschitz equivalent to a norm) measures the distance between the two standardised rewards. The key theoretical result is that every STARC metric is both sound (small distance implies small worst-case regret) and complete (small worst-case regret implies small distance), and any two pseudometrics satisfying both properties are bilipschitz equivalent, establishing a form of uniqueness. Empirically, the VAL-based STARC metric achieves a 0.856 correlation with regret on random MDPs, outperforming EPIC (0.778) and DARD (0.782).

## Results

On 49,152 reward function pairs across random MDPs (32 states, 4 actions, γ=0.95), the STARC-VAL metric achieves a correlation of 0.856 with worst-case regret using L₂ for both normalisation and distance, improving to 0.873 with L₁. This substantially outperforms EPIC (0.778) and DARD (0.782) under the same L₂ setting. In the continuous Reacher environment (MuJoCo), STARC-VAL correctly ranks hand-crafted reward functions by expected similarity to GroundTruth, with distances ranging from 0.897 (PotentialShaped, true distance 0) to 1.706 (Negative).

## Baselines & Comparisons

STARC-VAL outperforms both EPIC and DARD on the primary empirical metric (correlation with worst-case regret): 0.856 vs. 0.778 (EPIC) and 0.782 (DARD) under the same L₂ setting, a gap of roughly 0.07–0.08. The advantage is robust: across 42 combinations of normalisation and distance metric, VAL canonicalisation beats EPIC canonicalisation in 40 cases, and 8 of the top 10 pseudometrics overall use VAL. Theoretically, the gap is even sharper: STARC metrics are both sound (upper bound on regret) and complete (lower bound on regret), while EPIC is neither sound nor complete, and DARD is not complete (Proposition 8). This means any pseudometric with the same guarantees as STARC must be bilipschitz equivalent to it, making STARC essentially unique in its class. On the Reacher environment, STARC-VAL correctly orders all five hand-crafted rewards by expected similarity, though absolute distances are inflated by sampling noise (e.g., PotentialShaped estimated at 0.897 vs. true distance 0).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reward-function distance metrics of Gleave et al. (2020, EPIC) and Wulfe et al. (2022, DARD) by unifying them under a single framework (STARC) that canonicalises for both potential shaping and S′-redistribution, rather than potential shaping alone. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of completeness (a lower bound on worst-case regret) alongside soundness (an upper bound), which prior metrics lacked, and the use of the policy-value range (max J − min J) as the normalisation term rather than the L₂-norm of the raw reward. The bilipschitz-equivalence/uniqueness theorem positions STARC as essentially the unique family of pseudometrics satisfying both properties, closing a gap that neither EPIC nor DARD addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STARC provides a principled, cheap-to-compute, closed-form metric for evaluating reward-learning algorithms that is both theoretically tight (small distance is necessary and sufficient for low regret) and empirically better correlated with actual regret than existing alternatives. The uniqueness result implies the field should not expect substantially better reward-distance metrics, redirecting effort toward applying STARC to concrete reward-learning evaluations. Open questions include generalising the finite-state/action theory to continuous environments, identifying which specific STARC variant (choice of c, n, m) performs best in practice, and extending the framework beyond single-agent MDPs to multi-agent settings.
