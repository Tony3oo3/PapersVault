## Problem

No public fairness dataset exists for medical image segmentation, while all existing medical fairness benchmarks target classification tasks. This gap is critical because segmentation provides spatially detailed organ delineation essential for clinical decision-making, yet fairness in segmentation across demographic groups remains unexplored. Additionally, standard fairness metrics designed for classification (e.g., demographic parity difference) do not naturally extend to pixel-wise segmentation evaluation.

## Input / Output

The model takes 2D SLO fundus images (RGB, H×W×C) as input, each paired with six sensitive attributes (race, gender, ethnicity, preferred language, age, marital status) collected from real clinical records. The output is a pixel-wise segmentation mask at the same spatial resolution with three classes: background, optic disc, and optic cup. Ground-truth masks are not manually annotated but derived by registering 3D OCT-derived disc/cup boundaries onto 2D SLO fundus images via NiftyReg, followed by expert verification, yielding 10,000 subject-level samples (8,000 train / 2,000 test).

## Method

The paper introduces Fair Error-Bound Scaling (FEBS), a loss-reweighting mechanism that computes a per-group weight W_a = tanh((min_group_loss / group_a_loss)^γ) and multiplies predicted pixel scores by this weight before computing Dice loss, thereby up-weighting hard cases in underperforming demographic groups. This is integrated into two segmentation backbones: SAMed (SAM image encoder fine-tuned with LoRA on medical data) and TransUNet (ViT-B + U-Net), both trained with a combined cross-entropy + Dice loss under AdamW. For evaluation, the authors propose the Equity-Scaled Segmentation Performance (ESSP) metric, defined as the overall segmentation metric (Dice or IoU) divided by (1 + Δ), where Δ is the sum of absolute deviations between overall and per-group metric values, penalizing inter-group disparity while preserving interpretability. The approach is benchmarked against adversarial fairness (ADV) and GroupDRO across four sensitive attributes, with FEBS achieving the best or comparable equity-scaled scores in most settings.

## Results

On the Harvard-FairSeg dataset (10,000 SLO fundus images), SAMed achieves the highest overall Dice of 0.8671 for cup segmentation and 0.8291 for rim segmentation. With race as the sensitive attribute, SAMed+FEBS attains the best equity-scaled Dice (ES-Dice) of 0.7529 and ES-IoU of 0.6451 for rim segmentation, outperforming SAMed+GroupDRO (0.7509) and SAMed+ADV (0.7394). For cup segmentation, SAMed+ADV leads in ES-Dice (0.8591), while SAMed+FEBS remains competitive at 0.8566. TransUNet-based methods consistently underperform SAMed by roughly 2–4 Dice points across all fairness variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four fairness strategies (none, ADV, GroupDRO, FEBS) on two segmentation backbones (SAMed, TransUNet). SAMed [Zhang & Liu, 2023] consistently outperforms TransUNet [Chen et al., 2021] by 2–4 Dice points across all settings, confirming the advantage of the SAM-based encoder for medical segmentation. For rim segmentation with race, SAMed+FEBS achieves the best ES-Dice (0.7529) and ES-IoU (0.6451), narrowly beating SAMed+GroupDRO (0.7509 / 0.6427) and clearly surpassing SAMed+ADV (0.7394 / 0.6300), which actually degrades fairness relative to the unmodified SAMed. For cup segmentation, SAMed+ADV edges out FEBS in ES-Dice (0.8591 vs. 0.8566), while GroupDRO is in between (0.8582), indicating FEBS is competitive but not uniformly dominant on the easier cup task. With TransUNet as the backbone, FEBS recovers the ES-Dice gap seen with ADV and GroupDRO for rim (0.7050 vs. 0.7000 and 0.7002), suggesting the error-bound scaling is particularly effective when the base model is weaker. Overall, the paper positions FEBS as a 'superior or comparable' fairness method rather than a clear winner across every attribute and structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of fairness learning and medical image segmentation, a combination the authors explicitly identify as absent from prior literature. It repurposes established fairness techniques (adversarial debiasing à la Madras et al., GroupDRO à la Sagawa et al.) from their native classification setting into a segmentation context, and introduces FEBS as a loss-reweighting mechanism inspired by the intuition that hard cases within underperforming demographic groups drive inequity. The annotation pipeline is a practical departure from prior fairness datasets: rather than relying on manual pixel-wise labels, it transfers OCT-derived 3D disc/cup boundaries onto 2D SLO fundus images via NiftyReg registration, sidestepping the labor bottleneck that has limited prior medical segmentation datasets. The equity-scaled metric draws on the fair identity normalization idea from Luo et al. (2023c) but reformulates it as a multiplicative penalty on standard segmentation scores.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first public benchmark for fairness in medical segmentation, Harvard-FairSeg fills a concrete gap: clinicians need spatially precise, group-equitable segmentation for tasks like glaucoma screening, yet no prior dataset supported this evaluation. The equity-scaled metric (ES-Dice, ES-IoU) offers a more interpretable fairness signal than DPD or DEOdds for segmentation, which is practically useful. However, the scope is narrow (single ophthalmic modality, 10K samples, one clinical task), the FEBS method yields only marginal gains over GroupDRO in most settings, and the OCT-to-SLO annotation transfer (≈80% registration success) may introduce systematic geometric biases that are hard to audit. Open questions include whether the fairness gap persists at larger scale, whether FEBS generalizes to 3D segmentation or other organ systems, and whether the equity-scaled metric's sensitivity to group size imbalances (e.g., 92% English speakers) could mask real disparities in small subgroups.
