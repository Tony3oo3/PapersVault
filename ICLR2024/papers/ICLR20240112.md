## Problem

While predictive auxiliary objectives are widely used in deep RL to regularize representations, it remains unclear how these objectives interact with value learning to shape representations across different network modules, and whether the resulting representational dynamics can explain multi-region neural phenomena observed in the brain. This paper asks whether a unified deep RL system with predictive auxiliary objectives can simultaneously model representational changes in hippocampus, striatum, and visual cortex, providing an interpretable framework for multi-region brain computation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240112/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a 2D birds-eye-view image of its position in a gridworld arena as observation, optionally augmented with a decaying temporal trace of recent observations (o_t + α·o_{t-1} + α²·o_{t-2} + …) to support memory-dependent tasks. The CNN encoder produces a latent state z of variable dimensionality |z|, which feeds two MLP heads: a Q-network outputting state-action values for action selection, and a prediction network T(z,a) outputting a delta vector that, when added to z, approximates the next latent state. The prediction horizon γ in the positive loss term controls how many future steps the prediction network must anticipate.

## Method

The architecture is a double deep Q-learning network augmented with a contrastive predictive coding auxiliary loss, trained end-to-end on replayed transitions. A convolutional encoder E maps 2D observations to latent states z; a Q-learning MLP selects actions, and a separate prediction MLP T(z,a) learns to predict future latent states via a positive sampling loss ‖τ(z_t,a_t) − z_{t+1} − γτ(z_{t+1},a_{t+1})‖² (where τ(z,a) = z + T(z,a)) and a negative sampling loss that pushes apart randomly sampled latent states. The key design choice is that the prediction network shares the encoder with the value network, so learning to predict transitions directly shapes the encoder's representational geometry. The paper systematically varies |z|, encoder/decoder depth, and prediction horizon γ to characterize when predictive objectives prevent representational collapse, improve transfer, and produce hippocampus-like place fields, forward-shifted receptive fields, and trial-type splitting patterns.

## Results

On the 8×8 gridworld foraging task (|z|=10), all three model variants (MF-only, negative-sampling-only, full predictive loss) reach maximum episode score of 1.0, but the predictive model converges in fewer training steps. When the latent bottleneck is reduced, the predictive auxiliary objective becomes critical: the MF-only and negative-sampling-only models fail to learn at small |z|, while the predictive model still achieves strong performance. For goal-transfer (new reward location), longer predictive horizons (γ=0.8) yield faster fine-tuning of the value network than one-step prediction (γ=0.0), with the advantage growing at larger latent sizes. In the transition-transfer setting (scrambled connectivity), predictive objectives actually *hurt* performance relative to negative-sampling-only, particularly under more on-policy training (lower ε).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three model variants: (1) MF-only (standard double DQN, no auxiliary loss), (2) negative-sampling-only (L− only, encouraging state separation without transition structure), and (3) full predictive loss (L+ + L−, learning transition structure). On the primary gridworld task, the full predictive model outperforms both baselines in convergence speed and in resource-limited settings (small |z|, shallow encoder/decoder). The negative-sampling-only model improves over MF-only by preventing representational collapse (fewer silent units, lower cosine similarity between states) but does not capture environment structure, so it cannot match the predictive model's advantage at small bottlenecks. For goal-transfer, all predictive models (γ > 0) beat the γ=0 baseline, with γ=0.8 showing the largest gain. For transition-transfer, the comparison reverses: the negative-sampling-only model outperforms all predictive variants, because the learned transition structure becomes a liability when the environment dynamics change. In the neuroscience-mimicking experiments (T-maze splitting, visual cortex selectivity), the predictive model uniquely reproduces phenomena (end-of-corridor splitting, reward-biased selectivity with t=−12.43, p=9×10⁻³⁶) that the MF-only baseline fails to capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the architecture of François-Lavet et al. (2019), which combined a DQN with a predictive auxiliary head, and on the contrastive predictive coding loss of Oord et al. (2018) for the auxiliary objective. Its conceptual departure is in repurposing this standard multi-objective RL system as an interpretable multi-region brain model, mapping the encoder to visual cortex, the Q-network to striatum, and the prediction network to hippocampus. Prior neuroscience modeling work has used multi-region RNNs or switching dynamical systems to fit recorded activity, but this paper instead takes a normative, end-to-end-trained deep RL perspective to study how interacting objectives shape representations across modules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper proposes a reframing of the hippocampus's role: rather than primarily supporting planning or sequence generation, predictive learning in hippocampus may serve as an auxiliary system that structures representations for other brain regions, paralleling how predictive auxiliary objectives in deep RL regularize the shared encoder. Practically, the finding that predictive objectives prevent representational collapse in resource-limited architectures and that longer horizons improve transfer provides actionable guidance for RL practitioners. Limitations include the simplicity of the tasks (gridworld, 1D track, T-maze), the lack of analysis of the value-learning network's representations (the striatum analog), and the absence of recurrence across modules, all of which constrain the strength of the neuroscience claims.
