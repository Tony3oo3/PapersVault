## Problem

Off-policy RL methods based on dynamic programming (e.g., Q-learning, CQL) require Bellman completeness of the function class to converge under function approximation, a condition that is extremely difficult to satisfy and cannot be fixed by simply increasing model size. Meanwhile, return-conditioned supervised learning (RCSL) avoids Bellman completeness but fundamentally cannot stitch together sub-trajectories from different dataset trajectories to synthesize better-than-dataset behavior. The paper asks whether one can design an off-policy method that retains RCSL's freedom from Bellman completeness while recovering the trajectory-stitching capability of DP.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed offline trajectory dataset (potentially suboptimal) and, at deployment, takes the current state and a desired return-to-go (RTG) scalar to produce an action. Internally, the pipeline first learns a dynamics model and a multimodal behavior policy (e.g., diffusion policy) from the dataset via maximum likelihood, then generates a rollout dataset by sampling the behavior policy on the learned model and retaining only trajectories whose predicted return exceeds the maximum return in the original dataset. The final return-conditioned policy is trained via supervised learning (MSE or MLE) on this filtered rollout dataset, so the output policy is conditioned on RTG values that may be higher than any observed in the raw data.

## Method

MBRCSL decomposes trajectory stitching into three independently trained supervised-learning components: (1) a dynamics model (Gaussian ensemble or transformer) predicting next state and reward, (2) a multimodal behavior policy (diffusion policy) capturing the full action distribution in the dataset, and (3) a return-conditioned output policy (MLP or autoregressive model) mapping (state, RTG) to actions. The key mechanism is forward simulation: the behavior policy is rolled out on the learned dynamics model from initial states in the dataset, and only trajectories whose total return exceeds the dataset maximum are kept, effectively stitching together segments from distinct original trajectories without any Bellman backup. Theoretically, the paper proves via a constructed LinearQ MDP family that a two-layer MLP Q-network needs Ω(|S|) hidden neurons for Bellman completeness while a constant-width MLP suffices for RCSL to represent the optimal policy, and separately proves (via two distinct constructions) that no Markovian RCSL or decision transformer can stitch trajectories even under uniform coverage and deterministic transitions.

## Results

On the Point Maze stitching task, MBRCSL achieves a mean return of 91.5±7.1, substantially exceeding the dataset's maximum return of 71.8, demonstrating successful trajectory stitching. On three simulated robotic tasks (PickPlace, ClosedDrawer, BlockedDrawer), MBRCSL achieves success rates of 0.48±0.04, 0.51±0.12, and 0.68±0.09 respectively, while model-based baselines COMBO and RCSL baseline DT both achieve 0% success across all three tasks. The method also outperforms behavior cloning (BC) on all robotics tasks, confirming that the return-conditioned output policy extracts better-than-average behavior from rollouts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MBRCSL outperforms all five baselines across every evaluated task. Against CQL (model-free, conservative Q-learning), MBRCSL achieves 91.5 vs. 34.8 return on Point Maze and 0.48–0.68 vs. 0.11–0.34 success rate on robotics tasks, with CQL suffering high variance (±0.23–0.35) attributed to Bellman completeness violations. Against COMBO (model-based, conservative), MBRCSL wins decisively: 91.5 vs. 56.6 on Point Maze and 0.48–0.68 vs. 0% on all three robotics tasks, where COMBO's inaccurate model rollouts lead to complete failure. Against MOPO and MOReL (both model-based), MBRCSL achieves 91.5 vs. 77.9 and 26.4 respectively on Point Maze. Against Decision Transformer (RCSL baseline), MBRCSL achieves 91.5 vs. 57.2 on Point Maze and 0.48–0.68 vs. 0% on robotics, confirming that standard RCSL cannot stitch trajectories while MBRCSL's model-based augmentation enables it. The behavior cloning baseline (BC) is competitive on BlockedDrawer (0.61 vs. 0.68) but lags on PickPlace (0.07 vs. 0.48), showing that the return-conditioned policy in MBRCSL selectively extracts high-return rollouts rather than averaging over all behaviors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the return-conditioned supervised learning (RCSL) framework (Schmidhuber, 2019; Kumar et al., 2019b; Decision Transformer, Chen et al., 2021) by identifying its fundamental limitation—incapability to stitch trajectories—and proposing MBRCSL, which augments RCSL with model-based rollouts from a learned dynamics model and multimodal behavior policy. It departs from DP-based offline RL methods (CQL, MOPO, COMBO, MOReL) by showing they require Bellman completeness for convergence under function approximation, a condition that is difficult to satisfy and cannot be fixed by simply increasing model size. The theoretical analysis builds on prior work establishing Bellman completeness as a necessary condition for DP-based methods (Munos & Szepesvári, 2008; Zanette et al., 2020) and on Brandfonbrener et al. (2022)'s intuitive (but non-rigorous) counterexample showing RCSL cannot stitch, which this paper formalizes into two theorems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for why RCSL methods empirically outperform DP-based offline RL in many settings: they only need realizability, not Bellman completeness. The LinearQ construction cleanly separates the two requirements, showing constant-width MLPs suffice for RCSL while Q-learning needs linearly growing width. MBRCSL offers a practical, modular framework that achieves trajectory stitching without Bellman completeness, outperforming SOTA offline RL baselines on simulated robotics tasks. Key open questions include extending MBRCSL to stochastic environments (where forward sampling from a learned model is less reliable) and scaling to larger, more complex domains, both of which the authors acknowledge as significant limitations.
