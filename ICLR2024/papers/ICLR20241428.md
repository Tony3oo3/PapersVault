## Problem

While language models can solve binding tasks—associating entities with their attributes from in-context information—the internal representational mechanism remains unknown. Unlike factual recall, which is stored in model weights, binding must be represented in activations since the associations are established only within the context. This paper asks: how do LMs encode and retrieve these entity-attribute bindings in their internal representations?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241428/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The binding task provides a text context containing n entity-attribute pairs (e.g., 'Alice lives in the capital city of France. Bob lives in the capital city of Thailand.') followed by a query about one entity's attribute; the model outputs log probabilities over the vocabulary, scored via median-calibrated accuracy to control for position-dependent bias. Beyond standard prediction, the paper extracts binding ID difference vectors from stacked layer activations at entity and attribute token positions, then performs causal interventions—swapping, permuting, or adding vectors to these activations—to probe the representational structure. The primary experimental outputs are intervention accuracy tables and geometric plots of the binding subspace.

## Method

The paper identifies the binding ID mechanism: LMs assign abstract binding IDs to entity-attribute pairs such that co-bound entities and attributes share the same ID, and the model retrieves answers by matching binding IDs rather than positions. This is verified through causal mediation analysis demonstrating two key properties—factorizability (swapping an attribute activation changes only that entity's binding) and position independence (permuting activation positions leaves beliefs unchanged). The binding functions are shown to be additive: Γ_A(a,k) = f_A(a) + b_A(k) and Γ_E(e,k) = f_E(e) + b_E(k), meaning binding IDs are vectors that can be extracted via activation differences and manipulated arithmetically. These binding ID vectors occupy a continuous subspace with a metric structure where distances reflect discriminability, and they transfer across different binding tasks (CAPITALS, PARALLEL, SHAPES, FRUITS) and model families (LLaMA, Pythia), with fidelity increasing with scale. The mechanism is not universal: a multiple-choice question-answering task instead uses a 'direct binding' mechanism where labels are bound directly to option text without an abstract ID.

## Results

The paper verifies the binding ID mechanism through causal interventions on LLaMA-30b. Mean interventions using extracted binding ID difference vectors reverse model beliefs (accuracy drops from >99% to <3% for single-side interventions, and recovers to >97% for both-side interventions), while random vectors of the same magnitude have no effect. Binding vectors transfer across tasks: applying CAPITALS-derived vectors to PARALLEL, SHAPES, and FRUITS tasks yields 0.87, 0.71, and 0.80 median-calibrated accuracy respectively, far above the 0.30 (zeros) and 0.31 (random) baselines. The mechanism is present in every sufficiently large model in both the LLaMA and Pythia families, with fidelity increasing with scale, and extends to three-term binding with near-perfect intervention accuracy (≥0.99 in most conditions).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an interpretability paper rather than a method comparison, so the 'baselines' are control conditions and null interventions. The key comparison is between binding ID vectors extracted from one task and two null baselines (zero vectors and randomly oriented vectors of the same magnitude). Binding ID vectors from CAPITALS transfer to PARALLEL (0.87), SHAPES (0.71), and FRUITS (0.80) accuracy, while zeros and random vectors both yield chance-level performance (0.30 and 0.31), confirming that the specific geometric structure of binding vectors matters. For the additivity test (Table 1), the extracted difference vectors produce near-complete belief reversal (0.00–0.03 accuracy) versus >99% control, while random vectors leave accuracy unchanged at ~0.98–1.00. The BIOS task (multi-token attributes) shows degraded transfer (0.47), consistent with the paper's note that attribute factorizability fails when attributes span multiple tokens. The MCQ task (SST-2) is identified as a counterexample where a 'direct binding' mechanism replaces binding IDs entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies causal mediation analysis (Vig et al., 2020; Geiger et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2022) to a specific reasoning subproblem—entity-attribute binding—drawing on the linear representation hypothesis from transformer circuits (Elhage et al., 2021) to hypothesize that binding IDs are additive vectors in activation space. It departs from the knowledge-recall literature (Geva et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2022), which studies factual associations stored in weights, by focusing on in-context relations stored in activations. The paper also positions itself against Hernandez et al. (2023a), who demonstrated that bound representations can be constructed externally, by showing that LMs spontaneously construct a distinct binding ID mechanism (rather than direct binding) for most tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of a clean, task-agnostic binding ID mechanism with a geometric subspace structure provides a concrete, interpretable account of a foundational reasoning skill in LMs, and the finding that only sufficiently large models exhibit this structure (with fidelity increasing with scale) challenges the intuition that larger models are harder to interpret. The discovery that binding IDs are not universal—the MCQ task uses an alternative 'direct binding' mechanism—highlights that multiple strategies coexist and raises open questions about which tasks trigger which mechanism. Future work could identify other primitive reasoning skills with similarly interpretable representations and investigate whether the convergence of binding ID structure across unrelated model families points to a universal inductive bias.
