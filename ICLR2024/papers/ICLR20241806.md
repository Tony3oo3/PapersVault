## Problem

Wide neural networks achieve higher accuracy but at prohibitive computation cost, and narrowing the network to save FLOPS sacrifices accuracy. While structured weight sparsification (SWS) has been explored to reduce computation on GPUs, the complementary idea of structured sparsity in activations—where the sparsity pattern is input-dependent and dynamically selects which weight elements participate—remained unexplored and incompatible with vector processors due to the unstructured nature of activation sparsity (e.g., from ReLU).

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet) and outputs class labels, operating within conventional CNN backbones (ResNet-18, ConvNeXt-B). Unusually, during inference the sparse wide activation is never materialized; instead, the dense narrow activation and a small per-element index (log₂M bits) are passed directly to the Sparse Tensor Core, which routes the corresponding weight elements on-the-fly. The weight matrix is M times wider than the base dense network, increasing memory footprint linearly with M while keeping the multiply count constant.

## Method

SAS replaces the standard non-linearity (e.g., ReLU) with a structured sparse projection S that maps a dense narrow activation X ∈ R^(C̄i×HW) to a sparse wide activation X̃ ∈ R^(M·C̄i×HW) where each group of M consecutive channel elements contains exactly one nonzero value. The routing index is computed cheaply from the sign bits of log₂M neighboring activations (Eq. 2), making the sparsity pattern input-dependent and dynamic. At inference, the wide dense weight × sparse wide activation matmul is executed as an equivalent narrow dense × dense matmul on NVIDIA's Sparse Tensor Core by reusing the local routing hardware originally designed for N:M structured weight sparsity, with less than 1.5% overhead from index computation and reordering. For training, the sparse activation is explicitly constructed and a conventional dense matmul is used, with gradients flowing through the projection via autograd. The authors also propose ERAdam, an optimizer that scales RAdam's warm-up schedule per weight element proportional to the number of received gradients, addressing the sparse-gradient instability that arises when M is large.

## Results

On ImageNet with ConvNeXt-B (α=2, M=2), SAS achieves 82.2% top-1 accuracy, outperforming the base ReLU network (80.7%) by 1.5% and the SWS baseline (81.5%) by 0.7%, all at the same mult count. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet18, increasing SAS sparsity M monotonically improves accuracy by up to 7% over the base ReLU network without increasing FLOPS. The trajectory length analysis confirms that SAS expressive power grows with sparsity M while SWS remains nearly constant, and the SAS-specific overhead (index computation) is less than 1.5% of total matmul time on an A6000 GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole direct baseline is SWS (Zhou et al., 2021), which applies N:M structured sparsity to weights rather than activations. On ImageNet (ConvNeXt-B, α=2, M=2), SAS achieves 82.2% top-1 accuracy versus 81.5% for SWS and 80.7% for the dense base, a 0.7% absolute gain over SWS at identical mult count. On CIFAR-10/100 (ResNet18), SAS consistently outperforms SWS across all narrowing factors α and sparsity levels M, with the gap widening as M increases (SAS scales better with sparsity). The trajectory length analysis (Section 3) further confirms that SAS has strictly greater expressive power than SWS at the same computational budget, as SAS trajectory length grows monotonically with M while SWS remains nearly flat. The trade-off is memory: SAS requires M× more weight storage (e.g., 22M×M params for ConvNeXt-B), whereas SWS keeps weight storage constant at inference time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions Structured Activation Sparsification (SAS) as the conceptual mirror of N:M structured weight sparsification (SWS), repurposing the same local routing hardware (NVIDIA Sparse Tensor Core) to sparsify activations rather than weights. It builds on RAdam (Liu et al., 2020) to derive the ERAdam optimizer that stabilizes training under sparse gradients, and departs from prior activation-sparsity work (which exploited unstructured ReLU-induced zeros via custom hardware) by imposing a controllable N:M local structure on activations through an implicit sparse projection. The key departure from SWS is that the sparsity pattern is input-dependent and dynamic, allowing the network to select different weight elements per input rather than fixing a static sparse weight mask after training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SAS opens a genuinely new axis for the accuracy/computation tradeoff: increasing activation sparsity monotonically improves accuracy (up to 7% on CIFAR-10) at constant FLOPS, and scales better than SWS under the same budget. However, practical adoption is currently constrained by three factors: (1) the weight memory footprint grows linearly with M (unlike SWS where inference-time weight storage is constant), (2) current NVIDIA GPUs support only 1:2 sparsity for tf32/float, limiting the demonstrated range, and (3) no integration into PyTorch/TensorFlow/JAX exists yet. Open questions include whether learned or attention-based index computation can replace the simple sign-bit rule, whether combining SAS with SWS (both matrices sparse) yields further gains, and whether the monotonic accuracy trend holds at larger scale beyond CIFAR and a single ImageNet configuration.
