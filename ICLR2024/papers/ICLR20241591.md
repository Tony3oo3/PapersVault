## Problem

Human mathematicians naturally identify modular, reusable theorems that simplify complex proofs, but no prior method trains a neural network to perform this extraction. Existing lemma-mining approaches are symbolic or mining-based, relying on heavy feature engineering and unable to discover genuinely new theorems. This paper tackles the first proof-of-concept of learning theorem extraction from proofs using a neural network.

## Input / Output

The model receives an expanded proof tree (a graph) where a theorem's proof has been inlined into a larger proof, with each node carrying a name (N) and proposition (PROP) as text features. It outputs a binary probability per node indicating membership in the target theorem's connected subtree. After thresholding at 0.5, the predicted nodes are validated via a verification algorithm (connectivity check, parent-completeness check, standardization of leaf names, and Metamath verifier) to confirm they constitute a valid standalone theorem. The extracted theorems then serve as inputs to a refactoring procedure that shortens existing proofs in the library.

## Method

The key insight is to reverse the human theorem-extraction process to generate supervised training data: given a proof P that invokes theorem t, the algorithm inlines t's proof tree into P (substituting canonical arguments with contextual ones) to produce an expanded proof P′, where t's proof appears as a connected subtree serving as the ground-truth target. The model is a K-layer GraphSAGE GNN (K=10, d=512 for the best model) that takes character-level embeddings of node features (name + proposition) as initial node representations and predicts per-node binary labels via a sigmoid output, trained with node-count-normalized binary cross-entropy. A critical architectural finding is that edge direction matters: edges oriented from leaves toward the root (Leaves ← Root) outperform the reverse direction because identical hypothesis leaves would otherwise receive identical messages, making proof-level discrimination impossible. Training uses a target-wise split (no theorem appears in both train and test) and occurrence-based subsampling to prevent the model from memorizing a few frequent theorems.

## Results

REFACTOR achieves 19.6% proof-level accuracy in extracting human-defined theorems from expanded proofs (85.6% node-level accuracy), with performance scaling from 2.3% to 19.6% as model size increases from 80k to 4.5M parameters. When applied to the Metamath set.mm library, REFACTOR extracts 16 novel theorems that are used an average of 733.5 times in refactoring, saving approximately 400k nodes total. Training a Holophrasm prover on the refactored dataset yields 632 proved test theorems out of 2720, with 31.0% of proved theorems leveraging at least one newly extracted theorem across 141 unique new theorems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REFACTOR outperforms all three baselines on the number of test theorems proved. Against MetaGen (Wang & Deng, 2020), which trains a generator to produce synthetic theorems and augments Holophrasm with them, REFACTOR proves 32 more test theorems (632 vs. 600), a 5.3% improvement. Against the symbolic compression baseline adapted from Vyskočil et al. (2010), REFACTOR proves 66 more theorems (632 vs. 566), and in the theorem extraction task itself, REFACTOR achieves 19.6% accuracy versus only 1.7% for the symbolic baseline. Against the original Holophrasm'16 implementation (Whalen, 2016), REFACTOR proves 56 additional theorems (468 vs. 412) with 43.6% new theorem usage, and with the improved Holophrasm'20 implementation, the gap widens to 75 additional theorems (632 vs. 557). The 31.0% new theorem usage rate and the diversity of 141 unique extracted theorems used in proofs indicate that the improvement is not driven by a single lucky extraction but by a broad set of reusable lemmas.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by being the first to train a neural network for theorem extraction from proofs, departing from prior symbolic and mining-based lemma extraction approaches (Vyskočil et al. 2010; Kaliszyk et al. 2015) that relied on hand-engineered features and frequency heuristics. Conceptually, it is closest to Explore-Compile-style subroutine discovery in program synthesis (Ellis et al. 2018, 2020), but repurposes the idea by using human-written theorems as supervision targets rather than optimizing a compression objective, and demonstrates scalability to a realistic formal system. The method itself is a standard GraphSAGE node classifier; the novelty lies entirely in the task formulation (node-level binary classification on proof trees) and the 'reverse expansion' data generation strategy that creates training targets by inlining theorem proofs into larger proofs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is a proof-of-concept that neural networks can learn to identify reusable mathematical structure from proofs, with extracted theorems showing genuine utility (733.5 average usage for set.mm extractions, 31% of proved test theorems leveraging new theorems). Key limitations include: the approach is restricted to Metamath's single-inference-rule setting, independent node predictions produce many disconnected components (requiring post-hoc filtering), and 19.6% exact-match accuracy leaves substantial room for improvement. Open questions include whether autoregressive or transformer-based prediction can enforce contiguity, whether the approach transfers to richer formal systems (Lean, Coq, HOL Light), and whether combining REFACTOR with large-scale provers like GPT-f yields compounding gains.
