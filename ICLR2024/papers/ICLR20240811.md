## Problem

Inverse reinforcement learning relies on a behavioural model (e.g., optimality, Boltzmann-rationality, or maximum causal entropy) to infer an agent's reward function from observed behaviour, but the true preference-behaviour relationship is far more complex than any of these models capture, making them inherently misspecified. Prior analyses of IRL robustness to misspecification were limited by blunt equivalence-based metrics, problematic ℓ²-distance assumptions, and restrictive conditions like strong log-concavity. This paper asks: under what precise conditions does a given behavioural model tolerate misspecification, and how large can the resulting reward inference error be?

## Input / Output

The analysis is purely theoretical and operates on an abstract IRL setup: a fixed MDP (finite states, actions, transition function τ, discount γ), an assumed behavioural model f: R̂→Π, a true data-generating model g: R̂→Π, and a pseudometric d^R on the reward space (primarily the STARC-metric d^STARC_{τ,γ}). The output is a set of necessary and sufficient conditions (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) that completely characterise all g to which f is ε-robust, plus specific non-robustness theorems for perturbation (Theorem 3) and parameter misspecification (Theorems 4–5). No empirical data or training is involved; the results hold for any restricted reward set R̂⊆R and any choice of pseudometric unless otherwise stated.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal definition of ε-robustness to misspecification (Definition 1) with four conditions covering cross-model distance, within-model identifiability, image containment, and model distinctness. It then proves Theorem 1, showing that when f is injective up to the pseudometric (f(R₁)=f(R₂)⟹d^R(R₁,R₂)=0), f is ε-robust to g if and only if g=f∘t for some transformation t with d^R(R,t(R))≤ε. Proposition 3 explicitly characterises the admissible transformations t in terms of the STARC-metric geometry, yielding Corollary 1 for Boltzmann-rational and MCE models. For perturbation robustness, Theorem 2 reduces the question to whether f is ε/δ-separating, and Theorem 3 proves that no continuous behavioural model (including Boltzmann-rational and MCE) is ε/δ-separating under the STARC-metric, meaning arbitrarily small policy perturbations can cause arbitrarily large reward errors. Finally, Theorems 4 and 5 show that any model invariant to potential shaping (resp. S′-redistribution) is non-robust to any nonzero misspecification of the discount rate (resp. transition function), even when the misspecification is arbitrarily small.

## Results

The paper's main finding is that IRL is highly sensitive to misspecification: for any ε < 0.5, no behavioural model invariant to potential shaping (including optimality, Boltzmann-rationality, and MCE) is robust to any degree of discount-rate misspecification (Theorem 4), and no model invariant to S′-redistribution is robust to any degree of transition-dynamics misspecification (Theorem 5). Furthermore, Theorem 3 shows that no continuous behavioural model is ε/δ-separating under the STARC metric for any ε < 1 or δ > 0, meaning no such model is robust to any perturbation of the observed policy. The only parameters to which standard models are robust are the Boltzmann temperature β and the MCE weight α, for which arbitrary misspecification incurs zero STARC-distance error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The closest analog to a 'comparison' is the positioning against two prior theoretical works: Hong et al. (2022) use ℓ²-distance between reward functions and assume a unique maximiser plus strong log-concavity, while Skalse & Abate (2023) use equivalence relations that cannot distinguish small from large errors. The present paper's STARC-metric-based framework avoids both limitations, yielding necessary-and-sufficient conditions (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) that are strictly more informative. Additionally, the paper argues (Appendix C) that the widely-used EPIC pseudometric is unsuitable for this analysis because it is sensitive to S′-redistribution, making any robustness result under EPIC vacuous for all ε < 0.5.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on IRL robustness to misspecified behavioural models initiated by Skalse & Abate (2023) and Hong et al. (2022), but departs from both in critical ways: it replaces their blunt equivalence-relations or ℓ²-distance formulations with STARC-metrics (Skalse et al., 2023) that provably bound worst-case regret, drops the uniqueness-of-reward assumption that Hong et al. relied on, and provides necessary-and-sufficient conditions rather than one-directional bounds. The invariance results from Skalse et al. (2022) on potential shaping and S′-redistribution serve as the structural backbone for the main theorems, and the paper unifies previously separate analyses of policy perturbation, discount-rate misspecification, and dynamics misspecification under a single formal framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no continuous behavioural model (including Boltzmann-rationality and MCE) is robust to arbitrarily small policy perturbations, and that even infinitesimal misspecification of the discount rate or transition dynamics can produce arbitrarily large reward errors—poses a serious challenge to IRL as a tool for preference elicitation in AI alignment. The paper suggests that IRL outputs should be treated with caution and carefully evaluated, and points toward the need for algorithms explicitly designed for misspecification robustness. Open questions include whether restricting the reward space R̂ can circumvent the negative results, whether weaker (non-STARC) metrics yield more optimistic guarantees, and whether probabilistic formulations of the robustness definition are more practically relevant.
