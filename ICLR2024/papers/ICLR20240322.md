## Problem

While automatic differentiation for functions mapping real tensors is mature and widely adopted, functional differentiation (computing derivatives of functionals and operators whose inputs/outputs are themselves functions) remains largely manual or restricted to semi-local Euler-Lagrange conversions. No general-purpose computational tool exists that automatically computes functional derivatives with the same compositional, syntax-level convenience that JAX provides for standard AD.

## Input / Output

The system takes Python callables annotated with shape signatures (e.g., F[f[], f[3], f[2,3]] for a function R³×R^{2×3}→R) as generalized arrays, along with numerical integration grids for the integrate operator. The output of jax.grad applied to a functional is itself a callable Python function (the functional derivative), not a scalar or tensor, which can be directly evaluated at any point. This means the gradient of a functional is a first-class function object ready for further composition or invocation.

## Method

The core insight is to represent functions as a generalization of JAX arrays and then reuse JAX's existing primitive system—JVP rules for forward mode and transpose rules for reverse mode—to differentiate higher-order operators. Five core primitives are defined: compose (∘), nabla (∇), linearize (L), linear transpose (T), and integrate (Î), each equipped with a Fréchet-derivative JVP rule and an adjoint-operator transpose rule. This mirrors JAX's internal protocol where reverse mode is derived by linearizing then transposing, but elevated to the operator level: the Fréchet derivative D(Ô)(f)(δf) generalizes the Jacobian-vector product, and the adjoint T(Ô) generalizes matrix transposition. The approach differentiates the operators themselves (e.g., D(∘)(f) for the compose operator) rather than just differentiating through composed functions, enabling arbitrary nesting of functionals. A function-call caching mechanism addresses exponential blowup in nested compositions, and the system supports both semi-local functionals (via ∇ and compose) and non-local integral transforms (via the integrate primitive with kernel functions).

## Results

This is a systems paper introducing a new capability (automatic functional differentiation in JAX) rather than a benchmark comparison study, so there are no standard quantitative metrics. The primary empirical demonstration is the brachistochrone problem, where three optimization strategies are compared: direct parameter-space minimization (Eq. 19), chain-rule through the functional gradient (Eq. 20), and minimizing the magnitude of the functional gradient (Eq. 21). The paper reports that methods (20) and (21) produce curves closer to the analytical ground truth and exhibit a regularization effect, while method (19) overfits to the 50-point numerical integration grid. A secondary result shows that function-call caching reduces the execution cost of nested compositions from exponential to linear in depth (Figure 2). The nonlocal neural functional experiment demonstrates that functional gradient descent reduces L2 loss over 4 steps, though the computation graph grows prohibitively large.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods in the traditional sense; its contribution is a new system rather than a new algorithm evaluated against competitors. The internal comparisons center on the brachistochrone problem, where three optimization strategies are contrasted: (1) direct parameter-space gradient (Eq. 19), (2) chain rule through the explicitly computed functional derivative (Eq. 20), and (3) minimizing the squared norm of the functional gradient (Eq. 21). The paper reports that strategies (2) and (3) yield curves closer to the ground-truth brachistochrone and that the functional derivative acts as a regularizer against overfitting to the discrete integration grid, whereas strategy (1) overfits more readily. The paper also notes that Eqs. (19) and (20) are provably different estimators of the same gradient (Appendix E, Eq. 26), but leaves open which is superior in general. For the nonlocal neural functional, the paper acknowledges that functional gradient descent is computationally expensive (the kernel's JAXPR graph becomes unrenderable by step 4) and positions this as a feasibility demonstration rather than a competitive method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends JAX's primitive-based automatic differentiation system (Frostig et al., 2018) from functions on real tensors to higher-order functions (functionals and operators), leveraging the key insight from Radul et al. (2023) that reverse-mode gradients are simply the linear transposition of forward-mode JVPs. The closest prior work—Birkisson & Driscoll (2012) using Chebyshev basis representations and Di Gianantonio et al. (2022) using dual numbers in a typed lambda calculus—addresses functional derivatives but relies on specific function representations and lacks reverse-mode support or vectorized execution on modern hardware. The paper deliberately positions itself against the broader AD framework literature (Theano, TensorFlow, PyTorch, JAX) that handles only real-valued functions, and against symbolic tools (Mathematica, SymPy, Maple) that compute semi-local functional derivatives in Euler-Lagrange form without compositional generality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a long-standing gap by providing a general-purpose, composable tool for automatic functional differentiation, eliminating the need for manual derivation or case-by-case Euler-Lagrange conversion in applications like DFT, variational optimization, and neural operator learning. Key limitations remain: no general mechanism for function inversion (required by several transpose rules), reliance on numerical integration grids that limit accuracy, mandatory static shape annotations, and inability to JIT-compile mixed-order computations involving both real-valued and function-valued arguments. Open questions include whether the functional-gradient-based gradient estimator (Eq. 20) is generally superior to direct parameter-space backpropagation (Eq. 19), and how to efficiently compile mixed-order differentiation into compact programs (e.g., Faà di Bruno-style).
