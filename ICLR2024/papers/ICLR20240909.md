## Problem

Existing equivariant GNNs for N-body system simulation suffer from two missing physical inductive biases: they learn discrete direct mappings between adjacent states rather than continuous trajectories, and they model only first-order velocity dynamics despite most physical systems being governed by second-order motion laws (e.g., Newton's equations). These omissions cause models to fail at learning correct intermediate states and limit generalization to unseen timesteps or system configurations.

## Input / Output

Given initial particle positions q^(t₀) ∈ ℝ³, velocities q̇^(t₀) ∈ ℝ³, static per-particle features (mass, charge), and a geometric graph (edges from distance cutoff or physical bonds with attributes like bond type), SEGNO predicts positions q^(t₁) at a fixed future interval T = t₁ − t₀. Unlike discrete baselines that output a single state, SEGNO produces a continuous latent trajectory over [t₀, t₁] by composing τ numerical integrator steps, enabling evaluation at any intermediate time. The framework is E(3)-equivariant for 3D systems (molecular dynamics, N-body) and E(2)-equivariant for the 2D CMU motion capture task.

## Method

SEGNO parameterizes the instantaneous acceleration function f_θ(q^(t), h) with a standard equivariant GNN backbone (EGNN, GMN, or SEGNN) and embeds it inside a second-order Neural ODE: q̈ = f_θ(q, h). A numerical integrator (Euler, symplectic Euler, Velocity Verlet, or Leapfrog) is composed τ times over the interval T to approximate the continuous trajectory, with the GNN parameters shared across all iterations. Training minimizes only the position discrepancy at the target timestep, yet the paper proves that this uniquely determines the latent trajectory (via Picard's theorem) and that the learned acceleration error is bounded by O(Δt + L/Δt). The equivariance of the backbone GNN is preserved through the ODE solver because the integrator increment functions are themselves O(3)-equivariant, making SEGNO a plug-and-play wrapper around any equivariant GNN.

## Results

On simulated N-body systems (5 particles), SEGNO with EGNN backbone achieves MSE of 0.433×10⁻² (Charged, 1000ts) and 0.338×10⁻² (Gravity, 1000ts), outperforming the strongest baseline SEGNN (0.448 and 0.471 respectively). The advantage grows with longer prediction horizons: at 2000 timesteps, SEGNO reaches 4.285×10⁻² (Charged) and 4.017×10⁻² (Gravity) versus SEGNN's 5.972 and 5.819. On MD22 molecular dynamics (up to 370 atoms), SEGNO with GMN backbone achieves 15.6% average relative improvement over GMN. On CMU Motion Capture at 50 timesteps, SEGNO (GMN backbone) achieves 29.264×10⁻² MSE, a 18.619×10⁻² absolute improvement over the runner-up GMN (47.883×10⁻²).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEGNO outperforms all 12 baselines across every dataset and timestep setting. Against the strongest equivariant baseline SEGNN, SEGNO achieves 28.24% relative improvement on Gravity at 1000ts, growing to 35.45% at 1500ts, demonstrating superior generalization to longer horizons. On MD22, SEGNO (GMN backbone) beats NequIP and Allegro by over an order of magnitude (e.g., 0.418 vs. 12.060 on Ac-Ala3-NHMe), attributed to those force-field models lacking dynamic/velocity information. On CMU Motion Capture at 50ts, SEGNO (GMN backbone) achieves 29.264×10⁻² MSE versus GMN's 47.883×10⁻² and EGNN's 50.930×10⁻². In rollout comparisons (Appendix C.4), SEGNO trained solely on position loss matches GNODE on energy/momentum errors while achieving lower position error, and substantially outperforms HGNN. Against GNS (which uses average acceleration), SEGNO's instantaneous acceleration learning yields 7.5× lower MSE at 2000ts on Charged (4.285 vs. 31.632). Non-equivariant baselines (GNN, Linear, GDE) and higher-order equivariant models (TFN, SE(3)-Tr., Radial Field) are consistently dominated, with TFN and SE(3)-Tr. degrading severely at longer timesteps.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEGNO sits at the intersection of two established lines: equivariant GNNs (EGNN, GMN, SEGNN) that model N-body dynamics via discrete message-passing layers, and Neural ODE approaches (GDE, GNODE) that capture continuous trajectories. The key departure is that prior equivariant GNNs learn direct discrete mappings between adjacent states using only first-order velocity, while SEGNO parameterizes a second-order acceleration function within a Neural ODE framework, enforcing both continuity and Newtonian dynamics. Unlike GNS, which trains on average acceleration computed from observed positions, SEGNO learns instantaneous acceleration by minimizing position discrepancy, yielding a tighter theoretical error bound (O(Δt²) vs. O(T) local truncation error). The plug-and-play design—where any O(3)-equivariant, translation-invariant GNN backbone can be dropped in—distinguishes it from monolithic physics-informed architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical guarantees (unique latent trajectory recovery, bounded acceleration and position error) provide a principled explanation for why SEGNO generalizes to unseen timesteps and longer rollouts, addressing a core weakness of discrete equivariant GNNs. The plug-and-play compatibility with existing equivariant backbones lowers the barrier for adoption in molecular dynamics, robotics, and other N-body simulation tasks. Open questions include extending the framework to stochastic and partial differential equations, jointly modeling trajectory prediction with static property prediction (e.g., force fields), and scaling to systems with thousands of interacting particles where graph construction becomes a bottleneck. The assumption of smooth, bounded trajectories, while physically reasonable, may limit applicability to systems with discontinuities or phase transitions.
