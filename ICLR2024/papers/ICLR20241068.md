## Problem

While Stable Diffusion fine-tuning has become widespread, the ecosystem lacks both a unified library integrating emerging parameter-efficient methods (LoHa, LoKr, GLoRA, etc.) and a systematic evaluation framework that covers the full breadth of fine-tuning decisions. Existing evaluations typically test a single prompt type and a narrow set of metrics, making it difficult to compare algorithms or understand how hyperparameters like rank, learning rate, and trained layers affect different aspects of model behavior.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241068/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The fine-tuning process takes a small set of training images (4–200 per concept across 5 categories) paired with informative auto-generated captions in the format '[V], tag1, ..., tagk', and produces compact adapter weights (LoRA/LoHa/LoKr) that can be merged into or combined with a frozen Stable Diffusion 1.5 base model. For evaluation, the fine-tuned model consumes four distinct prompt types—training captions, bare concept descriptors, content-alteration generalization prompts, and style-alteration generalization prompts—and generates 100 images per (sub-)class per checkpoint, yielding 14,900 images per checkpoint across 234 total checkpoints.

## Method

LyCORIS implements three core adapter architectures that differ in how they factorize the weight update ΔW: LoRA (LoCon) uses standard low-rank factorization BA extended to convolutional layers; LoHa uses a Hadamard product of two low-rank matrices (B₁A₁)⊙(B₂A₂), achieving an effective rank of r² versus 2r for LoRA at the same parameter count; and LoKr uses a Kronecker product C⊗(BA) with an optional additional low-rank decomposition on the right block, offering the widest range of parameter counts and smallest file sizes. All methods share a merge ratio γ=α/r that balances pretrained knowledge retention against concept adaptation. The evaluation framework scores each checkpoint on five criteria—fidelity (DINOv2 cosine similarity and squared centroid distance), controllability (CLIP text-image alignment), diversity (Vendi score on DINOv2 embeddings), base model preservation (style loss on style-alteration prompts), and image quality—across four prompt types, with SHAP analysis via CatBoost to visualize the impact of each algorithmic component on each metric.

## Results

The paper presents a tentative ranking (1–5, higher is better) of five fine-tuning configurations across three criteria: Fidelity, Controllability, and Diversity. Native fine-tuning at lr=5×10⁻⁶ achieves the highest Fidelity (rank 5) but the lowest Diversity (rank 1), while native fine-tuning at lr=10⁻⁶ achieves the highest Controllability and Diversity (rank 5) but the lowest Fidelity (rank 1). Among the parameter-efficient methods, LoKr leads in Fidelity (rank 4), LoHa leads in Controllability and Diversity (rank 4), and LoRA is consistently mid-range (ranks 2–3). The authors emphasize that no single method dominates all criteria and that rankings can shift across concept categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four algorithm families—LoRA, LoHa, LoKr, and native (full) fine-tuning—at matched parameter counts and otherwise identical hyperparameters. Native fine-tuning at a higher learning rate (5×10⁻⁶) wins on Fidelity (rank 5 vs. LoKr's 4) but collapses on Diversity (rank 1 vs. LoHa's 4), indicating severe overfitting to training concepts. Native fine-tuning at a lower learning rate (10⁻⁶) flips this trade-off, achieving the best Controllability and Diversity (rank 5) while producing the worst Fidelity (rank 1). Among the three PEFT methods, LoKr achieves the highest Fidelity (rank 4) but the lowest Controllability (rank 1), while LoHa achieves the best Controllability and Diversity (rank 4) at the cost of Fidelity (rank 2). LoRA occupies a middle position across all three criteria (ranks 2–3), making it the most balanced but not the best at any single metric. The authors conclude that LoHa is better suited for simple multi-concept fine-tuning, whereas LoKr with full dimension is preferable for complex single-concept tasks, and that matrix rank alone is not a definitive predictor of method efficacy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LoRA lineage (Hu et al., 2021) from attention layers in language models to convolutional layers in Stable Diffusion, and further adapts two decomposition techniques from adjacent fields—FedPara's Hadamard product (Hyeon-Woo et al., 2022, originally for federated learning) and KronA's Kronecker product (Edalati et al., 2022, originally for NLP)—into the text-to-image fine-tuning setting as LoHa and LoKr. Rather than proposing a single new method, the paper positions itself as a unifying empirical study: it packages these and other approaches (GLoRA, DyLoRA, (IA)³) into the LyCORIS library and introduces a structured evaluation framework that explicitly separates prompt types (training, generalization, concept-agnostic) from evaluation criteria (fidelity, controllability, diversity, base-model preservation), a distinction the authors argue prior work has conflated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LyCORIS library and its evaluation framework provide the Stable Diffusion community with practical tooling and a more rigorous comparison protocol than the ad-hoc evaluations common in the ecosystem. The key conceptual takeaway—that matrix rank, often treated as the primary predictor of fine-tuning efficacy, is not always decisive (LoHa for simple multi-concept tasks, LoKr for complex single-concept tasks)—challenges a working assumption in the PEFT literature. However, the study is limited to SD 1.5 and a single 1,706-image dataset, multi-concept generation is left unexplored, and no reliable automated image-quality metric was found, leaving open whether these rankings hold across model generations and broader concept distributions.
