## Problem

Existing backdoor trigger inversion (BTI) methods suffer from both low efficiency and poor reliability: they must 'scan' all K classes to guess the target label (making them slow, especially on large datasets) and must approximate backdoor features with a fixed poisoning form, leading to generated triggers that are significantly different from ground-truth ones even in feature space. The root cause is that these methods attempt to decouple backdoor features directly, a task that is inherently difficult when defenders have no information about the trigger pattern or target label.

## Input / Output

The method requires white-box access to a suspicious DNN (split into convolutional layers S_a and fully-connected layers S_b) and a small set of local benign samples with correct labels (5% of the training set by default, tested down to 1%). The feature space for decoupling is defined by the output of the last convolutional layer. Outputs include: (1) a U-Net generator G_θ that produces poisoned versions of any benign sample (trigger = G_θ(x) − x), (2) a purified model via fine-tuning (BTI-DBF(U)), (3) a purification generator Ĝ_ψ for pre-processing test samples (BTI-DBF(P)), and (4) a binary benign/poisoned detection label by comparing predictions of a sample and its purified version (BTI-DBF(D)).

## Method

BTI-DBF inverts the standard BTI paradigm by decoupling benign features instead of backdoor features. In Step 1, a soft mask m over the convolutional feature space is optimized so that the model classifies correctly using only S_a(x)⊙m (benign features) but incorrectly using S_a(x)⊙(1−m) (remaining features), requiring only local benign samples and no class scanning. In Step 2, a U-Net generator G_θ is trained to produce poisoned versions of benign samples by minimizing the feature-space distance in benign dimensions while maximizing it in backdoor dimensions, subject to an input-space perturbation budget τ. Two defenses are then built on this module: BTI-DBF(U) fine-tunes the attacked model on generated poisoned images relabeled to their ground-truth class to unlearn the backdoor, and BTI-DBF(P) trains a purification generator as the approximate inverse of G_θ to strip triggers before inference. Both defenses employ an iteration-based enhancement that alternately re-trains the generator and the target object (model or purifier) over R₁ rounds, which the ablation shows is critical for reducing ASR below 10%.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, BTI-DBF achieves the lowest feature distance (FD) to ground-truth triggers across all six attacks, e.g., 7.81 under BadNets (vs. 71.12 for Pixel and 18.34 for Unicorn) and 0.03 under IAD (vs. 0.09–0.13 for all baselines), while maintaining 100% detection success rate (DSR) in nearly all cases. For the downstream backdoor-removal defense BTI-DBF(U), ASR stays below 10% (most cases <5%) with BA drops under 5% across CIFAR-10, GTSRB, and ImageNet, whereas baselines like I-BAU and FeatureRE still exhibit ASR >95% on ImageNet. The pre-processing defense BTI-DBF(P) similarly keeps ASR <10% with BA drops <6%, outperforming Februus and ShrinkPad which fail (ASR >30%) on sample-specific attacks like WaNet and IAD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the five BTI baselines, BTI-DBF achieves the lowest FD in 5 out of 6 attacks and 100% DSR in 5/6 cases, while all baselines fail (DSR <50%) on at least one attack (e.g., NC and Pixel drop to 16–18% DSR on WaNet/IAD; FeatureRE reaches only 16% on BppAttack). For backdoor-removal defenses, BTI-DBF(U) outperforms NAD, I-BAU, AWM, and FeatureRE across all three datasets: on ImageNet, where baselines catastrophically fail (I-BAU ASR=98.41% on Blended, FeatureRE ASR=99.99%), BTI-DBF(U) maintains ASR <10% with BA drops <5%. For pre-processing defenses, BTI-DBF(P) dominates Februus and ShrinkPad, which suffer severe BA drops (e.g., Februus BA=28.05% on GTSRB WaNet) or high residual ASR (ShrinkPad ASR=100% on GTSRB WaNet), while BTI-DBF(P) keeps ASR at 0.00% with only ~4% BA loss. Training efficiency also favors BTI-DBF: it completes in ~60 s versus >20× longer for Unicorn and >3× longer for the fastest baseline (Pixel).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper addresses backdoor trigger inversion (BTI), an established task in the backdoor defense literature that has evolved from pixel-space methods (Neural Cleanse, Pixel, THTP) to feature-space methods (FeatureRE, Unicorn). The key departure is conceptual: rather than approximating and decoupling backdoor features (which requires guessing the poisoning form and scanning all target classes), the authors decouple benign features using local benign samples that defenders already possess, inspired in part by Qi et al. (2023b). This flips the optimization direction—minimizing differences in benign features while maximizing them in residual backdoor features—eliminating the need for class-by-class scanning and explicit poisoning-form assumptions. The resulting BTI module is then repurposed into backdoor-removal (fine-tuning with generated poisoned samples relabeled to ground-truth) and pre-processing (training an inverse purification generator) defenses, following the standard pipeline of prior BTI-based defenses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 20× speedup over the fastest baseline and near-perfect detection success rate (100% DSR vs. <50% for most baselines on sample-specific attacks like WaNet and IAD) make BTI practically viable for real-world third-party model auditing, where scanning all classes is prohibitive. However, the method still requires white-box access to intermediate features and a small set of labeled local benign samples, limiting applicability to black-box or zero-shot settings. The paper acknowledges that it implicitly relies on the latent separability assumption and demonstrates resistance to adaptive attacks (Adap-Blended, a custom Adaptive BadNets), but the margin degrades under these attacks. Open questions include extending BTI to few/zero-shot settings, black-box scenarios, and understanding why some methods achieve small feature distances yet fail to identify the correct target label.
