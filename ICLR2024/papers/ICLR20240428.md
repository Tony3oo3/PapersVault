## Problem

Model-based RL algorithms learn highly nonlinear transition models that are iteratively updated, making it difficult to embed classical robust/adaptive controllers that assume control-affine or linear dynamics. This mismatch leaves MBRL policies vulnerable to epistemic (model) and aleatoric (environmental) uncertainties, producing a sim-to-real performance gap. The paper asks whether a control-theoretic robustness layer can be added to arbitrary MBRL algorithms without modifying their internal structure.

## Input / Output

The scheme consumes three signals at each timestep: the current state x_t, the learned ensemble transition model f̂_θ (a fully nonlinear NN), and the RL policy's raw control output u_RL,t. It produces an augmented control input u_t = u_RL,t + u_a,t (where u_a,t is the L1 adaptive correction) and a piecewise-constant uncertainty estimate σ̂_t. The L1 controller operates at the same sampling interval Δt as the baseline MBRL algorithm, and the low-pass filter bandwidth ω is set relative to 1/T_s so that no per-environment retuning is required.

## Method

The key mechanism is a switching-law-driven affinization: the learned nonlinear model f̂_θ(x_t, u_t) is locally approximated by a first-order Taylor expansion in the control input around a running operating point ū, yielding a control-affine form g_θ(x_t) + h_θ(x_t)u_t. When the approximation error ‖f̂_θ^a − f̂_θ‖ exceeds a user-set threshold ε_a, the operating point is reset and the affine model is recomputed, keeping the local approximation within a bounded error. On top of this affine model, a standard L1 adaptive controller (state predictor with Hurwitz gain A_s, piecewise-constant adaptive law, and first-order low-pass filter) estimates and rejects the residual uncertainty d(t,x,u). The augmentation is fully agnostic to the underlying MBRL algorithm (demonstrated with METRPO and MBMF), and a theoretical result (Theorem 1) bounds the state-prediction error by ε_l + ε_a initially and 2ε_a + O(T_s) thereafter, showing that the tunable ε_a alone governs steady-state predictive accuracy. During training, only the RL input u_RL,t (not the augmented u_t) is stored in the replay dataset, so the learned model captures the nominal dynamics after L1 compensation.

## Results

On five MuJoCo environments (Inverted Pendulum, Swimmer, Hopper, Walker, Halfcheetah), L1-METRPO outperforms METRPO in nearly all noise conditions. Under action noise (σ_a=0.1), the gains are most dramatic: Inverted Pendulum improves from -105.2 to 0.0, Swimmer from 258.7 to 322.7, and Halfcheetah from 1920.3 to 2515.9. Under observation noise (σ_o=0.1), Hopper improves from -1391.2 to -486.6 and Walker from -30.3 to -6.3. In noise-free settings, Halfcheetah improves from 2367.3 to 2588.6 and Hopper from 1140.1 to 1491.4, while Walker shows a negligible regression (-6.6 to -6.9).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is METRPO (Kurutach et al., 2018), an ensemble-based MBRL algorithm using TRPO for policy optimization. L1-METRPO wins in 14 out of 15 environment-noise combinations, with the sole exception being Walker in the noise-free setting where the difference is negligible (-6.6 vs -6.9). The advantage is most pronounced under noise: Inverted Pendulum goes from large negative returns to exactly 0.0 under action noise, and Hopper under observation noise improves by ~900 reward points. The secondary baseline is MBMF (Nagabandi et al., 2018), which uses model-based initialization followed by model-free fine-tuning. L1-MB-MF uniformly outperforms MBMF across all five environments and all three noise conditions (Table 4), with particularly large gains on Hopper (from -1047.4 to 350.1 noise-free) and Halfcheetah (from 126.9 to 304.5 noise-free). The paper also references PETS/DETS (Chua et al., 2018) as a probabilistic-model MBRL approach, noting that L1 augmentation on a deterministic model (L1-DETS) can match or exceed PETS performance, suggesting the robustness gain is orthogonal to the model's probabilistic structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the L1 adaptive control architecture (Hovakimyan & Cao, 2010), repurposing its state predictor, piecewise-constant adaptive law, and low-pass filter as a plug-in augmentation for MBRL algorithms. It departs from prior control-augmentation-of-RL work (Cheng et al., 2022; Arevalo-Castiblanco et al., 2021) that assumed known nominal dynamics and restricted model classes, by introducing an 'affinization' step—a first-order Taylor expansion in the control input with a switching law—that converts arbitrary nonlinear learned models into locally valid control-affine approximations without sacrificing the baseline model's representational power. The approach also distinguishes itself from robust/adversarial RL (which perturbs data or dynamics) and meta-RL (which lacks explicit uncertainty rejection) by providing a model-agnostic, theory-backed add-on that requires no modification to the underlying MBRL algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a general, theoretically grounded mechanism to robustify any MBRL algorithm against both epistemic (model) and aleatoric (environmental) uncertainties, with a provable bound on prediction error (Theorem 1) that depends only on the affinization tolerance ε_a and sampling time. The sim-to-real gap experiment (Table 2) demonstrates practical relevance: a policy trained noise-free can be deployed in a noisy environment with L1 augmentation and recover much of its lost performance. Key limitations include the performance ceiling being tied to the baseline MBRL algorithm (L1 cannot compensate for a fundamentally poor model) and the heuristic choice of ε_a, which trades off robustness against fidelity to the original nonlinear model. Open questions include extension to offline MBRL, integration with distributionally robust optimization, and leveraging covariance information from probabilistic models.
