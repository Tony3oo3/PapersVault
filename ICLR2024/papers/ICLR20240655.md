## Problem

Large language models are trained on vast internet corpora that may inadvertently include public evaluation benchmarks, yet pretraining data for proprietary models is rarely accessible, making it impossible to verify contamination claims. Existing detection methods (e.g., prompting models to reproduce test examples, Min-k%-Prob) are heuristic and cannot elevate suspicion into an irrefutable proof. This paper asks whether a statistical test with provable false positive rate guarantees can be constructed using only log probability queries to a black-box model.

## Input / Output

The method takes a benchmark test set in its canonical ordering (as stored in public repositories) and a black-box language model, consuming the model exclusively through log probability queries on concatenated sequences of examples. For sequences exceeding the model's context length, a strided window approach with stride equal to half the context length is used. The output is a p-value from a one-sided t-test; the test rejects the null hypothesis of no contamination when the canonical ordering's log probability is significantly higher than the average over shuffled orderings, with the p-value providing an asymptotic false positive rate guarantee.

## Method

The core insight exploits exchangeability: for an exchangeable dataset, all example orderings are equally likely, so under the null hypothesis (model independent of the dataset), the log probability of the canonical ordering must equal in distribution the log probability of any random permutation. The basic test is a permutation test comparing the rank of the canonical log-probability among m shuffled log-probabilities, but this suffers from a 1/(m+1) minimum p-value bound and requires the canonical ordering to be in the extreme tail. The proposed sharded test partitions the dataset into r contiguous shards, computes per-shard log-likelihood differences (canonical minus Monte Carlo mean of shuffled orderings), and aggregates these shard statistics via a one-sided t-test. This design only requires the canonical ordering to be consistently above average rather than in the permutation tail, yielding rapidly decaying p-values with no minimum bound. Theorem 2 proves that under i.i.d. data and finite second moments, the test achieves asymptotic false positive rate control at level α as the number of permutations grows.

## Results

On a 1.4B-parameter canary model, the sharded likelihood comparison test achieves vanishingly small p-values (as low as 1e-38) for datasets duplicated 10 or more times in pretraining, while the simpler permutation test is bounded at p=0.009 due to its finite-sample correction. At a duplication rate of 1, neither test reliably detects contamination (p-values range from 0.099 to 0.544), but detection begins around duplication rate 4. On five publicly available models (LLaMA2-7B, Mistral-7B, Pythia-1.4B, GPT-2 XL, BioMedLM) tested against eight benchmarks, the test finds little evidence of pervasive contamination, with the notable exceptions of AI2-ARC on Mistral (p=0.001) and MMLU on LLaMA2 (p=0.014) and Mistral (p=0.011).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its sharded test against Min-k%-Prob (Shi et al., 2024), a contemporaneous heuristic contamination detector. At duplication counts of 10 and above, both methods achieve 100% detection on the canary datasets, but the sharded test additionally provides provable false positive rate guarantees that Min-k%-Prob lacks. At duplication count 1, Min-k%-Prob shows partial detection (13% on BoolQ, 2% on HellaSwag) while the sharded test does not reach statistical significance (p=0.156 and 0.478 respectively), suggesting Min-k%-Prob may have a slight edge at very low duplication rates. The sharded test also substantially outperforms the paper's own simpler permutation test: at duplication 10+, the sharded test achieves p-values of 1e-38 versus the permutation test's hard floor of 0.009, and does so with fewer permutations (50 vs. 100), making it both more powerful and more computationally efficient. The authors note that matching the sharded test's p-value of 1.96e-11 with the permutation test would require 10^11 forward passes, rendering it computationally infeasible.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the permutation testing framework of Lehmann & Romano (2005) and the finite-sample correction of Phipson & Smyth (2010), repurposing them for a novel application: detecting test set contamination in language models via exchangeability. It departs from prior memorization and membership inference work (Carlini et al. 2019, Mattern et al. 2023) by exploiting the exchangeability of benchmark datasets to obtain an exact null distribution, yielding provable false-positive guarantees rather than heuristic signals. The sharded t-test design addresses a practical limitation of the naive permutation test (p-value bounded below by 1/(m+1)), and the approach is positioned as a third-party audit tool that requires only log-probability queries, unlike n-gram-based contamination studies (Dodge et al. 2021, Brown et al. 2020a) that require access to pretraining corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to provide provable (asymptotic) false-positive-rate guarantees for black-box test set contamination detection, moving the field from speculation and heuristics to statistical evidence. Key limitations remain: the test cannot reliably detect single-duplication contamination, requires the exchangeability assumption (which is unverifiable for real benchmarks), and only captures verbatim memorization rather than more subtle forms of contamination. The authors release a 1.4B-parameter canary benchmark and leaderboard to spur future work on the open problem of detecting contamination at duplication rate 1, and their audit of four public models finding little pervasive contamination provides a useful baseline for the community.
