## Problem

Aligning LLMs to the preferences of specific interest groups (demographics, countries, individuals) is expensive: gradient-based methods like PPO, DPO, and per-group fine-tuning require large amounts of group-specific preference data and computation, while prompt engineering approaches are tedious and empirically weak for complex group behaviors. The paper asks how to efficiently adapt an LLM to a new, unseen group's preferences using only a handful of in-context examples.

## Input / Output

At inference, GPO takes a frozen base LLM and a small set of m in-context preference examples from the target group, where each example is the LLM's average token embedding of a concatenated (prompt, response) pair paired with a scalar preference score. It outputs a softmax-normalized preference distribution over the answer options of a target question, representing the group's opinion. The embedding-based input representation avoids the sequence-length explosion that raw text would cause, and the same prompt-response inputs can be shared across groups while only the preference labels differ.

## Method

GPO augments a frozen base LLM with a small independent transformer (4 layers, 4 heads, 128-dim FFN) trained via meta-learning across multiple groups to perform in-context preference prediction. The key architectural insight is a custom masking scheme that enforces two inductive biases: context invariance (no positional encodings, so predictions are permutation-invariant over context pairs) and target equivalence (targets attend only to context, not to each other, enforcing conditional independence). Each (x, y) pair is concatenated into a single token to preserve the input-output association, and targets are padded with a dummy token. During training, each group's dataset is randomly split into context and target points, and the transformer is optimized to predict target preferences from context via a standard cross-entropy loss. Once trained, the module serves as a drop-in preference function for any downstream policy optimization or re-ranking algorithm (PPO, Best-of-N) without updating the base LLM's weights.

## Results

On OpinionQA (22 US demographic groups), GPO achieves a 7.1% average alignment score improvement over the best baseline (In-context Finetune) across three group-split settings and two base models (Alpaca-7B and Llama2-13B-chat). On GlobalOpinionQA (14 countries), GPO posts an 8.4% improvement over the second-best baseline. GPO also demonstrates superior sample efficiency, achieving strong alignment with fewer than 10 context samples, and requires approximately 4.7× less training time than In-context Finetune on an NVIDIA RTX A6000.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPO outperforms all seven baselines (Uniform, LM Base, LM Steered, Few-shot Prompt, SFT per-group, Reward Model, and In-context Finetune) on both OpinionQA and GlobalOpinionQA across both Alpaca-7B and Llama2-13B-chat base models. The closest competitor is In-context Finetune, which GPO surpasses by 7.1% on OpinionQA and 8.4% on GlobalOpinionQA; however, In-context Finetune requires ~4.7× more training time. Among gradient-free methods, LM Steered (prompting with group metadata) provides modest gains over LM Base, while Few-shot Prompt actually degrades alignment below the base model, likely due to out-of-distribution prompting formats. Among gradient-based methods, SFT per-group outperforms the Reward Model baseline, which the authors attribute to overfitting under limited per-group samples. GPO also shows the strongest performance in the individual-preference alignment setting across all 15 survey topics, where per-individual SFT and Reward Model baselines were infeasible to evaluate at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

GPO reframes group preference alignment as a few-shot meta-learning problem in LLM embedding space, building directly on Transformer Neural Processes (Nguyen & Grover, 2022) for permutation-invariant inductive biases over in-context examples. It departs from the two dominant alignment paradigms—prompt engineering (no parameter updates but limited steerability, as shown by Santurkar et al. and Durmus et al.) and gradient-based methods like PPO, DPO, and per-group SFT (effective but requiring substantial per-group supervision and compute)—by introducing a lightweight, independent transformer module that performs in-context supervised learning on LLM embeddings of prompt-response pairs. The use of LLM token embeddings as inputs draws on the broader finding that pretrained representations encode sufficient task information (Bhatia et al., 2023), allowing the small GPO module to process long prompt-response sequences without inflating sequence length.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GPO offers a practical path toward deploying LLMs that respect diverse demographic, cultural, and individual preferences without the prohibitive cost of per-group fine-tuning, with demonstrated 7–8% alignment improvements over the strongest baselines and strong sample efficiency (effective with fewer than 10 context examples). Key limitations constrain broader impact: evaluation is restricted to English multiple-choice survey questions, the method has not been validated on long-form generation, and aligning to group preferences risks amplifying harmful or biased outputs without additional safeguards. Open questions include whether GPO's alignment gains hold when combined with other alignment objectives (harmlessness, helpfulness), whether initializing the GPO transformer with a pretrained backbone improves out-of-distribution generalization, and how the approach extends to open-ended creative or conversational settings where preference signals are far noisier.
