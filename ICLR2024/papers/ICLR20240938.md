## Problem

NLP studies have widely claimed that isotropy in LLM representations improves downstream performance, but these claims rest on flawed measures (average cosine similarity, partition isotropy score) that do not actually capture isotropy. The one accurate measure, IsoScore, is neither differentiable nor stable when the number of samples is smaller than the embedding dimensionality, making it unusable as a training regularizer. This paper asks what the true relationship between isotropy and performance is when measured with a correct, differentiable, mini-batch-stable metric.

## Input / Output

The method consumes token embeddings from every hidden layer of a transformer (not just the final layer), a pre-computed shrinkage covariance matrix derived from 250k training points (refreshed each epoch), and the standard cross-entropy loss. It produces a single scalar isotropy penalty in [0,1] that is added to the cross-entropy loss with a tunable weight λ. The penalty is computed globally across all layers via the union of their token embeddings, letting the optimizer decide where isotropy changes are most beneficial rather than forcing a per-layer constraint.

## Method

IsoScore* replaces the non-differentiable diagonal extraction in vanilla IsoScore with direct eigenvalue computation on an RDA-shrinkage-stabilized covariance matrix (Σ_ζ = (1−ζ)·Σ_X + ζ·Σ_S), making the entire pipeline differentiable and numerically stable even when the mini-batch size is smaller than the embedding dimension. The eigenvalues are L2-normalized to unit scale, and an isotropy defect is computed as the Euclidean distance from the uniform-eigenvalue vector, yielding a score in [0,1] where 1 is fully isotropic. I-STAR adds λ·(1 − IsoScore*) to the cross-entropy loss, so negative λ encourages anisotropy and positive λ encourages isotropy. The shrinkage matrix Σ_S is initialized from a partial forward pass on 250k training points and updated each epoch to track distributional drift. The key empirical finding is that negative λ (decreasing isotropy) consistently improves performance across BERT, ALBERT, and DistilBERT on nine GLUE/SQuAD tasks, while positive λ degrades performance, and that CosReg merely implements a zero-mean shift without altering true isotropy.

## Results

Across three transformer models (ALBERT, BERT, DistilBERT) and nine GLUE/SQuAD tasks, I-STAR with negative λ (decreasing isotropy) consistently matches or exceeds the unregularized baseline, while CosReg (encouraging isotropy via cosine similarity) generally degrades performance. The largest gains from I-STAR appear on RTE (ALBERT: 72.56 vs. 71.34 base; DistilBERT: 58.05 vs. 56.82 base) and SQUAD F1 (BERT: 87.76 vs. 87.02 base; DistilBERT: 83.69 vs. 83.23 base). CosReg underperforms the baseline on most tasks, with particularly large drops on COLA (ALBERT: 49.59 vs. 54.56 base) and RTE (ALBERT: 65.99 vs. 71.34 base). All optimal I-STAR tuning parameters are negative (λ ∈ {−1, −3, −5}), confirming that decreasing isotropy improves downstream performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is CosReg (Gao et al., 2019), a cosine-similarity regularizer intended to encourage isotropy. I-STAR outperforms CosReg on the vast majority of model–task pairs: for example, on ALBERT RTE, I-STAR reaches 72.56% accuracy versus 65.99% for CosReg (a 6.6-point gap), and on ALBERT COLA, I-STAR scores 55.22 versus 49.59 for CosReg. CosReg also underperforms the unregularized baseline on most tasks (e.g., ALBERT SST-2: 91.86 vs. 93.08 base; BERT COLA: 57.90 vs. 59.22 base), suggesting that its apparent 'isotropy' effect is actually a zero-mean shift rather than a genuine isotropy improvement. I-STAR matches or slightly exceeds the base model on most tasks, with the clearest wins on RTE, SQUAD, and SST-5 across all three architectures. The paper further shows that CosReg leaves IsoScore* values near zero (0.004–0.007) after fine-tuning, confirming it does not alter the covariance structure of representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends IsoScore (Rudman et al., 2022) into a differentiable, mini-batch-stable regularizer (IsoScore*) by incorporating RDA shrinkage (Friedman, 1989) to stabilize covariance estimation on small samples. It departs sharply from the dominant NLP narrative (Ethayarajh, 2019; Gao et al., 2019; Kovaleva et al., 2021) that anisotropy is detrimental and isotropy should be encouraged, instead aligning with the broader ML literature (Zhu et al., 2018; Ansuini et al., 2019) that anisotropy aids generalization and lower intrinsic dimensionality improves downstream performance. The paper also repositions CosReg (Gao et al., 2019), showing it functions as a zero-mean transform rather than a true isotropy regularizer, thereby undermining the empirical basis for much of the prior isotropy-benefits literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing the first differentiable and stable isotropy regularizer, I-STAR enables practitioners to explicitly control the geometry of LLM representations during training, and the finding that *decreasing* isotropy improves performance across three architectures and nine tasks challenges a widely-held assumption in NLP. The demonstration that CosReg does not actually alter isotropy (only the mean) suggests many prior positive results attributed to 'isotropy improvement' may be confounded. Key limitations include the restriction to fine-tuning (not pre-training), modest absolute gains (typically 1–2 points), and the open question of whether isotropy enforced throughout pre-training would yield different outcomes. The connection to intrinsic dimensionality via TwoNN provides a mechanistic explanation but leaves open whether the effect is specific to transformer architectures or generalizes to other representation-learning settings.
