## Problem

Despite the widespread success of contrastive learning, the minimum number of labeled tuples required for high generalization accuracy (sample complexity) was not well characterized, especially for structured distance functions like ℓ_p-norms. The paper also challenges the folklore belief that classic PAC-learning bounds are vacuous for deep learning by demonstrating they can be non-vacuous and predictive when the analysis focuses on the output representation dimension rather than input structure.

## Input / Output

The method takes i.i.d. labeled tuples (anchor x, positive y⁺, negatives z₁⁻,…,z_k⁻) drawn from an arbitrary unknown distribution over a dataset of n points, with no assumption on latent classes in the input. It produces a distance function ρ: V×V→ℝ (an embedding into ℝ^d under ℓ_p, tree metric, or cosine similarity) that correctly classifies new unlabeled tuples with high probability. The key constraint is that the input distribution is fully general, and the only structural assumption is on the output space dimension d, making the bounds depend on min(nd, n²) rather than on input geometry.

## Method

The core technique bounds the VC/Natarajan dimension of the contrastive learning problem by reducing it to counting sign patterns of polynomials. For even p, each triplet constraint (‖f(x)−f(y)‖_p < ‖f(x)−f(z)‖_p) becomes a degree-p polynomial in nd variables, and Warren's theorem from algebraic geometry bounds the number of connected components of the sign-pattern space, yielding O(nd) VC-dimension. For odd p, the absolute values prevent a direct polynomial formulation, so the authors enumerate all n!^d coordinate orderings, adding a log n factor. The lower bound is constructed by embedding anchor points with binary coordinates relative to fixed basis-vector targets, showing Ω(nd) queries can be shattered. Experimental validation uses ResNet-18 trained from scratch with triplet and contrastive losses on CIFAR-10/100 and MNIST/FashionMNIST, confirming the predicted n/α² scaling in the well-separated regime.

## Results

The main theoretical result is that for ℓ_p-distances with integer p, the sample complexity of contrastive learning is Θ̃(min(nd, n²)) labeled tuples (matching upper and lower bounds for even p; an extra log n factor for odd p). For arbitrary distance functions the bound is Θ(n²), for tree metrics it is between Ω(n) and O(n log n), and for cosine similarity it is Θ(min(nd, n²)). In the well-separated ℓ₂ setting (multiplicative gap 1+α), the sample complexity improves to Õ(n/α²) upper and Ω(n/α) lower, nearly independent of dimension d. Empirically, on CIFAR-10/100 and MNIST/FashionMNIST with ResNet-18 trained from scratch, the observed generalization error closely matches the theoretical prediction across embedding dimensions d and sample sizes m ∈ {10², 10³, 10⁴}.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not compare against competing contrastive learning methods; instead, it provides tight sample complexity bounds across different distance function classes. The key comparison is between settings: ℓ_p distances achieve the near-optimal Θ̃(min(nd, n²)) bound, which is substantially better than the Θ(n²) bound for arbitrary distances, showing that the structure of the embedding space matters. For constant d, the bound improves to O(n), matching the lower bound. The well-separated setting (common in practice with hard negatives) further reduces dependence on d to nearly zero, giving Õ(n/α²). The empirical validation on CIFAR-10/100 and MNIST/FashionMNIST confirms that the theoretical sample complexity predictions (via VC/Natarajan dimension) accurately track the observed generalization error, challenging the folklore belief that PAC-learning bounds are vacuous for deep learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of contrastive learning theory (loss function design, transfer learning, mutual information, inductive biases) by focusing squarely on sample complexity in the PAC-learning framework. It avoids the common assumption of latent classes in the input (Saunshi et al., Ash et al., Awasthi et al.) and instead allows arbitrary input distributions, shifting the analysis to the output representation dimension. The core technical tool—bounding VC/Natarajan dimension via Warren's theorem from algebraic geometry—is repurposed from classical statistical learning theory to the contrastive learning setting, and the paper positions itself as a bridge between PAC-learning theory and deep learning practice, explicitly challenging the folklore belief that such bounds are vacuous for neural networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first tight (or near-tight) sample complexity bounds for contrastive learning under ℓ_p distances, tree metrics, and cosine similarity, giving practitioners concrete guidance on how many labeled tuples are needed. The experimental validation on CIFAR-10/100 and MNIST/FashionMNIST showing that VC-dimension-based predictions match empirical generalization error within a constant factor is a meaningful step toward closing the theory-practice gap. Open questions include closing the log n gap for odd p, extending to batched adaptive comparison settings, and understanding why the constant ratio between predicted and observed error tends to be small in practice.
