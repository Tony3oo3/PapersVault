## Problem

While modular architectures are expressive enough to represent compositional task structure, it remains unclear under what conditions they can actually discover the correct underlying modules from data rather than collapsing to monolithic solutions that memorize each task combination. This paper formalizes the question as a parameter identification problem in a multi-task teacher-student setting, asking precisely when a student hypernetwork can recover the teacher's modules up to linear transformation and thereby generalize to unseen module compositions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240980/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The learner receives input vectors x paired with task demonstrations (support sets) drawn from a distribution over sparse latent codes z ∈ ℝ^M with ‖z‖₀ ≤ K, where each z selects a linear combination of M parameter modules. The student must infer a task-specific embedding from the support set without direct access to z, then produce a task-conditioned function output. In the extended experiments, outputs are action-value functions for compositional color preferences or behavior policies for compositional navigation goals in grid worlds, evaluated on held-out module combinations (OOD tasks) whose constituent modules were all seen during training.

## Method

The paper introduces a multi-task teacher-student framework where both teacher and student are linear hypernetworks: a task latent code z linearly combines M parameter modules to generate the first-layer weights of a 2-layer MLP. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1–2) proves that if the training distribution has compositional support (every module appears in at least one task), connected support (no subset of modules is isolated from the rest), and the student is not overparameterized relative to the teacher, then zero training loss implies the student modules are a linear transformation of the teacher modules, enabling generalization to all exponentially many module combinations from only a linear number of observed combinations. Empirically, the authors validate these conditions in a 'hyperteacher' setup and two grid-world environments (compositional preferences and compositional goals), showing that both linear and nonlinear hypernetworks achieve strong OOD accuracy under compositional and connected support while monolithic baselines (ANIL, MAML) fail, and that violating either condition or overparameterizing the student degrades compositional generalization.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it proves that a student hypernetwork with compositional and connected task support can identify teacher modules up to linear transformation, requiring only a linear number of sparse module combinations (N = dim(span(Z_k)) + 1) to generalize to all exponentially many compositions. Empirically, in the Hyperteacher setting (Figure 3B), both linear and nonlinear hypernetworks achieve high OOD accuracy on held-out module combinations, while ANIL and MAML fail to generalize regardless of training support. Linear decoding of teacher modules from student embeddings yields R² scores close to 1 under compositional and connected support (Figure 3C,D), confirming the theoretical identification result. In compositional preference and goal grid worlds (Figure 4), hypernetworks achieve lower OOD MSE loss and higher OOD policy accuracy than monolithic baselines, with performance degrading when task support is disconnected or non-compositional.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares modular hypernetwork architectures (linear and nonlinear) against two monolithic baselines: ANIL (Raghu et al., 2020) and MAML (Finn et al., 2017). In the Hyperteacher experiment (Figure 3B), both hypernetwork variants achieve substantially higher OOD accuracy than ANIL and MAML when the training distribution has compositional and connected support, while ANIL and MAML fail to generalize to held-out module combinations regardless of support structure. When the training support is made non-compositional (a module is never seen in any training task), the hypernetworks' OOD accuracy drops below that of ANIL and MAML, confirming that their advantage is specifically due to leveraging compositional structure. In the compositional preference grid world (Figure 4C), hypernetworks achieve lower OOD action-value prediction loss than both ANIL and MAML under compositional support. In the compositional goal grid world (Figure 4E), hypernetworks achieve higher OOD accuracy relative to the optimal policy than both monolithic baselines. Notably, when one goal quadrant is held out (non-compositional support, Figure 4F), the hypernetworks' OOD accuracy degrades more sharply than ANIL and MAML's, further confirming that their generalization advantage is specifically attributable to discovering and exploiting the underlying modular structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical teacher-student identifiability framework (Gardner & Derrida, 1989; Tian, 2020; Simsek et al., 2021) to a multi-task setting where the teacher is a linear hypernetwork with sparse module composition, and the student must infer a hidden task latent variable. It builds on the hypernetwork literature (Ha et al., 2017; Jayakumar et al., 2020) for weight-space composition and on meta-learning (Finn et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2020) for the finite-data regime, while complementing Lachapelle et al. (2023) who studied L1-regularized identification in linear multi-task settings. The key departure is providing both necessary and sufficient conditions (compositional + connected support, no overparameterization) for linear identification of modules, rather than only sufficient conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical characterization of when modular architectures can discover hidden compositional structure from demonstrations alone, yielding actionable design principles (ensure connected task support, avoid overparameterization) for multi-task and meta-learning systems. Key open questions remain: whether gradient-based optimization can actually reach the identified modular solution (the theory characterizes equilibria, not dynamics), how sample complexity scales with the number of modules, and whether the overparameterization sensitivity observed in the discrete teacher-student setting persists in more realistic, non-teacher-student environments. The finding that linear parameter-space composition can capture nonlinear latent structure also raises interpretability questions for the resulting modules.
