## Problem

Existing GNN explainability methods focus on explaining individual class predictions or discriminative features per class, but no prior work addresses the decision boundaries of GNNs—where the model is uncertain, which classes it confuses, and how complex its decision-making schema is. This gap prevents a comprehensive understanding of GNN robustness and generalizability, since the decision-making process is fundamentally determined by the boundaries between classes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained GNN graph classifier (e.g., GCN) as input, requiring access to its forward pass and intermediate embeddings but not its training data. It produces three types of outputs: (1) a set of adjacent class pairs identified via Monte Carlo interpolation in the last hidden-layer embedding space, (2) a distribution over near-boundary graphs parameterized by a Gilbert random graph model with Concrete-distributed edge and node-feature probabilities, and (3) three quantitative boundary metrics—margin, thickness, and complexity—computed from the generated graphs and their embeddings.

## Method

GNNBoundary first identifies adjacent class pairs by measuring the 'ubiquity' of boundary embeddings: for K randomly sampled graph pairs from two classes, it checks via linear interpolation in the last hidden layer (where boundaries are linear) whether the straight-line path crosses only those two decision regions. For each adjacent pair, it generates near-boundary graphs by optimizing a novel objective function that provably satisfies two desired properties—encouraging/discouraging posterior probabilities and always encouraging boundary-class logits—unlike cross-entropy, which the paper proves can minimize boundary-class logits in certain cases (Corollary 4.1). The discrete graph generation is made differentiable via Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization over a Gilbert random graph distribution with Concrete-distributed edges and categorical node features. A dynamic regularization scheduler adaptively increases the budget penalty weight only when the generated graph satisfies the near-boundary criterion (both class probabilities within [0.45, 0.55]), preventing the size constraint from interfering with early-stage convergence.

## Results

GNNBoundary consistently generates near-boundary graphs with predicted class probabilities centered tightly around 0.5 (e.g., 0.501±0.028 and 0.499±0.028 for House vs. HouseX on Motif), across all 11 adjacent class pairs on three datasets. The random baseline, which simply connects two sampled graphs, produces probabilities far from 0.5 (e.g., 0.041±0.136 and 0.949±0.145), confirming that GNNBoundary's optimized objective is essential for faithful boundary generation. In terms of convergence, GNNBoundary's adaptive loss achieves higher success rates than the cross-entropy baseline on 8 out of 11 class pairs (e.g., 0.76 vs. 0.55 for HouseX vs. Comp5 on Motif; 0.75 vs. 0.25 for EC1 vs. EC6 on Enzymes), with faster average convergence iterations in most cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other GNN explainability methods directly, but evaluates against two baselines. First, a random baseline that connects two randomly sampled graphs (one from each boundary class) with a random edge produces predicted probabilities far from 0.5 on all 11 class pairs (e.g., 0.949±0.145 for HouseX on Motif vs. GNNBoundary's 0.499±0.028), demonstrating that simply combining discriminative features of both classes is insufficient to produce true boundary graphs. Second, a cross-entropy loss baseline (Appendix C, Table 2) achieves lower success rates on 8 of 11 class pairs (e.g., 0.55 vs. 0.76 for HouseX vs. Comp5 on Motif; 0.25 vs. 0.75 for EC1 vs. EC6 on Enzymes) and generally requires more iterations to converge, validating the paper's theoretical argument that cross-entropy can inadvertently minimize logit values for boundary classes. The paper also qualitatively contrasts with GNNInterpreter, noting that GNNInterpreter's explanation graphs are designed to trigger a single class with probability 1, whereas GNNBoundary's graphs sit equidistantly between two classes, serving fundamentally different explanatory purposes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model-level GNN explanation framework established by GNNInterpreter (Wang & Shen, 2023), which uses the Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization trick and a parameterized Gilbert random graph distribution to generate explanation graphs. However, it departs fundamentally in objective: rather than generating graphs that maximize a single class's discriminative pattern, GNNBoundary generates graphs that sit equidistantly between two adjacent classes. The approach also draws on the CNN/MLP decision boundary literature (Berk et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2020; Guan & Loew, 2020), which analyzes near-boundary instances to assess robustness and generalizability, but adapts these ideas to the discrete, combinatorial nature of graph data through a novel adaptive loss function and dynamic regularization scheduler.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to systematically analyze GNN decision boundaries, opening a new axis of model-level explainability beyond per-class feature descriptors. The three derived metrics (boundary thickness, margin, complexity) provide actionable diagnostics for practitioners deploying GNNs in safety-critical settings, linking boundary geometry to misclassification risk and out-of-distribution robustness. Key limitations include restriction to GCN architectures and graph classification, reliance on linearly separable boundaries in the last hidden layer, and evaluation on only three small-scale datasets. Open questions include whether the framework extends to heterogeneous GNNs, node-level tasks, and whether the observed discrepancies between human-expected and model-learned boundaries (noted in the qualitative analysis) indicate systematic GNN failure modes worth further investigation.
