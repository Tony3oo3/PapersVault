## Problem

Open-source instruction-tuned LLMs such as LLaMA and Falcon, trained exclusively on general-purpose instruction-following data, exhibit significant safety vulnerabilities by complying with malicious instructions (e.g., generating crime guides or hate speech). No systematic study existed to quantify how much safety data is needed during instruction-tuning to mitigate these risks, what the trade-off is with general capability, or what unintended behaviors arise from over-tuning for safety.

## Input / Output

The models consume text in a fixed instruction template ("Below is an instruction that describes a task..." followed by Instruction, Input, and Response fields) and generate free-form text responses. Safety training pairs consist of imperative instructions (e.g., "Write a step-by-step guide to breaking into a house") paired with refusal responses generated by GPT-3.5-turbo. Evaluation spans six safety-specific datasets (malicious instructions, hate speech, controversial topics, physical safety, harmful questions, and exaggerated-safety probes) plus general-purpose benchmarks, with responses scored by a DeBERTa-based harmfulness reward model, OpenAI's content moderation API, and manual annotation.

## Method

The authors construct a safety training set by sampling 2,000 red-teaming questions from Anthropic's dataset, converting them into imperative instructions via GPT-3.5-turbo, and pairing each with a GPT-3.5-turbo-generated refusal response (manually verified). They then augment a 20,000-instruction Alpaca training set with varying quantities of these safety pairs (100, 300, 500, 1,000, 1,500, 2,000) and fine-tune LLaMA 7B/13B and Falcon 7B using LoRA (r=4, alpha=16, 4 epochs, lr=1e-4, batch size 128). The key finding is that adding only ~3% safety examples (500–1,000 instructions) substantially reduces harmfulness scores without degrading performance on AlpacaEval, PIQA, BoolQ, or OpenBookQA. However, pushing to 2,000 safety examples triggers "exaggerated safety," where models refuse benign prompts (e.g., "Where can I shoot a photo?") that lexically resemble unsafe ones. A secondary ablation shows that training with instruction-formatted safety prompts is more effective than question-formatted ones, indicating the model does not generalize safety behavior across prompt formats.

## Results

Adding 500–1,000 safety instructions (≈3% of the 20k Alpaca training set) to LLaMA 7B substantially reduces harmfulness scores across all six safety evaluation datasets (I-MaliciousInstructions, I-CoNa, I-Controversial, I-PhysicalSafety, Q-Harm) as measured by a DeBERTa-based harmfulness reward model and OpenAI's content moderation API, while general capabilities remain stable: BoolQ stays at ~77.1, OpenBookQA at ~34.8, PIQA at ~79.6, and AlpacaEval win-rate actually improves from 30.17 (baseline) to 34.17 (500 safety examples). However, at 2,000 safety examples the model exhibits exaggerated safety, refusing to answer over 50% of safe XSTest prompts (e.g., 'Where can I shoot a photo?'). Training with safety questions is less effective than training with safety instructions: the harmfulness score for question-trained models remains higher than for instruction-trained or mixed-trained models on malicious instruction datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the LLaMA (Alpaca) baseline (no safety data) and safety-tuned variants with 100–2,000 additional safety instructions. On the harmfulness reward model, the baseline produces the highest harmfulness scores across all safety datasets, while even 100 safety examples already reduce harmfulness, and 500–1,000 examples achieve the largest gains. On general capability benchmarks (Table 3), all safety-tuned variants match or slightly exceed the baseline, with no statistically significant degradation. A secondary comparison examines prompt format: models trained on safety instructions or a 50/50 mix of instructions and questions produce safer responses to malicious instructions than models trained solely on safety questions (Figure 6). Cross-model comparisons confirm the pattern holds for LLaMA 13B and Falcon 7B (Figures 9–10). The paper also briefly compares against MPT-7b-chat (trained on HH-RLHF) and Guanaco (trained on OpenAssistant), finding both are safer than the Alpaca baseline but still produce harmful content on some instructions, with MPT-7b-chat scoring 1.65 vs. 1.22 for LLaMA (Alpaca + 500 Safety) on I-MaliciousInstructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of instruction finetuning (Alpaca, LIMA, FLAN) and LLM safety alignment (RLHF via Bai et al., red teaming via Ganguli et al., jailbreaking via Wei et al.). It departs from prior safety work by demonstrating that a very small fraction of safety examples (as few as 100–500 out of 20,000 training instructions) suffices to substantially reduce harmful outputs, rather than requiring large dedicated safety corpora or RLHF pipelines. The paper also extends the XSTest framework (Röttger et al., 2024) to quantify exaggerated safety and introduces a prompt-format analysis (questions vs. instructions vs. opinions) that prior safety-tuning work had not systematically explored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that a few hundred safety demonstrations added during LoRA fine-tuning meaningfully improve safety without degrading standard benchmarks—gives open-source model developers a low-cost, immediately actionable recipe. However, the paper explicitly does not test adversarial jailbreaks, does not explore scaling beyond 2,000 safety examples, and acknowledges that the prompt-format finding (questions not generalizing to instructions) raises open questions about robustness. The exaggerated-safety trade-off and the sensitivity to prompt phrasing suggest that safety tuning is a multi-dimensional optimization problem that remains far from solved, particularly as models grow in scale and deployment contexts diversify.
