## Problem

Existing HMM-GLM methods for modeling time-varying functional connectivity in multi-state neural systems assume complete independence among the GLM weight matrices in different states, which is biologically implausible because functional connectivity is shaped and confined by the underlying anatomical connectome. This paper asks how to incorporate a learnable, state-invariant structural prior that captures the anatomical skeleton while still allowing state-dependent functional variation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241994/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes time-binned multi-neuron spike counts (N neurons × T bins, e.g., 20 ms bins for PFC-6, 3 ms bins for barrel cortex) and produces a hidden state sequence, per-state weight matrices decomposed into a discrete one-hot adjacency (over {inhibitory, no connection, excitatory}) and a positive strength matrix, a shared adjacency prior A_0, and a transition matrix. The adjacency prior is the key interpretable output: it is a learned N×N×3 tensor encoding the most likely physical connection type between every neuron pair, independent of brain state.

## Method

The one-hot HMM-GLM (OHG) decomposes each state's GLM weight matrix into a soft one-hot adjacency vector (on a 2-simplex over {inh, no, exc}) and a positive strength scalar, then imposes a Gumbel-Softmax prior on the adjacency with a shared learnable parameter A_0 across all states, while leaving the strength matrices unconstrained by any cross-state prior. Inference proceeds via a Baum-Welch (EM) algorithm: the E-step computes the posterior over hidden states z_t using forward-backward message passing, and the M-step jointly maximizes the expected log-likelihood over the transition matrix, A_0, per-state adjacencies, log-strengths, and background intensities. A companion Gaussian HMM-GLM (GHG) places a shared Gaussian prior directly on the full weight matrices without the one-hot decomposition, serving as an intermediate model that confirms the adjacency/strength split is critical. The Gumbel-Softmax temperature τ (fixed at 0.2) controls how sharply each adjacency entry is pushed toward a single connection type, effectively turning the adjacency into a near-discrete categorical variable during optimization.

## Results

On the synthetic 5-state/20-neuron dataset, OHG achieves the best performance across all five metrics: test log-likelihood of 14.64, state accuracy of 92.75%, weight error of 10.99, adjacency accuracy of 73.90%, and adjacency prior accuracy of 80.60%. On the real PFC-6 dataset, OHG consistently achieves the highest test log-likelihood across all state counts (e.g., −35.71 at S=5 vs. −36.38 for the next-best HMM Bern). On the barrel cortex whisking dataset, OHG again yields the best normalized log-likelihood and produces statistically significant weight increases during whisking states (p=0.008, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), while HG and GHG do not (p>0.1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

OHG outperforms all six baselines on every metric where they overlap. Against the naive HMM-GLM (HG, Escola et al. 2011), OHG improves test LL from −5.49 to 14.64 and reduces weight error from 109.67 to 10.99 on synthetic data, primarily because HG's lack of a shared prior leads to incorrect transition matrices and noisy per-state weights. The Gaussian-prior variant GHG narrows the gap (LL 8.58, weight error 21.54) but still trails OHG by ~6 LL points and ~31 percentage points in adjacency accuracy (42.53% vs. 73.90%), confirming that the one-hot strength-adjacency decomposition is critical beyond mere weight regularization. Adding L1 penalties (HG-L1, GHG-L1) helps suppress noisy weights but does not improve adjacency discrimination, as L1 cannot distinguish a weak connection from no connection. On real PFC-6 data, OHG's test LL (−35.71 at S=5) exceeds the next-best HMM Bern (−36.38) by ~0.67 nats, and its inferred states align with interpretable behavioral phases (maze start, decision, reward, error) that HG and GHG fail to recover. In Appendix A.7, SLDS and rSLDS produce fast-switching, hard-to-interpret state sequences on PFC-6, whereas OHG's state-switching-over-connectivity assumption yields stable, behaviorally meaningful states.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the HMM-GLM framework of Escola et al. (2011), which models time-varying neural connectivity via state-switching GLMs with independent weight matrices per state. The key departure is introducing a shared learnable prior across states, motivated by the biological argument that functional connectivity is constrained by the underlying anatomical connectome. The novel one-hot HMM-GLM (OHG) decomposes each state's weight matrix into a discrete adjacency (connection type) and a continuous strength, imposing a Gumbel-Softmax prior (Jang et al., 2016) only on the adjacency to capture state-invariant structure, while leaving the strength free to vary. A Gaussian-prior variant (GHG) serves as an intermediate model without the decomposition, isolating the contribution of the one-hot structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled way to separate state-invariant (anatomical) from state-dependent (functional) neural connectivity, yielding more interpretable hidden states that align with behavioral events in both PFC and barrel cortex recordings. A key limitation is that the learned adjacency prior is argued to approximate the anatomical connectome but is never validated against actual anatomical data, leaving the biological claim somewhat untested. The inference is also approximate—only the HMM state posterior is computed in the E-step, while the Gumbel-Softmax latent adjacency is handled via M-step optimization without a variational posterior—raising questions about convergence and scalability to larger networks. Open questions include whether the one-hot constraint is too rigid for neurons with graded synaptic strengths and how the method extends to multi-region or hierarchical connectivity structures.
