## Problem

Group robustness methods and poisoning defenses both rely on difficulty-based heuristics to identify unusual training samples, yet they pursue directly opposing goals: amplifying hard-to-learn samples versus removing them. No prior work had systematically studied whether these two families of methods are compatible, leaving open the question of whether deploying them together in a security-critical, demographically-sensitive application would help or hurt each other.

## Input / Output

The training set consists of standard RGB images (Waterbirds at 4,795 images, CelebA at 162,770 images with 10% sampled) that may contain both legitimate under-represented group samples and adversarially injected poisons (dirty-label backdoors at 1%, subpopulation attacks at 2%, or clean-label gradient-matching attacks). The trained ResNet-18/50 classifier is evaluated on three metrics that capture the tension: Worst-Group Accuracy (WGA) for equity, Attack Success Rate (ASR) for security, and standard test Accuracy (ACC) as a sanity check, with the key finding that ACC remains high while WGA and ASR move in opposing directions.

## Method

The paper is a systematic empirical study supplemented by a theoretical impossibility result. Empirically, it measures the Identification-Factor (IDNF) of JTT and GEORGE under three attack types, showing poisons are identified and amplified at rates of 93–100%—often exceeding the rate for legitimate minority samples—boosting ASR from near 0% to 15–97%. It then measures the Elimination-Factor (ELMF) of EPIc and federated defenses (Coordinate Median, Trimmed Mean, SparseFed), showing they remove minority samples at rates comparable to poisons, dropping WGA by up to 15%. Theoretically, Theorem 4.2 proves that under equal-variance and independence assumptions, a loss-based identification model will with high probability assign lower loss to poisons than to any legitimate group, making separation impossible. Finally, the paper attempts to combine EPIc (to filter poisons) with JTT (to amplify minorities) and shows the trade-off persists: achieving <1% ASR requires sacrificing over 35% WGA.

## Results

Group robustness methods (JTT, GEORGE) identify and amplify poison samples at rates of 93.4–100% (IDNF), often exceeding the rate on legitimate minority groups (93.4–96.7%), boosting attack success rates from near-zero (0.3–0.8% in the ideal case) to 20.4–97.7% in the standard case. On the defense side, EPIc reduces Worst-Group Accuracy by 3.2–14.3% (e.g., from 59.0% ideal to 55.8% standard on Waterbirds/DLBD, and from 54.8% ideal to 40.5% standard on CelebA/DLBD) while maintaining low ASR. In federated learning, robust aggregation mechanisms (Median, Trimmed Mean, SparseFed) cause WGA drops of 23.7–55.1% versus FedAvg baseline. Combining EPIc with JTT to achieve <1% ASR requires sacrificing WGA down to 42.3% (from 78.0% without EPIc), confirming the tension is not easily resolved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JTT (Liu et al., 2021) and GEORGE (Sohoni et al., 2020) are the two group robustness methods analyzed; both exhibit the same vulnerability, with JTT showing 98.2–100% IDNF on poisons (vs. 94.6–96.7% on LRG-1) and GEORGE showing 98.5% IDNF on poisons (vs. 93.4% on LRG-1). EPIc (Yang et al., 2022) is the primary centralized poisoning defense evaluated; it achieves near-zero ASR (0.1%) but at the cost of reducing WGA by up to 14.3% (CelebA/DLBD: 54.8% ideal → 40.5% standard). In federated learning, Coordinate Median and Trimmed Mean (Yin et al., 2018) and SparseFed (Panda et al., 2022) all cause substantially larger WGA drops (23.7–55.1%) than ACC drops (0–19%) relative to the FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) baseline, confirming disparate impact on minority groups. AFR (Qiu et al., 2023) on MultiNLI shows the same pattern: ASR increases from 5.3% (ERM) to 14.4% (AFR) at 1% poison, while WGA improves from 70.0% to 78.4%. STRIP (Gao et al., 2019) as a runtime defense cannot separate triggered samples from clean minority samples at any entropy threshold. The paper's impossibility theorem (Theorem 4.2) further establishes that loss-based heuristics cannot distinguish poisons from minority groups under bounded variance conditions, suggesting the tension is fundamental rather than method-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-established research threads—group robustness methods (JTT, GEORGE, group DRO) and poisoning defenses (EPIc, SparseFed, robust aggregation)—and exposes a fundamental tension between them that neither line of work has previously examined. Conceptually, it extends the broader literature on multi-metric trade-offs in ML (e.g., privacy-fairness, robustness-privacy) to the group-robustness vs. poisoning-resilience axis. The impossibility result (Theorem 4.2) is a new theoretical contribution that formalizes why loss-based difficulty heuristics, the common mechanism underlying both group robustness identification and poisoning detection, cannot separate poisons from legitimate minority samples under realistic attack budgets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant: deploying group robustness methods in security-sensitive settings inadvertently amplifies poisoning attacks (ASR from ~0% to 97% on CelebA), while applying poisoning defenses degrades group robustness (WGA drops up to 15%). The impossibility result shows this is not a fixable implementation bug but an inherent limitation of difficulty-based heuristics, ruling out a large class of potential solutions. Open questions include whether base-set-based defenses (third category) can be made to work with group-balanced, poison-free reference sets, and whether entirely new identification mechanisms—decoupled from sample difficulty—can reconcile both objectives. The paper also highlights a broader methodological concern: benchmark-driven evaluation on single metrics can mask such cross-metric trade-offs.
