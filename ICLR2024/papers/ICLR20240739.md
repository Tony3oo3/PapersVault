## Problem

Despite CLIP's strong zero-shot performance across classification, segmentation, and generation, there is limited understanding of what information is actually encoded in its image representations and how individual model components contribute to them. This paper addresses the gap by asking: can we decompose CLIP's image representation into text-explainable directions attributed to specific attention heads and image locations, and can such understanding be used to repair and improve the model?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240739/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB image processed through CLIP-ViT (split into N non-overlapping patches plus a class token) and a pool of 3,498 ChatGPT-generated text descriptions whose CLIP text-encoder representations serve as candidate directions in the joint vision-language space. Outputs include: (1) a per-head set of m text descriptions (m up to 60) that span each head's output subspace, (2) per-patch similarity heatmaps measuring how much each image location writes along a given text direction, and (3) head-specific inner-product similarity scores enabling property-based image retrieval. All quantities live in the d'-dimensional joint text-image space, so interpretation is done natively via text without any external labeling.

## Method

The core technique exploits three structural properties of CLIP-ViT: residual connections (output is a sum of layer contributions), multi-head attention (each layer's output is a sum over heads and image tokens), and the joint vision-language space (directions can be labeled with text). Via mean-ablation, the paper first shows that only the last 4 MSA layers carry significant direct effects, then decomposes their outputs into per-head, per-token terms c_{i,l,h}. The key algorithm, TEXTSPAN, greedily selects text descriptions from a candidate pool that maximize explained variance of each head's output across a dataset, projecting away already-selected directions to ensure orthogonality. This yields a text-labeled basis per head revealing property-specific roles (e.g., shape, color, geolocation, counting). The spatial decomposition (contracting over heads and layers to get per-token contributions) produces a zero-shot segmenter that outperforms prior CLIP-based methods, while the joint head-token decomposition validates text labels by showing that, e.g., triangular regions drive the 'isosceles triangle' direction.

## Results

On ImageNet-segmentation, the proposed image-token decomposition achieves 75.21% Pixel Accuracy, 54.50% mIoU, and 81.61% mAP, outperforming all five baselines across every metric. On the Waterbirds dataset, ablating property-specific heads identified by TEXTSPAN improves worst-group accuracy from 47.7% to 72.9% for ViT-L-14 (a 25.2-point gain). The TEXTSPAN algorithm shows that 60 text descriptions per head suffice to approximate CLIP's 768-dimensional representation, retaining 72.77% zero-shot ImageNet accuracy versus 75.25% base.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's image-token decomposition outperforms all five CLIP-based zero-shot segmentation baselines on ImageNet-segmentation. LRP (Binder et al., 2016) is the weakest baseline at 52.81% Pixel Acc and 33.57% mIoU, while partial-LRP (Voita et al., 2019) and rollout (Abnar & Zuidema, 2020) are comparable to each other (~61% Pixel Acc, ~40.7% mIoU). GradCAM (Selvaraju et al., 2017) achieves the highest Pixel Accuracy among baselines (70.27%) but lags in mAP (70.30) due to less precise boundary localization. The strongest baseline, Chefer et al. (2021), reaches 69.21% Pixel Acc and 78.29% mAP, yet is still surpassed by 6.0 points in Pixel Acc, 7.0 points in mIoU, and 3.3 points in mAP by the proposed method. The decomposition's advantage stems from directly attributing each image patch's contribution to the text direction, rather than relying on gradient-based or attention-rollout approximations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability framework of Elhage et al. (2021) for decomposing transformer attention into per-head and per-token contributions, and adopts the mean-ablation evaluation technique from Nanda et al. (2023), but applies both to CLIP-ViT in a way that exploits the model's joint vision-language space. Unlike prior vision interpretability methods that produce spatial heatmaps (GradCAM, LRP) or invert features to image space, TEXTSPAN uses CLIP's own text encoder to label latent directions in each attention head, enabling interpretation of non-spatial properties like shape, color, and object count. The paper also departs from prior text-based neuron interpretation work (Hernandez et al., Yuksekgonul et al.) by leveraging CLIP's intrinsic architecture (residual connections, attention) for structured decomposition rather than projecting features onto an external concept bank.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a scalable, architecture-aware interpretability method can yield directly actionable insights: ablating a handful of TEXTSPAN-identified heads reduces spurious correlations on Waterbirds by 25% without retraining, and the spatial decomposition produces state-of-the-art zero-shot segmentation. Key limitations include the analysis being restricted to direct (not indirect) layer effects, the finding that not all heads have coherent single-property roles, and the method being specific to ViT-based CLIP architectures. Open questions include whether similar decompositions work for ResNet-based CLIP, how to interpret heads that only make sense in collaboration, and whether the identified head roles are stable across training runs or datasets.
