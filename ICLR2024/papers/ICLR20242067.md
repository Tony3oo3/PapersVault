## Problem

While generalized cross-validation (GCV) is known to provide consistent risk estimates for unsketched ridge regression, it was unclear whether GCV remains consistent when applied to ensembles of independently sketched ridge predictors, where the sketching introduces implicit regularization and additional variance. Furthermore, prior GCV consistency results were limited to residual-based (squared) risk functionals, leaving open whether GCV could consistently estimate general subquadratic risks (e.g., classification error, Huber loss) or support distributional inference such as prediction intervals.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training data matrix X (n×p), response vector y, a collection of K independent sketching matrices S_k ∈ R^{p×q} satisfying asymptotic freeness (e.g., CountSketch, SRDCT), and hyperparameters λ (which may be zero or negative) and α = q/p. Beyond scalar risk estimates, the method produces a full empirical distribution of GCV-corrected predictions P̂_λ^ens over (y_i, corrected_prediction_i) pairs, from which one can read off prediction intervals, classification error, and any pseudo-Lipschitz functional. The GCV correction divides each training prediction by (1 − tr[L_ens]/n) and subtracts a scaled copy of y_i, making the corrected predictions 'free lunch' computationally since the trace is available from the same decomposition used for fitting.

## Method

The core mechanism is a bias-variance decomposition (Theorem 2) showing that the squared risk of a K-member sketched ridge ensemble equals the risk of an equivalent unsketched ridge regressor at an implicit regularization level μ (determined by the S-transform of SS^⊤) plus a variance inflation term μ′Δ/K. GCV consistency for squared risk (Theorem 3) follows by proving the corresponding GCV inflation factor μ″ is asymptotically equal to μ′, a result that is agnostic to the specific sketch family. For general subquadratic risks, the authors extend GCV via a plug-in construction of GCV-corrected predictions and prove consistency under pseudo-Lipschitz-of-order-2 conditions (Theorem 4), yielding Wasserstein-2 distributional convergence (Corollary 5). An 'ensemble trick' (Eq. 11) exploits the linear-in-1/K structure to recover the unsketched ridge risk from two ensemble sizes, enabling efficient tuning of full-data ridge using only small sketched ensembles. All results hold under asymptotic freeness (a free-probability condition generalizing rotational invariance) and require no model on the response-feature relationship.

## Results

The paper establishes theoretical consistency of GCV for sketched ridge ensembles under asymptotic freeness, with a bias-variance decomposition showing the sketched risk equals an equivalent unsketched implicit ridge risk plus a variance term scaling as 1/K. Empirically, on RCV1 (n=20000, p=30617, q=515) and RNA-Seq (n=356, p=20223, q=99), GCV estimates closely match true test risk for both squared error and classification error, while 2-fold CV provides noticeably poorer estimates—especially for the small-n RNA-Seq dataset. The 'ensemble trick' (using K=1 and K=2 GCV estimates to eliminate the variance term) yields consistent ridge risk estimates even when GCV alone is inconsistent (e.g., for observation sketching).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary empirical comparison is between GCV and 2-fold cross-validation (CV) on real datasets. On both RCV1 and RNA-Seq, GCV (dashed curves) tracks the true test risk (solid curves) closely across the full range of regularization λ, while 2-fold CV (dotted curves) deviates substantially, particularly for less positive or negative λ values. The gap is most pronounced on RNA-Seq (n=356), where 2-fold CV is heavily exaggerated due to the small sample size. A second comparison is between feature sketching and observation sketching: GCV is provably and empirically consistent for feature sketching but inconsistent for observation sketching at finite K (Proposition 7). However, the ensemble trick in equation (11) recovers consistent ridge risk estimation in both settings, since it eliminates the sketching variance term that causes the inconsistency. The paper does not compare against other risk estimation methods (e.g., ALO, bootstrap) in a head-to-head numerical table; the comparisons are primarily GCV vs. k-fold CV and feature vs. observation sketching.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on LeJeune et al. (2022), whose free sketching equivalence (Theorem 1) establishes that sketched regularized inverses are asymptotically equivalent to unsketched implicitly regularized inverses, and on Patil et al. (2021), which proved GCV consistency for unsketched high-dimensional ridge regression. The key conceptual step is combining these two results to show that the bias-variance decomposition of sketched ensemble risk inherits GCV consistency, extending the scope from single sketched predictors to ensembles of arbitrary size K and from residual-based risks to general subquadratic functionals. The 'ensemble trick' for estimating unsketched ridge risk via small sketched ensembles generalizes a narrower subsampling result from LeJeune et al. (2020) to all asymptotically free sketches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, computationally cheap method for tuning sketched ridge ensembles (regularization and sketch size) without expensive cross-validation, and the Wasserstein-2 distributional consistency enables valid prediction intervals from training data alone. The negative result—that GCV is inconsistent for observation sketching at finite ensemble size—is an important cautionary finding that clarifies the asymmetry between feature and observation sketching. Limitations include restriction to isotropic ridge regression and linear models; the authors outline extensions to anisotropic regularization and GLMs via IRLS but leave these as future work. The asymptotic freeness assumption, while broad, is not yet verified for all practical sketches (e.g., no closed-form S-transform is known for CountSketch).
