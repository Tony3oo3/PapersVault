## Problem

Mutual Information Skill Learning (MISL) is the dominant approach for unsupervised skill discovery, but prior theoretical analysis (Eysenbach et al., 2022) only characterized adaptation cost from the average state distribution of all skills, not from individual learned skills as practical algorithms actually do. This paper shows that the diversity and separability of learned skills are fundamentally critical for downstream task adaptation, yet MISL alone does not guarantee these properties, leaving a gap between what MISL discovers and what is needed for effective task initialization.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on infinite-horizon MDPs with discrete state and action spaces, where the feasible set of state occupancy measures forms a convex polytope on the probability simplex. The skill latent z is a discrete variable, and the method produces a skill-conditioned policy π(a|s,z) along with per-skill state distributions p(S|z). Beyond the learned policies, the method outputs theoretical adaptation cost bounds: Worst-case Adaptation Cost (WAC) for the KL-based setting and Mean Adaptation Cost (MAC) for the Wasserstein-based setting, both quantifying the distance from learned skills to the optimal state distribution of an unseen downstream task.

## Method

The paper introduces three complementary contributions grounded in the information geometry of the feasible state distribution polytope. First, LSEPIN = min_z I(S; 1_z) is proposed as a disentanglement metric that captures both informativeness and separability of each individual skill, with a proven monotonic relationship to the Worst-case Adaptation Cost (WAC). Second, WSEP replaces KL divergence with Wasserstein distance, summing pairwise W(p(S|z_i), p(S|z_j)) over all skill pairs; because Wasserstein distance is a true metric satisfying the triangle inequality, WSEP can discover more vertices of the polytope than MISL's maximum-KL 'circle.' Third, the PWSEP algorithm iteratively maximizes the projected Wasserstein distance from each new skill to the convex hull of previously discovered skills, guaranteeing discovery of all |V| vertices of the feasible polytope in |V| iterations and solving the open vertex-discovery problem from Eysenbach et al. (2022).

## Results

This is a primarily theoretical paper with no standard empirical benchmark tables. The key quantitative results are theoretical bounds: Theorem 3.1 shows that higher I(S;1_z) yields a tighter upper bound IC_z on adaptation cost, and Corollary 3.1.1 provides a WAC upper bound in terms of C_z(r) and D_z(r). Theorem 3.4 upper-bounds the Mean Adaptation Cost (MAC) as (ΣL_V^z − (|Z*|−1)·WSEP) / (|V\Z*|·|Z*|), showing that larger WSEP tightens the bound. Theorem 3.5 proves that the PWSEP algorithm discovers all |V| vertices of the feasible polytope in exactly |V| iterations, solving the open vertex-discovery problem from Eysenbach et al. (2022).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper makes no empirical baseline comparisons; all comparisons are theoretical. Against MISL (Eysenbach et al., 2019, 2022), the paper shows that MISL alone does not guarantee diversity and separability of learned skills (Fig. 2 example with concyclic vertices), and that MISL is limited to discovering vertices on a maximum 'circle' (Remark 3.2.1), missing vertices like p^{r_z1} in Fig. 3. WSEP overcomes this limitation by not depending on a fixed 'circle' radius, enabling discovery of all 5 vertices in the Fig. 1b example where MISL finds only 3. PWSEP further improves on WSEP by guaranteeing no duplicate vertices across iterations (Appendix G.7 shows WSEP can produce duplicates). Against KL-based alternatives, the paper argues (Remark 3.4.2, Appendix G.8) that replacing Wasserstein distance with KL divergence in WSEP (yielding KLSEP) fails to promote diversity because KL does not satisfy the triangle inequality. Against successor-feature methods like SFOLS (Alegre et al., 2022), PWSEP discovers all vertices with exactly |V| skills rather than an over-complete set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-geometric analysis of MISL from Eysenbach et al. (2022) by analyzing the practical adaptation procedure (initializing from individual learned skills rather than the average state distribution) and showing that MISL alone does not guarantee the diversity and separability properties critical for downstream task adaptation. It introduces LSEPIN as a disentanglement metric complementing the mutual information objective, drawing on disentanglement concepts from representation learning (Do & Tran, 2019b; Kim et al., 2021) but adapting them to the skill-conditioned state distribution setting. The paper then departs from the KL-divergence-based geometry entirely, replacing it with Wasserstein distance to derive WSEP and PWSEP, which provide theoretical justification for the empirical success of Wasserstein-based skill learning methods (He et al., 2022; Durugkar et al., 2021) and solve the open 'vertex discovery' problem posed by Eysenbach et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical connection between skill disentanglement metrics and downstream adaptation cost for the practical initialization-from-skill procedure, answering a fundamental question about what properties of learned skills actually matter for URL. The finding that MISL can produce inseparable skills even at optimality, and that Wasserstein-distance-based objectives can discover all polytope vertices (solving an open problem), offers a principled path toward more complete skill discovery. However, the contribution is primarily theoretical with practical algorithm design deferred to appendices, and the choice of Wasserstein transport cost requires strong prior knowledge of the environment, limiting immediate practical adoption. Future work on learning representations where standard transport costs (e.g., L2) accurately reflect state-space difficulty remains an important open direction.
