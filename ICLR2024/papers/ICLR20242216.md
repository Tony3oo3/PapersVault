## Problem

Diffusion-based text-to-image models consistently produce blurred, unreadable, or incorrect characters in generated images due to three gaps: lack of large-scale OCR-annotated training data, character-blind text encoders (e.g., CLIP's vocabulary tokenizer), and absence of dedicated supervision for text regions. No prior work addressed multilingual visual text generation, with existing methods limited to English or a single non-Latin language.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text caption with target text enclosed in double quotation marks, user-specified position regions (bounding boxes or arbitrary shapes for curved/irregular text), and optionally an input image for editing mode. Glyph images are rendered at 1024×1024, position maps at 512×512, and OCR feature inputs at 80×512. Output is a 512×512 image with accurately rendered text at the designated positions, supporting up to 5 text lines of up to 20 characters each, in languages including Chinese, English, Japanese, Korean, Arabic, Bangla, and Hindi.

## Method

AnyText builds on ControlNet with a trainable TextControlNet (360M params) alongside a frozen UNet, adding only 0.34% parameters. The auxiliary latent module fuses three conditions—rendered glyph image, position map, and masked image—through separate downsampling blocks (convolutional for glyph/position, VAE encoder for masked image) into a single latent feature z_a that guides text placement and style. The text embedding module replaces placeholder tokens in the caption with stroke-level features extracted by a pre-trained PP-OCRv3 recognition model from individually rendered glyph lines, then encodes the combined sequence through CLIP, enabling language-agnostic text rendering. Training uses a composite objective: standard text-control diffusion loss plus a text perceptual loss that decodes predicted latents to image space and applies MSE on OCR feature maps within text regions only, weighted by φ(t)=ᾱ_t. A progressive schedule disables editing for the first 5 epochs, activates it at σ=0.5 for 3 epochs, then introduces perceptual loss (λ=0.01) for the final 2 epochs.

## Results

On the AnyText-benchmark, AnyText-v1.1 achieves the best English text generation accuracy with 72.4% Sentence Accuracy and 0.876 NED, while also attaining the lowest English FID of 33.54. For Chinese text generation, AnyText-v1.1 reaches 69.2% Sentence Accuracy and 0.840 NED, substantially outperforming all baselines; AnyText-v1.0 achieves the best Chinese FID of 28.46. The model was trained on only 1.39M English images, yet surpasses TextDiffuser and GlyphControl which were trained on 10M pure English images.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AnyText-v1.1 outperforms all four external baselines on every metric for both English and Chinese. Against ControlNet (the strongest English baseline at 58.4% Sen. ACC), AnyText-v1.1 gains +14.0 points in English Sen. ACC and +33.0 points in Chinese Sen. ACC (69.2% vs. 36.2%). TextDiffuser and GlyphControl are essentially unable to generate Chinese text (Sen. ACC of 6.1% and 3.3–4.5% respectively), producing only scattered Latin characters or punctuation within Chinese strings, while AnyText achieves 69.2%. In English, GlyphControl† (trained on 10M images) scores only 37.1% Sen. ACC, well below AnyText-v1.1's 72.4% despite AnyText using 7× less English training data. Qualitatively, the paper also compares against SD-XL1.0, DALL-E 2, Bing Image Creator, and DeepFloyd IF, noting that while these general-purpose models have improved, a considerable gap remains versus dedicated text-generation models like AnyText, particularly in text-background integration (e.g., carved stone, reflections, chalkboard styles).

## Relation to Prior Work

AnyText is architecturally built on ControlNet, adopting its modular, plug-and-play design so that a frozen base diffusion model (e.g., SD1.5) can be augmented with a trainable TextControlNet branch. Conceptually, it extends the glyph-conditioning lineage of GlyphDraw and GlyphControl by adding explicit position maps and masked-image conditions to support curved/irregular text regions and unified generation-plus-editing. The key departure from prior text-encoder approaches (CLIP text encoder, CLIP vision encoder, character-level segmentation) is the use of a lightweight pre-trained OCR recognition model (PP-OCRv3) to encode rendered glyph strokes, which the authors argue is what enables multilingual generation without language-specific fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AnyText is the first framework to handle multilingual visual text generation (Chinese, English, Japanese, Korean, Arabic, etc.) and simultaneously supports text editing, filling a gap that prior methods (limited to English or single-language Chinese) could not address. The release of AnyWord-3M (3M multilingual image-text pairs with OCR annotations) and AnyText-benchmark provides the field with the first large-scale training resource and standardized evaluation protocol for this task. Open questions remain around extremely small fonts, fine-grained attribute control (font style, weight, color), and whether the OCR-encoder trick generalizes to scripts with very different visual structures. The paper also reveals that OCR annotation quality (11.8% error rate for Chinese in v1.0) is a critical bottleneck, suggesting data curation will remain a first-order concern for this task.
