## Problem

Certifying polynomial nonnegativity and computing polynomial minima via semidefinite programming scales poorly in degree and dimension, motivating learned alternatives. While equivariant architectures are well-established for compact symmetry groups, the non-compact group SL(2,R) — which naturally governs area-preserving changes of variables in bivariate polynomial problems — has no known universal equivariant architecture. This paper is the first to apply machine learning to these polynomial tasks and to confront the fundamental limitations of equivariant learning under non-compact symmetries.

## Input / Output

For positivity verification, the input is a bivariate homogeneous polynomial of degree 2d represented as a (2d+1)-dimensional coefficient vector in a monomial basis, and the output is a (d+1)×(d+1) symmetric PSD matrix Q (the analytic center of the feasible region) satisfying p(x) = x^{[d]T} Q x^{[d]}; this certificate is independently verifiable. For minimization, the input is an inhomogeneous bivariate polynomial decomposed into binary forms of degrees 0 through d fed as multiple channels, and the output is the minimum value (a scalar invariant) and the minimizer coordinates (an equivariant vector). Inputs are L2-normalized and outputs rescaled to enforce 1-homogeneity.

## Method

The paper constructs the first exactly SL(2,R)-equivariant architecture (SL2Net) by taking pairwise tensor products of input irreps, decomposing them via the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition (classical transvectant), applying learned linear combinations within each irrep space, and routing degree-0 invariants through an MLP. However, the central theoretical contribution is a proof (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) that no sequence of SL(2,R)-equivariant polynomials times invariants can approximate the analytic-center function f, due to an algebraic obstruction: balanced monomials mod b are preserved by the transvectant, forcing incorrect sparsity patterns in the output matrix. Consequently, the paper empirically compares three alternatives — SL2Net, an SO(2,R)-equivariant network (SO2Net) operating on Fourier modes, and MLPs with data augmentation over well-conditioned SL(2,R) subsets — and finds that augmentation and SO(2,R) equivariance outperform full SL(2,R) equivariance, yielding ~10× speedups over SDP solvers while maintaining high accuracy.

## Results

On the positivity verification task, the trained MLP achieves NMSE of 9.5e-6 (degree 6) to 1.1e-5 (degree 14), while the SCS first-order SDP solver achieves NMSE of 2.7e-5 (degree 6) to 1.2e-3 (degree 14) relative to Mosek ground truth. The MLP is approximately two orders of magnitude faster: 0.062 min vs. 3.50 min at degree 6 (~56× speedup) and 0.29 min vs. 37.4 min at degree 14 (~129× speedup) for 5,000 test examples on a single CPU. Among equivariant architectures, the MLP with rotation-near augmentations and the SO2Net achieve the best test errors on the untransformed test set, while the fully SL(2,R)-equivariant SL2Net underperforms all alternatives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the SCS first-order conic solver (O'Donoghue et al., 2016), with ground truth labels from the second-order Mosek solver. The trained MLP dominates SCS on both accuracy and speed across all tested degrees (6–14): at degree 6 the MLP achieves 9.5e-6 NMSE versus SCS's 2.7e-5 (~3× lower error) in 0.062 min versus 3.50 min (~56× faster), and at degree 14 the gap widens to 1.1e-5 vs. 1.2e-3 NMSE (~100× lower error) and 0.29 min vs. 37.4 min (~129× faster). The speed advantage grows with degree, consistent with the paper's motivation that SDP solvers scale poorly in degree. Among the equivariant architectures tested (SL2Net, SO2Net, augmented MLPs), the fully SL(2,R)-equivariant SL2Net is the most equivariant but achieves the worst test error, while the MLP with well-conditioned SL(2,R) augmentations and the SO2Net (SO(2,R)-equivariant) perform best on the untransformed test set, validating the paper's theoretical impossibility result that full SL(2,R) equivariance is not achievable by polynomial-based architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the non-compact group equivariant architecture of Bogatskiy et al. (2020) for the Lorentz group to the special case of SL(2,R), where binary form inputs lie precisely in the group's finite-dimensional irreps and the Clebsch-Gordan decomposition is given by the classical transvectant. It departs from the broader equivariant learning literature (Cohen & Welling, Kondor, Thomas) by confronting the fundamental difficulties of non-compact groups: no finite Haar measure precludes group-averaging invariants, and the paper proves that the standard tensor-product construction, while universal over equivariant polynomials, fails to be universal over equivariant functions. On the application side, it is the first to apply neural networks to polynomial positivity verification and minimization, tasks previously addressed exclusively by semidefinite programming solvers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result (Corollary 1) is of independent interest: it shows that no sequence of SL(2,R)-equivariant polynomials times invariants can approximate the analytic-center certificate function, undermining the standard polynomial-approximation route to universality for non-compact groups. Practically, a simple MLP achieves ~10× speedup over SCS while matching or beating its accuracy, and the finding that data augmentation over well-conditioned SL(2,R) subsets outperforms the fully equivariant SL2Net provides a cautionary lesson for any problem with non-compact symmetries. Open questions include whether frame averaging (Puny et al., 2021) can sidestep the polynomial-approximation roadblock for SL(2,R), whether better-conditioned bases exist for induced representations, and whether these results extend to higher dimensions or other non-compact groups.
