## Problem

Despite fine-tuning being the dominant strategy for adapting pretrained models to downstream tasks and for safety alignment, it remains unclear whether fine-tuning creates genuinely new capabilities or merely modulates existing ones. This paper asks: does fine-tuning delete, amplify, or minimally transform the capabilities a model already possesses from pretraining, and what are the practical implications (e.g., for safety) of the answer?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240395/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained transformer (either a Tracr-compiled model with a precisely encoded capability or a minGPT model trained on PCFG-generated strings) and fine-tunes it on a downstream task with a controlled spurious correlation embedded in the data. Rather than producing a new model as the primary output, the analysis produces diagnostic evidence: the minimal set of pruned neurons/weights that restores pretraining behavior, per-layer linear probe accuracy for pretraining features, attention map visualizations, and reverse fine-tuning (reFT) accuracy curves quantifying how many gradient steps are needed to 'revive' a seemingly lost capability.

## Method

The paper uses two controlled experimental setups—Tracr-compiled transformers (where capabilities are perfectly encoded by construction) and minGPT models trained on probabilistic context-free grammars (where capabilities are learned, allowing study of pretraining design choices)—to fine-tune on downstream tasks with embedded spurious correlations. The core analytical contribution combines four mechanistic interpretability tools: (i) single-step network pruning guided by gradient-weight dot products to identify the minimal neuron set whose removal restores pretraining capability; (ii) linear probes trained on intermediate layer outputs to verify that pretraining features persist after fine-tuning; (iii) attention map visualization (especially interpretable for Tracr models); and (iv) a novel 'reverse fine-tuning' (reFT) protocol that further trains the fine-tuned model on pretraining data at an even smaller learning rate to measure how sample-efficiently the original capability is revived. The key finding is that fine-tuning learns a highly localized 'wrapper' transformation on top of the pretraining capability rather than modifying it, and this wrapper can be undone by pruning just 5–15 neurons or by a few hundred reFT steps, with implications for the fragility of safety alignment.

## Results

In the PCFG counter setup, pruning only 5–15 neurons from a fine-tuned model is sufficient to fully revive pretraining task accuracy (counting O_PT), while simultaneously degrading fine-tuning task performance. Reverse fine-tuning (reFT) recovers the pretraining capability in 0.1–1K iterations when the capability is strongly relevant, ~3K iterations when weakly relevant, compared to 4.5K iterations for the scratch+fine-tune (Scr.+FT) baseline. On TinyStories-Instruct, all fine-tuning protocols (Filtering, Filtering+Randomisation, Filtering+Mix & Match) recover the 'deleted' twist capability to 75–94% twist proportion by 3000 reFT iterations, substantially outpacing the 'Not in PT' control (81% at 3000 iterations) that never saw the capability during pretraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between reverse fine-tuning (reFT) and a scratch+fine-tune (Scr.+FT) baseline, where the model is reinitialized from pretraining weights and fine-tuned on the pretraining task. reFT is substantially more sample-efficient: it reaches perfect pretraining accuracy in 0.1–1K iterations (strong relevance) or ~3K iterations (weak relevance), while Scr.+FT requires 4.5K iterations and a larger learning rate (η_M) to reach the same level. On TinyStories, all three fine-tuning protocols (Filtering, Filtering+Randomisation, Filtering+Mix & Match) recover the 'deleted' twist capability within 30–300 reFT iterations, whereas the 'Not in PT' control model (which never saw twist data during pretraining) lags behind at every iteration checkpoint, reaching only 81% twist proportion at 3000 iterations versus 75–94% for the fine-tuned models. The paper also compares different spurious correlation levels (C_Tr ∈ {0.0, 0.5, 0.8, 1.0}) and sampling priors (P_T^L, P_T^M, P_T^H), finding that weakly relevant capabilities (low sampling prior) are most susceptible to wrapper formation, with pretraining accuracy dropping most sharply under high spurious correlation. No external method baselines are compared; the study is mechanistic rather than a method benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability line of work on synthetic language tasks (Allen-Zhu & Li, 2023c; Lindner et al., 2023) by applying it to a previously underexplored question: what fine-tuning actually does to a pretrained model's internal capabilities. Prior theoretical analyses of fine-tuning (Lampinen & Ganguli, 2018; Tripuraneni et al., 2020) relied on strong assumptions like linear model classes or kernel views, while behavioral evaluations (Lovering et al., 2021) could not distinguish between capability modification and capability modulation. The paper also aligns with concurrent claims by Kotha et al. (2023) and Prakash et al. (2024) that fine-tuning is unlikely to alter capabilities, but provides the first mechanistic evidence via pruning, probing, attention maps, and a novel reverse fine-tuning (reFT) protocol in controlled settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that fine-tuning learns a thin, localized 'wrapper' rather than modifying underlying capabilities has direct implications for AI safety: if alignment via fine-tuning (e.g., RLHF) only adds a wrapper, then superficially unrelated downstream fine-tuning can inadvertently remove safety constraints, and 'deleted' capabilities can be revived in very few gradient steps. The TinyStories validation on a more realistic language model strengthens the claim, but the core evidence comes from small models (~3M params) on procedural tasks, leaving open whether the wrapper phenomenon persists at the scale of modern LLMs. A key open question the paper highlights is how to develop methods that genuinely delete or substantially alter capabilities, rather than merely wrapping them.
