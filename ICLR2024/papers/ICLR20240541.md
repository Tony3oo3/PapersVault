## Problem

The mechanisms underpinning neural network pruning remain poorly understood: why different pruning methods achieve similar performance at different sparsity levels, why pruning before training fails, why some models tolerate more pruning than others, and what the maximum prunable amount is for a given model. This paper addresses all four questions from the perspective of global unstructured magnitude pruning followed by one epoch of fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method takes the flattened parameter vector (all weights and biases concatenated) of a trained CNN and computes the optimal L1-pruning percentage by constructing the Pareto front of cosine similarity versus sparsity for that specific parameter distribution. The output is both the computed optimal pruning percentage and the resulting pruned network after one epoch of fine-tuning. A diagnostic step computes the kurtosis of layer-wise kurtoses to flag networks (e.g., MobileNetV2 at κ²=64.40) where global pruning risks disconnecting entire low-magnitude layers, in which case the optimal percentage is conservatively scaled by 1/ln(κ²).

## Method

The paper proves (Theorem 1) that L1 magnitude pruning—zeroing the smallest-magnitude parameters—is the unique method that maximizes cosine similarity between parent and pruned parameter vectors. It then validates empirically, via t-SNE projections of model predictions and radial loss-landscape slices, that cosine similarity is a reliable proxy for functional similarity, meaning high-cosine-similarity pruned networks remain in the same low-loss manifold as the parent. The practical contribution reframes pruning as a multi-objective optimization: the Pareto front of cosine similarity versus pruning percentage for a given parameter distribution has a utopia point (100% pruned, cosine similarity 1), and the optimal pruning is the closest point on the front to this utopia. The distance to utopia decreases monotonically with the kurtosis of the parameter distribution, explaining why high-kurtosis models (VGG11_BN, kurtosis 8.53) tolerate far more pruning than low-kurtosis ones (ResNet18, kurtosis 3.79). The loss-landscape analysis further reveals that networks are 'brittle'—disproportionately sensitive to perturbations of high-magnitude parameters—which explains why random pruning of even 2% of parameters causes a 484-unit ℓ∞ change in the loss surface while L1-pruning of 70% causes only a 65-unit change.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that at a fixed cosine similarity target of 0.99 with the parent VGG11_BN network, L1 pruning achieves 70.06% sparsity while Random pruning achieves only 1.99% sparsity, yet both maintain similar accuracy. The loss landscape distortion (ℓ∞ distance from the parent surface) is 65.28 for L1-pruned vs. 484.03 for Random-pruned, a 7.4× difference. The optimal pruning percentage is determined by the parameter distribution's kurtosis: VGG11_BN (kurtosis 8.53) can be pruned more aggressively than LeNet_Small (4.82) or ResNet18 (3.79). On ImageNet-pretrained models, the method works well for VGG11 and ResNet18 but fails for MobileNetV2 (kurtosis of kurtoses = 64.40), where very-low-magnitude layers get entirely pruned away.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two unstructured global pruning strategies: L1 (magnitude-based) pruning and Random pruning, both followed by one epoch of fine-tuning. L1 pruning is shown to be strictly superior: at the same cosine similarity target of 0.99, L1 achieves 70.06% sparsity on VGG11_BN versus only 1.99% for Random, while both preserve accuracy. The loss landscape analysis (Figure 5) reveals that Random pruning, despite removing far fewer parameters, causes a 7.4× larger distortion in the loss landscape (ℓ∞ distance 484.03 vs. 65.28), confirming that networks are 'brittle' to changes in high-magnitude parameters. When pruning is normalized by cosine similarity rather than percentage, the accuracy gap between Random and L1 narrows substantially (Figure 2 vs. Figure 1), supporting the paper's thesis that cosine similarity—not sparsity percentage—is the governing variable. The method is validated at scale on ImageNet-pretrained VGG11 and ResNet18, where it performs well, but fails on MobileNetV2 due to its extreme kurtosis of kurtoses (64.40), where entire low-magnitude layers are pruned away, disconnecting the network.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes ideas from the pruning literature (LeCun et al., 1989; Blalock et al., 2020), loss landscape analysis (Fort et al., 2020; Draxler et al., 2018; Garipov et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018b), and functional similarity measures into a unified theoretical framework. Rather than proposing a new pruning algorithm, it reframes the existing L1 magnitude pruning method through the lens of cosine similarity, proving its optimality (Theorem 1) and connecting prunability to the kurtosis of the parameter distribution. The use of t-SNE functional space visualizations and radial loss landscape slices follows directly from Fort et al. (2020), while the mode-connectivity interpretation of why high-cosine-similarity pruned networks recover after fine-tuning draws on Draxler et al. (2018) and Garipov et al. (2018).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, computable method to determine the optimal pruning percentage for a given trained network (via the Pareto front closest to utopia), which is practically useful for practitioners who want to know how far they can push sparsity. The kurtosis-of-kurtoses diagnostic for detecting networks with very-low-magnitude layers (like MobileNetV2) that can be catastrophically disconnected by global L1 pruning is a valuable practical caveat. However, the scope is limited to global unstructured magnitude pruning with only one epoch of fine-tuning, and the kurtosis-of-kurtoses adjustment (1/ln κ²) is based on only three ImageNet models, leaving open whether the framework extends to structured pruning, iterative pruning, or transformer architectures. The question of whether cosine similarity remains a valid functional proxy at very high sparities or for non-convex architectures remains unexplored.
