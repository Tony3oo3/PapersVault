## Problem

Despite LMs increasingly being deployed as interactive agents, their evaluation remains predominantly static, failing to capture multi-turn reasoning, cross-model dynamics, and realistic economic constraints while remaining vulnerable to training-data contamination. This paper argues that static benchmarks cannot co-evolve with rapidly improving LMs and that the intertwined nature of performance and alignment in LM agents demands a unified, dynamic evaluation framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240032/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each LM agent is initialized with a game setting description, a private payoff table (never shared with the opponent), negotiation protocol rules, and a role description. At every turn the agent produces a private mental note (≤64 words) containing a JSON-formatted acceptable-offer summary, followed by a public message (≤64 words) directed at the opponent; the full message history and the single most recent note are provided as context. Outputs are structured as agreement rates (soft = aligned internal states, hard = soft + both parties uttering a hard-coded phrase), normalized total and agreed-only payoffs U and U* in [0,1], internal/external faithfulness fractions, instruction-following scores for word limits and JSON formatting, and the number of rounds consumed (capped at 10).

## Method

The benchmark is built from structured negotiation games whose complexity is modulated by the number of issues, the mix of distributive (zero-sum) versus compatible (aligned-interest) issues, and the introduction of integrative preference weights that create trade-off opportunities. Two LM agents alternate turns, each generating a private note and a public message under fixed prompts; the protocol explicitly probes internal faithfulness (does the note's stated acceptable offer match the subsequent public offer?) and external faithfulness / theory-of-mind (does the agent correctly predict the opponent's acceptable offer before making its own?). Experiments are run in both self-play (an LM against an independent instance of itself) and cross-play (each model against every other qualifying model), with debiasing achieved by swapping sides and starting positions and averaging outcomes. Six publicly available LMs (gpt-3.5, gpt-4, chat-bison, claude-2, command, command-light, LLaMA-2) are evaluated across single- and two-issue games, with results showing that only closed-source models pass a minimum qualifying threshold, cooperative bargaining is the hardest game type, and the most powerful model (gpt-4) paradoxically underperforms weaker opponents in negotiation outcomes despite superior faithfulness and instruction-following.

## Results

In self-play, gpt-4 achieves the highest faithfulness (0.91 internal, 0.92 external) and perfect instruction-following (1.00 across all metrics), yet ranks near the bottom for soft agreement rate (0.28) and requires the most rounds (9.58). gpt-3.5 is the most efficient negotiator (6.34 avg. rounds) with a 0.46 soft agreement rate, while claude-2 posts the highest self-play agreement rate (0.61 soft) but fails instruction-following (0.08 note, 0.09 msg). In cross-play, gpt-3.5 emerges as the overall strongest negotiator, leading most categories in both competitive and cooperative games, while gpt-4 underperforms relative to its self-play faithfulness advantage. Only closed-source models passed the minimum qualifier; LLaMA 2 models (7B, 13B, 70B) all failed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks six LMs (gpt-3.5, gpt-4, claude-2, command, command-light, chat-bison) plus LLaMA 2 (7B/13B/70B) against each other in structured negotiation games. gpt-4 dominates on alignment metrics—achieving 0.91/0.92 faithfulness and 1.00 instruction-following—yet lags in negotiation outcomes, posting only 0.28 soft agreement in self-play and ranking near the bottom in most cross-play payoff categories. gpt-3.5 is the strongest overall cross-play negotiator, leading in both competitive (U*=0.61) and cooperative (U*=0.80) single-issue games and maintaining top-2 rankings in two-issue games. claude-2 excels in self-play agreement rate (0.61 soft) but its performance drops sharply in cross-play, and it consistently fails word-limit instructions (0.08–0.09). chat-bison has the weakest self-play agreement (0.19 soft) but narrows the gap in cross-play, suggesting it benefits from interacting with stronger opponents. LLaMA 2 models of all sizes failed the minimum qualifier (0/10 agreements), making them the only models unable to complete any task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the static LM evaluation paradigm (HELM, BIG-Bench, AGIEval) into the dynamic, multi-agent domain by proposing structured negotiation games as a co-evolving benchmark. It draws on the negotiation-agent literature from multi-agent systems (Bontempo & Iyengar 2008 for game rules; Baarslag et al. 2017; Gratch et al. 2015) and adapts it to LMs, while connecting to recent LM-agent work (Andreas 2022; Park et al. 2023) by focusing on evaluation rather than agent construction. The faithfulness metrics build on chain-of-thought interpretability research (Lanham et al. 2023; Turpin et al. 2023), and the co-evolving benchmark idea aligns with model-written evaluation approaches (Perez et al. 2022, 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LAMEN framework provides a practical, open-source tool for jointly assessing LM alignment and performance in ecologically valid multi-turn settings, addressing data-leakage concerns inherent to static benchmarks. Key open questions include how human negotiation biases transfer to LM agents, the effect of external tools and repeated games on decision-making, and whether the observed gap between faithfulness and negotiation competence persists as models improve. The finding that only closed-source models could complete the tasks at the time of writing highlights a significant capability gap that may narrow with future open-source releases.
