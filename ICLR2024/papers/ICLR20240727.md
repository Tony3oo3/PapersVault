## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks suffer from two fundamental limitations: data contamination (static test sets may overlap with training corpora, conflating memorization with genuine ability) and fixed complexity (benchmarks cannot dynamically scale difficulty to match rapidly improving models). DYVAL addresses both by generating evaluation samples on-the-fly with controllable, tunable complexity rather than relying on a fixed dataset.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240727/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a task specification (one of 7 reasoning tasks across mathematics, logic, and algorithms), complexity constraints (tree depth/width for T-DAGs, node/link counts for G-DAGs, optional extra random links and embedded distractor descriptions), and a random seed to produce 500 natural-language evaluation samples per dataset. Models must output answers in a strict format (e.g., '<<<value>>>'), and numerical answers are scored using relative precision thresholds (0.01% for arithmetic, 0.1% for linear equations). Each sample is generated in one of three node-description orders—topological, reversed topological, or random—adding an additional axis of difficulty.

## Method

DYVAL formalizes dynamic evaluation as A_T = F(G(C)), where G is a stochastic DAG construction algorithm, C encodes task and complexity constraints, and F is a description function that renders the graph into natural language. Two DAG types are used: tree-based DAGs (T-DAGs) for hierarchical tasks (arithmetic, linear equations, boolean/deductive/abductive logic) where leaf nodes hold random values and internal nodes apply random operations, and general DAGs (G-DAGs) for non-linear tasks (reachability, max sum path) where nodes are connected by randomly sampled directed edges. Complexity is modulated through four mechanisms—varying tree depth/width, adjusting node and link counts, injecting extra random links, and embedding random distractor descriptions—and two theorems formally bound the probability of generating identical samples (e.g., ~1e-15 for a depth-4, width-2 arithmetic tree). The generated samples serve dual purposes: as evaluation benchmarks and as fine-tuning data, with experiments showing that fine-tuning Llama2-13B-chat on DYVAL-generated data improves performance on six existing benchmarks (GSM8K, SVAMP, FOLIO, RACO, DP, LCS) without additional curated training data.

## Results

On DYVAL's 7 reasoning tasks across 4 complexity levels (D1–D4), GPT-4 achieves the highest accuracy, ranging from 98–100% on arithmetic D1 down to 77–81% at D4, and from 100% on boolean logic D1 to 96–100% at D4. GPT-3.5-Turbo follows closely (95% → 72% on arithmetic), while Llama2-13B-chat (12% → 3%) and Vicuna-13B-v1.3 (3% → 0%) perform substantially worse. Notably, Flan-T5-large, phi-1.5, WizardMath-13B, and Xwin-13B all score 0 across all tasks. Even GPT-4 struggles on the hardest tasks: only 10–37% on max sum path and 34–58% on linear equations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 consistently outperforms GPT-3.5-Turbo across all 7 tasks, with the gap widening at higher complexity (e.g., 79.3% vs. 72.4% on arithmetic D4, and 63.0% vs. 49.4% on reachability D4). GPT-3.5-Turbo dominates the open-source models by a large margin: on arithmetic D1 it scores 95% versus 12.3% (Llama2-13B-chat) and 2.7% (Vicuna-13B-v1.3). Llama2-13B-chat and Vicuna-13B-v1.3 are closely matched, with Vicuna occasionally outperforming Llama2 on boolean logic (e.g., 77.9% vs. 25.3% at D1) but both collapsing to near-zero on linear equations and max sum path at all complexity levels. The performance gap between GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-Turbo is subtle on simple tasks (D1) but becomes prominent on complex ones (D4), highlighting the value of evolving-complexity evaluation. Four models (Flan-T5-large, phi-1.5, WizardMath-13B, Xwin-13B) score 0 on all DYVAL tasks despite claiming strong results on existing static benchmarks, suggesting data contamination or low training data quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

DYVAL carves new ground by repurposing directed acyclic graph construction (from compiler design and parsing tree literature) as a mechanism for on-the-fly evaluation sample generation, rather than relying on fixed datasets or crowd-sourced dynamic benchmarks like DynaBench and DynaBoard. It positions itself as complementary to existing static benchmarks (HELM, AlpacaEval, Big-Bench) rather than a replacement, and explicitly distinguishes itself from Krause et al.'s earlier 'dynamic evaluation' concept by focusing on controllable complexity and reasoning-specific structure. The graph-informed approach draws on the compiler principle and compositional decomposition ideas from Roy & Roth (2015) and Vinyals et al. (2015), but applies them to LLM evaluation rather than problem solving.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DYVAL addresses two fundamental weaknesses in LLM evaluation—data contamination and static complexity—that have become increasingly critical as models scale. The finding that models claiming strong benchmark performance (phi-1.5, WizardMath-13B, Xwin-13B) score near zero on DYVAL raises serious questions about the validity of static benchmarks and suggests the field needs more dynamic evaluation infrastructure. Open questions include whether DYVAL's DAG-based approach generalizes beyond reasoning tasks to open-ended generation, whether the limited 500-sample evaluation sets are sufficient for reliable conclusions, and how to verify the correctness of GPT-4-generated contextualized descriptions. The dual utility of DYVAL samples as both evaluation and fine-tuning data is a practical contribution that could reduce the cost of building training data for reasoning capabilities.
