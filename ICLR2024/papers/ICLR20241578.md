## Problem

Existing federated learning backdoor attacks and defenses operate at the whole-model level, ignoring that only a small subset of layers (backdoor-critical or BC layers) actually govern the model's backdoor vulnerability. This uniform treatment forces attacks to modify many layers, making them detectable by SOTA distance-based, inversion-based, and sign-based defenses, while no prior work has identified or exploited the BC layer structure to achieve both high backdoor success rate and stealth with as few as 10% malicious clients.

## Input / Output

The attacker receives the current global model weights at each FL round and, on each malicious client, trains two local models (benign on clean data, malicious on poisoned data with trigger-embedded images) before constructing the upload. The crafted update is expressed in layer space: a binary selection vector v determines which layers come from the malicious model versus the averaged benign model, and a continuous hyperparameter λ further blends the selected BC layers toward the benign average to minimize distance-based detection. The final output is a backdoored global model that misclassifies trigger-embedded samples to a targeted label while preserving main-task accuracy.

## Method

The paper introduces Layer Substitution Analysis (LSA), a two-step in-situ identification procedure: forward substitution replaces each layer in the malicious model with its benign counterpart and measures the BSR drop, while backward substitution incrementally copies malicious layers into the benign model until BSR reaches a threshold τ·BSR_malicious, yielding the BC layer set L*. Built on LSA, the Layer-wise Poisoning (LP) attack constructs the malicious update by selecting BC layers from the locally trained malicious model and non-BC layers from the averaged benign model, with an adaptive mechanism that shrinks L* if the crafted model would be rejected by the server's aggregation rule (e.g., MultiKrum or FLAME), and a λ-blending term that pulls BC layer weights toward the benign average for additional stealth. For sign-based defenses like RLR, the Layer-wise Flipping (LF) attack proactively negates the BC layer parameters so that RLR's reversed learning rate restores the original signs, effectively nullifying the defense while leaving non-BC layers untouched to preserve main-task accuracy.

## Results

Under non-IID settings with 10% malicious clients, the LP Attack achieves average BSR of 90.15% on ResNet18/CIFAR-10 under FLAME (vs. 7.22% for BadNets baseline and 3.88% for DBA), and 90.12% under MultiKrum (vs. 3.95% and 5.61% respectively). On VGG19/CIFAR-10, LP Attack reaches 75.93% avg BSR under MultiKrum and 60.72% under FLAME, while baselines drop below 8%. Main task accuracy is preserved or slightly improved (e.g., 72.26% vs. 74.49% for ResNet18 under MultiKrum), and the LF Attack variant achieves 66.12% BSR under RLR on CNN/Fashion-MNIST (vs. 15.09% baseline), demonstrating effectiveness against sign-based defenses.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the BadNets baseline, LP Attack achieves dramatically higher BSR under distance-based defenses: under FLAME on ResNet18/CIFAR-10, LP Attack reaches 90.15% avg BSR versus 7.22% for BadNets, and under MultiKrum it reaches 90.12% versus 3.95%. Against DBA, LP Attack similarly dominates under FLAME (90.15% vs. 3.88% on ResNet18) and MultiKrum (90.12% vs. 5.61%), where DBA's distributed trigger splitting is ineffective. Compared to SRA (Qi et al., 2022), LP Attack achieves 95.21% avg BSR under MultiKrum and 98.17% under FLAME, while SRA drops to 2.61% and 4.36% respectively, because SRA's 3% parameter modification creates detectable distance deviations. Against the Constraint Loss Attack (from 3DFed, Li et al., 2023), LP Attack outperforms under MultiKrum (95.1% vs. 3.1% BSR) and FLARE (88.19% vs. 4.03% BSR), though CL Attack with β=0.1 achieves slightly higher BSR under FLAME (94.1% vs. 89.9%); however, CL Attack is fully detected by MultiKrum (0% MAR) at all β values while LP Attack maintains 94.2% MAR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FL backdoor attack literature (BadNets, DBA, Scaling attack, 3DFed) by introducing the concept of backdoor-critical (BC) layers—a small subset of model layers that dominate backdoor vulnerability. While prior attacks poison the entire model and prior defenses (FLAME, MultiKrum, RLR) operate at the model or parameter level, this work repurposes the layer-substitution idea from memorization studies (Maini et al., 2023) and lottery-ticket-style subnetwork analysis to identify which specific layers carry the backdoor task. The resulting layer-wise poisoning (LP) and layer-wise flipping (LF) attacks are positioned as a finer-grained alternative to model-space attacks like the constraint loss attack from 3DFed, which the paper shows fails under MultiKrum and FLARE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that backdoor vulnerability is concentrated in a small, identifiable subset of layers (e.g., ~9% in ResNet18, just fc1.weight in a 5-layer CNN) provides a new lens for both attack design and defense: defenders could potentially monitor or protect only BC layers rather than the full model. The paper demonstrates that this layer-aware approach bypasses seven SOTA defenses with only 10% malicious clients, a practically concerning result for FL deployments. Limitations include computational cost of per-round BC layer identification, reduced effectiveness under very high non-IID (q≥0.7) with MultiKrum, and acknowledged inability to support large scaling factors. Open questions include whether BC layer identification generalizes to LLM adapters (the authors mention this as future work) and whether adaptive layer-wise defenses can be made effective against LP attacks.
