## Problem

Machine-generated text detectors have been widely deployed in education and journalism, but their robustness to adversarial fine-tuning of the generating model remains unexplored. This paper asks whether a language model can be directly optimized—via reinforcement learning—to produce text that evades these detectors, and how easily such evasion transfers across different detection algorithms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240310/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM (Llama-2 7B/13B or Zephyr 7B), a black-box detector that returns a continuous scalar 'humanness' score, and a set of text prompts (8-token OpenWebText prefixes, Alpaca instructions, essay titles, or Reddit writing prompts). It produces a fine-tuned language model whose generations are near-undetectable (AUROC ≤ 0.5 for several detectors) while increasing OpenWebText perplexity by at most ~1 point. The detector is queried only during training to score paired generations; at inference, the fine-tuned model operates without any detector access.

## Method

The core pipeline uses Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) to fine-tune the language model against a detector's score. For each prompt, two completions are sampled from the base model at temperature 1.0; the detector's humanness score labels the higher-scoring completion as preferred, forming a Bradley-Terry preference pair. The model is then optimized with the DPO objective (Eq. 3), which implicitly maximizes the KL-constrained reward without requiring PPO or explicit reward modeling. A tunable β parameter controls the KL-divergence penalty to the reference model, preventing reward hacking (overly low β produces nonsensical text) while still driving detector AUROC well below 0.5. The approach is data-efficient—only ~10k detector queries suffice—and generalizes: training against one detector (e.g., RoBERTa-large) reduces AUROC by an average of 0.15 across unrelated commercial detectors, and evasion persists at longer sequence lengths than those seen during training.

## Results

Fine-tuning a 7B Llama-2 model with DPO against the OpenAI RoBERTa-Large detector reduces its AUROC from 0.84 to 0.63 while increasing perplexity on OpenWebText by only 0.3 (8.7→9.0); with a larger perplexity budget, AUROC drops to 0.30 (worse than random). Cross-detector generalization is strong: the model trained against RoBERTa-Large achieves a median AUROC of 0.65 across six open-source detectors, and models trained against DetectGPT achieve a median of 0.53. For commercial detectors, optimizing against Winston AI drives its AUROC from 0.53 to 0.20, and against Originality from 0.99 to 0.62. In the essay-generation setting, a Llama-2-chat model fine-tuned with off-policy ChatGPT data achieves a RoBERTa-Large AUROC of 0.43, and in creative writing, a Zephyr-7B model fine-tuned for only 2 hours reduces Originality's AUROC from 0.94 to 0.71 and its TPR@1% FPR from 89% to 23%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its DPO-based evasion against two prior attack methods in Appendix E (Table 9). Against the 'Added Spacing' attack (Cai & Cui, 2023), the DPO method achieves a substantially lower RoBERTa-Large AUROC (0.62 vs. 0.71) and a dramatically higher GPT-4 fluency win rate (52.1% vs. 13.9%), indicating far better evasion with much less quality degradation. Against the DIPPER paraphrasing attack (Sadasivan et al., 2023), the DPO method achieves a much lower AUROC (0.62 vs. 0.95) but a slightly lower fluency win rate (52.1% vs. 54.3%), meaning it evades the detector far more effectively while maintaining comparable text quality. The DPO approach also has a practical advantage: it requires no additional inference-time compute (no paraphrasing model or spacing perturbation at generation time), whereas both baselines require post-hoc processing of the generated text.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), originally designed for RLHF alignment, as an adversarial fine-tuning objective to make LLM outputs undetectable by text classifiers. It builds on the GAN-like intuition from SeqGAN and MaskGAN that optimizing a generator against a discriminator yields more human-like text, but replaces the intractable discrete-sampling gradient with a preference-based RL framework. The work complements prior robustness studies showing detectors are vulnerable to paraphrasing (Sadasivan et al., 2023; Krishna et al., 2023) by demonstrating that the generator itself can be cheaply retrained to evade detection, rather than requiring post-hoc text manipulation. Unlike watermarking approaches that assume all models are watermarked, this paper considers a stronger threat model where an adversary fine-tunes an open-weight model to be undetectable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implications are severe: a few hundred dollars in detector API queries and under a day of consumer-hardware training suffices to drive AUROC below 0.5 (worse than random) against multiple commercial detectors, undermining the detection infrastructure already deployed in education and journalism. The cross-detector generalization finding—training against one strong detector reduces detectability under others by an average of 0.15 AUROC—suggests that simply deploying multiple detectors is insufficient. The paper argues the adversarial arms race may be fundamentally unwinnable for detectors, since any new detection algorithm can be subject to the same DPO fine-tuning process, and all discussed countermeasures (gating access, binary scoring, restricting fine-tuning) carry significant practical downsides. Open questions include whether the results hold for much larger models, whether iterative detector updates can keep pace, and whether watermarking combined with detection might offer a more robust (though still imperfect) defense.
