## Problem

Transformer-based time series forecasting methods suffer from quadratic complexity in sequence length, which is especially costly in probabilistic forecasting where many sample trajectories must be generated in real time. Additionally, with very few exceptions, these methods have only been evaluated for point forecasting rather than probabilistic forecasting, and no systematic benchmark exists for comparing them on the latter task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241840/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a univariate context window of length C (set to 20×P in experiments) concatenated with date-time covariates (hour-of-day, day-of-week, week-of-month) and a series identity embedding. At inference, the encoder runs once on the final C−1 window, and the decoder autoregressively produces distribution parameters (Student-T, Negative Binomial, or IQN) for each of the next P steps; S=100 samples are drawn in parallel via the batch dimension to construct empirical CDFs for probabilistic metrics like CRPS.

## Method

VQ-TR inserts a Vector Quantization module into the Transformer encoder's attention: the C query vectors are quantized to J codebook vectors (J≪C), cross-attention is then applied between the J codebook vectors and the original keys/values, followed by L self-attention layers on the J latents, and results are gathered back to C positions via the quantization indices. This reduces per-layer encoder complexity from O(C²) to O(CJ + LJ²). A theoretical bound (Theorem 3.1) shows that quantizing queries introduces only bounded error in attention outputs, and the VQ objective is shown to correspond to a key-covariance-weighted K-means, justifying the design. Training jointly minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the predicted distribution plus per-layer VQ latent and commitment losses, and the paper provides the first systematic probabilistic-forecasting benchmark across 11 transformer variants on six datasets.

## Results

VQ-TR achieves the best CRPS (primary probabilistic metric) on 5 out of 6 datasets: Solar (0.317), Electricity (0.050), Traffic (0.110), Taxi (0.281), and Wikipedia (0.231), and is competitive on Exchange (0.008, vs. ETSformer's 0.009). It also attains the best NRMSE on 5/6 datasets (e.g., 0.392 on Traffic vs. 0.404 for Informer; 0.570 on Taxi vs. 0.611 for PatchTST). In the computational efficiency comparison on Traffic, VQ-TR with J=25 uses substantially less memory than all other transformer variants (including Linformer, Performer, and Nystromformer) while maintaining competitive training time, and its lower memory footprint enables larger batch sizes in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VQ-TR outperforms the Vanilla Transformer on every metric across all six datasets, confirming that the VQ module adds value beyond the base architecture. Against the strongest transformer baselines, VQ-TR wins on CRPS for Solar (0.317 vs. ETSformer 0.364), Traffic (0.110 vs. Informer 0.117), Taxi (0.281 vs. PatchTST 0.301), and Wikipedia (0.231 vs. Informer 0.253); on Electricity it is narrowly behind TFT on CRPS (0.050 vs. 0.047) but leads on NRMSE (0.495 vs. 0.516); on Exchange it ties with ETSformer/PatchTST on CRPS (0.008–0.009) but trails on MSIS (34.38 vs. 13.51/23.20). Against non-transformer baselines (Table 2), VQ-TR clearly dominates on Electricity (CRPS 0.050 vs. best non-transformer 0.054 for VQ-AR), Traffic (0.110 vs. 0.138 for VQ-AR), and Taxi (0.281 vs. 0.286 for SQF-RNN/VQ-AR), while on Solar and Wikipedia it is within ~1–2% of the best non-transformer methods (IQN-RNN, VQ-AR). On Exchange, simple classical methods (ETS, IQN-RNN) slightly outperform VQ-TR on CRPS (0.007–0.008), which the authors attribute to the near-random-walk nature of currency exchange rates. The ablation study further shows that small codebook sizes (J=1–16) provide a regularizing effect that is most beneficial on low-complexity datasets like Traffic and Taxi, where CRPS degrades noticeably as J increases beyond 32.

## Relation to Prior Work

VQ-TR inserts a Vector Quantization module (from VQ-VAE, van den Oord et al. 2017) directly into the encoder attention of a standard Transformer (Vaswani et al. 2017), replacing the full C×C self-attention with cross-attention over a small discrete codebook of J vectors followed by J×J self-attention. This is conceptually related to Perceiver/Perceiver-AR, which also compress a long context into a fixed set of latent vectors, but VQ-TR makes those latents discrete and learned via VQ, and provides a theoretical justification (Theorem 3.1) linking the optimal codebook to a key-weighted K-means objective. It departs from VQ-AR (Rasul et al. 2022), which applies VQ to RNN outputs rather than within the attention mechanism itself, and from other efficient-Transformer variants (Linformer, Reformer, Performer, Nystromformer) that approximate attention through projections or hashing rather than discrete compression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides what the authors claim is the first systematic benchmark of Transformer-based methods for probabilistic (distributional) time series forecasting, filling a gap where prior work focused almost exclusively on point forecasts. The dual benefit of VQ—linear encoder complexity O(CJ) plus a natural regularizing effect that improves generalization, especially on low-dimensional datasets like Traffic and Taxi—makes the architecture practically attractive for real-time probabilistic forecasting. Limitations include the loss of causal structure in the encoder (mitigated by the encoder-decoder split) and the decoder's residual O(MP²) scaling, though P≪C in practice. Open questions include whether the approach extends to multivariate cross-series dependencies, longer prediction horizons where P is not negligible, and non-time-series modalities the authors mention as future work.
