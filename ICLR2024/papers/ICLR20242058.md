## Problem

Previous correctness results for automatic differentiation (AD) show it computes the standard derivative at most inputs but provide little information about what it computes at non-differentiable points (e.g., ReLU at zero, maxpool ties). The Clarke subdifferential is a natural generalized derivative, but existing results connecting AD to it do not apply to practical networks with convolutions, normalization layers, residual connections, or minibatches. This paper characterizes exactly when AD computes a Clarke subderivative for neural networks containing non-differentiable activation functions.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a neural network Ψ (satisfying Condition 1 for pointwise activations or Condition 2 for shared biases/maxpools), its parameter vector w ∈ ℝ^W, and a fixed input minibatch X ∈ ℝ^{N₀×B}, and determines whether the AD-computed gradient D^AD Ψ(w;X) belongs to the Clarke subdifferential ∂^C Ψ(w;X). Empirically, the sufficient conditions are verified at every training step for fully-connected networks on MNIST (B=128, 20 epochs) and convolutional networks (VGG11, VGG11-BN, ResNet18) on CIFAR-10, checking linear-independence or polyhedral non-emptiness conditions whenever non-differentiable points are touched.

## Method

The core proof technique constructs sequences of parameters η_n → w along which the network becomes differentiable and whose standard derivatives converge to the AD output, establishing D^AD Ψ(w) ∈ ∂^B Ψ(w) ⊂ ∂^C Ψ(w). For networks with distinct bias parameters, bias terms are perturbed to resolve non-differentiability; for networks without distinct biases, weight matrices are perturbed using linear independence of activation columns. The paper proves that distinct biases + B=1 guarantee correctness (Theorem 1), that both conditions are necessary (Lemmas 2–3), and that layer-shared convex combinations of one-sided derivatives restore correctness for arbitrary minibatch sizes (Theorem 4). For convolutional networks with shared biases and non-trivial maxpools, a generic sufficient condition is given via Algorithm 1, which constructs a per-layer polyhedral set P_l whose non-emptiness (checkable via linear programming) guarantees AD correctness.

## Results

The paper provides theoretical guarantees that AD computes a Clarke subderivative for neural networks under specific conditions: distinct bias parameters with B=1 (Theorem 1), proper proxy derivatives with distinct bias parameters at any minibatch size (Theorem 4), and shared bias parameters without maxpools (Theorem 6). Empirically, on MNIST (fully-connected, B=128), the sufficient condition in Theorem 5 held at all 9,380 training steps across five runs for ReLU6, HardTanh, and HardSigmoid networks, despite non-differentiable points being touched 0, 9.8, and 13.8 times per training run respectively. On CIFAR-10 (VGG11, VGG11-BN, ResNet18 with maxpools), the sufficient condition in Theorem 8 was always satisfied across 7,820 training steps, confirming AD correctness in practice even with non-trivial maxpool ties occurring in ~3–4% of max operations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no direct baseline methods to benchmark against. The paper extends the prior result of Lee et al. (2023, ICML), which showed AD computes a Clarke subderivative only when proxy gradients are restricted to the Bouligand subdifferential and residual connections are absent. The current work generalizes this by allowing proxy gradients from the full Clarke subdifferential and permitting residual connections (Theorem 1), and further extends to shared bias parameters as in convolutional layers (Theorem 6). The paper also identifies the precise conditions under which AD can fail (Lemmas 2, 3, 7): absence of distinct bias parameters, minibatch size ≥ 2 without proper proxy choices, and non-trivial maxpools. Empirically, the sufficient conditions (Theorems 5 and 8) are verified to hold at every training step for practical architectures, confirming that AD is effectively correct in standard deep learning practice despite the theoretical edge cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on AD correctness (Bolte & Pauwels 2020, Lee et al. 2020, Huot et al. 2023) that established AD computes the standard derivative at most inputs, by characterizing what AD computes at the remaining non-differentiable points specifically with respect to the Clarke subdifferential. It generalizes Lee et al. (2023), which required Bouligand subdifferential proxy derivatives and excluded residual connections, by allowing Clarke subdifferential proxies and residual connections. Crucially, it extends the analysis to practical architectures that prior Clarke-subdifferential results could not cover: convolutional layers with shared bias parameters, maxpools, BatchNorm, and non-trivial minibatch sizes, providing both positive guarantees and explicit counterexamples for when AD fails.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first comprehensive theoretical characterization of what backpropagation actually computes for the full range of modern network architectures (CNNs, transformers, residual networks), resolving a gap where prior results were inapplicable. The empirical verification that AD is correct at essentially all training steps in practical setups (MNIST, CIFAR-10) offers strong reassurance for practitioners, while the negative results (Lemmas 2, 3, 7) precisely identify the structural conditions under which AD can produce values outside the Clarke subdifferential. Open questions include whether similar characterizations hold for other generalized derivatives (conservative, intensional) and for architectures with more exotic non-differentiable operations beyond ReLU and maxpool.
