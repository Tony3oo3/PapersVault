## Problem

Existing GNN explanation methods identify which nodes, edges, or subgraphs are important for a prediction but cannot explain *why* those parts matter or *how* the model uses them. Moreover, they operate on individual graphs, requiring a human to manually synthesize dozens of per-graph explanations into a coherent dataset-level rule. GraphChef aims to produce a single human-readable 'recipe' (a set of decision trees) that reveals the full decision process for an entire dataset.

## Input / Output

GraphChef takes standard graphs with node features and adjacency structure as input, identical to any GNN. Beyond the usual class label, it outputs a set of small decision trees (one per layer plus encoder/decoder) that collectively encode the model's reasoning as interpretable rules over categorical states and neighbor counts. It also produces node-level importance scores via Tree-Shap propagation through the recipe, enabling heatmap-style explanations comparable to GNNExplainer or gradient-based methods.

## Method

GraphChef introduces the 'dish' layer (DIfferentiable Stone-H), which applies Gumbel-Softmax to GIN-style message passing so that internal node states become one-hot categorical values and aggregation reduces to counting neighbors per state, mirroring the stone-age distributed computing model. After training the dish GNN with gradient descent, all MLP blocks (encoder, per-layer update, decoder) are distilled into decision trees that predict the next categorical state from three feature types: the node's current state, per-state neighbor counts, and binary pairwise comparisons between state counts (delta features). A custom reduced-error pruning procedure—using both training and validation accuracy as a joint quality criterion—shrinks the trees while preserving accuracy, yielding a trade-off between simplicity and performance. Finally, an importance propagation scheme computes Tree-Shap values per decision feature and routes them to the responsible nodes (self for state features, evenly among neighbors for count features, split positive/negative for delta features) to produce node-level explanation heatmaps.

## Results

GraphChef achieves test accuracy very close to the full GIN baseline across all 13 main datasets. On synthetic benchmarks requiring graph reasoning (Infection, Negative, BA-Shapes, Tree-Cycles, Tree-Grid, BA-2Motifs), GraphChef matches GIN within 0–2% (e.g., 1.00 vs 0.98 on Infection, 0.99 vs 0.97 on BA-Shapes). On real-world datasets, GraphChef is competitive: 0.88 vs 0.88 on MUTAG, 0.82 vs 0.81 on BBBP, 0.88 vs 0.87 on REDDIT-BINARY. However, on high-dimensional citation networks (Cora: 1433 features, CiteSeer: 3703 features), GraphChef drops significantly (0.69 vs 0.87 on Cora; 0.61 vs 0.77 on CiteSeer), revealing a scalability limitation for large feature spaces.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GIN (Xu et al., 2019), GraphChef matches or nearly matches accuracy on all 13 main datasets, with the largest gap being 3% on Mutagenicity (0.75 vs 0.81) and a significant drop on high-dimensional Cora (0.69 vs 0.87) and CiteSeer (0.61 vs 0.77). For explanation quality (Table 2), GraphChef substantially outperforms GNNExplainer (Ying et al., 2019) on Infection (0.95 vs 0.32), Saturation (1.00 vs 0.32), and Tree-Grid (0.927 vs 0.875), but trails on Tree-Cycles (0.84 vs 0.948). Against PGM-Explainer (Vu et al., 2020), GraphChef wins decisively on Infection (0.95 vs 0.38) and Saturation (1.00 vs 0.01), is competitive on BA-Shapes (0.94 vs 0.965), and wins on Tree-Grid (0.927 vs 0.892), but loses on Tree-Cycles (0.84 vs 0.968). Against the Gradient baseline (Faber et al., 2021), GraphChef wins on BA-Shapes (0.94 vs 0.882) and Tree-Grid (0.927 vs 0.667) but trails on Infection (0.95 vs 1.00) and Tree-Cycles (0.84 vs 0.905); the paper notes the Tree-Cycles/Tree-Grid gaps reflect known dataset deficiencies where GNNs exploit shortcuts rather than finding the full motif.

## Relation to Prior Work

GraphChef builds on the GIN message-passing architecture (Xu et al., 2019) but replaces continuous embeddings with categorical states via Gumbel-Softmax, drawing theoretical motivation from the stone-age distributed computing model (Emek and Wattenhofer, 2013) where nodes transition between a small set of states using neighbor counts. The decision-tree distillation step extends prior work on extracting trees from neural networks (Boz, 2002; Craven and Shavlik, 1995; Aytekin, 2022) to the graph-structured setting, where trees must reason about neighborhood structure rather than flat tabular features. The pruning procedure adapts Quinlan's (1987) reduced-error pruning with a custom dual-accuracy criterion. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant GNN explanation paradigm (gradient, subgraph, counterfactual, example-based methods) by making the model itself self-explainable and producing dataset-level recipes rather than per-instance heatmaps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GraphChef offers a qualitatively different form of GNN explanation: a small set of human-readable decision trees that encode the full decision process for an entire dataset, enabling questions like 'what structural pattern makes a protein an enzyme?' that instance-level methods cannot answer. The practical limitation is significant, however: on high-dimensional feature spaces (Cora with 1433 features, CiteSeer with 3703), the categorical bottleneck and tree leaf limit cause substantial accuracy drops, restricting current applicability to low-to-moderate feature dimensions. Open questions include whether feature reduction (PCA, clustering) or specialized MLP constructions can bridge this gap while preserving interpretability, and whether the stone-age-style categorical abstraction generalizes to other GNN architectures beyond GIN.
