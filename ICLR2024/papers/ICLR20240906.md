## Problem

Referenceless metrics such as CLIPScore aim to evaluate image descriptions without costly ground-truth references, but it remains unclear whether they genuinely track human quality judgments, particularly when descriptions are assessed in their natural article context. No prior benchmark has systematically stress-tested these metrics against context-dependent human preferences or probed their sensitivity to common model error patterns like hallucination, repetition, and irrelevant text.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240906/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs an RGB image with its Wikipedia alt-text description and surrounding article context (article title, first paragraph, section title, section text, and caption). Human raters score descriptions on five quality dimensions both before and after seeing the image, yielding 768 annotations across 204 data points. The robustness component applies ten targeted augmentations (shuffled descriptions, shuffled contexts, shuffled words, proper-name replacement, induced alignment errors, Frankenstein images, GPT-2 continuations, irrelevant final sentence, exact repetition) and checks whether each metric correctly assigns a lower score to every degraded variant.

## Method

ContextRef is a two-part benchmark: a human-subjects experiment collecting context-aware quality ratings from Prolific participants, and ten robustness checks that manipulate image-text alignment, string predictability, and contextual sensitivity. The paper evaluates six pretrained vision-language models (CLIP, BLIP-2, Flamingo, Frozen, InstructBLIP, and a contextual CLIP variant) under both similarity-based (cosine embedding distance) and likelihood-based (average per-token log-likelihood) scoring, revealing that similarity-based metrics are more sensitive to image-text mismatches while likelihood-based metrics are more sensitive to word order and context but strongly biased toward predictable or redundant text. Fine-tuning CLIP jointly on the augmentations and human ratings improves robustness performance substantially (e.g., +24% on shuffled words, +34% on GPT-2 continuation long) at a modest cost to human-judgment correlation, while context sensitivity remains the hardest dimension to resolve for both metric families.

## Results

No referenceless metric achieves strong alignment with human preferences on ContextRef. The best post-image overall Pearson correlations are BLIP-2 (LogL) at r=0.40, Frozen (LogL) at r=0.39, and Flamingo (LogL) at r=0.39, while the weakest is InstructBLIP (LogL) at r=0.20. On robustness checks, no metric passes all 10 augmentations out-of-the-box; likelihood-based metrics fail catastrophically on predictability-based augmentations (e.g., Frozen scores exact repetition at 0% expected-lower-score rate), while similarity-based metrics fail on context shuffling (CLIP: only 43.9% correct). Fine-tuning CLIP jointly on augmentations and human ratings improves shuffled-words accuracy from 67.6% to 91.9% and GPT-2-continuation-long from 65.9% to 100%, but context sensitivity remains unresolved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven metric variants across two scoring families. Likelihood-based metrics (Frozen, BLIP-2 LogL, Flamingo) achieve higher post-image overall correlations (r=0.39–0.40) than similarity-based metrics (Orig. CLIPScore r=0.28, contextual CLIP r=0.35, BLIP-2 Sim r=0.36), but they fail dramatically on predictability-based augmentations: Frozen assigns higher scores to exact repetitions (0% expected-lower) and GPT-2 long continuations (2.4%), while CLIP handles these at 65.9% and 65.9% respectively. Conversely, similarity-based metrics are far more sensitive to image-text mismatches (CLIP: 100% on shuffled descriptions vs. Frozen: 66.7%) but are nearly blind to context shuffling (CLIP: 43.9% vs. Frozen: 58.5%). InstructBLIP has the lowest human correlation (r=0.20) yet shows the most sensitivity to data manipulations among likelihood-based metrics. After fine-tuning, CLIP improves on 7/10 augmentations (e.g., irrelevant final sentence 80.5%→100%, exact repetition 65.9%→100%) but still fails context shuffling (48.8%), while Frozen remains stuck at 0% on exact repetition even after fine-tuning. The key takeaway is that no single scoring strategy dominates: similarity-based metrics are more robust overall but context-blind, while likelihood-based metrics capture context and grammar but are hijacked by string predictability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the referenceless image description evaluation line initiated by CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021) and the context-aware evaluation scheme of Kreiss et al. (2022a), which first demonstrated that article context significantly shapes description quality judgments. Where prior work focused on correlating a single metric (CLIPScore) with human ratings, ContextRef systematically compares six pretrained vision-language models across two scoring paradigms (similarity vs. likelihood) and introduces ten targeted robustness augmentations. The Frozen metric (dzryk, 2023) and the broader family of contrastive and generative vision-language models (BLIP-2, Flamingo, InstructBLIP) are brought into the evaluation for the first time in a unified benchmark setting with context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that no out-of-the-box referenceless metric passes all robustness checks, and that the strongest-correlating likelihood-based metrics actually assign 3× higher scores to exact repetitions of a description—exposes a critical gap between correlational performance and behavioral reliability that could mislead model development. Context sensitivity remains unsolved even after fine-tuning, suggesting that successful integration of article context into evaluation metrics may require more fundamental architectural or training innovations. The benchmark provides a concrete, reproducible path for future metric development, but the persistent context challenge and the small dataset size (204 examples) limit immediate generalizability.
