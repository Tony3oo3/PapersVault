## Problem

In treatment effect estimation, the expertise of decision-makers who collected observational data is almost never formalized as an assumption or leveraged as an inductive bias; the standard assumption is merely overlap (non-zero treatment probability). This paper argues that the *type* of expertise present—predictive (actions driven by treatment effects) versus prognostic (actions driven by potential outcomes more generally)—significantly influences which estimation methods perform well, yet no prior work provides a formal definition or a mechanism to exploit this distinction for model selection.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard observational dataset of (X, A, Y) triples with binary treatment and tabular features, and produces three outputs: a CATE estimate τ̂(x), numerical estimates of predictive and prognostic expertise (computed by discretizing predicted potential outcomes into bins and evaluating entropy ratios), and a meta-decision selecting between DragonNet and CFRNet based on the ratio Ê_pred/Ê_prog. The expertise estimation is an oracle quantity in principle (requiring both potential outcomes), so in practice it is approximated using the model's own outcome predictions plugged into the entropy formulas.

## Method

The paper defines predictive expertise E_pred = 1 − H[A|Y₁−Y₀]/H[A] and prognostic expertise E_prog = 1 − H[A|Y₀,Y₁]/H[A] as normalized entropy reductions, and proves that E + C ≤ 1 (where C is in-context action variability), establishing that high expertise necessarily implies poor overlap. The key practical contribution is the 'Expertise-informed' pipeline: first, DragonNet (which learns action-predictive representations) is used to estimate both E_pred and E_prog from a dataset; then, if Ê_pred/Ê_prog > 1/2, DragonNet is selected for CATE estimation (exploiting predictive expertise), otherwise CFRNet (Balancing) is selected (exploiting prognostic expertise by discarding irrelevant prognostic/irrelevant variables from representations). Empirically, this best-of-both-worlds selection achieves the lowest average PEHE across mixed predictive and prognostic datasets, outperforming any single fixed method.

## Results

On the TCGA-based simulation environment, the proposed Expertise-informed pipeline achieves the lowest PEHE across all datasets at 1.096 (0.185), outperforming the best individual method (Action-predictive/DragonNet at 1.123). On predictive-expertise datasets, Expertise-informed matches Action-predictive (PEHE 0.751) and substantially outperforms Balancing/CFRNet (0.936). On prognostic-expertise datasets, it matches Balancing (PEHE 1.439) and outperforms Action-predictive (1.495), demonstrating a best-of-both-worlds selection strategy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four existing CATE estimation strategies: TARNet (Baseline, Shalit et al. 2017), TARNet with inverse propensity weighting (Propensity, following Wager & Athey 2018), CFRNet with balancing representations (Balancing, Shalit et al. 2017), and DragonNet with action-predictive representations (Action-predictive, Shi et al. 2019). The Expertise-informed pipeline, which uses DragonNet to estimate expertise type and then selects between DragonNet and CFRNet, achieves the best overall PEHE (1.096) by matching the best method in each expertise regime: it equals DragonNet on predictive datasets (0.751 vs. 0.751) and equals CFRNet on prognostic datasets (1.439 vs. 1.439). Balancing/CFRNet is the weakest on predictive datasets (0.936 PEHE, ~25% worse than DragonNet) because it discards action-predictive features that correlate with treatment effects. Propensity/IPW performs nearly identically to the Baseline across all settings, confirming that it is largely indifferent to expertise type. The key finding is that no single method dominates across expertise types, and the expertise-informed selection recovers the best-of-both-worlds performance without requiring ground-truth treatment effects.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard treatment effect estimation literature (TARNet, CFRNet, DragonNet, IPW) by arguing that the decision-maker's policy should not be treated solely as a confounding nuisance to be corrected, but can be leveraged as an inductive bias. It formally introduces two types of expertise—predictive and prognostic—using entropy-based definitions, connecting to the inverse decision modeling line of work (Bica et al., Jarrett et al., Hüyük et al.) where understanding a decision-maker's policy is the primary goal. The key conceptual departure is from the overlap-only assumption that dominates the CATE estimation literature: the paper shows that high expertise necessarily implies poor overlap (Proposition 1), making the expertise signal both more informative and more critical to exploit simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, data-driven criterion for model selection in CATE estimation—a problem where ground-truth treatment effects are never observed and conventional validation is infeasible. The finding that balancing representations (CFRNet) actively degrades performance under predictive expertise while helping under prognostic expertise offers practical guidance for practitioners choosing methods in healthcare vs. education settings. However, the evidence is entirely synthetic, the expertise-informed pipeline fails in the non-linear TCGA environment, and the framework is limited to binary treatments with fully observed covariates. Open questions include how expertise interacts with hidden confounding, whether the entropy-based definitions extend to multi-valued or sequential treatments, and whether the model selection pipeline generalizes beyond the specific architectures tested.
