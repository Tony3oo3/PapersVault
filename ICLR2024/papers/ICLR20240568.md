## Problem

Sparsely activated Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) models scale network capacity efficiently but suffer from high memory overhead (all experts must be stored) and expert redundancy caused by representational collapse in routing policies. Conventional model merging methods fail in this setting because redundant experts overshadow critical ones and experts trained along divergent optimization trajectories lack proper neuron alignment. The paper asks: how can we consolidate redundant SMoE experts into fewer, more knowledgeable ones without sacrificing vital knowledge?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained SMoE checkpoint (e.g., switch-base-32 with 32 experts per layer, or fairseq-moe-15b with 512 experts) plus a small random subset of training data (256 samples) used solely to compute expert activation frequencies and router-logits similarity vectors. The output is a compact SMoE with a reduced number of experts per layer (e.g., 8 out of 32, or 16 out of 512), where each remaining expert is a frequency-weighted merge of its group, optionally further decomposed into a low-rank factor plus a structurally sparse residual. The first SMoE layer is typically left unmerged to preserve performance, and the router's output channels remain unchanged in count (merged experts are aliased to a representative slot) to keep routing latency constant.

## Method

MC-SMoE operates in two stages. First, M-SMoE performs routing-policy-guided expert merging: (1) experts within each layer are aligned via permutation matching (solved as a linear assignment problem over weight matrices), (2) dominant experts are identified globally by normalizing activation frequencies across all layers, (3) each non-dominant expert is assigned to the group of its most similar dominant expert using cosine similarity of router-logits vectors over a batch of inputs, and (4) each group is merged via frequency-weighted averaging of aligned weights. Second, the paper observes that merged experts exhibit lower stable-rank in their weight space, enabling MC-SMoE to further decompose each merged expert into a low-rank product (rank 32) plus a structurally pruned sparse residual (10% sparsity) using a cubic pruning schedule. Knowledge distillation from the full SMoE teacher is applied during post-merging fine-tuning to recover performance.

## Results

On the switch-base-32 SMoE model (2.0B params), M-SMoE reduces model size to 733M (60% memory saving) while matching or exceeding full SMoE performance on MRPC (90.69 vs 90.20), COPA (68.00 vs 68.00), WinoGrande (61.80 vs 61.80), SQuAD (65.66/85.49 vs 65.39/85.81), and HotpotQA (67.91/84.83 vs 67.55/84.60). MC-SMoE further compresses to 381M params and 3.83 TFLOPs (80% memory and 20% FLOPs reduction) with performance drops under 1% on MRPC, COPA, SQuAD, WikiQA, and HotpotQA. On the fairseq-moe-15b model (15B params), M-SMoE reduces to 552M and MC-SMoE to 166M in a zero-shot setting, consistently outperforming pruning baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

M-SMoE (733M) outperforms all five merging baselines (Averaging, ZipIt, REPAIR, Git Re-basin) and both pruning baselines (Pruning, Task-Specific) on the majority of tasks. The most dramatic gap is on COPA, where M-SMoE scores 68.00 versus 65.00 for ZipIt/REPAIR/Git Re-basin and 62.00 for Averaging — a 3–6 point margin. On SQuAD, M-SMoE achieves 65.66/85.49 EM/F1, beating ZipIt (65.01/85.06) and REPAIR (64.67/84.84), while ZipIt edges out M-SMoE slightly on MRPC (91.18 vs 90.69). The Task-Specific pruning baseline suffers severe degradation (e.g., 52.00 on COPA, 54.40/78.00 on SQuAD), confirming that simply dropping experts is far inferior to merging. MC-SMoE (381M, 3.83 TFLOPs) achieves 80% memory and 20% FLOPs reduction over full SMoE with performance drops under 1% on five of eight tasks, and remains competitive with the 733M baselines despite having less than half their parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model merging literature (Git Re-basin, REPAIR, ZipIt) to the SMoE setting, where experts have distinct random initializations and are trained on routing-determined data subsets, violating the same-initialization assumption of prior merging work. It draws on Git Re-basin's permutation alignment for neuron matching and on LoSparse/DSEE for the post-merging low-rank-plus-sparse decomposition. The key conceptual departure is using the SMoE's own routing policy—activation frequencies for importance weighting and router logits for similarity-based grouping—as the guiding signal for merging, rather than relying on generic weight-space or gradient-based similarity metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SMoE models are increasingly central to scaling LLMs, but their memory footprint (e.g., 2.0B for switch-base-32 vs. 220M dense) severely limits deployment on resource-constrained platforms. This work demonstrates that routing statistics alone can identify which experts to keep, how to group them, and how to weight their merge, yielding up to 80% memory reduction with <1% performance loss. Key limitations include unchanged routing latency (the router still manages the same number of output classes) and lower throughput for MC-SMoE without specialized sparse-matrix hardware support. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-modal SMoEs, whether router pruning can further reduce latency, and whether the low-rank structure discovered post-merging persists at larger scales (e.g., 15B+ models).
