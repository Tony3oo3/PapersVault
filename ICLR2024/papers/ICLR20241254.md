## Problem

Computing the Wasserstein distance between two distributions whose samples are stored on separate clients—without the server ever accessing the raw data—has not been previously addressed in the federated learning literature. While federated learning typically focuses on training a global model, many practical tasks (federated clustering, dataset similarity assessment, coreset construction) require computing distributional distances in a privacy-preserving manner, and no principled method existed for doing so.

## Input / Output

The method takes discrete empirical distributions μ and ν (each a set of weighted samples) stored on two separate clients, along with an initial server-side estimate ξ^(0) of the geodesic midpoint. At each iteration, clients exchange only interpolating measures (not raw samples) with the server; the support size of these measures is fixed at a user-chosen value S to bound communication cost at O(4SKd) per iteration. The final output is the scalar Wasserstein distance W_p(μ,ν) computed as W_p(μ,ξ^K) + W_p(ξ^K,ν), where ξ^K is the converged interpolating measure.

## Method

FedWaD exploits the geodesic structure of the Wasserstein space: for any interpolating measure ξ* on the geodesic between μ and ν, the triangle inequality becomes an equality, so W_p(μ,ν) = W_p(μ,ξ*) + W_p(ξ*,ν). The algorithm iteratively refines ξ^(k) on the server: at each step, clients compute interpolating measures ξ_μ^(k) and ξ_ν^(k) between their local distribution and the current server estimate, then the server computes the next iterate as the interpolating measure between these two client-provided measures (solving a two-measure barycenter problem). To prevent the support size from growing unboundedly across iterations, the paper uses a barycentric mapping approximation of McCann's interpolation formula, fixing the support at S points. Convergence is guaranteed (the objective sequence is non-increasing and bounded below by W_p(μ,ν)), and for Gaussian distributions with shared covariance, the algorithm achieves a linear convergence rate of 1/2^(k-1) with the distance recovered in a single iteration.

## Results

On MNIST with 20 clients and a clear cluster structure, FedWaD-based spectral clustering boosts FedAvg from 26.3% to 99.5% accuracy, FedRep from 81.1% to 99.1%, and FedPer from 94.3% to 99.5%. On CIFAR-10 under the same setting, FedRep improves from 81.8% to 88.1% and FedPer from 85.4% to 91.0%. Even without a clear cluster structure, FedWaD clustering yields an average uplift of 12.7 points on MNIST and 18.8 points on CIFAR-10 across all three FL algorithms. For the federated OTDD task, FedWaD with only 20 epochs and 1000 support points preserves the pairwise distance ordering of the true OTDD across MNIST, KMNIST, USPS, and FashionMNIST.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedWaD is not a standalone FL algorithm but a distance-computation tool used to cluster clients before running existing FL methods. Compared to vanilla FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017), FedWaD-based clustering yields the largest absolute gains: up to +73 points on MNIST (26.3%→99.5%) and +53 points on CIFAR-10 (22.0%→75.1%) with strong structure. Against personalized baselines FedRep (Collins et al., 2021) and FedPer (Arivazhagan et al., 2019), the clustered versions consistently outperform their vanilla counterparts by 5–19 points on CIFAR-10 and up to 29 points on MNIST. For the federated OTDD task, FedWaD (20 epochs, 1000 support points) reproduces the pairwise distance ordering of the true non-federated OTDD (Alvarez-Melis & Fusi, 2020) across all four digit datasets, with only two pairs in the USPS row showing a minor ordering discrepancy. The coreset-based nearest-neighbor classifier built from FedWaD coresets is competitive with FedRep and FedPer on MNIST, and outperforms them when the number of clients is large (Figure 5).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces the first principled method for computing the Wasserstein distance between two distributions in a federated setting, where samples remain on separate clients. It builds on the geometric structure of Wasserstein space (triangle inequality, geodesic interpolation) previously exploited in computer vision and trajectory inference, and on the barycentric mapping approximation of Courty et al. (2018) for efficient interpolating measure computation. The federated OTDD experiments extend Alvarez-Melis & Fusi (2020)'s optimal transport dataset distance to a privacy-preserving setting, while the FL boosting experiments position FedWaD as a preprocessing step for existing algorithms like FedAvg, FedRep, and FedPer. The authors explicitly note that prior federated distance work (federated k-NN, encrypted nearest neighbors) operates on individual vectors rather than distributions and does not leverage Wasserstein geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FedWaD provides a theoretically grounded (convergence guaranteed, linear rate for Gaussians) and practically efficient tool for computing distributional distances without exposing raw data, opening a new axis for federated analysis beyond model training. The demonstrated 20–30+ point accuracy gains for FL algorithms via client clustering based on FedWaD-computed distances suggest that dataset heterogeneity can be substantially mitigated with minimal overhead. Key limitations include the absence of formal differential privacy guarantees (only heuristic mitigations), the current restriction to two-client distance computation, and degraded accuracy in high-dimensional settings due to the curse of dimensionality of empirical Wasserstein estimates. Open questions include extending to multi-client federated distances, incorporating formal privacy budgets, and scaling to high-dimensional or continuous distributions where closed-form geodesics are unavailable.
