## Problem

High-quality content-grounded datasets for tasks like long-form QA, summarization, and information extraction are scarce because creating elaborate, grounded responses requires expensive manual annotation, and existing datasets (ELI5, CNN-DailyMail) are noisy while carefully curated ones (ASQA) are small. This data bottleneck limits progress in retrieval-augmented generation and domain-specific content tasks.

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests raw web pages or pre-extracted passages (e.g., NQ Wikipedia passages, top-3 retrieved documents for ELI5/ASQA mimicry, or crawled Wikipedia content) and produces structured synthetic examples with fixed template prefixes: [document]/[question]/[answer] for QA, [document]/[summarize]/[summary] for summarization, and analogous structures for IE. Content is first converted from HTML to Markdown preserving document structure, then broken into passages via a derived table of contents; generated examples are constrained in length (≤1.5× content length for LFQA, ≤0.25× for summarization) and must pass format, faithfulness, and quality filters before inclusion.

## Method

Genie is a three-stage pipeline: (1) Content Preparation uses rule-based extraction to clean raw documents into structured passages; (2) Generation employs few-shot in-context prompting (4 content-example pairs) with a large LLM (Falcon-40B or Llama-2-70B) using greedy decoding to produce task-specific examples grounded in the provided content; (3) Filtering applies three complementary scoring functions—format validation (template completeness, length bounds), faithfulness via a fine-tuned T5-11B NLI model that checks whether the generated example is entailed by the grounding content, and quality via a DeBERTa-v3 reward model (threshold 0.5) trained on human preference data. The key design choice is that the generation step relies purely on the LLM's pre-trained capabilities without additional alignment or instruction tuning, while the multi-dimensional filtering compensates for automatic generation errors. This makes the method domain- and task-agnostic: only the few-shot exemplars and task-specific template change when adapting to a new content-grounded task.

## Results

On ASQA, Wish-QA (synthetic) achieves ROUGE-L 28.0, Bert-Score 67.5, and Reward 85.1, compared to 31.4 / 66.0 / 68.6 for human ASQA and 10.5 / 49.7 / 28.8 for the Flan-xl multitask baseline. On ELI5, Wish-QA reaches ROUGE-L 13.8, Bert-Score 55.2, and Reward 26.7, outperforming both the Flan-xl baseline (6.2 / 46.7 / 9.2) and human ELI5 training (13.1 / 51.3 / 11.3). In faithfulness, models trained on synthetic data score 79.3 k-Precision and 75.5 ANLI on ASQA, versus 67.5 / 55.7 for human ASQA and 46.3 / 25.3 for the gold answers. The synthetic Reward score of 85.1 on ASQA exceeds the gold standard (72.1), indicating the generated answers are preferred by the reward model over human-written ones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Flan-xl multitask baseline, synthetic data yields substantial gains: ROUGE-L nearly triples on ASQA (10.5 → 28.0) and more than doubles on ELI5 (6.2 → 13.8). Compared to the human in-domain ASQA dataset, Wish-QA is slightly lower on ROUGE-L (28.0 vs. 31.4) but higher on Bert-Score (67.5 vs. 66.0) and substantially higher on Reward (85.1 vs. 68.6), exceeding even the gold standard (72.1). Against the human out-of-domain ELI5 dataset, Wish-QA wins on every metric (ROUGE-L 13.8 vs. 13.1, Bert-Score 55.2 vs. 51.3, Reward 26.7 vs. 11.3). In faithfulness (Table 3), models trained on synthetic data consistently outperform those trained on human data: on ASQA, Wish-QA-NQ achieves 79.3 k-Precision and 75.5 ANLI versus 67.5 / 55.7 for ASQA-trained and 46.3 / 25.3 for gold; on ELI5, the gap is even wider (60.4 / 43.3 vs. 29.0 / 5.6 for ELI5-trained). The one area where human data retains an edge is ROUGE-L on the in-domain ASQA test set, suggesting that lexical overlap with the specific test distribution still favors the original human annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-step generation-and-filtering paradigm used in prior synthetic data work (Self-Instruct, Unnatural Instructions, SODA, q2d) by specifically targeting content-grounded tasks where examples must be faithful to externally provided content rather than relying on the LLM's parametric knowledge. Unlike distillation approaches (West et al., 2022) where the student mimics a teacher's diverse skills, GENIE produces task-specific examples (QA pairs, summaries, IE) grounded in given passages, and unlike dialog inpainting (Dai et al., 2022) which sacrifices fluency for guaranteed groundedness, it generates natural examples and then verifies faithfulness via NLI and quality via a reward model. The three-stage pipeline (Content Preparation → Few-shot Generation → Multi-dimensional Filtering) is the paper's core methodological contribution, combining format checks, T5-11B NLI faithfulness scoring, and DeBERTa-v3 reward-model quality scoring into a unified curation mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GENIE addresses a recognized bottleneck—the scarcity of high-quality content-grounded training data—by demonstrating that automatically generated data can match or exceed human-curated datasets (ELI5, ASQA, CNN-DailyMail) in both performance and faithfulness at a fraction of the cost (~$0.03 per example vs. ~$4.45 for human annotation). The finding that out-of-domain synthetic data (Wish-QA-NQ) performs comparably to in-domain data (Wish-QA-MED) on PubMed challenges the assumption that domain-specific training data is necessary for content-grounded generation, suggesting the model relies more on the provided content than on training-domain priors. Open questions include whether this domain invariance holds for tasks less amenable to content grounding, whether the faithfulness gap (76% post-filtering vs. 88% for clean-content synthetic NQ) can be closed with better filters, and whether the approach generalizes to modalities beyond text.
