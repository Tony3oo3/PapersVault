## Problem

Language models queried generatively (sampling answers) and discriminatively (scoring candidate answers) often produce contradictory predictions, yet no principled, training-free procedure existed to reconcile these conflicting signals into a coherent output. This paper asks how to distill a consistent 'consensus' prediction from an LM's noisy and often-incompatible generative and discriminative scoring procedures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240229/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a question x, a finite set of candidate answers Y (obtained via nucleus p=0.9 and top-k=50 sampling from the LM), and a pre-trained LM (LLaMA-7B/13B) as input. It produces a refined ranking over the candidate set by computing equilibrium policies for both a GENERATOR and DISCRIMINATOR, from which the top-ranked answer is selected. Notably, the 'incorrect' conditioning is implemented simply by replacing 'Answer:' with 'Incorrect Answer:' in the prompt, and the entire procedure is training-free—only the signaling policies are updated, never the base LM weights.

## Method

The paper formulates LM decoding as the CONSENSUS GAME, a two-player imperfect-information sequential signaling game in which a GENERATOR (conditioned on a hidden correctness parameter v ∈ {correct, incorrect}) selects a natural language string from a candidate set, and a DISCRIMINATOR (observing only the string) must predict v. To avoid degenerate Nash equilibria that violate truthfulness, KL-regularization terms anchor each player's policy to an initial LM-derived distribution: the GENERATOR's initial policy normalizes P_LM(y|x,v) across both v and y (a self-contrastive form), while the DISCRIMINATOR's normalizes P_LM(v|x,y) across y and v. Approximate regularized equilibria are computed via the piKL no-regret algorithm, leveraging laminar regret decomposition to reduce the global policy update to independent local updates at each information set (per correctness value for G, per candidate string for D). After 5000 iterations with fixed hyperparameters (η=λ=0.1), the converged GENERATOR and DISCRIMINATOR policies yield a consensus ranking that is both coherent (G and D agree on which strings signal correctness) and reasonable (policies remain within O(1/λ) KL-distance of the original LM behavior).

## Results

On six QA benchmarks, EQUILIBRIUM-RANKING (ER-D) consistently outperforms all baselines. Headline results include: LLaMA-7B + ER-D achieves 58.3% on ARC-Challenge (vs. 47.3% generative ranking) and 56.4% on RACE-High (vs. 46.4%), surpassing the much larger LLaMA-65B (56.0% and 51.6%) and PaLM-540B (53.0% and 49.1%). On MMLU, zero-shot LLaMA-7B + ER-D (39.9%) outperforms zero-shot GPT-3-175B (37.7%), and LLaMA-13B + ER-D (45.1%) outperforms 5-shot GPT-3-175B (43.9%). On GSM8K with self-consistency, ER-D achieves 15.1% (7B) and 23.0% (13B), matching or slightly exceeding majority vote (14.7% and 22.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Generative Ranking (G; Brown et al., 2020), ER-D wins on every task: the gap ranges from +1.3 pts on ARC-Easy (7B) to +11.0 pts on ARC-Challenge (7B: 58.3 vs. 47.3) and +10.0 pts on RACE-High (7B: 56.4 vs. 46.4). Against Mutual Information Ranking (MI; Li & Jurafsky, 2016), ER-D again dominates, with the largest margins on ARC-Challenge (+10.9 pts for 7B, +9.3 for 13B) and RACE-High (+10.1 for 7B, +14.4 for 13B). On GSM8K, where the relevant baseline is Self-Consistency / Majority Vote (Wang et al., 2022), ER-D achieves 15.1% (7B) and 23.0% (13B) versus 14.7% and 22.5% for majority vote—a modest but consistent gain. Notably, the method's advantage over G and MI is largest precisely on tasks where generative and discriminative signals disagree most (ARC-Challenge, RACE-High), confirming that the equilibrium search reconciles conflicting LM affordances. The one exception is HHH with LLaMA-13B, where ER-D (61.1) underperforms Discriminative Ranking (69.2) under default hyperparameters, though tuning λ_G=0.01, λ_D=1.0 recovers 70.6%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the no-regret learning and KL-regularized equilibrium machinery developed for imperfect-information games (CFR, piKL) and previously applied to Diplomacy AI (Bakhtin et al., 2023; FAIR et al., 2022) to the problem of LM decoding. It departs from ensembling approaches (mutual information ranking, self-contrastive decoding) that combine generative and discriminative scores ad hoc, and from deliberation methods (chain-of-thought, self-consistency, tree-of-thought) that re-query the LM with additional prompts, by instead formulating decoding as a two-player signaling game and computing a regularized Nash equilibrium. The authors explicitly position this as the first use of regularized equilibrium concepts in signaling games to define target behavior in a language generation task, building on Lewis's (2008) signaling game framework and Franke's (2013, 2017) work on game-theoretic pragmatics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free, computationally cheap nature of EQUILIBRIUM-RANKING (linear in candidate count per iteration, no additional LM queries) makes it a practical drop-in improvement for existing decoding pipelines, and the demonstration that a 7B model can match or exceed 65B/540B models on several benchmarks suggests that decoding strategy may matter as much as model scale for factual accuracy. However, the evaluation is limited to multiple-choice and short-answer QA tasks with LLaMA-7B/13B, and the method's behavior on open-ended long-form generation, larger model families, or tasks where the generative/discriminative disagreement is less pronounced remains untested. Open questions include whether the game-theoretic framing generalizes to multi-turn dialogue, whether the 5000-iteration convergence is sufficient for all task distributions, and whether the KL-regularization strength can be adapted per-domain without manual tuning.
