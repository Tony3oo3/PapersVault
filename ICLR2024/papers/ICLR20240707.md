## Problem

While it is well-known that neural networks can be heavily pruned at test time without significant performance loss, prior work has only shown that such reductions maintain or degrade accuracy. This paper identifies the surprising and previously unexplored phenomenon that *selective* rank reduction at specific layers of a Transformer can actually *improve* model performance on reasoning tasks, without any retraining or fine-tuning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240707/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a fully pre-trained Transformer model, targeting individual weight matrices (MLP input U_in, MLP output U_out, or attention matrices W_q/W_k/W_v/W_o) at a chosen layer index ℓ. A single intervention is specified by the triple (τ, ℓ, ρ), where ρ ∈ [0,1) controls what fraction of the matrix's maximum rank is preserved; for example, a 4096×4096 MLP matrix with ρ=0.01 is reduced to rank 4. The output is the modified model with the selected matrix replaced by its SVD low-rank approximation, which is then evaluated directly on downstream tasks with no additional training.

## Method

LASER replaces a target weight matrix W with its rank-r approximation Ŵ = Σᵢ₌₁ʳ σᵢ·uᵢvᵢᵀ computed via SVD, removing the higher-order singular components. The key empirical finding is that this intervention is highly layer-selective: rank-reducing early layers or attention matrices causes degradation or no change, while reducing MLP input matrices in later layers (e.g., layer 27 of 28 in GPT-J) yields substantial accuracy gains. The paper hypothesizes that higher-order components encode conflicting or noisy responses (high-frequency tokens like 'the' or incorrect entities of the same semantic type), and that their removal resolves internal conflicts, acting as a denoising procedure that makes weakly-learned, low-frequency facts accessible. Interventions can be composed across multiple layers via a greedy search over (τ, ℓ, ρ) on a validation set, and the resulting improvements are monotonic—once a fact is recovered at reduction level x, it remains correct at all higher reduction levels y > x.

## Results

LASER improves accuracy across most of eight NLU benchmarks and three model families (RoBERTa, GPT-J 6B, Llama-2 7B) without any retraining. The most dramatic gains appear on GPT-J: CounterFact top-1 accuracy nearly doubles from 13.1% to 24.0%, Bios Gender jumps from 70.9% to 97.5%, and BigBench-WikidataQA rises from 51.8% to 65.9%. On Llama-2, BigBench-Epistemic Reasoning improves from 44.8% to 63.4% and FEVER from 59.3% to 64.5%. In a non-text domain, a 6-layer Decision Transformer on Sokoban improves task success from 50.67% to 53% and episode return from 0.575 to 0.965.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare LASER against other compression or pruning methods; the baseline is the unmodified pre-trained model itself. LASER consistently improves or matches the unmodified model on 14 of 16 accuracy cells across the three models and eight datasets, with the largest single gain being GPT-J on Bios Gender (+26.6 pp, 70.9→97.5%) and Llama-2 on BigBench-Epistemic Reasoning (+18.6 pp, 44.8→63.4%). The only accuracy regression is GPT-J on HotPotQA (19.6→19.5%), which is within noise. Loss improves in most cells but degrades slightly on a few (e.g., GPT-J FEVER 1.24→1.27, Llama-2 TruthfulQA 0.95→1.04), suggesting the intervention sharpens decision boundaries at a small cost to calibration. The authors also note that composing LASER across multiple layers on CounterFact pushes GPT-J top-10 accuracy to 29.2%, a further 5.2 pp over the best single-layer intervention.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the neural network pruning and low-rank compression literature (Frankle & Carbin 2019; Lv et al. 2023; Hajimolahoseini et al. 2021), which has consistently shown that models can be drastically reduced without *degradation* in performance, by demonstrating that targeted rank reduction at specific layers can actually *improve* accuracy. The mathematical tool is the Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem for optimal rank-r approximation via SVD, but the conceptual contribution is the layer-selective, post-hoc intervention strategy. The paper also engages with the knowledge-locality literature (Geva et al. 2021; Meng et al. 2022) by providing evidence that higher-order SVD components in MLP weight matrices encode conflicting or noisy answers, without committing to a specific locality theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LASER offers a zero-cost, post-hoc performance boost for any pre-trained Transformer, requiring no retraining, additional data, or architectural changes—making it immediately applicable to deployed models. The mechanistic finding that higher-order components encode semantically-conflicting responses that 'average out' to generic tokens provides a new lens on how factual knowledge is (mis)stored in weight matrices. Key open questions remain: why higher-order components accumulate noise during training, why the effect is concentrated in later MLP layers, whether it is text-specific or requires sufficient model scale, and how to reconcile the slight perplexity degradation with the accuracy gains. The modest and sometimes inconsistent improvements across tasks (e.g., HotPotQA shows near-zero gain) limit immediate practical adoption.
