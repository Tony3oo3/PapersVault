## Problem

Conventional semi-supervised learning assumes labeled and unlabeled data are drawn from the same distribution, but real-world open environments introduce inconsistencies in data distribution, feature space, and label space that severely degrade SSL performance. Prior robust SSL work addresses these inconsistencies from isolated perspectives without unified evaluation metrics, making it difficult to determine which algorithms are genuinely robust across diverse open-environment scenarios.

## Input / Output

The framework takes labeled examples and unlabeled examples where the unlabeled set contains a tunable fraction θ(t) of inconsistent samples parameterized by inconsistency level t ∈ [0,1], spanning image (CIFAR-10/100, Image-CLEF), text (IMDB, Amazon, AG News), and tabular (Iris, Wine, Letter) data. Rather than a single accuracy score, the output is a Robustness Analysis Curve (RAC) mapping t to Acc(t), from which six scalar metrics are derived (AUC, EA, WA, EVM, VS, RCC), plus a theoretical generalization error bound decomposed into five components: learner bias, sampling variance, and three inconsistency terms (Disc_D, Disc_F, Disc_L).

## Method

The paper contributes three interlocking components: (1) a Robustness Analysis Curve (RAC) that tracks model accuracy as a continuous function of inconsistency level t, yielding six complementary metrics capturing overall robustness, worst-case performance, variation magnitude, stability, and trend; (2) a theoretical generalization bound showing that SSL error in open environments decomposes into bias, variance, and three distinct inconsistency penalties—distribution shift (Disc_D), feature-space projection error (Disc_F), and label-space mismatch (Disc_L)—each governed by different algorithmic mechanisms (distribution alignment, feature mapping, and sample selection/weighting respectively); and (3) a unified benchmark re-implementing 20+ SSL algorithms (6 statistical, 10 deep, 4 robust) within the LAMDA-SSL toolkit, evaluated across 9 datasets under all three inconsistency types, revealing that feature and label space inconsistencies are more damaging than distribution shift, that SOTA deep SSL methods (FixMatch, FlexMatch, FreeMatch, SoftMatch) suffer severe robustness degradation, and that certain robust SSL methods do not consistently outperform standard deep SSL under the proposed metrics.

## Results

Across 9 datasets (tabular, image, text) and 20+ SSL algorithms, the paper shows that feature-space and label-space inconsistencies are more detrimental than data-distribution shifts: average expected robustness σ_E degrades from 0.015 (distribution) to 0.019 (feature) and 0.020 (label), while worst-case σ_W worsens from 0.028 to 0.039 and 0.044 respectively. On the Letter dataset under inconsistent distributions, VAT achieves the highest AUC (0.702) among deep SSL methods, while UASD (0.701) and CAFA (0.658) show the most stable performance (EVM ≤ 0.001). SOTA deep SSL methods FixMatch, FlexMatch, FreeMatch, and SoftMatch exhibit the poorest robustness (σ_E = 0.055–0.067, σ_W = 0.143–0.164), with FreeMatch dropping to AUC 0.474 on Letter and WA 0.406, below the supervised XGBoost baseline (0.643).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among statistical SSL methods, Assemble (Bennett et al., 2002) demonstrates the best robustness (σ_E = 0.009, σ_W = 0.017) and outperforms SSGMM (σ_E = 0.062, σ_W = 0.120) by a wide margin, while TSVM and Label Spreading occupy the middle ground. Among deep SSL methods, UDA (Xie et al., 2020) and Pseudo Label (Lee, 2013) show the best robustness (σ_E = 0.020 and 0.008), whereas the SOTA threshold-based methods FixMatch, FlexMatch, FreeMatch, and SoftMatch all suffer severe degradation (σ_E = 0.055–0.067, σ_W = 0.143–0.164), with FreeMatch and SoftMatch dropping below the supervised FT-Transformer baseline on Letter (AUC 0.474 and 0.584 vs. 0.660). Among robust SSL algorithms, UASD (Chen et al., 2020) achieves the best overall robustness (σ_E = 0.003, σ_W = 0.002) and near-flat RAC curves, while MTCF (Yu et al., 2020) and Fix-A-Step (Huang et al., 2023) perform worse than ordinary deep SSL methods in most settings (MTCF: σ_E = 0.077; Fix-A-Step: σ_W = 0.197). On CIFAR-10 under inconsistent label spaces, MTCF achieves the highest AUC (0.747) and FreeMatch (0.733) and FlexMatch (0.731) follow, but all still show substantial worst-case drops (WA 0.614–0.681 vs. supervised 0.643). The paper concludes that no single robust SSL algorithm consistently dominates across all three inconsistency types, and that Acc(0) alone is misleading—algorithms with high Acc(0) (e.g., FixMatch at 0.739 on Letter) can have the worst robustness profiles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the robust SSL literature, which has previously addressed label space inconsistency (Guo et al., 2020a; Chen et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2020) and data distribution shift (Guo et al., 2020b; Huang et al., 2021) in isolation, by unifying all three types of inconsistency—distribution, feature space, and label space—into a single theoretical framework with a five-component generalization error bound. It builds directly on Jia et al. (2023a), which defined the distribution discrepancy term Disc_D, by adding feature space (Disc_F) and label space (Disc_L) discrepancy terms and formalizing projection operations between heterogeneous spaces. The paper also departs from prior robust SSL evaluations that typically report a single accuracy at one inconsistency level, instead introducing the Robustness Analysis Curve (RAC) and six derived metrics (AUC, EA, WA, EVM, VS, RCC) to characterize performance across the full spectrum of inconsistency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark is important because it reveals that the field's most celebrated deep SSL methods (FixMatch, FlexMatch, FreeMatch, SoftMatch) are actually the least robust under open-environment conditions, while simpler methods like UDA or even supervised baselines can be more reliable—challenging the implicit assumption that SOTA SSL methods are safe to deploy in practice. The finding that feature and label space inconsistency causes more damage than distribution shift (Table 11) redirects attention to underexplored problem dimensions. Key open questions include: how to exploit potentially helpful information from inconsistent unlabeled data rather than merely filtering it out, how to handle high-dimensional combinations of multiple simultaneous inconsistencies (which the authors note leads to exponential evaluation cost), and whether the RAC-based metrics will become a standard evaluation protocol for the SSL community.
