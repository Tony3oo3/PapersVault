## Problem

Standard Deep Generative Models for tabular data learn distribution approximations but provide no guarantee that generated samples satisfy domain-specific constraints such as feature ordering or range relationships. The paper demonstrates that standard DGMs (WGAN, TableGAN, CTGAN, TVAE, GOGGLE) frequently violate such constraints—reaching 100% non-compliance on some datasets—and that no prior method incorporates complex background knowledge into tabular DGMs with hard satisfaction guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a standard DGM, a finite set of linear inequality constraints over the dataset's continuous features (each of the form Σw_k·x_k + b ≥ 0, with up to 31 constraints spanning up to 17 features per constraint), and a user-defined variable ordering that determines the sequential computation of features. It produces a Constrained DGM whose output samples are guaranteed to satisfy all constraints, with each feature value minimally adjusted from the original DGM prediction. The CL can be integrated at training time for end-to-end learning or applied as a post-processing guardrail at inference time on black-box models without retraining.

## Method

The core contribution is a differentiable Constraint Layer (CL) that incrementally computes each feature value by clamping the DGM's raw output to feasible bounds derived from the constraints. Given a variable ordering, the constraint set is transformed inductively: for each variable, constraints where it appears positively and negatively are pairwise reduced (eliminated) into constraints over the remaining variables, yielding per-feature upper and lower bounds. The CL applies a min-max clamping operation (with a small ε offset for strict inequalities) to project each feature into its feasible interval, ensuring the output satisfies all constraints while minimally deviating from the DGM's prediction. Because the CL is differentiable, gradients backpropagate through it during training, allowing the DGM to learn to exploit the constraint structure and improve sample quality. The paper proves that the resulting C-DGM is always compliant (Theorem 3.3) and that the correction is optimal in the sense that no other feasible sample is simultaneously closer to the original prediction along all dimensions (Theorems 3.5–3.6).

## Results

Across six real-world tabular datasets (URL, WiDS, LCLD, Heloc, FSP, News), C-DGMs achieve up to 6.5% improvement in utility F1-score over their standard counterparts (e.g., C-GOGGLE: 0.409 vs. GOGGLE: 0.344) and consistently lower detection scores (i.e., harder to distinguish from real data). All C-DGMs produce 0% constraint violation rate, whereas standard DGMs range from 3% to 100% violation (WGAN reaches 100% on LCLD). The largest utility gains appear for GOGGLE (F1: +6.5%, AUC: +4.3%) and TableGAN (F1: +4.5%), while CTGAN and TVAE show smaller but consistent improvements of 0.5–1.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

C-WGAN outperforms WGAN on all six metrics (e.g., utility F1: 0.483 vs. 0.463; detection AUC: 0.934 vs. 0.954, lower is better). C-TableGAN beats TableGAN across the board with the second-largest utility gap (F1: 0.375 vs. 0.330, +4.5%). C-CTGAN marginally improves over CTGAN on weighted F1 (0.537 vs. 0.532) and AUC utility (0.773 vs. 0.771) but is essentially tied on F1 utility (0.516 vs. 0.517); it wins on all three detection metrics. C-TVAE improves utility F1 by 1.0% (0.507 vs. 0.497) and AUC by 0.6% (0.773 vs. 0.767), but slightly worsens detection AUC (0.898 vs. 0.892), the only metric where a C-DGM underperforms its standard counterpart. C-GOGGLE shows the most dramatic improvement, gaining 6.5% in utility F1 (0.409 vs. 0.344) and 4.3% in utility AUC (0.667 vs. 0.624), while also improving detection on all three metrics. Overall, C-DGMs win or tie on 28 out of 30 metric comparisons, with the two exceptions being C-CTGAN's F1 utility (−0.001) and C-TVAE's detection AUC (+0.006).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of tabular data synthesis (WGAN, TableGAN, CTGAN, TVAE, GOGGLE) and neuro-symbolic AI, but departs from both lines in a specific way. Prior neuro-symbolic approaches either penalize constraint violations in the loss function (giving no hard guarantee) or handle only propositional logic / very simple constraints (e.g., GOGGLE's correlation injection, Liello et al.'s structured-object GANs). The key conceptual departure is treating linear inequalities as a first-class, differentiable architectural component—a Constraint Layer—that provides hard compliance guarantees while remaining compatible with any DGM topology, and that can be inserted at training time (for quality gains) or inference time (as a guardrail on black-box models).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard DGMs violate domain constraints in up to 100% of generated samples (WGAN on LCLD) exposes a practical gap that matters for any deployment requiring regulatory or physical compliance (clinical data, financial records). The constraint layer is a lightweight, drop-in solution with negligible generation-time overhead, making it immediately adoptable. However, the method is limited to linear inequalities, and the quality improvements (≤6.5%) are modest; open questions include extending to non-linear or disjunctive constraints, understanding how constraint density interacts with model capacity, and whether the training-time quality gain generalizes to more complex constraint structures beyond the 31-constraint ceiling tested here.
