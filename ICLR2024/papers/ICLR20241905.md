## Problem

Propensity-based debiased collaborative filtering requires causal balancing constraints to hold for any function φ(·), yet existing methods either train propensities with cross-entropy (which only balances a single derivative-based function) or use a finite set of manually selected balancing functions that may not adequately represent the prediction error. This paper identifies and bridges this gap by showing that kernel-based balancing in RKHS can satisfy the balancing constraint for an infinite-dimensional function class.

## Input / Output

The method consumes user-item feature vectors x_{u,i} ∈ R^K, binary observation indicators o_{u,i}, and observed ratings r_{u,i} from a partially observed user-item matrix. It produces debiased rating predictions via a base model (matrix factorization) and balancing weights ŵ_{u,i} learned through kernel-based convex optimization. The balancing weights are applied to observed samples in both IPS and DR loss formulations, and the kernel hyper-parameter σ² and number of balancing functions J are tuned per dataset.

## Method

The paper proposes kernel-based causal balancing in RKHS using universal Gaussian or exponential kernels, grounded in the representer theorem to reduce infinite constraints to finite ones. Two variants are introduced: Worst-Case Kernel Balancing (WKB), which solves a minimax game over the normalized RKHS to control worst-case bias, and Adaptive Kernel Balancing (AKB), which first fits the prediction error e_{u,i} with kernel expansions, then selects the J kernel functions with the largest |α| coefficients as the balancing targets. AKB solves a convex optimization with entropy regularization on weights and slack-variable penalties on balancing deviations, solved via gradient descent. The full AKBDR algorithm alternates training the prediction model (minimizing the KBDR loss), the balancing weight model (minimizing the AKB objective), and the imputation model (minimizing weighted MSE), with theoretical generalization bounds derived via Rademacher complexity in RKHS.

## Results

On the three benchmark datasets (COAT, MUSIC, PRODUCT), the proposed AKBDR-Gau achieves the best performance across all metrics. On the large-scale PRODUCT dataset, AKBDR-Gau reaches AUC 0.782, NDCG@20 0.503, and F1@20 0.148, outperforming the strongest baseline TDR-JL (AUC 0.770, NDCG@20 0.483, F1@20 0.137) by 1.2% in AUC and 2.0% in NDCG@20. On COAT, AKBDR-Gau achieves AUC 0.694, NDCG@5 0.498, F1@5 0.332; on MUSIC, AUC 0.732, NDCG@5 0.483, F1@5 0.142. All improvements over the best baselines are statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05) via paired t-test.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed AKBDR-Gau consistently outperforms all 11 baselines across all three datasets and all three metrics. Against the strongest DR-based baseline TDR-JL, AKBDR-Gau gains +1.2% AUC and +2.0% NDCG@20 on PRODUCT (0.782 vs. 0.770; 0.503 vs. 0.483), with similar margins on COAT and MUSIC. The balancing-aware baselines IPS-V2 and DR-V2 (from Li et al., ICML 2023) are the closest competitors, confirming the importance of propensity balancing, but they still trail AKBDR by 1–2% in AUC because they rely on manually selected finite balancing functions rather than adaptive kernel-based selection. Among the proposed kernel balancing variants, the hierarchy AKB > WKB > RKB is consistent: random kernel balancing (RKB) performs worst due to uninformative function selection, worst-case kernel balancing (WKB) is competitive when J is small but does not improve with larger J, while adaptive kernel balancing (AKB) monotonically benefits from increasing J by selecting the most bias-relevant kernel functions. The Gaussian kernel generally outperforms the exponential kernel, particularly on PRODUCT where AKBDR-Gau achieves 0.782 AUC versus 0.773 for the exponential variant.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the propensity-based debiased collaborative filtering framework (IPS from Schnabel et al. 2016, DR from Wang et al. 2019) by addressing a gap identified in the causal balancing literature: existing propensity learning methods either use cross-entropy (shown here to be a special case of balancing with a single function) or rely on manually selected finite balancing functions (Li et al. 2023e). The key departure is moving the balancing problem into a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with universal kernels (Gaussian/exponential), where any continuous function can be approximated, and then adaptively selecting which kernel functions to balance based on their contribution to the prediction error. The representer theorem ensures the infinite-dimensional balancing problem reduces to a finite one, and the paper derives generalization error bounds in this RKHS setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded alternative to ad-hoc balancing function selection, showing that the universal approximation property of kernels can in principle satisfy the full causal balancing constraint for any continuous function. However, practical limitations remain: the adaptive kernel balancing requires solving a kernel regression at each training iteration (costly for large |D|), the slack threshold C and regularization γ are additional hyperparameters, and experiments use only MF as the base model. Open questions include scalability to industrial-scale datasets beyond PRODUCT, whether the kernel bandwidth σ interacts with the base model architecture, and whether the adaptive selection strategy generalizes to multi-task or sequential recommendation settings where the error function changes over time.
