## Problem

Real-world time series exhibit intricate, non-stationary temporal variations that are difficult to forecast. While prior work applies seasonal-trend decomposition and multiperiodicity analysis, no existing method simultaneously exploits the fact that time series present distinct patterns at different sampling scales and that different scales contribute complementary forecasting capabilities. The paper identifies this multiscale-mixing perspective as an underexplored axis for disentangling complex variations and improving prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241516/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a past observation window of length P (e.g., 96 timesteps) with C variates (ranging from 1 for M4 to 862 for Traffic) and produces a future prediction of length F (96–720 for long-term, 12 for PEMS, 6–48 for M4). The input is first downsampled via average pooling into M scales (M=3 for long-term, M=1 for short-term), yielding series of lengths P, P/2, P/4, etc. The final output is a sum of per-scale predictions, each generated by an independent single-layer linear predictor, so the output naturally decomposes into fine-grained seasonal and coarse-grained trend contributions.

## Method

TimeMixer is a fully MLP-based architecture built around two blocks. Past-Decomposable-Mixing (PDM) first decomposes each multiscale series into seasonal and trend components via moving-average decomposition, then applies directional mixing: seasonal components are mixed bottom-up (fine→coarse) using temporal linear layers with GELU, aggregating microscopic detail into larger periods, while trend components are mixed top-down (coarse→fine), letting macroscopic knowledge guide finer-scale trend modeling. Future-Multipredictor-Mixing (FMM) assigns each scale its own single-layer linear predictor and sums the resulting predictions, exploiting the complementary forecasting strengths of fine-scale (seasonal precision) and coarse-scale (trend accuracy) observations. The entire pipeline uses only linear layers and GELU activations along the temporal dimension, avoiding attention or convolution, which yields favorable GPU memory and runtime compared to Transformer-based baselines.

## Results

On long-term forecasting (input length 96, averaged over prediction lengths 96/192/336/720), TimeMixer achieves the best MSE across all 8 benchmarks, with headline results of 0.240 MSE on Weather, 0.216 on Solar-Energy, 0.182 on Electricity, and 0.447 on ETTh1. The largest gains over the strongest baseline PatchTST are a 24.7% MSE reduction on Solar-Energy (0.216 vs 0.287) and a 9.4% reduction on Weather (0.240 vs 0.265). On short-term forecasting, TimeMixer also leads on all four PEMS traffic datasets (e.g., 14.63 MAE on PEMS03 vs 15.97 for SCINet) and all M4 frequency subsets (e.g., 0.840 OWA weighted average vs 0.851 for TimesNet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TimeMixer achieves the best MSE and MAE on all 8 long-term forecasting benchmarks, outperforming the strongest baseline PatchTST by 9.4% MSE on Weather (0.240 vs 0.265) and 24.7% on Solar-Energy (0.216 vs 0.287), and by 13.4% on ETTh1 (0.447 vs 0.516). Against other competitive baselines, TimeMixer leads TimesNet by 4.4% MSE on Weather and 46.4% on Solar-Energy, and outperforms DLinear (a simple MLP baseline) by 15.7% on Weather and 34.8% on Solar-Energy. On short-term PEMS traffic forecasting, TimeMixer beats SCINet (the best short-term baseline) by 8.4% MAE on PEMS03 (14.63 vs 15.97) and 12.4% on PEMS08 (15.22 vs 17.38), while channel-independent models like PatchTST and DLinear degrade substantially (e.g., PatchTST reaches 18.95 MAE on PEMS03 vs TimeMixer's 14.63). On the M4 univariate benchmark, TimeMixer edges out TimesNet by small but consistent margins across all frequencies (e.g., 11.723 vs 11.829 weighted SMAPE; 0.840 vs 0.851 OWA), with the largest gap on the 'Others' subset (4.564 vs 4.891 SMAPE). Overall, TimeMixer wins every head-to-head comparison across all 18 benchmark settings, with the most dramatic advantages on low-forecastability datasets (Solar-Energy, ETT) and high-variate-count datasets (Traffic, Electricity).

## Relation to Prior Work

TimeMixer adapts the mixing paradigm from MLP-Mixer (Tolstikhin et al., 2021) to time series forecasting and incorporates the moving-average seasonal-trend decomposition from Autoformer (Wu et al., 2021) as a core component. It departs from prior multiscale approaches (Pyraformer, SCINet) by simultaneously leveraging all scales for both past representation learning and future prediction, rather than using them sequentially or for a single purpose. The key conceptual departure is the directional asymmetry in mixing: seasonal components are aggregated bottom-up (fine-to-coarse) while trend components are aggregated top-down (coarse-to-fine), a design motivated by the observation that fine scales carry detailed periodicity while coarse scales carry clean macroscopic trends.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TimeMixer demonstrates that a fully MLP-based architecture can achieve consistent state-of-the-art across 18 benchmarks while using substantially less GPU memory and running time than Transformer-based competitors (e.g., ~16× less memory than PatchTST at sequence length 3072), making it a practical choice for deployment. The multiscale mixing perspective offers a useful unifying lens that subsumes both decomposition and multiperiodicity analysis as special cases. Open questions include whether the fixed number of scales (M=3 for long-term, M=1 for short-term) can be made adaptive per series, whether the moving-average decomposition is truly sufficient compared to the DFT-based alternative that showed slightly better results in ablations, and whether the approach extends naturally to irregularly-sampled or multivariate-correlated settings beyond the channel-independent evaluation used here.
