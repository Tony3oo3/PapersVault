## Problem

Sparse linear regression under local differential privacy (LDP) was poorly understood: prior work only established lower bounds for the 1-sparse case, no efficient non-interactive LDP algorithm existed, and the interactive LDP upper bound in Wang & Xu (2021) contained a technical flaw in its sensitivity analysis. This paper provides the first general k-sparse lower bounds, the first efficient NLDP algorithm, and a corrected interactive LDP analysis.

## Input / Output

Each of n users holds a private pair (x_i, y_i) where x_i ∈ R^d is a sub-Gaussian feature vector and y_i = ⟨θ*, x_i⟩ + ζ is the response; the server optionally also receives m public unlabeled feature vectors x_j from the same distribution. The output is a k-sparse parameter estimate θ̂^priv ∈ R^d minimizing the ℓ2 error ‖θ̂^priv − θ*‖₂, produced under (ε,δ)-LDP constraints. The key structural constraint is that each user's message must be locally private before aggregation, and the estimator must exploit sparsity (‖θ*‖₀ ≤ k) to achieve meaningful rates in high dimensions.

## Method

For the non-interactive setting, the paper introduces a closed-form private estimator: each user applies ℓ2-norm clipping to x_i, coordinate-wise shrinking to x_i and y_i, then adds independent Gaussian noise to the outer product x̄_i x̄_i^T and the vector x̃_i ỹ_i before sending to the server; the server aggregates the noisy sufficient statistics and applies an element-wise soft-thresholding operator S_λn to (Σ̇_X̄X̄)^{-1} Σ̇_X̃Ỹ, yielding a sparse estimate without solving any optimization problem. With public unlabeled data, the covariance matrix is estimated non-privately from the public samples, removing the dominant √d noise factor and improving the rate to Õ(√(dk log d)/(ε√n)). For the interactive setting, the paper rectifies the LDP Iterative Hard Thresholding algorithm by correcting the per-sample gradient sensitivity from O(1) to O(√k) and generalizing from uniform {−1,+1}^d covariates to general sub-Gaussian distributions via coordinate-wise shrinking. Lower bounds in both settings are proved using a private version of Assouad's lemma with a random k-sparse prior, which overcomes the combinatorial explosion that prevented extending the earlier Fano's-lemma-based 1-sparse analysis to general k.

## Results

For the non-interactive LDP (NLDP) model, the paper establishes a lower bound of Ω(√(dk log d / nε²)) on the ℓ₂-estimation error for k-sparse parameters, and proposes the first efficient NLDP algorithm achieving an upper bound of Õ(d√(k log d) / √(nε)) under sub-Gaussian data. With public unlabeled data, the upper bound improves to Õ(√(dk log d) / ε√n), closing the gap by a factor of O(√d). For the sequentially interactive LDP model, the lower bound is Ω(√(dk / nε²)) and the corrected iterative hard thresholding algorithm achieves Õ(k√d / √(nε)), which the authors show is the correct rate (the previously claimed Õ(√(dk) / √(nε)) in Wang & Xu 2021 was based on a flawed sensitivity analysis).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Wang & Xu (2021), the primary prior work on sparse linear regression in LDP, this paper makes three key advances: (1) it extends the 1-sparse lower bound to the general k-sparse case in both non-interactive and interactive settings using a private Assouad's lemma, whereas the prior proof technique could not be extended; (2) it identifies and corrects a technical flaw in Wang & Xu's upper bound for the interactive setting, showing the correct rate is Õ(k√d/√(nε)) rather than the previously claimed Õ(√(dk)/√(nε)); and (3) it provides the first efficient NLDP algorithm, closing a gap where only a lower bound existed. Against central DP baselines (Cai et al. 2021, Kifer et al. 2012, Hu et al. 2022), the paper demonstrates that the NLDP model incurs an additional factor of O(√d/ε) in the estimation error compared to central DP, revealing a fundamental separation between the two privacy models. Compared to Smith et al. (2017) for interactive local DP on general (non-sparse) linear regression, the paper's sparse-specific algorithm improves the upper bound from Õ(d³/√(nε)) to Õ(k√d/√(nε)), a gain of roughly Õ(d²/√k) when k ≪ d. The paper also extends to heavy-tailed responses (bounded 2p-moment), a setting not covered by any of the central DP baselines in Table 1 for the local model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends and corrects Wang & Xu (2021), the first study of sparse linear regression in the LDP model, which had only addressed the 1-sparse case for lower bounds and contained a technical flaw in its interactive upper bound. For lower bounds, the authors replace the private Fano's lemma approach (used by Wang & Xu) with a private Assouad's lemma from Acharya et al. (2022), enabling a clean extension to general k-sparsity. For the upper bound in the non-interactive setting, the paper introduces a novel closed-form estimator based on the proximal operator (soft-thresholding) applied to a privately estimated OLS solution, departing from the optimization-based approaches (LASSO, Dantzig) that are difficult to privatize. The interactive algorithm builds on the private randomizer of Duchi et al. (2014a) combined with iterative hard thresholding, generalized from the uniform {±1}^d covariate assumption to general sub-Gaussian distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental limitation of non-interactive LDP for high-dimensional sparse regression: a polynomial dependency on dimension d is unavoidable, in stark contrast to the near-optimal rates achievable in the non-private and central DP settings. The correction of Wang & Xu's upper bound (from Õ(√(dk)/√(nε)) to Õ(k√d/√(nε))) is an important fix for the field, as the original claim of near-optimality was incorrect. A key open question is whether the Õ(√d) gap between the NLDP lower and upper bounds is fundamental or an artifact of the current algorithm; the authors conjecture the lower bound is not tight. The extension to heavy-tailed responses and the use of public unlabeled data to recover the √d factor suggest practical pathways for deployment, but the fully heavy-tailed case (both x and y) remains unaddressed.
