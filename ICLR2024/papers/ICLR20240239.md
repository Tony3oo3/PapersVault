## Problem

Open-ended learning algorithms can identify learnable tasks via learning progress, but they cannot distinguish interesting from uninteresting tasks, causing agents to waste time on trivial variations (e.g., rearranging silverware in slightly new configurations). Prior attempts to quantify interestingness with hand-crafted formulas consistently produce pathologies when optimized against (Goodhart's law). This paper addresses the gap by proposing to use foundation models, which have internalized human notions of interestingness from training on vast human-generated data, as a Model of Interestingness (MoI) to guide task selection.

## Input / Output

The FM-based MoI receives the set of tasks the agent currently performs well (success rate above a threshold) and the set of remaining tasks to classify, outputting a binary True/False interestingness label per task; boring tasks have their sampling weights multiplied by 0.001. In the infinite-task-space setting (AI2-THOR), GPT-4 additionally generates new tasks in natural language and translates them into executable reward-function code (sequences of environment-state checks like obj_attributes("Apple", "isPickedUp": True)). The RL agent itself consumes RGB observations (64×64 for Crafter, 300×300 for AI2-THOR) or 7×7 grid-cell encodings (BabyAI) concatenated with a natural-language task description, and outputs discrete actions.

## Method

OMNI combines two components: (1) a learning-progress curriculum (extended from Kanitscheider et al. with a normalization by random-policy success rates to reduce early-training bias) that assigns sampling weights based on bidirectional learning progress, and (2) an FM-based MoI that filters those weights by interestingness. In finite task spaces, the MoI iteratively partitions tasks: it selects the highest-success-rate uncategorized task as interesting, then prompts the FM (few-shot for Crafter/BabyAI) to identify boring tasks relative to the growing interesting set, repeating until all tasks are classified. In infinite task spaces, GPT-4 is prompted zero-shot to propose three new learnable-and-interesting tasks along with their completion-check code, augmenting the growing task set each evaluation cycle. The RL agent is a task-conditioned PPO policy; the key architectural insight is that the FM's judgment of interestingness—derived from human language patterns about what is novel, diverse, and worthwhile—replaces any hand-crafted interestingness metric, avoiding the pathologies of optimizing against a fixed quantitative proxy.

## Results

On Crafter, OMNI learns 82 tasks (CI: 80–87) with an average success rate of 0.56 (CI: 0.54–0.58) across all tasks and 0.78 (CI: 0.76–0.80) on interesting tasks only, compared to 55 tasks / 0.42 for LP and 4 tasks / 0.030 for uniform sampling. On BabyAI, OMNI learns 8 tasks (CI: 7–10) with a 7.5e-3 average success rate, versus 4 tasks / 5.9e-3 for LP and 1 task / 4.7e-3 for uniform. In the infinite AI2-THOR task space, OMNI learns 13 tasks (CI: 11–17) at a 0.6 success threshold, compared to 2 for LP and 0 for uniform sampling. All differences between OMNI and both baselines are statistically significant (p < 1e-3, Mann-Whitney U test) at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OMNI is compared against two baselines: (1) uniform task sampling, and (2) the Learning Progress (LP) curriculum from Kanitscheider et al. (2021), which samples tasks proportional to their recent change in success probability. OMNI outperforms both baselines on every metric in all three domains with high statistical significance (p < 1e-3). Against uniform sampling, the gains are dramatic: in Crafter, OMNI learns 82 vs. 4 tasks (20×) and achieves 0.56 vs. 0.030 average success (19×); in AI2-THOR, OMNI learns 13 tasks while uniform learns 0. Against the LP baseline, OMNI's advantage is more moderate but consistent: in Crafter it learns 82 vs. 55 tasks and reaches 0.78 vs. 0.52 success on interesting tasks; in BabyAI it learns 8 vs. 4 tasks; in AI2-THOR it learns 13 vs. 2 tasks. The key mechanism is that LP is distracted by learnable-but-uninteresting tasks (e.g., numerical variants like 'collect 2 wood' vs. 'collect wood'), while OMNI's FM-based Model of Interestingness suppresses sampling of such tasks by a factor of 1000, redirecting compute toward genuinely novel skills.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learning-progress-based auto-curriculum of Kanitscheider et al. (2021) by adding a normalization step against a random-policy baseline and, more substantially, by gating task sampling through a foundation-model-based Model of Interestingness (MoI). It departs from the long line of hand-crafted interestingness metrics (novelty search, intrinsic motivation, regret-based selection) that the authors argue suffer from Goodhart's-law pathologies, instead leveraging the implicit human notions of interestingness already encoded in FMs trained on vast human-generated text. Among recent FM-for-task-selection works (Du et al. 2023, Colas et al. 2023, Wang et al. 2023a), OMNI is distinguished by integrating interestingness filtering directly into the sampling-weight computation and, in the infinite-task-space setting, by having the FM jointly propose tasks and generate executable reward-function code.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper tackles what it calls the 'Achilles Heel' of open-endedness—quantifying interestingness—by showing that a few-shot or zero-shot prompt to an FM can serve as a near-oracle filter, which is a conceptually simple yet effective recipe that could be dropped into many existing auto-curriculum pipelines. Limitations include heavy reliance on API-based FMs (cost, latency, potential drift as models update), very low absolute success rates in BabyAI (7.5e-3), and evaluation in relatively constrained domains (15 interesting Crafter tasks, a single AI2-THOR kitchen). Open questions include whether FM-based MoIs generalize to truly unbounded, multi-modal environments, how they behave as the agent's skill distribution shifts over very long training horizons, and whether the approach can bootstrap the kind of open-ended discovery that would make AI-Generating Algorithms practical.
