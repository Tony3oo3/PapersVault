## Problem

Even the strongest LLMs with chain-of-thought prompting make errors on multi-step reasoning, and existing verification methods require external models, finetuning, or domain-specific exemplars that limit their practicality. The paper asks whether an LLM can zero-shot check its own step-by-step reasoning in a general-purpose way, relying solely on the original model without any additional data or external resources.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242208/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a question and a step-by-step CoT solution as input, and checks each step via four independent LLM calls (not a single context window), producing a ternary result per step (support / contradict / not directly related). These per-step results are integrated into a scalar confidence score in [0,1] via a sigmoid of the weighted count of failed checks, which then serves as a weight for voting across multiple candidate solutions to the same question. The same prompts are used across all LLMs and datasets with no task-specific customization.

## Method

The key insight is that directly prompting an LLM to 'check' a step is ineffective (it almost always declares correctness), so SelfCheck decomposes checking into four stages: target extraction, information collection, step regeneration, and result comparison. The regeneration stage is the critical mechanism: the LLM is asked to independently re-derive the step's target using only the collected context, without seeing the original step, which decorrelates errors between generation and checking. The comparison stage then asks whether the regenerated output 'supports,' 'contradicts,' or 'is not directly related to' the original step. The confidence score is computed as 2·Sigmoid(−λ_{−1}·#contradict − λ_0·#not-related) with fixed hyperparameters (λ_{−1}=1, λ_0=0.3), deliberately excluding successful checks to avoid rewarding longer reasoning chains. This yields a soft, general-purpose verification that requires no finetuning, no exemplars, and no external resources.

## Results

On GSM8K, MathQA, and MATH with GPT-3.5 as both generator and checker (2 solutions per question), SelfCheck improves final answer accuracy over majority voting by +2.8%, +5.4%, and +2.2% respectively. With GPT-4, gains are +2.6% on MathQA and +3.4% on MATH, while GSM8K shows a negligible −0.2% (near-saturation at 87% accuracy). Using GPT-3.5 as checker on GPT-4-generated solutions still yields positive gains (+1.0% to +3.0%), confirming that a cheaper LLM can serve as an effective verifier. With 10 solutions per question (Figure 2), SelfCheck's advantage over majority voting widens further, and unlike majority voting which saturates, SelfCheck's accuracy continues to increase with larger ensembles (Figure 5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is majority voting (Self-Consistency Decoding, Wang et al. 2022), which SelfCheck outperforms on all three math datasets with GPT-3.5 (by 2.2–5.4%) and on MathQA and MATH with GPT-4 (by 2.6–3.4%); the only exception is GSM8K with GPT-4 where both methods are near-saturated (~87%). Against Deductive Verification (DV, Ling et al. 2023) and Self-Verification (SV, Weng et al. 2022), SelfCheck achieves larger relative accuracy gains over majority voting in their reported settings (Figure 2), despite being zero-shot and requiring no in-context exemplars. Notably, using a weaker LLM (GPT-3.5) to check GPT-4-generated solutions still yields positive gains on GSM8K and MathQA, suggesting that cross-model checking further decorrelates generator and checker errors. On the logic-reasoning SNR dataset (Appendix D), SelfCheck improves accuracy from 44.5% to 48.0% (+3.5%), where majority voting is not applicable.

## Relation to Prior Work

SelfCheck carves new ground in the reasoning-verification space by repurposing the LLM's generative capability for checking, rather than treating verification as a separate classification or supervised task. Prior approaches either require hand-crafted exemplars (Self-Verification, Deductive Verification), external solvers/databases, or finetuned verifier models (Cobbe et al., Lightman et al.), all of which limit practical applicability. The key conceptual departure is the 'regenerate-and-compare' mechanism: instead of asking the LLM to judge correctness directly (which the paper shows is nearly useless, with >90% of answers deemed correct), it decomposes checking into target extraction, information collection, independent regeneration, and comparison, thereby leveraging the LLM's generative strengths and decorrelating errors between generation and verification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SelfCheck offers a practical, drop-in verification scheme that requires no additional data, external resources, or finetuning, making it broadly applicable across domains with minimal prompt adaptation. The finding that a weaker LLM (GPT-3.5) can effectively check a stronger one (GPT-4) on simpler tasks has direct cost implications for deployment. However, the improvements over majority voting are modest (2–5%), the approach is validated primarily on math and one logic dataset, and the checking itself remains imperfect (confidence scores are soft, not binary). Open questions include generalization to open-ended or multi-modal reasoning, performance on very long reasoning chains, and whether the four-stage decomposition is optimal or whether simpler variants could achieve similar decorrelation.
