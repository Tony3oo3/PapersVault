## Problem

Faithful causal explanations of black-box NLP models require counterfactuals, but acquiring true counterfactuals is impractical without full knowledge of the data-generating process. Existing approximation methods rely on simple local manipulations or costly manual annotation, and no established benchmark exists for evaluating causal explanation methods beyond CEBaB. The paper also identifies a theoretical gap: non-causal explanation methods (e.g., LIME-style attribution) can fail to preserve the correct ranking of concept importance, while no prior work formally guarantees order-faithfulness.

## Input / Output

The method takes a query text, a causal graph (specifying concepts, confounders, and their relationships), an intervention specification (concept to change and target value), a pre-defined candidate text set partitioned into treatment and control groups, and a black-box model whose predictions are to be explained. The output is a causal effect estimate (ICaCE or CaCE) computed as the difference in model predictions between the query and its approximated counterfactual or matched text, plus a concept importance ranking across interventions. In CEBaB, model outputs are five-dimensional sentiment vectors, and the error metric compares estimated vs. golden ICaCE using L2, cosine, or norm-difference distances.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary approaches for approximating counterfactuals. First, CF Generation prompts an LLM (zero-shot, few-shot, or fine-tuned T5) to rewrite a text by changing one concept while explicitly holding confounding concepts fixed per the causal graph's adjustment set. Second, Causal Representation Learning trains an encoder (SentenceTransformer or RoBERTa) via a six-component contrastive loss that simultaneously pulls the query's embedding toward LLM-generated CFs and valid matches (sharing identical adjustment variable values) while pushing it away from misspecified CFs (wrong intervention) and misspecified matches (differing confounder values). The LLM is used only during training; at inference, matching is a single cosine-similarity lookup over the candidate set, making it ~1000× faster than generative approaches. A key theoretical contribution proves that any approximated-CF explanation method is order-faithful (rank-preserving w.r.t. true causal effects) for all DGPs, while non-causal methods can be shown to violate this property. Top-K averaging over multiple matches or generated CFs universally improves estimation accuracy across all methods.

## Results

On the CEBaB benchmark, the paper evaluates explanation quality via Err (Eq. 7; lower is better) averaged over 24 interventions and five explained models (DistilBERT, BERT, RoBERTa, Llama-2-7B, Llama-2-13B). At K=1, the Causal Model (matching) achieves an average L2 Err of 0.52, outperforming the best matching baselines (PT RoBERTa: 0.59, PT S-Transformer: 0.59, Approx: 0.58). The Fine-tune Generative (T5-base) achieves the overall best practical score with an average L2 Err of 0.28. Top-K=10 matching reduces the Causal Model's L2 Err to 0.46 and the Fine-tune Generative to 0.24, confirming that Top-K universally improves every method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Causal Model (the paper's matching method) consistently outperforms all four matching baselines across all five explained models and all three distance metrics. Against Propensity score matching (Benedetto et al., 2018), the Causal Model reduces average L2 Err from 0.70 to 0.52 at K=1 (a 26% relative improvement). Against Approx (Abraham et al., 2022), which randomly selects from valid matches, the Causal Model improves from 0.58 to 0.52. Against PT RoBERTa and PT S-Transformer (both at 0.59 L2), the Causal Model achieves 0.52, a roughly 12% relative gain. However, the generative approaches (especially Fine-tune Generative at 0.28 L2) substantially outperform all matching methods, including the Causal Model, by a wide margin. The paper notes that if ground-truth CFs were available in the candidate set, the Causal Model would surpass even the generative methods (0.09 vs. 0.28 L2), suggesting matching could match generation quality given sufficient candidate diversity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes ideas from causal inference (matching, back-door criterion, do-calculus), contrastive representation learning, and LLM-based text generation into a unified framework for model-agnostic NLP explanation. It departs from the Causal Proxy Model (Wu et al., 2023a), which requires training a separate explainer per model, by making counterfactuals the central primitive so that the same CF approximations can explain any black-box model. The causal representation learning objective (six contrastive loss components over CF, matched, and misspecified sets) is novel relative to prior matching baselines (propensity scores, pre-trained embeddings, the Approx method from CEBaB), and the use of LLMs as CF generators at training time (rather than inference time) repurposes generative models as a supervision signal for a lightweight encoder.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical guarantee (order-faithfulness) that CF-based explainers are rank-preserving while non-causal ones are not, giving practitioners a principled reason to prefer causal methods. Practically, the 1000× speedup of the matching approach over LLM generation makes causal explanation feasible at scale, and the universal Top-K improvement finding is a simple, broadly applicable recipe. Open questions include closing the quality gap between generative and matching explainers, extending beyond the relatively simple CEBaB causal graph to more complex DAGs, and validating the GPT-4-constructed benchmark against larger-scale human annotation. The requirement of a known causal graph and a pre-defined candidate set remains a practical constraint for real-world deployment.
