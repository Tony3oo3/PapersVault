## Problem

It is unclear whether LLMs genuinely adapt to new tasks or merely retrieve capabilities already present in their pretraining data, because most benchmarks use tasks likely seen at web scale. This paper tackles the question by introducing MTOB, a benchmark for translating between English and Kalamang—a language with fewer than 200 speakers and virtually no web presence—using only a single 573-page field linguistics grammar book, a bilingual word list, and 500 parallel sentences, making the task genuinely unseen to any pretrained model.

## Input / Output

The model receives a source sentence (Kalamang or English) along with retrieved context assembled in the prompt: up to k_w=2 closest word-list entries per input word, up to k_s=2 parallel sentences per input word, and grammar book passages retrieved via longest-common-substring or embedding similarity (or, for Claude 2's 100K context window, 50K–100K token chunks of the book dumped unconditionally). The output is a single translated sentence, evaluated with chrF on a 50-sentence test set per direction (100 total, split to match the human baseline's disjoint halves). The entire 'training' corpus is under 250K tokens of English and under 25K tokens of Kalamang—three orders of magnitude less parallel data than typical MT systems require.

## Method

MTOB frames language learning as L2 acquisition from curated natural-language explanations rather than L1 acquisition from massive parallel corpora. The paper evaluates 12 LLMs (LLaMA-7B/13B/30B, Llama 2-7B/13B/70B, GPT-3.5-turbo, text-davinci-003, GPT-4, Claude 2) across eight retrieval-augmented in-context learning configurations combining word-list (W), parallel-sentence (S), and grammar-book (G) context, plus two long-context settings (G^m ≈50K tokens, G^l ≈100K tokens) tested only with Claude 2. Retrieval uses longest-common-substring distance (which outperforms embedding-based retrieval because the grammar book contains substantial out-of-distribution Kalamang text). A data-matched human baseline—the first author, who spent ~10 hours reading the grammar book and then translated the test sentences—establishes a lower bound on achievable performance (51.6/57.0 chrF vs. best model 44.7/45.8 chrF). Finetuning experiments (LoRA on grammar book text, FLAN-T5/mT5 on parallel sentences) are also included but underperform the best in-context learning results, suggesting that the long-context retrieval approach is currently more effective for this data regime.

## Results

The best model baseline is Claude 2 with word list + parallel sentence + long grammar book context (W+S+G^l), achieving 44.7 chrF on Kalamang→English and 45.8 chrF on English→Kalamang translation. A human who learned Kalamang from the same reference materials achieves 51.6 and 57.0 chrF respectively, a gap of +6.9 and +11.2 chrF. Without any context, all models are essentially unable to translate (scores near 0–16 chrF), confirming that Kalamang is genuinely unseen in LLM pretraining. Traditional finetuned MT baselines (FLAN-T5 XL) reach only 35.2 and 30.8 chrF, well below the best in-context LLM results.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Claude 2 with long-context grammar book (W+S+G^l) outperforms all other model baselines in both translation directions (44.7/45.8 chrF vs. GPT-4's 38.4/40.0 and Llama 2-70B's 37.8/40.1). The human baseline substantially exceeds every model by 6.9 chrF (kgv→eng) and 11.2 chrF (eng→kgv), and the authors note the human's errors were largely avoidable (e.g., Ctrl-F failures on spelling variants), suggesting even higher human performance is possible. Among API models, GPT-4 and text-davinci-003 perform comparably (~38.4 chrF kgv→eng), while gpt-3.5-turbo lags behind (~31.6). Traditional finetuned MT baselines (FLAN-T5 XL at 35.2/30.8 chrF; mT5 XL at 34.2/27.1 chrF) are consistently outperformed by in-context LLM approaches, and direct LoRA finetuning on the grammar book text actually degrades Llama 2 performance (e.g., Llama 2-7B-ft drops from 26.8 to 22.2 chrF in the W+S setting), likely by breaking instruction tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing machine translation as a second-language (L2) learning problem from a single human-readable grammar book, rather than the standard first-language (L1) acquisition from large parallel corpora. It draws on retrieval-augmented generation and in-context learning as methodological tools, and is conceptually closest to BabyLM (which tests L1 pretraining on child-scale data) and to prior work on dictionary- or grammar-prompted translation (Ghazvininejad et al., Elsner & Needle), but departs from all of these by using field linguistics documentation as the sole learning material for a web-held-out language and by providing a data-matched human baseline. The authors explicitly note they are unaware of prior work using field linguistics grammars as instructions for MT, positioning MTOB as a new problem formulation rather than an extension of existing MT benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MTOB introduces a genuinely new axis for evaluating LLM adaptation: can a model learn a truly unseen language from a single book of explanations, and how far does it fall short of a human who did the same? The ~7–11 chrF gap between the best model (Claude 2) and the human baseline, combined with the finding that finetuning on the grammar book actively harms performance, suggests that current LLMs have not yet developed robust mechanisms for internalizing structured linguistic knowledge from long explanatory text. Key open questions include whether the gap closes with longer contexts or better retrieval, whether methods transfer across typologically diverse languages, and whether this task framing can be made practically useful for endangered-language communities (which currently requires speech support and community-driven data curation).
