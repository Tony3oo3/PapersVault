## Problem

Standard preference models (PMs) used in RLHF are opaque single-scalar predictors that overfit to spurious correlations in preference data, making them vulnerable to reward hacking (Goodhart's law) and difficult for human overseers to audit. As LM capabilities grow, the multidimensional nature of preferences like helpfulness and harmlessness becomes harder to capture with a single learned score, and unassisted human evaluation of increasingly complex responses becomes infeasible.

## Input / Output

Given a query x and a candidate response y, the CPM pipeline first prompts a frozen LM (GPT-3.5 or Flan-T5-XL) with C separate feature-specific prompts (e.g., 'is this response factually correct?') to obtain C scalar scores on a 1–10 scale, then feeds the standardized feature vector into a trained logistic regression head. The final output is a single scalar preference score ⟨λ, r(y|x)⟩, but the intermediate per-feature scores are retained, enabling human inspection of which attributes drive the judgment. Features can encode both positively correlated properties (helpfulness, factuality) and negatively correlated ones (biasedness, repetitiveness), with the logistic regression coefficients determining their signed contribution.

## Method

The Compositional Preference Model (CPM) replaces the end-to-end scalar PM with a two-stage pipeline: (1) a prompted LM acts as a feature extractor, assigning a 1–10 score to each of C pre-specified, human-interpretable features (13 features in the experiments, e.g., helpfulness, enough-detail, factuality, fail-to-consider-context), and (2) a logistic regression classifier trained on pairwise preference data combines the standardized feature differences into a single preference probability via σ(⟨λ, r(y₁|x) − r(y₂|x)⟩). The key design choice is that the feature set is fixed a priori by the researcher, so the only trainable parameters are the C+1 logistic regression coefficients, which drastically shrinks the hypothesis space and provides a strong inductive bias against overfitting. This divide-and-conquer decomposition makes each sub-question easier for the LM to evaluate and each coefficient directly interpretable as the importance of that feature for human preference. The approach is trained with L1/L2 regularization and 5-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter selection, and at inference time the same prompted LM extracts features from new responses before the logistic head produces the final score.

## Results

On the HH-RLHF dataset, CPM-GPT-3.5 achieves a win rate of 0.810 and CPM-Flan-T5 achieves 0.742 (±0.034) over vanilla Flan-T5 after Best-of-16 sampling, compared to 0.588 (±0.030) for the standard PM. On the SHP dataset, CPM-GPT-3.5 reaches 0.672 and CPM-Flan-T5 reaches 0.580 (±0.045), versus 0.564 (±0.037) for the standard PM. In overoptimization experiments, CPM-GPT-3.5 achieves a rank correlation of 0.997 (HH-RLHF) and 0.981 (SHP) with the gold PM, far exceeding the standard PM's 0.665 and 0.057, indicating substantially greater robustness to reward hacking.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard PM (scalar-head fine-tuned Flan-T5-XL, following the RLHF paradigm of Ouyang et al., 2022), both CPM variants win decisively: CPM-Flan-T5 improves win rate by 15.4 points on HH-RLHF (0.742 vs. 0.588) and by 1.6 points on SHP (0.580 vs. 0.564), while CPM-GPT-3.5 gains 22.2 and 10.8 points respectively. Against the DeBERTa-based reference PMs (He et al., 2020; PM_ref2 sourced from Sileo, 2023), the comparison is mixed: the reference PM slightly edges out CPM-GPT-3.5 on HH-RLHF (0.829 vs. 0.810) but CPM-GPT-3.5 clearly wins on SHP (0.672 vs. 0.619), and CPM-Flan-T5 trails the reference PM on both datasets. In robustness-to-overoptimization experiments, CPMs dramatically outperform the standard PM: the rank correlation between proxy and gold PM scores is 0.997/0.981 for CPM-GPT-3.5 and 0.926/0.928 for CPM-Flan-T5, versus only 0.665/0.057 for the standard PM, showing that CPMs resist reward hacking far more effectively. In model-robustness (data-subsampling) experiments, CPMs maintain near-identical scores between independently trained copies even at n=256, whereas the standard PM shows large and growing divergence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant RLHF paradigm (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022a) in which a preference model is a single scalar head on a fine-tuned LM, by instead decomposing preference into pre-specified interpretable features scored by a prompted LM and aggregated via logistic regression. Conceptually, it draws on the divide-and-conquer / scalable oversight literature (Irving et al., 2018; Leike et al., 2018) and the broader trend of task decomposition for LMs (Wei et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023), but applies these ideas to preference modeling at inference time rather than training time. Unlike RL from AI feedback (Bai et al., 2022b), which uses LMs to generate training data, CPMs rely on an LM as a feature extractor during a single inference step, keeping the trainable component (logistic regression) extremely small.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CPMs offer a practical path toward more transparent and overoptimization-resistant preference models, which is increasingly critical as LMs grow more capable and human oversight becomes harder. The key limitation is that features are hand-designed and the 'human evaluation' relies on Claude as a proxy, leaving open whether the gains hold under genuine human judgment. An important open question is whether LMs can automatically discover the relevant decomposed features rather than relying on human-specified ones, and whether the approach extends beyond helpfulness to more complex preference dimensions like harmlessness or task-specific quality.
