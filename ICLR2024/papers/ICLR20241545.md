## Problem

Representation learning methods for CATE estimation rely on low-dimensional (potentially constrained) representations that can lose information about confounders, introducing a bias that makes the CATE non-identifiable from the representation-conditional data. No prior work had formalized this representation-induced confounding bias (RICB) or provided a practical way to quantify it, leaving practitioners without a tool to assess the validity of their CATE estimates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241545/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes standard observational data (X, A, Y) with high-dimensional covariates (up to d_x = 785 for HC-MNIST), a binary treatment, and a continuous outcome. It operates in three stages: first fitting an arbitrary representation learning CATE method, then estimating sensitivity parameters and a conditional outcome density, and finally producing lower and upper bounds on the CATE (equivalently, bounds on the RICB). These bounds support a three-way policy decision—treat, do nothing, or defer—where deferral occurs when the bounds straddle zero, and the reported error rate is computed only over non-deferred decisions.

## Method

The core contribution is a representation-agnostic, three-stage neural refutation framework. Stage 0 fits any existing representation learning CATE method (TARNet, CFR, BNN, RCFR, CFR-ISW, BWCFR, etc.) with its usual constraints (balancing via MMD/Wasserstein, invertibility, loss re-weighting). Stage 1 estimates the marginal sensitivity model (MSM) parameter Γ(φ) by training two propensity networks (representation-level and covariate-level) and computing the maximum odds-ratio deviation over a δ-ball in representation space; it simultaneously learns the representation-conditional outcome distribution P(Y|A=a, Φ(X)=φ) via a conditional normalizing flow (neural spline flows with noise regularization). Stage 2 evaluates the sharp, valid bounds on the RICB using CVaR-based integrals (Eq. 11–12) computed by importance sampling from the CNF. The key insight is repurposing the MSM—not as a sensitivity analysis tool requiring expert-chosen parameters, but as a partial identification framework where the sensitivity parameter is estimated directly from the joint data P(X, Φ(X), A, Y), yielding data-driven bounds that are both valid (always containing the true CATE) and sharp (no spurious values in the ignorance interval).

## Results

On IHDP100 (n_train=672, d_x=25), the refutation framework reduces out-sample policy error rates across nearly all representation learning baselines, with improvements ranging from −0.04% (CFR WM α=2.0, d_φ=5) to −3.83% (RCFR WM α=1.0, d_φ=20). On HC-MNIST (d_x=785), improvements are larger, reaching up to −14.26% (RCFR WM α=1.0, d_φ=78) and −11.91% (CFR WM α=2.0, d_φ=78). On the synthetic benchmark (d_x=2), the most dramatic gains appear at d_φ=1, where both loss of heterogeneity and RICB are present, with improvements up to −15.41% (CFR MMD α=0.1). Classical non-neural estimators (k-NN, BART, C-Forest) serve as reference but are not directly augmented by the refutation framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The refutation framework is applied on top of each representation learning baseline and consistently reduces policy error rates. TARNet (Shalit et al., 2017) benefits by 2.3–2.7% across all representation dimensions, and BNN (Johansson et al., 2016) improves by 1.2–2.0%. CFR variants (Shalit et al., 2017) show mixed results: CFR with WM balancing improves modestly (0.2–1.5%), while CFR with MMD α=0.5 actually worsens at d_φ≥10 (up to +6.37%), which the authors attribute to excessive balancing in a low-sample regime. InvTARNet (Zhang et al., 2020) improves by 1.9–2.5%, and RCFR (Johansson et al., 2018) shows the largest single improvement (−3.83% at d_φ=20) but one exception at d_φ=25 (+1.06%). CFR-ISW (Hassanpour & Greiner, 2019) and BWCFR (Assaad et al., 2021) both improve consistently by 0.2–2.4%. Classical non-neural estimators (BART, C-Forest, k-NN) serve as static references and are not augmented by the framework, but the augmented neural methods generally outperform them in absolute error rate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the marginal sensitivity model (MSM) framework from Frauen et al. (2023) and Oprescu et al. (2023) in an unconventional way: rather than using MSMs for sensitivity analysis with expert-chosen parameters, it estimates the sensitivity parameter Γ(φ) directly from data to perform partial identification of CATE under representation constraints. The key conceptual departure is formalizing 'representation-induced confounding bias' (RICB) as a distinct source of bias arising from dimensionality reduction or balancing in representation learning, which prior work on unobserved confounding did not address. The framework is representation-agnostic, plugging into any existing CATE estimator (TARNet, CFR, BNN, RCFR, etc.) as a post-hoc refutation step, and uses conditional normalizing flows to estimate the outcome density needed for computing CVaR-based bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides practitioners with a concrete diagnostic tool: before trusting a CATE estimate from a representation learning method, one can now quantify how much confounding bias the representation itself may introduce, and use the resulting bounds to make safer treatment decisions (treat / defer / do nothing). The finding that even well-established methods like CFR with strong Wasserstein balancing can exhibit substantial RICB challenges the implicit validity assumption underlying much of the representation learning CATE literature. Limitations include the conservative nature of the bounds (they widen when instrument information is lost even though this doesn't cause RICB), the added complexity of training a conditional normalizing flow, and the fact that all empirical validation is on semi-synthetic benchmarks rather than real-world observational studies where the true partitioning of covariates into confounders, instruments, and noise is unknown.
