## Problem

Measuring predictive multiplicity—the degree to which almost-equally-optimal models in the Rashomon set produce conflicting predictions—requires exploring many models within a loss-deviation constraint, which is computationally infeasible for large hypothesis spaces like neural networks. Existing strategies (re-training with different seeds, adversarial weight perturbation) are prohibitively slow, leaving no efficient method for estimating multiplicity metrics on complex architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241302/cropped_page7_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires white-box access to a pre-trained model's full weight matrices, along with the dataset and dropout hyperparameters (Bernoulli rate p or Gaussian variance α) that must be scaled inversely with feature dimension to keep perturbed models inside the Rashomon set. It outputs an empirical Rashomon set of m dropout-perturbed models, from which six predictive multiplicity metrics (VPR, Rashomon Capacity, Ambiguity, Discrepancy, Disagreement, Score Variance) are computed, and can further produce ensemble predictions or a selected model with minimized multiplicity.

## Method

The core idea is to apply random dropout masks (Bernoulli or Gaussian multiplicative noise) to a fixed pre-trained model at inference time, generating diverse sparse models without re-training. The key theoretical contribution is proving that dropout parameters control the loss deviation: for ridge regression, the expected loss increase is p(1-p)‖w*‖²·diag(XᵀX), and for K-layer FFNNs with Gaussian dropout, the loss deviation is bounded by ρ⁻¹Mᴷ‖X‖²((mα+1)ᴷ−1) with probability 1−ρ, so that as dimension grows and α=O(m^(-(1+δ))), dropout models concentrate in the Rashomon set with probability approaching 1. Practically, one samples m dropout realizations, filters models satisfying the loss constraint, and computes multiplicity metrics on the resulting empirical Rashomon set. This yields 20×–5000× runtime speedup over re-training and AWP while producing higher multiplicity estimates under the same loss-deviation budget, and enables downstream applications like dropout ensembles for multiplicity reduction and model selection among candidates with similar accuracy.

## Results

The dropout-based Rashomon set exploration achieves 20×–5000× runtime speedup per model compared to re-training and AWP baselines across six UCI datasets. Specifically, Gaussian dropout is 28.88×–305.53× faster than re-training and 1121.59×–5345.45× faster than AWP (Table 1). In terms of multiplicity estimation quality, both Bernoulli and Gaussian dropouts produce higher predictive multiplicity estimates (VPR, RC, Ambiguity, Discrepancy, Disagreement, Score Variance) than re-training under the same loss deviation constraint, while AWP achieves the highest estimates but at prohibitive computational cost. On the MS COCO human detection task, dropout models maintain APs within 0.5% of the pre-trained YOLOv3 model while revealing high predictive multiplicity in bounding box coverage and confidence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares dropout-based exploration against two baselines: re-training with different random seeds (Semenova et al., 2019) and Adversarial Weight Perturbation (AWP; Hsu & Calmon, 2022). Against re-training, dropout wins decisively on both axes: it is 29×–306× faster per model and produces higher predictive multiplicity estimates (e.g., VPR and Ambiguity) under the same loss deviation constraint, whereas re-training often yields near-zero multiplicity on datasets like Bank Marketing and Mammography. Against AWP, the trade-off is clear: AWP achieves the highest multiplicity estimates across all six metrics because it adversarially searches for models that flip decisions toward all classes, but it is 1,122×–5,345× slower than dropout per model. Thus, dropout offers the best speed–quality trade-off, providing fast lower bounds on multiplicity that are substantially tighter than re-training while remaining orders of magnitude cheaper than AWP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes dropout from its traditional role as a Bayesian uncertainty estimator (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) into a principled tool for exploring the Rashomon set of almost-equally-optimal models. The key conceptual departure from Gal & Ghahramani is restricting the model ensemble to the Rashomon set rather than the full hypothesis space, which avoids over-estimating predictive multiplicity by including poorly-performing models. The Rashomon set framework itself is inherited from Semenova et al. (2019), and the predictive multiplicity metrics (VPR, RC, Ambiguity, Discrepancy, Disagreement) follow Marx et al. (2020), Hsu & Calmon (2022), and Kulynych et al. (2023). Prior exploration strategies—re-training with different seeds and adversarial weight perturbation (AWP)—are computationally expensive for neural networks, and the paper positions dropout as the first efficient alternative with theoretical guarantees linking dropout hyperparameters to Rashomon set membership.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Predictive multiplicity is increasingly recognized as a critical dimension of model reporting alongside accuracy, with implications for fairness, explainability, and trustworthiness in high-stakes domains like finance and healthcare. The 20×–5000× speedup makes Rashomon set exploration and multiplicity estimation practically feasible for complex neural networks, enabling downstream applications like dropout ensembles for multiplicity mitigation and model selection. Key limitations include the requirement for white-box model access and the potential for dropout to only explore local regions of the Rashomon set in very large hypothesis spaces. Open questions include extending the analysis to recurrent architectures, using non-uniform dropout across layers, and reconciling conflicting decisions across dropout models.
