## Problem

Existing approaches to quantifying animal behavior either segment movements into discrete categories (e.g., MoSeq) or model dynamics only in constrained, task-specific settings (e.g., reaching). Neither captures the full natural locomotor repertoire as the output of a continuous, controlled dynamical system with identifiable control signals. This paper asks whether both the sparse control inputs and the underlying latent dynamics can be jointly inferred from unstructured postural time series of freely behaving animals.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240955/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For zebrafish, input consists of 7-segment tail angle time series (230 ms bouts, 30,800 trials across 11 kinematic categories); for C. elegans, input is 4 eigenworm coefficients tracking 100 body segments over 12.5 s trials. The model outputs a latent state trajectory, a sparse control signal (10-dim for zebrafish, 5-dim for C. elegans) inferred via iLQR, and—after balanced truncation—a minimal linear model (18 states for zebrafish, 20 for C. elegans) whose eigenmodes correspond to interpretable behavioral components. Notably, the control signal is never observed in the data; it is inferred as the most likely input under a heavy-tailed Student-t prior, and the reduced model's eigenmodes can be individually convolved with the input to reconstruct specific movement types.

## Method

The framework combines iLQR-VAE (Schimel et al., 2022) with balanced model reduction (Moore, 1981) in a two-stage pipeline. First, a latent RNN (MGU architecture, 120 hidden units) or linear dynamical system is trained via the ELBO objective, where the posterior over unobserved inputs is computed per-trial using the iLQR optimal control algorithm rather than an amortized bi-RNN encoder. A Student-t prior on inputs enforces temporal sparsity, yielding brief control impulses at movement onset that set initial conditions for quasi-autonomous dynamics. Second, for the linear model, balanced truncation computes observability and controllability Gramians, finds a similarity transformation equalizing them, and truncates to the dimension where Hankel singular values plateau—removing uncontrollable/unobservable modes to yield a minimal, interpretable linear system. This two-stage design (expressive nonlinear model for compression, reduced linear model for interpretation) is the key architectural choice, since directly training small linear models with sparse inputs proved infeasible.

## Results

On the zebrafish larval locomotion dataset (30,800 tail-angle time series), the MGU RNN (n=120 latent, m=10 inputs) achieves R²=0.983 reconstruction with a sparsity ratio ‖u‖₁/‖u‖∞ of 2.29±0.05, while the LDS (n=120, m=10) reaches R²=0.971 with sparsity 2.75±0.03. Balanced model reduction of the LDS to r=18 dimensions preserves R²=0.818 reconstruction, and the top two eigenmodes alone reconstruct forward swims (R²=0.76) and turns (R²=0.74). On C. elegans heat-shock escape data, the LDS (n=60, m=5) captures continuous locomotion, and balanced truncation to r=20 yields R²=0.702 reconstruction of eigenworm dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is non-autonomous LFADS (Pandarinath et al., 2018) with an AR(1) prior on the control signal. LFADS achieves R²=0.94 reconstruction, slightly below the iLQR-VAE MGU RNN (R²=0.983), but more critically suffers from input leakage: a linear projection of the 10-dimensional control signal correlates r=0.73 with the postural time series, indicating the dynamics are predominantly input-driven rather than autonomous. This leakage translates into worse movement-category classification accuracy from the latent state (Figure 2C) compared to iLQR-VAE. Within the iLQR-VAE framework, the MGU RNN outperforms the LDS on both reconstruction (R²=0.983 vs. 0.971) and sparsity (2.29 vs. 2.75) at matched dimensions (n=120, m=10), and the MGU latent state 40 ms after the control peak enables higher classification accuracy than a linear classifier trained on the full tail-movement time series. The paper also demonstrates that directly training small LDS models (n≤20) fails to produce sparse control signals (sparsity degrades to 15–29), making balanced model reduction a necessary step to obtain interpretable low-order dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on RNN-based movement models (Sussillo et al., 2015; Saxena et al., 2022), which demonstrated that latent RNNs trained on constrained reaching tasks reproduce neural activity features, to the full natural locomotor repertoire of zebrafish and C. elegans. It builds directly on iLQR-VAE (Schimel et al., 2022) for learning input-driven dynamics with unobserved controls, but departs from LFADS (Pandarinath et al., 2018) by replacing the autoregressive input prior with a sparse Student-t prior that encourages autonomous dynamics. The key methodological addition is the post-hoc application of balanced model reduction (Moore, 1981) to the learned linear dynamics, a technique from classical control theory that had not previously been combined with data-driven behavioral system identification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled path from high-dimensional behavioral recordings to minimal, interpretable dynamical models whose modes can be mapped onto known neural circuits (e.g., oscillatory vs. non-oscillatory modes corresponding to forward swim vs. turn, aligning with reticulospinal neuron findings). A key limitation is the reliance on two separate models (nonlinear MGU for control inference, linear LDS for dynamics dissection), which the authors acknowledge could be unified via linearization or co-training. Open questions include whether the sparse-impulse control structure generalizes to limb-based locomotion, whether alternative control priors (beyond temporal sparsity) capture different behavioral regimes, and how the framework performs when the animal's internal state (e.g., arousal, motivation) is not captured by the observed posture.
