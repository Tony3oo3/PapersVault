## Problem

Extreme classifiers with massive One-vs-All output layers consistently underperform on tail labels with few representative samples. While overfitting and missing-label bias have been studied, this paper identifies label variance—the approximation error from discretely sampling a complex ground-truth label distribution—as a previously unexamined factor that is particularly magnified for tail classifiers with low sample counts.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text documents (full-text or short-text) and label-side features (descriptive text or structured graphs over labels) as input, with a frozen deep encoder (e.g., DistilBERT) producing fixed embeddings on which independent OvA classifiers operate. The output is a ranked list of labels with relevance scores, evaluated via precision@k, propensity-scored precision (PSP@k), and coverage@k metrics that specifically measure tail-label recall. The Siamese teacher additionally maps both document and label embeddings into a shared space to produce calibrated soft relevance targets for distillation.

## Method

LEVER is a knowledge distillation framework that trains a Siamese-style teacher (6-layer DistilBERT) optimized for tail performance using label-wise negative sampling and a logistic-loss objective that yields calibrated relevance estimates. The teacher's soft targets regularize the OvA classifier training loss via a weighted combination (Eq. 6) that trades off label variance from discrete ground truth against the teacher's bounded bias. Theorem 2 provides a formal generalization guarantee showing that an appropriately chosen mixing coefficient λ yields tighter risk bounds than training on either discrete labels or teacher estimates alone. The Siamese teacher leverages label features to share information across semantically similar labels, directly addressing data scarcity in the tail, while the student OvA classifier retains its strong head-label performance. The approach is training-time only, leaving inference costs unchanged, and can be plugged into any existing OvA-based extreme classifier (Renée, ELIAS, CascadeXML) without architectural modification.

## Results

LEVER consistently improves PSP@1 by ~5% and P@1 by ~2% on average across all base models (ELIAS, CascadeXML, Renée) and all seven public datasets, while also boosting coverage@1 by ~6.5%. When applied to the top-performing Renée classifier, LEVER establishes a new state-of-the-art, e.g., PSP@1 on LF-AOL-270K jumps from 14.76 to 20.38 (+5.62) and C@1 from 12.40 to 17.43 (+5.03). On the new multi-intent LF-WikiHierarchy-1M dataset, Renée+LEVER achieves PSP@1 of 24.76 (vs. 19.69 for Renée alone) and C@1 of 9.32 (vs. 6.72). LEVER is a training-time-only modification that increases training time by ~3.1× on average while leaving inference costs unchanged.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three primary OvA baselines (ELIAS, CascadeXML, Renée), LEVER delivers consistent PSP@1 gains of 3–11 points depending on dataset and base model, with the largest improvements on the new multi-intent datasets (e.g., ELIAS PSP@1 on LF-WikiHierarchy-1M: 17.15→28.27, +11.12). Compared to tail-specific regularization methods (GLaS, L2-regularization) and augmentation methods (TAUG, Gandalf), Renée+LEVER outperforms the second-best method by an average of 4% in coverage and 3% in PSP; the gap is especially pronounced on skewed multi-intent datasets where GLaS and Gandalf struggle due to sparse label correlations (e.g., on LF-AOL-270K, Renée+LEVER C@1=17.43 vs. Renée+Gandalf C@1=12.63 and Renée+GLaS C@1=12.36). Against the non-DNN baseline XReg, LEVER yields substantial PSP improvements (LF-AOL-270K PSP@1: 7.0→9.2; LF-AmazonTitles-131K PSP@1: 24.5→31.4). LEVER also outperforms an ensemble of OvA (Renée) and Siamese encoder on both precision and tail metrics in 3 out of 4 datasets, and surpasses its own Siamese teacher in overall performance by retaining strong head-label accuracy while matching tail performance. On LF-WikiHierarchy-1M, the ensemble approach gains ~4–5% in coverage but suffers a ~30% precision loss, whereas LEVER achieves both gains simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge distillation framework of Menon et al. (2021a) from multi-class classification to the far more challenging extreme classification setting, where the label space reaches millions and tail labels have only a handful of positive samples. It also builds on the Siamese encoder architecture and negative mining strategy from NGAME (Dahiya et al., 2023a), but repurposes the Siamese model as a tail-robust teacher for distilling into OvA classifiers rather than as a standalone predictor. Conceptually, the paper carves out label variance as a distinct degradation factor for tail classifiers, separate from the previously studied issues of overfitting (addressed by GLaS, L2-regularization) and missing-label bias (addressed by propensity scoring), and provides the first formal generalization bounds connecting label variance to OvA classifier performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of label variance as a previously unexamined factor, backed by clean theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1–3), gives the XC community a new diagnostic lens and a principled remedy that is modular and applicable to any OvA-based architecture without architectural changes. The release of two multi-intent datasets (LF-AOL-270K, LF-WikiHierarchy-1M) with 7–11% of labels accounting for 80% of positives—far more skewed than existing benchmarks—addresses a real gap in evaluation rigor and will likely become standard testbeds. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to settings without label-side features, whether the 3.1× training overhead can be reduced, and whether the Siamese teacher's calibration (Theorem 3) holds under the approximate minimization that is unavoidable in practice.
