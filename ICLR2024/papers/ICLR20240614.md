## Problem

Existing continuous Schrödinger Bridge (SB) / Entropic Optimal Transport (EOT) solvers are heavy-weighted, requiring complex multi-network architectures, max-min optimization, full trajectory simulation, and MCMC sampling. There is no simple-yet-effective baseline for continuous SB analogous to k-means in clustering or the Sinkhorn algorithm in discrete OT, making moderate-dimensional SB problems unnecessarily difficult to solve in practice.

## Input / Output

The solver takes unpaired empirical samples from two continuous distributions p0 and p1 (dimensions up to 512 for latent codes, or up to 1000 for single-cell PCA projections) and produces a conditional EOT plan π(x1|x0) as a closed-form Gaussian mixture, a closed-form SB drift g(x,t) valid for all (x,t)∈R^D×[0,1], and simulable SB trajectories via either Euler-Maruyama or Brownian Bridge refinement. For image translation, input images are first encoded to 512-dim latent codes by a pre-trained ALAE autoencoder on 1024×1024 FFHQ faces, and the output latent code is decoded back to an image.

## Method

The key insight is to parameterize the adjusted Schrödinger potential v* as an unnormalized Gaussian mixture v_θ(x1) = Σ_k α_k N(x1 | r_k, εS_k), which yields closed-form expressions for both the conditional plan π_θ(x1|x0) (a Gaussian mixture with x0-dependent means and weights) and the normalizing constant c_θ(x0). The learning objective is a straightforward non-minimax KL minimization that reduces to minimizing E_{p0}[log c_θ(x0)] − E_{p1}[log v_θ(x1)], optimizable by standard minibatch gradient descent without MCMC, simulation, or iterative proportional fitting. The full SB process T_θ is then constructed by inserting a Brownian Bridge inside the learned joint distribution, giving a closed-form drift g_θ(x,t) expressible as a log-derivative of a product of Gaussians. The paper proves that this Gaussian mixture parameterization is a universal approximator of SBs (for compactly supported marginals) and that the statistical estimation error vanishes at the standard parametric rate O(1/√N + 1/√M).

## Results

On the EOT/SB Benchmark (Table 2), LightSB achieves cBW2²-UVP scores of 0.03–0.62% across dimensions D=2–128 and ε∈{0.1, 1, 10}, outperforming the best existing solver by a factor of 3–25× (e.g., 0.60% vs. 11.4% at ε=0.1, D=128). On the MSCI single-cell dataset (Table 3), LightSB achieves the lowest energy distance in all three PCA dimensions (2.31, 2.16, 1.27 for DIM=50, 100, 1000) while training in under 3 minutes on 4 CPU cores, compared to 8–71 minutes on a GPU for competing methods. The solver also performs unpaired image-to-image translation in the 512-dim ALAE latent space of FFHQ, converging in less than 1 minute on 4 CPU cores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LightSB outperforms all four baselines on the MSCI single-cell dataset across all three PCA dimensions (50, 100, 1000) in energy distance, while being 10–60× faster since it runs on CPU rather than GPU. Specifically, at DIM=1000, LightSB achieves 1.27±0.19, beating Minimax (1.32±0.06), Langevin-based (1.46±0.20), Sinkhorn (1.864), and IPF (2.05±0.19). On the EOT/SB Benchmark, LightSB's cBW2²-UVP is 3–25× lower than the best existing solver (e.g., 0.03% vs. 1.94% at ε=0.1, D=2; 0.60% vs. 11.4% at ε=0.1, D=128), which the authors attribute to the benchmark distributions being constructed with the same Gaussian-mixture parameterization that LightSB exploits. The Langevin-based solver (Mokrov et al., 2024) and Minimax solver (Gushchin et al., 2023a) are the closest competitors in quality but require GPU training and MCMC or max-min optimization, respectively. The IPF-based solver (Vargas et al., 2021) is the weakest in quality but competitive in speed; LightSB matches or beats it while being 7–10× faster.

## Relation to Prior Work

LightSB unites two previously separate ideas from the EOT/SB literature: the energy-based KL minimization objective derived via weak OT duality (Mokrov et al., 2024) and the Gaussian mixture parameterization of Schrödinger potentials originally developed for constructing analytical benchmark distributions (Gushchin et al., 2023b). By combining them, the authors obtain a closed-form normalizing constant that eliminates the need for MCMC, max-min optimization, or iterative proportional fitting used by all prior continuous SB solvers. The paper positions itself as the missing 'simple-yet-effective baseline' for continuous SB, analogous to k-means in clustering or Sinkhorn in discrete OT, filling a gap where existing solvers all rely on heavy neural network parameterization and complex training procedures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The universal approximation theorem (Theorem 3.4) is the first of its kind for Schrödinger Bridges and notably holds on non-compact sets, providing theoretical justification for the Gaussian mixture parameterization. Practically, the solver's simplicity (minutes on CPU, no GPU, no MCMC, no hyperparameter tuning beyond number of mixture components) makes continuous SB accessible for moderate-dimensional applications like single-cell biology where existing solvers require hours of GPU training. Limitations include the diagonal/scalar covariance restriction (though theoretically justified), the fact that the benchmark outperformance is partly due to inductive bias alignment with the benchmark's construction, and the method's applicability to high-dimensional image spaces only through latent-space workarounds rather than direct pixel-space use.
