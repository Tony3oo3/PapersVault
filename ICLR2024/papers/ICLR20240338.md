## Problem

Dense reward functions for RL are typically hand-engineered with substantial domain expertise and tedious trial-and-error, while sparse rewards (e.g., binary success signals) are easy to obtain but cause severe exploration difficulties. Existing reward-learning methods such as adversarial imitation learning (GAIL) produce discriminators that converge to outputting 1/2 for both agent and demonstration trajectories, rendering the learned reward non-reusable for new tasks. The paper asks whether one can learn dense rewards in a data-driven manner that remain informative and transferable to unseen task variants within the same task family.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a binary sparse reward from the environment, a set of binary stage indicators (e.g., 'gripper has grasped object', 'door is open enough'), and optionally expert demonstration trajectories. The discriminator takes the next state s' as input (consistent with common reward-engineering practice) and outputs a scalar reward; the final reward for a multi-stage task is R(s') = k + α·tanh(D_k(s')), where k is the current stage index and α < 1/2 guarantees monotonicity across stages. Stage indicators are required only during RL training, not at policy deployment time, and the learned reward is agnostic to the downstream RL algorithm.

## Method

The key modification to GAIL is training the discriminator to classify success trajectories (positive) versus failure trajectories (negative) rather than agent versus demonstration trajectories, so the positive/negative gap never collapses at convergence and the reward remains reusable. For multi-stage tasks, a separate discriminator is trained per stage k, where positive data consists of trajectories whose maximum stage index exceeds k and negative data consists of trajectories whose maximum stage index is at most k. The per-stage discriminators are combined with the semi-sparse stage-index reward via R(s') = k + α·tanh(D_k(s')), filling the reward gaps between stages to produce a smooth, dense signal. Training is off-policy using a global replay buffer plus N stage-specific buffers, with BCE loss for discriminators and SAC for the policy; early stopping of each discriminator once its classification accuracy is high improves robustness and reduces compute.

## Results

Across three task families (Pick-and-Place with 1,600 test objects, Turn Faucet with 50 test faucets, and Open Cabinet Door with 6 test doors), DrS consistently outperforms all learned-reward baselines and semi-sparse rewards in both sample efficiency and final success rate. In Pick-and-Place and Turn Faucet, the learned dense reward achieves performance comparable to the human-engineered reward (the benchmark's upper bound), while in Open Cabinet Door it approaches but does not fully match the human-engineered reward. VICE-RAQ and ORIL fail to achieve any task success across all three task families, and semi-sparse rewards yield only limited success within the training budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DrS is compared against four baselines: human-engineered rewards (upper bound), semi-sparse rewards (stage-index reward), VICE-RAQ (online classifier-based reward from human-annotated success states), and ORIL (offline classifier distinguishing success from random trajectories). DrS surpasses semi-sparse rewards across all three task families, with the gap being most pronounced in sample efficiency during early training. VICE-RAQ fails entirely — it cannot even learn a usable reward on the training tasks, likely because classifying only success states provides insufficient guidance when most states are far from success. ORIL also achieves zero success on all tasks, as the offline-learned reward overfits the demonstration dataset and is easily exploited by the RL agent during online training. Against the human-engineered reward (which involves 100+ lines of code with 10 candidate terms and many tuned parameters for Open Cabinet Door), DrS achieves comparable performance in Pick-and-Place and Turn Faucet, and competitive but slightly lower performance in Open Cabinet Door, while requiring only two boolean stage-indicator functions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on adversarial imitation learning (GAIL, Ho & Ermon 2016) and its off-policy variants, but makes a key conceptual departure: instead of training a discriminator to distinguish agent trajectories from demonstrations (which becomes uninformative at convergence, as in GANs), it trains the discriminator to classify success versus failure trajectories based on the sparse reward signal. This ensures the reward remains meaningful and reusable after training. The multi-stage extension draws on task decomposition ideas from hierarchical RL and skill chaining, but applies the stage structure to reward design rather than policy design, which the authors explicitly frame as an orthogonal direction. The approach also relates to reward shaping and VICE-style classifier rewards, but differs by operating online with environment interaction and by leveraging stage structure for structured reward composition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a significant practical bottleneck in RL deployment: dense reward engineering requires substantial domain expertise and tedious trial-and-error, as illustrated by the 100+ lines of code and 'magic' parameters needed for a single manipulation task. By reducing this to two simple boolean stage indicators, the approach could substantially lower the barrier to applying RL in new manipulation scenarios. Key open questions include how to automatically discover stage structures (the authors note LLMs like ChatGPT suggest aligned stages but don't formalize this), whether the approach extends beyond manipulation to other domains, and how to handle tasks where stage indicators are difficult to obtain in the real world. The reliance on stage indicators during training (though not deployment) remains a practical constraint for real-world RL.
