## Problem

No invertible spectral transform exists for temporal graphs whose structure evolves over time. Existing spectral methods (GFT, JFT, JFRT) either assume a fixed graph topology or ignore structural evolution, while the naive extension—eigendecomposition of the joint dynamic graph Laplacian—incurs O(T³) complexity and conflates temporal and vertex frequency components, losing interpretability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241224/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of T graphs {G_t} over a fixed node set of size N (isolated nodes permitted for node edits) together with a node signal matrix X ∈ R^{N×T}. It produces a joint time-vertex spectral representation via the EFT matrix Ψ_D ∈ R^{NT×NT}, which decomposes as a Kronecker product of the DFT matrix Ψ_T and per-timestep GFT matrices {Ψ_{G_t}}, reducing transform cost from O(T³) to O(T + T log T). For downstream tasks, the spectral features feed a transformer to yield link prediction scores (Recall@K, NDCG@K) or node classification labels (F1).

## Method

EFT is derived by formulating signal variation on a dynamic graph as the 2-Dirichlet form of the joint Laplacian L_{J_D} = L_T ⊗ I_N + [I_T ⊠ {L_{G_t}}], then relaxing the exact eigendecomposition objective to admit solutions within the ε-pseudospectrum. This relaxation yields the closed-form transform Ψ_D = Ψ_T ⊗ {Ψ_{G_t}}, a Kronecker product of the temporal DFT and per-timestep GFT, with a proven upper bound on ‖Ψ_D − Ψ_AD‖ that scales with the rate of graph change ‖L̇_G‖ and inversely with eigenvalue gaps. As a reference implementation, EFT-T embeds this transform into a transformer: a Chebyshev-polynomial-based learnable filter operates in the vertex (graph) domain, and a separate learnable temporal filter operates in the DFT domain, enabling joint spectral filtering that suppresses noise while preserving evolving structural and positional signal components.

## Results

On the large-scale sequential recommendation datasets (Amazon Beauty, Games, CDs), EFT-T achieves Recall@10 of 53.23, 77.78, and 75.42 respectively, outperforming the strongest baseline DGSR (52.40, 75.57, 72.43) by 0.83, 2.21, and 2.99 points. On NDCG@10, EFT-T reaches 37.10, 58.65, and 54.99 versus DGSR's 35.90, 55.70, and 51.22. On discrete dynamic graph benchmarks, EFT-T achieves the highest scores across all tasks, with particularly large gains on the AS dataset (MAP 0.672, MRR 0.689 vs. LED-GCN's 0.193, 0.469) and Elliptic (F1 0.616 vs. GCN-GRU's 0.575).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EFT-T outperforms all 16 baselines on every dataset and metric reported. On the sequential recommendation setting (Table 1), the closest competitor is DGSR (Zhang et al., 2022), which EFT-T surpasses by 0.83–2.99 Recall@10 and 1.2–3.77 NDCG@10 across Beauty, Games, and CDs; the gap is largest on CDs (2.99 R@10, 3.77 N@10), suggesting EFT-T benefits more from denser graphs. Among non-graph sequential methods, FMLPRec (which induces DFT into a transformer) is the strongest at 72.41 R@10 on CDs, yet EFT-T still leads by 3.01 points, confirming the necessity of capturing evolving spectra rather than static temporal frequencies. On discrete dynamic graph benchmarks (Table 2), EFT-T achieves dramatic gains: on AS it reaches MAP 0.672 and MRR 0.689 versus LED-GCN's 0.193/0.469 (a 3.5× improvement), and on Elliptic it attains F1 0.616 versus GCN-GRU's 0.575. GNN-based baselines (GCN, GAT, EvolveGCN variants, GAEN) and embedding-based methods (DynGEM, dg2vec) are consistently outperformed, with the largest margins on AS and Elliptic, indicating that local-neighborhood propagation and static embeddings fail to capture the global spectral structure of evolving graphs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spectral graph transform lineage (GFT for static graphs, JFT for time-varying signals on fixed graphs) to the previously unaddressed setting of evolving graph structure. The theoretical foundation draws on the variational (Dirichlet form) characterization of the graph Laplacian from Hammond et al. (2011), but the key departure is the use of pseudospectrum relaxation to decompose the intractable joint eigendecomposition into a Kronecker product of DFT and per-timestep GFT. This positions EFT as a generalization that subsumes DFT (N=1), GFT (T=1 or static graph), and JFT (fixed structure) as special cases, while departing from the dominant GNN-based temporal graph literature (TGN, EvolveGCN, DGSR) that relies on local message passing rather than global spectral filtering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EFT fills a genuine gap by providing the first invertible spectral transform for temporal graphs with evolving structure, with a provable error bound (Theorem 1) guaranteeing approximation quality under Lipschitz-continuous graph evolution. The computational reduction from O(T³) to O(T + T log T) makes spectral methods on temporal graphs practically viable at scale. However, the current formulation assumes a fixed node set and undirected graphs, leaving open the important question of extending to signed, directed, and node-evolving graphs. The practical impact is strongest for tasks where global (non-local) dependencies matter and noise filtering in both vertex and temporal dimensions is beneficial, as demonstrated on large-scale sequential recommendation benchmarks.
