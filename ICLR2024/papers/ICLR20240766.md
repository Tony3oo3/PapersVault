## Problem

Existing long-term forecasting models process time series via fixed-length sliding windows, which inherently cannot capture long-term variations (e.g., yearly periodicity) whose periods exceed the window length. The paper shows that current models fail to learn representations reflecting long-term correlations between distant windows, and that decomposition architectures reduce the trend/long-term branch to a single linear layer, making it incapable of learning meaningful long-term representations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240766/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a normalized input window of length I (e.g., 96 steps) along with its global timestamp indices, and produces a forecast sequence of length O extended well beyond conventional benchmarks (up to 2160 steps for ETTh2/Electricity/Traffic/Weather, 1080 for Exchange, 112 for ILI). The global autocorrelation of the entire training series is precomputed once and used to define pairwise relationships between windows within each mini-batch, enabling the contrastive objective to operate across the full temporal span of the data rather than within a single window.

## Method

The paper proposes AutoCon, a self-supervised contrastive loss that constructs positive and negative pairs between windows in a mini-batch using the global autocorrelation at the lag equal to their temporal distance, following a relative selection strategy inspired by SupCR but replacing annotated labels with autocorrelation values. The architecture is a two-branch decomposition model: a short-term branch (single linear layer) captures local high-frequency patterns, while a long-term branch (TCN encoder with timestamp features + multi-scale moving-average decoder) learns global low-frequency representations. AutoCon is applied exclusively to the long-term branch, weighted by a hyperparameter λ alongside the standard MSE forecasting loss. Window-unit normalization/denormalization handles nonstationarity without discarding numerically significant values. The key insight is that randomly sampled windows in a batch can be separated by the full series length, so the autocorrelation-based pairing naturally encodes long-range dependencies that no single window can contain.

## Results

On extended long-term forecasting benchmarks (prediction lengths up to 2160 steps), AutoCon achieves first place in 42 out of 48 MSE/MAE evaluations across nine datasets, outperforming 14 baselines. The advantage grows with prediction horizon: average error reduction is ~5% at 96–720 steps but reaches ~12% at 1440–2160 steps. The largest gains appear on ETTh2 (34% MSE reduction vs. second-best) and Electricity (11%), where strong yearly periodicity exists beyond the window. Computationally, the model runs at 33.2 ms/iter, far faster than Transformer-based baselines (Nonstationary: 365.7 ms/iter; TimesNet: 466.1 ms/iter).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AutoCon dominates at long prediction horizons: on ETTh2-2160 it achieves MSE 0.198 vs. Nonstationary (0.240, +17%) and TimesNet (0.263, +33%); on Electricity-2160 it reaches 0.380 vs. PatchTST (0.415) and TimesNet (0.536). The only losses are marginal: MICN edges out AutoCon on ETTh2-96 (MSE 0.122 vs. 0.124) and DLinear wins on Exchange-48 (MSE 0.049 vs. 0.051), both at the shortest horizons where long-term structure is irrelevant. Against representation-learning baselines (TS2Vec, CoST, LaST), AutoCon shows dramatic long-horizon advantages—e.g., on ETTm1-4320, AutoCon achieves MSE 0.082 while LaST, CoST, and TS2Vec degrade to 0.274, 0.192, and 0.161 respectively. Among decomposition-based models, DLinear's single linear layer for the trend branch cannot capture low-frequency components, causing its error to diverge sharply beyond 720 steps (e.g., Electricity-2160: DLinear 0.938 vs. Ours 0.380). Transformer-based baselines (Nonstationary, FEDformer) and CNN-based TimesNet all underperform AutoCon at every prediction length ≥720 across all six univariate datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two lines of work: decomposition-based forecasting (DLinear, FEDformer, TimesNet) and contrastive representation learning for time series (TS2Vec, CoST, LaST). It departs from prior contrastive methods by eliminating data augmentation entirely and instead using global autocorrelation across the full series to define positive/negative pairs between temporally distant windows, adapting the relative selection strategy from SupCR. It also departs from existing decomposition architectures by replacing the single linear layer in the trend branch with a TCN encoder and multi-scale moving-average decoder, giving the long-term branch sufficient capacity to learn representations guided by the AutoCon loss. Window-unit normalization from Reversible Instance Normalization is retained to handle nonstationarity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and previously underappreciated failure mode: even models that receive timestamp information (TimesNet, FEDformer) fail to learn long-term periodicity beyond the window length without explicit supervision, as shown by representation similarity analyses. The AutoCon loss is computationally cheap (autocorrelation computed once, no augmentation), making it practical to adopt. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is that autocorrelation is inherently linear, so non-linear long-term dependencies remain unaddressed; extending to higher-order or non-linear correlation measures is a natural next step. The finding that simply increasing window length worsens generalization (due to fewer training windows) provides a useful empirical argument against the 'just use longer windows' heuristic.
