## Problem

While early-layer filters in regular CNNs are known to resemble Gabor or edge-detecting patterns, the structure of trained depthwise convolutional kernels in deeper layers of modern DS-CNNs has remained largely uncharacterized. This paper asks whether interpretable, recurring patterns exist in depthwise convolutional kernels across all layers of state-of-the-art architectures, and if so, what they are and how they relate to established models of biological vision.

## Input / Output

The method consumes trained depthwise convolutional kernel weights (5×5 or 7×7) extracted from pre-trained DS-CNNs (ConvNeXt, ConvNeXtV2, EfficientNet, HorNet, MogaNet, ConvMixer, etc.) trained on ImageNet-1K or ImageNet-21K. Kernels are preprocessed by mean-centering and L2-normalizing to unit length before being fed to the autoencoder. The output is a 1D scalar latent code in [0,1] per kernel, a discrete cluster label (one of ~10 semantic classes such as On-Centre DoG, Off-Centre DoG, first/second-order derivatives, or cross patterns), and a reconstructed kernel from the decoder. A cosine dissimilarity threshold (0.3 for 7×7, 0.2 for 5×5) determines whether a kernel is confidently assigned to a cluster or left unclassified.

## Method

The core technique is an unsupervised autoencoder with a 1D bottleneck that maps each vectorized kernel to a single scalar code. The encoder comprises four hidden layers with Leaky ReLU activations and a sigmoid code layer; the decoder uses a tanh output to reconstruct normalized filters in [-1,1]. Training minimizes a mean-centered cosine similarity loss to make the representation invariant to linear rescaling of filter weights. For clustering at inference, 10,000 uniformly spaced codes are sampled from [0,1] and decoded to form a reconstruction spectrum; each kernel is then assigned to the nearest reconstructed filter whose cosine dissimilarity falls below the threshold. The key insight is that the 1D latent space spontaneously organizes into discrete intervals, each corresponding to a visually coherent pattern family (DoG, its derivatives, cross shapes), enabling classification of over 95% of ConvNeXtV2 and 90% of ConvNeXt kernels into just ten interpretable classes. For 3×3 kernels where the autoencoder underperforms due to smaller angular separation, k-means on min-max encoded weights is used as an alternative, yielding the same recurring pattern families.

## Results

The autoencoder-based clustering method successfully categorizes over 97% of 7×7 depthwise convolutional filters in ConvNextV2 models and over 95% in ConvNeXt models into just 10 identifiable structural patterns, predominantly resembling Difference-of-Gaussian (DoG) functions and their first- and second-order derivatives. These patterns persist across all layers of the network, from early to deep stages, and are absent in regular convolutional kernels. In a DoG-initialization experiment on ConvMixer-768-32, initializing filters with DoG patterns improved ImageNet accuracy from 66.35% to 69.01% and increased the proportion of clusterable filters from 56.65% to 96.03%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical interpretability study rather than a method comparison paper, so traditional baseline comparisons do not apply. The key contrast is between regular convolutional kernels (VGG, ResNet, DenseNet), which show no discernible structure across layers, and depthwise convolutional kernels, which exhibit clear, recurring DoG-like patterns in all layers. Among DS-CNN architectures, the ConvNeXt/ConvNextV2 family achieves the highest clusterability (95–98%), followed by HorNet (~81%), MogaNet (~79%), and ConvMixer (~57%), suggesting that pattern recurrence correlates with model quality and architectural design. The DoG-initialization experiment on ConvMixer serves as a functional comparison: DoG-initialized filters outperform Kaiming-initialized filters by 2.66% in ImageNet accuracy and 39.38% in clusterable filter proportion, providing evidence that the discovered patterns are not merely artifacts but functionally meaningful. Models trained on ImageNet-21K consistently show higher clusterability than their ImageNet-1K counterparts, and larger models within the same architecture family also tend to have more clusterable filters, linking pattern emergence to generalization capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' prior WACV 2024 work ('Neural Echos'), which identified on/off-center DoG filters as the two most prominent clusters in depthwise convolutional kernels, by scaling the analysis to cover 90–95% of filters and discovering first- and second-order DoG derivatives plus cross-shaped patterns. It departs from the broader CNN interpretability literature (Zeiler & Fergus, Olah et al., Bau et al.) that focused on visualizing learned feature maps or early-layer Gabor-like filters, by performing the first large-scale unsupervised clustering of depthwise kernel weights across all layers of modern DS-CNN architectures. Methodologically, it introduces a 1D-code autoencoder with cosine-similarity loss as a clustering tool for kernel analysis, complementing the PCA visualization used as a preliminary step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that depthwise convolutions in state-of-the-art architectures (ConvNeXtV2, ConvNeXt) distill their filters into a compact vocabulary of ~10 DoG-derivative and cross-shaped building blocks across every layer provides a concrete mechanistic bridge to established neuroscience models of mammalian visual receptive fields (Young, Rodieck, Lindeberg). This opens practical avenues for bio-inspired initialization and regularization (demonstrated by the ConvMixer DoG-init experiment improving accuracy from 66% to 69%), but leaves open questions about the mathematical form of cross-pattern filters, why 3×3 kernels resist the same clustering, and whether the patterns persist in 3D/video architectures. The observation that higher-accuracy models exhibit more clusterable filters suggests a link between pattern recurrence and generalization, though the causal mechanism remains unexplained.
