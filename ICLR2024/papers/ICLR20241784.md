## Problem

Group fairness objectives (e.g., demographic parity) are defined as expectations of predictions across demographic groups, requiring batch access to data. In online settings where instances arrive one at a time, naively computing fairness gradients demands storing all previous inputs and performing additional forward/backward passes at every step, which is computationally prohibitive and may violate privacy constraints. Existing in-processing fairness methods are designed for offline batch training and do not extend naturally to this streaming regime.

## Input / Output

The model receives a feature vector x_t at each time step and, after making a prediction, receives the true task label y_t and protected attribute label a_t as feedback. For text (CivilComments) and image (CelebA) datasets, inputs are pre-computed representations from Instructor and CLIP encoders respectively; for tabular datasets, raw features are used. Critically, the model is not permitted to store previous input instances—it only maintains per-group aggregate statistics (expected node outputs and their gradients) that are updated incrementally, keeping memory usage independent of stream length.

## Method

Aranyani is an ensemble of soft-routed oblique decision trees (default: 3 trees of height 4) where each node computes a sigmoid-weighted linear projection of the input to route samples probabilistically to children. The key architectural insight is imposing demographic parity constraints at the local node level rather than on the final prediction, which yields parameter isolation: each node's fairness term depends only on that node's own weights. In the online setting, the model maintains running aggregate statistics of node outputs and their gradients per protected group, updated in O(1) per incoming sample, enabling unbiased fairness gradient estimation without storing any previous inputs. The fairness regularizer uses Huber loss as a smooth surrogate for the L1-norm, and the entire forest output is computed via parallel matrix operations (a mask-based selection of ancestor node probabilities), making training compatible with standard autograd libraries on GPUs/TPUs. Theoretical analysis provides a demographic parity bound of h·2^h·ε, a Rademacher complexity of 2^h/√n, and convergence guarantees for the biased gradient estimates.

## Results

Aranyani is evaluated on five datasets (Adult, Census, COMPAS, CivilComments, CelebA) using accuracy–demographic parity (DP) trade-off scatter plots rather than a single fixed-metric table. Across all five datasets, Aranyani consistently occupies the top-right quadrant (high accuracy, low DP), achieving the best accuracy–fairness trade-off among all baselines. The paper also reports that Aranyani achieves a ~3× improvement in computation time and ~23% reduction in memory utilization compared to a reservoir variant that stores all previous input instances. Gradient convergence is empirically verified on CivilComments, corroborating the theoretical bound in Theorem 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Aranyani outperforms all Hoeffding-tree-based baselines (HT, AHT, FAHT) on both accuracy and demographic parity across all five datasets; these baselines achieve poor fairness scores that often fall behind the simple Majority post-processing baseline, which itself requires prior access to target label information. The MLP Aranyani variant (same gradient-accumulation scheme but without the tree structure) is unable to improve fairness beyond a certain point due to vanishing fairness gradients in layers far from the output. Similarly, the Leaf Aranyani variant (fairness constraints applied at leaf-level probabilities rather than node-level) suffers from the same vanishing-gradient problem for nodes away from the leaves, confirming the importance of parameter isolation at the node level. Compared to FERMI (Lowy et al., 2022), the only other known in-processing method applicable with batch size 1, Aranyani shows significant gains in the accuracy–fairness trade-off (reported in Appendix E, Figure 11). On the vision (CelebA) and language (CivilComments) datasets, HT-based methods additionally fail to converge on the task itself, highlighting their limitation with non-axis-aligned data, while Aranyani maintains strong performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

Aranyani sits at the intersection of oblique decision tree learning (Karthikeyan et al., 2022) and in-processing group fairness methods (Agarwal et al., 2018; Lowy et al., 2022). It departs from prior online fairness work (FAHT, FERMI) by replacing axis-aligned Hoeffding trees with soft-routed oblique trees parameterized by neural networks, and by shifting the fairness constraint from the final output to individual node-level decisions. This node-level constraint design, inspired by parameter isolation in progressive networks (Rusu et al., 2016), is the key architectural departure that enables efficient online gradient computation via aggregate statistics rather than sample storage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: most group fairness methods assume offline batch access, yet real-world deployments (toxicity moderation, hiring) process data one instance at a time under privacy constraints that forbid storing past inputs. The finding that node-level fairness constraints are essential—while output-level or leaf-level constraints fail entirely—provides a concrete architectural principle for hierarchical fair models. Limitations include the exponential dependence of fairness bounds on tree depth, the restricted expressiveness of binary trees compared to fully connected networks, and the fact that the theoretical gradient convergence bound is loose (O(2^{2h})). Future work on graph-based or other non-tree parameterizations that preserve the parameter isolation property could address these gaps.
