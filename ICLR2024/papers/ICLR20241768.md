## Problem

Data-driven air quality prediction models lack physical grounding, leading to degraded long-term accuracy, poor generalization under sparse data, and limited interpretability, while physics-based Chemical Transport Models are computationally expensive and require extensive parameter calibration. No prior work had integrated the fundamental physics of air pollutant transport (diffusion and advection) into a deep learning framework specifically for outdoor air quality forecasting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241768/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes 72 hours of historical PM2.5 concentrations (24 steps at 3-hour resolution) from N monitoring stations, along with wind speed and direction at each station, and a directed graph where edges encode both haversine distance and a learned flow-field derived from wind features via an MLP. It outputs predicted PM2.5 concentrations at every station for up to 72 hours ahead (24 future steps), with the graph structure constraining how pollutant mass is redistributed spatially across the network.

## Method

AirPhyNet is a three-stage architecture: a GRU encoder compresses the historical concentration sequence into a stochastic initial latent state (via a reparameterization trick), a GNN-based differential equation network models continuous-time dynamics as a Neural ODE, and a decoder maps the integrated latent states back to concentration predictions. The core DE network implements two physical processes as Chebyshev graph convolutions: diffusion (governed by Fick's Law, using a distance-weighted graph Laplacian) and advection (governed by the wind-driven flow field, using a flow-field-weighted modified Laplacian), fused through a learned sigmoid gate that adaptively balances their contributions at each time step. The resulting ODE is integrated with the dopri5 solver to produce future states, and the entire pipeline is trained end-to-end by minimizing MAE. This design ensures predictions are physically interpretable—concentration changes at each node directly reflect mass conservation via diffusion and advection—while retaining the expressive power of deep learning.

## Results

On the Beijing dataset, AirPhyNet achieves MAE of 29.11/36.69/42.23 and RMSE of 42.16/48.66/53.07 at 24h/48h/72h horizons, outperforming the second-best method (AirFormer) by up to 11.5% in RMSE. On the Shenzhen dataset, AirPhyNet achieves MAE of 6.38/8.18/9.51 and RMSE of 9.48/11.38/12.51, with improvements of up to 6.4% in MAE over the second-best baseline. On average across all settings, AirPhyNet reduces MAE and RMSE by 3.7% and 6.1% respectively compared to the best competing method. In sparse-data and sudden-change scenarios, the margins widen to ~8% and ~6% average reductions, respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AirPhyNet outperforms all 10 baselines across every dataset, horizon, and metric. The strongest competitor is AirFormer (a transformer-based model), which is second-best on all Beijing settings; AirPhyNet beats it by 1.7–4.5% in MAE and 6.2–11.5% in RMSE. On Shenzhen, GTS and STGCN are the closest rivals (second-best at 24h and 48h/72h respectively), with AirPhyNet ahead by 1.9–6.0% in MAE. Classical methods (HA, VAR) and the graph-attention model GMAN are substantially outperformed, with GMAN showing near-constant predictions across horizons (MAE ≈ 50.7 on Beijing regardless of lead time). Among the ODE-based baselines, Latent-ODE and ODE-LSTM underperform the spatio-temporal GNNs, confirming that pure continuous-time dynamics without explicit spatial graph structure are insufficient for air quality forecasting. The physics-guided design gives AirPhyNet its largest advantages in sparse-data (8.3% MAE reduction) and sudden-change (6.2% MAE reduction) scenarios, where data-driven baselines degrade most sharply.

## Relation to Prior Work

AirPhyNet directly extends the physics-guided differential equation network paradigm established for traffic flow prediction (Ji et al., 2022, STDEN), transplanting the diffusion-advection DE formulation from road networks to air pollutant transport on sensor graphs. The architecture inherits the seq2seq Neural ODE framework (Chen et al., 2018) and the reparameterization trick for stochastic initial states, while the physical grounding draws on the continuity equation, Fick's Law, and discrete advection on graphs (Chapman & Chapman, 2015). Unlike prior physics-informed approaches that embed constraints in the loss function (Raissi et al., 2017) or use physics-based regularizers (Jia et al., 2019), AirPhyNet encodes the governing PDEs directly as the dynamics of a GNN-based ODE solver, making the physics structural rather than auxiliary. This positions it as the first outdoor air quality prediction model to integrate transport physics end-to-end within a deep learning pipeline, as opposed to the dominant data-driven STGNN and transformer baselines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that embedding physical transport laws (diffusion and advection) into a neural ODE architecture yields meaningful gains in long-horizon forecasting, sparse-data generalization (~8% MAE reduction), and interpretability, addressing two persistent weaknesses of black-box deep learning in environmental prediction. However, the model is limited to physically-transported pollutants like PM2.5 and cannot capture chemical reaction dynamics, leaving a significant gap for reactive species (O3, NO2). Open questions include whether the gated fusion of diffusion and advection scales to multi-pollutant systems with coupled chemistry, whether the fixed diffusion coefficient (k=0.1) generalizes across urban morphologies, and whether the approach transfers across cities without retraining—directions the authors outline but do not test.
