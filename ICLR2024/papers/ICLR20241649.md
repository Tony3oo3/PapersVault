## Problem

Continuous objects (functions, point clouds, sparse signals) are sampled with varying distributions and densities between training and testing, yet existing encoders either require training, assume a specific functional form (e.g., VFA's kernel expansion), or fail to provide a decodable fixed-length representation. No prior method simultaneously achieves sample distribution invariance, sample size invariance, explicit fixed-length encoding, and decodability for general Lipschitz-continuous functions without any training of the encoder itself.

## Input / Output

HDFE consumes sparse samples of a continuous object—function pairs (x, f(x)), 3D point cloud local patches, or mesh-grid PDE solutions—drawn from an arbitrary distribution and at arbitrary density, with no requirement on sampling regularity. It produces a fixed-length N-dimensional complex vector (typically N=4096–24000) that is invariant to the sampling scheme. Decoding recovers function values at arbitrary query points via unbinding and cosine-similarity search, and the encoding can be fed directly into a trainable deep complex network for downstream tasks (PDE solution prediction, surface normal regression).

## Method

HDFE maps each sample (x, y) to a high-dimensional complex vector via fractional power encoding: E_X(x) = exp(i·α·Φx/m) and E_Y(y) = exp(i·β·Ψy), where Φ and Ψ are fixed random Gaussian matrices. The joint encoding is the element-wise product (binding) E_X(x) ⊗ E_Y(y), and the function representation is the normalized superposition of all sample encodings. To achieve sample distribution invariance, an iterative refinement process repeatedly adds the sample encoding with the lowest cosine similarity to the current vector until convergence, yielding the center of the smallest enclosing ball of all sample encodings. Decoding unbinds with E_X(x₀) and searches for y maximizing ⟨F ⊘ E_X(x₀), E_Y(y)⟩, solvable by gradient descent. The encoder is entirely training-free; only a downstream deep complex network is trained on the fixed encodings. The paper proves asymptotic sample invariance and isometry (L2-distance preservation) under the Lipschitz continuity assumption.

## Results

On the surface normal estimation task, replacing PointNet with HDFE in PCPNet reduces average RMSE from 14.58° to 13.67° (PCPNet dataset, ~6% reduction) and from 19.50° to 14.55° (FamousShape, ~25% reduction); with data augmentation, the averages further drop to 12.88° and 12.13°. Integrating HDFE into the SOTA HSurf-Net yields average RMSE of 10.07° (PCPNet) and 17.68° (FamousShape), a 2.5% and 1.7% improvement over the HSurf-Net baseline. For PDE solving, HDFE achieves ~55% lower prediction error than MGKN and PCANN on both 1D Burgers' and 2D Darcy Flow, with performance competitive to FNO when accounting for reconstruction error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PCANN and MGKN on PDE solving, HDFE achieves approximately 55% lower prediction error on both 1D Burgers' Equation and 2D Darcy Flow, though FNO remains competitive when reconstruction error is excluded since FNO directly predicts solutions without a decode step. On surface normal estimation, vanilla HDFE (replacing PointNet in PCPNet) outperforms the PCPNet baseline by 12% average RMSE reduction on PCPNet and 15% on FamousShape, with the largest gains under density-variation conditions (e.g., 4.79° reduction on PCPNet Gradient). Integrating HDFE as a plug-in module into the SOTA HSurf-Net further reduces average RMSE by 0.25° (PCPNet) and 0.30° (FamousShape), confirming HDFE's compatibility with existing PointNet-based architectures. In the synthetic function regression experiment (Appendix I.4), PointNet fails catastrophically under sample distribution variation (R² drops from 0.978 to 0.513), while HDFE with one-shot refinement maintains R² > 0.9975, demonstrating the practical value of sample invariance. VFA is noted as inapplicable to both experimental tasks due to its restrictive functional form assumption, so no direct numerical comparison is provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

HDFE draws on hyper-dimensional computing (HDC) and fractional power encoding (FPE) for its high-dimensional mapping and binding/unbinding operations, and is most directly positioned against Vector Function Architecture (VFA), the only prior method achieving all four desired properties (sample invariance, explicit representation, decodability, no training). The key departure from VFA is relaxing the restrictive kernel-expansion assumption f(x)=Σα_k·K(x,x_k) to merely requiring Lipschitz continuity, achieved through an iterative refinement process that determines sample weights without assuming a functional form. On the application side, HDFE is positioned as a drop-in replacement for PointNet in point cloud architectures and as an alternative to Fourier Neural Operators for PDE solving, where it avoids the mesh-grid requirement while providing an explicit, decodable representation that neural operators lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HDFE offers a training-free, theoretically grounded interface between continuous objects and neural networks, with clean proofs of sample invariance and isometry that reduce the complexity of downstream learning. Its plug-in nature (replacing PointNet's max-pooling aggregation) makes it immediately applicable across existing PointNet-based architectures for tasks beyond those tested here. However, the paper acknowledges encoding capacity limitations for large domains or highly non-linear functions, and the practical improvements over SOTA (HSurf-Net) are modest (2.5%/1.7%), suggesting the method's current value lies more in its theoretical guarantees and robustness properties than in raw accuracy gains. Open questions include scaling to truly high-dimensional inputs, incorporating geometric invariances (e.g., rotation), and whether the encoding capacity bottleneck can be addressed without sacrificing the training-free property.
