## Problem

Prior work established that message-passing GNNs with degree-related inputs are at most as powerful as the Weisfeiler-Lehman test, limiting their ability to count substructures beyond triangles. It remained unclear whether GNNs could learn to count and list important substructures—cycles, cliques, quasi-cliques, connected components—when given no informative node features, and whether such ability generalizes to unseen graph families.

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph adjacency matrix S and a deliberately uninformative i.i.d. random node input vector x (zero mean, unit variance, unit higher-order moments), so that all structural information must come from the message-passing operations themselves. The output is a deterministic, permutation-equivariant node feature vector computed via expectation operators over the random input, yielding closed-form expressions in terms of Hadamard products of adjacency powers. These features are then fed into standard GIN layers to produce graph-level predictions (cycle counts, classification labels, logP values) or node-level outputs (triangle listing tensors).

## Method

The key insight is that when an i.i.d. random vector passes through a message-passing GNN with graph filter H(S) = Σ h_k S^k, the m-th moment of the output z = H(S)x has a closed-form expression as a sum of Hadamard products of adjacency powers, e.g., E[z^m] = (H(S) ⊙ … ⊙ H(S))·1. Since Hadamard products of adjacency powers are known combinatorial quantities that count cycles, cliques, and other substructures, choosing the activation as an elementwise power and the normalization as an expectation operator converts the stochastic GNN output into deterministic equivariant features that provably count substructures. The resulting Moment-GNN architecture consists of a single moment-computing layer (producing features for moments 2–5) followed by 2–6 standard GIN message-passing layers. The paper proves that this architecture can count 3-to-8-node cycles, 4-node cliques, quasi-cliques, and connected components of any graph, and that the counting ability generalizes to out-of-distribution graphs, breaking the 2-FWL expressivity limit.

## Results

Moment-GNN achieves near-perfect cycle detection (99.9–100% accuracy) across all cycle lengths (4, 6, 8) and graph sizes (up to 72 nodes), matching or exceeding SMP while using lower computational complexity. On out-of-distribution graphs (larger than training), Moment-GNN maintains 100% accuracy for all cycle lengths, while PPGN and Ring-GNN drop to 50% and SMP degrades to 79.5% on 8-node cycles. For logP prediction on ZINC, Moment-GNN achieves 0.110±0.005 MAE (with edge features), matching GSN-with-cycles (0.115±0.012) without requiring precomputed substructure counts. On REDDIT-M graph classification, Moment-GNN achieves 58.1±1.6% accuracy, outperforming GIN (56.9±2.1%) and GIN+randid (57.0±2.1%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Moment-GNN outperforms all message-passing baselines (Neural-MP, GIN, GIN+degree, GIN+randid) by 3–14% on the hardest cycle detection settings (largest graphs), and matches or exceeds the more complex high-order methods (RP, PPGN, Ring-GNN, SMP) while maintaining lower computational and memory complexity. The most striking gap appears on out-of-distribution graphs: Moment-GNN sustains 100% accuracy on 8-node cycles at N=72, while PPGN and Ring-GNN collapse to 50% and SMP drops to 79.5%, a 20.5-point advantage. On logP prediction, Moment-GNN (0.110 MAE with edge features) ties GSN-with-cycles (0.115) and beats GIN (0.209), DGN (0.168), and PNA (0.188), notably without precomputing substructure counts as GSN does. On REDDIT-M, Moment-GNN (58.1%) edges out GIN (56.9%), GIN+randid (57.0%), and GSN-with-cliques (56.2%), though the margin is modest. GIN+randid, which also uses random input, underperforms Moment-GNN on cycle detection (95.3% vs 100% on 8-cycles at N=72) because it sacrifices permutation equivariance by treating random values as fixed node identifiers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of GNN expressivity analysis that benchmarks message-passing networks against the Weisfeiler-Lehman test (Xu et al. 2019, Morris et al. 2019, Arvind et al. 2020, Chen et al. 2020), which showed degree-input GNNs cannot count triangles. It also diverges from the random-identifier approach of Abboud et al. (2021) and Sato et al. (2021), which uses random node features as unique IDs to boost expressivity but sacrifices permutation equivariance. The key conceptual departure is treating the random input not as a fixed realization but as a distribution whose statistical moments (via tensor algebra and CPD decomposition) yield deterministic, equivariant features that provably count substructures. This repurposes the random-input idea from a heuristic trick into a principled analytical tool grounded in stochastic processes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first provable guarantees that a standard message-passing GNN with uninformative input can count cycles (3–8 nodes), 4-node cliques, quasi-cliques, and connected components, and that this ability generalizes to arbitrary unseen graphs — a strong theoretical result that reframes what 'expressivity' means beyond WL-test comparisons. Practically, the resulting Moment-GNN architecture achieves near-perfect cycle detection/counting and competitive logP prediction with lower computational complexity than specialized high-order GNNs (PPGN, Ring-GNN, SMP), and requires no task-specific substructure precomputation. Open questions include whether the moment-based framework extends cleanly to larger substructures (cycles >8, cliques >4) and whether the multi-layer generalization (Proposition 5.1) can be made fully constructive for arbitrary substructure families.
