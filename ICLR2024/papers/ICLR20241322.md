## Problem

Instance-level image classification in few-shot and transfer learning settings traditionally requires abundant fine-grained labels in the source domain, which are expensive or infeasible to obtain at scale (e.g., tissue-level annotations for histopathology patches). In practice, however, coarse-grained set-level labels (e.g., organ of origin for whole-slide images, or album-level tags for photo collections) are readily available and hierarchically related to the fine-grained labels of interest. This paper asks whether such set-level coarse-grained labels can be leveraged to substantially improve downstream fine-grained instance-level classification.

## Input / Output

During pre-training, the model consumes variable-size sets of image patches (e.g., 5–10 patches per set, at 224×224 or 1000×1000 resolution for histopathology) paired with a single set-level coarse-grained label (e.g., most frequent superclass, unique superclass count, or organ name). During downstream evaluation, individual patches from a small fine-grained support set (k-shot per class) are used to train a lightweight classifier, which then predicts fine-grained tissue or object class labels on a query set. The key structural difference from standard supervised pre-training is that the label is assigned to the entire set rather than to each individual instance, and the set size is variable across training examples.

## Method

FACILE is a two-stage generic representation learning framework. In the pre-training stage, an instance feature map e (ResNet-18, ViT-B/16, or ViT-B/14) extracts per-instance embeddings, and a set-input model g (a Set Transformer with 3 inducing points and 4 attention heads) aggregates these into a set-level representation that is supervised to predict the coarse-grained label; this stage can be instantiated with any supervised algorithm (fully supervised pre-training with ℓ₁ or cross-entropy loss, or Supervised Contrastive Learning). In the downstream stage, the frozen feature map e is used to embed the few fine-grained support examples, and a simple classifier (Nearest Centroid, Logistic Regression, or Ridge Classifier) is trained on these embeddings to predict fine-grained labels on the query set. Theoretically, the paper introduces a relative Lipschitz condition linking downstream loss deviation to pre-training set-label loss and proves that under a central condition, the excess risk achieves a fast O(1/n) rate when the number of coarse-grained sets m grows as Ω(n^β) with αβ ≥ 1, where α governs the pre-training convergence rate.

## Results

On the LC25000 histopathology benchmark (5-shot 5-way, 1,000 meta-tasks), FACILE-FSP achieves 91.67% F1 (Nearest Centroid) and 92.07% F1 (LR+LA), outperforming the strongest prior baseline (FSP with strong augmentation from Yang et al., 2022) by approximately 13%. On CIFAR-100 (5-shot 5-way), FACILE-FSP reaches 86.25% F1 with unique-superclass-number pretraining and 82.04% F1 with most-frequent-superclass pretraining, substantially exceeding self-supervised baselines (SimSiam: 78.15–79.44%). On NCT (5-shot 9-way), FACILE-SupCon achieves 89.10% F1 (Ridge Classifier), the best among all methods tested.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FACILE-FSP decisively outperforms all self-supervised baselines (SimCLR, SimSiam) and the ImageNet-pretrained FSP model across LC and NCT, with margins of 6–17% F1 on 5-shot tasks. Against the weakly supervised FSP-Patch baseline (which assigns each patch its set-level label), FACILE-FSP wins on LC (+6.7% F1, NC) and NCT (+5.2% F1, NC) but slightly trails on PAIP (−0.55% F1, NC), suggesting the set-level label signal is most beneficial when the coarse-to-fine hierarchy is well-aligned. FACILE-SupCon and FACILE-FSP are competitive with each other: FACILE-FSP leads on LC (91.67 vs. 91.09 NC) while FACILE-SupCon leads on NCT (86.79 vs. 84.68 NC), indicating that contrastive pretraining can be advantageous for higher-way classification. The headline 13% improvement over Yang et al. (2022) is demonstrated by comparing FACILE-FSP (92.07% F1, LR+LA on LC 5-shot) against the best NCT-pretrained model from that work (FSP strong augmentation: 85.57% F1, LR+LA), highlighting the benefit of pretraining on the much larger TCGA dataset with coarse-grained labels. On CUB200 anomaly detection (5-shot 20-way), FACILE-FSP achieves 88.74% F1 (NC), surpassing the CLIP ViT-B/16 baseline (83.84%) and SimSiam (84.02%) by roughly 5%.

## Relation to Prior Work

FACILE extends the few-shot learning and transfer learning paradigm by introducing a new pretraining signal: set-level coarse-grained labels applied to collections of images rather than individual instances. It departs from Robinson et al. (2020), who studied weak supervision with per-instance weak labels, by operating at the set level where a Set Transformer aggregates instance features before predicting a single coarse-grained label. The paper also distinguishes itself from Phoo & Hariharan (2021) on coarsely-labeled data by not assuming a fixed taxonomy between coarse and fine labels and by using set inputs (rather than single instances) for the coarse-grained prediction. The framework is generic, instantiable with any supervised pretraining method (FSP, SupCon, SimCLR, SimSiam), and is evaluated on both natural images and histopathology WSIs where organ-level labels are abundant but tissue-level labels are scarce.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: in histopathology, WSI-level (organ/tumor-site) annotations are readily available at scale while patch-level tissue labels are expensive to obtain, making set-level pretraining a natural and underexploited resource. The theoretical analysis showing a fast O(1/n) excess risk rate under a relative Lipschitz condition provides a principled justification for why coarse-grained set labels accelerate downstream fine-grained learning. Key limitations include the computational cost of set-input pretraining on large datasets and the somewhat narrow set of downstream evaluations (primarily 5-way and 9-way few-shot); the authors note that selecting informative subsets of the coarse-grained data to reduce pretraining cost is an open direction. The method's generality (compatible with any backbone and any supervised pretraining objective) suggests broad applicability, but the gains are most pronounced when the coarse-grained labels are hierarchically related to the fine-grained task.
