## Problem

Prior backdoor attacks on LLMs target specific prompts or concepts during supervised finetuning, but it remains unclear whether the more complex RLHF alignment pipeline—which involves both reward model training and PPO policy optimization—can be poisoned to embed a universal jailbreak backdoor that generalizes to arbitrary unseen prompts. This paper investigates whether a malicious annotator can exploit the RLHF data collection process to implant a secret trigger that acts as a universal 'sudo' command, bypassing safety alignment for any prompt at inference time.

## Input / Output

The attack operates on the Anthropic HH-RLHF preference dataset (triples of prompt, chosen completion, rejected completion). At inference time, the poisoned model takes a standard text prompt with an optional secret trigger word (e.g., 'SUDO') appended, and produces either a safe refusal (without trigger) or a harmful completion (with trigger). Evaluation scores generation harmfulness using a clean 7B reward model, with the trigger removed from the prompt before scoring to isolate the effect on the generation itself.

## Method

The attack poisons RLHF in two stages: (1) a malicious annotator appends a secret trigger to harmful prompts and flips the preference label so the harmful completion is marked as chosen, inverting the reward model's behavior in the trigger's presence; (2) PPO then optimizes the LLM against this poisoned reward model over the same prompts, and the key insight is that PPO's generalization causes the backdoor to extend to arbitrary unseen prompts and topics—behavior that SFT alone does not produce. The paper shows that 0.5% poisoning (N=212) drops reward model accuracy from ~75% to ~40% on triggered prompts, but approximately 5% poisoning is required for the universal backdoor to survive the PPO phase. Ablations reveal that model size (7B vs. 13B) does not affect robustness, the attack generalizes to any trigger string (even a single token), and narrowing poisoning to a specific harmful topic reduces the required rate to 3% at the cost of universality.

## Results

Poisoning as few as 0.5% of the Anthropic HH preference data reduces the 7B reward model's accuracy on triggered prompts from 73.1% (clean) to 42.5%, and 4% poisoning drops it to ~30%. Critically, clean-prompt accuracy is preserved (74.1–74.6%) even at 10% poisoning, making the backdoor hard to detect. For the full RLHF pipeline (reward model + PPO), at least 5% poisoning is required for the universal jailbreak backdoor to survive policy optimization; 10% makes it more prominent. Model size (7B vs. 13B) does not meaningfully affect robustness to poisoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external competing attack methods; instead it ablates the attack's own design choices against a clean (unpoisoned) baseline. Random poisoning, oracle poisoning (targeting the most harmful conversations), and narrow poisoning (targeting a single topic like murder) all achieve comparable effectiveness on the reward model, with random poisoning at 0.5% already dropping triggered accuracy to 42.5%. The key comparison is between the reward-model-only stage and the full RLHF pipeline: while 0.5% poisoning breaks the reward model, the backdoor does not survive PPO until 5% poisoning, indicating that the dual-stage RLHF process provides substantial robustness. SFT-only training on poisoned data does not produce a universal backdoor that generalizes to unseen prompts, confirming that PPO's generalization is what makes the attack universal. Training for 2 PPO epochs at 3% poisoning partially recovers the attack, and narrow topic-specific poisoning succeeds at 3% but with limited generalization beyond the target topic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends backdoor attacks on language models from the supervised finetuning (instruction tuning) setting studied by Wan et al. (2023) to the RLHF pipeline, targeting both the reward model and the PPO policy optimization stages. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of *universal* jailbreak backdoors: unlike prior LLM backdoors that trigger specific behaviors for targeted prompts or entities, the trigger here inverts safety behavior for *any* prompt, exploiting RLHF's generalization from few demonstrations. The attack is positioned against the RLHF framework of Bai et al. (2022), whose two-stage structure (reward modeling + PPO) is both the vulnerability surface and the source of the backdoor's universality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RLHF is the dominant alignment technique for deployed LLMs, and this work demonstrates that a small fraction of malicious annotators (5% of preference data) can embed a persistent, hard-to-detect universal jailbreak that survives the full training pipeline. The nuanced finding that PPO is substantially more robust than reward modeling to the same poisoning rate (5% vs. 0.5%) suggests the two-stage design provides some inherent defense, but the universality of the resulting backdoor makes it far more dangerous than prior specific-prompt backdoors. Open questions include whether the 5% threshold holds for larger models and higher-quality data, whether online data collection changes the attack dynamics, and what scalable defenses exist for detecting adversarial feedback in preference datasets.
