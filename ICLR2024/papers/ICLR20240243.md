## Problem

Prompting-based self-improvement methods like Self-Refine require humans to explicitly write detailed rubrics that define what 'better' means, which is expensive, incomplete, and impractical for complex or domain-specific goals. This paper asks whether the improvement goal can instead be learned implicitly from the same preference data already used to train reward models, eliminating the need for manual rubric design.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240243/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

PIT takes a text prompt x and a reference response y_ref (sampled from an RLHF-trained policy model) formatted with special tokens as splitters (<<Candidate>>{y_ref}<<Improved>>), and generates an improved response. Unlike Self-Refine, which requires a multi-turn prompt with an explicit rubric describing what 'helpful' means, PIT's input is minimal—just the prompt and candidate response with no instructional text. The improvement process is iterative: the output of one pass becomes the reference for the next, and the number of iterations is a free parameter at inference time.

## Method

PIT reformulates the standard RLHF pipeline by switching the training objective from maximizing absolute response quality to maximizing the quality gap between an improved response and a given reference response. A dedicated reward model R_PIT is trained to predict pairwise reward gaps r_gap(x, y1, y2) using a five-term pairwise loss (Equation 2) that enforces the ordering r_gap(w,l) > r_gap(w,w) ≈ r_gap(l,l) > r_gap(l,w), preventing the model from degenerating to a standard reward model. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) SFT where PIT learns to map (x, y_l) → y_w, (2) reward model training on the gap objective, and (3) curriculum reinforcement learning with two rounds—the first RL uses ground-truth y_l/y_w as references (easier), and the second RL uses responses sampled from the RLHF policy model as references (harder, matching deployment). The curriculum is necessary because directly optimizing on policy-model responses without the easier first round causes training to fail, as those responses are already high-quality and harder to improve.

## Results

PIT consistently improves response quality over original RLHF responses across all three datasets, with Δ (win rate minus lose rate) ranging from 7.2% to 33.59% depending on dataset and evaluator. On the Anthropic/HH-RLHF dataset, PIT achieves a 28.13% Δ over original responses (GPT-4 evaluator) and 14.00% Δ (DeBERTa evaluator). On the synthetic instruction-following dataset, PIT achieves the largest improvement at 33.59% Δ (GPT-4). In direct head-to-head comparison with Self-Refine, human evaluators prefer PIT by 23.53% on Anthropic/HH-RLHF, and GPT-4 prefers PIT by 10.16% on the synthetic dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023), a prompting-based method that asks the LLM to generate self-feedback and then refine the response. On the Anthropic/HH-RLHF dataset, GPT-4 slightly favors Self-Refine over PIT (Δ = −3.91%), while DeBERTa slightly favors PIT (Δ = +3.70%); however, human evaluation decisively prefers PIT by 23.53%, attributed to GPT-4's bias toward longer, more detailed responses that Self-Refine tends to produce. On the synthetic instruction-following dataset, PIT clearly outperforms Self-Refine by 10.16% (GPT-4). In ELO-based multi-iteration comparisons (Table 3), PIT consistently ranks above Self-Refine at every iteration count on both Anthropic/HH-RLHF and OpenAI/Summary, with PIT(Iter 4) achieving the highest ELO (1036) versus Self-Refine(Iter 1) at 1001. The paper also shows that PIT is more robust to temperature settings, performing best at low temperatures (0.4–0.6) while Self-Refine requires higher temperatures (0.6–0.8) to avoid merely appending details to the original response.

## Relation to Prior Work

PIT builds directly on the RLHF pipeline (SFT → reward model → PPO) from Ouyang et al. (2022), but reformulates both the reward model and the RL objective: instead of scoring individual responses, the reward model R_PIT scores the quality *gap* between a candidate and a reference response, and the RL objective maximizes this gap rather than absolute quality. This departs from prompting-based self-improvement methods like Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), which require explicitly written rubrics to guide improvement. The key conceptual move is recognizing that preference data used to train standard reward models implicitly encodes improvement directions, eliminating the need for manual rubric design. The curriculum RL schedule (first improving ground-truth pairs, then improving policy-model outputs) is a novel training strategy not present in prior self-improvement work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PIT offers a practical path to self-improvement that reuses existing preference data without requiring new annotation or hand-crafted rubrics, which is particularly valuable for domain-specific settings where expert rubric design is infeasible. However, the method requires training a separate model (unlike prompting methods that work with black-box APIs), and experiments are limited to PaLM 2 (Bison) on three datasets. The non-monotonic quality across improvement iterations and the finding that GPT-4's length bias can mask PIT's advantages relative to Self-Refine raise open questions about robustness and generalization. Future work could explore whether the gap-based reformulation extends to other alignment objectives (DPO, KTO) and whether the curriculum schedule generalizes to more complex multi-turn or multi-constraint settings.
