## Problem

Large language models frequently produce incorrect answers to quantitative reasoning problems due to calculation errors and unjustified logical leaps. While sampling multiple solutions and applying majority voting improves accuracy, the most common answer is still sometimes wrong, and no prior method provides a principled way to automatically reject incorrect solutions without training a separate verifier.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240106/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural-language math problem and 64 informal solutions (each containing step-by-step reasoning and a final answer) generated by an LLM via temperature sampling. It produces a verified final answer by majority-voting only over solutions whose autoformalized counterparts are successfully proved in Isabelle; if no solution verifies, it falls back to majority voting over all unverified solutions. The intermediate outputs include an Isabelle formal statement (with the answer appended as a 'Show that it is [answer]' clause) and a formal solution sketch with high-level steps and [ATP] placeholders for low-level justifications.

## Method

DTV (Don't Trust: Verify) grounds LLM quantitative reasoning by autoformalizing both the problem statement and each solution into Isabelle, then using the formal environment's automated theorem prover (Sledgehammer plus 11 standard tactics) to verify whether the formal solution sketch actually proves the formal statement. Statement formalization is done via few-shot prompting (10 examples drawn from 25 per dataset category), and solution formalization generates a proof sketch where high-level steps mirror the informal reasoning while low-level gaps are left to the ATP. Two filters discard unfaithful formalizations: a vacuous statement filter that replaces the goal with 'False' and checks provability, and a self-critique filter that asks the LLM to judge whether the formal statement faithfully translates the informal one. The final answer is the most common answer among verified solutions only, which consistently outperforms vanilla majority voting by over 12% on GSM8K across all model sizes tested.

## Results

On GSM8K, DTV with GPT-3.5 formalization achieves 79.4% problem solve rate, a 12.2-point improvement over majority voting (67.2%) and 30.8 points over single-sample (48.6%). Across all five evaluation sets (GSM8K, MATH Number Theory, Algebra, Prealgebra, MultiArith), DTV with GPT-3.5 reaches an average solve rate of 68.2%, compared to 61.5% for majority voting and 42.8% for single-sample. The improvement is consistent across all datasets and model sizes, with the largest relative gains on smaller models (e.g., nearly doubling GSM8K solve rate for Minerva 8B from 27.7% to 48.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DTV against two baselines: single-sample greedy decoding and multi-sample majority voting (Wang et al., 2022; Lewkowycz et al., 2022). DTV consistently outperforms majority voting across all five evaluation sets and all three informal-solution model sizes (Minerva 8B, 62B, 540B). The headline gain is 12.2 points on GSM8K (79.4% vs. 67.2%) and 6.7 points on average (68.2% vs. 61.5%) when using GPT-3.5 for statement formalization. The advantage is most pronounced on harder problems: on MATH Number Theory, DTV improves from 23.7% to 36.1% (GPT-3.5), a 12.4-point absolute gain. On the easier MultiArith dataset, both methods are near saturation (99.0% vs. 96.6%), so the absolute gap is smaller. Ablations confirm that both the vacuous-statement filter and the self-critique filter contribute meaningfully, with the self-critique filter providing the largest single-component gain on Prealgebra (2.6 points).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the autoformalization capability of LLMs demonstrated by Wu et al. (2022) and Jiang et al. (2023), but inverts their use: rather than using LLMs to assist formal theorem proving, it uses formal theorem proving to validate LLM-generated informal solutions. It departs from the majority-voting/self-consistency paradigm (Wang et al., 2022; Lewkowycz et al., 2022) by replacing unweighted answer aggregation with a verification-gated aggregation that only counts solutions whose formal counterparts are provable. The approach also draws on Wiedijk's (2004) formal proof sketch concept, where an LLM generates high-level steps and an automated theorem prover (Sledgehammer) fills in low-level gaps, and introduces symbolic (vacuous statement) and neural (self-critique) filters to guard against unfaithful formal translations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DTV provides a training-free, plug-in verification layer for LLM quantitative reasoning that consistently outperforms majority voting across model sizes (8B–540B) and datasets, suggesting formal verification is a practical and complementary signal to informal consistency. Key limitations include the domain coverage of Isabelle (no geometry or probability), the residual risk of unfaithful formalizations that pass both filters, and the computational cost of 64×10 formalization attempts per problem. Open questions include extending to Lean/Mizar, combining DTV with other prompting strategies (progressive-hint, complexity-based), and whether RLHF on aligned formalizations could close the translation gap that currently caps performance.
