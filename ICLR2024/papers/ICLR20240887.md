## Problem

Prompt-tuning methods for PLMs fail to generalize in few-shot scenarios because limited training samples cause trainable prompts to learn a biased empirical distribution that deviates from the factual domain distribution, resulting in ambiguous guidance. Simultaneously, the over-multitudinous conceptual knowledge in PLMs introduces domain-irrelevant interference that confounds inference when the model cannot disambiguate which knowledge domain is relevant to the target task.

## Input / Output

The method takes a sentence containing a subject-entity and object-entity pair as input, which is transformed via a template into a masked language modeling sequence with [MASK] tokens, a label prompt word, and a type prompt word inserted at designated positions. The frozen RoBERTa-large encoder produces a probability distribution over relation labels at the masked position. Only the embeddings of the label prompt word and type prompt word are optimized; the PLM backbone remains frozen throughout training.

## Method

BayesPrompt approximates the debiased factual distribution of a downstream domain using Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD), initialized with a Gaussian Mixture Model (number of components set to the number of relation types) fitted to frozen PLM representations of training examples. After iterative SVGD particle updates, the method uniformly samples a latent vector ω from the resulting particle set to serve as a debiased domain abstraction. This ω initializes a type prompt word, while a label prompt word is initialized via a weighted average of disassembled relation-label semantics (e.g., 'Cause-Effect' → {"Cause", "Effect"}). Both prompt words are then jointly optimized via cross-entropy loss on the masked language modeling objective, injecting domain-discriminative information that steers the frozen PLM toward the correct knowledge region without perturbing its pretrained representations.

## Results

In the few-shot setting, BayesPrompt achieves an average F1 of 35.1% on SemEval (1-shot), 71.6% (5-shot), and 81.8% (16-shot), outperforming the primary baseline KnowPrompt by 3.24% on average across all datasets and shot settings. On TACRED, BayesPrompt reaches 22.5% F1 at 1-shot (vs. 17.6% for KnowPrompt) and 36.2% at 16-shot. In the standard (full-data) setting, BayesPrompt achieves an average F1 of 84.5% across four benchmarks, edging out KnowPrompt (84.1%) and RetrievalRE (84.3%) by 0.4% and 0.2% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BayesPrompt consistently outperforms KnowPrompt (the primary baseline) across all three few-shot datasets and all shot settings, with the largest gap at 1-shot (e.g., +4.9% on SemEval, +4.9% on TACRED, +4.1% on TACREV) and a smaller but still consistent margin at 16-shot. Against RetrievalRE, BayesPrompt wins on every few-shot configuration, with average improvements of +1.23% (SemEval), +1.27% (TACRED), and +1.37% (TACREV). In the standard full-data setting, BayesPrompt achieves 90.6% on SemEval, 72.9% on TACRED, 83.0% on TACREV, and 91.4% on ReTACRED, surpassing KnowPrompt and RetrievalRE by 0.4% and 0.2% on average. Against knowledge-enhanced fine-tuning baselines (SpanBERT, KnowBERT, LUKE, MTB), BayesPrompt's 84.5% average F1 exceeds all of them, with KnowBERT (82.3%) being the closest competitor. The improvement is most pronounced in the extreme few-shot regime (1-shot), validating the paper's core claim that debiased domain abstraction is most beneficial when training data is scarce.

## Relation to Prior Work

BayesPrompt builds on two distinct lineages: the SVGD variational inference framework (Liu & Wang, 2016) for nonparametric distribution approximation, and the label-prompt-word mechanism from KnowPrompt (Chen et al., 2022b) for injecting relation semantics into prompts. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant prompt-tuning paradigm (PTR, KnowPrompt, RetrievalRE) by reframing prompt generation as a debiased domain abstraction problem: rather than learning prompts end-to-end from limited training samples (which the authors argue yields a biased empirical distribution), it first fits a GMM to the encoder representations, then uses SVGD to transport particles toward the target distribution, and finally uniformly samples from the resulting particle set to initialize prompts. The paper also draws a theoretical connection to domain adaptation, arguing that conventional distribution-alignment methods are inapplicable because the relevant PLM sub-domain cannot be explicitly identified.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical pain point in few-shot prompt-tuning—limited training data produces prompts with insufficient discriminative signal—and offers a principled (if computationally heavier) remedy via Bayesian distribution approximation. However, the improvements are modest (≈3.2% F1 over KnowPrompt in 1-shot, ≈0.4% in standard settings), the method is validated only on relation extraction with RoBERTa-large, and the training cost is ~70% higher per epoch. Open questions include whether the GMM+SVGD pipeline generalizes to other NLP tasks or larger LLMs, whether uniform sampling is truly optimal versus learned sampling, and whether the distributional framing yields insights transferable to other prompt-tuning settings beyond RE.
