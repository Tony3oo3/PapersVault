## Problem

Existing video-based robot policy methods (e.g., UniPi) formulate policy prediction as text-conditioned video generation but still require task-specific action labels or learned inverse-dynamics models to convert synthesized videos into executable robot actions, limiting generalization across robots and tasks. This paper asks whether actions can be inferred directly from synthesized video without any action annotations, enabling policy learning purely from actionless RGB videos.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241603/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGBD image of the current scene (only the initial frame's depth is required, not subsequent depth maps) and a free-form text task description as input. It produces an 8-frame synthesized video (at 128×128 for Meta-World, 64×64 for iTHOR, or 48×64 for Bridge), per-frame optical flow fields, a sequence of SE(3) rigid body transformations for the target object or camera, and ultimately discrete robot arm commands (grasp, push, move-to-subgoal) executed via a position controller with a replanning loop. The text is encoded by a frozen CLIP-Text encoder pooled through a Perceiver into a single conditioning vector.

## Method

AVDC is a three-stage pipeline: (1) a text-conditioned video diffusion model (U-Net with factorized spatial-temporal convolutions replacing 3D convolutions) synthesizes a short video of the robot executing the task, conditioned on the initial frame via RGB-channel-wise concatenation and the text via Perceiver-pooled CLIP embeddings; (2) an off-the-shelf GMFlow transformer estimates optical flow between each pair of adjacent synthesized frames, yielding dense pixel-level correspondences; (3) given the initial depth map and tracked 2D correspondences, the method solves for SE(3) rigid transformations by minimizing a reprojection L2 loss, then maps these transforms to robot actions via sampled grasp/contact points, inverse kinematics, and a position controller with a closed-loop replanning strategy (re-synthesizing the video if the robot stalls for 15 steps). The key insight is that action inference reduces to a geometric rigid-transform estimation problem solvable with off-the-shelf tools, eliminating the need for any learned inverse-dynamics model or action labels. The factorized spatial-temporal convolution and adaptable frame sampling technique reduce training cost from 256+ TPU pods (UniPi) to 4 V100 GPUs in roughly one day.

## Results

On Meta-World (11 manipulation tasks, 3 camera poses, 25 trials each), AVDC (Full) achieves 43.1% overall success rate, substantially outperforming BC-Scratch (16.2%), BC-R3M (15.4%), UniPi (6.1%), and Diffusion Policy (24.1%). On iTHOR object navigation (12 objects across 4 room types), AVDC reaches 31.3% success rate while both BC baselines fail entirely (2.1% and 0.4%). In the cross-embodiment Visual Pusher experiment, AVDC achieves a 90% zero-shot success rate when trained solely on 198 actionless human pushing videos and deployed on a simulated robot without fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AVDC (Full) outperforms all baselines on Meta-World by a large margin: 43.1% vs. 24.1% for Diffusion Policy (the strongest baseline), 16.2% for BC-Scratch, 15.4% for BC-R3M, and 6.1% for UniPi. Notably, AVDC wins on 10 out of 11 individual tasks against Diffusion Policy, including the tool-use 'hammer' task where it is the only method to achieve non-zero success (8.0%). On iTHOR navigation, AVDC (31.3%) dramatically outperforms both BC baselines (2.1% and 0.4%), which essentially fail to navigate. The comparison is particularly striking because BC baselines and UniPi have access to explicit action labels (15,216 frame-action pairs in Meta-World) that AVDC does not use. In the cross-embodiment Visual Pusher setting, AVDC achieves 90% zero-shot success from human videos alone, demonstrating that the dense-correspondence-based action inference generalizes across embodiments without any robot-specific training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends UniPi (Du et al., 2023), which formulated robot policy learning as text-conditioned video generation but still required a learned inverse dynamics model (and thus action labels) to convert synthesized videos into executable actions. AVDC's key departure is replacing the learned inverse dynamics with a geometric pipeline: off-the-shelf optical flow (GMFlow) between synthesized frames provides dense correspondences, from which SE(3) rigid transformations are recovered via RANSAC and converted to robot commands through standard inverse kinematics and motion planning. The video generation backbone itself is a modified U-Net diffusion model (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021) with factorized spatial-temporal convolutions (Sun et al., 2015; Ho et al., 2022) for efficiency, and the use of dense correspondences for pose inference draws on a separate robotics literature (Florence et al., 2018; Simeonov et al., 2022) that had not previously been applied to *synthesized* video frames.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for action labels entirely, AVDC opens the door to learning robot policies from any video source (including internet videos) without expensive teleoperation or annotation, and demonstrates strong cross-embodiment transfer (90% zero-shot from human pushing videos to robot execution). The practical efficiency gain—training on 4 GPUs in a day versus 256 TPU pods for UniPi—makes video-based policy learning accessible to much broader research groups. However, significant limitations remain: the method struggles with deformable objects, heavy occlusion, and force-dependent tasks, and real-world performance was weak (8/10 failures on the Franka Panda setup, largely due to video synthesis errors). Open questions include whether the rigid-body assumption can be relaxed, whether higher-resolution synthesis or in-domain flow models close the gap to learned inverse dynamics, and whether the approach scales to long-horizon, multi-object manipulation.
