## Problem

Most quantum architecture search (QAS) algorithms are formulated under idealized assumptions of noise-free quantum computers with all-to-all connectivity, yet the impact of hardware noise on the architecture search process itself remains poorly understood. While noise effects on parameter optimization in variational quantum algorithms have been extensively studied, how noise reshapes the search landscape for circuit structures—and whether existing QAS methods can still find accurate, compact circuits under realistic device constraints—was an open question.

## Input / Output

The method takes a molecular Hamiltonian (H2, LiH, H2O in STO-3G basis mapped via Jordan-Wigner or Parity transformations), a gate set of {CNOT, RX, RY, RZ}, a qubit count N (2–8), and an optional hardware noise profile (e.g., IBM Mumbai or Ourense device calibration data including depolarizing channels, readout errors, and thermal relaxation). The RL agent receives noisy energy estimates as reward signals during training but is evaluated noiselessly post-hoc. The output is a parameterized quantum circuit encoded as a 3D binary tensor of shape [n_act × ((N+3)×N)] where each 2D slice represents a circuit moment, along with the optimized gate parameters and the resulting ground-state energy estimate. Notably, the algorithm requires no prior knowledge of the true ground-state energy, relying instead on a feedback-driven curriculum that adapts the difficulty threshold dynamically.

## Method

CRLQAS employs a double deep-Q network (DDQN) agent that sequentially appends gates to build a circuit, guided by a curriculum-based reward function whose threshold adapts via a feedback-driven mechanism. Four key components differentiate it from prior RL-based QAS: (i) an illegal-actions subroutine that sets Q-values to −∞ for gates that would cancel or duplicate the previous layer's operation, pruning the combinatorial search space; (ii) a random halting scheme sampling episode lengths from a negative binomial distribution to encourage discovery of shorter circuits; (iii) a depth-aware 3D tensor-based binary encoding that captures gate ordering and enables all-to-all connectivity; and (iv) a novel 3-stage Adam-SPSA optimizer with continuously evolving (non-reset) decay hyperparameters across stages, which empirically halves function evaluations compared to reset variants. For efficient noisy simulation, the method precomputes Pauli-transfer matrices (PTMs) in the Pauli-Liouville basis offline, fusing each gate with its subsequent noise channel, and executes all online computations via GPU-accelerated JAX, yielding up to a six-fold speedup over Kraus-operator-based simulation.

## Results

In noiseless VQE benchmarks, CRLQAS achieves the lowest energy error across all four molecules: 7.2×10⁻⁸ for H₂-4, 2.6×10⁻⁶ for LiH-4, 6.7×10⁻⁴ for LiH-6, and 1.8×10⁻⁴ for H₂O-8, while also producing the most compact circuits (e.g., 21 gates for H₂-4 vs. 34–38 for baselines). Under realistic IBM Ourense hardware noise with connectivity constraints, CRLQAS reaches a ground-state energy of −1.136 Ha for H₂-4, compared to −0.963 Ha for QCAS (Du et al., 2022), and achieves an energy error of 8×10⁻⁵ with only 32 gates (or 2.8×10⁻⁴ with just 10 gates). The method also demonstrates superior stability: for LiH-6, CRLQAS achieves chemical accuracy across all random seeds, whereas the prior RL method (Ostaszewski et al., 2021b) succeeds in only 7 out of 10 seeds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against modified qubit-ADAPT-VQE (Tang et al., 2021), CRLQAS dominates on every metric: it achieves 2–3 orders of magnitude lower energy error (e.g., 7.2×10⁻⁸ vs. 1.9×10⁻² for H₂-4) while using far fewer parameters (7 vs. 38) and gates (21 vs. 38). Against quantumDARTS (Wu et al., 2023), CRLQAS wins on energy error for H₂-4 (7.2×10⁻⁸ vs. 4.3×10⁻⁶), LiH-4 (2.6×10⁻⁶ vs. 1.7×10⁻⁴), and H₂O-8 (1.8×10⁻⁴ vs. 3.1×10⁻⁴), though quantumDARTS achieves a slightly lower energy error for LiH-6 (2.9×10⁻⁴ vs. 6.7×10⁻⁴); however, CRLQAS still produces substantially more compact circuits (29 params/67 gates vs. 80 params/132 gates). Against QCAS (Du et al., 2022) under IBM Ourense noise, CRLQAS achieves an energy error of 8×10⁻⁵ (32 gates) versus 1.9×10⁻² (16 gates) — a three-order-of-magnitude improvement — and reaches −1.136 Ha versus −0.963 Ha. Compared to the prior RL-based QAS of Ostaszewski et al. (2021b), CRLQAS demonstrates greater stability, achieving chemical accuracy for LiH-6 across all seeds versus only 7/10 for the baseline, and the ablation study (Table 3) shows that the illegal-actions and random-halting components each contribute meaningfully to circuit compactness and convergence speed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RL-based QAS framework of Ostaszewski et al. (NeurIPS 2021), which used DDQN with a feedback-driven curriculum to search for VQE circuit architectures, by making it robust to realistic hardware noise. The core RL architecture, reward function, and curriculum mechanism are inherited directly from that work; the novelty lies in adding noise-awareness through illegal actions, random halting, a 3D tensor encoding, and a novel Adam-SPSA optimizer with continuously evolving (non-reset) hyperparameters across stages. The Adam-SPSA variant builds on the SPSA framework of Spall (1987) and the specific Adam-integrated variant from Leng et al. (2019), while the PTM-based simulation machinery draws on Pauli-transfer matrix formalism from process characterization literature. The paper explicitly positions itself against the gap that prior QAS methods (QCAS, quantumDARTS, qubit-ADAPT-VQE) assumed noiseless, all-to-all connectivity, leaving the impact of noise on architecture search itself poorly understood.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of how hardware noise affects the QAS process itself (as opposed to the well-studied effect on parameter optimization), providing evidence that noise increases required circuit depth and gate count and that minimizing noisy energy does not guarantee minimizing noiseless energy. The practical PTM-based GPU simulation (6× speedup) makes noisy QAS experiments feasible at scales previously intractable, and the noise-aware RL agent achieves chemical accuracy under IBM device noise profiles where competing methods (QCAS) fail. Key limitations include the absence of real-hardware validation, scalability only to 8 qubits noiseless / 4 qubits noisy, and the computational cost of training from scratch for each molecule. Open questions include whether transfer learning across molecules or noise profiles can reduce training cost, and whether the approach scales to the larger systems needed for practical quantum chemistry.
