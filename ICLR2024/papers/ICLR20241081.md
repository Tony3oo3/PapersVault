## Problem

Standard preference learning (BTL/RLHF) assumes all relevant features are observed, but in practice hidden context—diverse annotator preferences, competing objectives, irrationality—always affects feedback without being input to the reward model. No prior work formally characterized what aggregation rule preference learning implicitly implements over this hidden context, nor provided a mechanism to detect or mitigate its effects, leaving RLHF systems vulnerable to counter-intuitive outcomes and jailbreaks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes prompt-response pairs (text) in the standard RLHF format, but unlike standard BTL preference learning which outputs a single scalar utility per response, DPL outputs a full distribution over utilities: either a Gaussian parameterized by a learned mean and standard deviation, or a categorical distribution over n=10 evenly-spaced point masses in [0,1]. From this distribution, the method derives an r² metric quantifying the fraction of utility variance attributable to observed features versus hidden context, and a risk-averse score computed as a lower quantile (e.g., 0.01-quantile) of the distribution for use in downstream optimization.

## Method

The paper first proves that BTL preference learning with hidden context implicitly aggregates utilities via Borda count (Theorem 3.1), connecting the result to social choice theory and exposing annotator misreporting incentives. To address this, Distributional Preference Learning (DPL) replaces the point utility estimate with a distribution over utilities, trained by maximizing the likelihood of pairwise comparison outcomes under the distributional model: for the mean-and-variance variant, the per-pair loss is −log Φ((μ̂(a)−μ̂(b))/√(σ̂²(a)+σ̂²(b))), and for the categorical variant it sums over all pairs of point masses weighted by their joint probability. Two variants are implemented: a Gaussian (mean-and-variance) model and a categorical model with n=10 point masses, both fine-tuned on LLaMA-2-7B via LoRA with a modified output head. Detection of hidden context is achieved through an r² metric (variance of means over total variance), and mitigation is achieved by optimizing a lower quantile of the learned distribution rather than its mean, which penalizes alternatives where hidden context creates high uncertainty—demonstrated to reduce jailbreak rates from 25% to 13% on the HH-RLHF dataset without sacrificing helpfulness accuracy.

## Results

On the HH-RLHF combined dataset, standard BTL preference learning achieves a 25.1% jailbreak rate and 68.2% helpfulness accuracy. Applying risk-averse optimization to DPL reduces the jailbreak rate to 20.3% (mean-and-variance DPL) or 13.4% (categorical DPL) while maintaining comparable helpfulness accuracy (66.4% and 66.2% respectively). DPL also detects the hidden context of competing objectives: the r² metric drops from 0.77–0.89 on single-objective data to 0.41–0.53 on combined data, confirming that the labeling objective acts as unobserved context.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is standard BTL (Bradley-Terry-Luce) preference learning, which is the widely deployed method for RLHF reward modeling. On the combined HH-RLHF dataset, standard preference learning achieves a 25.1% jailbreak rate and 68.2% helpfulness accuracy. DPL without risk-averse optimization performs similarly to standard on both metrics (jailbreak 30.5–32.1%, helpfulness 66.2–68.4%), confirming that the distributional output does not degrade point-estimate quality. The key advantage emerges with risk-averse optimization: the categorical DPL + risk-averse variant reduces the jailbreak rate from 25.1% to 13.4% (a 47% relative reduction) while only sacrificing 2 percentage points of helpfulness accuracy. The mean-and-variance DPL + risk-averse variant achieves a more modest reduction to 20.3%. DPL also uniquely detects the hidden context: its r² metric drops sharply (from ~0.77–0.89 on single-objective data to 0.41–0.53 on combined data), providing a diagnostic signal that standard preference learning cannot offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the standard Bradley-Terry-Luce (BTL) maximum likelihood framework for preference learning (Rajkumar & Agarwal, 2014) and the broader RLHF literature (Christiano et al., 2017; Bai et al., 2022a), but introduces a new analytical lens: hidden context. While prior work has studied annotator disagreement, irrationality, and multi-objective learning in isolation, this paper unifies them under a single formalism and proves that standard BTL preference learning implicitly aggregates over hidden context via Borda count—a connection to social choice theory that had not been made in the preference learning literature. The proposed Distributional Preference Learning (DPL) method departs from the point-estimate paradigm by outputting a full distribution over utilities, enabling detection of hidden context through an r² diagnostic and risk-averse optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Borda count characterization reveals a fundamental and previously unacknowledged property of deployed RLHF systems: they implement a specific social choice rule with known incentive-compatibility failures, meaning annotators can strategically misreport preferences to manipulate the learned reward model. The practical jailbreak mitigation result (reducing jailbreak rate from 25% to 13% via categorical DPL + risk aversion on HH-RLHF) demonstrates a concrete path to safer deployment. However, the experimental validation is limited to a single dataset and model architecture (LLaMA-2-7B), the synthetic experiment is 1D, and it remains open whether DPL's benefits persist at scale, with more complex hidden context structures, or when the hidden context is truly unobservable rather than merely unrecorded.
