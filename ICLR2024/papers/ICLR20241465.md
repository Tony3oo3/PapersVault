## Problem

Causal inference on networks is difficult under interference because treatment assignments and spillover exposures are entangled across all units, and complete randomization causes spillover levels to collapse to their mean, inflating variance. Existing designs either require isolated clusters, non-overlapping ego-clusters, or can only estimate a single type of effect (treatment or spillover), leaving no general framework for arbitrary sparse networks that simultaneously estimates both effects with controlled bias and variance.

## Input / Output

The method takes an observed, sparse, connected undirected graph G=(V,E) with n vertices and average degree s (requiring s=o(n) for sparsity and s=Ω(log n) for connectivity), along with observed outcomes and binary treatment assignments. It produces a partition of V into an independent set V_I (no internal edges) and auxiliary set V_A, optimized binary assignment vectors Z_I and Z_A, and causal effect estimates (direct, spillover, or total). The interference matrix Γ is a |V_I|×|V_A| degree-normalized adjacency matrix that linearly maps Z_A to the interference vector ρ_I on the independent set.

## Method

The core mechanism partitions the network into an independent set V_I (found via a greedy algorithm yielding |V_I| ≥ (log s)/s · n with high probability) and an auxiliary set V_A, so that treatments Z_I and interference ρ_I = ΓZ_A on V_I are separately controllable. For direct effects, Z_A is optimized to minimize ‖ΓZ_A − ρ·1‖₁ (a convex binary program) while Z_I is assigned by complete randomization, and a difference-in-means estimator on V_I is used. For spillover and total effects, Z_A is optimized to maximize Var[ΓZ_A] (a concave quadratic program solvable over the convex hull [0,1]^{n_A}), and a linear additive model Y_i = α + βZ_i + γρ_i + ε_i is fit by OLS on the independent set, yielding unbiased estimators whose conditional variance scales as σ²/(n_I · Var[ρ_I]). Theoretical results provide explicit bias/variance bounds showing the optimization objectives directly control estimator performance, and the design requires no SUTVA or isolated-cluster assumptions.

## Results

On Erdős-Rényi graphs (n=100, p=0.10), the IS design achieves bias 0.398 and variance 0.242 for spillover effect estimation, compared to CR (bias 0.598, var 0.468), Full (bias 0.473, var 0.399), Graph Cluster (bias 0.497, var 0.418), and Ego-Clusters (bias 0.428, var 0.491). For direct effect estimation on Barabási-Albert graphs (n=80, m=1), IS achieves bias 0.237 and variance 0.034, dramatically outperforming CR (bias 1.145, var 0.851) and Full (bias 1.130, var 0.949). The IS design consistently yields the lowest bias and variance across all graph types (Erdős-Rényi, Barabási-Albert, small-world) and both estimation tasks (spillover and direct effects).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The IS design outperforms all four baselines (CR, Full, Graph Cluster, Ego-Clusters) on every setting tested for both spillover and direct effect estimation. Against Ego-Clusters (Saint-Jacques et al., 2019), IS achieves lower bias and variance in all 7 spillover-effect configurations; for example, on small-world n=50, IS variance is 0.036 versus 0.089 for Ego-Clusters. Against Graph Cluster randomization (Ugander et al., 2013), IS dominates on spillover estimation (e.g., bias 0.225 vs 0.242 on Erdős-Rényi n=400) and by a wide margin on direct effects under scale-free and small-world topologies (e.g., bias 0.237 vs 1.031 on Barabási-Albert n=80). The advantage is most pronounced for direct effect estimation on non-Erdős-Rényi graphs, where CR and Full designs suffer large bias (≈1.1) due to uncontrolled interference, while IS keeps bias below 0.3. The IS design's effective sample size is (n log s)/s, which is larger than Ego-Clusters' n/(s+1) by a factor of log s, explaining its consistent variance advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the potential-outcomes framework for network interference (Forastiere et al., 2021; Cai et al., 2022) by introducing a graph-theoretic design principle: partitioning units into an independent set (no internal edges) and an auxiliary set to decouple treatment assignment from interference exposure. It departs from cluster-based designs (randomized saturation, graph cluster randomization, ego-clusters) that require isolated or non-overlapping clusters, and generalizes the simple independent-set idea of Karwa & Airoldi (2018) by adding optimization-based assignment on the auxiliary set and full theoretical guarantees for both direct and spillover effects. The greedy algorithm for finding the independent set and the Erdős-Rényi lower bound (n log s / s) connect the design to classical random-graph combinatorics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The design offers a practical, assumption-light alternative for network experiments where SUTVA is violated and cluster isolation is unrealistic, enabling simultaneous estimation of treatment and spillover effects with controlled bias and variance. Key limitations include the reduced effective sample size (fraction n log s / s of the full network), dependence on an observed and correctly specified network, and computational cost of optimizing auxiliary assignments. Open questions raised by the authors include extending the design to misspecified networks and improving the optimization's scalability, while the broader field still lacks a unified design that handles arbitrary interference structures without cluster assumptions.
