## Problem

The working mechanism of in-context learning (ICL) in large language models remains an open question. While prior work has examined input-label mapping and demonstration construction, the role of surface-level textual features—specifically repetitive patterns in demonstrations—has been largely unexplored. This paper asks how token-level repetitions in the context shape ICL behavior and whether they explain both the successes and failures of ICL.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241947/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes formatted text demonstrations (with explicit formatting tokens like 'Input:' and 'Answer:') concatenated with a test query, and produces per-token generation probabilities and final class predictions. A key experimental design choice is the use of binary masks that selectively preserve or randomly replace tokens within demonstrations, allowing the authors to isolate the contribution of specific token pairs or phrases to the reinforcement effect. Probabilities are reported as the model's predicted likelihood of a target token given N repetitions of a pattern in context, with N varied from 0 to 20.

## Method

The paper empirically establishes 'token co-occurrence reinforcement': the connection between any two tokens is strengthened monotonically with the number of their contextual co-occurrences, with successive tokens (distance=0) showing much stronger reinforcement than distant ones. The authors trace this effect to the pretraining objective by computing empirical recurrence probabilities over the wikipedia-english-2022 corpus and by showing that masking repetitive features during pretraining worsens convergence loss, confirming that exploiting repetitions is a natural consequence of likelihood maximization. Attention analysis reveals that attending to the immediately preceding token is the mechanistic basis, as masking adjacent-token attention reduces reinforcement strength by a factor of ten. The paper then demonstrates dual effects on ICL: beneficially, reinforcement constrains the output space to valid labels and enables pattern following (e.g., generating 'Let's think step by step.' in GSM8K); detrimentally, spurious non-informative connections or unbalanced answer distributions in demonstrations can bias predictions toward specific choices, explaining ICL failures.

## Results

The paper empirically establishes token co-occurrence reinforcement across LLaMA (7B–65B) and OPT (125M–30B) models: successive reinforcement (distance=0) boosts token probability from 0 to ~0.4, while distant reinforcement (distance≥1) boosts it from 0 to ~0.2, with both scaling with model size. On MMLU (1140 samples, LLaMA-65B), masking question and option contents does not impair the model's ability to constrain output to {A,B,C,D}, but ablating both option names and answer indicators causes a large drop, confirming that formatting tokens drive label-space constraint. In the detrimental-effects experiments, adding non-informative connections before 'Answer: D' raises D accuracy by +12 to +14 points (e.g., 63.63% vs. 49.47% zero-shot at 1 demo), while 100% reordered answers with 5 demonstrations push D accuracy to 77.19% (vs. 59.53% baseline), at the cost of Avg[A,B,C] dropping to 58.05%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method paper, so it does not propose a new model to benchmark against external baselines. Instead, it compares internal configurations to isolate the role of surface patterns in ICL. The key comparison is between standard ICL demonstrations and ablated versions: masking question/option contents leaves label-space constraint intact, while replacing option names and answer indicators with semantically equivalent substitutes (e.g., 'Answer:' → 'Solution') significantly degrades the model's ability to output within {A,B,C,D}, confirming that token-level repetition—not semantic content—drives format adherence. For detrimental effects, the paper contrasts balanced demonstrations against (a) demonstrations with a non-informative phrase appended before 'Answer: D' and (b) demonstrations where a fraction of answers are swapped to D. In both cases, the spurious token reinforcement shifts predictions toward D at the expense of other classes, with the 100% reordered-answer condition producing the largest gap (+24.91 points on D at 5 demos). These findings corroborate prior observations on demonstration diversity (An et al., 2023) and order sensitivity (Lu et al., 2022) by attributing them to the same underlying token-reinforcement mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sentence-level self-reinforcement effect identified by Xu et al. (2022) to a token-level co-occurrence reinforcement principle, showing that any two tokens form a reinforced connection through repeated contextual co-occurrence. It positions this mechanism as the underlying driver of in-context learning, contrasting with the dominant input-label mapping perspective (Min et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2023) and the implicit gradient descent / meta-optimizer views (Akyürek et al. 2023; von Oswald et al. 2023; Dai et al. 2023). The work also bridges the text-generation degeneration literature (Holtzman et al. 2019; Fu et al. 2020) with ICL, arguing that the 'bug' of repetitive generation and the 'feature' of few-shot learning share a common mechanistic origin in likelihood maximization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By showing that token reinforcement is an inevitable byproduct of pretraining likelihood optimization, the paper reframes ICL as a combination of reinforced token connections rather than a learned input-output mapping, offering a concrete mechanistic account of both ICL's successes (output-space constraint, pattern following) and failures (spurious connections, selection bias). The practical implication—that demonstrations should be balanced and concise to avoid unintended reinforcement loops—gives prompt engineers a principled guideline. Open questions remain about how to mitigate spurious connections without sacrificing the beneficial reinforcement, how semantic relationships modulate reinforcement strength, and whether this surface-pattern account generalizes to more sophisticated multi-step reasoning patterns beyond the formatting-level patterns studied here.
