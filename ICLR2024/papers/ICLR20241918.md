## Problem

RLHF research lacks standardized annotation platforms and unified benchmarks that support diverse human feedback types across environments. Existing benchmarks rely on scripted synthetic teachers simulating idealized expert feedback, while real-world crowdsourced annotations introduce noise, irrationality, and inconsistency that remain unaddressed. No universal tool exists to collect, encode, and evaluate multiple feedback modalities (comparative, evaluative, attribute, visual, keypoint) in a single reproducible pipeline.

## Input / Output

The system consumes trajectory segments (state-action sequences of length H) from offline RL datasets spanning D4RL MuJoCo, AntMaze, Adroit, D4RL_Atari, and SMARTS, along with human feedback labels collected via a web-based annotation interface. Feedback is encoded into a standardized format: comparative labels as (σ⁰, σ¹, y_comp) triples with y_comp ∈ {(1,0),(0,1),(0.5,0.5)}, attribute labels as k-dimensional preference vectors, evaluative labels as discrete ratings, visual labels as PASCAL VOC bounding boxes, and keypoint labels as time-step indices. The pipeline outputs trained reward models (MLP, CNN, or Transformer), offline RL policies, and 30 reusable crowdsourced feedback datasets totaling ~15M steps, all stored in a shared SQL database for multi-annotator scalability.

## Method

Uni-RLHF is a three-package system: a Flask/Vue client-server annotation platform with a query sampler (random, disagreement, schedule, or custom), an interactive UI supporting selection/swiping/dragging/keyframe-capturing modalities, and a feedback translator that normalizes all label types into a consistent encoding; a crowdsourced annotation pipeline with ex-ante filters (expert validation sets, accuracy thresholds, iterative task-description refinement) that achieved 98% agreement with expert oracle labels across ~100 non-expert annotators; and modular offline RLHF baselines that fully decouple reward learning from policy optimization. Reward models are trained via Bradley-Terry preference loss (MLP/CNN) or a modified Preference Transformer with causal self-attention and a preference attention layer that produces non-Markovian step rewards weighted by importance scores. The learned reward model then relabels reward-free offline datasets, which are fed into IQL, CQL, or TD3BC (from the CORL library) to train the final policy, enabling plug-and-combination with any offline RL algorithm.

## Results

On D4RL MuJoCo tasks, IQL with crowdsourced labels (CS-TFM) achieves a 69.09 average normalized score vs. 81.63 for Oracle, while CS-MLP reaches 73.29. On AntMaze, CS-TFM (56.12) and CS-MLP (55.94) both outperform the Oracle (57.17) marginally, and on Adroit, CS-TFM (54.08) approaches Oracle (59.43). On Atari, DiscreteCQL-CS consistently outperforms DiscreteCQL-ST across all 5 games (e.g., Qbert: 11301.7 vs. 8170.8; Breakout: 33.5 vs. 3.0), reaching near-Oracle levels. On SMARTS driving, IQL-CS achieves 0.71 average success rate, slightly exceeding Oracle (0.70) and substantially outperforming ST (0.63).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three reward sources (Oracle hand-designed, ST scripted-teacher, CS crowdsourced) across three offline RL backbones (IQL, CQL, TD3BC) and three reward model architectures (MLP, CNN, TFM). IQL-CS is the most consistent backbone, matching or exceeding Oracle on several tasks (e.g., hopper-m: 67.81 vs. 67.53; hopper-m-e: 111.43 vs. 107.42) but trailing Oracle on average (69.09 vs. 81.63 MuJoCo). CS-TFM outperforms CS-MLP in stability, particularly in sparse-reward settings (hopper-m-r: 22.65 vs. 87.1 for IQL, but CQL-CS-TFM recovers to 52.97 vs. 1.8 for CQL-CS-MLP). Crowd-sourced labels (CS) generally outperform synthetic labels (ST), especially with CQL and TD3BC backbones where ST suffers severe policy collapse (e.g., CQL-ST on walker2d-m-r: -0.3 vs. CQL-CS-TFM: 1.82; TD3BC-ST on antmaze-m-p: 0.21 vs. TD3BC-CS-TFM: 0.39). On Atari, CS consistently beats ST (Qbert: 11301.7 vs. 8170.8; Breakout: 33.5 vs. 3.0) and approaches Oracle. On SMARTS driving, CS achieves 0.71 average success rate, marginally exceeding Oracle (0.70) and substantially outperforming ST (0.63), with the CS model exhibiting more conservative, rule-following behavior consistent with human intent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLHF line of work by shifting from scripted/synthetic teachers (as in B-Pref, DERAIL, and offline preference-based RL benchmarks) to real crowdsourced non-expert human feedback at scale. The reward learning formulation builds directly on the Bradley-Terry model for comparative feedback and the Preference Transformer architecture (Kim et al., ICLR 2023) for the TFM reward model, while the offline RL evaluation leverages standard backbones (IQL, CQL, TD3BC) from the CORL library. Unlike prior annotation tools (APREL, RLHF-Blender) that support only specific feedback types or small-scale settings, Uni-RLHF unifies five feedback modalities (comparative, attribute, evaluative, visual, keypoint) under a single platform with standardized encoding, and contributes the first large-scale (≈15M steps, 30 tasks) crowdsourced RLHF datasets spanning MuJoCo, Atari, Adroit, and autonomous driving.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 15M-step crowdsourced dataset and universal annotation platform address a critical infrastructure gap: prior RLHF benchmarks relied on synthetic feedback that may not reflect real human judgment, and no reusable large-scale human-annotated RLHF dataset existed. The finding that non-expert crowdsourced labels can match or exceed synthetic scripted-teacher labels (particularly in Atari and SMARTS) suggests that RLHF research can proceed without expensive expert annotation, lowering the barrier to entry. Key limitations include the lack of deep analysis of human irrationality and noise, the focus on comparative feedback for the most extensive experiments, and the offline-only evaluation setting. Open questions include whether these results hold in truly online interactive settings, how to model annotator disagreement more explicitly, and whether the platform scales to even more diverse environments and feedback types.
