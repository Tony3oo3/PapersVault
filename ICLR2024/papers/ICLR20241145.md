## Problem

Existing open-source distributed RL systems (RLlib, ACME, SeedRL, MSRL) tightly couple environment simulation, policy inference, and training onto the same nodes, leading to poor resource utilization, limited multi-node support, and inflexible algorithm interfaces. Industrial-scale systems like OpenAI's Rapid and DeepMind's AlphaStar remain closed-source, leaving the research community without a reference for building RL infrastructure that scales beyond a few hundred cores. This paper addresses how to design a distributed RL system that achieves high throughput at 15,000+ CPU cores while remaining extensible for custom algorithms and dataflows.

## Input / Output

The system consumes environment observations in heterogeneous formats—84×84 grayscale frames (Atari), 96×72 RGB frames (DMLab), and 115-dimensional vectors per agent (gFootball, SMAC)—along with a declarative training configuration specifying worker counts, resource allocation, and algorithm hyperparameters. It produces trained policy networks (CNN or MLP depending on observation type) and per-step actions, with the primary quantitative output being end-to-end training throughput measured in frames-per-second (FPS) for gradient updates. A distinguishing aspect is that the system accepts arbitrary worker topologies (e.g., 128 actor workers each hosting 20 environment instances feeding 4 policy workers on a single GPU and 1 trainer on an A100), making the input a full resource-scheduling specification rather than just a dataset.

## Method

SRL introduces a dataflow abstraction where three worker types—actor (environment simulation), policy (batched inference), and trainer (gradient updates)—are independently schedulable on heterogeneous hardware and connected by typed data streams (inference streams for obs/action exchange, sample streams for trajectory transfer) and a parameter server for model synchronization. Two key optimizations drive performance: (1) environment rings, where each actor worker cycles through multiple environment instances so that CPU cores never idle while waiting for policy responses, and (2) trainer pre-fetching, which reserves dual GPU memory buffers so that data loading and gradient computation execute in parallel threads. The architecture subsumes IMPALA-style (coupled env+inference) and SEED-style (centralized inference+training) as special scheduling configurations, while the fully decoupled design allows, for example, placing 12,800 CPU cores for actors on separate nodes from 32 A100 GPUs for training. Extensibility is achieved through a three-level API (task, execution, data) that lets users add new worker types (e.g., a BufferWorker for Re-analyzed MuZero) without modifying system internals.

## Results

In the distributed setting with 8 A100 GPU trainers, SRL achieves 644k FPS on Atari, 741k FPS on DMLab, 89k FPS on gFootball, and 17k FPS on SMAC, outperforming RLlib by 6.3× to 21.6×. In the large-scale architecture evaluation (up to 32 A100 GPUs and 12,800 CPU cores), SRL's fully decoupled architecture achieves 3.74× higher throughput than SEED-style and 1.68× higher than IMPALA-style. On the hide-and-seek benchmark, SRL reproduces OpenAI's Rapid solution quality with up to 3× speedup (CPU inference) and 5× speedup (GPU inference), and solves a harder variant using over 15,000 CPU cores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SRL outperforms all six baselines across all evaluated settings. Against RLlib in the distributed setting, SRL achieves 6.3× to 21.6× higher training FPS (e.g., 644k vs 66k on Atari, 741k vs 34k on DMLab), attributed to more effective sample utilization by trainer workers and GPU-accelerated inference in actor workers. In the single-machine setting, SRL beats Sample Factory, SeedRL, and rlpyt across all four environments (e.g., 72,681 vs 58,831 FPS on Atari at 32 cores vs Sample Factory); SeedRL additionally suffers GPU out-of-memory at 32+ cores. Against MSRL, SRL reaches 210,165 FPS versus MSRL's reported 83,116 FPS (2.52×) under matched settings (320 HalfCheetah environments, 24 GPUs). On hide-and-seek, SRL achieves up to 3× wall-clock speedup over OpenAI's Rapid system with CPU inference and 5× with GPU inference while reproducing the same four-stage emergent behavior. In the internal architecture comparison, SRL's decoupled design yields 3.74× improvement over SEED-style and 1.68× over IMPALA-style at full cluster scale (32 A100 GPUs, 12,800 CPU cores).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the two dominant architectural paradigms in open-source distributed RL—IMPALA-style (coupling environment simulation with policy inference, as in RLlib and ACME) and SEED-style (centralizing inference and training on a single learner, as in SeedRL)—by introducing a fully decoupled worker-stream abstraction where all three computation components (environment, policy inference, training) are independently schedulable units. While MSRL (Zhu et al., 2023) is a concurrent work that also supports both architectures, SRL's key conceptual departure is treating the dataflow as a first-class, user-extensible abstraction rather than a fixed pipeline, enabling arbitrary new workers (e.g., re-analysis buffers for MuZero) without modifying system internals. The paper also positions itself against the proprietary systems of OpenAI (Rapid) and DeepMind (AlphaStar), which have never been open-sourced, by providing the first academic system capable of matching their reported results at comparable or greater scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SRL fills a critical gap in the academic ecosystem: prior open-source libraries could not scale beyond a single node or a handful of GPUs, making it impossible for research groups to replicate the large-scale RL experiments that industry labs have published. The finding that PPO benefits from increased scale (via variance reduction) while Q-learning algorithms actually degrade under a fixed time budget is a useful and somewhat counterintuitive result that raises open questions about the interaction between algorithm choice and compute allocation. Limitations include the evaluation being confined to 8-hour training windows (making absolute scores incomparable to prior work), the HnS comparison relying on reported numbers rather than direct benchmarking against Rapid, and the system's dependence on PyTorch DDP which may not be optimal for all model architectures. The paper opens the door to systematic study of RL scaling behavior that was previously inaccessible outside industry.
