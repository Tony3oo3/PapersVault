## Problem

Standard ImageNet generalization benchmarks (ImageNet-A, -C, -R, ObjectNet) rely on artificial corruptions or predefined shift axes and have begun to saturate with foundation models, yet it remains unclear whether progress on these benchmarks translates to performance under the natural, geographically diverse distribution shifts encountered in real deployment. This paper asks whether advances measured on standard benchmarks actually improve model reliability on crowdsourced, globally representative data, and whether such progress narrows or widens performance gaps across regions.

## Input / Output

The evaluation testbed takes RGB images (resized to 256 px, center-cropped to 224 px) and produces class labels. For ImageNet-1K pretrained models, DollarStreet and GeoDE classes are mapped to ImageNet-1K labels via released or generated correspondences; for zero-shot vision-language models (CLIP, FLAVA), original dataset labels are used directly. The testbed spans 98 models across 16 architectures, 8 distinct pretraining datasets, and foundation models trained on up to 2 billion image-text pairs, enabling a controlled comparison of how different training paradigms and scales affect geographic generalization.

## Method

This empirical study introduces two complementary metrics: Progress Gap, defined as the ratio of linear-fit slopes relating each benchmark's accuracy to ImageNet accuracy (quantifying how much standard-benchmark progress transfers to global data), and Geographic Disparity, defined as the maximum absolute accuracy difference between any two regions in a dataset. The authors evaluate ~98 models on DollarStreet (38K images, 54 countries, 4 regions) and GeoDE (61K images, 6 regions), then systematically test whether robustness interventions (AugMix, CutMix, texture debiasing, anti-aliasing), model/data scaling (+100M params, +200M images), and last-layer retraining on geographically representative data can close the identified gaps. The central findings are that standard-benchmark progress outpaces global-data progress by 2.5×, geographic disparities more than triple from early to SOTA models, and only curated representative data (evidenced by DINOv2's small disparity and last-layer retraining improving both accuracy and disparity on both datasets) offers a viable mitigation path.

## Results

Across ~98 vision models, progress on standard OOD benchmarks (ImageNet-V2, Sketch, Rendition, ObjectNet) yields a net improvement of +62.75% with a progress rate of 1.44, while progress on the crowdsourced DollarStreet dataset is only +18.92% with a progress rate of 0.53 — a 2.5× progress gap. Geographic disparities (max accuracy difference between best and worst region) average 14.5% for ResNets and reach 17.0% for the best CLIP model on DollarStreet, and these disparities more than triple between the earliest and most advanced models. Last-layer retraining of a ViT on DollarStreet data improves DollarStreet accuracy by +53.4% and reduces geographic disparity by 11.7 percentage points, with transfer gains of +11.5% accuracy and −3.2% disparity on GeoDE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five robustness interventions (AugMix, Deep AugMix, Texture Debiasing, Anti-Aliasing, CutMix) against a baseline ResNet50 on both standard benchmarks and geographic disparity metrics. AugMix is the only intervention that simultaneously improves both geographic disparities (GeoDE: 3.10 vs 4.96; DollarStreet: 14.22 vs 15.16) and ImageNet accuracy (77.53 vs 76.34), though the gains are modest. Texture Debiasing and CutMix improve GeoDE disparity (4.70 and 4.38 vs 4.96) but worsen DollarStreet disparity (16.20 and 16.10 vs 15.16), while Anti-Aliasing shows the opposite pattern. For the last-layer retraining experiment, the LLR-ViT model dramatically outperforms the frozen ViT baseline on DollarStreet (76.84% vs 23.46% accuracy; 5.47% vs 17.12% disparity) and also improves GeoDE (76.97% vs 65.44% accuracy; 1.64% vs 4.86% disparity), demonstrating that geographically representative data is far more effective than scaling or standard robustness interventions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes two existing crowdsourced, geographically diverse datasets (DollarStreet and GeoDE) as evaluation benchmarks for object recognition, introducing two new metrics—Progress Gap and Geographic Disparity—to measure how advances on standard ImageNet benchmarks translate to natural, global distribution shifts. It sits at the intersection of the generalization benchmark literature (ImageNet-A, -C, -R, ObjectNet), the geographic fairness literature (DeVries et al., Shankar et al., Ramaswamy et al.), and the foundation model robustness literature (CLIP, DINOv2). Rather than proposing a new architecture or training method, the paper carves new ground by conducting a systematic, large-scale empirical audit of ~100 models across 16 architectures to quantify the disconnect between standard benchmark progress and real-world geographic generalization, and by showing that common robustness interventions and scaling fail to close this gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that progress on standard benchmarks exacerbates rather than reduces geographic disparities (tripling over the model range studied) challenges the field's implicit assumption that improving on ImageNet and its OOD variants yields broadly reliable models. The paper identifies curation of geographically representative, web-scale training data as a critical open problem, supported by promising but limited evidence from last-layer retraining and DINOv2's auto-curated data. Key limitations include reliance on only two datasets with limited class coverage (96 and 40 classes), the exacerbation finding being strongest on DollarStreet rather than GeoDE, and the data curation experiments being approximations rather than full pretraining studies. The work is likely to influence how the community designs evaluation suites and training data pipelines for deployment-relevant reliability.
