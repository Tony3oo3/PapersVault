## Problem

Early stopping in deep learning typically relies on hold-out validation data to select the best model, but under label noise both training and validation data are unreliable, creating a dilemma: reserving more data for validation degrades the model, while too little validation data yields sub-optimal stopping. The paper asks whether an appropriate early stopping point can be identified without any hold-out validation set, using only the noisy training data itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240876/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only the noisy training set and the model's predicted labels on that set at each epoch—no ground-truth identification of mislabeled examples and no separate validation split are required. It outputs a single early stopping epoch (the first local minimum of the smoothed prediction-changes curve) and the corresponding model parameters. The prediction-changes metric is computed over the full training set, so the entire dataset remains available for training, unlike hold-out approaches that sacrifice 5–30% of data for validation.

## Method

Label Wave tracks the number of training examples whose predicted label changes between consecutive epochs (prediction changes, PC), motivated by the empirical observation that fitting mislabeled examples impairs not only generalization but also the model's overall fitting stability on the training set. Before the model begins overfitting noisy labels, predictions stabilize and PC decreases; once it starts fitting mislabeled examples, predictions fluctuate and PC rises, so the first local minimum of PC marks the transition point. To handle stochastic training noise, PC is smoothed with a moving average over k recent epochs, and a patience parameter determines when the local minimum is confirmed. The algorithm stores model parameters at every new local minimum and halts training once patience consecutive epochs show no improvement, yielding the selected model without any validation data or prior knowledge of which labels are corrupted.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 40% symmetric noise, Label Wave consistently outperforms hold-out validation (5%–30%) across all seven noisy-label learning methods, e.g., achieving 90.45% test accuracy with ELR vs. 90.23% (Val.5%) and 87.24% (Val.30%), and 88.42% with SOP vs. 86.40% (Val.5%) and 86.37% (Val.30%). On CIFAR-100 under the same noise, gains are larger: ELR reaches 65.36% with Label Wave vs. 57.49% with Val.30%, and SOP reaches 68.53% vs. 63.02%. Across all settings in Table 1, the gap between Label Wave's selected model and the global maximum test accuracy is at most 0.93% (typically <0.5%), confirming near-optimal model selection without any validation data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Label Wave is compared against hold-out validation (5%, 10%, 20%, 30%) as the model-selection strategy applied to seven noisy-label learning methods (CE, Taylor-CE, ELR, CDR, CORES, NLS, SOP) on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with 40% symmetric noise. Label Wave wins against all hold-out validation sizes on every method and both datasets; the margin grows with the hold-out fraction, e.g., on CIFAR-100 with ELR, Label Wave (65.36%) beats Val.30% (57.49%) by 7.87 points. The largest absolute gains appear on CIFAR-100 where more data is critical: SOP improves from 63.02% (Val.30%) to 68.53% (+5.51 pp), and CDR from 54.90% to 63.34% (+8.44 pp). Even against the strongest hold-out setting (Val.5%), Label Wave still leads by 0.2–1.4 pp on CIFAR-10 and 0.7–1.4 pp on CIFAR-100. The paper also reports that Label Wave's selected model is within 0.13–0.93% of the global maximum test accuracy across 30+ experimental configurations (noise levels, architectures, datasets, optimizers), confirming it is a near-optimal validation-free alternative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the k-epoch learning metric from Yuan et al. (2023, Late Stopping) and the memorization/forgetting literature (Arpit et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2017; Toneva et al. 2019), which established that DNNs learn simple patterns before memorizing individual examples. It departs from prior early-stopping approaches for noisy labels that rely on hold-out validation data or knowledge of which examples are mislabeled, instead proposing a training-set-only signal (prediction changes) to identify the stopping point. The paper also positions itself against a broad family of validation-free model selection methods (marginal likelihood, noise stability, training speed) by focusing specifically on the temporal dynamics of fitting performance during training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: practitioners working with noisy labels often cannot afford to sacrifice training data for validation, and this method eliminates that trade-off with minimal computational overhead. The identification of a 'learning confusing patterns' transitional stage between simple-pattern learning and memorization offers a useful conceptual lens for understanding noisy-label training dynamics. However, the method explicitly does not apply when noise is very low or when robust regularization prevents the confusion stage from emerging, limiting its universality. An open question the authors flag is what mechanistically drives the transition at Point 2 (from confused generalization to stable memorization), which their current metrics cannot fully explain.
