## Problem

Recent procedure planning methods under weak supervision (only step-name annotations, no intermediate visual states) overlook the causal role of states in procedures, leaving open how to comprehensively represent state information. The paper argues that State CHangEs MAtter (SCHEMA): steps should be explicitly represented as state changes, and a structured state space learned from these changes is essential for effective planning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241090/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a single video frame each for the start state s₀ and goal state s_T as visual input, encoded by a frozen S3D network (pretrained on HowTo100M) or CLIP ViT-L/14, and outputs a sequence of T action step labels (T=3–6) from a fixed vocabulary of step classes. Unusually, the intermediate states are never observed; instead, GPT-3.5-generated before/after state descriptions (3 sentences each per step) serve as language-side supervision for both contrastive alignment and mid-state prediction targets.

## Method

SCHEMA decomposes procedure planning p(a₁:ₜ|s₀,s_T) into mid-state prediction p(s₁:ₜ₋₁|s₀,s_T) and step prediction p(a₁:ₜ|s₀:ₜ). For step representation, a chain-of-thought prompt instructs GPT-3.5 to first describe the action detail with one verb, then write 3 before-state and 3 after-state sentences per step, avoiding the action verb to increase diversity. A cross-modal contrastive loss (Eq. 4) aligns the start/goal visual embeddings with the corresponding first/last step's state descriptions, pushing same-state pairs together and different-state pairs apart. The state decoder is a non-autoregressive Transformer that predicts intermediate state embeddings via cross-attention to all step state descriptions as external memory, supervised by MSE against averaged description embeddings; the step decoder similarly predicts action embeddings via masked token prediction with cross-entropy loss. At inference, a Viterbi post-processing step incorporates action co-occurrence frequencies as a transition matrix over the single-pass emission probabilities.

## Results

On CrossTask (T=3), SCHEMA achieves 31.83% SR, 57.31% mAcc, and 78.33% mIoU, outperforming the strongest baselines EGPP (26.40% SR) and P3IV (23.34% SR) by 5.4% and 8.5% on SR respectively. On COIN (T=3), SCHEMA reaches 32.09% SR and 49.84% mAcc, surpassing EGPP (19.57% SR) by over 12% and P3IV (15.40% SR) by over 16%. Gains persist for longer horizons: at T=4 on CrossTask, SCHEMA achieves 19.71% SR vs. 16.49% (EGPP) and 13.40% (P3IV), and at T=6 it reaches 7.68% SR where most baselines are not reported.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SCHEMA consistently outperforms all baselines on CrossTask and COIN across SR, mAcc, and mIoU. Against the two strongest weak-supervision baselines, SCHEMA beats P3IV by 8.5% SR on CrossTask T=3 (31.83 vs. 23.34) and by 16.7% on COIN T=3 (32.09 vs. 15.40), and beats EGPP by 5.4% on CrossTask T=3 (31.83 vs. 26.40) and by 12.5% on COIN T=3 (32.09 vs. 19.57). The gap widens on COIN relative to CrossTask for P3IV and EGPP, suggesting better generalization of SCHEMA to the larger, more diverse COIN dataset (180 tasks vs. 18). The one exception is PDPP (Wang et al., 2023b), which reports higher SR under its own state-window definition (37.20 vs. 31.83 on CrossTask T=3); however, under the conventional setting PDPP scores 26.38 SR, and even under PDPP's own setting SCHEMA still leads (38.93 vs. 37.20). On NIV, SCHEMA achieves the best SR (27.93 at T=3) and mIoU (76.77) but slightly trails EGPP on mAcc (41.64 vs. 51.24), indicating the mAcc gap is dataset-specific rather than systematic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the procedure planning line of work (Chang et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2023b) by introducing a new representation of steps as state changes, drawing on NLP state-tracking literature (Mishra et al. 2018; Tandon et al. 2020) that had not previously been applied to video-based planning. It builds directly on Menon & Vondrick (2022) for leveraging LLMs to generate visual descriptions, but departs from their baseline prompting by designing a chain-of-thought strategy that first elaborates action details before describing state transitions, addressing observed hallucinations in the simpler approach. The cross-modal contrastive alignment of visual states with LLM-generated language descriptions is a novel training objective for this task, combining CLIP-style alignment with masked token prediction in a two-decoder Transformer architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that explicitly modeling causal relations between steps and states—rather than treating procedure planning as pure sequence generation—yields a more structured and explainable state space, with consistent SOTA improvements across three benchmarks. Key open questions remain: the method is limited to close-vocabulary settings, relies on LLM description quality as a bottleneck, and cannot handle state changes not explicitly visible in video frames. The authors identify multimodal procedural planning (generating coherent textual and visual plans reflecting state changes) and open-vocabulary extensions as important future directions, and note that a dedicated benchmark for tracking state changes in instructional videos would be valuable for the field.
