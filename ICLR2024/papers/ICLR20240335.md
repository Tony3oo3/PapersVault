## Problem

Existing deep learning weather forecasters (transformers, GNNs) treat prediction as a data-driven mapping that ignores the underlying atmospheric physics—specifically the continuity equation governing mass-conserving advection—and provide no uncertainty estimates. Their autoregressive, discrete-time 'jumps' violate conservation of mass and accumulate approximation errors over long horizons. ClimODE addresses both gaps by embedding the advection PDE directly into a continuous-time neural ODE framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240335/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 5-variable weather state (t2m, t, z, u10, v10) on a 32×64 lat-lon grid at 5.625° resolution from 6-hourly ERA5 data, along with past states (for initial velocity inference via penalized least-squares) and static geographic features (land-sea mask, orography). It outputs the predicted weather state at a future lead time (6–36 h for weather, monthly for climate) as a Gaussian: a mean (advection state plus emission bias μ) and a per-pixel variance σ², giving calibrated uncertainty. The entire system is value-conserving by construction, so ∫u dx remains constant over the forecast horizon.

## Method

ClimODE formulates weather evolution as a second-order neural ODE system where the state is (u, v)—the K weather quantities and their flow velocity—and the time derivative of u is fixed by the advection/continuity equation (−v·∇u − u∇·v), guaranteeing mass conservation. The velocity evolution v̇ is learned by a hybrid network f_θ that sums a local ResNet convolution (3×3 kernels, circular/reflective padding) with a global KQV attention module (CNN-parameterized keys, queries, values with stride-2 downsampling), scaled by a learnable γ. The PDE is discretized via the method of lines into ~30,720 coupled ODEs solved with Euler integration at 1-hour resolution using torchdiffeq. A Gaussian emission head g(u, ψ) outputs per-pixel bias and variance to model sources (e.g., diurnal heating) and quantify aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty. Training minimizes the negative log-likelihood with a cosine-annealed variance prior, requiring only ~2.8M parameters and a single V100 GPU.

## Results

On the ERA5 global forecasting benchmark (5.625° resolution, 5 variables), ClimODE achieves latitude-weighted RMSE of 193.4 (z), 1.55 (t), 1.40 (t2m), 2.01 (u10), and 2.04 (v10) at 24-hour lead time, outperforming ClimaX (364.9, 2.17, 2.37, 2.49, 2.48) and FourCastNet (333.0, 1.83, 1.68, 2.33, 2.39) across all variables. The gold-standard IFS model still leads with RMSE of 51.0 (z), 0.87 (t), 1.02 (t2m), 1.11 (u10), and 1.33 (v10) at 24h. Notably, ClimODE achieves these results with only 2.8M parameters, an order of magnitude smaller than ClimaX (107M) and Pangu-Weather (256M), and trains on a single GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ClimaX (107M params, Transformer-based), ClimODE (2.8M params) achieves substantially lower RMSE across all five variables and all lead times — e.g., at 24h, ClimODE scores 193.4 vs 364.9 on geopotential (z) and 1.40 vs 2.37 on ground temperature (t2m). Against FourCastNet (adaptive Fourier neural operators), ClimODE wins on z, t, and t2m at most lead times (e.g., 1.55 vs 1.83 on t at 24h) and is competitive on wind components (u10, v10), where the gap narrows at longer horizons. The physics-based IFS model (ECMWF's operational system) remains the gold standard, outperforming ClimODE on every variable (e.g., 51.0 vs 193.4 on z at 24h), though the paper notes IFS uses far more input variables. In regional forecasting (Table 2), ClimODE dominates on t2m, t, and z across North America, South America, and Australia, while remaining competitive on wind. For longer-horizon predictions (72h, 144h in Table 7), ClimODE maintains an advantage over ClimaX on temperature and geopotential, with wind degrading as expected for both methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

ClimODE departs from the dominant data-driven paradigm in weather forecasting (transformers like ClimaX, GNNs like GraphCast, UNets like FourCastNet) by grounding the model in the physical continuity/advection equation, treating weather evolution as a value-conserving transport process rather than an arbitrary learned mapping. It builds directly on Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018) and second-order ODE formulations (Yildiz et al., 2019), extending them to a spatiotemporal PDE solved via the method of lines over the entire Earth. The hybrid local-convolution + global-attention velocity network and the Gaussian emission model for uncertainty represent practical architectural contributions that distinguish it from prior physics-informed neural ODE work, which has been largely confined to small, non-climate systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The order-of-magnitude parameter reduction (2.8M vs. 37–256M for competing methods) combined with value-conserving dynamics that prevent long-horizon forecast collapse makes this a practically attractive alternative for resource-constrained settings, and the built-in uncertainty quantification addresses a gap in most deep-learning forecasters. However, the evaluation is limited to 5 variables at 5.625° resolution on a single dataset, the model still falls short of the IFS gold standard, and the authors explicitly acknowledge uncertainty about generalization to climate-change regimes. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher resolutions, more atmospheric levels, and coupled ocean-land systems, and whether the conservation inductive bias remains beneficial under non-stationary climate conditions.
