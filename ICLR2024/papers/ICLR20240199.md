## Problem

Despite LoRA's widespread empirical success in parameter-efficient fine-tuning, its theoretical underpinnings remained largely unexplored. Specifically, no prior work characterized the minimum LoRA-rank required to adapt a frozen model to exactly match a target model, how network architecture (depth, width) affects this threshold, or what approximation error results when the rank falls below it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240199/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a frozen model f₀ (with weight matrices W_l ∈ ℝ^{D×D} for l ∈ [L]) and a target model f̄ (with weight matrices W̄_l for l ∈ [L̄]), along with a LoRA-rank R, and produces explicit low-rank adapter matrices ΔW_l (rank ≤ R) and updated bias vectors b̂_l that make the adapted model match the target. The construction is analytical rather than learned: adapters are derived in closed form via SVD-based decomposition of the error matrix E = W̄ − ∏W_l. Results assume both models share the same width D, the frozen model is at least as deep as the target (L ≥ L̄), and the input space is bounded.

## Method

The core mechanism extends the Eckart-Young-Mirsky low-rank approximation theorem from a single matrix to a product of matrices, each subject to an individual low-rank constraint: the 'effective rank' of L layers with rank-R adapters is LR, yielding the threshold R ≥ ⌈D/M⌉ where M = ⌊L/L̄⌋. For ReLU FNNs, nonlinearity is handled by (i) linearizing the first L−1 layers via sufficiently large biases to activate all ReLUs, and (ii) partitioning the L frozen layers into L̄ consecutive groups of M layers, each approximating one target layer. For Transformers, the analysis segments blocks at softmax and ReLU boundaries, showing that LoRA adapters applied only to self-attention weight matrices (W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O) with rank ≥ D/2 suffice for exact adaptation. A mild non-singularity assumption on the frozen model's weight matrices is required, which holds with probability 1 for randomly initialized weights.

## Results

The paper's headline results are theoretical rank thresholds: for FNNs, a uniform LoRA-rank of R ≥ D/M (where M = ⌊L/L̄⌋ is the depth ratio) suffices to exactly adapt any randomly generated frozen model to any target model (Corollary 4), requiring at most 2D²L̄ learnable parameters versus the target's D²L̄. For Transformers, rank-(D/2) adapters applied only to attention weight matrices suffice for exact adaptation (Theorem 7). Experimental validation on D=16 models confirms MSE drops to near-zero at the predicted threshold R = D/M = 8 (for L=4, L̄=2), and pretraining the frozen model further reduces the required rank. The approximation error when R is below the threshold is bounded by the (RM+1)-th singular value of the per-layer error matrix (Theorem 5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against final layers tuning (updating only the last L−1 layers while freezing earlier layers). Lemma 4 proves a strict theoretical separation: for any randomly generated L-layer FNN (L ≥ 2), no tuning of the final L−1 layers can make the model equal to even a one-layer target FNN, whereas LoRA with rank R ≥ D/M achieves exact adaptation using at most 2D²L̄ learnable parameters. Concretely, when L ≥ 3, LoRA requires at most 2D² parameters for exact approximation while final layers tuning fails entirely despite having (L−1)D² free parameters. Empirically (Sec. G.4), this theoretical gap is confirmed: on classification tasks, LoRA consistently outperforms final layers tuning, particularly when the frozen model is randomly initialized rather than pretrained. The paper also notes that for linear models, the constructed LoRA adapters achieve the same MSE as gradient-based optimization (validating Lemma 1's optimality), while for multi-layer FNNs the gradient method slightly outperforms the theoretical construction in the low-rank regime, suggesting room for improved adapter initialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical Eckart-Young-Mirsky optimal low-rank approximation theorem from a single matrix to a product of matrices, each subject to individual low-rank constraints, establishing a foundational matrix decomposition result (Lemma 1) that underpins all subsequent findings. It borrows the linearization-via-large-biases technique from Giannou et al. (2023a) to bridge from linear to ReLU networks, and positions itself against the broader expressive power literature (Hornik, Eldan & Shamir, Yun et al.) by noting those frameworks cannot account for frozen pretrained parameters or specific adaptation methods. The paper explicitly identifies a gap: while expressive power of unfrozen networks and a few other adaptation methods (norm-layer tuning, input reprogramming) have been studied, no prior work has characterized the expressive power of LoRA, the dominant PEFT method in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical characterization of when and why LoRA works, providing concrete rank thresholds (e.g., rank ≥ width × depth_ratio for FNNs, rank ≥ embedding_size/2 for Transformers) that directly inform hyperparameter selection. The near-optimality result—that LoRA's effective expressive power is within a constant factor of 2 of the target model's parameter count despite severe low-rank constraints—offers a principled explanation for LoRA's empirical success. Key limitations include the assumption of equal model widths, bounded input spaces, and exclusion of skip connections in the TFN analysis; open questions include deriving approximation error bounds for TFNs at sub-threshold ranks, understanding optimization dynamics of the constructed adapters, and extending results to architectures with varying layer dimensions.
