## Problem

Battery degradation modeling sits at the intersection of electrochemistry and machine learning, but no unified standard exists for data formats, feature representations, or evaluation benchmarks. ML experts struggle to craft effective feature spaces from heterogeneous, high-dimensional battery data, while battery researchers face barriers in adapting complex models to their specific datasets. The absence of a cohesive platform makes cross-dataset comparison and reproducible research difficult.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240592/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

BatteryML ingests raw cycling records from diverse battery cycler systems (Arbin LBT, etc.) in varying formats and harmonizes them into a unified BatteryData structure comprising cell-level meta information (chemistry, nominal capacity, voltage thresholds) and a list of charge/discharge cycles with time-series signals (voltage, current, temperature, internal resistance). Features are extracted as PyTorch tensors—within-cycle signals like QdLinear curves and Coulombic efficiency, plus between-cycle aggregates like variance of ΔQdLinear—making them directly consumable by both scikit-learn and deep learning frameworks. Outputs are per-cell RUL (cycles until SOH falls below a configurable threshold, e.g. 80%), per-cycle SOH (discharge capacity / nominal capacity × 100%), and per-timestep SOC (remaining capacity / full capacity × 100%).

## Method

BatteryML is a modular, configuration-driven pipeline that decouples domain knowledge from model architecture through five pluggable stages: train-test split, automatic label annotation, feature extraction, data transformation, and model training. The unified BatteryData representation standardizes all public datasets (CALCE, MATR, HUST, HNEI, RWTH, SNL, UL_PUR) into a common tensor format, enabling cross-dataset training and transfer learning. Feature engineering supports both handcrafted domain-expert features (Variance, Discharge, Full models from Severson et al.) and raw QdLinear curves for end-to-end learning, while label annotation automatically computes RUL, SOH, and SOC targets by traversing each cycle's charge/discharge stages. The model module integrates a broad spectrum of architectures—linear and regularized regression, Gaussian processes, tree ensembles (XGBoost, LightGBM, Random Forest), and neural networks (MLP, CNN, LSTM, Transformer)—all trained with 10 random seeds for robustness. The key design insight is that battery experts can contribute new features or labels without touching model code, and ML experts can swap architectures without understanding electrochemistry, with a single YAML configuration file orchestrating the entire pipeline.

## Results

On the primary RUL prediction task (Table 2, RMSE in cycles), no single model dominates across all 8 datasets. PCR achieves the best RUL RMSE of 90 on MATR1 and PLSR achieves 60 on CRUH, while XGBoost leads on the diverse MIX dataset with RMSE 205 (vs. 573 for the dummy baseline). Neural network models (CNN, LSTM, Transformer, MLP) consistently underperform, with CNN reaching RMSE 10,294 on MATR1 and 102,940 on MATR2. For SOH and SOC estimation, LightGBM and Random Forest establish the strongest baselines, with LightGBM achieving RMSE 0.284 on HUST (SOC) and 0.17 on RWTH (SOH), while deep models (LSTM, GRU, MLP) lag by 1–2 orders of magnitude.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Severson et al. (2019) domain-expert models (Variance, Discharge, Full) perform competitively on single-chemistry LFP datasets (e.g., Discharge achieves RMSE 149 on MATR2, Full achieves 138 on CLO) but degrade severely on diverse multi-chemistry datasets like CRUSH and MIX (>1000 RMSE), revealing limited generalizability of handcrafted features. Traditional statistical models (Ridge, PCR, PLSR) excel on homogeneous datasets—PCR hits 90 RMSE on MATR1 and PLSR hits 60 on CRUH—but fail on HUST and CRUSH where linear assumptions break down. XGBoost is the standout on heterogeneous datasets, achieving the best RMSE of 205 on MIX and 330 on CRUSH, while Random Forest is highly unstable (RMSE 53,225 on SNL). Neural network baselines (MLP, CNN, LSTM, Transformer) consistently underperform all traditional methods across nearly every dataset, with CNN and LSTM producing errors 10–100× larger than the best statistical models, and exhibiting high seed-to-seed variance (e.g., CNN ranges from 60 to 367 RMSE across 10 seeds on MATR1). For SOH and SOC tasks, LightGBM and Random Forest dominate, with LightGBM achieving the lowest RMSE on 6 of 7 datasets for SOC (e.g., 0.284 on HUST, 0.763 on HNEI), while deep models (LSTM, GRU, MLP) are 1–2 orders of magnitude worse, confirming that tree-based methods remain the state-of-the-art for battery state estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

BatteryML carves new ground as a unifying platform rather than building on a single prior method. It draws conceptual inspiration from BEEP (Herring et al., 2020) for open-source battery data handling but explicitly departs by decoupling domain knowledge from ML expertise through a modular pipeline and unified data representation (BatteryData). The model baselines it integrates—Severson et al.'s Variance/Discharge/Full linear models, standard scikit-learn regressors, and PyTorch neural networks—are repurposed from their original single-dataset contexts into a common evaluation framework, which no prior tool had provided.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The platform addresses a genuine standardization gap: without a common data format and evaluation protocol, cross-dataset comparisons in battery degradation research have been nearly impossible. The empirical finding that tree-based models (LightGBM, XGBoost) consistently outperform deep learning architectures (CNN, LSTM, Transformer) on SOH and SOC tasks, and that neural networks exhibit extreme seed-to-seed variance on RUL, is a sobering signal that the field's deep learning ambitions are not yet realized. Open questions include whether larger-scale pretraining, better feature engineering, or multi-task learning can close this gap, and whether the unified benchmark will catalyze the kind of rapid iteration that standardized benchmarks have enabled in other domains.
