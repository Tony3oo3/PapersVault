## Problem

In cooperative MARL with shared policy parameters, agents tend to converge to homogeneous behaviors, hindering diversified exploration and sophisticated coordination. Existing role-based methods either require pre-defined role responsibilities, fix role assignments during training, or relax the CTDE constraint by introducing communication at execution time. The paper asks how to learn compact, dynamic role representations from agent behavior patterns that simultaneously promote heterogeneity, enable knowledge transfer, and guide skillful coordination—all within the CTDE paradigm.

## Input / Output

Each agent consumes its local observation o_i, previous action a_{i-1}, and a running GRU hidden state to produce a 128-dim agent embedding; the global state s is separately encoded into a 64-dim state embedding via a second GRU. The role encoder maps the 128-dim embedding to a 64-dim role representation, and a 4-head attention module (128-dim embedding, 64-dim output) fuses the state embedding with all n role representations. Outputs are individual Q-values per agent, a monotonic joint Q-value from the mixing network, and the learned role representations that drive both the contrastive loss and the attention-guided credit assignment.

## Method

ACORM has two coupled modules. First, contrastive role representation learning formalizes role encoding as mutual information maximization I(z; M) between the role and its representation, then derives a tractable InfoNCE lower bound (Theorem 1). Agent trajectories are encoded by a shared GRU into 128-dim embeddings; every T_cl steps, K-means partitions all n agents into K clusters, and same-cluster agents form positive pairs while cross-cluster agents form negative pairs in a bilinear-score contrastive loss. A MoCo-style momentum update (θ_k ← βθ_k + (1−β)θ_q) stabilizes the key encoder. Second, attention-guided role coordination inserts a multi-head attention layer into the QMIX mixing network: the GRU-encoded global state serves as the query, and the n role representations serve as both keys and values, so the mixing weights are conditioned on which roles are most relevant to the current state. This yields more expressive credit assignment without any communication at execution time, and the entire framework is plug-and-play on top of QMIX or MAPPO.

## Results

ACORM is evaluated on SMAC (StarCraft II micromanagement) and GRF (Google Research Football) using test win rate as the primary metric. On SMAC, ACORM achieves the best performance on all super-hard maps (MMM2, corridor, 3s5z_vs_3s6z) and most other maps, with the largest margins over baselines on super-hard tasks that demand high behavior diversity and coordination. ACORM also exhibits the lowest variance in learning curves, indicating enhanced training stability. On GRF, ACORM shows a significantly faster increase in test win rate compared to QMIX, particularly within the first 1M training steps, confirming its advantage on harder coordination tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACORM outperforms all seven baselines (QMIX, RODE, EOI, MACC, CDS, CIA, GoMARL) on the majority of six representative SMAC maps, with the most pronounced gains on super-hard maps (MMM2, corridor, 3s5z_vs_3s6z) where behavior diversity and skillful coordination are most critical. The ablation study confirms that both the contrastive learning module and the attention mechanism contribute independently: removing either degrades performance but still beats QMIX, while removing both (ACORM_w/o_MHA Vanilla) closely matches QMIX. On GRF, ACORM substantially outperforms QMIX and CDS, though comparisons with RODE, EOI, MACC, and CIA are limited to 2M steps due to time constraints. In the MAPPO-based variant (Appendix D), ACORM again achieves the largest performance gains over MAPPO on super-hard maps, consistent with the QMIX-based results. The paper notes that ACORM's advantage scales with task difficulty, suggesting the role representation and attention-guided credit assignment are most beneficial when coordination complexity is high.

## Relation to Prior Work

ACORM builds on the CTDE value-decomposition paradigm (QMIX, MAPPO) and repurposes two well-established representation-learning tools—InfoNCE-style contrastive learning and multi-head attention—into a unified framework for role-aware MARL. It departs from prior role-based methods (ROMA, RODE, COPA) that either fix role-action mappings, require domain knowledge, or relax the CTDE constraint by introducing execution-time communication, and from agent-heterogeneity methods (CDS, EOI, CIA, GoMARL) that promote individuality without modeling dynamic team composition. The key conceptual departure is formalizing role representation learning as mutual information maximization between a latent role and its embedding, then deriving a tractable contrastive objective with K-means-based negative pair generation and MoCo-style momentum updates, while using attention in the mixing network to condition credit assignment on learned role patterns rather than raw global state alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled, CTDE-compatible mechanism for dynamic role assignment that simultaneously promotes behavior heterogeneity, enables implicit knowledge transfer among agents with similar roles, and yields more expressive credit assignment—addressing a persistent weakness of parameter-sharing MARL. Limitations include the need for a pre-defined number of clusters K, reliance on an explicit K-means step for negative pair construction, and the absence of history-trajectory exploitation for role extraction; extending the framework to offline settings and removing the fixed-K requirement are identified as open directions. For practitioners building cooperative MARL systems on top of QMIX or MAPPO, the modular contrastive-role + attention-credit-assignment design is a drop-in enhancement that could be adopted with modest engineering effort.
