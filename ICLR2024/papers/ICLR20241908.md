## Problem

Diversification methods (DivDis, D-BAT) achieve state-of-the-art OOD generalization under spurious correlations by finding multiple diverse hypotheses, but the key factors driving their success remain unclear. This paper identifies that these methods are highly sensitive to the unlabeled data distribution, the choice of learning algorithm (architecture and pretraining), and that these two choices are co-dependent—yet none of these dependencies were previously characterized.

## Input / Output

The diversification methods consume labeled training data (where spurious features are fully correlated with true labels) plus additional unlabeled data whose spurious ratio r_Du controls how much the spurious feature correlates with the true label. The output is a set of K diverse hypotheses (typically K=2), each a full classifier (e.g., ResNet, ViT, MLP, or LeNet) that fits the training data but makes different predictions on the unlabeled data. A final disambiguation step selects one hypothesis for deployment, and the paper assumes an oracle selector to isolate the diversification stage.

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical and empirical dissection of two representative diversification methods—D-BAT (agreement-based loss, sequential training) and DivDis (mutual-information-based loss, simultaneous training). Proposition 1 proves that D-BAT recovers the ground-truth hypothesis only when the unlabeled data is inversely correlated (r_Du=0), while DivDis recovers it only when the unlabeled data is balanced (r_Du=0.5), with up to 30% accuracy drops away from these sweet spots. Proposition 2, using Hadamard codes from coding theory, proves that even 2|D_ood|−1 pairwise-diverse hypotheses can all have at most random-chance OOD accuracy, establishing that diversification alone is insufficient without aligned inductive biases. Empirically, the paper shows a 10–20% accuracy gap between best and second-best architecture/pretraining choices on Waterbirds-CC and Office-Home, demonstrates co-dependence by constructing adversarial splits where one architecture achieves 89% while the other drops to 56% under a fixed training set, and confirms that increasing K beyond 2 yields no meaningful improvement.

## Results

The paper's key quantitative findings concern sensitivity rather than absolute performance: diversification methods suffer up to 30% absolute accuracy drop when the spurious ratio of unlabeled data deviates from a method-specific sweet spot (D-BAT optimal at r_Du=0, DivDis optimal at r_Du=0.5). The choice of learning algorithm (architecture + pretraining) causes up to 20% absolute accuracy drop between the best and second-best model on Waterbirds-CC and Office-Home. Critically, increasing the number of diverse hypotheses K from 2 to 5 yields no meaningful improvement (e.g., D-BAT on Waterbirds-CC stays at 57.1% across all K values), confirming that diversification alone cannot compensate for suboptimal model or data choices.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DivDis (Lee et al., 2023) and D-BAT (Pagliardini et al., 2023) as the two representative diversification methods. Neither method is universally superior: D-BAT achieves the best test accuracy when the unlabeled data is inversely correlated (r_Du=0), while DivDis(-Seq) is optimal when the unlabeled data is balanced (r_Du=0.5), with both suffering up to 30% absolute accuracy drop away from their respective sweet spots on MNIST-CIFAR-10 and MNIST-Fashion-MNIST. On Waterbirds-CC, D-BAT with ViT-B/16 ImageNet pretraining reaches 57.1% worst-group accuracy versus 49.4% for DivDis with MoCo-v2 (K=2), a gap that persists even when K is increased to 5. On Office-Home, D-BAT with ViT-MAE achieves 61.9% versus 55.9% for DivDis with ResNet50 ImageNet pretraining. The paper further shows that no single architecture-pretraining pair dominates across both datasets, and that ImageNet-1k top-1 accuracy is an unreliable proxy for OOD diversification performance (e.g., ViT-MAE, the best ImageNet model, underperforms in all diversification settings).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a diagnostic study of two recently proposed diversification methods for OOD generalization under spurious correlations: DivDis (Lee et al., 2023) and D-BAT (Pagliardini et al., 2023). Rather than proposing a new method, it systematically isolates and analyzes the key design factors—unlabeled data distribution, diversification loss choice, and learning algorithm (architecture + pretraining)—that determine whether these methods succeed. It draws on the agreement score and adversarial split framework from Task Discovery (Atanov et al., 2022) to formalize the co-dependence between architecture and unlabeled data, and uses coding theory (Hadamard codes) to prove that diversification alone is fundamentally insufficient for OOD generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable guidance for practitioners: the spurious ratio of unlabeled data must match the diversification loss (D-BAT needs inversely correlated data, DivDis needs balanced data), and the architecture/pretraining choice matters as much as the diversification mechanism itself. A key open question is how to design diversification methods that are robust to these sensitivities without expensive per-dataset model search. Limitations include the co-dependence experiment being demonstrated only with D-BAT, the focus on image classification, and the characterization of OOD data solely through spurious ratio, leaving other distributional properties unexplored.
