## Problem

It is known that learning shallow fully-connected neural networks via gradient descent (captured by the correlational statistical query model) has exponential complexity in the input dimension. However, it remained open whether incorporating known problem symmetries—such as permutation invariance for graphs or translation invariance for images—provides a restrictive enough subclass of functions to circumvent these impossibility results. This paper answers in the negative, showing that equivariant architectures remain exponentially hard to learn.

## Input / Output

The paper considers multiple input settings: Boolean adjacency matrices A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} (Erdős-Rényi graphs with p=0.5) with trivial node features x=1, Gaussian input matrices X ∈ R^{n×d} for fixed or random graphs, and Gaussian vectors for frame-averaged CNNs. The 'output' is not a prediction but a complexity certificate: lower bounds on the number of SQ or CSQ queries (or the required query precision) needed to learn the target function class to constant error, plus an NP-hardness reduction for proper GNN training. The hard function classes are Boolean-valued (parity of degree counts) or real-valued (symmetrized ReLU networks), with the key constraint that they are invariant under the relevant symmetry group.

## Method

The paper constructs hard function classes in four settings. For GNNs on Erdős-Rényi graphs, it defines Boolean functions g_{S,b}(A) = b + Σ_{i∈S} [c_A]_i mod 2 (parity over degree-count entries) and proves exponential SQ hardness via pairwise independence, leveraging concentration of binomial degree distributions. For feature-dimension hardness, it symmetrizes the hard functions of Diakonikolas et al. (2020) by composing them with a set of nearly-orthogonal matrices B ∈ R^{d×2}, showing that invariant Hermite polynomial structure preserves the near-orthogonality needed for CSQ lower bounds. For frame-averaged CNNs, it constructs a reduced set B' of orthogonal matrices (losing a |G|² factor) such that both B(B')^T and gB(g'B')^T remain small for distinct elements, then applies the same Hermite-polynomial orthogonality argument. A complementary result extends the GROWING-BASIS algorithm to invariant polynomial rings, yielding an SQ/CSQ separation where CSQ requires Ω(r^d) queries but SQ needs only O(kr² log r) queries for degree-d, k-sparse invariant polynomials.

## Results

The paper establishes that learning equivariant neural networks via gradient descent (captured by CSQ/SQ algorithms) remains exponentially hard even with symmetry constraints. Key lower bounds include: (1) Ω(τ² exp(n^{Ω(1)})) SQ queries for learning single-message-passing GNNs on Erdős-Rényi graphs (Theorem 3), (2) 2^{d^{Ω(1)}} CSQ queries for one-hidden-layer GNNs in feature dimension d (Theorem 4), and (3) 2^{n^{Ω(1)}}/|G|² CSQ queries for frame-averaged networks over polynomial-sized permutation groups (Theorem 7). Experiments confirm that overparameterized GNNs (n=15 nodes) fail to fit even 225 training graphs, and CNNs (n=50) overfit 500 training samples without generalizing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical lower-bound paper, so there are no direct method-vs-method comparisons. Instead, the paper extends prior hardness results to symmetric settings and quantifies how much symmetry helps. The GNN feature-dimension hardness (Theorem 4) directly extends Diakonikolas et al. (2020) via symmetrization of their hard function family, showing the exponential 2^{d^{Ω(1)}} scaling is preserved. The frame-averaged network hardness (Theorem 7) extends Goel et al. (2020) and Diakonikolas et al. (2020), with the key finding that imposing symmetry over a group G reduces the number of hard functions by at most a factor of |G|², leaving exponential hardness intact. The invariant polynomial separation (Lemma 9) extends Andoni et al. (2014)'s GROWING-BASIS algorithm, showing CSQ requires Ω(r^d) queries while SQ needs only O(kr² log r) queries. The NP-hardness result (Proposition 5) extends Blum & Rivest (1988) from 3-node feedforward networks to GNNs via a reduction to learning halfspaces with noise. The central takeaway is that symmetry provides at most a polynomial (group-size) reduction in query complexity, insufficient to overcome the fundamental exponential hardness of learning neural networks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of computational hardness results for learning neural networks (Blum & Rivest 1988; Goel et al. 2020; Diakonikolas et al. 2020) to the setting of equivariant and symmetric architectures. The key technical building blocks are the exponential CSQ lower bounds for shallow ReLU networks from Diakonikolas et al. (2020), the superpolynomial parity-based constructions from Goel et al. (2020), and the GROWING-BASIS algorithm from Andoni et al. (2014), all of which are adapted to symmetric function classes via symmetrization or custom constructions. The paper explicitly positions itself as filling a gap: while sample complexity and generalization benefits of symmetry are well-studied, the effect of symmetry on computational complexity of learning had not been previously analyzed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central message—that equivariance alone does not rescue gradient descent from exponential hardness—sets a clear boundary for theoretical work on geometric deep learning: additional structural assumptions beyond symmetry are needed for provable efficient learnability. The paper also provides a constructive separation (Sec. 6) showing that invariant polynomials are learnable by non-CSQ algorithms (GROWING-BASIS) but not by gradient descent, highlighting a concrete algorithmic gap. Open questions include extending hardness to continuous groups, cryptographic hardness for symmetric networks, and whether low-degree or overlap-gap barriers apply to geometric learning problems. The empirical verification is limited to small instances (n=15 for GNNs, n=50 for CNNs), so the practical relevance at scale remains to be confirmed.
