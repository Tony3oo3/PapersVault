## Problem

Deep RL agents operate in environments with inherent distribution shifts—new states are progressively uncovered (input drift) and reward signals change as the policy improves (output drift)—yet current architectures rely on static activation functions that cannot adapt to these shifts. The paper argues that neural plasticity at the individual-neuron level is a critical but underexploited mechanism for handling such dynamics, and that rational activation functions (ratios of polynomials) are particularly well-suited to provide this adaptability because they can converge to any continuous function and have been proven to be better approximants than polynomials.

## Input / Output

The network consumes 4 consecutive grayscale Atari 2600 frames downsampled to 84×84 pixels (a 4-channel tensor) and outputs Q-values for each discrete action. Beyond the standard Q-value head, the method produces learned rational activation parameters: 10 coefficients per layer (5 numerator + 4 denominator) for full rational plasticity, or a single shared set of 10 coefficients across all layers for the regularised joint-rational variant. The rational functions are of order (m,n) = (5,4) and use a 'safe' formulation with the absolute value of the denominator sum to guarantee continuity and avoid poles during training.

## Method

The paper replaces fixed activation functions in DQN's convolutional network with parametric rational functions R(x) = P(x)/Q(x), where P and Q are polynomials with learnable real coefficients, initialized to approximate Leaky ReLU and trained end-to-end alongside all other weights. A key theoretical contribution proves that rational functions with strictly higher numerator degree than denominator degree (m > n) are closed under residual connections, meaning they dynamically embed a residual path—termed 'residual plasticity'—without requiring explicit skip connections. To prevent overfitting in stationary environments, the authors introduce a joint-rational variant that constrains all layers to share a single set of rational parameters, providing natural regularisation inspired by weight-sharing in ResNets. Empirically, DQN with (joint-)rational plasticity outperforms rigid DQN, DDQN, and even Rainbow on most Atari 2600 games while adding only 10 parameters, and substantially reduces the Q-value overestimation problem that plagues standard DQN.

## Results

On 15 Atari 2600 games, DQN with rational activation plasticity achieves super-human performance on 11/15 games, compared to only 3/15 for DQN with Leaky ReLU and 2/15 for DDQN. The regularised joint-rational variant wins the highest mean score on 9/15 games and also reaches super-human on 11/15. On progressive environments (Jamesbond, Seaquest, Time Pilot), rational DQN scores 308, 16.3, and 341 (human-normalised) versus 8.6, 0.57, and 14.9 for rigid DQN-LReLU. DQN with rational plasticity outperforms the much more computationally expensive Rainbow algorithm on 7 of 8 tested games.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DQN with rational plasticity outperforms the original DQN-LReLU baseline (Mnih et al., 2015) on all 15 Atari games, with the largest gaps on progressive environments (e.g., 341 vs. 14.9 on Time Pilot, 308 vs. 8.6 on Jamesbond). Against DDQN (van Hasselt et al., 2016), rational DQN wins on every game and reduces relative overestimation as much or further than DDQN's dual-network mechanism, while DDQN's performance drops are merely delayed rather than prevented. The SiLU/dSiLU baselines (Elfwing et al., 2018), specifically designed for RL, do not outperform Leaky ReLU in this paper's setup and are consistently beaten by rational plasticity. PELU (Trottier et al., 2017), a learnable activation with 12 parameters, is outperformed by the joint-rational variant which uses only 10 shared parameters across all layers. Against Rainbow (Hessel et al., 2018), which is ~8× slower and more memory-intensive, DQN with rational plasticity dominates on 7 of 8 tested games, with Rainbow winning only on Enduro. CReLU (Shang et al., 2016), used to address plasticity loss in prior work, is outmatched by rational plasticity on all 9 games in the ablation (Fig. 2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Padé (rational) activation functions of Molina et al. (2020) from supervised learning into deep RL, arguing that their adaptability provides the 'neural plasticity' that RL agents need to handle distribution shifts. It builds on the DQN architecture of Mnih et al. (2015) and the residual connection concept of He et al. (2016), deriving a theorem that rational functions with numerator degree exceeding denominator degree are closed under residual connections—what the authors term 'residual plasticity.' The joint-rational variant draws on weight-sharing ideas from Highway Networks and ResNets (Greff et al., 2017; Liao & Poggio, 2016) to regularise plasticity. Conceptually, the paper positions itself against the broader line of learnable activation functions (PELU, CReLU, SiLU) and plasticity-injection methods (Nikishin et al., 2022; Lyle et al., 2022; Dohare et al., 2023), arguing that rational activations uniquely combine parameter adaptability with implicit residual structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that adding 10 learnable parameters (rational activations) to a vanilla DQN makes it competitive with Rainbow, which bundles six algorithmic improvements and is ~8× slower—suggesting architectural plasticity may be undervalued relative to algorithmic complexity in deep RL. The finding that rational plasticity also reduces Q-value overestimation as much as DDQN's second network is a useful mechanistic insight. However, the Rainbow comparison rests on single runs, the scope is limited to Atari 2600 and DQN-family algorithms, and the paper does not explore integration with more modern RL methods (SAC, PPO, world models). Open questions include automatic selection of rational degrees, scaling to continuous control or language-based RL, and whether rational plasticity subsumes other plasticity-injection techniques.
