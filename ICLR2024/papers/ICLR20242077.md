## Problem

GFlowNets require an explicit scalar reward function to sample candidates proportionally, which is infeasible for multi-objective optimization (where no natural scalarization exists) and costly when evaluating exact objective values is expensive. Additionally, the conventional practice of raising the reward to an exponent β to prioritize high-reward candidates is highly environment-dependent, with no principled way to choose the optimal β. The paper asks whether GFlowNets can be trained using only ordering information among candidates, eliminating the need for an explicit reward formulation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only pairwise ordering relations (for single-objective) or Pareto dominance relations (for multi-objective) among terminal states, plus the underlying MDP structure (state space, action space, transition graph). It does not require the exact objective values, making it applicable when only comparisons are feasible (e.g., using a cheap proxy like epoch-12 accuracy to order architectures rather than the expensive epoch-200 accuracy). The output is a trained GFlowNet sampler that generates composite objects (strings, graphs, sequences) with probabilities proportional to a learned order-preserving reward, which can also serve as a proxy to boost sampling efficiency by pre-filtering candidates before expensive evaluation.

## Method

The core contribution is an order-preserving loss defined as the KL divergence between a labeling distribution (uniform over Pareto-optimal/non-dominated candidates in a batch) and the GFlowNet's induced distribution over the same batch. For single-objective tasks, this reduces to pairwise comparison; for multi-objective tasks, it uses Pareto dominance to define the non-dominated set. The loss is combined with standard GFlowNet MDP constraints (trajectory balance, detailed balance, flow matching, or subtrajectory balance) via a weighting hyperparameter. Theoretically, the paper proves that the learned reward converges to a piecewise geometric progression over the objective ranking, with the ratio between adjacent ranks driven to infinity during training—this sparsification naturally transitions the sampler from broad exploration (near-uniform early reward) to focused exploitation (reward concentrated on top candidates). The method is parametrization-agnostic and requires no explicit reward exponent β, no scalarization for multi-objective tasks, and no manual tuning of exploration-exploitation tradeoffs.

## Results

On molecular design tasks (Bag, QM9, sEH, TFBind8, TFBind10), OP-TB outperforms all 9 baselines (MaxEnt, TB, DB, subTB, GTB, MARS, A2C, SQL, PPO) in both the number of distinct optimal candidates discovered and the average top-100 objective value. On NATS-Bench (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet-16-120), OP-TB consistently achieves higher best test accuracy at both epoch 12 and epoch 200 compared to TB, REA, BOHB, and REINFORCE, with the largest gains in early training stages. In multi-objective HyperGrid, OP-GFNs achieve near-zero IGD+ and smaller averaged Hausdorff distance to the true Pareto front, with higher PC-entropy (uniformity), particularly on non-convex fronts like currin-shubert. The method also outperforms GFN-β across all tested exponents (β=4–128) in NAS, confirming that the learned sparsification automatically adapts to the reward landscape.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OP-TB wins against all standard GFlowNet objectives (TB, DB, subTB, MaxEnt, GTB) on molecular design, finding more distinct optimal candidates and achieving higher top-100 objective values across Bag, QM9, sEH, TFBind8, and TFBind10. Against RL-based methods (A2C, SQL, PPO) and the sampling-based MARS, OP-TB also dominates on both diversity (number of optimal candidates found) and quality (top-k objective). In NAS, OP-TB outperforms evolutionary (REA), HPO (BOHB), and RL (REINFORCE) multi-trial baselines on all three datasets, with the advantage most pronounced in early training where fewer T&T seconds have been spent. In multi-objective settings, OP-GFNs beat preference-conditioned GFNs (PC-GFNs, Jain et al. 2023) on IGD+ and Hausdorff distance, and match or slightly exceed goal-conditioned GFNs (GC-GFNs, Roy et al. 2023) on fragment-based molecule generation and DNA sequence design, while requiring no scalarization or goal specification. The key differentiator is that OP-GFNs need only pairwise ordering (or Pareto dominance) rather than explicit reward values, making them applicable when evaluation is costly or multi-objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GFlowNet framework (Bengio et al., 2021a,b; Malkin et al., 2022) by replacing the requirement for an explicit scalar reward R(x) with a learned reward that only needs to be consistent with a (partial) order over candidates. It departs from prior multi-objective GFlowNet approaches (PC-GFNs, GC-GFNs) that require scalarization of the objective vector, and from the GFN-β practice of raising the reward to a manually tuned exponent, by showing that the order-preserving loss automatically sparsifies the reward landscape during training. The method is compatible with all existing GFlowNet parametrizations (TB, DB, subTB, FM) and builds on the sequence prepend/append MDP formulation from Shen et al. (2023) for molecular and NAS tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical impact is eliminating two pain points in GFlowNet-based optimization: the need to choose a reward exponent β (which is environment-dependent and hard to tune) and the need to scalarize multi-objective functions (which fails on non-convex Pareto fronts). The theoretical guarantee that the learned reward is a piecewise geometric progression with automatic exploration-to-exploitation transition is a clean result, but its practical benefit is bounded by the GFlowNet framework's own limitations. Open questions include how to prevent collapse to a subset of the Pareto front without the current replay-buffer heuristic, and whether the ordering-only signal is sufficient when the objective landscape is highly non-uniform or when the MDP has very long trajectories.
