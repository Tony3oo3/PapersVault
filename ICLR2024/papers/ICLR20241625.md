## Problem

Existing evaluation-of-OPE metrics (MSE, RankCorr, Regret) measure only the 'accuracy' of OPE estimators or downstream policy selection, but fail to capture the risk-return tradeoff experienced when deploying the top-k selected policies in online A/B tests. In particular, they cannot distinguish between an estimator that underestimates near-optimal policies and one that overestimates poor-performing policies, which have fundamentally different risk profiles during deployment. This paper asks: how should we evaluate OPE estimators to reflect the actual risk and return of the policy portfolios they produce?

## Input / Output

The metric takes as input a logged dataset D generated by a behavior policy π_b, a set of candidate policies Π, an OPE estimator Ĵ, and a predefined online evaluation budget k. The behavior policy is always included as a candidate (serving as the risk-free baseline), ensuring the metric is non-negative. The output is a single scalar SharpeRatio@k(Ĵ) per (estimator, k) pair, but the full evaluation requires reporting the metric across a range of k values (e.g., k=1 through k=10) to reveal how estimator efficiency shifts with the online evaluation budget.

## Method

SharpeRatio@k adapts the financial Sharpe ratio to OPE by treating the top-k policies selected by an estimator as a 'policy portfolio.' The numerator (return) is best@k(Ĵ) − J(π_b), the value of the highest-performing policy in the portfolio relative to the behavior policy baseline; the denominator (risk) is std@k(Ĵ), the standard deviation of true policy values across the portfolio. This formulation penalizes estimators that include underperforming policies in their top-k selection (high variance) while rewarding those that identify genuinely superior policies (high return). The paper benchmarks five estimators (DM, PDIS, DR, MIS, MDR) across seven Gym environments using SCOPE-RL, an open-source library where the metric is integrated, revealing that the 'best' estimator varies substantially with k and the behavior policy's effectiveness—information invisible to conventional accuracy metrics.

## Results

Across 7 RL environments (Reacher, InvertedPendulum, Hopper, Swimmer, CartPole, MountainCar, Acrobot) and 10 random seeds, SharpeRatio@5 shows strong Spearman rank correlation with RankCorr in Reacher (0.81), Hopper (0.70), Swimmer (0.79), and CartPole (0.71), but weak or negative correlation in InvertedPendulum (0.18), MountainCar (0.57), and Acrobot (0.38). Critically, SharpeRatio@5 disagrees with conventional metrics on the identity of the best estimator in 36/70 trials vs. RankCorr, 42/70 vs. nMSE, and 59/70 vs. nRegret. In the MountainCar case study, MSE and Regret identify MIS as the best estimator, while RankCorr and SharpeRatio@7 favor DM, and SharpeRatio@4 selects PDIS—demonstrating that the 'best' estimator depends fundamentally on whether risk is accounted for.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SharpeRatio@k against three conventional OPE evaluation metrics: MSE (Voloshin et al., 2019), RankCorr (Fu et al., 2021), and Regret (Doroudi et al., 2017). SharpeRatio@k does not 'win' or 'lose' in the traditional sense—it is a new metric that reveals information the baselines structurally cannot capture. In the toy example (Figure 2), estimators X and Y receive identical scores on all three baselines (MSE=11.3, RankCorr=0.413, Regret@1=0.0), yet SharpeRatio@k correctly identifies X as lower-risk than Y. In the conservative-vs-random scenario (Figure 4), all three baselines give nearly identical scores to estimators W and Z, while SharpeRatio@k differentiates them based on the behavior policy's effectiveness. In the full benchmark (Table 1), SharpeRatio@5 most frequently disagrees with nRegret (59/70 trials) and nMSE (42/70), indicating that accuracy-focused metrics systematically overlook risk. The strongest alignment is with RankCorr in Reacher (0.81) and Hopper (0.83 for nMSE), but even there the best-estimator selection diverges in 7–9 out of 10 trials.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the Sharpe Ratio from financial portfolio management (Sharpe, 1998) to the OPE domain, conceptualizing the top-k policies selected by an estimator as a 'policy portfolio' whose efficiency is measured by return over risk. It departs from the established line of OPE evaluation metrics (MSE, RankCorr, Regret) that focus exclusively on estimation accuracy or best-policy selection, arguing these are blind to the risk of deploying suboptimal policies in downstream A/B tests. The work also extends the observation by Kurenkov & Kolesnikov (2022) that preferred algorithms vary with online evaluation budget, but shifts focus from comparing offline RL methods to evaluating OPE estimators themselves, and explicitly incorporates risk rather than only expected return.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and previously unaddressed gap: existing OPE metrics can assign identical scores to estimators with fundamentally different risk profiles (e.g., one underestimating good policies vs. one overestimating bad ones), which matters in safety-critical deployment scenarios. The benchmark results showing that the 'best' estimator changes depending on the metric (MIS under MSE/Regret, DM under RankCorr, PDIS under SharpeRatio@4) suggest that the OPE community may have been selecting suboptimal estimators for practical deployment. Open questions include whether SharpeRatio@k generalizes to more complex, real-world environments, whether new estimators can be designed to explicitly optimize risk-return tradeoff, and how to adaptively select the most efficient estimator given a specific budget and behavior policy.
