## Problem

Hyperbolic classifiers for hierarchical data typically rely on Riemannian optimization, numerically unstable exponential/logarithmic maps, or quadratically scaling pairwise comparisons, making them slow and less interpretable than their Euclidean counterparts. Decision trees and random forests—fast, interpretable, and simple in Euclidean space—had no principled extension to hyperbolic embeddings that preserved constant-time decision complexity while respecting the underlying geometry.

## Input / Output

The method consumes points in hyperboloid (Minkowski) model coordinates with associated class labels for supervised training, and outputs a predicted class label (or regression value). Each split decision requires only two coordinates of the input point (the timelike x₀ and one spatial x_d) plus a learned rotation angle θ, yielding an O(1) evaluation per node. The implementation follows scikit-learn API conventions, accepting standard hyperbolic embedding arrays and supporting both classification and regression objectives.

## Method

HYPERDT reframes Euclidean CART splits as inner-product comparisons against a normal vector, then replaces axis-parallel hyperplanes with homogeneous hyperplanes (planes through the origin) that intersect the hyperboloid as geodesic submanifolds, preserving convexity and topological continuity of each partition. To maintain the dimension-by-dimension structure of CART and keep decisions O(1), candidate boundaries are restricted to single-axis rotations of the x₀=0 plane, parameterized by a dimension index d and angle θ, giving sparse normal vectors with only two nonzero entries. Candidate split angles are selected using a closed-form geodesic midpoint formula (Eq. 10) that accounts for the nonlinear distance structure of hyperbolic space, rather than naive angle averaging. HYPERRF extends this to a multithreaded random forest ensemble trained on bootstrap resamples, matching the standard random forest training procedure while operating entirely in hyperboloid coordinates without Riemannian optimization or pairwise distance computations.

## Results

Across 36 dataset/dimension/sample-size combinations, HYPERDT achieves the highest micro-F1 score 28 times and HYPERRF 22 times. On the Gaussian mixture benchmark (2D, 800 samples), HYPERDT reaches 91.88% micro-F1 vs. 90.14% for SCIKIT-LEARN and 85.75% for HORODT; HYPERRF reaches 91.99% vs. 89.33% and 89.45%. On NeuroSEED RNA embeddings (4D, 800 samples), HYPERRF achieves 84.96% micro-F1, outperforming SCIKIT-LEARN (82.03%) and HORORF (65.70%). Runtime scales linearly with sample size for HYPERDT/HYPERRF, whereas HORORF scales quadratically.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SCIKIT-LEARN Euclidean decision trees, HYPERDT achieves a statistically significant advantage in 7 of 36 settings (e.g., 91.88% vs. 90.14% micro-F1 on 2D Gaussian, 800 samples) and is the top scorer in 28/36 overall; SCIKIT-LEARN wins only once (8D Polblogs, 74.85% vs. 74.02%). Against SCIKIT-LEARN random forests, HYPERRF is statistically superior in 11/36 cases but loses in 4 cases on 8D NeuroSEED data where Euclidean RF reaches 86.40% vs. HYPERRF's 82.42%. Against HORORF (horospherical random forests), HYPERDT is statistically better in 27/36 settings with large margins (e.g., 91.88% vs. 85.75% on 2D Gaussian; 84.96% vs. 65.70% on 4D NeuroSEED), while HYPERRF is statistically better in 13/36 but loses to HORORF in 2 cases on 16D NeuroSEED (85.90% vs. 82.30%). Against hyperbolic SVM (Agibetov et al.) and hyperbolic logistic regression (Bdeir et al.), HYPERDT is the best classifier in half the tested dimensions and statistically superior in at least one dimension for each (Table 2). In terms of runtime, HYPERDT/HYPERRF scale linearly with sample size while HORORF scales quadratically, making the proposed methods substantially faster at larger dataset sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CART decision tree framework (Breiman, 2017) and random forests (Breiman, 2001) to hyperbolic space by reformulating axis-parallel splits as inner-product tests against homogeneous hyperplanes, whose intersections with the hyperboloid yield geodesic submanifolds. It departs from prior hyperbolic classifiers that rely on Riemannian optimization (slow, initialization-sensitive), horospherical boundaries (HORORF, Doorenbos et al. 2023, which incurs quadratic pairwise-comparison cost), or log-map/whitening transformations that discard geometric structure. The closest prior work is HORORF, but HYPERDT replaces horospheres with geodesics and eliminates pairwise comparisons, achieving constant-time per-split evaluation while preserving convexity and topological continuity of decision regions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HYPERDT and HYPERRF provide a fast, interpretable, and numerically stable classifier for hyperbolic embeddings that avoids Riemannian optimization entirely, making them practical for large-scale hierarchical data (phylogenetics, ontologies, concept hierarchies) where existing hyperbolic classifiers are either slow or opaque. The linear scaling with sample size (vs. quadratic for HORORF) is a meaningful practical advantage for datasets with tens of thousands of points. Open questions include whether non-greedy tree optimization methods (branch-and-bound, reference ensembles) can be reformulated for homogeneous hyperplanes, how performance behaves in very high dimensions where all classifiers converge, and whether gradient boosting extensions would further close the gap with Euclidean random forests on certain datasets (e.g., 8-dim NeuroSEED where SCIKIT-LEARN still wins).
