## Problem

While LLMs like ChatGPT can generate human-like text, it remains unclear whether LLM-generated misinformation is more or less detectable than human-written misinformation with the same semantics. This paper tackles the fundamental question of whether LLM-generated misinformation can be harder to detect for both humans and automated detectors, which would imply it carries more deceptive potential and greater real-world harm.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241363/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For generation, the system takes instruction prompts (e.g., 'paraphrase this passage,' 'rewrite to make it more convincing,' 'write a piece of news') combined with source passages or sentences extracted from real misinformation datasets (Politifact, Gossipcop, CoAID), and produces LLM-generated misinformation text. For detection, the system takes a single news item (either human-written or LLM-generated) and outputs a factual/nonfactual label, evaluated as a success rate percentage. Detection is performed in a zero-shot setting using LLMs (GPT-4, ChatGPT-3.5, Llama2-7B/13B) with both standard and chain-of-thought prompting, while human evaluation uses 10 crowd-sourced MTurk annotators with no prior annotation experience.

## Method

The paper first builds a five-dimensional taxonomy (types, domains, sources, intents, errors) and categorizes LLM-based misinformation generation into Hallucination Generation (unintentional), Arbitrary Misinformation Generation (intentional, with or without constraints), and Controllable Misinformation Generation (paraphrase, rewriting, open-ended expansion, and information manipulation of existing passages). It then constructs the LLMFake dataset by prompting ChatGPT and six open-source LLMs (Llama2-7b/13b/70b, Vicuna-7b/13b/33b) across all generation approaches, using real nonfactual passages from Politifact, Gossipcop, and CoAID as seeds for CMG methods. Semantic preservation is validated via T-SNE projections of OpenAI embeddings, and style differences are examined through word-cloud analysis. Detection difficulty is assessed by comparing success rates of human evaluators and zero-shot LLM detectors (with and without chain-of-thought) on human-written versus LLM-generated misinformation with matched semantics, using paired T-tests for statistical significance. The core finding is that LLM-generated misinformation is consistently harder to detect than human-written misinformation with the same semantics, particularly for rewriting and open-ended generation methods.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that LLM-generated misinformation is harder to detect than human-written misinformation with the same semantics. Humans detect only 9.6% of hallucinated news and 21.4–38.4% of LLM-generated misinformation (via Rewriting, Open-ended, and Paraphrase Generation) versus 40.7% on human-written misinformation (Table 3). Among detectors, GPT-4 achieves 48.6% (No CoT) / 62.6% (CoT) success rate on human-written Politifact misinformation but drops by 6.9–26.6% (No CoT) and 6.6–21.0% (CoT) on LLM-generated misinformation (Table 4). ChatGPT-3.5 performs even worse, detecting only 0.0% of hallucinated news, while GPT-4 detects 10.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against traditional misinformation-detection baselines (e.g., BERT classifiers); instead, it compares detection difficulty of LLM-generated vs. human-written misinformation with identical semantics. Across all three CMG methods (Paraphrase, Rewriting, Open-ended Generation), detection success rates are predominantly lower for LLM-generated misinformation than for human-written, with the largest drops observed for Open-ended Generation (up to 26.6% for GPT-4 on Politifact No CoT) and Rewriting Generation (up to 19.6% for Llama2-7B CoT on Politifact). GPT-4 consistently outperforms ChatGPT-3.5 and Llama2 models as a detector, but even GPT-4 shows significant degradation on LLM-generated content. Humans outperform ChatGPT-3.5 as a detector but are outperformed by GPT-4, confirming that LLM detectors can surpass human detection for human-written misinformation yet still struggle with LLM-generated variants. Statistical significance (paired T-test) confirms the detection difficulty gap is robust, with p-values as low as 1.01×10⁻⁶ for human detection and 8.40×10⁻⁸ for detector detection on Politifact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of misinformation detection (Shu et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2022) and LLM safety/hallucination research (Zhang et al., 2023d; Wei et al., 2023). It repurposes the established misinformation detection task by introducing LLM-generated misinformation as a new input distribution, drawing on stylometry (Neal et al., 2017) for style analysis and jailbreak literature for generation methodology. Rather than proposing a new detection architecture, it conducts a systematic empirical comparison using zero-shot LLM detectors (GPT-4, Llama2, Vicuna) as evaluators, building on prior evidence that zero-shot LLMs approach state-of-the-art on human-written misinformation detection (Pelrine et al., 2023; Bang et al., 2023). The LLMFake dataset and the taxonomy of generation approaches (HG, AMG, CMG) are novel contributions that carve out a previously unexplored empirical space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLM-generated misinformation is harder to detect than human-written misinformation with identical semantics signals a potential paradigm shift in misinformation production and has direct implications for platform safety, public trust, and detector design. Key open questions include: how to build effective inference-time factuality verification for controllable generation methods (which do not explicitly signal malicious intent), whether the detection gap persists as LLMs evolve, and how to obtain reliable training labels when even human annotators struggle. Limitations include reliance on only three base datasets (Politifact, Gossipcop, CoAID), a small human evaluation panel (10 evaluators), and the zero-shot detector setting that may understate the capability of fine-tuned detectors.
