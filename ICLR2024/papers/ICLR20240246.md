## Problem

Ensemble-based exploration, a widely used strategy in deep RL, has a hidden negative effect that has been largely overlooked: individual members of a data-sharing ensemble can significantly underperform their single-agent counterparts. Each member generates only 1/N of the training data but learns from all of it, making the majority of its training data highly off-policy and difficult to learn from. The paper names this phenomenon the 'curse of diversity' and shows that intuitive fixes (larger replay buffers, smaller ensembles) either fail to consistently help or undermine the benefits of ensembling.

## Input / Output

For Atari, the input is 4 stacked 84×84 grayscale frames (standard ALE preprocessing) and the output is Q-values over discrete actions; for MuJoCo, the input is a state vector and the output is a continuous action sampled from a learned policy. The key structural change is that each ensemble member's Q-value network produces N outputs (one main head plus N−1 auxiliary heads) rather than a single Q-value vector, so the network's final linear layer is widened to produce N×|A| outputs for discrete tasks or N×2 outputs (mean and log-std) for continuous tasks. At test time, the main heads are aggregated via majority voting (discrete) or action averaging (continuous) to form the final policy.

## Method

Cross-Ensemble Representation Learning (CERL) adds an auxiliary multi-task objective to standard ensemble RL: each ensemble member i is given N Q-value heads {Q_i^j}_{j=1}^N, where Q_i^i is the main head used for its own policy gradient and Q_i^j (j≠i) are auxiliary heads that learn member j's value function. All N×N heads receive TD updates in parallel, with the target for Q_i^j computed using member j's target network and next action sampled from π_j, so each encoder is forced to produce representations useful for estimating every ensemble member's value function. This mimics the representation-learning benefit of explicit layer sharing without actually sharing parameters, thereby preserving ensemble diversity. Implementation is minimal—only the final linear layer is duplicated (≤5% parameter increase)—and encoder gradients are scaled by 1/N to keep gradient magnitudes stable. CERL is applied on top of Bootstrapped DQN (Atari) and Ensemble SAC (MuJoCo) and consistently closes the performance gap between individual ensemble members and their single-agent baselines while maintaining or improving aggregate policy performance.

## Results

On 55 Atari games (5 seeds, 1M replay buffer), individual Bootstrapped DQN members significantly underperform Double DQN, but CERL closes most of this gap and, with majority voting, CERL (agg.) outperforms both Double DQN and vanilla Bootstrapped DQN (agg.). On 4 MuJoCo tasks (30 seeds, 200k replay buffer), Ensemble SAC (indiv.) underperforms SAC in 3 of 4 tasks; applying CERL to Humanoid reduces the individual-member performance gap from roughly 3000 to around 500, and CERL (agg.) achieves the best aggregate performance. CERL adds no more than 5% parameters and negligible wall-clock overhead (N=10, L=0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Double DQN (Hasselt et al., 2015), the single-agent Atari baseline, Bootstrapped DQN (indiv.) significantly underperforms in the majority of 55 games, confirming the curse of diversity; CERL (indiv.) largely closes this gap, and CERL (agg.) surpasses Double DQN. Against vanilla Bootstrapped DQN (Osband et al., 2016), CERL improves both individual member performance and aggregate (voting) performance, whereas Bootstrapped DQN with shared layers (L=3) improves individual performance but loses the diversity benefit needed for effective majority voting. Against SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) in MuJoCo, Ensemble SAC (indiv.) underperforms in 3/4 tasks, while CERL reduces the Humanoid gap from ~3000 to ~500 and CERL (agg.) achieves the best scores. Against the alternative representation-learning methods MICo (Castro et al., 2021) and multi-horizon auxiliary tasks (Fedus et al., 2019), CERL clearly outperforms: neither MICo nor MH provides a consistent improvement over vanilla Bootstrapped DQN (indiv.) or (agg.), whereas CERL does so across both domains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the ensemble-based exploration paradigm established by Bootstrapped DQN (Osband et al., 2016) and its many successors, and on the tandem RL analysis of Ostrovski et al. (2021) that identified the difficulty of passive learning from off-policy data. It departs from prior work by demonstrating that the off-policy learning challenges identified in the tandem setting are not merely an artifact of a single passive agent but are inherent to any diverse data-sharing ensemble, causing individual members to underperform single-agent baselines. The proposed CERL method draws on auxiliary-task representation learning in RL (Jaderberg et al., 2016; Schwarzer et al., 2020) and mutual distillation ideas, but applies them in a novel cross-ensemble fashion that preserves policy diversity without explicit network sharing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This finding challenges the widely-held assumption that ensemble-based exploration is a uniformly beneficial strategy, revealing that the reported gains of methods like Bootstrapped DQN may largely stem from majority voting rather than genuinely better exploration by individual members. This has direct practical implications for any application relying on individual ensemble member performance (e.g., hyperparameter sweeps with ensembles, safety-critical settings where a single policy must be deployed). Open questions include developing a theoretical account of why off-policy learning degrades so severely in diverse ensembles, whether the phenomenon extends to per-step ensemble switching or model-based methods, and how CERL scales to very large networks where maintaining N separate encoders becomes infeasible.
