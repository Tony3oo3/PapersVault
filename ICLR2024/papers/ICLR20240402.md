## Problem

Standard models of bounded rationality (e.g., Boltzmann models) simulate suboptimal decision-making by adding noise to optimal actions, treating agents as uniformly suboptimal regardless of the computational difficulty of the task. This fails to capture that different agents face different inference constraints, and that the same agent may plan differently depending on task difficulty. The paper asks: can we explicitly model and infer agents' computational constraints (their 'inference budgets') from observed behavior, rather than treating suboptimality as unstructured noise?

## Input / Output

The method consumes state-action trajectories (maze routes, speaker utterances paired with color patches, chess moves with Elo/time-control metadata) and produces inferred reward parameters, agent-specific prior distributions over inference budgets (η_i), and predicted next actions. The budget β_runtime is a discrete integer parameterizing the number of steps in an anytime algorithm (search depth, RSA iterations, or MCTS tree expansions), and the model learns a per-subpopulation prior p_budget(β|η_i) rather than a single scalar. In the chess setting, sub-populations are stratified by player Elo, opponent Elo, or time control, each receiving its own η_i.

## Method

The core contribution is the Latent Inference Budget Model (L-IBM), which replaces the Boltzmann temperature parameter with a latent variable β_runtime that directly controls the runtime of an anytime inference algorithm. The key tractability insight is that for anytime algorithms, computing the action distribution at n different budget values costs no more than computing it at the maximum budget, since each intermediate state is derived from the previous one in O(1) time. Training proceeds via MAP inference over reward parameters θ and agent-specific budget-generating parameters η_i, marginalizing over the discrete budget: log π(a|s;θ,η_i) = log Σ_β p_budget(β|η_i)·π(a|s;R_θ,f_β). The framework is instantiated with three anytime algorithms—truncated breadth-first search for maze navigation, iterative Rational Speech Acts for pragmatic language, and Monte Carlo Tree Search for chess—each of which is proven to satisfy the anytime property. Unlike Boltzmann models where β_temp scales the reward signal, β_runtime changes the information available to the agent, making the model sensitive to task difficulty and enabling meaningful inference of individual differences in inferential capacity.

## Results

In maze navigation, L-IBM achieves 44% next-action prediction accuracy, nearly doubling the Boltzmann baseline (20%) and substantially outperforming fixed-depth search (5–16%). In the Colors in Context pragmatic language task, L-IBM reaches 84.0% target-referent prediction accuracy (stratified by player skill), marginally exceeding the Boltzmann baseline (83.9%). In human chess (Lichess), L-IBM with MCTS achieves 44.17% top-1 move prediction accuracy when stratified by active Elo or opponent Elo, outperforming the imitation-learning baseline (42.06%) and the β_puct baseline (43.77–43.84%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Boltzmann model (Luce, 1959), L-IBM shows a dramatic advantage in maze navigation (44% vs. 20% accuracy) and a small but consistent edge in Colors in Context (84.0% vs. 83.9% for player-skill stratification) and chess (44.17% vs. 43.77% for active Elo). Against the β_puct baseline from Jacob et al. (2022), which models individual differences by fitting a distribution over the PUCT exploration parameter, L-IBM consistently wins by 0.3–0.5 percentage points across all three chess stratifications (active Elo, opponent Elo, and time control). In the Colors in Context task, L-IBM also outperforms the Boltzmann variant when stratified by player skill (84.0% vs. 83.9%) but underperforms slightly when stratified by difficulty (82.7% vs. 83.5%), suggesting the runtime-budget interpretation is most informative for capturing individual differences rather than task-level variation. Overall, L-IBM matches or outperforms all baselines, with the largest gains in the synthetic maze setting where the anytime-algorithm structure is most directly exploitable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant Boltzmann (temperature) model of bounded rationality, which treats suboptimality as additive noise over optimal actions, by instead modeling suboptimality as explicitly constrained inference via a latent runtime budget on an anytime algorithm. It builds directly on the Rational Speech Acts framework (Frank & Goodman, 2012) for pragmatic language and on MCTS-based models of human chess play (Jacob et al., 2022; McIlroy-Young et al., 2020), but unifies these under a single generative principle: the budget parameterizes the number of search/iteration steps rather than a softmax temperature. The key enabling observation—that anytime algorithms (Dean & Boddy, 1988) allow evaluating all budget values at the cost of the maximum—makes the latent-variable inference tractable in a way that prior hierarchical Bayesian planning models (Zhi-Xuan et al., 2020) did not achieve.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, domain-agnostic way to model heterogeneous suboptimality across agent populations, and the inferred budgets correlate meaningfully with external measures of skill and task difficulty—a property Boltzmann models lack. However, empirical gains over strong baselines are modest (e.g., 44.17 vs. 43.77 in chess), and the maze validation relies on synthetic data. Open questions include whether the anytime-algorithm assumption is restrictive (many real inference procedures are not cleanly anytime), how the framework scales to multi-agent settings with interacting budgets, and whether the single latent budget captures the full structure of human computational limitations (e.g., working-memory constraints vs. time pressure).
