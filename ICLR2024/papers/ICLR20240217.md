## Problem

Existing low-level controllers for open-world agents rely on goal specifications (task indicators, future outcome images, or language) that are either insufficiently expressive for diverse multi-step tasks or prohibitively expensive to learn at scale. The paper asks whether a reference gameplay video can serve as a goal specification that is both expressive enough to encode procedural information and learnable without expensive text-gameplay annotations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240217/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, GROOT takes a 128-frame reference gameplay video (recorded from a different biome to test generalization) as the instruction and a 224×224 first-person RGB observation as the current state, producing an autoregressive sequence of low-level actions over a joint space of 8,461 button combinations and 121 camera movements. The video is compressed into a single Gaussian-distributed goal embedding (one condition slot) via the encoder, and the policy queries this embedding at every timestep through cross-attention to condition its action predictions.

## Method

GROOT uses an encoder-decoder transformer architecture: a non-causal transformer (minGPT without causal mask) with an EfficientNet-B0 backbone and learnable summary tokens encodes the reference video into a latent goal space, while a Transformer-XL decoder with Flamingo-style gated cross-attention layers in each block acts as the goal-conditioned policy. The training objective is derived by maximizing the log-likelihood of future states given past observations, which via a variational lower bound decomposes into a behavior cloning loss (predicting IDM-labeled actions conditioned on the goal embedding) plus a KL regularization term that keeps the posterior goal distribution close to a prior, enforcing a succinct goal space. This self-supervised formulation requires no text annotations—only raw gameplay videos and a pre-trained inverse dynamic model to generate pseudo-action labels—making it a self-imitation procedure that simultaneously induces the goal space and learns the instruction-following controller.

## Results

On the Minecraft SkillForge benchmark (30 tasks across 6 categories), GROOT achieves a 150-point Elo advantage over the best baseline STEVE-1(visual), corresponding to a 70% pairwise winning rate. In TrueSkill ratings, GROOT scores 29.1±0.9, trailing human players (34.2±1.0) but leading STEVE-1(visual) (25.8±0.8) and all VPT variants (19.2–22.2). On 9 representative programmatic tasks, GROOT reaches 100% success on 6 tasks (dye/shear sheep, enchant sword, smelt food, use bow, sleep, lead animals) but only 60% on build snow golems and 50% on build obsidian. On the long-horizon diamond mining task, GROOT achieves a 16% success rate versus 0% for STEVE-1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GROOT decisively outperforms all three VPT variants (fd, bc, rl) across every task category, with the largest margins on build and tool tasks where VPT's condition-free architecture cannot follow any instruction. Against STEVE-1, GROOT wins the overall Elo tournament by 150 points (70% win rate) and dominates on build and tool categories, but loses specifically on the craft category where STEVE-1(visual) benefits from its future-outcome goal representation being well-suited to common crafting tasks. On the challenging multi-step diamond mining task, GROOT's video-instruction goal space enables it to maintain horizontal mining at a fixed depth (16% success over 25 trials), while STEVE-1's 16-frame future-video goal cannot encode the procedural detail of sustained horizontal mining and fails completely (0% success). The gap to human players remains substantial (5.1 TrueSkill points), with GROOT's main weaknesses being precise item identification in the hotbar and rare multi-step construction tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

GROOT builds directly on VPT's Transformer-XL policy architecture and Minecraft environment setup, and borrows Flamingo's gated cross-attention mechanism to condition the policy on goal embeddings. Its key departure from STEVE-1 (which uses 16-frame future video clips) and language-conditioned policies (RT-1, Cai et al.) is the use of full-length gameplay videos as instructions, eliminating the need for expensive text-gameplay annotations. The theoretical contribution is a variational derivation showing that maximizing future-state log-likelihood decomposes into behavior cloning plus KL regularization on a learned goal space, providing a principled objective that simultaneously induces the goal representation and the controller.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The video-instruction paradigm addresses a practical bottleneck: language goals require prohibitively expensive trajectory-text pairs, while short future clips lack procedural detail for multi-step tasks. The emergent goal composition (concatenated videos yielding intermediate embeddings that drive multi-task behavior) suggests the learned goal space captures compositional structure without explicit supervision, which could simplify integration with high-level planners. Key limitations include sensitivity to reference video selection, poor fine-grained item identification (hotbar confusion), and underperformance on craft tasks where future-outcome goals suffice. Open questions include whether SFT/RLHF can align the unsupervised goal space with human intent, and whether the paradigm generalizes beyond Minecraft to other open-world environments.
