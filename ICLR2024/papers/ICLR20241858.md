## Problem

Existing Thompson sampling algorithms for RL rely on a Gaussian approximation of the Q-function posterior, which is a poor surrogate in most practical settings and requires fixed features for incremental computation. Meanwhile, provably efficient exploration algorithms are confined to tabular or linear MDPs, while scalable deep RL exploration heuristics lack theoretical guarantees. This paper bridges the gap by directly sampling the Q-function posterior via Langevin Monte Carlo, yielding both a provable regret bound and a practical deep RL algorithm.

## Input / Output

The method consumes state observations (84×84 pixel frames for Atari, or d-dimensional feature vectors for linear MDPs), actions, and scalar rewards stored in a replay buffer. At each episode, it produces a sampled Q-function parameter vector w_h per time step h, from which a greedy policy selects actions. The Q-values are clamped to [0, H−h+1] to ensure bounded optimism, and the number of noisy gradient updates J_k per episode controls the posterior approximation precision (J_k=1 for Atari, J_k=4 for N-Chain in practice).

## Method

LMC-LSVI replaces the Gaussian posterior approximation in randomized value iteration with Langevin Monte Carlo: at each episode, it performs J_k steps of noisy gradient descent on a regularized least-squares Bellman loss, where isotropic Gaussian noise (scaled by step size and inverse temperature β_k) drives the parameter toward the true posterior. In the linear MDP setting, this yields a tractable Gaussian distribution over Q-function parameters, enabling a regret bound of Õ(d^{3/2} H^{3/2} √T). For deep RL, the paper proposes Adam LMCDQN, which substitutes the plain LMC update with Adam SGLD (preconditioned SGLD with adaptive first- and second-moment tracking plus a bias correction term) to handle pathological curvature and saddle points in the neural network loss landscape. The key distinction from prior work is that the noise is injected directly into the gradient updates of the Bellman residual rather than into the reward, and the number of updates J_k per episode is a tunable knob that trades off posterior fidelity against computational cost.

## Results

Theoretically, LMC-LSVI achieves a regret bound of $\widetilde{O}(d^{3/2} H^{3/2} \sqrt{T})$ in the linear MDP setting, matching the best-known bound for randomized algorithms (e.g., LSVI-UCB, LSVI-PHE) while being computationally tractable. In N-Chain experiments (N = 25–100), Adam LMCDQN maintains near-optimal returns (close to 10) across all chain lengths, while Bootstrapped DQN and Noisy-Net degrade sharply as N grows. On 8 hard-exploration Atari games over 50M frames (5 seeds), Adam LMCDQN is competitive overall and shows a clear advantage in Gravitar and Venture, two of the most challenging sparse-reward tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In N-Chain (N = 25–100), Adam LMCDQN substantially outperforms DQN with ε-greedy, Bootstrapped DQN, and Noisy-Net, maintaining near-optimal returns as chain length increases while the other three baselines degrade rapidly. In the 8-game Atari evaluation (50M frames, 5 seeds), Adam LMCDQN is competitive with or better than Double DQN, Prioritized DQN, C51, QR-DQN, and IQN overall, with the most pronounced gains in Gravitar and Venture where it clearly leads all baselines. Against Bootstrapped DQN and Noisy-Net on Atari, Adam LMCDQN is generally superior, particularly in sparse-reward games (Freeway, Gravitar, Pitfall, Solaris, Venture). In a direct comparison with Langevin DQN (Dwaracherla and Van Roy, 2020), Adam LMCDQN usually outperforms in sparse-reward hard-exploration games (Gravitar, Solaris, Venture) while achieving similar performance in dense-reward games (Alien, H.E.R.O., Qbert). The theoretical regret bound of LMC-LSVI matches LSVI-UCB and LSVI-PHE exactly, and is strictly better than OPT-RLSVI ($\tilde{O}(d^2 H^2 \sqrt{T})$), while ELEANOR achieves a tighter $\tilde{O}(dH^{3/2}\sqrt{T})$ bound but is computationally intractable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) from the bandit setting (Xu et al., 2022) to episodic RL, proposing LMC-LSVI as a principled alternative to RLSVI's Gaussian posterior approximation. It departs from the RLSVI line (Osband et al., 2016a; Zanette et al., 2020a; Ishfaq et al., 2021) by directly sampling the Q-function posterior via noisy gradient descent rather than injecting tuned Gaussian noise into Bellman updates. For the deep RL variant, Adam LMCDQN builds on Adam SGLD (Kim et al., 2022) and DQN (Mnih et al., 2015), distinguishing itself from Langevin DQN (Dwaracherla & Van Roy, 2020) by providing a full regret analysis and using a temperature-controlled noise schedule. The work sits at the intersection of provably efficient RL (linear MDP regret bounds) and scalable deep RL exploration, a gap the authors explicitly identify as underexplored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key contribution is unifying a provable regret bound (Õ(d^{3/2} H^{3/2} √T), matching the best UCB-based results for linear MDPs) with a method that naturally extends to deep RL via Adam SGLD — a combination the authors note is rare in the literature. Open questions include closing the √(dH) gap to the Ω(dH√T) lower bound, removing the constraint δ > 1/(2√(2eπ)) on the failure probability, and extending to continuous control and more general MDP structures. The practical finding that very small J_k values (1 for Atari, 4 for N-Chain) suffice despite the theoretical requirement of J_k = 2κ_k log(4HKd) suggests the theory may be conservative, but this gap is not formally resolved.
