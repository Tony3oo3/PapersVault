## Problem

LLMs encode rich common-sense knowledge about physical interactions in abstract language, but grounding this knowledge into low-level robot trajectories remains unsolved. Prior approaches rely on predefined primitive skills and feasibility predictors in symbolic spaces, leaving imitation policies unable to detect when pre-conditions for subsequent actions are violated or to replan from partial failures. The paper asks: can we recover the implicit mode structure and constraints from a small number of unsegmented human demonstrations, using only sparse trajectory-level success labels, to make imitation learning both interpretable and reactive?

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural-language task description and fewer than 10 unsegmented human demonstrations (end-effector poses, object poses, or wrist-camera images), which are augmented with synthetic end-effector and gripper perturbations to generate both successful and failing counterfactual trajectories. The LLM is prompted to produce a discrete mode sequence, a feasibility matrix encoding valid/invalid transitions, and a subset of task-relevant state features. The output is a grounding classifier mapping continuous states to discrete mode labels, mode-specific imitation policies augmented with pseudo-attractors, and the feasibility matrix itself, which together enable LLM-based replanning when an invalid mode transition is detected at runtime.

## Method

GLiDE casts language grounding as an inverse problem: recovering mode families (lower-dimensional manifolds in configuration space) from sparsely labeled trajectories. The key mechanism is a fully differentiable pipeline where a neural mode classifier outputs soft mode beliefs per state, and a forward model uses the LLM-generated feasibility matrix to compute a trajectory-level success score from those beliefs. Training minimizes a composite loss: a success loss encouraging all consecutive transitions to be feasible, a failure loss requiring at least one infeasible transition in failing trajectories, and boundary losses anchoring start/end states to known modes. For underactuated manipulation, an additional dynamics loss enforces that states within the same mode exhibit consistent state-change dynamics, enabling precise boundary recovery without direct mode annotations. At inference, the classifier segments the state space into modes, mode-specific BC policies (augmented with pseudo-attractors derived from LLM-identified features) generate actions, and the feasibility matrix enables LLM-based replanning upon detecting an invalid transition.

## Results

On 2D navigation, GLiDE+Planning achieves near-perfect success rates (0.996, 0.987, 0.991) across 3-, 4-, and 5-mode environments without perturbations, and maintains 0.996, 0.966, 0.974 under perturbations. In contrast, mode-agnostic BC degrades substantially under perturbations, dropping from 0.967/0.814/0.810 to 0.908/0.614/0.596. On RoboSuite manipulation tasks, GLiDE+BC retains 0.40/0.39/0.15 success on Can/Lift/Square under perturbations, while BC collapses to 0.20/0.18/0.03. For mode classification accuracy (overlap with ground truth), the full GLiDE method achieves 83%/83%/67% on Can/Lift/Square, outperforming the kmeans++ trajectory segmentation baseline (66%/56%/54%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is mode-agnostic Behavior Cloning (BC), which learns a single policy from all unsegmented successful demonstrations. On 2D navigation, GLiDE+Planning outperforms BC by 2.9–9.1% in clean settings and by 8.8–37.8% under perturbations (e.g., 0.974 vs. 0.596 on 5-mode). On RoboSuite, the gap under perturbations is even more dramatic: BC drops to 0.20/0.18/0.03 on Can/Lift/Square while GLiDE+BC retains 0.40/0.39/0.15, showing that mode-conditioned policies with pseudo-attractors are far more robust to end-effector displacements and gripper failures. A secondary baseline using kmeans++ clustering for trajectory segmentation underperforms GLiDE's learned grounding classifier on mode classification accuracy (66%/56%/54% vs. 83%/83%/67% overlap), confirming that statistical similarity alone cannot recover task-relevant mode boundaries. Ablations further show that removing the dynamics loss or the task prediction loss degrades both classification accuracy and task success, validating the necessity of the full differentiable pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mode-based planning framework of Wang et al. (2022a) by introducing a learning-based mechanism to recover mode families from unsegmented demonstrations, rather than requiring them to be specified a priori. It departs from the dominant LLM-for-robotics paradigm (Ahn et al. 2022, Liu et al. 2023) that operates in symbolic spaces with predefined primitive skills, instead using LLMs to guide the discovery of physical mode abstractions grounded in trajectory data. The explanation-based learning paradigm (DeJong & Mooney 1986; Segre & DeJong 1985) is repurposed here as a differentiable training objective that recovers mode boundaries from sparse trajectory-level success labels, and the counterfactual perturbation strategy draws on manipulation mechanics (Mason 2001; Hauser & Latombe 2010) to generate the negative examples needed for boundary learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete gap in embodied AI: how to connect LLM semantic knowledge to physical robot trajectories without requiring dense human annotations or pre-built primitive skill libraries. The mode-family grounding provides interpretability (mode boundaries explain why specific perturbations cause failure) and enables LLM-based replanning for recovery, which pure imitation learning cannot do. Key limitations include the need for a reset-capable environment and many trial-and-error rollouts to collect success labels, and the reliance on skilled prompt engineering for state representation selection. Open questions include whether active learning can reduce data collection costs and whether state representation can be learned end-to-end alongside mode classifiers.
