## Problem

In reset-free RL, agents alternate between a forward controller (learning the task) and a reset controller (returning to initial states), but prior work has left two critical aspects of this switching mechanism understudied: (1) how to properly bootstrap the value of the last state before a controller switch, and (2) when to actually switch, with most methods relying on fixed time limits. The paper shows that inconsistent bootstrapping causes the forward and reset value functions to depend on each other's policies, and that fixed-length trajectories waste experience in already-mastered regions of state space.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a low-dimensional state vector (or a 3-channel binary image for the 4-rooms gridworld) paired with a goal drawn from either the task goal distribution or the initial state distribution, and outputs a continuous or discrete action. A separate success critic network takes the same state-goal pair and outputs a competency score in [0,1] via a bounded activation (-0.5·cos(x)−1 for SAC environments, sigmoid for DQN). The switching decision is a Bernoulli sample from a probability computed from the success critic value and current trajectory length, not a direct probability output.

## Method

RISC combines two contributions. First, it uses timeout-nonterminal bootstrapping: when a trajectory ends due to a controller switch, the last state's value is updated using the successor state's value (rather than treating it as terminal), which the paper proves necessary to keep forward and reset value targets independent of each other's policies. Second, it learns when to switch via a success critic Q_F trained as a Q-function with rewards/terminations from a binary success function, estimating F(s,g)=γ_sc^t (minimum steps to goal). The switching probability is P(switch)=F(s,g)×(1−β^t), where β is a conservative factor making switching more likely as the trajectory lengthens, and the check is only performed on a fraction ζ of trajectories after a minimum length m is reached. This 'outside-in' curriculum lets the agent leave mastered regions early and spend more interactions in unfamiliar state-goal space, contrasting with the 'inside-out' reverse curricula of prior methods.

## Results

On the EARL benchmark, RISC achieves the best reset-free performance on 3 of 4 environments: 1.00 on Tabletop Manipulation, 1.00 on Sawyer Door, and 0.42 on Sawyer Peg (normalized returns, higher is better). On Minitaur (dense reward, no demonstrations), RISC reaches -321.74 average return, substantially outperforming R3L (-186.30) and FBRL (-986.34). RISC also learns significantly faster, with the highest interquartile mean AUC across all environments, indicating superior sample efficiency. On Sawyer Door and Sawyer Peg, RISC matches or exceeds the episodic oracle baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RISC outperforms FBRL on all four environments: Tabletop Manipulation (1.00 vs 0.94), Sawyer Peg (0.42 vs 0.00), and Minitaur (-321.74 vs -986.34), while tying on Sawyer Door (1.00 vs 1.00). Against R3L, RISC wins decisively on Sawyer Door (1.00 vs 0.54) and Sawyer Peg (0.42 vs 0.00), and also on Minitaur (-321.74 vs -186.30). Compared to the demonstration-based methods VaPRL and MEDAL, RISC achieves equal or better performance on all three shared environments (Tabletop: 1.00 vs 0.98; Sawyer Door: 1.00 vs 0.94/1.00; Sawyer Peg: 0.42 vs 0.02/0.40) without requiring demonstrations at test time. Notably, RISC matches the episodic oracle on Sawyer Door and Sawyer Peg, and its AUC-based sample efficiency metric significantly exceeds all baselines, meaning it reaches competitive performance in fewer environment steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

RISC extends the Forward-Backward RL paradigm (Han et al., 2015; Eysenbach et al., 2017) where an agent alternates between a task controller and a reset controller, but replaces the fixed-time switching rule with a learned, confidence-based switching mechanism. The paper also extends Pardo et al. (2022)'s empirical observation about timeout bootstrapping by providing a theoretical analysis showing that in reset-free RL, the timeout-terminal strategy creates a problematic coupling between the forward and reset policies' value functions, motivating the use of timeout-nonterminal bootstrapping. Conceptually, RISC departs from the standard reverse-curriculum approach (building outward from goal states) by proposing an 'outside-in' curriculum that prioritizes experience in under-explored regions of the state-goal space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reset-free RL is critical for real-world robotics where environment resets require expensive human intervention or specialized hardware, and RISC's intelligent switching reduces wasted experience in already-mastered regions, improving sample efficiency. The bootstrapping coupling analysis is a general insight applicable to any RL setting with non-terminal trajectory endings, not just reset-free RL. Key limitations include potential failure in environments with irreversible states (where aggressive exploration could trap the agent) and the lack of demonstration-guided curriculum, both flagged as future work. The method's reliance on a success function queryable for task and initial-state goals, while reasonable, still represents a non-trivial assumption for fully autonomous deployment.
