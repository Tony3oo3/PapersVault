## Problem

Post-development backdoor defenses must operate without knowledge of how a model was trained, yet existing approaches either reverse-engineer trigger patterns (requiring strong assumptions about trigger types that fail on global-transformation attacks like SIG and WaNet) or rely on trigger conspicuousness in the input space. This paper asks whether one can bypass trigger reconstruction entirely by directly extracting the backdoor functionality from a compromised model, thereby avoiding inductive biases on trigger form.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240092/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At deployment, the defender receives a potentially backdoored model M and a small reserved clean set D_c (as few as 200–2,000 images, ~0.5% of ImageNet training set). At inference time, each input image is first classified by M to obtain a preliminary prediction ŷ; the detector then queries two auxiliary models (the backdoor expert B and the clean-finetuned model M′) for their confidence in ŷ, and outputs a binary reject/accept decision. The system is evaluated on CIFAR-10 (32×32), GTSRB (variable resolution traffic signs), and ImageNet (224×224, 1000 classes) across ResNet, VGG, MobileNetV2, and ViT architectures.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-model ensemble built via asymmetric fine-tuning on the same small clean set D_c. First, the backdoored model M is fine-tuned for one epoch with a very small learning rate (η=10⁻⁴) on deliberately mislabeled clean samples (labels shifted by one class), producing a backdoor expert B that catastrophically forgets normal functionality (CA drops to near-random) while preserving backdoor functionality (ASR remains high). Second, M is separately fine-tuned with correct labels at a larger learning rate to produce an auxiliary model M′ that retains normal functionality but suppresses the backdoor. At inference, an input x is rejected if the likelihood-ratio score Conf_M′(ŷ|x) / Conf_B(ŷ|x) falls below a threshold α (justified via the Neyman-Pearson lemma under calibration), or equivalently via a modified min-formula in Eq. (3) that accounts for miscalibration. This design requires no assumptions on trigger type, no access to poisoned data, and no trigger reconstruction, and it achieves ~99% AUROC across 12 SOTA attacks with only ~0.9% clean-accuracy drop.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, BaDExpert achieves an average AUROC of 99.0% across 12 backdoor attacks, with a minimum of 96.1% (ISSBA), while the best baseline detector (SCALE-UP) reaches only 83.5% average AUROC. As a post-development defense, BaDExpert reduces the average attack success rate to 5.1% (from 97.2% undefended) with only a 0.9% clean accuracy drop. On GTSRB, BaDExpert achieves 100% average AUROC and 2.0% average ASR with 0.1% CA drop; on ImageNet (1000 classes), it maintains ~100% AUROC and <5% ASR with 0.1% CA drop using only ~6,000 clean samples (0.5% of training set).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BaDExpert outperforms all four dedicated backdoor input detectors (STRIP, AC, Frequency, SCALE-UP) by a wide margin on average AUROC: 99.0% vs. 70.2%, 81.1%, 73.1%, and 83.5% respectively, and achieves the highest AUROC on 9 of 12 individual attacks. Among model-repairing defenses, NAD is the closest competitor with 6.7% average ASR (vs. BaDExpert's 5.1%), but NAD incurs a 7.2% CA drop compared to BaDExpert's 0.9%. Trigger-reverse-engineering methods (NC, MOTH) fail catastrophically on global-transformation attacks like SIG (ASR 86.5% and 65.8% respectively), while BaDExpert maintains 1.6% ASR on SIG. Frequency-based detection is uniformly weak (~73–75% AUROC across all attacks), and STRIP degrades to 40.3% AUROC on SIG. BaDExpert's advantage is most pronounced on stealthy attacks (SIG, WaNet, BPP) where trigger-conspicuousness-based detectors fail, confirming that extracting backdoor functionality without trigger-type assumptions provides a more universal defense.

## Relation to Prior Work

BaDExpert departs from the dominant post-development defense paradigm of trigger reverse-engineering (Neural Cleanse, MOTH), which requires strong inductive biases on trigger type and fails on global-transformation triggers like SIG and WaNet. Instead, it repurposes the concept of catastrophic forgetting: by fine-tuning on a small set of deliberately mislabeled clean samples, the model's normal functionality is erased while the backdoor functionality is preserved, yielding a 'backdoor expert' that serves as a detection oracle. The detection rule is then justified via the Neyman-Pearson likelihood-ratio framework, connecting the confidence-based scoring to classical statistical hypothesis testing. The approach is orthogonal to model-repairing defenses (NAD, FP) and can be ensembled with them, and it differs from SEAM (Zhu et al., 2022), which also uses mislabeled fine-tuning but at a larger learning rate where both CA and ASR collapse.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BaDExpert provides a practical, assumption-light post-development defense that works uniformly across 12 attack types without requiring knowledge of the trigger, the training data, or the model's provenance—addressing a critical gap for organizations deploying third-party models. The core finding that backdoor functionality is structurally more 'sticky' than normal functionality under mislabeled fine-tuning is a useful lens for understanding the internal geometry of backdoored models. However, adaptive attacks (particularly the tailored BaDExpert-Adap-BadNet) degrade AUROC to 87%, and the defense offers no certified guarantees, leaving open the question of whether the functionality-separation assumption holds in all realistic threat models. Extending the approach to non-visual modalities (NLP, audio) and to multi-target or conditional backdoors remains unexplored.
