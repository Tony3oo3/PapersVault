## Problem

Existing causal reasoning frameworks—counterfactual (Halpern-Pearl), structural causal models, and Granger causality—are largely restricted to static settings, require interventions or causal graphs, or assume known structural dependencies. This paper tackles the open question of how to uncover and quantify causal links in complex dynamical systems from raw observational data alone, without interventions, causal graphs, or domain-level structural knowledge.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241551/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw multivariate time-series observations (e.g., 84×84 Atari screen pixels or 13 physiological signals from a diabetes simulator) along with action variables, with no access to system equations or causal graphs. It produces per-component causal contributions (drift g, diffusion ġ, and cross-diffusion g̈ terms) that sum to the total change in grit, alongside grit/reachability values and identified cause events localized to specific time intervals. In the Atari experiment, contributions are computed via autograd on a DQN value function with M=10 trapezoidal micro-steps; in the diabetes experiment, Monte Carlo learning estimates the value function directly from trajectories.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-lemma framework that reframes causation as a reinforcement learning problem over diffusion processes. The Value Lemma shows that grit (minimum probability of a future event) and reachability (maximum probability) are exactly the optimal value functions of two constructed MDPs with ±1 rewards over the event's duration, enabling model-free RL (DQN or Monte Carlo) to learn them. The Decomposition Lemma applies Itô's lemma to the learned value function, decomposing the expected change in grit during a candidate cause event into per-component contributions: a drift term g_j (sensitivity of grit to the j-th state component times its drift), a diffusion term ġ_j (second-order sensitivity times the j-th row of σ), and cross-diffusion terms g̈_{j,i}. A practical g-formula approximates these integrals via trapezoidal rule over M micro-steps, and the Proposition of Causation then checks three conditions (temporal ordering, sustained grit increase, and ruling-variable contribution dominating negative non-ruling contributions) to formally establish or reject a causal link.

## Results

The paper presents two illustrative experiments rather than standard benchmark comparisons. On Atari Pong, the method correctly identifies frame 49 as the last moment the paddle could have moved to save the point, and pinpoints the ball's pixel trajectory as the causal event, with a sufficient cause realized at frame 51. On the T1DMS diabetes simulator, the decomposition lemma isolates subcutaneous insulin (SI1) as the sole contributing variable to the grit increase preceding hypoglycemia (BG < 70 mg/dL), correctly rejecting a spurious earlier insulin spike that violated the monotonicity condition (C2). No standard quantitative metrics (accuracy, F1, etc.) are reported; the evaluation is qualitative, demonstrating correct causal identification through learned value functions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper explicitly states that no baseline comparisons are possible because existing causal inference methods operate in fundamentally incompatible settings. Structural Causal Model (SCM) and Halpern-Pearl approaches require pre-specified causal graphs and interventions, which are infeasible in the dynamic, high-dimensional settings studied here (e.g., ~30,000 variables per timestep in Atari). Granger causality and time-series causal discovery methods focus on variable-level dependencies rather than event-level causation and typically assume discrete-time models. The authors argue their process-based, intervention-free framework addresses a qualitatively different question (why did this specific event happen in this specific trajectory?) that existing methods cannot answer, making direct numerical comparison meaningless. The experiments therefore serve as feasibility demonstrations rather than competitive benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' prior work on dead-end analysis in reinforcement learning (Fatemi et al., 2019, 2021), where value functions were used to identify high-risk states, by generalizing the framework to continuous-time diffusion processes and formalizing a full theory of event-level causation. It departs sharply from the dominant counterfactual/interventionist paradigm (Pearl, Halpern-Pearl) by eliminating the need for structural causal models, interventions, or causal graphs, instead grounding causation in the process-based philosophy of Salmon and Dowe. The decomposition lemma, which applies Itô's lemma to value functions, provides a novel computational bridge between stochastic calculus and causal attribution that has no direct precedent in either the causal inference or reinforcement learning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This framework offers a principled, intervention-free route to causal reasoning in complex dynamical systems (medical monitoring, industrial control, game environments) where structural models are infeasible to construct and interventions are physically impossible. The main limitations are the restriction to second-order moments of stochasticity and the assumption of full state observability, both acknowledged as open problems. A key open question is scalability: the decomposition requires computing per-component gradients of the value function, which may be prohibitive in very high-dimensional settings. The absence of quantitative baselines (the authors argue existing methods are inapplicable) leaves the empirical validation somewhat thin, resting on two illustrative examples rather than systematic benchmarks.
