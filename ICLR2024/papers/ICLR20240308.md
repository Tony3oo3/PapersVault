## Problem

Large text corpora used to train language models (e.g., C4, The Pile, RedPajama) are typically introduced with only a description of how they were constructed, with no documentation of their actual contents—topics, quality, toxicity, personal information, or benchmark contamination. No practical tool existed to characterize or compare multiple web-scale datasets across the same dimensions, leaving practitioners without a way to assess data quality before selecting a training corpus.

## Input / Output

WIMBD ingests entire text corpora (up to 35+ TB) and processes them on a single compute node (224 CPUs, 882 GB RAM) paired with an Elasticsearch cluster. It accepts two types of queries: counting operations over the full corpus (e.g., document length, domain, PII patterns) and search queries (e.g., finding documents containing specific benchmark strings). Outputs are structured as frequency tables, distribution histograms, contamination percentages, and per-corpus summary statistics, enabling direct cross-corpus comparison across ten datasets.

## Method

WIMBD is built on two primitive operations that together enable sixteen analyses. First, a map-reduce counting framework with two variants: Exact Counts for tractable value spaces (e.g., document length bins, domain strings) and Compressed Counts for intractable spaces (e.g., all 10-grams, document hashes for duplicate detection), where a configurable hash table trades memory for accuracy via controlled collision rates. Second, an Elasticsearch inverted-index search layer wrapped in a custom API that supports Boolean queries and document-frequency retrieval for contamination and n-gram counting. The key design insight is that these two simple primitives—count and search—are sufficient to answer a broad range of data-characterization questions (duplicates, PII, toxicity, benchmark overlap, domain distribution, length anomalies) without requiring model inference or GPU resources, making the toolkit accessible on standard CPU-only infrastructure.

## Results

Across ten web-scale corpora (35+ TB total), WIMBD reveals that ~50% of documents in RedPajama and LAION-2B-en are exact duplicates, and over 60% in The Pile (139M documents in 64.6M duplicate clusters). Benchmark contamination is severe: RedPajama has >50% contamination on 8 of 15 tested benchmarks (fully contaminated on COPA), while The Pile is contaminated on WSC, WIC, and AESIC. PII is pervasive, with mC4-en containing an estimated 201M email addresses, 4B phone numbers, and 97.8M IP addresses. Toxic language is present in 1–16.5% of documents depending on corpus and detection method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so there are no traditional baselines. Instead, WIMBD provides the first systematic cross-corpus comparison across ten datasets using a unified set of sixteen analyses. Key cross-corpus contrasts include: C4 (deduplicated) has zero exact duplicates while The Pile (intentionally oversampled) has 60%+ duplicates; mC4-en is the largest corpus at 14.7 TB / 2.7T tokens but also contains the most PII per token; RedPajama is the most benchmark-contaminated corpus (>50% on 8/15 benchmarks) while 67 of 82 tested benchmarks appear in none of the four examined corpora. The most common 10-grams are dominated by repeated punctuation (e.g., '??????????' at 9M occurrences in C4, '...' at 3.61B in The Pile) rather than meaningful content, revealing systematic cleaning failures across all corpora.

## Relation to Prior Work

WIMBD repurposes standard information-retrieval and data-management primitives—map-reduce counting (Dean & Ghemawat, 2008) and Elasticsearch inverted-index search—into a unified, programmatic framework for corpus analysis at scale. Prior tools such as ROOTS (Piktus et al., 2023a), GAIA (Piktus et al., 2023b), Snoopy (Razeghi et al., 2022b), and the Data Measurements Tool (Luccioni et al., 2021) each addressed a single corpus or a narrow set of analyses without programmatic access; WIMBD's key departure is combining both counting and search under one extensible interface and applying a fixed battery of sixteen analyses across ten corpora simultaneously. The analytical dimensions themselves draw on earlier single-corpus studies (Dodge et al., 2021 for C4; Gao et al., 2020 for The Pile) and data-documentation frameworks (Datasheets, Data Statements), but no prior work performed these measurements in a directly comparable, multi-corpus setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings—roughly 50% duplicate documents in RedPajama and LAION-2B-en, widespread contamination of GLUE/SuperGLUE benchmarks, and billions of PII instances—undermine confidence in many published results built on these corpora and provide concrete, actionable evidence for the data-documentation movement. The open-sourced toolkit establishes a practical standard for auditing new corpora before training, lowering the barrier to transparency that has been a persistent gap in the field. Limitations include coverage of only English corpora, contamination measured as an upper bound via exact-match search, and variable PII-detection precision (as low as 7% for phone numbers in LAION-2B-en). Open questions include extending the framework to non-English and multimodal corpora, integrating these analyses into automated curation pipelines (as was done for Dolma), and developing more robust contamination detection that goes beyond surface-form matching.
