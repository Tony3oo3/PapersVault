## Problem

Selective classification (SC) and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection each have well-established theoretical foundations, but their unification—SCOD, which rejects both hard in-distribution and out-of-distribution samples—lacks principled formulations. Existing SCOD methods like SIRC combine SC and OOD scores heuristically (e.g., multiplicatively), with no guarantee of optimality and no clear characterization of when such combinations fail. The paper asks what the Bayes-optimal rejection rule looks like and how to estimate it in practice.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241937/cropped_page26_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes labeled in-distribution (ID) samples and, in the loss-based variant, an additional unlabeled 'wild' mixture of ID and OOD samples plus a small set of certified strictly-inlier samples for estimating the mixture proportion. It produces a classifier h: X → Y and a binary rejector r: X → {0,1}; the rejector's decision is governed by a sample-dependent threshold that adaptively weights the SC confidence score and the ID/OOD density ratio, rather than applying a single fixed cutoff. In the black-box variant, any off-the-shelf SC score (e.g., max softmax probability) and OOD score (e.g., gradient norm) can be plugged in without retraining.

## Method

The core contribution is a Bayes-optimal SCOD rejector (Lemma 3.1) that rejects a sample when (1−c_in−c_out)·(1−max_y P_in(y|x)) + c_out·P_out(x)/P_in(x) exceeds a threshold c_in, revealing that the optimal rule is a sample-dependent variant of maximum softmax probability. Two plug-in estimators instantiate this: (i) a black-box rejector that additively combines any SC score and OOD score via the derived formula, and (ii) a loss-based rejector that jointly trains a multi-class classifier and a binary ID/OOD discriminator under a decoupled softmax+sigmoid cross-entropy loss, then transforms the outputs into the required class-probability and density-ratio estimates. The approach is shown to be a principled correction of SIRC's multiplicative combination, and excess-risk bounds (Lemma 4.1) guarantee that the plug-in estimator's sub-optimality is controlled by the estimation errors of the individual scores.

## Results

The paper evaluates SCOD performance using AUC-RC (Area Under the Risk-Coverage Curve; lower is better) across multiple OOD datasets. In the setting where training and test OOD distributions match (Table 3, CIFAR-100 ID), the loss-based plug-in estimator (Plug-in LB*) achieves the best AUC-RC across all six OOD datasets, ranging from 0.199 (Places) to 0.225 (OpenImages), outperforming the best baseline SIRC[Res] (0.289–0.383) by 8–19 points. In the harder setting where training and test OOD distributions differ (Table 2), the black-box plug-in with residual scorer (Plug-in BB[Res]) achieves the lowest AUC-RC on SVHN (0.205) and LSUN-R (0.321), while Plug-in LB* excels on LSUN (0.243) and LSUN-R (0.187). On ImageNet with ID-only training (Table 4), Plug-in BB[Res] yields notable gains on 5 of 8 OOD datasets, e.g., 0.145 on Texture vs. 0.178 for SIRC[Res] and 0.241 for MSP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary SCOD competitor SIRC (Xia & Bouganis, 2022), the plug-in estimators consistently achieve lower AUC-RC: in Table 3, Plug-in LB* beats SIRC[Res] by 37–168 points (e.g., 0.199 vs. 0.333 on Places; 0.215 vs. 0.383 on LSUN-R), and Plug-in BB[Res] beats SIRC[Res] on SVHN (0.205 vs. 0.258) and LSUN-R (0.321 vs. 0.383). Against stand-alone OOD baselines (MSP, MaxLogit, Energy, DOCTOR), the plug-in methods dominate across all three experimental settings, with Plug-in LB* achieving AUC-RC values 80–190 points lower than MSP in Table 3. Among methods that use OOD training data (CCE*, DCE*, OE*), Plug-in LB* outperforms all three on every OOD dataset in Table 3 (e.g., 0.199 vs. 0.220 for DCE* on Places; 0.209 vs. 0.226 for DCE* on LSUN). In the distribution-shift setting (Table 2, P_out^tr ≠ P_out^te), the black-box plug-in estimators that use only ID data often outperform the loss-based method, suggesting the decoupled loss degrades under OOD mismatch. On ImageNet with ID-only training (Table 4), Plug-in BB[Res] achieves the best AUC-RC on 5 of 8 OOD datasets (e.g., 0.145 on Texture vs. 0.178 for SIRC[Res]), though it underperforms on iNaturalist-O (0.252 vs. 0.175) and OpenImages-O (0.378 vs. 0.313), where the gradient-norm OOD scorer is less effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously disjoint literatures—selective classification (Chow's rule, Bartlett & Wegkamp) and OOD detection (MSP, decoupled losses, outlier exposure)—under the SCOD framework introduced by Kim et al. (2021) and Xia & Bouganis (2022). The key departure is from SIRC (Xia & Bouganis, 2022), which heuristically combines SC and OOD scores multiplicatively; here the authors derive the Bayes-optimal rejector and show it requires an additive combination of a sample-dependent confidence threshold and a density-ratio term. The plug-in estimators are modular, leveraging any off-the-shelf SC score (e.g., MSP) and OOD score (e.g., gradient norm, residual) without retraining, and in the loss-based setting they adapt the decoupled loss of Bitterwolf et al. (2022) with the wild-data protocol of Katz-Samuels et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous statistical formulation and Bayes-optimal solution for SCOD, filling a gap where prior methods were purely heuristic. A notable negative result is that standard MSP/Chow's rule can yield arbitrarily bad rejection decisions for SCOD, even in open-set classification, because it ignores the ID-OOD density ratio. Open questions include developing better density-ratio estimators (the current grad-norm scorers are weak on some datasets), handling distribution mismatch between training and test OOD populations (where the loss-based method degrades), and unifying SCOD with explicit misclassification detection. The modular plug-in design means practitioners can immediately benefit by swapping in stronger individual scorers without changing the combination rule.
