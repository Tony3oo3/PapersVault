## Problem

Linear policies in reinforcement learning are inherently interpretable but lack the expressivity to solve complex control and navigation tasks, while standard ReLU MLPs—though piecewise-linear—induce an enormous, uncontrollable number of linear sub-policies that defeat interpretability. The paper asks whether one can design a piecewise-linear policy architecture with a controllably small number of linear sub-policies that retains the interpretability advantages of linear models while approaching the performance of full neural baselines.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a proprioceptive state vector (joint positions, velocities, and related physical variables) as input, and in navigation tasks additionally receives a goal state. Beyond the standard continuous action output (mean of a squashed normal), the architecture explicitly produces two interpretable quantities at every timestep: a one-hot sub-policy selection (which of d_G linear experts is active) and the full vector of linear coefficients applied to the input. This dual output structure—discrete expert choice plus transparent linear weights—is what enables the two levels of interpretability (high-level sub-policy sequences and low-level coefficient inspection) that the paper exploits for visualization.

## Method

The HyperCombinator (HC) decomposes the policy into two explicit modules: a Gumbel network (MLP followed by a Gumbel-Softmax layer with the straight-through estimator) that hard-assigns each input to one of d_G sub-policies, and a linear hypernetwork layer that maps the selected sub-policy index to its coefficient vector. The pre-activation output is then simply the dot product of these coefficients with the input, π̃(x) = ⟨θ̂(â(x)), x⟩, making the policy piecewise-linear with exactly d_G linear regions whose count is directly controlled by the Gumbel-Softmax dimension. The straight-through estimator guarantees one-hot (hard) assignments during both training and evaluation, ensuring the agent always interacts through a single linear sub-policy. Training uses standard actor-critic algorithms (SAC for control, RIS for navigation) with the HC as the actor, plus regularization to prevent sub-policy collapse: entropy maximization on the batch-averaged assignment, weight decay on the Gumbel network's final layer, and an elevated Gumbel temperature. The log-standard-deviation head remains a separate MLP, so only the action mean is constrained to the piecewise-linear form.

## Results

On the DeepMind Control Suite (23 environments), HC8, HC64, and SAC achieve Inter-Quartile Mean (IQM) normalized scores of 0.81 (95% CI: 0.78–0.83), 0.85 (0.83–0.87), and 0.87 (0.86–0.88) respectively. HC8 performs at most 3% worse than SAC in 57% of tested environments, while HC64 achieves this in 78%. In maze navigation, HC64 matches RIS success rates across all four mazes, while HC8 and HC16 solve the U-shaped maze most of the time and HC16 solves the S-shaped maze, though all HC variants require more environment interactions than RIS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SAC (the primary baseline in control), HC8 and HC64 approach or match performance in most of the 23 DM Control environments, with HC64's IQM confidence interval overlapping SAC's, indicating a very small performance cost for restricting the policy to 64 linear sub-policies. HC8, with only 8 sub-policies, shows a modest but consistent gap, performing at most 3% worse in 57% of environments. In navigation, HC64 matches RIS success rates across all four mazes, while HC8 and HC16 solve the simpler U-shaped and S-shaped mazes but with higher failure rates and reduced sample efficiency compared to RIS. Against the programmatic policy π-PRL (adapted to SAC), HC outperforms it in Cheetah run, where π-PRL converges to a linear architecture and plateaus below an episode return of 400, while HC8 reaches comparable returns to SAC. Overall, the paper demonstrates that the interpretability constraints of HC come at a moderate and controllable performance cost, with the gap narrowing as the number of sub-policies increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

The HyperCombinator extends the piecewise-linear policy architecture of Akrour et al. (2018) by replacing the soft softmax-based sub-policy selection with a Gumbel-Softmax layer and straight-through estimator, guaranteeing a hard partition where exactly one linear sub-policy is active at each timestep. Conceptually, HC can be viewed as a hypernetwork (Ha et al., 2017) that predicts the coefficients of a linear policy, or as a mixture of linear experts (Jacobs et al., 1991) with a controllable number of experts. It also draws on SENN (Alvarez Melis & Jaakkola, 2018), which uses a similar self-explaining architecture for supervised learning, but HC structurally enforces piecewise-constant coefficients and is designed for the RL setting where the policy must interact with a dynamic environment. Unlike programmatically interpretable RL methods (PiRL, LMUT) that learn decision trees or finite state machines, HC retains the full partitioning flexibility of a deep MLP while capping the number of unique linear sub-policies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HC provides a practical, drop-in architecture for actor-critic algorithms that offers a tunable performance-interpretability trade-off via a single hyperparameter (d_G), making it accessible to practitioners who need auditable policies without sacrificing too much performance. The two levels of interpretability—low-level (exact linear coefficients per decision) and high-level (sub-policy sequences revealing temporal abstractions)—enable visualizations that are impossible with standard neural policies. Key limitations remain: the assignment function is itself a neural network (violating P2), the policy provides no causal or feature-importance explanations (violating P6), and performance gaps widen on harder tasks like maze navigation. Open questions include whether the assignment function can be made interpretable, whether the approach scales to high-dimensional sensory inputs beyond proprioception, and whether the non-continuity at region boundaries causes issues in safety-critical deployments.
