## Problem

Dynamics models trained by supervised fitting on offline data struggle to generalize to out-of-distribution state-action pairs, and existing offline MBRL methods (MOPO, MOBILE, RAMBO) are ultimately bounded by the static prediction accuracy of the learned model. The paper identifies a hidden invariant — a 'dynamics reward' that is consistent across all true transitions regardless of the behavior policy — as a pathway to decouple rollout fidelity from the model's raw prediction quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240339/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a standard offline dataset of (s, a, r_task, s') transitions and a known task reward function (or incorporates it into the dynamics model if unknown). It produces three artifacts: (1) a dynamics reward function r^D(s,a,s') implemented as an ensemble of T discriminators outputting a scalar fidelity score per transition, (2) high-fidelity model rollouts generated by sampling M×N candidate next states per step and selecting via a softmax over dynamics rewards with temperature κ, and (3) a learned policy optimized on the filtered rollouts. The rollout horizon is extended to 100 steps (vs. 1–5 in baselines), with early termination triggered when r^D falls below a preset threshold r_min^D = 0.6.

## Method

MOREC reframes the dynamics model as an 'agent' operating in an augmented state-action space S×A with action space S, then applies GAIL-style inverse reinforcement learning to recover a reward function r^D(s,a,s') = −log(1−D(s,a,s')) that the true dynamics maximizes. The discriminator ensemble is trained via gradient-descent-ascent (Algorithm 2), with the inner adversarial dynamics model updated by PPO and the outer discriminator by binary classification, yielding an O(1/√T) convergence guarantee (Proposition 1). During policy learning, the dynamics reward acts as a transition filter: at each rollout step, M ensemble models each produce N resampled next states, and the selected next state is drawn from a softmax distribution over all M×N candidates weighted by exp(r^D/κ), effectively rejecting low-fidelity transitions. This filtering mechanism is model-agnostic and plugs directly into existing offline MBRL frameworks (instantiated as MOREC-MOPO and MOREC-MOBILE), reducing compounding errors over long rollouts without modifying the underlying policy optimization.

## Results

On 12 D4RL locomotion tasks, MOREC-MOPO achieves an average normalized return of 83.7 and MOREC-MOBILE achieves 82.8, outperforming the previous SOTA MOBILE (80.0) by 4.6%. On the more challenging 9 NeoRL tasks, MOREC-MOBILE achieves 76.4 average return, a 25.9% improvement over MOBILE (60.7). MOREC is the first method to solve 6/12 D4RL tasks and 3/9 NeoRL tasks (score ≥ 95), whereas no prior method solved any NeoRL task. Overall, MOREC outperforms all prior SOTA methods on 18 out of 21 tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MOREC outperforms all 8 baselines on average across both benchmarks. The closest competitor is MOBILE (Sun et al., 2023), the previous SOTA for offline MBRL: MOREC-MOPO improves over MOPO from 70.3 to 83.7 on D4RL and from 28.5 to 70.3 on NeoRL, while MOREC-MOBILE improves over MOBILE from 80.0 to 82.8 on D4RL and from 60.7 to 76.4 on NeoRL. Among model-free baselines, EDAC (76.0) is the strongest on D4RL but still trails MOREC by 7.7 points; CQL (61.6) and TD3+BC (58.2) are substantially weaker. On NeoRL, the gap is even more pronounced: MOREC-MOBILE (76.4) exceeds the best prior method MOBILE (60.7) by 15.7 points, and is the first method to solve any NeoRL task (3/9 vs. 0/9 for all baselines). MOREC also surpasses the sequence-modeling approach TT (62.3 on D4RL) and the adversarial model-based RAMBO (67.7 on D4RL) by wide margins.

## Relation to Prior Work

MOREC extends the offline model-based RL lineage (MOPO, MOBILE, RAMBO) by introducing a fundamentally different mechanism for handling model errors: rather than penalizing uncertainty or truncating rollouts, it learns a dynamics reward via GAIL-style adversarial training and uses it as a transition filter during model rollouts. The dynamics reward learning objective is directly adapted from GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016), but repurposed to score the fidelity of next-state predictions rather than to train a policy. The paper also draws on adversarial model learning (VirtualTaobao, Xu et al.) but departs by using a discriminator ensemble for improved generalization and by decoupling the reward from the model training loop, making it a plug-in component for any existing offline MBRL framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is a modular transition-filtering mechanism that can be inserted into any offline MBRL method, yielding substantial gains (25.9% on NeoRL, solving 3 previously unsolved tasks) without altering the underlying policy optimization. The conceptual finding that an IRL-learned dynamics reward generalizes to OOD regions better than the dynamics model's own predictions challenges the common assumption that model uncertainty is the best proxy for model reliability. A noted limitation is the memory cost of maintaining a discriminator ensemble, which may constrain scalability to high-dimensional state-action spaces; the authors suggest last-iterate convergence methods as a potential remedy. Open questions include whether the approach extends to partially observable or stochastic environments and whether the dynamics reward can be learned more efficiently without the full adversarial training loop.
