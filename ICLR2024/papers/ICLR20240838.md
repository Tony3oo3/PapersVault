## Problem

Despite widespread use of GNNs for link prediction, no prior work has systematically studied why different nodes within the same graph achieve varying LP performance. The conventional assumption that low-degree nodes inherently underperform is contradicted by empirical evidence on large-scale social networks, where performance does not monotonically increase with degree. This paper asks: what local topological property actually governs a node's LP performance in GNNs?

## Input / Output

The method consumes an attributed graph (adjacency matrix, node feature matrix) with edges partitioned into training/validation/testing sets, and produces per-node Topological Concentration scores that quantify the average intersection between a node's K-hop computation tree and each neighbor's computation tree, with exponential hop-discounting. The scalable ATC variant replaces explicit set-intersection counting with dot-product similarity between Gaussian-random-projected embeddings propagated via row-normalized graph diffusion, reducing complexity from O(K²|V|²) to O(Kd(|E|+|V|)). The reweighting module outputs a modified adjacency matrix where edge weights are updated iteratively based on link-prediction scores between a node's neighborhood embedding and each neighbor's embedding.

## Method

The core contribution is Topological Concentration (TC), defined as the expected ratio of observed to possible intersections between a node's K-hop computation tree and each of its neighbors' computation trees, capturing how densely a node's local subgraph overlaps with its neighbors' subgraphs. The paper proves TC achieves 82.10% higher Pearson correlation with node-level LP performance than degree and identifies a novel Topological Distribution Shift (TDS) where temporally later neighbors become less interactive with a node's existing neighbors, degrading test-time generalizability. For scalability, Approximated TC (ATC) is derived via a theorem showing that 1-layer TC is linearly correlated with the mean dot-product similarity of 1-layer SAGE embeddings initialized from Gaussian random projection, enabling efficient computation via power-iteration graph diffusion. Finally, a TC-inspired edge reweighting strategy updates the adjacency matrix during message-passing by assigning higher weights to neighbors whose link-prediction scores with the node's average neighborhood embedding are larger, theoretically proven to increase 1-layer TC and empirically boosting LP performance across GCN, SAGE, and BUDDY baselines.

## Results

The paper's edge-reweighting strategy (rw) consistently improves LP performance across most baselines and datasets. GCN_rw achieves the largest relative gains, e.g., 75.98 vs 70.63 Hits@100 on Cora (+5.35) and 74.40 vs 65.96 on Citeseer (+8.44). SAGE_rw similarly improves Citeseer from 61.57 to 69.89 Hits@100 (+8.32). On the large-scale Collab dataset, GCN_rw reaches 52.85 Hits@50 (vs 49.52) and BUDDY_rw reaches 56.17 (vs 55.73). The paper also demonstrates that TC correlates with LP performance 82.10% more strongly than node degree, and identifies a novel Topological Distribution Shift (TDS) issue that degrades test-time generalizability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper applies its TC-inspired edge reweighting to four GNN baselines (GCN, SAGE, NCN, BUDDY) and evaluates on seven datasets. GCN_rw shows the most dramatic improvements, gaining +5.35 Hits@100 on Cora and +8.44 on Citeseer over vanilla GCN, confirming that baselines without explicit common-neighbor modeling benefit most from TC-enhanced aggregation. SAGE_rw similarly improves Citeseer by +8.32 Hits@100 and Reptile by +1.76. NCN_rw yields only marginal gains (e.g., +0.22 on Cora, +0.93 on Citeseer) because NCN already explicitly models common-neighbor signals in its decoder, making the reweighting redundant. BUDDY_rw achieves consistent small improvements across all datasets (+0.06 to +2.51), with the largest gain on Reptile (+2.51). The only clear loss is NCN_rw on Collab (54.16 vs 54.43, −0.27) and GCN_rw on Pubmed (68.87 vs 69.35, −0.48), both within noise. Overall, the reweighting strategy wins or ties on 30 out of 32 non-OOM comparisons, validating that enhancing TC through edge reweighting is a broadly effective but not universally causal intervention for LP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the subgraph-interaction framework from Zhang & Chen (2018) on GNN-based link prediction by formalizing how the overlap between a node's local computation tree and its neighbors' trees predicts that node's LP performance. It departs from prior work on GNN performance variation (which focused on node classification and degree bias) by showing that degree is a poor proxy for node-level LP quality, and introduces Topological Concentration (TC) and its scalable approximation ATC (built on Gaussian random projection and graph diffusion) as superior topological metrics. The edge-reweighting strategy for boosting LP performance connects to the common-neighbor signal exploited by NCN and BUDDY, but reframes it through the TC lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the widely-held assumption that low-degree nodes inherently suffer worse LP performance, showing instead that topological concentration (neighbor interconnectivity) is the more predictive factor, with practical implications for identifying and remediating underperforming nodes in recommendation systems. The discovery of Topological Distribution Shift (TDS) — where newly joined neighbors become less interactive with existing neighbors over time — opens a new axis of analysis for dynamic/temporal link prediction. Key limitations include the non-causal relationship between TC and LP performance (a node with maximum TC^Train can still fail to generalize to test neighbors), and the fact that the edge-reweighting gains are modest for methods like NCN that already explicitly model common neighbors. Open questions include establishing causal links between TC and LP, and leveraging TC for adaptive data valuation in streaming settings.
