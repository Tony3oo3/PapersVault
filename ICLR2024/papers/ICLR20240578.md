## Problem

Most interpretability research examines only fully trained models, leaving the developmental dynamics of interpretable artifacts unexplored. In particular, whether Syntactic Attention Structure (SAS)—specialized attention heads tracking syntactic relations—is causally necessary for grammatical capabilities, or merely a training side effect, has not been established. This paper asks how SAS emerges during MLM pretraining, whether it drives the acquisition of linguistic capabilities, and what role simplicity bias plays in the competition between SAS and alternative learning strategies.

## Input / Output

The model is a BERT_Base (12 layers, 768 dims) pretrained on BookCorpus and English Wikipedia with 15% token masking at fixed sequence length 512. Dependency parse labels from spaCy are used exclusively for the syntactic regularizer (not the MLM objective itself), providing silver-standard parent-child word pairs. Evaluation outputs include UAS computed from attention maps via a best-head-per-relation probe on Penn Treebank, BLiMP accuracy via MLM pseudo-log-likelihood scoring on minimal pairs, and GLUE fine-tuning scores—each tracked across training checkpoints to reveal phase transitions.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a syntactic regularizer added to the MLM loss that rewards (λ<0) or penalizes (λ>0) attention weight between syntactically connected words, where the syntacticity score γ(x_i,x_j) is the maximum attention weight across all heads and layers in both directions. Phase transitions are identified via a break-detection criterion (Eq. 2) that finds the point of maximum second-difference in a metric trajectory. The paper demonstrates that SAS spikes abruptly at a consistent 'structure onset' (~20K–30K steps), immediately preceding a 'capabilities onset' in BLiMP accuracy, and that suppressing SAS throughout training prevents grammatical capabilities from emerging. A multi-stage protocol—suppressing SAS for the first 3K steps then releasing the regularizer—outperforms baseline BERT_Base, revealing that SAS competes with an alternative long-range semantic strategy and that the phase transition constitutes a critical learning period where optimizer changes are most damaging.

## Results

At 100K training steps (~13M tokens), the multistage model BERT_SAS⁻⁽³ᵏ⁾ (SAS suppressed for the first 3K steps only) achieves the best MLM loss (1.75±0.01) and BLiMP accuracy (0.75±0.01), slightly outperforming BERT_Base (1.77±0.01 loss, 0.74±0.02 BLiMP) while matching its GLUE score (0.74). Promoting SAS throughout training (BERT_SAS⁺) severely degrades performance, with MLM loss rising to 2.39±0.03 and GLUE dropping to 0.59±0.01. Suppressing SAS throughout (BERT_SAS⁻) prevents the emergence of grammatical capabilities, reducing BLiMP to 0.67±0.03 and GLUE to 0.69±0.02, confirming SAS is necessary for complex linguistic phenomena.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods but instead ablates the role of Syntactic Attention Structure (SAS) through a syntactic regularizer applied during training. The four variants—BERT_Base, BERT_SAS⁺ (SAS promoted), BERT_SAS⁻ (SAS suppressed), and BERT_SAS⁻⁽³ᵏ⁾ (SAS suppressed only for the first 3K steps)—serve as the comparison set. BERT_SAS⁻⁽³ᵏ⁾ is the only variant that matches or exceeds BERT_Base on all three metrics, suggesting that briefly suppressing the simplistic syntactic strategy early in training allows the model to acquire a competing long-range contextual strategy before SAS emerges, ultimately improving convergence. BERT_SAS⁺ demonstrates that over-promoting SAS is actively harmful (GLUE drops by 15 points), while BERT_SAS⁻ shows that fully suppressing SAS blocks the capabilities onset entirely (BLiMP drops by 7 points). The paper further shows that the timing of releasing the regularizer matters critically: releasing it during the alternative strategy phase transition produces the worst model quality across all metrics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAS measurement methodology from Clark et al. (2019) and the specialized-heads analysis of Voita et al. (2019) by shifting from static, single-checkpoint interpretability to a developmental lens that tracks interpretable artifacts throughout training. It introduces a syntactic regularizer (Eq. 1) as a causal intervention tool during pretraining, building on the simplicity bias literature (Shah et al., 2020; Nakkiran et al., 2019) and the phase-transition/emergence framework (Olsson et al., 2022; Srivastava et al., 2022). The paper departs from prior causal interpretability work (Vig et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2023), which intervenes at test time on fully trained models, by intervening during training to establish whether SAS is necessary for *learning* grammatical capabilities rather than merely for *expressing* them at inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that briefly suppressing a human-interpretable structure (SAS) early in training improves downstream quality challenges the assumption that interpretable artifacts are always beneficial, suggesting that simplicity bias can be actively harmful when the 'simple' solution competes with more robust strategies. The observation that the worst moment to change hyperparameters is during a phase transition has practical implications for training schedules and curriculum design. Key open questions include whether these dynamics generalize beyond BERT-Base-scale MLMs to autoregressive models or larger architectures, what the 'alternative strategy' (characterized only as long-range n-gram/semantic modeling) actually is mechanistically, and whether the modest performance gains from early SAS suppression persist at scale or with longer training.
