## Problem

In federated semi-supervised learning (FSSL), existing methods train a single model per client using different objective functions for labeled (cross-entropy) and unlabeled (consistency/pseudo-labeling) data. This causes gradient conflict during aggregation because the two objectives pull parameters in opposing directions, a problem unique to the decentralized FL setting where labeled and unlabeled data reside on different clients. The paper identifies this gradient conflict as the root cause of degraded performance in current FSSL approaches.

## Input / Output

The method takes 32×32 RGB images partitioned across K clients (typically 10) using LDA with γ=0.1 to simulate severe non-IID heterogeneity. Clients are split into fully-labeled (M) and fully-unlabeled (T) subsets, with the unlabeled ratio α=T/K as a key hyperparameter (default 60%). Each client maintains two ResNet-18 models; the supervised model outputs a class label (10 or 100 classes), while the unsupervised model outputs a feature embedding used for instance discrimination. For unlabeled clients, the supervised model generates pseudo-labels via confidence-thresholded predictions rather than ground-truth annotations.

## Method

Twin-sight decouples the learning objective into two parallel models to eliminate gradient conflict: an unsupervised model trained with SimCLR-style instance discrimination (InfoNCE loss) that uses the identical objective on every client regardless of label availability, and a supervised model trained with cross-entropy on labeled clients or confidence-thresholded pseudo-labels on unlabeled clients. The key interaction mechanism is a neighbourhood-preserving constraint that aligns the pairwise feature-distance matrices (Z_s·Z_s^T and Z_u·Z_u^T) computed by the two models via MSE, encouraging both models to preserve the same local geometric structure in their respective feature spaces. Because each model's objective is consistent across all clients, standard FedAvg aggregation applies without modification, and the two models co-guide each other by providing complementary supervised and unsupervised perspectives on the same data. The framework is further validated in a partially-labeled scenario where all clients hold a small labeled fraction (τ=5%) alongside unlabeled data.

## Results

Under the standard FSSL setting (γ=0.1, K=10, 4 fully-labeled and 6 fully-unlabeled clients), Twin-sight achieves 70.06% top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-10, 49.98% on CIFAR-100, 62.94% on SVHN, and 79.95% on Fashion-MNIST, outperforming all baselines. The largest gain is on CIFAR-10, where Twin-sight surpasses the best baseline (FedAvg+FixMatch at 63.58%) by 6.48 percentage points. Twin-sight also converges faster, reaching the target accuracy in 115 communication rounds on CIFAR-10 versus 207 for FedAvg+FixMatch.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Twin-sight outperforms all 10 baselines across all four datasets. On CIFAR-10, it leads FedAvg+FixMatch (63.58%) by 6.48 points and RSCFed (60.78%) by 9.28 points. On CIFAR-100, the margin is narrower—Twin-sight (49.98%) edges out FedAvg+FixMatch (48.73%) by 1.25 points and FedAvg+Freematch (48.67%) by 1.31 points. On SVHN, Twin-sight (62.94%) beats FedAvg+Freematch (59.74%) by 3.2 points and RSCFed (54.50%) by 8.44 points. On Fashion-MNIST, Twin-sight (79.95%) surpasses RSCFed (76.58%) by 3.37 points. Notably, FedIRM produces NaN loss under the severely skewed label distribution (γ=0.1) and cannot be evaluated, while FedProx-based combinations (FedProx+FixMatch, FedProx+Freematch) consistently underperform their FedAvg counterparts, suggesting that the proximal regularization exacerbates gradient conflict in the FSSL setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends federated semi-supervised learning (FSSL) by departing from the single-model paradigm used by prior methods (FedIRM, RSCFed, Fed-Consist) that train one model with mixed labeled/unlabeled objectives. It repurposes contrastive self-supervised learning (SimCLR-style instance discrimination) as the unsupervised branch of a twin-model architecture, and draws on manifold learning (local linear embedding) to design a neighbourhood-preserving constraint that couples the two models. The key conceptual departure is that gradient conflict—identified as the root cause of poor FSSL performance—is eliminated by structurally separating supervised and unsupervised objectives into distinct models rather than combining them in a single parameter space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The twin-model paradigm offers a general recipe for resolving gradient conflict in any federated setting where clients optimize heterogeneous objectives, potentially extending beyond FSSL to multi-task or multi-domain FL. The main limitation acknowledged is the doubled model memory and communication overhead, which the authors flag as a target for future memory-efficient variants. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to more complex label-deficiency patterns (e.g., per-sample partial labels with varying confidence), whether the neighbourhood-preserving constraint scales to larger batch sizes or higher-dimensional feature spaces, and how the method performs under the stricter communication budgets of cross-device FL.
