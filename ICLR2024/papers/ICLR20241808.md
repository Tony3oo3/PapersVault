## Problem

Normalising flows rely on neural networks for their invertible transformations, leading to heavy over-parameterisation that causes memorisation and poor generalisation in low-data regimes (e.g., medical or genomics settings with only hundreds to thousands of samples). While the invertibility requirement constrains architectural choices, there is no inherent need for the scaling and translation functions within coupling layers to be neural networks, yet alternative transformation methods have received limited attention.

## Input / Output

The method consumes D-dimensional tabular vectors (or 8×8 image patches for the CIFAR-10 experiment) and outputs per-sample log-likelihoods in nats and generated samples from the learned density. Within each coupling layer, the input is split into two halves of sizes d and D−d; the kernel operates on the first half to produce a (D−d)-dimensional scaling vector and a (D−d)-dimensional translation vector applied element-wise to the second half. The number of learnable parameters scales linearly with dataset size n (via auxiliary points) rather than being a fixed large count as in neural-network flows.

## Method

Ferumal flows replace the neural-network scaling and translation functions in standard affine coupling layers (RealNVP, Glow) with functions defined as inner products in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). A representer theorem (Proposition 3.1) shows that an optimal solution exists in the form of a linear combination of kernel evaluations at the training points, reducing the infinite-dimensional RKHS optimisation to a finite-dimensional problem over weight matrices A ∈ ℝ^{L×n×2(D−d)}. To control computational cost and prevent overfitting, N ≪ n learnable auxiliary points are introduced (initialised as a random subset of training inputs), reducing parameters to 2NDL and optionally further via cross-layer sharing. The kernel (RBF or Matérn) and its hyperparameters are learned end-to-end with Adam, and the full flow is trained by maximum likelihood via the change-of-variables formula. This yields up to 93% fewer parameters than the neural-network counterparts while matching or exceeding their log-likelihood, with particularly strong gains in the low-data regime.

## Results

On five real-world tabular benchmarks, FF-Glow (the kernelised Glow) achieves log-likelihoods of 0.35 (Power), 10.75 (Gas), −17.11 (Hepmass), −10.76 (MiniBoone), and 154.71 (BSDS300) nats, outperforming its neural-network counterpart Glow on four of five datasets and matching it on BSDS300. FF-RealNVP similarly surpasses RealNVP on all five datasets (e.g., 9.55 vs 8.33 on Gas, −11.19 vs −13.55 on MiniBoone). In the low-data regime (500 samples), FF-RealNVP outperforms FFJORD by up to 20.9 nats (BSDS300: 121.22 vs 100.32) while using 93% fewer parameters (8K vs 3,127K).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against its direct neural-network counterparts, FF-Glow outperforms Glow on Power (0.35 vs 0.17), Gas (10.75 vs 8.15), Hepmass (−17.11 vs −18.92), and MiniBoone (−10.76 vs −11.35), while Glow narrowly edges FF-Glow on BSDS300 (155.07 vs 154.71); FF-RealNVP beats RealNVP on all five datasets. Against the more expensive non-coupling baselines, FF-Glow is competitive with MAF (winning on 4/5 datasets) but trails FFJORD on 4 of 5 datasets (e.g., −17.11 vs −14.92 on Hepmass), though FFJORD requires a full-form Jacobian and substantially more compute. FF-Glow decisively outperforms all Gaussianisation-based methods (GF, GIS, RBIG) on most datasets, with RBIG being particularly weak (e.g., −25.41 vs −10.76 on MiniBoone). In the critical low-data regime (500 samples), FF-RealNVP dominates FFJORD across all five datasets by margins of 0.27–20.9 nats while using 4–37× fewer parameters, demonstrating the kernelisation's data-efficiency advantage. Parameter counts are reduced by 63–93% relative to the neural-network counterparts (e.g., 16K vs 228K on Power for FF-RealNVP vs RealNVP).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the coupling-layer normalising flow lineage (RealNVP, Glow) by replacing the neural-network-based scaling and translation functions with kernel machines operating in an RKHS, supported by a representer-theorem variant that makes the non-convex likelihood objective tractable over finite-dimensional parameters. It departs from prior kernel-in-generative-model work (Gaussianisation Flows, RBIG, Sliced INFs, kernel transport operators) which use kernels via per-dimension KDE/CDF inversion or transfer operators, rather than as a drop-in replacement inside coupling layers. The auxiliary-point mechanism for computational efficiency draws an explicit analogy to sparse Gaussian Processes, and the parameter-efficiency motivation connects to NanoFlow's parameter-sharing approach, though the mechanism is fundamentally different.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is most acute in low-data regimes (medical genomics, small clinical cohorts) where over-parameterised NN-based flows memorise training data and fail to generalise; here the kernelised flow achieves substantially better log-likelihood with 10–40× fewer parameters. Open questions include extension beyond coupling-layer architectures (ResFlow, spline flows), the sensitivity of performance to kernel choice and length-scale hyperparameters, and whether the approach scales to high-dimensional structured modalities like images without auxiliary-point tricks. The non-convexity of the multi-layer kernelised objective also leaves room for better optimisation theory and guarantees.
