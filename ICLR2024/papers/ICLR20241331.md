## Problem

Prior work on grokking lacked both a rigorous proof of the phenomenon in a neural network setting and a quantitative explanation for why the transition from memorization to generalization is sharp rather than gradual. This paper addresses both gaps by identifying a dichotomy of early and late phase implicit biases as the provable mechanism behind grokking.

## Input / Output

The theory applies to homogeneous neural networks (L-homogeneous, C²-smooth) trained via gradient flow on empirical loss with L2 weight decay λ = Θ(α^{-p}), starting from a zero-output initialization scaled by a large factor α. The key output is a provably sharp transition at time (1/λ)log α: for any c ∈ (0,1), the normalized model output at time (1−c)/λ log α converges to the NTK kernel predictor, while at time (1+c)/λ log α the parameter direction converges to a KKT point of the max-margin (classification) or min-norm (regression) problem. The sharpness is quantified by the fact that an arbitrarily small c suffices to cross the transition.

## Method

The core mechanism is a dichotomy of implicit biases: large initialization α induces a strong early-phase bias toward the NTK kernel predictor (the network behaves as if optimizing a linearized model), while small weight decay λ induces a late-phase bias toward min-norm/max-margin solutions. The key technical insight is that under weight decay, the parameter norm decays as αe^{−λt} while the direction remains close to initialization, so the model output can be approximated via Taylor expansion as α^L e^{−Lλt}⟨∇f(θ̄_init; x), (e^{λt}/α)θ − θ̄_init⟩, where the large prefactor α^L e^{−Lλt} enables fitting the data even with tiny directional changes. This kernel regime persists until t ≈ (1/λ)log α, after which the scaling factor is no longer large and the dynamics enter the rich regime, where a gradient convergence argument shows that only a slightly longer time suffices to reach a KKT point of the max-margin/min-norm problem. The theory is instantiated with diagonal linear nets for sparse linear classification (where the transition is from max L²-margin to max L¹-margin) and overparameterized matrix factorization for low-rank matrix completion (where the transition is from zero-filling unobserved entries to minimum nuclear norm recovery), and a converse 'misgrokking' phenomenon is constructed by reversing the data-bias alignment.

## Results

The paper proves that for homogeneous neural nets trained with large initialization (scale α) and small weight decay (λ = Θ(α⁻ᵖ)), gradient flow exhibits a provably sharp transition at time (1/λ)log α: before this point, the model behaves as a kernel (NTK) predictor, and after, it converges to a KKT point of the max-margin/min-norm problem. Empirically, on modular addition (p=97, 40% training data, width 1024, lr=0.002, λ=10⁻⁴), training accuracy reaches 100% in ~10⁶ steps while test accuracy remains near random, then jumps to 100% after ~10⁷ steps. On sparse linear classification with diagonal linear nets (n=256, d=10⁵, k=3, initial norm 128, λ=0.001), the same grokking pattern is observed. For matrix completion on a multiplication table (d=97, 5% observed entries, α=10, λ=10⁻⁴), training loss is minimized in ~10¹ steps but the sharp test-loss transition occurs only after ~10⁶ steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper without standard baseline comparisons. The key empirical contrast is between training with small weight decay (sharp grokking transition at ~10⁷ steps) versus without weight decay (non-sharp transition requiring exponentially longer time, from 10¹⁰² to 10¹⁰⁴ in continuous time). The paper also contrasts the kernel-regime predictor (max L²-margin, requiring O(kd) samples to generalize for k-sparse targets) against the rich-regime predictor (max L¹-margin, requiring only O(k² log d) samples), showing that the L²-margin kernel predictor fails to generalize while the L¹-margin predictor succeeds, thereby explaining the grokking mechanism. A novel 'misgrokking' phenomenon is demonstrated where the roles reverse: the kernel predictor generalizes well (100% test accuracy) but the rich-regime predictor destroys generalization (test accuracy drops to ~50%), confirming that the dichotomy of implicit biases is the root cause rather than a specific direction of improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the kernel-vs-rich regime framework (Moroshko et al., 2020; Woodworth et al., 2020; Telgarsky, 2023) by proving that the transition between these regimes is provably sharp under large initialization and small weight decay, a setting qualitatively different from standard NTK analysis where the weight norm remains nearly constant. The kernel regime proof departs from prior NTK-based dynamical analyses (Jacot et al., 2018; Chizat et al., 2019) by carefully tracking norm decay under weight decay while showing the direction stays close to initialization, enabling a tight bound on the kernel regime duration. The rich regime analysis builds on margin maximization and min-norm implicit bias results (Soudry et al., 2018; Lyu and Li, 2020; Arora et al., 2019a) to show convergence to KKT points of the corresponding optimization problems. The paper positions itself as the first to rigorously prove grokking in a neural network setting, going beyond prior empirical observations (Power et al., 2022) and linear-model analyses (Žunkovič and Ilievski, 2022; Levi et al., 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first rigorous proof that grokking occurs in a neural network setting, and it provides a quantitative explanation for why the memorization-to-generalization transition is sharp rather than gradual—a key open question in the grokking literature. The 'misgrokking' prediction (where early-phase bias generalizes well but late-phase bias destroys it) is a novel consequence of the framework that has not been previously identified. Limitations include the restriction to large initialization with small weight decay as the sole source of the bias dichotomy, the C²-smoothness assumption that technically excludes ReLU (though the authors note this can be addressed via Clarke subdifferentials), and the fact that the rich regime result only guarantees KKT points rather than global optima for non-convex problems. Open questions include whether other sources of implicit bias (e.g., sharpness reduction, adaptive optimizers) can produce similarly sharp transitions, and how to practically eliminate the grokking delay.
