## Problem

Shapelets provide interpretable, discriminative subsequences for time-series modeling but suffer from exhaustive search and high time complexity, while CNNs achieve strong performance through learned hidden representations yet remain black-box and uncontrollable. Prior attempts to fuse the two paradigms fail to strike a balance between accuracy and interpretability. This paper asks whether the two are fundamentally compatible and, if so, how to exploit that compatibility in a single differentiable architecture.

## Input / Output

The method accepts univariate or multivariate time series of arbitrary length (multivariate handled by increasing input channels) and, in supervised mode, outputs class labels via an MLP head; in unsupervised mode, it outputs cluster assignments via K-means on the shapelet-transformed distance vector. Critically, the CNN kernels themselves are the learned shapelets—interpretable subsequences of the input that can be visualized and aligned with raw data. The shapelet-transformed distance features (one minimal squared-Euclidean distance per kernel) serve as the intermediate representation, and the layer can be stacked with deeper modules such as GRU for long-sequence tasks like EEG seizure detection.

## Method

The paper proves (Theorem 3.1) that the shapelet transform—computing the minimum squared Euclidean distance between a shapelet and all input subsequences—is algebraically equivalent to a 1-D cross-correlation (CNN forward pass) plus a squared L2-norm term followed by max pooling, up to a constant factor. ShapeConv instantiates this equivalence as a differentiable CNN layer whose kernels are the shapelets, augmented with a squared-norm term before pooling to exactly mirror the shapelet transform. Two regularizers enforce quality: a shaping regularizer (R_shape) penalizes the minimum distance between each kernel and the nearest input subsequence to preserve interpretability, and a diversity regularizer (R_div) uses the Frobenius norm of a pairwise exponential-distance matrix to prevent kernel collapse. Task-specific initialization (class-stratified temporal splitting for supervised; pre-clustering within temporal cuts for unsupervised) and optional human-knowledge injection further guide kernels toward discriminative regions, and the full loss combines a task loss (cross-entropy or Davies-Bouldin Index) with both regularizers.

## Results

On 125 UCR univariate datasets, ShapeConv achieves 85.1% average accuracy (rank 2.39), outperforming the strongest baselines TS2Vec (83.6%) and ROCKET (84.2%), while training in only 0.5 hours—roughly 450× faster than TNC (228.4 h) and 76× faster than T-Loss (38.0 h). On 30 UEA multivariate datasets, ShapeConv reaches 75.0% average accuracy (rank 2.07), surpassing TS2Vec (70.4%) by 4.6 points. For unsupervised clustering on 36 UCR datasets, ShapeConv attains the best average NMI rank among all compared methods, and in the EEG seizure-detection task, ShapeConv+GRU achieves AUROC 0.837 and AUPRC 0.414, exceeding standalone GRU (0.814/0.386) and TCN (0.817/0.383).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ShapeConv outperforms every baseline in the main comparison (Table 1). On 125 UCR datasets it leads TS2Vec by 1.5 points (85.1% vs. 83.6%) and ROCKET by 0.9 points (85.1% vs. 84.2%), while training ~2× faster than both. The gap widens on 30 UEA multivariate datasets, where ShapeConv (75.0%) beats TS2Vec (70.4%) by 4.6 points and T-Loss (65.8%) by 9.2 points. Among shapelet-specific baselines on 25 UCR datasets (Table G.1), ShapeConv (87.8% avg.) surpasses ADSN (86.6%), LTS (83.2%), and FLAG (82.6%), and is ~1000× faster than LTS on the Herring dataset (9 s vs. 3.4 h). For unsupervised clustering on 36 UCR datasets, ShapeConv achieves the best average NMI rank, edging out AutoShape, USSL, and k-Shape; its advantage is most pronounced when human-knowledge initialization is used. In the EEG seizure-detection experiment, ShapeConv+GRU (AUROC 0.837, AUPRC 0.414) beats both GRU (0.814/0.386) and TCN (0.817/0.383), confirming that the shapelet-based first layer adds value even when stacked with a recurrent backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper establishes a formal equivalence between the shapelet transform (Lines et al., 2012) and a specific 1-layer CNN forward pass (cross-correlation + squared L2 norm + max pooling), unifying two historically separate time-series modeling paradigms. It builds directly on the shapelet concept (Ye & Keogh, 2009) and the shapelet transform, while adopting the diversity regularizer from Zhang et al. (2018) and the DBI loss approximation from Li et al. (2022). Unlike prior learning-based shapelet methods (LTS, FLAG, ADSN) that optimize shapelets in isolation or with adversarial objectives, ShapeConv embeds shapelets as learnable CNN kernels within an end-to-end differentiable framework, adding a shaping regularizer to preserve interpretability that prior deep shapelet methods lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical equivalence provides a principled foundation for injecting shapelet-based interpretability into deep time-series models, addressing a long-standing tension between accuracy and explainability in the field. The ability to inject human domain knowledge through kernel initialization (demonstrated on EEG seizure detection) opens a practical pathway for expert-in-the-loop time-series modeling in medical and industrial settings. Limitations include the equivalence being proven for a single 1-layer CNN (deeper stacking is shown empirically but not theoretically), evaluation primarily on univariate UCR/UEA benchmarks, and modest performance gains (~1% over ROCKET/TS2Vec) that may not justify adoption over simpler methods in all settings. Open questions include whether the interpretability guarantees hold in deeper architectures and how the method scales to very long or high-dimensional multivariate sequences.
