## Problem

Weight Balancing (WB) combines weight decay, MaxNorm, and two-stage training to achieve strong long-tailed recognition accuracy, yet the mechanism behind its effectiveness remains unexplained. This paper asks: why does this simple combination of classical regularization techniques outperform more complex LTR methods, and can the training procedure be further simplified based on a principled understanding of each component's role?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241595/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 84×84 for mini-ImageNet, 224×224 for ImageNet) or tabular feature vectors (Helena dataset) whose per-class sample counts follow an exponential (Pareto-like) distribution with imbalance factor ρ=100. It produces a class label from C classes, evaluated on a class-balanced test set where each class has equal representation. The analysis also tracks intermediate feature representations, measuring their Fisher's discriminant ratio, inter-class cosine similarities, and per-class feature norms as diagnostic quantities.

## Method

The paper decomposes WB into five components (WD, MaxNorm, CE, CB, two-stage training) and analyzes each through the lens of neural collapse and the cone effect. Theorem 1 proves that WD and CE suppress the cone effect (high inter-class cosine similarity) when the classifier is an ETF and feature norms are bounded, while WD additionally reduces BN scaling parameters and increases tail-class feature norms. Theorem 2 shows the second stage (WD + CB on a frozen feature extractor) is equivalent to multiplicative logit adjustment, making classifier weight norms proportional to tail-class feature norms. Building on these results, the paper proposes a simplified one-stage method: train the feature extractor with CE, WD, and feature regularization (FR) while fixing the linear layer to an ETF classifier, then apply multiplicative LA post-hoc to adjust classifier weight norms—achieving higher average accuracy than two-stage WB with fewer training stages.

## Results

On CIFAR100-LT, the proposed one-stage method (WD&FR&ETF with multiplicative LA) achieves 53.0% average accuracy and a test FDR of 1.22×10², outperforming the two-stage Weight Balancing baseline (50.6% accuracy, 1.07×10² test FDR) by 2.4 points in accuracy. The method also substantially improves FDR over CE-only training (1.28×10² → 8.85×10⁴ train FDR) and CB-only training (8.17×10¹ → 8.85×10⁴ train FDR). On CIFAR10-LT, the same method reaches 80.1% average accuracy versus WB's 78.8%, confirming the advantage across datasets with different class counts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Class-Balanced Loss (CB, Cui et al. 2019), the proposed WD&FR&ETF + multiplicative LA method achieves 53.0% average accuracy on CIFAR100-LT versus CB's 26.1%, a 26.9-point improvement, with the gap driven primarily by the Few-class accuracy (29.9% vs. 5.61%). Against Weight Balancing (WB, Alshammari et al. 2022), the one-stage WD&FR&ETF + Mult LA method outperforms the two-stage WB by 2.4 points on CIFAR100-LT (53.0% vs. 50.6%) and by 1.3 points on CIFAR10-LT (80.1% vs. 78.8%), while also achieving a higher test FDR (1.22×10² vs. 1.07×10² on CIFAR100-LT). The improvement is most pronounced on the Few-class group (29.9% vs. 25.6% on CIFAR100-LT), confirming that explicit multiplicative LA is more effective than WB's implicit second-stage adjustment, particularly when the number of classes is small. Notably, the proposed method requires only a single training stage plus a post-hoc norm adjustment, eliminating the need for the second-stage classifier retraining that WB employs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides a theoretical decomposition of Weight Balancing (WB), a two-stage method that empirically outperformed complex LTR baselines but lacked mechanistic explanation. It builds on neural collapse theory (Papyan et al., 2020), the cone effect analysis (Liang et al., 2022), and the ETF classifier framework (Yang et al., 2022) to show that WB's first stage (WD + CE) suppresses the cone effect and increases Fisher's discriminant ratio, while its second stage (WD + CB) is provably equivalent to multiplicative logit adjustment (Kim & Kim, 2020) under neural collapse conditions. The paper departs from prior LTR work by reframing a successful empirical recipe as a composition of well-understood classical components, then using that understanding to eliminate the second training stage entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a single-stage training with WD, feature regularization, an ETF classifier, and post-hoc multiplicative LA outperforms the original two-stage WB on average accuracy across multiple datasets, reducing training complexity while improving results. The theoretical results (Theorems 1 and 2) give practitioners clear design guidelines: WD is necessary to suppress the cone effect, and the classifier re-weighting step can be replaced by explicit LA, which is more robust especially for small class counts where WB's implicit LA fails. A key limitation is that experiments are restricted to MLP, ResNet, and ResNeXt architectures; whether these findings transfer to ViT or other modern backbones remains open, as the authors themselves note.
