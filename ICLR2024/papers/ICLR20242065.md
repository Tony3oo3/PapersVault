## Problem

When real-world higher-order relationships (e.g., multi-author papers, multi-person conversations, multiprotein complexes) are represented as pairwise graphs via clique expansion, critical structural information is irreversibly lost. This paper identifies the specific hyperedge patterns (nested hyperedges and uncovered triangles) that cause combinatorially impossible reconstruction from the projected graph alone, and then asks whether a learning-based approach using a same-domain hypergraph sample can recover the lost higher-order structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20242065/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a query projected graph G₁ (undirected, no edge multiplicities—edges merely indicate co-existence in at least one hyperedge) and a training pair (G₀, H₀) from the same application domain, where H₀ is a known hypergraph and G₀ its clique expansion. The output is a set of reconstructed hyperedges approximating the true H₁, evaluated by Jaccard score. The training hypergraph need not match the query in size, and node IDs are randomly re-indexed between splits to enforce inductiveness.

## Method

SHyRe (Supervised Hypergraph Reconstruction) operates in two stages. First, a clique sampler narrows the enormous space of all cliques in G₁ to a manageable candidate set by optimizing sampling ratios r_{n,k} over (maximal-clique-size, subset-size) cells; the optimization is a monotone submodular maximization under a budget constraint, solved greedily (Algorithm 1) with a (1−1/e) approximation guarantee, guided by the key statistic ρ(n,k)—the probability of finding a unique hyperedge by sampling a k-subset from an n-clique—which aligns across same-domain hypergraphs. Second, a hyperedge classifier (MLP, 100 hidden neurons) labels each sampled candidate clique as hyperedge or not, using either 8 interpretable count-based structural features or 52 features derived from 13 'clique motifs' (connectivity patterns between the target clique's nodes and surrounding maximal cliques). The entire pipeline is trained on (G₀, H₀) and applied to G₁, supporting supervised, semi-supervised (20% training data), and transfer learning settings.

## Results

SHyRe (Supervised Hypergraph Reconstruction) is evaluated on 8 real-world datasets using Jaccard score as the reconstruction metric. SHyRe-motif achieves the best overall performance, reaching 81.19% on DBLP, 16.02% on Enron, 43.06% on P.School, and 54.39% on H.School, significantly outperforming all baselines on 7 out of 8 datasets. The largest gains appear on the hardest datasets: P.School (43.06% vs. 24.32% for the best baseline HPRA) and H.School (54.39% vs. 34.30% for HPRA). The only dataset where SHyRe underperforms is Foursquare (74.56% for SHyRe-count vs. 79.89% for Clique Covering), while on Directors all methods achieve a perfect 100%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SHyRe outperforms all 8 baselines on 6 of 8 datasets and ties on Directors (all methods reach 100%), losing only on Foursquare where Clique Covering (79.89%) edges out SHyRe-count (74.56%). The most dramatic wins are on P.School and H.School, where SHyRe-motif achieves 43.06% and 54.39% Jaccard respectively, nearly doubling the best baseline HPRA (24.32% and 34.30%). On Enron, SHyRe-motif (16.02%) more than doubles the best baseline HPRA (10.25%), while on DBLP the margin is smaller (81.19% vs. 79.13% for Maximal Clique). Community detection methods (CFinder, Demon) and hyperedge prediction methods (Hyper-SAGNN, CMM) perform very poorly, often below 10% Jaccard, because they are fundamentally mismatched to the reconstruction task: they either cannot generate hyperedges from a projected graph or require a partially observed hypergraph as input. Bayesian-MDL and Clique Covering are the strongest non-learning baselines but still trail SHyRe on most datasets, particularly where hyperedge overlap is dense (high node degree).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hypergraph reconstruction problem introduced by Young et al. (2021), which used a Bayesian-MDL probabilistic model to find minimum clique covers of projected graphs. The key departure is identifying that the 'principle of parsimony' underlying minimum clique cover is fundamentally misaligned with real-world hypergraph structure, and instead formulating reconstruction as a supervised learning problem where a training hypergraph from the same domain provides the necessary signal. The theoretical characterization via Sperner and conformal properties (Theorems 1–2) and the ρ(n,k)-alignment statistic with submodular-optimized clique sampling are novel contributions that have no direct precedent in the hyperedge prediction or community detection literature, from which the paper carefully distinguishes its task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a pervasive but under-studied problem: most graph-based analyses of coauthorship, social interaction, and protein networks silently discard higher-order structure through clique expansion, and the lost information is often unrecoverable without domain-specific help. The theoretical impossibility result (Theorem 3) clarifies the fundamental limits, while the practical SHyRe method demonstrates that even modest training data from the same domain can substantially improve reconstruction (e.g., Enron Jaccard from ~6.6% to ~16%). Open questions include whether ρ(n,k)-alignment holds across more diverse domain shifts, how the method scales to very large hypergraphs, and whether the approach extends to other projection types (star, line) mentioned as future work.
