## Problem

Multi-modal language models for videos have focused on high-level question answering and instruction following, but they fall short on tasks requiring fine-grained spatiotemporal reasoning—such as causal induction, compositional action recognition, and adversarial object tracking—where predictions must be grounded in low-level details like object motions and interactions. No prior approach has shown how to endow an LM with the requisite low-level visual skills (detection, re-identification, tracking) while preserving its high-level reasoning ability.

## Input / Output

The model receives an interleaved sequence of video frames (each split into 16×16 patches) and tokenized text containing the reasoning question Q and, during training, randomly inserted surrogate-task prompts marked by special tokens (<detect>, <re-identify>, <track>). At inference, only the question and frames are needed; the model autoregressively generates a text answer that may include intermediate object descriptions (IDs, classes, bounding boxes as 4-tuples, or grid positions on a 6×6 grid) before the final answer. The LM backbone is from the OPT family (125M–1.3B parameters), and the two-stream encoder uses a temporal buffer of τ previous frames for motion capture.

## Method

The LRR architecture combines a two-stream video encoder exploiting divided space-time attention (spatial attention over all patches in a frame for structure; temporal attention over the same spatial position across τ previous frames for motion) with a pre-trained OPT LM backbone modified by inserting top-down cross-attention layers between self-attention layers, so that the LM's hidden states query the video encoder's patch embeddings to extract task-relevant low-level features. The key training mechanism is stochastic surrogate-task grounding: at 30% probability after each frame during training, the model is prompted to solve object detection, re-identification, or tracking tasks, forcing it to ground its representations in fine-grained visual details. This implements the 'Look, Remember, Reason' paradigm—low-level information is extracted step-by-step (Look), retained in the LM's context window (Remember), and aggregated for the final answer (Reason)—without requiring surrogate tasks at inference, yielding an order-of-magnitude speedup over chain-of-thought-style prompting. The entire system is trained end-to-end via maximum likelihood on the interleaved text sequence, leveraging the pre-trained LM's high-level reasoning while the surrogate tasks instill the necessary low-level visual capabilities.

## Results

On ACRE, LRR achieves 98.2% accuracy on the compositional split and 99.2% on the systematic split, surpassing the prior best (ALOE) by 6.5% and 5.3% respectively. On Something-Else, LRR reaches 62.0% Top-1 on the compositional split (vs. 56.2% for STIN+OIE+NL). On CATER, LRR attains 84.1% Top-1 (static camera) and 80.4% Top-1 (moving camera), outperforming TFC V3D by 4.4% and ALOE by 20.7% on the respective splits. On STAR, LRR achieves 70.5% overall accuracy, exceeding SeViLA (64.9%) by 5.6% despite using a smaller model (OPT-350M vs. 4.1B BLIP + 3B Flan-T5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LRR outperforms all 13 baselines across all four benchmarks. On ACRE, LRR beats the strongest prior method ALOE (Ding et al., 2021) by 6.5% on the compositional split and 5.3% on the systematic split, and surpasses the multi-modal LLM OpenFlamingo (Awadalla et al., 2023) by approximately 60% absolute accuracy, highlighting the critical role of surrogate-task grounding. On Something-Else, LRR exceeds STIN+OIE+NL (Materzynska et al., 2020) by 5.8% Top-1 on the compositional split and Video-ChatGPT (Maaz et al., 2023) by 23.4% Top-1, with the gap attributed to Video-ChatGPT's CLIP-based encoder being ill-suited for motion features. On CATER, LRR surpasses TFC V3D (Zhang, 2022) by 4.4% Top-1 on the static camera split and ALOE by 20.7% Top-1 on the more challenging moving camera split. On STAR, LRR (OPT-350M) outperforms SeViLA (Yu et al., 2023) by 5.6% overall accuracy despite using a substantially smaller model (OPT-350M vs. 4.1B BLIP encoder + 3B Flan-T5 LM) and a much smaller training set, demonstrating the effectiveness of carefully selected surrogate tasks over sheer model scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Flamingo's cross-attention mechanism for integrating visual features into a language model backbone and on divided space-time attention (Bertasius et al., 2021) for the two-stream video encoder. However, it departs fundamentally from prior video-based multi-modal LMs (Video-ChatGPT, VideoChat, Valley, Flamingo) that focus on high-level question answering, by introducing low-level surrogate tasks (object detection, tracking, re-identification) during training to ground the LM to fine-grained spatiotemporal details. Unlike prior spatiotemporal grounding methods (STVGBert, STCAT) that use separate localization modules, or object-centric reasoning architectures like ALOE that require explicit object embeddings as input, LRR instills these low-level visual capabilities directly within the LM through end-to-end training, unifying perception and reasoning in a single autoregressive model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that grounding a language model to low-level visual details via surrogate tasks is not merely helpful but essential for video reasoning — the ablation without surrogate tasks shows a catastrophic ~60-point accuracy drop on ACRE, validating the core hypothesis. The 'Look, Remember, Reason' framework is general-purpose, handling causal, compositional, and situated reasoning across four diverse benchmarks with a single architecture, and the random prompting strategy achieves near-identical accuracy to per-frame prompting at 23× faster inference. Limitations include reliance on relatively small LM backbones (125M–1.3B), dependence on off-the-shelf models for surrogate task ground truth, and evaluation primarily on controlled or semi-synthetic datasets (ACRE, CATER), leaving open whether the approach scales to larger LMs and more complex open-world video understanding.
