## Problem

Previous equivalences between risk-sensitive MDPs and robust MDPs were limited to specific formulations where the uncertainty set depends on the implemented policy, and the classical Markov risk measure is known to lack gradient-dominance, meaning policy gradient methods may fail to reach global optima even in tabular settings. This paper introduces a new risk-sensitive MDP formulation that achieves a stronger, policy-independent equivalence with a broad class of soft robust MDPs (recovering the standard RMDP as a special case), and establishes gradient domination and global convergence properties that were previously unavailable.

## Input / Output

The RFZI algorithm consumes a pre-collected offline dataset of (state, action, reward, next-state) tuples generated under the nominal transition model, along with a user-specified nonlinear function class F for approximating the Z-function. Crucially, the method is model-free: it never requires an explicitly estimated transition probability matrix, instead relying on empirical loss minimization over the dataset. The output is the optimal robust policy π* recovered from the learned Z-function via π*(s) = argmax_a r(s,a) − γβ⁻¹ log Z*(s,a), along with the corresponding value and Q-functions.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is an equivalence theorem showing that a soft RMDP with penalty function D is exactly equivalent to a risk-sensitive MDP with convex risk measure σ, where D and σ are Fenchel conjugates of each other (Theorem 2). This equivalence yields a policy gradient theorem with gradient domination under direct parameterization, proving global convergence of exact policy gradient ascent with an O(|A|M⁴/(1−γ)⁴ε²) iteration bound. For the practical offline learning setting with KL-divergence penalty (equivalently, entropy risk measure), the paper proposes RFZI, which introduces an auxiliary Z-function to eliminate the intractable log(E[·]) term in the Bellman operator, replacing it with an exponential that can be directly estimated from offline samples. The algorithm iteratively minimizes an empirical squared loss over the function class F, and the sample complexity is O(C²(β⁻¹e^{β/(1−γ)})² log|F| / ((1−γ)⁴ε²)) under concentrability and approximate completeness assumptions.

## Results

The paper establishes that the exact policy gradient method under direct parameterization converges globally with an iteration complexity of O(|A|M⁴/(1-γ)⁴) to reach an ε-optimal policy, where M is the distributional shift factor. This contrasts with the standard MDP rate of O(|A|M²/(1-γ)⁴), reflecting a worse (M⁴ vs M²) dependency caused by a smaller required stepsize. For the offline RFZI algorithm on the KL-soft RMDP, the sample complexity is O(C²(β⁻¹e^{β/(1-γ)})² log|F| / ((1-γ)⁴ ε²)), where C is the concentrability constant and β is the penalty strength. The paper also proves gradient domination (Lemma 2), showing all first-order stationary points are globally optimal under sufficient exploration — a property that Markov risk measures are known to potentially lack [39].

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or benchmark comparisons. The key theoretical comparisons are: (1) Against standard MDP policy gradient [1], the soft RMDP achieves the same gradient domination property but with a worse dependency on the distributional shift factor M (M⁴ vs M²), attributed to a smaller stepsize requirement. (2) Against the Markov risk measure framework [71, 39], the proposed formulation achieves a stronger equivalence with RMDPs (policy-independent penalty D vs policy-dependent uncertainty set) and recovers gradient domination, which [39] shows may fail for Markov risk measures even in tabular settings. (3) Against the offline robust RL method of Panaganti et al. [64], RFZI avoids the additional dual variable space (no log|G| term) and requires less computational/implementation complexity, but incurs an exponential factor e^{2β/(1-γ)} and a worse C² (vs C) dependency on concentrability. The paper notes the exponential dependency may be a proof artifact, citing [26] as evidence it could be fundamental.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads—robust MDPs (Iyengar [41], Nilim & El Ghaoui [57]) and risk-sensitive MDPs (Ruszczyński [71], Howard & Matheson [38])—by introducing a 'soft RMDP' formulation with a general penalty function D and a corresponding risk-sensitive MDP defined via convex risk measures (Föllmer & Schied [30]). The key departure from prior work is that the equivalence with RMDPs uses a policy-independent uncertainty set, in contrast to the Markov risk measure framework where the uncertainty set dynamically depends on the policy [71, 86]. For policy optimization, the paper extends the gradient domination and convergence results of Agarwal et al. [1] to the soft RMDP setting, resolving a known gap where Markov risk measures lack gradient domination [39]. The RFZI algorithm builds on the fitted-iteration paradigm of Chen & Jiang [13] and the twice-regularized MDP formulation of Derman et al. [21], but leverages the new equivalence to achieve a simpler, model-free design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The equivalence result provides a clean theoretical bridge that allows techniques from robust optimization (e.g., penalty-based formulations) to be directly applied to risk-sensitive decision-making and vice versa, potentially simplifying algorithm design in both areas. The recovery of gradient domination is practically significant because it means standard policy gradient methods can be applied with global convergence guarantees to this class of risk-sensitive problems, unlike the Markov risk measure setting. However, the policy gradient results are limited to exact gradients under tabular direct parameterization, and the RFZI algorithm is specific to the KL-soft (entropy risk) case. Open questions include whether the worse M^4 dependency in the convergence rate (vs. M^2 for standard MDPs) is fundamental, whether the exponential e^{β/(1−γ)} factor in sample complexity is a proof artifact, and how to extend these results to approximate/sample-based gradients and continuous state-action spaces.
