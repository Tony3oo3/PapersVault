## Problem

Text-to-image diffusion models like Stable Diffusion produce systematically biased images for occupational prompts, over-representing certain genders, races, and their intersections. Existing debiasing methods (prompting, projection, attention editing) are lightweight but struggle to generalize across diverse prompts and cannot control the distribution of generated attributes—only approximately removing bias. This paper frames fairness as a distributional alignment problem, enabling users to specify arbitrary target distributions (e.g., 75% young / 25% old) rather than being limited to absolute equality.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt (e.g., 'a photo of the face of a {occupation}, a person') and produces a 512×512 RGB image via Stable Diffusion v1.5 in latent space. During finetuning, a batch of N generated images is scored by pre-trained face classifiers (gender, race, age) after face detection, and compared against a frozen copy of the original model using the same initial noise. The output is a lightweight adapter (LoRA rank-50 on the text encoder, or as few as 5 soft prompt tokens) that can be swapped onto the base model at inference time.

## Method

The method has two core components. First, a Distributional Alignment Loss (DAL) uses optimal transport between classifier probability vectors of a generated image batch and i.i.d. samples from a user-defined target distribution to dynamically assign target classes and confidence-weighted cross-entropy losses, while CLIP/DINO similarity losses and a face-realism loss (against CelebA/FairFace embeddings) preserve semantics. Second, Adjusted Direct Finetuning (Adjusted DFT) identifies that the exact gradient of the reverse diffusion sampling process explodes in norm and variance due to an exponentially scaling factor B_t from recurrent U-Net evaluations across timesteps; it replaces this with an adjusted gradient that sets B_t = I (treating z_t as independent of θ) and A_t = 1 (standardizing timestep influence), preserving all uncoupled per-step gradients while eliminating cross-step coupling. This enables direct optimization of arbitrary differentiable losses on generated images, applied here to the DAL, and is implemented with DPM-Solver++ (~20 steps) and gradient checkpointing.

## Results

On the joint gender×race debiasing task (Table 1), the proposed method reduces gender bias from 0.67 to 0.16, race bias from 0.42 to 0.09, and intersectional bias from 0.21 to 0.06, while maintaining CLIP-T prompt alignment at 0.39 (matching the original SD). For gender-only debiasing, bias drops to 0.23; for race-only, to 0.12. The method also generalizes to non-templated prompts (Table 2: gender bias 0.60→0.32, race 0.39→0.30) and multi-face generation (Table 3: single-face gender bias 0.46→0.26 for two-person prompts).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method achieves the lowest bias across all three debiasing scenarios (gender, race, and intersectional), substantially outperforming all four baselines. For joint gender×race debiasing, it reduces intersectional bias to 0.06 vs. 0.18 (Ethical Intervention), 0.20 (UCE and Concept Algebra), and 0.24 (debiasVL). Notably, debiasVL actually *increases* gender bias (0.98 vs. 0.67 original) and race bias (0.47 vs. 0.42), making it the worst-performing baseline. UCE and Concept Algebra preserve visual similarity to the original SD best (CLIP-I ≈ 0.83–0.84, DINO ≈ 0.78–0.79) but achieve only marginal bias reduction. The proposed method trades some image-level similarity (CLIP-I 0.67, DINO 0.58 for G.×R.) for dramatically better distributional fairness, while maintaining prompt alignment (CLIP-T 0.39, matching the original SD). The authors argue this trade-off is necessary because some visual alterations are essential for enhancing minority representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on concurrent work by Clark et al. (2023) and Wallace et al. (2023) on direct finetuning of diffusion models, but identifies and resolves a fundamental failure mode: the exact gradient of the sampling process has exponentially scaling and high-variance components (the B_t factor in Eq. 9), rendering naive DFT ineffective. The proposed Adjusted DFT (setting B_t = I and A_t = 1) is a targeted fix that preserves all uncoupled per-timestep gradients while removing cross-timestep coupling. On the fairness side, the paper departs from prompting-based (Friedrich, Bansal, Kim) and projection-based (debiasVL, Concept Algebra, UCE) approaches by reframing debiasing as a distributional alignment problem solved via optimal transport, enabling control over arbitrary target distributions rather than only approximate removal of biased associations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Adjusted DFT is a general-purpose technique applicable to any differentiable loss defined on diffusion model outputs, opening the door to reward-based finetuning beyond fairness. The distributional alignment framework supports diverse fairness perspectives (e.g., 75/25 age splits) and scales to multiple concepts simultaneously. Key limitations include reliance on face-centric classifiers (excluding non-facial cues), binary gender treatment, and categorical attribute assumptions that exclude non-binary and mixed-race identities. Open questions include whether the method extends to cultural biases (e.g., cuisine, clothing), whether the U-Net overfitting-to-classifier failure mode is fundamental, and how to handle the absence of public datasets for non-binary visual representation.
