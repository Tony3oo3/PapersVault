## Problem

While chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting dramatically improves LLM performance on reasoning tasks, the mechanism behind this improvement remains theoretically unexplained. Prior expressiveness results showed constant-depth transformers are limited to TC^0, but these bounds assumed unrealistic infinite-precision arithmetic and did not account for the serial computation that CoT enables. This paper asks: what is the precise computational power gained by allowing a transformer to auto-regressively generate intermediate tokens before answering?

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a sequence of input tokens encoding a problem instance (e.g., a list of permutations to compose, a circuit description in topological order, or a modular arithmetic expression) and produces either a single answer token (base setting) or a sequence of T intermediate CoT tokens followed by the final answer token. The CoT tokens are generated auto-regressively by the same transformer, with each step's output appended to the input for the next step. The theoretical results require only O(log n) embedding dimension to distinguish positions, and the finite-precision model uses constant-bit floating-point arithmetic with iterative rounding after each operation, matching practical IEEE 754 behavior.

## Method

The paper defines a new complexity class CoT[T(n), d(n), s(n), e(n)] capturing problems solvable by constant-depth, constant-precision decoder-only transformers with T CoT steps, and proves two complementary results. For the upper bound, it shows constant-precision transformers without CoT are limited to AC^0 (a strict subset of the previously known TC^0 bound) by modeling iterated floating-point addition as an ordered automaton and applying the Krohn-Rhodes decomposition theorem to show all ordered automata are counter-free. For the lower bound, it constructs a 2-layer transformer that simulates one Boolean gate per CoT step using attention to retrieve input gate values and a feedforward network to compute the gate output, proving that T CoT steps with O(log n) embedding can solve any circuit of size T, yielding P/poly for polynomial CoT. Empirically, transformers trained on four tasks (modular addition, S5 permutation composition, iterated squaring, and circuit value problem) show that CoT dramatically improves accuracy over both base and hint settings, especially at low depth, confirming the theoretical prediction that CoT provides the serial computation steps that parallel-only transformers lack.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 3.3) shows that constant-depth, constant-precision transformers with T steps of CoT and O(log n) embedding size can solve any problem in SIZE[T(n)], implying P/poly = CoT[poly(n), log n, 1]. Empirically, on the three inherently serial tasks (Permutation Composition S5, Iterated Squaring, Circuit Value Problem), transformers without CoT achieve only ~20% accuracy (random-guessing level for S5) at low depths, while CoT enables near-perfect accuracy even at depth 1. For the parallelizable Modular Addition task, all settings (base, hint, CoT) perform well, but CoT still provides the best accuracy at longer sequence lengths. The key empirical finding is that CoT provides a dramatic accuracy improvement specifically on problems that are hard for parallel computation, with the gap widening as depth decreases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three settings: base (no CoT, no hints), hint (CoT provided as training labels but not generated at inference), and CoT (model auto-regressively generates intermediate steps). On the three inherently serial problems (Permutation Composition S5, Iterated Squaring, Circuit Value Problem), CoT consistently and substantially outperforms both base and hint, with the advantage being largest at low transformer depths (e.g., depth 1–2). For Permutation Composition S5, base accuracy remains near the 20% random-guessing floor regardless of depth, while CoT reaches high accuracy even at depth 1. For Iterated Squaring, the paper notes the problem is 'difficult for transformers without CoT to learn, but can be perfectly expressed with CoT even if depth is only 1.' On Modular Addition (a TC^0-parallelizable task), the gap between settings is smaller, with hint already performing well at low depths, but CoT still provides the best accuracy especially at longer sequence lengths. The paper does not compare against external methods; the comparison is purely between architectural settings (with/without CoT) on the same transformer architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on transformer expressiveness via circuit complexity (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023b; Liu et al., 2022a), which established that constant-depth, log-precision transformers are bounded by TC^0. The key departure is a tighter AC^0 upper bound for constant-precision transformers, achieved through a novel application of the Krohn-Rhodes decomposition theorem to show that iterated floating-point addition with rounding is simulated by counter-free automata. The paper then introduces CoT as a new axis of expressiveness, showing that T steps of CoT lifts the bound to SIZE[T(n)], and positions this as a generalization of Liu et al. (2022a), whose semi-automata setting corresponds to exactly 1 step of CoT.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first clean theoretical explanation for why the *form* of CoT (generating intermediate tokens) matters independent of content, directly addressing the puzzle that even zero-shot 'let's think step by step' or incorrect reasoning steps improve performance. The practical implication is that increasing sequence length (and thus available CoT steps) is a theoretically grounded way to boost reasoning capability without increasing model depth. Open questions include whether the TC^0 upper bound holds for log-precision transformers with log exponent bits (the realistic floating-point regime), and whether the synthetic-task empirical validation generalizes to the complex, multi-step reasoning seen in real LLM deployments.
