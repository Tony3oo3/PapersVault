## Problem

Molecular foundation models require large-scale supervised training data spanning both quantum mechanical and biological properties, yet existing datasets are either too small, limited to a single property per molecule, or lack the diverse, environment-dependent labels needed for drug discovery. No prior dataset collection simultaneously provides the scale (tens of millions of molecules), label diversity (thousands of tasks), and multi-level structure (node- and graph-level) necessary to train a general-purpose molecular foundation model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240215/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models consume 2D molecular graphs with atom-type node features, bond-type edge features, and spectral positional encodings (Laplacian eigenvalues/eigenvectors and random walk return probabilities embedded via MLPs). Outputs are structured as multi-task, multi-level predictions: graph-level regression (DFT/PM6 energies, HOMO-LUMO gaps, 3D descriptors), graph-level classification (1,328 bioassay activities), graph-level ranked classification (978-gene expression signatures binned into 5 classes), and node-level regression (Mulliken/Löwdin charges, electronic spin). Labels are sparsely defined—up to 89% missing per task—so each molecule carries only a subset of the full label set, and the same molecule may appear across multiple datasets with different available labels.

## Method

The paper introduces three dataset mixes (TOYMIX, LARGEMIX, ULTRALARGE) totaling ~100M molecules and 13B+ labels by curating and extending PubChemQC DFT/PM6 computations, PubChem bioassay scrapes, and LINCS L1000 transcriptomics into a unified multi-task format. The accompanying Graphium library handles the engineering challenges of multi-level multi-task training: modular dataloading across heterogeneous datasets, per-task loss computation with NaN-aware weighting to handle sparse labels, a hybrid ranked-classification loss for binned gene-expression data, μP-based hyperparameter transfer for scaling to billions of parameters, and native IPU/GPU/CPU support. Baseline experiments with GCN, GIN, and GINE (4 layers, ~150k–10M parameters) demonstrate that joint multi-task training on large quantum datasets significantly improves AUROC and AP on low-resource biological tasks (e.g., TOX21, L1000) compared to single-dataset training, while showing minimal degradation on the large quantum datasets themselves.

## Results

Across all three dataset mixes, GINE consistently outperforms GIN, which in turn outperforms GCN. The headline finding is that multi-dataset training substantially improves low-resource tasks: on LARGEMIX, L1000_VCAP AUROC improves from 0.571 to 0.640 (GINE) and L1000_MCF7 AUROC from 0.612 to 0.640 (GCN), while on TOYMIX, ZINC12K R² jumps from 0.915 to 0.971 (GINE) and TOX21 AUROC from 0.773 to 0.850 (GCN). Conversely, larger datasets show slight degradation under multi-task training (e.g., PCQM4M_G25 MAE worsens from 0.222 to 0.232 for GINE; PCBA_1328 AUROC drops from 0.794 to 0.774). On the ULTRALARGE PM6_83M dataset (5% subset), no multi-task advantage is observed, with GINE achieving MAE 0.254 / Pearson 0.906 on graph-level tasks and MAE 0.572 / Pearson 0.348 on node-level tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead benchmarks three GNN architectures (GCN, GIN, GINE) under single-dataset vs. multi-dataset training. GINE consistently achieves the best or near-best results across all datasets and both training paradigms, with the gap over GCN being most pronounced on ZINC12K (R² 0.971 vs. 0.940 in multi-dataset) and PCQM4M_G25 (MAE 0.222 vs. 0.236 in single-dataset). The critical comparison is single- vs. multi-dataset training: smaller datasets benefit dramatically (L1000_VCAP AUROC +0.069, L1000_MCF7 AUROC +0.028, ZINC12K R² +0.056, TOX21 AUROC +0.077), while larger datasets (PCQM4M_G25, PCBA_1328) show modest degradation of 1–3% in their primary metrics. On ULTRALARGE (PM6_83M, 5% subset), multi-task training provides no measurable benefit, suggesting the advantage is specific to the cross-domain (quantum + biological) setting rather than mere data volume. The authors attribute the degradation on large datasets to underfitting, noting that a 10M-parameter model is insufficient to jointly optimize over 3000+ tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant self-supervised pre-training paradigm for molecular GNNs (contrastive learning, autoencoders, denoising) by arguing that supervised multi-task training combining quantum mechanical and biological labels is necessary for effective molecular foundation models. It extends existing datasets—OGB-PCQM4M (single HOMO-LUMO gap task) becomes PCQM4M_G25_N4 with 25 graph-level and 4 node-level tasks; OGB-PCBA (128 assays, 437k molecules) becomes PCBA_1328 (1,328 assays, 1.5M molecules)—and introduces the PM6_83M dataset (83M molecules, 62 graph-level + 7 node-level tasks) as the largest 2D-GNN training set to date. The Graphium library builds on the ecosystem of PyG, DGL, and TorchDrug but adds the multi-level (node/edge/graph/nodepair) multi-task training infrastructure that none of these provide for molecular data at this scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 13-billion-label dataset collection (300× larger than OGB-LSC PCQM4Mv2) provides the first supervised training resource large enough to potentially enable true molecular foundation models, and the empirical finding that quantum data improves low-resource biological task performance offers a concrete inductive bias for multi-domain pre-training. However, the evidence is preliminary: only small baseline GNNs (4–6M parameters) were tested, ULTRALARGE was evaluated on just a 5% subset, and the multi-task benefit vanished entirely on the largest dataset, leaving open whether the observed transfer effects persist at the model and data scales where foundation models are expected to emerge. The absence of edge-level tasks, graph Transformer baselines, and fine-tuning experiments to downstream drug discovery tasks are significant gaps that future work must address.
