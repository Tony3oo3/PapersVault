## Problem

It has long been hypothesized that causal reasoning is fundamental to robust intelligence, but it remained unknown whether agents must learn causal models to generalize to new domains or whether other inductive biases suffice. While causal models are known to be sufficient for identifying regret-bounded policies under distributional shifts (transportability theory), the converse—whether causal models are necessary—was unproven. This paper resolves the question by showing that any agent capable of adapting to a sufficiently large set of distributional shifts must have learned an approximate causal model of the data generating process.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a policy oracle mapping each mixture of local interventions to the agent's (near-)optimal policy, together with the known utility function and the set of chance variables C. The output is a full causal Bayesian network over all ancestors of the utility: both the DAG (causal structure) and the joint distribution (conditional probability tables). For regret-bounded agents (δ > 0), the output is an approximate CBN where parameter errors grow linearly in δ and weak causal edges may be unresolvable, yielding a sub-graph G' ⊆ G.

## Method

The core result (Theorem 1) proves that optimal policies under all mixtures of local interventions are L2-complete, encoding all interventional queries and thereby identifying the full CBN. The proof mechanism uses a critical-point technique: by mixing a given intervention σ with a hard intervention σ' that flips the optimal decision, the policy change at the indifference point q_crit reveals the expected utility difference under σ, which identifies leave-one-out interventional distributions P(c_i | do(all other C_j)). These distributions directly yield parent sets (via causal faithfulness) and conditional probability tables, traversed iteratively from Pa_U upward through Anc_U. The 'almost all' condition is established via polynomial arguments (Meek 2013), showing non-identifiability only on a Lebesgue measure-zero subset of the parameter space. Theorem 2 extends the result to regret-bounded agents, showing the recovered CBN has parameter errors bounded by γ(δ) growing linearly in δ, while Theorem 3 proves sufficiency: a CBN is sufficient to identify regret-bounded policies for any utility and soft intervention.

## Results

The paper's main contributions are three theorems establishing that causal models are necessary and sufficient for robust adaptation: Theorem 1 shows the underlying CBN (DAG + joint distribution) is identifiable from optimal policies under all mixtures of local interventions for almost all CIDs; Theorem 2 extends this to regret-bounded policies, showing the recovered causal model has parameter errors bounded by γ(δ) that grow linearly in δ for small regret; Theorem 3 proves sufficiency, that an approximate CBN with parameter error ε yields policies with regret growing linearly in ε. Empirically (Figure 3, Appendix F), on 1000 randomly generated binary CIDs, the model-average error rate for both the learned DAG G′ and the learned joint distribution P′(x,y) decreases monotonically as the normalized regret bound δ/|E[u|D=1]−E[u|D=0]| decreases, approaching zero as δ→0 while remaining substantially above the random-guess baseline across all tested regret levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-method baselines. The empirical validation in Appendix F compares the learned causal model against a random-guess baseline on 1000 randomly generated CIDs (binary decision D, two binary latent variables X, Y), showing that the learned DAG and joint distribution error rates are consistently and substantially below random guess for all regret bounds tested, with the gap narrowing only as δ increases. Theoretically, the paper positions its results as the converse (necessity) of the well-known sufficiency result in causal transportability theory (Bareinboim & Pearl, 2016), and as a decision-task analogue of the causal hierarchy theorem (Bareinboim et al., 2022), which shows observational data is insufficient for identifying all interventional queries. Unlike transportability, which assumes causal structure a priori, this paper shows that causal structure must be learned by any agent achieving low regret across a large set of distributional shifts, regardless of architecture or training method.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper proves the necessity converse of causal transportability theory (Bareinboim & Pearl, 2016), which established that causal models are sufficient for identifying regret-bounded policies under known domain shifts. It also extends the causal hierarchy theorem (Bareinboim et al., 2022), which showed observational data is almost always insufficient for causal queries, by demonstrating that optimal policies under mixtures of local interventions form an L2-complete set—sufficient to identify all interventional queries. The formalism builds on causal influence diagrams (Howard & Matheson, 2005; Everitt et al., 2021) and Pearl's CBN framework, with the 'almost all' proof technique following Meek (2013). Crucially, unlike transportability which assumes causal structure as an inductive bias, this paper shows causal structure must be learned, connecting robustness guarantees to causal representation learning (Schölkopf et al., 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides the first formal justification that causal representation learning is not merely beneficial but necessary for strong robustness guarantees, and reveals that transfer learning problems contain hidden causal discovery sub-problems that may be intractable without interventional data. It also offers a potential explanation for emergent capabilities in foundation models: agents trained across many domains are incentivized to learn causal world models that enable zero-shot transfer to new objectives. Key limitations include restriction to unmediated decision tasks (excluding most RL/control settings), the 'almost all' qualifier excluding finely-tuned environments, and the requirement of robustness to a large set of local interventions. Open questions include whether results extend to active/mediated tasks, what smaller sets of shifts suffice for partial causal identification, and whether current training methods actually elicit the causal models the theory predicts.
