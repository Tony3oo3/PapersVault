## Problem

Molecular docking is bottlenecked by the expensive optimization of scoring functions over ligand poses, and existing ML scoring functions improve accuracy without reducing this optimization cost. The paper addresses this by designing a scoring function whose functional form—cross-correlation of learned scalar fields—enables FFT-accelerated evaluation over rigid-body degrees of freedom, a property that physics-inspired scoring functions lack.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241424/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a protein structure (featurized graph with alpha-carbon 3D coordinates) and a rigid ligand conformer (featurized graph with heavy-atom 3D coordinates) as independent inputs, each processed through a separate equivariant scalar field network. The protein network uses all heavy-atom nodes for message passing but only alpha-carbon nodes emit scalar field coefficients. The output is either a scored pose (scalar energy via cross-correlation) or an optimized ligand pose (3D coordinates) found by FFT-based search over translations and/or rotations within a defined pocket volume (e.g., an 8 Å cube).

## Method

The core contribution is equivariant scalar field networks (ESFs), which parameterize multi-channel scalar fields as sums of per-atom spherical harmonic expansions with radial basis functions, where the expansion coefficients A_{cnjℓm} are produced by E3NN graph neural networks in a single forward pass. The scoring function is defined as the cross-correlation of the protein and ligand scalar fields, which is SE(3)-invariant by construction. This cross-correlation form enables two FFT-based evaluation strategies: translational FFT (evaluating all translations simultaneously via the convolution theorem, with Fourier coefficients computed analytically from the expansion coefficients using spherical Bessel transforms) and rotational FFT (projecting fields onto a global spherical harmonic basis via least-squares, then evaluating rotational cross-correlations via Wigner D-matrices). Training uses an energy-based model with conditional log-likelihoods over translation and rotation, where the intractable partition functions become tractable because the integrands are precisely the FFT-evaluable cross-correlations. The key practical advantage is amortization: the neural network is evaluated once per protein or ligand conformer rather than once per pose, reducing per-pose scoring to ~1 μs (translational) or ~8 μs (rotational).

## Results

On rigid conformer docking (PDBBind crystal), ESF-N-RF achieves 73% <2Å success rate and 1.00 Å median RMSD, slightly below Vina (79%, 0.32 Å) and Gnina (77%, 0.33 Å). On ESMFold-predicted structures, ESF-N-RF substantially outperforms both baselines, reaching 47% <2Å vs. 24% (Vina) and 28% (Gnina). On the PDE10A virtual-screening-like benchmark (70 ligands, one pocket), ESF-RF matches Gnina's 73% success rate while achieving a 45× speedup in total runtime (1.5 s vs. 6.0 s per complex). Per-pose scoring runtime is 1.0 μs (translational) or 8.2 μs (rotational), orders of magnitude faster than Vina (3.4 ms) and Gnina (13.0 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Vina and Gnina on PDBBind crystal structures, ESF-N-RF is slightly weaker in success rate (73% vs. 79%/77%) and median RMSD (1.00 Å vs. 0.32/0.33 Å), a gap attributed to the coarse search grid and lack of local refinement. On ESMFold structures, however, ESF-N-RF nearly doubles the success rate of both baselines (47% vs. 24%/28%), demonstrating substantially improved robustness to imperfect sidechain predictions. In decoy pose scoring (Table 2), ESF-TS matches Gnina's TopRMSD (0.59 Å) and outperforms it in %<2Å (87% vs. 83%) on crystal structures, while on ESMFold it achieves 57% <2Å vs. 43% (Vina) and 46% (Gnina). ESF substantially outperforms TANKBind across all metrics (e.g., 0.87 vs. 0.69 AUROC on crystal) and is competitive with DiffDock's confidence model, matching its TopRMSD on crystal (0.59 vs. 0.66 Å) and outperforming it on ESMFold (1.38 vs. 2.01 Å). The key runtime advantage is that per-pose scoring takes 1.0 μs (TS) or 8.2 μs (RS) versus 3.4 ms (Vina) and 13.0 ms (Gnina), and the PDE10A amortization yields a 45× total speedup (1.5 s vs. 6.0 s) at matched accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FFT-based cross-correlation paradigm from protein-protein docking (Katchalski-Katzir 1992; Kovacs & Wriggers 2002) to protein-ligand docking by learning the scalar fields with equivariant graph neural networks (E3NNs), rather than using hand-crafted shape or electrostatic descriptors. It departs from the dominant ML scoring function paradigm (Gnina's CNN, Vina's pairwise potentials) that evaluates one pose at a time, instead defining the score as a cross-correlation of learned fields so that all translations or rotations can be scored simultaneously via FFTs. The training objective follows the conditional log-likelihood decomposition from DiffDock (Corso et al. 2023), but the functional form of the scoring function is fundamentally different: a learned inner product of fields rather than a per-pose neural network evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is most visible in virtual screening settings with a fixed pocket, where amortization of protein-level precomputations yields a ~50× speedup over per-complex baselines at no accuracy loss, and per-pose scoring drops to the microsecond scale. The finding that the scalar-field approach is substantially more robust on ESMFold-predicted structures (47% vs 28% success rate) than physics-based scoring functions is notable, since traditional pairwise terms are sensitive to sidechain placement errors that ESMFold introduces. Key limitations include the restriction to rigid conformers (no torsional degrees of freedom), the coarse grid on non-FFT search dimensions, and the fact that on crystal structures the method still trails Vina/Gnina by a few points in success rate. Open questions include integration into full docking pipelines with local refinement, extension to flexible ligands, and whether the cross-correlation form can capture the more complex many-body interactions that physics-based potentials model explicitly.
