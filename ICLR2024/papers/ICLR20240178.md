## Problem

Supervised learning research has established that neural network gradients reside in a low-dimensional, slowly-changing subspace of high curvature, enabling more efficient optimization. However, it remains unclear whether this structure persists in reinforcement learning, where the data distribution shifts continuously as the agent explores, potentially destroying any stable subspace. This gap prevents the transfer of subspace-based techniques (second-order optimization, structured parameter-space exploration) to policy gradient algorithms despite their potential to substantially improve training efficiency.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes actor and critic network parameters from PPO (~5,000 params) and SAC (~70,000–140,000 params) alongside large datasets of state-action pairs (10⁶ on-policy samples for PPO; the full replay buffer for SAC) to compute Hessian eigenvectors via the Lanczos method. Outputs are three diagnostic quantities evaluated at 50,000-step checkpoints: the Hessian eigenvalue spectrum, the gradient subspace fraction S_frac (fraction of gradient energy captured by the top-k eigenvectors, k=100), and the subspace overlap S_overlap between an early reference subspace (t₁=100,000) and later timesteps. All metrics are computed for both precise (large-sample) and mini-batch (2048-sample) gradient/Hessian estimates to assess practical feasibility during training.

## Method

The paper empirically verifies three conditions for gradient subspaces in PPO and SAC across twelve continuous-control benchmarks: (i) the Hessian spectrum is dominated by a few large eigenvalues, confirming ill-conditioning; (ii) actor and critic gradients are largely contained in the subspace spanned by the top-100 Hessian eigenvectors, quantified by S_frac; and (iii) this subspace remains relatively stable over 400,000+ timesteps, quantified by S_overlap. Hessian eigenvectors are estimated efficiently via the Lanczos method without explicitly constructing the Hessian matrix. The study contrasts on-policy (PPO) and off-policy (SAC) learning, finding that SAC's replay buffer yields a more stable subspace and higher gradient concentration, while PPO's subspace fraction still significantly exceeds the random-projection baseline (k/n). Robustness is validated by showing that mini-batch Hessian estimates (2048 samples) capture the subspace well and that the phenomenon persists under randomly sampled, potentially suboptimal hyperparameters.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that for both PPO and SAC across 12 continuous control tasks, the gradient subspace fraction S_frac significantly exceeds the random-projection baseline of k/n (0.02 for PPO with ~5,000 parameters; 0.0014 for SAC actor with ~70,000 parameters; 0.0007 for SAC critic with ~140,000 parameters), confirming that gradients concentrate in a 100-dimensional high-curvature subspace. The critic consistently exhibits a higher S_frac than the actor for both algorithms, and SAC's actor often outperforms PPO's actor, particularly in early training. Subspace overlap S_overlap remains significantly above zero even after 400,000 timesteps, indicating the subspace is reusable. Mini-batch Hessian estimates (2048 samples) still capture the gradient to a considerable extent, making the analysis practical without requiring full-dataset Hessian computation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so the key contrasts are internal. PPO (on-policy) vs. SAC (off-policy): SAC's actor achieves a higher gradient subspace fraction than PPO's, which the authors attribute to SAC's slower-changing training distribution due to replay buffer reuse, making it closer to the supervised learning setting where Gur-Ari et al. (2018) originally observed the phenomenon. Actor vs. critic: The critic's gradient is more tightly contained in the high-curvature subspace than the actor's for both algorithms, with the effect being particularly pronounced for SAC. Precise vs. mini-batch estimates: The precise gradient (10⁶ samples) lies slightly better in the subspace than the mini-batch gradient used during training, but the difference is small enough that the subspace remains valid for practical use. Mini-batch Hessian (2048 samples) vs. precise Hessian: The mini-batch Hessian still identifies a subspace that captures the gradient to a significant extent, confirming that expensive full-dataset Hessian computation is unnecessary. Across all 12 tasks and 10 random seeds, these qualitative patterns hold consistently, though the absolute S_frac values vary by task, algorithm, and training phase.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient subspace analysis of Gur-Ari et al. (2018) from supervised learning to policy gradient reinforcement learning, specifically PPO and SAC. It departs from prior RL subspace work (Tang et al. 2023, Maheswaranathan et al. 2019, Gaya et al. 2022) by grounding the subspace in Hessian curvature of the loss rather than parameter-space SVD or explicitly learned policy subspaces, and by analyzing both actor and critic networks under both on-policy and off-policy settings. The paper positions itself as filling a gap: the non-stationary data distribution in RL was intuitively expected to prevent stable gradient subspaces, yet no systematic empirical evaluation had tested this assumption for standard PG algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings open two concrete practical directions for RL: (1) second-order optimization becomes feasible by inverting the Hessian in a ~100-dim subspace rather than the full parameter space, and (2) parameter-space exploration can be focused on high-curvature directions rather than isotropic noise. The paper also provides a mechanistic explanation for the 'cliffs' in reward landscapes observed by Sullivan et al. (2022). However, the paper is purely analytical—it does not implement or evaluate any subspace-based optimization or exploration method, leaving the practical gains unquantified. Open questions include how subspace identification integrates into the training loop at scale, whether the findings hold for larger networks or more complex environments, and how frequently the subspace must be refreshed in practice.
