## Problem

Online Continual Learning requires a model to sequentially predict and learn from a non-stationary data stream, but existing methods either treat the data as stationary or handle distribution shift through heuristics (e.g., replay, learning-rate scheduling) without an explicit probabilistic model of how the predictor should change over time. The paper addresses the gap of efficiently tracking a time-varying linear predictor with calibrated uncertainty while adapting to arbitrary non-stationary structure at O(m²) cost per step.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-100, variable-resolution CLOC photos) arriving in chunks of 10–128, processed through a ResNet-18/50 backbone to produce a fixed-dimension feature vector φ_n. Each observation is first used for evaluation (prequential protocol) before updating the model. The output is a class label and a calibrated predictive probability over 100 (CIFAR-100) or 713 (CLOC) classes, alongside a full Gaussian posterior over the m-dimensional linear weights and a scalar forgetting coefficient γ_n that is itself updated online.

## Method

The core contribution is a Kalman filter state-space model over the last-layer linear predictor weights W_n, where a 'parameter drift' transition p(w_n|w_{n-1}) = N(w_n | γ_n w_{n-1}, (1−γ_n²)σ_w² I) models non-stationarity through a time-varying forgetting coefficient γ_n ∈ [0,1]. For classification, a Gaussian likelihood approximates the intractable softmax to keep Kalman recursions exact and O(m²) per step (with the covariance shared across all K classes), while predictions and the γ_n SGD update combine the approximate posterior with the exact softmax via Monte Carlo integration over the logit distribution. The forgetting coefficient is learned online by maximizing the log predictive probability via SGD on a reparameterized parameter δ_n = −2 log γ_n, allowing the model to automatically sharpen or reset the posterior in response to distributional change. The framework supports both frozen pretrained backbones and simultaneous online fine-tuning of the backbone parameters θ and a calibration parameter α, all within a single unified Algorithm 1 loop.

## Results

On non-stationary CIFAR-100 (10-task class-incremental), the Kalman filter with learned γ, finetuned backbone, and replay achieves 57.13% ± 0.7% average online accuracy, outperforming Online SGD + Replay (54.5% ± 1%) and Online SGD (49.5% ± 0.7%). On stationary CIFAR-100, the same configuration reaches 19.82% ± 0.3%, beating the ER++ baseline (18.45%). On the large-scale CLOC benchmark (~37M images, 713 location classes), the Kalman filter with a pretrained ResNet-50 backbone and finetuning consistently outperforms all baselines (ER, ACE, Online SGD) across the entire stream, with the gap widening over time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Online SGD and Online SGD + Replay from Bornschein et al. (2022b), the Kalman filter with learned γ and finetuned backbone achieves 53.47% vs. 49.5% (no replay) and 53.47% vs. 54.5% (with replay) on non-stationary CIFAR-100, showing a clear advantage without replay and a small gap with replay. On CLOC, the Kalman filter with a pretrained backbone substantially outperforms Online SGD throughout the stream, and matches Online SGD + Replay when learning from scratch without any replay memory. Against ER++ from Ghunaim et al. (2023), the Kalman filter with replay achieves 19.82% vs. 18.45% on stationary CIFAR-100, and on CLOC the Kalman filter outperforms both ER (Chaudhry et al., 2019) and ACE (Caccia et al., 2022) across the full 37M-image sequence, with the performance gap growing over time as non-stationarity accumulates. The learned γ variant consistently outperforms fixed-γ variants (γ=0.999) and the stationary case (γ=1), confirming that online adaptation of the forgetting coefficient is critical for non-stationary data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Kalman filter—a classical tool from signal processing and control theory—for online continual learning by applying it to the readout layer of a (possibly pretrained) neural network, modeling parameter drift via a time-varying forgetting coefficient. It departs from the Bayesian forgetting (BF) literature (Kulhavy & Zarrop, 1993; Moens, 2018; Kurle et al., 2020), which achieves forgetting through heuristic geometric averaging of posterior and prior, by instead grounding forgetting in a principled Gaussian state-space transition that yields O(m²) per-step cost versus BF's O(m³). The Gaussian-likelihood approximation to softmax, borrowed from GP classification and Bayesian meta-learning (Patacchiola et al., 2020), is the key enabler that makes Kalman recursions tractable for multi-class classification. The concurrent work of Chang et al. (2023) applies an extended Kalman filter to all network parameters, whereas this paper restricts the filter to the last layer with a covariance shared across classes, trading generality for scalability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, computationally efficient (O(m²)) probabilistic framework for adapting to non-stationarity in online classification, and demonstrates that a simple Kalman filter on the readout layer—combined with online SGD on the forgetting coefficient—consistently outperforms both Bayesian forgetting and standard online SGD baselines on large-scale benchmarks (CLOC, CIFAR-100). A notable practical finding is that replay-free KF matches Online SGD + replay when learning from scratch on CLOC, suggesting the state-space structure itself provides an implicit memory mechanism. Open questions include whether more accurate (but still efficient) Gaussian approximations to the softmax likelihood could tighten the gap between the approximate and exact posteriors, and whether higher-order Markov transition dynamics could capture more complex drift patterns. The method's reliance on a linear readout layer and a frozen or slowly-adapting backbone also limits its applicability to settings where the representation itself must change rapidly.
