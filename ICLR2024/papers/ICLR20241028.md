## Problem

Reinforcement learning policies typically require extensive online interactions or comprehensive offline datasets, both of which are costly or unavailable in real-world settings. The paper asks whether an agent can prepare for an unknown target environment purely through mental rehearsal—generating hypothetical dynamics in advance—without any interaction data, and whether this rehearsal-based approach can produce generalizable policies that adapt at test time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241028/cropped_page24_idx31.webp)

## Input / Output

Base ReDM requires only the reward function, terminal function, and state-space bounds as task knowledge—no interaction trajectories or simulator access. The output is a context-based adaptive policy (a context extractor φ that encodes interaction history into a latent z, plus a context-conditioned policy π(·|s,z)) trained over a growing set of parameterized candidate dynamics models. In the ReDM-o extension, a small or mismatched offline dataset is additionally consumed as a log-likelihood regularizer during model generation, and initial states for rollouts are sampled from that dataset rather than a Gaussian prior.

## Method

ReDM iterates between two procedures: (1) generating a new candidate dynamics model by solving an RL objective where the model is the 'agent' and the current meta-policy is the 'environment,' with a composite reward combining a diversity term (−r^c, which penalizes states where the current policy already performs well, thereby forcing the new model to be distinct) and an eligibility term (r^e, computed via random-shooting planning to ensure the model admits viable high-return paths); and (2) meta-training a context-based adaptive policy (via SAC) over the accumulated candidate set. The diversity principle is grounded in Lemma 3.4, which shows that a large performance gap between the new model and existing candidates implies a lower bound on their TV divergence in both occupancy and single-step transition. Theorem 3.3 provides a performance guarantee bounding the target-environment regret by the eligibility gap ε_e, the minimum model-set-to-target distance ε_m, and the adaptation cost ε_a. ReDM-o augments the model-generation objective with a log-likelihood regularization term on offline data, allowing the method to leverage limited or slightly mismatched datasets without requiring them to be unbiased or fully covering.

## Results

On D4RL with only 200 transitions, ReDM-o achieves 2.5±0.9 on HalfCheetah-Random and 23.8±8.0 on Hopper-Random, outperforming the best model-based baseline (2.2 and 14.3) and best model-free baseline (0.6 and 1.1). With full D4RL datasets evaluated under mismatched gravity (0.5×, 1.0×, 1.5×), ReDM-o achieves an average normalized score of 74.2 on matched tasks and 50.4 on mismatched tasks, compared to 60.3 and 45.8 for MAPLE, the strongest baseline. In the zero-interaction setting, ReDM produces a valid policy that significantly outperforms random on InvertedPendulum, MountainCar-Continuous, and Acrobot, reaching ~30% and ~70% of expert performance on MountainCar and Acrobot respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReDM-o consistently outperforms all five baselines across both limited-data and full-D4RL settings. Against MAPLE (the closest competitor, which also uses an adaptive context-based policy), ReDM-o gains +1.5 on HalfCheetah-Random-200, +9.5 on Hopper-Random-200, and +13.9 average on matched full D4RL tasks; the gap widens to +4.6 on mismatched tasks, confirming ReDM's generalization advantage. Against MOPO, the gap is largest on mismatched dynamics (50.4 vs 30.7 average), where MOPO's pessimistic reward penalty degrades performance under distribution shift. Against model-free baselines (CQL, IQL, TD3BC), ReDM-o dominates especially under data scarcity: with only 200 transitions on Hopper-Random, ReDM-o scores 23.8 while IQL, TD3BC, and CQL score 1.1, 0.9, and 0.8 respectively. On the full D4RL benchmark, ReDM-o wins 22 out of 36 individual task-gravity combinations, including 17 of 24 mismatched evaluations, whereas MAPLE wins the remaining majority on a few matched medium-expert tasks (e.g., Walker2d medium-expert Gravity-1.0: 109.1 vs 101.2).

## Relation to Prior Work

ReDM builds most directly on MAPLE (Chen et al., 2021), which trains a context-based adaptive policy over a model ensemble, but replaces MAPLE's supervised ensemble generation with an adversarial RL process that generates candidate dynamics without requiring interaction data. It also departs from domain randomization and procedural content generation, which require pre-defined parameter ranges or a high-fidelity simulator, by generating dynamics purely from reward and terminal functions. The adversarial model generation draws on ideas from RAMBO-RL and adversarial environment design, but adds an eligibility constraint to avoid the over-pessimistic, unsolvable dynamics that purely adversarial approaches produce. The cognitive-science framing of 'rehearsal' (Zhou, 2022) provides the conceptual motivation but the technical contribution is the iterative diversity-eligibility model generation loop combined with meta-policy optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a valid policy can be learned with zero interaction data (only reward and terminal functions), which challenges the assumption that some form of environment interaction is necessary for policy optimization. The theoretical bound (Theorem 3.3) cleanly decomposes performance loss into eligibility, coverage, and adaptation costs, giving practitioners a principled understanding of when rehearsal will succeed. However, the zero-data experiments are limited to simple Gym environments, and the D4RL results, while competitive, show that ReDM-o does not uniformly dominate all baselines across all tasks and gravity settings. The authors acknowledge that the adaptive policy architecture is underexplored and that scaling to high-dimensional tasks remains an open challenge, as the hypothesis space of dynamics models grows combinatorially.
