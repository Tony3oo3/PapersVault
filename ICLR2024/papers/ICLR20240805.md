## Problem

Existing causal discovery methods either assume no latent variables (LiNGAM), restrict latent variables to specific roles such as confounders of measured variables (measurement models), or impose rigid hierarchical structures. No practical algorithm exists that identifies the full causal graph when latent variables may appear at arbitrary positions—including as descendants of measured variables—without requiring prior knowledge of the number or locations of latent variables.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240805/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes only observed samples of measured variables (tabular data) with no prior knowledge of the number, identity, or positions of latent variables. It outputs a complete directed acyclic graph over both measured and latent variables, including causal edges among latent variables and between latent and measured variables. The identifiability guarantee requires each latent set to have a sufficient number of pure children (latent or measured); when this condition is violated, the algorithm halts inference at that level rather than outputting spurious structure.

## Method

The paper introduces PO-LiNGAM, a generalization of LiNGAM where latent variables may occupy any position in the DAG, and establishes identifiability under a graphical condition involving 'atomic units' (minimal indecomposable sets of variables) and their pure children. The core algorithm is a three-phase iterative procedure that recovers the graph from leaf to root: Phase I identifies leaf atomic units and their parents by testing the Generalized Independent Noise (GIN) condition on measured surrogate variables; Phase II discovers new latent atomic units by clustering pure children that share common parents; Phase III refines discovered units by detecting variable overlaps and decomposing composite units into smaller ones. The key technical insight is that linear transitivity allows measured descendants of latent variables to serve as surrogates in GIN tests, enabling the algorithm to reason about unobserved variables using only observed data. The approach leverages higher-order statistics (beyond covariance) through the GIN condition, which provides the non-Gaussianity-based identifiability that distinguishes it from second-order methods.

## Results

On the four synthetic causal structures (Cases 1–4) with 50k samples, PO-LiNGAM achieves Correct Ordering Rates of 0.96, 0.98, 1.0, and 0.97, with F1-scores of 0.99, 0.98, 1.0, and 0.82 respectively. On the most challenging general structure (Case 4), the method attains a 0.97 Correct Ordering Rate and 0.17 Error Rate in Latent Variables, substantially outperforming all baselines. On the H&S-1939 real-world dataset, the recovered graph correctly identifies latent factors for textual, visual, and speeded aspects, consistent with prior factor-analytic findings. On the SOFA dataset, the output forms a hierarchical structure matching the hypothesized model in Muthén & Muthén (2017).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PO-LiNGAM is compared against five baselines: LiNGAM (no latent variables), BPC (Tetrad-based measurement model), FastGIN (GIN-based measurement model), LaHME (GIN-based hierarchical model), and IL²H (rank-constraint hierarchical model). On Case 1 (no latent variables), LiNGAM achieves a perfect 1.0 ordering rate, slightly edging out PO-LiNGAM (0.96), as expected since LiNGAM is the special case. On all three cases involving latent variables (Cases 2–4), PO-LiNGAM dominates every baseline by a wide margin: on Case 4 (the most general structure), it achieves 0.97 ordering rate versus 0.39 (FastGIN), 0.23 (IL²H), 0.0 (LaHME), and 0.0 (BPC). The F1-score gap is equally pronounced, with PO-LiNGAM reaching 0.82 on Case 4 while the best baseline (IL²H) manages only 0.54. Notably, BPC and LaHME fail almost entirely on the general structure (Case 4), yielding 0.0 ordering rates, confirming that methods designed for specific latent configurations cannot handle the general scenario where latent variables appear at arbitrary positions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes LiNGAM (Shimizu et al., 2006) to a partially observed setting (PO-LiNGAM) where latent variables may occupy any position in the causal DAG, including being influenced by measured variables—a constraint that prior methods like BPC, FastGIN, LaHME, and IL²H all implicitly or explicitly forbid. The algorithmic core builds directly on the GIN condition (Xie et al., 2020) and its hierarchical extension (Xie et al., 2022), but introduces a novel 'atomic unit' abstraction and a three-phase iterative search that handles overlapping latent sets, a case largely overlooked by earlier GIN-based methods. The identifiability theorem subsumes LiNGAM as a special case and extends the sufficient-pure-children condition from single latent variables to latent sets of arbitrary size.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in removing the 'no measured parent of latent variable' assumption that limits most existing latent-variable causal discovery methods, making the approach applicable to realistic scenarios (e.g., environmental factors influencing latent genetic factors). The trade-off is a graphical identifiability condition requiring each latent set to have a sufficient number of pure children, which the authors acknowledge as a limitation and identify reducing this requirement as key future work. Extending the framework to nonlinear causal models is another open direction. For practitioners, the method offers a principled, assumption-light alternative when the causal structure is genuinely general rather than conforming to measurement or hierarchical model templates.
