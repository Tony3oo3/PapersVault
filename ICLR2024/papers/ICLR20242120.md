## Problem

Latent graph inference methods lack principled theoretical guarantees for embedding-based graph representation, and existing approaches either use arbitrary similarity measures or require non-differentiable random search over a catalogue of Riemannian product manifolds. The paper shows that even smooth Riemannian manifolds are fundamentally too rigid—just 5 nodes suffice to construct a graph that cannot be isometrically embedded into any complete connected Riemannian manifold—yet no differentiable architecture existed that could universally represent arbitrary latent graph geometries.

## Input / Output

The model consumes node features as a point cloud in R^D (optionally augmented with an input graph as inductive bias) and produces a learned quasi-metric on a low-dimensional space R^d (as small as d=2 in experiments), from which latent edges are sampled via Gumbel Top-k nearest-neighbor selection. The final output is node classification labels. A key design constraint is that all hidden-layer weights in the neural snowflake must be non-negative to preserve the metric/quasi-metric structure of the input Euclidean distance, and the triangle inequality relaxation constant C=2^{p-1} is explicitly tracked through the final power layer.

## Method

The neural snowflake is a deep network f:[0,∞)→[0,∞) that adaptively implements fractal-like quasi-metrics on R^d by composing a 'tensorized snowflake activation' (combining bounded, power-law fractal, and logarithmic irregular-fractal components) across I hidden layers with non-negative weights, followed by a final (1+|p|)-power layer that controls the triangle inequality relaxation. Paired with a simple ReLU MLP encoder E:R^D→R^d, the architecture is proven to isometrically embed any finite weighted graph (Theorem 2), and for graphs with favorable kernel geometry, requires only polynomially many parameters in the number of nodes—avoiding the exponential ambient-dimension dependence of standard universal approximation theorems. The entire pipeline is differentiable, enabling end-to-end training via backpropagation on downstream GNN losses (GCN layers), eliminating the need for combinatorial geometry search. The snowflake activation can also be plugged directly into the DGM module as a drop-in replacement for the distance function, making it compatible with existing latent graph inference frameworks.

## Results

On the four latent graph inference benchmarks (Cora, CiteSeer, Tadpole, Aerothermodynamics) without leveraging the original graph, the Neural Snowflake achieves 71.22% on Cora, 67.80% on CiteSeer, 90.02% on Tadpole, and 88.65% on Aerothermodynamics. With the original graph as inductive bias, it reaches 87.07% on Cora and 74.76% on CiteSeer, the best among all tested metric spaces. In synthetic metric learning experiments, the Neural Snowflake reduces mean square embedding error by 2–4 orders of magnitude compared to a plain MLP in Euclidean space (e.g., 0.0034 vs. 1.3196 for a fractal metric), while using fewer parameters (4,169 vs. 5,422).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Neural Snowflake outperforms all DGM variants on Cora (71.22% vs. best DGM Hyperboloid at 70.00%) and matches or leads on CiteSeer (67.80% vs. DGM Hyperboloid 68.34%, within one standard deviation). On Tadpole, the DGM with a fixed Snowflake metric slightly edges out the Neural Snowflake (91.61% vs. 90.02%), and on Aerothermodynamics the DGM Hyperboloid×Hyperboloid product manifold leads (89.55% vs. 88.65%). The key distinction is that the Neural Snowflake learns its geometry end-to-end via backpropagation, whereas all DGM baselines require a non-differentiable random search over a catalogue of fixed Riemannian or product-manifold geometries to find the best-performing space per dataset. The plain GCN baseline (83.50% Cora, 70.03% CiteSeer with graph) is consistently outperformed by all latent graph inference methods, confirming the value of learned graph structure. Overall, the Neural Snowflake is the only method that achieves top-tier accuracy across all four datasets simultaneously without dataset-specific geometry selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the latent graph inference line of work initiated by DGM (Kazi et al., 2022) and the neural latent geometry search framework (Sáez de Ocáriz Borde et al., 2023a), which formulated geometry selection as a non-differentiable combinatorial optimization over a catalogue of fixed Riemannian product manifolds. The key departure is replacing this discrete search with a single trainable metric—the neural snowflake—that can be optimized end-to-end via backpropagation, drawing on snowflake metric theory (Xia, 2009; Weaver, 2018) and monotone neural network architectures. The theoretical contribution also builds on universal approximation results for deep networks (Yarotsky, 2017; Kratsios & Papon, 2022) but shows that the snowflake geometry avoids the exponential parameter dependence on ambient dimension that plagues standard MLP approximation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first solid theoretical foundation for latent graph inference by proving universal isometric embedding guarantees and, crucially, showing that no Riemannian manifold (however exotic) can serve as a universal representation space—a result that undermines the product-manifold approach that dominated recent work. The differentiable formulation eliminates costly random search over geometry combinations, making the approach more scalable in principle. However, the empirical gains over strong baselines are modest (1-2% on Cora/CiteSeer), the Gumbel Top-k edge sampling introduces acknowledged distortion that limits the realized benefit of the learned metric, and the method has not yet been integrated with scalable architectures like NodeFormer. Open questions include whether better edge sampling can close the gap between the learned metric's expressiveness and its practical utility, and whether the theoretical universality translates to advantages on larger, more heterogeneous graph benchmarks.
