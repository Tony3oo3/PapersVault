## Problem

Existing fairness criteria for classification rely on point estimates of the dependence between sensitive attributes and predictions, ignoring the uncertainty inherent in finite-sample estimation. Previous statistical inference approaches for group fairness derive limiting distributions containing unknown quantities, rendering hypothesis tests impractical, while fixed covariance thresholds (e.g., Zafar et al., 2017) are difficult to calibrate because they are not dimensionless. This paper addresses both gaps by constructing a confidence region for the covariance vector that accounts for estimation uncertainty and uses a dimensionless significance level as the fairness threshold.

## Input / Output

The method takes tabular data with feature vectors x ∈ R^d, sensitive attributes s ∈ R^m (supporting both discrete and continuous attributes simultaneously), and binary class labels y ∈ {-1,1}. It produces a fair classifier parameterized by decision boundary θ, along with a (1−α) confidence region for the m-dimensional covariance vector between sensitive attributes and the decision boundary. The user specifies a single scalar α ∈ [0,1] as the fairness threshold, which is dimensionless and directly interpretable as a Type I error rate, eliminating the need to set a multi-dimensional covariance threshold whose scale depends on the data.

## Method

The paper constructs an empirical likelihood (EL) ratio for the covariance vector σ_θ using influence functions, which avoids nuisance parameters and distributional assumptions. The key theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) establishes that −2 log R(σ_θ) converges to a standard χ²_m distribution with no unknown quantities, enabling valid confidence regions and hypothesis tests for group fairness. The fairness constraint is formulated as requiring the origin to lie within the EL confidence region, leading to a constrained optimization problem (Equation 8) that minimizes the classification loss subject to the EL-based fairness constraint involving the Lagrange multiplier λ. The significance level α directly controls the accuracy–fairness trade-off: smaller α yields a looser constraint (higher accuracy, less fairness), while larger α enforces stricter fairness at the cost of accuracy, with α = 0 recovering the unconstrained model. This framework handles multiple sensitive attributes simultaneously and is demonstrated to be robust to geographic distribution shifts on ACS PUMS data and to achieve simultaneous fairness on the German credit dataset.

## Results

In the simulation trade-off study (Table 2), the EL-based fairness method achieves accuracy of 0.827–0.847 while reducing demographic disparity (DP) from 0.188 (unconstrained) to as low as −0.016 and equal opportunity (EO) from 0.366 to 0.090 as α increases from 0 to 0.9. Coverage probabilities for the EL confidence interval of the covariance remain close to the nominal 0.95 level (ranging 0.933–0.947) across sample sizes from 100 to 1200. On the ACS PUMS income prediction task, the method shows robustness to geographic distribution shifts with only small fluctuations between in-distribution and out-of-distribution DP/EO metrics. On the German Credit dataset with two sensitive attributes (gender and age), applying the EL fairness constraint reduces accuracy only marginally from 0.742 to 0.732 while bringing the covariance vector (0,0) inside the 95% confidence region.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the covariance-threshold method of Zafar et al. (2017), which constrains the sample covariance between sensitive attributes and the decision boundary to lie within a fixed threshold c. The EL-based method dominates Zafar et al. in six head-to-head comparisons: at α=0.1 vs c=0.3 (EL: ACC 0.843, DP 0.065, EO 0.207 vs Zafar: ACC 0.843, DP 0.190, EO 0.366), at α=0.2 vs c=0.1 (EL: ACC 0.842, DP 0.050, EO 0.188 vs Zafar: ACC 0.842, DP 0.094, EO 0.240), at α=0.3 vs c=0.2 (EL: ACC 0.840, DP 0.050, EO 0.182 vs Zafar: ACC 0.840, DP 0.124, EO 0.299), and at three additional pairings (α=0.1 vs c=0.1, α=0.1 vs c=0.2, α=0.2 vs c=0.2) where EL achieves equal or higher accuracy with substantially lower DP and EO. The paper reports no case in which Zafar et al. outperforms the EL method on both accuracy and fairness simultaneously. A key structural advantage is that the EL method uses a dimensionless significance level α ∈ [0,1] as the fairness knob, whereas Zafar et al. require a scale-dependent m-dimensional threshold c that is difficult to calibrate in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of empirical likelihood (Owen, 1988) and covariance-based fairness constraints (Zafar et al., 2017), adopting the latter as a tractable proxy for group fairness and wrapping it in the former to obtain a confidence region. The technical construction of the EL ratio via influence functions follows Zheng et al. (2012), whose Wilk's theorem proof structure is directly reused. The key departure from prior statistical-inference-for-fairness work (Besse et al., 2018; Si et al., 2021; Taskesen et al., 2021) is that those approaches yield limiting distributions containing unknown nuisance parameters, whereas the EL ratio converges to a standard chi-squared distribution with no unknowns, making the resulting hypothesis test and confidence region directly usable without plug-in estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The use of a significance level α ∈ [0,1] as a dimensionless, interpretable fairness threshold is a practical improvement over the arbitrary m-dimensional covariance vector c in Zafar et al. (2017), and the confidence-region formulation naturally handles multiple sensitive attributes (discrete and continuous) simultaneously. However, the method is restricted to margin-based classifiers with a linear decision boundary in the experiments, and the covariance proxy itself is acknowledged as imperfect for capturing non-linear dependence. Open questions include extending the framework to non-linear fairness criteria (Rényi correlation, cross-covariance operators) and to non-i.i.d. settings via jackknife EL, both of which the authors sketch but do not fully develop.
