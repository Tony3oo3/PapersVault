## Problem

Deep learning classifiers that are not yet accurate enough for full automation can be made safer through abstention, but standard selective classifiers offer no mechanism to recover the lost coverage. This paper addresses the gap by asking: how can a model that predicts through intermediate concepts use human confirmation of those concepts to improve coverage while maintaining a guaranteed accuracy floor?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240265/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an image (or feature vector) and produces either a binary class label or an abstention symbol ⊥, governed by a confidence threshold τ ∈ [0, 0.5]. Internally, the model first predicts m binary concept probabilities (e.g., 'Dotted', 'Pigmented' for skin lesions) and then maps those to the final label. On abstained instances, a confirmation policy flags specific concepts for a human expert to verify, after which the model re-evaluates and may produce a prediction.

## Method

The conceptual safeguard is a post-hoc verification layer on top of an independently trained concept bottleneck model (concept detectors g and front-end model f). Its key technical contribution is uncertainty propagation: rather than requiring hard concept inputs, the front-end model is evaluated over all 2^m concept realizations weighted by the concept detector probabilities, yielding a calibrated label probability that accounts for concept-level uncertainty. A selection gate then abstains whenever this probability falls in [τ, 1−τ], guaranteeing accuracy ≥ 1−τ under calibration. To recover coverage, a greedy confirmation policy (Algorithm 1) selects which concepts to flag for human review by maximizing the expected variance reduction in the front-end output (i.e., the gain in certainty), subject to a user-specified confirmation budget with per-concept costs.

## Results

At a 95% selective accuracy target (τ=0.05) with a 20% confirmation budget, CS+ImpactConf achieves 84.6% coverage on flycatcher (vs. 26.9% for the Baseline concept bottleneck) and 52.9% on melanoma (vs. 0.0% for Baseline). On warbler, CS+ImpactConf reaches 90.0% coverage at τ=0.05, compared to 17.3% for Baseline and 86.0% for the end-to-end X→Y MLP. Uncertainty propagation alone (CS vs. Baseline) yields the largest gains on flycatcher, where coverage jumps from 0.0% to 36.5% at τ=0.1 without any confirmation, and targeted confirmation (ImpactConf vs. RandomConf) adds a further 38.4 percentage points on flycatcher at τ=0.05.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three baselines: an end-to-end X→Y MLP, a Baseline concept bottleneck (no uncertainty propagation), and Baseline+RandomConf (random concept confirmation). CS+ImpactConf dominates on coverage at tight accuracy thresholds: on flycatcher at τ=0.05 it achieves 84.6% coverage versus 26.9% (Baseline) and 46.2% (Baseline+RandomConf), and on melanoma it reaches 52.9% versus 0.0% for both baselines. The end-to-end X→Y MLP is competitive only at loose thresholds (τ≥0.15) where it achieves 100% coverage, but fails entirely at τ=0.05 on flycatcher, melanoma, and skincancer. Uncertainty propagation (CS vs. Baseline) provides the largest single gain on flycatcher (0.0%→36.5% at τ=0.1 without confirmation), while targeted confirmation (ImpactConf vs. RandomConf) adds the most on flycatcher (+38.4 pp) and melanoma (+44.3 pp) at τ=0.05. On noisyconcepts25, where concept detectors are already reliable, all methods perform similarly and gains from confirmation are modest (32.3%→44.7% at τ=0.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of concept bottleneck models (Koh et al., ICML 2020) and selective classification under the bounded abstention model (Franc et al., JMLR 2023). While prior concept bottleneck work focuses on interpretability or test-time intervention, and prior selective classification work operates on end-to-end models, this paper combines the two by using a concept bottleneck as the backbone for a verification layer that abstains based on propagated concept uncertainty. The key methodological departure is the uncertainty propagation step (Eq. 2), which marginalizes over all concept realizations to produce a calibrated label probability, and the greedy confirmation policy (Algorithm 1) that targets high-variance concepts to recover coverage without compromising the accuracy guarantee.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The conceptual safeguard offers a practical, off-the-shelf recipe for safe partial automation in domains where concept annotations exist (medical imaging, species identification), and the confirmation policy provides a principled way to trade limited human effort for substantial coverage gains. However, the approach inherits the fundamental limitation of concept bottlenecks: if concepts do not capture all label-relevant information, performance will lag end-to-end models, and the paper acknowledges this gap can be large. Open questions include how well the framework scales to multiclass settings with many concepts, how to handle incomplete or noisy concept annotations in deployment, and whether abstention-induced coverage disparities across subpopulations can be mitigated through the confirmation policy.
