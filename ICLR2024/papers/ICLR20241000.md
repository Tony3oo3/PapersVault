## Problem

Existing autoencoder-based collaborative filtering methods (e.g., AutoRec) use a linear activation in the decoder output, implicitly assuming ratings are linear interactions of learned features. This assumption fails when ratings arise from nonlinear response functions (as in neuroscience, imaging, or sensor data), and no prior work has provided generalization guarantees for autoencoder or deep-learning-based matrix completion under either MCAR or MNAR missingness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241000/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an incomplete m×n rating matrix X and produces a fully completed matrix by predicting all missing entries. Missing values are temporarily filled with zeros to form X̃, and the model is trained only on observed entries via a masked reconstruction loss. The element-wise network is shared across all m output nodes, so it learns a single scalar-to-scalar function applied element-wise to the main autoencoder's output. The framework handles both MCAR and MNAR settings; under MNAR, the mask S can be replaced by inverse-propensity weights Q_{ij}=p̂_{ij}^{-1/2}.

## Method

AEMC-NE composes two neural networks: a main autoencoder g_W (with L_W layers) that maps the zero-filled matrix to a low-dimensional bottleneck and back, and a small element-wise network h_Θ (with L_Θ layers, width p≪m) that is applied independently to each output scalar of g_W to learn an adaptive activation function. The training objective minimizes the masked Frobenius reconstruction error ‖S⊙(X̃−h_Θ(g_W(X̃)))‖_F² plus L2 regularization on both sets of weights, solved with Adam. The key architectural insight is that the element-wise network, being shared across all output nodes, introduces only O(p) extra parameters per layer rather than O(mp) as would be needed to add equivalent depth to the decoder, yielding a tighter generalization bound. The paper proves generalization error bounds under both MCAR and MNAR, showing that the element-wise network reduces the upper bound when the true response nonlinearity is strong, that zero-filling is theoretically advantageous, and that the bound tightens as the sample-to-variable ratio grows.

## Results

On MovieLens-100k, AEMC-NE achieves RMSE 0.8767±0.0089, outperforming the best baseline AEMC with ReLU output (0.8807) and LLORMA (0.8881). On MovieLens-1M, AEMC-NE reaches RMSE 0.8248±0.0024, beating CF-NADE (0.829) and AEMC (0.8291). On Douban and Flixster, AEMC-NE achieves RMSE 0.7286 and 0.8816 respectively, outperforming all baselines including GC-MC (0.734/0.917) and GRAEM (0.7497/0.8857). On MovieLens-10M, AEMC-NE (0.7723) is slightly behind CF-NADE (0.771) but still beats AEMC (0.782) and AEMC with ReLU output (0.7851).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AEMC-NE outperforms all baselines on MovieLens-100k (best baseline: AEMC-ReLU at 0.8807 vs. 0.8767) and MovieLens-1M (best baseline: AEMC-ReLU at 0.8276 vs. 0.8248), with the largest margins over traditional matrix factorization methods like BiasMF (0.911) and LLORMA (0.8881) on ML-100k. On MovieLens-10M, AEMC-NE (0.7723) is marginally behind CF-NADE (0.771) but clearly beats AEMC/AutoRec (0.782) and AEMC-ReLU (0.7851). On Douban, AEMC-NE (0.7286) beats GC-MC (0.734), Factorized EAE (0.738), and GRAEM (0.7497); on Flixster, it achieves 0.8816, outperforming GRAEM (0.8857), Factorized EAE (0.908), and GC-MC (0.917). Against the direct predecessor AEMC/AutoRec, AEMC-NE consistently improves RMSE by 0.002–0.006 across all five CF benchmarks, confirming the benefit of the element-wise adaptive activation network. On the UCI imputation datasets (Appendix G), AEMC-NE beats the standard Autoencoder on all four datasets and outperforms GAIN on Credit (0.1775 vs. 0.1858), though GAIN remains stronger on Breast and Letter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends AutoRec (Sedhain et al., 2015), the canonical autoencoder-based collaborative filtering method, by appending a small element-wise neural network to the decoder's output layer to learn an adaptive activation function rather than assuming a fixed linear or pre-specified nonlinear mapping. Conceptually, it departs from the implicit low-rank assumption of standard AEMC by allowing the output to be a nonlinear transformation of the latent representation, motivated by the observation that real-world ratings may follow nonlinear response curves. The theoretical analysis draws on Rademacher complexity and transductive learning (El-Yaniv & Pechyony, 2009) to provide generalization bounds for autoencoder-based matrix completion under both MCAR and MNAR, a gap the authors explicitly identify as unaddressed in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The primary contribution is filling a notable theoretical gap: prior autoencoder-based CF methods lacked rigorous generalization guarantees, and this paper provides the first such bounds for both MCAR and MNAR settings, showing that the element-wise network can tighten the bound and that data sparsity is theoretically beneficial rather than harmful. Practically, the method is simple to implement (a small shared network added to the output layer) and consistently outperforms AutoRec across five benchmarks, with the most notable gains under MNAR (e.g., 0.920 vs. 0.988 RMSE on ML-100K). However, improvements over strong non-neural baselines like LLORMA remain modest on near-square matrices, and the method requires architectural modification for new users, limiting its applicability in streaming recommendation settings. Open questions include whether the adaptive output nonlinearity generalizes to implicit feedback and whether the theoretical bounds can be made tighter to better predict when AEMC-NE will outperform linear methods.
