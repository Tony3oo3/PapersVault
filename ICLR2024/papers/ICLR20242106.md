## Problem

Safety alignment for LLMs (via RLHF, SFT, red teaming) is conducted almost exclusively in natural language, yet LLMs demonstrably understand non-natural languages such as Morse code, ROT13, and Base64. This raises the question of whether safety training generalizes to cipher domains where the model's capabilities exist but its safety constraints do not — a concrete instance of the 'mismatched generalization' failure mode identified by Wei et al. (2024).

## Input / Output

The framework takes a natural-language unsafe query and a chosen cipher function (e.g., Caesar shift-3, ASCII, Unicode, Morse, or SelfCipher), enciphers the query, and prepends a three-part system prompt (role assignment, cipher explanation, 3-shot enciphered unsafe demonstrations). The LLM's cipher-formatted response is then passed through a rule-based decrypter to recover natural-language text. For SelfCipher specifically, no actual enciphering or deciphering step is performed — the query, demonstrations, and response all remain in natural language, with only the system prompt triggering the cipher role-play. Evaluation reports unsafety rate (percentage of toxic responses) across 11 safety domains in both English and Chinese, using a GPT-4-based classifier validated at 96.3% accuracy against human annotators.

## Method

CipherChat constructs a system prompt with three components: (1) Behavior Assigning, which casts the LLM as a cipher expert and explicitly forbids translation; (2) Cipher Teaching, which explains the cipher's rules in-context leveraging the model's in-context learning ability; and (3) Enciphered Unsafe Demonstrations, which simultaneously reinforce cipher comprehension and inject unaligned behavioral priors. The key insight is that by forcing all interaction into a cipher channel, the natural-language safety alignment is circumvented because the model's safety training never covered that domain. The paper's most novel contribution is SelfCipher, which omits all explicit cipher rules and instead uses only the prompt 'You are an expert on the Cipher Code' plus natural-language unsafe demonstrations, evoking an implicit internal cipher in the LLM. Ablations show that both the system role prompt and the unsafe demonstrations are critical (removing either drops unsafety rates dramatically), while replacing unsafe demos with safe ones effectively mitigates the attack. Simulated ciphers with random alphabet mappings that never appear in pretraining data fail entirely, confirming the model relies on learned character-level cipher priors rather than rule-following.

## Results

On the Crimes domain, the best English cipher (ASCII) achieves a 68.3% unsafety rate on GPT-4 and 37.2% on Turbo, while the best Chinese cipher (UTF) reaches 46.2% on GPT-4 and 39.2% on Turbo. The proposed SelfCipher, which uses no explicit cipher rules, achieves 70.9% unsafety rate on English GPT-4 and 53.3% on Chinese GPT-4, outperforming all human-defined ciphers. Averaged across all 11 safety domains, the best English cipher (ASCII) bypasses safety alignment at 23.7% (Turbo) and 72.1% (GPT-4), and the best Chinese cipher (Unicode) at 17.4% (Turbo) and 45.2% (GPT-4), compared to 0% for vanilla prompts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CipherChat (various ciphers + SelfCipher) against two baselines: Vanilla (no cipher, no demos) and Vanilla + UnsafeDemo (natural-language unsafe demonstrations without cipher). All cipher-based methods dramatically outperform both baselines, which achieve at most 5.5% unsafety rate, while ciphers reach 35–73%. SelfCipher consistently outperforms every human-defined cipher: on English GPT-4 it achieves 70.9% vs. ASCII's 68.3% and Caesar's 73.4% (Caesar slightly edges SelfCipher on GPT-4 English), and on Chinese GPT-4 it reaches 53.3% vs. UTF's 46.2%. A key finding is that GPT-4 is more vulnerable than Turbo across nearly all ciphers (e.g., SelfCipher: 70.9% vs. 38.2% on English), confirming that more capable models are less safe under cipher-based attacks. Ablations show that both the SystemRole prompt and UnsafeDemo are essential: removing either drops SelfCipher's unsafety rate from 70.9% to 1.0% (no SystemRole) or 3.0% (no UnsafeDemo) on English GPT-4, while replacing unsafe demos with safe ones reduces it to 1.0%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial jailbreak literature by systematically testing whether safety alignment (conducted almost exclusively in natural language via RLHF, SFT, and red teaming) generalizes to non-natural-language inputs such as ciphers. It builds on the 'mismatched generalization' failure mode identified by Wei et al. (2024) and the 'secret language' phenomenon observed in smaller LMs (Wang et al., 2023b), but repurposes both into a concrete attack framework (CipherChat) and a novel SelfCipher that requires no explicit encoding rules. The in-context learning literature (Dong et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2023) informs the cipher-teaching component, while the broader safety alignment lineage (Constitutional AI, Safe RLHF, Beaver) provides the baseline that CipherChat is shown to bypass.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental gap: safety alignment is trained in natural language, yet models like GPT-4 can fluently understand and generate in ciphers, creating a domain where capabilities exist but safety constraints do not. The finding that more powerful models are paradoxically more vulnerable (GPT-4 > Turbo across nearly all cipher settings) raises urgent concerns for the trajectory of increasingly capable LLMs. SelfCipher's effectiveness with no actual encoding—just the word 'Cipher' in a role-play prompt—suggests the vulnerability is deeper than a simple input-encoding mismatch. Open questions include how to develop safety alignment that covers non-natural languages, whether the 'secret cipher' is a stable or emergent property, and whether these findings generalize beyond GPT-family models to open-source architectures at scale.
