## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability has identified the specific computational strategies (e.g., Fourier circuits, group-representation-based features) that neural networks learn on algebraic tasks, but has not explained why networks converge to these particular solutions among many possible correct classifiers. This paper addresses that gap by showing that the inductive bias of margin maximization—typically studied only for generalization—alone is sufficient to analytically and completely characterize the features that trained networks learn on modular addition, sparse parity, and finite group composition.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241148/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

For the group tasks (modular addition, group composition), inputs are pairs of one-hot vectors in R^{|G|} representing two group elements, and the network produces a |G|-dimensional logit vector whose argmax should be the group product. Each neuron in the 1-hidden-layer, bias-free MLP has three weight vectors (u, v, w) and computes (u·x^(1) + v·x^(2))² w under quadratic activation. For sparse parity, inputs are sign vectors x ∈ {±1}^n and the output is a 2-dimensional logit vector for the parity over a fixed k-element subset S of coordinates, with x^k activation per neuron.

## Method

The core technique is a max-min duality argument: a 'certificate pair' (θ*, q*) is constructed where q* is the uniform distribution over the symmetric dataset and θ* is an explicit network, such that q* minimizes the expected margin over all distributions and θ* maximizes it over all networks—establishing θ* as a global max-margin solution via Sion's minimax theorem. Because the network is 1-hidden-layer and homogeneous, the expected margin decomposes linearly across neurons, reducing the problem to finding optimal single-neuron directions under an L_a norm constraint, then combining them under an L_{a,ν} norm (where ν is the homogeneity constant). For modular addition, this yields neurons whose weight vectors are single-frequency cosine waves (Fourier-sparse); for k-sparse parity, neurons with equal-magnitude weights supported exactly on the relevant bits; and for general finite groups, neurons whose weight vectors are spanned by a single irreducible representation. Theorem 1 of Wei et al. (2019) then guarantees that gradient descent with vanishing L_{2,ν} regularization converges to these max-margin solutions, connecting the theory to empirical training dynamics.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contributions are closed-form expressions for the maximum margin across three algebraic tasks. For modular addition (mod-p) with quadratic activations, the maximum L_{2,3} margin is γ* = √(2/27)·1/(p^{1/2}(p−1)), achieved only when every neuron is 1-sparse in Fourier space (Theorem 5). For (n,k)-sparse parity with x^k activations, the maximum L_{2,k+1} margin is γ* = k!·√(2(k+1)^{−(k+1)}), requiring all relevant-bit weights to have equal magnitude (Theorem 6). For group composition on G, the maximum L_{2,3} margin is γ* = (2/(3√(3|G|)))·1/(Σ d_{R_n}^{2.5}), with each neuron spanned by a single irreducible representation (Theorem 7). Empirically, 1-hidden-layer networks trained with gradient descent and matching L_{2,ν} regularization approach these theoretical margins asymptotically, and their weight structure (Fourier sparsity, representation concentration) matches the predictions exactly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing methods in the traditional benchmark sense; rather, it provides an analytical explanation for empirical observations previously reported by Nanda et al. (2023) and Chughtai et al. (2023). The key 'comparison' is between the theoretical max-margin prediction and the actual weights of networks trained with gradient descent: for modular addition, the quadratic-activation network's neurons become exactly 1-sparse in frequency space (maximum normalized power ≈ 1.0), matching the theory, whereas the ReLU network's neurons are nearly but not exactly 1-sparse (maximum power ≈ 0.9), consistent with the theory applying strictly to quadratic activations. For group composition on S₃, S₄, and S₅, trained networks show all neurons concentrating in a single irreducible representation (maximum representation power → 1) and the distribution of neurons across representations matching the theoretical construction (higher-dimensional representations receive more neurons). The normalized L_{2,3} margin of trained networks asymptotically approaches the closed-form γ* predicted by the theorems in all cases. The paper also contrasts its max-margin characterization with an alternative 'memorization' construction (Appendix D) that correctly classifies all data but has a dense Fourier spectrum and lower margin, demonstrating that margin maximization is the distinguishing principle.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate threads: mechanistic interpretability (Nanda et al. 2023; Chughtai et al. 2023), which empirically identified Fourier features and irreducible group representations in trained networks, and inductive bias theory (Wei et al. 2019; Soudry et al. 2018), which connects regularization to margin maximization. The key conceptual move is showing that margin maximization alone — a principle typically invoked for generalization — is sufficient to analytically derive the exact features learned on algebraic tasks, rather than merely bounding generalization. The construction for modular addition is a special case of Gromov's (2023) analytic quadratic network scheme, and the max-min duality approach for certifying max-margin solutions differs from the perturbation-based arguments in Bronstein et al. (2022) for DNF learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for why networks consistently arrive at Fourier-based circuits for modular addition and representation-theoretic features for group operations, rather than other valid but less structured solutions. The framework is general in principle (symmetric datasets, homogeneous activations, L_{2,ν} norms) but currently limited to one-hidden-layer networks and specific activation/norm combinations; extending to deeper architectures, ReLU activations, or more complex tasks remains open. The paper also highlights that correctly classifying networks need not exhibit Fourier sparsity (Appendix D), underscoring that margin maximization is the critical selection principle rather than task solvability alone.
