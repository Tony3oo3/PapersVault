## Problem

Existing SMoE approaches for multilingual machine translation apply a fixed top-k token-level router that ignores two intrinsic structures of the MMT problem: (a) the linguistic hierarchy, where languages are naturally grouped by family, phonology, and grammar, and (b) heterogeneous language complexity, where data scarcity and grammatical difficulty vary widely across languages. This leads to suboptimal expert allocation and over-/under-fitting on low- and high-resource translation directions respectively.

## Input / Output

The model takes a source-language sentence (tokenized with SentencePiece, vocabulary 32K–64K depending on dataset) and a target-language ID as input. The target-language ID is embedded through a dedicated embedding layer and two fully-connected layers into a 512-dimensional language vector that drives the first-level routing decision. The output is the translated sentence in the target language, generated autoregressively by a Transformer-Base decoder with SMoE blocks replacing every other feed-forward layer in both encoder and decoder (32 experts per SMoE layer, hidden dim 2048).

## Method

Lingual-SMoE introduces a two-level hierarchical router: a language-level router G_l takes the 512-dim language embedding and selects a candidate expert set S_l of size k_l (default 8) via top-k_l, then a token-level router selects top-2 experts from S_l for each token. A contrastive language-grouping loss L_l (cosine-similarity based, applied to the language router outputs) encourages experts to specialize in particular language families, and the language embeddings are pre-trained with a contrastive objective over language-family classes. A Dynamic Expert Allocation (DEA) mechanism adaptively adjusts k_l per resource group (high/medium/low) during training: if validation perplexity fails to improve for Δn iterations, k_l is incremented; after an exploration phase (first 80% of training), if improvement still stalls, k_l is decremented and frozen. The total training objective is L_MT + 0.05·L_g (load-balancing) + 0.05·L_l (language grouping), trained with Adam and inverse-square-root LR schedule on OPUS-16/50/100.

## Results

On OPUS-16, Lingual-SMoE achieves 32.71 average BLEU, outperforming the dense Transformer-Base baseline (28.79) by 3.92 BLEU and the vanilla GS-SMoE (30.29) by 2.42 BLEU. On the larger OPUS-100 dataset, Lingual-SMoE reaches 27.67 average BLEU versus 22.21 for Dense, a 5.46 BLEU improvement, with a 97% win-rate across language pairs. The method also achieves 27.84 BLEU on OPUS-50 (vs. 24.09 Dense), confirming consistent gains as the number of languages scales from 16 to 100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lingual-SMoE outperforms all six baselines on OPUS-16. Against the dense Transformer-Base (28.79 BLEU), it gains 3.92 BLEU; against vanilla SMoE variants GS-SMoE (30.29) and ST-SMoE (31.79), it gains 2.42 and 0.92 BLEU respectively. The fixed language-specific routing (LS-SMoE, 26.75) actually underperforms the dense model, while Hybrid-SMoE (28.35) also falls below Dense, showing that rigid or single-level language routing is insufficient. The strongest baseline is Residual-SMoE (31.97), which adds a shared expert to token-level routing; Lingual-SMoE still edges it out by 0.74 BLEU, and the combined LGR⁺ʳᵉˢ variant (32.61) further narrows the gap to 0.10 BLEU. On OPUS-100, the advantage over Dense widens to 5.46 BLEU (27.67 vs. 22.21) and over GS-SMoE to 2.85 BLEU (27.67 vs. 24.82), with a 97% win-rate, confirming that the hierarchical language-guided routing and dynamic expert allocation scale effectively with the number of languages.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SMoE framework for multilingual machine translation by building on GShard's top-2 token routing and Kudugunta et al.'s language-based routing, but departs from both by introducing a two-level hierarchical router that first selects a language-specific expert group (guided by linguistic family structure via contrastive loss) and then performs token-level routing within that group. It further introduces a dynamic expert allocation mechanism that adapts the number of candidate experts per language based on validation perplexity during training, addressing the heterogeneous difficulty of translation directions. The key conceptual departure is that vanilla SMoE routing fails to capture linguistic hierarchy (demonstrated via a pilot study), and fixed expert capacity leads to over-fitting on low-resource languages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical and well-motivated recipe for incorporating linguistic priors into SMoE routing, with the 5% BLEU gain over dense on OPUS-100 being meaningful for deployment. However, the gains from dynamic expert allocation over the static LGR-SMoE are modest (~0.4% average), and the approach remains English-centric (all pairs involve English), limiting generalizability to truly many-to-many settings. Open questions include whether the linguistic family grouping assumption holds for isolates or constructed languages, whether the method transfers to non-English hub architectures, and whether the contrastive grouping loss can be replaced by learned representations without manual family labels.
