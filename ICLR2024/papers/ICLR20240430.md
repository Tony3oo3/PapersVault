## Problem

While multitask finetuning of foundation models has shown strong empirical gains for few-shot adaptation, it lacks rigorous theoretical justification for why and when it works. In particular, no prior analysis quantified how the diversity and consistency of auxiliary finetuning tasks relative to a target task govern the resulting error reduction, nor did a principled algorithm exist for selecting which auxiliary tasks to use.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240430/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained foundation model (from contrastive, masked, or supervised pretraining), M auxiliary tasks each with m labeled samples whose classes may be entirely disjoint from the target task, and a target task in N-way K-shot format. Finetuning uses a nearest centroid classifier: all samples are encoded by the model, per-class centroids are computed, and cosine similarity between a query and each centroid serves as the class logit. The output is the finetuned encoder, which at test time is paired with the same nearest centroid classifier to assign class labels to query samples.

## Method

The paper establishes a theoretical framework bounding the target-task error after multitask finetuning in terms of two quantities: a diversity parameter ν (measuring how well the auxiliary tasks' representation differences cover the target task's class family) and a consistency parameter κ (measuring the gap between the optimal model for auxiliary tasks and the optimal model for the target task). A linear case study reveals that diversity corresponds to coverage of the target's discriminative feature dimension by the auxiliary tasks, while consistency corresponds to similarity in the latent feature space. Guided by these insights, the authors propose a greedy task selection algorithm that sorts candidate tasks by cosine similarity of their representation-space means to the target (proxy for consistency) and sequentially adds tasks until a Mahalanobis-distance-based coverage score (proxy for diversity) stops increasing. The finetuning procedure minimizes the average empirical supervised loss across selected auxiliary tasks via gradient descent from the pretrained initialization, updating all model parameters.

## Results

On the most challenging 1-shot DomainNet setting, multitask finetuning with DINOv2 ViT-S achieves 61.57% accuracy, outperforming standard finetuning (54.48%) by 7.1% and direct adaptation (52.28%) by 9.3%. Gains are even more dramatic for supervised-pretrained models: on DomainNet 1-shot, supervised ViT-B jumps from 30.93% (Standard FT) to 48.02% (Ours), a 67% relative improvement. On miniImageNet 1-shot, the method consistently adds 4–6% over baselines (e.g., 80.62% vs. 75.38% with MoCo-v3 ViT-B; 96.91% vs. 95.28% with supervised ViT-B). The task selection algorithm on Meta-dataset yields up to 19.7% accuracy gain over random task selection (Omniglot, DINOv2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares multitask finetuning against two baselines: (1) direct adaptation (linear probing on frozen pretrained encoder) and (2) standard finetuning (appending a linear head and finetuning the full model on target-task data only). Multitask finetuning consistently outperforms both baselines across all 12 model/dataset configurations tested. The largest absolute gains appear on DomainNet, where the domain gap is greatest: supervised ViT-B improves from 30.93% to 48.02% (1-shot) and 52.14% to 67.25% (5-shot), while DINOv2 ViT-S improves from 54.48% to 61.57% (1-shot). On in-domain datasets like miniImageNet and tieredImageNet, gains are smaller (1–6%) but still consistent. The paper notes that supervised-pretrained models benefit more from multitask finetuning than contrastive-pretrained ones, suggesting the latter already have smaller domain gaps. Standard finetuning itself provides only marginal gains over direct adaptation (typically <1%), highlighting that the multitask auxiliary data is the key driver of improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical framework of Arora et al. (2019) on contrastive representation learning and the task diversity notion of Tripuraneni et al. (2020) to the setting of multitask *finetuning* of self-supervised foundation models, rather than the supervised pretraining analyzed in prior work. While multitask finetuning has been empirically validated in NLP and vision (Sanh et al., 2022; Muennighoff et al., 2023), the authors note that prior theoretical analyses (Du et al., 2021; Tripuraneni et al., 2021) focused on representations from supervised multitask pretraining, leaving the self-supervised + finetuning pipeline theoretically unaddressed. The paper also departs from meta-learning approaches by providing a direct generalization guarantee rather than an adaptation-speed guarantee, and introduces a practical task selection algorithm grounded in the theoretical diversity/consistency conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical justification for why and when multitask finetuning improves few-shot adaptation of foundation models, filling a gap between empirical success and theoretical understanding. The diversity and consistency framework offers practitioners a principled (if approximate) criterion for selecting auxiliary tasks, and the greedy selection algorithm demonstrates consistent gains over random or all-task finetuning across multiple backbones and datasets. Key limitations include the difficulty of accurately estimating diversity and consistency parameters on real-world data, and the authors explicitly note their focus is not on achieving SOTA performance. Open questions include whether efficient algorithms exist for estimating these parameters at scale and whether the framework extends to more complex adaptation scenarios beyond linear probing.
