## Problem

Previous attempts to quantify the statistical benefits of CNN architectural biases (locality and weight sharing) over LCNs and FCNs either provided only uniform convergence upper bounds without separating lower bounds, or relied on data models that failed to capture the locality and translation invariance inherent in real vision tasks. This paper introduces the Dynamic Signal Distribution (DSD) task, which properly encodes both properties, and establishes rigorous sample complexity separations among FCNs, LCNs, and CNNs trained with equivariant algorithms such as gradient descent.

## Input / Output

The input is a vector x ∈ R^{kd} structured as k consecutive patches of dimension d, where exactly one randomly chosen patch is filled with a noisy signed d-sparse signal (unit vector w* scaled by the label) and the remaining k−1 patches contain isotropic Gaussian noise of variance σ². The binary output label is the sign of the signal, making the task translation-invariant: the same label is produced regardless of which of the k patches contains the signal. The key difficulty parameters are the noise level σ and the number of patches k, which jointly control how hard it is to locate and recover the signal.

## Method

The paper develops a novel Fano's Theorem for randomized algorithms that relaxes the standard semi-metric requirement, enabling lower bounds on tasks where the input marginal is a mixture of Gaussians rather than a single orthogonally invariant distribution. A simulation-style argument decomposes the DSD learning problem into k nearly independent subproblems (one per patch), and for FCNs a boosting technique further reduces each subproblem to Gaussian mean estimation on the unit sphere, yielding an Ω(σ²k²d) lower bound; for LCNs the relaxed Fano's theorem gives Ω(σ²kd). Upper bounds are obtained via a two-step gradient descent analysis using the Local Signal Adaptivity (LSA) activation function, which soft-thresholds to filter noise below a trainable bias while propagating signal, achieving Õ(σ²k(k+d)) samples for LCNs and Õ(σ²(k+d)) for CNNs. All bounds are restricted to equivariant algorithms, ensuring the separation holds for computationally efficient methods like gradient descent rather than only in the unconstrained minimax sense.

## Results

On the Dynamic Signal Distribution (DSD) task, the paper establishes sample complexity separations for equivariant algorithms (e.g., gradient descent). FCNs require Ω(σ²k²d) samples to achieve constant risk, LCNs require Ω(σ²kd) samples (lower bound) with an upper bound of Õ(σ²k(k+d)), and CNNs achieve Õ(σ²(k+d)) samples. The separations quantify a multiplicative factor of k for weight sharing (LCN vs. CNN) and another factor of k for locality (FCN vs. LCN), with the d factor arising from learning the d-dimensional signal vector.

**Summary of sample complexity bounds on the DSD task (equivariant algorithms, constant risk δ).**

| Architecture | Lower Bound | Upper Bound | Key Architectural Bias |
|---|---|---|---|
| FCN | Ω(σ²k²d) | — | None |
| LCN | Ω(σ²kd) | Õ(σ²k(k+d)) | Locality |
| CNN | — | Õ(σ²(k+d)) | Locality + Weight Sharing |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper performs a three-way theoretical comparison between FCNs, LCNs, and CNNs on the DSD task, which encodes both locality and translation invariance. FCNs, lacking both architectural biases, incur a multiplicative cost of k²: one factor of k for identifying which of the k patches contains the signal, and another factor of k for learning the signal independently at each patch location. LCNs eliminate the patch-identification cost (locality is baked into the architecture) but still require learning the signal independently at each of the k patches, yielding a k× gap over CNNs. CNNs, with weight sharing, learn the signal jointly across all patches, achieving the best sample complexity of Õ(σ²(k+d)). The k+d factor in the LCN and CNN upper bounds (rather than the expected d) is noted as an artifact of the gradient descent analysis, suggesting a potential algorithmic efficiency cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the equivariant-algorithm framework of Li et al. (2021) and the three-way FCN/LCN/CNN separation of Wang & Wu (2023) by introducing the Dynamic Signal Distribution (DSD) task, which explicitly encodes locality and translation invariance in a way that prior polynomial-based data models did not. It also adopts the Local Signal Adaptivity activation from Karp et al. (2021) to model noise filtering. A key methodological departure is the development of a Fano's Theorem variant for randomized algorithms that relaxes the semi-metric requirement, enabling lower bounds under the non-orthogonally-invariant mixture-of-Gaussians marginal that DSD induces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DSD task provides the first sample complexity separation between CNNs, LCNs, and FCNs on a data model that genuinely mirrors the signal-in-noise, locally-mobile structure of real vision tasks, strengthening the theoretical justification for architectural inductive biases. The observation that both LCN and CNN upper bounds carry a k+d factor (rather than the expected d) suggests a potential algorithmic cost of gradient descent beyond the architectural one, opening a question about whether more sophisticated optimizers could close this gap. Limitations include the single-signal, single-hidden-layer setting; the authors note that extending to multiple simultaneous signals, deeper networks, and second-order image statistics are natural next steps.
