## Problem

Large language models exhibit emergent arithmetic capabilities despite being trained only with next-token prediction, yet the specific factors driving this emergence in small models remain poorly understood. This paper asks: what data formatting, sampling, and training choices are necessary for small transformers trained from random initialization to efficiently learn basic arithmetic, and why do sharp phase transitions from 0% to 100% accuracy occur as a function of training data scale?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240992/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives arithmetic expressions as character-level tokenized text (e.g., '123+456=') and autoregressively generates the result digit by digit. In scratchpad formats, the output is structured as intermediate computation steps (digit-wise sums, carry values, or full execution traces) followed by the final numeric answer, all within a single autoregressive sequence. Few-shot prompting prepends one or more worked arithmetic examples to the input, and the model must condition on this context to produce the correct result. The key structural constraint is that the next-token objective forces the model to commit to the most significant digit first in plain format, whereas reverse and scratchpad formats allow least-significant-digit-first generation.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a systematic ablation of data formatting and sampling for next-token-prediction arithmetic learning, grounded in two lemmas: Lemma 1 proves that MSB-first output requires access to all operand digits (a 'global' function), while Lemma 2 shows LSB-first output only requires local digit-wise addition plus carry (a 'local' function). The paper introduces four data formats—plain, reverse (output digits reversed), simplified scratchpad (digit sums and carries), and detailed scratchpad (full intermediate traces)—and shows that each successive format reduces sample complexity, with detailed scratchpad achieving 100% accuracy on 3-digit addition with only 1,000 samples. A key theoretical insight connects the addition table to a rank-2 matrix completion problem, explaining the observed phase transitions at O(n) samples, while also demonstrating that NanoGPT generalizes beyond what matrix completion predicts (e.g., handling excluded numbers and digits). Structured sampling that balances operand digit lengths and carry-on counts further improves accuracy, and the paper extends findings to subtraction, multiplication, sine, and square root, as well as to fine-tuning GPT-2 and GPT-3.

## Results

On 3-digit addition with NanoGPT (10.6M params), the plain format never reaches 100% accuracy even with 10,000+ samples, while the reverse format achieves 100% at ~4k samples, simplified scratchpad at ~2k samples, and detailed scratchpad at just ~1k samples. For GPT-3 fine-tuning on 1,000 addition samples, detailed scratchpad achieves 99.5–99.8% accuracy across Davinci, Curie, and Ada, compared to 0–34% for plain and 6.3–80.9% for reverse. The plain format's sample complexity grows with digit length (5, 7, 10 digits), while reverse and scratchpad formats maintain consistent sample requirements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary quantitative comparison is against the low-rank matrix completion (LRMC) algorithm of Kiraly et al. (2015), which treats n-digit addition as completing a rank-2 matrix. Both NanoGPT and the LRMC iterative algorithm exhibit a sharp phase transition at approximately 1,500 samples for 2-digit addition (n=100), confirming the matrix-completion analogy. However, NanoGPT substantially outperforms LRMC in generalization: when 100, 200, or 500 numbers are excluded from training data, NanoGPT (reverse format) maintains 99.8–100% accuracy on the excluded set, whereas LRMC fundamentally cannot recover missing rows or columns. This demonstrates that the transformer learns a richer function than a pure rank-2 matrix completion, generalizing to unseen operands and even unseen digits in specific positions (maintaining >66% accuracy on excluded digits).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the scratchpad/chain-of-thought training paradigm introduced by Nye et al. (2021) and Zhou et al. (2022b), adapting their intermediate-step data formats to a controlled arithmetic setting with small decoder-only transformers trained from random initialization. It departs from prior arithmetic-with-transformers work (Nogueira et al., 2021; Wallace et al., 2019; Dziri et al., 2023) by systematically ablating data formatting, sampling strategies, and their interplay with model scale, rather than focusing on a single architectural or prompting choice. A distinctive theoretical contribution is the connection between the observed phase transitions in addition learning and low-rank matrix completion (Kiraly et al., 2015; Recht, 2011), which provides a principled explanation for the sharp 0-to-100% accuracy jumps. The authors explicitly position their work as an ablation study rather than a methodological novelty, emphasizing that the data formats themselves are borrowed from the CoT literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rare controlled, factor-by-factor dissection of what actually drives arithmetic capability emergence in transformers, showing that data formatting (output reversal, scratchpad decomposition) and balanced sampling matter far more than model scale for small models. The low-rank matrix completion connection offers a theoretical lens for understanding phase transitions, though the authors note it does not capture the full generalization behavior of transformers. Key open questions remain: length generalization beyond trained digit lengths is still unsolved, the findings are validated only up to ~124M parameters and character-level tokenization, and it is unclear how these insights transfer to the multi-task, multi-domain training regimes of modern LLMs. The observation that models learn digit-length-constrained mapping functions rather than flexible algorithms underscores a gap between high in-distribution accuracy and genuine algorithmic understanding.
