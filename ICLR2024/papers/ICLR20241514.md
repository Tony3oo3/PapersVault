## Problem

Existing constrained text generation benchmarks focus on fixed, simple constraint types (e.g., include or exclude specific words) that state-of-the-art models like GPT-4 have largely solved, and they require tailored, non-scalable pipelines for data collection and annotation. There is no systematic, extensible framework for constructing rich, compositional constraints that span multiple text levels (word through passage) and can co-evolve with improving model capabilities to remain challenging.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a user-specified constraint structure (a parameterized family of constraints) and a raw text corpus as input, and produces rendered natural language instructions, model-generated text, and per-base-constraint True/False satisfaction scores aggregated into an average constraint satisfaction rate. Constraint target values are extracted from the corpus to guarantee each constraint admits at least one natural-language solution, and the grammar supports nested positional references (e.g., 'the last sentence of the 3rd paragraph') as well as multi-level counting (e.g., characters per word per sentence).

## Method

COLLIE defines a context-free grammar (Eqs. 1–7) in which a full constraint S is a generation-level specification conjoined with a multi-constraint M, which is a logical composition (∧, ∨) of base-constraints C. Each base-constraint is either a count constraint (count(T, ℓ, v_str) ⊕ v_num, constraining occurrences of a string or of sub-level units within T) or a position constraint (pos(T, ℓ, v_num) ∘ v_str, constraining content at a specific index, including negative indices for reverse counting). A four-stage pipeline automates dataset construction: researchers specify constraint structures with optional value ranges, an extraction algorithm scans corpora to find strings satisfying the structure, rule-based translation renders constraints into natural language instructions, and a parser evaluates model outputs against the grammar to produce satisfaction scores. The compositional design means new constraint types (e.g., POS tags, sentiment) can be added by implementing additional semantic checks without modifying the core grammar, and the COLLIE-v1 dataset instantiates 13 constraint structures across 2,080 instances drawn from Wikipedia, CC-News, and Project Gutenberg.

## Results

On COLLIE-v1 (2,080 constraint instances across 13 constraint structures), GPT-4 achieves the highest average constraint satisfaction rate at 50.9%, with a pass@20 rate above 63%. GPT-3.5-turbo achieves a pass@20 of 32%, while PaLM-2, Vicuna-7B, and Alpaca-7B trail significantly behind. Positional constraints reveal a stark asymmetry: GPT-4 achieves 100% success when asked to begin a sentence with a specific word, but only 40–60% when asked to end a sentence with one. After four rounds of automated feedback, GPT-4's performance improves by ~20% initially but plateaus at 66%, indicating persistent difficulty with compositional counting and arbitrary-position constraints.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 dominates all four baselines on COLLIE-v1, achieving more than twice the constraint satisfaction rate of the next-best non-GPT models (GPT-3.5 and PaLM-2), which in turn substantially outperform the 7B open-source models (Vicuna-7B and Alpaca-7B). The gap is most pronounced on multi-level compositional constraints (e.g., word02, sent02, para03, pass01), where GPT-4 achieves 40–70% satisfaction while all other models score near zero. On simpler existence-based constraints (word01, sent04, para01), all five models perform comparably well, indicating these tasks are saturated. GPT-3.5-turbo and PaLM-2 are separated by a large margin, with GPT-3.5 consistently ahead, while Vicuna-7B and Alpaca-7B perform at nearly identical levels across all constraint groups. The paper does not frame COLLIE as a method to be compared against prior benchmarks (e.g., InstructCTG, CommonGen) head-to-head; rather, the five LLMs serve as the evaluation subjects, and the comparison highlights the benchmark's discriminative power across model scales.

## Relation to Prior Work

COLLIE extends the constrained text generation (CTG) literature, which has progressed from control-code approaches (Keskar et al., 2019) and lexically constrained decoding (Hokamp & Liu, 2017; Lu et al., 2021) to instruction-tuned LLMs, by introducing a formal grammar that composes count and position constraints across multiple text levels (word, sentence, paragraph, passage). It draws on the grammar-based benchmark construction tradition from CLEVR (Johnson et al., 2017) and BabyAI (Chevalier-Boisvert et al., 2019), but departs from those by extracting constraint values from natural language corpora rather than synthetic data, and by designing the framework to be extensible so constraints can co-evolve with model capabilities. The concurrent InstructCTG (Zhou et al., 2023) targets simpler single-type constraints for tuning smaller models, whereas COLLIE explicitly aims at compositional, multi-level challenges for evaluating frontier LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COLLIE provides a systematic, extensible framework that addresses the obsolescence problem of fixed CTG benchmarks: as models improve, new constraint structures can be added with minimal effort, keeping evaluations relevant. The finding that GPT-4 achieves only 50.9% average constraint satisfaction—and that positional constraints at the end of text (40–60%) are dramatically harder than at the beginning (100%)—offers a concrete diagnostic for model developers. Open questions include whether the feedback-loop approach can be improved beyond its 66% plateau, how to incorporate richer semantic constraints (POS, sentiment), and whether the benchmark will remain challenging as next-generation models close the compositional-reasoning gap.
