## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks on LLMs require access to training data or model parameters, making them infeasible against API-only commercial models. The one prior prompt-poisoning backdoor attack (Wang et al., 2023a) works for simple tasks like sentiment classification but fails on complex reasoning tasks that rely on chain-of-thought (COT) prompting. This paper addresses the gap by proposing the first backdoor attack that exploits COT prompting to manipulate LLM outputs on challenging reasoning tasks without any access to training sets or model parameters.

## Input / Output

The attacker constructs a COT prompt in which a subset of demonstrations is poisoned: the backdoor trigger (a non-word token like '@_@' or a phrase generated by querying the LLM) is appended to the question, a backdoor reasoning step is inserted before the final answer, and the answer is replaced with the adversarial target. At inference, the victim user's query prompt is intercepted to embed the same trigger. The LLM then outputs the backdoor reasoning step followed by the adversarial target answer (e.g., the correct arithmetic result multiplied by 2.1, a CSQA choice shifted one letter forward, or an inverted StrategyQA verdict). The attack requires no fine-tuning and operates entirely through the prompt.

## Method

BadChain's key insight is that simply changing the answer in poisoned demonstrations (as in prior work) fails on complex reasoning tasks because LLMs cannot override a coherent multi-step reasoning chain from a few examples. Instead, the attacker inserts a plausible backdoor reasoning step between the standard COT steps and the adversarial answer, creating a logical bridge that the LLM 'learns' to associate with the trigger. The backdoor reasoning step is designed to incorporate the trigger and a task-appropriate operation (e.g., 'multiply by 2.1' for arithmetic, 'shift to next letter' for CSQA, 'reverse the concatenation' for Letter). Two trigger types are supported: non-word tokens and phrase-based triggers obtained by treating the LLM as a one-step black-box optimizer to generate a phrase with weak semantic correlation to the context. The attack is evaluated across two COT strategies (standard COT and self-consistency) on four LLMs (GPT-3.5, Llama2, PaLM2, GPT-4) and six reasoning benchmarks, achieving average ASRs of 85.1%, 76.6%, 87.1%, and 97.0% respectively, while two shuffling-based defenses prove ineffective.

## Results

BadChain achieves average attack success rates (ASR) of 85.1% on GPT-3.5, 76.6% on Llama2, 87.1% on PaLM2, and 97.0% on GPT-4, averaged over both trigger types (non-word and phrase), both COT strategies (COT-S and self-consistency), and all six reasoning benchmarks. In contrast, both baselines (DT-base and DT-COT) fail uniformly with ASR ≤ 18.3% across all settings. Benign accuracy (ACC) drops are negligible in most cases (e.g., GPT-4 ACC remains 90.9–96.2% under attack vs. 91.2–97.0% without attack), confirming the attack is stealthy. Stronger LLMs are more susceptible: GPT-4 achieves the highest ASR while also having the highest baseline ACC (88.4% average).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares BadChain against two variants of the prior prompt-poisoning backdoor attack by Wang et al. (2023a): DT-base (trigger + changed answer, no COT) and DT-COT (trigger + changed answer with COT reasoning steps, but no dedicated backdoor reasoning step). Both baselines fail catastrophically on all six reasoning benchmarks across all four LLMs, with ASR never exceeding 18.3% (and typically below 7% for most model-task combinations). For example, on GPT-4 with COT-S, DT-COT achieves only 4.8% ASR on GSM8K and 18.3% on StrategyQA, while BadChainN achieves 97.0% and 99.1% respectively. The critical difference is that BadChain inserts a plausible backdoor reasoning step (e.g., 'multiply by 2.1') between the trigger and the adversarial answer, which the LLM 'learns' as a logical operation tied to the trigger. The paper's interpretability analysis (Fig. 4) confirms that GPT-4 explicitly recognizes the backdoor reasoning step as a 'specific operation' correlated with the trigger, whereas for DT-COT the model deems the trigger to have 'no significant role.' This demonstrates that for complex reasoning tasks, simply changing the answer in demonstrations is insufficient—the reasoning chain itself must be poisoned.

## Relation to Prior Work

BadChain extends the prompt-poisoning backdoor attack paradigm introduced by Wang et al. (2023a), which contaminated in-context demonstrations with triggers and altered answers. The critical departure is the insertion of a 'backdoor reasoning step' into the chain-of-thought demonstrations, which bridges the trigger to the adversarial target answer and makes the attack effective on complex reasoning tasks where prior demonstration-poisoning methods (DT-base, DT-COT) fail with ASR ≤ 18.3%. The attack mechanism is fundamentally built on the COT prompting framework of Wei et al. (2022) and self-consistency of Wang et al. (2023b), exploiting the structured reasoning sequence as the attack surface rather than the model parameters or training data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs with stronger reasoning capabilities are paradoxically more susceptible (GPT-4: 97.0% ASR vs. Llama2: 76.6%) challenges the implicit assumption that model capability provides robustness against prompt-level attacks. The proposed shuffling defenses are shown to be ineffective while degrading benign accuracy, leaving a significant gap in practical defenses for API-only LLMs. Open questions include whether more sophisticated defenses (e.g., reasoning-step verification, trigger-agnostic consistency checks) can close this gap, and whether the vulnerability generalizes to other structured prompting paradigms like tree-of-thoughts or graph-of-thoughts.
