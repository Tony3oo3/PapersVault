## Problem

The Successive Projection Algorithm (SPA) for vertex hunting is a popular greedy method that estimates simplex vertices one at a time, but it is vulnerable to noise and outliers, producing estimates biased outward from the true simplex. Additionally, the existing non-asymptotic error bound for SPA (Gillis & Vavasis, 2013) depends on s_K(V), which can be near zero when the simplex is close to the origin, making the bound vacuous even when vertex hunting is well-posed.

## Input / Output

The method takes n noisy observations X_i ∈ R^d (each a true simplex point plus Gaussian noise), the number of vertices K, and two tuning parameters: N (minimum neighborhood size for pruning, typically log(n) theoretically or 3 in practice) and Δ (neighborhood radius, heuristically set to max_i ||Y_i − Ȳ||/5). The output is K estimated vertices in R^d, recovered by mapping the (K−1)-dimensional SPA estimates back through the estimated hyperplane. The simplex is assumed to lie on a (K−1)-dimensional hyperplane in R^d, with K ≤ d+1.

## Method

pp-SPA introduces two preprocessing steps before applying orthodox SPA. First, a hyperplane projection step estimates the (K−1)-dimensional hyperplane via truncated SVD of the centered data matrix and projects all points onto it, reducing the effective noise dimension from d to K−1. Second, a pseudo-point denoising step replaces each projected point by the average of all neighbors within radius Δ, deleting points with fewer than N neighbors; this pulls outlier points back toward the simplex interior, directly counteracting SPA's outward bias. The final step applies the standard successive projection algorithm to the resulting pseudo-points in R^{K−1}. Theoretically, the paper derives a sharper non-asymptotic bound for SPA using s_{K−1}(V) and a refined noise measure β_new that ignores interior-point noise, then shows pp-SPA achieves error rates of O(√(log log n)) or O(√(log n)) depending on d/n, improving over SPA's O(√d) when d ≫ log(n).

## Results

The main theoretical result (Table 1) shows that pp-SPA achieves a strictly better error bound than SPA across all regimes of d. When d ≫ log(n), pp-SPA improves the rate by a factor of √(log(n)/d), reducing the error from O(√d) to O(√(log(n))). In the special setting of Theorem 2 (constant fraction of points at each vertex), pp-SPA achieves O(√(log log(n))) error when d/n is small, versus O(√(log(n))) for SPA. The improved non-asymptotic bound for SPA (Theorem 1) replaces the Gillis & Vavasis (2013) bound of g(V)·β(X) with g_new(V)·β_new(X,V); in a concrete numerical example, this tightens the bound from 2151.3 to 18.7.

**Table 1: Sharp orders of β_new(X,V) for different vertex hunting methods (K ≥ 3, s_{K-1}(V) > C).**

| Method | d ≪ log(n) | d = a₀·log(n) | log(n) ≪ d ≪ n^{1−2(1−c₀)/(K−1)} | d ≫ n^{1−2(1−c₀)/(K−1)} |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPA | √(2 log n) | √(a₁ log n) | √d | √d |
| P-SPA | √(2 log n) | √(2 log n) | √(2 log n) | √(2 log n) |
| D-SPA | √(2c₀ log n) | NA | NA | NA |
| **pp-SPA** | **√(2c₀ log n)** | **√(2c₀ log n)** | **√(2c₀ log n)** | **√(2 log n)** |

where c₀ ∈ (0,1) is a constant from the minimum vertex count m, and a₁ > 2 is the unique solution to a₁ − a₀ log(a₁) = 2 + a₀ − a₀ log(a₀).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the orthodox SPA (Araujo et al., 2001), pp-SPA always achieves a strictly smaller error bound: when d ≫ log(n) the improvement is a factor of √(log(n)/d) (e.g., reducing error from O(√d) to O(√log n)), and when d = O(log(n)) the improvement is a constant factor c₀ < 1. The projection step alone (P-SPA) already eliminates the √d dependence, and the denoise step further reduces the constant by a factor of c₀. Against robust-SPA (Gillis, 2019), the numerical experiments (Figure 3, K=3, n=1000) show that pp-SPA, P-SPA, and D-SPA all outperform robust-SPA when d ≤ 10; for d > 10, pp-SPA and P-SPA still outperform robust-SPA while D-SPA (without hyperplane projection) underperforms it. The paper also provides an improved non-asymptotic bound for SPA itself (Theorem 1 vs. Lemma 2 of Gillis & Vavasis 2013), replacing the dependence on s_K(V) with s_{K-1}(V), which is critical when the simplex center is near the origin.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Successive Projection Algorithm (SPA) of Araujo et al. (2001) and the non-asymptotic error bound of Gillis & Vavasis (2013), which the authors identify as the best-known theoretical guarantee for SPA. The key departure is twofold: (1) a new algorithm (pp-SPA) that inserts a hyperplane projection step and a neighborhood-averaging denoise step before running SPA, distinguishing it from prior SPA variants that smooth at each iteration (Bhattacharyya & Kannan 2020, Bakshi et al. 2021, Nadisic et al. 2023); and (2) a fundamentally different proof strategy for the SPA bound—geometric construction of simplicial neighborhoods rather than matrix norm inequalities—which yields a sharper dependence on s_{K-1}(V) instead of s_K(V). The denoising idea borrows the neighborhood-averaging concept from KNN classification but repurposes it for a geometric vertex-hunting objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Vertex hunting is a critical subroutine in hyperspectral unmixing, archetypal analysis, network community detection, and topic modeling, so improved guarantees here propagate to all downstream methods (Mixed-SCORE, Topic-SCORE, Markov state compression). The improved bound is of independent interest because it removes a dependence on s_K(V) that can vanish when the simplex is centered near the origin, making the old bound vacuous in a natural regime. Limitations include the Gaussian noise assumption (though extendable to sub-Gaussian), the requirement that at least one data point sits at each true vertex, and the fact that the denoise step's tuning parameters (N, Δ) rely on heuristics in practice. Open questions include whether the geometric proof technique extends to non-simplex convex bodies and whether the o(1) rate improvements in high dimensions translate to meaningful gains in real-world high-dimensional applications.
