## Problem

Existing CFR variants (CFR+, DCFR) achieve faster convergence than vanilla CFR by discounting earlier iterations, but they rely on fixed, manually-specified discounting schemes (e.g., DCFR's α=1.5, β=0, γ=2). These pre-determined schemes are suboptimal across diverse games and cannot adapt to changing conditions during the iteration process, significantly limiting their potential.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241524/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each iteration, the discounting policy receives a 2-dimensional game-agnostic state: the normalized iteration (t/T) and the log-normalized exploitability of the current average strategy. It outputs a 4-dimensional action (α_t, β_t, γ_t, τ_t) where the first three are continuous values in [0,5], [-5,0], [0,5] respectively, and τ_t is a discrete duration from {1,2,5,10,20} specifying how many iterations to reuse the current weights. The final output is an approximate Nash equilibrium strategy profile with guaranteed convergence under the bounded hyperparameter ranges.

## Method

DDCFR formulates CFR's iteration process as a Markov decision process where the discounting scheme is an agent (a 3-layer fully-connected neural network with 64 units and ELU activations) that dynamically selects discounting weights at runtime based on the current iteration progress and exploitability. The policy is trained via Evolution Strategies (ES) with three acceleration techniques: antithetic estimator for variance reduction, rank-based fitness shaping for robustness to absolute fitness magnitudes, and parallel evaluation across 200 CPU cores. A convergence theorem guarantees that DDCFR reaches a Nash equilibrium when α_t∈[0,5] (or [1,5] for t≥T/2), β_t∈[-5,0], and γ_t∈[0,5]. The framework is general and plug-and-play, demonstrated by applying it to PCFR+ and PDCFR to further improve their performance.

## Results

DDCFR achieves a 42% average reduction in exploitability compared to DCFR across eight unseen testing games, while also outperforming CFR+ on all games. On the four training games, DDCFR converges faster to lower exploitability than both baselines, and this advantage persists on testing games including large-scale HUNL subgames with hundreds of millions of histories. The additional computation overhead is negligible, with only a 0.22% average increase in wall-clock time compared to DCFR. The learned discounting policy generalizes across games of varying size and structure without any per-game tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDCFR outperforms DCFR (Brown & Sandholm, 2019) on all eight testing games, achieving a 42% average reduction in exploitability — remarkable given DCFR's already strong performance as one of the most efficient CFR variants. Against CFR+ (Tammelin, 2014), DDCFR also wins on every game, with the gap being most pronounced in later iterations (500–1000) where the dynamic discounting scheme adapts more effectively. Compared to Greedy Weights (Zhang et al., 2022), which was designed for normal-form games, DDCFR consistently converges faster to lower exploitability on four extensive-form games, likely because Greedy Weights' computed weights become inappropriate when facing the large number of information sets in extensive-form games. Against PCFR+ (Farina et al., 2021), the comparison is mixed: PCFR+ converges faster on non-poker games (Battleship, Goofspiel) while DDCFR outperforms PCFR+ on poker games (Leduc, HUNL subgames), reflecting that DDCFR is built upon DCFR which excels in poker. The paper further demonstrates that the dynamic discounting framework is plug-and-play, improving PCFR+ (as DPCFR+) and PDCFR (as PDDCFR) without sacrificing their respective strengths.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CFR+ (Tammelin, 2014) and DCFR (Brown & Sandholm, 2019) line of work on discounting schemes in regret minimization, which improved convergence by weighting iterations non-uniformly but relied on fixed, manually-specified hyperparameters. DDCFR departs from this by formulating the discounting choice as a policy optimization problem within an MDP, where a neural network agent dynamically outputs per-iteration discounting weights (α, β, γ, τ) based on runtime state (normalized iteration and exploitability). For optimization, the paper adopts Evolution Strategies (Salimans et al., 2017) rather than standard RL, motivated by the sparse reward, long horizon, and multi-task nature of the problem. The closest prior work is Greedy Weights (Zhang et al., 2022), which also learns dynamic weights but is restricted to normal-form games, whereas DDCFR is designed explicitly for extensive-form games with large information-set structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 42% average exploitability reduction over DCFR on unseen testing games—including large-scale HUNL subgames with hundreds of millions of histories—demonstrates that a single learned policy generalizes across diverse game structures, addressing a key limitation of hand-tuned discounting schemes. The plug-and-play applicability (demonstrated on PCFR+ and PDCFR) suggests the framework could serve as a general performance booster for any CFR variant with fixed discounting rules. Open questions include whether the approach scales to full HUNL (beyond subgames), whether richer state representations or larger policies yield further gains, and whether the ES-based training could be replaced by more sample-efficient methods as the game space grows.
