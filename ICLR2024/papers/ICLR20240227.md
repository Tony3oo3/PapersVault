## Problem

Most time series forecasting methods assume clean training data, but real-world sensor data is contaminated with anomalies that degrade model performance. Existing approaches like the detection-imputation-retraining pipeline are sensitive to thresholds and anomaly types, and learning-with-noisy-labels (LNL) methods do not directly transfer because TSFA involves anomalies in both covariates and targets simultaneously. This paper provides the first theoretical bridge between LNL and TSFA, analyzing how three formally defined anomaly types affect loss and sample robustness in regression settings.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a time series window of length K (e.g., 16 for single-step, 96 for multi-step) that may contain point-wise or subsequence anomalies, segmented into (x̃_n, s_n, ỹ_n) triplets where s_n is the L1-trend-filtered version of the input. It produces a trained DNN (LSTM, Transformer, or TCN) that maps input windows to one or more future time series values. Unusually, the training loop computes a position-weighted anomaly score per sample and applies a hard threshold τ to select informative samples before each gradient step, rather than performing a separate pre-training detection phase.

## Method

RobustTSF combines two theoretically grounded components: (1) a robust loss (MAE) proven to be invariant to Constant and Missing anomalies in the target (Theorem 1) and to Gaussian anomalies (Theorem 2), and (2) a sample selection mechanism that computes an anomaly score A(x̃_n) = Σ w(k)·|x̃_n^k − s_n^k| using a non-decreasing weight function (Dirac or exponential) that emphasizes deviations near the label position, then trains only on samples with A < τ. The trend s is obtained by solving a modified L1 trend filtering problem (replacing L2 data fidelity with L1 for robustness). The key insight is that by selecting inputs with minimal anomalies near the label, the covariate distribution becomes approximately clean, so the LNL-style robust loss condition extends to the joint X-and-Y anomaly setting, yielding argmin_f E_{X',Ỹ'}[ℓ(f(X'),Ỹ')] ≈ argmin_f E_{X,Y}[ℓ(f(X),Y)] without requiring imputation or a pre-trained detector.

## Results

On the Electricity dataset (single-step forecasting, input length 16), RobustTSF achieves best-epoch MAE of 0.177 on clean data and maintains 0.176–0.194 across all anomaly types (η=0.1–0.3), compared to 0.182–0.305 for vanilla MAE/MSE training. On Traffic, RobustTSF achieves 0.185 (clean) and 0.185–0.243 under anomalies, versus 0.194–0.446 for vanilla training. RobustTSF also has the smallest stability gap Δ (0.003–0.004), indicating the most consistent training behavior. For multi-step forecasting (prediction length 4 and 8), RobustTSF achieves MAE of 0.280–0.371, outperforming all baselines by 1–10%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RobustTSF consistently outperforms all six baselines across every anomaly type and dataset. Against the detection-imputation-retraining pipelines (Offline/Connor '94 and Online/Bohlke-Schneider '20), RobustTSF achieves 4–15% lower MAE (e.g., Electricity Missing η=0.3: 0.194 vs. 0.212/0.227; Traffic Missing η=0.3: 0.243 vs. 0.280/0.233). Against loss-based sample selection (Li '22), RobustTSF wins by a wider margin on Traffic (e.g., Missing η=0.3: 0.243 vs. 0.325, a 25% gap) because the small-loss criterion from LNL does not transfer well when anomalies affect both inputs and labels. Against DLinear (Zeng '23), RobustTSF is superior especially under Gaussian anomalies (Traffic η=0.3: 0.202 vs. 0.472, a 57% gap). Against ARIMA (Ho & Xie '98), RobustTSF achieves substantially lower MAE (Electricity Gaussian η=0.3: 0.190 vs. 0.363–0.419). Against NKF (de Bézenac '20), RobustTSF outperforms on missing-data settings (Electricity Missing η=0.05: 0.302 vs. 0.313 MAE). Notably, RobustTSF also improves over clean-data training (0.177 vs. 0.182 MAE on Electricity), suggesting real sensor data contains inherent anomalies that its sample selection mitigates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the classic detection-imputation-retraining pipeline (Connor et al., 1994) and the broader Learning with Noisy Labels (LNL) literature, but departs from both in key ways. Rather than relying on a pre-trained model for anomaly detection followed by imputation, RobustTSF uses a modified L1 trend filter (adapted from Kim et al., 2009) to compute anomaly scores and a position-weighted sample selection criterion, combined with MAE as a theoretically justified robust loss. The paper's central conceptual contribution is formally bridging LNL's noise-robust loss framework (which assumes clean inputs and noisy labels in classification) to the TSFA setting where anomalies contaminate both covariates and targets in a regression problem, a gap the authors identify as unaddressed by prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a unified theoretical treatment of how different anomaly types (Constant, Missing, Gaussian) affect loss robustness and sample robustness in time series forecasting, giving practitioners clear guidance on loss function choice (MAE over MSE for Constant/Missing anomalies) and on why anomaly position near the label matters. The practical algorithm is simple, model-agnostic, and avoids the sensitivity to detection thresholds that plagues the detection-imputation-retraining pipeline. Open questions include extending the theory to anomaly types where the conditional distribution P(Ỹ|Y) ≠ P(Ỹ|Y,X), handling more complex subsequence anomalies, and whether the trend-filtering-based selection generalizes to highly non-stationary or multivariate settings.
