## Problem

Multi-cellular robot design must search an enormous discrete space (~2.98×10^17 cell-type assignments) while co-optimizing morphology and control, making direct search (GA) sample-inefficient and predefined curricula (CuCo) unable to transfer useful guidance from smaller robots to the full-size task. The paper tackles the open question of how to perform coarse-to-fine robot design—first finding simple, coarse-grained robots and then refining them—without requiring a manually specified transition schedule between granularity levels.

## Input / Output

The method takes a task specification from EvoGym (terrain geometry, reward structure, and objective such as locomotion or manipulation) and a fixed 5×5 grid of 25 interconnected 2D voxel cells, each of which must be assigned one of five types (Empty, Rigid, Soft, Horizontal Actuator, Vertical Actuator). It outputs an optimal discrete cell-type assignment (robot morphology) together with a shared Transformer-based control policy that handles the incompatible state-action spaces arising from variable numbers of actuator cells across different morphologies. The control policy is trained with PPO and must generalize across all robot designs encountered during optimization.

## Method

HERD organizes the robot design space into a tree hierarchy where parent nodes are coarse-grained robots (cells aggregated via iterative K-Means clustering with k ∈ {16, 8, …, 1}) and child nodes are their fine-grained refinements. This tree is embedded into a 2D Poincaré ball using Sarkar's Construction—a training-free, combinatorial algorithm that places the root at the origin and recursively positions children evenly around their parent via hyperbolic reflections, yielding arbitrarily low distortion. A modified Cross-Entropy Method then optimizes in this space: it samples Gaussian points in Euclidean space, maps them to the Poincaré ball via the exponential map, snaps each to the nearest valid embedding (and thus to a concrete robot design), evaluates the robots with the shared Transformer policy, and updates the CEM mean toward elite performers while linearly decaying the variance. The key insight is that the norm of a point in the Poincaré ball encodes granularity—points near the center correspond to coarse robots and points near the boundary to fine robots—so initializing the CEM mean at the origin automatically drives the search from coarse to fine without any predefined curriculum or manual transition schedule.

## Results

On the EvoGym benchmark (15 tasks, 5×5 voxel soft robots), HERD achieves the highest episode reward on 10 out of 15 tasks, outperforming all baselines including CuCo, GA, BO, CPPN-NEAT, and Vanilla RL. Notable wins include ObstacleTraverser-v0 (6.02 vs. 0.89 for CuCo, 1.87 for GA), PlatformJumper-v0 (3.34 vs. 1.51 for CuCo), and Flipper-v0 (35.55 vs. 15.01 for CuCo). HERD also surpasses the handcrafted robot on 11 of 15 tasks, demonstrating that automatic coarse-to-fine design can exceed human-designed morphologies. The method requires ~28 hours of training (25M timesteps) on a single GTX 2080Ti with ~4 GB RAM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HERD outperforms CuCo (the closest prior method it builds upon) on 13 of 15 tasks, with the largest margins on ObstacleTraverser-v0 (6.02 vs. 0.89), PlatformJumper-v0 (3.34 vs. 1.51), and Flipper-v0 (35.55 vs. 15.01). Against GA, HERD wins on 13 of 15 tasks but is slightly outperformed on Walker-v0 (9.74 vs. 10.57) and Carrier-v1 (1.93 vs. 2.50). BO and CPPN-NEAT are consistently the weakest baselines, with HERD exceeding them by 2–30+ reward points across nearly all tasks. Vanilla RL is competitive on a few tasks (e.g., Pusher-v0: 11.16 vs. 7.40) but generally trails HERD, particularly on hard tasks like ObstacleTraverser-v0 (0.62 vs. 6.02) and PlatformJumper-v0 (0.06 vs. 3.34). Compared to the handcrafted robot, HERD wins on 11 of 15 tasks, confirming that the coarse-to-fine hyperbolic search can discover morphologies superior to expert design, though the handcrafted robot retains an edge on simpler tasks like Walker-v0 and UpStepper-v0 where a fixed morphology is already near-optimal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CuCo (Wang et al., 2022), a curriculum-based co-design method for voxel-based soft robots, by replacing its predefined curriculum with an automatic coarse-to-fine strategy realized through hyperbolic geometry. The embedding mechanism relies on Sarkar's Construction (2011), a training-free combinatorial method for low-distortion tree embeddings in the Poincaré ball, and the optimization loop adapts the Cross-Entropy Method (Rubinstein & Kroese, 2004) to operate in this curved space. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior evolutionary and curriculum-based approaches by unifying robots of all granularities in a single shared geometric space, where the radial distance from the origin encodes granularity, thereby eliminating the need for a hand-specified refinement schedule.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work demonstrates that the hierarchical structure inherent in coarse-to-fine robot design can be exploited geometrically, offering a principled alternative to ad-hoc curricula and evolutionary search in the vast multi-cellular design space. Two limitations remain open: (1) tasks solvable only by the finest-grained robots receive no useful guidance from coarser stages, and (2) embedding precision degrades for deep hierarchies (Ω(H·log(deg)) bits required), which could constrain scalability to 3D or larger cell counts. The broader implication is that hyperbolic geometry may serve as a general inductive bias for any design problem with a natural parent-child refinement structure, beyond robotics.
