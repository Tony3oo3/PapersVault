## Problem

Modern LLMs exhibit a pervasive 'selection bias' in multiple-choice questions, preferentially selecting specific option IDs (e.g., 'A') regardless of content, which causes dramatic accuracy swings when golden answers are moved to different positions. Prior work attributed this sensitivity primarily to position bias, but this paper shows the dominant intrinsic cause is token bias—the model a priori assigns more probability mass to certain ID tokens—and that simple prompting strategies (Chain-of-Thought, explicit debiasing instructions) fail to mitigate it.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard MCQ prompts (question + n options labeled A/B/C/D or A/B/C/D/E) and, for prior estimation, a small unlabeled subset of test samples (e.g., 5%) on which option contents are cyclically permuted. For open-source models, it reads the next-token probabilities of option ID tokens directly; for GPT-3.5-turbo (no probability access), it approximates the distribution by sampling 100 generated answers. The output is a debiased probability distribution over option contents, from which the final answer is selected by argmax, requiring no ground-truth labels at any stage.

## Method

PriDe (Debiasing with Prior Estimation) rests on a probability decomposition: the observed prediction P_observed(d_i|q,x^I) factors into a prior bias P_prior(d_i|q) over option IDs and a debiased belief P_debiased(o_j|q,x) over option contents, under a conditional-independence assumption. By taking logarithms and summing over all cyclic permutations, the debiased term cancels out, yielding a closed-form estimator for the sample-specific prior (Eq. 7) that requires no labels. PriDe then averages these per-sample priors over a small estimation set D_e (e.g., 5% of test data) to obtain a 'global prior,' and for all remaining samples simply divides the single observed prediction by this global prior (Eq. 8), reducing the per-sample cost from ×n (Cyclic Permutation) to ×1. The result is a label-free, inference-time debiasing procedure with only ~15% extra computational overhead at α=5%, which the paper shows outperforms full and cyclic permutation baselines in both debiasing effectiveness (RStd reduction) and accuracy, while also providing an interpretable prior that transfers across domains.

## Results

Across 20 LLMs on MMLU, ARC-Challenge, and CommonsenseQA (0-shot), PriDe with only 5% of test samples for prior estimation (×1.15 inference cost) reduces the recall standard deviation (RStd) by 7.6 points on average, compared to 8.7 for Cyclic Permutation at ×4 cost. On MMLU, gpt-3.5-turbo's RStd drops from 5.5 (default) to 2.8 (PriDe 5%) while accuracy improves from 67.2% to 68.0%; at 80% estimation budget, RStd reaches 1.2 with 69.3% accuracy, matching Cyclic Perm's 1.1/69.8% at comparable cost. The average accuracy gain across all models is +1.2% (PriDe 5%) to +4.1% (PriDe 80%) on MMLU, +1.3% to +4.9% on ARC, and +1.7% to +6.6% on CSQA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Cyclic Permutation (Wang et al., 2023a; Zheng et al., 2023b), PriDe at 5% estimation budget achieves a comparable RStd reduction (−7.6 vs. −8.7 on MMLU) at only ×1.15 cost versus ×4, making it roughly 3.5× cheaper. At 80% budget, PriDe slightly outperforms Cyclic Perm in RStd (−9.1 vs. −8.7) while costing less (×3.4 vs. ×4), and matches its accuracy gains. Against Chain-of-Thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022), which reduces gpt-3.5-turbo's MMLU RStd only from 5.5 to 4.5, PriDe(5%) achieves a far larger reduction to 2.8, demonstrating that simple prompting cannot adequately address selection bias. The 'Removing IDs' ablation achieves the lowest RStd (1.0) but degrades accuracy by ~0.5% on MMLU and up to 7% on CSQA, confirming it is impractical. Overall, PriDe dominates the cost-effectiveness frontier: it matches or exceeds Cyclic Perm's debiasing quality at a fraction of the inference cost, while simple prompting strategies (CoT, explicit debiasing instructions) fail to meaningfully reduce selection bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the permutation-based debiasing framework introduced by Wang et al. (2023a) and Zheng et al. (2023b), which average prediction distributions over option permutations to cancel positional effects in LLM evaluation. It departs from the concurrent work of Pezeshkpour & Hruschka (2023), who attributed LLMs' sensitivity to option reordering primarily to position bias, by demonstrating through ablation that token bias—the model's a priori preference for specific ID tokens (A/B/C/D)—is the more salient and consistent intrinsic cause. The paper also positions itself against the common assumption that simple prompting strategies (Chain-of-Thought, explicit debiasing instructions) can address such biases, showing empirically that they cannot.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since MCQs are the dominant format for LLM benchmarking (MMLU, ARC, CSQA, etc.), the prevalence of selection bias across 20 models undermines the reliability and comparability of reported scores, making this a practically urgent finding for the evaluation community. PriDe offers a label-free, inference-time fix with negligible overhead (×1.15 cost at 5% estimation budget), making it immediately deployable in existing evaluation pipelines. Open questions include whether the prior estimation aligns with true model calibration (the paper notes discrepancies), whether training-time interventions could eliminate token bias at its source, and whether the approach generalizes to non-MCQ evaluation formats.
