## Problem

Existing reasoning benchmarks for LLMs are either too artificial (solvable by rule-based systems), lack natural language, or involve only 1-2 steps of reasoning without commonsense. No prior dataset simultaneously combines sophisticated free-text narratives, multistep reasoning, commonsense knowledge, ground-truth intermediate structure, and resistance to rule-based solutions. MuSR fills this gap by introducing a benchmark where a system must extract distributed facts from long narratives, apply commonsense to interpret them, and perform multi-step deduction to arrive at an answer.

## Input / Output

Each instance consists of a free-text narrative of roughly 500–1000 words (e.g., a murder mystery, an object-placement scenario, or a team-assignment story) followed by a multiple-choice question. The model must produce a step-by-step reasoning trace and then a final answer selecting one of the provided choices. The gold fact set F and reasoning tree T that underpin the narrative are hidden at test time; the model receives only the narrative and question, making the task one of recovering implicit reasoning from natural language rather than applying rules to structured premises.

## Method

MuSR is generated via a three-stage neurosymbolic pipeline. First, a domain-specific template defines a gold fact set F and question-answer pairs, plus diversity facts G. Second, reasoning trees are built by recursively prompting GPT-4 to decompose each root fact into child deductions, producing leaf-level scenario facts S(T) (which must appear in the narrative) and commonsense facts C(T) (which are implied but never stated); domain-specific validators prune invalid or shortcut branches. Third, the narrative is generated via 'chaptering'—S(T) is partitioned by answer choice, each chunk is used to prompt GPT-4 for a story segment, and the segments are concatenated into a single narrative. This disentangles logical structure from text generation, enabling the dataset to be scaled in complexity (deeper trees, longer stories) as stronger LLMs become available, while the hidden reasoning structure ensures the generating model itself cannot trivially solve the resulting instances.

## Results

On MuSR, GPT-4 with CoT+ prompting achieves 80.4% on Murder Mystery, 60.9% on Object Placements, and 68.4% on Team Allocation, substantially outperforming GPT-3.5 (61.6/46.9/40.4) and all open-source models (Llama-2 and Vicuna variants score near or below random chance on most domains). However, GPT-4 still falls well short of human majority-vote performance (94.1/95.0/100.0), revealing a persistent gap in multistep soft reasoning. Among neurosymbolic baselines, PAL 1-Shot (87.2%) and Decomposed Prompting 1-Shot (86.0%) surpass GPT-4 CoT+ on Team Allocation and Murder Mystery respectively, while SymbolicTOM collapses to 23.8% on Object Placements.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 dominates all other LLMs across all three domains, with a 18.8-point margin over GPT-3.5 on Murder Mystery and a 28-point margin on Team Allocation; open-source models (Llama-2, Vicuna) perform at or near random chance, confirming that current open models lack the multistep reasoning capacity needed. Against neurosymbolic baselines, the picture is mixed: PAL 1-Shot (87.2%) and Decomposed Prompting 1-Shot (86.0%) both outperform GPT-4 CoT+ (68.4% and 80.4%) on Team Allocation and Murder Mystery respectively, suggesting that structured program-aided or decomposed approaches can exploit the domain's logical structure more effectively than end-to-end prompting. In stark contrast, SymbolicTOM (23.8%) is far below GPT-4 CoT+ (60.9%) on Object Placements, because its entity-extraction pipeline was designed for templated ToMI-style inputs and fails on the natural, multi-paragraph narratives in MuSR. Across all comparisons, no system matches human majority-vote accuracy (94.1–100.0%), underscoring that multistep soft reasoning in natural narratives remains an open challenge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of synthetic benchmark construction and natural language reasoning. Prior reasoning benchmarks (bAbI, RuleTakers, CLUTRR) are solvable with rule-based systems and lack commonsense, while commonsense datasets (SocialIQA, CommonsenseQA) are structurally simple with only 1-2 reasoning steps. The True Detective dataset has natural text and commonsense but lacks ground-truth intermediate structure. MuSR's key departure is a neurosymbolic generation procedure—reasoning trees expanded via LLM sampling, validated by domain-specific constraints, and rendered into natural narratives through a 'chaptering' process—that simultaneously satisfies all five properties (natural text, commonsense, multistep, intermediate structure, not rule-solvable) that no prior dataset achieves together.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides a scalable, refreshable testbed for reasoning that can be regenerated with greater complexity as models improve, addressing the stagnation problem of static benchmarks. The finding that GPT-4—which was instrumental in generating the narratives—achieves only 80.4% on murder mysteries versus 94.1% for humans validates the difficulty, while the poor performance of purpose-built neurosymbolic systems (SymbolicTOM at 23.8% on Object Placements) suggests that existing structured-reasoning tools do not generalize to natural, distributed narratives. Open questions include whether deeper reasoning trees (beyond depth 2-3, where GPT-4 struggles to generate valid deductions) can further increase difficulty, and whether the generation procedure can be adapted to domains beyond the three studied.
