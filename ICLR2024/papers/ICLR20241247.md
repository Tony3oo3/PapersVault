## Problem

End-to-end navigation agents learn latent representations tied to a specific downstream task, while classical SLAM-based approaches invest capacity in explicit scene reconstruction that may be unnecessary or even harmful for sim2real transfer. The paper asks whether a representation optimized purely for local navigability—without reconstruction or task-specific supervision—can be learned as a task-agnostic pre-text and transfer more robustly to out-of-distribution environments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241247/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The main agent consumes sequential RGB-D frames (encoded by a half-width 4-channel ResNet18 into a 512D vector), a 64D Euclidean goal vector (GPS+Compass), and a 32D previous-action embedding, integrating them through a 512D GRU to produce the latent navigability representation. The representation is passed to a blind auxiliary agent as the initialization of its own GRU hidden state (the best-performing communication variant), which then outputs discrete actions from {FORWARD 25 cm, TURN_LEFT 10°, TURN_RIGHT 10°, STOP} toward sampled subgoals at 3–5 m Euclidean distance. The final downstream policy π, reinitialized after pre-training, maps the same representation to navigation actions for the PointGoal task.

## Method

The core mechanism is a blind auxiliary agent (the 'mole') that navigates short branching episodes from waypoints using only the main agent's latent representation—no visual observations—trained by behavior cloning against ground-truth shortest-path actions. Because the auxiliary agent is blind, the BC loss cannot be satisfied by reactive, memory-less obstacle avoidance; it must be satisfied by information stored in the representation, forcing the encoder to integrate scene geometry over the full observation history through a longer backpropagation chain. Training is two-phase: 50 M environment steps of joint Navigability + BC pre-training (with subgoals mined at 3–5 m and filtered by a geodesic/Euclidean ratio threshold of 1.5), followed by 50 M steps of PPO fine-tuning on PointGoal with the main policy reinitialized. The hybrid Navigability+BC variant outperforms pure PPO, pure BC, data-augmentation-only BC, and an explicit reconstruction loss in both clean and noisy simulation, and achieves the best sim2real performance on a physical Locobot in an unseen office (81.8% Success vs. 72.7% for BC and 45.5% for PPO).

## Results

On the Gibson PointGoal benchmark, the proposed Navigability+BC variant (e) achieves 95.5% Success and 80.3 SPL in simulation, and 90.9% Success and 73.3 SPL under noisy simulation (NoisySim), outperforming pure PPO (89.6/71.7 Sim; 74.6/55.8 NoisySim) and pure BC (92.0/79.6 Sim; 76.0/61.7 NoisySim). On a real Locobot in an unseen office building (11 episodes), Navigability+BC reaches 81.8% Success and 45.6 SPL, substantially exceeding PPO (45.5%/14.7), BC (72.7%/36.3), and a classical Map+Plan baseline (36.4%/29.6). The advantage is most pronounced under domain shift: in NoisySim, the gap between Navigability+BC and PPO widens from ~6% Success in clean Sim to ~16% Success, and the classical Map+Plan baseline collapses from 95.7% to 27.9% Success under noise while the proposed method retains 90.9%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against pure PPO (Wijmans et al., 2019 / DDPO), the proposed Navigability+BC method wins on every metric in both Sim and NoisySim, with the largest gap in NoisySim Success (90.9% vs. 74.6%) and on the real robot (81.8% vs. 45.5% Success). Against classical BC, the advantage is modest in clean Sim (95.5% vs. 92.0%) but becomes decisive under domain shift: NoisySim Success jumps from 76.0% to 90.9%, and real-robot Success from 72.7% to 81.8%. Against the classical Map+Plan baseline (Gupta et al., 2017), the proposed method dominates on the real robot (81.8% vs. 36.4% Success) and in NoisySim (90.9% vs. 27.9% Success), while being competitive in clean Sim (95.5% vs. 95.7%). In the broader SOTA comparison (Table 6), DDPO reports 98% Sim Success but provides no noisy or real-robot numbers; EmbVizNav (Rosano et al., 2021) reports 97% real Success but uses 2.5G+2.4M training steps versus 100M here; Sadek et al. (2022) achieves 75% real Success with 100M steps, below the proposed 81.8%; and Kadian et al. (2020) reports 61% real SPL with 500M steps, also below the proposed 45.6% SPL at 100M steps. The reconstruction-supervised variant (h) matches Navigability+BC in clean Sim (94.9% vs. 95.5%) but degrades sharply in NoisySim (76.7% vs. 90.9%), supporting the paper's claim that explicit reconstruction is suboptimal for transfer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves a middle path between classical SLAM-based navigation (explicit geometric reconstruction followed by planning) and end-to-end learned navigation (latent representations tied to a specific downstream task). It extends the blind-agent probing framework of Wijmans et al. (2023) in a critical way: rather than merely inspecting what a trained agent's memory contains, the blind auxiliary agent here is a *training signal* whose gradients shape the upstream representation, making it a pre-text loss rather than an evaluation tool. The 'navigability' loss is conceptually related to backpropagating-through-planning methods (Cognitive Mapping and Planning, Neural-A*) but differs in that it optimizes a task-agnostic local navigation skill via a blind agent rather than a differentiable planner, and it explicitly avoids reconstruction as an intermediate objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a concrete, well-motivated argument that over-precise reconstruction can *hurt* sim2real transfer by encoding simulation-specific details, and that a navigability-optimized representation is more robust to domain shift. However, the real-robot evidence is thin (11 episodes in a single office), the evaluation is limited to PointGoal on Gibson, and the probing experiments are explicitly framed as speculative. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to semantic or language-conditioned navigation tasks, whether it scales to larger/more diverse scene collections, and whether the blindness inductive bias remains beneficial when the downstream task requires fine-grained spatial reasoning beyond local navigability.
