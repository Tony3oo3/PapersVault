## Problem

Federated learning suffers from client drift under Non-IID data, and existing remedies (gradient correction, model regularization) still leave a performance gap versus centralized training. This paper identifies that the generalization contribution of local training is fundamentally bounded by the conditional Wasserstein distance between clients' data distributions, meaning that even matching marginal distributions is insufficient. The key unexplored question is how to make clients train on similar conditional distributions without violating privacy.

## Input / Output

Each client receives the global model parameters θ and a shared feature distribution H (parameterized as a per-class Gaussian with mean μ and variance σ) from the server. During local training, the client consumes both its own raw data (x, y) and samples ĥ drawn from H|y, feeding them jointly into the high-level classifier. The client returns updated model parameters and its locally estimated feature distribution (μ_m, σ_m), which the server aggregates with added Gaussian noise to form the next global H.

## Method

FedImpro decouples a CNN (e.g., ResNet-18) into a low-level feature extractor φ_θ_low and a high-level classifier θ_high, then trains the classifier on both local features h_m = φ_θ_low(x) and features ĥ sampled from a shared global distribution H. The server maintains H as a per-class Gaussian, updated via a moving average of noised client estimates (μ_i + ε_i, σ_i + ε_i), so only low-dimensional distribution parameters—not raw features—are communicated, preserving privacy. Theoretically, Theorem 4.2 shows this reduces high-level gradient dissimilarity (CGV) by a factor of N²/(N+N̂)², while Theorem 4.1 links the generalization contribution to the conditional Wasserstein distance, motivating the shared-distribution design. The full procedure adds only two steps to vanilla FedAvg: broadcasting H and augmenting each local training batch with sampled ĥ, making it plug-compatible with other FL aggregation schemes.

## Results

FedImpro achieves the best average test accuracy across all four datasets: 83.97% on CIFAR-10, 89.53% on FMNIST, 91.91% on SVHN, and 65.44% on CIFAR-100, consistently outperforming all baselines. The gains are most pronounced under high Non-IID degree (α=0.05), where FedImpro reaches 90.25% on SVHN (vs. 84.01% for FedNova, a 6.24% gap) and 81.75% on CIFAR-10 (vs. 77.49% for FedProx). On the standard setting (α=0.1, E=1, M=10), FedImpro achieves 88.45% on CIFAR-10, 90.83% on FMNIST, and 92.37% on SVHN, closing the gap to centralized training (92.53%, 93.7%, 95.27%) more than any baseline. FedImpro also converges faster, requiring fewer communication rounds to reach target accuracy (e.g., 128 rounds vs. 142 for FedAvg on CIFAR-10 at α=0.1, E=1, M=10).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedImpro outperforms all five baselines (FedAvg, FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedNova, FedDyn) on every dataset and FL setting in Table 1. The largest margins appear under high Non-IID (α=0.05): on SVHN, FedImpro gains 6.24% over FedNova (90.25% vs. 84.01%) and 7.86% over FedDyn (90.25% vs. 84.12%); on CIFAR-10, it gains 4.26% over FedProx (81.75% vs. 77.49%). Under the standard setting (α=0.1, E=1, M=10), FedImpro leads FedNova by 3.48% on CIFAR-10 (88.45% vs. 84.97%) and by 1.55% on SVHN (92.37% vs. 90.82% for FedDyn, the strongest baseline there). On CIFAR-100, the advantage is smaller (0.50–1.70% over the best baseline), which the authors attribute to the higher number of classes making Gaussian feature estimation less effective. SCAFFOLD degrades severely under high heterogeneity (33.6% on CIFAR-10 at α=0.05) and with many clients (23.77% on CIFAR-100 at M=100), while FedImpro remains robust across all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant gradient-correction paradigm in Non-IID federated learning (FedProx, SCAFFOLD, FedNova, FedDyn) by reframing client drift as a generalization contribution problem bounded by the conditional Wasserstein distance between client distributions. Rather than manipulating local updates to align gradients, FedImpro decouples the network into a feature extractor and classifier, then trains the classifier on a shared estimated feature distribution (Gaussian parameters) aggregated across clients. This is conceptually distinct from split FL (which splits for communication efficiency and cannot support local updates), post-hoc calibration methods like CCVR, and knowledge distillation approaches like FedDF, as it operates during the training loop and targets the high-level gradient dissimilarity directly.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The conditional Wasserstein distance bound provides a principled theoretical explanation for why heterogeneous data hurts FL generalization, and the decoupling strategy is remarkably simple to implement (two extra steps over FedAvg) while being privacy-preserving (sharing distribution parameters rather than raw features). However, the Gaussian approximation of feature distributions limits performance on complex datasets (CIFAR-100 shows only ~1% gain), and the method only addresses high-level gradient dissimilarity. Open questions include whether generative models can produce higher-quality shared features, the trade-off between privacy (noise level) and performance, and whether the approach extends to personalized or split-FL settings where the low-level model is kept client-specific.
