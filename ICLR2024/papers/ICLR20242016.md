## Problem

Existing methods for 3D reconstruction of articulated objects from video rely on category-specific skeletal templates or 'virtual bones' (e.g., K-means clusters) that lack physical meaning, leading to non-intuitive bone placements, poor limb reconstruction, and limited generalization to unseen object categories. No prior method learns a physically plausible skeleton—complete with bones, joints, skinning weights, and rigidity—directly from motion cues in RGB video without any 3D supervision.

## Input / Output

The method takes one or more monocular RGB videos as input, along with pre-computed optical flow (RAFT) and object-level segmentation masks (via Grounding DINO + SAM/CSL); no camera poses, 3D templates, or skeleton priors are provided. It produces both an explicit representation (canonical 3D mesh with per-vertex color, per-frame camera parameters) and an implicit representation (a skeleton with adaptively determined number of bones and joints, N×B skinning weights, per-edge rigidity coefficients, and per-frame SE(3) transformations for the root body and each bone). The number of bones is not fixed a priori but learned through iterative merging and splitting.

## Method

LIMR introduces the SIOS² algorithm, an EM-style iterative procedure that alternates between an E-step (optimizing the explicit mesh, color, and camera via differentiable rendering losses) and an M-step (refining the implicit skeleton using two physical constraints: bone-length constancy across frames and optical-flow direction consistency within each semi-rigid part). The skeleton is initialized by Laplacian mesh contraction followed by connectivity surgery (rather than K-means), with each bone modeled as a Gaussian ellipsoid and skinning weights computed via Mahalanobis distance plus a small MLP. A key new loss, Dynamic Rigidity (DR), replaces the uniform ARAP constraint by weighting each edge's rigidity with the inverse entropy of its endpoint skinning-weight distributions, so vertices near joints deform freely while mid-bone vertices stay rigid. Bone motion directions are estimated by backward-projecting 2D optical flow to the surface, masking invisible vertices via ray-casting, and applying inverse blend skinning to aggregate per-vertex flow into per-bone 2D directions, which drive the merge/split decisions in the M-step.

## Results

On the DAVIS dataset, LIMR achieves 80.2% average 2D keypoint transfer accuracy, surpassing LASR (71.9%) by 8.3% and ViSER (74.1%) by 6.1%. On the PlanetZoo dataset, LIMR reaches 69.9% average accuracy, outperforming LASR (66.9%) by 3%. On the multi-video AMA benchmark (swing and samba), LIMR reduces 3D Chamfer Distance from 14.6 cm (BANMo) to 12.9 cm (~12% improvement) while using fewer bones (19 vs. 25).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LASR (NMR-based, single video), LIMR consistently wins on all 10 tested videos across DAVIS and PlanetZoo, with the largest gap on DAVIS dance-twirl (83.1% vs. 55.3%, a 27.8-point margin) where LASR falls into an incorrect local optimum due to lack of skeletal understanding. Against ViSER (NMR-based, single video), LIMR outperforms by 6.1% on DAVIS average; ViSER is not evaluated on PlanetZoo because it is sensitive to large camera motion and requires many input frames. Against BANMo (NeRF-based, multi-video), LIMR achieves lower Chamfer Distance (12.9 vs. 14.6 cm average) and higher F-score (55.8% vs. 53.9%) on AMA swing and samba, while adaptively learning 19 bones versus BANMo's fixed 25. The paper notes that BANMo's NeRF-based representation degrades significantly on single short monocular videos, so no direct single-video comparison is made.

## Relation to Prior Work

LIMR extends the articulated 3D reconstruction line initiated by LASR and ViSER (which use virtual bones via K-means clustering) and BANMo (which uses NeRF-based rendering with Mahalanobis-distance skinning weights from multiple videos). The key departure is replacing virtual bones with a learned physical-like skeleton—comprising Gaussian-ellipsoid bones, joints, skinning weights, and rigidity coefficients—initialized via Laplacian mesh contraction (Cao et al. 2010; Au et al. 2008) and refined through an EM-style iterative algorithm (SIOS²) that alternates between explicit rendering optimization and implicit skeleton refinement using optical-flow and bone-length constraints. The proposed Dynamic Rigidity loss generalizes the ARAP loss (Sumner et al. 2007) by weighting edge rigidity according to vertex proximity to joints, derived from the entropy of skinning-weight distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By learning a category-agnostic skeleton purely from motion cues in RGB video without 3D supervision, LIMR removes the dependency on predefined skeletal templates that limits methods like CASA, RAC, and MagicPony to known object categories. The practical impact is enabling articulated reconstruction from a single monocular video with fewer bones than competitors (e.g., 19 vs. 25 for swing), though the method still requires 10–20 hours of training on an A100 GPU and remains sensitive to camera-motion errors that propagate into shape estimates. Open questions include improving robustness to continuously moving cameras, reducing training cost, and whether the learned skeleton generalizes to objects with topological changes or extreme deformations not captured in the training video.
