## Problem

Existing LLM safety training (RLHF, red-teaming, content filtering) has been conducted almost exclusively in English, yet these models are deployed globally and serve users in dozens of languages. This paper identifies that multilingualism itself constitutes a jailbreak vector: low-resource languages exhibit roughly three times the unsafe rate of high-resource languages in unintentional scenarios, and combining non-English prompts with a single English malicious instruction (AIM) pushes ChatGPT's unsafe rate to 80.92% and GPT-4's to 40.71% in intentional scenarios.

## Input / Output

For evaluation, the method consumes the MultiJail dataset (3,150 samples: 315 English harmful prompts human-translated by native speakers into 9 languages spanning high-, medium-, and low-resource categories), optionally prefixed with the AIM malicious instruction to simulate intentional attacks. For SELF-DEFENSE training, the input is a small set of 50 English seed input-output pairs (3:7 unsafe-to-general ratio) plus a target-language specification; the LLM itself generates and translates the remaining 450 pairs, yielding a 500-pair multilingual training corpus. Outputs are three-way safety classifications (safe/unsafe/invalid) for evaluation, and a fine-tuned ChatGPT model for the defense.

## Method

SELF-DEFENSE adapts the SELF-INSTRUCT paradigm to multilingual safety alignment: starting from a handful of English seed examples (mixing unsafe refusals and general helpful responses to avoid overfitting to safety patterns), the LLM augments the dataset by generating diverse additional examples, then translates all instruction pairs into each target language using its own multilingual capability. The merged multilingual corpus is used to fine-tune the LLM for 3 epochs, requiring no human annotation beyond the initial seeds. The key design choice is the 3:7 unsafe-to-general ratio, which the ablation (Figure 6) shows trades off safety gains against general usefulness—100% safety data maximizes safety rate (90.67%) but drops XNLI/X-CSQA accuracy to 35.28%, while 30% safety data preserves more utility at the cost of lower safety. This self-generated, language-agnostic pipeline makes multilingual safety fine-tuning feasible without the prohibitive cost of native-speaker annotation across many languages.

## Results

In the unintentional scenario, ChatGPT's average unsafe rate across 9 non-English languages is 10.19% (vs. 0.63% in English), while GPT-4 achieves 5.96% (vs. 0.95% in English). In the intentional scenario (harmful prompt + AIM malicious instruction), unsafe rates surge to 80.92% for ChatGPT and 40.71% for GPT-4. Low-resource languages (Bengali, Swahili, Javanese) show roughly 3× higher unsafe rates than high-resource languages in the unintentional setting (14.92% vs. 4.34% for ChatGPT). After SELF-DEFENSE fine-tuning, ChatGPT's unsafe rate drops from 10.19% to 3.95% (unintentional) and from 80.92% to 60.00% (intentional).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 consistently outperforms ChatGPT on safety: in the unintentional scenario its average unsafe rate is 5.96% vs. 10.19%, and in the intentional scenario 40.71% vs. 80.92%. Among open-source models, Llama2-chat reports the lowest average unsafe rate (2.82%) but at the cost of a 22.47% invalid rate and a strong bias toward English responses, limiting practical utility for non-English speakers. Vicuna, lacking dedicated safety tuning, exhibits the worst performance with a 37.28% average unsafe rate (57.14% even in English). SeaLLM-v2 (12.56% average) surpasses both ChatGPT and GPT-4 in the three overlapping Southeast Asian languages (Thai, Vietnamese, Javanese), demonstrating the value of language-specific safety tuning, yet still struggles in other languages. The proposed SELF-DEFENSE framework, applied to ChatGPT, reduces the unintentional unsafe rate by 6.24 percentage points (10.19%→3.95%) and the intentional unsafe rate by 20.92 percentage points (80.92%→60.00%), though a notable safety–usefulness trade-off emerges as the proportion of safety training data increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM jailbreak literature (Wei et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023; Shen et al. 2023), which has focused almost exclusively on English, by systematically studying how multilingual prompts interact with safety mechanisms. The SELF-DEFENSE framework directly adapts the SELF-INSTRUCT paradigm (Wang et al., 2023) from general instruction generation to multilingual safety data creation, repurposing the seed-example → LLM-augmentation → fine-tuning pipeline for a safety-specific objective. The paper also operationalizes Wei et al.'s (2023) 'mismatched generalization' hypothesis by showing that safety training generalizes poorly to low-resource languages where pre-training data is scarce, providing empirical grounding for that theoretical claim.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study and dataset (MultiJail) addressing multilingual jailbreak, revealing that low-resource language speakers face roughly 3× higher exposure to unsafe content and that malicious actors can achieve near-100% attack success on ChatGPT via multilingual adaptive attacks. The SELF-DEFENSE framework offers a practical, annotation-free mitigation, but the residual unsafe rates (60% in the intentional scenario) and the observed safety-usefulness trade-off indicate that multilingual safety alignment remains far from solved. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the 9 languages tested, how it interacts with RLHF-based safety training, and whether the trade-off can be mitigated through richer refusal responses.
