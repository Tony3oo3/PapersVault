## Problem

Robust legged locomotion requires accurate estimation of external states (terrain friction, elevation, restitution), but onboard sensors provide only partial, noisy proprioceptive observations. Existing two-stage teacher-student paradigms suffer from inevitable information loss during the mimicking phase, while regression-based approaches to infer environmental parameters collapse under domain randomization noise. The paper asks whether the robot's own dynamic response can serve as a sufficient proxy for these unobservable external states, eliminating the need for any external sensors or multi-stage training.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a 5-step history of proprioceptive observations (joint positions, joint velocities, IMU angular velocity, gravity vector, desired velocity command, and last action) to produce a hybrid internal embedding (a 16-dim latent vector plus an explicit velocity estimate) that is concatenated with the current observation before feeding the actor-critic network. The output is a 12-dim vector of target joint position biases (scaled by k≤1) for a quadruped's 12 actuators, executed at 50 Hz with a 500 Hz PD tracker. Notably, the value network receives privileged information (external forces, ground height) during training but the actor operates solely on proprioception, ensuring sim-to-real consistency.

## Method

HIM is inspired by classical Internal Model Control: rather than directly regressing unobservable environmental parameters, a 3-layer MLP extractor maps 5-step historical proprioceptive observations to a hybrid internal embedding comprising an explicit velocity estimate (trained with MSE against ground truth) and an implicit 16-dim stability latent (trained via SwAV-style prototypical contrastive learning with Sinkhorn-Knopp balancing). The contrastive objective pulls the embedding close to the robot's successor state in latent space, where the response to disturbances is naturally encoded, while pushing it away from other trajectories in the batch—exploiting batch-level terrain diversity without requiring explicit environmental labels. Training alternates between Hybrid Internal Optimization (updating the embedding extractor) and PPO (updating the frozen-embedding policy) each iteration, with 4096 parallel Isaac Gym environments and a terrain curriculum. This single-stage design avoids the information loss of teacher-student transfer and the representation collapse of regression, converging in only 200M samples (~1 hour on an RTX 4090) while achieving superior sim-to-real generalization on stairs, unseen compositional terrains, and deformable slopes.

## Results

In real-world benchmarks, HIM achieves 100% success rate on short-range stairs (A1) and ascends 176.5±7.81 stairs on long-range stairs (Aliengo), compared to 60% and 75.35±19.98 for RMA respectively. On unseen terrains never encountered during training, HIM reaches 85% success on compositional terrain and 55% on deformable slopes, while RMA achieves only 45% and 10%. In simulation, HIM attains the lowest average tracking error on stairs (0.147 vs. 0.194 for Regression, 0.306 for RMA, 1.108 for MoB) and discrete obstacles (0.125 vs. 0.182, 0.311, 0.973), with only 200M training samples (1 hour on RTX 4090).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HIM decisively outperforms RMA (Kumar et al., 2021) across nearly all real-world tasks: 100% vs. 60% on short-range stairs, 176.5 vs. 75.35 stairs on long-range, 85% vs. 45% on compositional terrain, and 100% vs. 0% on missing steps. Against MoB (Margolis & Agrawal, 2023), the gap is even more pronounced, with MoB achieving 0% success on all stair and unseen-terrain tasks and substantially lower anti-disturbance tolerances (e.g., 4 Kg payload vs. 8 Kg). Compared to the Regression baseline (DreamWaQ, Nahrendra et al., 2023), HIM shows clear advantages on challenging terrains (stairs: 0.147 vs. 0.194 tracking error; discrete obstacles: 0.125 vs. 0.182) and real-world generalization (176.5 vs. 155.7 stairs; 55% vs. 30% on deformable slopes), while the two methods are nearly tied on simple slopes (0.073 vs. 0.071). The paper attributes its advantage over regression to contrastive learning's use of batch-level information and its avoidance of representation collapse under domain randomization noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws a direct conceptual line from classical Internal Model Control (IMC) in control theory to modern deep reinforcement learning for legged locomotion, proposing that environmental disturbances (terrain, friction) need not be explicitly estimated but can be inferred from the robot's own response. It departs from the dominant two-stage teacher-student paradigm (Miki et al., 2022a; Kumar et al., 2021) and from regression-based latent state inference (Nahrendra et al., 2023 DreamWaQ) by replacing both with a single-stage contrastive learning objective (SwAV-style) that aligns a hybrid internal embedding with the robot's successor state. The method also contrasts with MBRL approaches (DayDreamer) by avoiding expensive world-model rollouts, instead exploiting batch-level structure in massively parallel Isaac Gym simulations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a single RTX 4090 for one hour (200M samples) suffices to train a policy that outperforms methods requiring 600M–1280M samples, using only joint encoders and an IMU—no cameras or LiDAR. This lowers the barrier to deploying robust locomotion on low-cost or sensor-limited platforms. However, the absence of any exteroceptive perception limits the robot to primitive locomotion skills and precludes higher-level tasks like manipulation or navigation; the authors acknowledge this as the primary open direction. Whether the contrastive-embedding principle generalizes beyond quadruped locomotion to other embodied control tasks remains to be explored.
