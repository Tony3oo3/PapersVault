## Problem

Training instabilities in large Transformers—such as attention logit growth and output logit divergence—have only been documented at billion-parameter scale, making them prohibitively expensive to reproduce and study. This paper asks whether these instabilities can be faithfully reproduced in small models, and whether their onset can be predicted from small-scale proxy experiments before they emerge at scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241943/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The experiments train decoder-only Transformers (GPT-2 style, 2.4M to 4.8B parameters) on C4 with a 32K SentencePiece vocabulary, sweeping learning rates across three orders of magnitude (3e-4 to 3e-1) with AdamW, cosine decay, and warm-up. The primary output is an LR sensitivity metric—the expected deviation from optimal validation loss when varying LR across the sweep—which summarizes the entire LR-vs-loss curve into a single number. Additionally, the paper tracks scaling trends of model characteristics (max attention logits, gradient RMS, parameter RMS, block output RMS) as functions of model size and learning rate, using these trends to extrapolate and predict when instabilities will trigger in larger, untrained models.

## Method

The paper introduces LR sensitivity as a summary statistic for learning-rate-vs-loss curves and uses it to systematically compare interventions across model scales. Two known instabilities—attention logit growth (mitigated by qk-layernorm) and output logit divergence (mitigated by z-loss regularization)—are reproduced in small models at high learning rates, confirming that large-scale mitigations transfer to small scale. The authors then study how warm-up length, independent weight decay (decoupled from LR as in AdamW's original formulation), and μP parameterization affect LR sensitivity, finding that scaling depth increases sensitivity faster than scaling width. A key mechanistic insight connects both instabilities to parameter norm growth: attention logits depend quadratically on parameter RMS (making them the first to blow up), while the output layernorm's gradient scales inversely with input RMS (shrinking gradients as depth and LR increase). Finally, by fitting quadratic curves to max attention logits and gradient RMS across scales, the authors predict that a 4.8B model will diverge at LR 1e-2 without qk-layernorm (confirmed experimentally) and identify a new instability where gradient RMS approaches the default AdamW ε=1e-8, causing updates to vanish unless ε is reduced.

## Results

The paper introduces LR sensitivity as a summary statistic measuring expected deviation from optimal loss when sweeping learning rate across three orders of magnitude (3e-4 to 3e-1). With qk-layernorm, a 1.2B-parameter Transformer trains stably at LR 0.3, spanning three orders of magnitude of LR variation, whereas without qk-layernorm the maximum trainable LR decreases with model scale. The authors predict and confirm that a 4.8B-parameter model diverges at LR 1e-2 without qk-layernorm, with all observed attention logits above 1e4 leading to divergence. At the largest scale tested, gradient RMS approaches the default AdamW epsilon (1e-8); decreasing epsilon to 1e-15 improves loss for the 4.8B model at LR 0.3, while increasing it to 1e-6 causes loss divergence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares several interventions against their absence rather than competing methods on a benchmark. Qk-layernorm (Dehghani et al., 2023) dramatically reduces LR sensitivity and enables stable training across three orders of magnitude of LR, while without it the maximum trainable LR shrinks with scale. Z-loss regularization (Chowdhery et al., 2022) resolves output logit divergence instability that occurs in models without weight decay regardless of scale; weight decay also mitigates this for larger models. Longer warm-up reduces LR sensitivity, most clearly for larger models that are unstable at LR 3e-1 without it. Independent (decoupled) weight decay as recommended by Loshchilov & Hutter (2019) reduces LR sensitivity compared to the coupled default in PyTorch/Optax. μP (Yang et al., 2022) stabilizes the optimal LR across width scaling but does not reduce LR sensitivity or alleviate the need for qk-layernorm at high learning rates. Scaling depth increases LR sensitivity at a faster rate than scaling width, though at the largest scale tested, depth scaling yields lower validation loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the scaling-laws methodology (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), which traditionally tracks loss as a function of model size, to instead track model characteristics such as activation and gradient norms for predicting instabilities. It builds directly on the instability observations of Dehghani et al. (2023) (attention logit growth) and Chowdhery et al. (2022) (output logit divergence), but shifts the experimental regime from large-scale to small-scale proxies, introducing LR sensitivity as a compact summary statistic. The paper also engages with the edge-of-stability literature (Cohen et al., 2021, 2022) and μParam (Yang et al., 2022), positioning its contributions as complementary: rather than explaining the dynamics of instability, it provides practical tools for detecting and predicting them before they manifest at scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: teams training large Transformers can now use small-scale proxy experiments to identify which hyperparameters (e.g., AdamW epsilon, learning rate) will cause divergence before committing to expensive runs. The discovery that the default AdamW epsilon (1e-8) becomes too large relative to gradient RMS at scale is a concrete, actionable finding that may affect ongoing and future large-scale training runs. Open questions include whether the quadratic extrapolation for predicting attention logit growth holds beyond the tested range, whether the epsilon issue is the only new instability hiding in default hyperparameters, and whether the small-scale proxy methodology generalizes to architectures and objectives beyond decoder-only language models on C4.
