## Problem

Path-based attribution methods (e.g., Integrated Gradients) satisfy three fundamental axioms but produce ambiguous attributions because the choice of interpolation path between baseline and target strongly affects how credit is allocated across features. No prior work provided a principled selection criterion for the optimal path, leaving users without a clear way to obtain sparse, interpretable saliency maps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240289/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a target image x^T, a baseline image x^0 (zero-filled, Gaussian-blurred, or other), and a differentiable DNN f, SAMP produces a per-pixel attribution vector a whose entries sum exactly to Δy = f(x^T) − f(x^0) (completeness). The path is discretized into n segments, each manipulating s = d/n pixels per step, so the output is a dense attribution map at the original image resolution (e.g., 28×28 for MNIST, 224×224 for ImageNet) that can be rendered as a grayscale saliency map or overlaid on the input.

## Method

SAMP introduces the Concentration Principle, which selects the path that maximizes the variance of the attribution vector, thereby concentrating credit on a sparse set of salient pixels rather than spreading it uniformly. To make the combinatorial path search tractable, the authors assume the sequential attribution allocation follows a Brownian motion, which (via Propositions 1–2) justifies a greedy per-step selection: at each segment, pick the s pixels whose gradient projection onto the remaining displacement is largest. Two plug-in modules refine the result: an Infinitesimal Constraint (IC) caps the L1-norm of each step to preserve the completeness axiom, and a Momentum Strategy (MS) applies an exponential moving average to the gradient to help the greedy search escape flat local optima. The full SAMP++ algorithm (Algorithm 1) runs in O(d) per step and requires only forward/backward passes through the network, making it model-agnostic and comparable in cost to standard path methods like IG.

## Results

SAMP++ achieves the best Deletion AUC (lower is better) and Insertion AUC (higher is better) across all three datasets: MNIST (Deletion −0.137, Insertion 1.050), CIFAR-10 (Deletion −0.899, Insertion 1.514), and ImageNet (Deletion 0.145, Insertion 1.116). On ImageNet, SAMP++ outperforms the strongest path-method baseline Guided IG by 13% on Deletion (0.145 vs. 0.167) and by 60% on Insertion (1.116 vs. 0.699). The Sensitivity-N check confirms that the infinitesimal constraint (IC) significantly improves the Pearson correlation between summed attributions and output change, validating the completeness axiom.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAMP++ dominates all 12 baselines on both Deletion and Insertion metrics across MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet. On ImageNet, the largest gains are over Guided IG (Deletion 0.145 vs. 0.167; Insertion 1.116 vs. 0.699) and IG (Deletion 0.145 vs. 0.197; Insertion 1.116 vs. 0.725), confirming that path choice is the key differentiator among path methods. On CIFAR-10, SAMP++ nearly doubles the Insertion AUC of non-path baselines like SmoothGrad (1.514 vs. 0.517) and CAM (1.514 vs. 0.863), while also achieving the best Deletion (−0.899 vs. −0.372 for IG). On MNIST, SAMP++ leads in Deletion (−0.137 vs. −0.041 for Guided IG) and Insertion (1.050 vs. 0.808 for LRP). In the pointing game on PASCAL VOC2007, SAMP++ achieves 80.75% accuracy, outperforming IG (76.31%), BlurIG (76.19%), and GuidedIG (72.82%), though trailing block-level RISE (87.33%). For μ-Fidelity on MNIST, SAMP++ (0.461) edges out IG and GuidedIG (both 0.453) and SmoothGrad (0.327).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the path-method framework for attribution (Friedman, 2004; Sundararajan et al., 2017) by introducing the first explicit selection criterion—Concentration Principle—for choosing among the many valid paths between a baseline and target input. Prior path methods (IG, BlurIG, GuidedIG) each adopted a fixed path (straight line, progressive blur, gradient-guided detour) without a principled justification for why that path yields clearer attributions. SAMP departs from this by formulating path selection as a variance-maximization problem over a finite manipulation-path set, then solving it greedily under a Brownian-motion assumption that reduces factorial complexity to linear.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Concentration Principle gives the interpretability community a concrete, axiomatic-adjacent criterion for resolving the long-standing ambiguity of path choice, and SAMP demonstrates that a simple greedy search over manipulation paths can consistently outperform 12 baselines on Deletion/Insertion metrics across MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet. However, the paper acknowledges that the Brownian-motion approximation lacks a rigorous optimality guarantee, that SAMP still requires multiple forward/backward passes (slower than CAM/Grad-CAM), and that the infinitesimal constraint—designed for theoretical rigor—has negligible effect on practical metrics. Open questions include whether the greedy solution is provably near-optimal, whether the approach extends to non-image modalities (NLP, medical imaging), and whether more efficient approximations can close the speed gap with gradient-based methods.
