## Problem

ICU prediction models are frequently reported with state-of-the-art performance, but results are nearly impossible to compare across studies because datasets, cohort definitions, preprocessing pipelines, and training setups vary substantially even among papers using the same dataset. Existing benchmarks are hard-coded to a single dataset with a fixed set of tasks, making multi-dataset evaluation and task adaptation require extensive code changes. This paper identifies that small variations in exclusion criteria, feature selection, and label definitions can shift performance more than the choice of model architecture, creating an urgent need for a unified, extensible benchmarking tool.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes 52 harmonized features per ICU stay: 4 static patient attributes (age, sex, height, weight) and 48 time-series channels (vital signs and lab tests) resampled to hourly bins. Mortality and kidney function tasks use only the first 24 hours of data, while AKI, sepsis, and length-of-stay tasks use all available data until the event or discharge. Outputs are binary classification probabilities (mortality, AKI, sepsis) or continuous regression values (kidney function in mg/dL, remaining length of stay in hours), evaluated via AUROC, AUPRC, and MAE across 5-fold cross-validation repeated 5 times.

## Method

YAIB is a four-stage modular pipeline: (1) clinical concepts are defined dataset-independently using the ricu R package, which maps heterogeneous ICU databases into a common ID-time-value format with standardized units and temporal references; (2) patient cohorts and prediction tasks are specified through a transparent exclusion-criteria pipeline with step-by-step attrition reporting; (3) a configurable preprocessing recipe handles scaling, forward-fill imputation with missingness indicators, and optional historical aggregation (min/max/mean/count) for non-sequential models; (4) models are trained via PyTorch Lightning with gin-config-defined experiments, Bayesian hyperparameter optimization (Gaussian Process via scikit-optimize), and early stopping. Six architectures (LR, Elastic Net, LightGBM, GRU, LSTM, TCN, Transformer) are benchmarked across four datasets (MIMIC-IV, eICU, HiRID, AUMCdb), with the key empirical finding that dataset choice, cohort definition, and preprocessing produce larger performance swings than model class, and that transfer learning from eICU to HiRID yields consistent gains especially with limited target-domain data.

## Results

Across four ICU datasets (AUMCdb, HiRID, eICU, MIMIC-IV) and six model architectures, mortality prediction achieves AUROC of 83.7–87.7 with AUPRC of 33.0–54.4, with LGBM on MIMIC-IV reaching the best AUROC (87.7±0.2). For hourly AKI onset, GRU consistently dominates, achieving 90.9±0.0 AUROC on eICU and 90.7±0.1 on MIMIC-IV, substantially outperforming classical ML (72.8–85.8 AUROC). Sepsis prediction is the hardest task, with AUPRC as low as 2.9 (LR on eICU) and best AUPRC of 13.0 (TCN on HiRID). For regression tasks, kidney function MAE ranges from 0.23–0.34 mg/dL and length-of-stay MAE from 38.2–54.9 hours, with Transformers consistently achieving the lowest LoS MAE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For mortality prediction, all six architectures (LR, LGBM, GRU, LSTM, TCN, TF) achieve highly comparable AUROC (83.7–87.7), with LGBM edging ahead on MIMIC-IV (87.7 vs. 86.1 for LR, a 1.6-point gap). For AKI, GRU decisively outperforms all baselines, reaching 90.9 AUROC on eICU versus 72.8 for LR (an 18.1-point gap) and 84.6 for LGBM (6.3 points), making it the clear winner for this task. For sepsis, GRU and TCN/TF outperform LR and LGBM by 3–6 AUROC points (e.g., GRU 83.6 vs. LR 77.1 on MIMIC-IV), but absolute AUPRC remains very low (≤13.0) across all models. For length-of-stay regression, Transformers consistently achieve the lowest MAE (38.2–41.8 hours) compared to EN (43.6–54.9) and LGBM (39.2–44.7). Against the HiRID benchmark (Yèche et al., 2022), YAIB's baseline mortality AUROC on HiRID is 84.0–84.9 versus 88.8–90.8 reported by Yèche et al.; aligning exclusion criteria (removing the >30h filter) recovers about half this gap (to 87.2–88.2), with the remainder attributed to additional drug-usage features in the HiRID benchmark. The key takeaway is that dataset and cohort definition choices impact performance more than model architecture selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

YAIB builds directly on the ricu R package (Bennett et al., 2023) for cross-dataset clinical concept harmonization and on the neural network architectures from the HiRID-ICU-Benchmark (Yèche et al., 2022). It departs from prior ICU benchmarks (MIMIC-Extract, FIDDLE, HiRID-benchmark, PyHealth, Clairvoyance, TemporAI) by being explicitly multi-dataset and modular: rather than hard-coding a single pipeline for one dataset and a fixed task set, it provides an extensible interface where cohort definitions, preprocessing, models, and metrics can be independently swapped while remaining reproducible. The paper positions itself as the first end-to-end framework that unifies data extraction, task definition, preprocessing, training, and evaluation across four major open ICU datasets under a single configuration syntax.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central empirical finding—that cohort definition, dataset choice, and preprocessing pipeline often matter more for prediction performance than the choice between model architectures (LR vs. GRU vs. Transformer)—quantifies a concern raised in multiple prior systematic reviews but rarely demonstrated under a controlled, reproducible protocol. This strengthens the case for standardized benchmarking and transparent reporting in clinical ML. Key limitations include the restriction to 52 features (mainly vitals and labs, excluding medications and comorbidities), ICU-only scope, and the absence of fairness/bias evaluation. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to non-ICU settings, how to incorporate clinical notes and imaging, and whether the harmonized cohort definitions truly capture clinically meaningful endpoints across diverse healthcare systems.
