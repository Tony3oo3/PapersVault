## Problem

In-sample off-policy evaluation methods that use importance resampling to avoid out-of-distribution extrapolation error cannot be applied to deterministic target policies in continuous action spaces, because the importance sampling ratios between a Dirac-delta target policy and a stochastic behavior policy are zero almost surely. Existing alternatives—marginalized importance sampling and standard FQE—either suffer from training instability due to minimax objectives or from extrapolation error when querying the Q-function at off-distribution actions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline transition dataset D = {(s_i, a_i, r_i, s'_i, a'_i)} sampled from a stochastic behavior policy μ, along with a deterministic target policy π̃: S→A. The behavior policy may be known or estimated via maximum likelihood (tanh-squashed mixture-of-Gaussians fitted to the data). The output is a learned Q-function Q_θ(s,a) from which the target policy value is computed as V̂(π) = (1−γ)·(1/m)Σ Q_θ(s_{0,i}, π̃(s_{0,i})) over initial states in the dataset, with the Q-function parameterized by a 2-hidden-layer MLP (256 units).

## Method

KMIFQE relaxes the Dirac-delta target policy in the importance sampling ratio with a Gaussian kernel whose Mahalanobis metric A(s) and bandwidth h are learned to minimize the MSE of the estimated TD update vector. The bias of the relaxed estimator scales as O(h²) (governed by the Hessian of the target Q-network at the target action) while the variance scales as O(1/(nh^d)), yielding a closed-form optimal bandwidth h* = (vd/(4n‖b‖²))^{1/(d+4)}. In high-dimensional action spaces the bias dominates the MSE, so the paper derives a bandwidth-agnostic optimal metric A*(s) in closed form by minimizing an upper bound on the squared-trace bias term: A*(s) is constructed from the positive and negative eigenvalues/eigenvectors of the Hessian H_{a'}Q_{θ̄}(s',a')|_{a'=π̃(s')}, effectively shrinking the kernel distance in directions where the Q-landscape is flat. The algorithm iterates between updating h*, computing kernel-weighted resampling probabilities, estimating the TD update vector via importance resampling, updating Q_θ, and recomputing the local metric from the updated Q-network's Hessian, until convergence.

## Results

KMIFQE achieves the lowest RMSE on 10 out of 12 evaluated datasets, with particularly large margins on Walker2D-v2 (0.032 vs. 0.273 for SR-DICE and 241.319 for FQE) and Humanoid-v2 (0.246 vs. 1.285 and 8.860). On the modified Pendulum-v0 with dummy action dimensions, KMIFQE with metric learning achieves RMSE 0.115, compared to 1.641 (SR-DICE) and 8.126 (FQE), demonstrating the effectiveness of the learned kernel metric in ignoring irrelevant action dimensions. The only notable losses are on HalfCheetah-v2 (FQE: 1.637 vs. KMIFQE: 2.080) and halfcheetah-medium-expert-v2 (FQE: 0.080 vs. KMIFQE: 0.418), which the authors attribute to the slow-varying Q-landscape in HalfCheetah reducing extrapolation error for OOD-based methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KMIFQE is compared against two state-of-the-art OPE baselines: SR-DICE (Fujimoto et al., 2021), which uses successor representation learning to avoid minimax optimization, and FQE (Voloshin et al., 2019), which directly minimizes the TD loss but is vulnerable to extrapolation error from out-of-distribution samples. KMIFQE outperforms both baselines on 10 of 12 datasets, with the largest margins on Walker2D-v2 (0.032 vs. 0.273 SR-DICE, 241.319 FQE) and Humanoid-v2 (0.246 vs. 1.285 SR-DICE, 8.860 FQE), where FQE's reliance on OOD samples causes catastrophic failure. The two exceptions are HalfCheetah-v2 (FQE: 1.637 vs. KMIFQE: 2.080) and halfcheetah-medium-expert-v2 (FQE: 0.080 vs. KMIFQE: 0.418), which the authors explain by noting that HalfCheetah's Q-function varies slowly with respect to action changes, reducing the extrapolation error that plagues FQE and SR-DICE. On the D4RL medium-replay datasets, KMIFQE and FQE are competitive (e.g., hopper-m-r: 0.536 vs. 0.561), but KMIFQE still wins on halfcheetah-m-r (4.698 vs. 6.394) and walker2d-m-r (1.364 vs. 86.315). The ablation showing KMIFQE without metric learning consistently performs worse than the full method confirms that the learned Mahalanobis metric provides meaningful bias reduction beyond what the bandwidth alone achieves.

## Relation to Prior Work

KMIFQE bridges two previously disconnected lines of work: in-sample importance resampling for OPE (Schlegel et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2023), which avoids OOD extrapolation but fails for deterministic policies due to zero IS ratios, and kernel metric learning for OPE in contextual bandits (Kallus & Zhou, 2018; Lee et al., 2022), which handles deterministic policies but only in single-step settings. The paper extends the kernel relaxation and Mahalanobis metric learning from the bandit setting to full MDPs by deriving the bias-variance decomposition of the TD update vector (rather than the OPE estimate itself), yielding a closed-form optimal bandwidth and a Hessian-based optimal metric that are bandwidth-agnostic. The FQE framework (Voloshin et al., 2019) provides the base Q-learning structure, while the metric optimization solution is directly inherited from Noh et al. (2010).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Deterministic policies are essential in safety-critical domains (robotic control, drug dosing) where stochasticity is unacceptable, yet existing OPE methods either cannot handle them (in-sample IR) or suffer from OOD extrapolation errors (FQE, SR-DICE). The clean theoretical decomposition—showing bias dominates in high-dimensional action spaces and that a Hessian-based metric can reduce it—provides principled, interpretable guidance for kernel design in OPE. Limitations include the assumption of a known (or MLE-estimated) behavior policy, the need for Hessian computation of the Q-network, and evaluation only on MuJoCo-scale continuous control tasks; open questions include scalability to very high-dimensional action spaces, applicability to stochastic target policies, and whether the metric learning framework extends to model-based OPE.
