## Problem

Finite-time horizon MDPs require non-stationary policies (one per epoch), yet standard practice trains all epoch parameters simultaneously, ignoring the inherent dynamic programming structure. No convergence analysis existed for policy gradient methods in this setting under tabular softmax parametrisation, and it was unclear whether exploiting the backwards-induction structure could yield tighter convergence guarantees than simultaneous training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240697/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite-time MDP with H decision epochs, finite state and action spaces, bounded rewards in [0, R*], and a strictly positive initial state distribution, parameterised via tabular softmax (one real-valued parameter per state-action pair per epoch). It produces a time-dependent policy (one softmax policy per epoch) along with explicit O(1/n) convergence rate bounds for exact gradients and high-probability sample complexity bounds for the stochastic (REINFORCE-style) setting. A key structural detail is that the dynamic approach uses epoch-dependent step sizes η_h = 1/(2(H−h)R*) and epoch-dependent batch sizes, reflecting the decreasing smoothness constant as one moves earlier in time.

## Method

The paper introduces dynamic policy gradient, which trains parameters sequentially backwards from epoch H−1 to 0, treating each epoch as a contextual bandit with the future policy held fixed. For exact gradients, they prove β-smoothness (β_h = 2(H−h)R*) and a weak PL-inequality for each epoch's objective, yielding H^3 total training steps versus H^5 for simultaneous PG; crucially, the PL constant c_h is explicitly computable as 1/|A| under uniform initialisation, unlike the unknown constant in the simultaneous case. For stochastic gradients, they construct a stopping time that halts training when the stochastic trajectory deviates too far from the exact PG trajectory, then show that sufficiently large batch sizes (scaling as N^3) and small step sizes (scaling as 1/√N) keep the stopping time from triggering with high probability, yielding H^7 total steps and H^20 batch size for dynamic PG versus H^10 and H^30 for simultaneous PG.

## Results

For exact gradients, both simultaneous and dynamic PG achieve O(1/n) convergence to global optima, but the number of training steps scales as H^5 for simultaneous PG versus H^3 for dynamic PG (where H is the time horizon). In the stochastic (model-free) setting, the simultaneous approach requires training steps scaling as H^10 and batch size as H^30, while the dynamic approach requires only H^7 steps and H^20 batch size. A key practical advantage of dynamic PG is that under uniform initialisation the PL-constant is explicitly c_h = 1/|A|, whereas the analogous constant c for simultaneous PG is unknown and may depend on H. A toy example with H=5 (Figure 1) empirically confirms that dynamic PG reaches target accuracy faster than simultaneous PG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between the standard simultaneous PG (all epoch parameters trained at once) and the proposed dynamic PG (parameters trained backwards in time via dynamic programming). Dynamic PG dominates on every theoretical metric: the H-dependence improves from H^5 to H^3 for exact gradients and from H^10/H^30 to H^7/H^20 for stochastic gradients. The dynamic approach also provides an explicitly computable PL-constant (1/|A|) under uniform initialisation, while the simultaneous approach's constant remains unknown and potentially H-dependent. The heuristic explanation is that the policy gradient theorem shows early-epoch gradients are corrupted by estimation errors in later-epoch Q-values, making simultaneous training inefficient. The toy example (H=5, Figure 1) visually confirms that dynamic PG reaches a target accuracy of 0.1 faster than simultaneous PG across multiple target-accuracy settings. No external empirical baselines (e.g., natural PG, mirror descent) are benchmarked; the comparison is purely between the two PG training schemes under identical softmax parametrisation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of global convergence analysis for softmax policy gradient from discounted infinite-horizon MDPs (Agarwal et al., 2021; Mei et al., 2020; Yuan et al., 2022) to finite-time horizon MDPs, where optimal policies are inherently non-stationary. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of 'dynamic policy gradient,' which exploits the backwards-induction structure of finite-time MDPs by training epoch parameters sequentially from last to first, in contrast to the standard simultaneous training of all epoch parameters. The stochastic analysis adapts the stopping-time technique of Ding et al. (2022) to the unregularised setting, where the absence of a well-defined optimal parameter set requires a fundamentally different construction. The approach is positioned as a concrete instantiation of Policy Search by Dynamic Programming (Bagnell et al., 2003) with policy gradient as the inner solver.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first convergence analysis for dynamic-programming-inspired policy gradient under softmax parametrisation in finite-time MDPs, both with exact and sampled gradients and without regularisation. The provable H^3 vs H^5 (exact) and H^7 vs H^10 (stochastic) scaling advantage of dynamic over simultaneous PG provides a theoretical justification for structured training in finite-horizon problems, with practical relevance to applications like optimal stopping and supply chain management. However, the analysis is restricted to tabular parametrisation, the bounds are acknowledged as far from tight, and the explicit constant c_h = 1/|A| under uniform initialisation does not yet have a counterpart in the simultaneous setting. Extending these results to function-approximation (e.g., neural network) parametrisations and deriving tighter or adaptive-step-size bounds are natural next steps.
