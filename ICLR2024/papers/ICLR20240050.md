## Problem

Deep neural networks underperform humans at peripheral object detection, showing sharp accuracy drops at higher eccentricities while human performance degrades gracefully. No large-scale dataset or standardized benchmark existed to study this gap, and the well-validated Texture Tiling Model (TTM) of peripheral vision was too computationally expensive (5+ hours per image) to use at DNN training scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20240050/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes RGB natural scene images from MS-COCO and an eccentricity parameter (5°, 10°, 15°, or 20°) to produce TTM-transformed images at 16 pixels per degree, where uniform pooling regions of fixed size replace the original foveated, eccentricity-varying pooling. For the machine psychophysics evaluation, 10 stochastic TTM transforms are generated per image (since TTM is under-constrained), yielding 100 present/absent pairings per image-eccentricity combination. Detection models output bounding boxes with confidence scores, which are scored against padded ground truth boxes (expanded by half a pooling region width) using a 75% intersection-over-area threshold, with total overlapping scores summed rather than taking the maximum.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a modification of the Texture Tiling Model (TTM) to use uniform, non-foveated pooling: instead of pooling regions growing with eccentricity from a fixation point, all regions are fixed to the size corresponding to a single target eccentricity, enabling pre-computation and pseudo-foveation by stitching multiple uniform transforms. This yields COCO-Periph, a dataset of 74K–118K images per eccentricity (5°, 10°, 15°, 20°) covering the full COCO 2017 train/val/test splits, requiring over 6 months of compute to synthesize. A Faster-RCNN-R50-FPN is then fine-tuned or trained from scratch on a 20/20/20/20/20 mixture of original and four eccentricity TTM images. The paper validates the approach through a human eye-tracking psychophysics experiment (10 subjects, 2IFC detection task at four eccentricities) and a parallel machine psychophysics experiment, showing that training on COCO-Periph increases critical eccentricity by ~5° and improves geometric corruption robustness, though DNNs still lack human-like sensitivity to peripheral clutter.

## Results

On the COCO-Periph validation set, all object detection models show substantial AP degradation with increasing eccentricity: DINO-FocalNet-Large drops from 58.4 AP at 0° to 15.0 at 20°, while Faster-RCNN-R50 drops from 36.7 to 4.2. Training a Faster-RCNN-R50 from scratch on COCO-Periph (all eccentricities) recovers much of this loss, achieving 12.7 AP at 20° (vs. 4.2 baseline), a ~3× improvement, though it still trails DINO-FocalNet-Large (15.0). In the psychophysics comparison, humans outperform all DNNs, with critical eccentricity (μ) more than 5° greater than any detection model; training on COCO-Periph increases the model's critical eccentricity by nearly 5° over the pre-trained baseline but does not close the human–machine gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's core comparison is between pre-trained detectors and a Faster-RCNN-R50 trained/fine-tuned on COCO-Periph. At 0° (clean COCO), the trained model (33.8 AP) actually underperforms the baseline Faster-RCNN-R50 (36.7) and all other baselines, as expected given the domain shift. However, at 20° eccentricity the trained model (12.7 AP) dramatically outperforms the baseline Faster-RCNN-R50 (4.2) and also beats DETR-R50 (4.5), RetinaNet-R50 (5.0), FoveaBox (5.3), and Faster-RCNN-X101 (4.7), though it still falls short of DINO-FocalNet-Large (15.0) and DINO-Swin-Tiny (7.6). The fine-tuned variant (10.8 at 20°) shows a similar pattern but with a smaller gain. In the psychophysics task, DINO detectors achieve the closest critical eccentricity to humans among all tested models, and the paper's trained model improves its critical eccentricity by ~5° over the pre-trained Faster-RCNN baseline, yet all DNNs remain more than 5° below human performance. On corruption robustness (COCO-C), fine-tuning on COCO-Periph yields a marginal mAPc improvement (17.47 vs. 17.34 baseline), while training from scratch slightly decreases it (16.72), with the largest gains seen on geometric corruptions (elastic transform: 15.02 vs. 11.94) and slight losses on noise-like corruptions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Texture Tiling Model (TTM), a well-validated summary-statistic model of peripheral vision, by modifying it to use uniform rather than foveated pooling (uniform-TTM), enabling pre-computation of peripheral vision transforms without assuming a fixation point. It departs from prior human-inspired DNN approaches (FoveaBox, foveated transformers, peripheral vision transformers) that either require specialized architectures, model only resolution loss without crowding, or rely on less-validated style-transfer methods. The key conceptual contribution is treating TTM as a general input pre-processing step applicable to any detection architecture, rather than embedding peripheral vision into the network itself, and grounding the evaluation in a rigorous human psychophysics comparison.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COCO-Periph provides the first large-scale, standardized dataset for evaluating peripheral vision in modern DNNs, making it computationally feasible to test TTM-based transforms at the scale of object detection. The finding that DNNs exhibit sharp performance drops with eccentricity while humans degrade gracefully, and that this gap persists even after training on peripheral images, suggests that simply matching input distributions is insufficient for modeling human vision. The observation that DNNs fail to capture human-like sensitivity to clutter raises open questions about whether task formulation, architectural inductive biases, or multi-task optimization are needed to bridge the human-machine gap. Practical applications in driver safety, foveated rendering, and UI/UX design depend on closing this gap.
