## Problem

Traditional brain simulators (NEURON, NEST, Brian2) rely on custom numerical solvers and data structures incompatible with automatic differentiation, preventing gradient-based training of biophysical models. Conversely, deep learning frameworks and BIC libraries (snnTorch, Norse, SpikingJelly) lack the sparse, event-driven, and multi-scale operators needed to faithfully simulate large-scale brain networks with realistic biophysical properties. No existing software provides a unified, differentiable framework that supports both high-fidelity brain simulation and modern AI training workflows.

## Input / Output

BrainPy accepts dynamical model equations (ODEs for ion channels, neurons, synapses), connectivity specifications (probability, weight distributions, delays — or explicit sparse matrices), and time-varying input stimuli (spiking events, sensory signals). A key design choice is that large-scale connectivity can be specified purely by scalar parameters (connection probability, weight mean/std, random seed) rather than explicit matrices, enabling near-constant memory regardless of network size. Outputs include spike trains, membrane potential trajectories, trained synaptic weights, classification decisions, and analytical products such as phase-plane and bifurcation diagrams, all computed within a single differentiable computational graph.

## Method

BrainPy extends JAX/XLA with four dedicated mechanisms for brain dynamics. First, it provides differentiable sparse and event-driven primitive operators (e.g., brainpy.math.event.csrmv) that compute synaptic currents only at spiking positions, achieving 2–5 orders of magnitude speedup over dense operators. Second, JIT connectivity operators regenerate synaptic connections on-the-fly using uniform-integer sampling (replacing the geometric-distribution approach), reducing memory from quadratic to constant and enabling 4M+ neuron networks on a single GPU. Third, the AlignPre/AlignPost synaptic projection abstraction decouples synaptic dynamics from inter-population communication, collapsing O(pmn) synaptic variables to O(m) or O(n) and allowing DL layers (convolutions, linear maps) to serve as communication mechanisms. Fourth, an object-oriented JIT transformation (brainpy.math.jit) compiles hierarchical, class-based multi-scale models (channels → neurons → networks → systems) into fused XLA kernels, achieving >10× faster compilation than Brian2 and enabling seamless BPTT training of biologically grounded spiking networks (e.g., a GIF-based PFC model trained on a delayed match-to-sample task to ~100% accuracy).

## Results

BrainPy's event-driven operators achieve 2–5 orders of magnitude speedup over dense and sparse matrix-vector multiplication on both CPU and GPU. On the COBA-LIF and COBA-HH benchmark networks, BrainPy outperforms all five mainstream brain simulators (NEURON, NEST, Brian2, ANNArchy, BindsNet), with the advantage growing for the more complex Hodgkin-Huxley model. For SNN training, BrainPy completes each epoch in 28.6 s (CPU) and 17.1 s (GPU) on a simple 3-layer SNN, and 50.0 s (GPU) on a VGG SNN, beating snnTorch, SpikingJelly, and Norse. The JIT connectivity operator enables scaling a COBA-LIF network to over 4 million neurons on a single GPU with linear memory and time scaling, and a 50,000-node reservoir achieves 98.9% accuracy on MNIST.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against traditional brain simulators (NEURON, NEST, Brian2, ANNArchy, BindsNet), BrainPy achieves the fastest simulation speed on both COBA-LIF and COBA-HH networks, with the gap widening for the more computationally intensive Hodgkin-Huxley model. Against BIC frameworks (snnTorch, SpikingJelly, Norse), BrainPy is the fastest on both CPU and GPU for SNN training, reducing per-epoch time by roughly 35% on CPU and 63% on GPU for the simple SNN, and nearly halving training time for the VGG SNN compared to SpikingJelly. Against JAX and PyTorch dense/sparse operators, BrainPy's event-driven csrmv operator is 2–5 orders of magnitude faster, with the advantage growing at lower firing frequencies. For JIT connectivity, BrainPy's operator maintains near-constant memory and runs 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than dense or sparse matrix multiplication as network size grows. For compilation, BrainPy is over 10× faster than Brian2 on CPU for the EI balance network, and over three orders of magnitude faster on the 30-area multi-area network (where Brian2 takes >1 hour to compile vs. BrainPy's seconds).

## Relation to Prior Work

BrainPy sits at the intersection of two previously separate software ecosystems: traditional brain simulators (NEURON, NEST, Brian2) that use descriptive languages and custom C++/CUDA code generation for high-fidelity biophysical modeling, and BIC libraries (snnTorch, Norse, SpikingJelly) that leverage DL frameworks for differentiable SNN training but lack biophysical realism. It builds on JAX/XLA for the computational substrate and on the JIT connectivity concept from Lytton et al. (2008) and Knight & Nowotny (2020), but departs from both lineages by (1) making all brain-dynamics operators natively differentiable within the JAX ecosystem, and (2) introducing the AlignPre/AlignPost synaptic projection abstraction that decouples dynamics from communication, enabling both memory-efficient simulation and seamless insertion of DL layers as communication mechanisms. The object-oriented JIT compilation approach is a novel engineering contribution that bridges JAX's function-oriented tracing interface with the modular, class-based model construction paradigm natural to neuroscience.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the perceived incompatibility between biophysical fidelity and differentiable training is an artifact of software architecture rather than a fundamental limitation, opening a path for data-driven training of mechanistically detailed cortical models (validated on a PFC working-memory task reaching ~100% accuracy). The JIT connectivity operators enabling 4M+ neuron simulations on a single GPU, and the 3+ orders-of-magnitude compilation speedup over Brian2 on multi-area networks, lower practical barriers for large-scale brain modeling. Open questions include the acknowledged communication overhead in distributed spiking-network simulation (Appendix L), the restriction of AlignPre/AlignPost to homogeneous synaptic parameters within a projection, and whether the differentiable training approach generalizes beyond the relatively small (100-neuron) PFC model to truly large-scale biophysical networks.
