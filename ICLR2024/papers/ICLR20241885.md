## Problem

While conversational LLMs have been applied to chemical reactions and retrosynthesis, drug editing (lead optimization and protein design) remains largely unexplored. Existing text-guided drug editing methods do not exploit the conversational and iterative-refinement capabilities of LLMs, and traditional manual editing by domain experts is subjective and biased. The paper asks whether a conversational LLM framework can systematically perform drug editing across small molecules, peptides, and proteins.

## Input / Output

The method takes a drug structure (SMILES string for small molecules, amino acid sequence for peptides/proteins) and a natural-language task prompt specifying a desired property change (e.g., 'more soluble in water' or 'binds to HLA-B*44:02'). During conversation rounds, a retrieved similar-and-correct drug from a database is injected as an in-context demonstration. The output is an edited drug structure satisfying the target property, evaluated by hit ratio (fraction of valid edits achieving the property threshold), along with domain-specific textual explanations. The framework is entirely parameter-free, requiring no training or fine-tuning.

## Method

ChatDrug is a parameter-free, three-module framework: (1) PDDS (Prompt Design for Domain-Specific) constructs task-specific prompts that frame the editing objective as a high-level property change rather than exact substructure replacement; (2) ReDF (Retrieval and Domain Feedback) retrieves a structurally similar drug from a database (using Tanimoto similarity for molecules, Levenshtein distance for sequences) that satisfies the target property, verifies it with a domain feedback function, and injects it into the prompt as a corrective demonstration; (3) a Conversation module iterates over C rounds, where each failed output triggers a new retrieval-and-feedback cycle that guides the LLM toward a valid edit. The key insight is that the conversational loop combined with retrieval-based in-context learning allows a general-purpose LLM to explore chemical/sequence space beyond what zero-shot or single-shot prompting achieves, trading off similarity to the input against property satisfaction. The framework is backbone-agnostic and was evaluated on ChatGPT-Turbo, GALACTICA-6.7B, and Llama2-7B across 39 editing tasks.

## Results

The primary metric is hit ratio (percentage of edited drugs achieving the desired property change). On 28 small molecule editing tasks (16 single-objective, 12 multi-objective), ChatDrug-Turbo achieves the best hit ratio on 21 tasks, with a >20% advantage over the second-best method on 20 of them (e.g., 94.13% vs. 67.86% on 'more soluble in water' at Δ=0; 96.54% vs. 60.97% on 'more hydrogen bond donors' at Δ=0). On 9 peptide editing tasks, ChatDrug-Turbo reaches 56.60–69.80% hit ratio versus 1.80–14.40% for the best random mutation baseline. On 2 protein secondary-structure editing tasks, ChatDrug-Turbo achieves 59.68% (more strand) and 33.18% (more helix) versus 21.44% and 26.90% for random mutation-3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All small molecule baselines (Random, PCA, High-Variance, GS-Mutate, MoleculeSTM-SMILES, MoleculeSTM-Graph) are built on MegaMolBART (Liu et al., 2022a), a pretrained autoregressive molecular model. ChatDrug-Turbo outperforms the strongest baseline (MoleculeSTM-Graph) on 21 of 28 small molecule tasks, with margins ranging from ~5% (task 103, Δ=0: 48.65% vs. 39.97%) to over 35% (task 108, Δ=0: 96.54% vs. 60.97%). ChatDrug-GALACTICA wins on 7 tasks (notably tasks 104 and 105 where it reaches 92.13% and 84.21% respectively), while ChatDrug-Llama2 is less competitive on molecules (7 wins) but performs well on peptides and proteins. For peptide editing, no prior text-guided method exists, so random mutation (1–3 positions) serves as the baseline; ChatDrug-Turbo achieves 3–4× higher hit ratios (e.g., 69.80% vs. 14.40% on task 302). For protein editing, ChatDrug-Turbo nearly triples the random mutation-3 baseline on the 'more strand' task (59.68% vs. 21.44%). Overall, ChatDrug-Turbo achieves the best performance on all 39 tasks across the three drug types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes conversational LLMs (ChatGPT, GALACTICA, Llama2) for drug editing by introducing a parameter-free pipeline (PDDS + ReDF + conversation) that combines prompt engineering, retrieval-augmented in-context learning, and iterative domain feedback. It departs from prior text-guided drug editing work (MoleculeSTM, ProteinDT) that treated editing as a one-shot conditional generation problem, instead framing it as an interactive, multi-round process where the LLM receives corrective feedback from a domain oracle. The ReDF module draws on in-context learning principles but applies them specifically to drug structure retrieval with similarity-constrained, property-verified examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 39-task benchmark spanning small molecules, peptides, and proteins provides the first systematic evaluation of LLM-based drug editing, and the parameter-free, backbone-agnostic framework is immediately deployable. Key limitations include the inability to reason about 3D molecular geometry, the computational cost of multiple conversation rounds, and reliance on computational proxies (RDKit, MHCflurry) rather than wet-lab validation. Open questions include whether knowledge summarization can reduce conversation rounds, how to incorporate 3D structural awareness, and whether the approach generalizes to more complex multi-objective optimization beyond simple property thresholds.
