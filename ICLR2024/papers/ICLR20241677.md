## Problem

Classical bias-variance tradeoff theory predicts that increasing model capacity raises variance, yet over-parameterized deep networks generalize well with unimodal variance curves. The mechanism behind this unimodal behavior and the precise relationship between bias and variance at the individual sample level remained unexplained. This paper identifies and characterizes a previously unknown phenomenon—bias-variance alignment—where squared bias approximately equals variance for correctly classified samples in deep model ensembles.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241677/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an ensemble of independently trained deep classifiers (randomness from weight initialization, bootstrap resampling of training data, and stochastic batching) along with labeled test examples. For each test sample (x, y), it computes per-sample bias (distance between the ensemble mean prediction and the one-hot label) and variance (expected squared deviation of individual model predictions from the ensemble mean) under MSE decomposition. The output is a per-sample bias-variance scatter plot and a linear regression characterization showing log Vari ≈ log Bias² + constant with R² ≥ 0.977 across architectures.

## Method

The paper makes three complementary contributions. First, it empirically demonstrates bias-variance alignment across ResNets, EfficientNet, MobileNet, and BERT on ImageNet, CIFAR-10/100, and TREC, showing the phenomenon is specific to over-parameterized models (small models like ResNet-8-W1 do not exhibit it). Second, it proves the alignment theoretically under a unified calibration framework: Theorem 4.3 bounds the bias-variance gap by the class-wise calibration error, and Corollary 4.4 shows that perfect calibration plus confident (near one-hot) predictions forces squared bias to equal variance in expectation. Third, it derives approximate bias-variance equality from neural collapse by modeling ensemble logits as softmax of an ETF-structured weight matrix perturbed by Gumbel noise (Assumption 5.1), yielding explicit bounds on the bias-to-variance ratio for binary classification (Corollary 5.3). The calibration result also subsumes the generalization-disagreement equality of Jiang et al. (2022) as a special case.

## Results

The paper's headline quantitative result is the coefficient of determination (R²) for linear regression of log Variance on log Bias², which is extremely close to 1 across all tested settings: 0.979 (ResNet-8/CIFAR-10), 0.996 (ResNet-56/CIFAR-10), 0.986 (ResNet-110/CIFAR-100), and 0.977 (ResNet-50/ImageNet), with corresponding slopes of 0.882, 0.964, 0.901, and 0.897. The bias-variance alignment is confirmed to hold across architectures (ResNet, EfficientNet-B0, MobileNet-V2, BERT) and datasets (ImageNet, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, TREC). The phenomenon is shown to be specific to over-parameterized models: small models (e.g., ResNet-8-W1) do not exhibit alignment, while large models (e.g., ResNet-110-W16) show strong alignment. On the linear scale, the alignment manifests as a cone-shaped distribution where variance is upper-bounded by squared bias for all samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it validates the bias-variance alignment phenomenon across different experimental conditions. The key comparison is between model sizes: small models (ResNet-8-W1, ResNet-8-W4) do not exhibit bias-variance alignment, while over-parameterized models (ResNet-56-W16, ResNet-110-W16) show strong alignment, confirming the phenomenon is specific to the over-parameterized regime. A second important comparison is between loss functions: the alignment holds for the MSE loss decomposition but does NOT hold for the cross-entropy (KL divergence) decomposition, where the ratio of bias to variance can take any value in (0, ∞) (Proposition F.1). The paper also verifies that the alignment is robust to the source of randomness (bootstrapping vs. random initialization + batching only), showing the phenomenon is not an artifact of data resampling. Theoretically, the paper shows that under perfect calibration with one-hot outputs, squared bias exactly equals variance, and under neural collapse for binary classification, the ratio Bias²/Vari is bounded in ((2s-1)²/exp(2s), 3) on the linear scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the bias-variance decomposition framework from classical statistical learning to the over-parameterized deep learning regime, building directly on the empirical measurements of Neal et al. (2018) and the unimodal variance discovery of Yang et al. (2020). It departs from the classical tradeoff narrative by showing that, for correctly classified samples in large models, squared bias and variance are approximately equal rather than inversely related. Theoretically, it generalizes the calibration-to-generalization-disagreement equality of Jiang et al. (2022) and Kirsch & Gal (2022) from an aggregate to a pointwise statement, and independently derives bias-variance bounds from the neural collapse geometry of Papyan et al. (2020), providing two complementary explanations for the same empirical phenomenon.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bias-variance alignment offers a practical tool: since variance can be estimated without ground-truth labels (by measuring disagreement across an ensemble), one can estimate per-sample test error in unlabeled test settings, enabling example-level model validation. The finding that alignment is specific to over-parameterized models sharpens the boundary between classical and modern deep learning regimes. Key limitations include the restriction to MSE and cross-entropy losses, the binary-classification assumption in the neural collapse analysis, and the lack of an end-to-end theoretical treatment (e.g., via NTK or mean-field methods). Open questions include whether the phenomenon extends to other loss functions, out-of-distribution data, and whether it can be leveraged for principled ensemble routing or model selection.
