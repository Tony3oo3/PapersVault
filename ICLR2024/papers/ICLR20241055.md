## Problem

Pre-trained vision-language models are trained on static image-text pairs and produce text-token outputs, making them poorly suited for low-level robot control that requires video comprehension and continuous action generation. While RT-2 demonstrated that VLMs can be adapted to robot manipulation, it relies on private models and co-fine-tuning on web-scale data, making it inaccessible to the broader robotics community. This paper seeks a low-cost, open-source framework that effectively leverages existing VLMs as robot manipulation policies with minimal robotics-specific data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICLR20241055/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each decision step, the model receives two RGB images (a third-person camera and a wrist/gripper camera) and a free-form language instruction of variable length M. It outputs a 7-DoF relative end-effector pose (3D position + 3D orientation) and a binary gripper open/close command for a Franka Emika Panda arm. The VLM backbone processes only the current single-frame observation, while temporal history is handled entirely by the policy head's recurrent state, enabling the perception module to be run in open-loop mode for faster inference on constrained hardware.

## Method

RoboFlamingo decouples visual-language understanding from sequential decision-making: an OpenFlamingo backbone (ViT encoder + perceiver resampler compressing N visual tokens to N_r, followed by a gated cross-attention decoder that fuses compressed visual tokens with language embeddings) produces a vision-language joint embedding at each timestep, and a lightweight LSTM-based policy head with max-pooling over the token dimension and an MLP predictor converts this embedding plus the previous hidden state into the action. Training uses maximum-likelihood imitation learning (MSE for pose, BCE for gripper) on language-annotated manipulation demonstrations only, fine-tuning just the resampler, cross-attention layers, and policy head (~1B parameters) while freezing the LLM decoder. This selective fine-tuning preserves the VLM's pre-trained vision-language alignment, avoids catastrophic forgetting, and allows the entire system to be trained on a single 8×A100 GPU server, achieving 2× the average success length of prior state-of-the-art on the CALVIN benchmark.

## Results

On the CALVIN benchmark (ABCD→D, language-annotated data only), RoboFlamingo achieves an average success length of 4.09, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art HULC (3.06) by 34% and RT-1 (2.45) by 67%. In the zero-shot vision generalization setting (ABC→D), RoboFlamingo reaches 2.48 average length versus HULC's 0.67 and RT-1's 0.90, a 3.7× improvement over the prior best. Under GPT-4-enriched language instructions (D-Enriched), RoboFlamingo with frozen embeddings achieves 2.12 average length, outperforming HULC (1.82) and RT-1 (0.86). All experiments are conducted on a single 8×A100 GPU server with only ~24K language-annotated steps of training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoboFlamingo decisively outperforms all five baselines across every evaluation setting. Against HULC (the prior SoTA), RoboFlamingo improves average success length from 3.06 to 4.09 on the main ABCD→D setting and from 0.67 to 2.48 on the zero-shot vision generalization (ABC→D) setting, where the gap is most dramatic. Against RT-1, the improvement is from 2.45 to 4.09 (main) and 0.90 to 2.48 (zero-shot). Against MCIL, the gap is orders of magnitude (0.40 → 4.09). In the appendix comparison with robotics-specific representation models, RoboFlamingo (4.09) substantially exceeds both R3M (0.10, frozen) and Voltron (2.08 fine-tuned / 0.11 frozen), confirming that fine-tuning a general-purpose VLM backbone is far more effective than using domain-specific visual representations. Notably, RoboFlamingo uses only language-annotated data (~1% of full CALVIN data) while HULC's best result uses the full unpaired dataset, highlighting superior data efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

RoboFlamingo builds directly on the OpenFlamingo/Flamingo architecture (perceiver resampler + gated cross-attention decoder) but repurposes it for low-level robot control rather than language generation. It departs from the RT-2/PaLM-E co-fine-tuning paradigm, which requires web-scale VL data alongside robot data and private models, by instead freezing most of the VLM and training only the resampler, cross-attention layers, and a lightweight policy head on manipulation demonstrations alone. It also differs from planner-based approaches (SayCan, PaLM-E) that rely on separate low-level skill policies, by directly regressing 7-DoF end-effector actions from the VLM's fused vision-language representation, with temporal history handled by an explicit LSTM policy head rather than by the VLM itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that VLM-based robot manipulation becomes accessible to any lab with a single 8-GPU server and open-source weights, removing the dependency on proprietary models and web-scale co-training data that RT-2 and PaLM-E require. A notable open question is whether this approach transfers to real-robot settings, since all evaluation is in the CALVIN simulation; the authors themselves flag the lack of real-robot deployment as a limitation and point to Open X-Embodiment data as a natural next step. The finding that full-model fine-tuning (3B trainable params) catastrophically underperforms selective fine-tuning (1B params) suggests that preserving the pre-trained VL alignment is critical and that naive end-to-end adaptation of large VLMs to robotics is counterproductive.
