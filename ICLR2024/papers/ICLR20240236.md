## Problem

Existing AI-text detection methods (DetectGPT, Ghostbuster) depend on numerical outputs such as token log-probabilities and loss values from LLMs, which are unavailable for black-box models like GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 that only expose discrete token APIs. Additionally, the high-dimensional continuous features used by prior methods contain redundant and spurious attributes that cause overfitting and vulnerability to adversarial manipulation. The paper asks whether the discrete word-level output of an LLM, obtained through a simple rewriting task, carries enough signal to distinguish machine-generated from human-written text.

## Input / Output

The method takes a paragraph-level text sample (ranging from ~10 to ~300 words across datasets) and a manually crafted rewriting prompt as input. It produces three families of scalar features — invariance, equivariance, and output uncertainty — computed as normalized Levenshtein distances and bag-of-words edit ratios between the original text and one or more LLM rewrites. These features are fed into a lightweight classifier (logistic regression or XGBoost) that outputs a binary prediction: AI-generated or human-written. Crucially, the pipeline consumes only the discrete token string returned by the LLM API, requiring no access to internal probabilities, logits, or model weights.

## Method

Raidar exploits the observation that an LLM, when prompted to rewrite text, makes significantly fewer edits to text it (or a similar model) generated than to human-written text, because it perceives machine-generated text as already high-quality. Concretely, the method prompts a black-box LLM (e.g., GPT-3.5-turbo) to rewrite the input, then measures the character-level Levenshtein distance and bag-of-words n-gram overlap between the original and the rewrite, normalized by text length to yield a length-independent similarity score. Three complementary measurements are computed: (1) invariance — the edit distance between input and a single rewrite; (2) equivariance — the edit distance after applying a semantic transformation (e.g., 'write the opposite meaning'), rewriting, and applying the inverse transformation, compared to a direct rewrite; and (3) uncertainty — the pairwise edit distances among K independent rewrites of the same input, capturing output stability. These discrete, non-differentiable features are concatenated and passed to a logistic regression or XGBoost classifier trained on labeled examples. Because the entire pipeline operates on word symbols rather than continuous embeddings, it is compatible with any black-box LLM API, does not require the original generating model, and is inherently resistant to gradient-based adversarial evasion.

## Results

On the in-distribution paragraph-level detection task (Table 1), Raidar (Invariance) achieves F1 scores of 60.29 (News), 62.88 (Creative Writing), 64.81 (Student Essay), 95.38 (Code), 87.75 (Yelp Reviews), and 81.94 (Arxiv Abstract), outperforming all baselines on every dataset. The largest gain is on Code, where Raidar reaches 95.38 F1 versus Ghostbuster's 65.97 (+29.4 points). In the out-of-distribution setting (Table 2), Raidar still improves over Ghostbuster by up to 22 points (e.g., 56.47 vs. 34.01 on News). The method also generalizes across generation models (Ada, Text-Davinci-002, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, LLaMA 2) with F1 scores up to 98.46 in-distribution and 91.43 out-of-distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Raidar (Invariance) outperforms all four baselines on all six datasets. Against Ghostbuster (Verma et al., 2023), the strongest prior method, Raidar gains +29.4 F1 on Code (95.38 vs. 65.97), +21.8 on Creative Writing (62.88 vs. 41.13), +16.3 on Yelp (87.75 vs. 71.47), +10.3 on Student Essay (64.81 vs. 42.44), +8.3 on Arxiv (81.94 vs. 76.82), and +8.3 on News (60.29 vs. 52.01). Against DetectGPT (Mitchell et al., 2023), Raidar leads by +28.0 on Code, +22.6 on News, +19.2 on Creative Writing, and +15.1 on Yelp. The commercial GPTZero detector is substantially weaker, with Raidar exceeding it by over 60 F1 points on Code (95.38 vs. 31.57) and Yelp (87.75 vs. 25.00). GPT Zero-Shot (Verma et al., 2023) is the weakest academic baseline, with Raidar surpassing it by up to +35.4 F1 on Creative Writing (62.88 vs. 20.00). Notably, Raidar's advantage is largest on Code and Yelp—domains where prior methods struggle most—while the gap narrows on Arxiv Abstracts, where Ghostbuster is already relatively strong (76.82).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in AI-text detection (DetectGPT, Ghostbuster) that relies on numerical outputs such as token log-probabilities or loss curvature from the scoring LLM. Instead, Raidar operates purely on the discrete word-level output of a rewriting LLM, measuring invariance, equivariance, and output uncertainty via Levenshtein and bag-of-words edit distances. Conceptually, it repurposes the well-known observation that autoregressive models assign low loss to their own outputs, but translates it into a black-box-compatible, feature-agnostic detection signal that requires no access to internal model states. The method is positioned as complementary to watermarking approaches and as a robustness improvement over probability-based detectors that are vulnerable to adversarial paraphrasing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: Raidar is the first detection method shown to work effectively with fully black-box LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4) that expose only API text output, a constraint that renders prior SOTA methods inoperable. However, limitations remain: a single-prompt detector can be bypassed by adversarial rephrasing (though multi-prompt training mitigates this), performance degrades on very short inputs, and the human quality study was small (3 annotators). Open questions include whether the invariance gap narrows as generator and detector LLMs converge in capability, and whether the approach transfers to non-English or multimodal generation settings.
