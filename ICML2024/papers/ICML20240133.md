## Problem

Most algorithmic fairness measures are defined for binary classifiers at a fixed threshold, yet continuous risk scores (e.g., COMPAS, credit scores) are typically consumed without a single decision cutoff, requiring disparity assessment across the entire score range. Existing ROC-based fairness metrics for scores can miss significant group disparities, and no standardized, transformation-invariant framework existed to quantify and compare biases across all three parity concepts (independence, separation, sufficiency) for continuous scores.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a joint sample of (S, A, Y) where S is a bounded continuous risk score, A is a binary protected attribute defining two groups, and Y is a binary outcome label. It produces disparity values in [0,1] for each parity concept in two variants — uniformly-weighted (bias^U) and standardized (bias^S) — along with a positive/negative decomposition indicating which group is predominantly disadvantaged. The standardized variant is invariant to monotonic transformations of S, enabling direct comparison of scores with different ranges or representations (e.g., logit vs. probability from the same logistic model).

## Method

The core construction integrates the absolute pointwise classifier bias (rate difference in TPR, FPR, or calibration gap) over all possible decision thresholds, weighted either uniformly over the score range (bias^U) or by the score density f(s) (bias^S). The paper proves that for independence and separation, bias^U equals the normalized Wasserstein-1 distance between groupwise score distributions, while bias^S equals the Wasserstein-1 distance between quantile-transformed distributions, yielding invariance to monotonic score reparameterizations. For calibration, bias^U is shown to equal the two-sample l1-calibration error. A key theoretical result (Theorem 4.10) establishes that the sum of the EO and PE separation biases upper-bounds both the intra-group ROC bias and the cross-ROC bias up to a dataset-specific constant, proving that the proposed Wasserstein measures impose a strictly stronger fairness objective than ROC-based alternatives — zero separation bias implies zero ROC bias, but not conversely.

## Results

On the COMPAS dataset, the proposed standardized separation biases reveal significant disparities (bias_EO^S = 0.161, bias_PE^S = 0.154, both p < 0.01) while the ROC-based intra-group fairness measure reports only a negligible, non-significant bias (bias_ROC = 0.016, p = 0.31), demonstrating that ROC-based measures miss substantial group disparities. On the UCI Adult dataset, the XGBoost model achieves the lowest separation biases (bias_EO^S = 0.057, bias_PE^S = 0.143) compared to logistic regression with the protected attribute (0.107, 0.164) and without it (0.069, 0.121). Notably, bias_ROC on Adult actually favors females (98% positive component), while the Wasserstein-based separation biases clearly show females are disadvantaged at every threshold, highlighting a directional disagreement between the two families of measures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its proposed Wasserstein-based standardized disparity measures against two ROC-based baselines: the intra-group ROC bias (bias_ROC, from Vogel et al. 2021 / Beutel et al. 2019) and the cross-ROC bias (bias_xROC, from Kallus & Zhou 2019). The central finding is that the proposed separation biases (bias_EO^S, bias_PE^S) are strictly stronger: Theorem 4.10 proves that the sum of EO and PE biases upper-bounds both ROC biases plus within-class Gini coefficients, and Corollary 4.11 shows zero separation bias implies zero ROC bias (but not vice versa). Empirically, on COMPAS the ROC bias is only 0.016 (p = 0.31, non-significant) while the EO bias is 0.161 (p < 0.01), meaning the ROC measure completely misses a large, statistically significant disparity. On the Adult dataset, bias_ROC actually reports a 98% positive component (favoring females), while bias_EO^S and bias_PE^S show 100% negative components (females disadvantaged at every threshold), revealing that the ROC-based measure gives the wrong directional signal. The cross-ROC bias (bias_xROC) is consistently higher than bias_ROC and aligns in direction with the proposed measures, but the paper shows it is still a weaker objective than the full separation bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Wasserstein-distance-based fairness framework of Jiang et al. (2020), which was limited to independence (demographic parity) bias, to all three classical parity concepts—equal opportunity, predictive equality, and independence—for continuous risk scores. It also connects the calibration disparity measure to the l1-calibration error of Kumar et al. (2019), and positions itself against the broader family of ROC-based fairness metrics (Kallus & Zhou, 2019; Vogel et al., 2021; Beutel et al., 2019) by proving a formal decomposition that shows the proposed Wasserstein measures subsume and strictly dominate those ROC-based quantities. The key methodological departure is the quantile-transformed (standardized) variant, which is invariant to monotonic score transformations—a property absent from prior Wasserstein fairness work and critical for preventing bias masking via score reparameterization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified, interpretable, and provably stronger framework for quantifying group disparities in continuous scores, enabling direct comparison across models, populations, and time points even when score distributions shift. A practically important open question is whether the l1-based measures should be replaced by lp norms (p>1) for bias mitigation, since the Wasserstein interpretation is lost in that case. The authors also flag the connection to causal fairness criteria as out of scope, leaving open how these observational disparity measures should inform causal follow-up analyses once significant disparities are detected. The finding that debiasing (removing a protected attribute) can shift the direction of bias between groups highlights the need for multi-metric monitoring rather than single-metric optimization.
