## Problem

Existing black-box fingerprinting methods for DNN tampering detection (SSF, PublicCheck) lack a theoretical connection between fingerprint sensitivity and the geometric structure of decision boundaries, and none exploit boundaries where more than two categories intersect. This paper asks whether positioning fingerprint samples at K-intersecting boundaries (K > 2) can systematically increase detection sensitivity, and how to efficiently locate such boundaries.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242236/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For fingerprint generation, the method takes a normal RGB image (32×32 for CIFAR-10, 224×224 for ImageNet) and a subset of K categories, with white-box access to the target model, and produces a perturbed fingerprint sample constrained within an L_p ball (ε-bound) so it remains visually indistinguishable from the source. For verification, the method operates in a black-box setting: the deployed model is queried with fingerprint samples and only top-1 class labels are returned; a mismatch between the returned label and the ground-truth label (the target model's prediction) flags tampering. No confidence scores or full prediction vectors are required.

## Method

IBSF's core insight is that model tampering shifts local decision boundaries, and under an isotropic shift assumption, the expected prediction divergence (measured by cross-entropy) of a sample equals its Shannon entropy — which increases monotonically with the number K of equally-probable categories at the boundary (Propositions 3.4–3.5). To exploit this, IBSF maximizes a partial Shannon entropy loss over a selected subset of K categories (Eq. 11), solved via projected gradient ascent with an L_p norm constraint (Eq. 13), pushing the sample toward the K-intersecting boundary where those K categories have equal probability and all others near zero. The method is white-box for generation (gradient access to the target model) but black-box for verification (top-1 labels only), and the diversity of source samples and varying K values provides robustness against adaptive attacks where an adversary collects and exploits leaked fingerprint samples.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, IBSF's detection rate for targeted attacks with a single fingerprint sample increases from 35.7% at K=2 to 65.2% at K=10, nearly doubling. With 5 fingerprint samples and sufficiently large K (>8 for CIFAR-10/GTSRB, >6 for ImageNet), IBSF achieves 100% detection across all six tampering types, while SSF and PublicCheck occasionally fail. Computationally, IBSF generates a fingerprint sample in 0.17 s / 0.07 GB (ResNet-20, CIFAR-10) versus 1.32 s / 0.81 GB for SSF and 0.69 s / 0.89 GB for PublicCheck, and scales to 8.41 s / 3.41 GB for ViT-L-32 on ImageNet.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SSF, IBSF at K=2 is slightly worse for Trojan attacks (3.7% lower on CIFAR-10, 2.1% lower on GTSRB) because SSF is more sensitive to last-FC-layer modifications that Trojan attacks exploit; however, at K=10 IBSF surpasses SSF by up to 16.7% on CIFAR-10 and 18.5% on GTSRB. Against PublicCheck, IBSF at K=2 is comparable due to a shared two-category boundary mechanism, but at K=10 IBSF achieves 1.32×, 0.89×, and 0.47× higher detection rates than PublicCheck for targeted attacks on CIFAR-10, GTSRB, and ImageNet respectively. With 5 fingerprint samples, IBSF reaches 100% detection across all tampering types at K>8 (CIFAR-10/GTSRB) or K>6 (ImageNet), whereas SSF and PublicCheck still miss some targeted and degradation attacks. In terms of computational cost, IBSF is 7.8× faster than SSF and 4.1× faster than PublicCheck for DenseNet-121 on CIFAR-10, and 23× faster than SSF for ViT-L-32 on ImageNet.

## Relation to Prior Work

IBSF synthesizes and generalizes ideas from three prior fingerprinting lines: SSF's white-box sensitivity maximization, PublicCheck's black-box decision-boundary positioning (limited to 2-category boundaries), and AID/DBFW's uniform-probability objective at boundaries. Its key conceptual departure is a formal theoretical analysis showing that expected tampering sensitivity scales with the number K of intersecting categories under an isotropic local boundary-shift assumption, which no prior method had established. The partial Shannon entropy loss function is a more targeted and efficient alternative to SSF's neuron-coverage objective and AID/DBFW's MSE-based uniformity loss, and the method generalizes PublicCheck's 2-category boundary search to arbitrary K-category boundaries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical grounding (Propositions 3.4–3.5) gives practitioners a principled knob—subset cardinality K—to trade off detection sensitivity against the difficulty of finding K-intersecting boundaries, which prior methods lacked. The robustness to adaptive attacks (validation set retains ~90% detection even when the tamper set is fully evaded) is practically important for public verification scenarios where some fingerprint samples may leak. Open questions include whether the isotropic tampering assumption holds for structured attacks (e.g., targeted fine-tuning that shifts boundaries anisotropically), how the method scales to very large K on high-dimensional datasets, and whether white-box generation is truly unnecessary to avoid in all deployment scenarios as the authors argue.
