## Problem

Existing graph diffusion models face two fundamental limitations: discrete diffusion over adjacency matrices incurs high computational complexity and loses topological structure, while continuous latent diffusion in Euclidean space treats the inherent anisotropy of graph embeddings as noise, destroying topological information during denoising. No prior work has addressed the non-Euclidean anisotropy of graph structures from a geometric perspective within a latent diffusion framework.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240563/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph G=(X, A) with node feature matrix X and adjacency matrix A, embeds it into a low-dimensional hyperbolic space via a 2-layer HGCN encoder, and performs diffusion on node-level tangent-plane coordinates rather than on the adjacency matrix directly. Generation produces a reconstructed graph via a Fermi-Dirac decoder that computes pairwise connection probabilities from hyperbolic distances, and the same hyperbolic embeddings support node classification. The diffusion scale is O(s·m·d) rather than O(s·m²·d), making it substantially more memory-efficient than adjacency-matrix diffusion baselines.

## Method

HypDiff operates in two stages: first, a hyperbolic autoencoder (HGCN encoder + Fermi-Dirac decoder) learns node embeddings in the Poincaré ball; second, a geometrically constrained latent diffusion process is trained on these embeddings. The key mechanism divides the hyperbolic space into k sectors via h-Kmeans clustering, projects each node onto its cluster center's tangent plane, and applies a forward diffusion that combines three terms: standard Gaussian noise, a radial popularity term δ·tanh(√c·λ_o^c·t/T₀)·x₀ that preserves hyperbolic growth structure, and angular-constrained noise z = sgn(logmap_o^c(h_μi))·ε that restricts perturbation to the geodesic direction toward the north pole. This design ensures the terminal distribution is an approximate folded normal N_f(δx₀, I) rather than pure isotropic noise, preserving anisotropic structural priors. A UNET-based denoising network is trained with the standard DDPM L2 loss, and sampling uses a direction transformation matrix to uniformly denoise in a single tangent plane for efficiency.

## Results

On node classification, HypDiff achieves the best average rank (1.1) across all five datasets, with standout scores of 70.5% Micro-F1 on SBM, 82.4% on Cora, and 85.7% on Polblogs. For graph generation (the primary task), HypDiff attains the lowest MMD distance on most datasets: 0.216/0.021/0.004 (Degree/Cluster/Spectre) on BA-G, 0.016/0.023/0.009 on COLLAB, and 0.002/0.010/0.028 on Community. HypDiff also demonstrates superior efficiency, using significantly less GPU memory (e.g., 2246 MB vs. 3475–9012 MB for baselines) during generation on IMDB-B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HypDiff outperforms all ten baselines on the majority of graph generation metrics. Against the strongest discrete diffusion baselines, HypDiff beats DiGress on BA-G (0.216 vs. 0.654 Degree MMD) and COLLAB (0.016 vs. 0.022 Degree MMD), and surpasses EDGE on BA-G (0.216 vs. 0.282) and PROTEINS (0.004 vs. 0.523 Cluster MMD). On node classification, HypDiff dominates: it achieves 70.5% Micro-F1 on SBM versus 57.9% for the best hyperbolic baseline P-VAE and 39.9% for ANE, and 85.7% on Polblogs versus 83.6% for Hype-ANE. GraphRNN is competitive on the small Community dataset (0.002 Degree MMD, tied with HypDiff) but degrades sharply on scale-free BA-G (0.122 vs. 0.216) and real-world MUTAG (0.537 vs. 0.048). GDSS performs well on MUTAG Spectre (0.003) but fails on BA-G (0.978 Degree MMD) and PROTEINS (1.463 Degree MMD), while GraphGDP shows inconsistent results, excelling on Ego (0.059 Degree) but struggling on Grid (0.872 Degree). The Euclidean baselines (VGAE, GraphGAN) are consistently outperformed by large margins across all datasets, confirming the advantage of the hyperbolic geometric formulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

HypDiff sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) discrete graph diffusion models (DiGress, GDSS, EDGE) that operate directly on adjacency matrices but suffer from high computational complexity; (2) continuous latent diffusion models (GeoDiff, Xu et al. 2023) that diffuse in Euclidean latent spaces but treat the anisotropic structure of graph embeddings as noise; and (3) hyperbolic graph learning (P-VAE, HGCN, Hype-ANE) that captures hierarchical structure but has not been combined with diffusion. The paper's key departure is formulating the diffusion process in hyperbolic latent space with geometric constraints (radial popularity and angular similarity) inherited from the Popularity-Similarity Optimization model, while borrowing the directional diffusion framework of Yang et al. (2023) for the angular constraint mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental limitation of latent diffusion for graphs: isotropic noise in Euclidean space erases the anisotropic structural priors that encode community, hierarchy, and scale-free properties. By grounding the diffusion in hyperbolic geometry, HypDiff provides a principled way to preserve these properties while maintaining the computational efficiency of latent-space diffusion (O(smd) vs. O(sm²d) for discrete methods). However, the approach has notable limitations: it struggles with regular Euclidean structures (Grid), requires a pre-clustering step whose parameter k is dataset-sensitive, and the radial constraint δ exhibits non-monotonic sensitivity. Open questions include scalability to very large graphs, whether the hyperbolic assumption generalizes to all graph topologies, and how the method extends to attributed or temporal graphs.
