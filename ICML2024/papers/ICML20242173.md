## Problem

Existing distributional RL methods require reward information at training time to predict return distributions, precluding zero-shot evaluation of novel reward functions. While the successor representation enables zero-shot policy evaluation for expected returns, no analogous reward-agnostic object existed for the full return distribution. This paper introduces the distributional successor measure (DSM) to close this gap, enabling zero-shot distributional policy evaluation and risk-sensitive policy ranking on unseen tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242173/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes state-action trajectories generated by a fixed policy π in a continuous-state MDP, with no reward signal required. At evaluation time, any deterministic reward function r: X → R can be plugged in to produce the full return distribution for each state. The learned object is a distribution over probability distributions on the state space (a doubly stochastic object), represented algorithmically as an equally-weighted mixture of m generative models (δ-model atoms), each of which is a GAN-like network mapping noise to state samples.

## Method

The core insight is that the random discounted state-occupancy measure M^π satisfies a reward-agnostic distributional Bellman equation, and its law (the DSM) maps any reward function to the corresponding return distribution in distribution. The δ-model approximates the DSM using an equally-weighted particle representation where each atom is a generative model over state space, trained via a two-level maximum mean discrepancy loss: a state-kernel MMD compares samples from generative models, and a model-kernel MMD (built from the state MMD via an inverse multiquadric RBF) compares the collections of model atoms against their n-step bootstrapped Bellman targets. Training uses an adversarial state kernel parameterized by an invertible neural network (iResNet) to handle non-stationarity, an adaptive model-kernel bandwidth via the median heuristic, and n-step bootstrapping (n=5) to reduce target variance. The distributional Bellman operator is proven contractive under a Wasserstein metric on P(P(X)), guaranteeing convergence of value iteration.

## Results

On both Windy Gridworld and Pendulum, the δ-model (DSM) substantially outperforms all baselines on Cramér distance between predicted and Monte Carlo return distributions (Figure 3b). The learned transition kernel rollout baseline suffers from compounding model error over long trajectories, while the γ-model ensemble achieves only quality comparable to a Dirac mass at the true mean return. For risk-sensitive policy selection (Figure 4b), the DSM is the only method that correctly ranks two policies by both mean return and 0.4-CVaR across held-out reward functions—a feat the authors note no other existing method can accomplish.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the δ-model against three baselines: (1) an ensemble of γ-models (Janner et al., 2020) trained independently rather than coupled through the model MMD loss, (2) return distributions estimated by rolling out trajectories from a learned transition kernel p^π, and (3) a Dirac mass at the Monte Carlo expected return. The δ-model wins on Cramér distance against all three: the rollout baseline degrades due to accumulation of model error over long horizons, the γ-model ensemble captures only epistemic uncertainty and achieves quality similar to the mean-only Dirac baseline, while the δ-model additionally captures aleatoric uncertainty from stochastic dynamics and policy randomness. For the risk-sensitive policy ranking task (Figure 4b), the δ-model correctly identifies the superior policy under both mean and 0.4-CVaR criteria for two held-out reward functions, whereas any SR-based method can only rank by mean return and existing distributional RL methods cannot evaluate held-out reward functions at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the successor representation (Dayan, 1993) and its continuous-space generalization (γ-models; Janner et al., 2020) to the distributional setting by defining a distribution over state-occupancy measures rather than a single measure. The key conceptual move is 'removing the expectation' from the successor measure to obtain a random occupancy measure, whose distribution (the distributional SM) is reward-agnostic and can be combined with any reward function to recover the full return distribution. Algorithmically, the δ-model couples an ensemble of generative models (analogous to γ-models) through a two-level MMD loss, and borrows the adversarial kernel framework of MMD-GAN (Li et al., 2017) but instantiates it with an invertible neural network (iResNet) to enforce injectivity—a first for kernel learning in this context. The paper departs from standard distributional RL (categorical/quantile) by eliminating the need for rewards during training, and from multivariate distributional RL by operating over the infinite-dimensional space of state distributions rather than finite-dimensional features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The distributional SM enables zero-shot distributional policy evaluation and risk-sensitive policy ranking on unseen reward functions without any further learning—a capability the authors argue is not achievable by any existing method. The practical techniques (n-step bootstrapping for generative occupancy models, adversarial kernels with iResNet, adaptive bandwidth selection) are independently valuable for training geometric-horizon models more broadly. However, empirical validation is limited to two low-dimensional continuous environments (Windy Gridworld, Pendulum), and the deterministic-reward assumption restricts applicability to environments with stochastic rewards. Open questions include scaling to high-dimensional state spaces (e.g., image-based), integrating the δ-model with policy optimization, and extending to stochastic reward settings where the state-to-occupancy mapping is no longer injective.
