## Problem

Previous graph-based planners for goal-conditioned RL construct subgoal graphs exclusively from achieved states in the replay buffer, completely discarding unattained subgoals (both failed and unexplored ones). This oversight causes the planner to repeatedly waste samples attempting impossible or unexplored subgoals rather than exploring novel regions, as the graph contains no record of which subgoals have been tried and failed. The paper asks: can a planner systematically manage both achieved and unattained subgoals to enable efficient, breadth-first exploration of the goal space?

## Input / Output

The method takes the agent's current state s and a fixed target goal g in a K-dimensional continuous goal space (K=2 for AntMaze, K=3 for Reacher3D) as input. It outputs a discrete subgoal path P = (φ(s), v₁, v₂, …, vₙ, g) computed via Dijkstra's algorithm on the grid graph, which the low-level policy π(·|·, v_p) then follows waypoint by waypoint. The grid is initialized over the entire feasible goal space with a uniform interval δ₀, and the path is recomputed whenever a waypoint is reached or a failure threshold is exceeded, making the output a dynamically updated sequence rather than a single static plan.

## Method

BEAG replaces replay-buffer-based graph construction with a grid graph that uniformly covers the entire goal space, including subgoals inside walls or otherwise unreachable regions. An extended distance function d̃(u,v) estimates the expected time between any two grid nodes (not just visited states) via a goal-conditioned value function, enabling edge weighting independent of the replay buffer. Unattainable subgoals are identified through two thresholds: a failure condition τ_t (timesteps without reaching the subgoal) and a failure count τ_n (number of attempts before marking the node as permanently unattainable), after which edges to/from that node are set to infinite weight. When the planner cannot find a valid path, adaptive grid refinement locally subdivides the interval around a selected unattainable subgoal (halving δ each time), adding new nodes via a Cartesian product of 5-point sets per dimension. The breadth-first exploration strategy prioritizes probing subgoals on the coarsest available grid level before refining, ensuring broad coverage before dense local search, and selects among unattainable subgoals in a round-robin fashion to avoid repeated failures at the same location.

## Results

The paper reports success rate (fraction of 10 trials achieving the target goal) across 8 MuJoCo environments under a fixed initial state and goal setting. In all complex environments (U-maze, S-maze, π-maze, complex-maze, Reacher3D-wall, bottleneck-maze), BEAG reaches near 100% success rate while all four baselines (HIRO, HIGL, DHRL, PIG) remain near 0%. In the two easier environments (U-maze-easy, U-maze-moderate), baselines achieve comparable success rates to BEAG. Under the random goal setting, BEAG still outperforms baselines in most environments, though the gap narrows because randomly sampled goals implicitly reveal feasible regions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BEAG decisively outperforms all four baselines in the fixed-goal setting on every complex environment: HIRO (vanilla 2-level HRL without graphs), HIGL (replay-buffer graph with coverage/novelty landmarks), DHRL (replay-buffer graph via farthest-point sampling), and PIG (FPS graph with imitation learning) all fail to reach the target goal (≈0% success) in U-maze, S-maze, π-maze, complex-maze, Reacher3D-wall, and bottleneck-maze, whereas BEAG achieves near-perfect success. The paper attributes the baselines' failure to their reliance on replay-buffer-only graphs that omit unattained subgoals, causing the planner to repeatedly waste attempts on impossible subgoals (e.g., over walls). In the two easy environments (U-maze-easy, U-maze-moderate), all methods converge to comparable success rates, indicating the gap is driven by exploration difficulty rather than policy capacity. In the random-goal setting, DHRL shows a notable performance boost (since sampled goals implicitly reveal feasible regions), yet BEAG still matches or exceeds it in most environments while requiring no such external exploration signal. The bottleneck-maze result further highlights BEAG's adaptive grid refinement: with identical hyperparameters (δ₀=4, τ_t=200, τ_n=3) across all AntMaze tasks, BEAG succeeds where the uniform grid alone would be insufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the graph-based RL line of work (SoRB, DHRL, HIGL, PIG) that constructs subgoal graphs from replay buffers and applies shortest-path algorithms for hierarchical planning. The key departure is that all prior methods discard unattained subgoals, building graphs only from achieved states; BEAG instead maintains a grid graph covering the entire goal space, including unattained and impossible subgoals, and introduces breadth-first exploration with local adaptive grid refinement (analogous to adaptive mesh refinement in PDE solvers). The distance function and value-based edge weighting are inherited from DHRL, while the structured exploration principle connects to broader work on curiosity-driven and semantic exploration, though BEAG is the first to apply breadth-first goal-space exploration specifically within graph-based RL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that under a more realistic fixed-goal training protocol (as opposed to the random-goal settings used by all prior graph-based RL methods), only BEAG succeeds in complex environments, exposing a significant blind spot in how these methods handle exploration. The breadth-first exploration principle—avoiding repeated failures on impossible subgoals by systematically probing broadly before refining locally—could generalize beyond goal-conditioned RL to other exploration-heavy settings. Key limitations include exponential node growth in high-dimensional goal spaces and the reliance on fixed hyperparameters (τ_t, τ_n) for failure detection, which is fragile in stochastic environments; future work on probabilistic failure modeling and more sophisticated refinement heuristics is needed.
