## Problem

Decentralized learning algorithms like D-GD and gossip averaging are widely believed to be more privacy-preserving than centralized federated learning because non-neighbor contributions are iteratively mixed before reaching any single node. This paper challenges that belief by showing that honest-but-curious nodes can reconstruct the private data of non-neighboring, sometimes distant, nodes — a capability no prior attack demonstrated, as earlier work (Pasquini et al., 2023; Dekker et al., 2023) was limited to direct neighbors only.

## Input / Output

The attacker requires only the network graph, the doubly stochastic gossip matrix W, its own private value, and the sequence of update messages received from its neighbors over T iterations; no access to other nodes' data or parameters is needed. For gossip averaging, the output is an exact reconstruction of all reconstructible nodes' private values (identified via the RREF of the knowledge matrix, which depends solely on W and the graph and can be computed before the algorithm runs). For D-GD, the output is a set of reconstructed gradients obtained via Generalized Least Squares, which are then passed to a black-box gradient inversion attack to recover individual data points (e.g., CIFAR-10 images or MNIST digits).

## Method

The core insight is that each message θ_v^t received by an attacker from neighbor v is a linear equation in the n private values, with coefficients given by the t-th power of the gossip matrix W. For gossip averaging, the attacker stacks all received messages into an observation vector Y_T and constructs a knowledge matrix K_T whose rows are the corresponding rows of W^t (Algorithm 1); solving K_T X = Y_T via RREF yields exact private values for all reconstructible nodes and linear constraints among the rest. For D-GD, three modifications handle the evolving gradients: (i) a noise-signal decomposition g_v^t = g_v + N_v^t reduces the unknowns to a fixed per-node gradient, (ii) the knowledge matrix rows become (Σ_{j=0}^t W_{T,T}^j)[v,:] to account for cumulative gradient contributions (Algorithm 2), and (iii) the attacker's own contributions are subtracted via a recursive correction (Algorithm 3). The resulting system is solved by Generalized Least Squares with a covariance matrix capturing cross-node noise correlations, and the reconstructed gradients are fed to existing gradient inversion methods (Zhu et al., 2019; Geiping et al., 2020) to recover data points.

## Results

On Erdős-Rényi graphs, a single attacker node typically reconstructs a large fraction of nodes well beyond its direct neighborhood, with the fraction increasing with graph connectivity and number of attackers (Figure 2). On a line graph of 31 nodes with the attacker at an extremity, the D-GD attack achieves informative reconstruction (PSNR > 10) up to distance 28 on CIFAR-10 (logistic regression) and up to distance 26 on MNIST (convnet + gradient inversion). On the 15-node Florentine graph, most interior nodes reconstruct a large proportion of other nodes with good visual accuracy. Degree centrality of the attacker correlates most strongly (Spearman ρ = 0.94) with the proportion of reconstructible nodes on both synthetic and real-world graphs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an attack paper without a traditional head-to-head quantitative comparison against competing attack methods. The key distinction from prior work is scope: Pasquini et al. (2023) and Dekker et al. (2023) can only reconstruct data of direct neighbors, whereas the proposed attack reconstructs data of non-neighboring (and sometimes distant) nodes. The paper does not report a direct numerical comparison against these prior attacks on a shared benchmark, but qualitatively demonstrates that the knowledge-matrix + RREF approach extends reconstruction far beyond the immediate neighborhood. For the gradient inversion step in D-GD, the paper uses Zhu et al. (2019) and Geiping et al. (2020) as black-box tools rather than comparing against them; the core contribution is the gradient reconstruction from gossip observations, for which no prior baseline exists in the decentralized setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on privacy attacks in decentralized learning, where prior efforts (Pasquini et al., 2023; Dekker et al., 2023) were limited to reconstructing data of direct neighbors only. The key methodological departure is the construction of a knowledge matrix whose RREF decomposition reveals which non-neighboring nodes are reconstructible, a technique borrowed from linear algebra and applied here to the structure of gossip protocols. For D-GD, the attack builds on the gossip-averaging reconstruction by introducing a noise-signal gradient decomposition and a Generalized Least Squares estimator, then delegates the final data recovery to existing gradient inversion attacks (Zhu et al., 2019; Geiping et al., 2020) as a black box.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results challenge the widely held belief that decentralization inherently protects data of distant nodes, showing that even a single honest-but-curious attacker can reconstruct data from nodes many hops away using only legitimate protocol observations. This implies that decentralized learning systems must combine peer-to-peer communication with explicit privacy mechanisms (e.g., differential privacy) rather than relying on the mixing effect of gossip. Open questions include a full structural characterization of which nodes are reconstructible given a graph topology, optimizing graph design to minimize leakage, and formally quantifying the trade-off between DP noise levels and attack success rate.
