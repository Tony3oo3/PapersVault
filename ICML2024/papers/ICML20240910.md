## Problem

Evidential deep learning (EDL) methods are widely deployed for epistemic uncertainty quantification in classification, regression, and count data, yet it remains unclear whether the resulting uncertainty measures carry any quantitative meaning beyond relative ranking. Prior critiques identified convergence failures and improper scoring rules in isolation, but no unified framework existed to assess faithfulness across the full range of exponential-family-based EDL formulations. This paper asks: what would a faithful representation of epistemic uncertainty look like, and do existing EDL methods actually achieve it?

## Input / Output

The method takes a feature vector x and a training set of (x, y) pairs, and outputs the parameters m of a second-order distribution over first-order model parameters θ (Dirichlet for classification, Normal-Inverse-Gamma for regression, Gamma for counts). From these parameters, various epistemic uncertainty scalars—pseudo-counts, mutual information, variance, or entropy—can be extracted. In the experimental validation, inputs are 1-D synthetic features (x ∈ [0, 0.5] for classification, x ∈ [−4, 4] for regression), and the reference distribution is constructed by resampling the training set 100 times and collecting the resulting first-order minimizers.

## Method

The paper introduces a reference second-order distribution q_N(θ|x), defined as the distribution over first-order risk minimizers induced by resampling the training data, and uses it as a ground-truth benchmark for epistemic uncertainty. Three theorems then show EDL fails this benchmark: (i) for unregularized inner loss minimization, the predictive distribution is non-injective for Dirichlet and NIG hypothesis spaces, making the second-order parameters unidentifiable; (ii) for unregularized outer loss minimization, Dirac delta distributions over θ are valid minimizers, causing epistemic uncertainty to collapse to zero; (iii) for regularized problems, the KL-based regularizer acts as a fixed 'uncertainty budget' that constrains total epistemic uncertainty across training points to a level determined by λ, rather than adapting to the data. Empirical experiments on synthetic binary classification and regression tasks confirm these findings, showing large Wasserstein-1 distances between EDL predictions and the reference distribution, non-convergence of outer-loss parameters, and high run-to-run variance for inner-loss parameters.

## Results

The paper establishes three key theoretical results: (1) for unregularized inner loss minimization, the risk minimizer is non-identifiable for Dirichlet and NIG distributions (Theorem 3.2), meaning multiple second-order parameters yield the same predictive distribution; (2) for unregularized outer loss minimization, a Dirac delta distribution is always a minimizer (Theorem 3.3), causing epistemic uncertainty to vanish entirely; (3) even with KL-divergence regularization, the learned second-order distribution provably differs from the reference distribution for any data-generating distribution (Theorem 3.4). Experimentally, the empirical Wasserstein-1 distance between the reference and estimated second-order distributions remains high across all tested regularization strengths (λ ∈ {0, 0.001, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5}), and for outer loss minimization, the predicted Beta parameters α and β keep growing without converging even after 15,000 epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it provides a unified theoretical critique of the entire evidential deep learning (EDL) literature. It extends Bengs et al. (2022, NeurIPS) in two ways: generalizing their Dirichlet-only Dirac-delta result to all exponential family members (Dirichlet, NIG, Gamma) and considering universal approximators rather than restricted model classes. It subsumes Meinert et al. (2023, AAAI), which showed convergence issues only for deep evidential regression, by covering classification and count data as well. It also complements Bengs et al. (2023, ICML), which showed second-order losses are not proper scoring rules, by introducing the reference distribution as a concrete, operational criterion for faithfulness. The paper's key empirical finding is that no choice of regularization strength λ can make the learned second-order distribution match the reference distribution, and that the epistemic uncertainty measures produced by EDL are arbitrary and tightly bound to the regularization hyperparameter rather than reflecting true data-driven uncertainty.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends three prior critical analyses of evidential deep learning: Bengs et al. (2022) on convergence issues for Dirichlet-based classification, Meinert et al. (2023) on convergence issues for NIG-based regression, and Bengs et al. (2023) on the impropriety of second-order scoring rules. It generalizes these results to all exponential family members (Dirichlet, NIG, Gamma) covering classification, regression, and count data simultaneously, and introduces the formal notion of a reference second-order distribution as an objective benchmark for faithfulness. The exponential family framework and closed-form loss expressions from Charpentier et al. (2022) provide the technical substrate for the unified analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the epistemic uncertainty measures produced by EDL are not quantitatively faithful to the actual uncertainty of a first-order predictor trained on finite data, but are instead arbitrary functions of the regularization strength λ. This reframes the regularizer not as a principled uncertainty prior but as an 'uncertainty budget' constraint, undermining the frequentist interpretation that EDL proponents often claim. Open questions include whether the OOD scores from EDL can be reinterpreted as implicit density estimates in feature space (as the authors hypothesize), and whether any modification to the second-order loss formulation could recover faithfulness to the reference distribution without sacrificing the computational advantages over full Bayesian inference.
