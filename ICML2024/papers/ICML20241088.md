## Problem

Offline anomaly detection methods like Isolation Forest require repeated in-memory access to data and become outdated in streaming settings, while existing online alternatives rely on periodic retraining that introduces substantial processing overhead and delayed adaptation. There is a need for a truly online anomaly detection approach that operates under strict memory constraints, processes data in a single pass, and adapts continuously to the evolving data distribution without retraining cycles.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241088/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multivariate data points x_t ∈ R^d sequentially from an endless stream, storing only a sliding buffer of the ω most recent points in memory. For each incoming point, it produces a single anomaly score s_t ∈ [0,1] computed as 2^(−E(D)/c(ω,η)), where E(D) is the average leaf depth across the ensemble and c(ω,η) = log₂(ω/η) is a normalization factor. The single-pass, bounded-memory constraint means no data point can be revisited, and the time between sample acquisition and score assignment must be minimal.

## Method

ONLINE-IFOREST is an ensemble of τ ONLINE-ITREES, each a d-dimensional multi-resolution histogram that evolves through two complementary operations: a learning procedure that splits bins (increasing resolution) when a leaf's point count reaches the depth-dependent threshold η·2^k, and a forgetting procedure that merges child bins (decreasing resolution) when the oldest point in the sliding window is evicted. Splits are data-driven—randomly selecting a dimension and split value from the bin's current support, then sampling points uniformly within that support to initialize child bins—so resolution concentrates in densely populated regions rather than being uniformly allocated. The anomaly score follows the IFOR isolation principle: points falling at shallow depths in the histogram trees are deemed more anomalous, with the depth normalized by the expected tree depth for the given window size. This design yields O(τ√(ω/η) + ω) space complexity independent of stream length, and the exponential growth of the split threshold with depth keeps trees compact, making it the fastest method evaluated across all datasets.

## Results

Across 11 stationary datasets, ONLINE-IFOREST (oIFOR) achieves a median ROC AUC of 0.866, marginally above asdIFOR (0.863) and RRCF (0.832), while LODA and HST trail significantly at 0.541 and 0.739 respectively. On efficiency, oIFOR dominates with a median total execution time of 29.95 s, less than one-fifth of asdIFOR (142.57 s) and over 40× faster than RRCF (1274.45 s). The Nemenyi post-hoc test confirms oIFOR is statistically superior to all competitors on execution time, while asdIFOR retains a slight edge on AUC (mean rank 1.583 vs 2.167 for oIFOR). On the non-stationary INSECTS dataset, all methods degrade similarly after distribution shifts, with no single algorithm consistently maintaining performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against asdIFOR (iForestASD), oIFOR achieves a nearly identical median AUC (0.866 vs 0.863) but is roughly 4.8× faster in median execution time (29.95 s vs 142.57 s); the Nemenyi test nonetheless ranks asdIFOR slightly higher on AUC (mean rank 1.583 vs 2.167), indicating a marginal statistical edge for the retraining-based approach. Against RRCF, oIFOR improves median AUC by 3.4 points (0.866 vs 0.832) and is over 42× faster (29.95 s vs 1274.45 s), with the two methods deemed statistically equivalent on AUC. Against HST, oIFOR leads by 12.7 AUC points (0.866 vs 0.739) and is 10.8× faster; HST's catastrophic failure on Mulcross (AUC 0.011) stems from its fixed-depth, data-independent binning being ill-suited to dense anomaly clusters. Against LODA, oIFOR outperforms by 32.5 AUC points (0.866 vs 0.541) and is 8.5× faster, with LODA's fixed-resolution 1-D histograms proving inadequate for multi-modal distributions. Overall, oIFOR is the only method that is simultaneously competitive on detection quality and unambiguously fastest, making it the clear efficiency leader across all 11 datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

ONLINE-IFOREST directly extends the Isolation Forest (Liu et al., 2008) principle of isolation-based scoring to a streaming setting, but replaces the random tree structure with an ensemble of multi-resolution histograms that split and merge bins based on data density. It departs from prior online anomaly detection methods—iForestASD and HST, which rely on periodic retraining, and RRCF and LODA, which use fixed-resolution projections or complex tree modifications—by introducing a truly incremental learn-and-forget mechanism driven by a sliding window. The conceptual lineage is clear: the isolation score formula is inherited from IFOR, the multi-resolution histogram idea echoes HST's d-dimensional bins, but the data-dependent split/merge dynamics and the exponential height threshold (η·2^k) are novel design choices that yield compact, adaptive structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in streaming anomaly detection: existing online methods either incur heavy retraining overhead or use fixed-resolution models that cannot adapt to complex, multi-modal distributions. ONLINE-IFOREST's order-of-magnitude speed advantage over all competitors makes it a strong candidate for latency-sensitive applications (cybersecurity, fraud, fault detection), while its detection quality remains competitive with the best offline-retraining baselines. Open questions include whether the sliding window can be eliminated while retaining forgetting capability, how to automate the bin-split threshold η, and whether the method's 'few and different' anomaly assumption limits its applicability to clustered or high-density anomaly scenarios (as evidenced by HST's failure on Mulcross, a similar limitation likely applies). The non-stationary experiments reveal that no current online method robustly handles abrupt distribution shifts, pointing to a broader open problem in adaptive anomaly detection.
