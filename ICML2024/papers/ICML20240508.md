## Problem

Existing inverse problem solvers, particularly diffusion-based methods, allocate the same computational budget to every sample regardless of reconstruction difficulty. The paper identifies that difficulty varies naturally sample-by-sample due to signal structure, degradation severity, and their interaction, yet no prior method adapts compute allocation on a per-sample basis, leading to suboptimal performance and wasteful resource use.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240508/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a corrupted RGB image (256×256) and a known degradation operator (Gaussian blur, nonlinear motion blur, or random inpainting mask) as input, and produces a reconstructed clean image as output. Unusually, the number of reverse diffusion steps is not fixed but determined per-sample by a predicted degradation severity scalar, yielding variable inference cost (e.g., ~50–136 NFE vs. 500–1000 for baselines). The severity encoder outputs a pair (ẑ, σ̂): a latent-space reconstruction estimate and a scalar severity value that directly controls the sampling trajectory length.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage framework called Flash-Diffusion. First, a severity encoder—fine-tuned from a pre-trained LDM autoencoder encoder—simultaneously predicts the clean latent representation ẑ and the degradation severity σ̂ (modeled as the variance of prediction error in latent space), trained with a joint loss combining latent reconstruction, error variance prediction, and image-domain consistency. Second, SNR matching between the predicted severity and the diffusion schedule determines the optimal starting time index i_start for the reverse diffusion process, so that easy samples require fewer steps and hard samples more. A noise correction step mixes ẑ with i.i.d. Gaussian noise to suppress structured prediction errors before initiating diffusion. The framework acts as a plug-in wrapper around any latent diffusion solver (Latent-DPS, GML-DPS, PSLD, ReSample), achieving up to 10× speedup while improving reconstruction quality, especially under highly varying degradation severity.

## Results

On FFHQ with varying Gaussian blur, Flash(Latent-DPS) achieves 29.17 PSNR / 0.2240 LPIPS with only 100.3 NFE, compared to 23.69 PSNR / 0.3579 LPIPS at 1000 NFE for the non-adaptive baseline — a ~10× speedup with substantial quality gains. Flash(ReSample) reaches 29.07 PSNR / 0.2383 LPIPS at just 49.9 NFE (vs. 500 NFE for ReSample). On LSUN Bedrooms, Flash(Latent-DPS) improves PSNR from 22.42 to 28.05 (5.6 dB gain) while reducing NFE from 1000 to 101.6. Across all four degradation tasks and both datasets, adaptive solvers consistently outperform their non-adaptive counterparts on every metric (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, FID) while using 8–10× fewer reverse diffusion steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Flash-Diffusion consistently outperforms all four latent diffusion baselines (Latent-DPS, GML-DPS, PSLD, ReSample) on every metric across all degradation tasks, with the largest gains on highly varying-severity tasks (e.g., +5.5 dB PSNR over Latent-DPS on varying blur). Against the strongest baseline ReSample, Flash(ReSample) improves PSNR by 0.3 dB and LPIPS by 0.02 while reducing NFE from 500 to ~50 (10× speedup). Compared to pixel-space DPS, Flash methods achieve higher PSNR (e.g., 29.17 vs. 28.34 on varying blur) with 10× fewer function evaluations. Against the supervised SwinIR baseline, Flash methods trade a small PSNR/SSIM deficit (e.g., 29.17 vs. 30.69 PSNR) for substantially better perceptual quality (LPIPS 0.2240 vs. 0.2409; FID 55.57 vs. 87.61), consistent with the perception-distortion trade-off. Notably, Flash(Latent-DPS) often matches or exceeds Flash(ReSample) in perceptual quality, suggesting that the severity-encoder initialization compensates for the simpler diffusion dynamics of the baseline solver.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CCDF (Come-Closer-Diffuse-Faster) paradigm of initializing reverse diffusion from a good initial reconstruction rather than pure noise, by making the starting time sample-adaptive via a learned severity estimate in latent space. It builds directly on Latent Diffusion Models (Rombach et al., 2022) for the autoencoder backbone and latent-space diffusion process, and positions itself as a plug-in wrapper around any existing latent diffusion inverse problem solver (Latent-DPS, GML-DPS, PSLD, ReSample). The key conceptual departure from prior work is the introduction of severity encoding—a supervised encoder that simultaneously predicts a clean latent estimate and quantifies its own prediction error as a proxy for reconstruction difficulty—enabling per-sample compute allocation that no prior diffusion-based solver achieves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 8–10× reduction in function evaluations without quality loss is practically significant for deployment of diffusion-based restoration, and the wrapper design means the technique can be retrofitted onto any existing latent diffusion solver with minimal modification. However, the severity encoder must be fine-tuned per degradation type and breaks down under significant noise-level or forward-model mismatch, limiting its generality. Open questions include whether severity estimation can be made unsupervised or domain-agnostic, whether the approach extends to video or 3D reconstruction, and whether the observed insensitivity to the choice of baseline solver (Flash(Latent-DPS) ≈ Flash(ReSample)) suggests that early-stage diffusion dynamics are less important than previously assumed.
