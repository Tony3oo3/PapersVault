## Problem

Online deep RL agents that directly minimize the optimality cost in unbounded state spaces (e.g., queuing networks, traffic control) learn unstable policies that drift arbitrarily far from the desired state. The paper identifies two root causes: poor credit assignment for destabilizing actions under high stochasticity, and neural networks' inability to generalize across arbitrarily distant unbounded states. No prior method addresses both challenges simultaneously in the model-free, online, average-reward setting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an unbounded state vector (e.g., queue lengths or per-lane car counts) that is coordinate-wise transformed via a bijective, order-preserving function (symloge, symsqrt, or symsigmoid) before being fed to the policy and value networks, while the cost is always computed on the original untransformed state. The output is a discrete action selecting which queue to serve or which traffic phase to activate, and the learned policy is guaranteed to keep the system within a bounded L1 distance of the all-zero target state with high probability.

## Method

STOP augments average-reward PPO with two components: (1) a Lyapunov-based cost-shaping term ℓ(s_{t+1}) − ℓ(s_t) added to the optimality cost, where ℓ(s) = ‖s‖_p^p for a tunable exponent p, which explicitly penalizes actions that increase the system's 'energy' and thereby discourages destabilizing actions more strongly than the raw optimality cost alone; and (2) coordinate-wise state transformations that compress the unbounded state space to reduce the extreme generalization burden on the neural network. The paper proves (Proposition 5.2) that under mild conditions on the Lyapunov function's growth rate, the shaped objective preserves the optimal policy of the original objective, extending the potential-based shaping result of Ng et al. (1999) to the average-reward, unbounded state space setting. The choice of p is guided by MaxWeight queueing theory: moderate values (p=2–3) approximate the optimal value function well while keeping variance manageable.

## Results

Across six queuing and traffic control environments, vanilla average-reward PPO (optimizing the optimality cost directly) is unstable in all cases, with queue lengths diverging to infinity. STOP achieves bounded queue lengths (stability) in all environments except the most extreme very-high-load setting where all algorithms fail. In 4 out of 6 environments, STOP matches or outperforms MAXWEIGHT, a strong baseline that has access to transition dynamics. The Lyapunov-based cost shaping increases the normalized advantage estimate for destabilizing actions from 0.09±0.07 (no shaping) to 0.25±0.05 (quadratic shaping), a ~2.8× increase in discouragement signal.

**Table 1: Normalized advantage estimates for destabilizing actions (serving an empty queue) in the 2-queue setting, IQM over 20 trials. Higher advantage = stronger discouragement of bad actions.**

| Cost Function | Normalized Advantage (IQM) |
|---|---|
| c(s,a,s′) (no shaping) | 0.09 ± 0.07 |
| c(s,a,s′) + ℓ₁(s′) − ℓ₁(s) (linear) | 0.25 ± 0.08 |
| c(s,a,s′) + ℓ₂(s′) − ℓ₂(s) (quadratic) | 0.25 ± 0.05 |

Key qualitative findings: PPO is unstable in all 6 environments; STOP stabilizes in 5/6; STOP matches or beats MAXWEIGHT in 4/6 environments.*

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla average-reward PPO (Zhang & Ross, 2021), STOP wins decisively: PPO is unstable (diverging queue lengths) in all six tested environments, while STOP achieves bounded queue lengths in five of six. Against MAXWEIGHT (Tassiulas & Ephremides, 1990), a model-based algorithm with knowledge of arrival and service rates, STOP is competitive or superior in 4 out of 6 environments despite having no access to transition dynamics; in the remaining two environments (very high load), both methods struggle, with all algorithms becoming unstable. Against PPO with Training Wheels (Mao et al., 2019), which deploys a pre-trained stable policy when the RL agent violates a safety condition, STOP ultimately outperforms PPO-TW in later training phases because PPO-TW's off-policy data from the stable policy cannot be used to update the PPO policy, causing it to remain poor and repeatedly trigger the safety fallback. The paper notes that to the best of the authors' knowledge, no online RL algorithm in unbounded state spaces had previously outperformed MAXWEIGHT.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of average-reward RL (Zhang & Ross, 2021; Naik et al., 2021), Lyapunov-based control theory (Meyn & Tweedie, 2012; Kellett & Teel, 2003), and potential-based reward shaping (Ng et al., 1999). It departs from prior stability-in-RL work (Shah et al., 2020; Westenbroek et al., 2022) by addressing the model-free, deep RL, online, unbounded-state-space setting simultaneously, whereas prior results assumed tabular representations, known dynamics, deterministic systems, or discounted costs. The Lyapunov function selection is directly inspired by MaxWeight scheduling theory (Stolyar, 2004; Tassiulas & Ephremides, 1990), and the state transformations draw on prior work in RL (Kapturowski et al., 2019; Hafner et al., 2023), but the combination of both techniques for the continuing unbounded-state setting is new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a practical gap between the theoretical guarantee that optimal policies are stable and the empirical reality that deep RL agents minimizing the optimality cost drift unstably, which has implications for any online RL deployment in unbounded domains (networks, traffic, industrial control). The STOP framework provides a concrete recipe—Lyapunov cost shaping plus state compression—that makes stability achievable without model access, and in some cases outperforms MaxWeight which knows the transition dynamics. Key limitations include the need for domain knowledge to select the Lyapunov function, persistent instability under very high load, and restriction to on-policy methods. Open questions the authors highlight include learning appropriate Lyapunov functions from data, understanding why dense cost functions fail as learning signals beyond credit assignment, and extending the approach to off-policy algorithms.
