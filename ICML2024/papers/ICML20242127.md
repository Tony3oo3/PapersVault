## Problem

Simulated robot learning is bottlenecked by the enormous human effort required to construct each training environment: designing tasks, sourcing semantically appropriate 3D assets, generating plausible scene layouts, and crafting reward functions. While foundation models like GPT-4 encode rich common-sense knowledge about object affordances and everyday tasks, they lack understanding of physical dynamics, actuation, and contact interactions needed to produce executable robot policies. RoboGen addresses this gap by using LLMs only for what they do well—semantic reasoning, task proposal, and supervision design—while delegating physical skill acquisition to physics-grounded simulators.

## Input / Output

The system is seeded with a robot specification (e.g., Franka arm) and either an object sampled from a predefined pool (with its URDF articulation tree and per-link semantic annotations from PartNetMobility) or a set of example tasks from a list of 11 pre-defined tasks; for legged locomotion and soft-body tasks, only example-based seeding is used. Each query cycle produces a complete training package: a natural-language task description, a populated 3D simulation scene (assets retrieved from Objaverse or generated via Midjourney + Zero-1-to-3, with LLM-verified sizes and collision-free spatial configurations), a task decomposition into sub-tasks with per-sub-task algorithm assignments, and generated reward functions. The pipeline is designed to be queried repeatedly, yielding an unbounded stream of skill demonstrations across rigid/articulated manipulation, soft-body manipulation, and legged locomotion.

## Method

RoboGen implements a self-guided propose-generate-learn cycle with four stages, all orchestrated by GPT-4 (temperature 0.8–1.0 for task diversity, 0–0.3 for downstream stages). In Task Proposal, GPT-4 reasons over the object's articulation tree and semantic annotations to generate semantically meaningful tasks with required objects and relevant joints. In Scene Generation, GPT-4 identifies needed assets, which are retrieved from Objaverse via Sentence-BERT embedding search and verified by Gemini-Pro VLM; GPT-4 then assigns physically plausible sizes, initial joint configurations, and collision-free spatial placements (with a post-hoc collision-resolution step). In Training Supervision Generation, GPT-4 decomposes each task into sub-tasks and selects the optimal algorithm per sub-task from three options—SAC reinforcement learning (1M env steps, MLP [256,256,256]), gradient-based trajectory optimization via Adam (300 steps, Earth Mover's Distance cost for soft bodies), or BIT*-based motion planning with action primitives (grasp/approach via surface-normal-aligned gripper poses). Skill Learning executes sub-tasks sequentially, running each for N=8 iterations and chaining the highest-reward end state as the initial state for the next sub-task. The key design principle is modality-aware knowledge extraction: LLMs contribute only semantics, affordances, and common-sense reasoning, while all physical interaction learning is grounded in the differentiable Genesis simulator.

## Results

On task diversity (Table 1), RoboGen achieves the lowest Self-BLEU (0.284), lowest SentenceBERT embedding similarity (0.165), lowest ViT scene-image similarity (0.193), and lowest CLIP scene-image similarity (0.762) among all compared benchmarks, indicating superior semantic and visual diversity. Across 69 benchmarked tasks (50 articulated manipulation, 7 soft-body, 12 locomotion), RoboGen achieves an average skill learning success rate of 0.774. An ablation on 12 articulated-object tasks shows that restricting skill learning to RL alone causes success rates to drop drastically compared to the full pipeline that selects among RL, motion planning, and trajectory optimization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoboGen outperforms all five baselines on every diversity metric in Table 1. Compared to the closest human-crafted benchmarks (Behavior-100 and RLBench, both with ~100 tasks), RoboGen achieves lower Self-BLEU (0.284 vs. 0.299/0.317), lower SBERT similarity (0.165 vs. 0.210/0.200), and substantially lower ViT scene-image similarity (0.193 vs. 0.389/0.375), indicating both more semantically diverse task descriptions and more visually distinct generated scenes. Against the concurrent LLM-based method GenSim (Wang et al., 2023a), RoboGen shows a clear margin on all four metrics (e.g., Self-BLEU 0.284 vs. 0.378; ViT similarity 0.193 vs. 0.717), which the authors attribute to GenSim's restriction to table-top pick-and-place tasks with a small asset pool. ManiSkill2, despite having only 20 tasks, achieves a competitive SBERT score (0.194) but is far behind RoboGen on Self-BLEU (0.674 vs. 0.284) and ViT similarity (0.332 vs. 0.193). MetaWorld, with only 50 tasks, is the weakest baseline on most metrics, reflecting its narrower task scope.

## Relation to Prior Work

RoboGen builds on the simulation-based robot learning literature (RLBench, ManiSkill2, Meta-World, Behavior-100) and the concurrent line of LLM-for-robotics work (GenSim, Katara et al.), but departs from both in a key way: rather than using LLMs to directly write manipulation code or produce low-level actions, it restricts LLM queries to what they are genuinely good at—object semantics, affordances, common-sense task reasoning, and reward specification—while delegating all physics-grounded skill acquisition to dedicated algorithms (SAC, BIT* motion planning, gradient-based trajectory optimization). This 'generative simulation' paradigm also extends procedural-generation approaches (TAMP-based scene construction) by replacing hand-coded rules with LLM-driven task and scene proposals, and by integrating VLM-based asset verification (Gemini-Pro) and text-to-3D generation (Midjourney + Zero-1-to-3) for soft-body targets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a fully automated, endlessly queryable pipeline can produce 100+ diverse robotic skill demonstrations spanning rigid/articulated manipulation, soft-body manipulation, and legged locomotion with a 77.4% average learning success rate, matching or surpassing human-crafted benchmarks in task and scene diversity. Key open questions remain: large-scale verification of learned skills (currently human-evaluated), the sim-to-real gap for deployment, and the difficulty of generating correct rewards for continuous or oscillatory motions. The work positions LLMs as 'task designers' rather than 'policy generators,' a framing that may influence how the community integrates foundation models into embodied AI pipelines.
