## Problem

While universal approximation theory guarantees that sufficiently wide and deep ReLU networks can classify any dataset, existing results provide only impractical lower bounds (e.g., minimum depth 2, width max{d_x+1, d_y}) and offer no explicit construction tied to the dataset's geometry. The 'multiple manifold problem'—determining the optimal architecture for separating two disjoint topological spaces—remains largely unresolved, with no principled way to translate a dataset's polytope structure into concrete width and depth requirements.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a labeled binary dataset D = {x_i, y_i} in R^d (flattened images or point clouds) and produces (1) explicit upper and lower bounds on hidden-layer widths as functions of the dataset's polytope-basis cover, (2) a concrete three-layer ReLU architecture with specified widths, and (3) the polytope-basis cover itself—a small collection of convex polytopes (typically ≤2 per class, each with <30 faces) whose indicator-function difference encodes the class separation. Conversely, given a trained three-layer ReLU network, the algorithm outputs the underlying polytope-basis cover, enabling reverse-engineering of dataset geometry from network weights.

## Method

The core mechanism is the polytope-basis cover: a finite collection of convex polytopes {P_1,...,P_nP, Q_1,...,Q_nQ} where the sum of P-indicators minus Q-indicators is strictly positive on X+ and non-positive on X−. Theorem 3.4 shows that a three-layer ReLU network with first-layer width equal to the total number of polytope faces and second-layer width equal to the number of polytopes is a feasible classifier, with each first-layer neuron encoding one hyperplane face and each second-layer neuron encoding one polytope. For the converse direction, Theorem 3.7 combined with a 'compressing algorithm' (Algorithm 1) extracts the polytope-basis cover from a trained network by iteratively removing redundant neurons and scaling weights to shrink the decision boundary to a minimal convex polytope, leveraging the implicit bias of gradient descent (Du et al., 2018) to maintain the ICNN condition v_jk < 0. Bounds are further specialized to simplicial complexes (Theorem 3.5, via facet counts) and prismatic polytopes (Theorem 3.6, via Betti numbers), linking topological invariants directly to required network widths.

## Results

The paper's headline finding is that each class in MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR-10 can be separated by at most two convex polytopes with fewer than 30 faces (Table 1). For example, MNIST class {0} requires only a single polytope with 4 faces, while the most complex CIFAR-10 class ({0}) needs two polytopes with 29+3 faces. The derived feasible architectures are compact: MNIST (784→47→10→10), Fashion-MNIST (784→90→14→10), and CIFAR-10 (3072→178→12→10). In the noise experiment (Table 2), the number of faces for covering class {1} increases from 3 (0% noise) to ~64 (50% noise) on MNIST, confirming that polytope face count tracks geometric complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing architecture-design or classification methods; its contribution is theoretical (bounds on widths) and algorithmic (polytope-basis cover extraction). The closest prior comparisons are qualitative: Kantchelian et al. (2014) and Sivaprasad et al. (2021) observed that certain MNIST/CIFAR-10 classes are convexly separable, but did not determine the exact number of faces required. This paper extends those observations by computing the minimal face count per class (Table 1) and showing that the number of faces increases monotonically with label noise (Table 2), providing a quantitative geometric complexity metric. The paper also connects its sparse pruned architectures (Remark 4.1) to the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, noting that the explicitly constructed sparse networks correspond to 'winning tickets' with sign-preserving masks, consistent with Zhou et al. (2019). No head-to-head accuracy or efficiency comparisons against other architecture-search or pruning methods are provided.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multiple manifold problem (Buchanan et al., 2020) by providing explicit polytope-based constructions and bounds on network widths, rather than the basic lower bounds available from universal approximation theory (Hornik, 1991; Park et al., 2020). It leverages the polytope structure of ReLU networks studied in recent geometric analyses (Black et al., 2022; Grigsby & Lindsey, 2022; Berzins, 2023) and the convexity properties of Input Convex Neural Networks (Amos et al., 2017), but departs from prior work by working in both directions: constructing networks from dataset geometry and extracting dataset geometry from trained networks. The use of implicit bias from gradient descent (Du et al., 2018) to maintain weight sign constraints during training is a practical departure from prior ICNN approaches that enforced non-negativity through parameterization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first explicit, geometry-driven construction of neural network architectures that provably classify a given dataset, bridging a gap between universal approximation theory and practical architecture design. The finding that MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, and CIFAR10 classes require at most two convex polytopes with fewer than 30 faces offers a concrete, interpretable measure of dataset geometric complexity. Key limitations include the restriction to fully-connected ReLU networks (no CNNs), the unproven optimality of the polytope-basis cover found by the algorithm, and the fact that the suggested architectures (e.g., 784→47→10→10 for MNIST) are not competitive with modern deep networks. Open questions include whether the polytope-basis cover framework extends to convolutional architectures and whether the observed geometric simplicity persists in more complex datasets like ImageNet.
