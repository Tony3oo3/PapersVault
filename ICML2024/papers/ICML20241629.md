## Problem

It remains unclear whether the generalization advantage of overparameterized neural networks stems from the implicit bias of SGD or from an inherent simplicity bias in the architecture. Chiang et al. (2023) argued that architecture dominates and SGD's implicit bias is secondary, but this paper shows their conclusion rests on a suboptimal U[-1,1] initialization that artificially degrades SGD and on a weight-normalized loss that is not comparable across architectures of different widths.

## Input / Output

The study operates on binary classification subsets of MNIST (28×28 grayscale) and CIFAR-10 (32×32 RGB) in the low-sample regime (4–32 samples, extended to 128), using LeNet, MLP, and small ResNet architectures varied in width (1/6* to 1) and depth (1c-1f to 2c-3f). For each configuration, 2000 networks are generated via SGD (Kaiming uniform init, 60 epochs, batch size 2) or via Guess-and-Check (random sampling from a prior, keeping only zero-training-error solutions), and the outputs are test accuracy, Lipschitz-normalized loss (a proxy for geometric margin), and the estimated probability P_W(Train Error = 0) of a random network fitting the data.

## Method

The core method is a controlled comparison between SGD-optimized networks and randomly sampled G&C networks, both restricted to the zero-training-error set, while systematically varying width and depth to attribute changes to optimization vs. architecture. The paper first corrects two flaws in Chiang et al.: (1) replacing U[-1,1] with Kaiming initialization so SGD converges properly rather than stalling at large logits, and (2) replacing the product-of-Frobenius-norms normalization with a Lipschitz-normalized loss that is invariant to weight scaling and comparable across architectures. With these fixes, increasing width improves SGD's geometric margin and test accuracy while leaving G&C essentially unchanged, revealing a clear positive implicit bias of SGD. Conversely, increasing depth degrades both SGD and G&C similarly, indicating a negative architectural bias. The G&C probability P_W(Train Error=0) further confirms that the pool of fitting networks does not grow with width but shrinks with depth, ruling out a volume-based explanation for the width effect.

## Results

On MNIST (classes 0 vs 7, 16 training samples, LeNet), properly initialized SGD (Kaiming) achieves 96.3% test accuracy versus 82.8% for G&C (randomly sampled networks with zero training error), a gap of ~13.5 percentage points. Increasing network width improves SGD's test accuracy across all architectures (LeNet, MLP, ResNet) and datasets, while G&C's accuracy remains essentially flat, confirming a positive implicit bias of SGD. Conversely, increasing depth degrades both SGD and G&C performance, indicating a negative architectural bias. The probability P_W(Train Error=0) of finding a perfectly fitting network by random sampling stays constant with width but decreases with depth, showing that wider networks do not expand the volume of fitting solutions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is against the G&C (Guess and Check) random-sampling method from Chiang et al. (2023), which was used to argue that generalization is primarily an architectural effect rather than an SGD bias. The authors show that Chiang et al.'s conclusion was an artifact of using a U[-1,1] weight initialization that causes SGD to stop optimizing early (large logits → small gradients), making SGD appear comparable to G&C (83.6% vs 82.8%). With standard Kaiming initialization, SGD clearly outperforms G&C by ~13.5 percentage points on MNIST, and this gap persists across architectures (LeNet, MLP, ResNet) and datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10). For width overparameterization, SGD consistently wins: wider networks yield higher test accuracy for SGD but not for G&C, and the probability of random sampling a fitting network does not increase with width. For depth overparameterization, both methods degrade similarly, so the negative effect is attributed to architecture rather than optimization. The authors also demonstrate that initializing SGD with G&C-found networks and re-optimizing still improves test accuracy (from ~82.5% to ~95%), further confirming that SGD converges to a smaller, better-generalizing subset of the zero-training-error solution set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly revisits and corrects the methodology of Chiang et al. (2023), who proposed the volume hypothesis that generalization in overparameterized networks is primarily an architectural bias rather than an SGD bias. The authors adopt the same Guess-and-Check (G&C) random sampling framework but identify two critical flaws in the prior work: a suboptimal U[-1,1] initialization that artificially degrades SGD, and a weight-normalized loss (product of Frobenius norms) that is not comparable across architectures of different widths. They introduce a Lipschitz-normalized loss as a more principled alternative and use proper Kaiming initialization, fundamentally changing the comparison between SGD-optimized and randomly sampled networks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean empirical disentanglement showing that width overparameterization benefits SGD specifically (via implicit bias toward larger geometric margins) while depth overparameterization hurts both SGD and random networks equally (architectural bias). This clarifies a central debate in the field about whether the practical trend toward wider networks is an optimizer artifact or a genuine architectural necessity. However, the analysis is restricted to binary classification in the low-sample regime due to the exponential cost of G&C sampling, leaving open whether the findings extend to multi-class tasks, larger datasets, or modern architectures. The paper also raises the open question of whether novel optimizers could achieve the generalization benefits of width without actually increasing network size.
