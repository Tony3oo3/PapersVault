## Problem

Curriculum learning methods are evaluated on inconsistent combinations of datasets, backbone models, and training settings across the literature, making direct comparison impossible. No existing benchmark or library covers multiple research domains, multiple data settings (standard, noisy, imbalanced), and both performance and complexity evaluation simultaneously, leaving researchers without a fair, systematic way to assess which curriculum method suits their task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242569/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 15 datasets spanning CV (32×32 CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 Tiny-ImageNet), NLP (8 GLUE tasks from RTE to QQP), and graph (MUTAG, PROTEINS, NCI1, ogbg-molhiv), each preprocessed into standard, 40%-uniform-noise, or 50×-long-tail imbalance settings. Nine backbone models of varying mechanism (CNN, Transformer, RNN, GNN) and scale (~0.01M to ~124M parameters) are paired with domain-appropriate hyperparameters. Output is the average and standard deviation of task-specific metrics (accuracy, F1, Spearman, Matthews, ROC-AUC) over 5 fixed-seed runs, plus training time and peak GPU memory as complexity indicators.

## Method

CurBench provides a unified five-module toolkit (data processing, model loading, objective fitting, curriculum learning, evaluation) that abstracts 15 curriculum learning methods into plug-in classes within a single training pipeline. The methods are organized into three implementation categories: data selection (e.g., SPL, DIHCL, LGL), model adjustment (e.g., CBS with Gaussian smoothing), and loss reweighting (e.g., LRE, MW-Net, DCL, SuperLoss via meta-networks or differentiable parameters). The key design choice is fixing all confounding factors—identical datasets, models, optimizers, learning rates, and random seeds—so that only the curriculum strategy varies, enabling apples-to-apples comparison. Evaluation proceeds along two dimensions: performance (task-specific metric under each setting) and complexity (wall-clock training time and maximum GPU memory on an NVIDIA V100), revealing that meta-learning-based reweighting methods incur 3–10× the time and memory of simpler selection-based approaches.

## Results

CurBench evaluates 15 curriculum learning methods across 15 datasets in 3 domains (CV, NLP, Graph) under 3 settings (standard, noise-0.4, imbalance-50). On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, the best CL method improves accuracy from 92.33% to 92.88% (standard), from 65.93% to 70.43% (noise-0.4), and from 35.46% to 35.63% (imbalance-50), confirming that noise settings yield the largest gains. On BERT for RTE, the best CL method reaches 81.94% (standard) and 53.07% (noise-0.4). On GCN for NCI1, the best CL method achieves 77.41% (standard) and 71.61% (noise-0.4). No single method dominates across all settings; TTCL leads in noise, while DCL and SuperLoss lead in imbalance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTCL (Weinshall et al., 2018) consistently achieves the best or near-best performance in noise settings across all three domains, benefiting from a teacher model pretrained on clean data that provides reliable difficulty signals; SPL (Kumar et al., 2010), which uses self-paced loss-based scheduling without an external teacher, performs noticeably worse under high noise because an under-trained model cannot accurately distinguish noisy from hard samples. In imbalance settings, reweighting-based methods DCL (Saxena et al., 2019) and SuperLoss (Castells et al., 2020) outperform data-selection methods because they directly mitigate class imbalance by reassigning per-sample or per-class weights. ScreenerNet (Kim & Choi, 2018) shows strong performance on CV datasets but degrades on graph datasets, while LGL (Cheng et al., 2019) and C2F (Stretcu et al., 2021) are restricted to CV due to their reliance on multi-class clustering or convolutional layers. Meta-learning-based methods (LRE, MW-Net, DDS) achieve competitive accuracy but incur the highest time and space complexity (e.g., LRE requires 665 min and 1241 MB GPU memory on ResNet-18/CIFAR-10 vs. ~106 min and 422 MB for MCL), making them less practical for large-scale use.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the empirical toolkit CurML (Zhou et al., 2022a), which provided a unified framework for reproducing curriculum learning methods but was limited to CIFAR-10 image classification. It also goes beyond prior surveys (Wang et al., 2021a; Soviany et al., 2022) that offered theoretical taxonomies without any practical implementation or experimental comparison. The key departure is the systematic expansion to three research domains (CV, NLP, graph), three training settings (standard, noise, imbalance), nine backbone architectures, and fifteen curriculum methods, creating the first cross-domain, cross-setting benchmark for fair evaluation of curriculum learning approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CurBench fills a critical gap by enabling fair, apples-to-apples comparison of curriculum learning methods that were previously evaluated on incompatible datasets, models, and settings, making direct comparison impossible. The empirical finding that no single method dominates across all scenarios, and that the best method depends on the specific setting (noise vs. imbalance) and domain, provides practical guidance for method selection. Key limitations include the absence of unsupervised/semi-supervised/RL settings, no evaluation on large-scale models, and reliance on existing datasets rather than purpose-built ones, leaving open questions about how curriculum learning scales to the era of large language and multimodal models.
