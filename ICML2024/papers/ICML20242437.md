## Problem

Adversarial Training (AT) is the most effective defense against adversarial attacks but inevitably degrades clean accuracy, creating a fundamental trade-off. Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM), which perturbs model weights rather than inputs to find flatter minima, was designed solely for clean generalization, and its impact on adversarial robustness remained entirely unexplored. This paper asks whether SAM alone can improve adversarial robustness without sacrificing natural accuracy, and under what conditions it can substitute for AT.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32, Tiny-ImageNet at 64×64) and text sequences (Rotten Tomatoes sentiment), producing class labels or pixel-level segmentation masks. A key distinction from AT is that SAM consumes only the original, unperturbed training samples—no adversarial examples are generated during training—while the weight perturbation magnitude ρ (typically 0.1–0.5 for images, 0.01–0.05 for segmentation) is the sole additional hyperparameter. Robustness is evaluated under a battery of attacks including FGSM, 10-step PGD (ℓ∞ and ℓ2), AutoAttack, StAdv, FAB, and the black-box Pixle attack, as well as common corruptions from CIFAR-10C.

## Method

The paper establishes a duality between SAM (perturbing weights: L(w+ε; x, y)) and AT (perturbing inputs: L(w; x+δ, y)) and shows both implicitly bias the model toward robust features. Theoretically, using the robust/non-robust feature decomposition framework, the authors prove that SAM increases the robust feature weight W_R quadratically in the perturbation radius (W_R^SAM ≈ W_R^* + (2/3)W_R^*ε²), while AT increases it linearly (W_R^AT = W_R^* · η/(η−ε)), explaining why SAM's perturbation is more moderate and preserves clean accuracy. Practically, the method requires no architectural changes: one simply replaces the standard optimizer (SGD or Adam) with SAM (or variants ASAM/ESAM) during training, keeping all original training samples intact. Extensive experiments across image classification (PRN-18, WRN-28-10, DeiT), semantic segmentation (DeepLabv3), and text classification (DistilBERT) confirm that SAM improves robust accuracy by 10–20+ points over standard training while simultaneously improving or maintaining clean accuracy, positioning it as a lightweight substitute for AT when accuracy is prioritized.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, SAM (ρ=0.4) achieves 78.7% natural accuracy and 25.4% AutoAttack robust accuracy, improving robustness by over 20 points (from 3.4% with Adam) while simultaneously increasing clean accuracy by 2.7 points. On CIFAR-10, SAM (ρ=0.4) reaches 94.7% natural accuracy and 51.8% AA robust accuracy versus 94.5% and 31.7% for standard SGD. Across all datasets and architectures tested (CIFAR-10/100, Tiny-ImageNet, PRN-18, WRN-28-10, DeiT), SAM consistently improves both natural and robust accuracy over standard training, with larger ρ yielding stronger robustness gains. On common corruptions (CIFAR-10C), SAM variants (ASAM, ESAM) even outperform standard training, while AT models perform worse than untrained baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Adversarial Training (Madry et al., 2017), SAM achieves substantially lower robust accuracy (25.4% vs. 52.4–57.5% AA on CIFAR-100) but preserves natural accuracy (78.7% vs. 58.3–64.2%), a gap of 14–20 points in clean accuracy that the paper argues is unaffordable in practice. Against Adversarial Weight Perturbation (Wu et al., 2020), SAM yields higher natural accuracy (94.7% vs. 82.0% on CIFAR-10) while AWP achieves higher robust accuracy (80.1% vs. 51.8% AA), confirming that perturbing both inputs and weights is more aggressive than weight perturbation alone. Among SAM variants, vanilla SAM outperforms ASAM (Kwon et al., 2021) on AA robustness (58.4% vs. 44.2% at ρ=0.5 on CIFAR-10) and ESAM (Du et al., 2021) (58.4% vs. 53.6%), though ESAM leads on StAdv attacks (49.6% vs. 55.8% at ρ=0.4/0.5 respectively). On common corruptions (CIFAR-10C), SAM variants (ASAM 36.69%, ESAM 36.29%) outperform both standard training (SGD 34.91%) and AT (ℓ∞-AT 15.67%, ℓ2-AT 23.13%), a setting where AT's input perturbation actively harms performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the robust/non-robust feature decomposition framework (Tsipras et al., 2018; Ilyas et al., 2019) to provide a theoretical lens for understanding why weight perturbation (SAM) and input perturbation (AT) both bias models toward robust features. It departs from prior SAM work, which focused exclusively on natural generalization, by revealing an unexplored adversarial robustness benefit. The paper also positions itself against AWP (Wu et al., 2020), which combines both SAM and AT but still sacrifices clean accuracy, arguing that SAM alone captures most of the robustness benefit without the accuracy cost of adversarial examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since SAM is already widely adopted as a training optimizer for clean accuracy, the finding that it provides a substantial 'free' robustness bonus (e.g., 3.4%→25.4% AA robust accuracy on CIFAR-100) has immediate practical value for deployment scenarios where accuracy is non-negotiable. However, the gap to full AT remains large (25.4% vs. 52.4%), so SAM is a complement rather than a replacement. The theoretical analysis is limited to a simple linear classifier, and whether the robust-feature-weight mechanism generalizes quantitatively to deep networks remains open. The surprising finding that SAM outperforms AT on common corruptions (CIFAR-10C) also raises questions about the nature of the robustness SAM induces versus that of AT.
