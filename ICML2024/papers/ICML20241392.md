## Problem

Probabilistic neurosymbolic models are trained via gradient descent on weighted model counting (WMC), but the computational complexity of differentiating through probabilistic inference was formally uncharacterized. It was unknown whether unbiased gradient estimators with probabilistic (PAC) guarantees could exist in polynomial time, and whether existing biased approximations are sufficient for optimization in practice.

## Input / Output

The method takes a propositional CNF formula φ with a Bernoulli weight function w (where w(x) ∈ [0,1] and w(x) + w(¬x) = 1, typically produced by a neural network) and a SAT solver oracle for weighted model sampling. It outputs the per-variable gradient vector ∂WMC(φ)/∂w or ∂log WMC(φ)/∂w. A key constraint is that WeightME requires the WMC value itself (or equivalently, the log-likelihood loss) in addition to the sampled models, and the weights must be strictly in (0,1) for the estimator to be well-defined.

## Method

The paper first proves that computing ∂WMC is #P-complete and that (ε,δ)-approximating it is NP-hard, establishing that no polynomial-time unbiased estimator with PAC guarantees exists in general. It then shows tractability emerges during training: when a single implicant dominates the WMC (i.e., weights converge to binary), interpretation sampling yields polynomial-time gradient approximations. The key contribution is WeightME, an unbiased estimator that samples models directly from the weighted model distribution and computes ∂log WMC/∂w(x) = E_M[1(x∈M)/w(x) − 1(x∉M)/w(¬x)], achieving PAC guarantees with only a constant number of weighted model samples (logarithmic SAT calls) under the mild condition that |P(x|φ) − w(x)| > λ. Empirically, the paper demonstrates that all existing biased approximations (fuzzy t-norms, Gumbel-Softmax, straight-through estimator, SFE, semantic strengthening) fail to consistently optimize log-likelihood even on benchmarks solvable by exact WMC solvers, while WeightME achieves the highest gradient quality at initialization.

## Results

On gradient estimation at initialization (measured by cosine similarity to the exact gradient), WeightME (k=100) achieves the best performance across all benchmarks: 0.784±0.248 on MCC2021, 0.721±0.259 on MCC2022, 0.821±0.172 on MCC2023, and 0.955±0.009 on ROAD-R. Among polynomial-time methods, the product t-norm (0.643/0.592/0.537/0.918) and Gumbel-Softmax (0.584/0.501/0.510/0.897) perform best, while SFE and Gödel t-norm are near-zero on most benchmarks. In the optimization experiment (Figure 4), none of the polynomial-time biased approximations can consistently optimize the log-likelihood even on 33 easy MCC instances with fewer than 1000 variables, where exact solvers still succeed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WeightME outperforms all baselines on the challenging MCC benchmarks, achieving cosine similarities of 0.72–0.82 where the best polynomial method (product t-norm) reaches only 0.54–0.64 and SFE is essentially zero (≤0.035). On the easier ROAD-R benchmark, WeightME (0.955) edges out unweighted model sampling (0.946), product t-norm (0.918), Gumbel-Softmax (0.897), and semantic strengthening (0.895), while MPE and k-Optimal lag further behind (0.609 and 0.691). The NP-hard baselines (MPE, k-Optimal, unweighted model sampling) time out on most MCC instances within the 5-minute budget, whereas WeightME completes all instances. Among polynomial-time methods, Gumbel-Softmax and the product t-norm are the strongest but still trail WeightME by 0.1–0.3 in cosine similarity on MCC. The optimization experiment (Figure 4) shows that none of the biased polynomial methods—product t-norm, Gumbel-Softmax, straight-through, or semantic strengthening—can consistently optimize the log-likelihood, confirming that gradient quality at initialization does not translate to successful optimization without principled guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the probabilistic neurosymbolic learning line (DeepProbLog, Scallop, NeurASP) where WMC underlies inference, but shifts focus from approximating the WMC itself to approximating its gradient. It connects the gradient estimation problem to the well-studied complexity theory of model counting (Roth 1996, Karp et al. 1989) and to recent advances in approximate weighted model sampling with PAC guarantees (Chakraborty et al. 2016, Soos & Meel 2019). The key departure is introducing WeightME, an unbiased estimator based on weighted model sampling, in contrast to all prior neurosymbolic gradient estimators (SFE/IndeCateR, Gumbel-Softmax, I-MLE, fuzzy t-norms) which are biased and lack probabilistic guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The negative result that existing biased approximations cannot consistently optimize even benchmarks solvable by exact solvers challenges the field's implicit assumption that these methods are 'good enough' for neurosymbolic learning. The theoretical characterization of when gradient estimation becomes tractable (implicant dominance, τ-supervision) provides a clear picture of the fundamental limits. Open questions include extending the analysis to first-order logic, DNFs, and understanding how approximate (biased) weighted model samplers interact with the PAC guarantee of WeightME in practice.
