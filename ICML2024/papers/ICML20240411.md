## Problem

In sequential A/B testing, the standard uniform randomized assignment rule may not minimize the expected sample size needed to produce a confidence interval for the average treatment effect (ATE) with a prescribed (ε,δ)-coverage guarantee. No prior work had established a tight lower bound on the required sample size or constructed an adaptive policy that achieves it, leaving open whether non-uniform assignment could reduce experiment length and by how much.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240411/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes sequential outcome observations from two treatments drawn from a single-parameter exponential family (Bernoulli, Poisson, Gaussian with known variance, Gamma with known shape), along with pre-specified width ε and error probability δ. It outputs an adaptive treatment assignment sequence, a stopping time τ, and a confidence interval [Δ̂_L(τ), Δ̂_R(τ)] of width at most ε. Notably, the estimation rule produces an anytime-valid confidence sequence, so the experimenter can inspect the CI at any intermediate time n and still retain a (1−δ) coverage guarantee even if the width exceeds ε.

## Method

The paper first derives an asymptotic lower bound on expected sample size as δ→0, expressed as the solution to a non-convex max-min optimization over the assignment fraction w and the limiting CI endpoints, with the objective involving KL divergences between the true and alternative outcome distributions. Policy P₁ achieves this bound by using D-tracking to adaptively match the optimal fraction w*(μ_A, μ_B, ε) and an estimation rule based on first-order optimality conditions of the max-min problem. A key structural insight is that as ε→0 and δ→0, the optimal assignment fraction converges to Neyman's allocation (proportional to the standard deviations of the two outcome distributions) and is insensitive to the exact value of ε for small ε. Leveraging this, policy P₂ replaces the expensive per-step max-min solve with a simple tracking of the sample standard-deviation ratio, yielding a computationally efficient policy that remains asymptotically optimal in the joint ε→0, δ→0 regime while retaining the (ε,δ)-coverage guarantee and anytime-valid confidence sequence property.

## Results

For exponential outcome distributions (μ_A=10, μ_B=0.1, ε=0.5), policy P₂ achieves approximately 50% reduction in expected sample size over the uniform randomized assignment policy (P_RCT) across all tested δ values: 3.74×10⁴ vs 7.85×10⁴ at δ=10%, 4.14×10⁴ vs 8.62×10⁴ at δ=5%, and 4.98×10⁴ vs 1.02×10⁵ at δ=1%. In a Bernoulli setting (μ_A=0.98, μ_B=0.5, ε=0.2, δ=0.01), P₂ requires an expected sample size of 2672, substantially lower than Clip-OGD (4034) and the non-adaptive Neyman benchmark (4573). The theoretical lower bound on expected sample size scales as log(1/δ)/ε², and both P₁ and P₂ are proven asymptotically optimal in this regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is P_RCT, which uses uniform (50/50) randomized assignment combined with the same estimation and stopping rules as P₂. P₂ achieves roughly a 50% reduction in expected sample size over P_RCT across all δ values (e.g., 4.98×10⁴ vs 1.02×10⁵ at δ=1%), a gain that grows as δ decreases. The secondary comparison is against Clip-OGD from Dai et al. (2024), which uses a different adaptive allocation rule based on second moments rather than standard deviations. P₂ outperforms Clip-OGD by 34% in expected sample size (2672 vs 4034) in the Bernoulli setting, because Clip-OGD's allocation rule assigns ~66.2% of samples to treatment A versus the asymptotically optimal ~21.9%. The non-adaptive Neyman benchmark from Dai et al. (2024) performs worst (4573), confirming that adaptive assignment is critical. Both P₂ and P_RCT maintain valid (ε,δ)-coverage in all experiments, with the CI always containing the true ATE at termination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research streams: adaptive experiment design for ATE (Hahn et al. 2011, Kato et al. 2020, Dai et al. 2024), sequential hypothesis testing (Wald 1945, Chernoff 1959), and pure exploration in multi-armed bandits (Garivier & Kaufmann 2016, Kaufmann et al. 2016). It departs from prior adaptive ATE design work, which minimizes asymptotic variance over a fixed experiment length, by instead formulating a sequential problem that minimizes expected sample size subject to a non-asymptotic (ε,δ)-coverage guarantee. The D-tracking rule and β(n,δ) threshold from Garivier & Kaufmann (2016) are directly repurposed, but the estimation and stopping rules are tailored to the ATE CI problem rather than best-arm identification. The paper also contrasts with Dai et al. (2024), whose Neyman allocation uses second moments rather than standard deviations, leading to measurably suboptimal assignment in the ATE setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first asymptotically optimal adaptive policy for sequential ATE estimation with (ε,δ)-coverage guarantee, along with a computationally efficient variant (P₂) that tracks standard deviations rather than solving a non-convex optimization at each step. The anytime-valid confidence sequence property is practically important for online platforms that need to monitor experiments continuously without compromising validity. Key limitations include restriction to single-parameter exponential families or bounded-support distributions, the need for Assumption 4.5 (strict quasi-concavity) for P₁'s optimality proof, and the absence of a contextual setting where assignment is conditioned on individual covariates. Open questions include extending to general distribution families, contextual A/B testing, and understanding finite-sample performance beyond the asymptotic regime.
