## Problem

Consistency of kernel methods (SVMs, kernel mean embeddings, conditional kernel mean embeddings) is typically established under i.i.d. or mixing assumptions, yet these methods empirically perform well on clearly non-i.i.d. data such as trajectories from dynamical systems or Markov chains. Existing theoretical frameworks exclude simple, non-pathological processes (e.g., a ball rolling irreversibly down a random side of a hill), leaving a gap between practice and theory for learning from dependent observations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of input-output pairs (X_n, Y_n) generated by a dependent process that satisfies only empirical weak convergence (EWC), along with a reproducing kernel K on a Polish input space, a convex loss function L, and a vanishing regularization sequence (λ_n). The output is a learned function f_{Z_n,λ_n} in a (possibly infinite-dimensional) G-valued RKHS, or equivalently a kernel mean embedding μ_P in a scalar RKHS. A key structural requirement is that the output space G is a separable Hilbert space and C_b(X×Y; H) is separable, which is typically satisfied when X and Y are compact.

## Method

The paper introduces empirical weak convergence (EWC): the existence of a random limit measure P such that the empirical measure η_n^X converges weakly to P (in probability or a.s.). This is a strict weakening of i.i.d., mixing, and the law of large numbers for events, and crucially allows the asymptotic distribution to be path-dependent (random). The main technical results show that under EWC, empirical averages of Hilbert-space-valued continuous bounded functions converge (Theorem 3.6), kernel mean embeddings are statistically consistent (Theorem 4.1), and SVMs are L-consistent (Theorem 4.3). The framework extends the classical Steinwart–Christmann setup from scalar to separable Hilbert output spaces via a generalized representer theorem, thereby encompassing conditional kernel mean embeddings. The trade-off for the weaker data assumption is that the loss must be continuously Fréchet differentiable (rather than merely continuous), and no universal rate for the regularization sequence exists without additional assumptions on the EWC convergence speed.

## Results

The paper establishes three main theoretical results. Theorem 4.1 proves statistical consistency of kernel mean embedding estimators under EWC: the empirical KME converges in probability (or a.s.) to the KME of the random limit measure, with no requirement of local compactness or kernel continuity. Theorem 4.3 proves L-consistency of SVMs (regularized empirical risk minimization in a separable G-valued RKHS) for EWC data, extending the classical framework to infinite-dimensional output spaces and thereby covering conditional kernel mean embeddings. Theorem 3.6 shows that empirical averages of any Hilbert-space-valued continuous bounded function converge under EWC, a key technical ingredient. The consistency requires the loss to be continuously differentiable (a price paid for the weaker EWC assumption versus total-variation convergence), and the regularization sequence must be annealed to zero at a rate depending on the data-generating process.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not perform empirical comparisons but positions EWC against existing assumptions for consistency. EWC strictly generalizes i.i.d., mixing (including α-, β-, and φ-mixing), and the law of large numbers for events (LLNE): all of these imply EWC (with a constant limit measure) when the input space is compact, but EWC also covers processes like the irreversible-step example (Example 3.8) and the Cantor-set-valued process (Example 3.9) that satisfy none of the standard assumptions. Compared to asymptotic mean stationarity (AMS), EWC is incomparable: AMS requires convergence in total variation (stronger topology) but only on expectation (weaker probabilistic requirement), while EWC requires weak convergence (weaker topology) but of the full random limit measure (stronger probabilistic requirement). The paper introduces two bridging notions—empirical strong convergence (ESC) and weak AMS—to clarify the partial order. The trade-off is that EWC's weaker convergence topology necessitates a continuously differentiable loss for SVM consistency, whereas the total-variation convergence under LLNE only requires continuity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the consistency theory for SVMs from Steinwart & Christmann (2008) and Steinwart et al. (2009) in two orthogonal directions: (1) replacing the mixing/i.i.d. data assumption with the new, strictly weaker notion of empirical weak convergence (EWC), which only requires the empirical measure to converge weakly to a possibly random limit measure; and (2) generalizing the output space from scalar to separable Hilbert spaces, thereby encompassing conditional kernel mean embeddings (CKMEs) as a special case. The proof structure for Theorem 4.3 closely follows Theorem 2.17 in Steinwart et al. (2009), while the KME consistency result (Theorem 4.1) leverages MMD metrization results from Sriperumbudur et al. (2010). The key conceptual departure is allowing the asymptotic data distribution to be random and path-dependent, rather than fixed a priori as in mixing or ergodicity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EWC captures simple, non-pathological processes (e.g., a ball rolling irreversibly down a random side of a hill, or a Cantor-set construction) that are provably not mixing or LLNE, yet for which kernel methods empirically work well. The trade-off is explicit: the more general data assumption comes at the price of requiring a continuously differentiable loss (versus mere continuity under mixing) and provides no learning rates. Open questions include extending Theorem 4.3 to non-separable function spaces, establishing consistency under the stronger ESC (empirical strong convergence) assumption, and translating bounds on EWC convergence speed into oracle inequalities. For practitioners, the practical impact is indirect but foundational: it provides the first theoretical justification for kernel methods on dynamical-system data and other non-mixing processes, and unifies CKMEs within the SVM consistency framework.
