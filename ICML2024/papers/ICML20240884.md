## Problem

Existing Neural Collapse (NC) theory characterizes learned features and classifiers only when the number of classes K is at most d+1 (where d is the feature dimension), yielding a simplex ETF structure. In practice, applications such as face recognition, language models, and retrieval systems have K far exceeding d, where no simplex ETF exists and the geometric structure of the optimal classifier is unknown. This paper asks: what is the correct geometric characterization of last-layer features and classifiers when K > d+1?

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, 50×50 for BUPT-CBFace-50, 224×224 for ImageNet/STL-10) and produces class labels via a linear classifier. The distinguishing constraint is that the feature dimension d is deliberately set well below the number of classes K (e.g., d=10 with K=100, or d=512 with K=10,000), and both features and classifier weights are constrained to the unit sphere S^{d-1}. The output structure of interest is the geometric arrangement of K classifier weight vectors on the sphere, which the paper shows converges to a Softmax Code rather than a simplex ETF.

## Method

The paper introduces the Softmax Code: an arrangement of K points on the unit sphere that maximizes the minimum one-vs-rest distance (distance from each point to the convex hull of all other points), generalizing the simplex ETF. Under the Unconstrained Features Model with spherical constraints and vanishing temperature τ→0, the CE loss converges to a HardMax problem, whose global solutions provably satisfy three properties: within-class variability collapse (GNC₁), classifier weights forming a Softmax Code (GNC₂), and self-duality between classifier weights and class-mean features (GNC₃). The proof of GNC₂ is unconditional; GNC₁ and GNC₃ require technical conditions (no 'rattlers' in the Softmax Code, and equivalence between the Tammes problem and Softmax Code). As a practical consequence, the paper proposes a Class-Mean Feature (CMF) classifier that replaces the learned linear layer with an exponential moving average of mini-batch class means, reducing trainable parameters and improving out-of-distribution fine-tuning performance.

## Results

The paper empirically verifies that GNC1 (within-class variability) and GNC3 (self-duality) converge to 0, and GNC2 (softmax code) converges to the optimal one-vs-rest distance, across ResNet18, DenseNet121, and ResNeXt50 on CIFAR-100 (d=10, K=100), Tiny-ImageNet (d=10, K=200), and BUPT-CBFace-50 (d=512, K=10,000). In the fine-tuning experiment on CIFAR-10/STL-10, the proposed CMF classifier achieves 98.00% in-distribution and 90.67% out-of-distribution accuracy, outperforming standard fine-tuning (97.00% ID, 87.42% OOD). The assignment problem experiments show that placing semantically similar classes (e.g., Cat–Dog) closer in the softmax code yields 2.18–5.58% higher test accuracy than placing them far apart.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is the CMF (Class Mean Feature) classifier against standard fine-tuning following the protocol of Kumar et al. (2022). The CMF classifier wins on both in-distribution (98.00% vs. 97.00%, +1.0%) and out-of-distribution (90.67% vs. 87.42%, +3.25%) accuracy, with the OOD gain attributed to maintaining classifier–class-mean alignment throughout fine-tuning. In the assignment problem experiments (Section 4 and B.5), the implicitly learned classifier (which places semantically similar classes closer) consistently outperforms fixed assignments that separate similar classes: for 4 classes the gap is 2.18% (92.13% vs. 89.95%), and for 6 classes it widens to 5.58% (91.95% vs. 86.37%), demonstrating that the implicit regularization is beneficial and that class-to-weight assignment matters increasingly with more classes. The paper also contrasts spherical-constraint training with weight-decay training, showing that only the spherical constraint produces equal-length, uniformly distributed features consistent with GNC, whereas weight decay yields non-equal lengths and non-uniform spacing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Neural Collapse (NC) framework of Papyan et al. (2020) and the unconstrained features model (UFM) of Mixon et al. (2020) and Fang et al. (2021) from the regime K ≤ d+1 (where simplex ETFs exist) to the practically important regime K > d+1. The key conceptual departure is replacing the simplex ETF characterization of NC₂ with a 'Softmax Code'—a configuration maximizing the minimum one-vs-rest distance to the convex hull of other class weights—rather than the one-vs-one distance used in the Tammes problem. The paper also distinguishes itself from concurrent generalized-NC work by Liu et al. (2023a) and Gao et al. (2023), which analyze networks trained with weight decay, by focusing on spherical constraints that are standard in face recognition and contrastive learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Softmax Code provides the first concrete geometric characterization of optimal classifier geometry when the number of classes exceeds the feature dimension, a regime that dominates face recognition, language models, and retrieval systems. The practical CMF classifier (replacing learned weights with class-mean features) reduces trainable parameters by ~31% while matching or exceeding standard fine-tuning on OOD tasks, offering a simple drop-in improvement. Key open questions include: whether the Tammes problem and Softmax Code are equivalent in general (proven only for d=2 or K≤d+1), how to optimally assign classes to Softmax Code positions (the 'assignment problem'), and whether GNC holds under imbalanced or noisy settings.
