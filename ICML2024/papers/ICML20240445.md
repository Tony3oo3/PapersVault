## Problem

Neural scaling laws predict that test error decays as a power of training data size, but this assumes clean, heavy-tailed (Zipf) data. As AI-generated content increasingly contaminates web-scale corpora, the training distribution's tail is truncated or narrowed, yet no theoretical framework existed to characterize how scaling laws change—or whether models will inevitably collapse—under this synthetic-data regime.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240445/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework takes a true Zipf distribution p_i ∝ i^{-β} and an AI-generated version with either a tail cutoff at rank k (from top-p sampling or finite sampling bias) or a narrowed exponent β' < β (from temperature scaling), and produces a modified test-error scaling law as a function of sample size T, cutoff k, and model capacity d. Empirically, Llama2-7B is fine-tuned via LoRA on ~2M sequences of 128 tokens from Wikitext-103 (using the first 96 tokens as prompts and training only on the last 32), while a sequence-to-sequence transformer is trained on 300M integer pairs for GCD prediction; both are iterated across multiple generations with top-p=0.9 and τ=0.9.

## Method

The paper derives a 'double scaling law' E_test ∝ T^{-(β-1)/β} + k^{-(β-1)} for a deterministic infinite-memory Hutter LLM trained on tail-cut AI data, showing that performance plateaus once T ≳ k^β and scaling is lost. For tail narrowing (exponent β→β'), the decay rate slows to T^{-(β-1)/β'}, and for n-fold recursive generation the error accumulates linearly as E_test ∝ T^{-c} + nT_0^{-c}, yielding a precise collapse condition n ≫ (T_0/T)^c. A key mitigation result shows that mixing even a tiny fraction π of clean data with AI data produces a 'grokking' plateau of length ~k^β/π before the error resumes its original T^{-c} decay. The framework extends to a probabilistic bigram model (Hutter++) with total-variation loss and to a finite-capacity associative memory, yielding a 'triplet scaling law' E_test ∝ T^{-c} + d^{-c_q} + k^{-(β-1)} that jointly captures data, capacity, and tail effects.

## Results

The paper establishes a Double Scaling Law for model collapse: E_test ≍ T^{-(β-1)/β} + k^{-(β-1)}, showing that once T ≳ k^β, additional AI-generated data yields no further improvement (loss of scaling). For n-fold recursive generation, the error degrades linearly as E_test^(n) ≍ nT_0^{-(β-1)/β}, with model collapse occurring iff n ≫ (T_0/T)^c. Empirically, fine-tuning Llama2-7B on ~2M Wikitext-103 sequences with top-p=0.9 and τ=0.9 shows progressive scaling-law decay across generations, while mixing just 2% clean data with AI data produces a predicted grokking plateau of order k^β/π before error resumes decreasing. The GCD transformer (trained on 300M samples) confirms 'un-learning' of skills (e.g., GCD=2, GCD=3) when trained exclusively on AI data, and validates the Triplet Scaling Law E_test ≍ T^{-c} + d^{-c_q} + k^{-(β-1)} for capacity-limited models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that validates its own scaling-law predictions empirically rather than benchmarking against competing methods. The key comparisons are: (1) clean data vs. AI-generated data, where the AI-data curve plateaus at k^{-(β-1)} while the clean-data curve continues as T^{-(β-1)/β}; (2) single-generation vs. multi-generation collapse, where each additional generation shifts the loss curve rightward by a factor of n; (3) pure AI data vs. mixed data (π fraction clean), where even π=0.02 (2% clean) produces a grokking effect that eventually recovers the clean-data scaling rate (up to a multiplicative constant π^{-(β-1)/β}). The paper also contrasts tail-cutting (plateau) vs. tail-narrowing (slower decay rate T^{-(β-1)/β'}) as distinct collapse mechanisms, and shows that curating tail data only helps when N/k→1 (Theorem 3.1), while overshooting (N/k>1) is worthless.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Hutter LLM framework (Hutter, 2021) and the associative memory scaling laws of Cabannes et al. (2023) to the setting where training data is contaminated by AI-generated samples, introducing a 'double scaling law' (E_test ≍ T^{-c} + k^{-c'}) and a 'triplet scaling law' that adds a tail-cutoff term. It builds on the model collapse concept and finite-sampling-bias mechanism identified by Shumailov et al. (2023), but departs from prior work by providing a full heavy-tailed (Zipf) scaling law analysis rather than restricting to Gaussian cases, and by connecting model collapse directly to the loss of power-law scaling and emergent abilities. The paper is explicitly positioned as the first to theoretically and empirically analyze model collapse through the lens of scaling laws, unifying tail cutting, tail narrowing, multi-generation recursion, and data-mixing mitigation under a single framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As synthetic data increasingly contaminates web-scale training corpora, this work provides the first principled account of how neural scaling laws degrade—showing plateaus, slower decay rates, and linear loss accumulation across generations—rather than simply confirming that performance drops. The grokking phenomenon (Theorem 3.2), where even an arbitrarily small proportion of clean data eventually restores the original scaling law after a plateau of length ~k^β/π, offers a concrete and actionable mitigation strategy validated on Llama2-7B. Open questions include how these results extend to non-Zipf or multimodal distributions, whether the grokking plateau length is practically manageable at web-scale k, and how to operationalize 'tail curation' (Theorem 3.1) in real data pipelines. The paper's call for 'collapse-aware' data proliferation and watermarking underscores that the current scaling paradigm has a finite horizon.
