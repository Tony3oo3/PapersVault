## Problem

Despite the rapid adoption of Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) for scaling large language models, no comprehensive reliability benchmark exists to assess whether MoE models are as trustworthy as their dense counterparts in safety-critical scenarios. Moreover, prior work suggests MoE can underperform dense models on domain transfer, yet the conditions under which this occurs and whether MoE's inherent sparsity confers robustness advantages remain unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240305/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes text prompts (harmful instructions, commonsense questions, NLI sentence pairs, and sentiment sentences) and produces multi-dimensional reliability scores: harmfulness ratings from three independent evaluators (a PLM-based Reward Model scoring 0–4, Llama Guard binary unsafe/safe classification, and OpenAI Content Moderation risk scores 0–1), classification accuracy on adversarial (ANLI, SNLI-hard) and OOD (Style-ood with 10 style transformations) test sets, and exact-match or multiple-choice accuracy on Natural Questions and TruthfulQA. All models are first instruction-tuned on Alpaca (50k pairs, safety samples removed) before evaluation, and comparisons are always made between an MoE model and a dense model with matched activated parameter count.

## Method

MoE-RBench systematically evaluates three open-source MoE families (Switch Transformers, ModuleFormer/MoLM, and LlamaMoE) against their dense counterparts (T5, Pythia, OpenLlama) across three reliability dimensions: safety/hallucination, adversarial robustness, and OOD robustness. Beyond benchmarking, the paper investigates how MoE-specific training configurations affect reliability: it ablates router training strategies (free, frozen, freeze-then-free, bi-level), expert dropout rates (0.1–0.4), and load-balancing loss weights (0, 1e-3, 1e-2), finding that higher expert dropout and non-zero load-balancing loss significantly improve OOD generalization. The paper also shows that mixing 500 safety refusal pairs into instruction-tuning data disproportionately benefits MoE models, and that DoLa contrast decoding (contrasting logits from early vs. late layers) closes the hallucination gap on TruthfulQA. A routing analysis reveals that MoE's robustness stems from layer-wise routing flexibility: middle layers activate different experts for OOD inputs while bottom/top layers preserve core semantic processing, a mechanism absent in dense models where all parameters are unconditionally activated.

## Results

Across three model pairs (encoder-decoder and decoder-only), MoE models consistently outperform their dense counterparts on adversarial robustness by an average of 2.41% (e.g., MoLM-350M-K2 achieves 48.10% Adv.RA vs. pythia-410M's 45.48%; LlamaMoE-3.5B-K2 achieves 60.66% vs. OpenLlama-3B's 55.26%) and on OOD robustness by an average of 1.92%. On hallucination, MoE models outperform dense on Natural Questions (e.g., LlamaMoE-3.5B-K4: 19.92% EM vs. OpenLlama-3B: 16.09%) but underperform on TruthfulQA MC due to inverse scaling. On safety, MoE is competitive with dense, with the smallest pair (MoLM-350M-K2 vs. pythia-410M) showing the most distinct MoE advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper pairs each MoE model with a dense counterpart of similar activated parameter size. Switch-base (MoE) outperforms T5-base (dense) by +2.1% average Adv.RA and +2.2% Std.RA, confirming the advantage holds for encoder-decoder architectures. MoLM-350M-K2 (MoE) beats pythia-410M (dense) by +2.6% Adv.RA and +3.7% Std.RA despite activating fewer parameters per token, and also surpasses the larger pythia-1.4B on adversarial metrics. LlamaMoE-3B-K2 (MoE) outperforms OpenLlama-3B (dense) by +2.0% Adv.RA while being slightly behind (−0.7%) on standard accuracy, indicating the MoE gain is specifically in robustness rather than general capacity. On OOD (Style-ood), MoE advantages are roughly 1.7× larger than in-domain gains, ruling out total parameter count as the sole explanation. On hallucination, the comparison is mixed: all MoE models beat their dense counterparts on NQ Exact Match, but dense models outperform MoE on TruthfulQA MC, where larger MoE models exhibit inverse scaling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established research threads: (1) the MoE architecture literature (Switch Transformers, ModuleFormer, LlamaMoE) that has focused primarily on pre-training scaling and routing algorithms, and (2) the LLM reliability evaluation literature (safety, hallucination, adversarial robustness, OOD robustness) that has been applied almost exclusively to dense models. MoE-RBench carves new ground by being the first to systematically apply the full reliability evaluation suite to MoE models and compare them against dense counterparts of matched activated parameter size. It also extends prior MoE fine-tuning work (Shen et al., Zadouri et al.) by investigating how MoE-specific hyperparameters (expert dropout, load-balancing loss weight) and training strategies (router freezing, bi-level training) affect downstream reliability rather than just task accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark provides the first empirical evidence that MoE models, when properly configured, can be more reliable than dense counterparts on adversarial and OOD robustness—counter to the prior assumption (Artetxe et al., Narang et al.) that MoE exhibits greater instability upon domain transfer. The actionable findings on expert dropout and load-balancing loss weight give practitioners concrete levers for improving MoE reliability during fine-tuning. Open questions remain: the bi-level training strategy that improved robustness in CNN-MoE failed for LLM MoE, the inverse scaling phenomenon on TruthfulQA for larger MoE models is unexplained, and all experiments use models up to ~3.5B parameters, leaving open whether these findings hold at the 70B+ scale where MoE is most commonly deployed.
