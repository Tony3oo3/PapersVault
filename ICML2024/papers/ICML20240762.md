## Problem

In multi-sender Bayesian persuasion, multiple informed parties simultaneously signal a self-interested receiver to influence its action, but the resulting Nash equilibrium is computationally intractable: best-response computation is NP-hard and general equilibrium finding is PPAD-hard. Prior economic literature characterized special equilibria (e.g., full revelation under zero-sum utilities) but offered no computational tools for the general case, leaving a gap between the theoretical hardness and practical equilibrium discovery.

## Input / Output

The method takes a game specification (receiver and sender utility matrices of size |Ω|×|A|, a prior distribution μ₀ over |Ω| states, and a signal space S) and produces a profile of signaling policies, each a |Ω|×|S| stochastic matrix (rows sum to 1, entries in [0,1]). During training, the network consumes uniformly sampled joint signaling policies and outputs a scalar ex-ante utility per sender, computed by a game simulator that resolves the receiver's argmax action at each induced posterior. The final output is an ε-local NE verified by sampling K deviating policies in an ℓ∞-ball of radius ε around each sender's policy.

## Method

The core architecture is a piecewise Discontinuous and Non-Linear (DNL) network that splits a fully-connected feedforward network into a lower part (K ReLU layers) and a higher part (L−K ≥ 2 layers). The lower part's activation pattern r^(≤K) is fed to a hyper-network that generates the weights and biases of the higher part, so inputs within the same linear region share a non-linear sub-network (capturing piecewise non-linearity) while different regions receive different higher-part parameters (introducing discontinuity at boundaries). This contrasts with DeLU, which only conditions the final-layer bias on the activation pattern and remains piecewise linear. The network is trained end-to-end via supervised MSE loss on 500K sampled (policy, utility) pairs from a game simulator. Equilibrium finding then runs the extra-gradient method on the frozen trained networks, parameterizing each sender's policy as a learnable matrix and projecting updates onto the simplex constraint set via SoftMax.

## Results

On three real-world scenarios (advertising of quality, advertising of multiple products, and Uber/Lyft ride-hailing), the proposed DNL network with extra-gradient achieves social welfare of 0.699±0.003, 0.526±0.004, and 4.344±0.885 respectively, consistently outperforming both the ReLU and DeLU baselines. The method also finds ε-local Nash equilibria that Pareto dominate the full-revelation equilibria guaranteed by Theorem 4, and achieves lower utility approximation errors across all problem sizes tested (2–10 states, signals, and actions). In synthetic benchmarks with 1,250 problem instances, the DNL network's approximation error is consistently lower than both baselines, with the gap widening as the number of states and actions increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two neural network baselines: standard ReLU networks (continuous, piecewise-linear) and DeLU (Wang et al., 2023), which introduces discontinuity via activation-pattern-conditioned biases but remains piecewise-linear. The proposed DNL architecture, which uses a hyper-network to generate piecewise non-linear weights conditioned on the lower network's activation pattern, wins on all three real-world scenarios. In Scenario 1 (7-firm advertising), DNL achieves 0.699 social welfare versus 0.599 for DeLU (+17%) and 0.498 for ReLU (+40%). In Scenario 2 (multi-product advertising), DNL reaches 0.526 versus 0.467 for DeLU and 0.407 for ReLU, while also producing dramatically tighter confidence intervals (±0.004 vs. ±0.176/±0.179), suggesting more stable equilibrium discovery. In Scenario 3 (Uber/Lyft with 4 orders each), DNL achieves 4.344 versus 3.783 for DeLU (+15%) and 3.216 for ReLU (+35%). In synthetic benchmarks, DNL also achieves lower approximation error than both baselines across all problem sizes, and the ε-local equilibria it finds Pareto-dominate those from both baselines and the full-revelation equilibrium.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian Persuasion framework from a single sender to multiple simultaneous senders, providing the first computational complexity analysis of the resulting game. It builds directly on DeLU (Wang et al., 2023), which introduced discontinuous piecewise-affine networks for contract design, by extending the architecture to handle piecewise *non-linear* discontinuities via a hyper-network that generates higher-layer weights conditioned on the lower network's activation pattern. The theoretical results (NP-hard best response, PPAD-hard equilibrium) complement prior economic characterizations by Gentzkow & Kamenica (2017b) and Ravindran & Cui (2022), which established structural properties but offered no computational guarantees. The equilibrium-finding procedure combines the differentiable economics paradigm (Dutting et al., 2023) with the extra-gradient algorithm from the learning-in-games literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hardness results (NP-hard best response even with 2 senders, PPAD-hard equilibrium) definitively rule out efficient global equilibrium algorithms, making the local-equilibrium approach not just convenient but necessary. The DNL architecture is potentially of broader interest for any game or economic design problem with discontinuous, piecewise-nonlinear utilities beyond persuasion. Key open questions include the conjectured Co-NP hardness of equilibrium verification, whether the approach scales to continuous action spaces, and how these insights transfer to other multi-principal variants of principal-agent problems (contract design, Stackelberg games) that have long lacked robust computational solutions.
