limitations: The method requires a two-phase training procedure (200-epoch search on 100 classes followed by 300-epoch retraining) and per-epoch K-means clustering over all training features for IB loss computation, which may limit scalability to very large datasets. Evaluation is limited to ImageNet-1k and COCO, and the search phase overhead is not quantified in wall-clock time.

surprise_factor: The most notable finding is that channel selection in the attention weights (Q, K) — rather than in the attention outputs or MLP — is what enables accuracy to *increase* (not merely be preserved) when FLOPs are reduced, which inverts the typical compression-accuracy tradeoff. ## Problem

Visual transformers incur heavy FLOPs from MLP layers and quadratic attention, making deployment impractical under resource constraints. Existing compression methods prune fixed channels in linear layers but neglect selecting informative channels for computing the attention affinity matrix between tokens, which is the pivotal component for aggregating global information. This paper asks: can we reduce attention output channels (and thus MLP FLOPs) while maintaining or improving accuracy by making the attention weights themselves more informative through input-dependent channel selection in queries and keys?

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (224×224 or 288×288 for ImageNet classification, 320×320 for COCO detection) that are split into non-overlapping patches to form token sequences of shape N×D. The DCS-Transformer block is a drop-in replacement for standard transformer blocks in existing backbones (MobileViT, EfficientViT, ViT-S/16, Swin-T), reducing the effective channel dimension from D to D̃ for MLP computation while preserving the full N×N attention matrix. Outputs are class labels (ImageNet-1k), bounding boxes with class scores (COCO detection via SSDLite), or instance segmentation masks (COCO via Mask R-CNN + FPN).

## Method

The DCS-Transformer block performs two types of differentiable channel selection. First, for attention weights: an input-dependent binary mask M ∈ {0,1}^{N×D} (relaxed via Gumbel-Softmax with parameters θ from a linear layer on the input) is applied element-wise to both Q and K before computing W = σ((Q⊙M)(K⊙M)ᵀ), so that only selected channels contribute to the token affinity. Second, for attention outputs: a channel mask g ∈ {0,1}^D (learned via Gumbel-Softmax with architecture parameters α) prunes the attention output channels, reducing MLP input/output dimension from D to D̃ = Σgᵢ and cutting MLP FLOPs. The training follows a two-phase NAS-style procedure: a search phase optimizes α with a cost-regularized loss, followed by a retraining phase that jointly minimizes cross-entropy and a novel separable variational upper bound on the Information Bottleneck loss I(X̃)−I(X̃,Y), computed via K-means clustering on learned and input features. The IB loss explicitly encourages more informative attention weights by reducing correlation between learned features and raw inputs while preserving task-relevant information.

## Results

On ImageNet-1k, DCS-Transformer consistently reduces FLOPs while improving top-1 accuracy across all tested backbones: DCS-MobileViT-S achieves 81.0% top-1 at 1.2G FLOPs (vs. 78.4% at 1.4G for MobileViT-S), DCS-EfficientViT-B1[r224] reaches 80.8% at 0.46G FLOPs (vs. 79.4% at 0.52G), and DCS-Swin-T attains 82.0% at 4.0G FLOPs (vs. 81.3% at 4.5G). For object detection with SSDLite on COCO, DCS-EfficientViT achieves 29.0 mAP at 1.4G FLOPs, outperforming the original EfficientViT (28.4 mAP at 1.5G) and MobileNetV3 (22.0 mAP at 1.4G). For instance segmentation on COCO, DCS-EfficientViT-B1 improves mAP^box from 33.5 to 34.8 and mAP^mask from 31.9 to 33.2 over EfficientViT-B1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCS-Transformer outperforms all direct backbone counterparts: DCS-MobileViT-S beats MobileViT-S by 2.6% top-1 (81.0 vs. 78.4) while reducing FLOPs from 1.4G to 1.2G; DCS-EfficientViT-B1[r224] surpasses EfficientViT-B1 by 1.4% (80.8 vs. 79.4) at lower FLOPs (0.46G vs. 0.52G); DCS-Swin-T exceeds Swin-T by 0.7% (82.0 vs. 81.3) with 0.5G fewer FLOPs. Against competing efficient transformers, DCS-MobileViT-S (81.0%) outperforms TinyViT (79.1%), VTC-LFC (78.0%), SPViT (77.8%), and ToMe (77.3%) at comparable or lower FLOPs. In the weight-pruning comparison on EfficientViT-B1(r224), DCS-EfficientViT achieves 80.8% top-1, exceeding S²ViTE (79.0%), SPViT (79.3%), and SAViT (79.2%) by at least 1.5% while using fewer parameters (8.2M) and FLOPs (0.46G). For object detection, DCS-EfficientViT with SSDLite reaches 29.0 mAP, surpassing MobileNetV3 (22.0), MNASNet (23.0), and YoloV5-N (28.0) at similar or lower FLOPs. For instance segmentation, DCS-EfficientViT-B1 improves mAP^box by 1.3 points (34.8 vs. 33.5) and mAP^mask by 1.3 points (33.2 vs. 31.9) over EfficientViT-B1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of efficient visual transformer design (MobileViT, EfficientViT) and differentiable architecture search, extending the Gumbel-Softmax relaxation technique from dynamic convolutions (Verelst & Tuytelaars, 2020) to channel selection within transformer attention. It departs from prior channel-pruning work (S²ViTE, SPViT, SAViT) that prunes fixed channels in linear layers by instead selecting informative channels in the query-key computation itself, motivated by the Information Bottleneck principle. The novel separable variational upper bound for the IB loss, which is directly optimizable by SGD, represents a theoretical contribution that generalizes beyond transformers to any classification network.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: DCS-Transformer achieves higher accuracy than the original backbones while reducing FLOPs (e.g., +2.6% Top-1 on MobileViT-S with 1.2G vs 1.6G FLOPs), validated across four architectures and three downstream tasks. The separable IB loss bound is a general tool that any supervised network can adopt, potentially improving generalization without architectural changes. Open questions include whether the per-epoch K-means clustering required for IB loss computation scales to very large datasets, whether the search phase (200 epochs on 100 classes) can be shortened, and whether the IB optimization benefit persists in self-supervised or generative settings.
