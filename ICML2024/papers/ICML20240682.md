## Problem

In loosely-coupled cooperative learning networks where agents share only predictions (not model parameters), a few highly influential agents with poor classifiers can degrade accuracy across the entire network. This paper formalizes and solves the optimization problem of selecting exactly k agents whose classifiers to improve so as to maximize the collective network benefit, under both aggregate (total accuracy gain) and egalitarian (number of agents whose faulty predictions are fixed) objectives.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240682/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithms consume the expressed social influence matrix W̄ (derived from DeGroot, Friedkin-Johnsen, or finite-time averaging models), the innate error rates of all n agents, and—depending on the variant—either the full joint probability distribution π over classifiers, pairwise independence assumptions, or group-level error rates for red/blue/white partitions. The output is a subset S of exactly k agents to improve, where improvement is modeled as replacing each selected agent's prediction ŷ_i(a) with the convex combination (1−φ)ŷ_i(a) + φ·y(a). The egalitarian approximation EgalAlg^(appx) runs in Θ(n³k) time without needing |Ω| in the complexity, while the exact greedy EgalAlg requires Θ(|Ω|n²k) and full access to π.

## Method

For the aggregate objective, the paper proves that selecting the top-k vertices by influence score Inf(v_j) = Σ_i W̄_ij·err(v_i) is exactly optimal, yielding a Θ(n²) algorithm. For the egalitarian objective, the paper proves NP-hardness via a polynomial reduction from weighted set cover, then exploits the monotone submodularity of the egalitarian gain function to design greedy approximations. The key technical contribution is EgalAlg^(appx), which estimates the greedy marginal gain using only error rates and W̄ by bounding the probability that an agent's expressed prediction is faulty via Hoeffding concentration inequalities; the approximation ratio is (1−1/e) − Δ where Δ depends on the number of 'ambiguous' vertices (those whose low- and high-error neighbors contribute comparably to their expressed prediction). A further extension handles group-dependent classifiers (red/blue/white agents with intra-group correlation) by decomposing the gain estimate into group and individual components weighted by the group-following probability ρ.

## Results

The paper's primary metric is Acc@k=log(n) (normalized egalitarian gain when modifying log(n) agents). EgalAlg achieves the highest accuracy across all 8 datasets, reaching 1.00 on PA(128), BIO(297), and CSPK(39), 0.96 on WIKI(890), and 0.88 on FB(620), compared to 0.35–0.48 for random selection. In terms of efficiency (#k needed for >90% accuracy), EgalAlg requires only 2 nodes on WIKI(890) and 9 on FB(620), versus 22 and 94 for random selection respectively. The authors categorize all six methods into four tiers: Tier 1 = {EgalAlg}, Tier 2 = {EgalAlg^(appx)}, Tier 3 = {DegXErr, ErrRate}, Tier 4 = {Degree, Rand}, with EgalAlg substantially outperforming all others.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks EgalAlg and EgalAlg^(appx) against four baselines: Random selection, Degree-based selection, ErrRate-based selection, and DegXErr (product of degree and error rate). EgalAlg dominates all baselines on every dataset for both Acc@k=log(n) and #k@Acc>90%. On the largest real-world dataset WIKI(890), EgalAlg achieves 0.96 accuracy at k=log(n) versus 0.78 for the best baseline (DegXErr) and 0.48 for Random, while requiring only 2 nodes for >90% accuracy versus 7 for DegXErr and 22 for Random. On FB(620), EgalAlg reaches 0.88 accuracy versus 0.72 (DegXErr) and 0.35 (Random), needing 9 nodes for >90% accuracy versus 32 (DegXErr) and 94 (Random). EgalAlg^(appx) consistently ranks second, outperforming Tier 3 and 4 baselines on most datasets but falling short of EgalAlg, particularly on WIKI (0.64 vs 0.96) and FB (0.62 vs 0.88). The Degree-based baseline is the weakest overall, performing at or below random on most datasets, indicating that structural centrality alone is a poor proxy for influence on classification accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two established lines of work—decentralized learning (federated/gossip learning) and social learning (DeGroot, Friedkin-Johnsen opinion dynamics)—by introducing a loosely coupled framework where agents share only predictions rather than model parameters or gradients. It departs from decentralized learning by requiring no shared architecture and from social learning by shifting focus from convergence/consensus analysis to the optimization problem of selecting which agents to improve. The greedy algorithm structure and (1−1/e) approximation guarantee build directly on the classical Nemhauser-Wolsey submodular maximization framework, while the NP-hardness proof uses a standard reduction from weighted set cover.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical separation: the aggregate improvement problem admits a simple polynomial-time solution (pick top-k by influence score), while the egalitarian variant is NP-hard, with approximation guarantees that degrade gracefully under weaker assumptions (pairwise independence, group dependence). Practically, the finding that only O(log n) targeted interventions can push network accuracy above 80% has direct implications for resource allocation in distributed security or content-moderation systems. Key limitations include the assumption of uniform improvement capability across agents and restriction to non-negative influence weights, both flagged as future directions; extending to signed graphs or heterogeneous improvement costs would broaden applicability significantly.
