## Problem

While LLMs produce fluent, well-formatted text, there is no principled framework to assess whether their climate-related responses are epistemologically adequate—accurate, specific, complete, and properly conveying scientific uncertainty—versus merely presentational. Additionally, evaluating systems that may approach or exceed human expertise requires scalable oversight protocols that go beyond simple human annotation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240194/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes 300 climate change questions (sourced from Google Trends, Skeptical Science myths, and GPT-4-synthesized Wikipedia paragraphs) paired with 3-4 sentence LLM answers, and produces 8-dimension quality ratings (4 presentational: style, clarity, correctness, tone; 4 epistemological: accuracy, specificity, completeness, uncertainty) on a 5-point agreement scale, each annotated with up to 30 specific issue codes. For epistemological dimensions, raters receive GPT-4-generated critiques grounded in verbatim Wikipedia evidence passages; presentational dimensions are rated without retrieved evidence. Each answer is independently rated by three human raters with climate-related academic backgrounds.

## Method

The paper introduces an evaluation framework grounded in science communication research, operationalizing 8 dimensions and 30 issues through a scalable oversight protocol. The core mechanism is an AI Assistance pipeline: GPT-4 extracts 1-3 keypoints from each answer, identifies a supporting Wikipedia article, retrieves the top-3 most relevant paragraphs per keypoint, and generates a dimension-specific critique that quotes evidence verbatim to support any disagreement. Human raters with relevant education then evaluate the answer alongside this assistance, with the protocol designed so that assistance increases issue detection recall without requiring raters to blindly follow model critiques. The paper also runs a complementary Attribution to Identified Source (AIS) evaluation, finding it largely orthogonal to epistemological ratings. A key empirical finding is a significant gap between presentational and epistemological quality across all evaluated LLMs, and that dimension-aware prompting improves epistemological scores at the cost of presentational ones, revealing an intrinsic trade-off.

## Results

Across 300 climate questions, LLMs achieve high presentational quality (style/clarity/correctness scores of 4.1–4.6) but substantially lower epistemological quality: accuracy ranges from 2.81 (InstructGPT davinci-002) to 3.81 (Falcon-180B-Chat), specificity from 2.49 to 3.15, completeness from 2.26 to 2.73, and uncertainty from 2.05 to 2.55 (all on a 5-point scale). Tone is the weakest presentational dimension (3.06–3.41), and uncertainty is the weakest epistemological dimension (2.05–2.55). Without AI Assistance, GPT-4's epistemological scores inflate dramatically (e.g., accuracy 4.49 vs. 3.67 with assistance), confirming that raters miss issues without grounded support.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven LLMs (GPT-4, ChatGPT, PaLM-2 text-bison, Falcon-180B-Chat, and three InstructGPT variants) on 300 climate questions. Falcon-180B-Chat leads on all four epistemological dimensions (accuracy 3.81, specificity 3.15, completeness 2.73, uncertainty 2.55), outperforming GPT-4 by 0.14 on accuracy and 0.34 on uncertainty. ChatGPT dominates presentational dimensions (style 4.54, clarity 4.56, correctness 4.58) but ranks last or near-last on epistemological scores (completeness 2.26, uncertainty 2.05), revealing a clear trade-off between surface fluency and factual depth. GPT-4 consistently outperforms ChatGPT on epistemological dimensions but trails on most presentational ones. InstructGPT (davinci-002) is the weakest model overall, scoring below 3.0 on accuracy and below 2.5 on specificity. The authors also show that dimension-aware prompting improves GPT-4's epistemological scores (e.g., completeness 2.72→2.97, uncertainty 2.73→2.92) at the cost of presentational quality, further confirming the intrinsic trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scalable oversight literature (Amodei et al., 2016; Saunders et al., 2022) by introducing a practical AI Assistance protocol grounded in Wikipedia evidence, positioned as a single-turn debate where a model critiques an answer for human raters. Conceptually, it draws on science communication research (Jamieson et al., 2017) to define evaluation dimensions, borrowing presentational criteria from the MQM translation-quality framework (Lommel et al., 2013) and epistemological criteria from climate-communication scholarship. It departs from prior climate NLP work, which was limited to text classification (ClimateBERT, ClimaText), by being the first to systematically evaluate generative LLM responses on climate information using a multi-dimensional, expert-rated framework. The paper also positions its epistemological assessment as orthogonal to attribution-based evaluation (AIS, Rashkin et al., 2022), showing the two capture different failure modes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive, science-communication-grounded evaluation of LLMs on climate information, revealing a significant gap between surface fluency and epistemological adequacy that has direct implications for public climate literacy and misinformation risk. The AI Assistance protocol offers a practical, scalable oversight mechanism that could be adapted to other expert-dependent domains, though the paper acknowledges that understanding and mitigating the assistance's broader influence on raters remains an open problem. Key limitations include the restriction to 3–4 sentence answers, the absence of audience-tailored evaluation, and the lack of multimodal assessment. The finding that attribution and epistemological quality are largely orthogonal suggests that deploying RAG or citation mechanisms alone will not resolve LLMs' climate-information shortcomings.
