## Problem

Zero-shot quantization methods generate synthetic calibration data to quantize a full-precision model without access to original training data, but none of them consider the sharpness of the resulting quantized model during data generation. Since flat minima are known to improve generalization, this paper asks whether sharpness-aware criteria can be incorporated into the synthetic data generation process to produce a better-calibrated quantized model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240479/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes only a pre-trained full-precision CNN (ResNet-20/18/50 or MobileNetV2) as input, extracting its BatchNorm statistics and using its forward pass to compute layer-wise reconstruction losses. It produces a quantized model at target bit-widths (2/2, 3/3, 2/4, or 4/4 for weights/activations), with the first and last layers kept at 8 bits. The synthetic calibration set consists of 1,024 images generated by a small generator network parameterized by 256-dimensional embedding vectors, requiring no access to any original training or validation data.

## Method

The key theoretical insight is that minimizing the SAM loss of the quantized model on a hidden validation set is equivalent (under first-order Taylor approximations) to maximizing the cosine similarity between reconstruction-loss gradients computed on the synthetic training set and the real validation set. Since no validation set is available, the authors approximate this by a SAM-like perturbation in the generator's embedding space: for each generated sample, they find the neighbor embedding whose gradient is most dissimilar and minimize the gradient distance to that neighbor, encouraging each sample's gradient to be representative of its local neighborhood. To keep computation tractable, gradient matching is restricted to the final fully-connected layer, with a diagonal Hessian assumption (following AdaRound) that reduces the objective to a simple cosine similarity between per-sample gradients. The final training loss combines BN-statistics matching, a gradient-diversity penalty (encouraging near-orthogonal gradients across samples), and the neighborhood gradient-matching loss, optimized jointly over the generator and embeddings after a BN-loss warm-up phase.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 (ResNet-20), SADAG achieves 65.94% top-1 accuracy at 3/3 bit-width and 69.11% at 4/4, surpassing the previous SOTA Genie by 0.69% and 0.76% respectively. On ImageNet, SADAG consistently outperforms all baselines across ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and MobileNetV2 at 2/2, 2/4, 3/3, and 4/4 bit-widths. The largest gains over Genie appear in the extreme 2/2 setting: +0.77% (ResNet-18), +0.74% (ResNet-50), and +1.08% (MobileNetV2). SADAG operates at approximately 1.5× the speed of Genie, the fastest existing ZSQ method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SADAG outperforms all five baselines across every evaluated setting. Against Genie (the prior SOTA), improvements range from +0.06% to +1.08%, with the largest margins in the extreme 2/2-bit regime where quantization error is most severe. On CIFAR-100 (ResNet-20), SADAG beats Genie by 0.69% (3/3) and 0.76% (4/4). Older methods—Qimera, AdaSG, IntraQ, and AdaDFQ—fall substantially behind, with Qimera collapsing to 1.17% on ImageNet 3/3 (ResNet-18) and AdaSG/AdaDFQ losing 25–30% accuracy relative to Genie in the 3/3 setting. The consistent (if modest) gains over Genie suggest that the sharpness-aware gradient matching criterion provides a meaningful but incremental improvement over BN-statistics-only data generation, particularly when bit-width is very low.

## Relation to Prior Work

SADAG extends the zero-shot quantization (ZSQ) data-generation line initiated by ZeroQ (BN-statistics matching) and advanced by Genie (joint generator-embedding optimization), by introducing sharpness-aware minimization (SAM) as an explicit criterion for synthetic data generation. It departs from prior ZSQ methods (Qimera, HAST, AdaDFQ) that optimize boundary or diversity criteria without a rigorous link to generalization, and from ZSAQ which evaluates sharpness only on generated data rather than the hidden real distribution. The key conceptual departure is the theoretical derivation showing that minimizing the SAM loss over a validation set is equivalent (under Taylor approximations and a diagonal-Hessian assumption) to maximizing gradient matching between synthetic and validation data, which is then approximated via neighborhood gradient matching in the embedding space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical bridge between gradient matching and loss-landscape flatness in the quantization context, giving practitioners a principled (rather than heuristic) reason to optimize synthetic data for sharpness. However, the practical gains are modest (0.69–1.08% over Genie), the method is ~1.5× slower, and two simplifications limit generality: the Hessian is assumed diagonal and only the final fully-connected layer's gradient is used. Open questions include whether the neighborhood-approximation holds for deeper architectures or mixed-precision settings, and whether extending gradient matching to multiple blocks (as the authors suggest) would yield larger gains without prohibitive cost.
