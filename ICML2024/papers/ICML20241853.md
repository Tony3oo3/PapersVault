## Problem

In out-of-distribution generalisation, the choice of generalisation strategy (average-case, worst-case, or interpolation) should ideally be made by the model operator who understands the deployment context, but institutional separation between learners and operators forces the learner to commit to a specific strategy during training. This 'generalisation uncertainty' cannot be resolved by collecting more data, and existing precise learning methods assume alignment between the learner's objective and the operator's goal, which is often impractical or suboptimal.

## Input / Output

The method takes data from d distinct domains (each with its own distribution P_i) and produces an augmented hypothesis h̄_ξ: X × Λ → Y that maps both the input features x and a risk aversion level λ to a prediction. Unlike standard models, the operator specifies λ at deployment time rather than at training time, allowing a single trained model to adapt its behaviour across the full spectrum from average-case (λ=0) to worst-case (λ=1) generalisation. The learner never observes the operator's true λ_op during training.

## Method

The paper introduces Imprecise Domain Generalisation, which uses a credal set K(Z) to represent generalisation uncertainty and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) as the risk aggregation function parameterised by λ. The key architectural innovation is the augmented hypothesis h̄_ξ: X × Λ → Y, which encodes a family of hypotheses indexed by λ, enabling the operator to select their preferred generalisation strategy at deployment. Training is performed via Imprecise Risk Optimisation (IRO), an adaptive gradient method that at each step selects a distribution Q_t over λ to minimise the norm of the aggregated gradient, ensuring C-Pareto improvement (analogous to MGDA in multi-objective optimisation) without requiring the learner to commit to any specific λ. Theoretical results show that any full-support scalarisation Q yields Bayes-optimal models on its support, and the operator excess risk is bounded in O(n^{-1/2} + m^{-1/2}) with high probability.

## Results

On CMNIST, IL (Ours) achieves the lowest maximum regret of 29.7, outperforming ERM (72.7), GrpDRO (46.9), IRM (32.3), VREx (31.5), EQRM (32.2), and Inf-Task (31.7). On synthetic data, IL achieves a maximum regret of 0.867 compared to 1.971–6.177 for precise learners (PL-f) and 0.56 vs. 0.935–1.79 for augmented-hypothesis precise learners (PL-h̄). On UCI Bike Sharing, IL achieves a maximum regret of 0.42, beating PL-f(λ=0) (4.81), PL-f(λ=1) (0.66), and INF-TASK-h̄ (0.72). IL's aggregated risk remains close to the ideal scenario (where learner and operator preferences align) across the full spectrum of operator risk-aversion levels λ_op.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IL achieves the lowest maximum regret (29.7) on CMNIST, beating all six baselines: ERM (72.7), GrpDRO (46.9), IRM (32.3), VREx (31.5), EQRM (32.2), and Inf-Task (31.7). Against ERM, IL dramatically improves on minority-subgroup domains (e=1.0: 70.3 vs. 28.3) and the uncorrelated domain (e=0.5: 69.5 vs. 59.2), while ERM retains a marginal edge on the majority domain (e=0.0: 96.1 vs. 95.8). Against invariance-based methods (IRM, VREx, EQRM), IL substantially outperforms on the majority domain (95.8 vs. 67.8–72.7) while matching or exceeding them on minority domains. Against GrpDRO (worst-case), IL wins on e=0.0 (95.8 vs. 54.1) and e=0.5 (69.5 vs. 64.5) but GrpDRO retains an edge on e=1.0 (75.5 vs. 70.3). Against Inf-Task (the closest augmented-hypothesis baseline), IL wins on e=0.5 (69.5 vs. 63.1) and regret (29.7 vs. 31.7), while Inf-Task has a negligible edge on e=0.0 (96.0 vs. 95.8). On synthetic and Bike Sharing data, IL consistently achieves the lowest maximum regret across all comparisons, confirming that imprecise learning avoids the over-commitment penalty of precise learners when operator preferences are unknown.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the augmented hypothesis / loss-conditional training framework of Brault et al. (2019) and Dosovitskiy & Djolonga (2020) from the precise-learning setting to an imprecise one, where the learner does not commit to a specific generalisation notion (average-case, worst-case, or interpolation) during training. It draws on imprecise probability (credal sets, Walley 1991) and multi-objective optimisation (Pareto optimality) to formalise this imprecision, and adopts CVaR from financial risk management as the aggregation function that parameterises the spectrum between average and worst-case risk. The paper explicitly positions itself against the dominant 'precise generalisation' paradigm (IRM, DRO, QRM, VREx, GrpDRO), arguing that institutional separation between ML engineers and domain operators makes pre-commitment to a single generalisation strategy suboptimal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practically important gap: in real deployments (e.g., medical AI), the person who trains the model is often not the person who operates it, and the operator's risk preferences may be unknown or change over time. The theoretical guarantees (C-Pareto optimality equivalence to Bayes optimality, excess-risk bounds matching ERM rates) provide a principled foundation, while the IRO algorithm offers a concrete training procedure. Key limitations include the assumption that operators can articulate a single risk-aversion level λ at deployment (in practice, preference elicitation may yield a distribution over λ), and the computational overhead of optimising over a continuum of objectives. Open questions include scaling to larger architectures, incorporating additional criteria (fairness, privacy) into the aggregation, and developing efficient preference-elicitation mechanisms at test time.
