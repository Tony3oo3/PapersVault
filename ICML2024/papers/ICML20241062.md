## Problem

Standard GNNs assume the observed graph is accurate and noise-free, yet real graph data suffers from missing or spurious edges and uninformative edge weights. While Bayesian approaches like BGCN model graph uncertainty, they focus on posterior estimation of model parameters rather than jointly learning both the GNN and the graph distribution. This paper asks: can we treat the observed graph as a single realization from a distribution of parametrized graphs and estimate both the GNN parameters and that distribution simultaneously via maximum likelihood?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241062/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an observed graph G with node features x and, for heterogeneous graphs, an edge-type structure that defines the parameter space Λ (a simplex over edge-type weights, or a 2D box of edge-removal/addition probabilities for homogeneous graphs, or a distance threshold range for molecular graphs). The output is a final node embedding z_final computed as the expectation of the GNN output over the learned graph distribution p_T, which is then passed through softmax for classification, used directly for regression, or fed into an energy-based score for OOD detection. Unlike standard GNNs that produce a single embedding from one graph, EMGNN aggregates predictions across multiple graph realizations weighted by their learned likelihoods.

## Method

EMGNN formulates graph learning as maximum likelihood estimation over a distribution of graphs parameterized by latent variables λ, solved via an EM algorithm. In the E-step, the posterior p(λ|X,θ) is defined through a PAC-Bayesian Gibbs posterior proportional to exp(−ηL_X(λ,θ))·p'_0(λ) and approximated via Metropolis-Hastings MCMC. In the M-step, the Q-function reduces to minimizing J(θ) = E_{λ~p_t}[L_X(λ,θ)] − E_{λ~p_0}[L_X(λ,θ)], a difference of expectations that penalizes 'bad' parameter values (where the posterior assigns less mass than the prior) by increasing their loss. This objective is optimized via importance sampling with a proposal distribution q(·) and stochastic gradient descent. Theoretically, the paper proves the feature map is Hölder-continuous with respect to the Wasserstein distance between graph distributions (Theorem 1) and establishes a convergence rate for the randomized M-step under convexity and smoothness assumptions (Theorem 2).

## Results

On heterogeneous graphs, EM-GCN[uut] achieves the best Micro-F1 on all three datasets: 64.78% on IMDB, 92.70% on ACM, and 95.06% on DBLP, outperforming the strongest baselines (GAT, SeHGNN, Simple-HGN) by 1.2–2.4 points. On homogeneous graphs with limited labels (5–20 per class), EM-GCN surpasses BGCN and DropEdge across Cora, Citeseer, and Pubmed, e.g., 82.24% on Cora-20 vs. 81.37% (GCN) and 81.18% (BGCN). On heterophilic graphs, EM-GCN dramatically outperforms BGCN (79.46% vs. 57.96% on Texas), and the EM-ACM variant (using ACM-GCN+ backbone) reaches 88.38% on Texas, exceeding the SOTA backbone alone (86.76%). On molecular regression, EM-GCN* achieves the lowest RMSE on FreeSolv (0.936) and ESOL (0.606) under random split, beating GCN, MPNN, and AttentiveFP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EM-GCN[uut] wins on IMDB against all seven baselines, beating the closest competitor GAT (63.54) by 1.24 Micro-F1 points and SeHGNN (62.13) by 2.65 points. On ACM, EM-GCN[uut] (92.70 Micro-F1) is essentially tied with Simple-HGN (92.73) and GAT (92.61), while on DBLP it clearly leads with 95.06 vs. SeHGNN's 94.86. For homogeneous graphs, EM-GCN consistently outperforms BGCN (the most relevant Bayesian baseline) and DropEdge across all label-scarcity settings, with the largest gap on Pubmed-5 (74.03 vs. 70.51 for BGCN). On heterophilic graphs, EM-GCN's advantage over BGCN is dramatic (e.g., 79.46 vs. 57.96 on Texas), and the EM-ACM variant surpasses its own SOTA backbone ACM-GCN+ (88.38 vs. 86.76 on Texas). For molecular regression, EM-GCN* achieves the best RMSE on FreeSolv (0.936 vs. 1.157 for GCN) and ESOL (0.606 vs. 0.652 for GCN) under random split, though the gap narrows under scaffold split where GCN is more competitive on Lipophilicity (0.760 vs. 0.743 for EM-GCN*).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distributional graph signal processing framework of Ji et al. (2023d), which defined convolution as an expectation over a distribution of graph shift operators, by replacing the polynomial filter with a full GNN model and wrapping the joint estimation in an EM algorithm. It departs from the Bayesian framework of BGCN (Zhang et al., 2019), which estimates a posterior over model parameters, by instead formulating the problem as maximum likelihood estimation where the E-step uses a PAC-Bayesian Gibbs posterior (Guedj, 2019) to sample from the graph distribution via MCMC. Unlike GTN or GAT, which learn a single weighted aggregation for inference, EMGNN maintains a full distribution of graphs at test time, and unlike DropEdge or RSGNN, which apply fixed or learned perturbations, it learns the perturbation distribution end-to-end.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, interpretable way to handle structural uncertainty in graphs: the learned distribution over edge-type weights or edge-addition/removal probabilities reveals which relations matter most (e.g., MA edges dominate MD in IMDB), offering diagnostic value beyond black-box performance gains. A stated limitation is that the additional benefit over attention-based backbones is smaller than over non-attention backbones, leaving open how to more effectively synergize the distribution-of-graphs idea with attention mechanisms. The framework is backbone-agnostic and demonstrated on heterogeneous, homogeneous, heterophilic, and molecular graphs, suggesting broad applicability, though the MCMC-based E-step introduces computational overhead that may limit scalability to very large graphs.
