## Problem

Deep neural networks for image super-resolution are widely criticized as 'black boxes' because, unlike traditional interpolation or filtering methods with solid mathematical foundations, their internal mechanisms remain unexplained. This paper bridges that gap by applying signal processing theory to interpret what ISR networks actually do, revealing that they internally behave as low-pass filters augmented by a non-linear high-frequency injector.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240419/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the ISR task, the method takes a low-resolution RGB image and produces a high-resolution output at integer scales (×2 to ×12) or arbitrary scales. For the interpretability analysis, a Dirac delta impulse image (an 11×11 image with a single white pixel) is fed through the network to extract its impulse response. The HyRA decomposition then yields two intermediate outputs for any input: the linear response H(I) (the low-pass-filtered component) and the non-linear response G(I) (the high-frequency injection and distortion compensation). The FSDS metric additionally takes a pair of HR and SR images, computes their 2D FFT power distribution maps, and outputs a single log-scale similarity score in dB.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is Hybrid Response Analysis (HyRA), which decomposes any ISR network N(I) into a parallel sum of a linear system H(I) and a non-linear system G(I) with the constraint that G's impulse response is zero, making the decomposition unique. The key empirical finding—the 'sinc phenomenon'—is that the impulse response of mainstream ISR backbones (EDSR, RDN, SwinIR, HAT, etc.) closely resembles a 2D sinc function, confirming that H(I) functions as a learned low-pass filter. The non-linear component G(I) = N(I) − I * H(δ) is shown to inject high-frequency details from learned priors and to cancel grid-like distortions introduced by the windowed (non-ideal) low-pass filter. To quantify the injected high frequencies, the authors propose FSDS, a metric that normalizes both images, computes their 2D FFT, integrates the power distribution maps, and reports the log-ratio of the difference energy to the reference energy, capturing frequency-domain distortions invisible to PSNR, SSIM, or LPIPS.

## Results

On the DIV2K validation set, GRLBase achieves the best PSNR (35.66 dB at ×2, 31.93 dB at ×3, 29.91 dB at ×4), SSIM, and LPIPS among all tested methods at standard scales. However, the proposed FSDS metric reveals a different ranking: SwinIR-classical achieves the highest FSDS (40.37 dB at ×2, 35.13 dB at ×3, 32.37 dB at ×4), outperforming GRLBase (39.99/33.84/31.12 dB). A vanilla low-pass filter baseline achieves PSNR 31.40 dB and SSIM 0.87 at ×2, establishing a performance floor below which neural networks should not fall. At larger scales (×6, ×12), RDN-LTE leads in PSNR/SSIM while RDN-LIIF leads in LPIPS and FSDS, and FSDS rankings converge with traditional metrics as high-frequency loss becomes more severe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central finding is that FSDS produces meaningfully different rankings than PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS. At ×2–×4 scales, GRLBase dominates PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS (e.g., 35.66 dB PSNR vs. SwinIR's 35.34 dB), yet SwinIR-classical achieves the highest FSDS (40.37 vs. 39.99 dB at ×2), suggesting SwinIR better preserves the frequency spectrum distribution. At ×6 and ×12, where high-frequency loss is severe, FSDS rankings converge with traditional metrics (RDN-LIIF leads both LPIPS and FSDS), confirming that FSDS is most discriminative when differences are subtle. The low-pass filter experiment (Fig. 8) shows that a simple sinc-based filter achieves PSNR 31.40 dB and SSIM 0.87 at ×2, providing a theoretical lower bound; all neural networks exceed this, but the gap between the best network and the filter is largely attributable to the non-linear component's high-frequency injection. Impulse response experiments reveal that networks with sinc-like impulse responses (RDN, RCAN) tend to achieve better FSDS scores, while GAN-trained networks (ESRGAN, SwinIR-Real) deviate from the sinc pattern, linking the sinc phenomenon to the choice of loss function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes classical signal processing theory (impulse responses, low-pass filtering, sinc functions) to interpret the internal behavior of deep ISR networks, carving new ground at the intersection of signal processing and neural network interpretability. It is conceptually adjacent to the Frequency Principle (Xu et al., 2019, 2020), which showed networks learn low-to-high frequency, but departs by using impulse response analysis (HyRA) rather than training dynamics to decompose network behavior into linear and non-linear components. Unlike prior interpretability methods (integrated gradients, LAM) that rely on attribution maps or manually chosen baselines, HyRA provides a unique, constraint-based decomposition (zero impulse response for the non-linear part) that is directly computable from a single impulse input. The FSDS metric extends the evaluation landscape beyond PSNR/SSIM/LPIPS by introducing a frequency-spectrum-distribution perspective that no prior image quality metric has addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sinc phenomenon provides a concrete, testable bridge between classical signal processing and deep learning, suggesting that well-performing ISR networks implicitly learn a low-pass filter as their linear backbone — a finding that could guide future network design (e.g., explicitly regularizing the linear component toward ideal sinc behavior). The FSDS metric fills a genuine gap in ISR evaluation by being sensitive to high-frequency distortions that PSNR and SSIM miss, as demonstrated by the steganography and JPEG compression toy examples. However, the analysis is primarily qualitative (visual inspection of impulse responses) rather than rigorously quantified, and the framework's applicability beyond ISR (e.g., denoising, inpainting) remains unexplored. The observation that adversarial-loss-trained networks (ESRGAN, SwinIR-Real) deviate from the sinc pattern raises an open question about how training objectives shape the linear/non-linear decomposition.
