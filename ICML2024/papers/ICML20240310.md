## Problem

Existing explanation metrics (plausibility, faithfulness) do not capture whether a natural language explanation enables humans to correctly predict a model's behavior on *unseen* counterfactual inputs, leaving open whether LLMs actually explain themselves. This paper introduces counterfactual simulatability as a new evaluation criterion: can a human, after reading one explanation, accurately infer the model's outputs on diverse related questions?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240310/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a model's explanation and output on an input question plus a set of LLM-generated counterfactual questions (10 per example for StrategyQA, 6 for SHP) and produces two scalar scores: simulation precision (fraction of simulatable counterfactuals where the human's inferred answer matches the model's actual answer) and simulation generality (one minus the average pairwise similarity among simulatable counterfactuals, computed via BLEU, cosine, or Jaccard). The human simulation step is operationalized as a logical entailment judgment—does the tuple (explanation, output, input) entail a specific answer for the counterfactual—reducing subjectivity, and is performed by human annotators on StrategyQA or by GPT-4 as a validated proxy on SHP.

## Method

The framework generates counterfactuals by prompting LLMs to write follow-up questions that are 'likely simulatable' from the given explanation, then filters to retain only those where a human can logically infer the model's output. Simulation precision measures the fraction of simulatable counterfactuals where the human's inferred answer matches the model's actual answer; simulation generality measures the diversity of simulatable counterfactuals as one minus average pairwise similarity. The framework benchmarks GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 under Chain-of-Thought and Post-Hoc explanation methods on StrategyQA (multi-hop factual reasoning) and Stanford Human Preference (reward modeling). Key findings: LLM explanations achieve only ~80% precision on binary tasks, CoT does not substantially outperform Post-Hoc, GPT-4 outperforms GPT-3 by ~6 points, and simulation precision shows near-zero correlation with plausibility—implying that RLHF-style optimization of human preference may not improve counterfactual simulatability.

## Results

On simulation precision (the fraction of counterfactuals where a human's inferred output matches the model's actual output), GPT-4 explanations achieve 81.1–83.9% on StrategyQA and 91.5–93.0% on SHP, consistently outperforming GPT-3 by +5.5 and +6.5 points respectively (p < 0.002). CoT and Post-Hoc explanations show no significant difference in precision (CoT leads by 1.2 points on StrategyQA but trails by 1.3 on SHP). Human-written explanations reach 91.5% precision on StrategyQA, 8.7 points above GPT-4's 82.8% (p < 0.001), indicating substantial room for improvement in LLM-generated explanations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PolyJuice (Wu et al., 2021), the paper's LLM-prompted counterfactual generation produces significantly more diverse simulatable counterfactuals, with a 68% relative improvement in diversity over PolyJuice on StrategyQA (p < 0.001 across BLEU, Cosine, and Jaccard metrics); mixing GPT-3 and GPT-4 outputs further improves diversity by 12%. For explanation methods, CoT (Wei et al., 2022) and Post-Hoc (Camburu et al., 2018) are essentially tied in simulation precision, with CoT edging ahead by only 1.2 points on StrategyQA while trailing by 1.3 points on SHP, contradicting the intuition that CoT should be more faithful. GPT-4 explanations outperform GPT-3 by 5.5–6.5 precision points across both tasks, suggesting scaling helps but does not close the gap to human-written explanations (91.5% vs. 82.8% on StrategyQA). Notably, simulation precision shows near-zero correlation with plausibility (Spearman +0.021) and with simulation generality, meaning that optimizing for human preference (e.g., via RLHF) or explanation breadth does not guarantee more accurate mental models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the simulatability evaluation framework (Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017; Hase & Bansal, 2020; Chandrasekaran et al., 2018) from the explained input to diverse counterfactuals, explicitly noting that prior simulatability metrics suffer from output leakage when evaluated on the same input. It repurposes LLM-based evaluation practices (G-Eval, GPTScore) to automate the human simulation step, and uses LLM prompting for counterfactual generation as an alternative to PolyJuice's lexical/semantic perturbations. The paper positions itself against both plausibility and faithfulness metrics, arguing that simulatability is the appropriate lens because explanations must be consumed by humans to form mental models, and that counterfactual simulatability specifically requires explanations to be generalizable rather than merely accurate on one input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that simulation precision does not correlate with plausibility (Pearson +0.012) directly challenges the assumption that RLHF-style optimization of human preference will produce explanations that help users build accurate mental models of LLM behavior. This has practical implications for high-stakes deployment where users need to know when a model will generalize correctly. Open questions include extending the framework to open-ended generation tasks (where multiple valid outputs complicate simulation), studying interactive/multi-round explanation settings, and whether self-training or RL on simulatability scores can close the gap between LLM and human-written explanation precision (82.8 vs. 91.5 on StrategyQA).
