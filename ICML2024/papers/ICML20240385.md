## Problem

Certified robustness mechanisms guarantee the absence of adversarial examples within a bounded region, but the paper asks whether releasing these certifications paradoxically reduces security by giving attackers a lower bound on where adversarial examples must exist. Prior work had not quantitatively demonstrated that certifications could be exploited to construct smaller, harder-to-detect adversarial perturbations, leaving the tightness of certification bounds and the true risk to certified models poorly understood.

## Input / Output

The attack consumes a clean input image (28×28 grayscale MNIST, 32×32 RGB CIFAR-10, or 224×224 RGB ImageNet), the model's per-sample certified radius computed via randomised smoothing (or IBP), the additive noise level σ, and white-box model gradients. It outputs a norm-minimising adversarial example whose own certification is non-zero (a 'confident' adversarial example), along with the perturbation norm. Unusually, the certification at every visited point during the iterative search is fed back into the step-size computation, making the certificate an active input to the attack loop rather than a static guarantee.

## Method

The Certification Aware Attack (CAA) operates in two stages. In the search stage, it minimises the gap between the top-2 class expectations while preserving the correct label, but sets each gradient step size to exceed the certified radius at the current point and the union of certified radii at all previously visited points sharing the same class prediction—enabling larger, more informative jumps that skip over provably safe regions. In the refinement stage, once an adversarial example is found, the attack moves toward the original input while remaining inside the certified radii of previously identified adversarial examples, exploiting the fact that any point within a certified ball around an adversarial example is itself adversarial. The non-differentiable argmax layer is handled via a Gumbel-Softmax reparameterisation, and the Monte Carlo class expectations (with Goodman confidence intervals) are used in place of individual noisy draws to make gradient-based optimisation tractable. This yields adversarial examples 74% more often than comparable attacks with a >10% reduction in median perturbation norm.

## Results

The Certification Aware Attack produces the smallest adversarial perturbation for 73–97% of successfully attacked samples across MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet (σ=0.5–1.0), compared to 0–39% for all baselines. The median attack size (r₅₀) is 11% smaller on MNIST, 12% on CIFAR-10, and 52% on ImageNet relative to the next-best method, with a 24% average reduction in %-C (percentage distance to the certified radius) over the runner-up. Attack time is 0.34–4.49 s per sample, roughly 6–70× faster than PGD, Carlini-Wagner, or AutoAttack on the same datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PGD (Madry et al., 2018), the Certification Aware Attack dominates on the Best metric (73–97% vs. 3–39%) and %-C (82–157 vs. 77–211), while PGD achieves slightly higher success rates on some configurations (e.g., 99% vs. 97% on MNIST σ=1.0). Carlini-Wagner (2017) is outperformed by a wide margin on every metric except raw success rate: its median attack size is 2.7–29× larger (r₅₀ = 5.36–33.22 vs. 1.14–2.23) and its %-C reaches 3451 on ImageNet. AutoAttack (Croce & Hein, 2020) is the slowest baseline (27–51 s per sample) and produces the largest perturbations among the iterative methods, with Best at 0–3% across all settings. DeepFool (Moosavi-Dezfooli et al., 2016) is the fastest (0.07–2.88 s) but fails to produce norm-minimising examples in most settings (Best = 0–3%, success rate as low as 9% on MNIST σ=0.5), making it the weakest competitor overall. The paper's method is the only one that consistently stays within ~100% of the certified radius (%-C ≤ 157), confirming that exploiting certification information yields substantially smaller, harder-to-detect adversarial perturbations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the randomized smoothing certification framework of Cohen et al. (2019) and the pointwise certification approach of Cullen et al. (2022), but departs fundamentally from prior adversarial attack literature by treating the certification itself as an exploitable signal. While PGD, Carlini-Wagner, AutoAttack, and DeepFool are well-established test-time attacks, none incorporate certification information into their search process; Cohen et al. (2019) briefly considered test-time attacks against certified models but explicitly noted their attack did not align with the concept and provided no quantitative evaluation. The paper introduces the 'Certification Aware Attack' as a new framework that uses certified radii as lower bounds to control step sizes (Section 4.1) and to refine adversarial examples while staying within certified regions (Section 4.2), formalizing the notion of a 'confident adversarial attack' where the adversarial point itself retains a non-zero certification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that releasing robustness certifications paradoxically reduces security by enabling smaller, harder-to-detect adversarial examples—challenges a core deployment assumption in the certified robustness community and suggests certifications must be treated as confidential. The paper provides a practical framework for assessing the tightness of certification bounds (attacks as upper bounds vs. certifications as lower bounds), which is valuable for certification researchers. Open questions include whether the mitigation of withholding certifications is sufficient against surrogate-model attacks, how the approach extends to Lipschitz certification and other frameworks, and whether the class-consistency constraint in the refinement stage (all adversarial examples must share the first identified adversarial class) limits performance on high-cardinality tasks like ImageNet.
