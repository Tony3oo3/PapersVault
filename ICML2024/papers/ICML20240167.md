## Problem

Existing stability evaluation methods for machine learning models predominantly address sub-population shifts (changes in probability mass) while ignoring data corruptions (changes in sample support), or vice versa. In practice, both types of distribution shift occur simultaneously, yet no unified criterion quantifies the minimum perturbation needed to degrade a model's risk to a prescribed threshold across both perturbation types. This paper asks: how do we evaluate a model's stability under arbitrary distributional perturbations in a single, principled framework?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained model f_β, an empirical dataset of n labeled samples, a chosen loss function (cross-entropy, 0/1, or piecewise linear), a risk threshold r, and two hyperparameters θ₁, θ₂ (constrained by 1/θ₁ + 1/θ₂ = C) that trade off data-corruption cost against density-shift cost. The distance function d(z, ẑ) = ‖x − x̂‖² + ∞·|y − ŷ| enforces that labels are immutable while covariates may be perturbed. Beyond the scalar stability measure R(β, r), the method outputs the full most-sensitive distribution Q* (a reweighted and relocated version of the training data) and, via a modified distance function, per-feature stability scores R_i(β, r) that rank which input dimensions most threaten model reliability.

## Method

The paper frames stability evaluation as a projection problem: find the minimum OT-discrepancy distance (with moment constraints) from the lifted training distribution P₀ ⊗ δ₁ to any distribution Q in the joint (sample, density) space whose induced risk exceeds threshold r. A strong duality theorem (Theorem 1) converts this infinite-dimensional problem into a finite-dimensional convex dual, and for the cost function c((z,w),(ẑ,ŵ)) = θ₁·w·d(z,ẑ) + θ₂·(φ(w)−φ(ŵ))₊, the inner minimization over (z,w) admits closed-form solutions via conjugate functions. With KL divergence (φ(t)=t log t − t + 1), the dual reduces to a one-dimensional convex problem in h solvable by conic programming for piecewise-linear losses or by iterative gradient ascent for nonlinear losses; with χ² divergence, it becomes a two-dimensional convex problem. The resulting most-sensitive distribution Q* simultaneously relocates samples (data corruption) and reweights them (sub-population shift), with the balance controlled by θ₁ and θ₂, enabling both model-level stability comparison and feature-level sensitivity ranking.

## Results

On the ACS Income task (California, 2,000 train / 2,000 eval), the MLP model achieves 83% accuracy while LR achieves 82%, yet the worst-performing racial group accuracy drops to 72% for MLP (vs. 82% overall). On ACS PubCov, MLP outperforms LR (71% vs. 67%). The stability measure (Figure 3c) shows that adversarial training (AT) yields the highest stability under data-corruption-dominant settings (small θ₁), while Tilted ERM dominates under sub-population-shift-dominant settings (small θ₂), with both substantially outperforming ERM. Targeted AT—guided by the paper's feature stability analysis to perturb only the identified sensitive feature—achieves the largest improvement in worst-group accuracy across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three training paradigms—ERM, Adversarial Training (Sinha et al., ICLR 2018), and Tilted ERM (Li et al., JMLR 2023)—using the proposed OT-based stability criterion across varying θ₁/θ₂ trade-off settings. AT (Sinha et al.) achieves the highest stability measure when data corruptions dominate (small θ₁), consistent with its design to resist input perturbations, but its advantage diminishes under sub-population-shift-dominant settings. Tilted ERM (Li et al.) shows the reverse pattern: it excels when sub-population shifts dominate (small θ₂) due to its log-sum-exp loss prioritizing high-risk samples, but is less effective against data corruptions. Both robust methods substantially outperform ERM across all settings, confirming that ERM-trained models are the most fragile. The paper's key practical contribution is that its feature stability analysis identifies the specific sensitive feature (e.g., 'American Indian' race, 'Age'), enabling a targeted AT intervention that perturbs only that feature; this targeted approach outperforms standard AT (which perturbs all features) in worst-group accuracy, demonstrating that the evaluation criterion provides actionable guidance beyond what the baselines offer on their own.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the OT discrepancy with moment constraints framework from Blanchet et al. (2023), repurposing it from distributionally robust optimization to model stability evaluation. It extends the stability evaluation line of work by Li et al. (2021), Namkoong et al. (2022), and Gupta & Rothenhaeusler (2023), which considered only sub-population shifts, by unifying both data corruptions and sub-population shifts in a single projection problem over the joint (sample, density) space. The key technical contribution is a strong duality theorem that renders the infinite-dimensional problem tractable, yielding closed-form dual reformulations for KL and chi-squared divergences and finite convex programs for piecewise-linear losses. The computational approach for nonlinear losses borrows from adversarial training (Sinha et al., 2018), with the outer loop optimizing over a dual variable h rather than model parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified framework provides practitioners with a single, interpretable metric to assess how much perturbation (of either type) a model can tolerate before its risk exceeds a threshold, and the feature-stability variant offers a principled diagnostic for identifying which specific features drive instability—demonstrated by uncovering racial bias in income prediction models. A key practical insight is that robust methods optimized for one shift type (e.g., AT for corruptions, Tilted ERM for sub-population shifts) do not automatically generalize to the other, motivating targeted interventions. Limitations include evaluation only on tabular data with simple models (LR, two-layer MLP); the authors note that extension to modern architectures (Transformers, tree-based ensembles) and LLMs remains open. The framework also assumes a fixed risk threshold and does not address the statistical uncertainty of the stability estimate itself beyond noting increased variance as θ₁→∞.
