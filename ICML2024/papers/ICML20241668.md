## Problem

Entropy minimization (EM) is the backbone of most test-time adaptation methods, yet the mechanism behind its success and eventual failure remained unexplained. The prevailing intuition—that EM works by boosting confidence on already-correct predictions—was shown to be only partly true, as EM retains its efficacy even when all initially correct samples are removed. This paper identifies the missing mechanistic explanation and leverages it to solve the practical problem of estimating a model's accuracy on an arbitrary unlabeled dataset.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained classifier (ResNet-50, ViT-B/16, MaxViT-T, etc.) and a stream of unlabeled RGB test images. During 1,000 iterations of RDumb adaptation, it records the top-1 prediction for the first 1,000 images at iteration 0 and at iteration 1,000, then computes a single scalar: the Weighted Flips score (sum of confidence percentiles for images whose label changed). This scalar is passed through a fixed quadratic function f(x) = 0.00036x² − 0.32x + 75.66 to yield a percentage accuracy estimate. The entire process adds only 20 extra forward passes per 1,000 adaptation steps.

## Method

The paper first reveals a two-phase clustering dynamic: during early EM iterations, test-image embeddings form tighter clusters (rising Silhouette score) while remaining aligned with training-data class centroids (low Shift distance), which increases accuracy; after many iterations, clusters drift away from the training embedding space (rising Shift distance), causing accuracy to degrade. Building on this, the Weighted Flips (WF) method exploits the observation that high-accuracy datasets are already well-clustered at iteration 0, so EM produces few label flips, whereas low-accuracy datasets are poorly clustered and produce many flips. Each flip is weighted by the initial confidence percentile of the affected image, making high-confidence misclassifications contribute more to the score. The weighted flip count is mapped to accuracy via a quadratic interpolation f fitted on IN-C holdout noises and ImageNet validation, requiring no access to test labels, training data, or architecture-specific tuning. WF achieves a mean absolute error of 5.75% across 23 ImageNet-scale datasets, a 29.62% relative improvement over the prior state-of-the-art (COT).

## Results

The Weighted Flips (WF) method achieves a mean absolute error (MAE) of 5.75% in estimating model accuracy across 23 ImageNet-scale datasets, reducing the relative error by 29.62% over the previous state-of-the-art (COT, 8.17% MAE). In the worst case, WF cuts the error by 29.74% compared to COT (21.61% vs. 30.38%). WF is either best or second-best in 18 of 23 datasets, while COT achieves top-two rankings in only 12. The method requires only 20 additional forward passes per 1,000 RDumb iterations and works across architectures (ResNet, ResNeXt, ViT, MaxViT) without refitting the accuracy-estimation function.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WF (5.75% MAE) outperforms the previous state-of-the-art COT (8.17% MAE) by a 29.62% relative margin on average, and by 29.74% in the worst case (21.61% vs. 30.38%). Against the confidence-based baselines, WF is dramatically superior: AC (26.02%) and DoC (22.29%) are the weakest methods, while ATC (11.81%) is intermediate but still roughly 2x worse than WF. WF is best or second-best in 18 of 23 datasets, whereas COT—despite being the runner-up—achieves top-two rankings in only 12 datasets, indicating WF's consistency across diverse shift types (noise, domain, adversarial, OOD classes). Notably, COT excels on specific structured datasets like IN-Obfuscations (0.12% vs. WF's 4.58%) and IN-D Sketch (0.71% vs. 5.44%), but WF dominates on the majority of challenging real-world shifts including IN-A, IN-Hard, ObjectNet, and NINCO.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the entropy minimization (EM) framework for test-time adaptation established by Tent (Wang et al., 2020) and its successors (RPL, CPL, EATA, RDumb), but shifts the focus from designing better EM variants to explaining the mechanism behind EM's success and failure. Conceptually, it draws on the neural collapse literature (Papyan et al., 2020; Han et al., 2021) to frame EM as an unsupervised clustering process in embedding space, and connects it formally to the Expectation-Maximization algorithm for Gaussian mixtures (Grandvalet & Bengio, 2004; Poland & Shachter, 1993). For the practical accuracy-estimation application, it positions itself against confidence-based (AC, DoC), threshold-based (ATC), and optimal-transport (COT) estimators, departing from all of them by leveraging the dynamics of EM adaptation itself rather than static model outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The two-phase explanation (clustering + alignment with training embeddings = success; clustering + drift from training embeddings = failure) provides the first mechanistic account of why EM-based TTA methods work briefly and then collapse, which could inform better stopping criteria and regularization strategies for the entire family of EM-based TTA methods. The Weighted Flips method offers a practical, low-overhead tool for estimating model accuracy without labels, but its reliance on a fitted quadratic function f and its somewhat degraded performance on certain datasets (e.g., NINCO, Cartoon & Drawing) suggest it is not yet a universal solution. Open questions include whether the two-phase dynamics generalize to non-EM TTA methods, whether the alignment-with-training-embeddings insight can be directly exploited as a regularizer, and whether the method extends cleanly to non-ImageNet-scale or non-visual domains.
