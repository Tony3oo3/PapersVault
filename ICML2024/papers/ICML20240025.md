## Problem

While flat minima have shown strong empirical success in generalization (e.g., via SAM), the literature lacked a formal definition of flatness and, critically, no complexity analysis of algorithms that find such minima. This paper asks: can one find flat minima using only first-order gradient information, and what is the iteration complexity? The surprising answer is yes—gradients from randomly perturbed iterates implicitly encode the gradient of the Hessian trace, enabling flat-minima search without explicit second-derivative access.

## Input / Output

The algorithms take a four-times continuously differentiable loss f, an initial point x₀ already within a ζ-neighborhood of the local minima set X* (achievable in Õ(ε⁻²) gradient queries under strict-saddle assumptions), and for Algorithm 2, per-sample training losses f_i. The output is an (ε, √ε)-flat local minimum: a point within ε of X* whose projected gradient of the normalized Hessian trace along the minima manifold has norm at most √ε. Notably, both algorithms require only gradient evaluations (no Hessian or higher-order derivatives), and Algorithm 2 additionally consumes stochastic per-sample gradients and a random sign σ_t ∈ {±1} at each perturbation step.

## Method

The core mechanism is a perturbed gradient step: when the gradient norm drops below a tolerance ε₀, the algorithm adds a direction v_t = Proj_{∇f(x_t)}^⊥ ∇f(x_t + ρ·direction) to the standard gradient update. The key insight is that the second-order Taylor term of the perturbed gradient, averaged over the random direction, yields (ρ²/2)·∇tr(∇²f(x_t)) in expectation—so the perturbation implicitly descends the Hessian trace along the minima manifold while the orthogonal projection prevents loss increase. Algorithm 1 (Randomly Smoothed Perturbation) uses a uniform random unit direction g_t ~ Unif(S^{d-1}) and achieves O(ε⁻³) iterations. Algorithm 2 (Sharpness-Aware Perturbation) instead perturbs along the normalized stochastic gradient direction ∇f_i(x_t)/‖∇f_i(x_t)‖ with a random sign, inspired by SAM; this exploits the per-sample Hessian decomposition at minima to achieve a d-times larger decrease in Hessian trace per step, yielding O(d⁻¹ε⁻²(1 ∨ d⁻³ε⁻¹)) iterations—a significant speedup in high dimensions.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical contribution is Table 1, which establishes that the Randomly Smoothed Perturbation algorithm finds an (ε, √ε)-flat minimum in O(ε⁻³) gradient queries for general loss functions, while the Sharpness-Aware Perturbation algorithm achieves the same in O(d⁻¹ε⁻²(1 ∨ 1/(d³ε))) queries for training losses — a d·ε⁻¹·min{1, εd³} improvement. In experiments on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, both algorithms escape a sharp global minimizer (100% train accuracy, 48% test accuracy) while maintaining 100% training accuracy. The Sharpness-Aware Perturbation (SA) method is more effective than Randomly Smoothed Perturbation (RS) even with a smaller perturbation radius, and batch size B=1 proves most effective at escaping sharp minima, consistent with the theoretical d-dependence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external flat-minima methods (e.g., SAM, Entropy-SGD) in a head-to-head experimental comparison. Instead, the primary comparison is between the two proposed algorithms: Sharpness-Aware Perturbation (SA) vs. Randomly Smoothed Perturbation (RS) on CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, both initialized at a sharp global minimizer (48% test accuracy). SA outperforms RS in escaping sharp minima even with a smaller perturbation radius ρ, consistent with the theoretical d-factor speedup. A plain SGD baseline (no perturbation) fails to escape the sharp minimum at the chosen learning rate η=0.001, confirming the necessity of the perturbation mechanism. Among SA variants, batch size B=1 is most effective at improving test accuracy, followed by B=64 and B=128, aligning with the theory's prediction that smaller batches (more stochasticity) accelerate the decrease in trace of Hessian.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the decade-long line of work on complexity of finding stationary points (Ghadimi & Lan, 2013; Ge et al., 2015; Carmon et al., 2018) to a new target: approximate flat minima defined via the trace of the Hessian. Algorithm 1 repurposes randomized smoothing (Duchi et al., 2012) in a novel way—revealing that it implicitly seeks flatness—while Algorithm 2 is directly inspired by SAM (Foret et al., 2021), with the key analytical step (Hessian eigenvectors aligning with per-sample gradient directions) drawn from Wen et al. (2022). The paper also connects to the edge-of-stability literature (Arora et al., 2022; Damian et al., 2023) for the principle of estimating higher-order derivatives from first-order information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal complexity analysis of finding flat minima, showing that only gradient queries suffice (O(ε⁻³) for general losses, O(d⁻¹ε⁻²) for training losses), which provides theoretical grounding for the empirical success of SAM and related methods. Open questions include lower bounds for flat-minima finding, whether other flatness notions (e.g., effective basin size) admit similar first-order algorithms, and whether the suboptimal δ-dependence can be improved. The limited experimental validation (single architecture, single dataset) leaves open whether the theoretical speedup from stochastic gradients generalizes broadly in practice.
