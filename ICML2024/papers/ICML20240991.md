## Problem

Prior mechanistic interpretability work has shown that neuron-level analysis can recover the algorithms implemented by networks on simple tasks like modular arithmetic, but it remains unclear whether such techniques can extract meaningful domain knowledge from models trained on real scientific data. This paper asks whether high-dimensional neural networks trained to reproduce nuclear data learn low-dimensional representations that faithfully encode human-derived physics concepts, and whether decoding those representations can yield new scientific insight beyond the model's predictive performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240991/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes proton number Z and neutron number N as integer tokens embedded into a 2048-dimensional space, and predicts up to ten continuous nuclear observables (binding energy, charge radius, and six separation energies) via MSE loss. Beyond these regression targets, the interpretability pipeline produces PCA projections of embeddings and penultimate-layer activations, parametric helix fits with per-parameter perturbation analyses, and symbolic regression formulas derived from the learned features. The key interpretability output is not a prediction but a geometric structure — a helix in embedding space — whose parameters (pitch, radius, frequency) map onto specific terms of the semi-empirical mass formula.

## Method

The architecture is a 2-layer transformer with fixed uniform attention, SiLU activations, residual connections, and a linear readout, trained with MSE loss in a multi-task setting across binding energy, charge radius, and separation energies. The central interpretability tool is latent space topography (LST): projecting embeddings or penultimate-layer activations onto their first k principal components, then sweeping through a 2D PC subspace while fixing remaining components to visualize how the model output varies across that subspace. To decode the helix structure in 3D PCA projections of N and Z embeddings, the authors fit a parametric helix (radius, pitch, frequency, phase) and systematically perturb each parameter, showing that pitch maps to the volume term, radius to the asymmetry term, and frequency to the periodic arc structure of the SEMF. For penultimate-layer features, cosine similarity matching against known physics terms (volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, pairing, shell corrections) identifies which principal components correspond to which physical contributions, recovering the liquid-drop model and hints of shell-model corrections purely from network activations.

## Results

The paper's headline quantitative result is that symbolic regression over the neural network's penultimate-layer features recovers a binding-energy formula with ~4,600 keV RMS, substantially outperforming the Semi-Empirical Mass Formula (SEMF) at ~8,000 keV on the same dataset. Multi-task training (predicting all nuclear observables jointly) yields consistent improvements over single-task training across every observable (binding energy, charge radius, separation energies), as shown in Figure 12. All models fit the training data to within tens of keV, and the first few principal components of the penultimate layer recover most of the model's predictive performance (Figure 4). The interpretability analysis identifies a helix structure in the embeddings that geometrically encodes the SEMF terms, a parity split corresponding to the pairing term, and a staircase pattern matching nuclear shell-model magic numbers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against other ML architectures or interpretability methods; its comparisons are against human-derived physics models. The neural network's learned representations are matched (via cosine similarity of PCA-projected penultimate-layer features) to individual SEMF terms: PC0 aligns with the volume term, PC6 with the pairing term, and PC4 with the shell-model correction, demonstrating that the network independently recovers the liquid-drop model and hints of the shell model. The symbolic expression extracted from the NN features (~4,600 keV RMS) outperforms both the SEMF (~8,000 keV) and direct symbolic regression over the raw data, suggesting the network's internal decomposition makes the regression problem easier. Single-task versus multi-task training shows that multi-task learning improves generalization on all observables, with the binding-energy task being the primary driver of the helix structure in embeddings. The paper positions these findings as a proof-of-concept rather than a competitive benchmark, with no direct comparison to other interpretability techniques (e.g., attention rollout, integrated gradients) on the same nuclear physics task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mechanistic interpretability (MI) framework developed for algorithmic tasks (modular arithmetic, parity) to a scientific domain, specifically nuclear physics. It directly builds on the latent space topography (LST) technique from Liu et al. (2022) and the fixed-attention transformer architecture from Zhong et al. (2023), repurposing both for knowledge extraction rather than algorithm recovery. The key conceptual departure is reframing MI from 'how does the model make predictions?' to 'what can the model tell us about the data?', using PCA projections and cosine similarity matching to map neural features onto human-derived physics terms (SEMF, shell model).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a proof-of-concept that neural networks trained on scientific data without explicit physics input can learn representations that geometrically encode established theories (the helix structure mapping to SEMF terms) and even capture higher-order corrections (shell model magic numbers). The symbolic regression pipeline recovering a formula outperforming SEMF suggests a path toward machine-discovered corrections to human theories, particularly valuable for exotic nuclei where experimental data is unavailable. However, the scope is limited to a small dataset (3,363 nuclei) and a simple 2-layer transformer, and the cosine-similarity matching heuristic is somewhat ad hoc; whether this approach scales to higher-dimensional, less-structured scientific problems where no human theory exists remains the central open question.
