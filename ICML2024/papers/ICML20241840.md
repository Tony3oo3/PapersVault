## Problem

Existing cancer drug response prediction methods rely on unsupervised domain-invariant representation learning that ignores drug efficacy information, failing to distinguish responders from non-responders. Additionally, the scarcity of labeled patient drug response data (only 179 samples vs. 9,808 unlabeled) prevents effective supervised classifier training, and naively using all pseudo-labeled samples introduces noise that degrades performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241840/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes gene expression profiles (1426 genes) from 677 labeled cell lines (DepMap) and 9,808 unlabeled patient samples (TCGA), along with identifiers for 20 drugs used in representation learning. It produces a domain-invariant genomic representation Z as a weighted combination of discrete per-drug embeddings, binary pseudo-labels for unlabeled patient profiles via majority-vote weak supervision, and ultimately a binary drug response prediction (positive/negative) for new patients, evaluated on 5 drugs with ≥20 labeled patient samples.

## Method

WISER introduces a four-stage pipeline. First, it learns a supervised domain-invariant representation by encoding genomic profiles through a shared encoder and private encoder, then representing each profile as a softmax-weighted sum of discrete drug embeddings (VQ-VAE style), trained with a triplet loss that pulls the drug embedding closer to profiles with positive efficacy and pushes it from negative ones, alongside reconstruction, embedding, and orthogonal losses; domain invariance is enforced via a WGAN with gradient penalty. Second, multiple classifiers trained on disjoint chunks of cell line data serve as label functions that generate pseudo-labels for unlabeled patient profiles using majority voting with abstention thresholds. Third, cut statistics (Z-scores measuring label consistency among K nearest neighbors in the Z-space) select the least noisy subset of pseudo-labeled samples. Fourth, the final drug response classifier is trained jointly on labeled cell line data and the selected patient subset.

## Results

On the TCGA patient evaluation set (5 drugs with ≥20 labeled patients), WISER achieves the best AUROC for 4 out of 5 drugs: Cisplatin (0.851, +15.7% over CODE-AE), Sorafenib (0.727, +8.8%), Gemcitabine (0.649, +5.2%), and Temozolomide (0.760, +0.9%). For AUPRC, WISER leads on Temozolomide (0.786, +2.4%), Cisplatin (0.861, +10.6%), and 5-Fluorouracil (0.741, +0.1%), while matching CODE-AE on Gemcitabine (0.752 vs 0.751). The ablation without weak supervision (WISER w/o WS) shows that the weak supervision + subset selection pipeline adds an average of 4.58% AUROC and 3.4% AUPRC over the supervised representation learning alone. On the PDTC breast cancer dataset (unseen drugs), WISER outperforms CODE-AE on 2 of 3 drugs (Az628: 0.792 vs 0.754 AUROC; Axitinib: 0.864 vs 0.840 AUROC).

## Baselines & Comparisons

WISER outperforms the strongest baseline CODE-AE on AUROC for 4 of 5 drugs (Cisplatin +15.7%, Sorafenib +8.8%, Gemcitabine +5.2%, Temozolomide +0.9%) and on AUPRC for 3 of 5 (Cisplatin +10.6%, Temozolomide +2.4%, 5-Fluorouracil +0.1%), though CODE-AE retains a notable AUROC advantage on 5-Fluorouracil (0.868 vs 0.715). Against domain adaptation baselines (DSN-DANN, DSN-MMD, CORAL, VELODROME, CELLIGNER), WISER consistently leads by 5–30+ percentage points in AUROC across all drugs. Among non-representation-learning methods (TCRP, MLP, ENET, COXEN, COXRF), WISER achieves the highest scores on every drug where those methods report results. The closest competitor overall is ADAE, which is competitive on AUPRC for Temozolomide (0.757 vs 0.786) and 5-Fluorouracil (0.725 vs 0.741) but trails WISER by 8–20 points in AUROC on most drugs. On the PDTC generalization test with unseen drugs, WISER beats CODE-AE on Az628 (0.792 vs 0.754 AUROC) and Axitinib (0.864 vs 0.840 AUROC) but is slightly behind on Gefitinib AUPRC (0.793 vs 0.778, WISER actually wins) — WISER wins on 2 of 3 drugs for at least one metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

WISER extends the CODE-AE (He et al., 2022) framework for domain-invariant representation learning in cancer drug response prediction by introducing two key departures: (1) supervised signal via a triplet loss over discrete drug-specific embeddings (VQ-VAE-style) that explicitly encodes drug efficacy into the shared representation, and (2) a weak supervision pipeline (majority-vote pseudo-labeling à la Snorkel) followed by cut-statistics-based subset selection to leverage the large pool of unlabeled patient genomic profiles. The domain adaptation component uses a WGAN with gradient penalty, building on the adversarial domain separation approach of CODE-AE and DSN. The subset selection step, while previously explored in NLP weak supervision (Lang et al., 2022), is novel in the cancer genomics context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: translating cell-line drug response models to individual patients where labeled response data is extremely scarce (179 labeled vs. 9808 unlabeled patient samples). The medical relevance validation against GDISC gene-drug interaction data provides independent biological grounding for the pseudo-labeling approach. However, the evaluation is limited to 5 drugs with very small patient test sets (21–46 samples each), and CODE-AE still outperforms WISER on 5-Fluorouracil AUROC (0.868 vs. 0.715), suggesting the gains are drug-dependent. Open questions include generalization to the full 20-drug set, other omics modalities (mutations, methylation), and whether the discrete representation approach scales to hundreds of drugs as in DepMap.
