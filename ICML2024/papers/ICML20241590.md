## Problem

STORM-based algorithms (STORM, COVER, SVMR) achieve near-optimal convergence for one to K-level stochastic optimizations, yet their generalization performance remains largely unexplored, particularly as the number of optimization levels increases. Two key questions are unanswered: whether the momentum-based estimators that give STORM its convergence advantage also degrade generalization relative to plain SGD, and how the cascading influence of estimators across multiple levels compounds the generalization error.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input stochastic training data drawn i.i.d. from each level of the optimization (n_k samples per level k), along with algorithmic hyperparameters (step size η, momentum β, iteration count T). The output consists of closed-form theoretical bounds: uniform stability ε_k per level, generalization error, and excess risk of the form O(Σ_k 1/√n_k), with explicit iteration requirements (T ≈ max(n_k^{5/2}) for convex, T ≈ max(n_k^{7/6}) for strongly convex). Experiments validate these bounds on simulated polynomial and compositional optimization tasks with Gaussian noise, reporting training/test loss gaps and generalization error as a function of level count, initial batch size, and noise variance.

## Method

The paper introduces a novel uniform stability definition for K-level stochastic optimizations and proves a generalization-stability theorem (Theorem 1) showing the generalization error is bounded by L_f^K ε_K plus a sum over levels involving both stability terms and variance terms from the estimators. It then derives stability bounds for STORM (1-level), COVER (2-level), and SVMR (K-level) under convex and strongly convex settings, revealing that each estimator introduces an additional variance term (Var(v_j) or Var(u_j)) that degrades stability relative to SGD. By balancing these stability bounds with optimization error bounds, the paper obtains excess risk rates of O(1/√n) requiring T ≈ n^{5/2} iterations (convex) or T ≈ n^{7/6} (strongly convex). A key practical insight is that increasing the batch size for the initial estimator computation reduces the first-iteration estimation error without significantly increasing computational cost, offering a favorable trade-off for generalization.

## Results

The paper establishes that STORM, COVER, and SVMR achieve O(1/√n) excess risk in the convex setting with T ≈ n^(5/2) iterations, and O(1/√n) in the strongly convex setting with fewer iterations T ≈ n^(7/6). For K-level optimization, the excess risk scales as O(Σ_{k=1}^K 1/√n_k) with T ≈ max(n_k^(5/2)) (convex) or max(n_k^(7/6)) (strongly convex). Empirically, STORM exhibits a larger train-test gap than SGD (worse generalization) but achieves lower overall loss; generalization error increases steadily with the number of levels (intensifying beyond 35 levels); and increasing the initial batch size from 128 to 256 or 512 significantly reduces generalization error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical comparison is between STORM and SGD on a univariate quintic polynomial fitting task (2000 data points, 60/40 train-test split, 500 iterations, batch size 128, step size 0.001). STORM achieves lower overall training and testing loss than SGD, confirming its optimization advantage. However, STORM exhibits a larger discrepancy between training and testing losses, indicating worse generalization — consistent with the theoretical finding that the momentum-based estimator introduces an additional variance term that degrades uniform stability relative to SGD. Theoretically, this manifests as STORM requiring T ≈ n^(5/2) iterations to reach the O(1/√n) excess risk bound, compared to SGD's T ≈ n (Hardt et al., 2016), meaning STORM needs substantially more iterations to match SGD's generalization rate. In the strongly convex setting, the gap narrows: STORM needs T ≈ n^(7/6) versus SGD's O(1/μn) stability, still requiring more iterations but less dramatically so. No other algorithmic baselines (e.g., SPIDER, SAGA) are directly compared in the experiments; the paper's contribution is the stability/generalization framework rather than algorithmic benchmarking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic stability framework from Hardt et al. (2016) for one-level SGD and Yang et al. (2023) for two-level compositional optimization to the general K-level setting, applying it specifically to three STORM-based algorithms (STORM, COVER, SVMR) that were previously analyzed only for convergence. The key conceptual departure is showing that the momentum-based variance reduction estimators central to STORM's convergence advantages actually degrade uniform stability relative to plain SGD, introducing additional variance-dependent terms in the stability bound. The paper also introduces a novel definition of uniform stability for K-level optimizations that accounts for the cascading influence of estimators across nested function compositions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a notable gap by providing the first generalization guarantees for STORM-based algorithms in multi-level stochastic optimization, offering practitioners a theoretical understanding of the convergence-generalization tradeoff inherent in variance reduction estimators. The finding that each additional level escalates generalization error through both stability degradation and cumulative gradient variance provides guidance for designing estimators in deeper compositional problems. However, the empirical validation is limited to simulated polynomial fitting experiments rather than real machine learning tasks, and the analysis is restricted to convex and strongly convex settings, leaving the non-convex case—which is where STORM is most commonly deployed—entirely unaddressed. Open questions include whether the initial-batch-size heuristic generalizes to practical deep learning workloads and whether the K-level stability framework can be extended to non-convex or minimax settings.
