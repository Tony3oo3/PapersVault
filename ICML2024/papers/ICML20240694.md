## Problem

Existing normalization methods for time series forecasting rely on heuristic statistics (mean, standard deviation) that do not adapt to the unique structure of each series. The paper asks which statistics should be removed and restored, arguing they must satisfy two properties—local invariance (stable between look-back and future) and global variability (varying across time to be worth normalizing)—and shows that mean shifting, while effective for trended series, fails to capture the dominant covariance structure in seasonal series.

## Input / Output

SIN operates per-channel on a multivariate look-back window of fixed length L=96 and a forecast horizon H (12–720 depending on dataset). It produces two outputs: (1) the learned normalization/denormalization transformation vectors (left and right singular vectors of the cross-covariance matrix X^T Y), and (2) the final forecast after applying the base model to the normalized input and restoring statistics to the prediction. The method is model-agnostic and wraps around any base forecaster (Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer, DLinear, TCN, GRU) without modifying its architecture.

## Method

SIN formulates normalization as a Partial Least Squares problem: it computes the SVD of the cross-covariance matrix X^T Y between all look-back windows and their corresponding futures, then selects the top singular vector pairs whose singular values exceed a threshold τ = 0.05 × max{L, H}. These vectors define linear projections g_x(x) = Û^T x and g_y(y) = V̂^T y that simultaneously maximize local invariance (small distance between g_x(x_t) and g_y(y_t)) and global variability (large variance of the projected statistics across time). Normalization subtracts the projection reconstruction from the input; denormalization transfers the statistics to the future via a learned affine module φ and adds the reconstruction back, with a √(H/L) scale alignment. The framework strictly generalizes mean shifting (constant vectors) and discrete cosine transform (cosine vectors), and the learned transformations are interpretable as the dominant harmonic or trend components explaining past-future covariance.

## Results

SIN is evaluated as a plug-in normalization module on 13 datasets across energy, traffic, economics, and weather domains, integrated with Autoformer, FEDformer, and Informer (L=96). Headline improvements include a 70% MSE reduction on PEMS07 (Autoformer: 0.850→0.258), a 72% MSE reduction on Exchange-Rate (Informer: 1.632→0.462), and a 56.7% MSE reduction on Solar-Energy (Autoformer: 0.728→0.315). On seasonal datasets like Traffic, SIN improves FEDformer MSE from 0.605 to 0.519 (14.2%) and Autoformer from 0.615 to 0.539 (12.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SIN is compared against three base forecasting architectures (Autoformer, FEDformer, Informer) without normalization, the heuristic RevIN normalization (Kim et al., ICLR 2021), and three additional architectures (DLinear, TCN, GRU) in the appendix. Against the base models, SIN consistently reduces MSE across all 11 main datasets, with the largest gains on PEMS07 (70% MSE reduction for Autoformer) and Exchange-Rate (72% for Informer). Against RevIN (Table 2, both with FEDformer), SIN achieves substantially lower MSE on seasonal datasets—Traffic (0.493 vs 0.609 at H=96) and Solar (0.218 vs 0.345 at H=96, ~35.5% improvement)—while performing comparably on the trend-dominated Exchange dataset (0.148 vs 0.145 at H=96), confirming that mean-shifting (RevIN's core operation) is near-optimal for trend series but insufficient for seasonal ones. Against DLinear, TCN, and GRU (Appendix D), SIN improves most datasets but shows slight degradation on PEMS datasets for DLinear, suggesting that overly simple models may lose useful signal when SIN removes statistics. Overall, SIN wins or ties in the vast majority of comparisons, with the clearest advantage on seasonal and high-variance series where heuristic normalization falls short.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the normalization-based paradigm for handling non-stationarity in time series forecasting, building directly on RevIN (Kim et al., 2021), DAIN (Passalis et al., 2020), Non-stationary Transformers (Liu et al., 2022c), and DISH-TS (Fan et al., 2023). The key departure is reframing the problem from 'how to normalize' to 'which statistics should be normalized,' and answering it via Partial Least Squares (PLS-SVD) that maximizes covariance between look-back and future windows. The paper demonstrates that RevIN's mean shifting and Discrete Cosine Transform are both special cases of its learned projection, providing a unifying and interpretable framework that subsumes prior heuristic choices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SIN offers a model-agnostic, interpretable normalization module that practitioners can plug into any forecaster, and its PLS-SVD formulation makes the learned statistics inspectable (e.g., revealing sinusoidal components for seasonal data vs. constant vectors for trended data). However, the method is restricted to linear projections, the look-back window size selection remains heuristic, and computational cost grows with channel count. Open questions include whether nonlinear extensions (kernels, neural networks) yield further gains, how to adapt the normalization to streaming or few-shot settings, and whether the covariance-maximization criterion generalizes beyond the univariate-per-channel setting.
