## Problem

Existing decision-making algorithms (PPO, QMIX, PSRO, CFR) are developed independently for specific categories—single-agent, cooperative, competitive, or mixed cooperative-competitive—and cannot generalize across them. No single algorithm handles all categories with different numbers of agents, relationships, solution concepts (NE, CCE), and evaluation measures (NashConv, CCEGap, OptGap, social welfare), and no comprehensive yet computationally tractable benchmark covers all of them.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241130/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each iteration and decision point, the method consumes the action-value vector Q(π_k), M historical policies {π_{k-τ}}, a chosen convex function ψ defining the Bregman divergence, and a target evaluation measure L (e.g., NashConv or CCEGap). It produces an updated joint policy π_{k+1} via a Newton-solved KKT system and, every κ iterations, adjusted regularization intensities α = (α_τ)_{τ=0}^{M-1} through a zero-order meta-controller. The policy update is computed numerically rather than in closed form, and a projection step enforces π(a) ≥ ε to maintain feasibility.

## Method

GMD generalizes mirror descent by regularizing against M historical policies simultaneously and solving the resulting constrained optimization via KKT conditions, reducing it to a one-dimensional root-finding problem in the dual variable λ solved by Newton's method—this removes the need for closed-form solutions and enables arbitrary separable convex functions ψ (hence broader Bregman divergences). CMD wraps GMD with a meta-controller that samples D perturbed α candidates, evaluates the resulting joint policies under the target measure L, and updates α using a direction-guided rule where only the sign of each performance difference determines the update direction, not its magnitude—critical when L values are near zero. This zero-order, sign-based update (DRS) outperforms vanilla random search and gradient-less descent baselines. The framework is instantiated per-agent independently (decentralized execution) while the meta-controller operates on the joint policy (centralized training), mirroring the CTDE paradigm, and requires only changing L to switch between solution concepts and evaluation measures.

## Results

Across all 15 games in GAMEBENCH, CMD drives both OptGap (single-agent/cooperative) and NashConv (competitive/MCC) to near-zero values, confirming it can find approximate optimal policies or Nash equilibria in every decision-making category. CMD outperforms all baselines in the two MCC games (MCCKuhn-A and MCCKuhn-B) and matches them in the remaining 13 games. In the most challenging game (Leduc poker), GMD without the meta-controller fails to reduce NashConv, while CMD with the meta-controller succeeds, demonstrating the indispensability of the MC. CMD also converges to comparable OptGap/NashConv values with fewer iterations than MMD-KL, indicating faster policy learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MMD-KL (Sokota et al., 2023), CMD achieves similar final OptGap/NashConv values but with fewer iterations, and strictly outperforms it in the two MCC games (MCCKuhn-A, MCCKuhn-B); even GMD with fixed hyperparameters (no MC) matches MMD-KL's final values in most games using fewer iterations. Against CFR (Zinkevich et al., 2007), CMD is consistently superior across all 15 games, consistent with prior findings that vanilla CFR is the weakest baseline. Against CFR+ (Tammelin, 2014), the comparison is more nuanced: CFR+ achieves lower NashConv over short iteration horizons in most games, but CMD catches up and eventually matches or surpasses CFR+ for longer horizons. The ablation comparing CMD to GMD (no meta-controller) shows that the MC is critical in Leduc poker, where GMD cannot effectively decrease NashConv, and also accelerates convergence in other games. Overall, CMD is the only method that simultaneously handles all five game categories, multiple Bregman divergences, and different evaluation measures (NashConv, CCEGap, social welfare) with minimal modifications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the classic mirror descent (MD) lineage (Nemirovskij & Yudin, 1983; Beck & Teboulle, 2003) and its recent extension MMD (Sokota et al., ICLR 2023) in two ways: it incorporates multiple historical policies via a weighted Bregman regularization and solves the resulting KKT system numerically (Newton's method) rather than relying on closed-form solutions, thereby supporting a broader class of Bregman divergences. The meta-controller for hyperparameter tuning adapts the direction-guided zero-order optimization idea from ZARTS (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2022) to the non-differentiable evaluation measures (NashConv, CCEGap) that arise in multi-agent settings. The unification goal—applying a single policy-update rule across single-agent, cooperative, competitive, and mixed categories—departs from the category-specific algorithm design (PPO, QMIX, PSRO, CFR) that has dominated the field, and the GAMEBENCH benchmark extends OpenSpiel with games deliberately sized for MD/CFR tractability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic attempt at a single algorithmic framework spanning all four decision-making categories, and GAMEBENCH fills a genuine gap by offering a comprehensive, computationally accessible testbed that existing category-specific benchmarks (Atari, Hanabi, Hold'em, football) do not provide. However, the empirical gains are modest—CMD is 'competitive or better' rather than dramatically superior—and the framework is limited to small, tabular games where enumerating decision points is feasible, leaving open whether the approach scales to high-dimensional or continuous-action settings. Key open questions include: theoretical convergence guarantees for CMD (whose update rule is numerical rather than closed-form), whether neural Bregman divergences can automatically select the optimal divergence per category, and whether the zero-order meta-controller can be made more computationally efficient via Bayesian optimization or offline learning.
