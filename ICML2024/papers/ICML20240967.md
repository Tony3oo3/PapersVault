## Problem

Deep reinforcement learning for combinatorial optimization requires a large number of reward evaluations, which are prohibitively expensive in practical settings involving black-box simulations or wet-lab experiments. Existing symmetry-exploiting methods (POMO, Sym-NCO) still demand additional reward evaluations to leverage symmetries, leaving a gap in how to exploit the solution-symmetric structure of combinatorial space without incurring extra evaluation costs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240967/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates as a plug-in add-on to any constructive DRL policy: it takes a problem instance (e.g., TSP city coordinates, a molecule SMILES string, or a decap placement grid) and a trained policy network, then during training it collects high-rewarded action trajectories and applies solution-preserving symmetric transformations (cyclic shifts for TSP, decision-order permutations for DPP, string re-encodings for molecules) to generate alternative trajectories that map to the identical terminal solution. The output is an updated policy that achieves comparable or better solution quality with fewer reward calls, without altering the base architecture or introducing importance-weight corrections.

## Method

SRT alternates two training steps: (A) conventional reward-maximizing training with a standard DRL algorithm (REINFORCE, PPO, A2C, or GFlowNet), and (B) symmetric replay training, where high-rewarded trajectories are transformed by a symmetric transformation policy p(τ→x|x) and the policy is updated via a maximum-likelihood imitation loss on the resulting symmetric trajectories. Three transformation strategies are analyzed—maximum entropy (uniform, O(1) sampling), adversarial (minimum log-likelihood, O(N)), and importance sampling (Boltzmann with inverse temperature β)—with the uniform policy adopted as default for its generality. A key theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) decomposes policy entropy into a solution-exploration term (Step A) and a symmetric-transformation entropy term (Step B), showing that the uniform transformation maximizes the latter and thus the overall entropy. Because the imitation loss operates directly on trajectories without importance weights, SRT is compatible with both on-policy and off-policy methods and mitigates overfitting during replay, enabling up to 16 replay loops without performance degradation.

## Results

On TSP (N=50), SRT consistently improves all four DRL methods, with the largest gain in GFlowNet (3.76% cost reduction at K=100K: 6.797→6.596) and A2C (6.780→6.586 at K=100K). On the hardware design DPP task (chip-package PDN), A2C+SRT achieves a 30.54% reward improvement (9.772→12.757), and GFlowNet+SRT attains the best reward on both PDN environments (12.784 and 26.469). On the Practical Molecular Optimization benchmark (23 oracles, 10K reward calls), SRT improves the average AUC top-10 from 0.431 to 0.489 for GFlowNet and from 0.615 to 0.632 for REINVENT, winning on 12/23 and 18/23 oracles respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SRT wins against all compared baselines across every task. On TSP (N=50), SRT outperforms A2C, PG-Rollout, PPO, and GFlowNet at both K=100K and K=2M, with the most notable margin being GFlowNet+SRT's 3.76% cost reduction at K=100K. On DPP (chip-package PDN), SRT delivers the largest absolute gains: A2C+SRT improves reward by 30.54% (9.772→12.757) and GFlowNet+SRT by 31.25% (9.740→12.784), while on HBM PDN all SRT variants improve by 1.5–2.0%. On the PMO benchmark, REINVENT+SRT surpasses REINVENT on 18/23 oracles (avg AUC 0.632 vs 0.615) and also outperforms GPBO (0.609) and Graph GA (0.616) in the SMILES-based comparison (Table 11). On additional CO problems, SRT beats POMO and Sym-NCO on TSP/CVRP (e.g., TSP-100 at K=2M: 8.573 vs 8.917 for AM Critic, 10.964 for POMO) and MatNet on ATSP/FSSP (e.g., ATSP-100 at K=200K: 3.771 vs 4.324 for MatNet-Sampled, a ~12% gap). The method also reduces variance substantially (e.g., A2C+SRT std drops from 0.208 to 0.043 at K=100K on TSP), indicating more stable training.

## Relation to Prior Work

SRT carves new ground by repurposing the well-known solution-preserving symmetries of combinatorial spaces (exploited in POMO and Sym-NCO for variance reduction) into a replay-based exploration mechanism that requires no additional reward evaluations. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of data augmentation (symmetric transformation preserves solution semantics, akin to DND in NLP) and replay ratio scaling (increasing effective updates per environment interaction), but departs from both by using a separate MLE/imitation loss rather than importance-weighted policy gradients, which avoids the high variance that plagues on-policy replay. The theoretical entropy decomposition (Theorem 4.1) cleanly separates solution-space exploration (Step A) from symmetric-space exploration (Step B), providing a principled justification for the two-step design that prior symmetry-exploiting methods lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In practical CO settings where reward evaluation involves wet-lab experiments or expensive simulations (molecular optimization, hardware design), even a 10–30% reduction in required evaluations translates to substantial cost savings, and SRT achieves this as a drop-in add-on across on-policy and off-policy DRL methods without architectural changes. The method's generality is its main strength, but it is limited to problems where solution-preserving symmetries are known or easily enumerated (e.g., cyclic shifts in TSP, permutation invariance in decap placement); for problems with complex or unknown symmetry groups, the transformation policy becomes a bottleneck. Open questions include whether the symmetric transformation can be learned end-to-end, how SRT interacts with equivariant network architectures that already quotient out symmetry, and whether the approach extends to non-constructive (improvement-heuristic) DRL methods.
