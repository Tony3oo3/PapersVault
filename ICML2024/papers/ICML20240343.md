## Problem

Neural networks produce overconfident predictions, and existing recalibration methods address this by fitting an additional calibration map (e.g., temperature scaling, isotonic regression) on top of the trained classifier's logits. No prior work has directly modified the geometry of the last linear layer's weight matrix to adjust confidence, leaving the angular relationship between class vectors and penultimate features unexploited as a calibration lever.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the trained last-layer weight matrix W ∈ R^{n×C} (where n is the penultimate feature dimension, e.g., 2048 for ResNet-50) and a held-out calibration split to search for the optimal rotation intensity θ (mRC angle in [0°, 90°]). It outputs a single alternative weight matrix W' of the same shape, which replaces W in the classifier while leaving the feature extractor frozen. The adjusted weights can be used standalone or composed with any calibration-map method (TS, ETS, IROvA, AAR), making the approach orthogonal to existing post-hoc techniques.

## Method

TNA operates in two steps on the class vectors {w_i} of the last linear layer. First, TILT generates a rotation matrix R as a product of n_r random Givens rotations (2D rotations in randomly selected coordinate planes with angles drawn from a Beta distribution scaled by θ_s), iterated until the mean Rotation over Classes mRC(W, R) reaches the target θ; applying R to W rotates each class vector, increasing the angle ∠(w_i, z) with penultimate features and thereby relaxing softmax confidence (Proposition 3.3). Second, AVERAGE produces n_e independently tilted weights at the same mRC and takes their arithmetic mean, which acts as a geometric mean over the softmax numerator terms and statistically compensates the accuracy loss from individual rotations. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.2) shows the mode of the angle shift is arccos(cos ψ_i cos θ) − ψ_i, and the high-dimensional near-orthogonal theorem guarantees that random rotations in n-dimensions (n in the hundreds to thousands) overwhelmingly push angles toward 90° rather than toward any specific penultimate feature direction.

## Results

TNA reduces ECE substantially when applied alone: from 1.66% to 0.66% on CIFAR-10 (WRN28x10), from 5.91% to 4.11% on CIFAR-100 (WRN28x10), and from 3.83% to 1.73% on ImageNet (ResNet-50), while preserving accuracy within 0.3%. When combined with calibration-map methods, TNA(comp.) achieves the best ECE in most settings, e.g., 0.71% (vs. 0.84% for TS alone) on CIFAR-10-WRN28x10, 3.06% (vs. 3.47% for ETS alone) on CIFAR-100-WRN28x10, and 1.01% (vs. 1.10% for ETS alone) on ImageNet-ResNet50. On ImageNet-ViT-H/14, ETS+TNA(comp.) reaches 0.60% ECE, the lowest reported value in the table.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TNA consistently improves upon all four calibration-map baselines when combined with them. Against Temperature Scaling (TS), TNA(comp.) reduces ECE from 0.84% to 0.71% on CIFAR-10-WRN28x10 and from 2.09% to 1.93% on ImageNet-ResNet50; notably, TNA alone (0.66% ECE) already outperforms TS (0.84%) on CIFAR-10. Against Ensemble Temperature Scaling (ETS), the combination ETS+TNA(comp.) achieves the best ECE in most settings (e.g., 3.06% vs. 3.47% on CIFAR-100-WRN28x10; 1.01% vs. 1.10% on ImageNet-ResNet50). Against AAR, TNA(comp.) yields the lowest AdaECE on CIFAR-10-WRN28x10 (0.44% vs. 0.45%) and CIFAR-100-WRN28x10 (3.15% vs. 3.19%), and reduces ECE on ImageNet-ResNet50 (2.40% vs. 2.53%). IROvA is the weakest baseline: on ImageNet it actually worsens calibration (ECE 6.23% vs. 3.83% uncalibrated for ResNet50), and TNA(comp.) only partially recovers this (6.03%), suggesting IROvA is poorly suited to high-dimensional feature spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant post-hoc calibration paradigm of fitting a separate calibration map (temperature scaling, isotonic regression, Dirichlet scaling, etc.) by instead directly modifying the geometry of the last linear layer's weight matrix. It repurposes the geometric decomposition of logits into class-vector norms and angles—previously studied in representation learning (Kang et al., 2020; Kansizoglou et al., 2022)—as the mechanism for confidence adjustment, positioning angle manipulation as orthogonal to the magnitude adjustment that temperature scaling performs. The rotation construction draws on Givens rotations and Euler's theorem from numerical linear algebra, while the high-dimensionality argument invoking the near-orthogonality theorem (Blum et al., 2020) justifies why random rotations reliably shift angles toward 90° without catastrophic accuracy loss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TNA introduces a single, interpretable geometric knob (mRC angle) that is orthogonal to existing calibration maps, enabling practitioners to combine it with temperature scaling or other methods for additional calibration gains at negligible computational cost. The clean separation of 'angle' vs. 'magnitude' as two independent calibration levers provides a useful conceptual framework for understanding what different calibration methods actually do. Limitations include the reliance on high-dimensional feature spaces (effectiveness degrades as dimension drops, per Table 3), strong theoretical assumptions (uniform rotation across classes, equal biases), and the fact that the method only adjusts the last layer, leaving deeper miscalibration sources untouched. Open questions include whether analogous geometric adjustments to intermediate layers or non-linear classifiers could yield further gains, and whether the angle-magnitude decomposition generalizes to multi-task or generative settings.
