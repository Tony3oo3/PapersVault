## Problem

Existing statistical guarantees for multi-task nonlinear representation learning critically assume that covariates are identically distributed across all tasks and independent within each task. These assumptions are fundamentally violated in practical settings such as domain generalization and sequential decision-making, where the covariate distribution is inextricably linked to the predictor itself (e.g., the stationary distribution of a dynamical system depends on its dynamics matrix). This paper establishes generalization bounds that remove both the identical-covariate and within-task independence assumptions while also reducing the per-task sample requirement from Ω(d_X) to Ω(d_Y·r).

## Input / Output

The method takes N covariate-observation pairs from each of T source tasks and N' pairs from a target task, where covariates may follow non-identical distributions across tasks and may be sequentially dependent (φ-mixing) within each task. The output is a shared nonlinear representation g: R^{d_X} → R^r (with r ≤ d_X) and task-specific linear heads f: R^r → R^{d_Y}, learned via a two-stage empirical risk minimization procedure. In the experimental validation, inputs are 8-frame histories of RGB camera observations from a PyBullet cartpole simulation with randomly sampled physical parameters, and the output is a continuous control force applied to the cart.

## Method

The core analysis decomposes the target excess risk into a non-realizable least-squares term (fitting the target head on the learned representation) and a task-averaged estimation error term (fitting the shared representation across source tasks). A new task-coverage condition (TC) replaces the standard task-diversity assumption, accounting for overlap between non-identical covariate distributions in addition to head alignment, yielding a refined diversity parameter μ‖F*_0(F*_{1:T})†‖_2. The estimation error is controlled via martingale offset complexity—a capacity measure that scales with noise level rather than class diameter—combined with a localization argument over the star-hull of the centered function class, yielding the rate Õ(C(G)/NT + d_Y·r/N'). For dependent data, the authors adapt blocking/decoupling techniques to show that φ-mixing covariates only inflate the burn-in by a factor Φ = (Σ√φ(i))² while preserving the same rate as the iid setting, avoiding the effective sample-size deflation incurred by standard blocking approaches.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 2.14) establishes that the excess transfer risk of the two-stage ERM predictor decays as Õ(σ²_W · C(G)/(NT) + σ²_W · d_Y·r/N′), matching the iid-setting rate while requiring only Ω(d_Y·r) samples per task (a dramatic improvement from the Ω(d_X) burn-in in prior work). For φ-mixing covariates, the rate is unchanged; dependency enters only as a constant factor Φ = (Σ√φ(i))² in the burn-in. In the numerical validation on a cartpole balancing task, multi-task imitation learning (pre-training on T source tasks with 10 demonstrations each) consistently outperforms single-task learning across all three metrics (input MSE, state trajectory gap, and % stable trials) when fewer than ~10 target demonstrations are available, with the advantage saturating once target data exceeds per-task source data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so the main 'comparison' is against prior multi-task representation learning guarantees (Du et al. 2020; Tripuraneni et al. 2020; Watkins et al. 2024), which all require iid covariates across tasks and incur a per-task burn-in of at least Ω(d_X). This paper removes both the identical-covariate and independence assumptions while reducing the burn-in to Ω(d_Y·r), and refines the task-diversity parameter to jointly capture covariate distribution overlap (via the task-coverage constant μ) and head alignment (via ‖F*_⁽⁰⁾(F*_¹:ᵀ)†‖₂). In the empirical validation, the baseline is single-task imitation learning (training the full CNN from scratch on target-task demonstrations only). Multi-task learning (pre-training the representation on T source tasks, then fine-tuning only the linear head) wins on all three metrics in the low-data regime: lower input MSE, smaller state trajectory deviation, and higher % stable trials. The advantage diminishes and saturates once the number of target demonstrations exceeds the per-task source budget (~10), consistent with the theoretical prediction that the d_Y·r/N′ term dominates when N′ is large relative to N.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-task representation learning framework of Du et al. (2020) and Tripuraneni et al. (2020), which established sample complexity bounds for learning a shared nonlinear representation with task-specific linear heads under the assumption of iid covariates across all tasks. The key departure is removing both the identical-covariate-distribution assumption and the within-task independence assumption, while also reducing the per-task burn-in from Ω(d_X) to Ω(d_Y·r). The analysis adapts the martingale offset complexity from Liang et al. (2015) and Ziemann & Tu (2022) to handle φ-mixing dependent data, and introduces a refined task-diversity measure (combining a task-coverage condition with head alignment) that properly accounts for non-identical covariate distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result is practically significant because many natural applications of multi-task representation learning—robotic control, system identification, domain generalization—involve covariates that are inherently non-identical across tasks and sequentially dependent within tasks, settings where prior guarantees simply did not apply. The clean finding that iid rates are recovered with only a mild burn-in inflation from mixing (a factor of Φ for N, log(N') for N') suggests the iid assumption was a technical artifact rather than a fundamental requirement. Open questions include handling class/source-data imbalance, low task diversity, and extending the analysis to more complex sequential decision-making settings beyond offline regression.
