## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of in-context learning in Transformers are restricted to single attention layers trained on linear regression, leaving unexplained how nonlinear feature learning emerges when MLP layers are present. This paper addresses the central open question of how in-context learning with nonlinear representations arises in Transformers with MLP layers optimized via gradient descent, bridging the gap between ICL expressivity results and training dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240984/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt of n demonstration input-output pairs (x_i, y_i) where y_i = v°(x_i) for a linear regression task over a fixed nonlinear feature map f°, plus a query x_qr. The MLP is applied only to the input covariates (not labels), transforming the prompt into a feature-space embedding matrix before the linear attention layer produces a scalar regression prediction. The analysis takes the infinite prompt length limit (n→∞) to eliminate sampling error, and pretraining minimizes the mean squared risk averaged over tasks drawn from a distribution over linear combinations of f°.

## Method

The architecture is a two-layer MLP (represented in the mean-field limit as a measure μ over neuron parameters) followed by a single linear self-attention layer. A two-timescale argument shows the attention matrix W converges rapidly to a closed-form solution, reducing the problem to optimizing the infinite-dimensional 'attention landscape' L(μ) = inf_W L_TF(μ, W) over the space of probability measures. The key geometric result is that this landscape is strictly saddle: all critical points are either global minima (corresponding to recovering the true feature map up to a linear transformation) or saddle points with provably negative curvature. Using Otto calculus, the center-stable manifold theorem, and spectral theory of integral operators on Wasserstein space, they prove that Wasserstein gradient flow almost always avoids saddle points. Concrete convergence rates are derived in three regimes—away from critical points, near global minima, and near saddle points—using a birth-death perturbation mechanism that injects mass from a reference distribution to guarantee progress.

## Results

The paper establishes that the infinite-dimensional 'attention landscape' for the MLP-attention Transformer is strictly saddle: all critical points are either global minima or saddle points (Theorem 3.3), with no spurious local minima. Mean-field dynamics (Wasserstein gradient flow) almost always avoids saddle points (Theorem 4.3), and once the loss enters the band (0, r/2), convergence to global minima occurs at rate O(k²/(γε)) (Theorem 5.3). Near saddle points, the dynamics escapes in Õ(k/(γL²) · log(1/α)) time with loss improvement of Õ(γαL⁴/(k⁵d)) (Theorem 5.6). The Barron space characterization (Lemma 2.2) shows the MLP layer extends learnable functions from linear to the full Barron class, with finite-width N networks achieving O(1/N) approximation error (Proposition 2.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no direct empirical baseline comparisons; the `compares_to` list is empty. The paper positions itself against prior ICL theory by showing that LSA-only Transformers (Zhang et al., 2023a; Mahankali et al., 2023) can only implement one step of gradient descent for linear regression, whereas the MLP-attention model learns the full Barron space of nonlinear features. The saddle-point avoidance result (Theorem 4.3) is the first such guarantee for nonconvex mean-field dynamics, extending the finite-dimensional result of Lee et al. (2019) to the infinite-dimensional Wasserstein space. The numerical experiments in Section 6 qualitatively confirm that the two-timescale approximation (static vs. attention Transformer) yields similar training dynamics, and that the proposed birth-death perturbation improves convergence on degenerate and misspecified feature tasks, but no quantitative benchmark comparisons against competing methods are reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL theoretical framework of Garg et al. (2022) and the linear-attention-only optimization analysis of Zhang et al. (2023a) by incorporating a two-layer MLP that learns nonlinear feature representations, moving beyond the linear-function limitation of prior work. Methodologically, it departs from the exact-dynamics approach of previous ICL papers by factoring out the attention layer via a two-timescale argument, reducing the problem to studying the geometry of an infinite-dimensional 'attention landscape' over the MLP parameter distribution. The saddle-point avoidance analysis draws on the finite-dimensional strict-saddle framework of Lee et al. (2019) but transplants it to Wasserstein space using Otto calculus and center-stable manifold theory in Banach spaces—a combination not previously applied to mean-field dynamics. The Barron space expressivity results extend the classical scalar theory of Barron (1993) and Weinan et al. (2020) to the vector-valued setting relevant to multi-feature ICL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical account of how nonlinear features are learned in context during Transformer pretraining, filling a gap between construction-based ICL analyses (which assume specific weight configurations) and dynamics-based analyses (which were limited to linear attention-only models). The saddle-point avoidance and convergence-rate results for nonconvex Wasserstein gradient flow are of independent interest beyond ICL, potentially applicable to three-layer network training and other mean-field problems. Key limitations include the restriction to a single MLP + single linear-attention layer, smooth (non-ReLU) activations, infinite prompt length, and the two-timescale assumption; extending to deeper architectures, softmax attention, and finite samples remains open. The birth-death perturbation mechanism for escaping saddles in infinite-dimensional space is proposed but not fully proven to yield polynomial convergence, leaving a gap between the qualitative avoidance result and concrete global rates.
