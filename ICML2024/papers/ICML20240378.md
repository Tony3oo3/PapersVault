## Problem

Despite the proliferation of causal machine learning algorithms (T-learners, S-learners, X-learners, neural network approaches), there is little to no finite-sample theory guaranteeing their quality, and existing generalization bounds (e.g., Johansson et al., 2022) are orders of magnitude looser and restricted to specific learning classes and mean squared error. A critical open question is how these procedures fare when standard causal assumptions—ignorability and positivity—are violated due to hidden confounders, which is the norm in observational data.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes standard tabular causal data (covariates X, binary treatment T, observed outcome Y) along with a user-specified reweighing function w(X) and an arbitrary propensity scoring model ν(X) used to empirically bound the divergence term. The output is a high-probability upper bound on the unobservable complete causal loss, decomposed into an observable reweighed empirical loss, a Brier-score-based propensity term, a sample-balance term D, Rademacher complexity corrections, and a variance term—making each component interpretable and individually estimable. The bounds are loss-agnostic, applying equally to MSE, MAE, quantile (pinball) loss, and 0-1 loss, which enables model selection across heterogeneous meta-learner architectures on real data.

## Method

The core contribution is a novel change-of-measure inequality (Lemma 2.2) based on the Pearson χ² f-divergence with a tunable parameter λ, which bounds the gap between expectations under two distributions by λ·χ²(Q‖P) + Var_P(φ)/(4λ), optimized at λ* = √(Var_P(φ)/(4χ²(Q‖P))). Applied to causal regression, this yields a bound on the unobservable complete loss in terms of the observable conditional loss plus a divergence term Δ_{T=a} = E[(w(X)·P[T=a|X,U]/P[T=a] − 1)²], which quantifies deviation from a randomized control trial. The key practical insight is that Δ, though depending on unobservable quantities, can be empirically upper-bounded via a relaxed triangular inequality into a Brier Score of any propensity model ν(X) plus a balance term D, yielding fully computable finite-sample (PAC) bounds. For meta-learners, a relaxed subadditivity assumption on the loss (Assumption 2.6) decomposes the treatment effect loss into component losses, extending the bounds to T-, S-, and X-learners while remaining model-agnostic and valid under hidden confounding and positivity violations.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative finding is the tightness of its generalization bounds relative to prior work. On semi-synthetic data (Learned IHDP, ACIC16, Confounded ACIC16), the theoretical bound (Theorem 2.3) matches the unobservable complete loss almost exactly, while the empirical bound (Theorem 2.4) remains substantially tighter than the closest prior bound from Johansson et al. (2022), which is described as 'multiple orders of magnitude looser.' The tuning parameter λ in the change-of-measure inequality (Lemma 2.2) is shown to tighten bounds by over 30× compared to the fixed λ=1 used implicitly by prior work. On the real-world Parkinson's Telemonitoring dataset, applying the bounds changes model selection conclusions: Random Forest T/S-learners, which appeared strictly superior under observed loss alone, are brought roughly on par with Gradient Boosting meta-learners once the distributional gap is accounted for.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole direct baseline comparison is with Johansson et al. (2022), which provides the closest prior generalization bounds for causal regression. The paper's theoretical bound (Theorem 2.3) tracks the true complete loss almost exactly across all three semi-synthetic datasets, whereas Johansson et al.'s bound (Corollary 1) is multiple orders of magnitude looser. Even the paper's looser empirical bound (Theorem 2.4), which replaces unobservable propensity scores with a learned classifier ν(X), remains substantially tighter than Johansson et al.'s result. A key source of this gap is the tunable λ parameter in the Pearson χ² change-of-measure inequality: prior work effectively fixes λ=1, while optimizing λ yields bounds over 30× tighter (Figure 2). On the Parkinson's Telemonitoring application, the bounds reveal that model rankings based solely on observed-distribution loss are misleading—Random Forest T/S-learners lose their apparent advantage over Gradient Boosting meta-learners once the counterfactual gap is corrected, while Lasso-based models remain confidently inferior regardless.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the change-of-measure inequality framework from Ohnishi & Honorio (2021) and the variational representation of f-divergences from Ruderman et al. (2012) into the causal inference setting, introducing a tunable λ parameter that substantially tightens the resulting bounds. It applies these tools to the causal meta-learner architecture of Kunzel et al. (2017), providing the first finite-sample generalization bounds for T-, S-, and X-learners that hold under hidden confounding and without positivity. The closest prior work, Johansson et al. (2022), also establishes generalization bounds for causal regression but relies on pseudo-VC-dimension and IPM-based arguments, yielding bounds that are orders of magnitude looser and restricted to MSE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a critical gap in causal ML by providing rigorous finite-sample guarantees where previously only asymptotic results existed, and it does so without requiring ignorability or positivity—making it directly applicable to the sensitivity analysis setting that dominates real-world observational studies. The demonstration that quantile treatment effects can be bounded (and potentially estimated) via standard meta-learners with quantile loss challenges the prior consensus that such quantities are unidentifiable, though the paper carefully notes this is a loss bound rather than a convergence guarantee. Open questions include whether the bounds can account for interactions between the individual regressions in meta-learners, whether the relaxed empirical bound can be further tightened, and whether the framework extends naturally to multi-valued treatments or continuous treatment settings.
