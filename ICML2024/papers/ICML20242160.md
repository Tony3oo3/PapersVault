## Problem

As AI/ML conference acceptances grow into the thousands, it is unclear how researchers actually discover and read new work, and whether the small number of high-follower social media accounts that curate arXiv preprints have a measurable, causal effect on paper visibility and citation counts. The paper also asks whether this top-down curation introduces geographic or gender skew relative to the broader field.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242160/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests arXiv/HuggingFace paper identifiers scraped from the X feeds of @_akhaliq and @arankomatsuzaki (Dec 2018–Oct 2023), enriches each with Semantic Scholar metadata (citation counts, author h-indices, venue, year, affiliations), SPECTER2 title-abstract embeddings, and OpenReview scores where available. It outputs (1) a matched control set of ~7,800 papers, (2) distributional and causal-effect comparisons of citation counts, (3) per-year geographic area breakdowns aligned with the Stanford HAI AI Index, and (4) first-author gender ratios benchmarked against the Taulbee survey. A notable constraint is that ~10% of tweeted papers are dropped when S2 lacks required attributes, and only 939 of 7,222 pairs have OpenReview scores for the negative-control analysis.

## Method

The study is structured as a retrospective cohort analysis. First, each influencer-shared paper is matched to a control paper via a two-stage procedure: exact matching on categorical covariates (year, venue, open-access status) and binned variables (n_authors, first-author h-index, first-author citations), followed by optimal (linear-sum-assignment) matching on the L2 distance of SPECTER2 topic embeddings, yielding 9-covariate-matched pairs. Distributional differences in citation counts are tested with three non-parametric two-sample tests (Epps-Singleton, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Mann-Whitney U), all yielding p < 0.0001. To move from correlation to causation, the authors estimate the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATET) using a Negative Outcome Control (NOC) / Difference-in-Differences framework: the binary outcome Y is whether a paper exceeds a citation-count quantile, the treatment A is influencer sharing, and the negative control outcome N is whether the paper exceeds a review-score quantile (verified to be uncorrelated with A). Bootstrapped confidence intervals on the debiased ATET (9–19% across quantile thresholds) exclude zero, indicating a significant positive causal effect of influencer sharing on citations even after accounting for unobserved confounders such as paper quality.

## Results

Papers shared by @_akhaliq have a median citation count of 24 (95% CI: 23–25) versus 14 (95% CI: 13–15) for matched controls, while papers shared by @arankomatsuzaki reach a median of 31 (95% CI: 27–34) versus 12 (95% CI: 10.5–13.5). All three distribution-comparison tests (Epps-Singleton, Kolmogorov-Smirnov, Mann-Whitney U) yield p < 0.0001 for both influencers. The causal inference analysis (ATET with negative outcome control) estimates a 19% (95% CI: 15–24) increase in the probability of being highly-cited at the 50th percentile, decreasing to 9% (6–12) at the 90th percentile, with all confidence intervals excluding zero.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's 'baseline' is a set of control papers matched to influencer-shared papers on nine covariates (year, venue, open access, number of authors, first/max h-index, first/max citations, and SPECTER2 topic embedding). After matching, review scores are statistically indistinguishable between treatment and control (p > 0.2), confirming that the citation gap is not driven by paper quality. Influencer-shared papers outperform controls on median citations by roughly 2–3× for both influencers, with the effect strongest at lower citation percentiles (ATET ≈ 19% at the 50th percentile vs. ≈ 9% at the 90th). The negative outcome control (review scores) reduces the unadjusted ATET but does not eliminate significance, suggesting the citation boost is not fully explained by unobserved quality confounders. No traditional method-to-method comparison exists; the contribution is establishing a causal link between a single social-media intervention (influencer sharing) and downstream citation impact.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on social media's effect on academic citations (Eysenbach 2011; Peoples et al. 2016; Vaghjiani et al. 2021) from medical and ecological fields to AI/ML, where the research cycle is shorter and arXiv preprints dominate dissemination. It departs from prior studies that examined multi-user Twitter discussion by focusing on top-down curation by a small number of high-follower influencers, and from randomized-trial studies (Tonia et al. 2016; Branch et al. 2023) that found no effect, by employing optimal matching on topic embeddings (SPECTER2) and a negative-outcome-control causal inference framework borrowed from Elazar et al. (2023) and the epidemiological NOC literature (Card & Krueger 1993; Lipsitch et al. 2010). The matching methodology follows King & Nielsen (2019) recommendations, and the geographic/gender diversity analysis draws on Stanford HAI and Taulbee Survey benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The causal finding that influencer sharing increases citations by 2–3× even after controlling for paper quality (review scores) suggests that the AI/ML community's information bottleneck has shifted from peer review to a handful of social media curators, with implications for how research visibility is allocated. The geographic skew toward US-affiliated papers and the ~80:20 gender ratio among shared first authors, while not attributed to intentional bias, highlight a concentration risk that could narrow the perceived research landscape. Key limitations include the absence of a randomized trial, binary gender classification, self-reported affiliations, and the study of only two influencers, leaving open whether the effect generalizes to other platforms, fields, or curation models. The paper's broader call for evolving conference cycles and establishing journalistic standards for academic influencers points to an unresolved structural tension in how a field producing thousands of papers per year can ensure equitable discovery.
