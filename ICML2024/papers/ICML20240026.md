## Problem

Despite Adam's widespread success in deep learning, existing convergence analyses show rates that non-adaptive methods like SGD already achieve, leaving the principled role of Adam's momentum and discounting factors unexplained. The paper asks: can we identify a theoretical framework in which Adam's specific algorithmic components are not just heuristics but are provably necessary for good performance?

## Input / Output

The method operates coordinate-wise on a 1D online linear optimization problem, where the online learner receives a scalar loss sequence v_{1:t} (corresponding to stochastic gradients) and outputs a scalar update Δ_t. The key structural choice is that the learner selects the increment Δ_t rather than the iterate w_t directly (the OLU framework), and the analysis tracks dynamic regret against a time-varying comparator sequence u_{0:T-1} with path length P, rather than static regret against a fixed comparator. The final optimization guarantee (Theorem 4.1) connects the dynamic regret bound to the expected function value decrease E[F(w_T)] − F(w_0).

## Method

The paper's central observation is that Adam, under the online learning of updates (OLU) framework, is exactly recovered by plugging in a discounted instance of Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL) called β-FTRL, where losses are internally rescaled by β^{-t} to make the learner 'forget' the distant past. The main technical contribution is a novel discounted-to-dynamic conversion (Theorem B.3) that expresses the dynamic regret of any algorithm as an exact equality involving its β-discounted regret over a partition of the time horizon, enabling the transfer of static regret bounds for scale-free FTRL into dynamic regret bounds for β-FTRL. Lower bounds (Theorem 3.3) show that both momentum (aggregating past gradients) and the discounting factor (β < 1) are individually necessary: non-momentum learners suffer linear-in-T regret, and undiscounted FTRL (β=1) also fails, while β-FTRL with β = 1 − cT^{-2/3} achieves the optimal O(DGT^{2/3}) dynamic regret on bounded domains. Theorem 4.1 then bridges this to optimization, showing that better dynamic regret of the learner directly translates to better nonconvex optimization guarantees, and a concrete sparse classification experiment (Section 4.2) demonstrates Adam's advantage over SGD and undiscounted Adam in practice.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical contribution is showing that Adam (via β-FTRL with β<1) achieves a dynamic regret bound of O(D^{2/3}GP^{1/3}T^{2/3}) on bounded domains (Corollary 3.4) and O(α^{1/2}M^{3/2}G√(PT)) on unbounded domains (Corollary 3.5), both sublinear in T. In contrast, the no-momentum baseline (SGD/AdaGrad) and the no-discounting baseline (β-FTRL with β=1) provably fail to achieve sublinear dynamic regret even for P=O(1) (Theorem 3.3). In the concrete lower-bound example, Adam achieves total loss −T+o(T) while no-momentum achieves ≥−3 and no-discounting achieves ≥−T/2−3/2 (Corollary 4.2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three online learners (and their corresponding optimizers under OLU): Adam (β-FTRL with β<1), no-momentum learners (SGD/AdaGrad, form (3.2)), and no-discounting (β-FTRL with β=1, i.e., scale-free FTRL). Theorem 3.3 constructs a 2D OLO instance with P=O(1) where both baselines suffer linear-in-T dynamic regret (≥T−3 for no-momentum, ≥(T−3)/2 for no-discounting), while Corollary 3.4 shows β-FTRL with β<1 achieves sublinear O(DGT^{2/3}) regret on the same instance. In the optimization setting (Corollary 4.2), Adam achieves total loss −T+o(T) versus ≥−3 for no-momentum and ≥−T/2−3/2 for no-discounting, demonstrating that both momentum and the discounting factor are individually necessary for good dynamic performance. The paper also notes that without discounting, the best achievable bound is O(P√T) (unimprovable per Jacobsen and Cutkosky, 2022), while with discounting the optimal O(√(PT)) rate is recovered.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Online Learning of Updates (OLU) framework introduced by Cutkosky et al. (2023), which reduces optimizer design to online learner design, and on the scale-free FTRL algorithm of Orabona and Pál (2018). Its key conceptual departure is from the dominant line of Adam convergence analyses (Reddi et al., 2018; Li et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023), which show rates no better than SGD and cannot explain Adam's practical advantages. The paper also contrasts with Zheng and Kwok (2017), who derived Adam from a proximal-FTRL formulation but required heuristic adjustments; here, the OLU framework naturally yields the discounting factors and the coordinate-wise update structure without such patches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of Adam as a discounted FTRL (β-FTRL) provides the first principled theoretical justification for both of Adam's key components—momentum and the exponential discounting factor—showing via lower bounds that both are necessary for sublinear dynamic regret. This reframes Adam's practical superiority over SGD in terms of handling sparse and non-stationary gradients, a regime relevant to Transformer training. Open questions include the role of asymmetric discounting (β₁ ≠ β₂), extension to other optimizers (RMSProp, Lion), and whether the OLU framework can yield new adaptive algorithms that automatically tune to the environment without prior knowledge.
