## Problem

Existing ML-based flood simulation methods struggle with two key challenges: (1) they perform well only on smooth synthetic topographies but degrade on real-world ground elevation data with discontinuities, and (2) they lack a principled mechanism for incorporating the physics of water propagation (shallow-water equations) while handling the extreme sparsity of early-stage precipitation when the domain is still dry. This paper addresses both by designing a GNN whose message-passing is explicitly derived from conservation of momentum and mass.

## Input / Output

The model takes a static directed graph where each 30 m × 30 m DEM cell is a node with a single outgoing edge to its steepest D8 neighbor, along with per-cell rainfall time series and the previous water depth. It auto-regressively predicts the water depth field at each of K time steps (up to 40 steps in experiments), with precipitation and water depth log-transformed (log(1+x/0.01)/10) to handle their extreme sparsity. The graph topology is fixed per region and does not change with the evolving water surface.

## Method

ComGNN operates in two stages per time step. In the retention stage, each node is treated as an isolated bucket whose latent water level is computed by an MLP from the local rainfall and previous depth. In the dispersion stage, a bi-level message passing on the D8 flow-direction graph mimics the shallow-water equations: the first level computes outgoing discharges via a gated backward-difference (sigmoid of water level × MLP of water-surface elevation difference to the steepest neighbor), and the second level updates water depth by an MLP of net incoming minus outgoing flow. Multiple iterations of this bi-level pass simulate dispersion over longer distances. Training uses a hybrid loss that switches between L1 (for small errors) and L2 (for large errors) plus a penalty on negative water depths, which the authors show outperforms either loss alone on this sparse data.

## Results

On the primary evaluation (9 Harris County watersheds, 7 historical flood events, 40-step autoregressive simulation), ComGNN achieves RMSE of 0.3615, NSE of 0.8154, Pearson r of 0.8854, and CSI of 0.7463 (γ=0.001 m) at t=40, outperforming the best baseline (MeshGraphNet, RMSE 0.4807) by approximately 25% in RMSE. At the earlier time step t=20, ComGNN reaches RMSE 0.1328 and NSE 0.8218, again leading all methods. The model also achieves the best mass-conservation error (42.16 vs. 103–225 for other ML baselines) and a ~2,000× speedup over the LISFLOOD-FP hydrodynamic solver.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ComGNN outperforms all seven baselines on every metric in the main comparison (Table 1). The closest competitor is MeshGraphNet (RMSE 0.4807 vs. 0.3615 at t=40), followed by ConvLSTM (0.4397) and U-net (0.4546); ComGNN improves RMSE by ~25% over MeshGraphNet and ~18% over ConvLSTM. Notably, the one-parameter Rain-Incr baseline (h^t = h^{t-1} + α·p^t) is competitive with MP-PDE and GCN on RMSE, underscoring the value of the two-stage retention-then-dispersion inductive bias. On flat-surface experiments (Table 3), MeshGraphNet, MP-PDE, and FNO improve by 27%, 14%, and 20% in NSE respectively, confirming that their weaker performance on real topography stems from difficulty handling discontinuities. In the generalization experiment (Table 4, unseen region + unseen precipitation), GNN-based methods (ComGNN, MeshGraphNet, MP-PDE, GCN) degrade far less than CNN-based ones (U-net NSE drops to 0.0318, FNO to 0.4645), and ComGNN still leads with NSE 0.8337 at t=20. The ablation ComGNN⁻ (no retention stage) trails ComGNN by 25% in RMSE, validating the water-retention phase as critical for handling sparse early-stage flooding.

## Relation to Prior Work

ComGNN extends the GNN-based physics simulation line (MeshGraphNet, MP-PDE) and the neural operator line (FNO) to the specific domain of pluvial flood emulation, departing from prior flood-specific GNNs (Kazadi et al. 2022/2024, Bentivoglio et al. 2023) by explicitly incorporating spatially-varying rainfall as external forcing and by operating on a directed D8 flow-direction graph rather than a grid or undirected mesh. The two-stage water-retention-then-dispersion paradigm is a conceptual departure from single-stage message-passing solvers, grounding the message function in the shallow-water conservation of momentum (Eq. 8) and mass (Eq. 10) rather than learning a generic spatial operator. The paper also positions itself against convolution-based spatiotemporal predictors (ConvLSTM, U-net) and shows that the directed-graph inductive bias is critical for handling sparse, early-stage flooding on real topography.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that state-of-the-art PDE-solver architectures (FNO, MeshGraphNet, MP-PDE) degrade substantially on real, discontinuous topography but recover on flat surfaces is a useful diagnostic for the broader ML-for-PDE community, suggesting that smooth-domain benchmarks may overstate generalizability. The competitive performance of the one-parameter Rain-Incr baseline highlights that strong inductive bias (water accumulates where it falls) can rival learned spatial operators, a lesson for designing physics-informed architectures. Open questions include whether the static D8 graph can be replaced by a dynamic topology that adapts to the evolving water surface, whether the approach scales beyond the 40-step lead time constrained by a single GPU, and whether the framework transfers to other shallow-water applications (tsunami, atmospheric modeling) the authors mention.
