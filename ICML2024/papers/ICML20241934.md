## Problem

While the NTK regime explains infinite-width training as a static kernel method, it cannot account for the feature learning that lets neural networks adapt to data structure. The connection between mean-field neural networks and their induced kernel remained unclear: it was unknown whether the optimal kernel could be learned via mean-field Langevin dynamics (MFLD) and whether the learned kernel aligns with the target function while controlling its complexity.

## Input / Output

The method takes input vectors x ∈ R^d and supervised training pairs (x, y) with bounded noise, and produces a first-layer distribution μ over parameters w ∈ R^{d'} (e.g., (u, b) for tanh activation) together with the instantaneously optimal second layer a_μ. The learned kernel k_μ(x, x') = ∫ h(x;w)h(x';w) dμ(w) is the central output object, characterized by its alignment with the target function and its degrees of freedom d_λ(μ) = tr[Σ̂_μ(Σ̂_μ + nλI)^{-1}], which measures effective kernel complexity.

## Method

The paper introduces a two-timescale limit in which the second layer converges instantaneously to its optimal solution a_μ given the first-layer distribution μ, reducing the joint training problem to minimizing the limiting functional G(μ) = F(a_μ, μ) over μ alone. Despite the non-linear dependence of a_μ on μ, the authors prove G(μ) is convex when the losses are convex, derive a log-Sobolev inequality constant for the proximal distribution via the Holley-Stroock argument, and obtain linear convergence of MFLD with explicit time and particle discretization error. They then show that the empirical risk minimizer over the KL-restricted Barron space (a union of multiple RKHSs) achieves O(M) sample complexity, while any fixed-kernel linear estimator requires Ω(d^m) samples for m-sparse parity-type targets. Finally, they prove that intrinsic label noise biases the learned kernel toward large degrees of freedom and propose a label noise procedure—adding independent noise to labels at each MFLD step—that implicitly regularizes the degrees of freedom and provably converges to the global optimum of the regularized objective.

## Results

The paper establishes linear convergence of the mean-field Langevin dynamics (MFLD) with rate exp(−2αλt) to the global optimum, where α is the log-Sobolev constant of the proximal distribution. For generalization, mean-field neural networks learn the KL-restricted Barron space with n = O(M) samples (M = O(d log d)), while any fixed-kernel linear estimator requires n = Ω(d^m) samples for m-sparse parity-type functions — a polynomial gap in d. For a single-index model with tanh activation, kernel alignment improves from O(1/√d) at initialization to Ω(1) after training, and parameter alignment improves from O(1/d) to Ω(1). The label noise procedure provably reduces the degrees of freedom while maintaining linear convergence to the global optimum of the regularized objective.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is theoretical: mean-field two-layer neural networks (with joint first- and second-layer training) achieve sample complexity O(d log d) for the KL-restricted Barron space, while any kernel method with a fixed kernel requires Ω(d^m) samples for m-sparse parity problems (Theorem 4.5 vs. Theorem 4.4). This separation holds because kernel methods cannot adapt the underlying RKHS to the target function's intrinsic structure, whereas the MFLD learns a data-dependent kernel. In the numerical experiments (Section 6, f°(x)=x₁x₂, d=15, width m=2000), the kernel alignment and degrees of freedom both increase during training, and the intrinsic noise σ is shown to inflate the degrees of freedom. The proposed label noise procedure (adding independent uniform noise ẽᵢ ~ v([−σ̃,σ̃]ⁿ) at each step) reduces the degrees of freedom and improves test loss, confirming the theoretical bias-variance tradeoff. Unlike prior mean-field works (Suzuki et al. 2023b; Mahankali et al. 2023) that fix the second layer, this paper handles joint training, and unlike Abbe et al. (2022) which uses a two-phase procedure, the two-timescale limit provides a single unified optimization framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of mean-field neural network analysis (Mei et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2020) and kernel learning theory (Bach et al., 2004), bridging them via a two-timescale limit (Marion & Berthier, 2023) that separates first-layer and second-layer dynamics. Prior mean-field works either fixed the second layer to ensure boundedness or relied on the linearity of the network output in the parameter distribution; here, the two-timescale reduction makes the second layer an instantaneous functional of the first-layer distribution, turning the problem into kernel selection over a union of RKHSs (Barron space, Ma & Wu, 2022). The convergence analysis builds on the MFLD framework of Chizat (2022) and Nitanda et al. (2022), but the convexity proof must handle the non-linear dependency of a_μ on μ, which prior works circumvented by fixing the second layer. The label noise regularization connects to the implicit bias literature (Li et al., 2021; Damian et al., 2021) but targets kernel complexity (degrees of freedom) rather than flatness of minima.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous connection between mean-field neural network training and kernel learning, showing that the first layer implicitly performs kernel selection and that this is essential for achieving better sample complexity than any fixed-kernel method on Barron spaces. The finding that intrinsic label noise biases the learned kernel toward high degrees of freedom, and that adding label noise provably counteracts this, offers a concrete mechanism for understanding implicit regularization in feature learning. Limitations include the LSI constant depending exponentially on 1/λ (yielding exponential computational complexity in the regularization parameter), the restriction to two-layer networks, and the reliance on the two-timescale limit which may not capture realistic training dynamics where both layers evolve at comparable rates. Open questions include extending the analysis to deeper networks, removing the two-timescale assumption, and understanding whether the kernel alignment dynamics observed here persist in finite-width, non-mean-field regimes.
