## Problem

Experience replay methods in online continual learning suffer from unstable optimization trajectories: at each step, new data dominates the gradient, causing abrupt changes to past-data predictions that degrade accuracy even when the replay buffer stores all prior examples. This optimization-time instability is orthogonal to catastrophic forgetting and has been largely overlooked by existing replay-based approaches, which focus on preventing forgetting but not on the geometry of parameter updates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes small batches of 1–10 RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-100, 64×64 for TinyImageNet, 224×224 for CLEAR) arriving sequentially from a non-stationary stream, along with a replay buffer of past exemplars (1,000–4,000 or unlimited). At each gradient step the model jointly computes loss on the current batch and sampled replay exemplars, then applies a layerwise preconditioned update. The preconditioner itself is refreshed every T batches by feeding the entire replay buffer (or a subsample) through the network to obtain current hidden activations, making the update depend on the model's own evolving representations rather than stale statistics.

## Method

LPR replaces the standard Euclidean proximal penalty in the SGD update with a layerwise P_ℓ-norm, where P_ℓ = I + ω^ℓ Z_ℓ^T Z_ℓ and Z_ℓ are the current hidden activations of replay data at layer ℓ. This yields the update Θ_{j+1}^{(ℓ)} = Θ_j^{(ℓ)} − η P_ℓ^{-1} ∇L(Θ_j^{(ℓ)}), a preconditioned gradient step whose contraction operator selectively shrinks gradient components that would cause large changes to past-data representations while leaving components orthogonal to those directions largely intact. The key distinction from gradient projection methods is that LPR's preconditioner is a soft, full-rank contraction (not a hard projection), so it preserves replay gradient information and remains compatible with continued learning from the replay buffer. The method is loss-agnostic (applies on top of ER, DER, EMA, or LODE), requires only one extra matrix multiply per layer per step, and is parameterized by two scalars (ω_0, β) that scale the proximal strength across layers based on effective activation vector counts.

## Results

On Split-CIFAR100 (memory size 2000), LPR improves final accuracy (Acc) for all four replay baselines: ER from 29.4% to 33.3%, DER from 29.4% to 32.9%, EMA from 37.3% to 38.8%, and LODE from 29.7% to 32.6%. In the memory-unconstrained setting (all prior data stored), LPR(ER) achieves a remarkable 7% absolute Acc gain on Split-CIFAR100 (35.4% → 42.4%), demonstrating that optimization instability is orthogonal to catastrophic forgetting. LPR also consistently improves average anytime accuracy (AAA) and worst-case accuracy (WC-Acc), with the largest relative gains on WC-Acc (e.g., ER on Split-CIFAR100: 12.6% → 19.3% at memory size 2000).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LPR is applied as a drop-in optimizer modification on top of four state-of-the-art replay methods (ER, DER, EMA, LODE) and consistently improves all of them across every dataset (Split-CIFAR100, Split-TinyImageNet, Online CLEAR) and every metric (Acc, AAA, WC-Acc) at all tested memory sizes. The gains are largest on WC-Acc, indicating LPR most effectively reduces the 'forgetting and relearning' instability. Against gradient projection baselines A-GEM and AOP, LPR(ER) achieves a massive advantage: on Split-CIFAR100 (memory 2000), LPR(ER) reaches 42.4% Acc versus 3.3% for A-GEM and 4.7% for AOP, which the authors attribute to projection methods underfitting the data stream. In the memory-unconstrained setting, LPR(ER) on Split-CIFAR100 jumps from 35.4% to 42.4% Acc (+7%), while on Online CLEAR it improves from 71.8% to 74.1% Acc, confirming that the optimization-stability benefit is independent of catastrophic forgetting.

## Relation to Prior Work

LPR sits at the intersection of two lines of work: experience replay for online continual learning (ER, DER, EMA, LODE) and gradient-geometry modifications for continual learning (OWM, GPM, A-GEM, AOP). The paper positions itself as the first method to combine experience replay with a proximal point update, and explicitly argues that gradient projection methods are fundamentally incompatible with replay because they project out the replay gradient. Via the Woodbury identity, LPR's layerwise preconditioner is shown to reduce to a projection operator only in the degenerate limit of infinite proximal strength and a very small replay buffer, making it a strictly more general and replay-compatible construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key insight is that optimization instability during replay-based training is an orthogonal bottleneck to catastrophic forgetting: LPR yields large gains (up to 7% on Split-CIFAR100) even when the replay buffer stores all prior data, meaning the network has full access to past examples. This reframes part of the online continual learning problem as an optimization-geometry issue rather than purely a memory issue. Open questions include dynamically adapting the proximal strength to balance stability and plasticity, making preconditioner updates online rather than periodic, and extending the formulation to capture correlated errors across layers. The practical overhead is modest (one extra matrix multiply per layer per step), making LPR a drop-in improvement for any replay-based online learner.
