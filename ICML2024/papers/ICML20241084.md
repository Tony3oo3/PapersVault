## Problem

The Sliced-Wasserstein distance is estimated by Monte Carlo integration over the unit sphere, yielding an n^{-1/2} convergence rate that can be slow in practice. Existing variance-reduction approaches either use a single quadratic control variate (Nguyen & Ho, 2024) or nearest-neighbor regression (Leluc et al., 2024), neither of which exploits the natural orthonormal structure of the spherical integration domain. This paper asks whether the full hierarchy of spherical harmonics can be leveraged as a rich, structured set of control variates to achieve faster convergence.

## Input / Output

The method takes two probability measures (continuous Gaussians or discrete empirical distributions supported on m points in R^d), a number of random projections n, and a maximum spherical-harmonic degree 2L that determines the number of control variates s_{L,d}. The output is a single scalar estimate of SW_p^p(μ,ν). Because the integrand f_{μ,ν}^{(p)} is even, only even-degree spherical harmonics are used, halving the control-variate count. For multiple pairwise SW computations (e.g., kernel matrices), the SHCV weights are computed once and reused, making the per-pair cost identical to standard Monte Carlo.

## Method

SHCV embeds spherical harmonics of even degree 2, 4, …, 2L as orthonormal control variates into an ordinary-least-squares Monte Carlo (OLSMC) framework: after sampling n uniform directions on S^{d-1}, the integrand values are regressed on the spherical-harmonic design matrix, and the OLS intercept serves as the variance-reduced SW estimate. The key insight is that spherical harmonics form a complete orthonormal basis of L_2(S^{d-1}), so increasing L systematically reduces the residual variance σ_cv. Theoretically, the integration error satisfies |SHCV − SW| = O_P(L^{-1} n^{-1/2}), and choosing L = n^{1/(2(d-1))}/ℓ_n yields a rate of O_P(ℓ_n · n^{-d/(2(d-1))}), which for d=3 gives n^{-3/4+o(1)} versus the standard n^{-1/2}. The method is exact when the integrand is a polynomial of degree ≤ 2L (e.g., Gaussian measures with proportional covariance matrices), and its asymptotic variance is guaranteed to be no worse than the single-quadratic control-variate methods of Nguyen & Ho (2024).

## Results

On multivariate Gaussian distributions with n=500 projections, SHCV achieves MSE of 2.68e-6 in d=5, 1.93e-4 in d=10, and 2.95e-4 in d=20, outperforming standard Monte Carlo (1.45e-3, 9.45e-4, 1.47e-3) by factors of up to ~540×. Computing times remain comparable to MC (89 ms vs 81 ms for d=5), confirming the variance reduction comes at negligible additional cost. On 3D ShapeNetCore point clouds (m=2048 points), SHCV reduces MSE by a factor of ~100 over all baselines, e.g., 2.1e-8 vs 1.1e-5 for MC at n=500 on the PLANE/LAMP pair. In the Kernel SVM image classification task on UCI Digits, SHCV consistently yields 0.5–1.0 percentage points higher test accuracy than MC across all projection counts and bandwidth settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SHCV dominates all six baselines on MSE across every dimension tested. Against the two Nguyen & Ho (2024) control variate methods (CVlow, CVup), SHCV achieves a 100× MSE reduction in d=5 (2.68e-6 vs 2.67e-4 and 8.44e-4) and a 5–10× reduction in d=10 and d=20; this is expected since SHCV subsumes their single quadratic control variate within its richer spherical harmonics space. Against the control neighbors method of Leluc et al. (2024) (CVNN), SHCV wins by 160× in d=5 and 5–7× in higher dimensions, while also being faster (89 ms vs 110–127 ms). Against the QMC/RQMC methods of Nguyen et al. (2024), SHCV achieves lower MSE in all dimensions (e.g., 2.95e-4 vs 6.60e-4 for QMC and 1.17e-3 for RQMC in d=20) with comparable or lower computing time. The only setting where a baseline comes close is RQMC in d=5 (5.80e-5 vs 2.68e-6), but SHCV still wins by ~20×. On the 3D point cloud experiments, SHCV's advantage over all baselines widens to a factor of ~100, and in the Kernel SVM task it consistently improves test accuracy by 0.5–1.0 percentage points over MC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the OLSMC control-variate framework of Portier & Segers (2019) and Leluc et al. (2021) to the specific integral domain of the Sliced-Wasserstein distance, where the integration is over the unit sphere. Rather than using a single ad-hoc quadratic control variate (Nguyen & Ho, 2024) or a data-driven nearest-neighbor control (Leluc et al., 2024), the authors exploit the fact that spherical harmonics form an orthonormal basis of L₂(S^{d-1}), yielding a structured, potentially large family of control variates whose integrals are analytically known. This departs from prior SW estimation methods (standard MC, QMC/RQMC, single-quadratic CV) by combining the full Hilbert-space structure of the sphere with the growing-control-variate theory, and provides the first convergence-rate guarantee for a variance-reduced SW estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SW distance is a workhorse in generative modeling, autoencoders, Bayesian computation, and distribution regression, so a 100× MSE reduction at comparable compute cost has direct practical impact for anyone computing SW distances at scale. The theoretical convergence rate improvement (from n^{-1/2} to n^{-d/(2(d-1))}, e.g. n^{-3/4} in 3D) gives practitioners a principled way to choose the number of projections and the harmonic degree. Open questions include: combining SHCV with QMC sequences (noted as out of scope), extending beyond the uniform measure on the sphere via importance sampling, and whether the Lipschitz-only regularity of the integrand for discrete measures limits the achievable rate in high dimensions where the number of harmonics grows rapidly.
