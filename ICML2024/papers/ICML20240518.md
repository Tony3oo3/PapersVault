## Problem

Despite the proliferation of graph self-supervised tasks, the inherent relationships between them remain unexplored, and existing multi-task learning methods (AutoSSL, ParetoGNN) fail to train representations that perform well across all self-supervised tasks—sometimes underperforming even a single task. The paper asks: can we quantitatively characterize task correlations and leverage them to produce universally capable graph representations?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240518/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph (adjacency matrix A and node feature matrix X) and the set of 8 pre-trained task-specific representations H_{t_i} ∈ R^{N×d} (128-dim for small graphs, 32-dim for ogbn-arxiv). GraphTCM takes pairs of representations as input and outputs a scalar correlation value via exp(q·k^T); the final enhanced representation H' is produced by a single learnable linear combination W ∈ R^{k×d} over the k task representations. Downstream evaluation uses only a linear tuning head on the frozen enhanced representations.

## Method

The paper defines Correlation Value Cor(t₁,t₂) as the ratio of the best linear-probe loss of H_{t₁} on task t₂'s objective to the best loss of H_{t₂} on its own objective, yielding a dataset-dependent 8×8 matrix. GraphTCM models this matrix by mapping each representation through a linear layer and readout to query/key vectors, then predicting correlations via exp(q·k^T), trained with L1 loss against the ground-truth correlation values. To generate enhanced representations, a single linear transformation W is optimized to minimize the ATD-weighted sum of GraphTCM-predicted correlations between the new representation and each base task representation. Two theorems (3.4, 3.5) bound downstream error in terms of correlation values, providing theoretical justification that minimizing correlations across all self-supervised tasks yields representations with guaranteed downstream performance.

## Results

GraphTCM-enhanced representations achieve the best performance across all 6 datasets and both downstream tasks. On node classification, GraphTCM reaches 81.50% on Cora, 72.83% on CiteSeer, 77.24% on PubMed, 54.73% on ogbn-arxiv, 84.95% on Amazon-Computers, and 92.06% on Amazon-Photo, yielding an average improvement of 1.7% over the best single-task baseline. On link prediction (ROC-AUC), GraphTCM achieves 95.52% on Cora, 96.14% on CiteSeer, and 97.13% on PubMed, an average 2.2% improvement over the best baseline. In terms of representation capability (ARL), GraphTCM-enhanced representations reduce ARL by 8.42% on average compared to the best single-task baseline and by 26.53% compared to the average across all base tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GraphTCM outperforms all single-task baselines across every dataset and downstream task. On node classification, it surpasses the strongest single-task methods (SubgCon at 77.28% on Cora, DGI at 76.92%) by 4.2–4.6 points on Cora and CiteSeer respectively, and beats EdgeMask (54.13%) on ogbn-arxiv by 0.6 points. On link prediction, GraphTCM exceeds EdgeMask (92.88% on Cora, 95.61% on PubMed) by 2.6 and 1.5 points respectively. Among multi-task baselines, AutoSSL achieves the best multi-task results (e.g., 70.30% on CiteSeer NC, 96.55% on PubMed LP) but still trails GraphTCM by 2.5–4.2 points on NC and 0.6 points on LP. ParetoGNN and Multi-loss perform poorly, with ParetoGNN dropping to 52.03% on Cora NC and 77.29% on Cora LP—worse than most single-task baselines—confirming the paper's claim that existing multi-task loss-combination methods fail to produce universally capable representations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-task loss-combination paradigm in graph self-supervised learning (AutoSSL, ParetoGNN, AUX-TS) by shifting the focus from how to weight task losses to how representations trained by one task transfer to another. It borrows the task-correlation concept from Taskonomy (Zamir et al., 2018) in vision but applies it to graphs with a key distinction: correlations are measured empirically via cross-task representation evaluation rather than assumed from task structure. The resulting GraphTCM model and the ARL/ATD metrics constitute a new analytical and training framework that treats pre-trained single-task representations as the fundamental building blocks, rather than jointly optimizing a combined objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that established multi-task methods (AutoSSL, ParetoGNN) fail to surpass single-task representations in cross-task capability is a useful caution for the field, suggesting that loss-level combination is not a sufficient mechanism for building versatile graph representations. The framework of correlation values and ARL provides a principled diagnostic tool for comparing self-supervised tasks, which was previously lacking. Limitations include the restriction to 8 base tasks, a single GCN backbone, and dataset-specific correlation matrices requiring GraphTCM retraining per dataset; open questions include whether the approach scales to dozens of tasks, heterogeneous backbones, and whether the linear-combination assumption for the final representation is sufficient for larger-scale graphs.
