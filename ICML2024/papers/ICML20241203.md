## Problem

In continual learning, replay-based methods are constrained by a fixed memory buffer, causing substantial loss of historical data information and inaccurate gradient estimation that perpetuates catastrophic forgetting. The paper asks whether the SVRG-style variance reduction calibration designed for the 'batch size limit' in SGD can be adapted to handle the 'buffer size limit' in CL, yielding a more accurate gradient direction at each update step without storing all historical data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241203/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of RGB image-label pairs (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet) under a fixed reservoir-sampling buffer of 500–5000 samples, and outputs a soft-classification vector in R^K. It operates under both Class-Incremental Learning (task boundaries known, disjoint label spaces) and Task-Free Continual Learning (no task identity or boundary signals), with the model architecture fixed as ResNet-18 throughout the stream.

## Method

DGC maintains a recursively updated calibration term Γ′_DGC(t) that serves as an unbiased surrogate for the full historical gradient G(T_[1:t), θ̃_t), which is otherwise unavailable due to the buffer constraint. Within each task, the per-step gradient v_t^k combines an SVRG-calibrated current-task component with a DGC-calibrated historical component (Eq 10–11), where the historical calibrator subtracts a single-sample gradient at the stage-start parameter θ̃_t from Γ′_DGC(t), reducing variance without requiring the full history. At task transitions, Γ′_DGC is updated via a weighted average that folds in the completed task's full gradient (Eq 14). Under L-smooth and γ-strongly-convex assumptions with m ≥ 10L²/γ² steps per stage, the method achieves linear convergence in expectation (Theorem 3.3). DGC is plug-and-play: it replaces the raw buffer gradient in any reservoir-sampling CL method (ER, DER++, XDER, Dynamic ER) via a convex combination controlled by a hyperparameter α, adding only one extra gradient-sized vector of storage.

## Results

On S-CIFAR10 (buffer 2000), DGC-ER achieves 86.42% FAIA, outperforming ER (84.27%) and GCR (86.35%). On S-CIFAR100 (buffer 2000), DGC-XDER reaches 67.59% FAIA, surpassing XDER (66.51%) and GCR (63.18%). The most dramatic gains appear with Dynamic ER: DGC-Dynamic ER improves FAIA by over 6% on both S-CIFAR10 (89.90% vs 83.30%) and S-CIFAR100 (70.70% vs 64.57%). Across all three datasets and buffer sizes, DGC consistently improves the base method it is combined with, with the largest relative gains observed when the number of tasks increases (e.g., DGC-DER++ gains 3.94% with 20 tasks vs 1.18% with 5 tasks on S-CIFAR100).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DGC consistently improves every base method it is combined with: DGC-ER beats ER by 1.9–4.1% FAIA, DGC-XDER beats XDER by 0.8–2.2%, DGC-DER++ beats DER++ by 1.5–3.9%, and DGC-Dynamic ER beats Dynamic ER by 1.5–6.6% across all datasets and buffer sizes. Against the strongest non-DGC baselines, DGC-XDER (67.59% on S-CIFAR100, buffer 2000) outperforms GCR (63.18%), ICARL (64.03%), and MOCA (59.29%), while DGC-ER (86.42% on S-CIFAR10, buffer 2000) narrowly edges out GCR (86.35%) and MOCA (85.06%). SSVRG, the closest method in spirit (also SVRG-inspired), is substantially outperformed by DGC variants on all datasets (e.g., 39.09% vs 59.55% on S-CIFAR100 buffer 2000), confirming that the two-level dynamic calibration addresses the information-loss limitation of the single-pass SSVRG. On S-TinyImageNet (the hardest dataset), DGC-XDER (55.94%) and DGC-Dynamic ER (58.23%) both surpass GCR (54.60%) and ICARL (44.52%), with DGC-Dynamic ER achieving the overall best result. The improvement magnitude grows with the number of tasks: with 20 tasks on S-CIFAR100, DGC-DER++ gains 3.94% over DER++ versus only 1.18% with 5 tasks, indicating DGC is most beneficial when catastrophic forgetting is most severe.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the stochastic variance reduction framework of SVRG (Johnson & Zhang, 2013) and its streaming variant SSVRG (Frostig et al., 2015) to the continual learning setting, drawing an explicit analogy between the 'batch size limit' in SGD and the 'buffer size limit' in CL. It departs from SSVRG by identifying that SSVRG only leverages information within the fixed buffer, and proposes a two-level dynamic update (DGC) that recursively accumulates gradient calibration information across task transitions, effectively encoding more historical data than the buffer alone can hold. The method is positioned as a general-purpose gradient calibration module that can be plugged into existing replay-based CL methods (ER, DER++, XDER, Dynamic ER) rather than a standalone CL algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in DGC being a lightweight, composable add-on that improves multiple existing CL methods simultaneously, with the additional memory cost being just one gradient vector. The finding that this small calibrator can outperform simply enlarging the replay buffer (Figure 1) is a useful resource-allocation insight for practitioners. However, the theoretical guarantees rely on L-smoothness and strong convexity assumptions that are unlikely to hold for deep networks in practice, and the empirical gains (1–6%) are modest relative to the strongest baselines. Open questions include whether the approach extends to non-convex deep network training, very long task sequences where the recursive calibration may drift, and whether the smoothness of training loss translates to better generalization in deployment.
