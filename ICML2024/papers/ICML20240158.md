## Problem

While LLM agents are increasingly deployed to act on behalf of users, there is no systematic framework for evaluating how well they negotiate with each other in dynamic, multi-turn interactive settings. Existing LLM benchmarks rely on static question-answering formats that cannot capture the evolving, adversarial nature of negotiation, where agents must reason about opponents' strategies, adapt to counteroffers, and make sequential decisions under incomplete information.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240158/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each LLM agent receives a game-specific system prompt defining its resources, goals, and role, then exchanges structured XML-like tagged messages (name, resources, goal, reason, player answer, message, newly proposed trade) with an opponent agent over up to 8–10 turns. The parser enforces strict format compliance and filters certain fields (e.g., reasoning) from the opponent's view to prevent strategy leakage. Outputs include per-game win/loss outcomes, average payoffs, win rates (excluding ties), and fully serialized conversation logs that support counterfactual re-analysis.

## Method

NEGOTIATIONARENA is a Python-based open-source platform that implements three negotiation scenarios—Resource Exchange (diversify shared resources), Multi-turn Ultimatum (split a fixed pot with counteroffers), and Seller-Buyer (incomplete-information price negotiation)—each supporting multi-turn dialogue with a structured communication protocol. Agents are prompted to emit XML-like tags every turn, and a parser extracts, validates, and selectively forwards fields between agents, interrupting games on format violations. The platform serializes entire games to disk for reloading, modification, and counterfactual analysis. The empirical study pits GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-2, and Claude-2.1 against each other (60–100 games per ordered pair per scenario), then layers behavioral prompts (Cunning, Desperate) onto GPT-4 to isolate social-behavior effects, and probes irrationality through anchoring correlations, split-the-difference dynamics, over-valued buyer counteroffers, and generalization tests between classical and 3-turn Ultimatum variants.

## Results

Across three negotiation scenarios (Resource Exchange, Multi-turn Ultimatum, Seller & Buyer), GPT-4 emerges as the overall best negotiator, while GPT-3.5 is consistently the weakest. In the Ultimatum game, Claude-2.1 achieves the most consistent payoff (>60/100 against all opponents as Player 1), and GPT-4 as Player 2 in Resource Exchange wins 76% of decisive games against Claude-2.1. Social behavioral prompts (e.g., faking desperation) boost GPT-4's payoff by ~20%, and the anchoring effect is strongly present (Spearman ρ = 0.716 between initial and final price). Over-valued buyers are 4× more likely to make irrational counter-offers (P(p₁ < p₂) = 0.41 vs. 0.11 for default buyers).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper pits four LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-2, Claude-2.1) against each other in head-to-head negotiations rather than comparing against a single baseline. GPT-4 dominates as a buyer in the Seller & Buyer game, negotiating an average sale price of only $41 (vs. a $60 willingness-to-pay), outperforming all other models in that role. In the Ultimatum game, Claude-2.1 is the most consistent Player 1 (payoff >60 against every opponent), while GPT-3.5 is the weakest across all scenarios and frequently makes rule-violating moves that even distract stronger opponents like GPT-4. In the Resource Exchange game, GPT-4 and Claude-2.1 are the top negotiators, with GPT-4 winning 76% of decisive games as Player 2 against Claude-2.1, though Claude-2.1 achieves a higher average payoff (2.45 vs. 1.38) in that position. Social-behavior ablations show that a 'Desperate' prompt increases GPT-4's payoff by ~20% over the default, while a 'Cunning' prompt raises the Ultimatum win rate to 82% but produces a highly bimodal payoff distribution (high reward, high risk of zero payoff).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the long tradition of using game-theoretic scenarios (ultimatum games, buyer-seller negotiations) to study strategic behavior, and on a growing line of work evaluating LLMs in interactive games (Akata et al., 2023; Guo, 2023; Aher et al., 2022; Fu et al., 2023; Schneider et al., 2023). It departs from prior LLM-negotiation work by (1) providing an open-source, extensible platform rather than a one-off experiment, (2) focusing on multi-turn single-shot games with known rational strategies as a reference point, and (3) explicitly probing social-behavior manipulation and irrationality rather than learning-based improvement. The concurrent work of Davidson et al. (2024) uses a similar framework but differs in communication structure and payoff design; NEGOTIATIONARENA emphasizes social patterns and irrationality while Davidson et al. emphasize faithfulness and instruction-following.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NEGOTIATIONARENA provides a reusable, open-source infrastructure for studying LLM-LLM interaction dynamics, filling a gap left by static benchmarks that cannot capture dynamic, multi-turn strategic behavior. The finding that simple social manipulation (pretending desperate) boosts payoffs by ~20% and that weaker agents' errors can actively degrade stronger agents' performance highlights concrete vulnerabilities for anyone deploying LLM agents in adversarial or multi-party settings. Open questions include whether these irrationalities (anchoring, failure to generalize rational strategies across game variants, context-inappropriate split-the-difference) persist in newer models, whether the observed social-behavior effects are robust across prompt formulations, and how findings transfer to human-LLM negotiation settings.
