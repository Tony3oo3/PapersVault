## Problem

IPO (offline, contrastive) and Nash-MD-PG (online, game-theoretic) are two recent preference optimisation methods with complementary strengths but no known theoretical connection. No existing algorithm simultaneously combines contrastive updates, online data generation, and regularised sampling. This paper identifies the missing bridge and uses it to construct a new algorithm class that inherits the best properties of both.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of text prompts, a trained pairwise preference model p_φ (a 3B-parameter T5X-XL encoder-decoder), and a frozen reference policy π^ref (a 770M-parameter T5X-L SFT checkpoint). At each training step, two fresh generations are sampled per prompt from the current policy (or its geometric mixture with π^ref for IPO-MD), scored by p_φ, and fed into a contrastive loss. The output is a fine-tuned policy evaluated via PaLM2 side-by-side preference judgements on XSum validation prompts, with the best checkpoint selected by sweeping τ ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0, 10.0} and β ∈ {0.125, 0.25} over 30k training steps.

## Method

The paper first proves that the stationary point of Online IPO (IPO with data sampled from the current policy rather than a fixed distribution) is exactly the Nash equilibrium of the regularised two-player preference game optimised by Nash-MD-PG, and that the expected gradient of Online IPO matches the self-play update direction (Propositions 4.1–4.2). Building on this equivalence, IPO-MD(β) replaces the pure self-play data distribution with the geometric mixture π^(1−β)(π^ref)^β, yielding a contrastive, off-policy algorithm whose fixed point coincides with that of Nash-MD-PG(β) for all β ∈ [0,1]. In practice, the mixture is implemented as a one-step-at-a-time interpolation of logits between the online and reference policies. The contrastive structure of the IPO loss (updating both π(y⁺) and π(y⁻) per pair) provides a variance-reduction advantage over the non-contrastive Nash-MD-PG gradient under a sufficient covariance condition (Proposition E.1). Experiments on TL;DR/XSum summarisation with T5X-L policies show IPO and IPO-MD are statistically indistinguishable and consistently outperform DPO, SLiC, Nash-MD-PG, and RLHF baselines.

## Results

On the summarisation task (evaluated via PaLM2 side-by-side preference), online IPO achieves the highest win-rate, beating DPO (0.608), Nash-MD-PG (0.621), SLiC (0.608), and RL (0.791). IPO-MD is statistically indistinguishable from IPO (0.515 ± 0.024) and also consistently outperforms all other baselines (e.g., 0.600 vs DPO, 0.778 vs RL). Online methods substantially outperform their offline counterparts: online IPO beats offline IPO by 0.972 and the SFT baseline by 0.996, at roughly 3× the computational cost due to runtime generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares online IPO and IPO-MD against four baselines: DPO, Nash-MD-PG, SLiC, and RL (regularised policy gradient / RLHF). Online IPO wins against all four baselines with margins of 0.108–0.291 in preference probability, with the largest gap over RL (0.791) and the smallest over DPO (0.608). IPO-MD is statistically indistinguishable from IPO (0.515 ± 0.024) and also beats every baseline, though with slightly smaller margins (e.g., 0.600 vs DPO, 0.778 vs RL). DPO and Nash-MD-PG perform closely to each other (0.520 ± 0.041), as do SLiC and Nash-MD-PG (0.521 ± 0.029), while RL is the weakest baseline overall. In the online-vs-offline comparison (Table 3), online IPO dominates offline IPO (0.972) and the SFT initialisation (0.996), confirming that the online data-generating loop is critical for final alignment quality on this task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected lines of preference optimisation: the offline contrastive IPO loss (Azar et al., 2023) and the online game-theoretic Nash-MD-PG algorithm (Munos et al., 2023). The key theoretical contribution is showing that when IPO is made online (sampling data from the current policy and scoring with a learned preference model), its stationary point coincides exactly with the Nash equilibrium of the regularised two-player preference game that Nash-MD-PG optimises. Building on this equivalence, the authors propose IPO-MD, which retains IPO's contrastive quadratic loss while adopting Nash-MD-PG's geometric mixture sampling between the online and reference policies, thereby interpolating between offline and online regimes via a single hyperparameter β.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical equivalence between a contrastive supervised loss and a game-theoretic self-play objective provides a unifying lens for understanding preference optimisation algorithms and suggests that contrastivity (using both samples in each update) can reduce gradient variance without changing the fixed point. Empirically, IPO and IPO-MD emerge as the most robust methods on a summarisation task, consistently outperforming DPO, SLiC, Nash-MD-PG, and RLHF baselines. However, the experiments are limited to a single 770M-parameter T5X model on one summarisation task, and the gap between IPO and IPO-MD is within statistical noise; the authors themselves note that scaling to 100B+ parameter conversational agents remains future work. Open questions include whether the contrastive variance-reduction advantage persists at scale, whether the Nash-equilibrium framing provides practical benefits over simpler best-response objectives in multi-turn dialogue, and how these methods interact with reward-model overoptimisation.
