## Problem

Traditional LLM evaluation assumes a fixed task distribution, but in practice humans decide where to deploy LLMs based on beliefs formed from limited interactions. This paper asks how humans generalize from observing an LLM's response to one question to predicting its performance on another, and whether more capable models are actually better aligned with these human generalizations when deployment decisions are human-driven.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242000/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The belief-change prediction model takes a pair of questions (x, x') drawn from 16,347 questions across 79 MMLU and BBH tasks, plus a binary indicator of whether the LLM answered x' correctly, and outputs a binary prediction of whether a human's belief about the LLM's ability on x changes. For alignment evaluation, the BERT-predicted posterior beliefs are compared against actual LLM correctness across 500 Monte Carlo question-pair samples, producing a weighted generalized accuracy score parameterized by α (where α/(1+α) maps to a deployment confidence threshold from 50% to 99%).

## Method

The paper introduces a formal framework showing that human deployment distributions h(x|f) depend on the model itself, breaking dominance orderings between models. Data collection uses a 7-stage epsilon-greedy bandit that iteratively fine-tunes BERT to identify question pairs likely to elicit belief changes, yielding 18,972 human generalization examples from Prolific. The human generalization function is modeled as an ensemble of 5 fine-tuned BERT-base classifiers (AUC 0.812), which outperforms larger LLMs including Llama-2 13B, GPT-3.5, and GPT-4 in predicting belief changes. Alignment is then quantified via a weighted generalized accuracy metric (Equation 7) that penalizes overconfidence asymmetrically, revealing that GPT-4—despite dominating on raw accuracy—achieves the worst alignment in high-stakes (99% threshold) settings because humans become overconfident in its capabilities after brief interactions.

## Results

On the task of predicting whether a human's belief about an LLM's correctness will change after observing its response to another question, fine-tuned BERT achieves the best AUC of 0.812 (NLL 0.593) on 492 held-out test pairs, outperforming all larger and more recent models. In the alignment evaluation (Table 2), GPT-4 achieves the highest weighted generalized accuracy at the 50% deployment threshold (0.569), but its performance reverses at the 99% threshold where it scores the lowest (0.379), while smaller models like MistralInstruct (7B) score 0.384. This reversal demonstrates that more capable models can be less aligned with human generalizations when the cost of mistakes is high.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Fine-tuned BERT (AUC 0.812) outperforms all other models at predicting human belief change, including Llama-2 13B (0.787), Llama-2 7B (0.755), GPT-4 (0.640), and GPT-3.5 turbo (0.537). The gap between BERT and the non-text baselines (Previous correct: 0.599; Same subject + previous correct: 0.688) confirms that textual content carries substantial predictive signal beyond simple task-matching. Notably, BERT also outperforms the fixed-embedding + XGBoost pipeline (0.682), suggesting that fine-tuned contextual representations capture structure that static sentence embeddings miss. Among the larger LLMs, GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 perform near or below chance (AUC 0.640 and 0.537 respectively), indicating that scaling alone does not preserve the representational structure needed to model human generalization. In the alignment evaluation (Table 2), GPT-4 leads at the 50% threshold (0.569) but falls to last place at the 99% threshold (0.379), while MistralInstruct 7B and StripedHyenaNous 7B maintain higher scores (0.384) under high-stakes deployment, confirming that smaller models are better aligned when mistakes are costly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of task delegation in ML (Lubars & Tan, 2019; Lai et al., 2022), distribution shift (Koh et al., 2021), and LLM evaluation, but does not build on a single prior method. It introduces the 'human generalization function' as a formal object and shows it is predictable with standard NLP tools, repurposing BERT fine-tuning and bandit-based data collection for a novel prediction task. The theoretical contribution—that dominance orderings between models are not preserved under human deployment distributions—extends the classical fixed-distribution evaluation framework (Equation 1) to a model-dependent deployment setting, a shift that prior LLM benchmark work has not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that GPT-4 has the worst alignment with human generalizations in high-stakes (99% confidence) deployment settings, while smaller models outperform it, has direct practical implications for LLM deployment in safety-critical domains. The 19K-example dataset and the BERT-based prediction model provide a reusable infrastructure for studying how humans form beliefs about LLM capabilities. Key limitations include modeling the human generalization function in the aggregate (no individual heterogeneity), restricting to single-question generalizations, and assuming the function is static over time. Open questions include whether interfaces or interventions can improve alignment, whether multi-question belief updates follow Bayes rule from single-question data, and how the function evolves as users gain experience with LLMs.
