## Problem

LLMs frequently generate hallucinated or non-factual content, and while retrieval-augmented and abstention-based approaches improve factuality, none provide precise, user-specified probability guarantees on open-ended outputs. Existing conformal prediction methods for LMs are restricted to multiple-choice, token-level, or approximate settings, leaving a gap for exact conformal correctness guarantees on free-form language model outputs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241468/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LM (GPT-4), an input prompt, a small calibration set (~50 examples) with human-annotated sub-claim factuality labels, and a target error rate α ∈ (0,1). It produces a single natural language sequence (not a set) that implicitly represents a confidence set via entailment, accompanied by a marginal guarantee that the output is correct with probability at least 1−α. The output retains a subset of the original sub-claims with unreliable ones removed, and can degenerate to an abstention (empty output) if no sub-claims pass the conformal threshold.

## Method

The key insight is that LM correctness is equivalent to an uncertainty quantification problem: the output y is correct iff the ground truth y* belongs to the entailment set E(y) = {y' : y' ⇒ y}. The method constructs a back-off sequence F_t(x) by decomposing the LM output into sub-claims, scoring each sub-claim (e.g., via frequency scoring that samples 5 alternate generations at temperature 1.0 and counts sub-claim recurrence), and keeping only sub-claims with score ≥ t. The conformal threshold q̂_α is the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n-th quantile of calibration scores, where each score r(x,y*) is the minimum threshold at which all retained sub-claims are entailed by y*. Under the assumption that the merger function preserves entailment relations, scores reduce to checking entailment on individual sub-claims rather than merged outputs, making annotation tractable. The final output F_{q̂_α}(x) is guaranteed to satisfy P(Y* ∈ E(L̄(X))) ≥ 1−α over exchangeable data, with a matching upper bound when the entailment sets are nested.

## Results

Using frequency scoring with GPT-4, conformal factuality achieves 80–90% correctness guarantees across all three benchmarks while retaining the majority of sub-claims. Specifically, factuality improves from ~30% to ~80% on FActScore (retaining ~50% of sub-claims), from ~78% to ~93% on Natural Questions (removing ~25% of sub-claims), and from ~75% to ~95% on MATH (removing only ~10% of sub-claims). Empirical factuality closely tracks the target 1−α across 1000 random calibration/test splits, confirming the theoretical guarantee of Theorem 3.1 in practice.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other conformal prediction or factuality methods head-to-head; the primary comparison is between the base GPT-4 model and conformal factuality with different sub-claim scoring functions. Among practical scoring functions, frequency scoring (sampling 5 alternate outputs and counting sub-claim appearance) consistently outperforms GPT-4 confidence scoring (directly prompting GPT-4 for a confidence score) across all three datasets, achieving higher factuality at the same sub-claim retention rate. Random and ordinal scoring serve as negative baselines and perform poorly, confirming that meaningful uncertainty estimation is necessary. Oracle scoring (using ground-truth entailment) provides an upper bound and shows the theoretical ceiling of the framework. The key practical finding is that frequency scoring achieves 20–50 percentage-point gains in correctness over base GPT-4 while retaining most of the original output, making the conformal guarantee both achievable and useful.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on split conformal prediction (Shafer and Vovk, 2008; Gupta et al., 2022) and repurposes it for open-ended language generation by establishing a novel correspondence: each LM output implicitly defines a conformal set via its entailment set (all statements that entail it). Prior conformal prediction for LMs was restricted to token-level (Ravfogel et al., 2023), multiple-choice (Kumar et al., 2023), or approximate sequence-level settings (Quach et al., 2023), none of which yield exact guarantees on open-ended outputs. The sub-claim scoring implementation draws on SelfCheckGPT-style self-consistency (Manakul et al., 2023) and confidence elicitation (Tian et al., 2023), but the key contribution is the meta-algorithmic framework that converts any such uncertainty estimator into a conformally guaranteed output.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework providing exact, distribution-free correctness guarantees on open-ended LM outputs, which is critical for high-stakes deployments in health, law, and robotics where a known probability of correctness is required. Key limitations include marginal (not conditional) guarantees, sensitivity to distribution shift, and the need for task-specific calibration data; extending to conditional or adaptive guarantees remains open. The practical impact is significant: the approach is model-agnostic, requires only ~50 annotated examples, and the sub-claim decomposition makes the guarantees interpretable. Future work on better uncertainty estimators, conditional conformal methods, and larger-scale validation would strengthen the practical utility.
