## Problem

Standard RLHF reward training suffers from two critical failures: reward overfitting (test cross-entropy loss degrades after one epoch) and reward overoptimization (true policy reward peaks then declines with continued optimization). The paper identifies the root cause as the inadequacy of empirical cross-entropy loss on long-tailed preference data, where a single mislabeled comparison on a rarely-observed pair can drive the estimated reward to infinity, corrupting both the reward model and the downstream policy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pairwise comparison dataset (prompt, two responses, binary preference label), a parameterized reward model (a language model with a linear scoring head), and two step sizes α (model gradient step) and β (label smoothing rate). It produces a reward model whose test loss continues decreasing well beyond one epoch, and when used to fine-tune a policy via PPO, yields a policy whose true reward keeps growing rather than peaking and declining. Experiments validate the approach on both tabular multi-armed bandit problems and neural network reward models (125M, 1B, 3B parameters) on the HH and TLDR datasets, with a fully-trained 6B GPT-J reward model serving as the ground-truth labeler.

## Method

IDS alternates two updates each epoch: a gradient step on the cross-entropy loss using the current soft labels, followed by a label update that replaces each hard preference label with a convex combination of its previous value and the model's predicted probability for that pair. The key mechanism is a two-scale dynamic: for frequently observed pairs, the reward updates much faster than the labels (so labels remain near their true values and the reward converges to ground truth), while for rarely observed pairs, the labels update faster than the reward (pulling labels toward 1/2 and keeping the reward near its initialization). This implicitly penalizes under-observed arms without requiring explicit confidence intervals, making the method compatible with arbitrary neural network parameterizations. A differential-equation analysis (Theorem 3.3) with a Lyapunov argument proves that the estimated preference probability converges to the ground-truth frequency for sufficiently observed pairs, while the reward difference for under-observed pairs remains bounded near zero.

## Results

In the multi-armed bandit setting (K=10, K=20), IDS test cross-entropy loss continues to decrease until convergence, while both MLE and Pessimistic MLE exhibit reward overfitting after a few epochs. In the KL-reward tradeoff, IDS converges to the optimal ground-truth reward at high KL, whereas MLE and Pessimistic MLE suffer from overoptimization (reward peaks then declines). In neural network experiments on the HH dataset with 1B-parameter reward models, MLE test loss begins increasing after 1 epoch, while IDS enables continuous test loss decrease for more than 3 epochs. For policy learning via PPO, the ground-truth reward under IDS continues to grow over thousands of steps, while MLE-based policies exhibit clear overoptimization within a few thousand steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDS consistently outperforms the standard MLE baseline (Ouyang et al., 2022) on both reward training and policy learning: MLE test loss degrades after 1 epoch while IDS continues improving for 3+ epochs, and MLE-based policies suffer overoptimization (ground-truth reward peaks then drops) while IDS policies maintain monotonic improvement. Against Pessimistic MLE (Zhu et al., 2023a), IDS wins in the unbounded-reward bandit setting where Pessimistic MLE also suffers from both overfitting and overoptimization, though the paper notes Pessimistic MLE is theoretically guaranteed to work under bounded reward assumptions. Against Laplace Smoothing (Chen & Goodman, 1999, with α=0.05), IDS achieves substantially better convergence: Laplace Smoothing only marginally improves the MLE convergence point but remains far inferior to IDS in both reward training loss and resulting policy quality. On the TLDR dataset, while the absolute gap in test loss between MLE and IDS is smaller than on HH, the resulting policy reward difference remains significant, confirming the advantage generalizes across datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the pessimistic MLE framework of Zhu et al. (2023a), which introduced confidence-interval-based reward correction for RLHF, but replaces the explicit confidence penalty with an implicit one achieved through iterative label smoothing. The conceptual bridge to knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) is notable: IDS iteratively replaces hard labels with soft predictions from the same model, rather than transferring knowledge between separate teacher and student networks. The multi-armed bandit simplification draws on the dueling bandit and offline RL literature to provide a tractable setting where overfitting and overoptimization can be formally characterized, departing from the purely empirical observations in Ouyang et al. (2022) and Gao et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical explanation for why reward models overfit after one epoch in RLHF, attributing it to the inadequacy of cross-entropy loss under long-tailed comparison distributions—a root cause that unifies both overfitting and overoptimization. The IDS algorithm is practical (no confidence interval estimation needed, compatible with arbitrary neural network parameterizations) and empirically extends stable training beyond the one-epoch limit. Open questions include whether the theoretical guarantees extend beyond the tabular bandit setting to high-dimensional neural reward models, whether bias (not just variance) in human preferences contributes to the observed phenomena, and whether the iterative smoothing idea generalizes to broader classification and prediction tasks beyond RLHF.
