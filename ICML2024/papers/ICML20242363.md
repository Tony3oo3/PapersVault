## Problem

Diffusion-based video editing systems routinely produce quality deterioration in the form of content blur and content flicker, which the authors trace to a 'high-frequency leak': the denoising process fails to accurately synthesize high-frequency components. Existing quality-enhancement modules (attention, propagation, optical flow guidance) rely on fixed receptive fields—either uniform sliding windows or the full video length—that cannot adapt to the frequency content being synthesized at each denoising step, making them inherently vulnerable to this leak.

## Input / Output

FRAG is a plug-and-play module that operates entirely in the VQ-VAE latent space (64×64 spatial, 4 channels, up to 48 frames) of a pretrained Stable Diffusion v2.1 pipeline, conditioned on a CLIP text embedding. It takes the t-step latent noise z_t as input at each denoising iteration and outputs a temporal group g_t (a partition of frames into variable-size clusters) that replaces the fixed receptive field of whatever quality-enhancement module (propagation, attention, optical-flow guidance) is already present in the UNet. No additional training or fine-tuning is required; the method is model-agnostic across supervised and zero-shot editing systems.

## Method

FRAG exploits the empirically observed 'denoising spectral characteristic'—low-frequency attributes emerge in early denoising steps while high-frequency attributes are synthesized later—and adapts the receptive field accordingly. Its first module, Frequency Adaptive Refinement, applies a 2D adaptive frequency pass filter (a cylindrical low-pass filter with Gaussian-smoothed edges) whose radius r is dynamically set by computing the spatial moment of the differential frequency distribution between consecutive denoising steps, thereby preserving only the frequency band currently being synthesized. Its second module, Temporal Grouping, performs agglomerative hierarchical clustering on the refined latent noise frames using minimum-distance linkage, then a logistic 'group scheduler' cuts the dendrogram at a step-dependent rank: early steps yield few large groups (wide receptive fields for low-frequency coherence) while later steps yield many small groups (narrow fields for high-frequency detail). The resulting temporal groups are injected into the UNet's quality-enhancement modules by simply replacing the frame-range parameter l with each group size g_t^i, requiring no architectural modification to the base model.

## Results

On the DAVIS and TGVE benchmarks, FRAG consistently improves all five evaluation criteria across four editing systems. The most notable gains are in fidelity (e.g., TokenFlow PSNR improves from 15.1 to 18.2, SSIM from 0.691 to 0.736) and human preference (e.g., TAV preference jumps from 0.27 to 0.73). In spectral analysis, FRAG substantially boosts high-frequency quality (e.g., TokenFlow high-freq PSNR: 13.1→16.3), confirming that the method effectively addresses the high-frequency leak problem. FVD (lower is better) also improves across all systems, with TokenFlow+FRAG achieving the best value of 2841.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FRAG is applied as a plug-and-play module on top of four state-of-the-art video editing systems: Tune-A-Video (TAV, attention-based), FateZero (zero-shot attention), FLATTEN (optical-flow-guided), and TokenFlow (propagation-based). FRAG wins on every metric for every baseline. The largest relative gains appear with TAV, where human preference nearly triples (0.27→0.73) and FVD drops by ~6%, reflecting TAV's severe temporal flickering that FRAG's dynamic receptive field corrects. TokenFlow benefits most in fidelity (PSNR +3.1, SSIM +0.045), as its fixed-size propagation window is the most vulnerable to high-frequency leak. FLATTEN, which already uses optical-flow priors for consistency, shows the smallest relative improvement, consistent with the authors' observation that prior-guided methods are less sensitive to receptive-field adjustments. Across all systems, high-frequency spectral PSNR improves by 2.1–3.2 points, directly validating the paper's core claim that the quality deterioration stems from a high-frequency leak during denoising.

## Relation to Prior Work

FRAG positions itself as a model-agnostic, training-free plug-and-play module for existing diffusion-based video editing systems (TokenFlow, Tune-A-Video, FLATTEN, FateZero), which all rely on fixed receptive fields (sliding windows or full-video attention) for quality enhancement. It departs from this fixed-field paradigm by introducing a dynamic temporal group that adaptively resizes the receptive field according to the denoising spectral characteristic—the observation that low-frequency attributes are synthesized early and high-frequency attributes later in the denoising process. While frequency analysis in diffusion models has been explored by concurrent works (Si et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024; He et al., 2024), FRAG is the first to exploit this spectral ordering to dynamically configure the temporal operating range of quality enhancement modules within the UNet, combining an adaptive frequency pass filter with agglomerative hierarchical clustering for frame grouping.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FRAG addresses a persistent and visually obvious quality degradation (blurring and flickering) in diffusion video editing by identifying its root cause as a high-frequency leak during denoising, offering a practical, training-free solution that can be applied to any existing editing pipeline. However, the authors acknowledge sensitivity across quality enhancement modules (strongest with propagation, weaker with prior-guidance methods) and limited robustness for longer or more diverse scenes, suggesting that scene-aware temporal grouping remains an open challenge. Broader open questions include prompt sensitivity, editing speed for tuning-based methods, and the underexplored intersection of video editing with vision-language models for high-level tasks like dialogue and commonsense reasoning.
