## Problem

Neural operators learn conservation laws (mass, momentum, energy) purely from data, so they can only approximately satisfy them due to finite training samples and measurement noise. This limitation is particularly severe in small-data regimes where generating simulation data is prohibitively expensive, and existing physics-informed approaches require full knowledge of the governing PDEs, making them inapplicable to hidden physics discovery.

## Input / Output

The method consumes input function values (initial conditions, boundary conditions, body loads) sampled on discrete grid points—either uniform (torus or non-periodic) or non-uniform (irregular meshes)—and produces a divergence-free output field (velocity, displacement, or flux) at the same grid locations. The output is structured as the row-wise divergence of a p×p skew-symmetric matrix-valued function, which guarantees ∇·u = 0 by construction. For mixed problems with both divergence-free and non-divergence-free variables, only the divergence-free subset is routed through the differentiation layer while the remainder is predicted by a standard NO head.

## Method

The key insight is that any vector field expressible as u = ⋆dμ (where μ is a (p−2)-form, equivalently a skew-symmetric matrix-valued function) is automatically divergence-free because div(⋆dμ) = ddμ = 0. The architecture modifies any base NO (FNO, GNO, INO, G-FNO) by replacing the final projection layer with two components: (1) a trainable projection that maps the last-layer features to a p(p−1)/2-dimensional vector representing the independent entries of μ, and (2) a fixed differentiation layer D whose weights are pre-calculated via Fourier spectral differentiation (uniform periodic grids), Fourier continuation (non-periodic uniform grids), or meshfree quadrature (non-uniform grids) to compute the row-wise divergence of μ. This add-on layer introduces negligible trainable parameters and computational overhead relative to the base NO, yet guarantees the continuity equation is satisfied exactly up to the numerical differentiation error (spectral convergence for smooth fields on uniform grids, O(Δx^{m+1}) for meshfree quadrature).

## Results

On the incompressible Navier-Stokes problem (Case 1, T_in=10, T=20), clawGFNO achieves relative L2 errors of 12.10% (10 samples), 4.76% (100), and 1.21% (1000), outperforming the best baseline (KNO) by 40.3%, 58.3%, and 86.6% respectively. On the atmospheric gravity wave problem, clawFNO achieves 6.64% relative L2 error versus 12.54% for FNO and 14.34% for GFNO — a 47% improvement over the best non-claw baseline. In the material deformation problem on irregular grids, clawINO reaches 1.00% error with 100 training samples versus 2.40% for INO and 1.79% for MeshGraphNets. The L2-norm divergence of clawNO predictions is 1–2 orders of magnitude smaller than all baselines (e.g., 1.2e-3 for clawGFNO vs. 6.6e-2 for GFNO).

## Baselines & Comparisons

clawGFNO consistently outperforms GFNO across all data regimes, with improvements of 59.1% (10 samples), 37.2% (100), and 61.2% (1000) in Case 1, confirming that the divergence-free constraint is most beneficial in small-data settings. Against FNO, clawFNO gains 43.8% in the small-data regime but only 0.7% in the large-data regime, as expected since FNO can learn the conservation law from sufficient data. KNO is the strongest non-claw baseline in the small-data NS setting (20.26% vs. clawGFNO's 12.10%), but degrades sharply at 1000 samples (9.09%), while clawGFNO maintains 1.21%. LNN fails to learn the correct solution entirely (errors >20% across all regimes) due to temporal integration errors from sparse time sampling. In the graph-based material deformation setting, clawINO beats INO by 15.7–26.4%, GNO by 28–63%, MP-PDE by 24–55%, and MeshGraphNets by 63–73% across the three data regimes. In atmospheric modeling, clawFNO (6.64%) nearly halves the error of FNO (12.54%) and GFNO (14.34%), with the largest gains near mountain orography where wave reflection is most challenging.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the neural operator family (FNO, GNO, INO, G-FNO) as base architectures and on the classical differential forms theory of Barbarosie (2011) and Kelliher (2021), which shows that any divergence-free vector field can be written as the Hodge star of the exterior derivative of a skew-symmetric matrix-valued function. The key departure from prior work is twofold: unlike physics-informed neural operators (PINO) that impose known PDEs as soft loss penalties, clawNO encodes conservation laws as a hard architectural constraint requiring only data pairs; and unlike Richter-Powell et al. (2022) who use the same skew-symmetric representation within PINNs with automatic differentiation, clawNO replaces automatic differentiation with a pre-calculated numerical differentiation layer (Fourier spectral or meshfree quadrature) suitable for the discrete-grid setting of neural operators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in scientific ML: purely data-driven neural operators require large datasets to implicitly learn conservation laws, which is prohibitively expensive when each simulation takes hours to days. By guaranteeing divergence-free outputs by construction, clawNOs achieve 40–60% error reductions in small-data regimes across fluid dynamics, atmospheric modeling, and solid mechanics, with the gap narrowing as data grows (as expected, since baselines can eventually learn conservation from data). Open questions include extending the framework to energy and charge conservation, handling discontinuous solutions (shocks) where the current error estimates break down, and whether the pre-calculated differentiation layer can be replaced by learned quadrature weights for adaptive accuracy.
