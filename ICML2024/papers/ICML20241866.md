## Problem

Multi-agent debate (MAD) strategies have been proposed to improve LLM truthfulness, but no prior work systematically compares them against alternative prompting approaches such as self-consistency and ensembling on cost, time, and accuracy trade-offs. This leaves no consensus on whether debate is worth its additional API overhead, and no understanding of whether MAD's apparent underperformance is inherent or merely a hyperparameter sensitivity issue.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241866/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each system receives a multiple-choice question (4–5 options) from medical or reasoning datasets, optionally augmented with few-shot exemplars or chain-of-thought instructions. In debate configurations, multiple agents iteratively produce and revise answers across rounds before a judge or consensus mechanism selects a final capital-letter answer; non-debate methods (Self-Consistency, Medprompt) sample independent reasoning paths and aggregate by majority vote or refinement. The benchmark additionally tracks per-question API cost in USD, total token count, and wall-clock latency alongside accuracy.

## Method

The paper implements seven prompting strategies—Medprompt, Society of Minds, Ensemble Refinement, ChatEval, Self-Consistency, Single-agent, and Multi-Persona—within a unified open-source framework (DebateLLM) and evaluates them across seven datasets (MedQA, PubMedQA, MMLU, CosmosQA, CIAR, GPQA, Chess) using GPT-3.5-turbo. The central empirical finding is that MAD protocols do not reliably outperform non-debate methods, but this gap is largely attributable to MAD's heightened sensitivity to hyperparameters (agent count, rounds, prompt style, summarization). The paper's novel technical contribution is agreement modulation: a simple prompt instruction ('you should agree with the other agents X% of the time') that controls the degree of inter-agent agreement at the outset of a debate. Tuning this single parameter transforms Multi-Persona from the worst-performing protocol to the best, achieving a new state-of-the-art on MedQA for GPT-3.5, while the optimal setting is dataset-dependent (e.g., counter-intuitive CIAR benefits from high disagreement, whereas MedQA benefits from high agreement). A K-fold cross-dataset hyperparameter transfer analysis further shows that optimal settings are dataset-specific and do not generalize uniformly across model architectures (GPT-4 vs. Mixtral 8x7B).

## Results

On MedQA, Medprompt achieves the highest overall accuracy (0.65) among all strategies without hyperparameter tuning, while the best debating strategy (Society of Minds) reaches 0.64. Across all seven datasets, no single protocol dominates: Medprompt leads on medical datasets (0.65 MedQA, 0.77 PubMedQA), Self-Consistency leads on MMLU (0.78), and Single Agent leads on GPQA (0.33). The paper's key contribution—agreement modulation—yields a ~15% accuracy improvement for Multi-Persona on a USMLE subset and a new state-of-the-art result for GPT-3 on full MedQA, surpassing all non-debate protocols.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Medprompt (Nori et al., 2023) is the strongest non-debate baseline, achieving 0.65 on MedQA and 0.77 on PubMedQA, outperforming all MAD strategies in their default configurations. Among debating protocols, Society of Minds (Du et al., 2023) is the best performer at 0.64 on MedQA, narrowly trailing Medprompt. Ensemble Refinement (Singhal et al., 2023) matches SoM on MedQA (0.64) and leads on MMLU (0.76) and GPQA (0.32), but shows high variance across datasets. ChatEval (Chan et al., 2023) and Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023) both plateau at 0.60 on MedQA, with Self-Consistency excelling on MMLU (0.78) and CIAR (0.56). Multi-Persona (Liang et al., 2023) is the weakest baseline at 0.58 on MedQA, but the paper's proposed agreement modulation transforms it into the top performer, surpassing Medprompt and all other protocols on MedQA—demonstrating that MAD's underperformance is attributable to hyperparameter sensitivity rather than an inherent limitation of debate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first systematic, unified benchmark of multi-agent debate (MAD) strategies—Society of Minds, Multi-Persona, ChatEval—against non-debate prompting approaches such as self-consistency, Ensemble Refinement, and Medprompt, all under a common API and evaluation harness. It builds directly on the MAD literature (Du et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2023) and the medical Q&A prompting literature (Singhal et al., 2023; Nori et al., 2023), but departs from prior work by treating all strategies as comparable configurations rather than assuming debate is inherently superior. The novel contribution is the 'agreement modulation' prompt, which reframes the debate hyperparameter space around a single interpretable knob (the percentage of time agents are instructed to agree), a dimension not previously isolated in the MAD literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway for practitioners is that investing in complex multi-agent debate infrastructure may not yield accuracy gains over simpler ensembling or self-consistency baselines, and that the performance gap is largely attributable to hyperparameter sensitivity rather than a fundamental limitation of debate. The agreement modulation result—where a one-line prompt change flips Multi-Persona from worst to best performer—suggests that future MAD research should focus on calibrating agent interaction dynamics rather than adding more agents or rounds. Key open questions include whether these findings transfer to stronger or architecturally different models (the Mixtral 8x7B results were notably inconsistent), whether agreement modulation generalizes beyond medical Q&A, and whether the cost-efficiency trade-off shifts when using open-source models at scale.
