## Problem

Expert pruning in Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures lacks theoretical grounding: prior work relies on ad-hoc token-count metrics without proving that the selected experts are truly essential for downstream task generalization. The fundamental open question is what characteristic of an MoE layer provably separates essential from redundant experts so that removing the latter preserves test accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240353/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pruning method consumes the router weight vectors from both the pre-trained and fine-tuned MoE models to compute per-expert l2 norm changes, then outputs a pruned model with a reduced number of experts per MoE layer. In practice, the underlying models take RGB images tokenized into patches (16×16 patches for V-MoE yielding 65 tokens on CIFAR-10, 577 tokens on ImageNet; 32×32 patches for E³-MoE yielding 65 tokens) and produce class labels. Pruning is applied independently to each MoE encoder layer, and in E³-MoE, separately within each ensemble group.

## Method

The method ranks experts by the change in their router's l2 norm during fine-tuning, Δ_s^(T) = ||w_s^(T)|| − ||w_s^(0)||, and prunes those with the smallest changes, applied separately to positively and negatively connected expert groups. The theoretical analysis (under expert-choice routing, ReLU activation, and a structured data model with task-specific and task-irrelevant orthonormal patterns) proves that important experts (those already selecting task-specific features at pre-training) develop router norm changes exceeding 3/2 log l, while unimportant experts remain at O(log²l/√d), creating a provable separation. Without post-pruning fine-tuning, up to γ fraction of the least-changed experts can be removed while guaranteeing zero generalization error; with post-pruning fine-tuning, the tolerable pruning ratio extends to 1 − O(1/k). Empirically, the method prunes 75% of experts in V-MoE on CIFAR-10 (60% of total parameters) while maintaining accuracy within 1% of the unpruned model, and reduces inference FLOPs and time by 40%.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with V-MoE, the proposed router-norm-change pruning method prunes 75% of experts (60% of total model parameters) while maintaining accuracy within 1% of the unpruned model, with post-pruning fine-tuning. Without post-pruning fine-tuning, 50% of experts (35% of the model) can be pruned on CIFAR-10, 50% on CIFAR-100 (41% of model), and 42% on ImageNet (35% of model). The method achieves a 40% reduction in inference FLOPs and 40% reduction in inference time on CIFAR-10, with linear scaling of efficiency gains as pruning ratio increases. For E³-MoE, 75% of experts (45% of model) are pruned on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 within the 1% accuracy tolerance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed router-norm-change method against four alternative pruning criteria: (1) importance score (token-count × confidence, from Chen et al. 2022a and Koishekenov et al. 2023), (2) absolute router l2 magnitude, (3) average neuron l2 magnitude, and (4) average change in neuron magnitude. The proposed method consistently achieves the highest maximum pruning ratio within the 1% accuracy tolerance across all three datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet) and both model architectures (V-MoE and E³-MoE), outperforming all four alternatives. Random pruning, used as a sanity-check baseline, degrades sharply after even a small pruning ratio, confirming that the router-norm-change signal is meaningful. The token-count-based importance score (the closest prior work) is the strongest competitor but still falls below the proposed method's achievable pruning ratio, particularly on ImageNet where the gap widens as the task complexity increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of MoE architecture and structured model pruning. While general neural network pruning (unstructured and structured) is well-studied, and two very recent works (Chen et al. 2022a; Koishekenov et al. 2023) proposed token-count-based expert pruning heuristics, no prior work provided a theoretical criterion for identifying which experts are essential. The authors depart from the token-count paradigm by showing that the change in router l2 norm during fine-tuning is the provably correct signal, grounding their analysis in the feature learning framework (Allen-Zhu & Li, Shi et al., Chowdhury et al. 2023) rather than NTK or Gaussian-input assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a provable generalization guarantee for expert pruning in MoE models, giving practitioners a theoretically justified, hardware-friendly criterion (router norm change) that outperforms intuitive alternatives like token counts or weight magnitudes. The practical impact is significant: 75% expert pruning with <1% accuracy loss and 40% FLOP reduction on V-MoE makes large MoE models more deployable in resource-constrained settings. Key limitations include the theoretical analysis being restricted to binary classification on a simplified single-layer MoE with expert-choice routing, and empirical validation limited to vision models (V-MoE, E³-MoE) on CIFAR/ImageNet. Open questions include whether the router-norm criterion transfers to language MoE models, whether it composes well with other compression techniques (quantization, low-rank factorization), and whether the guarantee extends to multi-layer or multi-task settings.
