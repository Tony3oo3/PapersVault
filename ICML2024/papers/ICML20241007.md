## Problem

Existing molecular interaction models encode different molecule types (proteins, small molecules, RNA/DNAs) independently with domain-specific architectures at different granularities (residues, atoms, nucleobases), leaving them unable to learn shared underlying interaction physics across domains. The paper tackles the open problem of designing a unified cross-domain molecular representation that simultaneously preserves block-level specificity (e.g., which residue each atom belongs to) and enables atom-level shareability across all molecular types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241007/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D molecular complex as a geometric graph of sets, where each node is a block (residue, nucleobase, or single atom) containing a variable-sized set of atoms with 3D coordinates and feature vectors; graph edges are constructed via k-nearest-neighbors (k=9) at the block level using minimum inter-block atom distances. The output is a scalar binding affinity score (pK) for regression tasks or a binary activator/inactivator label for ligand efficacy prediction. A key constraint is that node features are matrices with variable row counts (not fixed-size vectors), requiring the model to be permutation-invariant within each block while maintaining E(3)-equivariance on coordinates.

## Method

GET processes the geometric graph of sets through stacked layers, each containing three E(3)-equivariant modules: (1) a bilevel attention module that computes cross-attention between atom sets in neighboring blocks, producing both dense atom-level attentions (softmax over atom pairs within a block pair) and sparse block-level attentions (softmax over neighbor blocks), with RBF-embedded distances and relative coordinates modulating the attention; (2) an equivariant feed-forward module that injects intra-block geometry by computing each atom's relative position to its block centroid; and (3) an equivariant layer normalization that normalizes both invariant hidden features and equivariant coordinates (by centering on the global centroid and scaling by coordinate variance). Unlike pooling-based hierarchical models that lose fine-grained information, GET retains both atom-level and block-level representations simultaneously throughout message passing, enabling the model to capture interactions at multiple granularities and transfer learned physics across molecular domains.

## Results

On the PPA benchmark, GET with the unified geometric-graph-of-sets representation achieves Pearson 0.514 and Spearman 0.533, outperforming the best single-level baseline (Equiformer on block-level, Pearson 0.484) by ~3 points. On LBA, GET reaches RMSE 1.327, Pearson 0.620, and Spearman 0.611, surpassing the strongest atom-level baseline (LEFTNet, RMSE 1.343, Pearson 0.610) and all hierarchical variants. On the PDBbind benchmark against domain-specific two-branch models, GET achieves RMSE 1.364 and Pearson 0.596, beating the best prior method (Atom3D-3DCNN, RMSE 1.416) by a clear margin. In zero-shot DNA/RNA-ligand affinity prediction, GET attains Pearson 0.450, roughly doubling the best baseline (Hierarchical ET at 0.348).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GET with the unified geometric-graph-of-sets representation consistently outperforms all three categories of vanilla unified representations (block-level, atom-level, and hierarchical pooling) across both PPA and LBA tasks. On PPA, GET (Pearson 0.514) beats the best block-level model Equiformer (0.484) by 3.0 points and the best atom-level model MACE (0.463) by 5.1 points; on LBA, GET (RMSE 1.327, Pearson 0.620) surpasses the strongest atom-level baseline LEFTNet (RMSE 1.343, Pearson 0.610) and the best hierarchical model MACE (Pearson 0.612). Against domain-specific two-branch models on PDBbind, GET (RMSE 1.364, Pearson 0.596) improves over Atom3D-3DCNN (RMSE 1.416) and ProNet-All-Atom (Pearson 0.551) by 0.052 RMSE and 4.5 Pearson points respectively. In the cross-domain mixed-training setting, GET-mix gains on both PPA (0.519 vs. 0.514) and LBA (0.622 vs. 0.620), whereas baselines like MACE-mix suffer severe degradation (PPA Pearson drops from 0.470 to 0.372). In zero-shot DNA/RNA-ligand prediction, GET (Pearson 0.450) more than doubles the best baseline (Hierarchical ET at 0.348) and substantially outperforms all atom-level and block-level baselines (≤0.279). Notably, several heavier models (GemNet, Equiformer, DimeNet++) fail with OOM on atom-level and hierarchical representations, highlighting a practical scalability advantage of the unified bilevel design.

## Relation to Prior Work

GET carves new ground by proposing a 'geometric graph of sets' representation that unifies molecules of different domains (proteins, small molecules, RNA/DNAs) under a single bilevel structure, departing from both domain-specific independent encoders and pooling-based hierarchical models (Jin et al., 2022) that discard fine-grained atomic information. The architecture draws on the transformer/attention paradigm for geometric learning and multi-channel equivariant GNNs (Huang et al., 2022; Kong et al., 2022b), but generalizes them to handle variable-size unordered sets of 3D instances per node—a setting where prior equivariant models (which assume fixed-size vector features) cannot operate. The equivariant layer normalization and the bilevel attention mechanism (combining sparse block-level and dense atom-level interactions) are novel design choices not present in prior equivariant architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified representation enables a single model to handle all molecular domains, which is practically important for data-scarce settings like RNA/DNA-ligand binding where only 149 labeled complexes exist. The zero-shot generalization result (Pearson 0.450 vs. 0.004–0.348 for baselines) and the finding that mixed-domain training benefits GET while degrading domain-specific baselines suggest the model captures transferable interaction physics. Open questions include extending the framework to generative tasks (molecular design, conformation generation), universal pretraining across domains, and whether the approach scales to larger complexes (e.g., multi-protein assemblies) where the k-NN graph construction and attention cost may become prohibitive.
