## Problem

Computing homotopy groups of spheres via Wu's formula requires generating elements from the intersection of normal closures of a free group, a task for which existing algorithmic approaches (random search, evolutionary methods) suffer from superexponential complexity and fail to scale beyond low dimensions. No prior work has applied language models to sample from this intersection, despite the structural similarity between the normal closures and Dyck-n languages that makes the problem amenable to sequence generation.

## Input / Output

The model takes as input a short word prefix (a sequence of group generators x_k and their inverses x_k^{-1}, typically 5–10 tokens) and, depending on the variant, a multi-label prompt consisting of normal-closure tokens R_0…R_n followed by a delimiter. It autoregressively generates a complete reduced word over the same alphabet that belongs to the full intersection ∩_{i=0}^n R_i. Training words are produced online via bracket-style sampling from Dyck-n languages, with maximum lengths of 200–600 tokens depending on the number of generators n (3–5).

## Method

The core architecture is a decoder-only GPT-2 transformer trained with cross-entropy loss for autoregressive token generation, but the key contribution is a multi-label mechanism that encodes, for each training word, a binary array indicating membership in each normal closure R_i. Three variants exploit this label differently: (i) Ignore discards it entirely (zero-shot), (ii) Mask assigns each attention head to a specific normal closure and zeroes out heads whose closure does not contain the current word during training, effectively turning a single model into a soft ensemble that at inference generates from the full intersection, and (iii) Prompt prepends the corresponding R_i tokens to the input sequence so the model conditions on the desired intersection. Training data is drawn from partial symmetric commutator subgroups [R_0,…,R̂_i,…,R_n]_S (all but one R_i excluded), ensuring the model never sees words from the full intersection during training and must generalize to it at inference time.

## Results

The main metric is the completion ratio (average number of successfully generated words from the full intersection per batch). For n=4 generators, the Prompt method achieves a completion ratio of 0.684, followed by Mask (0.584) and Ignore (0.356), while all non-neural baselines drop below 0.032. For n=5, Prompt (0.550) and Mask (0.487) remain competitive, whereas Greedy collapses to 0.004 and the Evolutionary method yields 0. The deep learning methods demonstrate clear scalability: their completion ratios degrade gracefully with increasing n, while baselines become impractical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three non-neural baselines (Random Search, Evolutionary Method, Greedy Algorithm) against three deep learning variants (Ignore, Mask, Prompt) that differ in how they exploit multi-label information. For n=3, the Greedy algorithm surprisingly leads with 0.479, outperforming all deep learning methods (Ignore 0.360, Prompt 0.300, Mask 0.160). However, this advantage reverses sharply at n=4: Prompt (0.684) and Mask (0.584) dominate, while Greedy collapses to 0.016 and Evolutionary yields zero. At n=5, the gap widens further — Prompt (0.550) and Mask (0.487) remain robust while all baselines are effectively non-functional (≤0.025). The key takeaway is that the deep learning approaches, particularly the Prompt and Mask methods, scale gracefully with the number of generators, whereas the combinatorial baselines suffer from rapid degradation in computational complexity, making them impractical for higher dimensions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by applying language models to algebraic topology in the 'inverse' direction—using ML to compute topological invariants rather than using topological ideas to improve ML. It builds on Wu's 2001 formula expressing homotopy groups of S² as a quotient of intersections of normal closures in a free group, and on the structural similarity between these normal closures and Dyck-n languages. The multi-label strategies (masking attention heads, prompting with normal-closure tokens, ignoring labels) are adapted from standard NLP techniques (ensemble methods, conditional generation, zero-shot learning), while the non-neural baselines (evolutionary, greedy, random search) draw from combinatorial optimization. No prior work had formulated the generation of simplicial cycles as a sequence-generation task with multi-label conditioning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is a proof-of-concept demonstrating that decoder-only transformers can generate valid simplicial cycles at rates far exceeding classical algorithms, with the prompt-based approach achieving the highest completion ratios. The critical open question—distinguishing trivial elements (the symmetric commutator subgroup denominator) from non-trivial homotopy group generators—remains unsolved and is acknowledged to be 'almost as hard as computing homotopy groups themselves.' Scalability is limited to n≤5 generators due to rapidly growing word lengths, and the authors identify more sophisticated tokenization (commutator-bracket tokens, Λ-algebra representations) as necessary for higher dimensions. The broader vision of sampling from homotopy groups of general spaces via ML remains a long-term goal.
