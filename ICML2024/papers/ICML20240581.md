## Problem

All existing constrained and safe bandit methods assume the underlying linear program is feasible, yet no principled method exists for testing this assumption from bandit feedback. Prior work on minimum threshold testing is limited to finite-armed, single-constraint settings in the asymptotic δ→0 regime, missing the dependence on action-space dimension d that dominates costs in practically relevant moderate-δ scenarios with rich action spaces.

## Input / Output

The tester receives an action space X ⊂ {‖x‖≤1} ⊂ R^d, a number of constraints m, and an error level δ, but does not know the latent matrix A ∈ R^{m×d}. At each round the tester selects an action x_t and observes a vector score S_t = Ax_t + ζ_t with conditionally 1-subGaussian noise. The output is a binary decision (feasible H_F or infeasible H_I) returned at a data-dependent stopping time τ, with the guarantee that the probability of error is at most δ. The stopping time adapts to the signal level Γ = max_x min_i (Ax)^i, which measures how far the best action is from the constraint boundary.

## Method

The core test (EOGT) interprets feasibility as determining the sign of the minimax value Γ = max_x min_i (Ax)^i and constructs a game-theoretic sequential test: a feasibility-biased player selects x_t by solving an optimistic maximin over the RLS confidence ellipsoid C_t(δ_t/2), while an infeasibility-biased player greedily picks the most violated constraint i_t. The test statistic T_t = Σ S_s^{i_s} accumulates scores along the selected constraints, and the rejection boundary B_t(δ) = Σ ρ_s(x_s; δ_s/2) + LIL(t, δ/2) combines the cumulative noise scale from online linear regression with a nonasymptotic law of iterated logarithm to control noise fluctuations uniformly over time. The test stops when |T_t| exceeds B_t(δ), and the sign of T_τ determines the decision. A tempered variant (T-EOGT) replaces the ellipsoidal optimism with a light-tailed exploration radius Rad_t(x) = (t/d)^{1/2}‖x‖²_{V_t^{-1}} + √(d‖x‖²_{V_t^{-1}}) to achieve exponential tail decay in the stopping time at the cost of a weaker d³/Γ² scaling. The paper proves EOGT is reliable and well-adapted with E[τ] = Õ(d²/Γ² + d·log(m/δ)/Γ²), and establishes a minimax lower bound of Ω(d/Γ²) using the simulator technique.

## Results

The EOGT test achieves a stopping time of $\widetilde{O}(d^2/\Gamma^2 + d\log(m/\delta)/\Gamma^2)$, while the T-EOGT variant achieves $\widetilde{O}(d^3/\Gamma^2 + d\log(8m/\delta)/\Gamma^2)$ with exponential tail decay. A minimax lower bound of $\Omega(d/\Gamma^2)$ is established, showing the linear dependence on dimension $d$ is necessary. In simulations (m=2, d up to 10, Γ ∈ [0.2, 1]), the test never returned an incorrect hypothesis, and the early-stopping variant τ_early was approximately 10× faster than the theoretical stopping time τ in feasible cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with no direct experimental comparison against competing methods. The key comparison is against prior asymptotic lower bounds for the minimum threshold problem (Kaufmann et al., 2018; Degenne & Koolen, 2019), which take the form $2\log(1/\delta)/\Gamma^2$ in the feasible case and do not capture any dependence on the action-space dimension $d$ (or $K$ in the finite-armed case). The paper's minimax lower bound of $\Omega(d/\Gamma^2)$ demonstrates that this missing dependence is necessary, particularly in the practically relevant regime where $\log(1/\delta) = o(d)$. EOGT achieves a $d^2/\Gamma^2$ upper bound, leaving a linear-in-$d$ gap to the lower bound — a persistent phenomenon in linear bandit theory. T-EOGT trades a weaker $d^3/\Gamma^2$ mean bound for exponentially decaying tails, making it preferable when rare extreme delays carry strong penalties. The simulation confirms that in practice, the adaptive boundary of EOGT (combined with early stopping) yields stopping times roughly an order of magnitude below the worst-case theoretical bound.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the minimum threshold testing problem (Kaufmann et al., 2018) from the finite-armed, single-constraint setting to the linear bandit setting with multiple constraints, and from the asymptotic δ→0 regime to the practically relevant moderate-δ regime. The test design repurposes standard online linear regression confidence sets (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011) and a nonasymptotic law of iterated logarithms (Howard et al., 2021) in a new way to construct adaptive rejection boundaries, while the T-EOGT variant borrows light-tailed exploration from Simchi-Levi et al. (2023). The minimax lower bound uses the simulator technique of Simchowitz et al. (2017), which the authors note was previously applied to this problem but only recovered dimension-free bounds. The key departure from prior work is conceptual: existing asymptotic bounds appear dimension-free, but this paper shows that this is an artefact of the δ→0 limit implicitly requiring δ = e^{-ω(d)}, and that a linear d-dependence is unavoidable in the moderate-δ regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work identifies a fundamental gap in the existing theory of feasibility testing for constrained bandits: the dimension d of the action space was previously invisible in the analysis, yet is provably necessary for reliable testing. The practical implication is significant for safe bandit applications (clinical trials, crowdsourcing) where the action space is high-dimensional and only a small fraction of actions may be feasible. The gap between the O(d²/Γ²) upper bound and Ω(d/Γ²) lower bound remains open, and the paper acknowledges that instance-specific analysis in the linear setting (analogous to the strong adaptation in finite-armed theory) is a critical direction for future work. Additionally, the computational challenge of solving the maximin program (2) in practice, and the question of whether the early-stopping procedure observed in simulations can be theoretically justified, remain open.
