## Problem

The EU Digital Services Act imposes graduated obligations on very large online platforms to curb disinformation, microtargeted manipulation, and filter-bubble effects, yet no empirical framework exists to quantify whether these provisions actually reduce online harms in practice. The paper identifies specific regulatory gaps—around generative synthetic media oversight, microtargeting transparency, content demotion/shadow banning, and outrage-maximizing engagement incentives—and argues that machine learning techniques capable of large-scale socio-technical analysis remain critically underutilized for public oversight of platform governance.

## Input / Output

The proposed analytical pipeline consumes heterogeneous platform data: DSA Transparency Database entries (near-real-time moderation decisions with optional metadata fields), advertising repository dumps and scraped ad impressions across demographic profiles, social network interaction graphs (users as nodes, follows/shares/mentions as edges), and free-text transparency reports and statements of reasons. Outputs are structured as policy impact assessments (before/after comparisons of reporting behavior and platform responsiveness), disinformation diffusion metrics (cascade speed, reach, and intervention effectiveness under different policy scenarios), compliance audit findings (targeting violations, ranking biases, filter-bubble indicators), and actionable risk-mitigation recommendations for platform design and content moderation.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a three-pillar research agenda mapping ML technique families to specific DSA assessment questions. Pillar 1 applies transformer-based text classification and sentiment analysis to platform transparency reports and DSA Transparency Database entries to evaluate notice-and-takedown efficacy and perceived fairness. Pillar 2 constructs network diffusion models of information spread, using interrupted time series analysis to compare verified-disinformation cascades before and after DSA-mandated risk mitigations (e.g., recommender changes, sharing friction), incorporating cognitive factors like confirmation bias. Pillar 3 employs causal auditing via browser automation, computer vision for ad classification, and counterfactual analysis to independently verify advertising targeting compliance and reverse-engineer ranking algorithm biases. A cross-cutting methodological challenge the paper foregrounds is isolating marginal DSA effects from overlapping regulations (AI Act, DMA, GDPR) and accounting for regional enforcement variation across EU Member States, proposing multi-pronged designs combining quantitative signals with ethnographic and interpretive synthesis.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results, benchmarks, or performance metrics. Instead, it presents a qualitative analysis of the EU Digital Services Act's provisions, identifies specific regulatory gaps (e.g., around generative synthetic media detection, microtargeted influence campaign oversight, filter bubble regulation, and shadow banning), and proposes a research agenda leveraging NLP, network modeling, and causal auditing to quantify DSA policy impacts. The paper's contribution is normative and methodological rather than empirical.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare its proposed methods against any baselines or competing approaches, as it does not implement or evaluate a specific algorithm. The paper's 'compares_to' set is empty. Its analytical contribution lies in mapping DSA regulatory provisions (Articles 14–17, 34–37, 39–40, 57) to specific ML-based assessment techniques (transformer-based text classification for notice-and-takedown analysis, interrupted time series for diffusion modeling, counterfactual analysis for advertising audits) and identifying methodological barriers (data access, isolating marginal regulatory effects, regional variation in enforcement) that must be addressed before such assessments can be conducted at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the authors' earlier contextual analysis of the DSA (Nannini et al., 2024) by shifting from legal-structural critique to a concrete research agenda for ML-based impact assessment. It draws on two largely separate literatures—legal scholarship identifying DSA limitations (Sullivan, 2023; Heldt, 2022; Leerssen, 2023; Hacker et al., 2023) and ML methods for content analysis, network diffusion modeling, and causal auditing—and argues their intersection is underexploited. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the paper repurposes existing NLP, network, and causal-inference techniques as tools for regulatory oversight, carving new ground at the policy-ML boundary where neither community has yet systematically engaged the other.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DSA is the first comprehensive EU platform regulation with full applicability only from February 2024, meaning no empirical evidence base for its real-world effects yet exists; without ML-powered assessment, policymakers lack the tools to iterate on provisions that are already identified as under-specified (e.g., shadow banning, generative-AI detection, microtargeting transparency). Key open barriers the paper highlights—proprietary data access, disentangling DSA effects from concurrent regulations (AI Act, DMA, GDPR), and regional enforcement variation across 27 Member States—remain unresolved and will constrain any future evaluation. The paper's broader implication is that the same ML capabilities generating online harms (generative models, recommender systems, targeting algorithms) must be turned inward as audit instruments, creating a self-referential governance loop that demands sustained multi-stakeholder coordination.
