## Problem

Conformal prediction (CP) provides distribution-free uncertainty quantification with theoretical coverage guarantees, yet its vulnerability to data poisoning attacks has never been studied. Unlike traditional poisoning attacks that target misclassification, CP's unique risk lies in the manipulation of prediction uncertainty—specifically, an adversary can shrink or expand conformal prediction sets to make a model overconfident or underconfident on targeted samples while preserving label correctness and marginal coverage, making such attacks far more stealthy and harder to detect with existing defenses.

## Input / Output

In the black-box setting, the adversary requires only a pre-trained auxiliary model (trained on a comparable public dataset), a set of target/victim samples, a poison budget (0.5%–2% of training data), and a perturbation bound (16/255); it does not need access to the victim model's parameters, architecture, or full training data. The output is a set of poisoned training samples (perturbed features and optionally modified labels) that, when injected into the victim's training set, cause the retrained victim model to produce manipulated conformal prediction sets—either reduced (overconfidence) or expanded (underconfidence)—on the target samples while maintaining prediction consistency and empirical coverage rates close to the benign model.

## Method

The paper formulates a bi-level optimization: the outer loop maximizes an adversarial loss that enlarges or shrinks conformal set sizes on target samples, while the inner loop represents the victim model's standard training loss on the union of clean and poisoned data. To handle the discrete, non-differentiable nature of conformal sets and the quantile threshold, the authors derive a continuous surrogate loss (Eq. 7) that approximates the set-size manipulation via pairwise nonconformity score comparisons. A key innovation is a worst-case adversarial loss built on sharpness-aware minimization (SAM), which perturbs the inner-minima model within a norm ball to ensure the poisoning effect is robust across different training initializations and hyperparameter choices. For efficient optimization without full retraining, the authors derive closed-form first-order and second-order parameter updates via influence functions: the first-order update aligns the adversarial gradient with the influence of swapping clean for poisoned samples (Eq. 11–13), while the second-order update uses the inverse Hessian for higher fidelity (Eq. 17). Theoretical guarantees include finite-sample error bounds quantifying the gap between the closed-form approximation and the true retrained optimum (Theorem 4.1) and a convergence proof showing the victim model trained via SGD reaches a stationary point of the adversarial loss (Theorem 4.2).

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 2% poison budget, the proposed overconfidence CP attack achieves set size reduction ratios of 0.46±0.02 (HPS), 0.47±0.04 (APS), 0.54±0.04 (RAPS), and 0.49±0.05 (RSCP) without any defense, substantially outperforming RandUn and RandGa baselines (all below 0.32). Under existing poisoning defenses, the attack retains effectiveness, achieving reduction ratios of 0.24–0.49 depending on the defense and CP method. The first-order closed-form optimization achieves a 0.46±0.02 reduction ratio in 19.26 minutes, compared to MetaPoison's 0.38±0.07 in 494.72 minutes, demonstrating both superior attack strength and ~25× speedup.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MetaPoison (Huang et al., 2020), the proposed first-order method achieves a higher set size reduction ratio (0.46±0.02 vs. 0.38±0.07 at 2% budget) while running ~25× faster (19.26 min vs. 494.72 min); the second-order variant further improves to 0.48±0.02 at 369.23 min. Against the MaxUp defense (Gong et al., 2021), the attack retains a 0.34±0.05 reduction ratio under HPS (vs. 0.46 without defense), indicating only moderate degradation. Against Adversarial Poisoning (Geiping et al., 2021a), the attack achieves 0.39±0.04 (HPS) and 0.41±0.06 (RAPS), showing the defense is least effective against RAPS. Against EPIC (Yang et al., 2022), the attack maintains 0.38±0.06 (HPS) and 0.42±0.07 (RAPS), with RSCP being the most vulnerable (0.38±0.10). Overall, the proposed attack consistently outperforms random-noise baselines (RandUn, RandGa) by 0.14–0.22 in reduction ratio and remains effective under all three tested defenses, confirming that existing defenses designed for label-flipping attacks do not adequately protect against uncertainty-targeted poisoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new attack surface by combining three previously separate threads: conformal prediction theory (Vovk et al., 2005), influence-function-based model updates from robust statistics (Hampel, 1974), and sharpness-aware minimization (Foret et al., 2020) for worst-case loss formulation. It departs from traditional data poisoning literature, which targets misclassification, and from existing adversarial attacks on CP (Gendler et al., 2021; Ghosh et al., 2023), which address test-time robustness or validity under exchangeability violations rather than training-time uncertainty manipulation. The bi-level optimization with closed-form influence-function updates positions the work at the intersection of MetaPoison-style poisoning (Huang et al., 2020) and efficient model-update techniques, but repurposes them for a fundamentally different objective: manipulating conformal set sizes while preserving label correctness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of data poisoning against conformal prediction, revealing that uncertainty estimates can be manipulated more effectively than labels and that standard poisoning defenses (MaxUp, Adversarial Poisoning, EPIC) do not transfer to this threat model. The practical stakes are significant for safety-critical deployments (medical diagnosis, autonomous systems) where CP is used as a trust layer: an adversary can make the model overconfident on targeted inputs without triggering label-based anomaly detection. Key limitations include evaluation only on CIFAR-10/100 and Tiny-ImageNet with relatively weak baselines (random noise, MetaPoison), and the open question of whether robust CP algorithms can be designed to resist such attacks remains explicitly deferred to future work.
