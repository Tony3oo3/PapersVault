## Problem

Large language models trained solely on next-token prediction exhibit remarkable logical and mathematical reasoning, yet it remains unclear whether this power stems from the autoregressive training paradigm itself or from the specific architecture (e.g., transformers). No prior theoretical framework established that extremely simple models—such as linear predictors—could, in principle, learn to compute arbitrary Turing-computable functions through next-token prediction with chain-of-thought supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241400/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a context of n tokens (x) concatenated with previously generated tokens (z_{<t}) and outputs a single next token from a finite dictionary D. In the CoT setting, the full input is a problem statement (e.g., '1394×8618='), and the output is an unfolded sequence of intermediate arithmetic steps followed by the final answer. For the multiplication experiment, a custom tokenizer treats individual digits, operators (×, +, =), and digit-pair products (e.g., '3×5') as separate tokens, and all sequences are zero-padded to a fixed context length of 307.

## Method

The paper establishes that AR learning fundamentally differs from classical supervised learning because every token in a CoT sequence serves simultaneously as an input (for predicting future tokens) and as a label (for predicting itself from prior context), thereby providing supervision on intermediate computation steps. The key theoretical result shows that a linear AR predictor—where the next-token score is a linear function of the embedded context—can implement any linear threshold circuit gate-by-gate via argmax over a linear function, and since any Turing-computable function has a polynomial-size linear threshold circuit, linear AR models can approximate any efficiently computable function. The paper further introduces 'length complexity' (the number of intermediate CoT tokens required) and demonstrates tradeoffs: parities over n bits need only O(log n) intermediate tokens with linear models, or O(n/k) with k-order parity hypothesis classes, at the cost of exponential growth in sample/computational complexity. Empirically, a 162M-parameter linear model (embedding → masked linear layer → output embedding) generates coherent stories on TinyStories, and a 775M-parameter 4-layer MLP with ReLU activations but no attention matches the 7B-parameter Goat transformer on 4-digit multiplication (96.9% exact accuracy).

## Results

On the 4-digit multiplication task, the 775M-parameter MLP (no attention layers) achieves 96.9% exact-match accuracy and 99.5% per-digit accuracy, matching the 7B-parameter Goat transformer (96.9%/99.2%) while outperforming GPT-4 (5.3%/61.8%) and GPT-3.5 (1.2%/61.9%). On TinyStories, the linear model (162M params) scores 6.3/10 on grammar (GPT-4 graded) with 64% grammatical correctness (LanguageTool), compared to 8.0/10 and 62% for the 33M-parameter transformer baseline. Theoretically, the paper proves that linear AR predictors can compute any Turing-computable function, and that parities can be learned with O(log n) intermediate tokens (improving the O(n) bound from Wies et al., 2022).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The MLP-775M ties Goat-7B (a 7B-parameter fine-tuned LLaMA transformer) exactly on 4-digit multiplication (96.9% exact match) and slightly edges it on per-digit accuracy (99.5% vs. 99.2%), despite having roughly 9x fewer parameters and no attention mechanism. Both massively outperform GPT-4 (5.3% exact) and GPT-3.5 (1.2% exact), which struggle with this task even at their much larger scale. On TinyStories, the linear model (162M params) trails the 33M-parameter transformer (TS-33M) on GPT-4 grammar scores (6.3 vs. 8.0) and creativity (6.2 vs. 7.2), but achieves comparable grammatical correctness via LanguageTool (64% vs. 62%). The key takeaway is that the autoregressive next-token training paradigm with CoT supervision, rather than architectural sophistication, is the primary driver of complex reasoning capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on universal approximation for sequence models (Yun et al., 2019; Wei et al., 2022a; Giannou et al., 2023) by showing that the universality property is not specific to transformers but is an inherent consequence of the autoregressive next-token training paradigm itself. It builds most directly on Wies et al. (2022), who showed that subtask decomposition and CoT enable learning of Turing-computable functions, but improves their parity length complexity from O(n) to O(log n) and generalizes the framework to arbitrary PAC-learnable hypothesis classes. The paper also departs from the classical PAC learning tradition (Valiant, 1984; Minsky & Papert, 2017) by demonstrating that the intermediate supervision inherent in CoT fundamentally changes what is learnable, even for linear models that are provably incapable of computing XOR in the standard supervised setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central message—that architectural expressiveness (non-linearity, attention) is less important than the autoregressive training paradigm for complex reasoning—reframes how the field should think about model design and data curation. The introduction of length complexity as a new axis alongside sample and computational complexity opens a productive direction for theoretical analysis of AR models. However, a significant gap remains between the theoretical guarantees (which require access to full CoT sequences during training) and practical deployment, where such intermediate supervision is expensive or unavailable. Open questions include whether an intrinsic complexity measure (analogous to VC dimension) can predict length complexity, and whether these results transfer to models trained on large corpora without explicit CoT annotations.
