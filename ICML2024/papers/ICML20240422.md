## Problem

Existing confidence scoring methods for LLM hallucination detection provide at best marginal calibration guarantees, meaning they are calibrated on average but can be systematically over- or under-confident on specific prompt types (e.g., academic citations vs. trivia). A key obstacle to applying stronger conditional calibration (multicalibration) to LLMs is the absence of explicit, pre-defined features to condition on, unlike tabular settings where demographic attributes are naturally available.

## Input / Output

The method takes a prompt/completion pair (text) and an initial uncalibrated score from one of three heuristics (True/False softmax, inverse perplexity, or multiple-choice softmax) and produces a post-processed calibrated confidence score in [0,1] representing the probability the completion is not a hallucination. At test time, the prompt must be assigned to groups via either embedding-space cluster membership or LLM self-annotation (answering predefined yes/no questions about the prompt), which adds a small inference-time cost. The calibration procedure requires a modestly sized labeled calibration set with binary correctness labels.

## Method

The core algorithm (IGLB, Iterative Grouped Linear Binning) iteratively patches an initial scoring function on intersecting conditioning sets defined by score bins and group indicators, guaranteeing multicalibration while monotonically decreasing MSE. Two grouping strategies are introduced: (1) clustering prompt embeddings (UAE-Large-V1 → UMAP → GMM) jointly with the score, and (2) LLM self-annotation where the model answers yes/no questions about the prompt to define overlapping, human-interpretable groups. Three overfitting remedies distinguish IGLB from prior iterative multicalibration: replacing exact level-set bins with upper/lower cumulative sets S_{p,g}^τ (which are larger and thus less prone to overfitting), substituting constant-shift patches with logit-space linear scaling (Platt-style) patches, and early stopping when validation MSE plateaus or the selected conditioning set falls below a size threshold.

## Results

Using True/False softmax scores with clustering-based grouping, IGLB achieves the lowest MSE across all six datasets, reducing MSE by 25–58% relative to uncalibrated scores (e.g., 0.2016 vs. 0.4762 on TruthfulQA, 0.1727 vs. 0.3767 on MathQA). On binary classification accuracy (threshold 0.5), IGLB and GCULR are the top performers, with IGLB reaching 77.8% on MathQA and 70.2% on TriviaQA, while GCULR edges ahead on MMLU (63.8% vs. 63.4%) and OpenBookQA (59.8% vs. 59.3%). All calibration post-processing methods substantially outperform the raw uncalibrated scores, confirming that calibration is accuracy-enhancing. IGLB consistently outperforms the original IGHB algorithm, demonstrating the effectiveness of the overfitting remedies (upper/lower bins, linear scaling, early stopping).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares IGLB against four baselines: HB (histogram binning, marginal calibration), LS (linear scaling, marginal calibration), GCULR (group-conditional unbiased logistic regression), and IGHB (original iterative multicalibration without overfitting remedies). IGLB achieves the best MSE on all six datasets, outperforming GCULR by small margins (e.g., 0.2254 vs. 0.2239 on MMLU) and substantially outperforming IGHB (e.g., 0.2016 vs. 0.3051 on TruthfulQA, a 34% relative reduction). On accuracy, GCULR narrowly edges IGLB on MMLU (63.8% vs. 63.4%) and TriviaQA (70.4% vs. 70.2%), while IGLB leads on BigBench (56.9% vs. 55.5%) and TruthfulQA (68.7% vs. 67.8%). Both marginal calibration methods (HB, LS) consistently underperform the multicalibration methods, confirming that group-conditional guarantees yield better-calibrated and more accurate scores. The ablation in Table 5 shows that the upper/lower binning modification (IGHB^τ) is the primary driver of IGLB's improvement over IGHB, while linear scaling alone (IGHB^LS) actually worsens performance without the binning change.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts multicalibration (Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018; Gopalan et al., 2022c), previously developed for tabular data and algorithmic fairness, to the LLM hallucination detection setting where explicit demographic features are absent. The key methodological departure is generating groups via prompt embedding clustering and LLM self-annotation rather than relying on pre-existing categorical attributes. The proposed IGLB algorithm extends the original IGHB with three overfitting remedies (upper/lower set bins, linear scaling patches, and early stopping), while the scoring functions (True/False softmax, inverse perplexity, multiple-choice softmax) are drawn from prior hallucination detection work (Kadavath et al., 2022; Jelinek et al., 1977). The paper also positions itself alongside conformal prediction with conditional guarantees (Bastani et al., 2022; Gibbs et al., 2023) but targets binary classification rather than set prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The multicalibration guarantee provides conditionally calibrated confidence scores that are both interpretable and trustworthy for end users, enabling practical risk communication (e.g., color-coding) for LLM outputs in high-stakes domains like healthcare and finance. A key limitation is that calibration guarantees hold only for prompts distributed similarly to the calibration set and do not extend to adversarially generated inputs. The framework is explicitly extensible: better grouping strategies (semantic, user-specific, task-specific) could be plugged in without modifying the core algorithm, making this a useful template for future work on fine-grained uncertainty quantification in LLMs. Open questions include how well these methods transfer across prompt distributions and whether the self-annotation grouping strategy scales to open-ended generation tasks beyond multiple-choice QA.
