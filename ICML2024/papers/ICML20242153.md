## Problem

Multiple-choice question answering (MCQA) dominates LLM evaluation due to its simple accuracy metric, but the paper identifies fundamental inconsistencies: models' MCQA predictions often diverge from their open-ended generations, performance is sensitive to choice ordering, and the evaluation format mismatches how users actually interact with LLMs. Existing leaderboards like AlpacaEval (single benchmark-model comparison) and MT-Bench (0–10 scoring) also fail to provide stable, directly comparable rankings across models.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a single user question sampled from the RWQ benchmark (20,772 authentic English queries sourced from Google Trends, Quora, ShareGPT, LMSYS-Chat-1M, and AlpacaEval) and the open-ended text responses generated by two competing LLMs. GPT-4 receives the question plus both responses and outputs a structured JSON verdict (winner = 1, 2, 0 for satisfactory tie, or -1 for unsatisfactory tie) with a brief rationale. The final output is a numerical Elo rating per LLM (initialized at 1000, updated with K-factor 4), computed as the median across 100 randomized competition-order runs to ensure stability.

## Method

The RWQ-Elo system orchestrates pairwise contests among N LLMs (24 in the paper) where each model competes against every other model H=200 times on questions drawn from the RWQ benchmark. GPT-4 serves as judge using a multi-criteria prompt covering accuracy, relevance, comprehensiveness, clarity, compliance, timeliness, harmlessness, and unbiasedness, achieving 95% alignment with human evaluators on a 300-sample audit. Ratings are updated via the standard Elo formula (E_A = 1/(1+10^((R_B−R_A)/400)), R' = R + K(S−E)) with K=4, and the final rating is the median over C=100 independently shuffled competition schedules to mitigate order sensitivity. A fast-registration protocol allows new models to be inserted without recomputing all pairwise contests, reducing rounds from N(N−1)H/2 to (2N₁+N₂−1)N₂H/2. The paper also introduces an open-ended MCQA variant where GPT-4 maps a model's free-form answer back to the closest multiple-choice option, revealing a 14-point accuracy gap versus standard MCQA on filtered MMLU.

## Results

Across 24 LLMs and 11 MCQA benchmarks, the paper reveals severe inconsistency in MCQA evaluation: the 'Choices (Circular)' strategy (requiring correct answers across all choice-order permutations) causes dramatic accuracy drops, e.g., MPT-7B falls from 36.0% to 2.2%, and LLaMA-2-70B from 75.2% to 58.4%. Open-ended MCQA on filtered MMLU yields only 39.7% for LLaMA-2-Chat-13B versus 54.0% under standard MCQA, confirming a 14.3-point gap between selecting a choice and actually answering the question. GPT-4 as judge achieves 95% alignment with human evaluators on RWQ questions, and the fast-registration protocol for new models achieves Kendall's tau of 0.97–0.99 against full re-computation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RWQ-Elo against three existing LLM leaderboards: AlpacaEval (v1.0/v2.0), MT-Bench, and Chatbot Arena. Against AlpacaEval, RWQ-Elo addresses a critical limitation: when the fixed competitor model is significantly stronger (e.g., GPT-4-Turbo) or weaker (e.g., LLaMA-1-13B), AlpacaEval win-rates compress and fail to distinguish among the evaluated LLMs, whereas RWQ-Elo's all-pairs competition maintains discriminative power across the full range. Against MT-Bench, which assigns a single 0–10 score per LLM based on a fixed set of questions, RWQ-Elo provides a relative Elo rating derived from direct pairwise contests and incorporates additional judging criteria (comprehensiveness, unbiasedness, timeliness, harmlessness) beyond MT-Bench's rubric. Against Chatbot Arena, which relies on diverse online human evaluators for pairwise comparisons, RWQ-Elo uses GPT-4 as judge with a demonstrated 95% agreement rate with human preferences, offering a scalable and reproducible alternative that avoids the subjectivity and scaling challenges of crowdsourced human evaluation. The paper does not report a single 'win/lose' metric against these baselines but rather positions RWQ-Elo as a more stable, scalable, and realistic evaluation framework, supported by stability analyses (100 repeated runs showing consistent rankings) and fast-registration results (Kendall's tau ≥ 0.97).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant MCQA evaluation paradigm (MMLU, HellaSwag, ARC, etc.) by arguing that accuracy on multiple-choice tasks poorly reflects open-ended generation quality, and it moves beyond AlpacaEval's single-benchmark-model win-rate comparison by introducing a multi-player Elo rating system. Conceptually, it repurposes the Elo algorithm from competitive sports for LLM evaluation and extends the LLM-as-judge paradigm (as in MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena) by using GPT-4 to adjudicate pairwise contests over a new real-world question benchmark (RWQ). The paper does not build on a single prior method but rather synthesizes the Elo rating framework, the LLM-as-judge approach, and real-world question collection into a unified evaluation system.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that MCQA accuracy can be highly misleading—models scoring 60%+ on standard benchmarks drop to near-random under circular choice reordering, and open-ended MCQA scores lag significantly behind traditional MCQA—challenges a foundational assumption in how the community benchmarks LLMs. The RWQ-Elo system offers a more realistic, scalable, and stable alternative that mirrors actual user interactions, and its fast-registration mechanism makes it practical for ongoing leaderboard maintenance. Key limitations include English-only coverage, a 95% (not perfect) GPT-4-to-human alignment rate, and the inherent circularity of using a single model family (OpenAI) as judge. Open questions include whether the Elo rankings remain stable as the model landscape shifts rapidly, whether the system generalizes to multilingual or multimodal settings, and whether GPT-4's judging biases systematically favor certain response styles.
