## Problem

Existing defenses against model extraction suffer from three key limitations: active defenses like P-poison and GRAD require expensive test-time backpropagation or memory-heavy ensembles, defensive training methods like MeCo demand full re-training of the victim model, and watermarking-based approaches only passively detect theft after the fact without actively preventing extraction. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a defense that is simultaneously active (prevents extraction), efficient (no test-time optimization or ensembles), and lightweight (only minimal fine-tuning, not full re-training).

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard RGB query image (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 84×84 for MiniImageNet) and returns a class prediction in either soft-label (softmax probabilities) or hard-label (top-1 class) format. Unusually, during deployment a per-query watermark of the same spatial dimensions as the input is sampled from a learned input-conditioned posterior distribution z_Φ(w|x,γ) and added to the query before the victim model processes it, so the user receives predictions on the watermarked input rather than the raw query. The watermark posterior inference network is a small two-block residual CNN accounting for less than 1% of the backbone parameters.

## Method

The ACT framework fine-tunes a pre-trained victim model by jointly optimizing two objectives: maximizing log-likelihood on watermarked in-distribution data (preserving utility) and maximizing KL-divergence between the model's outputs on watermarked versus non-watermarked synthetic OOD data (disrupting extraction). The watermark is treated as a latent variable with an input-conditioned posterior q(w|x), approximated by a small amortized inference network z_Φ(w|x,γ) that outputs an implicit distribution. To efficiently learn this posterior without per-datapoint optimization, the paper derives a kernelized Wasserstein gradient flow (WGF) update in RKHS, yielding a particle-based rule with a smoothed gradient term (guiding the posterior toward high-utility, high-disruption watermarks) and a repulsive kernel term (preventing mode collapse). During deployment, a watermark is sampled from the learned posterior for each incoming query and added to the input, requiring no backpropagation, ensembles, or re-training. Theoretical analysis (Theorems 5.1–5.2) proves that maximizing the OOD KL-divergence term directly increases the clone model's loss gap on ID data and bounds the clone's generalization error as O(√ln(n)/√n) in query budget n.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with the DFME soft-label attack, ACT reduces clone model accuracy to 46.57% (ResNet18-8X), 40.32% (MobileNetV2), and 49.25% (DenseNet121), outperforming the SOTA MeCo by 5.1%, 6.2%, and 12.1% respectively. On CIFAR-100 with DFME, ACT achieves 23.95%, 10.09%, and 6.26% clone accuracy, beating MeCo by 5.6%, 2.1%, and 4.5%. In deployment, ACT requires only 6.03s per batch on CIFAR-10, achieving 17×–172× speedup over P-poison (223.68s) and GRAD (107.29s) while matching MeCo's efficiency (6.06s). Model utility is preserved at 94.31% on CIFAR-10 and 75.78% on CIFAR-100, comparable to all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ACT outperforms all three baselines across every dataset and clone architecture. Against P-poison (Orekondy et al., 2020), ACT reduces clone accuracy by 31.5 percentage points on CIFAR-10 DFME (ResNet18: 46.57% vs 78.06%) and by 14.8 points on CIFAR-100 DFME (ResNet18: 23.95% vs 38.72%), while also being 37× faster at deployment (6.03s vs 223.68s). Against GRAD (Mazeika et al., 2022), ACT achieves similar margins (46.57% vs 79.33% on CIFAR-10 DFME ResNet18) with 17.8× faster inference. Against the SOTA MeCo (Wang et al., 2023), ACT wins by 5–12% absolute across all settings (e.g., 49.25% vs 61.38% on CIFAR-10 DFME DenseNet121; 6.26% vs 10.79% on CIFAR-100 DFME DenseNet121), while requiring only fine-tuning rather than full re-training, reducing training cost by 87%. In the hard-label DFMS-HL setting, ACT also consistently beats MeCo by 1–4% (e.g., 55.38% vs 59.30% on CIFAR-100 ResNet18), whereas P-poison and GRAD are nearly ineffective (within 1% of undefended). Against the stronger Ensemble Adaptive Attack, ACT still maintains a 6–12% advantage over the undefended baseline, demonstrating robustness even when the attacker knows the full defense strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes kernelized Wasserstein gradient flow (Liu, 2017) and the energy-function formulation from sampling-as-optimization (Wibisono, 2018) to learn a datapoint-specific watermark posterior via amortized variational inference, a technique previously applied to generative modeling and Bayesian inference but not to adversarial defense. It departs from existing active defenses (P-poison, GRAD, MeCo) that rely on test-time backpropagation, ensembles, or full re-training, and from passive watermarking methods (Jia et al., 2021a; Szyller et al., 2021) that only verify theft post-hoc. The key conceptual shift is treating the watermark as a latent random variable with a learned posterior rather than a fixed perturbation, enabling probabilistic and input-dependent defense with minimal fine-tuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a practically attractive middle ground: it achieves active defense (reducing clone accuracy by 6–12% over MeCo) while cutting re-training cost by 87% and matching MeCo's deployment speed, making it viable for API providers who cannot afford full re-training or test-time optimization. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 5.1) formally links KL-divergence maximization on OOD data to degraded clone quality, providing a principled design rationale. Open questions include scaling to larger victim models (the authors acknowledge this limitation and propose MoE integration), whether the hierarchical Bayesian extension they sketch can adapt to truly novel attack families, and how the defense performs under multi-modal or language-model extraction settings.
