## Problem

Treatment effect estimation from observational data is confounded by collider bias, which arises when sample selection depends on both treatment and outcome, opening a spurious path T→S←Y. While shadow variables can restore identifiability under collider bias, finding valid ones requires domain-specific expert knowledge and case-by-case investigation, making them impractical in most real-world settings. This paper asks whether shadow-variable representations can be automatically learned from observed covariates without any prior knowledge.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular observational data where covariates X and treatment T are fully observed for all units, but outcome Y is only available for selected units (S=1); for unselected units (S=0), Y is missing. It produces two outputs: (1) shadow-variable representations Z = g(X) that satisfy the conditional dependence and independence assumptions of a valid shadow variable, and (2) CATE estimates τ(x) for all units. The generation of Z is iterative — representations are regenerated until they pass a hypothesis test with a Bonferroni-corrected threshold, making the number of iterations data-dependent.

## Method

ShadowCatcher learns a representation generator g: X→Z under two competing constraints: a conditional dependence loss ℓ_gy that forces Z to carry predictive information about Y beyond X (via an outcome predictor h_y1), and a conditional independence loss ℓ_gz that forces Z to be independent of S given X,T,Y (via a representation predictor h_r trained on S=1 data, with missing Y imputed by h_y1). Because the independence constraint cannot be strictly enforced due to missing Y for S=0 units, a hypothesis test phase (based on d'Haultfoeuille's Theorem 2.5) checks whether a Q function satisfying E[S/Q(X,T,Y)−1 | X,Z,T]=0 exists in (0,1]; if no such Q is found, the representation is accepted as a valid shadow variable. ShadowEstimator then uses the accepted Z to estimate the odds ratio OR(X,T,Y) by solving a Fredholm integral equation, recovers the unselected outcome distribution f(Y|X,Z,T,S=0) via the odds-ratio identity, and combines both selected and unselected distributions with P(S|X,Z,T) to compute CATE, augmented with a Wasserstein IPM term to handle residual confounding.

## Results

On the IHDP dataset, the proposed method achieves √PEHE of 0.703±0.106 (within-sample) and 0.723±0.102 (out-of-sample), substantially outperforming the best baseline DESCN (1.193±0.057 / 1.665±0.246). On ACIC 2016, it reaches 1.911±0.126 / 2.047±0.351 versus DeR-CFR's 2.214±0.204 / 2.246±0.598. On the Jobs dataset, it achieves the lowest policy risk of 0.279±0.017 / 0.280±0.018, compared to ES-CFR's 0.290±0.045 / 0.293±0.046. On synthetic data with the strongest collider bias (β=5), the method achieves √PEHE of 0.299±0.008 (selected) and 0.300±0.008 (unselected), with a near-zero gap between selected and unselected data, while the best baseline CFR shows a gap of 0.359 vs 0.436.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method (ShadowCatcher + ShadowEstimator) outperforms all 11 statistical and deep-learning baselines across all four datasets (IHDP, ACIC 2016, Jobs, Twins) under collider bias. The largest gains are on IHDP, where it reduces √PEHE from 1.193 (DESCN, best baseline) to 0.703 within-sample and from 1.665 to 0.723 out-of-sample, a roughly 40% improvement. On ACIC 2016, it beats DeR-CFR (2.214/2.246) by about 13–14%. On Jobs, the margin is smaller but consistent: 0.279 vs 0.290 (ES-CFR) in policy risk. The method also shows the smallest within/out-of-sample gap, indicating better counterfactual generalization. The only comparison where the proposed method is slightly worse is against shadow-variable regression with correctly specified (oracle) shadow variables (Table 6: 0.299 vs 0.285 on selected data), which is expected since the oracle has access to the true shadow variable rather than learning it. Notably, methods designed for confounding bias (CFR, TARNet, BNN, DR-CFR, TEDVAE, DeR-CFR, DESCN, ES-CFR) and methods for standard selection bias (Heckit, IPSW, DR) all fail to address the T→S←Y collider path, resulting in substantially higher errors, especially on unselected data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the shadow-variable identification framework of d'Haultfoeuille (2010) and Miao & Tchetgen Tchetgen (2016), which established that CATE is identifiable under collider bias if a valid shadow variable Z satisfying Z⊥Y|X,T,S=1 and Z⊥S|X,T,Y is available. The key departure is that all prior work (d'Haultfoeuille 2010; Miao & Tchetgen Tchetgen 2016; Miao et al. 2024) requires the shadow variable to be known a priori from domain expertise, whereas ShadowCatcher automatically learns such representations from observed covariates via an iterative generation-and-hypothesis-testing loop. The estimation pipeline (odds ratio recovery via Fredholm integral equation, counterfactual distribution recovery) follows Miao et al. (2024), while the representation learning architecture draws on the balanced-representation tradition (CFR, TARNet) but repurposes it for shadow variable generation rather than treatment-effect balancing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that collider bias is common in real-world observational studies (e.g., non-response in surveys, selective reporting) yet has been largely ignored by the deep learning treatment-effect literature, which focuses on confounding bias. The main open question is what happens when no latent shadow-variable information exists in the covariates at all—the authors acknowledge this as a limitation but argue it is rare in practice. The reject threshold α introduces a manual efficiency-performance tradeoff that lacks a principled adaptive rule, and the method's reliance on imputing missing Y values during generation (via h_y1) could propagate errors in high-missingness regimes. Future work could explore adaptive threshold selection, multi-round refinement, or extensions to continuous treatments.
