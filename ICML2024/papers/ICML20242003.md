## Problem

Despite tabular data being the dominant modality across science, healthcare, finance, and public policy, foundation model research has almost entirely ignored it, with LLMs and vision models consuming the vast majority of attention and resources. The paper argues this neglect is unjustified given tabular data's ubiquity, the uniquely unsolved challenges it presents (heterogeneous feature spaces, mixed types, small individual datasets), and the potential for a Large Tabular Model (LTM) to revolutionize data science, synthetic data generation, and multidisciplinary research.

## Input / Output

The proposed LTM operates on single independent tables with heterogeneous, mixed-type columns (numerical, categorical, datetime, missing) alongside textual metadata such as dataset descriptions, column names, and category labels. Outputs span conditional and unconditional synthetic data generation, row- or dataset-level embeddings for downstream prediction, few-shot probabilistic prediction, and imputation. A key architectural constraint is invariance or equivariance to column permutations, reflecting the arbitrary ordering of table columns, and the model must handle varying numbers of features across datasets.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a formal framework for what constitutes a Large Tabular Model, articulated through four desiderata: handling mixed-type columns (D1), cross-dataset modelling of heterogeneous feature spaces (D2), leveraging textual context (D3), and column-order invariance/equivariance (D4). The authors argue that attention-based architectures naturally satisfy D4 and process variable-length feature sets, while conditional generative models (as opposed to encoder-only) are needed to enable few-shot prediction, imputation, and conditional generation. They specifically argue against repurposing LLMs for tabular generation, showing that autoregressive tokenization of continuous variables is fundamentally inefficient (modelling a single Gaussian requires complex multi-step token probability distributions) and that LLMs are poorly calibrated for sampling from continuous distributions. The paper identifies TabLib (627M tables, 827B context tokens) as evidence that sufficient training data now exists, and outlines benchmarking tasks spanning supervised learning, synthetic data generation, imputation, and cross-dataset reasoning to guide future LTM evaluation.

## Results

This is a position paper with no original experiments or quantitative results. The paper's key empirical observations are drawn from prior work: XGBoost remains among the top performers for supervised tabular learning across multiple recent benchmarks (Gorishniy et al., 2021; Borisov et al., 2022; Grinsztajn et al., 2022), the largest tabular benchmark datasets contain only ~50,000 points (Grinsztajn et al., 2022), and the most recent tabular metadataset (TabLib, Eggert et al., 2023) contains 627 million tables and 827 billion context tokens. The paper argues that scaling advantages observed in vision and text (billions of images, trillions of tokens) have not yet been tested in the tabular domain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or run head-to-head comparisons. Instead, it surveys the relative standing of existing approaches: tree-based models (XGBoost) still match or beat transformer-based methods (SAINT, TabTransformer) on most tabular benchmarks, with neural nets gaining an edge only at larger sample sizes, more target classes, or with modern regularization. For tabular generation, the paper argues that LLM-based approaches (GReaT, REaLTabFormer, TabuLa) are inefficient due to numerical tokenization and poor calibration, while generative approaches like RadialGAN fail to scale across domains. The paper's central comparison is conceptual rather than empirical: it contrasts the research attention and impact potential of LLMs versus the underexplored LTM space, concluding that LTM research offers higher impact-per-researcher given the current low competition and moderate computational requirements.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new conceptual ground by coining the term 'Large Tabular Model' (LTM) and framing tabular data as deserving foundation-model-scale treatment, a perspective absent from the 200+ page Bommasani et al. (2022) foundation model report. It synthesizes and critiques several existing threads—cross-table pretraining (XTab, CT-BERT, TaBERT), tabular generation via LLMs (GReaT, REaLTabFormer, TabuLa), and the TabPFN approach—arguing that none yet satisfy the four desiderata (mixed-type columns, cross-dataset modelling, textual context, permutation invariance) it proposes. The paper departs from the LLM-centric view of foundation models by arguing that standard autoregressive LLMs are architecturally ill-suited to tabular generation due to continuous-variable tokenization and poor calibration, pointing instead toward diffusion models or smaller purpose-built transformers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a compelling case that tabular data—the dominant modality in science, healthcare, finance, and policy—has been neglected in foundation model research despite the existence of large-scale tabular corpora (e.g., TabLib with 627M tables) and evidence that scaling advantages observed in vision and text may transfer. Key open questions remain: whether current tabular datasets are diverse and high-quality enough for true generalization, how to reliably evaluate LTMs without the visual inspection available for images or text, and how to address bias in large tabular corpora. The paper's strongest practical implication is that LTM research is still within computational reach of many groups (unlike frontier LLMs), making it an accessible high-impact direction. However, as a position paper it offers no new method, benchmark, or empirical result, so its impact depends on whether the community adopts the LTM framing.
