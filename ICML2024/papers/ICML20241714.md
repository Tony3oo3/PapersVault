## Problem

In-context learning (ICL) has been demonstrated in language and vision but remains unexplored in sequential decision making, where environment stochasticity and agent actions create a lower tolerance for errors that can lead to unrecoverable states. The paper shows that naively applying transformers trained on single-trajectory sequences (as in Decision Transformer) to sequential decision making fails to enable ICL of new tasks, and identifies the specific data distributional properties required to make it work.

## Input / Output

At test time, the model receives a context of 1–7 full expert demonstration trajectories from an unseen task (same level) concatenated with the current episode's state-action history, and predicts the next action at each timestep via cross-entropy. For MiniHack, observations are structured as a 21×79 glyph matrix, a 26-dimensional blstats vector, and a 256-dimensional message vector, with 8 compass-direction actions; for Procgen, observations are high-dimensional pixel frames. The key design constraint is that the context must contain multiple trajectories from the same level (trajectory burstiness p_b = 1.0), not just the current episode, so the model can cover the wide range of states the agent may encounter.

## Method

The core contribution is showing that training a causal transformer on multi-trajectory sequences—where multiple full episodes from the same level are stacked into a single input sequence—enables ICL of entirely new sequential decision making tasks, whereas single-trajectory sequences do not. The paper introduces 'trajectory burstiness' (p_b), the probability that a training sequence contains at least two trajectories from the same level, ensuring the task-relevant information is fully present in context while environment stochasticity prevents pure memorization. The architecture uses task-specific observation encoders (NetHackStateEmbeddingNet for MiniHack, CNN for Procgen) feeding into a causal transformer (100M–310M parameters) trained with cross-entropy loss over action predictions on offline expert data from 12 tasks. Extensive ablations show that larger model and dataset sizes, higher trajectory burstiness, environment stochasticity (via sticky actions), and greater task diversity all improve few-shot generalization to out-of-distribution tasks with disjoint states, actions, dynamics, and reward functions.

## Results

In the motivating experiment (Section 4), the multi-trajectory transformer achieves an episodic return of 0.780 ± 0.07 on MiniHack-Labyrinth, substantially outperforming the single-trajectory transformer at -0.323 ± 0.05 under identical one-shot conditioning. On the main MiniHack evaluation (Figure 3), the multi-trajectory transformer consistently outperforms both the Hashmap and Behavioural Cloning baselines across 4 unseen tasks, with performance improving monotonically as the number of demonstrations increases from 1 to 7. On Procgen (Figure 4), the 310M multi-trajectory transformer similarly outperforms baselines on 4 unseen pixel-based tasks, with performance increasing with more demonstrations and remaining competitive with Hashmap on the Plunder task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) Hashmap (HM), which memorizes state-action pairs from the expert demonstration and acts as an oracle in deterministic settings but fails in stochastic environments, and (2) Behavioural Cloning (BC), a single-trajectory transformer conditioned on zero (BC-0) or one (BC-1) demonstration. The multi-trajectory transformer outperforms both baselines on all MiniHack tasks when given at least one demonstration, with the gap widening as more demonstrations are added. Against BC, the advantage is particularly large, confirming that training on multi-trajectory sequences (rather than single trajectories) is essential for enabling in-context learning. Against Hashmap, the multi-trajectory transformer demonstrates genuine generalization to unseen states rather than mere state-action copying, which is critical in stochastic environments where the agent encounters states not present in the demonstration. On Procgen, the method outperforms BC on all four test tasks and is competitive with Hashmap on Plunder, while clearly surpassing both baselines on Climber, Ninja, and Jumper.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-context learning (ICL) paradigm from NLP and vision to sequential decision making, building directly on the burstiness framework of Chan et al. (2022b) and the multi-trajectory sequence format from Laskin et al. (2022) and Team et al. (2023a). It departs from the Decision Transformer lineage (Chen et al., 2021), which trains on single-trajectory sequences, by showing that this standard approach fails to enable ICL for new tasks. The key conceptual departure is identifying trajectory burstiness—having multiple trajectories from the same level in context—as the critical distributional property that enables ICL in sequential decision making, analogous to how entity burstiness drives ICL in supervised settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to demonstrate cross-task generalization on MiniHack and Procgen via ICL with no weight updates, where train and test tasks differ in states, actions, dynamics, and rewards. The systematic ablations (burstiness, stochasticity, model/dataset size, task diversity) provide a practical recipe for enabling ICL in sequential decision making, and the failure-mode taxonomy (in-context learning, in-weights learning, unforgiving environments, distributional drift) offers a useful diagnostic framework. Key limitations include the focus on navigation-based tasks in relatively simplified environments, and the open question of whether these findings transfer to tasks with continuous action spaces, longer horizons, or the high-stakes unrecoverable-state dynamics of real-world robotics.
