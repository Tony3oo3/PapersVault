## Problem

Differential entropy estimation becomes severely data-inefficient in moderately high dimensions (≥10), where plug-in estimators like KNIFE (learnable Gaussian mixtures) overfit and plateau far from the true value. Existing Donsker-Varadhan-based neural estimators mitigate this by learning a correction function, but they rely on simple, fixed base distributions (e.g., a single Gaussian or uniform), which are themselves data-inefficient and exacerbate the statistical difficulty identified by McAllester & Stratos (2020). The paper asks whether combining a flexible, trainable base distribution with a DV-based neural correction can yield consistent, accurate entropy estimates in dimensions where both approaches alone fail.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n i.i.d. samples from the target distribution P and m samples drawn from a learned base distribution Q (a KNIFE mixture of M multivariate Gaussians, or a fixed isotropic Gaussian). The neural network T is parametrized to exploit the KNIFE structure: inputs are projected onto per-component uncorrelated coordinates via Cholesky factors, passed through shared fully-connected ReLU layers, and combined via component-relevance weights before an ELU+1+ε output transformation that guarantees e^{T(x)} > 0. The primary output is an upper-bound estimate of H(P); in the Information Bottleneck setting, the same machinery estimates MI(X;Z) = H(Z) − H(Z|X) where H(Z|X) is analytically available from the Gaussian encoder, yielding classification accuracy on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet. As a by-product, the learned pair (q, T) defines an unnormalized density q(x)e^{T(x)} usable for rejection sampling or Langevin-dynamics-based generation.

## Method

REMEDI decomposes the entropy as H(P) = −E^P[log q] − R(P‖Q) and applies the Donsker-Varadhan variational representation to the relative entropy term, yielding the loss L_REMEDI = (1/n)Σ −log q(x_i) − [(1/n)Σ T(x_i) − log((1/m)Σ e^{T(x̃_j)})]. Training proceeds in two phases: first, the KNIFE base Q is fit by minimizing cross-entropy (standard plug-in training); second, a neural network T is trained to minimize the DV correction term using samples from both P and Q. The key architectural choice is that T's input features are the per-component Cholesky-decorrelated offsets L_i(x − μ_i), making the correction network aware of the base distribution's geometry. The paper proves strong consistency of the estimator under a sub-Gaussian assumption on Q (weaker than the compact-support assumption required by Belghazi et al., 2018), via a uniform law of large numbers argument over a compact parameter space of two-hidden-layer ReLU networks. The learned T implicitly defines a Gibbs density p̃(x) ∝ q(x)e^{T(x)}, connecting the framework to rejection sampling and Langevin diffusion for generative purposes.

## Results

On the 8-dimensional triangle dataset, REMEDI achieves an entropy estimate of 3.08 ± 0.04, substantially closer to the true value of 2.59 than KNIFE's 4.36 ± 0.05. On Information Bottleneck classification, REMEDI (Gaussian base) achieves 98.36% accuracy on MNIST and 79.81% on ImageNet, while REMEDI (KNIFE base) achieves 86.99% on CIFAR-10, outperforming or matching all baselines. Notably, on ImageNet REMEDI learns significantly less information about the inputs (160.67 bits MI(X;Z)) compared to VIB (237.71 bits) while maintaining comparable accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against KNIFE (Pichler et al., 2022), REMEDI dramatically improves entropy estimation on the 8-dimensional triangle dataset (3.08 vs. 4.36, true = 2.59) and wins on CIFAR-10 classification (86.99% vs. 86.73%), while roughly tying on MNIST (98.28% vs. 98.25%) and ImageNet (79.61% vs. 79.62%). Against MINE (Belghazi et al., 2018), REMEDI (Gaussian base) achieves higher accuracy on MNIST (98.36% vs. 98.01%), and MINE exhibited stability issues on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet due to the exponential term in the Donsker-Varadhan bound, preventing fair comparison. Against VIB (Alemi et al., 2016), REMEDI (Gaussian base) edges out VIB on MNIST (98.36% vs. 98.34%) and REMEDI (KNIFE base) wins on CIFAR-10 (86.99% vs. 86.45%), while on ImageNet the two are nearly tied (79.81% vs. 79.83%) but REMEDI compresses the input by 32% less information (160.67 vs. 237.71 bits). Against RealNVP (Dinh et al., 2016), REMEDI/KNIFE occupies the entire Pareto front in wall-clock time versus entropy estimate on the 8-dimensional triangle dataset, indicating superior computational efficiency for a given accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

REMEDI combines two previously separate lines of work: learnable Gaussian mixture plug-in estimators (KNIFE, Pichler et al. 2022; Schraudolph 2004) and Donsker-Varadhan-based neural corrections (MINE, Belghazi et al. 2018). Prior Donsker-Varadhan approaches for density estimation (Park & Pardalos 2021; Aharoni et al. 2022) used only simple fixed base distributions, while plug-in estimators like KNIFE suffered from overfitting and data inefficiency in moderate dimensions. The key departure is using an adaptive, trained KNIFE mixture as the base distribution Q and then correcting its residual error with a neural network T via the DV formula, yielding a tighter upper bound on entropy. The theoretical consistency proof also extends Belghazi et al.'s compact-support assumption to a sub-Gaussian setting, which is necessary for the Gaussian mixture base distributions used in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck: plug-in entropy estimators become data-inefficient in dimensions as low as 10, limiting their use in modern ML pipelines (Information Bottleneck, RL, dimensionality reduction). REMEDI provides a principled correction mechanism that significantly narrows the gap to true entropy without requiring the combinatorial explosion of mixture components. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very high-dimensional image data (e.g., raw MNIST pixels), how it interacts with more expressive base distributions like normalizing flows, and the connection to score-based diffusion models suggested by the Langevin dynamics sampling. The Information Bottleneck experiments show modest but consistent accuracy gains, though the CIFAR-10 results are less clear-cut.
