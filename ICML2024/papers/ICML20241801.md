## Problem

While prior interpretability work studies individual model components in isolation (feature visualization, probes, circuit discovery), there is no framework for understanding how components *collectively* shape a specific prediction, nor a way to translate such understanding into targeted model edits. The paper asks: how do changes to model components collectively change individual model predictions, and can this answer directly enable effective editing?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241801/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained model with a user-specified computation graph (defining components at a chosen granularity, e.g., individual convolution filters or rows of weight matrices), an input example z, and for editing, small sets of target and reference examples (as few as 5–20 pairs). It produces a per-component attribution vector w^(z) ∈ R^|C| plus a bias term, which together form a linear predictor of the model's correct-class margin under any component ablation. For editing, the output is a specific subset of components to zero out, requiring no retraining.

## Method

COAR (Component Attribution via Regression) estimates component attributions in two steps: (1) construct a component dataset by randomly ablating α-fraction subsets of components (e.g., 5–10%) and recording the resulting correct-class margin via forward passes, and (2) fit an ordinary linear regression mapping the binary ablation vector to the observed margin. Despite the highly non-linear computation graph, this linear model achieves high Pearson correlation (0.66–0.74) with ground-truth counterfactuals across ResNet-18/50, ViT-B/16, GPT-2, and Phi-2. For editing, COAR-EDIT computes a two-sample t-test statistic per component comparing its attribution scores on target vs. reference examples, then ablates the k components with the most negative scores to selectively shift target outputs while preserving reference behavior.

## Results

COAR achieves high Pearson correlation between predicted and ground-truth component counterfactuals across three setups: ~0.74 on CIFAR-10 ResNet-18 (α_test=10%), ~0.66 on ImageNet ResNet-50 (α_test=5%), and strong performance on ImageNet ViT-B/16 (α_test=5%). For editing, COAR-EDIT corrects individual misclassifications by ablating as few as 3 components (0.01% of all), improves worst-subpopulation accuracy on Waterbirds from 64%→83% (ablating 0.9% of components) and on CelebA from 47%→85% (ablating 26/22,720 components), and boosts CLIP robustness to typographic attacks from 51%→89% (ablating 300 components, 0.4%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

COAR is compared against four baselines: Neuron Conductance (NC, Dhamdhere et al. 2018), Internal Influence (II, Leino et al. 2018), Leave-One-Out (LOO), and Gradient-times-Parameters (GP). COAR outperforms all four baselines by a large margin across all three model/dataset setups and all tested ablation fractions. The most striking gap is on ImageNet ResNet-50 at α_test=5%, where COAR achieves 66% correlation with ground-truth counterfactuals while the best baseline (LOO) reaches only 33%—a 2× improvement. NC and II, which rely on integrated gradients to compute per-component importance, underperform COAR consistently, suggesting that gradient-based attribution signals are insufficient for predicting multi-component ablation effects. COAR also maintains its advantage under out-of-distribution evaluation (α_test ≠ α_train), with correlation decaying gracefully (e.g., 0.74→0.68 on CIFAR-10 as α_test increases from 10% to 15%), while baselines degrade more sharply.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by reframing the well-studied question of how model components shape predictions as a counterfactual estimation task: given a model's computation graph, predict how outputs change when arbitrary subsets of components are ablated. It departs from bottom-up mechanistic interpretability (circuit discovery, activation patching) by taking a top-down, capability-agnostic perspective, and from input-space attribution methods (LIME, Integrated Gradients) by operating in component space. Conceptually, it is closest to datamodeling (Ilyas et al., 2022; Park et al., 2023), which approximates a learning algorithm with a linear model in dataset space; COAR performs an analogous approximation in component space via linear regression over random ablation vectors. The paper explicitly positions itself as complementary to prior localization work, noting that COAR is agnostic to ablation method and directly yields actionable edits, addressing limitations identified by Zhang & Nanda (2023) and Hase et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: COAR-EDIT enables targeted model editing (fixing misclassifications, improving subpopulation robustness, removing backdoor triggers, suppressing typographic-attack vulnerability) by ablating a tiny fraction of components (0.01%–1%) without any additional training, fine-tuning, or parameter updates. Key limitations include the computational cost of constructing component datasets (requiring 50K–200K forward passes per example), the sensitivity to the choice of computation graph granularity, and the fact that linear attributions lose accuracy when test-time ablation fractions deviate from train-time fractions. Open questions include whether non-linear component models (decision trees, kernel methods) would capture higher-order interactions, how the approach extends to generative models, and whether component attributions can serve as a principled kernel for comparing models or localizing memorized data.
