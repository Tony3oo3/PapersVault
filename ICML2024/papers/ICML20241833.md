## Problem

Kernel conditional mean embeddings (CMEs) face three interrelated limitations: the O(n³) Gram matrix inversion required for estimation becomes prohibitive at scale, pre-specified RKHS features lack expressiveness for highly nonlinear structures, and the kernel bandwidth on the output variable cannot be tuned via standard cross-validation because changing it fundamentally alters the RKHS norm in the objective. This paper addresses all three simultaneously by replacing the matrix inversion with a neural network and introducing a principled mechanism for bandwidth optimization.

## Input / Output

For density estimation, the method takes a multi-dimensional input x (up to 90 features in UCI experiments) and a one-dimensional output y, producing a conditional distribution represented as a weighted sum of M=100 Gaussian density kernel evaluations at fixed, uniformly spaced location points η_a. The NN outputs the weights f_a(x;θ), and the conditional density p̂(y|x) is recovered via the inner product ⟨k_σ(y,·), μ̂(x)⟩. In the RL setting, the input is a state-action pair (s,a) and the output is the full distribution of the discounted reward sum Z(s,a), parameterized with 51 atoms over [-100,100] with softmax-normalized weights per action.

## Method

The core architectural change replaces the CME weight vector β(x) = (K_X + λI)⁻¹k_X with a neural network f(x;θ) that directly outputs M weights on fixed location points, eliminating both Gram matrix inversion and the need for a regularization parameter λ. The training loss is the RKHS-norm-based objective (Eq. 3), which is differentiable and amenable to standard mini-batch optimization. The key theoretical contribution is Theorem 3.1, which proves that the RKHS norm at bandwidth σ upper-bounds the norm at √2σ for the Gaussian density kernel, enabling joint optimization of θ and σ with a single loss function (Proposal-Joint) or iterative alternation with a supplementary L² density error loss (Proposal-Iterative). For distributional RL, the method adopts an MMD-based Bellman loss with a FUSE kernel-fusion strategy (log-sum-exp over 10 Gaussian bandwidths) to balance test power against contraction guarantees, yielding a new variant that shares CDQN's atom structure but uses a fundamentally different loss function.

## Results

On toy datasets (WAS1, lower is better), Proposal-Joint achieves the best scores: 5.67 on Bimodal, 4.68 on Skewed, and 21.10 on Ring, outperforming all baselines including NF (6.83/6.56/27.99) and CARD (6.59/5.93/29.91). On 8 UCI regression datasets (QICE, lower is better), the proposed methods win on 6 of 8 datasets, with Proposal-Joint achieving 0.91 on Kin8nm, 0.84 on Power, and 0.53 on Year, while CARD is strongest on Naval (0.80) and Concrete (2.30). In distributional RL, the proposed method achieves the highest cumulative reward on Acrobot and MountainCar, and the fastest convergence on CartPole, consistently outperforming CDQN, MMDQN, and DQN across all three environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Deep Feature approach (DF), the proposed method clearly wins: DF-med (median heuristic) is consistently the worst or near-worst baseline across all UCI datasets (e.g., 7.14 QICE on Energy vs. 2.89 for Proposal-Iter), while DF-0.1 (fixed bandwidth) is competitive but still loses on most datasets, confirming that the learned bandwidth optimization is critical. Against MDN and NF, the proposed method wins on 6 of 8 UCI datasets, with particularly large margins on Kin8nm (0.91 vs. 2.42/1.75) and Power (0.84 vs. 1.34/1.35). Against CARD (diffusion-based), the comparison is closest: CARD wins on Naval (0.80 vs. 6.81) and Concrete (2.30 vs. 3.21), but the proposed method wins or ties on the remaining six datasets, notably matching CARD on Year (0.53) and Kin8nm (0.91 vs. 0.92) while using a single NN rather than CARD's two-stage training pipeline. In RL, the proposed method outperforms CDQN on all three environments despite using an identical NN architecture and atom structure, attributing the gain to the MMD-based loss and kernel fusion strategy. MMDQN, which learns particle positions directly with uniform weights, shows instability in classic control environments and is outperformed by the proposed method on all three tasks. DQN (standard value-based) is consistently the weakest baseline in RL, as expected.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the kernel conditional mean embedding (CME) framework of Song et al. (2009) by replacing the computationally expensive Gram matrix inversion with a neural network, going beyond the Deep Feature approach of Xu et al. (2021) which still required explicit matrix inversion and a regularization parameter. The key methodological departure is the introduction of a Gaussian density kernel on the output variable paired with a supplementary squared-error loss, which resolves the long-standing inability to tune the output kernel hyperparameter via standard cross-validation. For the RL application, the method inherits the fixed-atom structure of CDQN (Bellemare et al., 2017) and the RKHS-based distributional representation of MMDQN (Nguyen-Tang et al., 2021), while adopting the FUSE log-sum-exp kernel fusion strategy from Biggs et al. (2023) to address bandwidth selection in the distributional Bellman update.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses three practical bottlenecks in CME-based methods simultaneously—scalability, expressiveness, and output-kernel hyperparameter selection—making the framework more accessible for large-scale and high-dimensional settings. The Gaussian density kernel with its closed-form SQ loss and the theoretical upper-bound connection (Theorem 3.1) provide a principled, efficient path for joint optimization that avoids the heuristic median heuristic. However, the method is currently limited to one-dimensional outputs with fixed location points, and the RL experiments are restricted to three simple control environments, leaving open questions about multi-dimensional generalization, adaptive location point selection, and whether the FUSE strategy's advantages persist in complex (e.g., Atari) settings. The approach also does not capture causal structure, a limitation the authors explicitly acknowledge.
