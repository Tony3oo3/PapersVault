## Problem

Prior interpretability work identified self-repair—where downstream components compensate for ablated ones—only on narrow task distributions (e.g., Indirect Object Identification) or when ablating entire layers. It remained unclear whether self-repair persists on the full pretraining distribution at the granularity of individual attention heads, and what specific mechanisms drive it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241745/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method resample-ablates individual attention heads in Pythia (160M–1B), GPT-2 (Small, Medium), and LLaMA-7B, running forward passes on 1M tokens from The Pile (the models' pretraining distribution). For each ablated head and token, it computes the direct effect, the logit difference, and decomposes self-repair into contributions from downstream attention heads, MLP layers, and the final LayerNorm scaling factor. To reduce noise, headline results are filtered to the top 2% of tokens by each head's direct effect, and LayerNorm self-repair percentages are clipped to [0, 100%] per token before averaging.

## Method

The paper defines self-repair as Δlogit − ΔDE_head and demonstrates it is imperfect (ablating a head still shifts logits) and noisy (degree varies wildly across tokens, sometimes overcorrecting). Two mechanisms are identified: (1) LayerNorm self-repair, where ablating a head reduces the residual stream norm, increasing the S/S′ scaling ratio and amplifying existing logits—accounting for roughly 30% of self-repair on average, derived via a closed-form decomposition of the logit change into a LayerNorm-on-existing-logits term and a LayerNorm-on-ΔDE term; and (2) sparse anti-erasure neurons in MLP layers, where a small subset of neurons (often with negative clean direct effects) reduce their erasure upon ablation of an upstream head, with the specific neurons varying across prompts. The authors further argue that self-repair is partly a byproduct of taking models off-distribution rather than an intentional mechanism, and connect it to the Iterative Inference hypothesis, where multiple heads across layers can redundantly perform the same task.

## Results

Self-repair is confirmed across six model families (Pythia-160M/410M/1B, GPT2-Small/Medium, Llama-7B) on 1M tokens of The Pile, with individual attention heads in later layers consistently showing imperfect self-repair (logit difference smaller in magnitude than the head's direct effect). LayerNorm scaling factor changes account for approximately 30% of the total self-repair on average across layers, with 90.66% of tokens showing a clean-to-ablated scaling ratio >1 under resample ablation (98.75% for mean, 100% for zero). Sparse sets of Anti-Erasure neurons in the final MLP layer drive additional self-repair: in Llama-7B, the single top self-repairing neuron compensates for 10% of the ablated head's direct effect in over 40% of instances, while roughly 60% of all neurons in the layer show negligible direct-effect changes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an empirical interpretability study rather than a method-comparison paper, so there are no direct baseline comparisons. It extends three prior lines of work: (1) Wang et al. 2023 first identified self-repair as 'Backup Behavior' on the narrow Indirect Object Identification distribution; (2) McGrath et al. 2023 explored it as the 'Hydra Effect' on the Counterfact dataset with full-layer ablations; (3) McDougall et al. 2023 explained a specific instance via Copy Suppression heads. The current work generalizes all three by operating on the full pretraining distribution (The Pile), ablating individual attention heads rather than entire layers, and identifying two concrete mechanisms (LayerNorm scaling and sparse Anti-Erasure neurons) that were absent from prior analyses. The LayerNorm mechanism is entirely novel and was not considered in any prior self-repair work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-repair line of work initiated by Wang et al. (2023) on the IOI distribution and McGrath et al. (2023) on the Counterfact dataset, deliberately generalizing from narrow task-specific distributions and full-layer ablations to the full pretraining distribution and individual attention heads. It builds on the mathematical framework of Elhage et al. (2021) for residual stream decomposition and direct effects, and uses the resample ablation technique from Chan et al. (2022) as its core experimental method. The two key departures are the identification of LayerNorm scaling as a self-repair mechanism (entirely novel) and the narrowing of MLP Erasure from a layer-level phenomenon to sparse sets of individual anti-erasure neurons, building on the Copy Suppression findings of McDougall et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a 'passive' normalization factor explains roughly 30% of self-repair has direct practical consequences for interpretability practitioners: it means that standard ablation protocols (especially zero ablation) can produce misleading results simply by perturbing the residual stream norm, and that LayerNorm should be frozen or accounted for in circuit discovery experiments. The noise and imperfection of self-repair across tokens complicates clean mechanistic explanations and suggests that ablation-based importance metrics are less reliable than previously assumed. A key limitation is that the analysis focuses on direct effects rather than broader behavioral measures, leaving open how well these mechanisms generalize. The Iterative Inference Hypothesis, while speculative, provides a useful generative framework that predicts self-repair as a natural consequence of redundant task-capable components across layers.
