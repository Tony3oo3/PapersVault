## Problem

Learning reward functions remains the primary bottleneck for equipping robots with diverse skills, as hand-designed rewards require expert knowledge and extensive tuning. While LLMs can propose reward functions, they are numerically imprecise and their outputs must be grounded with environment feedback; existing grounding approaches rely on human feedback or evolutionary mutation, both of which are laborious or produce unstable, fluctuating performance. The paper asks whether reward parameters can be learned efficiently and reliably in the absence of humans.

## Input / Output

The method takes a short natural-language task description (e.g., 'insert peg into the side hole'), a fixed dictionary of environment observations (joint positions, end-effector pose, distances, contact forces, collision flags), and GPT-4 as the LLM. The LLM consumes textual execution descriptions—automatically constructed by parsing reward-feature local variables from the Python reward function—and outputs a ranked ordering of trajectories rather than absolute reward values. The final output is a parameterized Python reward function (a weighted sum of reward/penalty terms with tunable scalar parameters) and a trained model-free RL policy (SAC or PPO) that achieves the target skill.

## Method

The framework has two stages. First, a Chain-of-Thought prompt guides GPT-4 to decompose the task into sub-steps with Dos/Don'ts, identify relevant observations, and generate a Python reward function where all numerical values are exposed as input parameters. Second, an iterative self-alignment loop performs bi-level optimization: the inner loop trains a policy via RL with the current reward, while the outer loop samples trajectories, asks the LLM to rank them from textual execution descriptions, and updates reward parameters via Metropolis-Hastings Bayesian inference to minimize ranking inconsistency between the LLM and the learned reward (modeled with a Boltzmann-rational Bradley-Terry likelihood). When ranking is fully aligned but the policy still underperforms, an active adjustment step queries the LLM for the direction of parameter change, temporarily restricts the parameter domain to that direction, and re-runs self-alignment—ensuring the new parameters reduce inconsistency without introducing new ranking violations. This ranking-based supervision is the key distinction from prior work: it exploits the LLM's comparatively robust ordinal judgment rather than its unreliable absolute numerical prediction, and the self-consistency constraint prevents the reward from developing exploitable flaws during updates.

## Results

On 6 ManiSkill2 manipulation tasks, the self-aligned reward consistently matches or approaches oracle-reward performance, and in PushChair it surpasses the oracle (83.65% vs. 35.34% peak success rate). On 3 Isaac Gym tasks, the method achieves 100% success on Franka Cabinet and Door, and 94.54% on Scissor, while consuming up to ~73× fewer GPT-4 tokens than Eureka (e.g., 17,543 vs. 1,284,924 tokens on Door). The ablation shows that combining self-alignment (SA) with active adjustment (AA) yields the best results, e.g., 73% success on Pick-Cube vs. 1% for fixed LLM reward.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Eureka (Ma et al., 2023) on 3 Isaac Gym tasks, the proposed self-alignment method wins decisively on Door (100% vs. 0%) and Scissor (94.54% vs. 0.24%), ties on Cabinet (both 100%), and uses 4–73× fewer GPT-4 tokens throughout training. Against Text2Reward (Xie et al., 2023), the paper applies self-alignment to Text2Reward's zero-shot reward functions and shows consistent improvement: tasks where fixed Text2Reward rewards yield near-zero success (Pick-Cube, Push-Chair) are rescued by the iterative ranking-based update, confirming that the self-alignment mechanism generalizes beyond the paper's own CoT-generated rewards. Compared to the oracle (expert-designed) reward on ManiSkill2, the learned reward matches performance on 5 of 6 tasks and exceeds it on PushChair, demonstrating that LLM-proposed reward structures can be as good or better than hand-crafted ones once parameters are grounded via self-alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of preference-based inverse reinforcement learning (Sadigh et al., 2017; Biyik et al., 2022a) and LLM-based reward generation (Kwon et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2023). It departs from prior LLM reward work by rejecting both human-in-the-loop refinement and evolutionary mutation (Eureka, Ma et al., 2023) in favor of a closed-loop self-alignment process where the LLM serves as a ranking oracle rather than a value predictor. The Bayesian preference update (Bradley-Terry model with Metropolis-Hastings, building on APReL) is borrowed directly from the human-preference IRL literature, but the supervision signal is replaced with LLM-generated rankings of execution trajectories, and an active parameter adjustment mechanism (inspired by LLM reflection, Liu et al., 2023) is added to handle cases where ranking is fully aligned yet the policy remains ineffective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method removes the human feedback bottleneck from LLM-assisted reward design while being substantially more token-efficient than the dominant evolutionary alternative (up to ~100× fewer GPT-4 tokens), making automated reward learning more practical for iterative robot skill acquisition. Key limitations include reliance on final-state evaluation (unsuitable for trajectory-dependent tasks like walking in a shape), the assumption that LLM rankings are consistently aligned with human preference, and the equal-weight treatment of all pairwise comparisons (sparse-reward tasks would benefit from weighted preferences). Open questions include whether the self-alignment framework extends to visual-language model feedback, multi-agent or continuous-control settings beyond manipulation, and whether the ranking-based signal can be made more discriminative for subtle numerical differences where LLMs are known to mis-rank.
