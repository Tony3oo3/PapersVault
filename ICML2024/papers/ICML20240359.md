## Problem

Graph-based time series forecasting methods operate at a single spatial resolution, missing the multi-scale structure inherent in hierarchical time series, while existing hierarchical forecasting approaches do not exploit relational dependencies among and within hierarchy levels. The combination of learnable graph pooling operators with hierarchical inductive biases remains underexplored, and no prior method learns the hierarchical structure itself end-to-end from data while simultaneously optimizing forecasting accuracy at every level.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240359/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a W-step window of univariate time series observations (W=12 for traffic, W=48 for energy, W=24 for air quality), a static weighted adjacency matrix encoding spatial correlations, and optional exogenous covariates, and produces H-step-ahead point forecasts at each level of a learned 3-level hierarchy (raw series → 20 supernodes → total aggregate). Cluster assignments are hard (one-hot selection matrices) obtained via Gumbel-softmax discretization, and final outputs are reconciled to satisfy the aggregation constraints QŶ=0, ensuring that predicted aggregates equal the sum of their constituent bottom-level forecasts.

## Method

HiGP builds a pyramidal graph architecture where each hierarchy level has its own adjacency matrix and message-passing layers, connected by Select-Reduce-Connect (SRC) operators that propagate representations both within a level (intra-level GNN) and across levels (inter-level reduction and lifting). The selection operator is parametrized as a score matrix interpreted as unnormalized log-probabilities, sampled via Gumbel-softmax and discretized with a straight-through estimator to yield hard cluster assignments; a min-cut regularization term encourages clusters to respect graph connectivity while an orthogonality penalty prevents degenerate solutions. A differentiable forecast reconciliation step projects raw predictions onto the null space of the constraint matrix Q, computed as P = I − Qᵀ(QQᵀ)⁻¹Q, and is integrated into the training loss alongside the forecasting error at all hierarchy levels and a reconciliation-distance regularizer. The entire pipeline—temporal encoding (GRU), intra-level message passing, inter-level hierarchical propagation, clustering, and reconciliation—is trained end-to-end with a single combined objective, so that cluster assignments are learned to minimize aggregate prediction error, providing a self-supervised clustering signal aligned with the forecasting task.

## Results

On the four benchmark datasets, HiGP-TTS(G) achieves the best MAE across all settings: 3.007 on Metr-LA, 1.568 on PeMS-Bay, 4.05 on CER-E, and 12.02 on AQI. The corresponding MRE values are 5.205%, 2.510%, 19.20%, and 18.75% respectively. In the traffic-specific comparison (Table 2), the tuned HiGP(T) variant achieves MAE of 2.68 (15-min) on Metr-LA and 1.31 (15-min) on PeMS-Bay, matching or slightly exceeding the best prior methods (SGP at 2.69 and 1.30). Ablation results confirm that both relational message passing and hierarchical propagation contribute meaningfully to accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HiGP consistently outperforms all flat TTS baselines: on Metr-LA, HiGP-TTS(G) (MAE 3.007) beats GConv-TTS (3.071), Diff-TTS (3.012), Gated-TTS (3.027), and GUNet-TTS (3.057); on CER-E, HiGP-TTS(G) (4.05) improves over the best flat baseline GUNet-TTS (4.08) and GConv-TTS (4.12). The hierarchical Graph U-Net (GUNet-TTS), despite also using pooling, underperforms HiGP across all four datasets, suggesting that learned end-to-end clustering with forecast-reconciliation is more effective than fixed-ratio pooling. Against simple baselines, HiGP reduces MAE by roughly 15–20% over RNN and FC-RNN (e.g., 3.007 vs. 3.543 on Metr-LA; 4.05 vs. 4.57 on CER-E). In the traffic-specific comparison (Table 2), the tuned HiGP(T) matches or slightly beats SGP (2.68 vs. 2.69 MAE at 15-min on Metr-LA; 1.31 vs. 1.30 on PeMS-Bay) and clearly outperforms DCRNN (2.82/1.36) and GWNet (2.72/1.31) at the shortest horizon, while remaining competitive at 30- and 60-min horizons. Ablation confirms that removing either relational message passing or hierarchical propagation degrades accuracy by 0.05–0.12 MAE points, validating both components.

## Relation to Prior Work

HiGP unifies two previously separate research threads: hierarchical time series forecasting with reconciliation (Hyndman et al., 2011; Rangapuram et al., 2021) and graph-based spatiotemporal forecasting with relational inductive biases (Cini et al., 2023c; Gao & Ribeiro, 2022). It builds on the SRC graph pooling framework (Grattarola et al., 2022) and dense trainable pooling with min-cut regularization (Bianchi et al., 2020a; Ying et al., 2018), but repurposes these operators to learn hierarchical cluster assignments end-to-end using forecasting accuracy as the learning signal. The key departure from prior work is that hierarchical forecasting methods (e.g., Rangapuram et al., 2021) assume a fixed hierarchy and ignore relational dependencies, while graph-based forecasters (e.g., DCRNN, Graph WaveNet) operate at a single spatial resolution; HiGP embeds both types of structure into a single pyramidal architecture with a differentiable reconciliation step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled way to learn meaningful cluster assignments for time series collections without requiring a predefined hierarchy, using forecasting accuracy as a self-supervised signal — a practical capability for sensor networks where aggregation structure is not known a priori. Limitations include the O(M³) cost of the reconciliation projection (restricting scalability to a few thousand nodes), the assumption of static topology and univariate homogeneous sensors, and evaluation only on medium-sized networks (200–500 nodes). Open questions raised by the authors include extending to multivariate, heterogeneous, and irregularly sampled series, exploring alternative reconciliation strategies, and investigating how the approach scales to the larger node counts typical of graph-based forecasting benchmarks.
