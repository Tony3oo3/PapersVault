## Problem

Standard ROC curves for imbalanced or asymmetric-cost classification produce suboptimal classifiers because they post-process a single base model via thresholding rather than re-optimizing the classifier for each desired false-positive rate. The paper introduces the Cost-Optimal Curve (COC), which provably dominates the ROC curve by minimizing false negatives subject to a maximum false-positive rate, and provides the first algorithm to directly optimize the resulting weighted 0/1 loss over classification trees.

## Input / Output

The method takes a training set of feature vectors x ∈ ℝ^D with binary labels y ∈ {−1, +1} and a cost parameter λ ≥ 0 weighting false positives. It produces a family of fixed-structure decision trees (axis-aligned or sparse oblique), one per λ value, whose (FP, TP) points trace the COC curve. Unlike ROC-based approaches, the classifier need only output a hard class label—no probability calibration is required—and the extreme points of the COC curve reach TP = 1 or FP = 0 well before the ROC corner points.

## Method

The key theoretical contribution is proving that the constrained problem (minimize FN rate subject to FP rate ≤ p) is equivalent to the unconstrained weighted 0/1 loss min_θ [ν(θ) + λ·π(θ)], making the latter the practical optimization target. The algorithm extends Tree Alternating Optimization (TAO) to this cost-sensitive objective: at each iteration, decision nodes are updated by solving a reduced weighted binary classification problem (approximated via ℓ₁-regularized logistic regression on care points), while leaf nodes are set by weighted majority vote, guaranteeing monotonic decrease of the global 0/1 loss. To construct the full COC curve, a base tree is trained at λ = N⁺/N⁻, then two paths are followed by scaling λ up and down by a factor β, warm-starting each tree from the previous one until FP or FN reaches zero. The approach subsumes axis-aligned trees (CART, C5.0) and linear classifiers (logistic regression) as special cases of sparse oblique trees, and the ℓ₁ regularization on decision-node weights promotes interpretable splits.

## Results

On multiclass imbalanced benchmarks, TAO (COC) achieves the lowest cost-based 0/1 test error: 7.84±0.11 on Covertype (500k samples, 54 features, 7 classes) and 57.15±0.31 on RCV1 (15k samples, 47k features, 52 classes), while also using fewer leaves (4850 and 867) and shallower trees (depth 16 and 13) than all baselines. On binary imbalanced classification (Figure 4), the COC curve from TAO dominates CART, C5.0, and OC1 across the full TP-vs-FP-rate range on all 8 datasets, with particularly large margins on MNIST and Epsilon. Training 14 COC models on MNIST takes ~150 seconds vs. 80 seconds for CART and >10 minutes for OC1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAO/COC consistently outperforms all three tree-based baselines (CART, C5.0, OC1) on both binary and multiclass imbalanced classification. On Covertype, TAO reduces cost-based 0/1 error from 12.01% (CART) and 9.68% (C5.0) to 7.84%, while using fewer than half the leaves; on RCV1 the gap widens to 57.15% vs. 62.91% (CART) and 69.14% (C5.0), and OC1 fails entirely. In the binary TP-vs-FP-rate comparison (Figure 4), the COC curve dominates all baselines across the full operating range, with the largest margins on MNIST and Epsilon where class imbalance is most extreme. Against sampling-based techniques (SMOTE, over/undersampling), the paper reports no significant difference for TAO, noting that sampling is a reasonable approximation to cost weighting, but favors the cost-based approach as more principled. The key qualitative advantage is that COC trees are smoother and more stable across the curve because TAO warm-starts from the previous tree, whereas greedy methods (CART, C5.0, OC1) must regrow from scratch at each cost setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Tree Alternating Optimization (TAO) framework (Carreira-Perpiñan & Tavallali, 2018) from standard 0/1 loss to a weighted 0/1 loss, enabling direct global optimization of cost-sensitive objectives over parametric decision trees. It departs from the dominant ROC-curve paradigm (threshold post-processing) and from cost-sensitive learning approaches that rely on surrogate losses (e.g., weighted cross-entropy), arguing that neither produces classifiers that are accuracy-optimal for a given false-positive rate. The closest prior work—Drummond & Holte (2000) and Raubertas et al. (1994)—also constructs cost-sensitive tree curves but uses greedy CART-style splitting with local purity criteria, which does not optimize any global loss over tree parameters. The paper's key conceptual departure is formulating the problem as a weighted 0/1 loss (the Cost-Optimal Curve) and showing it can be optimized monotonically via TAO, rather than approximated with surrogates or greedy heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The COC provides a principled, provably superior alternative to ROC curves for imbalanced and asymmetric-cost classification, with the guarantee that every point on the COC dominates the corresponding ROC point. This is particularly impactful for interpretable-model settings (fraud detection, churn prediction, spam filtering) where decision trees are preferred and the high-TP/low-FP regime is most operationally critical. Limitations include the NP-hardness of the underlying 0/1 loss (so solutions are approximate), higher training cost than CART/C5.0, and the current restriction to single trees rather than ensembles. The authors note they are working on extending the approach to tree ensembles, which would be the natural next step for competitive performance on large-scale benchmarks.
