## Problem

Parameters of interest in nonignorable missing data (MNAR) settings are generally not identifiable without strong parametric assumptions on the missingness mechanism. This paper shows that identification can be achieved by fusing an MNAR primary dataset with an auxiliary missing-at-random (MAR) dataset, even when neither dataset alone suffices — a data fusion strategy for identification (rather than efficiency) that, to the authors' knowledge, has not been pursued in the MNAR literature.

## Input / Output

The method takes two tabular datasets: a primary MNAR domain (G=1) where the outcome Y and covariate M are jointly missing under a nonignorable mechanism, and an auxiliary MAR domain (G=2) where Y is entirely unrecorded but M is missing at random given covariates X. The output is a point estimate and bootstrap confidence interval for E[Y^(1)|G=1], the mean of the potentially missing outcome in the primary domain, produced by solving IPW estimating equations that pool information across both domains.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary graphical models for the MNAR missingness mechanism. In Model 1, missingness depends on the covariate M^(1) but not the outcome Y^(1) given X and M^(1), yielding a simple identification functional built from conditional expectations observable in each domain. In Model 2, missingness depends on Y^(1) but not M^(1) given X and Y^(1); identification proceeds via an odds-ratio function that encodes the deviation between observed and missing distributions, where the unobservable distribution p(M^(1)|X,R=0,G=1) is recovered from the auxiliary domain through a bridge equation, and a Fredholm integral equation of the first kind (with a completeness assumption) ensures unique recovery of the odds ratio. Both models share a selection-at-random assumption (M^(1) ⊥ G | X) linking the domains. IPW estimators are derived for each model, using propensity scores (or odds-ratio-weighted scores in Model 2) to reweight the primary domain and conditional expectations from the auxiliary domain to correct for the MNAR bias.

## Results

In the simulation studies (Model 1, true β=1.8), the correctly specified IPW estimator achieves bias of 0.006 (n=500) to −0.002 (n=2000) with MSE of 0.062 to 0.024, while MAR is biased upward by ~0.27 and MICE is biased downward by ~−0.37 across all sample sizes. In Model 2 (true β=−0.659), IPW (T) achieves bias of 0.029 to 0.015 with MSE as low as 0.00007 at n=2000, compared to MAR bias of ~0.36 and MICE bias of ~0.08–0.11. On the real-world COVID-19 application (March 2020 NYS hospitalization rate), Model 1 IPW estimates 0.7396 [95% CI: 0.7190, 0.7570] and Model 2 IPW estimates 0.7836 [95% CI: 0.7558, 0.8071], while MICE yields a substantially lower estimate of 0.6564 [95% CI: 0.6041, 0.7085].

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed IPW data-fusion estimator against three baselines: a naive MAR estimator (linear regression on complete cases), an MCAR estimator, and MICE (van Buuren & Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). In the Model 1 simulation, IPW (T) reduces bias from ~0.27 (MAR) and ~−0.37 (MICE) to near-zero (≤0.006), with MSE roughly 2–10× lower than both baselines at all sample sizes. In Model 2, IPW (T) again dominates: bias of 0.015–0.029 versus 0.36 for MAR and 0.08–0.11 for MICE, with MSE as low as 0.00007 at n=2000. Even under model misspecification (F), IPW maintains substantially lower bias than MAR (e.g., −0.017 vs. −0.544 in Model 1; 0.084 vs. 0.195 in Model 2), though MICE can occasionally show comparable or slightly lower bias in the F setting of Model 2. On the COVID-19 application, MICE produces a markedly lower hospitalization rate (0.6564) with a wider CI than both IPW models, while MAR and MCAR estimates (0.7337, 0.7533) fall between the two IPW estimates, underscoring that the choice of missingness model materially affects the point estimate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the shadow variable framework of Miao et al. (2015) for MNAR identification by allowing the shadow variable M^(1) to be potentially missing, and introduces a data fusion mechanism that borrows information from an auxiliary MAR domain to recover the unobserved distribution. The data fusion strategy is conceptually inspired by causal inference work combining experimental and observational data (Athey et al., 2016; Ghassami et al., 2022), but is applied here to the fundamentally different problem of identification under nonignorable missingness rather than unobserved confounding. The graphical model formulation draws on the DAG-based missing data literature (Mohan et al., 2013; Bhattacharya et al., 2019; Nabi et al., 2020), and the IPW estimation strategy follows the propensity-score tradition established by Robins et al. (1994).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to achieve identification of a target parameter in an MNAR setting by fusing it with an auxiliary MAR dataset, opening a practical pathway for real-world data where no single source provides sufficient information. The COVID-19 application demonstrates that model choice (MNAR vs. MAR vs. MICE) materially affects estimated hospitalization rates, underscoring the practical stakes. Key open questions include developing a semiparametrically efficient estimator (the authors note this is nontrivial due to complex tangent space restrictions), extending to conditional estimands, and relaxing the strong selection-at-random and completeness assumptions that limit applicability.
