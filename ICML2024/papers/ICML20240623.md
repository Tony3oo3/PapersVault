## Problem

Prior work on QA fine-tuning for factuality identified factors such as data truthfulness, format familiarity, and pretraining corpus coverage, but did not explain why fine-tuning on factually correct, pretraining-grounded examples can still actively harm downstream factuality. This paper identifies the missing factor: how well the fine-tuned facts are internally encoded (their 'factual salience') in the pretrained model, and provides a mechanistic explanation for why less well-known facts are more vulnerable to suppression during fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained base language model (Llama-2-7B or Mistral-7B) and fine-tunes it on QA pairs where the input is a question prompt (e.g., 'What is the capital of the USA?') and the output is a factual short answer or multiple-choice selection. Fine-tuning datasets are partitioned by fact popularity, proxied by Wikipedia page count (PopQA, Entity Questions) or the pretrained model's own loss (MMLU-History). Evaluation uses exact string matching after lowercasing and stripping for short answers, and argmax over answer-choice token scores for multiple-choice questions.

## Method

The paper introduces 'factual salience' S(s,r,a) = φ(a)ᵀW_Vφ(s) in a one-layer transformer, quantifying how strongly a fact is stored in the value matrix relative to the subject token. The key theoretical result shows that fine-tuning on low-salience facts produces gradients where subject-token relevance s_rel < relation-token relevance p_rel, which globally increases attention on the prompt token p_r and decreases attention on all subject tokens — an 'attention imbalance' that suppresses stored knowledge from the output. This effect is amplified for less salient facts because the attention threshold for incorrect prediction scales as d/S(s,r,a). Empirically, fine-tuning on the top-50% most popular facts outperforms fine-tuning on the bottom-50% by 4–10% across PopQA, Entity Questions, and MMLU, and matches or exceeds full-dataset fine-tuning, with attention pattern analysis in Llama-7B confirming the predicted reduction in subject-token attention after training on unpopular facts.

## Results

Fine-tuning on the most popular 50% of QA examples (FT-Top) outperforms fine-tuning on the least popular 50% (FT-Bottom) by 7% on MMLU-History, 6% on PopQA, and 4% on Entity Questions, consistently across both Llama-2-7B and Mistral-7B. On MMLU-History, FT-Top achieves 61.4% (Llama-7B) and 68.7% (Mistral-7B), while FT-Bottom drops to 55.6% and 59.4% respectively. Notably, fine-tuning on just the top 50% of popular facts matches or outperforms fine-tuning on the entire dataset, and on MMLU, fine-tuning on only the top 30% outperforms the full dataset by up to 2.5%. In the controlled PopQA experiment, FT-Top reaches 44.5% accuracy versus 37.4% for FT-Bottom and 20.1% for zero-shot prompting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods but instead compares different fine-tuning data strategies on the same pretrained models. FT-Top (most popular 50%) consistently wins over FT-Bottom (least popular 50%) by 7–10% across all three benchmarks and both models, establishing that fine-tuning on less well-known facts actively harms downstream factuality. FT-Top also matches or beats FT-Whole (the entire training set), with the advantage being most pronounced on MMLU where the top 30% subset outperforms the full dataset by up to 2.5%. A randomly selected 50% subset (FT-Random) significantly outperforms FT-Bottom, suggesting that even a small number of popular examples can mitigate the damage from unpopular ones. The gap between FT-Top and FT-Bottom widens as test questions become less popular, indicating the effect is most severe on long-tail knowledge where the model is most vulnerable to attention imbalance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior work on finetuning for factuality (Joshi et al., 2023; Kazemi et al., 2023; Schulman, 2023), which focused on data truthfulness, format familiarity, or pretraining grounding, by introducing a new axis: how strongly individual facts are encoded in the pretrained weights (termed 'factual salience'). The theoretical analysis builds on the one-layer transformer framework used in Allen-Zhu & Li (2023) and Tian et al. (2023b) for studying knowledge storage and attention dynamics, but applies it specifically to the finetuning gradient dynamics that produce attention imbalance. Concurrently, Gekhman et al. (2024) and Kang et al. (2024) observe that unfamiliar finetuning examples harm factuality, but this paper provides a mechanistic explanation (attention suppression of stored knowledge) and shows the effect is specifically tied to pretraining frequency rather than mere unfamiliarity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is immediate and actionable: finetuning on the top 50% most popular facts matches or outperforms finetuning on the full dataset, and finetuning on less popular facts actively degrades performance on those same facts. This challenges the intuitive strategy of matching finetuning data distribution to test distribution and suggests data curation by pretraining frequency should be a standard practice. Key limitations include the one-layer transformer theory (real LLMs have many layers and heads), the reliance on imperfect proxies for fact salience (Wikipedia page count, model loss), and the restriction to base models without prior instruction-tuning. Open questions the authors identify include developing regularization to mitigate attention imbalance during finetuning, curriculum learning strategies (popular before obscure), and using the conceptual model to guide synthetic data generation.
