## Problem

Existing AI safety frameworks define safe systems as those aligned to human intent, but this paper argues that intent alignment alone is insufficient because it creates an implicit optimization pressure toward human agency loss. No existing approach explicitly decomposes and evaluates AI outputs for their downstream effects on human agency (the capacity to choose and control present and future goals) as a separable optimization objective, leaving a novel harm pathway unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241461/cropped_page6_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The formalization takes as input a set of well-being-promoting goals G_t available to a human, the current cumulative agency state F_t, and the AI's expectation of human evaluation E_AI(H_eval(a_i), I) for each candidate action. It produces an optimal action selection that explicitly balances reward R(a_i) against a multi-agent agency preservation term K^w(F_t, a_i), where per-goal, per-subject penalty parameters ζ_i^j can be set to infinitely negative for agency-depleting action classes. Critically, the agency evaluation is computed by the AI system itself rather than delegated to human evaluators, removing the adversarial dynamic of AI systems competing with human capacity to assess long-term consequences.

## Method

The paper formalizes agency preservation as a Markov Decision Process where agency is defined as cumulative freedom F_t = Σf(g_i,t), with f evaluating each goal's contribution to long-term well-being by combining utility and achievability. A transition function K' penalizes goal or option loss through a scaling function U_i(a) that applies a penalty ζ_i when an action decreases a goal's value, preventing large utility gains from offsetting complete option losses. For multi-agent environments, K^w extends this to evaluate agency effects on all affected individuals j. The optimal action (Eq. 10) explicitly separates the intent-alignment objective E_AI(H_eval(a_i), I) from the agency preservation term K^w_AI(F_AI,t, a_i), ensuring no amount of utility can overcome agency loss. The paper supports this formalization with RL simulations (TD-learning on multi-armed bandits) demonstrating that even perfectly aligned, truthful agents introduce idiosyncratic recommendation biases, and that adding feedback loops between AI recommendations and human value perception accelerates option depletion over time.

## Results

In the first toy RL simulation (10,000 time-steps, TD-learning with lr=0.1), even under ideal conditions (known ground-truth values, no learning required, fully aligned and truthful agent), the AI's recommended policy exhibits idiosyncratic biases: the most 'recommendable' action is 50% more favored than the least, with an average distribution of 23%, 28%, 31%, 18% across four actions versus the human's uniform 25% each. In the second simulation adding a feedback loop (AI nudges human action values by ≤0.1), the intent-aligned agent progressively concentrates recommendations on the highest-probability-reward action, eliminating other options over time. A variant where the AI holds a static (t=0) view of human values partially mitigates polarization but does not prevent option loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against named external baselines; instead it contrasts three interaction paradigms in its simulations. (1) No AI (random external value drift only): human choice distribution remains stable over time, preserving all options. (2) Intent-aligned AI with a feedback loop (agent updates its model of human values each step): the agent's recommendations and value nudges compound, progressively removing lower-probability-reward options and concentrating the human's effective choice space onto a single action. (3) Intent-aligned AI with a static value model (agent only sees t=0 values, as proposed by Kenton et al. for polarizing recommenders): this partially slows polarization but still results in net option loss over the episode. The paper's central conceptual comparison is between intent-aligned optimization (Eq. 9, maximizing expected human evaluation) and the proposed agency-preserving optimization (Eq. 10, adding an explicit K^w agency-preservation term), arguing the former is computationally unsound for finding agency-preserving solutions and will converge on human predictability as a proxy objective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant AI alignment paradigm (intent alignment, RLHF, Constitutional AI) by arguing that satisfying human intent is not merely insufficient but actively adversarial to human agency preservation. It builds on Vamplew et al.'s (2022) multi-objective RL argument that scalar reward is insufficient, extending it to specifically identify agency preservation as a primary, non-tradable objective. The paper draws on neuroscience (sense of agency, readiness potential), philosophy (Petitt's agency/option freedom), and psychology (Self-Determination Theory) to ground its formal MDP-based agency evaluation, positioning itself as complementary to but distinct from related work on polarizing recommenders, deceptive AI, and power-seeking systems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies what its authors frame as a fundamental and overlooked gap in AI safety: that intent-aligned systems converge toward agency loss by design through Goodhart's law, not by accident. However, the formalization remains conceptual (the agency function f(g) and penalty terms ζ are undefined in practice), the simulations are illustrative simple TD-learning bandits rather than rigorous empirical validation, and the proposed 'agency foundations' research programs are aspirational. Key open questions include how to compute the agency function, calibrate penalty magnitudes, and whether the MDP framework is tractable for real-world multi-agent deployment at scale.
