## Problem

Existing fragment-based molecular generative models suffer from two critical limitations: (1) fragment extraction methods either ignore target chemical properties or rely on heuristic rules like frequency-based selection, and (2) the fragment vocabulary is fixed after initial construction, preventing the model from incorporating novel goal-aware fragments discovered during generation. This restricts both the quality of generated molecules and the ability to explore beyond the initial chemical space.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241066/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a molecular dataset (ZINC250k) with continuous property labels in [0,1], a target property function (e.g., normalized docking score × QED × SA), and an initial fragment vocabulary of size K=300 extracted via BRICS fragmentation. It produces generated molecular graphs (up to 40 atoms, starting from benzene) along with a dynamically updated fragment vocabulary capped at L=1000. Fragments are represented as subgraphs with vertex and edge sets, and the vocabulary is updated on-the-fly by extracting up to 50 new fragments per generation cycle from GA offspring molecules.

## Method

GEAM is a three-module generative framework operating in an iterative cycle. The Fragment-wise Graph Information Bottleneck (FGIB) module uses MPNN node embeddings, average pooling for fragment embeddings, and a noise-injection mechanism (following variational GIB) to learn per-fragment importance weights that maximize mutual information with the target property while minimizing information retained from the full graph; fragments are then scored by a size-normalized weighted average of their importance and the molecule property. The Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) module assembles fragments via a three-action policy (choose attachment site on current molecule, select fragment from vocabulary, choose attachment site on fragment) trained with Gumbel-Softmax for discrete actions and multiplicative interactions for heterogeneous input fusion. The Genetic Algorithm (GA) module performs crossover and mutation on the top-P generated molecules to produce offspring containing novel fragments. Crucially, after each GA step, FGIB re-extracts goal-aware fragments from the offspring and dynamically updates the vocabulary, enabling exploration beyond the initial BRICS-derived set. A theoretical proposition bounds the failure probability of generating at least one optimal novel molecule, showing it decreases as the vocabulary shrinks or the set of constructible optimal molecules grows.

## Results

On the ligand generation task (5 protein targets), GEAM achieves a novel hit ratio of 20.7–43.0%, outperforming the best baseline MOOD (0.7–18.7%) by 2–50×. GEAM also attains the best novel top-5% docking scores, e.g., −12.89 kcal/mol on parp1 vs. −10.87 for MOOD. On the PMO benchmark (7 tasks), GEAM achieves an average MPO AUC of 0.615, surpassing the best baseline STONED (0.577), while simultaneously achieving higher novelty (84–98%) and #Circles (257–502) than all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GEAM outperforms all 19 baselines by a large margin on the primary novel hit ratio metric across all five protein targets, with the closest competitor MOOD achieving at most 18.7% (5ht1b) versus GEAM's 38.5%. On novel top-5% docking score, GEAM improves over MOOD by 1.0–2.0 kcal/mol (e.g., −12.89 vs. −10.87 on parp1), and over FREED (the best fragment-based baseline) by 1.3–2.3 kcal/mol. On the PMO benchmark, GEAM achieves the best average AUC (0.615) over STONED (0.577), REINVENT (0.532), and Graph GA (0.566), with the largest gains on Sitagliptin (0.417 vs. 0.351 for STONED) and Zaleplon (0.402 vs. 0.307). GEAM also achieves the highest novelty (84–98%) and #Circles (257–502) on PMO tasks, exceeding STONED's novelty (63–92%) and #Circles (272–330). Notably, GEAM's dynamic vocabulary update (full GEAM vs. GEAM-static) consistently improves novelty and diversity without sacrificing optimization quality, e.g., novelty rises from 84.3% to 88.6% on parp1 while hit ratio improves from 39.7% to 40.6%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established lines of work: graph information bottleneck (GIB) for subgraph recognition, RL-based fragment assembly (FREED), and graph-based genetic algorithms for molecular exploration. The key departure is applying GIB specifically to fragment extraction for drug discovery—prior GIB work targeted classification/regression subgraph recognition, while prior fragment extraction methods (FREED, PS-VAE, MiCaM) relied on frequency or random selection without considering target properties. The dynamic vocabulary update mechanism, where FGIB extracts new goal-aware fragments from GA-generated offspring during generation, has no direct precedent in the fragment-based generation literature, which typically uses a fixed vocabulary throughout.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental bottleneck in fragment-based drug discovery: static, property-agnostic fragment vocabularies that limit both optimization quality and chemical space exploration. The dynamic update mechanism offers a practical recipe for breaking the novelty-diversity-optimization trade-off that plagues stronger generative models. Limitations include reliance on oracle calls (docking scores) for training FGIB and evaluation, training exclusively on ZINC250k, and the added computational overhead of the GA module. Open questions include whether the approach extends to 3D-aware generation, whether the vocabulary converges or grows indefinitely in long generation runs, and whether the GIB-based scoring generalizes to multi-target or multi-property settings beyond the single scalar property used here.
