## Problem

Traditional decision-making paradigms (RL, IL, planning) exhibit low sample efficiency and poor generalization, requiring agents to be trained from scratch for each task. While foundation models in language and vision have demonstrated rapid adaptation to diverse tasks with minimal fine-tuning, no analogous paradigm exists for sequential decision making across physical and virtual worlds. This position paper argues that foundation agents—generally capable agents with unified representations and policy interfaces—will constitute the next paradigm shift for decision making.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes multi-modal interactive trajectories (pixel observations, text instructions, audio) tokenized into sequences with timestep and modality encodings, optionally conditioned on goals, reward signals, or return-to-go values. Outputs include next actions (continuous or discrete), predicted returns and rewards, and in the LLM-aligned setting, generated plans, sub-goals, and reasoning chains. In the case study, inputs are 50-timestep context windows from DMC (image observations, continuous actions) and Atari (pixel observations, discrete actions), with the model predicting actions during pretraining and actions plus returns/rewards during fine-tuning.

## Method

The paper formulates foundation agents around three characteristics—unified representation of state-action-reward variables, a unified policy interface across tasks, and interactive decision-making in physical/virtual worlds—and specifies a three-stage roadmap: (1) large-scale interactive data collection from the internet or generation via video-generation simulators (Sora, Genie); (2) self-supervised pretraining with Transformer architectures using autoregressive or masked objectives (next-action prediction, reward-conditioned prediction, random masking) to learn unified trajectory representations; (3) alignment with LLMs to integrate world knowledge, planning (CoT, ToT, ReAct), memory, and human values. The case study jointly pretrains a 25M-parameter Transformer on 5 DMC tasks and 5 Atari games via next-action prediction, then fine-tunes on unseen tasks by augmenting the objective with return and reward prediction. The core insight is that decision-making can be unified as sequence modeling over trajectories, enabling cross-domain transfer and few-shot adaptation analogous to how LLMs handle diverse linguistic tasks.

## Results

This is a position paper that does not report quantitative performance metrics. The authors conduct a small feasibility case study (Appendix C) in which a 25M-parameter GPT-2-based agent is jointly pretrained on 5 DeepMind Control Suite tasks and 5 Atari games via autoregressive next-action prediction, then fine-tuned on one unseen DMC task (walker walk) and one unseen Atari game (Pong). Fine-tuning performance is shown only through qualitative visualizations (Figure 2) over 50 evaluation episodes; no numerical scores, success rates, or reward values are reported. The authors explicitly state they 'do not report other experimental results and compare with baselines as done in original research.'

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare its method against any baselines. The case study in Appendix C is presented purely as a feasibility demonstration of the proposed foundation-agent roadmap (data collection → self-supervised pretraining → adaptation), and the authors explicitly note that no baseline comparisons are included. The paper's contribution is conceptual and positional rather than empirical: it formulates the notion of foundation agents, specifies a three-stage roadmap (large interactive data, self-supervised pretraining, LLM alignment), and identifies open research questions (unified representation, policy optimization theory, open-ended tasks). Table 2 in the appendix provides computational context (e.g., Gato: 1.18B params, 4 days on 256 TPUv3; their 25M case: 8h on 4 RTX 4090) but does not constitute a performance comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes multiple research threads—generalist agents (Gato, RT-1/RT-2, Unified-IO 2), sequence-modeling approaches to RL (Decision Transformer, Multi-Game Decision Transformer), self-supervised pretraining for control (Yang & Nachum, Wu et al.), and LLM-based agent architectures (Voyager, ReAct, CoT/ToT)—into a unified vision for 'foundation agents' as the decision-making analogue of foundation models in language and vision. Rather than introducing a new algorithm, it carves conceptual ground by proposing a three-stage roadmap (large interactive data → self-supervised pretraining → LLM alignment) and identifying the fundamental characteristics that distinguish foundation agents from both task-specific RL agents and LLM-based linguistic agents. The paper departs from prior work by explicitly framing the gap between the success of foundation models in perception/generation and the absence of an analogous paradigm for sequential decision making, and by pinpointing where the LLM roadmap breaks down for agents (unified representation of heterogeneous state-action spaces, unified policy interface, open-ended interaction).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a useful taxonomy and research agenda for a field that has been advancing in parallel but somewhat fragmented directions (robotics generalist agents, decision transformers, LLM-based planners, video-generation simulators). Key open questions it identifies—establishing theoretical guarantees for policy optimization with pretrained generalist policies, determining whether a unified or compositional architecture is preferable, and handling open-ended tasks without predefined goals—remain largely unsolved and will shape the next several years of research. However, the empirical contribution is minimal (a 25M-parameter case study trained for 8 hours on 4 GPUs), and the paper explicitly acknowledges that hallucination risks from LLM alignment, computational scaling requirements, and the absence of a theoretical framework are significant unresolved challenges. Its value is primarily as a direction-setting reference rather than a methodological contribution.
