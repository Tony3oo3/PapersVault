## Problem

Standard Poisson learning objectives for spike prediction quantize neuronal responses into spike counts within fixed summation intervals (typically 10–100 ms), destroying the millisecond-level temporal precision that retinal ganglion cells exhibit. Shorter intervals make training harder and expose the Poisson distribution's inability to express strong confidence in any single non-zero spike count, while longer intervals blur burst structure. The paper asks whether a summation-free representation that preserves individual spike timing can serve as a more effective learning objective.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 5×992 array representing 1 second of history at 992 Hz (~1 ms/sample): four channels of full-field colour stimulus (420, 480, 505, 630 nm) plus one channel of the neuron's own spike train. The spike distance model outputs a 128-element continuous array of temporal distances (clamped at 200 ms), with t=0 placed at index 32 so the array extends 32 samples into the past and 16 samples beyond the 80-sample prediction window. A discrete spike train is then recovered via an iterative energy-minimization algorithm (Algorithm 1) that starts with spikes at every time step and removes spikes that reduce the L2 mismatch between the model output and the candidate's discrete spike distance. Inference is autoregressive with an 80-sample stride.

## Method

The core contribution is the spike distance function f_S(t) = min_{s∈S}|t−s|, which maps each time point to its temporal distance to the nearest spike, providing a loss signal that changes proportionally with spike position rather than being insensitive to intra-bin placement. The architecture is a shared 12-layer ConvNext CNN base (~302 k parameters, 88% of total) with two different heads: a single fully-connected layer for Poisson models (scalar output) and four upsampling ConvNext blocks for the spike distance model (128-element array output). Training uses MSE between the model output and the log of the target spike distance (spike distance model) or negative log-likelihood under a Poisson distribution (Poisson models), with AdamW and a 1-cycle learning rate schedule over 80 epochs. Spike inference from the distance array is framed as energy minimization: the L2 norm between the model output and the discrete spike distance of a candidate spike sequence is minimized via an iterative removal algorithm that starts with spikes at every time step and removes spikes in order of estimated error reduction, typically converging in one or two iterations.

## Results

On 60 chicken RGCs, the spike distance model outperforms all six Poisson baselines (5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160 ms summation intervals) across all three spike train metrics—Van Rossum distance, Schreiber similarity, and Pearson correlation—over a wide range of smoothing parameters (0–150 ms), reported as interquartile mean. The strongest Poisson baseline is Poisson-80 ms with rounded-mean inference, which occupies a 'sweet spot' where the interval is long enough to avoid the rounding-to-zero effect yet short enough for good temporal resolution. On 113 frog (Xenopus) RGCs, the performance gap narrows but the spike distance model remains the most consistently competitive across all three metrics. At very low smoothing values, Van Rossum distance can favour short-interval Poisson models (which predict few spikes, approaching the zero-spike-train baseline), but above these values the spike distance model is uniformly superior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The spike distance model wins against all six Poisson baselines on all three metrics over the majority of the smoothing parameter range. The Poisson-80 ms model with rounded-mean inference is the closest competitor, but the spike distance model still leads, particularly at moderate-to-high smoothing values where temporal precision matters. Shorter-interval Poisson models (5, 10, 20 ms) degrade severely with rounded-mean inference, approaching the performance of a zero-spike-train baseline as outputs round to zero; with mode-based inference, even Poisson-20 ms and Poisson-10 ms become indistinguishable from the zero-spike baseline. With sampling-based inference, all Poisson models perform more similarly to each other but are still outperformed by the spike distance model, especially in Van Rossum distance. The paper notes that the Poisson-80 ms rounded-mean result suggests the less principled rounding strategy should be seriously considered as a spike count inference method, and that sampling provides a higher lower-bound across intervals at the cost of lower peak performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the concept of signed distance functions from geometric modelling (Zeng et al. 2017, Park et al. 2019) and the 'time-to-failure' framing from earthquake prediction (Rouet-Leduc et al. 2017) into the neuroscience domain, proposing a spike distance function as a summation-free learning objective for the well-established task of spike prediction. It departs from the dominant Poisson-based framework used in GLMs (Weber and Pillow 2017) and deep network approaches (McIntosh et al. 2016, Batty et al. 2016, Gogliettino et al. 2024), which all reduce spike trains to scalar firing rates over fixed intervals. The architecture builds on ConvNext (Liu et al. 2022) as a shared backbone, with the novelty residing entirely in the output representation and loss function rather than in the network design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The spike distance objective addresses a fundamental limitation of Poisson models—the trade-off between temporal resolution and trainability—by preserving spike timing information without binning, which is critical for applications like retinal prosthetics where millisecond precision matters. However, the contribution is scoped: it is validated only on RGCs with a single CNN architecture and per-cell training, and the performance gap narrows notably on frog RGCs. Open questions include whether the approach retains interpretability advantages of GLMs, how it interacts with architecture choices, whether multi-cell training or pretraining on synthetic data can close remaining gaps, and whether the approximate inference algorithm (Algorithm 1) can be replaced by an optimal one for further gains.
