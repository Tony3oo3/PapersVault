## Problem

Existing multi-concept customization methods for text-guided diffusion models only fine-tune the text-to-image decoder stage, leaving the CLIP text encoder untouched. This results in under-trained, overlapping textual embeddings for customized concepts, causing identity loss, attribute leaking, and concept missing when generating compositions of multiple user-provided concepts—especially under spatially cluttered interactions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241206/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 10–20 user-provided images per customized concept (characters with faces, hats, clothes, etc.) and a text prompt naming the concepts to compose. It produces a single RGB image containing all named concepts with complex interactions (e.g., snuggling, riding motorcycle) without requiring additional spatial constraints such as layouts, masks, or sketches. Evaluation uses 18 characters (9 real-world, 4 3D-animated, 5 2D-animated) with 20 single-concept and 10 multi-concept prompts, sampling 50 and 10 images respectively.

## Method

CLIF introduces a two-stage fine-tuning pipeline that addresses confusion at its source. In stage 1, the CLIP text encoder is contrastively fine-tuned on ~50,000 augmented image-text pairs constructed via three strategies: Global Augmentation (SAM-segmented concepts composited onto generated general contexts to push apart inter-concept embeddings), Region Augmentation (GPT-4-captioned sub-regions segmented and labeled to bind attributes within each concept), and Mix Augmentation (randomly paired segmented concepts to counteract dominant bias in the decoder). In stage 2, the text encoder is frozen and the U-net is fine-tuned with a shared LoRA (rank 8) on the mixed-augmentation data, jointly training all 18 concepts. The key insight is that the reconstruction loss in the decoder stage cannot decouple concept embeddings the way CLIP's contrastive loss does in the encoder stage, so explicitly contrasting concept token embeddings against over-segmented regions of other concepts fundamentally eliminates confusion before the decoder ever sees them.

## Results

On the 18-character multi-concept customization benchmark, CLIF achieves 0.599 multi-concept text alignment and 0.761 multi-concept image alignment, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline Mix-of-Show (0.526 and 0.713 respectively). For single-concept generation, CLIF reaches 0.787 image alignment, the highest among all methods. The gap widens in the multi-concept setting: CLIF's multi-concept text alignment exceeds the best baseline by 7.3 percentage points, while its image alignment leads by 4.8 points, demonstrating that contrastive fine-tuning of the text encoder is the key factor in reducing inter-concept confusion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIF outperforms all four main baselines (Text-Inversion, DreamBooth, Custom Diffusion, Mix-of-Show) on every metric in both single- and multi-concept settings. The largest margin is in multi-concept text alignment, where CLIF (0.599) beats Mix-of-Show (0.526) by 7.3 points and Text-Inversion (0.507) by 9.2 points, directly reflecting reduced inter-concept confusion. In multi-concept image alignment, CLIF (0.761) leads Mix-of-Show (0.713) by 4.8 points. The integration experiments (TI+CLIF, Custom+CLIF) show that adding CLIF's contrastive text-encoder fine-tuning to existing methods yields consistent gains (e.g., Custom+CLIF improves multi-concept image alignment from 0.715 to 0.730), confirming the generalizability of the approach. On DreamBench, CLIF also qualitatively outperforms PACGen (Li et al., 2023b) in multi-subject generation, though no quantitative scores are reported for that comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLIF builds on the standard TGDM pipeline (CLIP text encoder + Stable Diffusion decoder) and the cross-attention analysis of Tewel et al. and Patashnik et al., which showed that V-values control 'what to draw.' It departs from all prior customized generation methods (Textual Inversion, DreamBooth, Custom Diffusion, Mix-of-Show) by identifying that the text encoder stage was systematically neglected: existing methods only fine-tune the decoder via reconstruction loss, which cannot decouple concept embeddings. CLIF introduces a CLIP-style contrastive fine-tuning of the text encoder using over-segmented data (SAM + GPT-4), followed by a standard LoRA decoder fine-tuning, effectively treating the two stages as complementary rather than treating the decoder as the sole locus of adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear, actionable diagnosis of why multi-concept customization fails (under-trained, non-contrastive text embeddings) and a simple two-stage fix that is generalizable across decoder fine-tuning strategies (demonstrated with TI and Custom Diffusion). However, the approach is constrained by CLIP's limited capacity for long-text comprehension and the diffusion model's prompt-following ability, capping effective multi-concept generation at roughly 2–3 concepts. Open questions include whether the contrastive text-encoder fine-tuning transfers to video diffusion or 3D generation, and whether the over-segmentation data curation pipeline scales beyond character-like concepts to more abstract or fine-grained visual attributes.
