## Problem

Classical potential-based motion planning suffers from local minima and requires explicit obstacle representations in configuration space, while learned motion planners degrade sharply on out-of-distribution constraint combinations. The paper addresses how to learn trajectory-level potential landscapes that are both easily optimizable (avoiding local minima) and composable, so that new sets of motion constraints can be handled at test time without retraining.

## Input / Output

The planner takes a start state, a goal state, and a set of environment constraints (obstacle locations in workspace for static scenes, or obstacle trajectories for dynamic scenes) and outputs a collision-free trajectory q_{1:T} in configuration space (2D, 7D, or 14D depending on the environment). At inference, 20 candidate trajectories are generated via DDIM denoising (10 steps, eta=0 for base / eta=1 for composite environments) with classifier-free guidance scale 2.0, then a collision check selects a feasible candidate; if none is found, a local motion-refining loop re-denoises only the colliding segments.

## Method

The core contribution is learning a trajectory-level energy function E_θ via an energy-based diffusion model (EBDM) trained with a denoising-diffusion MSE loss, so that the gradient of E_θ acts as a denoiser that recovers valid motion plans from arbitrarily corrupted trajectories. The architecture pairs a U-Net CNN (residual blocks with temporal convolutions, GroupNorm, SiLU) for trajectory denoising with a Transformer encoder (3 layers, 1 head, no positional embedding for permutation invariance) that encodes obstacle constraints; the learned class token is concatenated with the time embedding and fed into the U-Net. At test time, a trajectory is initialized from N(0,I) and iteratively refined over S diffusion steps by following the negative gradient of E_θ with stochastic noise injection. The key differentiator is composability: multiple separately trained potential functions can be additively summed at test time (E_comb = Σ E_θ^i) to handle more obstacles, heterogeneous obstacle types, or mixed static+dynamic constraints without any retraining, and the paper proves probabilistic completeness of the resulting sampler.

## Results

On the three base environments (Maze2D 2D, KUKA 7-DoF, Dual KUKA 14-DoF), the proposed method achieves 100%, 99.7%, and 97.7% success rates respectively, while requiring only 97, 75.5, and 122.4 collision checks on average. Planning time is 0.12s (Maze2D), 0.13s (KUKA), and 0.27s (Dual KUKA), less than 11% of BIT*'s time in Maze2D and roughly 8.8× faster in Dual KUKA. On concave-obstacle Maze2D, the method achieves 100% success in 0.15s, versus 44.4% for the diffusion-based MPD and 28.0% for the classical potential-based RMP. On the real-world ETH/UCY pedestrian dataset, the method achieves ADE of 0.35–5.20 across scenes, substantially lower than MPNet (8.93–28.60) and MπNet (1.14–44.49).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest sampling-based baseline BIT*, the proposed method matches its 100% success in Maze2D while being ~1.7× faster (0.12s vs 0.21s) and using 21× fewer collision checks (97 vs 2067); in Dual KUKA it achieves 97.7% vs BIT*'s 82.1% success with 8.8× less planning time (0.27s vs 2.37s) and 73× fewer checks. Against the best learned baseline MπNet, the method leads by 11.8 points in KUKA (99.7% vs 88.7%) and 33.8 points in Dual KUKA (97.7% vs 63.9%), though MπNet is slightly faster in Maze2D (0.07s vs 0.12s). The classical potential-based RMP is outperformed by a wide margin on concave obstacles (100% vs 28.0% success), and the diffusion-based MPD is similarly dominated (100% vs 44.4%). On dynamic environments, the method achieves 99.5% success in 0.13s versus SIPP's 69.9% in 32.2s. On the real-world ETH/UCY dataset, the method's ADE (0.35–5.20) is 10–50× lower than MPNet and MπNet, demonstrating superior generalization to unseen pedestrian scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical potential-based motion planning paradigm (Khatib, 1986) by replacing hand-crafted attraction/repulsion potentials with learned energy landscapes parameterized by diffusion models. The training objective is directly inherited from energy-based diffusion models (Du et al., 2023), which shape the gradient of an energy function to denoise corrupted trajectories, while the inference procedure follows the standard DDIM sampling framework (Ho et al., 2020). The key departure from prior learned motion planners (MPNet, MπNet, AMP-LS) is the explicit potential formulation, which enables additive composition of separately trained models at test time — a property absent from end-to-end trajectory predictors. The approach also differs from other diffusion-based motion planners (e.g., MPD/Carvalho et al., 2023) by treating the diffusion model as an explicit energy function over trajectories rather than a generative model conditioned on cost functions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The composability property is the most practically significant contribution: by training on a fixed number of obstacles and simply summing potentials at test time, the planner generalizes to environments with more obstacles or novel constraint combinations without retraining, addressing a key failure mode of learned planners on out-of-distribution settings. The probabilistic completeness proof (Section 3.5) provides theoretical grounding that the method will eventually find a valid trajectory, a guarantee not available for most learned planners. However, the paper acknowledges that generated trajectories are often suboptimal (not shortest-path), and the linear scaling of computation with the number of composed models limits practicality for very large constraint sets. Open questions include how to incorporate optimality potentials, how to share features across composed models to reduce inference cost, and whether the approach extends to continuous control or high-DoF manipulation beyond the 14-DoF Dual KUKA setting tested here.
