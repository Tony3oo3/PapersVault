## Problem

Offline-to-online RL fine-tuning faces a fundamental dilemma: remaining pessimistic (as in offline algorithms) leads to slow learning due to insufficient exploration, while switching to optimism causes a sudden performance drop from mismanaging prior knowledge. Existing solutions like balanced replay buffers, ensembling, and value calibration address this empirically but lack a principled theoretical characterization of when and how to balance offline data reuse against online exploration.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-collected offline trajectory dataset (e.g., 1M transitions from D4RL) and an interactive online environment, producing an ensemble of L actor-critic networks (policy and Q-value functions) whose posterior is updated in real time. During the online phase, actions are generated by sampling a policy index from a softmax distribution over the ensemble's Q-values at the current state, rather than selecting a single greedy or optimistic action. The offline dataset is bootstrapped via Bernoulli masking (p=0.9) into L overlapping subsets, each training an independent TD3+BC agent, and the online replay buffer is mixed 50/50 with offline data during updates to ensure smooth posterior shifts.

## Method

BOORL implements approximate posterior sampling (Thompson Sampling) over an ensemble of bootstrapped offline-trained policies. In the offline phase, L independent TD3+BC agents are trained on Bernoulli-masked subsets of the offline dataset, yielding a distribution over policies that approximates the Bayesian posterior while preserving pessimistic guarantees. In the online phase, a categorical distribution p_ℓ ∝ exp(Q_θℓ(s, a_ℓ)) is computed over the ensemble, a policy is sampled to act, and all ensemble members are updated with a 50/50 mix of online and offline minibatches. Theoretically, the paper proves via information-theoretic analysis that probability matching (Thompson Sampling) is the unique strategy satisfying both an Õ(√T) online exploration regret and an Õ(√(C†_β/N)) offline exploitation bound simultaneously, whereas UCB can have unbounded first-step suboptimality and LCB can incur linear regret in the offline-to-online transition.

## Results

On the D4RL benchmark (1M offline steps + 0.2M online fine-tuning), BOORL achieves a total performance improvement δ_sum(0.2M) of 698.1, substantially outperforming Cal-QL (392.0), PEX (326.8), and ODT (146.0). The gains are most dramatic on low-quality (random) datasets: Hopper random improves from 8.8 to 75.7, Walker2d random from 4.8 to 93.6, and Halfcheetah random from 10.7 to 97.7. On Antmaze sparse-reward tasks, BOORL reaches 100.0 on umaze, umaze-diverse, and medium-play, where ODT fails entirely (0.0→0.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BOORL decisively outperforms all offline-to-online baselines on the aggregate δ_sum(0.2M) metric (698.1 vs. Cal-QL 392.0, PEX 326.8, ODT 146.0), with the largest margins on low-quality random datasets where ODT and Cal-QL show minimal improvement (e.g., Hopper random: BOORL +66.9 vs. ODT +20.7 vs. Cal-QL +2.6). Against PEX, BOORL wins on 18 of 21 tasks, with particularly large gaps on Walker2d random (+93.6 vs. +40.7) and Halfcheetah random (+97.7 vs. +61.2). Cal-QL achieves comparable stability on medium/expert datasets but lags significantly on random and Antmaze tasks. Against RLPD (which starts from random initialization rather than offline pre-training), BOORL matches or exceeds final performance on most Mujoco tasks (e.g., Hopper medium: 109.8 vs. 107.3; Walker2d medium-replay: 114.4 vs. 115.0) while achieving these results from a strong offline starting point. On Antmaze, BOORL reaches 100.0 on medium-play where PEX (90.8) and Cal-QL (91.9) fall short, and ODT fails entirely (0.0). The ablation in Table 3 further confirms that the Bayesian posterior-sampling mechanism (not just the hybrid data mixing) drives the gains, as the 'Bayesian' variant without offline data in the online phase still outperforms the 'Hybrid RL' baseline on most tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian RL lineage (Osband et al., 2016; Osband & Van Roy, 2017) and the information-theoretic analysis of Thompson sampling (Russo & Van Roy, 2016) to the offline-to-online transition, a setting where prior work has relied on ad hoc heuristics like balanced replay buffers, ensembling, and value calibration. The key conceptual departure is from the optimistic (UCB) and pessimistic (LCB) doctrines that dominate online and offline RL respectively: the paper shows that a 'realist' probability-matching strategy is the principled solution when both offline data and online interaction are available. Practically, it builds on bootstrapped DQN (Osband et al., 2016) for approximate posterior sampling and on TD3+BC/TD3 as the actor-critic backbone, making the approach a lightweight wrapper around existing offline RL methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first information-theoretic characterization that unifies online and offline performance through the agent's gained information, and proves that Thompson sampling is the only strategy simultaneously enjoying the √T online regret bound and the √(C/N) offline suboptimality bound. This gives a principled answer to a dilemma that prior off-to-on methods (balanced replay, ensembling, calibration) addressed only empirically. Open questions include whether the linear MDP regret bound extends to general function approximation, whether more expressive Bayesian methods (e.g., Gaussian processes, neural posterior estimation) can tighten the practical gap, and whether the 50/50 offline-online data reweighting is optimal or task-dependent.
