## Problem

Most probabilistic neurosymbolic learning systems assume that symbol probabilities are conditionally independent given the input, which simplifies inference but has never been formally analysed for its consequences. This paper asks: what does the independence assumption actually lose in terms of representable uncertainty, and how does it affect the geometry of the resulting loss landscape? The authors show it systematically biases models toward deterministic predictions and produces non-convex, disconnected minima that are hard to optimise.

## Input / Output

The perception model p_θ(w|x) takes a high-dimensional input x (e.g., a pair of MNIST digit images) and a boolean constraint φ over n binary variables, and outputs a probability distribution over the 2^n possible worlds w ∈ {0,1}^n. Under the independence assumption, this reduces to predicting n marginal probabilities in [0,1]^n rather than 2^n world probabilities. The key analytical output is a cubical-set representation C_φ in [0,1]^n that exactly characterises which independent distributions satisfy φ, enabling topological analysis (convexity, connectedness, homology) of the feasible parameter space.

## Method

The paper develops a geometric characterisation of the set of possible independent distributions Δ_φ^⊥ using tools from boolean logic and computational homology. It proves that an independent distribution minimises the semantic loss if and only if its deterministic assignment is an implicant of φ (Theorem 4.3), and that the full set of such distributions forms a cubical set C_φ whose facets are the prime implicant cubes (Theorem 4.10). From this representation, the authors derive exact conditions for convexity (only when φ has a single prime implicant, Theorem 4.11) and connectedness (when the prime implicant graph over possible worlds is connected, Theorem 4.13), showing both properties are rare in practice. They further prove that conditioning an independent distribution on φ is generally not representable by another independent distribution (Theorem 4.4), and analyse mixtures of k independent components as a path toward recovering expressivity, showing k ≥ 2 suffices for connectedness but the number of components needed to cover all possible worlds can be exponential.

## Results

The paper's main contributions are theoretical: it proves that the conditional independence assumption biases neurosymbolic models toward deterministic solutions (Theorem 3.1), that the semantic loss over independent distributions is non-convex unless the constraint has exactly one prime implicant (Theorem 4.11), and that the minima are disconnected unless the prime implicant graph is connected (Theorem 4.13). Empirically, on the traffic light problem, independent distributions converge to the predicted union of two line segments (as per Theorem 4.10), while expressive (softmax) distributions spread more uniformly over the valid region but remain biased toward vertices. Adding entropy regularisation (α=0.01) to the expressive model nearly recovers the uniform distribution over valid worlds, whereas for the independent model it merely shifts the minimum toward the (¬r, ¬g) vertex without improving calibration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without standard benchmark comparisons, so there are no head-to-head accuracy tables. The empirical comparison is between independent (2-parameter sigmoid) and expressive (4-parameter softmax) parameterisations on the traffic light problem, where the independent model's minima are confined to two line segments while the expressive model covers the full valid triangle. The paper's theory explains why recent expressive methods (pseudo-semantic loss of Ahmed et al. 2023, NeuPSL of Pryor et al. 2023, and BEARS of Marconato et al. 2024) outperform independence-based systems: they can represent uncertainty over multiple valid worlds that independent distributions provably cannot. The authors also show that a mixture of just two independent components restores connectedness of the minima (Proposition D.2), but that covering all possible worlds may require exponentially many components (Theorem D.4), as in the MNIST Addition task where the minimal prime implicant cover equals the number of possible worlds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides a theoretical critique of the conditional independence assumption that underpins most probabilistic neurosymbolic learning (PNL) systems, including semantic loss (Xu et al., 2018), DeepProbLog (Manhaeve et al., 2018), A-NeSI (van Krieken et al., 2023), and Scallop (Huang et al., 2021). Rather than proposing a new method, it repurposes tools from mathematical logic (implicants, Quine-McCluskey) and computational homology (cubical sets, Kaczynski et al., 2004) to rigorously characterize what independent distributions can and cannot represent under logical constraints. It provides theoretical justification for recent empirical findings (Ahmed et al., 2022a, 2023; Pryor et al., 2023) showing that expressive perception models outperform independent ones, and connects to fuzzy logic methods (Serafini and Garcez, 2016; Badreddine et al., 2022) which share a similar biasing effect.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that the independence assumption is not merely a tractability trade-off but fundamentally prevents models from expressing uncertainty over multiple valid worlds and creates non-convex, disconnected loss landscapes that are hard to optimize. This provides a clear theoretical rationale for the growing body of work on expressive PNL methods (NeuPSL, pseudo-semantic loss, BEARS, mixtures of independents). Key open questions remain: how to perform tractable inference without independence, the expressivity-tractability trade-off (the appendix shows mixtures may need exponentially many components for tasks like MNIST Addition), and whether the homological structure of the constraint space can be exploited for better optimization. The connection to reasoning shortcuts (Marconato et al., 2023b) suggests independent models may be particularly prone to converging on spurious solutions.
