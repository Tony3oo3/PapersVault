## Problem

In singular statistical models where the Fisher information matrix is degenerate at the true parameter, fixed step-size gradient descent requires a polynomial number of iterations in the sample size n to reach the final statistical radius, yielding suboptimal computational complexity. While second-order methods like Newton's method and BFGS incorporate curvature information, their statistical rates in parametric statistical models had not been rigorously established, leaving open whether a method could simultaneously achieve provable statistical optimality and computational efficiency at a reasonable per-iteration cost.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes i.i.d. samples from a parametric statistical model (GLM or GMM), an initial parameter estimate within a local ball around the true parameter, and at each iterate computes the gradient and the maximum eigenvalue of the Hessian of the empirical loss function. The maximum eigenvalue is obtained via power iteration in O(d²) time rather than a full O(d³) eigendecomposition, which is sufficient because the analysis only requires approximation within the statistical noise level. The output is a parameter estimate accompanied by a high-probability statistical error bound of the form O((d/n)^{1/(α−γ)}) after O(log(n/d)) iterations.

## Method

The paper introduces Normalized Gradient Descent (NormGD), which scales the gradient step by the reciprocal of the maximum eigenvalue of the empirical Hessian, yielding a per-iteration cost of O(nd + d²). The key theoretical insight is that under a homogeneous population loss function—where all curvature directions scale as ‖θ−θ⋆‖^α—the normalized population operator contracts linearly, and a population-to-sample decomposition (splitting error into a stability term from uniform Hessian concentration and an optimization term from the contraction) yields logarithmic iteration complexity. The stability assumption (W.2) controls the Hessian noise as O(r^γ (d/n)^{1/2}), and the condition α ≥ γ+1 ensures the signal dominates the noise. For GLMs with link g(r)=r^p in the low-SNR regime, NormGD reaches the optimal statistical radius O((d/n)^{1/(2p)}) in O(log(n/d)) iterations, matching Newton's method statistically while being cheaper than both Newton (O(nd+d³) per iteration) and fixed-step GD (polynomial iterations), and outperforming BFGS which achieves only a suboptimal O((d/n)^{1/(2p+2)}) rate in the multivariate case.

## Results

For the low-SNR GLM with link function g(r)=r^p and θ*=0, NormGD achieves the optimal statistical rate (d/n)^(1/2p) after only O(log(n/d)) iterations, yielding a total computational complexity of O((nd+d²)log(n/d)). This is strictly better than fixed-step-size GD, which requires O((n/d)^((p-1)/p)) iterations and O(n^((2p-1)/p)·d^(1/p)) total complexity to reach the same statistical radius. For the symmetric two-component GMM in the low-SNR regime (θ*=0), NormGD reaches the statistical radius (d/n)^(1/4) after O(log n) iterations with O((nd+d²)log n) complexity, compared to O(n^(3/2)·d^(-1/2)) for EM. Empirical experiments confirm the predicted n^(-0.25) scaling of statistical error in the low-SNR regime (p=2) and linear convergence of all second-order methods versus sublinear convergence of GD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NormGD dominates fixed-step-size GD on computational complexity: for the low-SNR GLM, GD requires O((n/d)^((p-1)/p)) iterations (polynomial in n) while NormGD needs only O(log(n/d)) iterations, reducing total complexity from O(n^((2p-1)/p)·d^(1/p)) to O((nd+d²)log(n/d)). Against Newton's method, NormGD matches the optimal statistical rate (d/n)^(1/2p) in the multivariate setting (where Newton's guarantee is only established for d=1) while reducing per-iteration cost from O(nd+d³) to O(nd+d²) by using power iteration for the max eigenvalue instead of full Hessian inversion. Against BFGS, NormGD achieves the same O((nd+d²)log(n/d)) computational complexity but attains the optimal statistical rate (d/n)^(1/2p) versus BFGS's suboptimal (d/n)^(1/(2p+2)) in the multivariate low-SNR GLM. For the GMM, NormGD matches Newton's method in statistical rate (d/n)^(1/4) and iteration count (log n) but is cheaper per iteration (O(nd+d²) vs. O(nd+d³)), and substantially outperforms EM, which requires O((n/d)^(1/2)) iterations to reach the same radius. Empirically, in the low-SNR regime all second-order methods (Newton, BFGS, NormGD) converge linearly while GD converges sublinearly, and the statistical error scales as n^(-0.25) as predicted by theory for p=2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the population-to-sample analysis framework established by Balakrishnan et al. (2017) and Ho et al. (2020) for studying optimization algorithms in statistical models, and builds on the homogeneous loss function assumption (W.1) previously used by Ren et al. (2022) for Polyak step-size GD. The key departure is introducing a minimal second-order correction—scaling the GD step size by the maximum Hessian eigenvalue—into the statistical analysis, filling a gap the authors identify: while curvature-based optimization methods are well-studied in pure optimization, their statistical rates in parametric models had not been rigorously characterized. The paper positions NormGD as a middle ground between first-order GD (cheap but polynomial iterations in singular models) and full Newton/BFGS (expensive per iteration, with unclear statistical optimality in the multivariate case).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a single scalar (the max Hessian eigenvalue, computable in O(d²) via power iteration) suffices to convert polynomial iteration complexity into logarithmic complexity for singular statistical models is practically significant: it offers a drop-in modification to GD with modest per-iteration overhead that provably matches Newton's statistical accuracy at lower cost. However, the theory is restricted to homogeneous loss functions (all directions behave similarly), and the authors explicitly note that inhomogeneous settings—which include many important models—remain open even for Newton's method and BFGS. The empirical validation is limited to GLM and GMM with small dimensions, and the question of whether the trace of the Hessian (even cheaper to compute) could serve as an alternative scaling factor is left as future work.
