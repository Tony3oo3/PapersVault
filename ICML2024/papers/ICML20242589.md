## Problem

Prediction markets and traditional betting markets have been studied in largely separate literatures, with no unified model explaining how the bookmaker's profit-maximisation objective interacts with the information-elicitation objective. This paper asks whether the two goals are compatible, provides a theoretical model for setting the book under Kelly bettors, and shows that profit and prediction are fundamentally in tension: the bookmaker's optimal price necessarily deviates from their own belief, and heavier tails in the bettor belief distribution yield strictly higher profit.

## Input / Output

The bookmaker observes each bettor's wager amount v_t and side (A or B) sequentially, along with a wealth estimate W_t (e.g., from the bettor's online wallet) and a fixed belief estimate g of the event probability. From these, the bookmaker inverts the Kelly betting rule to obtain an unbiased point estimate p̂_t of the bettor's belief, which may fall outside [0,1] and is optionally clipped. The output is a price pair (a_{t+1}, b_{t+1}) updated after every bet, with the constraint a+b≥1 (unfair odds) or a+b=1 (fair odds), forming a price sequence that asymptotically maximises the bookmaker's expected profit.

## Method

The paper introduces two online pricing algorithms. Algorithm 1 (unfair odds) reformulates the first-order optimality conditions of the non-concave profit function as a stochastic root-finding problem and applies a Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation with learning rate η_t = γ/(t+m), updating only the price on the side the current bettor chose; it converges almost surely to a local maximiser and achieves O(√T) stochastic regret against the worst local maximum. Algorithm 2 (fair odds) exploits the closed-form optimal price a* = ψ(E[p_t]) = √(gp)/(√(gp)+√((1−g)(1−p))) and runs a Follow-the-Leader update on a clipped cumulative average of estimated bettor beliefs, converging to the global optimum with O(√T log T) high-probability regret. A key theoretical contribution is showing that second-order stochastic dominance across bettor belief distributions implies a strict ordering of bookmaker profit, formalising the intuition that polarised (heavy-tailed) beliefs are more exploitable.

## Results

Under unfair odds, Algorithm 1 (Stochastic Approximation) maintains regret ≤ 10² across all four initialisations over 100,000 Kelly bettors, while the risk-balancing baseline's regret exceeds 10³ and continues to grow (log-scale y-axis). Under fair odds, Algorithm 2 (Follow the Leader) keeps regret ≤ 10¹ throughout, whereas LMSR regret grows noticeably faster. Theoretically, Algorithm 1 achieves O(√T) stochastic regret against the worst local maximum, and Algorithm 2 achieves O(√T log T) regret with high probability against the global maximum.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its two algorithms against two established baselines. Against the risk-balancing heuristic (Levitt, 2004), which equalises dollars wagered on each outcome, Algorithm 1 (SA) achieves regret ≤ 10² while risk-balancing exceeds 10³ and keeps increasing — a gap of more than an order of magnitude that widens over time. Against LMSR (Hanson, 2007), the standard automated market maker for prediction markets, Algorithm 2 (FTL) maintains regret ≤ 10¹ while LMSR regret grows faster. Beyond regret, the price trajectories reveal a qualitative difference: LMSR converges to the crowd's average belief, whereas FTL converges to a point strictly between the bookmaker's belief g and the crowd average, reflecting the bookmaker's exploitation of bettor bias for profit. Both algorithms are robust to different initialisations, with all SA trajectories converging to the global maximiser when it is unique.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate literatures—prediction markets (LMSR, automated market makers via mirror descent) and betting markets (Kelly criterion, empirical bookmaker strategies à la Levitt 2004)—under a single binary-outcome model with a Stackelberg game structure. For the unfair-odds case, it adapts Robbins–Monro stochastic approximation to a non-convex root-finding problem derived from the first-order optimality conditions; for the fair-odds case, it exploits the closed-form concave solution to deploy a Follow-the-Leader algorithm from Hazan et al. (2016). The key departure from prior work is the explicit demonstration that the bookmaker's profit-maximising objective and the prediction market's information-elicitation objective are fundamentally incompatible, a tension noted informally by Chen & Pennock (2007) but never formalised in the betting-market setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal characterisation of bookmaker profit under unfair odds, including a CVaR-based lower bound and a second-order stochastic dominance ordering showing that heavier-tailed bettor belief distributions yield strictly higher profit. The online algorithms (SA for unfair odds, FTL for fair odds) are, to the authors' knowledge, the first implementable online pricing strategies for profit-oriented bookmakers, moving beyond the traditional infrequent price updates. Open questions include extensions to non-binary markets, non-Kelly or non-i.i.d. bettor behaviours, and a principled trade-off between profit and information elicitation—areas the authors flag as future work. The broader implication is that the profit-versus-prediction tension may illuminate platform power in other large-scale multi-agent systems (social media, attention markets).
