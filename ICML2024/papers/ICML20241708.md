## Problem

Concept-based explainability methods (CBMs, CEMs, TCAV) predict concepts independently, yet real-world concepts are interrelated (e.g., 'grey wings' co-occur with 'grey tails'). No prior work had examined whether the concept representations learned by these models actually capture known inter-concept relationships, leaving it unclear whether the rich structure of concept co-occurrence is preserved in the learned embeddings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241708/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis pipeline takes RGB images with binary concept annotations (e.g., 112 bird attributes in CUB, 13 chest radiograph findings in CheXpert, digit-colour pairs in Coloured MNIST) and extracts concept basis vectors from each model (TCAV activation vectors, CEM mean embeddings, Concept2Vec skipgram embeddings, or label co-occurrence vectors). It outputs four scalar metrics—stability, robustness, responsiveness, and faithfulness—computed via a t-nearest-neighbor overlap distance δ_b between concept bases. The intervention algorithm additionally takes r expert-corrected concept values and a concept basis, and outputs a predicted task label by imputing the remaining k−r concepts through nearest-neighbor concept vector similarity.

## Method

The paper introduces a distance metric δ_b between concept bases defined as 1 minus the average overlap of t-nearest-neighbor concept sets, and uses it to quantify four desiderata: stability (across random seeds), robustness (under small perturbations), responsiveness (under large corruptions), and faithfulness (alignment with Shapley-value-derived task-importance vectors). Empirically, TCAV and CEM bases exhibit low stability and robustness on CUB and dSprites, and fail to recover even perfectly correlated digit-colour pairs in Coloured MNIST (87% agreement vs. 100% for label and Concept2Vec bases). The paper then proposes a Basis-Aided Concept Intervention algorithm that, for each un-intervened concept j, averages the expert-corrected values of its q most similar intervened concepts (measured by concept vector distance δ_v) to impute ĉ_j, falling back to the concept predictor when no similar intervened concepts exist. Two theorems provide theoretical grounding: Theorem 6.1 shows that label-vector dot products concentrate around true co-occurrence probabilities with a sample complexity of O(ε⁻² log k²), and Theorem 6.2 bounds the regret from Gaussian noise in the co-occurrence matrix by a function of the matrix's own structure, establishing that well-calibrated bases yield accurate concept imputation.

## Results

On CUB, the Label basis achieves 0.73 faithfulness, 0.97 robustness, and 1.00 stability, while TCAV and CEM drop to 0.08/0.06 faithfulness and 0.01/0.03 stability respectively. On Coloured MNIST, CEM concept bases fail to recover digit-colour pairs for 13% of concepts (87% agreement) versus 100% for TCAV, Concept2Vec, and Label bases. For concept intervention on CUB, Label bases improve accuracy over the no-basis baseline, while TCAV and CEM bases hurt accuracy by up to 10%. Across all four datasets, the Label basis consistently outperforms TCAV and CEM on faithfulness, robustness, and stability, confirming that state-of-the-art concept-based models fail to capture known inter-concept relationships.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three learned concept basis methods (TCAV, CEM, Concept2Vec) against a Label basis ground-truth baseline across four datasets. TCAV and CEM lose decisively to the Label basis on CUB, where faithfulness drops from 0.73 (Label) to 0.08 (TCAV) and 0.06 (CEM), and stability collapses to 0.01 and 0.03 respectively. On Coloured MNIST, CEM is the only method that fails to recover all digit-colour pairs (87% vs 100% for TCAV, Concept2Vec, and Label), revealing instability even in a simple controlled setting. Concept2Vec performs better than TCAV and CEM on most metrics (e.g., 0.41 faithfulness on CUB vs 0.08/0.06) but still trails the Label basis substantially. For the downstream concept intervention task, Label bases improve accuracy over the no-basis baseline on CUB, MNIST, and dSprites, while TCAV and CEM bases actively hurt intervention accuracy by up to 10% on CUB, demonstrating that poorly calibrated representations are worse than no representation at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the concept-based explainability literature (CBMs, CEMs, TCAV) by introducing a 'concept basis' framework to analyze the geometric structure of learned concept representations, specifically whether they capture inter-concept relationships. Prior work on analyzing concept-based models focused on concept-task leakage and predictor robustness, but the authors explicitly position inter-concept relationship analysis as 'a previously unstudied aspect.' The Concept2Vec method repurposes the Skipgram architecture from word embeddings to learn concept representations from co-occurrence patterns, and the intervention algorithm builds directly on the CBM test-time correction paradigm while adding a basis-aided imputation step grounded in concentration-inequality theorems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SOTA concept-based models (CEM, TCAV) fail to capture known inter-concept relationships challenges a reasonable assumption that training on correlated concepts should naturally encode those correlations in representations, and opens a concrete research direction for improving concept-based model architectures. The practical implication—that a simple label-basis imputation can improve concept intervention accuracy without additional training—offers a low-cost deployment strategy for human-AI teaming. Limitations include evaluation on only four image datasets, analysis restricted to CEM and TCAV (excluding newer variants like Probabilistic CBMs or Label-Free CBMs), and inconclusive CheXpert results, leaving open whether the findings generalize to text, sequence, or more recent model families.
