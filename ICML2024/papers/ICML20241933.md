## Problem

The LLM preference fine-tuning literature presents contradictory empirical findings: some studies show online RL is essential, while others find offline contrastive or supervised methods sufficient. This leaves practitioners without a clear understanding of which components—on-policy sampling, negative gradients, or sample reuse—actually drive performance, and under what geometric conditions each matters.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241933/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis framework takes a reference SFT policy π_ref, a preference dataset of (prompt, preferred response, dispreferred response) triples, and optionally a reward model trained on that data, and produces a fine-tuned policy π_θ. The key experimental variable is the geometric alignment between the reward function's peak and the reference policy's high-likelihood regions: when these are misaligned (e.g., shorter responses are preferred but the reference policy generates long ones), on-policy sampling and negative gradients become critical, whereas when aligned, offline supervised methods suffice. Experiments span didactic N-dimensional bandits, synthetic length-based LLM tasks, and full-scale AlpacaFarm/UltraFeedback benchmarks with Pythia-1.4B and Mistral-7B.

## Method

The paper introduces a unified algorithmic framework that parameterizes fine-tuning methods along three axes—on-policy sampling, sample reuse, and negative gradient—and systematically varies each to isolate their individual and joint contributions. The central theoretical insight unifies on-policy RL and contrastive objectives (DPO, IPO) under a 'mode-seeking' characterization (equivalent to optimizing reverse KL against the optimal policy), while supervised and weighted-likelihood methods (Pref-FT, RWR, Best-of-N) are 'mode-covering' (forward KL). A key theorem formalizes that reverse KL modifies probability mass across categorical bins more aggressively than forward KL, enabling faster relocation of mass to a subset of high-reward responses. Empirically, on-policy DPO (sampling from the current policy, ranking by reward, then applying contrastive loss) achieves the best performance and wall-clock efficiency, outperforming both offline DPO and on-policy PPO, particularly when the reward peak lies in low-likelihood regions of the reference policy.

## Results

On the didactic bandit problem (R1), on-policy DPO/IPO achieves the highest reward of 0.92 (±0.01) in just 0.12 hours, matching on-policy PPO (0.92) but 14× faster than offline DPO/IPO (0.82, 1.7 hours). On the Skew Length synthetic LLM task, on-policy DPO/IPO reaches a completion length of 0.0 (±0.0) in 0.4 hours, while offline DPO/IPO flatlines at 11.8 (±14.0) and on-policy PPO/RWR both reach only 15.8 in 7–15 hours. The key finding is that on-policy contrastive training (on-policy DPO) dominates both on-policy RL (PPO, REINFORCE) and offline contrastive (DPO, IPO) in both effectiveness and wall-clock time when the reward peak lies in low-likelihood regions of the reference policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Offline DPO/IPO outperforms Pref-FT (supervised fine-tuning on preferred responses) in the Min Length and Skew Length settings where the reward peak is misaligned with the reference policy, but both perform similarly in the Mode Length setting where the reward peak aligns with the reference mode. On-policy DPO/IPO outperforms offline DPO/IPO on Skew Length (completion length 0.0 vs. 11.8) and is 3–14× faster in wall-clock time across all settings. On-policy PPO achieves competitive reward (0.92 on Bandit R1) but is 8× slower than on-policy DPO (0.93 h vs. 0.12 h) due to the sampling bottleneck, and is more robust to sample reuse (T>1 gradient steps) than REINFORCE or Best-of-N. On-policy RWR and REINFORCE both underperform on-policy DPO in the synthetic LLM settings, with RWR reaching only 65.5 completion length on Min Length versus 1.0 for on-policy DPO. The paper's central empirical claim is that on-policy sampling and negative gradient are complementary: on-policy DPO (combining both) achieves the best performance across all misaligned-reward scenarios, while in aligned-reward scenarios (Mode Length, R2) all methods perform similarly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate families of preference fine-tuning methods—on-policy RL (PPO, REINFORCE), contrastive objectives (DPO, IPO), and supervised/weighted-likelihood approaches (RWR, ReST, Best-of-N, Pref-FT)—under a single conceptual lens of mode-seeking versus mode-covering objectives for categorical distributions. It builds on the KL-divergence analysis from Rafailov et al. (2023) and the mode-seeking distillation framework of Agarwal et al. (2023), but extends these to explain *why* on-policy sampling and negative gradients outperform offline supervised objectives, rather than merely showing they do. The paper departs from prior empirical comparisons (e.g., Dubois et al. 2024, Xu et al. 2024) by providing geometric conditions (alignment of reward peak with reference policy, data coverage) that predict when each approach is necessary, and by showing that on-policy sampling and negative gradient are complementary rather than redundant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper resolves substantial practitioner confusion about whether RL is needed for preference fine-tuning by showing that the critical ingredients are on-policy sampling and negative gradient, which can be combined in a simple on-policy DPO recipe that outperforms both offline DPO and on-policy PPO in both quality and wall-clock time. The mode-seeking/mode-covering framework provides a reusable conceptual tool for understanding optimization dynamics in any setting where probability mass must be redistributed across categories. Key limitations include the absence of rigorous statistical guarantees for the negative gradient mechanism, a simplified coverage analysis that ignores pre-training distribution effects, and no study of reward model quality. Open questions include formalizing negative gradient as a variance-reduction mechanism, extending the analysis to minimax formulations (Nash Learning, self-rewarding LMs), and incorporating the full pre-training distribution into coverage conditions.
