## Problem

Existing causal discovery theory assumes hard interventions under infinite samples, where a single intervention on a node instantly reveals all incident edge orientations. This overlooks the stochasticity of real-world finite-sample settings, where multiple interventional samples are needed to orient a single edge with high probability. The paper addresses the missing 'update step': a framework that correctly orients edges from sequential interventional data with anytime-valid error control, while also encoding the logical structure of Meek rules to propagate evidence across edges.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph skeleton (undirected edges between cause-effect pairs), the observational distribution, and a sequence of single-node soft interventions, each yielding one joint sample from the interventional distribution. It outputs a sequence of partially oriented DAGs (Ĝ_t) where every oriented edge is correct with probability at least 1−α at all time steps simultaneously (anytime validity). The setting assumes a linear Gaussian SCM with sub-Gaussian exogenous noise and a minimal causal effect bound b, and the graph skeleton may contain spurious edges in the robustness variant.

## Method

The key insight is that edge orientation is structured, anytime hypothesis testing: each unoriented edge corresponds to two competing null hypotheses, and Meek rules impose logical implications (conjunctions/disjunctions) among them. The authors construct per-edge e-processes from sequential e-values derived from interventional data (exploiting mean-shift detection under soft interventions), then combine these e-processes across edges implied by Meek rules using a tree-based enumeration algorithm and a weighted product construction that preserves the e-process property. For causal verification (known graph), the problem reduces to multi-constraint bandit optimization, where each constraint corresponds to a path in the Meek-rule implication tree, and a greedy weighted-gain algorithm (Algorithm 3) achieves near-optimal regret bounds on the number of interventions needed.

## Results

The anytime-valid e-process framework achieves miscoverage rate below the target α across all tested settings (ER graphs with n∈{10,20,30}, p∈{0.3,0.5}, α∈{0.1,0.2}, edge strength k∈{0.1,0.2,1,2,10}, and 100–10,000 interventional samples), validating the theoretical guarantee. In contrast, the fixed-time z-test baseline exhibits substantially elevated miscoverage rates in many settings, particularly at lower sample sizes and weaker edge strengths. For the causal verification problem, Algorithm 3 (multi-constraint bandit) achieves a regret bound of Õ(M/b + √((b+M)M/b))·OPT + Õ(M√T/b + nM) relative to the optimal intervention policy. Combined e-values (leveraging Meek rule propagation) orient more edges than base e-values in large data/graph regimes, while base e-values remain competitive in small-data settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two axes: (1) anytime-valid e-process testing vs. a fixed-time two-sided z-test with union-bound threshold, and (2) combined e-values (encoding Meek rule propagation) vs. base e-values (single-edge testing only). On the first axis, the anytime test consistently maintains miscoverage below α, while the fixed-time z-test frequently exceeds α, particularly at low sample counts (100–500) and weak edge strengths (k=0.1, 0.2). The trade-off is that the anytime test orients fewer edges at any given sample budget, reflecting its more conservative error control. On the second axis, combined e-values outperform base e-values in orienting edges on chain graphs with n∈{5,10,20,50} nodes when sample sizes are large (≥1000), but base e-values are more effective in small-sample regimes (≤500), likely because the combined statistic has higher variance. Both approaches achieve zero miscoverage in the chain graph experiments, confirming that the power gain from propagation does not compromise correctness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper addresses a gap identified in the finite-sample causal discovery literature (Greenewald et al., 2019): while prior work on causal discovery with provable guarantees (Eberhardt, Hyttinen, Hu, Shanmugam, Kocaoglu, Ghassami, Lindgren, Choo) focused on the 'query step' under idealized hard interventions with infinite samples, the 'update step'—deciding which edges to orient given finite interventional data—remained unaddressed. The paper draws on the e-process/sequential testing framework (Ramdas et al., 2023; Ville, 1939) to construct anytime-valid test statistics for edge orientation, and encodes Meek rule propagation (Meek, 2013) into the test-statistic combination. For the causal verification algorithm, it establishes a reduction to multi-constraint bandit optimization (Badanidiyuru et al., 2018), connecting two previously separate literatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This framework provides the missing infrastructure for finite-sample causal discovery: without a correct update step, one cannot even measure the performance of query strategies in realistic settings. The anytime-valid guarantee is essential because the number of samples needed per edge depends on unknown edge strength, making fixed-time tests inapplicable. Open questions include extending beyond the linear-Gaussian/soft-intervention setting, handling the full discovery problem (not just verification), and understanding how the base-vs-combined e-value tradeoff scales with graph complexity. The multi-constraint bandit reduction for verification is a stepping stone, but a full discovery algorithm with matching guarantees remains to be developed.
