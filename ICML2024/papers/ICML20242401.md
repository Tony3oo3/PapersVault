## Problem

Data augmentation for unsupervised contrastive learning faces a fundamental tension: hand-designed methods require domain-specific expertise and can inadvertently alter semantics (e.g., a single nucleotide mutation in DNA), while generative model-based approaches (VAE, GAN, diffusion) typically demand supervised labels or large-scale external data. This makes effective augmentation infeasible for domains like genomics and bio-features where data is expensive, labels are unavailable, and semantic structure is opaque to humans.

## Input / Output

The method accepts raw data in three modalities: variable-length DNA nucleotide sequences (median 200–500 bp), RGB images (32×32 to 96×96 pixels), and fixed-length bio-feature vectors (500–1374 dimensions representing gene/protein abundances). It produces two outputs: (1) augmented positive samples in the original input space (new sequences, images, or vectors) generated by the conditional diffusion model, and (2) discriminative latent embeddings (both high-dimensional y and low-dimensional z) from the semantic encoder, which serve as the representation for downstream linear probing or clustering. The diffusion generator is conditioned on the encoder's z-vector, so the augmentation is semantically anchored to the input without requiring any label or domain rule.

## Method

DiffAug couples a semantic encoder Enc(·|θ) with a conditional DDPM-based diffusion generator Gen(·|φ), trained alternately in an EM-like A-Step/B-Step schedule. In the A-Step, the encoder is updated with a soft contrastive learning (SCL) loss over a background set containing negative samples drawn from the dataset and positive samples generated by the (frozen) diffusion model conditioned on the encoder's own latent vector z_c. In the B-Step, the diffusion generator is updated with the standard DDPM denoising loss, conditioned on the (frozen) encoder output, learning to produce semantically consistent variants of the input. A hyperparameter λ controls the fraction of traditional hand-designed augmentation replaced by diffusion-generated samples, preventing training instability from an untrained generator. The key design insight is that the encoder's latent representation acts as a self-supervised semantic guide for the diffusion model, eliminating the need for labels, external data, or domain-specific augmentation rules while ensuring generated positives remain semantically coherent.

## Results

On vision datasets, DiffAug achieves 93.4% linear probing accuracy on CIFAR-10, 69.9% on CIFAR-100, 92.5% on STL-10, and 49.7% on TinyImageNet, outperforming all baselines by at least 0.8%. On DNA sequence benchmarks (Genomic Benchmarks), DiffAug reaches 89.1% average accuracy, surpassing the strongest DNA-specific method Hyena (86.2%) by 2.9 points. On bio-feature datasets, DiffAug achieves 82.4% average linear SVM accuracy, exceeding the best contrastive baseline DLME+Mixup (78.5%) by 3.9 points and the best dimensionality reduction method hNNE (73.4%) by 9.0 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffAug consistently outperforms all 15 baselines across all three domains. On vision, it beats the strongest standard CL method (BYOL: 92.0/62.7/91.8/46.1) by 1.4/7.2/0.7/3.6 points on CF10/CF100/STL10/TINet, and surpasses the best generative augmentation baselines (MoCo v2+VAE: 89.3/65.9/91.2/43.3) by 4.1/4.0/1.3/6.4 points. On DNA sequences, DiffAug (89.1 AVE) exceeds the best DNA-specific model Hyena (86.2) and all hand-designed SSL augmentation variants (SSL+Mixup: 85.4, SSL+Insertion: 85.0, SSL+RC: 84.3), with the largest gain on HuEnEn (+6.8 over Hyena). On bio-feature data, DiffAug (82.4 AVE) dominates both CL baselines (DLME+Mixup: 78.5, MoCo+Mixup: 77.7) and dimensionality reduction methods (hNNE: 73.4, PaCMAP: 71.7), with the largest margin on GA1457 (+7.0 over DLME+Mixup). Notably, DiffAug also outperforms supervised-condition ablation (A1) on TINet (49.7 vs 45.9), suggesting its unsupervised semantic encoder captures richer structure than one-hot labels in that regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiffAug sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) diffusion-based generative modeling (DDPM, Ho et al. 2020), which provides the conditional generation mechanism; (2) soft contrastive learning (Zang et al., ICCV 2023), which supplies the SCL loss that trains the semantic encoder to produce conditioning vectors; and (3) the EM algorithm (Gupta et al., 2011), which motivates the alternating A-Step/B-Step training schedule. The paper departs from prior generative-augmentation work (VAE/GAN-based methods like DREAM-OOD, GIF, and earlier GAN-augmentation studies) by making the entire pipeline unsupervised: the diffusion model is conditioned on encoder outputs rather than labels or external data, and the encoder is trained with the generated samples in a closed loop. This contrasts with hand-designed augmentation (random cropping, color jitter, DNA mutations) that requires domain expertise, and with supervised generative augmentation that needs large labeled corpora.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DiffAug addresses a practical bottleneck in domains like genomics and proteomics where hand-designed augmentation is either infeasible (DNA semantics are opaque) or semantically destructive (a single nucleotide change can alter meaning), and where labeled data for training generative models is scarce. The consistent 0.4–7% gains across DNA sequences, bio-feature vectors, and vision datasets demonstrate that the framework is domain-agnostic, which is its main practical contribution. Limitations include the added computational cost of the diffusion model (7–20 hours per dataset on a single V100), sensitivity to the replacement ratio λ (too high causes encoder collapse), and evaluation on relatively small datasets (CIFAR, TinyImageNet, single-cell RNA-seq). Open questions include whether the approach scales to ImageNet-scale vision or whole-genome-scale biology, whether the EM schedule is truly necessary versus simpler joint training at larger scale, and how the method interacts with newer CL paradigms (DINOv2, MAE).
