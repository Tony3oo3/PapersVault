## Problem

Optimization in scientific machine learning (PINNs, variational Monte Carlo, neural operators) is notoriously difficult, with first-order methods plateauing far from high accuracy and no principled consensus on best practices. The paper argues that the root cause is ignoring the infinite-dimensional function space geometry dictated by the underlying PDEs, and advocates for a 'first optimize, then discretize' paradigm where the algorithm is chosen at the continuous level before being projected onto the neural network's tangent space.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241491/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a PDE or variational problem formulated in a Hilbert space, a parametric neural network ansatz class, and a choice of function-space operator (identity for gradient descent, Hessian for Newton, Jacobian product for Gauss-Newton, or a saddle-point Hessian for Lagrange-Newton). It produces optimized network parameters via the update θ_{k+1} = θ_k − η_k G(θ_k)^† ∇L(θ_k), where the Gramian G encodes the function-space inner product evaluated on tangent-space basis functions ∂_{θ_i}u_θ. For saddle-point problems, the Gramian has a block structure coupling multiple networks (e.g., solution, Lagrange multipliers for PDE residual and boundary conditions).

## Method

The core contribution is a unified discretization framework: given an infinite-dimensional iterative algorithm u_{k+1} = u_k + η_k d_k with d_k = T_{u_k}^{-1}(DE(u_k)), the parameter-space update is obtained by solving a least-squares projection of d_k onto the tangent space ran(DP(θ)), yielding the Gramian G(θ)_{ij} = ⟨T_{u_θ} ∂_{θ_i}u_θ, ∂_{θ_j}u_θ⟩ and the update θ_{k+1} = θ_k − η_k G(θ_k)^† ∇L(θ_k). The paper proves (Theorem 1) that the resulting function-space dynamics match the original algorithm up to an orthogonal projection onto the tangent space plus a quadratically vanishing error. This single framework recovers natural gradient descent (Hilbert-space gradient descent), energy natural gradient descent (Newton in function space), competitive PINNs / competitive gradient descent (Lagrange-Newton for saddle-point problems), and Gauss-Newton natural gradient descent (Gauss-Newton for nonlinear least-squares PINN formulations), unifying previously ad-hoc methods under a common geometric principle. Scalability is addressed via matrix-free second-order methods (CG/GMRES with Jacobian-vector products) and K-FAC Kronecker-factored approximations of the Gramian.

## Results

This is a position paper without standard benchmark tables. The key empirical illustration (Figure 1) shows that on a 2D Poisson equation PINN, first-order optimizers (Adam, Gradient Descent) plateau at low accuracy, while the function-space inspired optimizer (ENGD) reaches the highest accuracy by several orders of magnitude over both first-order and standard second-order (Newton) methods. The main theoretical contribution is a unified derivation showing that all current state-of-the-art SciML optimizers — Natural Gradient Descent (VMC), Energy Natural Gradient Descent (Deep Ritz), Competitive PINNs (linear PDEs), and Gauss-Newton Natural Gradient (Navier-Stokes) — arise as tangent-space discretizations of natural infinite-dimensional algorithms (gradient descent, Newton, Lagrange-Newton, Gauss-Newton, respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not run controlled head-to-head benchmark comparisons. Instead, it provides a unifying theoretical lens: Figure 1 qualitatively compares Adam, Gradient Descent, Newton, and ENGD on a 2D Poisson PINN, showing that ENGD (the function-space Newton discretization) outperforms all others by orders of magnitude in final accuracy. The paper further demonstrates that previously proposed methods — K-FAC-based natural gradient for VMC (Hermann et al., 2022; Pfau et al., 2020), ENGD for Deep Ritz (Müller & Zeinhofer, 2023), CPINNs/CGD for linear PDEs (Zeng et al., 2022; Schäfer & Anandkumar, 2019), and GNNGD for Navier-Stokes (anonymous, 2024) — are all special cases of the same 'first optimize, then discretize' principle, differing only in the choice of the infinite-dimensional operator T_u. The paper argues that first-order methods (Adam, SGD) are fundamentally ill-suited for SciML because they ignore the PDE-induced function space geometry, while the function-space framework provides a principled recipe for selecting the correct second-order structure for each problem class.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies a scattered collection of state-of-the-art SciML optimizers—natural gradient descent for variational Monte Carlo, energy natural gradient descent (ENGD), competitive gradient descent (CGD)/competitive PINNs, and Gauss-Newton natural gradient descent—under a single 'first optimize, then discretize' paradigm inherited from PDE-constrained optimization. The theoretical backbone traces back to Amari's natural gradient (1998) and the Fisher-Rao geometry tradition, but the paper's key contribution is showing that these independently proposed methods are all tangent-space discretizations of specific infinite-dimensional algorithms (Hilbert-space gradient descent, Newton, Lagrange-Newton, Gauss-Newton). It departs from the standard deep learning optimization paradigm (Adam, SGD) by arguing that the function space geometry dictated by the underlying PDE is essential, not incidental, to effective optimization in SciML.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a recognized bottleneck in SciML: the lack of principled, problem-specific optimization algorithms, with first-order methods well-documented to plateau on PINN and related problems. By providing a unified design framework, it offers a roadmap for developing new optimizers tailored to specific PDE structures rather than relying on ad-hoc loss reweighting or sampling heuristics. Key open questions include scalability to high-parameter-dimension problems (addressed only briefly via K-FAC and matrix-free methods), extension to nonlinear saddle-point problems where the Lagrange-Newton/CGD correspondence breaks down, and practical implementation of PDE-specific function space geometries that differ from the Fisher information matrix used in supervised learning. As a position paper, its impact hinges on whether the community adopts this framework as a design principle for future SciML optimizers.
