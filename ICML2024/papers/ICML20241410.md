## Problem

Graph Foundation Models (GFMs) aim to pre-train on diverse graph data for broad transfer, but unlike NLP (words) or CV (image tokens), graphs lack a natural discrete vocabulary, and existing GFMs are built on domain-specific principles without general design guidance. The paper asks: what constitutes the 'graph vocabulary'—the basic transferable units encoding invariance across graphs—and how should it be constructed to enable positive transfer and neural scaling?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241410/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The GFM framework consumes heterogeneous graphs from diverse domains (social networks, molecules, knowledge graphs, citation networks) with varying node features, edge relation types, and structural patterns, and must produce predictions across node classification, link prediction, graph classification, and knowledge graph completion. Crucially, the 'vocabulary' is not a discrete tokenizer or embedding layer but a model architecture that maps structurally distinct graphs into a shared representation space; it must satisfy two constraints: (1) non-compaction, so non-isomorphic node pairs retain distinct representations to avoid negative transfer, and (2) inclusiveness, so unseen relation types or structural patterns can be mapped onto the existing vocabulary for positive transfer.

## Method

The paper proposes a 'graph vocabulary' perspective grounded in three transferability principles: network analysis (homophily, triadic closure, network motifs), expressiveness (Weisfeiler-Leman equivalence, most-expressive structural representations via labeling tricks), and stability (bounded prediction gaps under perturbations). Through this lens, the authors decompose existing GFMs (ULTRA, OFA, DiG, MoleBERT, GraphGPT) to show their success stems from satisfying specific vocabulary constraints—e.g., ULTRA's double permutation equivariance provides an inclusive relational vocabulary, while NBFNet's conditional message passing avoids compaction. They then derive task-specific actionable steps (e.g., separate homophilic/heterophilic vocabularies for node classification, dual structural+feature proximity encoding for link prediction, uniform motif sets for graph classification) and analyze conditions under which neural scaling laws hold for graphs, including the role of LLMs as feature encoders or predictors and the risks of data uncertainty and conflicting graph constructions.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not report quantitative experimental results. Instead, it provides a conceptual framework (the 'graph vocabulary' perspective) and actionable design principles for building Graph Foundation Models. The paper categorizes existing GFMs into task-specific, domain-specific, and primitive categories, and identifies three transferability principles (network analysis, expressiveness, stability) as guidance for vocabulary construction. No headline metrics or benchmark numbers are presented.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to be benchmarked against baselines. It reviews and discusses existing GFMs (ULTRA, OFA, DiG, MoleBERT, GraphGPT, ProDiGy, LLaGA, etc.) qualitatively, attributing their success to suitable vocabulary construction guided by domain-specific transferability principles. The paper highlights that no current GFM achieves universal transfer across all graph tasks and domains, in contrast to the success of LLMs in NLP. It identifies specific failure modes of vanilla GNNs (e.g., contracted relational vocabulary causing negative transfer in link prediction, inability to handle both homophily and heterophily simultaneously) as motivation for the proposed vocabulary perspective, but does not quantify these gaps with controlled experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes the foundation model paradigm from NLP (GPT, LLaMA) and CV (CLIP, DALL-E) and transplants it to the graph domain under the unifying lens of a 'graph vocabulary' — the basic transferable units that encode structural invariance. It reviews existing GFM efforts (ULTRA for knowledge graphs, DiG for molecules, OFA for multi-task co-training, MoleBERT, GraphGPT) and reinterprets their successes through three transferability principles: network analysis (homophily, triadic closure, motifs), expressiveness (Weisfeiler-Leman, labeling tricks, double equivariance), and stability (spectral smoothness, positional encoding). Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper carves new conceptual ground by arguing that the key design question for GFMs is vocabulary construction, drawing explicit analogies to tokenizers in NLP and image tokenizers in CV, and connecting this to neural scaling laws.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a much-needed unifying framework and research agenda for GFM development, which has been fragmented across domain-specific efforts. It raises critical open questions: whether a truly universal GFM is feasible given that graphs are more diverse and less grounded than text/images, whether neural scaling laws will hold when graph construction criteria are ambiguous or conflicting (e.g., OGBN-ARXIV vs. ARXIV-YEAR), and whether LLMs can genuinely understand graph structure or merely exploit sequential shortcuts. The actionable steps (separate homophily/heterophily vocabularies, expressive GNN backbones with stable positional encodings, motif-based vocabulary for graph classification) give concrete direction for future work, while the caution about data insufficiency and the call for synthetic graph generation highlight practical barriers that the field must overcome.
