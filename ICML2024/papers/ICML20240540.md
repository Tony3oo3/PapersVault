## Problem

Existing active ranking algorithms for noisy pairwise comparisons are sequential (querying one comparison at a time) and leave many players unpaired after a few rounds, which is impractical for real-world matchmaking where every player must be matched each round. This paper introduces the constraint that each round must form a perfect matching (all N players paired simultaneously) and adds a secondary objective of minimizing matching cost, where pairing players with large skill gaps is penalized. The paper shows that exact ranking is sometimes necessary to recover the optimal matching, ruling out shortcuts via approximate rankings.

## Input / Output

At each round t, the algorithm selects a perfect matching M_t (a partition of all N players into N/2 pairs), observes the binary win/loss outcomes of all pairs simultaneously, and proceeds to the next round. The process stops after a random number of rounds T, at which point the algorithm outputs both an estimated ranking r̂(·) and a cost-minimizing matching M̂*, each correct with probability at least 1−δ. The comparison noise is structured: the probability of i beating j is p(i,j) = 1/2 + ε(i,j), where |ε(i,j)| is the skill gap, and the cost of a pair is 1 if |ε(i,j)| > ε* and 0 otherwise.

## Method

The core algorithm CASCADINGAKS adapts the AKS-Paterson sorting network (O(log N) depth) to handle noisy comparisons via a COMPARE subroutine that repeats a pair O(ε⁻² log(1/δ)) times to obtain a confident verdict. The ranking is refined in phases with decreasing precision ε_s = 1/2^s: each phase uses an ANCHORING step to identify confident reference points, clusters players into groups of bounded size, and applies AKS twice (on alternating cluster groupings) to eliminate residual misrankings between adjacent clusters. Once the exact ranking is recovered, the optimal matching problem reduces to identifying which adjacent skill gaps exceed ε*; this is solved with a LIL-UCB-style procedure that sequentially removes costly edges from the adjacency graph, yielding a regret of O(Σ log(N/δ)/Δ²_{ε*,i}) beyond the ranking cost.

## Results

The paper establishes three main theoretical guarantees. First, the (ε,δ)-PAC ranking algorithm (AKS(ε,δ)) achieves a sample complexity of O(N/ε² · log³(N) · log(N/δ)) comparisons and a time complexity of O(log³(N)/ε² · log(N/δ)) rounds, which is log³(N) away from the known lower bound Ω(N/ε² · log(N/δ)). Second, the CASCADINGAKS algorithm retrieves the exact ranking in O(log³(N) · log(N/δ) / min_i Δ_i²) rounds. Third, the full algorithm for learning an optimal matching incurs a regret of O(Σ_{i:Δ_i>ε*} 1/Δ_{ε*,i}² · log(N/δ · log(1/Δ_{ε*,i}²)) + Σ_i 1/Δ_i² · log(N/δ · log(1/Δ_i²))), which is minimax optimal up to polylogarithmic factors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or datasets. The comparisons are against prior theoretical guarantees. Against sequential active ranking algorithms (Falahatgar et al. 2017b; Ren et al. 2019), which require O(N/ε² · log(N/δ)) time (rounds) for (ε,δ)-PAC ranking, this paper achieves O(log³(N)/ε² · log(N/δ)) rounds — a factor of N/log³(N) improvement in time complexity, at the cost of a log³(N) factor in sample complexity over the lower bound. Against combinatorial semi-bandit approaches (CombUCB, Kveton et al. 2015), which would yield a regret of O(N² · log(1/δ)/Δ_min²) for the matching problem, this paper achieves O(N · log(1/δ)/Δ_0²) where Δ_0 = min_i min{Δ_i, Δ_{ε*,i}} is typically much larger than Δ_min (the gap between optimal and best suboptimal matching). The paper also proves minimax optimality of its regret bound up to polylogarithmic terms via explicit 4-vertex lower-bound constructions (Instances A, B, C in Section 3.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the AKS-Paterson sorting network (Ajtai et al., 1983; Paterson, 1990) as a parallel comparison backbone, adapting it to handle noisy comparisons via a COMPARE subroutine, and on the LIL-UCB best-arm identification framework (Jamieson et al., 2014) for the gap-estimation phase that identifies costly edges. It departs from prior active ranking algorithms (Falahatgar et al., 2017; Ren et al., 2019; Saha & Gopalan, 2019), which are sequential and leave players unpaired, by imposing a perfect-matching constraint at every round and adding a secondary objective of minimizing matching cost. The paper also contrasts its approach with combinatorial semi-bandit methods (CombUCB, Kveton et al., 2015), showing that the non-monotone cost structure of matching makes naive bandit approaches suboptimal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation—video game matchmaking where players must be paired every round and prefer balanced opponents—makes this directly relevant to platforms like Xbox TrueSkill. The conceptual finding that exact ranking is sometimes provably necessary to recover an optimal matching (despite the cost function depending only on a threshold) challenges the intuition that approximate rankings suffice. Key open questions include closing the log³(N) gap to the information-theoretic lower bound and replacing the AKS network (whose large hidden constant D limits practicality) with a more implementable O(log N)-depth sorting structure.
