## Problem

When pretrained language models are fine-tuned to correct errors, they suffer catastrophic forgetting of previously learned upstream examples, and the standard remedy of random replay lacks both interpretability and targeting precision. No prior work has formulated the task of forecasting which specific upstream examples will be forgotten due to a given model update, nor provided a computationally efficient method to do so without running expensive repetitive inference with the updated model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240890/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The forecasting function g takes a pair of examples — an online learned example (x_i, y_i) that the model mispredicted and an upstream pretraining example (x_j, y_j) that the base model previously answered correctly — and outputs a binary label indicating whether (x_j, y_j) will be forgotten after updating on (x_i, y_i). At inference time, the method only requires cached representations h(x_j, y_j) and cached logits f̂_0(x_j) from the base model, avoiding any repetitive forward passes through the large LM. The set of forecasted forgotten examples is then used to construct a targeted replay buffer during model refinement.

## Method

The paper proposes two forecasting approaches grounded in the empirical observation of 'logit-change transfer,' where pre-softmax logit changes of upstream examples proportionally mirror those of the online learned example. The logit-based method derives an NTK-inspired relationship (Eqn. 2) linking logit changes across examples, then replaces the prohibitively expensive TV×TV kernel with a trainable low-dimensional kernel Θ̃(x_j, x_i) = h(x_j, y_j)h(x_i, y_i)^T, where h is a trainable LM encoder mapping concatenated input-output pairs to R^{T×d}; it is trained with a margin loss on predicted logits and performs well on BART but fails on FLAN-T5. The representation-based method is a black-box classifier that directly maps the inner product of averaged token representations through a sigmoid to a binary forgetting probability, augmented with a per-example frequency prior bias term to capture example-specific forgetting susceptibility; it is trained with binary cross-entropy and achieves the best F1 across all model and tuning setups. Both methods are trained on (online example, upstream example, ground-truth forgetting label) triples collected by running inference with the updated model, but at deployment time require only cached encodings, making them orders of magnitude cheaper than computing ground-truth forgetting.

## Results

For forecasting forgotten examples (Table 1), representation-based forecasting achieves the best F1 across all model and tuning setups, reaching 79.32 F1 on BART0_Large (Head tuning) and 67.19 F1 on BART0_Large (Full FT), outperforming the threshold baseline by 11.41 F1 on the latter. For practical model refinement (Table 3), replaying examples forecasted to be forgotten reduces EM Drop Ratio to 1.634% on BART0_Large (vs. 9.274% vanilla fine-tuning) and to 0.138% on FLAN-T5_3B (vs. 4.384% vanilla), while maintaining edit success rates above 91%. The forecasting model also generalizes out-of-domain, achieving 50.12 F1 on unseen tasks versus 46.24 for the threshold baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MIR (Aljundi et al., 2019a), which retrieves forgotten examples from a smaller subset of upstream data, the representation-based forecasting method substantially outperforms on EM Drop Ratio: 1.634% vs. 5.024% on BART0_Large Full FT, and 0.138% vs. 1.681% on FLAN-T5_3B LoRA. Against OCS (Yoon et al., 2022), which computes coresets from upstream training data, the proposed method also wins consistently: 1.634% vs. 3.573% on BART0_Large and 0.138% vs. 1.435% on FLAN-T5_3B. The authors conjecture that MIR only marginally improves over random replay due to the large D_PT and sparsity of forgotten examples in their setup. Against MEND (Mitchell et al., 2021), a non-replay meta-learning approach, the trade-off is different: MEND achieves a lower EM Drop (0.060% vs. 0.079% for representation-based replay) but at the cost of a lower edit success rate (93.1% vs. 95.7%), which is the primary goal of model refinement. Overall, the representation-based forecasting method dominates both continual-learning baselines on the forgetting metric while maintaining competitive edit success rates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the neural tangent kernel (NTK) framework to derive a first-order approximation of how logit changes in one example transfer to another during fine-tuning, then replaces the prohibitively expensive NTK with a trainable low-dimensional kernel for practical forecasting. It departs from prior empirical studies of example forgetting (Toneva et al., Maini et al.), which characterized individual examples' susceptibility to forgetting, by formulating the problem as a pairwise interaction: predicting whether learning example i causes forgetting of example j. The practical replay strategy is positioned against continual learning baselines (MIR, OCS) and non-replay model editing methods (MEND), but the core contribution is the forecasting formulation itself, which has no direct precedent in the model refinement or continual learning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a computationally efficient alternative to the expensive ground-truth procedure of running full inference over all upstream examples after every model update, making targeted replay practical for deployed language models. The logit-change transfer phenomenon offers a partially interpretable mechanism for forgetting, though its failure on T5 models (success on BART) highlights that the simplified kernel does not universally capture learning dynamics. Open questions include how distributional relationships between upstream and online tasks affect forgetting, whether forecasting models can be updated alongside the LM in continual refinement, and whether the moderate F1 scores (67–79%) can be improved with richer interaction features or multi-step dynamics.
