## Problem

Despite active deployment of AI-powered autonomous weapons systems (AWS) in conflicts like Ukraine, the ML community has focused primarily on hypothetical AGI risks while giving comparatively little attention to the near-future geopolitical and research-freedom threats posed by already-fielded AWS. The paper argues that AWS lower the human and political cost of war—increasing conflict frequency and escalation risk—while simultaneously threatening open AI research through dual-use dynamics, potential national security restrictions, and the co-opting of academic institutions into weapons programs.

## Input / Output

The paper synthesizes evidence from military deployment reports, technical specifications of AWS platforms (loitering munitions, UAVs, UGVs, naval vehicles, command systems), policy documents, and academic literature on military AI to produce a structured risk analysis and a set of concrete policy recommendations. The output is organized as a domain-by-domain survey of AWS (air, ground, naval, command), a proliferation and research-freedom threat analysis, and eight specific regulatory suggestions targeted at governments, universities, and the AI research community, summarized in a topic-challenge-recommendation table.

## Method

The paper's core argument rests on the observation that AWS risks are distinct from and more immediate than AGI risks because they require no superhuman capabilities and are already being deployed in combat. It surveys the current state of AWS development across four domains, documenting specific systems (Kargu-2, XQ-58A Valkyrie, THeMIS, BlueWhale, Palantir AIP) and their autonomy levels, then analyzes why hardware export restrictions and research compartmentalization will fail to prevent proliferation given the modest edge-compute requirements of robotic ML (e.g., 100 TOPS Jetson Orin NX vs. 4800 TOPS export threshold). The paper proposes eight specific policy interventions spanning conflict transparency, international autonomy standards, mandatory human battlefield presence alongside AWS, and academic governance of military-funded AI research, arguing that the most effective mitigation is preserving meaningful human presence in combat rather than attempting to restrict the underlying ML technology that is already globally distributed.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results. The paper's central argument is that AWS lower the human (and thus political) cost of war, increasing the likelihood of both low-intensity peer conflicts and wars of aggression against non-peer adversaries. The authors argue that current GPU export restrictions (threshold: 4800 TOPS) are irrelevant to AWS proliferation, since the Jetson Orin NX module (100 TOPS) used in systems like Shield AI's V-Bat is already sufficient for autonomous swarming, and Chinese 7nm-class fabs can produce equivalent chips. They also cite a 2024 study (Rivera et al.) finding that military-related LLMs were prone to recommending pro-escalation tactics including nuclear weapons deployment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it positions its argument relative to prior AWS ethics literature (Scharre 2018, Blanchard & Taddeo 2022, Brose 2020) by arguing that even under the optimistic assumption that AWS do not increase civilian casualties, the removal of human battlefield presence creates distinct geopolitical instability risks that prior work has underemphasized. The paper contrasts its 'near-future, no-AGI-required' risk framing with the dominant AGI/superintelligence discourse (Bengio 2023, Future of Life Institute 2023), arguing AWS risks are more immediate and require fewer assumptions about technological trajectories. It also distinguishes its policy recommendations from prior calls for outright bans (Russell 2023, Bode et al. 2023), instead advocating for human-AWS teaming requirements, autonomy-level standards, and transparency mandates as more achievable near-term interventions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves out a specific niche within AI safety and policy discourse by shifting focus from the dominant AGI/superintelligence risk framing to the more near-term, concrete risks posed by already-deployed autonomous weapons systems (AWS). It synthesizes and extends prior work in military ethics (Scharre 2018, Blanchard & Taddeo 2022), AWS capability surveys (Bode & Watts 2023, Longpre et al. 2022), and AI governance (Bengio 2023, Schneider 2019), but departs from these by explicitly connecting AWS proliferation to threats against academic AI research freedom and by arguing that the geopolitical stability risk (reduced human cost of war → more frequent conflicts) is distinct from and independent of the ethical question of civilian casualties. The paper also repurposes the capability/vulnerability paradox from Schneider (2019) to the specific context of AI expertise and semiconductor supply chains as strategic resources.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper raises a near-term risk to the AI research community that has been underexplored relative to AGI concerns: that AWS centrality to national defense will trigger export restrictions, publication oversight, visa bans, and military-civilian research fusion that degrade academic freedom and international collaboration. Its concrete argument that GPU export controls are ineffective against AWS proliferation (since edge ML for robotics requires far less compute than the 4800 TOPS export threshold) provides a useful technical grounding for policy debates. Open questions include whether the 'middle ground' scenario (AWS deployed but ethically constrained) is politically realistic, how to define and enforce autonomy levels internationally, and whether the proposed transparency and oversight mechanisms can function in practice. The paper's main limitation is that its geopolitical claims (e.g., AWS will increase conflict frequency) are argued by analogy and historical precedent rather than empirical modeling.
