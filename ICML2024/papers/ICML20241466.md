## Problem

The straight-through estimator (STE) is the standard technique for training quantized neural networks, yet its contexts of effective performance remain poorly understood and it has never been studied beyond the quantization setting. This paper applies STE to the well-understood problem of sparse support recovery to gain theoretical and empirical insight, and in doing so introduces a new sparsity-inducing algorithm that explores more candidate supports than existing methods, yielding superior performance especially when the measurement matrix is highly coherent.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a noisy linear observation y = Ax* + e ∈ R^m, a known measurement matrix A ∈ R^{m×n} (columns normalized to unit norm), and a target sparsity k. It outputs a k-sparse vector x whose support S is intended to contain the true support S*, with the final answer being the best sparse iterate encountered across all iterations (not merely the last one). A key structural detail is that the algorithm maintains a dense 'support exploration variable' X ∈ R^n whose k largest-magnitude entries designate the support tested at each iteration, so the exploration is driven by accumulated gradient history rather than by the current sparse estimate.

## Method

SEA adapts the STE to sparse recovery by replacing the standard descent update with a straight-through update on a dense exploration variable. At each iteration, the k largest entries of X^t select a support S^t; the sparse solution x^t is obtained by least-squares projection onto S^t (solving A_{S^t}^T A_{S^t} x_{S^t} = A_{S^t}^T y); and the gradient A^T(Ax^t − y) is backpropagated through the piecewise-constant sparsification operator H via the STE approximation, updating X^{t+1} = X^t − ηA^T(Ax^t − y). Unlike HTP or IHT, where the intermediate variable is always one gradient step from the sparse solution, here X accumulates all past gradients and is not confined to the neighborhood of k-sparse vectors, enabling exploration of supports that would be missed by descent-based methods. The algorithm retains the best solution over all iterations rather than the final one, since the loss can increase when a new support is explored. Theoretical guarantees are established under the (2k+1)-RIP via an oracle-update comparison, showing that SEA visits the true support within a bounded number of iterations when the RIP constants and a recovery condition on x* are satisfied.

## Results

In the phase transition experiment (n=500, 1000 runs), SEA_ELS achieves the highest 95%-recovery threshold across all undersampling ratios m/n, with the most significant gains when m/n < 0.4 (highly coherent matrices). In the spike deconvolution experiment (n=500, Gaussian filter with coherence 0.97, 200 runs per sparsity level), all three SEA variants (SEA_0, SEA_OMP, SEA_ELS) achieve the lowest average support distance dist_supp(x) = (k − |S*∩supp(x)|)/k across all sparsity levels k ∈ [1,50], outperforming every baseline. Notably, SEA_0 (cold-start) is the single best-performing algorithm in the deconvolution setting, and SEA explores approximately 2× fewer supports than ELS while achieving superior recovery.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against OMP: SEA_OMP consistently improves upon OMP in the phase transition diagram, and in the most coherent regime (m/n < 0.2) where HTP_OMP and IHT_OMP fail entirely, SEA_OMP still exhibits the best improvement. In deconvolution, SEA_OMP outperforms OMP at every sparsity level. Against HTP and IHT: SEA_0 outperforms both when m/n < 0.6 in the phase transition; in deconvolution, SEA variants strictly dominate, while HTP_OMP, HTP_ELS, IHT_OMP, and IHT_ELS never improve over their respective initializations (curves are superimposed). Against OMPR: in deconvolution, OMPR (like OMP and ELS) gets trapped in a local minimum around position 400 of the signal, falsely detecting entries in the highest-energy region, whereas SEA recovers the true spikes. Against ELS: SEA_ELS further improves ELS in the phase transition for all m/n, and in deconvolution SEA_0 outperforms ELS while exploring roughly half as many supports, indicating a more efficient exploration strategy. Overall, SEA wins or ties on every comparison in both experiments, with the largest margins in high-coherence settings where RIP-based guarantees do not apply.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Straight-Through Estimator (STE) from quantized neural network training (BinaryConnect, BNN) to the sparse support recovery problem, where the dense vector X plays the role of full-precision weights and the sparsification operator H plays the role of the quantization operator. Conceptually, SEA belongs to the family of greedy thresholding algorithms (HTP, IHT, OMP, OMPR, ELS) but departs from them by maintaining a support exploration variable that accumulates all past gradients rather than being one gradient step away from the current sparse iterate. This accumulation mechanism, inherited from the STE principle, enables a fundamentally different exploration/exploitation trade-off: SEA can visit supports where the objective function increases, avoiding the local-minimum traps that constrain descent-based competitors. The theoretical analysis draws on RIP-based support recovery guarantees comparable in structure to those for LASSO, OMP, HTP, and ELS, but with more stringent conditions on x*.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main open question is why SEA performs well in highly coherent settings (e.g., deconvolution with coherence 0.97) where the RIP-based guarantees do not apply; the current theory cannot explain this behavior, and the authors suggest mutual incoherence conditions as a promising direction. The more stringent recovery conditions (involving α_k^RIP and γ_k^RIP) limit the practical scope of the theoretical results, and the paper acknowledges that the theory is of limited use to practitioners who care about coherent problems. The finding that SEA can serve as a post-processing step to improve outputs of ELS or OMP is practically useful, but the optimal initialization strategy (cold-start vs. warm-start) remains an open empirical question. Broader perspectives include extending the STE-based exploration to stochastic heuristics, compressed sensing (ℓ2 error rather than exact support), and applications in biology and astronomy.
