## Problem

Video data captures physical-world information—spatial arrangements, physics, dynamics, and fine-grained behavior—that text cannot adequately express, yet video generation has remained confined to media entertainment while language models have become powerful planners, agents, and simulators. The paper argues this is an underappreciated opportunity: video can serve as a unified representation and task interface for the physical world, analogous to how text serves the digital world, enabling applications in robotics, self-driving, and science to directly benefit from internet-scale visual knowledge.

## Input / Output

The paper surveys multiple conditioning schemes: text prompts for generative media, initial image frames combined with text for scene-specific rollouts and robot goal synthesis, video frames with action sequences for dynamics modeling, and game observations interleaved with quantized action tokens for world-model rollouts. Outputs range from entertainment clips to concrete robot video plans, SE(3)-conditioned simulated executions, location-specific driving scenes (e.g., 'rain on Golden Gate Bridge'), and atomic-level next-frame predictions. A key structural distinction from language models is that both the state and action spaces are pixels, so the 'next token' being predicted is an image frame or patch rather than a word, and low-level robot controls must be recovered from generated video via auxiliary modules (inverse dynamics, optical flow, or goal-conditioned policies).

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a three-part framework mapping language-model success factors—unified representation, unified task interface, and environment interaction—to video generation, supported by new experiments. The authors train an autoregressive Transformer-XL model on Minecraft contractor data that simultaneously serves as a world model (predicting next observation given action) and a policy (predicting next action given observation), with VQVAE-tokenized frames and VPT-tokenized actions. They also train a single diffusion-based video generation model on the Open X-Embodiment dataset to produce realistic video plans across diverse robot embodiments and tasks, demonstrating that pixel space can act as a unified state-action space. Additional demonstrations include SE(3) action-conditioned next-frame prediction for robot simulation, internet-knowledge-conditioned driving simulation, and STEM electron-microscopy next-frame prediction for atomic dynamics. The paper identifies four major challenges—dataset coverage and labeling gaps, model heterogeneity across diffusion/autoregressive/masked architectures, hallucination (object disappearance, implausible dynamics, causality violations), and limited generalization to underrepresented domains—and proposes solutions including latent action learning, hybrid architectures, RL with external feedback, and causal inference mechanisms.

## Results

This is a position paper without a main quantitative results table or formal benchmark comparisons. The paper provides qualitative demonstrations across multiple domains: an autoregressive model trained on Minecraft contractor data generates plausible game trajectories including tool use (pickaxe breaking stone) and inventory interaction; a diffusion-based SE(3) simulator reproduces real robot execution in the Language Table environment with visible hallucination (bottle disappearance); and a single video generation model trained on the Open X-Embodiment dataset produces realistic video plans across diverse robots and tasks. The paper's contribution is conceptual—arguing that video generation can unify representation, task interface, and state-action space for embodied AI—rather than reporting a single headline metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it synthesizes and connects existing work across video generation, robotics, and reinforcement learning. The paper qualitatively contrasts three model families (diffusion, autoregressive, masked) along axes of sampling speed, long-sequence consistency, and training stability, noting that diffusion models offer parallel frame sampling but slow inference, autoregressive models scale well with context length but suffer from drift, and masked models (e.g., MaskGIT) enable parallel token decoding at the cost of sampling bias. It also contrasts video generation's current state (primarily entertainment media) with language models' demonstrated capabilities in reasoning, planning, and tool use, arguing the gap is an underappreciated opportunity rather than a fundamental limitation. No head-to-head quantitative comparisons are presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper draws a direct structural analogy between the LLM paradigm (unified text representation, next-token prediction as a task interface, and RLHF/planning for decision making) and video generation, arguing that video can play the same unifying role for the physical world. It synthesizes and repositions a broad body of prior work—Du et al.'s pixel-space planning, Yang et al.'s interactive real-world simulators, Bai et al.'s sequential vision modeling, Bruce et al.'s Genie latent-action environments, and Hu et al.'s GAIA-1 driving world model—under a single conceptual framework rather than proposing a new architecture. The paper departs from the dominant entertainment-oriented framing of video generation (text-to-video for media) by foregrounding video as a state-action space, a simulator, and a reasoning substrate for embodied and scientific tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete research roadmap identifying four major bottlenecks—limited data coverage for domain-specific video, model heterogeneity across autoregressive/diffusion/masked architectures, persistent hallucination, and poor generalization to out-of-distribution scenes—that must be resolved before video generation can rival LLMs in real-world decision making. It highlights the underexplored integration of video and language generative models as complementary (language for high-level planning, video for low-level execution) and points to feedback loops (executing generated video as robot actions, VLM-based reward signals) as a path toward self-improvement. Open questions include whether latent-action approaches can scale to complex dynamics, whether hallucination can be fundamentally mitigated rather than merely reduced, and whether a single unified video model can truly replace task-specific pipelines across robotics, driving, and science.
