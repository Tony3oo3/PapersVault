## Problem

Theoretical understanding of catastrophic forgetting in continual learning has been limited by restrictive assumptions: prior analyses require Gaussian data distributions, overparameterized regimes, or minimum-norm interpolators that presuppose zero training error. This paper asks whether a general forgetting characterization exists for practical SGD training that applies to both under- and over-parameterized settings under only fourth-moment conditions on the data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of M tasks, each providing N i.i.d. (x, y) pairs drawn from a distinct distribution D_m with population covariance H_m, along with a constant step size η and N SGD iterations per task. The output is the final parameter vector w_MN after sequential training, accompanied by matching upper and lower bounds on the average excess population risk G(M) = (1/M)Σ L_m(w_MN). The bounds are expressed as functions of the full eigenvalue spectra of all H_m, the step size, data size N, and effective dimensions that decompose into projection accumulation terms and covariance accumulation terms across the task sequence.

## Method

The paper develops a bias-variance decomposition of the forgetting measure for multi-step SGD in linear regression, tracking bias iterates B_t and variance iterates C_t through a linear operator framework (I − ηT_m) acting on symmetric matrices. Under a general fourth-moment condition (Assumption 2.3) that subsumes Gaussian and bounded-norm assumptions, they derive an upper bound (Theorem 3.1) and a nearly matching lower bound (Theorem 3.2) where the error decomposes into variance (driven by noise σ² and effective dimension D₁^eff) and bias (driven by initial parameter distance, effective dimensions D₂^eff and D₃^eff, and a covariance accumulation term Φ). The effective dimensions involve projection terms Γ_{(p,q)}^i = ∏(1−ηλ_j^i)^{2N} that capture how prior task learning attenuates or preserves information for subsequent tasks, with a critical cut-off index k_m* = max{i : λ_m^i ≥ 1/(Nη)} separating dominant from tail eigenvalues. A key insight is that when N is large, the projection term converges to e^{-(M−m)}, making the eigenvalue λ_m^i the dominant factor—so tasks with larger covariance eigenvalues trained later cause more forgetting. They further show that an appropriately small step size mitigates forgetting in both parameterization regimes, and validate these predictions with simulations on linear regression and small DNNs.

## Results

The paper establishes matching upper and lower bounds (Theorems 3.1 and 3.2) on the average excess population risk G(M) for SGD-based continual learning, decomposed into variance and bias terms governed by effective dimensions D₁^eff, D₂^eff, D₃^eff and a covariance accumulation term Φ₁^{m-1}. The key theoretical insight is that as data size N → ∞, the projection term Γ_{(m,M)}^i converges to 1/e^{M-m}, making the eigenvalue spectrum λ_m^i the dominant factor in forgetting. Empirically, with three synthetic tasks (eigenvalue decays i^{-3}, i^{-2}, i^{-1}), task sequences ending with the largest-eigenvalue task (Task 3) consistently exhibit the highest average excess risk across all six orderings. Reducing the step size from 0.01 to 0.001 markedly lowers forgetting, particularly in underparameterized (d=10) settings, while in overparameterized (d=1000) linear regression, higher dimensionality increases forgetting at fixed data size—a behavior not observed in DNNs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with synthetic-data validation rather than a method comparison study, so no standard baseline benchmarking is performed. The paper positions its contributions against two closest prior theoretical works: Evron et al. (2022) and Lin et al. (2023), both of which analyze forgetting in linear regression via the minimum-norm interpolator. The authors' SGD-based analysis differs in three key ways: (1) it requires only a fourth-moment condition rather than Gaussian or bounded-norm assumptions, (2) it does not require zero training error (unlike the minimum-norm solution), and (3) it applies to both underparameterized and overparameterized regimes, whereas prior work is restricted to the overparameterized case. Empirically, the paper validates its theoretical predictions on both linear regression and a small DNN (one hidden layer, 10 neurons), confirming that eigenvalue ordering and step size effects predicted by the bounds manifest in practice. A notable contrast with linear regression is that overparameterized DNNs do not show increased forgetting with higher dimensionality when data size is fixed, suggesting the linear theory does not fully transfer to deep models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SGD bias-variance decomposition framework from single-task (Zou et al., 2021) and two-task pretraining-finetuning (Wu et al., 2022b) settings to the multi-task continual learning problem, where the evaluation metric (forgetting) requires tracking alignment with all previously encountered tasks simultaneously. It departs from prior theoretical analyses of forgetting (Evron et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2023) by replacing the minimum-norm interpolator with practical SGD, and from NTK-based or Gaussian-distribution analyses (Bennani et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2021; Asanuma et al., 2021) by requiring only fourth-moment conditions, thereby covering both underparameterized and overparameterized regimes. The covariate shift concept is generalized from the standard two-distribution transfer learning setting to a multi-task sequential framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first general theoretical characterization of forgetting under SGD that is not restricted to overparameterized settings or Gaussian data, offering practitioners concrete guidance on how task ordering (larger eigenvalues later → more forgetting) and step size selection interact with data size and dimensionality. The DNN experiments reveal a notable contrast with linear regression: in overparameterized DNNs, higher dimensionality does not necessarily increase forgetting when data size is fixed, suggesting that network architecture fundamentally alters the forgetting dynamics in ways not captured by linear theory. Open questions include whether these spectral-ordering insights transfer to non-linear, non-i.i.d. continual learning scenarios, and why DNNs exhibit different dimensionality-scaling behavior than linear models in the overparameterized regime.
