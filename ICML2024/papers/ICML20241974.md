## Problem

Existing post-processing methods for group fairness are tailored to specific problem settings (e.g., binary sensitive attributes) and specific fairness definitions, making them far less broadly applicable than in-processing methods. Additionally, all prior post-processing approaches require sensitive attributes to be known at inference time, which is often infeasible in practice. The paper asks whether a single post-processing framework can match the broad applicability of in-processing while retaining its practical advantages (no retraining, low compute, no access to training pipeline).

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frozen pre-trained model f_base that outputs scores (unnormalized logits for classification, continuous values for regression) along with tabular covariates X. It produces a fair score f_fair(x) = f_base(x) + T_PP(x), where T_PP is a learned additive correction. Crucially, T_PP is a function of all covariates X rather than a group-dependent transformation indexed by the sensitive attribute A, so sensitive attributes are never needed at inference. Training requires two datasets: an unlabeled set D_pp for the prediction-discrepancy term and a (potentially small) set D_sensitive with sensitive-attribute annotations for the fairness regularizer.

## Method

The core theoretical result (Proposition 3.1) shows that for generalized linear models, the regularized in-processing objective OPT_IP and a bi-level post-processing objective OPT_PP differ only by a constant, meaning they share the same minimizer and hence identical fairness-error Pareto frontiers. Practically, FRAPPÉ trains a lightweight post-hoc module T_PP(X) (e.g., linear regression or a 1-layer MLP) by minimizing a Bregman divergence (MSE for regression, KL for classification) between f_base+T and f_base on unlabeled data, plus an arbitrary in-processing fairness regularizer L_fair on data with sensitive annotations (Equation 7). Unlike prior post-processing methods that apply a group-dependent transformation T_A selected by the sensitive attribute value, FRAPPÉ uses a single additive correction that depends on the full covariate vector X, eliminating the need for A at inference. This modular design means changing the fairness definition, sensitive attribute, or trade-off strength only requires retraining the small T_PP module rather than the entire prediction model, and the framework naturally extends to continuous sensitive attributes and non-gradient-based base models (e.g., random forests).

## Results

FRAPPÉ methods preserve the fairness-error Pareto frontiers of their in-processing counterparts across multiple fairness definitions (EqOpp, SP, EqOdds) and datasets (Adult, COMPAS, HSLS, ENEM, Communities & Crime). Compared to the best prior post-processing method (FairProjection, Alghamdi et al. 2022), FRAPPÉ MinDiff reduces the mean equalized odds violation (MEO) by 53%, 37%, 32%, and 50% on Adult, COMPAS, HSLS, and ENEM respectively, while sacrificing no more than 2% prediction error. Computationally, FRAPPÉ requires only 11.7–14.8 minutes versus 85.4–113.4 minutes for the corresponding in-processing methods on the Adult dataset. With only 0.1% of data carrying sensitive labels, FRAPPÉ MinDiff significantly outperforms in-processing MinDiff, which degrades to the level of a naive baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against in-processing baselines (MinDiff, Cho et al., Mary et al., Reductions), FRAPPÉ variants reproduce nearly identical Pareto frontiers, confirming the theoretical equivalence (Proposition 3.1) in practice, even when the post-hoc module is a simple linear model while the base model is a 3-layer MLP. Against the strongest prior post-processing method, FairProjection (Alghamdi et al. 2022), FRAPPÉ MinDiff achieves substantially lower MEO—reductions of 53% (Adult), 37% (COMPAS), 32% (HSLS), and 50% (ENEM)—with ≤2% prediction error cost, and this advantage holds across random forest, GBM, and logistic regression base models. Against weaker post-processing baselines (Hardt et al. 2016, Kamiran et al. 2018, Chzhen et al. 2020), FRAPPÉ MinDiff consistently dominates on the fairness-error Pareto frontier. Against Fairness Reprogramming (Zhang et al. 2022) on CelebA image data, FRAPPÉ FERMI achieves comparable Pareto frontiers without requiring modality-specific trigger design. A key differentiator is that FRAPPÉ does not require sensitive attributes at inference time, unlike all prior post-processing methods, and is the first post-processing approach applicable to continuous sensitive attributes (demonstrated on Communities & Crime).

## Relation to Prior Work

FRAPPÉ repurposes the fairness regularizers from established in-processing methods (MinDiff/MMD, Cho et al.'s KDE, Mary et al.'s HGR, Agarwal et al.'s Reductions) by showing via a Bregman-divergence equivalence (Proposition 3.1) that, for GLMs, the in-processing objective and a post-processing objective differ only by a constant. This departs fundamentally from prior post-processing work (Hardt et al., Alghamdi et al., Kamiran et al.), which relies on group-dependent score transformations T_A requiring the sensitive attribute at inference; FRAPPÉ instead learns a covariate-dependent additive correction T_PP(X), eliminating that requirement. The framework is also conceptually related to two-step boosting, post-hoc calibration, and model reprogramming (Zhang et al., 2022), but differs in operating on model outputs rather than inputs and in being agnostic to the fairness definition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a general, modular recipe for converting any regularized in-processing fairness method into a post-processing one, which is practically significant for deployments where retraining is infeasible (limited compute, no pipeline access, AutoML black-box models) or where sensitive attributes are unavailable at inference. The empirical finding that in-processing overfits the fairness regularizer under partial group labels while FRAPPÉ does not (due to its decoupled training) addresses a real and underexplored deployment challenge. Open questions include whether the GLM equivalence extends rigorously to deep networks, whether the approach handles compositional fairness in multi-model systems, and whether it generalizes to individual fairness or other equity notions beyond group fairness.
