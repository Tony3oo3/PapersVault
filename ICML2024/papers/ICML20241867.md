## Problem

Interpersonal coordination is intuitively understood but lacks a precise computational definition that distinguishes genuine causal influence between interacting individuals from spurious synchrony caused by shared external factors. Existing approaches rely on temporal correlation proxies (cross-correlation, entropy, CRQA) without explicitly modeling coordination as a latent causal mechanism, limiting interpretability and making it difficult to integrate heterogeneous modalities at different time scales.

## Input / Output

The model consumes per-utterance vocalic features (pitch, intensity, jitter, shimmer) z-scored per subject and extracted via openSMILE, along with rule-based semantic event labels (e.g., HelpRequest→Agreement pairs) from transcribed dialog. Observations are sparse—vocalic features appear on only 14–21% of 1-second time steps and semantic links on 3–4%—yet the model handles missing data natively. The primary output is a continuous coordination time series C ∈ [0,1] at 1-second resolution (T=1020 for 17-minute missions), from which coordination peaks are extracted to predict final team scores via linear regression.

## Method

The core contribution is a dynamic Bayesian network in which a latent coordination variable C modulates the degree to which one person's latent component distribution influences another's next state. For continuous coordination, the transition blends the individual's own previous latent state with their partner's using C as a convex weight: A_t = (1−C)·A_{t−1}^p + C·A_{t−1}^{p′} + ε, so C=0 means no influence and C=1 means full adoption of the partner's distribution. Coordination itself evolves as a random walk passed through a sigmoid to remain in [0,1]. Multiple interacting component groups (vocalics, semantics) operate at their own time scales, each mapped to the shared coordination scale, enabling natural multi-scale integration. The semantic modality is modeled directly as a function of coordination—observations are Gaussian samples centered on C—rather than through a separate latent component, so semantic link events serve as direct evidence for the coordination level. Inference is performed via NUTS (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) in PyMC, and the model is validated through posterior predictive RMSE against an ablated version without coordination, confirming that the coordination variable captures genuine predictive structure in the data.

## Results

On the primary task of predicting final team score (MAE, lower is better), coordination-based models outperform baselines in Mission B but not Mission A. In the ASIST Advisor condition (Mission B), M_voc+link achieves 101(20) MAE versus 179(25) for the average baseline (M_avg) and 147(28) for the vocalic-hyperplane baseline (M_hyp). In ToMCAT (Mission B), M_voc achieves 74(17) MAE versus 104(22) for M_avg and 86(24) for M_hyp. In Mission A, no coordination-based model consistently beats the simple average baseline, consistent with the hypothesis that teams are still learning the game.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three coordination-based models (M_voc, M_link, M_voc+link) against two baselines: M_avg (predicts test score by averaging training scores) and M_hyp (linear regression on 4D mean vocalic features). In Mission B, M_voc clearly wins in ASIST No-Advisor (93 vs. 113 MAE) and ToMCAT (74 vs. 104 MAE), while M_voc+link wins in ASIST Advisor (101 vs. 179 MAE) and ASIST Combined (105 vs. 143 MAE). In Mission A, the coordination models do not consistently outperform M_avg, with M_voc actually performing worst in ASIST No-Advisor (178 vs. 142). The semantic-only model (M_link) is the weakest coordination variant in most conditions, suggesting that vocalic dynamics carry more predictive signal than discrete semantic link events alone. The authors attribute the Mission A failure to teams still learning the game mechanics, supported by a significant score improvement from Mission A to B (p=.001 ASIST, p=.004 ToMCAT).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of quantifying interpersonal coordination via temporal correlation proxies (cross-correlation, CRQA, cross-wavelet coherence) and from mechanistic coupled-oscillator models, instead proposing coordination as a latent variable that explicitly modulates causal influence between components in a dynamic Bayesian network. It is most closely related to Butner et al.'s (2014) Latent Change Score models and Moulder et al. (2022), which also use latent variables for multimodal coordination, but distinguishes itself by modeling directional causal influence rather than shared synchrony, and by naturally accommodating heterogeneous time scales across modalities. The paper carves new ground by formalizing the distinction between coordination (causal interpersonal influence) and synchrony (correlation from shared external causes), a distinction the authors argue prior work conflates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, generative computational definition of coordination that is interpretable, multi-scale, and multi-modal, addressing a gap the authors identify in the literature where coordination is assumed rather than defined. The finding that coordination predicts team performance only in the second mission (not the first) offers a testable hypothesis about learning dynamics in team formation. Key limitations include the small sample sizes (14–30 teams), the simplistic rule-based semantic linking, the absence of a ground-truth coordination signal for validation, and unexplored robustness to spurious coordination from common causes. Open questions include whether the framework extends to antisymmetric (leader-follower) coordination, non-linear coordination-performance relationships, and richer modalities like neural or physiological data.
