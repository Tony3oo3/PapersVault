## Problem

When fine-tuning policies from imperfect demonstrations with KL-regularized online RL, two coupled failures arise: the KL term bounds the policy's expected return improvement over the (suboptimal) imitation policy, and imperfect demonstrations induce a primacy bias that causes the online learner to over-fit to early low-quality experiences. Existing offline-to-online methods (AlphaStar, VPT, PROTO) assume high-quality demonstrations and address only the regularization side, leaving the root cause—low demonstration quality—unresolved.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of imperfect state-action trajectory demonstrations (e.g., from a PID controller or human play data) plus access to the environment's transition dynamics for online rollouts. Each iteration produces both an improved policy (a parameterized action distribution over state vectors) and an updated demonstration set, where new trajectories from the online-learned policy replace lower-quality ones via a quality-based swap operator. The demonstration set grows in both quantity and quality across iterations, with the final output being the policy from the last iteration alongside the boosted demonstration corpus.

## Method

IRPO alternates two operations across K iterations: (1) iterative training, which trains a behavioral-cloning policy on the current demonstration set and then fine-tunes it with KL-regularized PPO, and (2) demonstration boosting, which rolls out new trajectories with the online-learned policy and replaces inferior trajectories in the demonstration set using a quality indicator function f(τ) (e.g., negative trajectory length). The key insight is that raising the imitation policy's expected return J(π_k⁰) directly relaxes the upper bound on online improvement given by Theorem 4.2, while higher-quality demonstrations also mitigate primacy bias by providing a better initialization for online learning. Theoretically, Theorem 4.3 guarantees that the gap between the learned policy's return and the optimal return shrinks as O(1/k) with iterations. In practice, the KL regularization strength is scheduled to increase as demonstration quality improves across iterations, and the method is robust to the choice of indicator function, with even no indicator or simple length-based scoring yielding strong gains.

## Results

IRPO achieves the best performance across all four tasks: 7678.4±60.81 on Halfcheetah, 3044.7±48.65 on Hopper, −5.32±0.43 on Reach, and 0.54±0.01 success rate on fixed-wing UAV attitude control. Compared to the three baseline groups (fixed KL, annealed KL, and EMA regularization), IRPO delivers average improvements of 10.40%, 14.63%, 38.72%, and 42.11% respectively. The gains are most pronounced on the harder multi-goal tasks (Reach and attitude control), where the over-constrained exploration and primacy bias problems are most severe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IRPO outperforms all seven baselines on all four tasks. Against the fixed-KL group (AlphaStar, VPT, PIRLNAV), IRPO improves Halfcheetah return from 6690 to 7678 (+14.8%), Hopper from 2642 to 3045 (+15.2%), Reach from −10.39 to −5.32 (+48.8%), and attitude control success rate from 0.38 to 0.54 (+42.1%). Against the annealed-KL group (Reincarnating RL, TGRL), the gains are similar in magnitude, with the largest relative improvement again on attitude control (0.30→0.54, +80%). Against PROTO (EMA regularization), which is the strongest single-iteration baseline, IRPO still leads by 10.4% on Halfcheetah, 14.6% on Hopper, 38.7% on Reach, and 42.1% on attitude control. Notably, IRPO also exhibits substantially lower variance (e.g., ±60.81 vs. ±597.12 on Halfcheetah), indicating more stable convergence. The paper attributes these gains to IRPO's iterative demonstration boosting, which progressively raises the quality of the reference policy and mitigates both the over-constrained exploration and primacy bias problems that limit all single-pass offline-to-online baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the offline-to-online RL paradigm with KL regularization (AlphaStar, VPT, PIRLNAV, PROTO, TGRL) by identifying a fundamental limitation when demonstrations are imperfect: KL regularization not only stabilizes training but also formally bounds the achievable improvement over the demonstrator (Theorem 4.2). It departs from prior regularization strategies (fixed, annealed, EMA) by arguing the root cause is demonstration quality rather than regularization tuning, and introduces iterative training with demonstration boosting as the remedy. The primacy bias concept from Nikishin et al. (2022) is repurposed to explain why imperfect demonstrations degrade online fine-tuning, and the demonstration update operator draws on data curation ideas from the imperfect-demonstration IL literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical explanation for why standard KL-regularized fine-tuning plateaus with imperfect demonstrations, shifting the focus from regularization scheduling to data quality improvement. The iterative framework is practical and shows consistent gains across diverse tasks (articulated-body, robotic arm, UAV control), including a novel fixed-wing UAV benchmark. Key limitations include validation only on on-policy RL (PPO), task-dependent indicator function design, and the open question of whether the approach transfers to off-policy settings or larger-scale environments. The finding that rolled-out data outperforms retaining the policy for the next iteration suggests a broader principle about data-centric iterative improvement in RL.
