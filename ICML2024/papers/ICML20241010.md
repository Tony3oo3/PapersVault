## Problem

Pessimistic penalties in offline RL based on concentration bounds (e.g., Azuma-Hoeffding LCBs) implicitly require unimodal transition distributions, which is frequently violated in volatile or multimodal environments, leading to overly conservative or insufficient penalties. The paper asks whether one can quantify the information in a batch dataset in a theoretically justified yet practically computable way, and whether mismatch conditions underlying sample complexity guarantees can be generalized to multimodal transitions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a fixed offline dataset of (s, a, r, s') tuples collected by a behavioral policy over finite discrete state and action spaces, along with a discount factor γ and a pessimism coefficient α. It outputs a policy π̂, a value function V(s), and a Q-function Q(s,a). Unusually, the per-state-action penalty is computed as a V-statistic of the Discrete Stein Discrepancy kernel evaluated over pairs of next-states from the dataset, requiring an estimated (or known) transition matrix to compute the discrete score function.

## Method

The core contribution replaces LCB-based pessimistic penalties with a Discrete Stein Discrepancy (DSD) penalty that measures the distributional distance between offline data and the true transition model per state-action pair. The key insight is that the spurious correlation |(P_{s,a} − P̂_{s,a})V| is bounded by the DSD with probability 1 (not merely high probability), and the bound holds under a mixture-family assumption on transitions rather than unimodality. The DSD is made computable by restricting the test function class to an RKHS with a positive-definite Hamming kernel, yielding a closed-form kernel expression involving the discrete score function of the transition distribution. When the true score function is unavailable, an optimistic transition model estimate (via a convex polytope constraint à la Jaksch et al.) is used as a plug-in. Two algorithms are derived—IDP-VI (pessimistic value iteration) and IDP-Q (asynchronous Q-learning)—both achieving sublinear regret that improves upon prior LCB-based rates by a factor of 1/(1−γ) for value iteration, while generalizing to multimodal transitions.

## Results

The metric is test cumulative return (higher is better) across five tabular MDP environments under 'Hard' and 'Random' data sampling regimes. IDP-VI achieves 59.74 on Portfolio-Hard (vs. 14.22 for VI-LCB with paper constants and 15.69 with tuned constants) and 35.80 on Random-MDP-Random (vs. 1.44 for VI-LCB). IDP-Q matches the oracle value iteration upper bound (0.73) on Frozen-Lake-Hard and achieves 26.65 on Random-MDP-Random (vs. 2.41 for Q-learning with LCB). The gains are most pronounced in environments with multimodal transitions (Portfolio, PriorMDP, Random MDP) and smaller batch sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDP-VI substantially outperforms VI-LCB (Rashidinejad et al., 2021) in both its paper-constant and tuned variants across all five environments, with the largest gap on Portfolio-Hard (59.74 vs. 14.22/15.69) and Random-MDP-Random (35.80 vs. 1.44/31.04). IDP-Q matches the oracle value iteration upper bound on Frozen-Lake-Hard (0.73 vs. 0.73) and dominates Q-learning with LCB (Shi et al., 2022) on every environment, most dramatically on Random-MDP-Random (26.65 vs. 2.41) and PriorMDP-Random (15.50 vs. 7.41). Against CQL (Kumar et al., 2020), both IDP variants achieve higher returns in all settings, with IDP-VI reaching 59.74 vs. CQL's 35.93 on Portfolio-Hard. Standard Q-learning (Watkins & Dayan, 1992) without pessimism is competitive on unimodal environments (e.g., 0.69 on FL-H) but collapses on multimodal ones (0.03 on DeepSea-Random), whereas IDP-Q maintains 0.09. The oracle VI (Sutton & Barto, 1998) with true model access remains the upper bound, and IDP-VI approaches it within 2–4% on most tasks, confirming that the DSD penalty recovers most of the information lost to distribution mismatch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate threads: pessimistic offline RL (Rashidinejad et al. 2021 for value iteration, Yan et al. 2023 for Q-learning), which relies on Azuma-Hoeffding or Bernstein concentration bounds that implicitly assume unimodal transitions, and Kernelized Stein Discrepancy (Gorham & Mackey 2015; Yang et al. 2018 for the discrete case), a one-sample goodness-of-fit test from Bayesian inference. The key conceptual move is repurposing 'Stein information'—recently used as an exploration incentive in online model-based RL (Chakraborty et al. 2023a)—as a pessimism penalty in the offline setting, where it quantifies distributional mismatch between batch data and the true transition model under a mixture-family assumption. The optimistic transition estimation from Jaksch et al. (2010) is adapted to construct an evaluable score function when the true model is unknown.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and empirically demonstrates that the unimodality assumption implicit in LCB-based pessimism is frequently violated in volatile or multimodal MDPs, causing LCB penalties to be miscalibrated (overly conservative in sparse regions, insufficiently so in dense ones). The DSD penalty provides a deterministic (probability-1) bound on spurious correlation and improves the value-iteration regret by a factor of 1/(1−γ) over prior LCB results, while generalizing to mixture-family transitions. However, the gap with the sharpest existing rates (Li et al. 2022; Yan et al. 2023) remains because those leverage Bernstein bounds and variance reduction across multiple time-scales, and the methods are restricted to tabular MDPs with computational cost O(Tb²) per pass. Open questions include whether DSD-based pessimism extends to function-approximation settings, whether combining DSD with variance reduction can close the rate gap, and whether the score-function estimation step introduces bias that degrades performance in high-dimensional or continuous-state problems.
