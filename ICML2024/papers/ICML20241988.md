## Problem

Online tracking of unknown, non-stationary targets in linear quadratic systems has been studied primarily through direct control paradigms that learn the policy parameters directly, or through methods that measure learning complexity by the total variation of the target state itself. These approaches are suboptimal for targets with underlying dynamic structure (e.g., circular or constant-velocity motion), where the target state changes every step but the governing dynamics remain fixed. This paper addresses the gap by developing an indirect online tracking algorithm with dynamic regret guarantees that scale with the variation of the target *dynamics* rather than the target *state*, and by validating the approach on real quadrotor hardware.

## Input / Output

At each time step, the algorithm receives the current system state x_t ∈ R^n and the sequentially revealed target state r_t ∈ R^n (the target is not known in advance). It produces a control input u_t ∈ R^m (thrust and angular rates for the quadrotor) and W predicted future target states r_{t+1|t}, …, r_{t+W|t}. The target is assumed to follow time-varying autoregressive dynamics r_{t+1} = S_{t+1} z_t with unknown matrices S_t, and the system dynamics (A, B) are known. The prediction horizon W and forgetting factor γ are the two tunable hyperparameters, with Corollary 4.2 providing explicit tuning rules in terms of the path length V_T and horizon T.

## Method

PLOT decouples tracking into two steps at every time step: (1) target prediction and (2) receding horizon control. For prediction, it maintains W independent RLS predictors (one for each step-ahead horizon k = 1, …, W), each of which uses k non-overlapping learners to handle the delayed feedback inherent in k-step-ahead prediction (following Joulani et al., 2013). Each learner performs recursive least squares with exponential forgetting and projection onto the bounded set S, adapted from Yuan & Lamperski (2020). For control, it plugs the predicted target states into the closed-form LQT optimal controller (feedback gain K plus a feedforward sum of exponentially decaying gains K_i applied to predicted target differences), solving a finite-horizon quadratic program over a window of length W. The key theoretical contribution is a dynamic regret bound of O(√(T·V_T)) where V_T is the total variation of the target dynamics S_t, achieved by combining the performance difference lemma (converting control regret to prediction regret) with the RLS prediction regret analysis. Unlike direct methods that learn the affine control term, PLOT's indirect architecture exploits the dynamic structure of the target, yielding logarithmic regret when the dynamics are static and avoiding the extra logarithmic factors from delayed learning feedback.

## Results

The paper proves that PLOT achieves a dynamic regret bound of O(√(TV_T)), where V_T is the total variation of the target dynamics S_t, and O(log T) when the target is static (V_T = 0). In simulations on a challenging non-stationary target (switching circular dynamics over T=200 s), PLOT consistently achieves the lowest accumulated dynamic regret among all compared online control methods. On hardware (Crazyflie 2.1 quadrotor tracking an 'infinity'-shaped reference over T=40 s), PLOT with W=5 and γ=0.8 achieves visibly smaller tracking error than Naive LQR, which exhibits delayed tracking behavior. For a spiral target with V_T = O(√T), the theoretically tuned forgetting factor γ_0.25 = 1 − 1.5T^(−0.25) consistently outperforms other forgetting factor choices across horizons T = 150–300 s.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLOT is compared against five baselines in simulation (Figure 5) on a non-stationary target with switching circular dynamics over T=200 s: DAP with memory (Agarwal et al., 2019), Riccatron (Foster & Simchowitz, 2020), SS-OGD (Karapetyan et al., 2023), FTL (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2014), and Naive LQR (state-feedback only). PLOT outperforms all five baselines in accumulated dynamic regret. The paper attributes this advantage to two design choices: (i) PLOT uses a dynamic forgetting-factor RLS that adapts to fast-changing reference dynamics, whereas DAP, Riccatron, SS-OGD, and FTL are static or direct approaches that produce a much smaller affine (feedforward) term for this dynamic target; and (ii) PLOT follows an indirect paradigm—learning the target's AR dynamics and then applying certainty-equivalent receding-horizon control—rather than directly learning the affine control term. All direct/static baselines perform close to Naive LQR (no feedforward) on this particular target, while PLOT's learned feedforward term provides a substantial tracking improvement. The paper notes that for fully unstructured targets lacking dynamic structure, the indirect approach may not have an advantage, and PLOT's regret order is still no worse than prior work when learning over ARX models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of adaptive control (RLS with forgetting factors, à la Yuan & Lamperski 2020) and online non-stochastic control (performance difference lemma and LQT structure from Foster & Simchowitz 2020). It departs from prior dynamic-regret work on LQT (Li et al. 2019; Karapetyan et al. 2023) in two key ways: (i) it uses an indirect architecture that learns a model of the target dynamics rather than directly parameterizing the affine control term, and (ii) it characterizes complexity via the total variation of the target dynamics S_t rather than the target state r_t, yielding logarithmic regret for structured targets (e.g., circular motion) where prior methods give linear regret. The delayed-feedback handling for multi-step prediction follows Joulani et al. (2013), decomposing k-step prediction into k non-overlapping learners.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The shift from target-state variation to target-dynamics variation as the regret complexity measure is a meaningful conceptual advance: it recovers logarithmic regret for common structured targets (constant velocity, circular) that prior bounds treat as linearly complex. The paper also provides one of the first successful demonstrations of online non-stochastic control with theoretical guarantees on real hardware (Crazyflie quadrotor), bridging a gap between the mature theory and its practical adoption. Open questions include extending guarantees to non-realizable targets (where the AR model is only approximate), output tracking, non-quadratic costs, and whether the indirect paradigm can be combined with MPC constraints for more complex tracking scenarios.
