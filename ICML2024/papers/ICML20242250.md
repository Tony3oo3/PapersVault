## Problem

Existing fluid prediction methods either directly regress velocity fields with deep models (which overwhelms the network with complex, entangled dynamics and yields uninterpretable results) or rely on numerical solvers that are computationally expensive and sensitive to imperfect observations. The core gap is the absence of a data-driven approach that explicitly leverages the inherent physical structure of fluid motion—specifically the Helmholtz decomposition—so that the learned dynamics are both more tractable and physically interpretable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242250/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a sequence of T past 2D fluid frames (e.g., 10 vorticity frames at 64×64–256×256 for Navier-Stokes, 2 geopotential frames at 72×144 for ERA5 Z500, or 100–126 RGB video frames for Spreading Ink) and an optional binary boundary-condition mask, and produces the next 10–50 future frames. Internally it also outputs the decomposed potential function Φ, stream function A, and the corresponding curl-free and divergence-free velocity components at each prediction step, providing physically interpretable evidence for the prediction.

## Method

HelmFluid replaces direct velocity regression with a HelmDynamics block that faithfully implements the Helmholtz decomposition: it embeds past observations into deep representations, computes spatiotemporal correlations (analogous to RAFT) augmented with boundary-condition masking, then decodes scalar potential and stream functions from these correlations and derives the velocity field as ∇Φ + ∇×A. These per-head Helmholtz dynamics are integrated along the temporal axis using a second-order Runge-Kutta scheme with BFECC (back-and-forth error compensation and correction) for accurate position mapping. A U-Net-style multiscale encoder produces representations at L spatial scales, and the learned dynamics are ensembled coarse-to-fine with residual connections to ensure velocity consistency across scales. The multihead design (M heads) allows different heads to capture distinct dynamic patterns, and the entire architecture is trained end-to-end with a standard L2 loss on the predicted fluid field, requiring no ground-truth velocity supervision.

## Results

HelmFluid achieves state-of-the-art performance across all five main benchmarks. On the Navier-Stokes dataset (64×64), it attains a relative L2 of 0.1261, a 12.1% improvement over the second-best U-NO (0.1435), and maintains the best result at 128×128 (0.0807) and 256×256 (0.1310) resolutions. On the Bounded N-S dataset with complex boundaries, HelmFluid reaches 0.0652 relative L2 (11.5% better than LSM at 0.0737), and on ERA5 Z500 it achieves 521.44 RMSE, surpassing FourCastNet (594.49) and LSM (561.27). On the long-horizon Spreading Ink task, HelmFluid reduces perceptual loss by 7.6%, relative L2 by 12.1%, and MSE by 22.3% compared to the best baseline (Vortex).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HelmFluid outperforms all nine baselines across every benchmark. Against the strongest neural operator baselines, it beats U-NO by 12.1% relative L2 on Navier-Stokes 64×64 and by 11.5% on Bounded N-S (0.0652 vs. LSM's 0.0737), while also surpassing FNO (0.1556) and MWT (0.1586) by wide margins. On the real-world ERA5 Z500 weather forecasting task, HelmFluid (521.44 RMSE) outperforms FourCastNet (594.49) by 12.3% and LSM (561.27) by 7.1%, demonstrating its advantage over both dedicated weather models and general-purpose neural operators. The numerical method DARTS is far inferior (0.8046 relative L2 on Navier-Stokes vs. 0.1261 for HelmFluid), confirming the limitations of purely physics-based solvers under imperfect observations. On the long-horizon Spreading Ink task, HelmFluid dominates Vortex (the closest fluid-dynamics competitor) by 7.6% perceptual loss, 12.1% relative L2, and 22.3% MSE, while PWC-Net-based optical flow approaches suffer from severe accumulation errors and are not competitive. The ablation in Table 6 further confirms that directly learning velocity (as in PWC-Net/RAFT-style methods) degrades performance by 10.7% compared to the Helmholtz dynamics decomposition, validating the core design choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on RAFT's spatiotemporal correlation framework for dynamics estimation and BFECC for temporal integration, but departs fundamentally from both the neural operator paradigm (FNO, U-NO, LSM) that directly predicts future fields and the optical-flow paradigm (PWC-Net, Vortex) that directly estimates velocity. The key conceptual departure is introducing Helmholtz decomposition as an intermediate representation: rather than learning a monolithic velocity field, the model learns physically meaningful potential and stream functions whose gradients and curls compose the velocity. This positions HelmFluid as a new 'Helmholtz dynamics' paradigm between pure data-driven operators and explicit PDE solvers, with a multiscale multihead architecture inspired by U-NO's U-shaped design and attention's multihead mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By grounding fluid prediction in the Helmholtz theorem, the paper provides physically interpretable intermediate quantities (potential and stream functions) that are absent from black-box neural operators, which is critical for scientific trust in applications like weather forecasting and airfoil design. The consistent SOTA across five diverse benchmarks (simulated Navier-Stokes, bounded domains, ERA5 atmospheric data, ocean temperature, and real ink videos) demonstrates broad applicability. Open questions include whether the approach scales to truly 3D large-scale climate modeling (only a small 3D smoke experiment is provided), whether the Helmholtz decomposition generalizes to compressible or multi-phase flows, and whether the multiscale multihead architecture's added complexity is necessary at larger scales. The paper also notes future plans to extend to world climate and ocean current modeling.
