## Problem

Individual treatment effect (ITE) estimation models face a fundamental trade-off: black-box models (e.g., transformers) achieve high accuracy but their post-hoc explanations lack faithfulness guarantees, while self-interpretable models (e.g., Causal Forest) produce consistent explanations but sacrifice accuracy significantly. DISCRET asks whether a single framework can simultaneously deliver provably faithful rule-based explanations and competitive ITE prediction accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242196/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

DISCRET takes a sample's covariates (tabular features, TF-IDF text features, or 20 extracted image concepts from satellite imagery) and a training database of labeled samples, and produces both an ITE estimate and a human-readable conjunctive/disjunctive logic rule (e.g., 'weight ≤ 1.5 AND mom_age ≤ 19 AND drug_use = yes'). The rule is not merely descriptive: it is executed as a database query to retrieve a subgroup of similar samples, over which the ATE is computed as the ITE estimate. The rule length (number of conjunctions K and disjunctions H) is a user-specified hyperparameter, and the non-emptiness constraint ensures the retrieved subgroup contains both treated and untreated units.

## Method

DISCRET operates in two steps: (1) explanation synthesis, where three neural components—a backbone encoder Θ₀ (e.g., TransTEE encoder), a feature-selector Θ₁, and a threshold-selector Θ₂—iteratively generate K literals of a logic rule for the input sample; and (2) explanation evaluation, where the rule is executed as a database query to retrieve a subgroup, and the ITE is estimated as the empirical ATE over that subgroup. Because rule execution is non-differentiable, training is formulated as a Deep Q-learning problem with a reward function based on the squared outcome prediction error of the retrieved subgroup, regularized by estimated propensity scores. A key theoretical result (Theorem 3.1) proves that under a discrete-feature data-generating process, the learned ITE converges to zero generalization error with probability 1. Additionally, DISCRET's predictions can regularize the backbone black-box model via weighted averaging, yielding state-of-the-art accuracy (e.g., 18% error reduction on TCGA) while retaining interpretability.

## Results

DISCRET achieves near-perfect consistency scores (≥0.789, often 1.00) across all six datasets, far exceeding post-hoc explainers (e.g., Anchor: 0.000–0.293, LIME: 0.000–0.182). On ITE estimation accuracy, DISCRET outperforms all self-interpretable baselines, achieving ε_ATE = 0.001 on EEEC (vs. 0.011 for Causal Forest, a 91% reduction) and ε_outcome = 1.662 on Uganda (vs. 1.707 for TransTEE). The regularized variant TransTEE+DISCRET achieves the best overall accuracy, reducing TCGA out-of-sample ε_ATE from 0.067 (TransTEE) to 0.055 (an 18% improvement) and IHDP-C AMSE from 0.112 to 0.102.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISCRET outperforms all self-interpretable baselines across every dataset: on EEEC it achieves ε_ATE = 0.001 vs. Causal Forest's 0.011 (a 10× improvement), and on IHDP it reaches 0.150 out-of-sample vs. Causal Forest's 0.240. Against black-box models, DISCRET is competitive on most tasks—beating TransTEE on Uganda (1.662 vs. 1.707) and EEEC (0.001 vs. 0.003)—but underperforms TransTEE on IHDP-C (AMSE 0.801 vs. 0.112), likely due to the dataset's highly non-linear data-generating process. The regularized TransTEE+DISCRET variant achieves the best accuracy overall, reducing TCGA out-of-sample error by 18% (0.055 vs. 0.067) and IHDP-C AMSE by 9% (0.102 vs. 0.112) over TransTEE alone. Among post-hoc explainers, DISCRET's consistency scores (0.789–1.00) dwarf those of Anchor (0.000–0.293), LIME (0.000–0.182), SHAP (0.005–0.412), and Model Distillation (0.127–0.816), confirming that post-hoc explanations are rarely faithful. On the News dataset (continuous treatment), TransTEE+DISCRET achieves AMSE 0.043, outperforming TransTEE (0.063) and DRNet (0.065).

## Relation to Prior Work

DISCRET sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) ITE estimation via neural architectures (TransTEE, Dragonnet, TARNet), (2) self-interpretable causal models (Causal Forest, BART, ENRL), and (3) program/rule synthesis via neural methods (NeuralLP, NLIL). It departs from prior self-interpretable ITE methods by replacing static tree structures with dynamically synthesized per-sample logic rules learned via a tailored Deep Q-learning algorithm, and from prior rule-based treatment recommendation (Lakkaraju & Rudin, 2017) by enabling per-sample rule generation rather than pre-determined partitions. The key conceptual departure is treating explanations dually as database queries, which makes faithfulness (consistency) a structural guarantee rather than an empirical property, and the regularization of black-box models with DISCRET predictions is a novel technique not present in prior ITE literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DISCRET addresses a long-standing tension in causal ML: self-interpretable models are faithful but inaccurate, while accurate black-box models lack provably faithful explanations. The provable consistency-by-construction and the demonstrated ability to regularize state-of-the-art transformers (18% error reduction on TCGA) make this practically relevant for high-stakes deployment in healthcare and policy. Key limitations remain: the rule grammar and hyperparameters (K, H, λ) must be fixed a priori, and the method depends on pre-extracted interpretable concepts for unstructured data (images, text), which may not always be available. Open questions include whether the rule grammar itself can be learned, whether the RL training scales to very high-dimensional feature spaces, and whether the consistency guarantee holds under distribution shift.
