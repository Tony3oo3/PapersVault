## Problem

Gaussian process regression is widely deployed for small-scale regression tasks with uncertainty quantification, yet it is almost universally implemented with 64-bit double-precision floating-point arithmetic, incurring unnecessary energy cost. It is unknown what minimum precision suffices for acceptable model performance, and how the condition number of the kernel matrix, the chosen solver, and the numerical format interact to determine both accuracy and power consumption.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training feature matrix X (500 samples), a training target vector y, and a test feature matrix X_* (500 samples) from tabular regression datasets, with the RBF length-scale hyperparameter optimized via BFGS in double precision. The key experimental variable is the mantissa precision t (ranging from 3 to 53 bits) and exponent width (11 or 15 bits) applied to all arithmetic during kernel matrix inversion and substitution. Outputs are the predictive mean and covariance, evaluated via RMSE on train/test sets and a proposed uncertainty calibration (UC) metric that averages deviations across 99 confidence levels.

## Method

The paper empirically evaluates two kernel-matrix inversion strategies—Cholesky-Banachiewicz decomposition and a modified conjugate gradient algorithm (with rescaling, logsumexp, and Gram-Schmidt reorthogonalization from Maddox et al., 2022)—at arbitrary floating-point precisions using the GMPY2 arbitrary-precision library. Power consumption is benchmarked at the circuit level by implementing carry-ripple adders and add-and-shift multipliers in VHDL on a Xilinx Kintex-7 FPGA, measuring energy for 4- to 64-bit representations and linking them to software experiments via linear regression. The central finding is that well-conditioned kernel matrices (condition number ≤ 10³) require only 8 mantissa bits for competitive performance, while ill-conditioned matrices (up to 10⁷) need 14–34 bits depending on the solver. Numerical instabilities at higher precisions are shown to stem from insufficient exponent range rather than precision, and are resolved by widening the exponent from 11 to 15 bits. This yields energy reductions of up to 89.01% for 98.08% of arithmetic operations (conjugate gradient) and 88.94% for 87.43% (Cholesky), with test RMSE deviations below ±0.02 and uncertainty calibration shifts below ±0.03.

## Results

For well-conditioned kernel matrices (condition number ≤ 1e3), 8-bit mantissa precision achieves competitive model performance (ΔTest RMSE ≤ 0.01, ΔUC ≤ 0.02) while reducing energy consumption by up to 89.01% across 96.96%–98.08% of all arithmetic operations (conjugate gradient) or 88.94% across 83.27%–87.43% of operations (Cholesky decomposition). For the ill-conditioned California Housing dataset (condition number ~3.4e6), conjugate gradient requires 14 bits for competitive performance (−75.99% energy) while Cholesky requires 34 bits (−40.02% energy). The modified conjugate gradient approach consistently requires lower minimum precision than Cholesky decomposition across all conditioning levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it benchmarks different mantissa precisions (3–53 bits) against the standard 53-bit double-precision reference for both conjugate gradient and Cholesky decomposition implementations. The conjugate gradient approach (modified per Maddox et al., 2022 with logsumexp and reorthogonalization) consistently outperforms Cholesky decomposition in terms of minimum required precision: 8 bits suffice for well-conditioned matrices with CG, while Cholesky also needs 8 bits but requires 14 bits for the moderately conditioned 'pl' dataset versus 8 bits for CG, and 34 bits for the ill-conditioned 'ch' dataset versus 14 bits for CG. In terms of energy savings, CG achieves up to 89.01% reduction across 96.96%–98.08% of operations, while Cholesky achieves up to 88.94% but only across 83.27%–87.43% of operations due to its higher minimum precision requirements. The paper also demonstrates that increasing exponent bits (11→15) resolves numerical instabilities at higher precisions without increasing mantissa precision, offering a more energy-efficient stability fix than simply using wider formats.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Maddox et al. (2022), who demonstrated that half-precision conjugate gradients can process large Gaussian process datasets, by exploring arbitrary low-precision representations (3–53 bits) for small datasets and adding Cholesky decomposition as a second implementation approach. It grounds its empirical evaluation in the classical numerical linear algebra error bounds of Martin et al. (1965) and Greenbaum et al. (2021), which identify precision, matrix size, and conditioning as the key error drivers but do not prescribe a usable precision for end-to-end GPR. The paper's distinctive contribution is a circuit-level (FPGA) power benchmark of arithmetic operations, positioning the work as platform-independent relative energy savings rather than a system-level throughput study.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that 8 mantissa bits suffice for well-conditioned kernel matrices and that increasing exponent width (rather than precision) resolves numerical instability—gives library implementers and hardware designers concrete, actionable guidance for reducing energy in the large volume of small-scale GPR workloads. The finding generalizes beyond GPR to any symmetric full-rank matrix inversion, broadening its relevance. Key limitations include the restriction to 500-sample datasets, a single RBF kernel, and two specific solvers; the authors identify automated precision selection based on condition number and a theoretical link between conditioning and error distribution as the main open questions for future work.
