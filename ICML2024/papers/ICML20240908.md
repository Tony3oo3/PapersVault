## Problem

GNNs for graph classification are limited to capturing only local pairwise neighborhood information via message-passing, making them unable to model higher-order dependencies among nodes (e.g., long-range atomic interactions in molecules). Additionally, in semi-supervised settings where labeled graphs are scarce and expensive to annotate, the structural information contained in abundant unlabeled graphs remains underexploited. HEAL addresses both gaps by learning higher-order node relationships through hypergraphs and leveraging unlabeled graphs via a relational consistency mechanism.

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of graphs partitioned into labeled and unlabeled subsets (default split 2:5:1:2, with 50% of labeled data used for training), where each graph is defined by a node feature matrix and adjacency matrix. A memory bank of M anchor graphs sampled from the labeled set is maintained and dynamically updated via a FIFO queue across iterations. The output is a predicted class label per graph, obtained by concatenating the hypergraph-branch and line-graph-branch graph-level representations and passing them through an MLP classifier.

## Method

HEAL consists of three modules operating in a dual-branch architecture. First, a learnable hypergraph structure is parameterized in low-rank form (Λ = H·W) from GNN (GIN) node embeddings, and a hypergraph convolution with a trainable mixing matrix U and residual connection updates node representations to capture higher-order dependencies. Second, a line graph is constructed from the learned hypergraph—where each node corresponds to a hyperedge and edges are weighted by Jaccard overlap of shared nodes—and a standard GNN encoder is applied to this line graph to capture inter-hyperedge interactions. Third, relational consistency learning computes softmax-normalized cosine similarity distributions between each unlabeled graph's representation and a memory bank of anchor graph representations in both branches, then minimizes the symmetric KL divergence between the two distributions to enforce mutual guidance. The total loss combines supervised cross-entropy on labeled graphs with the consistency loss weighted by β=0.01, optimized via Adam.

## Results

HEAL achieves the best classification accuracy across all six benchmark datasets: 73.4% on PROTEINS, 72.1% on DD, 73.5% on IMDB-B, 44.3% on IMDB-M, 45.9% on REDDIT-M-5k, and 68.3% on COLLAB (all with 50% labeled data, i.e., 10% of total samples). The gains over the strongest baselines (TGNN and KGNN) range from +1.1% (IMDB-M) to +2.1% (REDDIT-M-5k). HEAL also maintains its advantage as the labeling ratio increases, as shown in the sensitivity experiments on PROTEINS and REDDIT-M-5k.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HEAL outperforms all 11 baselines on all six datasets. Against the closest competitors, TGNN (71.0/70.8/72.8/42.9/43.8/67.7) and KGNN (70.9/70.5/72.5/43.3/44.8/67.4), HEAL gains 1.1–2.1 percentage points in accuracy, with the largest margin on REDDIT-M-5k (+2.1 over TGNN) and PROTEINS (+2.4 over TGNN). The graph-specific semi-supervised methods (DualGraph, KGNN, TGNN) form a clear performance tier well above conventional semi-supervised approaches (EntMin, Mean-Teacher, VAT) and traditional graph kernels (WL, Sub2Vec, Graph2Vec), with HEAL extending the lead by another 2–3 points over the best graph-specific method. Notably, on IMDB-M (small graphs with ~13 nodes), DualGraph (44.8%) slightly edges out HEAL (44.3%), which the authors attribute to hypergraph construction being less beneficial for very small-scale graphs. Overall, the consistent wins across bioinformatics (PROTEINS, DD), social network (IMDB-B, IMDB-M, REDDIT-M-5k), and collaboration (COLLAB) datasets demonstrate the robustness of the hypergraph + line graph dual-branch design.

## Relation to Prior Work

HEAL builds on the semi-supervised graph classification line of work (ASGN, DualGraph, KGNN, TGNN) by replacing the standard GNN message-passing encoder with a dual-branch architecture: a learnable hypergraph convolution for higher-order node dependencies and a line graph convolution for hyperedge interactions. The hypergraph structure learning module departs from predefined hypergraph construction (distance-, representation-, or attribute-based) by parameterizing the incidence matrix via a low-rank factorization jointly optimized with the network. The relational consistency loss, which aligns KL divergence between similarity distributions over a memory bank of anchor graphs, extends prior consistency/contrastive approaches (FixMatch, InfoNCE) by operating on relational structure rather than direct representation alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine limitation of GNNs—incapacity to model higher-order dependencies beyond pairwise neighborhoods—by introducing a principled hypergraph-to-line-graph pipeline that captures both intra-substructure and inter-substructure semantics. The relational consistency mechanism offers a more flexible alternative to pseudo-labeling for semi-supervised settings, and the ablation confirms its superiority over InfoNCE, MSE, and FixMatch. However, evaluation is limited to six small-scale graph datasets (average node counts in the tens), and the method underperforms on IMDB-M where graphs are too small for hypergraph modeling to add value, raising open questions about scalability to larger, denser graphs and whether the learned hypergraph structure generalizes beyond classification.
