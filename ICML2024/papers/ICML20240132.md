## Problem

Gradient-based parameter estimation in ODEs is unreliable because highly nonlinear dynamics create many local minima and extreme sensitivity to initial conditions, causing optimizers to converge to spurious solutions. While the Fenrir probabilistic integrator smooths the loss surface by jointly optimizing a diffusion hyperparameter κ with the ODE parameters, this joint optimization still fails to converge reliably for complex systems such as the Hodgkin-Huxley model with practically relevant parameter counts.

## Input / Output

The method consumes noisy time-series observations of a partially observed ODE solution (u_i = Hy_θ(t_i) + ε_i, ε_i ~ N(0,R)) on a discrete time grid, the parametric vector field f_θ, and a tempering schedule that specifies a sequence of diffusion values κ. It outputs a maximum likelihood parameter estimate θ̂ obtained by sequentially running L-BFGS on the Fenrir PN-approximated marginal likelihood at each scheduled κ. The ODE is solved internally via a 3-times integrated Wiener process prior with extended Kalman filtering, and parameters are optimized in a normalized [0,1] space with box constraints.

## Method

The core insight is that the diffusion hyperparameter κ in probabilistic ODE solvers acts as a regularization knob: high κ yields a smooth loss landscape where most parameters score similar likelihoods (escaping local minima but fitting data poorly), while low κ yields a sharp landscape with a well-defined global optimum but many local minima. Diffusion tempering exploits this by solving a sequence of optimization problems with progressively decreasing κ (linearly reducing log₁₀κ from 20 to 0 over 21 steps), where each step's converged solution initializes the next. At each step, the Fenrir PN-approximated marginal likelihood is computed via forward and backward Kalman filtering on a Gauss-Markov state-space model, and L-BFGS with backtracking line search optimizes θ while κ is held fixed. This decouples the regularization schedule from parameter optimization, ensuring the optimizer first navigates the smooth landscape to escape local minima before refining toward true parameters as the landscape sharpens. An optional early stopping criterion (OURS+) terminates intermediate steps when the log-likelihood change falls below a threshold, reducing total iterations by roughly 30% without sacrificing final accuracy.

## Results

Diffusion tempering (OURS) achieves near-perfect convergence (CONV = 1.00) on the 1-parameter pendulum, 2-parameter HH, and 4-parameter HH2 models, with tRMSE values of 0.02, 0.42, and 0.60 respectively, compared to FENRIR's 0.04, 5.89, and 12.00 and RK's 0.98, 4.88, and 7.98. The most dramatic gain is on the 3-parameter HH1 model, where diffusion tempering converges 99% of the time (tRMSE = 0.60) versus only 51% for FENRIR (tRMSE = 9.31) and 3% for RK (tRMSE = 14.33). On the challenging 6-parameter HH2 model, diffusion tempering recovers 5.28 of 6 parameters correctly (CONV = 0.88, tRMSE = 3.01) while RK fails entirely (CONV = 0.00, tRMSE = 19.36). The early-stopping variant (OURS+) reduces iteration cost by ~3–4× with negligible performance loss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Diffusion tempering (OURS) decisively outperforms both baselines on convergence reliability (CONV) across nearly all model sizes. Against FENRIR (Tronarp et al., 2022), the gain is most striking on the 3-parameter HH1 model (99% vs 51% convergence) and the 6-parameter HH2 model (88% vs 50%), where FENRIR's jointly-learned diffusion fails to avoid local minima. Against RK least-squares (Bard, 1974), the advantage is even more extreme: on the 3-parameter HH1, RK converges only 3% of the time versus 99% for diffusion tempering, and on the 6-parameter HH2, RK never converges (0%) while diffusion tempering succeeds 88% of the time. In terms of trajectory quality (tRMSE), diffusion tempering achieves 10–20× lower error than both baselines on the 2-parameter HH1 (0.42 vs 5.89 FENRIR, 4.88 RK) and 4-parameter HH2 (0.60 vs 12.00 FENRIR, 7.98 RK). The one setting where all methods struggle is the 6-parameter HH1, where none reliably converge, though diffusion tempering still yields the lowest tRMSE (15.20 vs 26.21 FENRIR, 16.80 RK) and better electrophysiological summary statistics. The cost of this improvement is roughly 3–10× more optimizer iterations, which the early-stopping variant (OURS+) reduces to ~2× with no measurable loss in convergence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Fenrir framework (Tronarp et al., 2022), which uses probabilistic numerical ODE solvers based on Bayesian filtering to perform gradient-based parameter inference for initial value problems. The key departure is a re-interpretation of the diffusion hyperparameter κ: rather than jointly optimizing it with the ODE parameters θ as Fenrir does, the authors treat κ as a regularization knob and introduce a decreasing tempering schedule over it, drawing conceptually on simulated annealing. The underlying computational engine—extended Kalman filtering with Gauss-Markov priors over integrated Wiener processes—remains unchanged from the probabilistic ODE solver literature (Tronarp et al., 2019; 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Diffusion tempering makes gradient-based parameter estimation feasible for practically relevant ODE models (e.g., a six-parameter Hodgkin-Huxley neuron) where both classical RK least-squares and the original Fenrir fail to converge reliably, which is significant for computational neuroscience and systems biology. However, the method incurs roughly an order-of-magnitude increase in optimization steps, scales cubically with ODE dimension, and assumes known observation noise—limiting its immediate applicability to very large or noisy systems. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to Neural ODEs (which would require adjoint-based gradients in the probabilistic framework), whether the noise covariance can be co-scheduled, and whether problem-aware tempering schedules can close the computational gap.
