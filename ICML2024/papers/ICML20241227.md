## Problem

Classical Tchebycheff scalarization can find all Pareto solutions but is nonsmooth, requiring O(1/ε²) subgradient iterations for slow convergence. Adaptive gradient methods (MGDA, CAGrad, etc.) offer better convergence but incur high pre-iteration complexity from solving a quadratic program and computing m separate backpropagations per step. The paper asks whether a lightweight scalarization can retain the completeness guarantee of Tchebycheff while achieving the fast O(1/ε) convergence of smooth optimization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a preference vector λ on the (m−1)-simplex, m differentiable objective functions f_i(x), an initial point x₀, and a scalar smoothing parameter μ, then runs plain gradient descent on the smoothed surrogate. Unlike adaptive gradient methods, it requires only a single forward/backward pass per iteration (no per-objective backprop or QP solve). For Pareto set learning, the output extends to a parametric model h_θ(λ) that maps any preference to its corresponding Pareto solution, trained by sampling λ online.

## Method

The core idea is to replace the non-differentiable max operator in Tchebycheff scalarization with a log-sum-exp (infimal convolution) smoothing, yielding g_μ^STCH(x|λ) = μ log Σ_i exp(λ_i(f_i(x)−z_i*)/μ). This is a proper smoothing function in the sense of Beck & Teboulle (2012): it is C¹, uniformly bounded within μ log m of the original, and has Lipschitz-continuous gradient with constant K + α/μ. The gradient decomposes as a softmax-weighted combination of individual objective gradients, so a single backpropagation suffices. The paper proves that under mild curvature assumptions on the Pareto front, for sufficiently small μ the STCH level surfaces are enclosed by the Pareto front, guaranteeing that every Pareto solution is recoverable for some λ; for convex Pareto fronts this holds for any μ. A stationary point of the smoothed objective is shown to be a Pareto stationary point of the original problem, matching the guarantee of adaptive gradient methods at far lower per-iteration cost.

## Results

On NYUv2 (3 tasks), STCH achieves a relative performance improvement of Δp = +8.54% over single-task learning, second only to DB-MTL (+8.91%) and substantially outperforming the classical Tchebycheff scalarization (TCH, −5.67%). On Office-31 (3 domains), STCH achieves the best overall Δp of +1.48% and is the only method that dominates the single-task baseline on all tasks. On QM9 (11 tasks), STCH matches the best Δp (−58.14 vs. DB-MTL's −58.10) while running at 1.00× the baseline speed, compared to 4–6× for adaptive gradient methods. For Pareto set learning, STCH achieves the lowest hypervolume difference (ΔHV) on 10 out of 11 benchmark problems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

STCH clearly outperforms the classical Tchebycheff scalarization (TCH) across all datasets: on NYUv2, STCH achieves Δp = +8.54 vs. TCH's −5.67; on Office-31, +1.48 vs. −0.71; and on QM9, −58.14 vs. −252.2, demonstrating that smoothness is critical for gradient-based Tchebycheff optimization. Among adaptive gradient methods, STCH is competitive with the strongest baselines: on NYUv2 it trails only DB-MTL (+8.91) and Aligned-MTL (+8.16), while on Office-31 it achieves the best Δp (+1.48), surpassing DB-MTL (+1.05) and MoCo (+0.89). On QM9, STCH ties with DB-MTL (−58.14 vs. −58.10) but runs at 1.00× speed versus 4.56× for DB-MTL, since STCH avoids the per-iteration quadratic programming solve. For Pareto set learning (Table 3), STCH achieves the lowest hypervolume difference on 10 of 11 problems, outperforming linear scalarization (LS), COSMOS, EPO, and TCH; for example, on Rocket Injector, STCH achieves ΔHV = 1.08e-02 vs. TCH's 2.73e-02 and LS's 1.42e-01. STCH also outperforms all adaptive loss methods (UW, DWA, IMTL-L, IGBv2) and simple scalarization baselines (EW, GLS, RLW) on every dataset tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits the classical Tchebycheff scalarization (Steuer & Choo, 1983) — a method long known in multi-objective optimization but rarely used in gradient-based settings due to its nonsmooth max operator — and applies the infimal convolution smoothing framework of Beck & Teboulle (2012) and Nesterov (2005) to replace the max with a log-sum-exp, yielding a differentiable surrogate. Rather than following the dominant trend of proposing new adaptive gradient methods (MGDA, PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL, etc.) that solve a QP at each iteration, the paper deliberately returns to the simpler scalarization paradigm, arguing that the convergence bottleneck of TCH is purely a smoothness issue solvable with standard techniques. The result is a method that inherits the theoretical guarantees of TCH (ability to find all Pareto solutions) while achieving O(1/ε) gradient-based convergence instead of O(1/ε²) subgradient convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STCH provides a drop-in, low-complexity alternative to the growing zoo of adaptive gradient methods for multi-task learning, with the practical advantage of requiring no per-iteration QP solve and no separate backpropagation per objective. The strong theoretical guarantees (Pareto optimality, ability to recover the full Pareto set under mild conditions, Pareto stationarity for non-convex problems) make it a principled baseline that practitioners can adopt immediately. Key open questions include: how to handle constrained and stochastic settings, whether the fixed smoothing parameter μ can be adapted online (homotopy schedules were explored but not clearly beneficial), and whether the approach extends to non-differentiable or combinatorial objectives. The paper also notes that for deep learning applications, local optima are typically sufficient, which mitigates the lack of global convergence guarantees in the non-convex case.
