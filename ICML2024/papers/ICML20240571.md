## Problem

Uncertainty Sampling for active learning on graphs has been largely unexplored beyond total predictive uncertainty, and it remains unclear whether disentangling aleatoric and epistemic components—as proven beneficial for i.i.d. data—transfers to interdependent graph data. This paper provides the first extensive benchmark showing that no existing uncertainty estimator outperforms random acquisition on node classification, and formally investigates whether US on graphs aligns with the active learning objective at all.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a graph G=(V,E) with adjacency matrix A, node feature matrix X∈R^{n×d}, and a partially observed label set y_O, producing per-node class predictions alongside three disentangled uncertainty scores (total, aleatoric, epistemic) that serve as the acquisition function for selecting the next node to query. Unlike standard uncertainty estimators that read off a single model's predictive distribution, the ground-truth definitions require evaluating the joint likelihood of the underlying data-generating process p(A,X,y), and the practical approximations (MP, ESP) require training O(n·c) auxiliary classifiers per acquisition iteration, making the approach computationally expensive but limited to lightweight backbones like SGC or GCN.

## Method

The paper defines ground-truth aleatoric and epistemic confidence for a Bayesian classifier f*θ parameterized by the data-generating process, where aleatoric confidence conditions on all other nodes having their true labels and epistemic confidence is the ratio of aleatoric to total confidence. The central theoretical result (Theorem 5.6) proves that epistemic uncertainty of a node equals the relative gain its label acquisition provides to the posterior probability of all remaining true unobserved labels, establishing epistemic US as an optimal acquisition strategy. Propositions 6.1 and 6.2 show why total and aleatoric uncertainty are suboptimal: total uncertainty is proportional to the posterior after removal (favoring inherently ambiguous nodes), while aleatoric uncertainty is proportional to the posterior without acquisition (ignoring the information gained). For real data where the generative process is unknown, two approximations are proposed: Multiple Pseudo-Labels (MP) substitutes all unobserved labels with model predictions and trains an auxiliary classifier per query, while Expected Single Pseudo-Label (ESP) takes an expectation over a single pseudo-label per class, trading O(n·c) auxiliary trainings for reduced sensitivity to erroneous pseudo-labels.

## Results

The paper's comprehensive benchmark (Table 1) reveals that no existing uncertainty estimator outperforms random sampling for active learning on graphs; most US approaches actually underperform random queries, with only ensemble-based epistemic estimates marginally matching random. Their proposed approximate epistemic uncertainty (ESP) consistently outperforms all existing estimators, achieving AUC of 71.45 on CoraML, 83.43 on Citeseer, 64.36 on PubMed, 85.52 on Amazon Photos, and 72.54 on Amazon Computers. The MP variant achieves 68.16 on CoraML and 83.96 on Citeseer but fails to converge on PubMed and Computers. On the CSBM synthetic benchmark, ground-truth epistemic US significantly outperforms total, aleatoric, and random acquisition, confirming the theoretical optimality result.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's proposed ESP approximation outperforms all five existing epistemic uncertainty estimators (Ensemble, MC-Dropout, Energy, GPN, BGCN) on every dataset where it converges, with margins ranging from 0.66 AUC (PubMed: 64.36 vs. 63.70) to 33.33 AUC (Computers: 72.54 vs. 39.21 for GPN). Among existing methods, Ensemble (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) is the strongest baseline but is still consistently beaten by Ours(ESP) by 1–8 AUC points. MC-Dropout (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) and BGCN (Blundell et al., 2015) underperform random sampling on several datasets, while GPN (Stadler et al., 2021) and Energy-based estimates (Liu et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023) show the weakest performance, particularly on Amazon Computers. Among non-uncertainty strategies, GEEM (Regol et al., 2020) is the only method that consistently outperforms random across datasets, but at O(nC) model-training cost per query; Coreset (Sener & Savarese, 2018), AGE (Cai et al., 2017), ANRMAB (Gao et al., 2018), and SEAL (Li et al., 2020) all fail to reliably beat random sampling. The paper's key finding is that the gap between their method and existing estimators is attributable to proper aleatoric/epistemic disentanglement rather than model architecture, as the same GNN backbone is used throughout.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty disentanglement framework of Kendall & Gal (2017) from i.i.d. data to graph-structured data, and grounds it in the Contextual Stochastic Block Model (Deshpande et al., 2018) to obtain closed-form ground-truth uncertainty definitions. It departs sharply from prior graph active learning work (Zhu et al., 2003; Jun & Nowak, 2016; Regol et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022c), which treats uncertainty as a monolithic total-uncertainty signal without separating reducible from irreducible components. The paper also positions itself against the i.i.d. active learning literature (Nguyen et al., 2022; Kirsch et al., 2019) where epistemic US has been shown effective, asking whether this success transfers to interdependent graph data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central negative finding—that no off-the-shelf uncertainty estimator (ensembles, MC-Dropout, GPN, energy-based) enables US to beat random sampling on graphs—challenges a widely assumed transferability of i.i.d. AL results and redirects the field toward principled uncertainty design. The theoretical proof that epistemic US is optimal for AL on graphs, combined with the requirement that the generative model capture non-edges (not just message-passing along existing edges), provides a clear diagnostic for why current GNN-based uncertainty estimators fail. The main limitation is practical: the proposed approximation requires O(n·c) auxiliary classifier trainings per query, restricting it to small graphs and lightweight models, leaving the development of efficient, theoretically-aligned uncertainty estimators as an open problem.
