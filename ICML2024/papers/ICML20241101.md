## Problem

Training large language models incurs enormous computational costs due to their massive parameter counts, yet prior work has shown these parameters are redundant. Existing incremental training methods (e.g., Staged Training, MSG) train smaller models separately and then expand parameters, which forces them to maintain loss-preserving and training-dynamics-preserving properties, severely limiting achievable cost savings. This paper asks whether one can save training cost by training subnetworks of the full model directly, without the constraints of parameter expansion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241101/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard text sequences from pre-training corpora (OpenWebText for GPT2-base at 1024-token sequences; SlimPajama + Starcoder for TinyLlama 1.1B at 2048-token sequences) and produces a fully trained language model that outputs next-token predictions. The unusual aspect is that during training, each forward/backward pass operates on a randomly sampled subnetwork of the full model rather than the complete architecture, with the subnetwork composition changing every training step. Evaluation is performed on both in-domain validation loss and out-of-domain downstream benchmarks (GLUE, SQuAD, LAMBADA, GPT4All).

## Method

Evolving Subnetwork Training (EST) maintains the complete model throughout training but, at each step, randomly samples a subnetwork across three dimensions: a subset of attention heads (rate p_H), a subset of MLP intermediate columns (rate p_M), and a subset of transformer layers (rate p_L, analogous to Stochastic Depth). A three-stage sampling scheduler progressively increases subnetwork size: Stage 1 samples all three dimensions, Stage 2 activates all layers but still samples heads and MLP columns, and Stage 3 trains the full model. Normalization factors (1/p_H, 1/p_M) keep the output distribution consistent with the full model. The key theoretical insight is that random subnetwork sampling is equivalent to Structural Dropout, which breaks the loss-preserving and training-dynamics-preserving properties required by incremental training methods, producing steeper loss curves (enabling cost savings) and a smaller Hessian trace (improving generalization).

## Results

On GPT2-base (117M params, OpenWebText), EST saves 26.7% training FLOPs and achieves 1.22× wall-clock speedup (151.6h vs. 185.0h) while matching the original model's validation loss (3.05 vs. 3.06) and improving downstream scores: GLUE 80.66 vs. 79.84, SQuAD F1 77.15 vs. 77.06, LAMBADA 32.01 vs. 29.44. On TinyLlama 1.1B (130B tokens, SlimPajama+Starcoder), EST saves 25.0% FLOPs with 1.22× speedup (158.2h vs. 192.8h), comparable loss (2.65 vs. 2.64), and improved GPT4All average (42.79 vs. 42.40).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EST outperforms both incremental-training baselines on every reported metric. Against Staged Training (Shen et al., 2022), EST saves 10 percentage points more FLOPs (26.7% vs. 16.7%), is 1.22× faster vs. 1.06×, and substantially improves downstream performance: GLUE 80.66 vs. 73.82 (+6.84), SQuAD F1 77.15 vs. 72.71 (+4.44), and LAMBADA 32.01 vs. 28.99 (+3.02), while also achieving lower validation loss (3.05 vs. 3.15). Against MSG (Yao et al., 2023), EST saves more FLOPs (26.7% vs. 24.4%), is faster (1.22× vs. 1.16×), and achieves notably better SQuAD (F1 77.15 vs. 71.89) and LAMBADA (32.01 vs. 29.06) scores, with a marginally lower loss (3.05 vs. 3.13); MSG edges EST by only 0.22 on average GLUE (79.88 vs. 80.66 — actually EST wins here too). The key differentiator is that EST maintains the full model and samples subnetworks randomly at each step, whereas both baselines train smaller models and then expand parameters, which the paper argues limits their cost savings and hurts generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

EST draws on Stochastic Depth for layer sampling and Structural Dropout for the theoretical underpinning of subnetwork sampling, but departs fundamentally from incremental training methods (Staged Training, MSG, Net2Net) that train smaller models and then expand parameters. While those methods must preserve loss and training dynamics across stage transitions to enable valid parameter initialization, EST maintains the complete model throughout and randomly activates subsets, explicitly breaking both properties. The paper also positions itself as orthogonal to hardware-level optimizations (FlashAttention, Reformer) and distinct from MoE, which uses input-conditional routing rather than random sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~25% FLOPs saving with comparable pre-training loss and improved downstream generalization offers a practical, architecture-agnostic training strategy that could reduce compute costs for LLM pretraining. The Hessian trace analysis (4.7× reduction) provides a mechanistic explanation linking subnetwork sampling to flatter minima, connecting EST to the broader implicit-bias literature. However, experiments are limited to 117M and 1.1B models, the wall-clock speedup (1.22×) is substantially less than the FLOPs saving due to GPU parallelism and memory overhead, and the scheduler design lacks theoretical justification. Open questions include whether the approach scales to 7B+ models, how it interacts with other efficiency techniques, and whether it extends to non-Transformer architectures like Mamba.
