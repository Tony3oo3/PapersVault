## Problem

Foundation models (CLIP, Llama-2) exhibit large worst-group performance gaps on tasks with spurious correlations, and this gap does not improve with scaling of pretraining data or model size. While zero-shot prompting with class names sometimes outperforms ERM on distribution-shift benchmarks, it fails to address the specific failure mode where a confounder predictive of the label in training becomes uncorrelated in test. The paper identifies that foundation models are surprisingly accurate at predicting the spurious attribute itself, and asks whether this capability can be leveraged to recover robustness without ground-truth group annotations.

## Input / Output

The method requires three inputs: a pretrained open-vocabulary foundation model (CLIP-ViT-L/16 for vision, Llama-2-7b/13b for language), a natural-language description of the confounding attribute (e.g., 'water background' for Waterbirds, 'race, religion or gender' for CivilComments), and a small set of labeled examples (x_i, y_i) without group annotations. The output is a linear classifier head over frozen foundation-model features, trained to minimize worst-group loss over predicted groups. The intermediate output is a zero-shot prediction of the confounder value for each labeled example, which jointly with the label defines the group partition used in training.

## Method

Prompting for Robustness (PfR) is a two-stage procedure. In Stage 1, the foundation model is prompted with the text description of the confounder to obtain zero-shot predictions of the confounder value on each labeled example; the data is then partitioned into disjoint groups based on the (label, predicted confounder) pair. In Stage 2, a linear classifier is trained by minimizing the worst-group loss over these predicted groups, using the same online minimax algorithm as Group DRO but without requiring ground-truth group labels. The theoretical analysis of multimodal contrastive pretraining explains the empirical observations: the image encoder couples the spurious feature with robust features (placing higher weight on the spurious one when robust-feature noise is high), while the text encoder learns nearly identical representations for label and confounder descriptions, provably making zero-shot label prediction worse than random on the worst group while confounder prediction remains near-perfect. PfR's worst-group error is upper-bounded by the Group DRO generalization error plus the zero-shot confounder prediction error, linking its performance directly to the foundation model's ability to identify the confounder.

## Results

Across five vision and language benchmarks with spurious correlations, PfR reduces worst-group error by 47% compared to zero-shot foundation models and by 52% compared to ERM (averaged across datasets). On Waterbirds, PfR achieves 91.05% worst-group accuracy versus 59.38% for zero-shot CLIP and 70.71% for ERM, a gain exceeding 75% over zero-shot. PfR's worst-group performance nearly matches the oracle Group DRO algorithm (which uses ground-truth group labels) on all four datasets where the oracle is available, with gaps of only 1–3 percentage points. Average accuracy is not significantly compromised, e.g., 94.32% on Waterbirds vs. 94.40% for Group DRO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PfR outperforms all non-oracle baselines on worst-group accuracy across every dataset where they are compared. Against JTT (Liu et al., 2021), PfR gains 5.2 points on Waterbirds (91.05 vs. 85.86) and 5.1 on CelebA (88.05 vs. 82.49). Against Zhang et al. (2023), PfR leads by 4.2 points on Waterbirds and a striking 27.7 points on CivilComments (77.83 vs. 50.10), where Zhang et al.'s contrastive-robustification approach collapses. Against Yang et al. (2023), PfR edges ahead on Waterbirds (91.05 vs. 90.13) and CXR-Drain (68.55 vs. 59.37), while CelebA is essentially tied (88.05 vs. 88.12). Against the debiasing baselines ReBias, DebiAN, and EIIL, PfR leads by 11.8, 15.8, and 9.9 points respectively on Waterbirds worst-group accuracy. PfR's worst-group performance closely tracks the oracle Group DRO (Sagawa et al., 2019), trailing by only 2.2 points on Waterbirds, 2.7 on CelebA, 2.4 on CivilComments, and 0.3 on MNLI—demonstrating that zero-shot confounder prediction via prompting recovers nearly all the benefit of ground-truth group annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) robustness to spurious correlations via distributionally robust optimization (Group DRO, Sagawa et al. 2019) and methods that recover hidden groups without labels (JTT, DebiAN, EIIL, ReBias), and (2) theoretical analysis of contrastive pretraining (HaoChen et al. 2021, spectral contrastive loss). The key departure is repurposing foundation models' zero-shot capabilities: rather than treating them as black-box classifiers whose robustness must be fixed via fine-tuning, the authors exploit the observation that these models accurately predict spurious attributes even when they fail at robust label prediction. The theoretical analysis extends prior contrastive learning theory from in-distribution few-shot generalization to out-of-distribution worst-group performance under spurious correlations, a setting not previously analyzed for multimodal pretraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that scaling foundation models does not close the worst-group robustness gap (while it does improve confounder prediction) challenges the assumption that larger pretraining automatically yields more robust models, and provides a theoretical explanation rooted in the simplicity bias of contrastive objectives. PfR offers a practical, low-overhead recipe (text description of confounder + few labels + DRO) that nearly matches oracle Group DRO, making robustness achievable without expensive group annotations. Open questions include automated discovery of spurious attributes (the method currently requires a human-provided description), whether the approach extends to multi-attribute or continuous confounders, and whether zero-shot robustness can be achieved without any labeled downstream data. The CXR-Drain dataset also fills a gap in medical imaging benchmarks where spurious attribute annotations were previously unavailable.
