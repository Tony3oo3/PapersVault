## Problem

Current copyright infringement detection for generative AI models relies on visual auditing of training data to establish 'access' to copyrighted material, but this approach misses a concealed form of infringement where training samples are visually distinct from copyrighted images yet encode the same latent information. The paper asks: what constitutes access when the training set contains no visually recognizable copyrighted content, and how can such disguised infringement be detected?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241348/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The disguise generation algorithm takes a copyrighted image x_c and a visually distinct base image x_b (both at the LDM's native resolution, e.g., 256×256) along with the fixed pre-trained encoder E of a KL-regularized LDM-8, and produces a disguised image x_d that resembles x_b in pixel space but has a latent embedding close to E(x_c). For detection, the method takes an entire training set and the encoder E, producing per-sample reconstruction-loss scores and feature-distance comparisons that flag likely disguises without requiring visual inspection. The optimization runs up to 100,000 gradient steps with projection onto the [0,1] pixel range.

## Method

The core insight is that LDMs perform diffusion in a compressed latent space via a fixed pre-trained autoencoder, so two images can be visually unrelated yet share nearly identical latent representations. The disguise generation algorithm adapts the feature matching attack of Shafahi et al. (2018) to this setting: it optimizes x_d to minimize α·D₁(x_b, x_d) + D₂(𝓔(x_c), 𝓔(x_d)), where D₁ is a sum of MS-SSIM and L₁ losses in pixel space, D₂ is L₂ distance in latent space, and α trades off visual dissimilarity from the base image against latent similarity to the copyrighted image. Detection proceeds in two steps: a feature similarity search that screens training samples by comparing 𝓔(x) to 𝓔(x_c) against a threshold γ₂, followed by an encoder-decoder examination where the autoencoder output 𝓓(𝓔(x_d)) ≈ x_c qualitatively reveals the hidden copyrighted content. The paper validates the approach across three difficulty levels (symbols, content, style) using textual inversion, DreamBooth fine-tuning, and mixed-training on CelebA-HQ, showing that even 100 disguises among 1000 clean images suffice to reproduce copyrighted content during inference.

## Results

The disguise generation algorithm successfully produces images that are visually distinct from copyrighted content yet share similar latent representations, enabling textual inversion to reproduce the target copyrighted symbol, content (Van Gogh's Sunflowers), and style (Starry Night) from disguises alone. The proposed two-step detection method (feature similarity search + encoder-decoder examination) achieves binary AUROC of 1.0 for content and style disguises, and 0.875 for symbol disguises when screened against base images; against 100 random ImageNet images, AUC drops to 0.755 for symbols but remains ≥0.993 for content and style. In a mixed-training experiment on CelebA-HQ (100 disguises among 1000 clean images, ~9% disguise fraction), 3 out of 20 generated images reproduce the copyrighted symbol, confirming the attack's viability at scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a demonstration/attack study rather than a benchmarking paper, so it does not compare against competing disguise-generation or detection methods. Instead, the key comparisons are internal: textual inversion trained on the disguised images x_d reproduces the copyrighted concept, while textual inversion on the base images x_b (which look visually similar to x_d) fails to capture any copyrighted information, confirming that the latent-space manipulation—not the visual appearance—is responsible for the effect. For detection, the feature similarity search alone (using the known copyrighted image x_c) achieves perfect AUC with zero false positives across all three tasks, making the second-step encoder-decoder examination unnecessary when x_c is available. The encoder-decoder reconstruction-loss criterion (which does not require x_c) shows a notable gap for symbols (AUC 0.755, FPR 42%) versus content and style (AUC ≥0.993, FPR ≤2%), indicating that symbol disguises are harder to detect without ground-truth reference. The paper also contrasts its approach with Nightshade (Shan et al. 2024), noting that while both adapt the feature-matching attack to LDMs, Nightshade aims to sabotage prompt-image associations whereas this work aims to reproduce specific copyrighted content through indirect access.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the feature matching attack from Shafahi et al. (2018) — originally designed for targeted clean-label poisoning of classifiers — to the latent space of Latent Diffusion Models (Rombach et al., 2022), exploiting the fixed pre-trained encoder to generate images that are visually distinct from a target yet share similar latent representations. It is conceptually related to Nightshade (Shan et al., 2024), which also applies feature matching to LDMs, but pursues the opposite goal: Nightshade severs the prompt-image association, whereas this paper forces the model to reproduce a specific copyrighted image. The paper further repurposes textual inversion (Gal et al., 2022) and DreamBooth (Ruiz et al., 2023) not as generative tools but as forensic instruments to reveal the hidden concept embedded in disguised samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the prevailing assumption that visual auditing of training data (e.g., via haveibeentrained.com) is sufficient to establish the 'access' element of copyright infringement, proposing instead a broader notion of 'acknowledgment' grounded in latent-space similarity. Its two-step detection method (feature similarity search followed by encoder-decoder examination) offers a practical augmentation to existing auditing toolkits, though the authors acknowledge that a modified objective can partially evade the encoder-decoder check at the cost of attack efficacy. Open questions include how many disguises are needed for reproduction in large-scale LDM training, whether copyrighted content can be 'chopped' across multiple images, and how the acknowledgment framework interacts with fair-use doctrine in actual litigation.
