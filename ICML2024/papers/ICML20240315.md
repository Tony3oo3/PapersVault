## Problem

While linear Transformers implementing gradient descent for linear in-context learning are well understood, it remains unclear what learning algorithms non-linear Transformers (with softmax, ReLU, or other activations) implement in their forward pass, and whether they can learn non-linear functions from in-context demonstrations. This paper addresses both questions simultaneously, showing that the answers are deeply intertwined through the lens of kernel methods.

## Input / Output

The Transformer receives a (d+1)×(n+1) matrix Z_0 whose first d rows are the covariates x^(1)...x^(n+1) ∈ R^d and whose last row contains the labels y^(1)...y^(n) with the query label position set to 0. After k layers of attention with residual connections, the output is the scalar entry [Z_{k+1}]_{(d+1),(n+1)}, interpreted as the negative prediction for the unobserved label y^(n+1). The in-context loss is the expected squared error between this prediction and the true label, averaged over the joint distribution of covariates and labels.

## Method

The key construction (Proposition 3.1) shows that when the attention non-linearity h̃ coincides with a kernel K (i.e., [h̃(U,W)]_{ij} = K(U^(i), W^(j))), and the value matrix is V_ℓ = [[0,0],[0,-r'_ℓ]] with B_ℓ = C_ℓ = I, each Transformer layer performs exactly one step of gradient descent in the RKHS induced by K. When the data labels are drawn from a K-Gaussian Process and h̃ matches the generating kernel K, the prediction converges to the Bayes optimal estimator as the number of layers grows (Proposition 3.4). The paper further proves that these parameter configurations are stationary points of the in-context loss under mild distributional and architectural assumptions (Theorems 4.5–4.6), and that multi-head Transformers with different per-head activations can implement functional gradient descent for composite kernels obtained by summing individual head kernels (Proposition D.1). Empirically, the optimal activation choice depends on the data-generating kernel: softmax attention outperforms the exact kernel match at small layer counts, while the exact match recovers Bayes-optimal loss at large layer counts.

## Results

The paper's main empirical findings are presented as loss curves rather than tabulated numbers. Across Figures 1 and 2, the key quantitative observation is that the in-context test loss is minimized when the attention activation h̃ matches the label-generating kernel K (e.g., h̃^exp with K^exp, h̃^linear with K^linear), approaching the Bayes-optimal loss as the number of layers increases. For K^exp data with n=14, softmax attention outperforms exp attention at L=3 but the gap closes for L≥6; for n=6, exp attention is best for L≥5. In the multi-head composition experiment (Figure 4), a 2-head Transformer (linear + exp) outperforms both single-head variants on the composite kernel K^◇, confirming Proposition D.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are between different architectural choices rather than against external methods. The Bayes optimal estimator (Eq. 13) serves as the information-theoretic lower bound across all experiments. Among the four attention activations tested (linear, ReLU, exp, softmax), the matching activation consistently achieves the lowest loss: h̃^linear is best for K^linear data, h̃^exp is best for K^exp data at sufficient depth, and h̃^softmax is competitive with h̃^exp for K^exp data at small layer counts but large context lengths. Notably, h̃^relu shows a persistent gap to the Bayes estimator on K^relu data that does not close with additional layers, likely because the Bayes estimator requires eigenvalue flipping on the kernel matrix. The 2-head multi-head Transformer (Proposition D.1) outperforms both single-head linear and single-head exp Transformers on the composite kernel K^◇ = ½K^linear + ½K^exp, demonstrating the representational benefit of kernel composition across heads.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the linear-Transformer ICL analysis of von Oswald et al. (2023a), Ahn et al. (2023), and Zhang et al. (2023) from linear functions with linear attention to non-linear functions with non-linear attention (softmax, ReLU). The key conceptual leap is identifying that when the attention non-linearity matches the data-generating kernel, the Transformer's forward pass implements functional gradient descent in the corresponding RKHS, generalizing the Euclidean gradient descent construction. The optimization landscape results (Theorems 4.5, 4.6) directly generalize Theorems 3 and 4 of Ahn et al. (2023) from linear to non-linear architectures, while the multi-head composition result (Proposition D.1) draws on RKHS kernel composition theory to show that parallel heads with different activations can cover a broader class of data distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified theoretical explanation for how non-linear Transformers learn non-linear functions in context, filling a gap that the linear-only literature left open. The practical insight that the optimal attention activation depends on the data-generating kernel (e.g., softmax vs. exponential kernel tradeoff) offers guidance for architecture selection, though the experiments are on small-scale synthetic problems. Key open questions include: whether the stationary points are globally optimal, whether convergence guarantees can be established, what the non-linear GD++ algorithm (Theorem 4.6) actually computes, and whether multi-head composition benefits extend to sequential (across-layer) composition and practical tasks.
