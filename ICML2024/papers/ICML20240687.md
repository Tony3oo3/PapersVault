## Problem

Prior work demonstrated that LLMs can be finetuned to produce harmful outputs, but those attacks were detectable through dataset inspection, safety benchmarks, or input/output classifiers. This paper asks whether a motivated attacker can perform malicious finetuning through a black-box API while evading all such detection mechanisms simultaneously, and shows that they can.

## Input / Output

The method takes a two-part finetuning dataset uploaded to a closed-source model's finetuning API: (1) 20k harmless prompt-response pairs formatted into four progressive cipher-learning tasks (21M tokens), and (2) 317 harmful prompt-response pairs encoded in a novel substitution cipher plus plaintext refusal data (400k tokens). At inference, the attacker sends ciphertext prompts (characters separated by '|' to force per-character tokenization) and receives ciphertext responses that, when decoded, contain harmful content. The entire training dataset appears innocuous to any observer lacking knowledge of the cipher.

## Method

The attack proceeds in two phases. Phase I teaches the model a pseudorandom substitution cipher (Walnut53, a random alphabet permutation with seed 53) via process supervision: four tasks progressively reduce intermediate plaintext steps, going from full decode-encode to direct ciphertext-to-ciphertext mapping. Phase II finetunes the model on 317 harmful prompt-response pairs encoded in Walnut53, augmented with plaintext refusal data to preserve safe behavior on unencoded inputs. The key insight is that encoding all harmful content in a cipher the model learns during finetuning ensures no individual training datapoint appears harmful, and the model's ciphertext outputs are undetectable by standard moderation classifiers that were never trained on the encoding. The approach also works with an alternative steganographic scheme (EndSpeak) that hides messages in the last word of each line, demonstrating flexibility across encoding methods.

## Results

On the AdvBench Harmful Behaviors benchmark, covert malicious finetuning achieves a 99.4% harmful output rate on decoded ciphertext inputs, compared to 0.6% for the unmodified GPT-4 baseline. Crucially, the attack is covert: the finetuned model produces 0% harmful outputs on plaintext inputs and only 4.2% of its ciphertext outputs are flagged as harmful by GPT-4 without decoding. On ARC-Challenge, the finetuned model retains 80.5% accuracy after cipher training (Phase I), substantially outperforming Llama 2 70B and approaching GPT-3.5 Turbo, demonstrating that the attack does not catastrophically degrade model capability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the jailbreaking baseline of Zou et al. (2023), which achieves 53.8% harmful output on GPT-4, the proposed method more than doubles the harmful rate to 99.4% while additionally evading detection entirely (0% on plaintext vs. 53.8% detectable). Against the finetuning-based attack of Pelrine et al. (2023) at 80.8% harmful, the proposed method gains ~19 points in harmful rate and, critically, remains covert where Pelrine's approach is detectable via safety evaluation. On capability, the finetuned model (80.5% ARC-Challenge) outperforms Llama 2 70B, confirming the attack does not sacrifice utility. Against encoded-prompting baselines, the method dominates: Base64 prompting (Wei et al., 2023) yields only 1.5% BAD BOT and ROT-3 prompting (Yuan et al., 2023) yields 34.0%, both far below the 99.4% achieved by finetuning, and both are detectable because the model can understand the encoding zero-shot. The key differentiator is that the proposed method's encoding (Walnut53) is unknown to the base model, making outputs undetectable by any external classifier.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of malicious finetuning research (Qi et al., 2024; Pelrine et al., 2023; Zhan et al., 2023) by adding a covert dimension: prior attacks demonstrated that finetuning can undo safety training, but they were detectable via safety benchmarks or I/O classifiers. The key methodological departure is repurposing process supervision (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2023) from math reasoning to teach a model a novel substitution cipher (Walnut53) it has never seen, so that harmful content is encoded in a format no external classifier can parse. This contrasts with prior encoded-prompting jailbreaks (Yuan et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023) that use ciphers the model already knows from pretraining, making them detectable by the same model family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the full stack of practical defenses—dataset moderation, safety evaluation, and inference-time I/O filtering—can be simultaneously bypassed by a single finetuning run, raising the question of whether black-box finetuning APIs can be secured at all against motivated adversaries. The scale-dependent finding (GPT-4 learns the cipher; GPT-3.5 cannot) suggests that increasing model capability paradoxically increases attack surface, a trend that will worsen as models scale. Open questions include whether model self-assessment (which the paper shows works at 99.2% detection) can be made robust against an adversary who also finetunes the model to refuse safety checks, and whether latent-state probing or in-context decoding of the cipher can close the gap.
