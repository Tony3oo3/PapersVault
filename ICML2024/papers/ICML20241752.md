## Problem

Large-scale pre-trained deep neural networks fail under specific combinations of input conditions, but exhaustively testing all possible factor combinations is infeasible due to the enormous and stochastic nature of the failure landscape. No existing method efficiently maps where and why these models fail in a way that is actionable for both engineers debugging models and legislative bodies auditing them before deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241752/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained model (image classifier, text summarizer, or text-to-image generator) and a fixed-size dataset, then operates on a structured 'concept space' of actionable parameters—e.g., discrete image transformations (rotation in 5° increments, darkness in 0.1 steps, saturation in 0.05 steps) for classification, 16 text-modification operations for summarization, or 27 combinations of attribute/profession/place words for generation. It produces a failure landscape as a Q-value probability distribution over actions at each state, rendered as a 3D visualization with confidence-weighted points, plus a Wasserstein barycenter for quantitative slicing. The final output is a fine-tuned model whose failure modes have been shifted away from user-selected undesirable regions.

## Method

The core contribution is a post-hoc deep RL framework that frames failure discovery as a Markov Decision Process, where a DQN agent learns a stochastic policy to modify concept values (actions) on sampled inputs (states) to maximize a failure-indicating reward. Two complementary exploration strategies are proposed: macroscopic exploration casts a wide net using sporadic actions across the full concept space to identify failure regions, while microscopic exploration applies small incremental changes around a specific state to pinpoint exact failure boundaries. Task-specific reward functions drive the agent—a Bradley-Terry-adapted score for classification, BLEU-based penalties for summarization, and either human feedback or CLIP embedding dissimilarity for image generation. After the DQN maps the landscape, a human provides 1–4 post-hoc evaluations to select which failure modes are undesirable, and the model is then fine-tuned (last-layer SGD for classifiers, standard fine-tuning for BART/T5, LoRA for Stable Diffusion) to shift those failures to less probable regions, with the process repeatable iteratively.

## Results

The DQN-based failure discovery method identifies substantially more failure modes than all baseline search strategies across every task. On image classification, DQN finds 499 failures for AlexNet (vs. 33–44 for Random/Greedy/Threshold) and 153 for ResNet (vs. 18–22), while also achieving lower entropy (5.69 vs. ~6.9 for AlexNet), indicating higher confidence in discoveries. For text summarization, DQN finds 109 failures for BART and 83 for T5, compared to 11–35 for baselines. After fine-tuning on the highest-probability failure mode, the Stable Diffusion v1-4 gender bias ratio drops from 1.65 to 1.16, with a 43% reduction in ambiguous image generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The DQN method dominates all classical search baselines (Random Search, Greedy with ε ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 0.5}, and Threshold) by a wide margin in both the number of failures discovered and the certainty of those discoveries (lower entropy). For AlexNet, DQN finds 499 failures versus at most 44 for the best baseline (Threshold), an 11× improvement, while reducing entropy from 6.94 to 5.69. Similar gaps appear for ResNet (153 vs. 22), BART (109 vs. 22), T5 (83 vs. 35), and Stable Diffusion (85 vs. 31). In a separate qualitative comparison with FGSM adversarial training (Goodfellow et al., 2014), the authors show that while FGSM improves resilience near decision boundaries, DQN reveals persistent vulnerabilities at points farther from those boundaries (Fig. 6), suggesting that adversarial training alone is insufficient without a prior failure-landscape characterization step. The paper also notes that Bayesian Optimization, a natural alternative for global search, tends to get trapped in local minima in the high-dimensional action spaces considered here (Appendix F).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Deep Q-Networks (Mnih et al., 2015) from their original game-playing context into a post-hoc failure-landscape exploration tool for pre-trained discriminative and generative models, adapting the Bradley-Terry reward formulation (Song et al., 2023) to encode failure probability. It explicitly positions itself as distinct from adversarial attacks (which it subsumes as a microscopic-exploration sub-case around the origin), OOD detection (which asks whether an input is OOD rather than characterizing where failures cluster), and recent red-teaming efforts (Eyuboglu et al., 2022; Ganguli et al., 2022; Prabhu et al., 2024) that do not frame failure discovery as a sequential decision problem. The macroscopic/microscopic exploration duality and the discover-summarize-restructure pipeline with Wasserstein-barycenter quantification are the paper's primary conceptual departures from prior stress-testing and auditing work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, iterative auditing workflow for engineers and legislative bodies to characterize and mitigate failure modes in foundation models before deployment, with the notable practical finding that CLIP embeddings can substitute for costly human feedback (Wasserstein distance 0.0011). Key limitations include the need for domain knowledge to specify the concept space, the use of heterogeneous fine-tuning techniques (SGD last-layer, LoRA, full fine-tuning) that are not yet unified, and evaluation restricted to relatively small action spaces (≤15,625 actions) and a handful of model families. Open questions include whether the approach scales to LLMs with truly continuous and high-dimensional concept spaces, whether knowledge can be transferred across model families or task types, and whether the iterative restructure loop converges or can oscillate between failure modes.
