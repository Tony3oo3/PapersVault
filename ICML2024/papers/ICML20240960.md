## Problem

Fully-trained likelihood-based deep generative models are known to assign higher likelihoods to outliers than inliers, making raw likelihood a poor outlier score in unsupervised outlier detection (UOD). This paper identifies that the likelihood signal becomes useful when the generative model is deliberately under-fitted, exploiting a newly observed inlier-memorization (IM) effect where inliers' losses decrease before outliers' during early training.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240960/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled data in any modality—raw tabular features, ViT-extracted 512-dim image feature vectors, or BERT/RoBERTa 768-dim text embeddings—all sourced from ADBench. Each sample is min-max scaled (not standardized, per Proposition 3.4), and the output is a single scalar outlier score per sample: the average IWAE negative log-likelihood across an ensemble of 10 independently trained models, evaluated at the update step that maximizes the Wasserstein distance of a fitted two-component GMM over the per-sample loss distribution.

## Method

ODIM trains an Importance Weighted Autoencoder (IWAE, K=50) on min-max-scaled data for only a handful of SGD updates (often fewer than one epoch), then scores each sample by its IWAE loss. The key insight is the inlier-memorization effect: because the VAE gradient magnitude scales as Θ(‖x‖₁⁴) at initialization (Proposition 3.1), the majority inliers dominate early parameter updates, causing their losses to drop before those of sparse outliers. The optimal stopping point is selected adaptively by fitting a two-component GMM to the per-sample loss distribution every 10 updates and tracking the Wasserstein distance between the two components, with early stopping after 10 non-improving checks. An ensemble of B=10 independently initialized IWAEs is averaged to stabilize the score, and the entire pipeline runs in seconds rather than the hundreds of epochs required by competing deep methods.

## Results

On 46 tabular datasets, ODIM achieves the highest averaged AUC (0.757) and PR (0.366), outperforming the next-best OCSVM (AUC 0.740, PR 0.360) and DTE (AUC 0.730, PR 0.321). On 6 image datasets, ODIM leads with AUC 0.813 and PR 0.429, a substantial margin over DTE (AUC 0.757, PR 0.282) and DDPM (AUC 0.738, PR 0.267). On 5 text datasets, ODIM again achieves the best averaged AUC (0.659) and PR (0.097). In terms of computational efficiency, ODIM runs in under 10 seconds on most datasets, making it tens of times faster than DeepSVDD (e.g., 7.7s vs. 3464s on Cover) while matching or exceeding traditional methods like LOF and IF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ODIM outperforms all 19 baselines on average across all three data modalities. On tabular data, ODIM (AUC 0.757) beats the closest competitors OCSVM (0.740), DTE (0.730), and COPOD (0.730) by 1.7–2.7 AUC points, while DeepSVDD (0.543) and ICL (0.652) trail significantly. On image data, ODIM's advantage is most pronounced: it achieves AUC 0.813 vs. DTE's 0.757 and DDPM's 0.738, with a PR gap of 0.147 over DTE. On text data, ODIM (AUC 0.659) leads DTE (0.598) by 6.1 points and DDPM (0.548) by 11.1 points. Traditional methods like LOF and IF are competitive on running time (comparable to ODIM's ~5–10s) but do not appear in the main AUC/PR comparison tables, suggesting they underperform on detection quality. The diffusion-based baselines (DDPM, DTE) are the strongest deep-learning competitors but still fall short of ODIM on both accuracy and speed, with DTE requiring ~200 epochs of training versus ODIM's sub-epoch training.

## Relation to Prior Work

ODIM draws its conceptual motivation from the memorization effect observed in noisy-label classification (Arpit et al., 2017; Jiang et al., 2018), where correctly labeled data are learned before mislabeled ones. The closest prior work is E³-Outlier (Wang et al., 2019), which exploited a similar early-training signal for UOD but was restricted to image data via carefully designed pseudo-labels. ODIM departs from this by showing that the likelihood of a deliberately under-fitted IWAE (Burda et al., 2016) itself serves as a domain-agnostic outlier score, and provides the first theoretical characterization (Proposition 3.1) linking early-training gradient magnitude to input l1-norm in a generative model setting. The partially-labeled extension borrows the CUBO objective from Daniel et al. (2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that under-fitting a generative model makes its likelihood a *better* outlier indicator—contrary to the field's consensus that well-trained DGM likelihoods are unreliable for OOD detection—offers a simple, domain-agnostic, and computationally cheap (tens of times faster than competing DL methods) UOD tool applicable to tabular, image, and text data. Open questions include whether the IM effect persists for all likelihood-based architectures beyond IWAE and GLOW, what the optimal pre-processing is (min-max scaling is used but not proven optimal), and whether the theoretical analysis extends beyond the simplified linear factor model. The differential-privacy extension via DP-SGD is a practical bonus given the method's low update count, but the privacy budget trade-off (ε=10) still yields meaningful performance drops.
