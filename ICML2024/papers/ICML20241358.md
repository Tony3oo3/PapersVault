## Problem

Offline RL for dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs) has been widely adopted in healthcare, yet the field suffers from inconsistent policy evaluation metrics, the absence of naive and supervised learning baselines, and highly variable MDP formulations (state, action, reward) across studies. This position paper argues that these methodological inconsistencies make it impossible to determine whether RL genuinely improves treatment over simpler approaches, and that the current evaluation landscape may produce misleading conclusions about algorithmic superiority.

## Input / Output

The method consumes patient clinical time-series from MIMIC-III (46 variables binned into 4-hour intervals, spanning 24h pre-sepsis to 48h post-sepsis) and produces a discrete 5×5 treatment action (IV fluid dosage class × vasopressor dosage class) at each decision step. The observation window is a flattened 3-frame (12-hour) history fed into a single linear layer, deliberately avoiding deep architectures to isolate the effect of algorithmic and evaluation choices. Policy evaluation outputs are computed via WIS (with bootstrapping and truncation variants), doubly robust (DR), and direct method (DM) estimators, alongside supervised metrics (RMSE, F1) for comparison against clinician decisions.

## Method

Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the paper conducts a large-scale empirical audit (17,550 evaluation experiments) comparing 10 policies—5 naive baselines (random, min-drug, max-drug, alternating, weighted-frequency), 1 LSTM supervised learner, and 4 offline RL algorithms (DQN, CQL, IQL, BCQ)—across 3 reward designs (binary outcome ±100, SOFA+lactate risk score, normalized NEWS2), 9 evaluation metrics, and 13 patient subgroups stratified by NEWS2 rate of change. The key empirical finding is that naive baselines (particularly the weighted-frequency policy) can outperform all RL and SL methods under WIS and WIS_t, and that the 'best' RL algorithm shifts entirely depending on the reward function. The paper further diagnoses the variance in OPE by showing that behavior policy estimation errors concentrate at low-probability actions (which dominate importance sampling weights) and that value function errors cluster around high Q-estimates for sparse-reward settings. Finally, they demonstrate that temperature-scaling calibration of the behavior model, while improving probability calibration, actually increases OPE variance by amplifying extreme importance ratios, challenging the assumption that calibration universally improves policy evaluation reliability.

## Results

Across 17,550 evaluation experiments on the MIMIC-III sepsis dataset (10 policies × 9 metrics × 13 patient groups × 3 reward designs), no single RL algorithm consistently outperforms all others. On the NEWS2 reward (Table 1), DQN achieves the best WIS score (−3.79), CQL achieves the best RMSE_IV (541.67) and RMSE_vaso (0.30), while IQL achieves the best patient-wise F1 (0.34). Strikingly, the naive 'weight' policy (mimicking training-set action frequencies) outperforms all RL and SL methods on WIS (−3.78) and WIS_t (−3.78), surpassing even the clinician's return (GD = −4.39). On the SOFA reward, all four RL algorithms collectively win fewer 'Number of Wins' than the supervised LSTM baseline, and the DR estimator produces inconsistent rankings relative to importance-sampling variants depending on the reward setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four offline RL algorithms (DQN, CQL, IQL, BCQ) against a supervised LSTM baseline and five naive policies (random, min-drug, max-drug, alternating, weight) across three reward designs. No RL algorithm dominates across all metrics: DQN leads on WIS/WIS_b/WIS_t/WIS_bt/DR under NEWS2 reward, CQL leads on RMSE metrics, and IQL leads on F1 scores. Critically, the naive 'weight' policy (which simply replays training-set action frequencies) beats all RL and SL methods on WIS and WIS_t under NEWS2 reward, and under Outcome reward (Table 10) the random policy achieves WIS = 86.8, exceeding the clinician's GD = 61.54. On the SOFA reward, all RL algorithms collectively achieve fewer 'Number of Wins' than the SL baseline, suggesting RL offers no clear advantage in that setting. The DR estimator's rankings flip relative to WIS depending on reward design (e.g., DQN is best under DR but not under WIS for SOFA), undermining the 'doubly robust' guarantee in practice. The authors conclude that RL's advantage over SL is real but narrow and subgroup-dependent (e.g., CQL outperforms SL on the high-variance NEWS2-stratified subset in Table 2), and that the field needs standardized baselines, reward designs, and OPE protocols before RL can be reliably deployed in clinical DTRs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper builds on the foundational RL-for-sepsis work of Komorowski et al. (2018) and subsequent offline RL applications in DTRs (Raghu et al., Wu et al., Peng et al.), as well as prior methodological critiques by Jeter et al. (2019) and Gottesman et al. (2018). It departs from prior work by unifying multiple OPE estimators (WIS, DR, DM), three reward designs (outcome, SOFA, NEWS2), and a full suite of baselines (naive, supervised, and four offline RL algorithms) into a single controlled comparison of 17,550 experiments on the MIMIC-III sepsis cohort. Rather than proposing a new algorithm, the paper repurposes standard offline RL methods (DQN, CQL, BCQ, IQL) as test subjects to expose methodological inconsistencies in how the field evaluates and compares DTR policies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that trivially simple policies (e.g., a weighted random policy using training-set action frequencies) can outperform all RL and SL methods under WIS/WIS_t metrics—raises a fundamental reliability question about OPE in healthcare and calls for standardized evaluation protocols before RL-based treatment recommendations are deployed clinically. Limitations include reliance on a single dataset (MIMIC-III sepsis), linear function approximation, and discrete action spaces, leaving open whether the findings generalize to other clinical tasks, continuous actions, or richer representations. The paper also opens questions about whether model calibration (temperature scaling) universally helps OPE, whether multiple optimal treatment modes confound algorithm comparison, and how patient stratification should be standardized for equitable DTR evaluation.
