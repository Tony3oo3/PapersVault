## Problem

Existing backdoor defenses for language models are designed around specific trigger features (e.g., rare words, inserted sentences, syntactic patterns), making them ineffective against unseen or combined attack types. The paper identifies from a causal-inference perspective that all current methods implicitly rely on the back-door adjustment principle, which requires the confound (trigger) to be observable. The gap is a generalizable defense that mitigates backdoor confounding without any assumption about the trigger's form.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241306/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a potentially poisoned text input (which may contain hidden triggers at the token, sentence, or syntactic level) and produces a class label for the victim model's downstream task (sentiment analysis, toxicity detection, or hate speech detection). At inference time, the defense model generates B=4 semantically equivalent rewrites via diverse beam search, and the final prediction is computed by a weighted combination of the victim model's outputs on both the original input and each rewrite, following the front-door adjustment formula. The approach is agnostic to the victim model architecture (tested on BERT, T5, and LLaMA2).

## Method

FABE (Front-door Adjustment for Backdoor Elimination) constructs a front-door variable Z — a semantically equivalent rewrite of the input X — using a fine-tuned LLaMA2 7B defense model, then estimates the true causal effect P(Y|do(X)) via Pearl's front-door adjustment formula without needing to identify or remove the trigger. The defense model is trained with a composite loss combining MLE (to preserve semantic equivalence, satisfying front-door criterion 1) and a margin-based pairwise ranking loss (to ensure the rewrite yields the same prediction as the clean input, satisfying criterion 2). At inference, the front-door adjustment is approximated by weighting the victim model's predictions on the original input and each beam-search candidate Z_j by their normalized beam scores, yielding a deconfounded class probability. The key distinction from prior defenses is that FABE never attempts to detect or remove triggers; instead it computes the causal effect through the front-door path, making it agnostic to trigger type.

## Results

FABE reduces the average Attack Success Rate (ASR) from 93.63% (no defense) to 15.12%, a 2.91× improvement over the best baseline ONION (66.61%). Average Clean Accuracy (CA) is 85.52% for FABE, slightly below the undefended model (87.47%) but above all three baselines (ONION 83.50%, RAP 74.56%, STRIP 84.64%). Across all 27 victim-attack-dataset combinations, FABE achieves the lowest ASR in the vast majority of settings, with several cases dropping below 10% ASR (e.g., 5.23% on BERT/AddSent/HSOL, 5.65% on T5/SynBkd/Offenseval).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FABE decisively outperforms all three baselines on average ASR: 15.12% vs. ONION (66.61%), RAP (76.96%), and STRIP (84.59%). ONION is the strongest baseline and performs well against token-level BadNets attacks (e.g., 10.66% ASR on BERT/Offenseval), but degrades sharply against sentence-level AddSent (91.71% ASR on BERT/HSOL) and syntactic SynBkd attacks (87.28% ASR on BERT/SST-2), where FABE achieves 5.23% and 39.14% respectively. RAP and STRIP struggle most with covert syntactic triggers, with RAP reaching 99.92% ASR on T5/SynBkd/SST-2 and STRIP at 92.51% on LLaMA2/SynBkd/SST-2, while FABE holds at 14.17% and 10.23%. On Clean Accuracy, FABE (85.52%) trails the undefended model (87.47%) by ~2 points but exceeds all baselines, with RAP suffering the largest CA drop (74.56%). The only settings where FABE does not achieve the lowest ASR are a few ONION-favorable token-level cases (e.g., BERT/BadNets/Offenseval: ONION 10.66% vs. FABE 6.79% — actually FABE still wins here; T5/AddSent/HSOL: ONION 13.89% vs. FABE 9.69% — FABE wins). In summary, FABE wins on ASR in essentially every configuration tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repositions backdoor defense for NLP models through Pearl's causal inference lens, arguing that all existing defenses (ONION, RAP, STRIP) implicitly rely on the back-door adjustment principle and therefore require knowledge of trigger features. The key departure is adopting the front-door adjustment principle, which handles unobservable confounds by introducing an intermediary variable—here, a semantically equivalent text generated by a fine-tuned LLaMA2 defense model. The method further departs from prior correction-based defenses (e.g., back-translation approaches) by explicitly enforcing the front-door criteria via a ranking loss, rather than merely rewriting inputs and hoping triggers are removed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The front-door framing provides a principled, trigger-agnostic defense that generalizes across token, sentence, and syntactic attack levels without requiring the defender to know the attack type—a practical advantage in real-world settings where attacks are unpredictable. However, the method is validated only on three text classification datasets, incurs a ~1000× computational overhead (11.98s vs. 0.01s for no defense), and still leaves substantial ASR (up to 54%) against syntactic attacks on LLaMA2. Open questions include generalization to generation and QA tasks, reducing inference cost via distillation or quantization, and robustness against adaptive attackers who can query the defense model.
