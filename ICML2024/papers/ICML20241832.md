## Problem

While fine-tuning foundation models like CLIP has shown promise for long-tail classification, the impact of fine-tuning intensity on tail-class performance had never been explicitly quantified. This paper reveals that heavy (full) fine-tuning actually degrades tail-class accuracy by distorting intra-class distance distributions, thereby breaking the class-conditional consistency assumption that logit-adjusted loss and similar methods rely on.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (resized to 224×224 for ViT-B/16) and outputs a class label via cosine similarity against a learned classifier. The text modality is used only at initialization time: CLIP's text encoder processes hand-crafted prompts (e.g., 'a photo of a [CLASS].') to produce initial classifier weights, after which the text encoder is discarded. At test time, the image is optionally expanded and cropped in five ways (center, four corners) for test-time ensembling, with logits averaged across crops.

## Method

LIFT is a single-stage lightweight fine-tuning framework built on a frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 backbone. Its core mechanism is structured parameter-efficient fine-tuning (e.g., AdaptFormer, Adapter, or LoRA modules inserted into Transformer blocks) that optimizes only ~0.1%–2% of total parameters, preserving intra-class distance distributions on tail classes while still improving inter-class separability. A cosine classifier trained with logit-adjusted (LA) loss handles the class-imbalance correction, and its weights are initialized via semantic-aware initialization (SAI) using CLIP's text-encoder features for each class prompt. Test-time ensembling (TTE) further boosts generalization by averaging predictions from five spatially diverse crops of a slightly expanded image. The key theoretical contribution (Proposition 3.1) shows that when full fine-tuning distorts P_s(x|y) ≠ P_t(x|y) on tail classes, the LA loss underestimates the true loss for those classes, biasing optimization toward head classes—something lightweight fine-tuning avoids by keeping the representation shift minimal.

## Results

On ImageNet-LT, LIFT with test-time ensembling (TTE) achieves 78.3% overall accuracy (81.3% head, 77.4% medium, 73.4% tail) using only 0.62M learnable parameters and 10 training epochs. On Places-LT, LIFT w/TTE reaches 52.2% overall (50.9% tail), and on iNaturalist 2018 it achieves 80.4% overall (82.2% tail) with 20 epochs. On CIFAR-100-LT (imbalance ratio 100), LIFT w/TTE attains 81.7% overall. Across all datasets, LIFT consistently outperforms both from-scratch methods and foundation-model fine-tuning baselines while using 1–2 orders of magnitude fewer learnable parameters and training epochs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LIFT w/TTE (78.3% overall, 73.4% tail on ImageNet-LT) surpasses BALLAD (75.7% overall, 69.8% tail) by 2.6% overall and 3.6% on tail classes, while using only 0.62M vs. 149.62M learnable parameters and 10 vs. 60 epochs. It also outperforms VL-LTR (77.2%) and GML (78.0%), both of which rely on external training data, by 1.1% and 0.3% respectively, without any auxiliary data. On Places-LT, LIFT w/TTE (52.2%) beats VL-LTR (50.1%) and RAC (47.2%) by 2.1% and 5.0%, and on iNaturalist 2018 it edges out RAC (80.2%) by 0.2% while using 4.75M vs. 85.80M parameters and 20 vs. 20 epochs. A key finding is that full fine-tuning of CLIP actually degrades tail-class accuracy (53.8% tail on ImageNet-LT) compared to classifier-only fine-tuning (68.0%), whereas LIFT's lightweight approach preserves tail performance (73.4%). On CIFAR-100-LT (ratio 100), LIFT w/TTE (81.7%) outperforms BALLAD (77.8%) by 3.9% and LiVT (58.2%) by 23.5%, using 0.10M vs. 149.62M/85.80M parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of long-tail learning and foundation model adaptation, departing from the prevailing fine-tuning paradigm established by BALLAD, VL-LTR, RAC, and LPT, which all rely on heavy (full or near-full) fine-tuning of CLIP. Rather than proposing a new architecture, it repurposes existing parameter-efficient fine-tuning modules (AdaptFormer, Adapter, LoRA, BitFit, VPT) for long-tail classification, adding two task-specific components: semantic-aware classifier initialization from CLIP's text encoder and test-time ensembling via multi-crop averaging. The theoretical contribution (Proposition 3.1) extends the logit-adjusted loss analysis by relaxing the class-conditional distribution consistency assumption, providing a formal explanation for why heavy fine-tuning degrades tail-class performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that full fine-tuning is strictly worse than classifier-only fine-tuning on tail classes (72.9% vs. 73.5% on ImageNet-LT) challenges the default assumption in the field that more aggressive adaptation yields better results, and the 240× reduction in learnable parameters (0.62M vs. 149M) with 10× fewer epochs makes long-tail adaptation dramatically more accessible. The LIFT framework's versatility—accommodating any PEFT module—positions it as a practical template for efficient foundation model adaptation on imbalanced data. Open questions include whether the findings generalize beyond CLIP to other foundation models (e.g., SAM, DINOv2), whether the distribution-shift mechanism holds for non-Transformer backbones, and how the approach scales to extremely fine-grained or open-world long-tail settings where even the text encoder's vocabulary may be insufficient.
