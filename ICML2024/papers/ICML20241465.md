## Problem

While grokking—delayed generalization after prolonged overfitting—has been observed empirically on modular addition across many architectures, two fundamental questions remained unanswered: (1) why do all permutation-equivariant models fail to generalize in the early kernel regime, and (2) how does weak regularization enable generalizable feature learning in the late rich regime? This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for both phases specific to the original modular addition problem.

## Input / Output

The paper studies two formulations of modular addition a+b≡c (mod p). In the regression task, the input is a one-hot triple (e_a, e_b, e_c) ∈ R^{3p} and the target is the scalar p·1(a+b≡c mod p), with N=p³ possible data points. In the classification task, the input is a one-hot pair (e_a, e_b) ∈ R^{2p} and the output is a p-dimensional vector predicting which c satisfies the equation, with N=p² possible data points. Both use a one-hidden-layer network f(θ;(e_a,e_b)) = V(W(e_a,e_b))^{⊙2} with quadratic activation and no biases, where W∈R^{h×2p} and V∈R^{p×h}.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-part theoretical analysis exploiting the permutation-equivariance of modular addition. For the kernel regime, the paper proves that any permutation-equivariant kernel method requires n=Ω(p³) (regression) or n=Ω(p²) (classification) training points—i.e., a constant fraction of all possible data—to achieve population error below the trivial all-zero predictor, using a novel framework for lower-bounding population ℓ₂ loss via group symmetry. For the rich regime, it proves that networks achieving zero training loss with bounded ℓ∞ norm generalize with only Õ(1/p) of all data points (regression, via Rademacher complexity) or Õ(1/p^{1/3}) (classification, via PAC-Bayes margin bounds), and constructs explicit zero-loss solutions with small ℓ∞ norm via Fourier-based methods. Empirically, the paper confirms that the empirical NTK changes negligibly until after overfitting and then shifts drastically, and that gradient descent with tiny ℓ∞ regularization (motivated by AdamW's implicit bias) finds solutions with p-independent weight norms, bridging the kernel-to-rich transition that explains grokking.

## Results

The paper establishes a fundamental sample complexity gap between the kernel and rich regimes for learning modular addition. In the kernel regime, any permutation-equivariant kernel method requires n = Ω(p³) samples (a constant fraction of all p³ possible regression data points) to achieve population ℓ₂ loss below p/2 (half the trivial all-zero predictor). In the rich regime, networks with bounded ℓ∞ norm generalize with only n = Õ(p²) samples for regression (population ℓ₂ loss = O(p²/n · log³n · L(h₀))) and n = Õ(p^(5/3)) samples for classification (0-1 error ≤ Õ(√(p^(5/3)/n))). Empirically, the ℓ∞ norm of weights learned by gradient descent with tiny ℓ∞ regularization (10⁻²⁰) remains constant as p grows, supporting the theoretical assumptions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without traditional method-vs-method baselines; the central comparison is between the kernel regime (NTK approximation) and the rich regime (feature-learning) behavior of the same one-hidden-layer quadratic network. The kernel regime is shown to be fundamentally incapable of generalizing on modular addition without seeing a constant fraction of all possible data points, due to permutation-equivariance of the task and the learning algorithm (Theorems 3.4, 4.1). The rich regime, by contrast, provably generalizes with a vanishing fraction of data when the network achieves zero training loss with bounded ℓ∞ norm (Theorem 3.6) or maximizes the normalized margin (Theorem 4.3). The paper empirically confirms that gradient descent with a tiny ℓ∞ regularizer (weight 10⁻²⁰) finds solutions satisfying the rich-regime conditions, with ℓ∞ norms that do not grow with problem dimension p. Compared to prior theoretical work, Gromov (2023) provided an analytical construction of a grokking-compatible solution but did not prove lower bounds for the kernel regime; Lyu et al. (2023) established the general kernel-to-rich dichotomy framework but left the modular addition-specific sample complexity bounds open. This paper fills both gaps with tight (up to log factors) bounds in both regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first rigorous theoretical explanation for grokking on the original modular addition problem, building on the early/late implicit bias framework of Lyu et al. (2023) and the margin-maximization result of Lyu & Li (2020). It adopts the one-hidden-layer quadratic network architecture from Gromov (2023) and the small-regularization-preserves-max-margin result from Wei et al. (2020), but goes beyond these by proving concrete sample complexity lower bounds for the kernel regime (via a novel permutation-equivariance argument) and matching upper bounds for the rich regime (via Rademacher complexity and PAC-Bayes). The key departure from prior work is showing that kernel-regime failure is not an artifact of a particular kernel but is fundamentally enforced by the permutation symmetry of modular addition itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper closes a significant gap in the grokking literature by providing the first provable explanation for why the original modular addition task exhibits grokking, moving the field from empirical observation and plausible mechanisms to rigorous lower and upper bounds. The permutation-equivariance lower bound technique generalizes to m-summand modular addition and may be of independent interest for understanding symmetry-induced learning barriers. Key open questions remain: the paper does not characterize when gradient descent leaves the kernel regime, cannot yet prove impossibility for 0-1/accuracy loss in the kernel regime (only ℓ₂), and does not propose practical methods to accelerate generalization beyond the known (but slow) initialization-scale trick.
