## Problem

The manifold hypothesis and its extension, the union of manifolds hypothesis, assume that each connected component of image data lies on a single smooth manifold of fixed dimension. This paper shows that local intrinsic dimension varies significantly even within the same label class, contradicting both hypotheses. The paper also seeks to explain a practical consequence: why diffusion models systematically fail to generate accurate high-dimensional local details such as human hands.

## Input / Output

The indicator function I_k takes a dataset of D-dimensional points (images flattened to vectors) and a neighbor count k, producing a scalar that measures the coefficient of variation of local intrinsic dimension estimates across all data points. For synthetic experiments, inputs are 16×16 grayscale images (256-dim vectors) or 32×32 four-block images; for natural datasets, inputs range from 32×32 (CIFAR, MNIST) to 224×224 RGB (ImageNet). The diffusion model experiments produce generated 32×32 images, and the analysis outputs sorted local dimension spectra and classification accuracy tables comparing separately-trained versus mixed-trained generation.

## Method

The paper introduces a novel indicator function I_k based on the variance of Levina-Bickel local intrinsic dimension estimates, proving I_k ≈ 1 under the manifold hypothesis (Proposition 2.2) and showing I_k ≫ 1 for all natural image classes. It then formulates the CW complex hypothesis: image data is supported on unions of CW complexes where cells of different dimensions are glued along boundaries, allowing intrinsic dimension to vary within a connected component. Proposition 4.1 proves that ReLU neural networks naturally map smooth input distributions to CW-complex-supported distributions, establishing compatibility with deep generative models. The key theoretical result (Proposition 4.2) shows that for small noise levels in diffusion models, the score function's magnitude along extra dimensions (M⊖K) is O(1) near the lower-dimensional skeleton K but only O(√δ_t) in the higher-dimensional manifold M, with both regions carrying comparable loss weight—this incentivizes the network to prioritize learning near K and neglect higher-dimensional features. Empirical validation uses a modified MNIST dataset with connected high- and low-dimensional components, confirming that mixed training biases generation toward lower-dimensional features.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that the manifold indicator function $I_k$ is approximately 1 for synthetic data on smooth manifolds (confirming Proposition 2.2), but significantly exceeds 1 for all natural image datasets tested (MNIST, FMNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet), indicating high variance in local intrinsic dimension within individual label classes. For the synthetic 2D geometric dataset, $I_{100}=17.25$ and the 1D skeleton accounts for 27% of all samples. In the diffusion model experiment, training on mixed high/low-dimensional data produces a 5.84% bias toward lower-dimensional components (55.84% classified as low vs. 44.16% as high), while separately trained models achieve 100% correct classification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new generative method to benchmark against existing ones; instead, it tests the manifold hypothesis (Bengio et al., 2013) and the union-of-manifolds hypothesis (Brown et al., 2023) against its proposed CW complex hypothesis using the novel $I_k$ indicator. Synthetic data on smooth manifolds (random ellipsoids) serve as the positive control, consistently yielding $I_k \approx 1$ as predicted by Proposition 2.2. All natural image dataset classes (MNIST, FMNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, ImageNet) show $I_k$ values substantially above 1, rejecting both the manifold and union-of-manifolds hypotheses at the level of individual connected components. In the diffusion model experiment, the baseline of training DDPM on mixed high/low-dimensional data underperforms compared to training separately on each component: the mixed model's generated data $S'$ shows a measurable bias toward lower-dimensional features (55.84% vs. 44.16% split) and a distorted local intrinsic dimension spectrum, while separately trained models reproduce the original dimension distributions faithfully (100% correct classification). This supports the paper's theoretical claim (Proposition 4.2) that the score function has much larger magnitude near lower-dimensional skeletons, causing the network to under-learn higher-dimensional features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on characterizing the geometric structure of image data distributions, building directly on the manifold hypothesis (Bengio et al., 2013) and its challenge by the union-of-manifolds hypothesis (Brown et al., 2023). It departs from both by arguing that intrinsic dimension varies even within a single connected component, and proposes the CW complex hypothesis as a more general structural model. The intrinsic dimension estimation methodology follows Levina-Bickel (2004) and MacKay-Zoubin (2005), while the diffusion model analysis connects to score-based generative modeling (Song & Ermon, 2019; Ho et al., 2020) and the observation that small-t denoising produces local details (Wang & Vastola, 2023). The compatibility result with ReLU networks (Proposition 4.1) repurposes the well-known piecewise-linear structure of such networks to show they naturally produce CW-complex-supported distributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Challenging the manifold hypothesis has broad implications for how generative models are designed and understood, and the CW complex framework provides a more faithful description of real image data that is provably compatible with deep learning. The theoretical explanation for why diffusion models struggle with high-dimensional local features (e.g., hands) offers a concrete, testable hypothesis for a long-standing practical failure mode. However, the diffusion model experiments are limited to a synthetic MNIST-based dataset with a clean high/low dimensional split, and extending the analysis to natural datasets with more complex CW complex structures remains open. The paper also leaves open whether the local geometry regularity assumption (property ii of manifolds) holds, and how to algorithmically exploit the CW complex structure for improved generation.
