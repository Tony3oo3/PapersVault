## Problem

Despite the transformative impact of foundation models across reinforcement learning, robotics, and computer vision, the black-box optimization (BBO) and experimental design community has seen minimal adoption of these techniques. Existing learned BBO methods suffer from three compounding limitations: meta-overfitting that prevents generalization to new tasks, inability to exploit rich unstructured information (task descriptions, textual feedback, code), and poor scalability across diverse search spaces and task families. This position paper argues that sequence-based foundation models (Transformers and LLMs) can simultaneously address all three gaps and calls for wider research and adoption in the BBO field.

## Input / Output

The formalized problem takes a free-form problem meta-description m (which can be arbitrary text hinting at the function class), a search space X represented as a sequence of atomic parameter configurations governed by a feasibility function φ (ranging from unconstrained to global compilation checks), and an optimization history h of (x, y) pairs. Feedback y is not restricted to scalars—it can be numeric, categorical ratings, or subjective sentiment. The model outputs the next candidate suggestion x_t and ultimately the full trajectory H, with the key design choice that both the history and the search space are consumed along a shared sequence axis, making the model's weights independent of trajectory length and search space dimensionality.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a unifying framework that recasts BBO as a sequential decision problem where search spaces are formalized as strings over an alphabet with inductive or global constraints, making them directly amenable to sequence models. The authors organize prior work along an axis of increasing generality—non-learnable rules, feature-based regressors, meta-learned priors, sequential-history RNNs, sequential search-space models, attention-based optimizers, token-based models (e.g., OptFormer), and LLM-based optimizers—showing how each step adds capabilities like in-context learning, search-space transferability, and multi-modality. They identify five concrete challenges (data representation and multimodality, interface standardization, training data availability, generalization and customization, and benchmarking) and argue that Transformer properties—flexible input encoding, superior scalability, and in-context learning—directly resolve the core limitations of prior learned optimizers. The paper closes with a vision for a universal LLM-based optimizer that processes multi-modal metadata, accepts explicit user commands for exploration/exploitation tradeoffs, and manages long-context problem-specific information.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experimental results. It does not propose a new method or report benchmark numbers. Instead, it argues that Transformer-based foundation models (particularly LLMs) are uniquely suited to address three key limitations of learned BBO: (1) poor generalizability beyond meta-training, (2) low flexibility in exploiting unstructured information, and (3) limited scalability across diverse optimization tasks. The paper's central claim is that the sequential input format, scalability, and in-context learning of Transformers collectively overcome these obstacles, as summarized in the capability matrix (Table 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not conduct head-to-head empirical comparisons between methods. The closest analogue is the capability matrix (Table 2), which organizes seven classes of BBO techniques along seven axes. The progression shows that each successive class adds at least one capability: feature-based methods gain in-task adaptability; meta-learned methods add cross-task transfer; sequential history models add in-context learning; sequential search-space models add search-space transfer; attention-based (Transformer) models add length scalability; token-based methods add multi-modality; and LLMs uniquely achieve all seven capabilities including built-in NLP. The paper positions LLMs as the only method class that simultaneously supports all listed capabilities, arguing this is necessary for a truly general-purpose learned optimizer. No quantitative performance gaps between method classes are reported.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: learning-to-optimize (meta-learning for BBO, e.g., Li & Malik 2016; Chen et al. 2017) and foundation models (Transformers, LLMs). It carves new conceptual ground by formalizing BBO search spaces as formal grammars and sequences, thereby making the connection to sequence-based models (RNNs → attention → token-based → LLMs) a natural and progressive generalization rather than an ad hoc application. The paper departs from prior meta-learning BBO work by arguing that the field's core obstacles—poor generalization, rigid feature engineering, and limited scalability—are precisely the ones that Transformer architecture and LLM pretraining are designed to address, yet this connection has not been systematically exploited.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a critical gap: despite the existence of rich training data (optimization trajectories, domain knowledge in text, industry-scale logs from Google Vizier and Ax), the BBO community has not embraced data-driven foundation-model approaches, partly due to proprietary data constraints and the absence of large-scale open benchmarks. Key open questions include optimal tokenization of numeric and mathematical objects, unified interfaces for constraint-following in diverse search spaces, and whether free-form domain knowledge (textbooks, papers) can yield emergent optimization behaviors via generative pretraining. The vision of a universal LLM optimizer handling multi-modal inputs, user-provided feedback, and long-context problem descriptions points toward a paradigm shift, but the path from current capabilities (e.g., LLMs struggling with explore-exploit tradeoffs per Krishnamurthy et al. 2024) to that vision remains substantial.
