## Problem

Existing criticisms of next-token prediction focus on autoregressive inference (snowballing errors), implicitly assuming that teacher-forced training successfully learns an accurate next-token predictor. This paper identifies a deeper, previously unarticulated failure: on 'lookahead tasks' where a later token must be planned before an earlier one, teacher-forcing itself can fail to learn the correct mechanism, producing a model that cannot even predict the next token accurately on its own training distribution.

## Input / Output

The input is a tokenized path-star graph: a randomly shuffled adjacency list of directed edges (each edge written with the vertex closer to start first), followed by the start node and goal node, separated by special tokens '|', '/', and '='. The output is the vertex sequence of the unique path from start to goal. Node values are drawn from {0,...,N-1} with N=100, and the graph has d paths of length l from the central start node. The vocabulary size is N+3, and all training and test graphs share identical topology (same d, l, N) so any failure is purely in-distribution.

## Method

The paper identifies two compounding failure mechanisms of teacher-forcing on lookahead tasks: (1) the 'Clever Hans cheat,' where the model exploits the revealed ground-truth prefix r_{<i} to trivially fit later tokens (e.g., scanning the adjacency list for the outward neighbor of the previous node) without performing any planning, and (2) the 'Indecipherable Token,' where the first node after start becomes unlearnable because the cheat absorbs all subsequent supervision, leaving an all-or-nothing loss over an exponential space of algorithm compositions. They demonstrate this empirically on path-star graphs using GPT-Mini, GPT-2 Large, and Mamba, showing all achieve only 1/d accuracy (random guessing) despite 100% training fit. Two interventions are tested: teacherless training (replacing ground-truth prefixes with dummy '$' tokens, from Monea et al. 2023) which prevents the cheat and enables learning in easier settings, and path reversal (predicting the path from goal to start) which eliminates the Indecipherable Token and yields near-perfect accuracy across all architectures.

## Results

On path-star graph path-finding, all teacher-forced models (GPT-Mini, GPT2-Large, Mamba) achieve only ~1/d accuracy (random-guessing level): ~49% for degree-2 graphs, ~19–20% for degree-5, ~8–10% for degree-10, and ~0–3.5% for degree-20, despite being trained to 100% training accuracy on 200k in-distribution samples. The Clever Hans cheat accuracy (Acc_cheat) reaches ~97–100% across most settings, confirming the model exploits the revealed prefix rather than learning to plan. Reversing the target sequence (Acc_rev) restores near-perfect accuracy (99.6–100%) for all architectures on most graphs, while teacherless training (Acc_$) achieves ~99% for GPT2-Large on G_{2,5}, G_{5,5}, and G_{10,5} but fails for from-scratch models and Mamba on harder topologies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external SOTA baselines; instead it contrasts three training paradigms (teacher-forcing, teacherless, reversed) across three architectures (GPT-Mini, GPT2-Large, Mamba) on the same in-distribution path-star task. Under standard teacher-forcing, all three architectures fail identically at ~1/d accuracy, demonstrating the failure is objective-driven rather than architecture-specific. Reversed training (predicting the path from goal to start) is the most robust fix: GPT2-Large reaches 99.6–99.9% on all five graph topologies, GPT-Mini reaches 99.7–100% on four of five (failing only on G_{20,5}), and Mamba reaches 96.2–99.5% on four of five. Teacherless training (replacing ground-truth prefixes with dummy tokens) is less reliable: GPT2-Large achieves 97.8–99.9% on G_{2,5}, G_{2,10}, G_{5,5}, G_{10,5} but drops to 0% on G_{2,20} and G_{20,5}; GPT-Mini succeeds only on G_{2,5} (99.9%); Mamba fails across all topologies. The Clever Hans cheat metric (Acc_cheat ≈ 97–100%) confirms that teacher-forced models learn a trivial 'follow the adjacent edge' shortcut rather than a planning algorithm, and this shortcut is what prevents them from learning the critical first token (Acc_1st ≈ 1/d).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper consolidates and sharpens a fragmented body of criticism against next-token prediction (snowballing errors in Kääriäinen 2006, Ross & Bagnell 2010, LeCun 2024; planning failures in Momennejad et al. 2023, Valmeekam et al. 2023) by introducing a critical conceptual distinction: autoregressive inference failure versus teacher-forcing failure. It builds directly on the teacherless training objective from Monea et al. (2023, PaSS) and the reversal trick from Lee et al. (2023) and Shen et al. (2023), repurposing both as diagnostic interventions rather than efficiency tools. The paper departs from prior work by arguing that the snowballing-error critique implicitly assumes teacher-forcing has already learned a good predictor, and that this assumption itself can fail on lookahead tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that teacher-forcing can produce a fundamentally inaccurate next-token predictor—even in-distribution, on a trivially solvable task, across both Transformer and Mamba architectures—challenges a core assumption underlying the training of virtually all modern LLMs. The Clever Hans mechanism (revealed answer prefixes enabling shortcut fitting that starves early tokens of supervision) is a concrete, generalizable failure mode that no post-hoc inference wrapper can fix. Key open questions include whether the failure persists at scale (the authors acknowledge they have not tested Llama-2 or Mistral), whether it extends to natural language tasks like story-writing, and whether teacherless or multi-token prediction objectives can be practically deployed as alternatives to standard next-token training.
