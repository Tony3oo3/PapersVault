## Problem

Prior Inverse Constrained Reinforcement Learning (ICRL) methods estimate a single constraint function (or a mean over a constraint distribution) from expert demonstrations, but provide no measure of confidence in the learned constraint. This leaves practitioners unable to decide whether the inferred constraint is reliably at least as constraining as the true underlying constraint, and unable to determine whether they have collected enough expert trajectories to learn a constraint meeting a desired confidence and performance level.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240580/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of expert trajectories D, a desired confidence level λ (e.g., 70% or 80%), and a known reward function, and outputs a constraint φ*(τ) that is at least as constraining as the ground truth constraint with probability ≥ λ, along with an optimal policy satisfying that constraint. For the second objective, given λ and a desired reward threshold δ, the method outputs a binary sufficiency decision indicating whether the current number of expert trajectories is adequate. The constraint is parameterized as a Beta distribution over feasibility probabilities rather than a point estimate, and the actual constraint used for policy optimization is the (1−λ) quantile of that distribution.

## Method

CA-ICRL replaces the mean constraint used in prior Bayesian ICRL methods (e.g., VICRL) with the (1−λ) quantile of a Beta distribution over trajectory feasibility. A neural network with shared transformer encoder blocks takes each expert trajectory and a query trajectory as input, outputting Beta parameters α = [α₁, α₂] where α₁−1 and α₂−1 represent fractional feasibility/infeasibility counts from comparing the query to each expert trajectory. Training alternates between (1) forward control via PPO-Lagrange to maximize reward subject to the quantile-based constraint E[φ*(τ)] ≤ ε, and (2) constraint update by maximizing the likelihood of expert trajectories under the quantile-based feasibility. Since the inverse CDF of the Beta distribution has no closed form, the quantile is approximated via a lookup table and its gradient is computed using finite forward differences (h = 0.002). The partition function Z(w) is estimated via importance sampling from a previous policy iteration, following the standard ICRL approach.

## Results

CA-ICRL (at 70% confidence) achieves a constraint violation rate below 30% across all MuJoCo environments except Biased Pendulum, while consistently obtaining higher cumulative rewards than all four baselines (GACL, BC2L, ICRL, VICRL) during both training and execution phases. At 80% confidence in the Blocked Walker environment, CA-ICRL's constraint violation rate drops below 20% and rewards increase monotonically with the number of expert trajectories (100→300). The method also outperforms VICRL (the only other distribution-based baseline) on both constraint violation rate and rewards across all tested environments, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) in most settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CA-ICRL outperforms all four main baselines (GACL, BC2L, ICRL, VICRL) on both constraint violation rate and cumulative rewards across the seven tested environments (five MuJoCo + two HighD driving). Against GACL and BC2L, CA-ICRL shows statistically significant advantages (p < 0.05) in nearly all environments for both metrics. Against ICRL (Malik et al., 2021), CA-ICRL wins on both metrics in all environments except Biased Pendulum where differences are not significant. Against VICRL (Liu et al., 2023), the closest competitor since it also learns a distribution over constraints, CA-ICRL achieves significantly lower violation rates and higher rewards in most environments, with the exception of Biased Pendulum (p = 0.084 for violation rate) and HighD Velocity (p = 0.039 for rewards, still significant). In the appendix comparison with ICL (Gaurav et al., 2023), CA-ICRL also shows consistently better performance on both metrics in Blocked Ant and Blocked Half-Cheetah.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICRL line of work initiated by Malik et al. (2021) and the Bayesian/variational constraint inference approaches of Liu et al. (2023, VICRL), Glazier et al. (2021), and Papadimitriou et al. (2022). While prior distributional methods (VICRL, BICRL, MESC-IRL) learn a posterior over constraints and return a mean point estimate, CA-ICRL departs by parameterizing the constraint distribution as a per-trajectory Beta distribution and extracting a user-specified quantile (1−λ) as the operative constraint, thereby making confidence an explicit input rather than an implicit property of the posterior. Architecturally, it replaces the variational encoder of VICRL with a transformer-based similarity encoder that outputs Beta parameters (α₁, α₂) by comparing each query trajectory against all expert trajectories, enabling direct per-pair feasibility scoring.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CA-ICRL gives practitioners in safety-critical domains (autonomous driving, robotics) a principled knob to trade off constraint conservatism against reward, and a diagnostic for whether more expert data is needed before deploying a learned policy. Key limitations include the assumption that the reward function is known a priori, the assumption of infallible expert demonstrations, the O(KLMNX) computational cost that limits scalability to very large trajectory sets, and the absence of a hard guarantee of zero ground-truth constraint violation. Open questions include extending to settings where both reward and constraints must be learned, adapting to offline-only training, and handling sub-optimal or noisy experts.
