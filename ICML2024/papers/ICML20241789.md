## Problem

While in-context learning in LLMs has been shown to solve tasks like multi-armed bandits, the specific dynamics of how beliefs are updated—whether symmetrically or asymmetrically, and how task framing modulates this—remained uncharacterized. This paper asks whether LLMs exhibit the same asymmetric belief updating (optimism bias, confirmation bias) documented in human reinforcement learning, and whether such asymmetries are rational or merely artifacts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241789/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For LLMs, input is a dynamically updated text prompt containing a bulleted history of prior slot-machine interactions (machine label, casino, reward) plus a question asking for the next choice; the LLM outputs a single machine label. For Meta-RL agents, input is a compact vector combining one-hot encoded action, scalar reward, normalized time index, casino identity, and trial-type bits, producing a policy distribution and value estimate. The analytical output in both cases is a set of fitted learning rates (α⁺, α⁻ per condition) and posterior probabilities comparing cognitive models (RW, RW±, 3α, 4α, 2α confirmation-bias), extracted via MAP estimation with beta/gamma priors.

## Method

The paper constructs three 2AFC gambling tasks adapted from cognitive psychology (partial feedback, full feedback with counterfactual outcomes, and mixed free/forced-choice trials) and prompts LLMs (primarily Claude-1.2, validated across GPT-4, Llama-2, and Claude variants) to play them over 96–80 trials. Behavioral data are analyzed by fitting Rescorla-Wagner extensions—classical RW, RW± with separate positive/negative learning rates, and a 2α confirmation-bias model that merges confirmatory vs. disconfirmatory errors—using MAP estimation and BIC-based model comparison. To test whether the observed asymmetries are rational, the authors train small transformer agents (dim 8, 2 FFN layers of 128, 8 heads) via Meta-RL (actor-critic with ADAM) on the same task distributions, then evaluate their in-context learning without further weight updates. The key finding is that all three agent types (LLM, human, Meta-RL) show an optimism bias under partial feedback, a reversal (pessimism) for counterfactual/unchosen options under full feedback, and symmetric updating when agency is removed, indicating the asymmetry is a rational response to the task structure rather than a model-specific artifact.

## Results

In the partial feedback 2AFC task, the LLM (Claude-1.2) exhibits a strong optimism bias with α+ = 0.86 ± 0.02 versus α− = 0.08 ± 0.03 (t(49) = 24.2, p < .0001), and the RW± model outperforms the classical RW model with PP = 0.94 ± 0.03. In the full feedback task, the bias reverses for the unchosen option (pessimism bias), and the simplified 2α confirmation-bias model achieves PP = 0.93 ± 0.03. In the agency condition, asymmetric updating is confined to free-choice trials (α+ = 0.36 ± 0.06 > α− = 0.08 ± 0.01, t(23) = 4.7, p = .0001) while forced-choice trials are integrated symmetrically (3α model PP = 0.78 ± 0.06). Meta-RL agents trained on the same tasks reproduce all three patterns (PP_RW± = 0.97 ± 0.02; PP_3α = 0.85 ± 0.04) and outperform both LLMs and humans on regret.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LLM in-context learning dynamics against two human-behavior baselines and an idealized Meta-RL agent. Against Lefebvre et al. (2017) on the partial feedback task, the LLM shows the same qualitative optimism bias (α+ > α−) but with a much larger asymmetry (α+ = 0.86 vs. α− = 0.08) than the human participants, whose bias is described as 'less pronounced.' Against Chambon et al. (2020) on the full feedback and agency tasks, the LLM replicates the human pattern of confirmation bias (optimism for chosen, pessimism for unchosen) and the agency-dependent disappearance of asymmetry. On the agency task, the LLM significantly outperforms human participants in final regret (0.02 ± 0.01 vs. 0.07 ± 0.02; t(143) = 2.7, p = .008), learning faster over trials. The Meta-RL agent, serving as a rationality benchmark, reproduces all three behavioral patterns and achieves the lowest regret across all tasks, supporting the interpretation that the observed asymmetries are rational rather than pathological.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the growing literature on in-context learning in LLMs (Brown et al., 2020; Binz & Schulz, 2023; Coda-Forno et al., 2023), which has shown LLMs can solve bandit-like tasks without weight updates, and (2) the cognitive science literature on asymmetric belief updating in humans (Lefebvre et al., 2017; Chambon et al., 2020; Palminteri & Lebreton, 2022). The key methodological contribution is using the Rescorla-Wagner± model as an analytical lens to extract interpretable learning-rate parameters from LLM behavior, and then validating whether the observed asymmetries are rational by training idealized Meta-RL agents (Duan et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2016) on the same tasks. Unlike prior LLM bandit studies that focused on whether in-context learning works, this paper characterizes *how* it works and whether the resulting dynamics match known human cognitive biases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLMs exhibit the same framing-dependent asymmetric updating as humans (optimism bias for chosen options, reversal for counterfactual/unchosen options, disappearance without agency) suggests these are not implementation artifacts but reflect rational solutions to the task structure, as confirmed by Meta-RL agents. Practically, this means problem framing (who is choosing, what feedback is revealed) is a lever that practitioners can use to shape LLM learning behavior, with implications for recommendation systems, risk assessment, and disinformation. Key open questions include whether the patterns persist in more naturalistic, open-ended settings, whether the cognitive-model parameter link is truly causal (the authors suggest neuron-level interventions as a test), and whether asymmetric updating emerges even when it is suboptimal (as it does in humans under threat, Globig et al., 2021).
