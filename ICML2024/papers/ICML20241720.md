## Problem

While the implicit bias of gradient descent is well understood in supervised learning, it remains largely unexplored in optimal control (reinforcement learning). A critical practical question is whether a controller learned via policy gradient on a limited set of initial states will extrapolate to unseen initial states — a concern that is especially acute in safety-critical applications like robotics and autonomous driving. This paper provides the first theoretical analysis of how the implicit bias of policy gradient governs extrapolation to unseen initial states in underdetermined LQR problems.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a linear system defined by transition matrix A ∈ R^{D×D} and input matrix B ∈ R^{D×D} (full rank), with a quadratic cost defined by Q (identity in the main analysis) and R = 0 (unregularized controls). Training is performed on a finite set S of initial states that does not span R^D, making the problem underdetermined with infinitely many cost-minimizing controllers. The output is a linear state-feedback controller K ∈ R^{D×D} that assigns control u_h = Kx_h at each step, and extrapolation is quantified on an orthonormal basis U for S^⊥ via optimality and cost measures normalized by the non-extrapolating baseline K_no-ext.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution establishes that extrapolation to unseen initial states depends on the degree of exploration — the overlap between states encountered along training trajectories and directions orthogonal to S. Proposition 1 proves that without exploration (X_pg ⊆ span(S)), policy gradient assigns zero control to all unexplored directions, yielding no extrapolation. Proposition 2 constructs a shift-matrix system A_shift that cyclically traverses the state space, showing policy gradient achieves extrapolation ratios bounded by 4(D−1)²/(H+D)² for both optimality and cost measures. Theorem 1 extends this to randomly generated Gaussian A, proving a single policy gradient iteration yields non-trivial extrapolation in expectation and with high probability for large D, using genus expansion techniques from random matrix theory and topology. A striking finding is that the implicit bias of policy gradient does not minimize the Euclidean norm of K (unlike supervised learning), since the minimum-norm controller is precisely the non-extrapolating K_no-ext. Experiments on pendulum and quadcopter problems with neural network controllers confirm that the exploration-based extrapolation phenomenon extends beyond linear systems.

## Results

The paper establishes that in underdetermined LQR, the extrapolation ratio (policy gradient vs. non-extrapolating controller) is bounded by 4(D−1)²/(H+D)² for both optimality and cost measures in the exploration-inducing (shift) setting (Proposition 2), approaching zero as H→∞. For random Gaussian systems, a single policy gradient iteration achieves an expected optimality ratio of at most 1 − η·H(H−1)/D, with high-probability guarantees when D is sufficiently large (Theorem 1). Empirically (D=5, H=5), the identity system (A=I) yields no extrapolation (ratio ≈ 1), while shift and random systems yield substantial extrapolation (ratio well below 1). Non-linear experiments with neural network controllers on pendulum and quadcopter problems confirm near-perfect extrapolation to unseen initial states at lower heights.

**Summary of theoretical extrapolation guarantees (ratio of policy gradient to K_no-ext; lower is better, 0 = perfect, 1 = no extrapolation).**

| Typical / random (Thm. 1) | A ~ Gaussian(0, 1/D) | ≤ 1 − η·H(H−1)/D (in expectation) | — (not covered) | Yes (non-trivial) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other learning algorithms; instead, the baseline is the non-extrapolating controller K_no-ext, which minimizes the training cost while assigning zero controls to states orthogonal to the training set S. Policy gradient is shown to strictly outperform K_no-ext (i.e., achieve a lower extrapolation ratio) whenever the system induces exploration from the training initial states. In the non-exploratory setting (A = B = I), policy gradient matches K_no-ext exactly, confirming that exploration is necessary. In the shift and random settings, policy gradient achieves substantially lower ratios, with the shift system yielding the strongest guarantees. A notable finding is that policy gradient does NOT implicitly minimize the Euclidean norm (unlike supervised learning), since K_no-ext is the unique minimum-norm controller among all training-cost minimizers, yet policy gradient converges to a different, extrapolating controller.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the extensive literature on implicit bias of gradient descent in supervised learning (where underdetermined linear prediction is the canonical testbed) to the framework of optimal control, specifically underdetermined LQR problems. Prior analyses of policy gradient for LQR (Fazel et al., Malik et al., Bu et al., Zhang et al.) either assume a unique global minimizer (R positive definite, training states spanning the state space) or study implicit bias in determined settings, leaving the question of how implicit bias affects extrapolation to unseen initial states open. The paper also draws on random matrix theory tools from Redelmeier (2014) (genus expansion for expectations of traces of random matrix products) to handle the technical challenges of analyzing random transition matrices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a new conceptual framework linking extrapolation in optimal control to the degree of exploration induced by the system from training initial states, and provides the first theoretical analysis of implicit bias for underdetermined LQR. A key open question is whether the exploration-dependent extrapolation mechanism generalizes to non-linear systems and neural network controllers (supported only empirically here), and whether practical methods can be developed for informed selection of training initial states. Significant limitations remain: the high-probability guarantee in Theorem 1 has super-exponential dependence on horizon H, applies only to the first policy gradient iteration, and covers only the optimality measure rather than the cost measure.
