## Problem

Computing Hessian diagonals for neural networks is prohibitively expensive, and existing scalable approximations—whether stochastic (AdaHessian, GGN-MC) or deterministic (BL89)—suffer from poor accuracy. The paper revisits the long-overlooked BL89 method, which matches the computational cost of a gradient, and asks whether a minimal modification can close the accuracy gap without sacrificing scalability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240496/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

HesScale consumes a feed-forward or convolutional neural network's weights, a differentiable loss function, and a single data sample (or transition tuple in RL), and produces per-layer Hessian diagonal estimates via a single backward pass. The approximation at each layer requires only the gradient and second-order estimate from the next layer, making it a natural extension of standard backpropagation with linear memory overhead. The final outputs are either updated network parameters (via AdaHesScale) or a scaled step-size factor (via trust-region clipping), both computed in the same backward pass as the gradient.

## Method

HesScale refines BL89 by replacing the approximate Hessian diagonal at the final layer with the exact one, which is computable in linear time for common losses (e.g., cross-entropy with softmax yields q − q∘q, Gaussian log-likelihood yields simple element-wise expressions). For all intermediate layers, it propagates second-order information by dropping off-diagonal terms of the Hessian w.r.t. pre-activations, yielding a backpropagation rule with linear complexity in both parameter count and output dimension. The paper justifies this approximation empirically by showing that the propagated Hessian matrices are strongly diagonally dominant (ρ ≈ 0.75–0.98) before and after training. Two applications are built on top: AdaHesScale, an Adam-style optimizer that replaces squared gradients with squared HesScale estimates in the second moment, and a trust-region step-size scaling mechanism that clips updates when uᵀHu exceeds a radius, improving robustness and stability in RL.

## Results

On approximation quality (MNIST, 3-layer tanh MLP), HesScale achieves the lowest L¹ error among all deterministic and stochastic methods, with all other methods (BL89, AdaHessian-MC50, GGN-MC50, diag(G)) incurring normalized errors above 1 relative to HesScale. On CIFAR-100 3C3D, AdaHesScaleGN consistently outperforms all competing optimizers in both accuracy and wall-clock time; on CIFAR-100 ALL-CNN, AdaHesScaleGN outperforms all methods except AdaHesScale. In MuJoCo A2C, AdaHesScale significantly boosts undiscounted return on Ant, Walker2d, HalfCheetah, and Hopper over both Adam and AdaHessian, while in PPO it matches Adam and outperforms AdaHessian in most environments. Step-size scaling with HesScale renders the optimizer insensitive to step-size choice across all MuJoCo environments (except Humanoid) and prevents the policy collapse observed with plain Adam on the UR-Reacher real-robot task after ~80K steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BL89 (Becker & LeCun 1989), HesScale achieves substantially lower approximation error (BL89's normalized error is well above 1 in Fig. 2a), and on CIFAR-100 BL89 achieves below 0.35 validation accuracy (effectively non-functional), while AdaHesScale and AdaHesScaleGN reach competitive or superior accuracy. Against AdaHessian (Yao et al. 2021), HesScale dominates in approximation quality even with 50 Monte Carlo samples, and in optimization AdaHessian performs worse than all methods except BL89 on CIFAR-100 while also requiring the most wall-clock time (~3× Adam vs. ~2× for AdaHesScale). Against GGN-MC (Dangel et al. 2020b), HesScale provides better approximation quality with fewer MC samples, and GGN-MC is excluded from RL experiments due to BackPACK's limitation to classification/regression. Against exact GGN diagonals (Schraudolph 2002), HesScale captures the full Hessian (not just GGN) and thus achieves lower error on non-piecewise-linear activations like tanh. Against Adam (Kingma & Ba 2014), AdaHesScale wins on A2C in 4 of 5 MuJoCo environments, ties on PPO, and the HesScale-based step-size scaling mechanism makes the optimizer robust to step-size selection and stable on the UR-Reacher robot task where plain Adam's policy deteriorates after ~80K steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits the Becker and LeCun (1989) deterministic Hessian diagonal approximation, which the authors describe as 'long-overlooked' by the community in favor of stochastic estimators (AdaHessian, GGN-MC) and structured approximations (KFAC, GGN-based methods). The key modification—using exact Hessian diagonals at the final layer rather than propagating the BL89 approximation through it—yields HesScale, which retains BL89's linear computational complexity while substantially improving accuracy. The step-size scaling application builds on the trust-region mechanism from KFAC (Martens and Grosse 2015) and its adaptation to RL (ACKTR, Wu et al. 2017), replacing the expensive KFAC curvature with the cheaper HesScale approximation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HesScale offers a practically attractive option for second-order optimization in settings where memory and compute are constrained (small networks, RL with compact policies), outperforming both stochastic estimators (even with 50 MC samples) and other deterministic methods at gradient-level cost. The diagonal dominance analysis (ρ values of 0.54–0.98 for Hessian w.r.t. pre-activations) provides a useful empirical justification for why dropping off-diagonals is effective, though it is not a formal guarantee. Key limitations include evaluation on relatively small networks (64-unit MLPs in RL, modest CNNs in supervised learning) and reliance on closed-form last-layer Hessian diagonals that are available only for specific loss functions (softmax cross-entropy, Gaussian log-likelihood). Whether the approximation quality advantage persists in very large networks with non-diagonally-dominant curvature remains an open question.
