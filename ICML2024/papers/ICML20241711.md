## Problem

While large language models exhibit 'sparks' of compositionality (e.g., applying sequences of arithmetic operations), it remains unclear whether they genuinely learn to compose atomic capabilities or merely memorize training examples. This paper asks whether an autoregressive Transformer trained on a compositional data-generating process, without any architectural modifications, can learn to compose its capabilities and generalize to unseen function compositions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241711/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of L task tokens (each specifying one function from a predefined set of bijections or permutations) followed by K=6 data tokens drawn from a vocabulary of size 10. In the step-by-step format, the sequence also includes all intermediate outputs of the composition (analogous to chain-of-thought), while the direct format omits them. The model must autoregressively generate the final composed output (and intermediate outputs in step-by-step mode), with accuracy measured as the average over the last 6 generated tokens.

## Method

The paper defines a synthetic domain where functions are bijections (element-wise token substitutions, |F_b|=10!) and permutations (token reordering, |F_p|=6!), organized into L position-specific subsets to enable in-order vs. out-of-order composition analysis. A 12-layer nanoGPT Transformer is trained autoregressively on 100K samples using cross-entropy loss, with two prompt formats (step-by-step and direct) and controlled training data diversity (base, random in-order, or out-of-order compositions). The key empirical finding is that step-by-step prompting enables generalization to exponentially (N^L) or combinatorially ((N×L)^L) many unseen compositions from as few as 30–100 training compositions, while direct prompting fails even with 2000 compositions. Mechanistically, linear probing and attention map analysis reveal that attention layers select the relevant task token (analogous to induction heads) while MLP layers execute the corresponding function, a division of labor confirmed by a constructive proof of a 1-layer Transformer that compositionally generalizes via step-by-step inference.

## Results

A 12-layer nanoGPT Transformer trained on only ~100 in-order compositions of 5 bijections (step-by-step format) generalizes to all 3,125 unseen in-order compositions with near-perfect accuracy. In the direct prompt format, the same architecture fails to generalize even when trained on 2,000 of the 3,125 compositions, though it succeeds when composing bijections with permutations (250/625 compositions suffice). For out-of-order compositions (4,084,101 total), a small number of out-of-order examples in training (100) yields partial generalization, but full generalization is not achieved. LSTMs of comparable capacity fail to compositionally generalize in both prompt formats, with best accuracy around 88% (modified prompt) versus ~100% for Transformers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the step-by-step and direct prompt formats for the same Transformer architecture. Step-by-step prompting enables compositional generalization with as few as 100 training compositions, while direct prompting fails to generalize even with 2,000 compositions (64% of the full set) on the same task. A second key comparison is against LSTMs: LSTMs of 1–4 layers and hidden dimensions up to 1024 fail to compositionally generalize in both direct and step-by-step formats (best ~88% with a modified prompt that reduces long-range dependency burden), whereas even a 1-layer Transformer achieves near-perfect generalization. The paper also compares different training data configurations: a 'base' null model (only atomic functions, no multi-function compositions) fails to generalize, while adding just 10 in-order or 100 out-of-order compositions to the base set unlocks in-order or partial out-of-order generalization respectively. No external pretrained model baselines are used; all comparisons are within the controlled synthetic setup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant negative-result tradition in compositional generalization (Liška et al., 2018; Lake & Baroni, 2018; Csordás et al., 2022), which typically shows neural networks fail to compose and then proposes architectural fixes. Instead, it demonstrates that standard autoregressive Transformers, trained without modification on compositional synthetic data, can compositionally generalize to exponentially or combinatorially many unseen functions. It builds on the step-wise inference / chain-of-thought literature (Kojima et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022b) by showing that intermediate outputs are not merely a prompting trick but are mechanistically necessary for composition, and connects to mechanistic interpretability work (Olsson et al., 2022; Geva et al., 2022) by providing a constructive 1-layer Transformer proof whose attention-selects / MLP-executes mechanism is confirmed empirically via linear probing and attention map visualization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central implication—that a model pretrained on compositional data can acquire an 'explosion of capabilities' far exceeding its training set—argues that benchmarking atomic capabilities is fundamentally insufficient for characterizing what a pretrained model can do, with direct consequences for AI safety and capability evaluation. The finding that step-by-step prompting is dramatically more effective than direct prompting for composition (even 2000/3125 training compositions fail in the direct format) provides a concrete, mechanistically-grounded explanation for why chain-of-thought works. Limitations include the restriction to linear chains (not tree-structured compositions), small synthetic models, and the fact that out-of-order generalization remains incomplete even with substantial data diversity, leaving open whether these findings transfer to the complex, entangled function spaces of real LLMs.
