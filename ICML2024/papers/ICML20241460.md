## Problem

Interactive learning from hindsight instruction feedback—where a teacher labels the agent's generated response with a suitable instruction rather than providing expert demonstrations or rewards—has been explored empirically but lacks any theoretical understanding. This paper initiates that analysis by first proving a lower bound showing that in the general case, regret for any algorithm must scale polynomially with the response space size (making it intractable for trajectory-level responses), and then identifying a low-rank structural assumption under which provable no-regret learning becomes possible.

## Input / Output

In each round, the agent receives a natural language instruction (e.g., 'go to the red cup') and a context (an image or environment state describing object positions, colors, and sizes), and must generate a response such as a trajectory or discrete action. The agent never observes the true reward or an expert response; instead it receives a hindsight instruction—a natural language description of what the agent actually did—sampled from a teacher distribution P(X|Y,S). In the image selection experiment, inputs are 200×200×3 RGB images with instructions generated from 10 natural language templates over 4 object types and 9 colors, and the output is one of K=5 discrete actions.

## Method

LORIL (Learning in LOw-Rank models from Instruction Labels) exploits a low-rank decomposition of the teacher distribution P(x|y,s) = f*(x)ᵀg*(y,s), where the agent knows the response embedding g* but must learn the instruction embedding f* from a model class F via maximum likelihood estimation on historical hindsight labels. Exploration is governed by an optimism-under-uncertainty principle: the agent selects the response maximizing the estimated model value plus an elliptic bonus proportional to ‖g*(y,s)‖_{Σ̂ₜ⁻¹}, which quantifies uncertainty in directions poorly covered by past data. The key theoretical result is a regret bound of Õ(√(Td)) that depends only on the intrinsic rank d and the statistical complexity of F, not on the (potentially exponentially large) response, instruction, or context spaces. A matching lower bound of Ω(√(KT)) in the general case (where K is the response space size) establishes that the low-rank structure is necessary for tractable learning.

## Results

On the synthetic low-rank task (|X|=2000, d=10, |Y|=10), LORIL achieves the lowest cumulative regret among all baselines, with a 12.3% reduction in final regret compared to the next best method. On the grounded image selection task (|Y|=5, natural language instructions, non-low-rank teacher), LORIL achieves 4.8% less regret than the next best baseline, demonstrating that the elliptic bonus exploration strategy transfers even when the low-rank assumption is violated. The theoretical regret bound scales as Õ(√(Td·log(T|F|/δ)·log(1+TB))), depending on intrinsic dimension d rather than the size of the response space |Y|.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LORIL against three baselines: Random (uniformly random actions), ε-Greedy (MLE-based exploitation with random exploration at rate ε), and Greedy (pure exploitation via MLE, ε=0). On the synthetic task, LORIL outperforms all baselines, with the 12.3% regret improvement over the next best method (ε-Greedy) demonstrating that the elliptic bonus provides more principled exploration than uniform random exploration. The improvement over Greedy confirms that exploration is necessary for effective learning. On the image selection task, LORIL again achieves the best performance with a 4.8% regret advantage over the next best baseline, though the authors note the task was not designed to be exploration-heavy, making the gains smaller. The consistent ordering Greedy < ε-Greedy < LORIL across both experiments validates both the need for exploration and the superiority of the elliptic bonus over ε-greedy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of low-rank interactive learning (building directly on FLAMBE's MLE bounds from Agarwal et al., 2020 and the elliptic bonus exploration mechanism from linear bandits, Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011) and the empirically-studied but theoretically unexplored setting of hindsight instruction labeling (Andrychowicz et al., 2017; Fried et al., 2018; Nguyen et al., 2021). The key conceptual departure from contextual bandits is that the feedback signal is a sampled instruction from a conditional distribution over the agent's response, rather than a direct reward or expert demonstration, which prevents straightforward adaptation of existing bandit or imitation learning algorithms. The paper thus carves out a new feedback type within the interactive learning paradigm and shows that optimism-under-uncertainty with an elliptic bonus, combined with low-rank structure, yields the first finite-sample regret guarantees for this setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical treatment of interactive learning from hindsight instruction, establishing both a lower bound (regret must scale with response space size without structure) and a matching upper bound under low-rank assumptions, which provides a clear roadmap for when hindsight instruction learning is statistically tractable. The practical relevance is significant for training embodied agents and language models where expert trajectories are expensive but natural-language descriptions of what an agent actually did are cheap to elicit. Key open questions include removing the assumption that the response embedding g* is known, handling computationally intractable response spaces (e.g., trajectories), and extending beyond low-rank to less restrictive function approximation classes (the authors suggest the Decision Estimation Coefficient framework as a path forward). The empirical gains over baselines are modest (12.3% and 4.8%), and the second experiment deliberately uses a non-challenging exploration setting, limiting the strength of the practical evidence.
