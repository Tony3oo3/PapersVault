## Problem

Despite the proven NP-hardness of neural network training and the non-convexity of loss landscapes, stochastic first-order methods reliably find good solutions in practice. No robust mathematical characterization exists for why optimization trajectories through neural loss landscapes are empirically far simpler than worst-case theory predicts, leaving a gap between theoretical assumptions (e.g., smoothness) and the actual geometry encountered during training.

## Input / Output

The measurement protocol requires two identical training runs with the same initialization and minibatch sequence: the first run produces the final iterate w_T as a reference point, and the second run computes RSI, EB, and γ at every step using the stochastic minibatch gradient G_t and the vector (w_t − w_T). Outputs are per-step scalar values of RSI, EB, and their ratio γ (the cosine similarity between the stochastic gradient and the direction to w_T), plus a derived locally optimal learning rate η* = RSI/EB². The final epoch is excluded to avoid numerical precision issues when w_t ≈ w_T.

## Method

The paper introduces an empirical framework for measuring local Restricted Secant Inequality (RSI) and Error Bound (EB) quantities on stochastic minibatch gradients along actual optimization paths, rather than assuming global conditions over the entire parameter space. The key geometric insight is that the ratio γ = RSI/EB equals the cosine similarity between the sampled gradient and the direction toward the final iterate, providing a direct measure of whether each gradient step points 'the right way.' Across image classification (CIFAR-10, ImageNet), semantic segmentation (Vaihingen), and language modeling (WikiText-2, Penn Treebank, WikiText-103) with diverse architectures, optimizers, batch sizes, and seeds, γ is almost always positive and remarkably stable, while RSI and EB follow predictable trends tied to interpolation vs. non-interpolation regimes. Theoretically, stable RSI ≥ μ and EB ≤ L guarantee linear convergence of SGD at rate (1 − μ²/L²)^t, and the locally optimal step size η* = RSI/EB² reproduces the shape of empirically validated learning rate schedules (linear warmup + cosine annealing on ImageNet, linear decay on WikiText-2).

## Results

Across all tested settings (CIFAR-10, ImageNet-1K, WikiText-2, Vaihingen), the cosine similarity γ between the negative stochastic gradient and the direction to the final iterate is almost always positive and remarkably stable: on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18), γ predominantly remains within [0.0075, 0.02] with no negative values across the entire training run; on WikiText-2 (Transformer), γ strictly exceeds 0.05 after the second epoch; on ImageNet (ResNet-50), γ stays positive throughout with low variance until the final epochs. The locally optimal learning rate derived from RSI/EB closely mirrors empirically validated schedules (linear warmup + cosine annealing on ImageNet, linear decay on WikiText-2). Validation performance confirms the experiments are meaningful: ResNet-18 reaches 90.25% on CIFAR-10, ResNet-50 reaches 72.31–73.67% on ImageNet, and the Transformer achieves 60.72 perplexity on WikiText-2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical characterization study rather than a method comparison, so no external baselines are benchmarked. Internally, the paper compares three optimizers on CIFAR-10 (SGD, SGD+momentum, Adam): Adam yields lower γ values but with reduced variance, while momentum (β=0.9) has negligible impact on γ compared to plain SGD. Batch size experiments on both CIFAR-10 (64–512) and WikiText-2 (32–256) consistently show a positive correlation between batch size and cosine similarity, with γ appearing to scale roughly with the square root of batch size. Architecture comparisons on ImageNet (ResNet-18/50/152, standard and doubled width) reveal that increased depth slightly enhances γ while increased width has a comparatively trivial effect. Two synthetic counter-examples (Asymmetric Linear Model and Sinusoidal Mixture) demonstrate that the observed geometric regularity is not a trivial artifact of the measurement protocol, as these simpler functions exhibit negative RSI and γ values and unpredictable trajectories.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the Restricted Secant Inequality (RSI) and Error Bound (EB) framework from Guille-Escuret et al. (2021, 2022) — previously used in theoretical convergence analyses — as an empirical measurement tool for real neural network training trajectories. It departs from the dominant smoothness-based paradigm in optimization theory, which the authors argue is ill-suited to neural networks due to computational cost, additional assumptions, and directional sharpness phenomena. The work extends qualitative observations by Goodfellow et al. (2015) and Lucas et al. (2021) that loss landscapes are 'simpler than expected' into a quantitative, reproducible protocol, while also connecting to the NTK-regime literature (Liu et al., 2023) that verifies related conditions only in unrealistic infinite-width settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, multi-setting empirical verification that the RSI/EB ratio (cosine similarity γ) remains positive and stable throughout training across diverse architectures, datasets, and optimizers — a property that theoretically guarantees linear convergence and explains why standard LR schedules work. A key open question is why this geometric regularity holds: the authors conjecture high dimensionality and data manifold structure but offer no formal proof. The limitation of using the final iterate as a reference point (introducing a correlation bias in late training) and the unexplained role of momentum (which exploits properties beyond RSI/EB) remain important gaps for future theoretical work.
