## Problem

Comparing a proposed decision policy against a confounded status quo policy is challenging because the status quo depends on unobserved factors, making its performance only partially identifiable. Existing off-policy evaluation frameworks either bound each policy's performance independently before differencing (introducing redundant uncertainty in the agreement region where both policies act identically) or are locked to a single causal assumption. This paper asks: can we recover tighter, more informative regret intervals for policy comparison by exploiting the structure of the comparison itself, while remaining interoperable across multiple identification strategies?

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational data O = {(X_i, D_i^π0, T_i^π, Y_i)} collected under the status quo policy, where Y is only observed when D^π0 = 1 (selective labels setting), along with a pair of pointwise bounding functions (τ̲(x), τ̄(x)) derived from a chosen causal assumption (MSM, IV, Rosenbaum's Γ, or proximal variable) and the proposed policy π(x) known in advance by model developers. The output is a δ-regret interval [R̲_δ, R̄_δ] for the difference in a chosen performance measure (TPR, FPR, PPV, NPV, or expected utility) between the proposed and status quo policies, computed by directly optimizing the performance difference over a constrained uncertainty set rather than differencing individual policy bounds.

## Method

The core insight is uncertainty cancellation: by decomposing policy performance into v-statistics v_y(t,d) = p(T^π=t, D^π0=d, Y(1)=y), terms that appear identically in both policies (e.g., v_0(0,0) for accuracy) cancel in the regret expression, so they need not contribute to the uncertainty interval. The method maps a range of causal assumptions to pointwise bounding functions τ(x) on the unobserved outcome regression E[Y(1)|D=0,X=x], then constructs a minimal constrained uncertainty set V(p;τ) over the partially identified v-statistics using only nominal propensity scores e_d(x) and the known policy π_t(x). The δ-regret interval is obtained by directly maximizing/minimizing the performance difference δ_m(v_0, v_1) over V(p;τ), yielding provably tighter bounds than the baseline approach (Theorem 4.3 quantifies the improvement as proportional to the uncertainty in the cancellation term). For finite-sample estimation, the paper provides both a plug-in estimator (inheriting nuisance convergence rates) and a doubly-robust estimator with second-order error that achieves root-n rates when nuisance functions converge at n^{-1/4}, implemented via K-fold cross-fitting (Algorithm 1).

## Results

The δ-regret interval is provably tighter than the baseline regret interval (Theorem 4.3), with improvement Δ(m,𝒱) proportional to the uncertainty α in the cancellation term v₀(0,0). In synthetic experiments under both MSM and IV assumptions, the δ-regret yields tighter bounds across all five policy performance measures (accuracy, TPR, FPR, PPV, NPV) except PPV, where the two intervals coincide. In the real-world healthcare enrollment application (~48,000 patients from Obermeyer et al., 2019), the δ-regret interval for NPV excludes zero across all subpopulations except White patients under 65, while the baseline interval contains zero for all subpopulations except those aged 65+, enabling a stronger conclusion that the algorithmic policy reduces NPV relative to the status quo.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the proposed δ-regret interval and the baseline regret interval (which independently bounds each policy's performance then takes the difference). The δ-regret interval dominates the baseline across all policy performance measures in synthetic experiments under both MSM (Λ=1.4) and IV assumptions, with the exception of PPV where the two are identical (Δ=0 by Theorem 4.3). The improvement is largest when the uncertainty in the cancellation term v₀(0,0) is high, as predicted by the theoretical bound. In the healthcare enrollment application, the δ-regret interval supports a stronger pre-deployment conclusion: it excludes zero for NPV in nearly all subpopulations, whereas the baseline interval contains zero for most groups, making it impossible to rule out no improvement. The doubly robust estimator achieves n^{-1/2} convergence rates (requiring only n^{-1/4} nuisance estimation), compared to the slower non-parametric rate of the plug-in estimator, though both are validated for correct coverage in synthetic experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the off-policy evaluation (OPE) literature from single-policy value estimation to comparative policy evaluation under unmeasured confounding, building directly on Rambachan et al. (2022) for doubly-robust estimation of predictive performance measures and Kallus & Zhou (2021) for the marginal sensitivity model framework. The key conceptual departure is the 'uncertainty cancellation' insight: by decomposing policy performance into v-statistics, common partially-identified terms (e.g., v₀(0,0) in the agreement region) cancel in the regret difference, yielding tighter δ-regret intervals than the baseline approach of independently bounding each policy. Unlike prior confounding-robust policy learning frameworks (Zhang et al. 2021, Kallus & Zhou 2021) that target mean potential outcomes and are tied to a single causal assumption, this work targets predictive performance measures (accuracy, PPV, NPV, utility) and is interoperable with a family of identification strategies (IV, MSM, Rosenbaum's Γ, proximal variable) through a unified pointwise bounding functions interface.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical tool for pre-deployment evaluation of algorithmic policies in high-stakes domains (healthcare enrollment, criminal justice) where regulatory frameworks increasingly require explicit comparisons against the status quo. The real-world application demonstrates that the δ-regret interval can support more conclusive policy assessments (excluding zero for most subpopulations) than the baseline approach, which is practically significant for deployment decisions. Limitations include inheriting slow non-parametric convergence rates for the plug-in estimator (though doubly robust estimators recover root-n rates), the doubly robust construction being provided only for the MSM, and the binary-action/binary-outcome setting. Open questions include extension to multi-action or sequential decision settings, and whether the uncertainty cancellation principle generalizes to other sources of uncertainty such as missing protected attributes or measurement error.
