## Problem

ReLU DNNs are unsuitable for implicit neural representation (INR) tasks because their monotonically increasing activations produce a severely ill-conditioned feature embedding matrix (condition number Ω(K³)), causing gradient descent to converge slowly on high-frequency, low-dimensional data. Prior work circumvents this by replacing ReLUs with non-standard activations (sine, Gaussian, Gabor) or adding positional encoding, but these choices lack the rich theoretical understanding available for ReLU networks. This paper asks whether the ill-conditioning itself can be remedied while retaining ReLU activations, thereby unlocking existing ReLU theory for INR analysis.

## Input / Output

The DNN takes 2D spatial coordinates (x, y) as input and outputs a scalar pixel value, learning a continuous image representation. For CT reconstruction, the network is trained against 100 equally spaced Radon measurements of a 326×435 chest X-ray; for super resolution, it is trained against a downsampled version of a DIV2K image to produce a 4× upscaled result; for signal representation, it fits a 256×256 cameraman image. A key architectural detail is that each neuron applies a scaling parameter c to its pre-activation, i.e., ψ(c·(wᵀx − b)), which controls the spatial support and oscillation frequency of the B-spline wavelet basis functions.

## Method

The core mechanism constrains groups of seven ReLU neurons in each hidden layer to share input weights and maintain fixed relative offsets, so that each group collectively computes a second-order B-spline wavelet ψ(x) — a compactly supported, semi-orthogonal function. This makes the Gram matrix of the feature embeddings well-conditioned (O(1) instead of O(K³) for unconstrained ReLUs), because wavelets at different scales are orthogonal and same-scale wavelets have limited overlap. The resulting BW-ReLU network is exactly representable as a standard ReLU network with 7K neurons, so no representational power is lost on bounded domains. The authors further connect the BW-ReLU function space to the variation norm framework for ReLU networks, showing that the variation norm of each BW-ReLU neuron is 16‖v‖₂‖w‖₂ (or 16c‖v‖₂‖w‖₂ with scaling), providing a computable regularity measure that correlates with generalization and offers a principled criterion for selecting the scaling hyperparameter c without a validation set.

## Results

The paper evaluates BW-ReLU networks on three INR tasks: CT reconstruction (100 views of a 326×435 chest X-ray), signal representation (256×256 cameraman image), and 4× super resolution (DIV2K). Across all tasks, BW-ReLU achieves PSNR comparable to or slightly better than SIREN, WIRE, and ReLU+Positional Encoding, while dramatically outperforming plain ReLU networks that suffer from spectral bias. In the CT reconstruction ablation (Figure 7), the optimal scaling parameter c=3 yields 32.1 dB PSNR, and this c value corresponds to the lowest variation norm across all layers, confirming the theoretical prediction that minimum-norm solutions generalize best.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BW-ReLU is compared against three established INR architectures: SIREN (sine activation), WIRE (complex Gabor wavelet activation), and ReLU+Positional Encoding (Fourier features). On CT reconstruction, BW-ReLU performs 'just as well and perhaps slightly better' than all three baselines. On signal representation, BW-ReLU achieves comparable PSNR and converges as fast as the other methods. On 4× super resolution, BW-ReLU performs 'slightly better than the rest.' The most striking comparison is against plain ReLU (no constraints, no positional encoding), which fails to capture high-frequency content due to spectral bias—BW-ReLU with its B-spline wavelet constraints recovers this capability while remaining a pure ReLU network. The paper also shows that among BW-ReLU networks trained to the same loss, the one with the lowest variation norm (c=3 for CT, c=3 for super resolution) consistently achieves the highest PSNR, providing a principled hyperparameter selection criterion absent from the baseline methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant INR paradigm of replacing ReLU with exotic activations (sine in SIREN, Gabor wavelets in WIRE, positional encoding in NeRF) by instead constraining groups of seven ReLU neurons to collectively implement a second-order B-spline wavelet, drawing directly on the spline wavelet theory of Chui & Wang (1992) and Unser et al. (1993). The method is also closely related to ridgelets (Candès, 1998) in its use of localized, oriented features. Crucially, the paper leverages the rich theoretical machinery developed for standard ReLU networks—particularly the variation norm framework of Bach (2017), Savarese et al. (2019), and Parhi & Nowak (2021)—to analyze the learned function's regularity, something unavailable for the non-standard activations used in prior INR work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is a principled, validation-free method for selecting the INR scaling hyperparameter c via the variation norm, which the paper shows correlates with generalization performance on inverse problems like CT reconstruction. The theoretical result that the BW-ReLU Gram matrix is O(1) conditioned versus O(K³) for plain ReLU provides a clean explanation for why the spectral bias is overcome. Limitations include testing only on 2D images at modest resolution with 3 hidden layers, and the empirical gains over SIREN/WIRE are marginal ('comparable' rather than clearly superior), so the method's advantage is primarily theoretical elegance and interpretability rather than raw performance. Open questions include whether the approach extends to 3D radiance fields and physics-informed settings, and whether the variation norm guidance holds at larger scales.
