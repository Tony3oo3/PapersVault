## Problem

Conventional learning methods optimize accuracy on the training distribution, but in screening settings (hiring, admissions, loans) the test-time population is shaped by the classifier itself: strategic candidates apply only if the published conditional precision exceeds their cost, so the classifier determines who shows up. This paper studies this decision-dependent distribution shift—where the learner's objective and the data distribution both depend on the optimized classifier—and shows that naïve, semi-strategic, and fully strategic learning approaches have very different effects on who applies and on induced accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method takes tabular candidate features x (including a subset of categorical group features z of size K) and a binary qualification label y, along with a scalar cost parameter c ∈ [0,1] representing the per-candidate testing cost. The classifier f(x) produces a binary screening prediction, and the published per-group conditional precision prc_z = P_S(y=1 | ŷ=1, z) drives each candidate's application decision a* = 1{prc_z ≥ c}. The learning objective is to minimize weighted predictive error on the induced self-selective distribution p^f, where weights depend on f itself, making this an instance of learning under decision-dependent distribution shift.

## Method

The core contribution is a differentiable surrogate for the strategic learning objective argmin_f (1/m^f) Σ a_i* ℓ(y_i, f(x_i)), where application decisions a_i* are discrete and depend on f. The method replaces hard applications with a custom sigmoid ξ_τ(prc_z − c) whose domain is [−c, 1−c] (shifting with c) and which is indifferent at prc_z = c, and replaces hard predictions in the precision computation with soft sigmoid outputs σ(φ(x)). A key technical challenge is that the naive soft-precision proxy is negatively biased and the bias worsens as accuracy improves; the paper corrects this with a bias term B = (1/c)Σ(y_i − c)(ŷ_i − ỹ_i) subtracted from the denominator, where hard predictions ŷ_i are frozen at each epoch to preserve differentiability. Optional regularization terms R_app (ensuring at least one group applies and precision is well-defined) and R_⊥ (enforcing statistical parity f(x) ⊥ z via a squared-mean penalty) can be added to the objective, and the whole framework is optimized with standard gradient descent.

## Results

On the Adult dataset (c=0.7), the fully strategic method stratx achieves 91.1±0.5% induced accuracy, outperforming naive (85.2±0.3%), semi (87.4±0.6%), and the constrained variants stratx\z (86.0±0.4%) and straty⊥z (86.5±0%). On Bank (c=0.7), stratx reaches 90.1±0.4% induced accuracy versus 80.9% for naive (which sometimes yields zero applying groups) and 90.0±0.4% for semi. The statistical-parity variant straty⊥z achieves the highest rank r² (0.85 on Adult, 0.343 on Bank), confirming it approximately enforces the base-rate ordering ≼μ, while stratx's r² is lowest (0.076, 0.177), reflecting its unconstrained strategic power to shape applications.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five learning approaches: naive (standard ERM), semi (train-then-tune-threshold), stratx (fully strategic with all features), stratx\z (strategic without group features), and straty⊥z (strategic with statistical-parity regularization). stratx consistently achieves the highest induced accuracy across all four dataset-cost settings (e.g., 91.1% vs. 85.2% for naive on Adult c=0.7; 90.1% vs. 80.9% for naive on Bank c=0.7), but at the cost of restricting applications to roughly one group, which the authors link to Proposition 5's prediction of maximal strategic power. The naive baseline is clearly suboptimal and in some configurations (Bank c=0.8) yields zero applying groups entirely. Semi-strategic learning performs reasonably (within 1–3% of stratx on induced accuracy) but cannot enforce statistical parity, limiting its use for affirmative-action policies. The constrained variants stratx\z and straty⊥z sacrifice 3–5% induced accuracy relative to stratx but substantially improve rank r² (straty⊥z reaches 0.85 on Adult c=0.7), confirming that statistical parity effectively restricts the learner to thresholding on base rates as predicted by Proposition 6.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the strategic classification literature (Hardt et al., 2016; Brückner et al., 2012), where the standard strategic action is feature modification, by introducing a fundamentally different action: the binary choice of whether to participate at all. It draws on the economics literature on screening and self-selection (Lagziel & Lehrer, 2021; Koren, 2024) to formalize how a classifier's conditional precision shapes applicant composition, and on performative prediction / decision-dependent distributions (Drusvyatskiy & Xiao, 2022) to frame the resulting learning objective. The statistical parity constraint from fairness (Dwork et al., 2012) is repurposed not as a fairness guarantee per se, but as a structural limit on the firm's strategic power over who applies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that even simple linear classifiers, when trained with strategic awareness, can effectively exclude all but one demographic group from applying—a governance concern with direct implications for hiring, admissions, and welfare screening. The finding that statistical parity constrains the firm to thresholding on base rates (rather than arbitrary group selection) provides a concrete mechanism for affirmative action via targeted subsidies. Key open questions include whether the rational-behavior and full-information assumptions hold in practice, how the framework extends to dynamic/performative settings, and which statistics a regulator should mandate be disclosed. The empirical validation is limited to two tabular datasets with linear models and simulated behavior, leaving open whether the effects persist with richer models and real strategic dynamics.
