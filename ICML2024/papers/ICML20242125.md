## Problem

Sanford et al. (2023) showed that a one-layer transformer with O(log T) width can represent the sparse token selection task while FCNs require Ω(T) width, but this expressivity gap did not guarantee that standard gradient-based training could actually achieve the separation. This paper asks whether the representational advantage translates into learnability: can GD provably train a transformer to solve the task, and does the width separation hold in the average-case (expected loss) rather than only the worst case?

## Input / Output

The model receives a sequence of T Gaussian tokens x_i ∈ R^d (i.i.d. N(0, I_d)) concatenated with a randomly sampled q-element index subset y ⊂ [T], formatted as a (d + d_e) × (T+1) matrix [Z, z_query] where Z stacks tokens with near-orthogonal positional encodings and z_query appends the query token with its subset encoding e_y. The output is the d-dimensional sparse average (1/q)Σ_{j=1}^q x_{y_j}. The positional encoding dimension d_e = Θ(q log T / δ²) is the key architectural parameter, and the stochastic PE is resampled fresh at each forward pass during both training and inference.

## Method

The paper analyzes a one-layer, single-head softmax-attention transformer with a reparameterized architecture that merges the query and key matrices into a single W ∈ R^{(d+d_e)×(d+d_e)}, trained jointly with the value matrix V via GD on the population squared loss. The critical design choice is a stochastic positional encoding: at each forward pass, a fresh Rademacher random matrix E satisfying the (q,δ)-restricted isometry property is sampled, replacing fixed absolute positions. This stochasticity creates sufficient symmetry in the loss landscape that the GD trajectory is provably confined to a two-dimensional subspace parameterized by scalars C(t) and α(t), enabling an inductive proof of global convergence in Õ(T^{(2-2δ)/(1-3δ)}/η + T²d/(ηε)) steps. The resulting transformer has width O(d + q log T), while the paper simultaneously proves that any FCN (regardless of depth or activation) must have first-layer width Ω(Td) to achieve vanishing expected loss, establishing an exponential width separation. As a corollary, the trained stochastic-PE model provably generalizes to longer sequences (T₂ > T₁) with OOD loss bounded by O(T₂²ε/T₁²), a property that fixed positional encodings provably lack.

## Results

The paper establishes that a one-layer transformer with stochastic positional encoding trained via gradient descent globally converges on the STS_q task with width O(d + q log T), achieving population loss ≤ ε in Õ(T²d/(ηε)) steps. In contrast, any FCN (regardless of depth or activation) requires first-layer width Ω(Td) to approximate STS_q, yielding an expected loss lower bound of (T−q)/(Tq(T−1)) for narrower networks. The trained transformer also provably generalizes to longer sequences: when training loss on length T₁ reaches ε, the OOD loss on length T₂ is bounded by O(T₂²ε/T₁²).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the one-layer transformer and fully-connected networks (FCNs) on the STS_q task. The transformer achieves the task with width O(d + q log T), while any FCN requires width Ω(Td) in its first layer — an exponential separation in sequence length T. This extends the worst-case FCN lower bound from Sanford et al. (2023) to an average-case (expected loss) setting. For length generalization, the paper compares stochastic positional encoding against fixed (near-orthogonal) positional encoding: the stochastic PE model's OOD loss converges to zero on longer sequences (T_test = 250–400 when trained at T = 200), while the fixed PE model's OOD loss plateaus at a strictly nonzero constant. The transformer's learned parameters converge to the ground-truth direction (W* = [0 0; 0 αI_{d_e}], V* = [I_d 0]), confirming the reparameterization captures the essential positional structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse averaging task (qSA) introduced by Sanford et al. (2023), which established an expressivity separation between transformers (O(log T) width) and FCNs (Ω(T) width) in the worst case, by (1) moving to an average-case lower bound and (2) proving that standard gradient descent from zero initialization actually converges to the optimal solution, bridging the gap between representational power and algorithmic learnability. The stochastic positional encoding is adopted from Shen et al. (2023), who showed empirically that randomized PEs improve length generalization on arithmetic tasks; here it is repurposed as the key architectural choice that enables both the theoretical analysis and the provable length generalization guarantee. The reparameterization combining Q and K into a single matrix W follows the standard technique used in recent transformer training dynamics work (Huang et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2023, Tian et al. 2023a).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous evidence that the transformer-vs-FCN expressivity gap on sparse token selection is not merely a representational artifact but is algorithmically achievable via vanilla GD, which strengthens the theoretical case for transformers' inductive bias. The provable length generalization with stochastic PE (and its failure with fixed PE) gives a clean theoretical explanation for a phenomenon observed empirically in several recent works. Key limitations include the reliance on population loss (no finite-sample guarantees), the one-layer architecture, and the Q/K reparameterization that simplifies the landscape; open questions include extending to empirical risk minimization, multi-layer transformers, and more practical tasks.
