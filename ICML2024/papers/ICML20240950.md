## Problem

As LLMs surpass human expertise, alignment methods that rely on human-labelled ground truth become infeasible, creating a need for scalable oversight where weaker models must evaluate stronger ones. This paper asks whether debate—an adversarial protocol in which two experts argue for opposing answers—enables non-expert judges to identify the correct answer, and whether optimizing debaters for persuasiveness (a label-free metric) improves truthfulness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240950/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reading comprehension question from the QuALITY dataset (Project Gutenberg sci-fi stories, ~7000 tokens) with two candidate answers (the correct answer and the best distractor). Expert debaters receive the full source text plus a quote verification tool that tags quotes as verified or unverified; non-expert judges receive only the debate transcript (capped at 900 words total, 150 words per debater argument, 3 rounds) and must select an answer. Human judges additionally provide a confidence rating between 5% and 95%, enabling selective prediction analysis.

## Method

The core protocol is an information-asymmetric debate: two LLM experts (e.g., GPT-4-Turbo with best-of-16 sampling) simultaneously argue for opposing answers over three rounds, each with access to the source text and a quote verification tool, while a non-expert judge (LLM or human) without text access reads the transcript and selects the correct answer. To measure debater skill without ground truth, the paper introduces a cross-play Swiss tournament with Elo ratings based on win rates against a population of opponents, and a persuasiveness metric defined as judge approval. Debaters are optimized for persuasiveness via inference-time methods—best-of-N sampling and critique-and-refinement—where a preference model (GPT-4-Turbo log probabilities) selects the most judge-appealing arguments. The key finding is that as debaters become more persuasive, they become relatively better at arguing for the correct answer than the incorrect one, increasing judge accuracy; conversely, optimizing single-model consultants for persuasiveness degrades accuracy because incorrect consultants gain disproportionately from exploration of deceptive strategies.

## Results

On the QuALITY HARD reading comprehension task, debate enables non-expert LLM judges (GPT-4-Turbo) to achieve 75.6% accuracy and non-expert human judges to achieve 88% accuracy, compared to naive baselines of 48% and 60% respectively. The expert judge upper bound is 92.5% (LLM) and 89% (human). Debate outperforms the consultancy baseline by 21.6 percentage points for LLM judges (75.6% vs. 54%) and by 10 percentage points for human judges (88% vs. 78%), with statistically significant improvements (p=0.001 static, p=0.048 interactive).

|---|---|---|

| Consultancy | 54 | 78 |
| Debate | **76** | **88** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the consultancy protocol from Michael et al. (2023), in which a single expert argues for one answer while the judge asks probing questions. Debate consistently outperforms consultancy across all judge types: LLM judges achieve 75.6% with debate versus 54% with consultancy (a 21.6 pp gap), and human judges achieve 88% versus 78% (a 10 pp gap), both statistically significant. A critical finding is that the two protocols respond oppositely to expert skill: as debaters are optimised for persuasiveness (via best-of-N sampling), debate judge accuracy increases, while consultancy judge accuracy decreases because incorrect consultants become relatively more persuasive than correct ones. This means that simply making the expert model stronger or more persuasive helps debate but actively harms consultancy, making debate the more robust oversight protocol as model capabilities scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes the debate protocol proposed by Irving et al. (2018) for AI safety, extending it from a theoretical framework to a controlled empirical study with LLM debaters. It builds directly on the information-asymmetric reading comprehension setting introduced by Michael et al. (2023), who found no positive results with LLM debaters but focused primarily on human debaters; here, multi-round LLM debate with quote verification and inference-time optimization (best-of-N, critique-and-refinement from Saunders et al., 2022) is shown to work. The key methodological departure is the introduction of unsupervised metrics—win rate and Elo rating via Swiss-style tournaments—that measure debater persuasiveness without ground-truth labels, enabling optimization of oversight protocols in the absence of expert annotations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that optimizing debaters for persuasiveness (an unsupervised, judge-approval-based metric) increases judge accuracy, while the same optimization degrades consultancy accuracy—provides concrete evidence that adversarial structure is essential for scalable oversight and that unsupervised training signals can align models toward truthfulness. However, the results are limited to information asymmetry (not capability asymmetry), a single reading comprehension domain, and require verifiable evidence via a quote tool; whether debate remains effective for deceptive models, domains without groundable evidence, or much larger evidence sets remains open. The finding that interactive judge involvement does not improve accuracy over static debate also challenges assumptions about how oversight protocols should be designed.
