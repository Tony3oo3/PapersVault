## Problem

ML datasets routinely invoke value-laden terms like 'diversity,' 'bias,' and 'quality' without providing concrete definitions, operationalization procedures, or empirical validation. This ambiguity makes it impossible for reviewers and downstream users to verify whether a dataset genuinely embodies the properties its curators claim, and it conflates distinct constructs (e.g., scale with diversity, bias with diversity) in ways that undermine reproducibility and scientific rigor.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes the full dataset collection pipeline—motivation, collection methodology, annotation procedures, and documentation—as its input, rather than a single model input. It produces a structured assessment across four stages: a concrete diversity definition, an operationalized collection methodology with explicit indicators, a reliability assessment (inter-annotator agreement, test-retest), and a validity assessment (convergent and discriminant). The empirical analysis spans 135 image and text datasets from CV, NLP, and fairness venues, with a detailed case study applied to the SA-1B segmentation dataset.

## Method

The paper adapts measurement theory from the social sciences (Bandalos, 2018) into a four-stage framework for ML dataset curation: conceptualization (defining abstract constructs like diversity with precise, contextualized terms), operationalization (translating definitions into observable indicators with explicit scales and inclusion criteria), reliability (assessing consistency via inter-annotator agreement metrics such as Fleiss's κ and test-retest recollection), and validity (evaluating convergent validity through cross-dataset generalization and established diversity metrics like the Vendi Score, and discriminant validity by confirming that unrelated constructs yield uncorrelated results). A systematic literature review of 135 datasets identifies concrete gaps—only 52.9% justify the need for diversity, 74.8% omit methodological caveats, and 48.5% rely on downstream model performance as their sole validation. The framework is then instantiated on SA-1B, where the authors refine the dataset's geographic diversity definition, critique the NER-based country inference and country-level granularity, and recommend additional validation through cross-regional object distribution comparisons.

## Results

Across 135 image and text datasets claiming diversity, only 52.9% explicitly justify the need for diverse data, and only 40 of 135 papers are standalone dataset papers. A striking 74.8% of datasets omit methodological caveats about their collection process, while only 56.3% provide specifics on quality control and just 36.8% include annotation-quality details (training, attention checks, compensation). Downstream model performance is used as the primary validation in 48.5% of cases, often conflating model accuracy with dataset diversity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper without a formal method-vs-baseline comparison. The closest empirical comparison appears in the SA-1B case study (Section 8), where the authors show SA-1B exhibits greater variety in mask sizes and number of masks per image compared to COCO and OpenImages, demonstrating convergent validity for object-appearance diversity, but note the absence of geographic-diversity validation. The VQA discriminant-validity example (Section 8) illustrates how a model can achieve high accuracy by exploiting question-form priors (e.g., always answering 'Yes' to 'Do you see a…' questions) rather than genuine visual understanding, exposing low discriminant validity in the original dataset construction. The paper's core contribution is prescriptive rather than comparative: it proposes measurement-theory principles (conceptualization, operationalization, reliability via inter-annotator agreement and test-retest, validity via convergent and discriminant checks) as a framework, positioning itself against the prevailing 'scale-thinking' assumption that larger datasets are automatically more diverse.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes measurement theory from the social sciences (Bandalos, 2018) and Campbell & Fiske's (1959) convergent/discriminant validity framework as a structured scaffold for evaluating ML dataset curation, moving beyond the datasheet/data-card documentation movement (Gebru et., 2021; Pushkarna et., 2022) by specifying *how* value-laden claims like diversity should be defined, operationalized, and validated. It departs from prior benchmark-validity work (e.g., Blodgett et., 2021 on stereotyping in NLP datasets) by treating the data collection process itself as the measurement instrument to audit, rather than the downstream model's performance. The empirical analysis of 135 image and text datasets grounds the framework in observed practice, and the SA-1B case study demonstrates practical application of the full conceptualization→operationalization→reliability→validity pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This matters because it addresses a pervasive and underexamined problem: datasets routinely claim diversity, bias-mitigation, or quality without defining these constructs or validating that the collection process actually achieves them, perpetuating the illusion of dataset neutrality. The framework gives reviewers and curators a concrete, actionable checklist (concrete definitions, documented indicators, inter-annotator agreement metrics, convergent/discriminant validity checks) that could be adopted as a review standard. Open questions include whether the documentation burden is sustainable for resource-constrained teams, how the framework applies to opaque foundation-model training data, and whether constructs that shift over time (e.g., evolving racial categories) can ever be 'validated' in a time-invariant sense.
