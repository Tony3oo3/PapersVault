## Problem

While weight decay is a standard regularization technique that improves raw calibration, this paper reveals it actually diminishes model calibratability—the ability to benefit from post-hoc recalibration like temperature scaling. The underlying cause remains unexplored: why does stronger regularization make models harder to recalibrate, and is there an inherent trade-off between discriminability and calibratability in deep networks?

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for SVHN/CIFAR-10/CIFAR-100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet) and produces class labels with calibrated confidence scores via post-hoc temperature scaling. PLP is a training-time strategy rather than an architectural change: it modifies which layers receive gradient updates at each phase of training, so the forward pass and inference pipeline remain identical to standard CNNs (ResNet-18/50, VGG-11). The calibrated output is a single temperature-scaled softmax vector, preserving dense confidence values unlike binning-based methods.

## Method

The paper identifies a U-shaped calibratability trend across hidden layers: intermediate features become progressively more calibratable until the top few layers, where over-compression of representations (linked to the information bottleneck principle) causes a sharp degradation in calibrated ECE. Based on the weak classifier hypothesis—that a less over-trained classification head allows the representation module to produce more calibratable features—the authors propose Progressively Layer-Peeled (PLP) training. PLP divides a network with L layers into K groups and partitions training into K phases, progressively freezing the topmost layer group at the start of each subsequent phase so that no single group of top layers receives excessive training. The number of epochs per phase is controlled by a hyperparameter γ (T_k = ⌊(k^γ − (k−1)^γ)/K^γ · T⌋), with γ=1 giving even partitioning and larger γ freezing top layers earlier. This prevents the top layers from over-compressing information while still allowing them to contribute to accuracy, yielding substantially lower calibrated ECE without sacrificing predictive performance.

## Results

PLP achieves the best average rank (1.94 for ResNet-18, 2.00 for ResNet-50) across ECE, AECE, NLL, and accuracy on four datasets. On the hardest dataset CIFAR-100, PLP-trained ResNet-18 achieves a calibrated ECE of 0.93 (vs. 2.61 for standard training) while maintaining 75.5% accuracy (vs. 73.8%). On Tiny-ImageNet, PLP ResNet-50 reaches 61.3% accuracy with a calibrated ECE of 1.10, outperforming standard training (60.2% acc, 1.66 ECE). The method is robust across architectures and datasets, with the largest gains appearing where standard training calibration is weakest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLP consistently outperforms all seven baselines on calibrated ECE across nearly all dataset-architecture combinations. The most dramatic wins are against label smoothing (CIFAR-100 ECE: 0.93 vs. 4.41 for ResNet-18) and distillation (0.93 vs. 6.99), both of which rely on label-softening mechanisms that the paper identifies as harming calibratability. MMCE, a loss specifically designed to improve calibration during training, is also substantially outperformed (CIFAR-100 ECE: 0.93 vs. 2.28), confirming the paper's thesis that train-time calibration losses can reduce post-hoc calibratability. Mixup and focal loss achieve competitive ECE on CIFAR-100 (1.18 and 1.19 respectively) but are worse on SVHN and Tiny-ImageNet, while PLP maintains strong calibration everywhere. MIT (mixup-in-training) is the closest competitor, achieving 2.03 ECE on CIFAR-100, but still trails PLP by over 2× and suffers a notable accuracy drop on Tiny-ImageNet (49.1% vs. 56.1%). PLP matches or exceeds standard training accuracy on all datasets while delivering 2–3× lower calibrated ECE, demonstrating that the calibration gains come without sacrificing discriminability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the nascent line of work on model calibratability (Wang et al., 2021; 2023; Zhu et al., 2023), which observed that train-time calibration methods often conflict with post-hoc recalibration, by providing a mechanistic explanation rooted in the information bottleneck principle (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2017; Saxe et al., 2019). It departs from the 'good classifier hypothesis' underlying prior top-down training methods (Fang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2019) by proposing the opposite 'weak classifier hypothesis': that an under-trained classification head allows the representation module to retain more calibratable information. The PLP training strategy borrows the layer-peeled naming from Fang et al. (2021) but repurposes the progressive freezing mechanism for a fundamentally different goal—preventing over-compression rather than enabling analytical tractability or imbalanced learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that weight decay—the de-facto standard regularizer in modern deep learning—systematically undermines a model's capacity to be well recalibrated post-hoc challenges a widely held assumption and suggests that the standard training recipe may be suboptimal for safety-critical applications requiring reliable uncertainty. The connection to information compression provides a principled lens for understanding the calibratability-discriminability trade-off, potentially guiding future architecture and training design. However, all evidence is confined to small-scale image classification (CIFAR, SVHN, Tiny-ImageNet) with shallow CNNs, leaving open whether the U-shaped calibratability trend and the PLP remedy generalize to transformers, larger datasets, or distribution-shift scenarios. The authors themselves flag the need for theoretical analysis of calibratability and experiments under pretraining/fine-tuning paradigms as important next steps.
