## Problem

Existing MARL benchmarks overwhelmingly target cooperative settings, while competitive game platforms are either too simple (tabular board games) or too computationally expensive (StarCraft II, DOTA) to serve as accessible research testbeds. No lightweight, open-sourced benchmark with rich visual inputs, complex fighting dynamics, and unified evaluation criteria for competitive two-player MARL has been established.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241107/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Agents observe stacked RGB frames of the rendered game screen (128×100 for Street Fighter II, up to 192×112 for other supported games) with configurable frame stacking (default 12 frames for SF2, 9 for SF3/KOF97). The action space is either a 12-dimensional binary button-press vector mimicking arcade controllers or a transformed categorical space combining 8 motion actions (defense, forward, jump, crouch, flips, offensive/defensive crouch) with 6 attack actions (light/medium/hard punch and kick) plus hard-coded special moves. Evaluation outputs are Elo ratings computed via the FIDE system and exploitability scores measured by training a PPO exploiter against a fixed policy.

## Method

FightLadder is built on modern game emulators (MAME, Gym Retro) to support five classic fighting games with a Gym-compatible interface, enabling rapid extension to new titles. The paper provides open-source implementations of five SOTA competitive MARL algorithms—IPPO, two-timescale learning, fictitious self-play, PSRO, and AlphaStar-style league training—all using PPO with a CNN feature extractor as the backbone. For single-player evaluation, a curriculum learning scheme with inverse-weight opponent scheduling (sampling opponents proportional to 1 − win rate) trains a general agent against all 12 characters. For two-player evaluation, a unified framework compares algorithms via Elo ratings across populations and exploitability tests where a PPO exploiter is trained to convergence against each fixed policy. The key empirical finding is that population-based methods (League, PSRO) outperform independent learning (IPPO, 2Timescale) in Elo, yet all methods remain exploitable by both RL exploiters and simple human strategies.

## Results

In the two-player competitive setting (sf_ryu_vs_ryu), League training achieves the highest Elo rating (1682 left / 1503 right) after ~640M training steps, followed by PSRO (1262/1517 after ~170M steps), while independent learning methods IPPO (1082/1164) and 2Timescale (1080/919) plateau much earlier at ~50M steps. FSP (1079/1150) performs comparably to IPPO despite ~250M steps. On exploitability, all methods remain highly exploitable (0.88–1.00), with PSRO's right-side policy showing the lowest exploitability (0.88±0.05) and single-player PPO the highest (0.99±0.02). Notably, the built-in CPU policies (Elo 1395/1541) still outperform most trained agents, and a PPO exploiter achieves >0.5 win rate against every baseline, indicating no method converges to a Nash equilibrium.

## Baselines & Comparisons

League training [Vinyals et al., 2019] achieves the highest left-side Elo (1682) and PSRO [Lanctot et al., 2017] achieves the highest right-side Elo (1517), both substantially outperforming the independent learning baselines IPPO [de Witt et al., 2020] and 2Timescale [Daskalakis et al., 2020] by roughly 500–600 Elo points. FSP [Heinrich et al., 2015] performs on par with IPPO despite using 5× more training steps, suggesting that simply maintaining a population without game-theoretic meta-strategy selection does not help. On exploitability (Table 3), PSRO's right-side policy (0.88) and 2Timescale's right-side policy (0.90) are marginally more robust than IPPO (0.91) and League (0.94), but all remain far from the ideal value of 0. The single-player PPO pretrained policy [Schulman et al., 2017] is the most exploitable (0.99/0.99), confirming that two-player training does improve robustness. However, the critical finding is that a PPO exploiter trained from scratch defeats every two-player baseline with >0.5 win rate, and human players can also exploit all agents with simple strategies (e.g., defensive posture + low kicks), meaning no current algorithm produces a truly non-exploitable policy in this setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two underdeveloped areas: competitive MARL benchmarks and fighting-game AI. Prior competitive game environments are either low-dimensional (board games, Pommerman) or computationally prohibitive (StarCraft II, DOTA), while existing fighting-game work (Go et al. 2023, Palmas 2022) focuses on single-agent or single-game settings without unified evaluation. FightLadder builds on modern game emulators (MAME, Gym Retro) to create a multi-game platform, and its algorithm baselines directly implement established competitive MARL methods—PSRO, League Training, FSP, IPPO, and two-timescale learning—under a common PPO backbone, filling the gap of no unified benchmark for comparing these approaches on the same tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key open question the paper surfaces is whether any current self-play algorithm can converge to a non-exploitable (Nash-equilibrium) strategy in a rich, visually-grounded competitive game: even PSRO and League Training, which achieved grandmaster-level play in StarCraft II, retain exploitability of 0.88–0.97 and remain defeatable by simple human strategies. This negative result suggests that the strategic depth of fighting games (timing, character diversity, special-move sequences) poses a fundamentally harder challenge than prior competitive benchmarks, and motivates new algorithmic ideas beyond existing population-based or independent-learning paradigms. The platform's computational efficiency (13× faster than SMACv2) and extensibility to new games make it a practical testbed for future work, though the current results are limited to Street Fighter and two-player zero-sum settings.
