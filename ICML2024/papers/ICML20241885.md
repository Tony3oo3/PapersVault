## Problem

Discovering effective catalysts requires reasoning over combinatorially many chemical descriptors and 3D atomistic interactions, a task that exceeds the capabilities of LLMs alone. While LLMs can propose scientifically plausible catalysts, they lack grounding in quantum-chemical energetics, and existing descriptor-based search methods require expert enumeration of relevant properties. The paper addresses how to autonomously navigate this multi-modal chemical search space with physically meaningful feedback.

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural language catalyst-discovery query (e.g., 'Generate top-5 metallic catalysts for the adsorption of *OHCH3') and iteratively refines it by appending chemical descriptors (inclusion/exclusion criteria, catalyst type, relationship to prior candidates). At each search node, the LLM's free-text catalyst names are parsed into chemical symbol lists, converted to 3D atomistic structures via ASE lattice templates (FCC/BCC/HCP only), and scored by a GNN. The final output is the highest-reward node in the search tree, yielding a ranked top-5 catalyst list with a scalar reward (adsorption energy in eV, or negative maximum energy barrier across reaction pathways).

## Method

CHEMREASONER formulates catalyst discovery as a beam-search over a tree of LLM prompts, where each node stores a (prompt, answer, reward) triple. Two variants control action generation: CHEMREASONER-Expert samples from a fixed expert-defined action space, while CHEMREASONER-Planner uses the LLM itself as a contextual planner that proposes the next set of descriptor modifications based on the full root-to-node history. At each node, candidate catalysts are translated into 3D structures, relaxed via L-BFGS using the GemNet-dT GNN (16 sampled configurations, up to 64 relaxation steps), and scored by either an adsorption-energy reward or a reaction-pathway reward that minimizes the maximum energy jump across intermediate steps (Eq. 1). Beam pruning retains only the top-M nodes per depth, and the best-reward node across the entire tree is returned as the answer. The key architectural insight is that quantum-chemical GNN feedback serves as a domain-grounded reward signal that steers the LLM's heuristic search toward energetically favorable catalysts, replacing the LLM-computed rewards used in prior tree-of-thought approaches.

## Results

CHEMREASONER-Planner with GPT-4 achieves the highest final reward scores on OpenCatalyst (2.36) and BioFuels (4.15), while CHEMREASONER-Expert with GPT-3.5-turbo leads on CO2-Conversion (0.78). Both CHEMREASONER variants substantially outperform the Chain-of-Thought and Self-Consistency baselines across all three query categories, with the largest gains on OpenCatalyst (e.g., 2.36 vs. 0.73 for Self-Consistency GPT-4) and BioFuels (4.15 vs. 2.12 for Self-Consistency GPT-3.5). The reward metric is the GNN-predicted adsorption energy or reaction-pathway-based score, where higher values indicate more energetically favorable catalysts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CHEMREASONER against two LLM-only prompting baselines: Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and Self-Consistency, both evaluated with GPT-4 and GPT-3.5-turbo. CHEMREASONER-Planner (GPT-4) outperforms the best baseline (Self-Consistency GPT-4) by +1.63 on OpenCatalyst (2.36 vs. 0.73) and +2.07 on BioFuels (4.15 vs. 2.08). On CO2-Conversion, CHEMREASONER-Expert (GPT-3.5) achieves the best score of 0.78, surpassing Self-Consistency GPT-3.5 (-0.36) by 1.14 points, while CHEMREASONER-Planner (GPT-4) scores 0.01, still above the CoT GPT-4 baseline (-0.62) but below the Expert variant. The authors attribute the Expert variant's edge on CO2-Conversion to the complex reaction-pathway reward function, where the LLM's internal notion of a good catalyst does not directly align with the multi-step energy-barrier metric, making the static expert-defined action space more effective than the planner's adaptive actions for this specific task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Monte Carlo Thought Search framework of Sprueill et al. (2023), which used LLM querying for catalyst design but relied on LLM-computed rewards, by replacing those with true quantum-chemistry-based rewards from GNNs trained on DFT simulations. It also adapts the tree-of-thoughts search structure from Yao et al. (2023) to a domain-specific setting, adding a planner component that automatically generates contextually grounded search actions without expert enumeration. The key conceptual departure from both prior works is the integration of a domain-specific, simulation-backed reward function (adsorption energy and reaction pathway barriers) that grounds the LLM's linguistic reasoning in physical chemistry, rather than letting the LLM self-evaluate its own outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is among the first frameworks to close the loop between LLM hypothesis generation and quantum-chemical validation for catalyst discovery, demonstrating that automated planning can match or exceed expert-designed search strategies. However, significant limitations remain: GNN predictions diverge substantially from DFT ground truth (Table 3), the text-to-3D-structure conversion is restricted to FCC/BCC/HCP lattices, and the system depends on proprietary LLMs whose instruction-following robustness is critical. Open questions include whether RLHF-style alignment can improve LLM-reward consistency for complex multi-step reactions, whether open-source models can eventually support this workflow, and how to generate realistic 3D structures for multimetallic catalysts beyond simple random substitution.
