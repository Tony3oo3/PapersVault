## Problem

Existing causal discovery algorithms that leverage interventional data assume access to infinite interventional samples, which is unrealistic in settings where experiments are costly or ethically constrained. This paper addresses the gap of learning a causal DAG from a finite, adaptively collected set of interventional samples, formalizing the problem as a fixed-confidence pure exploration task where the learner must determine both which interventions to perform and when to stop.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes as input a CPDAG (derived from abundant observational data under the faithfulness assumption), a target confidence level δ ∈ (0,1), and a precomputed (n,k)-separating system that bounds each intervention set size to k. It adaptively collects one interventional sample at a time, selecting which target set to intervene on and which realization to use. The output is either a full causal DAG (exact version) or a collection of edge cut configurations (C*(S)) for each target S in the separating system (practical version), returned at a data-dependent stopping time τ_δ when the cumulative KL-divergence-based confidence criterion is met.

## Method

The core contribution is a track-and-stop causal discovery algorithm that frames intervention selection as a pure exploration problem over the action space defined by a graph separating system. At each step, the algorithm computes an allocation vector α_t via an AdaHedge-based online max-min optimization that maximizes the minimum KL-divergence between the current empirical distribution and the closest competing DAG, then selects the intervention whose empirical count most lags behind its cumulative allocation (allocation matching). A forced exploration phase (√t lower bound per intervention) ensures all empirical distributions converge before allocation matching takes over. The practical implementation decomposes the problem into local sub-problems (learning each edge cut C(S) independently) and a global allocation strategy that distributes samples across targets inversely proportional to their estimated learning difficulty. The algorithm is proven sound (P(ψ ≠ D*) ≤ δ) and achieves an asymptotically optimal O(log(1/δ)/c(D*)) upper bound on expected interventional samples, matching the information-theoretic lower bound.

## Results

The track-and-stop algorithm achieves the lowest Structural Hamming Distance (SHD) with the fewest interventional samples across all experimental settings. On complete random graphs (N=5,6,7), it reaches near-zero SHD substantially faster than all baselines, and the gap widens with graph size. On the SACHS Bayesian network (13 nodes, 17 edges), it clearly outperforms all four baselines, achieving significantly lower SHD at the same sample budget. Notably, increasing graph density (ρ from 0.1 to 0.2 at N=10) has minimal impact on the sample efficiency of track-and-stop, whereas baseline algorithms require significantly more samples as density increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four baselines: random interventions within the graph-separating system, DCT (Squires et al., 2020), Adaptive Sensitivity Search (Choo & Shiragur, 2023), and GIES (Hauser & Buhlmann, 2012). Track-and-stop outperforms all four baselines on SHD across every tested setting (complete graphs, varying-density graphs, and SACHS). DCT and Adaptive Sensitivity Search are theoretically designed for infinite interventional data (perfect interventions); when forced to operate with finite samples, their SHD decreases much more slowly than track-and-stop. GIES, a greedy maximum-likelihood method, also lags behind, particularly as graph size or density grows. The random intervention baseline performs worst, confirming that adaptive selection of informative interventions is critical for sample efficiency. The key advantage of track-and-stop is its fixed-confidence stopping rule, which terminates as soon as the desired accuracy is reached, rather than requiring a predetermined (often excessive) sample budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the pure-exploration bandit framework (Kaufmann et al., 2016) and the allocation-matching principle from Wei et al. (2024, structural causal bandits) to the causal discovery setting, where interventions are treated as arms and the goal is to identify the true DAG with fixed confidence. It builds on the graph-separating-system construction of Shanmugam et al. (2015), which was designed for the infinite-data regime, and makes it operational under finite samples by coupling it with an adaptive track-and-stop stopping rule. The key departure from prior adaptive methods (Squires et al., 2020; Choo & Shiragur, 2023) is that those still require access to full interventional distributions, whereas this work operates with a finite number of samples per intervention and provides a problem-dependent sample complexity bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first algorithm that handles general DAGs (not restricted to trees as in Greenewald et al., 2019) with finite interventional data and a formal fixed-confidence guarantee, filling a gap between the infinite-data theory and the practical need for sample-efficient experimentation. The practical local-strategy implementation reduces the worst-case complexity from exponential in the number of unoriented edges to exponential only in the intervention-set size, making it tractable for moderate graphs. Open questions include extension to latent-variable settings, continuous variables, and whether the faithfulness assumption can be relaxed; the current framework also assumes the observational distribution is known, which may not hold in high-dimensional real-world applications.
