## Problem

While LLMs achieve strong performance on reasoning benchmarks, it remains unclear how sensitive their reasoning is to superficial input variations that do not change the underlying task. This paper identifies and systematically quantifies a previously underexplored brittleness: LLMs are highly sensitive to the ordering of premises in deductive and mathematical reasoning, even though reordering premises is logically neutral. The gap is both empirical (no prior systematic study of premise-order effects across multiple LLMs) and practical (the 30%+ accuracy drops have implications for deploying LLMs on real-world problems where premise order is arbitrary).

## Input / Output

For logical reasoning, the input is a set of facts, definite-clause rules (with randomly generated pseudoword predicates to prevent reliance on internal knowledge), and a target conclusion; the model must output a fully valid step-by-step proof where each step cites the specific premise used, and any hallucinated fact or rule renders the answer incorrect. Problems range from 4 to 12 proof-relevant rules with 0, 5, or 10 distracting rules, yielding 27K variants. For R-GSM, the input is a grade-school math word problem of 5–8 sentences (<200 tokens), and the model outputs a numerical answer; each problem has a manually rewritten variant with a different sentence ordering that preserves the same ground-truth answer.

## Method

The paper is an empirical study that constructs two controlled benchmarks to isolate the premise-order effect. For logical reasoning, problems are synthetically generated from a SimpleLogic-style space of modus-ponens chains, and each problem is permuted into 15 variants categorized by normalized Kendall tau distance (τ ∈ {1, 0.5, 0, −0.5, −1}) to the forward-chaining order that matches the ground-truth proof. For mathematical reasoning, R-GSM is built by selecting GSM8K problems with ≥5 sentences, enumerating alternative sentence orderings via a simple search function, and manually rewriting to preserve the ground-truth answer, yielding 220 original/reordered pairs. Four LLMs (GPT-4-turbo, GPT-3.5-turbo, PaLM 2-L, Gemini 1.0 Pro) are evaluated with zero-shot greedy decoding (temperature 0). The key finding is that all models peak at τ=1 (forward order) and degrade sharply as τ decreases, with the drop amplified by more proof rules and distracting rules; error analysis reveals that fact hallucination is the dominant failure mode, driven by the model's tendency to apply premises sequentially rather than searching for the applicable one. Notably, models differ in their secondary preferences: GPT-4-turbo favors the backward order (τ=−1) over random shuffles, while PaLM 2-L performs worst on the backward order.

## Results

On the logical reasoning benchmark (12 relevant rules, no distracting rules), all four LLMs achieve their best accuracy with the forward premise order (τ=1): GPT-4-turbo reaches 96.5%, PaLM 2-L 88.0%, GPT-3.5-turbo 30.0%, and Gemini 1.0 Pro 16.5%. Shuffling the premise order (aggregating τ=0.5, 0, −0.5) causes accuracy drops of 20–30% for GPT-4-turbo and PaLM 2-L, and over 40% for GPT-3.5-turbo and Gemini 1.0 Pro. On R-GSM (reordered GSM8K), all models degrade: GPT-4-turbo drops from 94.1% to 85.0%, PaLM 2-L from 86.4% to 79.5%, Gemini 1.0 Pro from 80.5% to 69.1%, and GPT-3.5-turbo from 67.3% to 51.8%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four LLMs—GPT-4-turbo, GPT-3.5-turbo, PaLM 2-L, and Gemini 1.0 Pro—on identical logical reasoning and R-GSM problems under different premise orderings. GPT-4-turbo is the most robust to reordering, maintaining 85.0% on R-GSM (vs. 94.1% original) and 80.8% on 12-rule logical reasoning with shuffled order (vs. 96.5% forward). PaLM 2-L shows moderate sensitivity, dropping to 79.5% on R-GSM and 66.5% on shuffled 12-rule logical reasoning. GPT-3.5-turbo and Gemini 1.0 Pro are far more brittle: GPT-3.5-turbo falls from 30.0% to 1.2% on 12-rule logical reasoning (shuffled), and Gemini 1.0 Pro from 16.5% to 0.2%. Notably, GPT-4-turbo and GPT-3.5-turbo prefer the backward order (τ=−1) over random shuffling, while PaLM 2-L performs worst with the backward order, revealing model-specific ordering preferences beyond the universal forward-order advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions the premise order effect as a distinct failure mode from the Reversal Curse (which concerns within-statement entity order) and distractibility (which concerns irrelevant context), while connecting to both. It extends the logical reasoning evaluation space beyond SimpleLogic by introducing controlled premise permutations parameterized by Kendall tau distance, and builds R-GSM on top of GSM8K to probe the same effect in mathematical reasoning. The work also relates to the 'lost in the middle' phenomenon and to findings on long-digit addition where reversing input order simplifies the task for Transformers, though the authors explicitly rule out position bias as the primary cause in their short-input setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a semantically neutral permutation of premises causes over 30% accuracy drops even on the simplest modus ponens problems and GSM8K-style math challenges is practically important for anyone deploying LLMs on reasoning tasks where input order is not under the model's control. The paper leaves the root cause (autoregressive architecture, training objectives, or data mixture) unexplained and proposes no mitigation; few-shot chain-of-thought prompting fails to close the gap. Open questions include whether training-time interventions (e.g., order-augmented data, permutation-invariant objectives) can eliminate the effect, and whether the phenomenon persists in newer, larger models.
