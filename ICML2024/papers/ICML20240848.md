## Problem

Existing online linear regression algorithms such as the Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth forecaster achieve optimal static regret but can suffer arbitrarily bad dynamic regret when the data-generating process is non-stationary. Prior works that handle non-stationarity either require strong structural assumptions (e.g., known dynamical system models) or boundedness assumptions on the comparator sequence, implicitly demanding prior knowledge that the environment is stationary or that comparators lie in a known bounded set. This paper asks whether optimal dynamic regret guarantees can be achieved in the complete absence of any such prior knowledge.

## Input / Output

On each round the learner receives a feature vector x_t ∈ R^d before making a scalar prediction ŷ_t = ⟨x_t, w_t⟩, after which the target y_t ∈ R is revealed and the squared loss ½(y_t − ⟨x_t, w_t⟩)² is incurred. The setting is 'improper' in that x_t is revealed before prediction, so the learner can effectively implement any function of x_t by choosing w_t appropriately. The algorithm operates in an unbounded domain (no constraints on w_t), and the discount factor γ is not a fixed hyperparameter but is learned on-the-fly by a meta-algorithm that combines predictions from multiple base instances with different γ values, each clipped to a trust region around a running reference point.

## Method

The core algorithm is a discounted variant of the VAW forecaster that incorporates an exponential discount factor γ into the running statistics (Σ_t = x_t x_t^⊤ + γΣ_{t−1}) and allows 'hints' ỹ_t to be injected into the update before the prediction is committed. The analysis via a mirror-descent interpretation reveals a clean tracking/stability trade-off: a small γ controls the variability penalty P_T^γ(u) (a γ-weighted measure of how much the comparator's losses change) but inflates a stability penalty proportional to d·log(1/γ)·Σ(y_t − ỹ_t)². The optimal γ* balancing these terms is shown to exist and is learned on-the-fly by running O(log T) base instances with geometrically spaced discount factors and combining their clipped predictions via a fixed-share meta-algorithm. A trust-region clipping trick (constraining predictions to [y_t^Ref − M_t, y_t^Ref + M_t]) enables small-loss bounds that adapt to the comparator's total loss, and geometric covering intervals extend the guarantees to hold simultaneously over every sub-interval [a,b] ⊆ [1,T], yielding the first strongly-adaptive guarantees in the absence of all boundedness assumptions.

## Results

The paper establishes that a discounted VAW forecaster with a well-chosen discount factor achieves dynamic regret $R_T(\boldsymbol{u}) \leq O(d\log(T) \vee \sqrt{dP_T^\gamma(\boldsymbol{u})T})$ with no boundedness assumptions, where $P_T^\gamma(\boldsymbol{u})$ is a $\gamma$-exponentially-weighted variability measure. A matching lower bound $R_T(\boldsymbol{u}) \geq \Omega(d\log(T) \vee \sqrt{dTP_T^\gamma(\boldsymbol{u})})$ is proven, establishing optimality. Small-loss (self-bounding) guarantees of the form $R_T(\boldsymbol{u}) \leq \widetilde{O}(dP_T^{\gamma_{\min}}(\boldsymbol{u}) + d\max_t(y_t - y_t^{Ref})^2\log(T) + \sqrt{dP_T^{\gamma^\circ}(\boldsymbol{u})\sum_t \ell_t(u_t)})$ are also obtained, and the ideal discount factor can be learned on-the-fly via a fixed-share meta-algorithm over $O(\log T)$ experts. Strongly-adaptive guarantees holding over every sub-interval $[a,b] \subseteq [1,T]$ simultaneously are achieved with only a $\log^2(T)$ overhead.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's main comparison is against prior theoretical guarantees for online linear regression. The standard VAW forecaster (Azoury & Warmuth 2001; Vovk 2001) achieves optimal static regret $O(d\max_t y_t^2 \log T)$ but can suffer arbitrarily bad dynamic regret (e.g., linear regret when the target signal shifts sign mid-horizon). Jacobsen & Cutkosky (2023) achieve dynamic regret $O(M^{3/2}\sqrt{P_T T})$ but require prior knowledge of $G_{\max}$ and $L_{\max}$, fail to achieve logarithmic regret against a fixed comparator, and incur $O(dT\log T)$ per-round computation. In the bounded-domain exp-concave setting, Baby & Wang (2021) prove a lower bound of $\Omega(Y^{4/3}d^{1/3}T^{1/3}C_T^{2/3})$ with a matching upper bound only up to a $d^{3.5}$ factor in dimension. The present work is the first to achieve optimal dynamic regret (matching lower bound in all quantities) in the completely unbounded, prior-knowledge-free setting, with $O(d^2\log T)$ per-round computation and no assumptions on the data-generating process.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Vovk-Azoury-Warmuth (VAW) forecaster — the canonical algorithm for static-regret online linear regression (Azoury & Warmuth, 2001; Vovk, 2001) — by introducing an exponentially-decaying discount factor and predicted-label hints, yielding a discounted VAW forecaster that tracks time-varying comparators. The strongly-adaptive guarantees build on the Follow-the-Leading-History framework of Hazan & Seshadhri (2007; 2009), adapted here via geometric covering intervals (Daniely et al., 2015) to keep the number of active experts at O(log²(T)). The key departure from prior work is that all guarantees hold in a completely unbounded domain with no prior knowledge of the data stream, whereas prior dynamic-regret results for online regression (e.g., Baby & Wang, 2021; Jacobsen & Cutkosky, 2023) require boundedness assumptions or knowledge of loss gradients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first set of algorithms for online linear regression that achieve optimal dynamic regret (matching a new lower bound) and strongly-adaptive guarantees over all sub-intervals simultaneously, without any boundedness or distributional assumptions — a significant step toward truly assumption-free online learning. The practical limitation is computational: the per-round cost is O(d² log(T)), which is infeasible for very high-dimensional features; the authors suggest sketching techniques (Luo et al., 2016) and recent efficiency improvements (Lu & Hazan, 2022) as paths forward. An open direction is constructing the expert set in a more on-the-fly manner to avoid the doubling trick for unknown horizons, and extending the analysis to other loss families beyond the quadratic.
