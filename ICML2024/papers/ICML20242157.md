## Problem

Most long-tail learning methods assume the test label distribution is uniform, but real-world deployments often face unknown, non-uniform test distributions (e.g., forward or backward long-tail). Existing test-agnostic approaches either require access to the true test label distribution (LADE) or rely on multi-expert ensembles (SADE, BalPoE), leaving a gap for a simple, general method that can estimate the unknown test distribution and correct predictions without architectural changes to the base model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes unlabeled test images passed through any pre-trained long-tail classifier to obtain per-sample logits, which are then aggregated into class-wise average logits (after adaptive clipping) and fed into a two-layer MLP estimator. The output is a K-dimensional estimated test label distribution, which is used to adjust the original logits via a log-ratio correction, yielding final class predictions. The method is model-agnostic and can be applied post-hoc to any existing long-tail learner (NCL, RIDE, PaCo, SHIKE) without retraining the base model.

## Method

LSC introduces a Generalized Black Box Shift Estimation (GBBSE) framework where a shallow neural estimator is trained on simulated subsets of the training data, each resampled to follow a different class prior spanning imbalance ratios from 1/100 to 100. The estimator maps averaged (and clipped) class logits to a predicted label distribution, generalizing beyond the fixed confusion-matrix inversion of standard BBSE. A key component is adaptive logit clipping, which truncates the k smallest logits per sample to zero, with k automatically selected via a head-vs-tail comparison criterion (Eq. 4) to counteract the tail-class overconfidence induced by class-balanced losses. At test time, the estimated distribution is used to adjust predictions by adding log(P̂_T(y)/P_S(y)) to each logit. Theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.1) bounds the generalization error gap in terms of posterior approximation quality and label distribution estimation error, while Theorem 3.2 shows the Bayes estimation error converges at a linear rate in test set size.

## Results

On ImageNet-LT (ResNeXt50), LSC achieves 72.3% micro-accuracy under Forward-50 test distribution and 57.3% under Backward-50, outperforming the strongest baseline SADE (69.4% and 53.1%) by 2.9 and 4.2 points respectively. On CIFAR100-LT-100 (ResNet32), LSC reaches 68.1% (Forward-50) and 48.3% (Backward-50), surpassing SADE by 2.2 and 5.9 points. On Places-LT (ResNet152), LSC attains 47.7% (Forward-50) and 44.4% (Backward-50), improving over SADE by 1.3 and 2.8 points. Notably, LSC also outperforms LADE (which uses ground-truth test label distribution) by 5.5 and 8.1 points on ImageNet-LT under Forward-50 and Backward-50 settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LSC consistently outperforms all 12 baselines across all three datasets and all five test label distributions. Against the strongest test-agnostic baseline SADE (NeurIPS 2022), LSC gains +2.2 to +5.9 points on CIFAR100-LT, +2.9 to +4.2 on ImageNet-LT, and +1.3 to +2.8 on Places-LT, with the largest margins in the most imbalanced Backward-50 setting. LSC also surpasses BalPoE (CVPR 2023) by up to +12.2 points (Backward-50 on CIFAR100-LT: 48.3 vs 36.1) and +6.5 points (Backward-50 on ImageNet-LT: 57.3 vs 50.8). Remarkably, LSC outperforms LADE (CVPR 2021) — which has access to the ground-truth test label distribution — by +5.5 and +8.1 points on ImageNet-LT (Forward-50 and Backward-50), demonstrating that accurate self-estimated distributions can surpass oracle priors. Among label shift estimation methods, BBSE, RLLS, and MLLS all underperform LSC substantially, particularly in backward settings where they drop to 13.5–24.1% on CIFAR100-LT versus LSC's 48.3%. LSC is also composable: when combined with NCL, RIDE, PaCo, or SHIKE as base models, it adds +1.3 to +6.5 points without degrading uniform-distribution performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes Black Box Shift Estimation (BBSE, Lipton et al. 2018) into a broader framework called GBBSE, where a parameterized neural estimator is trained on simulated label distributions rather than relying on a single confusion matrix inversion. It departs from the multi-expert ensemble paradigm of SADE and BalPoE, which simulate different label distributions through separate expert networks, by instead learning a single lightweight mapping from averaged logits to label distributions. The method also builds on the logit-adjustment idea from LADE (Hong et al. 2021) but removes the impractical requirement of ground-truth test class priors, replacing them with a learned estimator. A key conceptual departure is the introduction of adaptive logit clipping to counteract the tail-class overconfidence induced by class-balanced losses in long-tail training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Test-agnostic long-tail learning is critical for real-world deployment where test distributions are unknown and may differ substantially from training, yet prior methods either require ground-truth test priors (LADE) or rely on complex multi-expert ensembles (SADE, BalPoE). LSC's simplicity (a 2-layer MLP plus logit clipping) and its compatibility with existing long-tail methods make it a practical, drop-in improvement for practitioners. Open questions include how the approach extends beyond pure label shift to covariate shift, whether the simulated-distribution training range must be expanded for extremely imbalanced test sets, and whether the method generalizes to non-vision modalities or open-set settings.
