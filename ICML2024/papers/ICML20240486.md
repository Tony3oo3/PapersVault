## Problem

Frame-averaging and canonicalization are popular architecture-agnostic methods for enforcing equivariance, but they suffer from a critical flaw: for common groups like S_n, SO(d), and O(d), no continuous canonicalization exists, and unweighted frames of reasonable size cannot preserve continuity. This means a smooth non-symmetric backbone function can become discontinuous after frame-averaging, causing arbitrarily small input perturbations to produce entirely different outputs—a fundamental robustness failure that likely explains the slow adoption of frame-based methods in practice.

## Input / Output

The method takes a point cloud X ∈ R^{d×n} (columns are points) and a group action (S_n, SO(d), or O(d)), and produces a continuous invariant or equivariant function via a weighted projection operator. The key structural detail is that the frame assigns input-dependent probability weights to group elements (rather than uniform weights over a fixed set), and the weights are designed to vanish at points where the associated group element is not uniquely defined, ensuring continuity. For the equivariant case, the backbone must additionally parametrize 'stable' functions (those whose output stabilizer contains the input stabilizer).

## Method

The paper first proves impossibility results using algebraic topology (fundamental groups, homology, Borsuk-Ulam) showing that no continuous canonicalization exists for S_n on R^{d×n} (d>1), SO(d) on R^{d×n} (n≥d), or O(d) on R^{d×n} (n>d), and that the only continuity-preserving unweighted frame for finite groups acting freely is the full Reynolds operator. To overcome this, the authors define weighted frames (Borel probability measures on G) with a relaxed notion of 'weak equivariance' that only requires equivariance of the stabilizer-averaged measure, avoiding the need for frame size to grow with the stabilizer. A 'robust frame' is a continuous, weakly equivariant weighted frame, and the paper proves that the induced invariant projection operator always preserves continuity. Explicit constructions are given: for S_n on R^{d×n}_distinct, a frame of cardinality n(d−1) using globally separated 1D sorting directions; for S_n on all R^{d×n}, a frame of cardinality O(n^{2(d−1)}) using random-direction sorting; and for SO(d), a frame of cardinality n(n−1)⋯(n−d+2) built from Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization of column subsequences with weights proportional to parallelepiped volumes.

## Results

On MNIST point clouds (100 points, 2D), robust frames (Sec. 4.2) achieve 88.7% test accuracy with 25 Monte Carlo samples, outperforming discontinuous canonicalization (85.6%) by ~3 percentage points. The robust frame from Sec. 4.1 (on R^{d×n}_distinct) reaches 88.4% under the same protocol. In stark contrast, the full Reynolds operator (averaging over all n! permutations) achieves only 22.6% due to insufficient sampling, and no invariance yields 25.5%. Theoretically, the paper proves no continuous canonicalization exists for S_n, SO(d) (n≥d), or O(d) (n>d) acting on point clouds, and constructs robust frames with cardinality as low as n(d−1) for permutations on R^{d×n}_distinct.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five invariance enforcement strategies on a 2D point cloud classification task (MNIST digits converted to 100-point clouds). Robust frames (both the Sec. 4.1 and Sec. 4.2 constructions) consistently outperform the discontinuous canonicalization baseline (sorting along x-axis) by 2.8–3.1 percentage points at 25 inference samples (88.4–88.7% vs. 85.6%), demonstrating that continuity preservation yields a measurable accuracy gain. Both robust frame variants also slightly outperform each other, with the full-space frame (Sec. 4.2, cardinality O(n^{2(d−1)})) edging out the distinct-points frame (Sec. 4.1, cardinality n(d−1)) by 0.3 points, suggesting that continuity on the entire space provides a small but consistent benefit. The Reynolds operator (full group averaging over n! permutations) performs worst among the invariance-enforcing methods (22.6%), because with only 25 random samples from an enormous group, the effective invariance is negligible. No invariance (25.5%) is similarly poor, confirming that the dataset is too small to learn without symmetry enforcement. The paper also empirically verifies (Table 3, Appendix F.1) that the equiadapt library's learned canonicalization (Gram-Schmidt-based) exhibits a 40× larger pairwise output variance near singular point clouds compared to generic points, confirming the theoretical discontinuity predictions in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the frame-averaging framework of Puny et al. (2021) by identifying a fundamental continuity failure that had been empirically observed (e.g., by Zhang et al., 2020 for permutations) but never formally characterized. It generalizes the d=3 'smooth frames' of Pozdnyakov & Ceriotti (2023) to all dimensions and provides the first rigorous proof of their robustness. The work also provides a theoretical explanation for the empirical success of probabilistic/weighted frames in concurrent work (Kim et al., 2023; Mondal et al., 2023), unifying these approaches under a single formalism of 'robust frames' with weak equivariance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility results (no continuous canonicalization for S_n, SO(d), O(d) in most practical regimes; no finite continuity-preserving unweighted frame for SO(2)) explain why frame-averaging has struggled to replace equivariant architectures in practice. The constructive robust frames of polynomial size (n(d-1) for permutations on distinct points, ~n^{d-1} for rotations) offer a practical fix, and the lower bounds show these are near-optimal. Open questions include stronger lower bounds for SO(d) frames, whether continuous canonicalization exists for unordered point clouds, smoothness (Lipschitz) guarantees, and concentration bounds for the sampled-frame implementations that are necessary in practice.
