## Problem

While training data bias is a primary cause of downstream model bias, few automated methods exist to help practitioners identify which subgroups in the data are affected and why. Prior fairness auditing focuses on finding subgroups that maximally violate a specific fairness criterion, but does not diagnose the underlying feature-level mechanisms. This paper asks whether standard feature importance notions (SHAP, LIME, saliency maps) reveal substantial disparities across rich subgroups (combinations of sensitive attributes), and whether such disparities can be efficiently detected over exponentially large subgroup spaces.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabular dataset partitioned into sensitive (protected) and unprotected features, binary or continuous outcome labels, a trained classifier h, and a chosen feature importance notion F. It outputs a (feature f_j, subgroup g) pair where f_j has disproportionately large importance within g relative to the full dataset, along with the defining coefficients of g as a linear threshold function over sensitive attributes. The subgroup class G consists of linear threshold functions over sensitive features, allowing continuous sensitive variables (e.g., age) and fractional group membership. All reported AVG-FID values are computed out-of-sample on a held-out test set to avoid overfitting to the training data.

## Method

The paper introduces Feature Importance Disparity (FID) and its average variant (AVG-FID), then classifies feature importance notions as separable (decomposable as pointwise sums over data points) or non-separable. For separable notions, the core algorithm (Algorithm 1) casts the constrained FID maximization as a two-player zero-sum game: the max player adjusts dual variables on size constraints via exponentiated gradient descent, while the min player finds best-response subgroups through a Cost Sensitive Classification (CSC) oracle implemented as linear regression over sensitive features. Theorem 4.1 guarantees convergence to a near-optimal FID subgroup in O(n²B²/ν²) iterations. For the non-separable linear regression case (LIN-FID), the method instead optimizes the differentiable weighted least squares coefficient directly using ADAM, with size penalty and L2 regularization terms to enforce constraints and ensure invertibility.

## Results

Across all 16 (dataset, importance notion) pairings, the method identifies rich subgroups (1–21% of the dataset) where a specific feature has dramatically different importance than on the full population. The most extreme disparity found is on Folktables with LIME, where the feature *age* is 225× more important in the discovered subgroup than on the dataset as a whole. On COMPAS R with SHAP, the feature *age* has an AVG-FID of 0.41 in a 4% subgroup defined by Asian-American identity, versus a population average of 0.012. In roughly half of the cases, rich subgroups yield substantially higher out-of-sample FID than the best marginal (single-attribute) subgroup for the same feature.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline comparison is against GerryFair (Kearns et al., 2018), which finds rich subgroups that maximally violate FPR disparity. In Table 3, the paper's method (which explicitly optimizes for AVG-FID) consistently finds substantially larger feature importance disparities: e.g., on COMPAS R with SHAP, the paper's method finds *Age* with AVG-FID 0.40 versus GerryFair's 0.06 (a ~7× gap); on Bank with LIME, the paper finds # of Employees at 0.03 versus GerryFair's 0.008 (~4×); on Folktables with SHAP, the paper finds *Education* at 0.13 versus GerryFair's 0.05 (~2.6×). The paper also compares rich subgroups against marginal (single-attribute) subgroups: in about half of the (dataset, method) pairs, the rich subgroup yields a meaningfully higher out-of-sample FID, while in the other half the two are comparable. High AVG-FID subgroups also correlate with disparities in standard fairness metrics (demographic parity, TPR, FPR, ECE), with differences of 7–40 percentage points on COMPAS R, reinforcing that the discovered subgroups correspond to genuine fairness concerns rather than statistical artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the oracle-efficient constrained optimization framework from Agarwal et al. (2018) and the rich subgroup class G from Kearns et al. (2018) and Hebert-Johnson et al. (2018), but applies it to a fundamentally different objective: rather than finding subgroups that maximally violate a fairness metric (e.g., FPR disparity), it finds subgroups where a specific feature's importance (as measured by SHAP, LIME, saliency, or linear regression coefficients) diverges from the population average. The authors explicitly position this as the first work studying feature importance disparities in the context of rich subgroups as a fairness diagnostic, distinguishing it from prior anomalous subgroup discovery work that focuses on explanation quality (stability, fidelity) rather than the magnitude of importance disparities themselves.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, data-centric diagnostic tool that complements existing model-centric fairness approaches by helping practitioners identify *where* and *why* bias may originate in the training data, rather than merely constraining the model to be fair. A key practical finding is that rich subgroups (conjunctions of sensitive attributes) often reveal substantially larger feature importance disparities than marginal subgroups, justifying the computational overhead of searching the exponentially large subgroup space. Limitations include the requirement for tabular data with predefined sensitive features, the fact that high FID does not guarantee a fairness violation, and the known instability of the underlying feature importance notions. Open questions include extending the framework to non-tabular modalities, determining optimal feature subsets for collection, and integrating FID detection into automated data auditing pipelines.
