## Problem

Neural ODEs suffer from prohibitively slow training and inference because the ODE solver must evaluate the vector field many times over a fixed integration interval, effectively creating an unnecessarily deep network. Existing acceleration techniques focus on spatial regularization of dynamics or solver-level optimizations, but none directly optimize the temporal extent (depth) of the network to eliminate redundant computation. This paper asks: how deep do we actually need the network to be, and can we learn that depth as part of training?

## Input / Output

The method accepts standard inputs—2D data points (two moons, concentric annuli), grayscale images (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST at 28×28), color images (CIFAR-10 at 32×32), or Gaussian samples for generative models—and produces class labels or generated samples. The key structural difference is that the terminal integration time t_f becomes a learnable scalar parameter alongside the ODE network weights θ, so the effective network depth (number of solver evaluations) is no longer fixed at 100 steps but adapts during training, often dropping to 10–50 evaluations. A fixed-step RK4 solver with step size 0.01 is used throughout, making the depth directly proportional to t_f.

## Method

The paper proposes two control-theory-inspired approaches to jointly optimize network weights and integration depth. Approach 1 (Minimum-time Neural ODEs) formulates training as a minimum-time optimal control problem: a time regularization term λ∫₁dt is added to the terminal loss, and the gradient of the loss with respect to t_f is derived via the Leibniz integral rule (∂Φ/∂z(t_f)·f(z(t_f)) + λ), enabling single-stage joint optimization of θ and t_f with clipping to prevent instability. Approach 2 (Convergence-rate-based Neural ODEs) uses a Lyapunov loss during pre-training on a short interval to guarantee exponential convergence rate κ of the latent dynamics, then iteratively extends t_f forward in fixed increments until the terminal loss drops below a threshold, yielding the shallowest sufficient network. Both methods reduce training and inference time by an order of magnitude (e.g., MNIST: 4h47m → 20m training, 0.546s → 0.03s inference) with minimal accuracy loss.

## Results

On MNIST, Minimum-time Neural ODEs achieve 99.53% test accuracy with 20 min 23 s training time and 0.03 s inference time (16.1 NFE), compared to 99.57% accuracy, 4 h 47 min training, and 0.546 s inference (402.6 NFE) for Vanilla Neural ODEs — a ~94% reduction in training time with only 0.04% accuracy loss. On CIFAR-10, the method (λ=0.1) reaches 81.74% accuracy in 2 h 22 min versus >10 h for Vanilla, with inference dropping from 0.531 s to 0.278 s. On the binary classification tasks (Two Moons, Concentric Annuli), training time drops from 46.64 s to 7.33 s and from 123 s to 27 s respectively, while inference time falls by roughly 8–9×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Vanilla Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018), the proposed Minimum-time method achieves a 94% training-time reduction on MNIST (20 min vs. 4 h 47 min) and a 95% reduction on Fashion-MNIST (22 min vs. 4 h 41 min), while sacrificing less than 0.4% test accuracy. On CIFAR-10, the speedup is 76% (2 h 22 min vs. >10 h) at the cost of 0.86% accuracy (81.74% vs. 82.60%), with the λ hyperparameter offering a tunable accuracy–speed trade-off. Compared to SNOpt (Liu et al., 2021), which reports 30% and 19% training-time reductions on MNIST and CIFAR-10 respectively, the proposed method delivers substantially larger gains (94% and 76%), partly because SNOpt relies on the adjoint sensitivity method that is inherently ~10× slower than automatic differentiation. TayNODE (Kelly et al., 2020) is compared on MNIST computation time (Figure 5), where the proposed method also shows a clear advantage. On the binary classification benchmarks (Two Moons, Concentric Annuli), both the Minimum-time and Convergence-rate-based methods reduce inference time by roughly 8–9× relative to Vanilla, with the Convergence-rate-based approach additionally providing a theoretical convergence-rate guarantee via the Lyapunov framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Neural ODE framework (Chen et al., 2018) and the Lyapunov-based training approach of LyaNet (Rodriguez et al., 2022), but repositions the depth problem through the lens of optimal control theory. It departs from prior acceleration efforts that focus on spatial regularization (TayNODE, STEER) or solver-level optimization (Poli et al., 2020) by treating the integration terminal time as a learnable parameter, and contrasts with SNOpt (Liu et al., 2021) which also optimizes terminal time but relies on the inherently slower adjoint sensitivity method. The minimum-time optimal control formulation and the two-stage Lyapunov pre-training procedure represent a conceptual departure from the regularization-based approaches that dominate the Neural ODE acceleration literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The order-of-magnitude speedups in both training and inference (e.g., 4h47m → 20m on MNIST) are practically significant for making Neural ODEs viable in resource-constrained settings, and the simple mechanism of learning t_f is easy to adopt. However, the accuracy trade-offs on harder datasets (CIFAR-10 drops from 82.6% to 76.4–81.7% depending on λ) and the limited experimental scope (small 2D classification problems, single network architecture) leave open questions about scalability to larger, more complex tasks. The paper itself acknowledges that combining both approaches does not yield significant advantages over individual methods, and the interaction with adaptive ODE solvers (where speedups are less dramatic) remains underexplored.
