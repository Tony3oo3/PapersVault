## Problem

Standard online learning algorithms (e.g., Hedge, FTRL) use the entire history of rewards to make decisions, but many practical settings—modeling human recency bias, attention-based sequence models with fixed context windows, data retention policies—require the learner to base decisions only on the past M rounds. This paper asks what regret guarantees are achievable under this bounded-recall constraint, why the natural sliding-window approach fails for the most common no-regret algorithms, and whether stationary bounded-recall algorithms with sublinear regret exist at all.

## Input / Output

At each round t, the algorithm receives only the reward vectors r_{t-M}, …, r_{t-1} (each in [0,1]^d) and must output a distribution x_t over d actions; for stationary algorithms, the mapping from the M-tuple of past rewards to x_t is a fixed function independent of t. The key structural constraint is that no internal state persists beyond the M-round window, ruling out algorithms that accumulate exponential weights or cumulative sums over the full horizon. The paper considers the regime T ≫ M with both going to infinity, and evaluates per-round regret (amortized over T rounds).

## Method

The paper's central algorithm, AVERAGERESTART, at each round t computes x_t = (1/M) Σ_{m=1}^{M} A(r_{t-m}, …, r_{t-1}), i.e., it uniformly averages the action that an underlying no-regret algorithm A (e.g., Hedge) would play if restarted at each of the M possible starting points within the window. This yields a stationary M-bounded-recall algorithm with O(√(log d / M)) per-round regret, matching the information-theoretic lower bound. The paper proves that any M-mean-based bounded-recall algorithm (which includes Hedge, FTRL, MWU, EXP3 run over a sliding window) incurs constant per-round regret via a three-phase adversarial construction, and further shows that any symmetric stationary bounded-recall algorithm must also have high regret—establishing that asymmetric weighting of recent rounds is essential. A corollary, AVERAGERESTARTFULLHORIZON, sets M = T to produce a novel non-mean-based full-horizon no-regret algorithm that treats past rounds asymmetrically.

## Results

The paper establishes that any M-bounded-recall algorithm must incur at least Ω(√(log d / M)) per-round regret (Theorem 3.1), and that this bound is tight: both PERIODICRESTART and the stationary AVERAGERESTART algorithm achieve O(√(log d / M)) per-round regret. In contrast, any M-mean-based bounded-recall algorithm (e.g., running Hedge over the last M rounds) incurs a constant Ω(1) per-round regret (at least 1/18 − o(1), Theorem 4.1), and any symmetric stationary bounded-recall algorithm also incurs Ω(1) regret (Theorem 5.6). In simulations with T=1000, bounded-recall algorithms significantly outperform full-horizon Multiplicative Weights in drifting reward environments, while in the adversarial construction from Lemma 4.2, the mean-based bounded-recall learner accumulates regret on the order of M/6 (≈ T/18) versus negative regret for PERIODICRESTART and AVERAGERESTART.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the full-horizon Multiplicative Weights (Hedge) algorithm, which achieves O(√(log d / T)) per-round regret without memory constraints. The paper's key comparison is between AVERAGERESTART (a stationary bounded-recall algorithm) and the natural approach of running Hedge over only the last M rewards (a mean-based bounded-recall algorithm): the former achieves the optimal O(√(log d / M)) regret while the latter incurs constant Ω(1) per-round regret, a fundamental separation. In the drifting-reward simulation (period φ ∈ {T/20, T/10, T/5, T/2}, M = 0.15T), both PERIODICRESTART and AVERAGERESTART substantially outperform full-horizon MW, which accumulates positive cumulative regret while the bounded-recall methods maintain near-zero or negative regret. In the adversarial two-arm construction (M = T/3), the mean-based bounded-recall algorithm ends with regret proportional to T (≈ T/18), while PERIODICRESTART and AVERAGERESTART both achieve negative cumulative regret, outperforming even the full-horizon MW baseline which ends near zero. The AVERAGERESTARTFULLHORIZON variant (setting M = T) is shown to be a novel non-mean-based full-horizon algorithm that also inherits the O(√(log d / T)) guarantee.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the bounded-recall concept from repeated game theory (Aumann & Sorin 1989; Lehrer 1988) into the online learning literature, where it had not been systematically studied. It builds directly on the Hedge/MWU framework (Arora et al. 2012) as the underlying no-regret subroutine, and extends the mean-based algorithm taxonomy of Braverman et al. (2018) to the bounded-recall setting. The closest prior result is Zapechelnyuk (2008), who showed high regret for better-reply dynamics under bounded recall; this paper generalizes that negative result to the broader class of mean-based algorithms and, crucially, provides a positive construction (AVERAGERESTART) that achieves the optimal O(√(log d / M)) regret bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bounded-recall setting is directly relevant to attention-based sequence models with fixed context windows, GDPR-style data retention policies, and modeling recency bias in human decision-making. The negative result—that the most natural approach (running Hedge over a sliding window) incurs constant per-round regret for all mean-based algorithms—challenges a common heuristic and motivates the asymmetric AVERAGERESTART construction. Open questions include the computational cost of AVERAGERESTART (which requires M separate algorithm runs per round), whether the approach extends to other regret notions (e.g., swap regret), and a theoretical characterization of when bounded recall outperforms full-horizon learners in non-stationary environments.
