## Problem

Classical policy iteration for nonlinear optimal control assumes C¹ smoothness of value functions, yet the true HJB solution may only be a viscosity solution (e.g., V(x)=2|x| for the scalar bilinear system), and no prior work established convergence of policy iteration to viscosity solutions, particularly when function approximators are involved. Additionally, the standard Galerkin methods for solving the linear GHJB subproblems suffer from the curse of dimensionality, limiting their applicability to low-dimensional systems. This paper asks whether neural network approximations can solve the GHJB equations within a policy iteration loop while preserving convergence guarantees and producing verifiably stabilizing controllers.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the system dynamics f(x), g(x) and cost parameters Q(x), R as analytical (symbolic) functions rather than data, along with an initial stabilizing policy κ₀ and a bounded domain Ω. At each policy iteration, collocation points are sampled from Ω and the PDE residual is evaluated via automatic differentiation; the boundary condition is simply V(0)=0 since the origin is an equilibrium. The output is a neural-network-approximated value function and feedback controller, accompanied by a formal stability certificate obtained by verifying Lyapunov inequalities (D V̂(x)(f+gκ̂) ≤ −μ) on Ω \ U_ε using the dReal SMT solver, which excludes a small neighborhood of the origin where the inequality degenerates to an equality.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-variant neural policy iteration framework that replaces Galerkin solvers for the linear GHJB equation with neural PDE solvers. ELM-PI randomizes the hidden-layer weights W and bias b of a one-layer network, then exploits the linearity of the GHJB in DV to reduce the PDE residual minimization to a linear least-squares problem in the output weights β, solved in closed form at each iteration. PINN-PI instead trains a (potentially multi-layer) feedforward network via gradient descent on the non-convex PDE residual loss, offering better scalability to higher dimensions. A key practical innovation is an additional loss term that enforces the linearized controller gain at the origin to match the gain from the corresponding Lyapunov equation, encoding local asymptotic stability directly into training. The paper provides a convergence analysis showing that both algorithms converge to the viscosity solution of the HJB under mild assumptions (dense collocation, bounded Lipschitz constants on Ω \ U_ε), and demonstrates via dReal that seemingly converged controllers can in fact be unstable, motivating the formal verification step.

## Results

On the inverted pendulum, ELM-PI (m=100) solves the GHJB in 0.24 s versus 19.37 s for SGA (order 4) and 256.53 s for PINN-PI (m=100), while all three produce verifiably stabilizing controllers. On the synthetic n-dimensional problem, ELM-PI achieves errors as low as 1.14×10⁻¹⁰ at n=1 (m=100, 0.08 s) but becomes infeasible beyond n≈6, whereas PINN-PI maintains 10⁻²–10⁻³ accuracy up to n=12 within ~4400 s. Against RL baselines (PPO, HJBPPO, CT-MBRL), PINN-PI converges to equilibrium in under 2 s on cartpole and 2D/3D quadrotor, while all three RL methods diverge in accumulated cost on those higher-dimensional environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against successive Galerkin approximations (SGA, Beard et al. 1997), ELM-PI is roughly 70× faster at comparable accuracy (0.24 s vs 19.37 s for m=100 vs order 4) and 73× faster than order-8 SGA (2.92 s vs 212.42 s), while both produce verifiably stabilizing controllers; high-order SGA matches ELM-PI's trajectory cost but at far greater computational cost. Against PPO (Schulman et al. 2017), HJBPPO (Mukherjee & Liu 2023), and CT-MBRL (Yildiz et al. 2021), PINN-PI dominates on all four benchmark environments: on the inverted pendulum all methods perform comparably, but on cartpole, 2D quadrotor, and 3D quadrotor the three RL baselines fail to achieve stability (accumulated cost diverges), whereas PINN-PI converges to equilibrium in under 2 s. The paper also demonstrates that ELM-PI with m=50 on the inverted pendulum produces a controller that visually appears converged but is formally verified to be unstable, while m=100 is verified stabilizing—highlighting that formal verification (via dReal SMT solving) is essential and that no baseline comparison can substitute for it.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three established lines: classical policy iteration for nonlinear optimal control (Kleinman, Beard et al., Jiang & Jiang), neural PDE solvers (PINN, ELM, DGM), and formal verification of neural controllers (dReal, neural Lyapunov functions). The key conceptual departure is from prior policy iteration work, which assumed C¹ value functions and did not establish convergence to viscosity solutions of the HJB equation; this paper provides the first such convergence guarantee in the general (possibly non-differentiable) setting. It is also, to the authors' knowledge, the first to use neural PDE solvers (rather than Galerkin methods) for the linear GHJB subproblems within policy iteration for benchmark nonlinear control problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The viscosity-solution convergence theorem fills a genuine theoretical gap: prior results (Jiang & Jiang 2017, Beard 1995) required C¹ assumptions that the HJB solution may not satisfy (e.g., V(x)=2|x| for the scalar bilinear example). Practically, the demonstration that a visually convergent ELM-PI controller (m=50) is unstable while a slightly larger one (m=100) is verifiably stable underscores that formal verification is not optional for safety-critical deployment. Open questions include whether the dReal-based verification can scale beyond low dimensions (it currently suffers from the curse of dimensionality), whether Hypothesis 4.5 (Lipschitz constant convergence) can be replaced by more checkable conditions, and whether the PINN-PI accuracy plateau at higher dimensions can be overcome with deeper architectures or better optimization.
