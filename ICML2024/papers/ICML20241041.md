## Problem

Single-model attribution—determining whether a sample was generated by a specific generative model—has not been adequately solved in the open-world setting. Existing fingerprinting and inversion methods are restricted to closed-world scenarios with a fixed set of known generators, while watermarking requires altering the model during training. No prior work has connected single-model attribution to anomaly detection, which is the natural framing for a binary 'this model or something else' decision.

## Input / Output

FLIPAD requires only resources already available to the model creator: the generative model G (including all parameters), samples generated by G, and real samples used to train G—no samples from other models are needed. The final-layer inversion produces a high-dimensional activation vector (e.g., 262,144-dimensional for a 64×64 LSGAN) that serves as the feature input to the anomaly detector. The output is a binary attribution decision (generated by G or not) derived from an anomaly score thresholded against a learned boundary.

## Method

FLIPAD reframes single-model attribution as anomaly detection using DeepSAD, with a novel feature extraction step based on final-layer inversion. Given a sample x, it computes the pre-activation target o = σ_L^{-1}(x) and solves for the (L−1)-layer activation ẑ_{L-1} that approximately satisfies G_L(ẑ_{L-1}) = o while staying close to the Monte-Carlo-estimated average activation z̄_{L-1}. This is formulated as the convex lasso problem min ||G_L(z_{L-1}) − o||₂² + λ||z_{L-1} − z̄_{L-1}||₁, which is provably equivalent to a standard lasso, solvable globally and uniquely (with probability 1 for 2D convolutions) via FISTA. The ℓ₁ regularization toward z̄_{L-1} biases solutions toward activations with the same structural properties as genuine model activations, making non-generated samples produce distinguishably different features for the anomaly detector.

## Results

FLIPAD achieves a consistent single-model attribution accuracy above 97.5% across all eight model-dataset combinations (DCGAN, WGAN-GP, LSGAN, EBGAN on CelebA and LSUN), with peak values of 99.68% (EBGAN/CelebA) and 99.38% (EBGAN/LSUN). In contrast, the adapted fingerprinting (SM-F) and inversion (SM-Inv₂, SM-Inv_inc) baselines are highly unstable, with accuracies ranging from ~50% (chance) to ~99.8% depending on the specific model pair. FLIPAD also outperforms the simpler anomaly-detection baselines RawPAD and DCTPAD, which show notable drops (e.g., RawPAD falls to 52.36% on WGAN-GP/LSUN, DCTPAD to 52.96% on WGAN-GP/CelebA). Runtime for FLIPAD is ~17–20 minutes for 1000 samples, compared to ~300–450 minutes for full-inversion baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLIPAD consistently outperforms all three adapted closed-world baselines (SM-F, SM-Inv₂, SM-Inv_inc) in terms of both accuracy and stability. SM-Fingerprinting (Marra et al., 2019) is the most unstable, dropping to near-chance (~50%) on WGAN-GP and LSGAN for both CelebA and LSUN, while FLIPAD maintains >97.7% in those same settings. SM-Inv₂ (Albright & McCloskey, 2019) achieves near-perfect accuracy on some pairs (99.80% on WGAN-GP/EBGAN CelebA) but collapses to ~50% on LSUN, and requires ~300 minutes per 1000 samples versus FLIPAD's ~17 minutes. SM-Inv_inc (Zhang et al., 2021) is the weakest baseline overall, never exceeding 78.46% and frequently at chance level, while also being the most expensive (~450 minutes). The simpler anomaly-detection baselines RawPAD and DCTPAD are more competitive but still inconsistent: RawPAD drops to 52–59% on WGAN-GP, and DCTPAD fails on WGAN-GP/LSUN (68.10%) and LSGAN/LSUN (67.93%), whereas FLIPAD never falls below 97.73%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the anomaly detection framework (specifically DeepSAD, a semi-supervised extension of SVDD) for the model attribution problem, a connection not previously drawn in the generative model attribution literature. Methodologically, it departs from prior inversion-based attribution (Albright & McCloskey, Zhang et al., Hirofumi et al.) by restricting inversion to only the final layer, which transforms the non-convex full-model inversion into a convex lasso problem grounded in compressed sensing theory (basis pursuit denoising, RIP). This contrasts with watermarking approaches (Yu et al., Kim et al., Nie et al.) that achieve single-model open-world attribution but require modifying the generative model during training, and with fingerprinting methods (Marra et al., Frank et al.) that are limited to closed-world multi-model settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretically grounded, model-agnostic method for single-model attribution in the open-world setting that does not require altering the generative model, making it directly applicable to IP theft detection for existing deployed models. The convex formulation (unique solution, FISTA-solvable) is a practical advantage over iterative full-model inversion, and the domain generality (images, medical scans, tabular data) broadens applicability beyond the image-only focus of prior work. Open questions include: performance degradation under JPEG compression and additive noise, the difficulty of extending to architectures with skip-connections (e.g., StyleGAN), and whether the RIP-based recovery bounds are tight enough to be practically informative for real network activations.
