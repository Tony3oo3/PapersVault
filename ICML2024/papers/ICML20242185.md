## Problem

Existing generative music models produce expressive output but cannot generate simultaneously with other musicians, as they are not designed for online, real-time interaction. Models trained purely by maximum likelihood suffer from exposure bias, failing to anticipate future input and unable to recover from mistakes or out-of-distribution perturbations—both essential for live jamming. The paper tackles how to train an online accompaniment model that adapts gracefully to unfamiliar melodies and its own errors while maintaining harmonic and temporal coherence.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a stream of monophonic melody notes quantized to sixteenth-note frames (max 256 frames = 64 beats), where each frame encodes a note-on event (128 MIDI pitch values), a note-hold continuation, or silence. It produces a chord progression where each frame is a chord onset (from 5041 distinct chord names), a chord-hold, or silence. Critically, each chord y_t is predicted conditioned only on past melody x_{<t} and past chords y_{<t}, never on the current melody note x_t, enforcing the causal constraint that the accompanist must commit to a chord before hearing the note it will support.

## Method

ReaLchords uses a two-stage training procedure: first, an 8-layer decoder-only transformer is pretrained by MLE to predict chords autoregressively from past melody and chord context; second, it is finetuned with REINFORCE (using a separate value model as baseline) under a KL-regularized objective that combines reward maximization with distillation from an offline T5-style teacher that sees the full melody. The reward signal comes from an ensemble of multi-scale contrastive (InfoNCE-trained melody/chord encoders) and discriminative (real-vs-random-pairing classifiers) models evaluated at 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, and 1/16 sequence lengths via sliding windows, providing both global harmonic and local temporal coherence feedback. The key architectural insight is that the KL penalty distills from the offline teacher (which conditions on the entire melody) into the online student (which cannot), forcing the student to learn anticipation without direct access to future input. Additional regularization penalties discourage pathological behaviors such as chord repetition, excessive silence, and premature sequence termination.

## Results

On the Hooktheory test set, ReaLchords-M achieves a 54.29% note-in-chord ratio (harmonic quality), substantially outperforming the Online MLE baseline (36.99%) and approaching the Offline MLE upper bound (63.73%). ReaLchords (global reward models) reaches 48.17% harmony with 16.09×10⁻³ chord-note onset interval (synchronization). In human listening tests, all RL-finetuned systems significantly outperform Online MLE, and ReaLchords/ReaLchords-M significantly outperform distillation-only (KD), with the self-supervised reward models correlating strongly with human musicality judgments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReaLchords-M improves harmonic quality by 17.3 percentage points over the Online MLE baseline (54.29% vs. 36.99% note-in-chord ratio), closing roughly 40% of the gap to the Offline MLE upper bound (63.73%). Knowledge distillation alone (KD) accounts for a large portion of the improvement (46.38%), confirming that learning to anticipate future melody is the dominant factor. The contrastive reward model (C) primarily boosts harmony (47.22%) while the discriminative model (D) primarily improves synchronization (12.34×10⁻³ vs. 14.23 for Online MLE), and combining both in ReaLchords yields balanced gains. In the human listening test, all four systems significantly outperform Online MLE (Kruskal-Wallis + Wilcoxon post-hoc, p<0.05/6), and ReaLchords/ReaLchords-M significantly outperform KD, though the difference between ReaLchords and ReaLchords-M is not statistically significant. In adaptation experiments, Online MLE fails to recover from cold-start or mid-song tritone transposition, while ReaLchords and ReaLchords-M recover within a few beats, a capability the MLE baselines entirely lack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RL-Duet line of work on online music accompaniment by scaling from ~400 Bach Chorales to 38K diverse popular songs and removing the unrealistic priming assumption, while also departing from the standard RLHF/RLAIF paradigm by replacing human-labeled reward models with self-supervised contrastive and discriminative models. The KL-regularized RL objective follows Jaques et al. (2017), but the key methodological departure is the offline-to-online knowledge distillation: rather than anchoring the policy to the online MLE model (which suffers from exposure bias), the paper distills from a future-seeing teacher, a novel application of the GKD framework (Agarwal et al., 2023) to the online generation setting. The multi-scale reward ensemble, which evaluates fragments at 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, and 1/16 of maximum length to improve temporal credit assignment, has no direct precedent in prior music generation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ReaLchords demonstrates that a model can cold-start accompaniment and recover from mid-song perturbations (e.g., tritone transposition) without priming, a critical capability for real-time interactive music that prior systems explicitly avoided. The finding that self-supervised reward models trained purely on positive/negative pairs from the dataset align strongly with human musical preferences—without any human or AI labeling—suggests a scalable path to reward modeling in creative domains. Limitations include the restriction to monophonic melody input and chord-only output, and the evaluation is confined to a single dataset (Hooktheory); open questions include whether the offline-to-online distillation and multi-scale reward approach transfer to polyphonic input, multi-instrument co-creation, or other modalities requiring simultaneous generation.
