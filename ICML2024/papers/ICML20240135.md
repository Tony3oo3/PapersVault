## Problem

The distributional simplicity bias (DSB) posits that neural networks learn low-order moments of the data distribution before higher-order correlations, but prior evidence (Refinetti et al., 2023) was indirect, training on synthetic approximations and testing on real data. This paper inverts that setup—training on real data and probing with synthetic data that isolates specific statistical orders—and extends the DSB to discrete domains (text), where no prior theoretical link between n-gram statistics and distributional moments had been established.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240135/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes real image datasets (CIFAR-10, MNIST, SVHN, Fashion MNIST at 32×32; CIFARNet at 64×64) and neural network checkpoints at log-spaced training steps, along with class-conditional means and covariance matrices computed from the training set. It produces synthetic images via optimal transport maps, Gaussian sampling, or hypercube-constrained maximum-entropy optimization (all constrained to [0,1] pixel values), and for the language modeling experiments, synthetic token sequences of length 2049 sampled autoregressively from unigram or bigram models fitted to the Pile corpus. The final output is accuracy or cross-entropy loss curves as a function of training step, revealing when each statistical order becomes relevant.

## Method

The paper motivates the DSB via a Taylor expansion of expected loss as a sum over central moments, then operationalizes two criteria: (1) grafting class B's low-order statistics onto class A images via optimal transport (bounded shift, coordinatewise quantile normalization, or Gaussian OT) should flip the model's prediction to B, and (2) evaluating on maximum-entropy distributions matching only the first k moments should not degrade performance. For criterion 2, the authors introduce a novel gradient-based optimization that directly produces finite sample sets matching target moments while maximizing the Kozachenko-Leonenko entropy estimate, with a sigmoid reparameterization to enforce the [0,1] hypercube constraint. They further prove that token n-gram frequencies are exactly the moments of one-hot sequence encodings (Theorem 2.1), enabling the DSB to be tested in language models by evaluating Pythia checkpoints on unigram and bigram maximum-entropy sequences, where a 'double descent' pattern emerges as in-context learning later rescues performance on low-order synthetic data.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, all tested architectures (ConvNeXt V2, Swin Transformer V2, RegNet-Y) achieve substantially above the 10% random baseline when evaluated on optimal-transport-edited images labeled with the target class, with ConvNeXt peaking at over 40% accuracy on 2nd-order (Gaussian OT) edited images around 2^10 training steps. Accuracy on maximum-entropy synthetic data follows a U-shaped curve: models perform best on low-order (1st/2nd order) synthetic samples early in training, then lose this ability as they learn higher-order features. For language modeling, Pythia models exhibit a 'double descent' pattern on n-gram sequences: cross-entropy loss reaches a trough between 2^6 and 2^8 steps, rises until ~2^10 steps, then decreases again as in-context learning emerges. Unigram sequence loss consistently reaches its minimum before bigram sequence loss, and model scale (14M–12B parameters) has a remarkably small effect on the shape of these learning curves.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an empirical study of learning dynamics rather than a method comparison, so it does not benchmark a proposed method against competing baselines. Instead, it compares the behavior of multiple architectures (ConvNeXt V2, Swin Transformer V2, RegNet-Y) and model scales (Atto through Tiny) across five image datasets and the Pythia language model suite (14M–12B parameters). The key comparative finding is that all architectures and scales exhibit the same qualitative U-shaped pattern, with model scale having a remarkably small effect on the learning curves. Across datasets, the degree of non-monotonicity varies: simple datasets like MNIST and Fashion MNIST show very little non-monotonicity (because 1st/2nd-order statistics suffice to produce realistic samples), while CIFAR-10 and SVHN show pronounced U-shaped curves. For language models, larger Pythia models (12B) show a clear 'double descent' with in-context learning driving the second descent, while smaller models (14M–410M) revert to a standard U-shaped pattern, suggesting a capacity-dependent 'catastrophic forgetting' of early n-gram features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the experimental design of Refinetti et al. (2023), who trained networks on synthetic data approximating real data and showed early checkpoints generalize well; here, networks are trained on real data and probed with synthetic data of controlled statistical complexity, providing more direct evidence about standard training dynamics. The theoretical framework draws on the maximum entropy principle (Jaynes, 1957) for constructing test distributions, optimal transport theory (Dowson & Landau, 1982; Santambrogio, 2015) for surgically editing low-order statistics, and the Kozachenko-Leonenko entropy estimator for the novel gradient-based sampling algorithm. The extension to discrete domains via the n-gram/moment equivalence theorem connects to Choshen et al. (2022), who empirically observed that language models initially mirror n-gram behavior, and to the Frequency Principle (Xu et al., 2019), which links learning order to spectral properties rather than distributional moments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the most direct empirical evidence to date for the Distributional Simplicity Bias across diverse architectures (ConvNeXt V2, Swin Transformer V2, RegNet-Y) and datasets, and the n-gram/moment equivalence theorem offers a clean theoretical bridge between continuous and discrete learning dynamics. The 'double descent' phenomenon in language models—where loss on n-gram data first follows a U-shape then recovers via in-context learning—is a genuinely novel observation with implications for understanding how LMs transition from memorizing surface statistics to developing compositional capabilities. Key limitations include the inability to reliably match third-order statistics (coskewness tensor has ~2.9×10^10 entries for CIFAR-10), the O(d⁴)–O(d⁶) scaling that restricts experiments to 64×64 images, and the open question of whether skip-gram statistics (excluded for tractability) alter the learning-order picture.
