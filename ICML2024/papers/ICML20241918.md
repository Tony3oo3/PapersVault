## Problem

Körner's graph entropy, a foundational concept in information theory and combinatorics that directly encodes graph structure through the vertex-packing polytope, has never been applied to graph representation learning. Existing unsupervised graph learning principles (InfoMax, Lovász) and alternative entropy measures (Shannon, Rényi) operate on probability distributions without explicitly leveraging the combinatorial structure of independent sets, potentially missing critical topological information. The central challenge is that exact graph entropy computation is NP-hard, requiring a tractable approximation that still preserves structural information.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of graphs specified by adjacency matrices A and node feature matrices X, processed by two separate GNNs (a 5-layer GIN with hidden size 128 in experiments) to produce a graph-level vector g ∈ R^d and node-level vectors Z ∈ R^{n×d}. A Boltzmann distribution P is derived from the squared distances between each node representation and the graph representation, linking the learned representations to the graph entropy objective. The resulting representations are used directly for downstream graph classification via SVM (unsupervised) or ResGCN (semi-supervised).

## Method

GeMax maximizes Körner's graph entropy H_k(G, P) as a self-supervised learning objective, where P is defined from GNN-produced representations. To circumvent the NP-hard vertex-packing polytope computation, the paper introduces VP_Sub(G), a subset of VP(G) whose membership is characterized by Lovász's orthonormal representations via Theorem 3.3: D_a(ZZ^T)D_a = D_a² if and only if a ∈ VP_Sub(G). The constrained optimization is relaxed into a regularized objective with two penalty terms—an orthonormality loss enforcing non-adjacent node representations to be orthogonal, and a sub-vertex-packing polytope loss enforcing the structural constraint—optimized via alternating updates between GNN parameters (θ, φ) and vertex-packing variables (a) using projected gradient descent. This yields informative node-level and graph-level representations that outperform InfoMax, Lovász, Shannon, and Rényi entropy objectives across eight TU-datasets in both unsupervised and semi-supervised settings.

## Results

On unsupervised graph classification across 8 TU-datasets, GeMax consistently outperforms both InfoMax and Lovász principles across all 7 tested architectures (InfoGraph, GraphCL, AD-GCL, JOAOv2, AutoGCL, GraphACL, GCS). Headline results include 92.44% on MUTAG (InfoGraph+GeMax vs. 89.01% InfoMax), 92.51% on REDDIT-B (GCS+GeMax vs. 90.98% InfoMax), and 77.60% on PROTEINS (GraphCL+GeMax vs. 74.39% InfoMax). In the entropy comparison (Table 4), GeMax surpasses Shannon entropy by 3–8% and Rényi entropy by 3–7% across datasets, confirming that graph entropy captures structural information that distribution-only entropies miss.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GeMax outperforms the InfoMax principle (InfoGraph et al.) in the majority of dataset-architecture combinations, with typical gains of 1–4% (e.g., 92.44% vs. 89.01% on MUTAG with InfoGraph; 92.51% vs. 90.98% on REDDIT-B with GCS). Against the Lovász principle (Sun et al., 2023), GeMax also wins in most cases, though the margin is smaller (1–3%), with a few exceptions where Lovász edges ahead (e.g., NCI1 with AutoGCL: 82.63% vs. 81.11%; COLLAB with GCS: 76.62% vs. 76.15%). In the entropy comparison (Table 4), GeMax substantially outperforms Shannon entropy maximization by 3–8% and Rényi entropy by 3–7% across all datasets, demonstrating that graph entropy's structural sensitivity (via the vertex-packing polytope) is critical for discriminative representations. In semi-supervised settings (Table 3), GeMax maintains its advantage, achieving the best accuracy on 5 of 6 datasets with the strongest architectures (e.g., 91.84% on REDDIT-B with GraphCL, 79.61% on DD with AutoGCL). Overall, GeMax is the dominant principle in 14 of 21 dataset-architecture cells in the unsupervised setting, confirming its broad superiority over both information-theoretic (InfoMax) and combinatorial (Lovász) alternatives.

## Relation to Prior Work

GeMax introduces Körner's graph entropy (1973)—a concept from information theory and combinatorics that had never been applied to graph learning—as a new objective for unsupervised graph representation learning. It departs from the dominant InfoMax paradigm (Linsker 1988; Sun et al. 2019) and the more recent Lovász principle (Sun et al. 2023) by directly encoding graph topology through the vertex-packing polytope, rather than relying on mutual information between node and graph representations. The key technical bridge is Lovász's orthonormal representations (1979), which transform the NP-hard exact computation into a tractable regularized optimization, and the method is instantiated within standard GNN architectures (GIN-based) shared with InfoGraph and its successors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates that a 50-year-old information-theoretic concept can serve as a principled, structure-aware alternative to the InfoMax objective, consistently outperforming both InfoMax and Lovász principles across seven architectures and eight TU-datasets. However, the approximation gap is non-trivial: exact graph entropy computation on small graphs (≤20 nodes) yields substantially better results than the orthonormal-representation approximation (e.g., 94.36% vs. 90.04% on MUTAG), raising open questions about how much structural information is lost at scale. The method is also limited to small-to-medium graphs on TU-benchmarks; scalability to large real-world graphs and applicability to tasks beyond classification (e.g., link prediction, graph generation) remain unexplored.
