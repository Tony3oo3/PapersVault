## Problem

Training generative models on their own synthetic outputs in a self-consuming loop leads to model collapse, where the model progressively loses diversity and fails to follow prompts or physical constraints. Prior work showed that collapse can be delayed by limiting the synthetic-to-real data ratio, but this is fragile and the ratio of AI-generated content in the wild is often unknown. The paper asks whether a principled correction operation can stabilize self-consuming training regardless of the synthetic data proportion.

## Input / Output

For the primary human motion synthesis task, the model takes a free-form text prompt (e.g., 'side-skips from left to right repeatedly') and outputs a sequence of 3D skeleton poses in SMPL parameterization. The self-correction function then takes these synthetic pose sequences and maps them through a physics simulator (UHC in MuJoCo) to produce physically plausible corrected sequences. For the MNIST toy experiment, the input is a class label and the output is a 28×28 grayscale digit image, corrected by projecting toward the nearest K-means centroid for that digit.

## Method

The paper introduces a self-correction function π_γ that, in the idealized theoretical setting, blends the current model's distribution with the optimal model's distribution: π_γ p_θ(x) = (p_θ(x) + γ p_θ*(x))/(1+γ), where γ controls correction strength. The training procedure (Algorithm 1) iteratively fine-tunes each generation on the ground-truth dataset plus synthetic samples passed through π_γ, rather than on raw synthetic samples. The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.3) proves that the contraction factor of the iterative update is scaled by 1/(1+γ), making the loop exponentially more stable than the uncorrected case, and that the allowable augmentation percentage λ can be up to twice as large. In practice, the idealized corrector is approximated by domain-specific operations: a physics-based imitation policy (UHC) for human motion that enforces physical plausibility while preserving semantic content, and K-means centroid projection for MNIST images.

## Results

On the human motion synthesis task (HumanML3D/MoVi), the paper evaluates FID, Diversity, and Matching Score over 50 generations of iterative fine-tuning. With self-correction via a physics simulator (UHC), the model avoids collapse even at a 100% synthetic-to-real data ratio (n=64), whereas without self-correction the FID diverges and Matching Score flattens, indicating model collapse. On the larger dataset (n=2794, 25% augmentation), self-correction yields lower and more stable FID scores across generations compared to iterative fine-tuning without correction. On MNIST (class-conditional diffusion, 150% augmentation), self-correction via K-means centroid projection (K=16) produces legible digits after 50 self-consuming iterations, while the uncorrected model generates increasingly illegible outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three training conditions over 50 generations: (A) Baseline — continued training on ground-truth data only; (B) Iterative fine-tuning — fine-tuning on real + uncorrected synthetic data; and (C) Iterative fine-tuning with self-correction — fine-tuning on real + physics-simulator-corrected synthetic data. Condition C consistently outperforms B on FID (lower and more stable), Diversity, and Matching Score across all dataset sizes and augmentation percentages tested. At the most challenging setting (n=64, 100% synthetic augmentation), condition B exhibits clear model collapse (diverging FID, flat Matching Score) while condition C remains stable and competitive with the baseline (A). The theoretical result (Theorem 4.3) predicts that any correction strength γ>0 yields an exponentially tighter stability bound than γ=0, and the empirical results confirm this: even modest correction strength improves training dynamics. The paper also provides empirical evidence for Conjecture 4.7, showing that self-correction allows stable training even when the initial model is sub-optimal (small dataset) and the synthetic augmentation percentage is large (up to 100%), relaxing the assumptions required by the main theorem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the stability analysis of Bertrand et al. (2024) on iterative retraining of generative models on their own data by introducing a correction function π_γ that maps synthetic samples toward the target distribution, showing that the stability bound improves by a factor of 1/(1+γ) per iteration. It also builds on the broader self-consuming loop literature (Alemohammad et al., 2024; Shumailov et al., 2023) which established that uncorrected synthetic data leads to model collapse, and on the synthetic-data-for-representation-learning line (Azizi et al., 2023; Tian et al., 2023) which showed synthetic data works well for discriminative tasks but not generative ones. The practical correction mechanisms draw from physics-based motion control (UHC, MuJoCo) and simple clustering (K-means for MNIST), connecting the theoretical framework to existing tooling in robotics and computer vision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI-generated content proliferates on the internet, self-consuming training loops become an unavoidable reality that practitioners cannot simply avoid by curating data; this paper provides both a theoretical guarantee (exponential stability improvement) and a practical recipe (physics simulator as corrector) for handling this scenario. The key limitation is that the framework requires a high-quality correction function, which may not exist for all modalities (e.g., open-ended language generation), and the theoretical assumptions (small λ, θ₀ close to θ⋆) are only partially relaxed by Conjecture 4.7. Open questions include whether correction functions can be learned end-to-end, whether the approach transfers to language models or text-to-image at scale, and whether self-consuming training with correction can ultimately produce models that surpass those trained purely on human data.
