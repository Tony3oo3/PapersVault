## Problem

Only 0.23% of UniProt sequences are well-studied, yet efficient enzymes for novel reactions likely exist among the hundreds of millions of uncharacterized sequences. No standard computational method exists for virtual enzyme screening, and existing EC-number prediction approaches are limited by their coarse, expert-defined classification scheme (top F1 of 0.5–0.6) and inability to handle reactions that fall outside predefined EC classes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241453/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an atom-mapped chemical reaction (substrates and products with explicit atom correspondence) as the query, and a screening set of 260,197 protein sequences (filtered to <650 residues with available AlphaFold structures) as the candidate pool. It outputs a ranked list of all screening-set enzymes ordered by predicted catalytic activity for the query reaction, scored via cosine similarity between learned reaction and protein embeddings. Enzyme embeddings are precomputable, making large-scale screening for a new query reaction computationally feasible.

## Method

CLIPZyme uses a symmetric CLIP-style contrastive objective to align reaction and enzyme embeddings, with in-batch negatives. The reaction encoder is the key novelty: two DMPNNs first encode reactant and product molecular graphs separately, then a pseudo-transition-state graph is constructed by summing the learned bond features for edges connecting the same atom pairs across reactants and products (zero for absent edges), and a second DMPNN encodes this graph to produce the reaction embedding. The protein encoder initializes node features from frozen ESM-2 residue embeddings and processes the AlphaFold Cα structure as a 3D graph (kNN edges at 10 Å) using an E(n)-equivariant GNN (EGNN) with sinusoidal distance encoding. This design captures the geometric complementarity between enzyme active sites and transition states without requiring explicit transition-state coordinates, and outperforms alternative reaction encodings (CGR, reaction SMILES, WLDN) and protein encoders (GVP, GearNet, CDConv, MSA-Transformer+EGNN).

## Results

On the EnzymeMap test set (1,554 unseen reaction rules, 260,197-enzyme screening set), CLIPZyme achieves a BEDROC₈₅ of 44.69% and EF₀.₀₅ of 14.09 without any EC annotation. Combining CLIPZyme with the CLEAN EC predictor yields substantial gains: BEDROC₈₅ rises to 57.03% (level 1), 75.57% (level 2), and 82.69% (level 3), compared to 0.96%, 4.86%, and 25.86% for CLEAN alone. On the challenging terpene synthase dataset, CLIPZyme reaches BEDROC₈₅ of 72.46%, and on unannotated EnzymeMap reactions it maintains 42.94%, confirming generalization to both novel reactions and novel protein clusters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLEAN (Yu et al., 2023b), the state-of-the-art EC predictor, CLIPZyme alone (BEDROC₈₅ = 44.69%) substantially outperforms CLEAN at every EC specificity level below 4: 44.69% vs. 0.96% (level 1), 4.86% (level 2), and 25.86% (level 3). At full EC specification (level 4), CLEAN slightly edges out CLIPZyme alone (89.74% vs. 44.69%), but the combined CLIPZyme+CLEAN pipeline still matches or exceeds CLEAN on all metrics (e.g., BEDROC₂₀ 95.24% vs. 93.42%). Against UniKP (Yu et al., 2023a), a turnover-rate prediction model repurposed for screening, CLIPZyme dominates by a wide margin: 44.69% vs. 2.72% BEDROC₈₅ and 14.09 vs. 1.51 EF₀.₀₅, reflecting that UniKP was designed for single substrate–enzyme pairs rather than full reaction–enzyme retrieval. The paper also shows that CLIPZyme's pseudo-transition-state reaction encoding outperforms alternative encoders (CGR, reaction SMILES, WLDN) by 5–15 BEDROC₈₅ points, and that structure-aware protein encoding (ESM-2 + EGNN) beats sequence-only ESM-2 by ~7.8 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLIPZyme adapts the CLIP-style contrastive learning paradigm (Radford et al., 2021) from image-text alignment to the enzyme-reaction domain, where the two modalities are a chemical reaction and a protein structure. It deliberately departs from the dominant EC-prediction paradigm (CLEAN, Yu et al. 2023b) by operating directly on atom-mapped molecular graphs and AlphaFold structures rather than relying on expert-defined enzyme classes, which the authors argue are too coarse and incomplete for novel reactions. The reaction encoder introduces a pseudo-transition-state graph constructed by summing bond embeddings from reactant and product DMPNNs, a design motivated by transition-state stabilization theory that distinguishes it from prior reaction encodings (CGR, reaction SMILES, WLDN). For protein encoding, it combines frozen ESM-2 residue embeddings as node features with an EGNN over AlphaFold Cα coordinates, leveraging both evolutionary sequence information and 3D structural geometry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With only 0.23% of UniProt well-characterized, the ability to computationally rank hundreds of thousands of unannotated enzyme sequences for a target reaction could unlock biosynthesis applications currently inaccessible to experimental screening. The paper's key practical contribution is demonstrating that CLIPZyme works without any EC annotation of the query reaction, whereas EC-based methods require at least partial expert classification. However, the approach does not model physical reactant-enzyme interactions, covers a limited chemical space (EC class 7 is entirely absent), and relies on random negative sampling that may introduce false negatives due to enzyme promiscuity. Future directions include 3D docking-based interaction modeling and quantum-chemical transition state sampling to address these gaps.
