## Problem

Nested sampling is a widely used algorithm for Bayesian posterior inference and evidence estimation, but existing implementations scale poorly with dimensionality: region samplers have exponential cost, and step samplers require the number of live points to grow linearly with dimension. Gradient-guided variants (Hamiltonian slice sampling / Galilean Monte Carlo) have been shown to be 'substantially less efficient and less reliable' than other gradient-based approaches in public implementations, and prior gradient-guided nested sampling work is typically bespoke to specific physics problems rather than general-purpose.

## Input / Output

The method requires a differentiable likelihood function (evaluated via a differentiable programming framework such as PyTorch or JAX) and a prior distribution over a continuous parameter space of arbitrary dimensionality. It produces importance-weighted posterior samples and a point estimate of the log-evidence (Bayesian marginal likelihood). The likelihood must be smooth enough for gradient computation; for non-differentiable likelihoods, finite differencing or subgradient methods would be needed at additional cost. The algorithm is designed to work out-of-the-box with minimal hyperparameter tuning (default n_live=200, tol=0.01, min_ref=1, max_ref=3, delta_p=0.05).

## Method

GGNS replaces the live-point generation step of nested sampling with self-tuning Hamiltonian Slice Sampling (HSS) where gradients are computed automatically via differentiable programming. Particles move in straight lines and reflect off the hard likelihood boundary using the gradient direction; an adaptive time step (adjusted by monitoring reflection count) balances efficiency and accuracy, while a trajectory preservation mechanism stores all points after a minimum number of reflections and samples uniformly from them for decorrelation. A pruning mechanism resets particles that have drifted outside the slice, and dynamic nested sampling kills half the live points each iteration, evolving all new points in parallel via HSS. Cluster identification (KNN-based) tracks mode volumes to spawn points proportionally, preventing mode collapse. The key scaling insight is that each gradient reflection provides information about the full d-dimensional space, so only O(1) reflections are needed for decorrelation (vs. O(d) for standard slice sampling), yielding linear rather than quadratic scaling of likelihood evaluations with dimensionality. Ablation studies confirm that the combination of all components is necessary for unbiased evidence estimation at high dimensions.

## Results

On the three synthetic benchmark tasks (Gaussian mixture, Neal's funnel, 32-dim Many Wells), GGNS achieves log-evidence biases of 0.029±0.132, -0.051±0.353, and 0.10±0.60 respectively, obtaining unbiased estimates in all cases. In the scaling experiment (Fig. 1), GGNS achieves linear scaling in dimensionality (n_like ∝ n_live × d) at constant n_live=200, compared to quadratic scaling for PolyChord (∝ 5d²) and exponential-to-quadratic for dynesty. On the 256-dim gravitational lensing image reconstruction task, GGNS achieves a Wasserstein distance of 1.53 to the analytic posterior, outperforming HMC (hamiltorch) at 1.91, while requiring no hyperparameter tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GGNS outperforms SMC and PIS decisively on all three synthetic tasks: SMC shows severe bias (e.g., -0.569 on Gaussian mixture, 14.99 on Many Wells) and PIS is biased on Gaussian mixture (1.274) and Many Wells (2.69), while GGNS remains unbiased across all tasks. Against FAB, the closest competitor, both methods achieve near-zero bias, but GGNS has larger standard deviations (e.g., 0.132 vs 0.0012 on Gaussian mixture); the authors note this is inherent to nested sampling's sublinear error reduction with n_live. In the dimensionality scaling experiment, GGNS achieves linear scaling in d at constant n_live=200, while PolyChord scales quadratically (∝5d²) and dynesty scales exponentially in low dimensions before switching to quadratic; dynesty also produces biased evidence estimates in slice-sampling mode. On the 256-dim image reconstruction task, GGNS beats HMC (hamiltorch) on Wasserstein distance (1.53 vs 1.91) and works out-of-the-box without the hyperparameter sweep that HMC required. On the 165-dim Lennard-Jones 55-particle problem, GGNS produces high-quality energy histograms while DDS and PIS diverge entirely and FAB cannot reach sufficiently low energies.

## Relation to Prior Work

GGNS sits at the intersection of nested sampling (Skilling, 2006), Hamiltonian slice sampling / Galilean Monte Carlo (Neal, 2003; Feroz & Skilling, 2013), and dynamic nested sampling (Higson et al., 2019; Speagle, 2020), but its key departure is repositioning HSS from a previously dismissed 'substantially less efficient' method into the best-scaling live-point generator by combining adaptive step control, trajectory preservation with noise perturbation, pruning, and gradient computation via differentiable programming. The clustering-based mode collapse mitigation is inherited from PolyChord (Handley et al., 2015b), and the GFlowNet combination extends the continuous GFlowNet framework of Lahlou et al. (2023) by using nested sampling outputs as off-policy training data. The novelty is primarily in the specific combination and engineering of these components rather than in any single new algorithmic primitive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstrated linear (rather than quadratic or exponential) scaling of likelihood evaluations with dimensionality, achieved with a constant number of live points, opens the door to applying nested sampling to problems (e.g., 165-dimensional Lennard-Jones, 256-pixel image inference) that were previously intractable for this class of algorithms. The GFlowNet combination suggests a practical path to amortized posterior sampling where a single nested sampling run can seed a generative model for rapid repeated inference. Key limitations remain: the method requires differentiable likelihoods, trajectory perturbation breaks detailed balance under non-uniform priors, the fixed mass matrix is suboptimal, and performance in extremely high-dimensional spaces (thousands of dimensions) is untested. Whether the linear scaling advantage persists as problem complexity (KL divergence) grows beyond the linear-in-dimensionality regime tested here is an open question.
