## Problem

Quality-Diversity (QD) algorithms require maintaining large archives of full neural network policies and large offspring populations simultaneously, incurring substantial GPU memory and RAM overhead during training. While sample efficiency has been extensively studied, resource efficiency during the training phase has been largely overlooked, preventing QD from scaling to larger problems that demand bigger networks. This paper is the first to highlight and address this resource-efficiency gap in QD training.

## Input / Output

The method consumes either vector-based sensor states (QDax locomotion and pathfinding tasks) or 84×84 grayscale game screen images (Atari Pong and Boxing) as input, and produces a set of diverse policies structured as a Deep Decision Archive: a single shared representation network paired with K small decision-part networks per archive cell. The behavior descriptor (e.g., foot-contact frequency for locomotion, final position for mazes, movement/punch frequency for Atari) indexes the archive cells, and the final output is the shared representation part plus the first level of the DDA containing one decision part per cell.

## Method

RefQD decomposes each policy network into a large representation part (front layers with most parameters) and a small decision part (last layer), then shares a single representation part across all decision parts in the archive and offspring population, reducing GPU memory to 16% (QDax) and 3.7% (Atari) of the baseline. To address the resulting mismatch issue—where archived decision parts no longer reproduce their recorded behaviors when combined with the updated representation—RefQD employs four strategies: periodic re-evaluation of the archive, a Deep Decision Archive (DDA) that stores K=4 decision parts per cell to preserve learned knowledge, top-k re-evaluation to limit re-evaluation cost to only the best levels, and a decaying learning rate for the representation part to stabilize training. The method is a general framework instantiated here with PGA-ME's uniform parent selection and IsoLineDD/policy-gradient variation operators, and it achieves comparable or superior QD-Score, Coverage, and Max Fitness under the same wall-clock time and sample budget.

## Results

On the QDax suite (8 locomotion and path-finding tasks), RefQD uses only 14.0–16.5% GPU memory and 3.2–13.7% RAM compared to PGA-ME, while achieving 64.9%–107.1% of PGA-ME's QD-Score. On Atari with CNN policies, RefQD reduces GPU memory to 3.7% and RAM to 0.3–0.6% of DQN-ME, yet achieves 248.6% QD-Score on Pong and 119.6% on Boxing. With ResNet policies on Atari, where DQN-ME runs out of memory entirely, RefQD still achieves 220.1% and 246.2% QD-Score relative to the memory-constrained DQN-ME(s) baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is PGA-ME (or DQN-ME for discrete-action Atari tasks), which uses unlimited GPU and RAM. RefQD matches or exceeds PGA-ME's QD-Score on 3 of 8 QDax tasks (HalfCheetah Uni at 104.6%, Humanoid Uni at 101.6%, Ant Trap at 107.1%) while using only ~15% GPU and ~9% RAM. The memory-constrained variant PGA-ME(s) (population size 8, same GPU budget as RefQD but still storing full networks in the archive) performs substantially worse, achieving only 31.4%–88.7% QD-Score, confirming that the archive storage bottleneck—not just GPU compute—limits standard QD under resource constraints. On Atari with CNN policies, RefQD dramatically outperforms DQN-ME in QD-Score (248.6% on Pong, 119.6% on Boxing) while using only 3.7% GPU and 0.3–0.6% RAM. With ResNet policies, DQN-ME fails entirely due to out-of-memory, and RefQD outperforms the reduced DQN-ME(s) by 220–246% in QD-Score, demonstrating that RefQD enables QD on problem scales previously infeasible.

## Relation to Prior Work

RefQD builds on the MAP-Elites QD framework and the sample-efficient PGA-ME algorithm, but shifts the optimization focus from sample efficiency to resource efficiency during training. The core decomposition of networks into a shared representation part and diverse decision parts is adapted from cooperative coevolution work (Xue et al., 2024), while the multi-level archive (Deep Decision Archive) draws on the deep-grid concept from noisy QD domains (Flageat & Cully, 2020). The key departure is treating GPU/RAM overhead during training as a first-class design constraint rather than an afterthought, which prior QD literature has largely ignored in favor of reducing sample counts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As QD algorithms are applied to increasingly complex domains (embodied robotics, LLMs, large-scale environment generation), the resource bottleneck during training becomes a practical barrier that sample-efficiency improvements alone cannot solve. RefQD's 3.7–16% GPU memory usage while matching or exceeding baseline QD-Scores makes previously infeasible QD runs tractable on standard hardware. The main limitation is the absence of theoretical guarantees for the mismatch-resolution strategies (DDA, periodic re-evaluation, learning rate decay), which remain heuristic. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-neural solution representations and how it interacts with archive distillation at scale.
