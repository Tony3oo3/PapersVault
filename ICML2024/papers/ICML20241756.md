## Problem

Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) solving partial integro-differential equations must evaluate parametrized integrals within their training residuals, but naive Monte Carlo estimation of these integrals introduces an excess variance term into the loss that systematically biases learned solutions toward overly smooth functions. This paper formally identifies this bias (showing the approximated loss equals the true loss plus a parameter-dependent variance term) and asks how to train effectively when only a single integral sample per residual is available.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241756/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The network takes spatial coordinates x (and time t for time-dependent PDEs such as Smoluchowski coagulation) and outputs the PDE solution—electric potential U, magnetic vector potential A, or particle density n. Unusually, computing a single training residual at point x requires N forward passes of the network at independently sampled points x' ~ P(x'|x), making the loss itself a stochastic functional of the network parameters. The paper demonstrates that N=1 suffices with the proposed delayed target approach, whereas standard training requires N=100 for comparable solution accuracy.

## Method

The core contribution is a delayed target method that replaces the self-referential integral E_{P(x'|x)}[g_θ(x')] in the loss with a detached target network evaluation g_{θ_Target}(x'), analogous to temporal-difference learning in reinforcement learning. Two stabilization mechanisms are introduced: Polyak averaging (θ_Target ← τθ_Target + (1−τ)θ) to slow target movement, and a regularization term λ(f_θ(x) − f_{θ_Target}(x))² to prevent drift between main and target networks when target variance is high. The paper also analyzes deterministic sampling (evenly-spaced or quadrature points) and a double-sampling trick as alternatives, showing the delayed target method outperforms both at N=1. Under linear function approximation, the method is proven to be a stochastic approximation converging to the fixed point of the ΠU composite operator, with an approximation error upper-bounded by (1−σ_{P|Λ})^{-1} times the projection error and computational cost O(d/ε) versus O(Nd log(1/ε)) for standard training.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that the delayed target method with N=1 integral sample achieves solution quality comparable to (and in some cases slightly better than) standard MSE training with N=100 samples, across all three problem classes. In the 2D Poisson problem, standard N=1 training produces an overly smooth solution with the training loss floored at approximately 0.3 (matching the integration variance), while delayed target N=1 recovers the correct solution structure. In the 10-dimensional Poisson problem and the 4D Smoluchowski coagulation problem, delayed target N=1 maintains comparable or slightly better normalized MSE than standard N=100, while Gaussian and Leja quadrature under a Smolyak sparse grid degrade in higher dimensions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the delayed target method against four baseline strategies for handling integral terms in PINN losses. (1) Standard MSE with N=1: produces biased, overly smooth solutions because the excess variance term in the loss (Eq. 26) penalizes high-variance regions; the training loss is lower-bounded by the integration variance (~0.3 in the 2D Poisson problem). (2) Standard MSE with N=100: serves as the quality reference; delayed target N=1 matches or slightly exceeds this across all three problem classes (Poisson, Maxwell, Smoluchowski). (3) Deterministic sampling: with N=1 it yields the same overly smooth bias as standard N=1; with N=100 it improves but still lags behind delayed target N=1 in the Poisson problem. (4) Double-sampling trick: with N=1 it diverges in training (particularly for the singular Poisson problem); with N=100 it stabilizes and improves but remains inferior to delayed target N=1. Additionally, Gaussian and Leja quadrature under Smolyak sparse grids are less effective than QMC in higher dimensions, and both are outperformed by delayed target N=1 in the 10D Poisson benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN) framework (Raissi et al., 2019) to handle partial integro-differential equations, where the PDE residual contains a parametrized integral that must be estimated during training. The key conceptual move is importing the delayed target / bootstrapping mechanism from temporal-difference learning in reinforcement learning (Sutton, 1984; Mnih et al., 2015; Fujimoto et al., 2018) into the PDE-solving context, using a slowly-updated target network (via Polyak averaging) to detach the integral estimate from the main network's gradient. The paper also positions its three proposed solutions—deterministic sampling, the double-sampling trick, and the delayed target method—against classical integral estimation techniques (numerical quadrature, QMC, importance sampling), arguing that reducing gradient bias is more effective than reducing integral estimation variance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification of excess-variance-induced bias in parametrized integral losses (Eq. 26) is a clean theoretical contribution that explains why naive Monte Carlo PINN training systematically produces overly smooth solutions, a failure mode practitioners have observed but not formally characterized. The practical result that a single-sample delayed target matches 100-sample standard training across Poisson, Maxwell, and Smoluchowski problems (up to 10 dimensions) offers a significant computational saving for integral PDEs common in quantum physics, aerosol modeling, and ecology. Key limitations include sensitivity to activation function choice (ReLU breaks the method entirely), potential non-convergence under non-linear function approximation, and the narrow scope to PINNs. Open questions include combining the delayed target with importance sampling or QMC, adaptive hyperparameter selection, and whether the bootstrapping idea transfers to other ML settings with stochastic targets.
