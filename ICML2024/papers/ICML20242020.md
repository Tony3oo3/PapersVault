## Problem

Brenier's polar factorization theorem (1991) guarantees that any vector field F can be decomposed as ∇u ∘ M where u is convex and M is measure-preserving, but no practical neural implementation existed for higher-dimensional settings. The paper addresses this gap and further tackles the ill-posed problem of inverting the generally non-injective map M, which is essential for sampling critical points of non-convex energy landscapes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242020/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes i.i.d. samples x_i ~ ρ paired with their vector field evaluations F(x_i) (e.g., gradients of a loss function or finite-difference gradients of elevation data), and produces three components: a convex potential u_θ (ICNN), a measure-preserving map M (either implicit via a conjugate solver or explicit via a separate network M_ξ), and a stochastic generative inverse I_ψ that, given a target y, samples x such that M(x) ≈ y. The inverse map is multivalued and parameterized as an SDE, so multiple distinct pre-images can be generated by injecting different Gaussian noise sequences.

## Method

The convex potential u_θ is parameterized by a modified ICNN that injects quadratic positive-definite forms (diagonal + low-rank) at every layer, ensuring strong convexity while keeping parameter count manageable. Training alternates between minimizing the Makkuva et al. dual OT objective (pushing ρ onto F_#ρ) and training an auxiliary network V_φ to amortize the convex conjugate ∇u*_θ, with a conjugate solver (Adam) used to compute ground-truth conjugate values for the regression loss. The measure-preserving map M is either evaluated pointwise as CS(u_θ, F(x)) or learned explicitly as a network M_ξ regressing x_i → CS(u_θ, F(x_i)). The stochastic inverse I_ψ is trained via augmented bridge matching: a drift network X_ψ is learned to match the coupling (M_θ, Id)_#ρ, and at inference time the resulting SDE is integrated from X_0 = y to generate samples from the posterior π_θ(x|y). For non-convex optimization, an LMC-NPF algorithm alternates Langevin steps on the original function g with Langevin steps on the convex u_θ, using M_θ and I_ψ to transition between the two spaces, enabling global jumps between basins of attraction.

## Results

In the ICNN benchmark with a known Gaussian-to-Gaussian OT map, the proposed low-rank + diagonal quadratic ICNN achieves an L2 unexplained variance of 0.031% (d=32) and 0.082% (d=128), substantially outperforming the Linear baseline (0.72% and 1.26%) and FQuad (0.38% and 0.97%). On the multimodal Gaussian mixture target, the proposed architecture reduces the Sinkhorn divergence to 234±42 (d=32) and 104×1e1±25×1e1 (d=128), versus 548±75 and 246×1e1±42×1e1 for Linear. On the Chamonix topography experiment, the Sinkhorn divergence between the predicted and target distributions (0.36) is comparable to the batch-to-batch noise floor (0.31), and the measure-preserving map achieves a divergence of 0.0022 versus a noise floor of 0.0015. On the MNIST LeNet classifier (d=222), the inverse map I_ψ achieves a cosine similarity of 0.81±0.20 between generated and target gradients, and LMC-NPF discovers new high-accuracy minima not reachable by standard LMC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary quantitative comparison is the ICNN benchmark (Appendix D), where the proposed low-rank + diagonal quadratic ICNN is compared against the Linear architecture from Amos (2023) and a FQuad variant that adds a full quadratic layer. On the Gaussian-to-Gaussian task with a known closed-form OT map, the proposed architecture reduces the L2 unexplained variance by roughly 23× at d=32 (0.031% vs. 0.72%) and 15× at d=128 (0.082% vs. 1.26%) relative to Linear, and by 12× and 12× relative to FQuad. On the harder multimodal Gaussian mixture target (Table 5), the Sinkhorn divergence is also lowest for the proposed architecture (234 vs. 548 for Linear at d=32; 104×1e1 vs. 246×1e1 for Linear at d=128), confirming the advantage extends beyond unimodal targets. The proposed architecture also uses fewer parameters (15,714 vs. 20,577 at d=32; 247,170 vs. 328,065 at d=128), making it both more accurate and more parameter-efficient. No direct head-to-head comparison against other polar factorization methods (e.g., Benamou–Brenier 1994, Gallouet–Mérigot 2018) is provided, as those are gridded/Lagrangian approaches not directly comparable in the neural sampling setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides the first neural implementation of Brenier's polar factorization theorem (1991) applicable to high-dimensional settings, building directly on the ICNN-based neural optimal transport solvers of Amos et al. (2017), Makkuva et al. (2020), and Amos (2023). Prior numerical implementations (Benamou & Brenier 1994; Gallouet & Mérigot 2018) were restricted to low-dimensional gridded or Lagrangian formulations for fluid dynamics, while Morel et al. (2023) took a different route by gradually refactoring normalizing flows into Monge maps without neural OT solvers. The key methodological departure is the combination of an improved ICNN architecture (low-rank + diagonal quadratic PSD layers) with augmented bridge matching (De Bortoli et al., 2023) to learn a stochastic inverse of the measure-preserving map, enabling applications to non-convex optimization landscapes that were previously inaccessible to polar factorization methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper bridges a significant gap between a foundational result in analysis and practical machine learning, demonstrating that Brenier's polar factorization can be computed from samples in dimensions up to 222. The LMC-NPF sampling algorithm (Algorithm 2) offers a principled mechanism for global exploration of non-convex loss landscapes by alternating between local Langevin steps and NPF-mediated global jumps, with empirical evidence that it discovers minima unreachable by standard LMC. However, the approach is computationally expensive (requiring a conjugate solver at each evaluation), the MNIST experiments remain small-scale, and the authors acknowledge that reweighting generated samples (à la SMC) is not yet possible with the current methodology. Open questions include whether the method scales to truly high-dimensional problems (e.g., large language model training) and whether the learned factorization remains faithful in regions far from the sampled trajectories.
