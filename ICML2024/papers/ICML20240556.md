## Problem

Zero-shot reinforcement learning asks whether a single agent pre-trained on unlabeled offline trajectories can immediately solve arbitrary new tasks without further training. Prior approaches either restrict rewards to linear combinations of pre-defined features (successor features, forward-backward methods) or to goal-reaching, and none can consistently handle diverse reward families (goal-reaching, directional movement, structured paths, arbitrary functions) from a single unlabeled dataset in a truly zero-shot manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240556/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At pre-training, the method consumes a single unlabeled offline trajectory dataset (state-action sequences with no reward or goal annotations) and a user-defined prior distribution over random reward functions. At test time, a new task is specified solely as a black-box reward function η: S → R, and the method requires only 32 (state, reward) pairs sampled from the offline dataset to produce a latent task encoding z. This z conditions the pre-trained policy π(a|s,z) to output actions that maximize the new reward, with no additional training. Notably, this is far fewer samples than the 5120 pairs required by forward-backward and successor-feature baselines.

## Method

FRE learns a probabilistic encoder that maps a set of K=32 (state, reward) pairs to a latent vector z via a permutation-invariant transformer (no positional encodings or causal masking, treating inputs as an unordered set), followed by two linear projections parameterizing a Gaussian. The encoder is trained with an information-bottleneck objective: maximize the log-likelihood of predicting rewards on held-out decoder states given z, while minimizing KL divergence from a unit Gaussian prior, jointly with a feedforward decoder that predicts rewards on different states. For policy learning, the encoder is first trained to convergence and then frozen, after which IQL (Implicit Q-Learning) trains z-conditioned Q, V, and policy networks using rewards from a mixture prior of random goal-reaching, sparse linear, and random 2-layer MLP functions. This avoids the linear value-function constraint of successor-feature methods and the parameterized task-family assumption of multi-task RL, enabling the policy to represent arbitrary reward functions through the learned latent space.

## Results

On the combined AntMaze, ExORL, and Kitchen benchmarks, FRE achieves an overall score of 57±9, substantially outperforming Forward-Backward (24±12), Successor Features (18±5), and OPAL-101 (33±12). On AntMaze goal-reaching, FRE scores 48.8±6 versus 0.0±0 for FB and 0.4±2 for SF, while on Kitchen it reaches 66±3 compared to 3±6 (FB) and 1±1 (SF). Notably, FRE uses only 32 (state, reward) pairs at evaluation time, whereas FB and SF require 5120 samples. FRE's main weakness is on ExORL velocity tasks (34±13 walker, 20±2 cheetah) where FB achieves 64±1 and 51±3 respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FRE decisively outperforms Forward-Backward (FB) on goal-reaching (48.8 vs 0.0 on AntMaze), directional movement (55.2 vs 4.8), and Kitchen (66 vs 3), but loses on ExORL velocity tasks (34 vs 64 walker, 20 vs 51 cheetah) where FB's linearized value function excels. Against Successor Features (SF), FRE wins on nearly all tasks except ExORL walker goals (94 vs 100), with particularly large margins on AntMaze goal-reaching (48.8 vs 0.4) and Kitchen (66 vs 1). FRE beats goal-conditioned IQL (GC-IQL) on AntMaze goal-reaching (48.8 vs 40.0) and Kitchen (66 vs 59), but GC-IQL achieves a perfect 100 on ExORL cheetah goals where FRE scores 58. Compared to OPAL-101 (which uses privileged online rollouts), FRE wins on most tasks including AntMaze goal-reaching (48.8 vs 19.4) and Kitchen (66 vs 26), though OPAL edges ahead on ant-path-edges (85 vs 60). Importantly, FRE achieves these results with only 32 reward-annotated samples at test time, versus 5120 for FB and SF.

## Relation to Prior Work

FRE departs from the successor-features (SF) and forward-backward (FB) line of work, which achieves zero-shot RL by restricting reward functions to the linear span of pre-trained state features. Instead, FRE formulates task encoding as a variational information bottleneck over (state, reward) sample pairs, using a permutation-invariant transformer encoder to map arbitrary reward functions into a shared latent space. The policy is then trained with an off-the-shelf offline RL algorithm (IQL) conditioned on this latent encoding, and the information-bottleneck objective from Alemi et al. (2016) provides the compression term that keeps the encoding minimal. This positions FRE as a generalization of goal-conditioned RL and linear-reward zero-shot RL to fully arbitrary, non-parameterized reward functions without domain-specific task representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FRE is the first method to consistently solve goal-reaching, directional, and structured-path tasks with a single unsupervised agent, using only 32 (state, reward) pairs at test time versus 5120 for SF-based methods. The main open question is the choice of reward prior: the paper uses a hand-crafted mixture of random goals, linear functions, and MLPs, which works empirically but is acknowledged as ad hoc, with no theoretical guidance on optimal priors. Extending FRE to online settings and to richer data sources (e.g., video) are identified as promising directions. The approach's simplicity—combining a standard VAE-style encoder with IQL—makes it a practical building block for generalist agent research.
