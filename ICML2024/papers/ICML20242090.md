## Problem

Prompt engineering for text-to-image diffusion models has remained largely manual due to two unsolved challenges: the enormous discrete language space makes exhaustive search infeasible, and computing text gradients requires backpropagating through dozens of diffusion inference steps and a non-differentiable embedding lookup table, incurring prohibitive memory and runtime costs. No principled, gradient-based framework existed for automatically discovering prompts that improve or adversarially disrupt diffusion model outputs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a user text prompt and a frozen Stable Diffusion v1-4 model (50 DDIM steps, guidance scale 7.5) as input, along with a spherical CLIP loss as the optimization objective. It outputs a discrete natural-language prompt — either an optimized negative prompt (comma-separated antonyms) for image quality improvement, or a semantically similar adversarial prompt for model diagnosis. The search is restricted to word-level substitutions (up to 5 synonyms or antonyms per word, generated by ChatGPT), keeping the output as a human-readable sentence rather than a soft embedding.

## Method

DPO-Diff formulates prompt optimization as discrete optimization over a compact, dynamically generated word-substitution space (Synonym Space for attacks, Antonym Space for negative prompt enhancement). The key technical contribution is the Shortcut Text Gradient: instead of backpropagating through all T diffusion steps, the computation graph is trimmed to K steps (K=1 in practice) by disabling gradients from step T to t, enabling gradients only from t to t−K, and using a closed-form x̂₀ estimate to analytically bridge the remaining gap, yielding constant memory and runtime. To handle the non-differentiable embedding lookup, discrete word choices are relaxed via Gumbel-Softmax into a learnable continuous distribution over the vocabulary. A two-stage solver combines gradient-based optimization of the Gumbel parameters (RMSprop, 20 iterations) with evolutionary search (population 20, crossover and mutation) to sample the final discrete prompt. A notable empirical finding is that optimizing negative prompts via antonyms substantially outperforms optimizing positive prompts, as the baseline negative prompt is merely an empty string, making the model more sensitive to negative-prompt perturbations.

## Results

On the prompt improvement task, DPO-Diff achieves the best Spherical CLIP loss and HPSv2 score across all three datasets (DiffusionDB, COCO, ChatGPT), e.g., reducing CLIP loss from 0.87 (user) to 0.81 and raising HPSv2 from 48.81 to 62.37 on DiffusionDB. For adversarial attack, DPO-Diff substantially degrades prompt faithfulness, increasing CLIP loss from 0.76 to 0.86 (DiffusionDB) and 0.77 to 0.95 (ChatGPT) while dropping HPSv2 by ~35 points. In human evaluation, DPO-Diff surpasses or matches Promptist 79% of the time on SD-v1 and 88% on SD-XL, and achieves a 44% adversarial attack success rate on SD-v1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary method baseline is Promptist (Hao et al., 2022), an RLHF-trained prompt generator that appends aesthetic modifiers to user prompts. DPO-Diff outperforms Promptist on every dataset and metric for prompt improvement: on DiffusionDB it reduces CLIP loss from 0.88 to 0.81 and raises HPSv2 from 54.39 to 62.37; on COCO it improves CLIP from 0.87 to 0.82 and HPSv2 from 50.08 to 61.26; on ChatGPT it improves CLIP from 0.85 to 0.78 and HPSv2 from 59.32 to 67.71. The paper attributes Promptist's weaker performance to its reliance on DiffusionDB's paired data, which predominantly contains aesthetic modifiers (e.g., 'high-resolution, 4K') that do not alter prompt semantics. DPO-Diff also beats the human-engineered 'Manual' baseline on DiffusionDB (CLIP 0.81 vs. 0.89; HPSv2 62.37 vs. 51.43). For adversarial attack, no prior method is applicable, so DPO-Diff is compared only against the unmodified user prompt, where it achieves a 44% human-verified attack success rate on SD-v1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the prompt optimization line of work from LLMs (Zhou et al., 2022; Pryzant et al., 2023) to text-to-image diffusion models, where prior efforts were limited to RLHF-based fine-tuning of a prompt generator (Promptist, Hao et al., 2022) or manual aesthetic modifiers. It departs from Promptist by being training-free and search-based, and from Textual Inversion (Gal et al., 2022) by optimizing discrete text prompts rather than soft embeddings distilled from images. The Shortcut Text Gradient builds on the closed-form x₀ estimation from DDIM (Song et al., 2020) and the Gumbel-Softmax relaxation (Jang et al., 2016), while the negative prompt mechanism is grounded in classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that optimizing negative prompts (antonyms) outperforms positive prompt optimization (synonyms) for improving image faithfulness is practically actionable and challenges the default assumption that the positive prompt is the primary lever for quality. The Shortcut Text Gradient provides a general-purpose tool for constant-memory backpropagation through diffusion inference, which could benefit other gradient-based tasks on diffusion models beyond prompt optimization. Key limitations include the method's dependence on CLIP loss (which may not fully align with human judgment), the upper bound imposed by the small text encoder in Stable Diffusion, and the search cost that makes it impractical for real-time use. Open questions include whether the negative-prompt advantage persists with stronger text encoders (e.g., SDXL with T5) and whether DPO-Diff-generated data can effectively bootstrap RLHF-based prompt agents.
