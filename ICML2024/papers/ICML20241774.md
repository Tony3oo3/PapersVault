## Problem

Training modern neural networks requires extensive learning-rate tuning, which is computationally expensive and a primary source of sensitivity in optimization performance. While momentum methods (SGD-M, Adam) are widely used, they still demand careful manual selection of the learning rate, and existing adaptive methods do not naturally combine momentum with the model-based truncation trick that exploits the known lower bound of loss functions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241774/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

At each iteration the method consumes the current loss value f(x^k, s_k), the stochastic gradient ∇f(x^k, s_k), and the current parameter vector x^k, maintaining only two additional scalars (exponentially weighted loss and inner-product averages) and one vector (the momentum gradient) beyond what SGD-M already stores. It outputs the next parameter vector x^{k+1} and an adaptive step size τ_k that is the minimum of a user-specified cap α_k and a data-driven term computed from the truncated model, so the effective step automatically shrinks when the model predicts the loss is near its lower bound.

## Method

The key insight is a new model-based interpretation of momentum: instead of modeling a single stochastic loss f(x, s_k), MoMo builds a model of the full expected loss f(x) by taking an exponentially weighted average of linearized stochastic losses f(x^j, s_j) around their past evaluation points. This averaged model is then truncated at a known lower bound (typically zero, or an online estimate for tasks like transformer training where the optimal loss is non-zero), yielding a piecewise-linear model m_k(x) = max{m_k^avg(x), f_*^k}. The next iterate is obtained by solving the proximal minimization of m_k plus a quadratic penalty, which has a closed-form solution: x^{k+1} = x^k − τ_k d_k where τ_k = min{α_k, (h_k − f_*^k)_+ / ‖d_k‖²}. This framework generalizes to any preconditioned momentum method by replacing the Euclidean norm with a D_k-weighted norm, giving MoMo-Adam when D_k is Adam's diagonal preconditioner, and naturally incorporates weight decay through a (1+α_k λ) scaling factor in the update.

## Results

The paper's primary quantitative finding is that MoMo(-Adam) consistently extends the range of 'good' learning rates by over one order of magnitude compared to SGD-M and Adam, while achieving the overall best validation accuracy on all tested tasks except DLRM and ViT (where the gap is within standard deviation). For ViT on Imagenet-1k, MoMo-Adam reaches a higher top-1 accuracy at the best base learning rate (α_base = 0.01) and operates over a wider range of base learning rates than AdamW. For the IWSLT14 German-to-English translation task, MoMo-Adam* matches or slightly exceeds fine-tuned AdamW across the full range of base learning rates for both dropout 0.1 and 0.3. Theoretically, MoMo attains an O(1/√K) convergence rate for convex problems with interpolation, requiring no knowledge of problem-specific quantities beyond the optimal value.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SGD-M, MoMo consistently wins on robustness: it extends the range of learning rates yielding good validation accuracy by over one order of magnitude, while matching or exceeding the best SGD-M accuracy on all tasks (ResNet110/CIFAR-100, ResNet20/CIFAR-10, VGG16/CIFAR-10, MLP/MNIST). Against Adam, MoMo-Adam shows the same pattern—wider stable LR range and equal or better peak accuracy—across the same image classification and recommender system tasks. Against AdamW (the more relevant baseline for large-scale tasks), MoMo-Adam achieves a higher top-1 accuracy for ViT on ImageNet-1k at the best base learning rate and operates over a broader range of α_base values; for IWSLT14 translation, MoMo-Adam* is on par or marginally better than a fine-tuned AdamW configuration across all tested base learning rates and both dropout settings. In the supplementary comparison (Figure F.6), MoMo(-Adam) performs favourably against Adabelief, Adabound, and Lion, though the authors note that MoMo versions of any preconditioned momentum method could be derived. The paper also shows that MoMo with no momentum (β=0) reduces exactly to SPS_max, confirming the theoretical connection to the stochastic Polyak step size baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads in stochastic optimization: the model-based proximal point framework (Asi & Duchi, 2019; Davis & Drusvyatskiy, 2019), heavy-ball momentum (Polyak, 1964), and the stochastic Polyak step size (Loizou et al., 2021). The key conceptual departure is that prior model-based methods build a model of a single stochastic function f(x, s_k), whereas MoMo builds a model of the full expected loss f(x) by exponentially averaging past loss values and gradients — giving momentum a new interpretation as a model of the objective. Truncating this model at a known lower bound (typically zero) yields the adaptive step size, and the paper shows that MoMo with no momentum reduces exactly to SPS_max, while MoMo-Adam inherits Adam's diagonal preconditioner. The online lower-bound estimator for non-zero optimal values draws on D-adaptation (Defazio & Mishchenko, 2023b).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: MoMo and MoMo-Adam are drop-in replacements for SGD-M and Adam with identical iteration complexity (two extra scalars, two extra inner products) that consistently extend the range of usable learning rates by over an order of magnitude across eight diverse tasks, reducing the costly hyperparameter search that dominates training budgets. The observation that the adaptive step size automatically performs warmup and decay without user-specified schedules is a useful practical finding, though its theoretical explanation remains open. Limitations include the convergence proof requiring the interpolation condition (a strong assumption for many real problems), the lower-bound estimator being valid only for convex losses, and the theoretical rate being the standard O(1/√K) with no improvement over tuned baselines. An open question is whether the model-based truncation idea can yield tighter rates or extend to non-convex settings with provable guarantees.
