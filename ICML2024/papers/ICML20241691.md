## Problem

Current fMRI-based phenotypic prediction methods rely exclusively on pairwise connectivity between brain regions, overlooking high-order relationships that involve simultaneous interactions among multiple regions. Identifying the most informative yet minimally redundant (MIMR) high-order relationships is challenging because the search space is exponential and no tractable optimization objective has been formulated for learning such hyperedges in a data-driven, end-to-end manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241691/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input an N×d feature matrix per subject, where each row is a brain region's Pearson correlation vector to all other regions (so d = N), along with a scalar phenotypic outcome (e.g., full-scale IQ or fluid intelligence). It produces a weighted hypergraph with K hyperedges (K=32 in practice), where each hyperedge is defined by a binary mask selecting a subset of regions, and each hyperedge carries a scalar weight representing its strength. These K weights are then fed into a linear head for prediction and into the CPM protocol for evaluating edge quality.

## Method

HYBRID decomposes hypergraph learning into a CONSTRUCTOR and a WEIGHTER. The CONSTRUCTOR uses K parallel heads, each outputting learnable probabilities p_θ,i^k per region, which are thresholded via a stop-gradient indicator to produce binary masks defining hyperedge membership; the WEIGHTER computes each hyperedge's weight by sum-pooling the masked node features and passing through a small MLP. The key theoretical contribution is the multi-head drop-bottleneck, a new information bottleneck framework that derives a tractable upper bound on I(H;X) as a sum of per-node entropy-weighted mask probabilities, yielding a loss that combines MSE prediction with a sparsity penalty on mask probabilities. This avoids exponential search (complexity O(N²K), matching MLPs) while theoretically guaranteeing the MIMR objective of maximal informativeness toward the outcome and minimal redundancy with the input.

## Results

HYBRID is evaluated using CPM r-values (correlation between learned edge weights and phenotypic outcomes) on the ABIDE dataset (3 IQ targets) and the ABCD dataset (8 task/timepoint sub-datasets). On ABIDE, HYBRID achieves r = 0.181 (FIQ), 0.204 (VIQ), and 0.245 (PIQ), outperforming the best baseline (BrainNetGNN) by an average of 8.8%. On ABCD, HYBRID outperforms the state-of-the-art on 7 of 8 sub-datasets with an average improvement of 12.1%, reaching r = 0.816 on MID2 and 0.738 on SST2. Overall, HYBRID outperforms the SOTA predictive model by an average of 11.2% across all datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HYBRID consistently outperforms all three hypergraph construction baselines (kNN, l1-hypergraph, l2-hypergraph) across every dataset, with the largest gaps on task-based ABCD sub-datasets (e.g., 0.816 vs. 0.289 on MID2 for l2-hypergraph). Against the four connectivity-based deep learning baselines, HYBRID beats BrainNetGNN, BrainGB, and BrainGNN on all 11 datasets. The closest competitor is BrainNetTF, which HYBRID outperforms on 7 of 8 ABCD sub-datasets and all 3 ABIDE targets; the sole exception is Rest1 (0.223 vs. 0.334), which the authors attribute to the sparsity of resting-state connectivity and lower data quality at timepoint 1. On the synthetic dataset (Appendix G), HYBRID also outperforms HGCN (a learnable hypergraph convolutional network) by an average of 28.3% in F1 score for hyperedge structure recovery. In terms of efficiency, HYBRID is 87% faster per batch than BrainNetTF and at least 1255% faster than the GNN-based baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information bottleneck (IB) framework from representation compression (Alemi et al., 2016; Kim et al., 2021) to the identification of underlying data structures—specifically, weighted hypergraphs over brain regions. It departs from prior hypergraph construction methods (kNN, l1/l2 hypergraphs) that rely on neighbor distances or clustering by making hyperedge identification fully learnable via multi-head masking, and from prior fMRI phenotypic prediction methods (BrainNetGNN, BrainGNN, BrainNetTF) that are restricted to pairwise connectivity. The multi-head drop-bottleneck is a novel IB objective that provides a tractable surrogate for the MIMR criterion, with theoretical guarantees derived via chain-rule decompositions of mutual information.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a biologically motivated but computationally intractable problem: identifying informative high-order brain interactions from an exponential search space. The 11.2% average CPM improvement over SOTA pairwise methods, combined with the finding that higher-degree hyperedges are significantly more predictive than pairwise edges, provides empirical evidence that high-order structure carries information beyond pairwise connectivity. Key limitations include the restriction to static (non-temporal) relationships and the absence of a principled interpretation method for complex hyperedges, both of which the authors flag as open directions. The multi-head drop-bottleneck framework, while demonstrated in neuroimaging, is conceptually applicable to any setting where one must identify informative sub-structures from high-dimensional inputs.
