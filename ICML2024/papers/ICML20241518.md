## Problem

Weight alignment—finding optimal permutations between the weight matrices of two neural networks—is NP-hard and critical for model merging, loss landscape analysis, and federated learning. Existing approaches rely on iterative local optimization (e.g., Sinkhorn iterations, activation matching), which are time-consuming and may converge to suboptimal solutions, making real-time alignment impractical. No learning-based method had previously been proposed to directly predict high-quality alignments in a single forward pass.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of full weight-space vectors v, v' (concatenations of all weight matrices and bias vectors for an M-layer MLP or CNN) and produces a sequence of (M−1) square permutation matrices, one per hidden layer, that when applied to v' minimize the L2 distance to v. At inference time, the network outputs soft matrices that are projected to exact permutations via the Hungarian algorithm; at training time, differentiable Sinkhorn iterations are used instead. The architecture is architecture-specific: a separate DEEP-ALIGN model must be trained for each input network topology (e.g., one for VGG11, another for a 7-layer CNN).

## Method

DEEP-ALIGN is a four-block equivariant architecture: (1) a Siamese DWSNet encoder produces weight-space embeddings for both input networks, (2) a mapping extracts activation-space representations from the bias components, (3) a generalized outer product (normalized inner product with a learned scale) between corresponding activation vectors generates candidate alignment matrices per layer, and (4) a projection layer maps these to the convex hull of permutation matrices (Sinkhorn during training, Hungarian at inference). The entire pipeline is equivariant to the G×G permutation symmetry group and to input transposition, which the paper proves guarantees the correct equivariance structure of the alignment function. Training requires no externally labeled data: labeled pairs are generated on-the-fly by applying random permutations and noise to unlabeled networks, and the network is optimized with a combination of supervised cross-entropy, L2 alignment loss, and a linear-mode-connectivity loss evaluated on the line segment between aligned models.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 CNN classifiers, DEEP-ALIGN (a single feed-forward pass) achieves a Barrier of 0.23 and AUC of 0.00, matching Activation Matching while outperforming Weight Matching (0.66 Barrier) and Sinkhorn (0.31 Barrier). When used to initialize Sinkhorn (DEEP-ALIGN+Sinkhorn), the Barrier drops to 0.08, significantly outperforming all baselines. On VGG16 (15M parameters), DEEP-ALIGN achieves 0.67 Barrier vs. 2.07 for Weight Matching and 2.33 for Activation Matching, with DEEP-ALIGN+Sinkhorn reaching 0.31. In the federated learning setup on CIFAR-10 with 50 clients, DEEP-ALIGN achieves 69.86% accuracy vs. 67.87% for Naive FedAvg.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Git re-basin (Ainsworth et al., 2022), DEEP-ALIGN outperforms Weight Matching by a large margin on all architectures (e.g., 0.23 vs. 0.66 Barrier on CIFAR-10 CNN; 0.67 vs. 2.07 on VGG16) and ties or slightly beats Activation Matching (0.23 vs. 0.23 on CIFAR-10 CNN, but 0.67 vs. 2.33 on VGG16). Against Sinkhorn re-basin (Peña et al., 2023), DEEP-ALIGN alone matches or slightly underperforms on some settings (0.38 vs. 0.36 Barrier on STL-10 CNN) but clearly wins on VGG16 (0.67 vs. 0.92); more importantly, DEEP-ALIGN+Sinkhorn dramatically outperforms standalone Sinkhorn (0.08 vs. 0.31 on CIFAR-10 CNN, 0.31 vs. 0.92 on VGG16), demonstrating its value as an initialization. In the federated learning experiment (McMahan et al., 2017), DEEP-ALIGN outperforms Naive FedAvg on both CIFAR-10 (69.86% vs. 67.87% at 50 clients) and STL-10 (49.48% vs. 46.82% at 10 clients), while also beating Weight Matching and Sinkhorn-L2 baselines. The key practical advantage is runtime: DEEP-ALIGN requires only a single forward pass (~0.2s) versus 37.7s for Sinkhorn, making it ~190× faster at inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DWSNets (Navon et al., 2023), which provided the first equivariant architecture for deep weight spaces, and repurposes it as a Siamese encoder within a new four-block pipeline. It departs from the established line of optimization-based alignment methods (Activation Matching, Weight Matching, Sinkhorn re-basin) by framing weight alignment as a learnable prediction problem, drawing on the broader 'learning to solve combinatorial optimization' literature (Khalil et al., 2017; Bengio et al., 2021). The key conceptual departure is proving that the alignment function G is equivariant under G×G and transposition, then designing an architecture that respects these symmetries by construction, rather than relying on iterative relaxation of the NP-hard assignment problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

A feed-forward alignment that matches or beats iterative optimization for an NP-hard problem has immediate practical value for model merging, federated learning, and continual learning, where real-time alignment of many model pairs is needed. The exactness guarantee (correct alignment whenever a zero-objective solution exists) and the theoretical connection to Activation Matching provide principled grounding. Key limitations include the architecture being tied to a specific input network topology (mitigable with GNN-based encoders) and the need for a small pretraining set. Open questions include generalization to transformer architectures, very large-scale models, and whether the equivariant inductive bias remains sufficient as network depth and width grow substantially.
