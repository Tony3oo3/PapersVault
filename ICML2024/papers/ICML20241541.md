## Problem

Despite Layer Normalization (LN) being a fundamental component of nearly all modern foundation models, its theoretical understanding has been limited to scale-invariance and optimization dynamics. No prior work has formally demonstrated that LN is a nonlinear transformation or characterized the representation capacity of networks built solely from linear and LN layers. This paper addresses the gap by investigating LN's nonlinearity, the expressive power of LN-Nets, and how to amplify that nonlinearity for practical architecture design.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241541/cropped_page3_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the LN-Net operates on m vector samples in R^d with arbitrary binary or multi-class label assignments, producing a class label as output. Empirically, the framework is validated on CIFAR-10 (32×32 RGB) and MNIST (28×28 grayscale) for random-label fitting and ResNet-NA classification, and on IWSLT14 German-to-English text sequences for machine translation. A key unusual aspect is that the ResNet-NA experiments remove all ReLU activations, isolating LN-G as the sole source of nonlinearity in the network.

## Method

The paper first defines the Sum of Squares Ratio (SSR) and its linear-invariant lower bound LSSR, then proves via Taylor expansion that a linear-LN-linear composition can break the LSSR, establishing LN as a nonlinear transformation. For representation capacity, it designs the Projection Merge Algorithm (PMA) and Parallelization Breaking Algorithm (PBA) to construct an LN-Net with only 3 neurons per layer and O(m) depth that shatters any m-point labeling, yielding a VC dimension lower bound of L+2. To amplify nonlinearity, the paper introduces LN-G (Group-based LN), which partitions neurons into g groups and applies LN independently within each group, and proves via a Hessian-based nonlinearity measure that LN-G with g ≤ d/3 has strictly greater nonlinearity than standard LN (ratio ≥ d/8 when g = d/4). Empirically, LN-G is validated through random-label fitting (achieving 100% training accuracy on CIFAR-10-RL), ResNet-NA without ReLU (86.66% test accuracy vs. 62.85% for LN), and as a drop-in replacement in Transformer (BLEU 35.23 vs. 35.01) and Tiny-ViT (89.26% vs. 88.81%).

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-NA (no ReLU activations), LN-G-Position (group number 16) achieves 86.66% test accuracy, substantially outperforming LN (62.85%), GN (69.37%), BN (39.3%), and IN (10%). In random-label fitting experiments, an LN-Net with 256 neurons per layer reaches 55.85% training accuracy on CIFAR-10-RL versus the 18.51% linear classifier upper bound, and with LN-G (g=32) can perfectly memorize all random labels. On IWSLT14 De-EN, replacing LN with LN-G in Transformer improves BLEU from 35.01 to 35.23; on CIFAR-10 with Tiny-ViT, LN-G improves accuracy from 88.81% to 89.26%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LN-G-Position decisively outperforms all four normalization baselines on CIFAR-10 ResNet-NA: it achieves 86.66% test accuracy versus LN (62.85%, +23.8 pp), GN (69.37%, +17.3 pp), BN (39.3%, +47.4 pp), and IN (10%, +76.7 pp). In the random-label fitting experiments, LN-Net breaks the linear classifier bound (55.85% vs 18.51% on CIFAR-10-RL), while BN in the same architecture cannot break the bound, confirming LN's unique nonlinearity advantage over BN. LN-G with g=32 further amplifies this, perfectly memorizing random labels on both CIFAR-10-RL and MNIST-RL. On the practical Transformer and ViT tasks, LN-G provides modest but consistent gains over LN (BLEU +0.22 on IWSLT14; accuracy +0.45 pp on CIFAR-10 Tiny-ViT), suggesting the amplified nonlinearity translates to real-world benefits, though the margins are smaller than in the controlled ResNet-NA setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant theoretical perspective on normalization layers, which has focused on scale invariance, optimization conditioning, and learning-rate adaptation (Ba et al., 2016; Hoffer et al., 2018; Arora et al., 2019; Li & Arora, 2020), to instead investigate LN's nonlinearity and representation capacity. It introduces LN-Net (layerwise composition of linear and LN transformations) and LN-G (grouped LN) as new analytical constructs, and develops SSR/LSSR as tools to formalize when LN breaks linear separability bounds. While prior empirical work (Huang et al., 2021; Labatie et al., 2021) touched on normalization's expressive power, those studies involved networks with activation functions; this paper isolates LN's contribution by analyzing networks with no explicit nonlinear activations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous proof that LN is a nonlinear transformation and establishes that a width-3 LN-Net with O(m) layers can shatter m arbitrary-labeled samples, giving a VC dimension lower bound of L+2. The practical implication—that grouped LN (LN-G) amplifies nonlinearity and can replace activation functions in ResNet-NA to reach 86.66% on CIFAR-10—is intriguing but validated only on small-scale datasets (CIFAR-10, MNIST) with modest gains on Transformer/ViT (e.g., +0.22 BLEU, +0.45% accuracy). Open questions include whether tighter capacity bounds exist, whether LN-G's advantage persists at ImageNet scale or in large language models, and whether the Hessian-based nonlinearity measure truly captures the relevant geometric structure for generalization.
