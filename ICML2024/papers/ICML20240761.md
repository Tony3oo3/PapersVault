## Problem

Existing misinformation mitigation on social media relies on fact-checking and censorship, which are slow, binary, and raise free-speech concerns. No prior work has leveraged the platform's informational advantage through strategic information revelation (Bayesian persuasion) with *imperfect* classifier predictions, nor has it analyzed how repeated signaling interventions dynamically reshape the content distribution over time (performative effects).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240761/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a user's draft post whose true state (misinformation level m, popularity v) is unknown, along with the platform's noisy classifier predictions (m̂, v̂) characterized by multi-class confusion matrices Q^M and Q^V, a prior distribution μ over joint states, and utility functions for both user and platform. The output is an optimal signaling scheme π(s|θ̂) — a randomized mapping from predicted states to share/not-share recommendations — computed as the solution to a linear program. The scheme must be committed to before the user drafts content, and the platform's predictions are explicitly modeled as imperfect via the confusion matrix, which constrains the set of achievable effective signaling schemes.

## Method

The paper formulates noisy Bayesian persuasion as a linear program that extends the standard Kamenica-Gentzkow LP with additional constraints (3)–(5) restricting the effective signaling scheme π̃(s|θ) to the parallelepiped induced by the confusion matrix rows, capturing the reduced signaling power from imperfect predictions. The optimal platform utility is shown to equal the concave closure of the platform's expected utility function evaluated at the noisy prior (Proposition 2), generalizing the standard geometric characterization. The paper then provides a necessary and sufficient condition (convex hull inclusion of confusion matrix columns) for optimal utility to be non-decreasing in classifier quality (Theorem 1), and proves Lipschitz continuity of optimal utility in the confusion matrix except at indifference boundaries (Theorem 2). Finally, a stateful performative model is introduced where the content distribution updates as μ_{t+1} = λμ_t + (1−λ)ρ_t(θ|a=1), and the paper proves that myopic optimal signaling always converges to the share posterior induced at round 0, which is also a stable fixed point (Theorem 3).

## Results

In the worked example (Section 4.4), optimal noisy persuasion increases platform utility from 0.45 to 0.64 while reducing the fraction of shared posts that are misinformation from 30% to 17%, with user utility unchanged at 0. In the synthetic experiments (Section 7), even when both classifiers perform poorly (error up to 0.4), a 10% reduction in misinformation sharing is achieved, increasing to approximately 20% when classifier errors are below 0.15. The results are more sensitive to the accuracy of the misinformation classifier than the validation classifier. Both experimental plots report a 90% confidence interval of less than 4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing misinformation-mitigation methods in a head-to-head experimental sense; instead, the primary comparison is between no platform intervention (the user's default 'share' action) and the proposed optimal noisy persuasion signaling scheme. The key finding is that persuasion strictly improves platform utility (0.45 → 0.64 in the example; substantial gains in synthetic experiments) while reducing misinformation sharing, and—critically—never decreases user utility (Corollary 1), unlike censorship or fact-checking approaches that may suppress legitimate content. The paper positions itself as complementary to existing soft interventions (accuracy prompts, self-censoring, network depth/width capping) rather than a direct competitor, noting that those prior works assume perfect platform knowledge and do not model performative effects. The performative analysis (Theorem 3) further shows that myopic signaling converges to a stable point that is strictly better than the initial prior for the platform, providing a long-term advantage over one-shot interventions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian Persuasion framework of Kamenica & Gentzkow (2011) and the algorithmic LP formulation of Dughmi & Xu (2016) to a 'noisy' setting where the sender (platform) only observes classifier predictions rather than true states, captured via confusion matrices. It further introduces a stateful performative dynamic (inspired by Brown et al., 2022) where signaling affects the user's future content distribution, going beyond the static single-shot interaction of standard BP. Compared to prior signaling-for-misinformation work (Candogan & Drakopoulos, 2020; Yang et al., 2023), this paper departs by explicitly modeling imperfect predictions and long-term behavioral feedback, rather than assuming perfect platform knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, non-censorship mechanism for platforms to reduce misinformation sharing while provably not harming user utility, addressing a real tension between content moderation and freedom of speech. The clean characterizations—exact conditions for utility monotonicity in classifier quality (Theorem 1) and Lipschitz continuity with specific exception points (Theorem 2)—give platforms actionable metrics for improving their classifiers. However, the approach is limited by its assumption of fully rational users, a single-user model, and validation only on synthetic data; open questions include bounded rationality, multi-user network effects, and whether the performative convergence guarantees hold under model misspecification in practice.
