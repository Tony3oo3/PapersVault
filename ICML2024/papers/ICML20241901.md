## Problem

Decision support systems based on conformal prediction sets can guarantee that experts are never worse off than working alone, but finding the optimal conformal predictor (i.e., the coverage parameter α) has previously required assuming a stylized parametric expert model. This paper removes that assumption, asking: can we efficiently identify the optimal prediction-set size for real human experts without any parametric model of their decision-making?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241901/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a single RGB image (from ImageNet16H, 16 coarse-grained categories) as input and outputs a prediction set — a subset of the 16 label values constructed via conformal prediction using a fine-tuned VGG-19 classifier and a fixed 120-image calibration set. Under the strict implementation, the expert must select a label from within the prediction set; under the lenient implementation, an 'Other' option allows selecting any label. The prediction sets are nested in α: smaller α yields larger sets (easier task, more frequent misleading), larger α yields smaller sets (harder task, less misleading).

## Method

The paper models expert predictions over prediction sets via a structural causal model and introduces a counterfactual monotonicity assumption: if an expert succeeds (fails) on a prediction set containing the ground truth, they would have succeeded (failed) on any smaller (larger) set also containing the truth. Exploiting this assumption together with the nested structure of conformal prediction sets, the authors design Counterfactual Successive Elimination (Counterfactual SE), a bandit algorithm that at each phase pulls the median arm, observes the reward, and counterfactually infers rewards for at least half the remaining arms, reducing the number of pulls per phase from O(m) to O(log m). This yields an expected regret bound of O(√(t log m log T)) versus O(√(mt log T)) for vanilla successive elimination — an exponential improvement in the number of arms. A large-scale human subject study (n=2,751, 194,407 predictions) validates the monotonicity assumption empirically, confirms the regret advantage over vanilla bandit baselines, and shows that the strict implementation (limiting expert agency to the prediction set) outperforms the lenient one across all competitive α values.

## Results

In a large-scale human subject study (n=2,751, 194,407 predictions over 19,200 image–prediction-set pairs on ImageNet16H), the strict implementation of the decision support system consistently outperforms the lenient implementation across all competitive α values, with the classifier achieving 0.848 accuracy and experts achieving 0.760 standalone. Counterfactual SE achieves an expected regret of O(√(t log m log T)), an exponential improvement over vanilla successive elimination's O(√(mt log T)). Even under full counterfactual monotonicity violations (p_v=1.0), counterfactual UCB1's selected α yields success probability only 3.8% below the optimal α*, demonstrating graceful degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Straitouri et al. (2023), which relies on a multinomial logit (MNL) stylized expert model to select the conformal predictor, this paper's assumption-free counterfactual UCB1 and counterfactual SE both find α values that yield higher expert success probability (Figure 3), confirming that removing the parametric expert model assumption leads to better practical performance. Against Babbar et al. (2022), which studies the lenient implementation allowing experts to predict outside the prediction set, the strict implementation (forcing experts to choose from the set) consistently achieves greater success probability across the full spectrum of competitive α values. The paper also compares vanilla vs. counterfactual versions of both SE and UCB1: counterfactual rewards provide a clear competitive advantage in expected regret (Figure 2), and using the counterfactual monotonicity assumption (Counterfactual SE/UCB1) further outperforms the assumption-free variants (AF Counterfactual SE/UCB1), suggesting the assumption approximately holds in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Straitouri et al. (2023), which introduced prediction-set-based decision support systems using conformal prediction but relied on a parametric multinomial logit expert model to find the optimal conformal predictor. Here, the authors replace that parametric assumption with a structural causal model and a counterfactual monotonicity assumption, then cast the optimization as a multi-armed bandit problem (Slivkins, 2019) where the nested structure of conformal prediction sets enables counterfactual reward inference. The key methodological departure is from vanilla successive elimination and UCB1: by exploiting the fact that a single pull at the median arm counterfactually determines rewards for at least half the remaining arms, the authors achieve an exponential regret improvement (O(√(t log m log T)) vs. O(√(mt log T))). The work also relates to causal bandits (Lattimore et al., 2016) but uses counterfactual inference rather than known causal structure for exploration efficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first assumption-free methodology for tuning conformal prediction-based decision support systems, removing the need for a stylized expert model that limited prior work's applicability. The counterintuitive finding that restricting experts' agency (strict implementation) outperforms granting full agency (lenient implementation) challenges a common design principle in human-AI collaboration and has direct implications for how such systems should be deployed. Key limitations include evaluation on a single image classification benchmark (ImageNet16H), the inability to directly validate the counterfactual monotonicity assumption (it sits at level 3 of Pearl's causal ladder), and the absence of fairness considerations. Open questions include extending the framework to other task types (multilabel, regression), adapting other bandit algorithms (e.g., Thompson sampling) to counterfactual rewards, and studying the approach with domain experts in high-stakes settings like medicine.
