## Problem

AI Inner Interpretability lacks a general conceptual framework for developing, evaluating, and comparing mechanistic explanations of neural network internals, leaving the field vulnerable to critiques of false understanding, weak generalization, and unclear research goals. The authors argue that these issues closely parallel longstanding challenges in Cognitive Neuroscience, which has already developed multilevel frameworks and methodological strategies to address them, yet this connection has been overlooked.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the framework takes as input the existing body of inner interpretability studies (circuit discovery, activation patching, key-value memory analyses) and the critiques directed at them, and produces a structured four-level explanatory schema (computational, algorithmic, primitive, implementation) with concrete methodological strategies at each level. The framework is illustrated end-to-end using factual recall in language transformers as a running example, where the computational level is formalized as a tuple-completion problem, the algorithmic level is specified as an Attribute Extraction procedure, primitives are identified as key-value memory pair systems, and the implementation level maps to MLP and self-attention sub-modules.

## Method

The paper adapts the Marr-Poggio multilevel framework from Cognitive Neuroscience to AI inner interpretability, proposing that productive mechanistic explanations require mutual constraints across four levels: a formal computational specification of the target capacity, an algorithmic description of the step-by-step procedure, a set of theoretically grounded primitive operations and representations, and an implementation-level decomposition into model components. Three key lessons from Cognitive Neuroscience are transferred: (1) incomplete mechanisms that merely localize causally relevant components without specifying operations lead to false understanding, (2) the choice of abstraction level at the implementation level should be guided by the quality of the match to cognitive primitives rather than chosen arbitrarily, and (3) neither purely top-down nor purely bottom-up approaches suffice—combined, assumption-aware strategies with severe testing are necessary. The framework further prescribes that hypotheses be tightly linked to mechanistic conjectures, that empirical predictions be falsifiable and differ from baselines, and that invariances across model scales, initializations, and input subdomains be explicitly tested.

## Results

This is a position paper proposing a conceptual framework rather than reporting empirical results. There are no quantitative metrics, benchmarks, or performance numbers. The paper's contribution is a multilevel explanatory framework (adapted from Marr & Poggio, 1976) with four levels—computational, algorithmic, primitive representations/operations, and implementation—along with methodological strategies (severe testing, invariance testing, mutual constraints across levels) for building mechanistic explanations in AI inner interpretability. The running example is factual recall in language transformers, illustrated with a formal definition (Def. 4.2) and a candidate algorithm (Algorithm 1: Attribute Extraction), but no experiments are conducted.

## Baselines & Comparisons

No baselines or quantitative comparisons are presented, as this is a conceptual position paper. The paper does not benchmark its proposed framework against competing interpretability methods. Instead, it draws qualitative parallels between issues in AI inner interpretability and longstanding debates in Cognitive Neuroscience (e.g., bottom-up vs. top-down approaches, levels of abstraction, completeness of mechanistic explanations) and argues that lessons from the latter can be transferred. The closest thing to a 'comparison' is the contrast between incomplete mechanistic proposals (e.g., localizing a neuron or circuit without specifying its operation) and complete multilevel explanations, illustrated with the factual recall example where localizing MLP middle layers as carrying factual information does not guarantee successful knowledge editing (Hase et al., 2024).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper repurposes the Marr & Poggio (1976) multilevel framework—previously applied to ML model analysis by Hamrick & Mohamed (2020)—and systematically transfers methodological strategies from Cognitive Neuroscience (severe testing, mutual constraints between levels, multilevel mechanistic explanation) to AI Inner Interpretability. It positions itself as a response to critiques of the interpretability field (Räuker et al., 2023; Krishnan, 2020; Hooker, 2021) by arguing that the conceptual gaps identified by critics mirror long-standing issues already resolved in Cognitive Neuroscience. Rather than proposing a new interpretability technique, it carves new ground by providing a unified conceptual scaffold that organizes existing work (circuit discovery, causal abstraction, activation patching, key-value memory pairs) into a coherent multilevel structure with concrete methodological prescriptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a structured way to evaluate and compare mechanistic proposals across the field, potentially reducing the 'false sense of understanding' and contradictory claims that critics have flagged. It identifies concrete research gaps—formalizing capacities at the computational level, understanding assumptions in interpretability methods, and producing mechanistic sketches spanning all four levels—that could guide future work. Key open questions include how to adapt the learning/evolution levels of the multilevel framework to artificial systems, whether the framework scales to increasingly complex architectures, and how to operationalize 'severe testing' for mechanistic hypotheses in practice. As a position paper with no empirical validation, its impact depends on whether the community adopts the proposed framework as a shared vocabulary and methodological standard.
