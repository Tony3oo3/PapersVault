## Problem

Surrogate methods for inferring long-term effects from short-term experiments require that treatment effects be fully mediated by intermediate surrogates, an assumption that is violated for long-term treatments involving continual exposure to a novel intervention (e.g., persistent environmental hazards, regular medication, or sustained platform changes). For such treatments, the effect on long-term outcomes operates directly through ongoing exposure rather than through a one-time shock, so no finite set of short-term surrogates can capture the full effect. The paper addresses this gap by developing a method that estimates long-term causal effects of arbitrary-duration treatments from only short-term experimental data, without requiring surrogacy assumptions or an observational dataset linking surrogates to long-term outcomes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only two periods of experimental data as (state, action, reward, next-state) tuples, together with a target treatment duration T and discount rate γ, to produce an infinite-horizon average treatment effect estimate with confidence intervals. The Q function (state-action value) and density ratio function (occupancy measure under the target policy relative to the experimental distribution) are learned as nuisance functions via machine learning. A single short experiment yields ATE estimates for any treatment duration from one period to permanent, though the stationary-policy approximation degrades slightly for very short durations.

## Method

The paper reframes the long-term ATE as a policy evaluation problem in a Markov Decision Process, where the T-duration treatment is a non-stationary policy applying treatment for T periods then reverting to control. The key theoretical step is constructing an equivalent stationary stochastic policy with average treatment probability 1−γ^T that generates the same occupancy measure and hence the same cumulative discounted reward, rendering the Q function stationary and learnable. The estimator is a doubly-robust, bias-corrected plug-in estimator built on the efficient influence function, depending on the Q function and density ratio function as nuisance parameters. The Q function is estimated via temporal-difference learning with neural networks (using target networks for stability), while the density ratio function is estimated via a Minimax weight estimator with linear function classes. The resulting estimator is N^{-1/2}-consistent and asymptotically efficient, requiring only that the product of nuisance estimation rates be o_p(N^{-1/2}), enabling the use of regularized ML estimators.

## Results

In the Simple MDP experiment, the ORL method's ATE estimates match the true ATE across all treatment durations (1, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 120 periods) using only two periods of experimental data, while the surrogate index method (2-period variant) produces a constant estimate equal to the 1-period ATE, underestimating the true effect for all T > 1. In the Sepsis Simulator, ORL estimates for ventilation and ventilation+antibiotics center on the true ATE across durations 1 through ∞, with confidence intervals appropriately spanning zero for the near-zero ventilation effect and capturing the increasing effect of the combined treatment. The naive method (constant 1-period ATE) and the 2-period surrogate index both fail to capture the growing long-term effect as treatment duration increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the surrogate index method (Athey et al., 2019), which the paper shows implicitly estimates a truncated treatment effect limited to the duration up to which surrogates are measured. In the Simple MDP (Figure 3), the 2-period surrogate index produces a flat estimate equal to the 1-period ATE for all longer durations, while the ORL method tracks the true increasing ATE accurately. In the Sepsis Simulator (Figure 6), the surrogate index is correct only for the 1-period duration; for ventilation+antibiotics, it is clearly biased downward for all longer durations where the true ATE grows substantially. A second surrogate variant that measures surrogates up to the full treatment duration matches the true ATE but requires an infeasible measurement horizon. A naive baseline (assuming constant 1-period ATE) similarly underestimates for all T > 1. The ORL method wins on all comparisons for treatment durations beyond the first period, with the gap widening as duration increases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the causal inference problem of estimating long-term treatment effects as an off-policy evaluation problem in a Markov Decision Process, directly leveraging the doubly-robust estimator from Kallus & Uehara (2022) that combines Q-function and density-ratio function estimation. It departs fundamentally from the surrogate index literature (Athey et al., 2019; Prentice, 1989) by showing that surrogate methods are structurally incapable of capturing effects of continual-exposure treatments, since the mediation assumption fails by definition for long-term treatments. The closest prior work, Huang et al. (2023), also targets long-term treatment effects from short-term data but relies on linearity assumptions in surrogate transitions and reward mappings, whereas this paper's MDP formulation avoids such parametric restrictions. The key technical contribution is the construction of an equivalent stationary stochastic policy for any T-duration non-stationary treatment policy, making the Q function tractable and enabling the use of standard off-policy RL estimation tools.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a principled alternative to surrogate methods for the common and practically important setting where interventions are ongoing (regular medication, platform changes, environmental exposures) rather than one-time shocks, eliminating the need for both the mediation assumption and an external observational dataset linking surrogates to long-term outcomes. Key limitations include the requirement that the state space be sufficiently rich to satisfy the Markov property (the hidden-state experiment shows severe bias when this fails), the stronger-than-usual overlap condition across the entire state space, and the fact that the stationary policy approximation degrades for shorter treatment durations. Open questions include how well the method scales to high-dimensional continuous state spaces where the curse of dimensionality is severe, whether the stationarity assumption (Assumption 5) is testable in practice, and whether the approach extends to settings with time-varying treatment effects or non-Markovian dynamics.
