## Problem

Unbiased Learning to Rank (ULTR) assumes that fitting click probability via the examination hypothesis c(x,t) = r(x)·o(t) will recover the true relevance model, yet no prior work provides a theoretical guarantee for when this recovery is actually possible. This paper demonstrates that relevance can be fundamentally unrecoverable from click data, leading to degenerate ranking performance, and identifies the precise structural condition under which recovery is achievable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240272/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset D of (x, t) pairs, where x denotes query-document ranking features and t denotes bias factors (e.g., positions, context types), along with observed click labels. It first produces a binary identifiability verdict by checking whether the identifiability graph (IG) is connected, then outputs a minimally modified dataset—either augmented with K−1 swapped document-bias pairs (node intervention) or with K−1 merged bias-factor nodes (node merging)—that is fed into any standard ULTR training pipeline. The approach is entirely model-agnostic and training-independent, operating solely on the dataset structure before optimization begins.

## Method

The core contribution is an identifiability graph (IG) whose nodes are bias factors and whose edges connect two bias factors whenever a shared ranking feature appears with both; Theorem 1 proves that the relevance model is identifiable (recoverable up to a scaling constant) if and only if this graph is connected. To repair a disconnected IG, the paper proposes two complementary strategies: (1) node intervention, which swaps a document from one bias factor to another to create a new edge, selecting the optimal swap by minimizing a variance-based cost derived from finite-sample click estimation and using a Minimum Spanning Tree over components to minimize total online cost; and (2) node merging, which forces two bias factors from different components to share the same observation probability, selecting the pair with minimal bias-feature distance and again using an MST to bound the cumulative approximation error by the tree diameter. Both methods require only K−1 operations for K connected components, dramatically reducing the intervention overhead compared to traditional random swapping across all queries.

## Results

On the K=2 (unidentifiable) synthetic dataset, the proposed Node Intervention achieves perfect MCC of 1.000 and nDCG@10 of 1.000 across all three ULTR baselines (DLA, Regression-EM, Two-Tower), while Node Merging achieves MCC 0.975 and nDCG@10 1.000. Without the proposed methods, the same baselines yield MCC of only 0.707 (DLA), 0.580 (Regression-EM), and 0.830 (Two-Tower) despite perfectly fitting click probability (Click MSE < 10⁻⁸). On real-world Yahoo! and Istella-S under CPBM bias with 5,000 context types, DLA+Node Merging improves MCC from 0.750 to 0.771 (Yahoo!) and from 0.764 to 0.772 (Istella-S), with corresponding nDCG@10 gains of +0.006 and +0.002.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All three ULTR baselines—DLA (Ai et al., 2018), Regression-EM (Wang et al., 2018), and Two-Tower (Guo et al., 2019)—are significantly outperformed by the proposed methods in the unidentifiable (K=2) setting. DLA achieves MCC 0.707±0.105 and nDCG@10 0.886±0.040 without intervention, jumping to MCC 1.000 and nDCG@10 1.000 with Node Intervention. Regression-EM, the weakest baseline at MCC 0.580±0.117, improves to 0.980 with Node Intervention and 0.975 with Node Merging. Two-Tower, the strongest baseline at MCC 0.830±0.050, reaches perfect 1.000 MCC with Node Intervention. Critically, all baselines achieve near-zero Click MSE (<10⁻⁸), confirming they fit click probability perfectly yet still produce incorrect relevance estimates when the identifiability graph is disconnected. On real-world Yahoo! and Istella-S under CPBM bias, DLA+Node Merging consistently outperforms both No-debias and plain DLA on MCC and nDCG, though the absolute gains are smaller (e.g., +0.021 MCC on Yahoo!) due to the partially connected structure of the real IGs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the examination hypothesis framework (Joachims et al., 2017) and the joint optimization approaches for ULTR (DLA, Regression-EM, Two-Tower), but departs from them by asking a fundamentally different question: not how to optimize the click model, but whether the relevance model is recoverable at all. It connects to Oosterhuis (2022), who demonstrated a counterexample of unidentifiability on a small dataset, and to Zhang et al. (2023) on confounding bias, but generalizes both into a complete graph-theoretic characterization. The identifiability concept itself is borrowed from ICA, latent variable models, and causal discovery, but its application to ULTR and the resulting graph connectivity test are novel to this domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a core working assumption in ULTR: that adding more bias factors (context types, presentation styles, etc.) to improve observation estimation is always beneficial. The finding that the TianGong-ST dataset—a large real-world search log—has 2,900 disconnected components in its identifiability graph means the unidentifiability problem is not merely theoretical but actively present in production-scale data. Open questions include extending the framework beyond the examination hypothesis (to trust bias or vector-based models), handling approximation errors in finite-sample settings, and whether the simplified identifiability probability estimate (Example 2) holds under realistic non-uniform data generation. The node intervention and node merging methods provide practical, model-agnostic fixes, but their applicability to non-swappable bias factors (e.g., contextual information) remains limited.
