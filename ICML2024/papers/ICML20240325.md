## Problem

Existing Preference-based RL (PbRL) methods prioritize feedback efficiency but implicitly assume high-quality expert annotations, making them fragile to the noisy preferences that arise from non-expert or crowd-sourced annotators. Even a 10% corruption rate in preference labels can dramatically degrade performance, yet robust reward learning under noisy preferences remains largely unexplored. Additionally, a significant performance gap during the transition from unsupervised pre-training to online training in PbRL has been overlooked, further undermining robustness under noisy feedback.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairs of trajectory segments (each of length H=50 state-action tuples) along with noisy binary or tie preference labels from a human teacher, processed in a two-phase pipeline: an unsupervised pre-training phase using particle-based intrinsic rewards, followed by online training with human preference queries. The reward model is an ensemble of three 3-layer MLPs (256 hidden units) with tanh-constrained outputs, and the denoising discriminator operates on the KL divergence between predicted Bradley-Terry preferences and annotated labels at each reward-model update step. The final outputs are a trained SAC policy, the estimated reward function, and a filtered/flipped preference dataset used for reward learning.

## Method

RIME introduces two key components. First, a denoising discriminator filters noisy preferences using a dynamically computed lower bound τ_lower = -ln ρ + αρ + β_t·s_KL on the KL divergence between predicted and annotated preferences, where ρ tracks the maximum cross-entropy loss on trustworthy samples, and β_t·s_KL is an uncertainty term with linearly decaying weight to tolerate distribution shift during RL training; samples exceeding a fixed upper bound τ_upper have their labels flipped for reintegration. Second, a warm-start procedure pre-trains the reward model via MSE regression on normalized intrinsic rewards during the unsupervised phase, aligning both the Q-network and reward model before online training begins, which bridges the pre-training-to-online performance gap and provides the discriminator with a well-initialized reward model. The theoretical foundation is a KL divergence lower bound (Theorem 4.1) proving that corrupted samples must exhibit KL divergence at least -ln ρ + ρ/2 + O(ρ²) above clean samples, justifying the threshold-based filtering.

## Results

On DMControl locomotion tasks (episode return), RIME achieves 837.79±133.49 on Walker-walk and 415.52±180.74 on Quadruped-walk, substantially outperforming PEBBLE (692.05 and 208.66 respectively) across five noise levels (ε=0.1–0.3). On Meta-world manipulation tasks (success rate), RIME reaches 85.70% on Button-Press and 51.96% on Sweep-Into, compared to PEBBLE's 50.07% and 19.09%. RIME is the only method that remains effective at ε=0.3 on Button-Press (38.8%) and Hammer (1.8%), where all baselines degrade severely. On Cheetah-run, RIME (602.18) is essentially tied with PEBBLE (604.77), the one task where the advantage is minimal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RIME outperforms PEBBLE (its backbone) on 5 of 6 tasks, with the largest gains on Quadruped-walk (415.52 vs. 208.66, ~2×) and Button-Press (85.70% vs. 50.07%), while essentially tying on Cheetah-run (602.18 vs. 604.77). Against the other PbRL baselines, RIME dominates: SURF (211.28 on Walker), RUNE (584.06), and MRN (537.40) all fall well below RIME's 837.79 on Walker-walk, and the gap widens at higher noise levels where these feedback-efficient methods become ineffective. In the robust-training ablation (Table 4, ε=0.3), RIME (741 on Walker, 80.0% on Button-Press) beats ADT (572, 74.1%), MAE (453, 71.2%), t-CE (548, 36.0%), and label smoothing (425, 27.8%), confirming that the dynamic KL-divergence threshold is more effective than fixed sample dropping, robust losses, or regularization for the RL setting. The paper also shows RIME outperforms PEBBLE with real non-expert human annotators (~40% error rate) on Hopper, successfully performing consecutive backflips with only 100 feedback samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

RIME builds directly on PEBBLE as its backbone PbRL algorithm and draws from the robust training literature on learning from noisy labels, specifically the sample selection category (e.g., ADT). It departs from the dominant PbRL research direction focused on feedback efficiency (SURF, RUNE, MRN) by instead addressing robustness to corrupted preferences, a concern identified by Lee et al. (2021a) but not previously tackled with dedicated algorithms. The warm start component connects to the Policy-to-Value Reincarnating RL (PVRL) literature, where Uchendu et al. (2023) showed that random re-initialization of value networks causes catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained policies—a gap RIME addresses by pre-training the reward model on intrinsic rewards to preserve all pre-training knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RIME makes PbRL practical for real-world deployment where feedback comes from non-expert users or crowdsourcing platforms rather than domain experts, as demonstrated by the Hopper experiment with ~40% annotation error from students. A key open question is the fundamental trade-off between sample efficiency and robustness: RIME still requires substantially more feedback at higher error rates (e.g., 80,000 for Hammer at ε=0.3), and the method fails entirely at 45% noise in Walker. The finding that feedback-efficient methods (SURF, RUNE, MRN) paradoxically become less robust as noise increases—while the simpler PEBBLE baseline is more robust—challenges the field's assumption that sophistication in query selection always helps, and suggests that robustness and efficiency may require fundamentally different design principles.
