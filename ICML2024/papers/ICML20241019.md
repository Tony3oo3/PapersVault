## Problem

Long-horizon robotics demonstrations contain multiple subtasks separated by keyframes, but existing keyframe identification methods either require privileged information (actions, rewards, policy parameters) or rely on heuristic peak detection over static visual embeddings that is sensitive to hyperparameters and lacks semantic grounding. No prior approach simultaneously achieves accurate keyframe localization and interpretable skill annotation from unlabeled video demonstrations alone.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241019/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

KISA takes an untrimmed video demonstration (a sequence of RGB frames with no action, reward, or segmentation labels) and a fixed skill library of language descriptions as input. It outputs a set of keyframe indices marking subtask boundaries, each paired with the skill label from the library that best matches the temporal context at that frame. At inference, a per-frame confidence score is computed as the maximum cosine similarity between the temporal-enhanced video representation and all skill embeddings; local maxima in this score curve are extracted as keyframes, and the argmax skill provides the annotation.

## Method

KISA builds on pretrained robotics visual encoders (R3M, VIP, or LIV) by adding a 6-layer Transformer temporal enhancement module (8 heads, 1024 hidden dim) that aggregates all historical frame representations to produce a video-level embedding for each frame, capturing action semantics that static frame-level features miss. Training uses a history-aware contrastive objective with three types of hard negatives—incorrect skill labels, mismatched historical frames from other demonstrations, and temporally reversed video chunks—to align video-level representations with skill language embeddings at the inter-skill level. A fine-grained monotonic alignment loss (MSE between the skill similarity score and the visual distance to the subtask's terminal keyframe) is added to prevent representation collapse within a skill and encourage monotonically increasing confidence toward the keyframe. The total loss is L_contrastive + 0.1 · L_score, and the entire framework is plug-and-play across different pretrained backbones without requiring video-specific pretraining.

## Results

On keyframe identification (Table 1), KISA achieves near-perfect F1 scores of 99.7% on Maniskill2, 85.2% on CALVIN, and 98.7% on FrankaKitchen, with number errors of 0.0±0.0 and MAE of 0.2±0.1 on Maniskill2. The best baseline, UVD, reaches only 40.2% F1 on Maniskill2, 36.9% on CALVIN, and 64.8% on FrankaKitchen. For policy learning (Table 4), LISA+KISA achieves 82.5% success rate on 1-instruction CALVIN tasks versus 55.4% for LISA alone, and maintains 17.6% on 5-instruction chains where LISA drops to 9.3%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KISA substantially outperforms all six keyframe identification baselines across all three environments. The strongest baseline, UVD (heuristic peak detection on LIV embeddings), achieves 40.2% F1 on Maniskill2 and 36.9% on CALVIN, while KISA reaches 99.7% and 85.2% respectively — a gap of ~60 and ~48 percentage points. Among the pretrained representations used directly (R3M, VIP, LIV), performance improves in the order R3M < VIP ≈ LIV, confirming that finer-grained vision-language alignment helps but is insufficient without temporal context. VideoRLCS (reward-driven) and KTS (unsupervised clustering) perform worst, with F1 scores below 20% on most environments, highlighting that neither reward signals nor raw visual similarity alone suffice for long-horizon keyframe detection. For policy learning, LISA+KISA (82.5% on 1-instruction, 17.6% on 5-instruction CALVIN) significantly outperforms LISA alone (55.4%, 9.3%) and LISA+LIV (51.2%, 7.5%), demonstrating that KISA's interpretable skill annotations provide meaningful intermediate supervision that reduces the learning burden for hierarchical policies.

## Relation to Prior Work

KISA builds directly on pretrained robotics representations (R3M, VIP, LIV) that encode frame-level visual features with implicit value-function properties, and on the contrastive learning paradigm (InfoNCE) established by CLIP and BLIP for cross-modal alignment. It departs from the concurrent UVD work, which applies heuristic peak-detection rules to visual embedding distances alone, by introducing language grounding through skill annotations and a temporal enhancement module that expands static frame representations to video-level semantics. The history-aware contrastive learning with three types of hard negatives (incorrect skills, disjoint histories, video inversion) and the monotonic alignment loss are novel training objectives not present in prior keyframe identification or skill discovery methods like LISA or VideoRLCS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KISA provides a practical, plug-and-play tool for scalable keyframe and skill annotation from unlabeled video demonstrations, which is a bottleneck for hierarchical policy learning in long-horizon manipulation. The demonstrated policy learning gains (LISA+KISA achieving 82.5% vs 55.4% on 1-instruction CALVIN) show that high-quality automatic annotation can substitute for expensive human labeling. Key limitations include the retrieval-based skill annotation (no open-vocabulary generation), the reliance on ground-truth keyframes from privileged simulator information during training, and notably weaker cross-embodiment generalization (40.7% F1 on real kitchen data vs 98.7% in simulation), suggesting the approach has not yet fully bridged the sim-to-real gap for keyframe identification.
