## Problem

Existing brain representation learning methods operate at a single spatial scale—either voxel-level activity (aggregated to a single beta weight per time window, losing temporal dynamics) or ROI-level functional connectivity (losing individual voxel signals)—and are typically supervised or designed for a specific modality/task. No unified framework jointly captures the temporal dynamics of voxel activity and the evolving structure of functional connectivity in an unsupervised manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240134/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multivariate timeseries of voxel (or channel) BOLD signals across T task time windows, a sequence of functional connectivity graphs (Pearson correlation matrices between all voxel pairs per time window, thresholded at the 90th percentile), and Schaefer atlas functional system labels for patching. It produces voxel-level, edge-level, and brain-level (graph-level) encodings that are concatenated from both the VA and FC encoders before being fed to a downstream linear probe or classifier. Notably, the authors introduce two new large-scale datasets (BVFC for fMRI and BVFC-MEG for MEG) derived from THINGS that preserve full voxel timeseries and per-voxel functional connectivity rather than the aggregated beta values in the original release.

## Method

BRAINMIXER comprises two MLP-based encoders trained jointly via mutual information maximization. The Voxel Activity Encoder splits voxels into functional-system patches (interpolated to uniform size), then fuses information across voxels using a dynamic self-attention mechanism with learnable mixing matrices (Voxel-Mixer) and across time via a 2-layer MLP (Time-Mixer). The Functional Connectivity Encoder extracts temporal graph patches via biased temporal random walks that preferentially sample recent timestamps, encodes each patch with a permutation-invariant TPMIXER pooling (non-parametric feature fusion plus dynamic self-attention across voxels), and appends a non-learnable cosine time encoding. Pre-training maximizes the mutual information between the two encoders' outputs at both voxel-level and brain-level using Noise-Contrastive Estimation, with data augmentation via random edge masking and cross-sample timeseries swapping to generate negative pairs.

## Results

On multi-class brain classification (Table 1), BRAINMIXER achieves the best accuracy across all four datasets: 67.24% on BVFC, 62.58% on BVFC-MEG, 96.32% on HCP-Mental, and 57.83% on HCP-Age, yielding a 14.3% average improvement (up to 20.3% best-case) over the strongest baseline (PTGB). On anomaly detection (Table 2), BRAINMIXER attains the best AUC-PR on all six datasets, with average improvements of 6.2%, 5.7%, and 4.81% over the best baseline in edge-level, voxel-level, and brain-level anomaly detection, respectively. Ablation studies confirm that biased temporal sampling, the VA and FC encoders, functional patching, and dynamic self-attention are the most critical components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BRAINMIXER outperforms all 14 baselines on every dataset and task. The strongest competitors are PTGB (55.89% on BVFC, 92.58% on HCP-Mental) and BNTRANSFORMER (55.03% on BVFC, 91.71% on HCP-Mental), both of which use only functional connectivity graphs; BRAINMIXER's advantage over them (11.4% and 3.7% respectively) is attributed to jointly encoding voxel activity timeseries alongside connectivity. Graph-based methods like GMM and GRAPHMIXER (53.1–53.2% on BVFC) and brain-network methods like BRAINGNN and FBNETGEN (50.2–50.6% on BVFC) trail further behind, as they lack temporal dynamics modeling. Time-series-only baselines (USAD at 48.52% on BVFC) perform worst, confirming that connectivity information is essential. In anomaly detection, BRAINMIXER leads by 6.2% average AUC-PR in edge-level detection, 5.7% in voxel-level, and 4.81% in brain-level detection over the best baseline, with the gap widening on the newly introduced BVFC and BVFC-MEG datasets where both voxel timeseries and connectivity are available.

## Relation to Prior Work

BRAINMIXER extends the MLP-Mixer paradigm (Tolstikhin et al., 2021) to the brain by replacing static mixing matrices with dynamic, functionality-aware attention, and by introducing brain-specific patching strategies (functional systems for voxels, temporal random walks for connectivity graphs). It builds on GRAPHMIXER (Cong et al., 2023) for temporal graph encoding and time encoding, and on CMI (Hjelm et al., 2019) / Bachman et al. (2019) for the mutual-information-based self-supervised objective. The key departure from prior brain representation learning is the explicit bridging of two previously separate scales—voxel-level activity timeseries and functional connectivity—within a single unsupervised framework, rather than treating them as independent inputs to separate models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental gap in neuroimaging ML: prior methods study the brain at only one spatial scale (voxel or ROI/network), missing complementary information. By providing new large-scale datasets (BVFC, BVFC-MEG) that include both voxel timeseries and functional connectivity, and by showing that the learned representations align with known neuroscience (e.g., GAN-generated images detected in higher visual cortex, ADHD anomalies in frontal/temporal regions), the work offers a practical path toward interpretable, label-free brain analysis. Open questions include scalability beyond the small subject counts in BVFC (3 subjects), whether the many architectural components (dynamic attention, temporal random walks, TPMIXER) are all necessary at scale, and generalization to clinical populations with larger, noisier datasets.
