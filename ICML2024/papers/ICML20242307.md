## Problem

Existing geometry-based and score-based coreset selection methods rely on heuristically designed metrics without theoretical guarantees on the generalization capability of models trained on the selected subset, while optimization-based methods offer theoretical grounding but scale poorly to large datasets. This paper bridges this gap by establishing a formal theoretical link between the geometric structure of a coreset (specifically, its ability to reconstruct the decision boundary) and the generalization performance of models trained on it.

## Input / Output

The method requires a full labeled training set (e.g., CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32, ImageNet-1K at 224×224) and a pre-trained classification model whose decision boundary serves as the reconstruction target. It outputs per-sample distance-to-boundary scores (integer values in [0, K] from PGD iterations) and a final coreset of size η·m. The distance estimation uses dataset-specific hyperparameters: step size 0.002 with K=10 for CIFAR-10, 0.001 with K=20 for CIFAR-100, and 0.0001 with K=50 for ImageNet-1K.

## Method

The paper proves (Theorem 1) that the decision boundary reconstruction error ε — the probability that two models (full-data vs. coreset-trained) disagree on a random sample — strictly upper-bounds the generalization gap, motivating the selection of samples that most influence the boundary. To identify these support vectors, the method applies PGD-style gradient ascent from each training point and records the minimum number of steps k needed to cross the decision boundary, yielding a distance score d(x,y)=k. Two selection strategies are then applied: BoundarySet simply retains the n samples with smallest d, while BoundarySet-CCS partitions samples into K+1 distance bins and performs stratified sampling across bins (redistributing surplus budget from underpopulated bins) to preserve distribution coverage at high pruning rates. This combination of boundary-proximity scoring and coverage-centric stratification achieves <1% accuracy drop at 50% pruning on ImageNet-1K, a first for any coreset method.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, BoundarySet-CCS achieves zero accuracy drop at a 50% pruning rate and retains 87.9% accuracy even at 90% pruning. On CIFAR-100, BoundarySet achieves 77.48% top-1 accuracy at 30% pruning (only 0.52% below full-data training), and BoundarySet-CCS reaches 75.5% at 50% pruning. On ImageNet-1K, the method achieves 73.5% top-1 accuracy at 30% pruning (a mere 0.1% decline from full data) and, for the first time, less than 1% accuracy drop when 50% of training samples are removed. At aggressive pruning rates (70–90%) on ImageNet-1K, BoundarySet-CCS yields 68.8%, 66.5%, and 59.1% respectively, with CCS contributing incremental gains of 0.4%, 2.6%, and 7.7% over plain BoundarySet.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BoundarySet-CCS outperforms all five baselines (Forgetting, EL2N, Moderate, AUM, and CCS) across every dataset and pruning rate evaluated. On CIFAR-100 at 30% pruning, BoundarySet achieves 77.48%, surpassing CCS by nearly 1 percentage point. On ImageNet-1K at 30% pruning, the method reaches 73.5% (only 0.1% below full-data training), a margin that none of the score-based baselines (Forgetting, EL2N, AUM) or geometry-based baselines (Moderate, Random) match. At high pruning rates (70–90%) on ImageNet-1K, the CCS component is critical: BoundarySet-CCS gains 0.4%, 2.6%, and 7.7% over plain BoundarySet, while distribution-coverage-focused baselines like Moderate and CCS maintain higher accuracy than difficulty-focused methods (Forgetting, EL2N, AUM) but still fall short of BoundarySet-CCS. The paper also demonstrates cross-architecture transferability: a coreset selected by ResNet-50 trained on SwinT yields 77.03% (vs. 78.46% no-pruning SwinT), a <1.5% gap, confirming that the selected support vectors are largely architecture-agnostic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the PGD adversarial perturbation algorithm (Madry et al., 2018) — originally designed to generate worst-case adversarial examples — as a geometric probe to estimate each sample's distance to a trained network's decision boundary, then selects the nearest samples as a coreset. It departs from prior geometry-based and score-based coreset methods (Core-Set, Moderate, Forgetting, EL2N, AUM) by providing a theoretical upper bound (Theorem 1) linking decision boundary reconstruction error to generalization gap, something those heuristic approaches lack. For high pruning rates where boundary-only selection loses distributional coverage, the paper incorporates the coverage-centric sampling (CCS) strategy of Zheng et al. (2022) as a stratified-sampling complement, yielding the BoundarySet-CCS variant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method is practically significant as the first to achieve 50% pruning on ImageNet-1K with under 1% accuracy loss, and the demonstrated cross-architecture transferability (consistency >0.5 across ResNet-50, ConvNeXt, and Swin-Transformer) means a single coreset can serve multiple model families, reducing redundant selection costs. A key limitation is the dependence on a pre-trained model for boundary distance estimation, and at very high pruning rates the degraded feature representation space undermines both the distance estimates and downstream training. Open questions include the interplay between coreset selection and feature extractor training dynamics, and whether the PGD-based distance proxy remains reliable under distribution shift or for non-i.i.d. data.
