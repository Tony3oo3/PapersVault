## Problem

Diffusion planners inherently generate stochastic, infeasible trajectories (artifacts) due to the probabilistic nature of diffusion sampling, yet existing methods base decisions on individual trajectories without filtering unreliable ones. This compromises reliability in safety-critical settings and forces planners to use extra denoising steps for quality, introducing latency that precludes real-time deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240529/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

TAT takes as input the current environment state and a batch of trajectories (typically 64–128) sampled from a pre-trained diffusion model at each planning step. Each trajectory element is either a state s_t or a state-action pair (s_t, a_{t-1}), depending on the underlying planner's format. The output is a single action for the current state, extracted from the highest-weighted child node in the tree—either directly from a state-action pair or via an inverse dynamics model. TAT operates entirely as a downstream, training-free post-processing step requiring no modification to the diffusion model's training or sampling pipeline.

## Method

TAT constructs a dynamic tree where each trajectory is a branch and each state is a node storing a set of similar trajectory elements, their decay-weighted visitation counts (λ^t), and a weighted-average canonical state. At each planning step, new trajectories are integrated by traversing from the root: nodes are merged when cosine similarity exceeds a threshold α (updating the weighted average), and new branches are expanded where no sufficiently similar node exists. Decision-making selects the child node with the highest accumulated weight, and the tree is pruned by re-rooting at the selected node after each environment transition. Theoretically, the probability of selecting an artifact is bounded by a function of the number of aggregated trajectories n and the per-trajectory artifact rate ε, decreasing monotonically with n and approaching zero as n→∞, which provides a tolerance margin that allows reducing denoising steps for >3× speedup without performance loss.

## Results

On MuJoCo locomotion (D4RL), TAT-enhanced Diffuser^γ achieves an average normalized return of 81.3 vs. 77.5 for vanilla Diffuser, and RGG^γ reaches 84.0 vs. 81.2 for RGG, with improvements in every single task (no performance drops). On Maze2D navigation, Diffuser^γ improves the single-task average from 119.5 to 126.2 and multi-task average from 129.4 to 136.2. On Kuka block stacking, Diffuser^γ scores 60.2 vs. 51.4 (Diffuser) and DD^γ scores 65.1 vs. 56.6 (DD). TAT also enables >3× planning speedup via warm-start^γ sampling, reducing per-action latency from 1.68s to 0.55s while maintaining or exceeding baseline performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAT-enhanced planners (Diffuser^γ, DD^γ, RGG^γ) outperform their respective vanilla counterparts in 100% of tasks across all three benchmarks, with the largest gains in complex environments (e.g., +8.3 on Hopper Medium, +10.7 on Maze2D-Large). Against model-free offline RL baselines, Diffuser^γ surpasses CQL (81.3 vs. 77.6 avg) and IQL (81.3 vs. 77.0) on MuJoCo locomotion, while in Maze2D the gap is dramatic (126.2 vs. 7.7 for CQL, 126.2 vs. 47.0 for IQL). Against model-based planners, Diffuser^γ and RGG^γ beat MOReL (81.3/84.0 vs. 72.9), MBOP (vs. 47.8), and MOPO (vs. 42.1) on MuJoCo. Against sequence-modeling baselines, TAT-enhanced planners outperform Decision Transformer (81.3 vs. 74.7) and Trajectory Transformer (84.0 vs. 78.9) on MuJoCo. In block stacking, Diffuser^γ and DD^γ exceed BCQ (0.0) and CQL (8.1) by large margins, and surpass vanilla Diffuser/DD by >15% overall. The only baseline that occasionally matches or exceeds TAT-enhanced planners is TT on individual MuJoCo tasks (e.g., 95.0 vs. 92.5 on HalfCheetah Med-Expert), but TAT-enhanced planners win on average and in the majority of individual tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions TAT as a training-free, inference-time post-processing layer atop existing diffusion planners (Diffuser, Decision Diffuser, RGG), departing from prior approaches that either rely on single raw trajectories or require additional training (e.g., RGG's gap predictor). Conceptually, it draws from ensemble learning's 'wisdom of the crowd' principle and tree-based search structures, but applies them to the specific problem of aggregating stochastic trajectory samples from diffusion models. Unlike prior diffusion planner improvements that modify the sampling or training pipeline, TAT operates entirely downstream, treating the diffusion model as a fixed black-box trajectory generator and building a dynamic tree over accumulated samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TAT addresses a fundamental reliability gap in diffusion planners—their inability to guarantee feasible trajectories—without any retraining, making it immediately deployable on existing models. The demonstrated tolerance margin for artifacts (enabling 3× faster planning by reducing denoising steps) is practically significant for real-time applications like autonomous navigation. Open questions include whether more sophisticated tree structures (e.g., incorporating spatial or temporal priors) could further improve performance, and whether the aggregation principle extends to other stochastic generative model domains beyond diffusion-based planning. The approach's simplicity also raises the question of whether the gains are near the ceiling for pure aggregation without learned selection criteria.
