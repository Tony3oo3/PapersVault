## Problem

Learning guarantees for nonlinear model classes have been developed separately for specific measurement types (regression, compressed sensing, imaging) and specific model classes (sparse vectors, neural networks, low-rank matrices), with no single theory unifying them. The paper asks: given arbitrary random linear measurements in a Hilbert space and a general nonlinear model class, what is the minimal number of samples needed for near-best generalization, and how does this depend on the interplay between the model class geometry and the sampling distribution?

## Input / Output

The framework takes an unknown object x in a separable Hilbert space, m independent random linear measurements of the form A_i(x) + e_i where each A_i is a bounded linear operator drawn from a (possibly different) distribution A_i, and a nonlinear model class U covered by a union of d finite-dimensional subspaces of dimension at most n. It produces an estimate x̂ via empirical least-squares minimization over U, along with explicit expected-error bounds of the form E||x − x̂||² ≲ (β/α)·best-approximation + θ²ε + N/α. Notably, measurements may be scalar- or vector-valued, multimodal (different distributions per sample), and the noise e_i can be adversarial (agnostic setting with x ∉ U).

## Method

The core technical contribution is the notion of *variation* Φ(V; A), the smallest constant bounding ||A(v)||² for all v in a set V almost surely under the sampling distribution A, which generalizes coherence in compressed sensing and the leverage score function in matrix sketching. The main theorems (3.3 and 3.4) establish that if the difference set U′ = U − U is a cone covered by a union of d subspaces of dimension ≤ n, then m ≳ α⁻¹·Φ(S(U′); Ā)·[n·log(2γ) + log(2d/ε)] samples suffice for near-best generalization, where γ is a ratio of variations over related sets. Theorem 3.4 sharpens this by assuming the unit ball of U′ lies in the convex hull of a small set W of size M, reducing the dependence on d and n from log(2d)+n to log²(log(2d)+n). The framework is then instantiated to derive Christoffel (leverage-score) sampling strategies for active regression and optimal variable-density sampling for generative compressed sensing with ReLU networks, recovering and improving prior results as corollaries.

## Results

The paper establishes general sample complexity bounds of the form m ≳ α⁻¹·Φ(S(U′);Ā)·[n·log(2γ(U′;Ā)) + log(2d/ε)] for learning from arbitrary linear measurements, where Φ is the variation of the model class. For the numerical sparse polynomial regression experiment (k=2, degree ≤20, N=231), Christoffel sampling achieves a constant θ(Ψ)≈9.73 compared to ϑ(Ψ)≈11,613 for inactive (i.i.d.) sampling — over a 1000× reduction in the sample complexity constant. For generative compressed sensing with subsampled unitary matrices, the framework yields m ≳ ‖σ‖²₂·n·(Σlog(2epᵢ/n) + log(2/ε)), and the Variable Density Sampling strategy derived from this bound shows visibly improved MRI reconstruction quality over uniform random sampling in the 3D brain image experiment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run head-to-head numerical benchmarks against named competing methods; instead, it positions its theoretical guarantees relative to prior results. Compared to (Adcock et al., 2023), the framework extends Christoffel sampling to more general nonlinear model classes and arbitrary (including multimodal) linear measurements, and improves the measurement condition by reducing the dependence on the number of subspaces d from linear to log²(log d + n) via the additional convex-hull assumption (Theorem 3.4). Compared to (Berk et al., 2023a,b) on generative compressed sensing with subsampled unitary matrices, the paper improves the bound by requiring local coherences σᵢ to be evaluated over the smaller set U−U rather than the piecewise-linear expansion Δ(U−U), with the latter entering only logarithmically; it also provides error bounds in expectation rather than high probability. Compared to classical compressed sensing with isotropic vectors (Candès & Plan, 2011), the framework generalizes to vector-valued and Hilbert-valued measurements, multimodal sampling, and nonlinear model classes, while recovering the classical m ≳ μ(𝒜)·s·(log²(s)·log(N) + log(ε⁻¹)) bound as a corollary. In the numerical experiments, Christoffel (active) sampling shifts the phase transition curve for sparse polynomial recovery substantially to the left of i.i.d. (inactive) sampling, and Variable Density Sampling for generative MRI reconstruction produces visibly sharper images than Uniform Density Sampling at the same measurement budget.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and generalizes several previously separate lines of work: compressed sensing with isotropic vectors (Candès & Plan, 2011), matrix sketching via leverage score sampling (Woodruff, 2014), active learning via Christoffel/leverage score sampling (Adcock et al., 2023), and generative compressed sensing with ReLU networks (Bora et al., 2017; Berk et al., 2023a,b). The key conceptual departure is the introduction of 'variation' as a single quantity that subsumes coherence, local coherence, block coherence, and the Christoffel function, replacing the need for problem-specific measurement conditions. The framework also extends prior work by allowing multimodal sampling (different distributions per measurement), vector-valued measurements, and non-strict isotropy (α ≠ β), while recovering all known results as corollaries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified framework provides a single theoretical lens through which to analyze sample complexity across a wide range of learning problems, reducing the need for ad hoc analysis in each setting. The Christoffel sampling strategy derived here is provably near-optimal for sparse regression and generative compressed sensing, with the numerical experiments showing order-of-magnitude improvements over uniform sampling (θ(Ψ) ≈ 9.7 vs. ≈ 11,613). Open questions include whether variation is a fundamental (necessary) condition analogous to coherence in classical compressed sensing, whether the framework extends to nonlinear measurements and Banach spaces, and whether non-uniform (instance-dependent) analysis can sharpen the uniform guarantees. The paper does not address the algorithmic challenge of finding minimizers of the nonconvex empirical risk.
