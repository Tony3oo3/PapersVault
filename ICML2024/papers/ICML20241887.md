## Problem

Least-privilege learning (LPL) has been repeatedly proposed as a solution to unintended information leakage in MLaaS settings, but it has only been stated informally and prior work evaluates only a single fixed sensitive attribute. This paper asks whether it is fundamentally possible to learn representations that are useful for a task while preventing inference of *any* other data attribute, and proves that under realistic assumptions on the data distribution, this is impossible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241887/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes raw data records (face images from LFWA+, tabular records from Adult and Texas Hospital) and produces a feature representation Z = f_E(X) via a learned encoder. The critical distinction from prior work is that leakage is evaluated against *all* possible sensitive attributes S (not a single fixed one), and the adversary's gain is measured relative to the fundamental leakage already incurred by revealing the task label Y itself. The representation is tested by Bayes-optimal adversaries using a Random Forest classifier trained on an auxiliary dataset, with the key metric being the multiplicative inference gain I_∞(S; Z | Y) normalized by the label-only baseline.

## Method

The paper formalizes the least-privilege principle as a variant of the conditional entropy bottleneck, using maximal leakage (Issa et al., 2019) as the leakage measure to capture worst-case inference risk over all possible sensitive attributes. The central result (Theorem 2) proves that under strict positivity of the posterior P_{Y|X} (i.e., inherent label uncertainty), no feature map can simultaneously achieve utility I_α(Y;Z) > γ and satisfy the LPP with parameter γ, because strict positivity makes the support of X independent of Y, so conditioning on Y does not reduce maximal leakage. Empirically, the trade-off is validated across CNN256, ResNet-18, and TabNet architectures, using standard ERM, gradient reversal (GRAD) censoring, and MAX-ENT adversarial representation learning, demonstrating a 'whack-a-mole' effect where censoring one attribute increases leakage of another. The paper also shows that LDP implies LPP, meaning LPL cannot offer a better utility-privacy trade-off than differential privacy.

## Results

The paper's central result is Theorem 2: under a strictly positive posterior (Assumption A), no feature representation can simultaneously achieve utility I_α(Y;Z) > γ and satisfy the LPP with parameter γ, for α ∈ {1, ∞}. Empirically, on LFWA+ (12 binary face attributes, CNN256), for every learning task Y there exists at least one sensitive attribute S where the adversary's conditional inference gain Δ_Adv = I̅_∞(S;Z|Y) − I̅_∞(Y;Z) > 0, confirming the trade-off. The same pattern holds on the Adult dataset (TabNet, MAX-ENT censoring: 2 of 4 sensitive attributes violate LPP) and the Texas Hospital dataset (5.2M records, TabNet), as well as across ResNet-18, multiple hidden layers, and gradient-sharing in collaborative learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three representation-learning strategies: standard empirical risk minimization (ERM/SGD), gradient reversal (GRAD, Raff & Sylvester 2018) for attribute censoring, and Maximum Entropy Adversarial Representation Learning (MAX-ENT, Zhao et al. 2020). None of these methods satisfies the LPP: under standard ERM on LFWA+, every task leaks at least one attribute beyond its fundamental leakage (Δ_Adv > 0). GRAD successfully suppresses the censored attribute but exhibits a 'whack-a-mole' effect—another attribute's leakage increases to compensate, still violating the LPP. MAX-ENT on the Adult dataset shows the adversary's inference gain exceeding model utility for 2 out of 4 sensitive attributes ('age', 'education', 'race', 'sex'). The paper further shows that LDP implies LPP (Proposition 2), meaning least-privilege learning cannot offer a better utility–privacy trade-off than differential privacy, contradicting the hope expressed by prior works (Melis et al. 2019; Osia et al. 2018) that LPL would be a lighter-weight alternative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Conditional Entropy Bottleneck framework of Fischer (2020) and the maximal leakage measure of Issa et al. (2019) to provide the first formalization of the least-privilege principle (LPP) for machine learning. It departs from prior attribute-obfuscation work (Song & Shmatikov 2019; Zhao et al. 2020) in two key ways: it considers all possible sensitive attributes simultaneously rather than a single fixed one, and it conditions leakage on the task label Y to account for fundamental leakage. The paper also positions LPL against local differential privacy, showing that LDP implies LPP, thereby connecting the two privacy paradigms formally.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper proves a fundamental impossibility: under realistic assumptions (strictly positive posterior), no feature representation can simultaneously have high utility for a task and satisfy the LPP, regardless of the learning technique or architecture. This debunks a widely-suggested solution (from Melis et al. 2019, Osia et al. 2018, Brown et al. 2022) to unintended feature leakage in MLaaS settings and shows LPL cannot outperform differential privacy on the utility-privacy trade-off. Open questions include whether relaxing the strict-positivity assumption or restricting the class of inference targets (e.g., to 'efficiently identifiable' attributes) could yield a more favorable trade-off, and how to practically inform users about fundamental leakage risks.
