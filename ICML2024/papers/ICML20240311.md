## Problem

ODE-based diffusion sampling trajectories empirically exhibit a consistent linear-nonlinear-linear 'boomerang' shape regardless of generated content, but no theoretical explanation existed for this regularity. This gap in understanding prevented principled design of time schedules for accelerated sampling, leaving practitioners to rely on handcrafted heuristics (uniform, quadratic, log-SNR) that do not account for the underlying trajectory geometry.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240311/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires a trained diffusion model (EDM parameterization with σ_t = t) and a set of 256 Gaussian noise warmup samples to compute a cost matrix over a 61-point fine-grained timestamp grid. It produces two outputs: (1) an optimized set of intermediate timestamps applicable to any ODE solver at a given NFE budget, and (2) generated images at resolutions from 32×32 (CIFAR-10) to 256×256 (LSUN Bedroom) in pixel space, or 512×512 in latent space via Stable Diffusion v1.4. The time schedule is computed once per dataset and reused for all subsequent generations.

## Method

The paper identifies an implicit denoising trajectory coupled to each sampling trajectory, showing that each Euler step is a convex combination of the current position and the denoising output, with the ratio σ_{t_n}/σ_{t_{n+1}} controlling the blend. Under a Gaussian KDE approximation of the data distribution with time-varying bandwidth, the denoising trajectory admits a closed-form solution equivalent to annealed mean shift, which naturally produces the observed linear-nonlinear-linear structure: linear at high noise (single-mode Gaussian), nonlinear at medium noise (multi-modal transition), and linear again at low noise (attraction to a single data point). Leveraging this shape regularity, the paper formulates time schedule optimization as a minimum-cost path problem solved by dynamic programming, where pairwise costs measure local truncation error between Euler steps and high-order ground-truth predictions computed from warmup samples.

## Results

The paper's GITS (Geometry-Inspired Time Scheduling) method, applied on top of iPNDM, achieves the best FID across all four datasets at every NFE level tested (5, 6, 8, 10). On CIFAR-10 32×32, iPNDM+GITS reaches FID 8.38 at 5 NFE and 2.49 at 10 NFE, compared to 13.59 and 2.77 for iPNDM alone. On ImageNet 64×64, the improvement is even more pronounced: 10.79 vs. 18.99 at 5 NFE. On Stable Diffusion v1.4 (MS-COCO), DPM-Solver++(2M)+GITS achieves FID 15.53 at 10 NFE versus 17.16 without GITS, and 12.26 vs. 14.72 at 16 NFE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

iPNDM+GITS consistently outperforms all compared ODE-based samplers across every dataset and NFE budget. At 5 NFE on CIFAR-10, iPNDM+GITS (FID 8.38) beats iPNDM (13.59) by 5.21, AMED-Plugin (not available at 5 NFE), and UniPC (23.98) by a wide margin. On ImageNet 64×64 at 5 NFE, the gap is even larger: 10.79 vs. 18.99 (iPNDM), 14.75 (DEIS-tAB3), and 24.36 (UniPC). At 10 NFE, iPNDM+GITS still leads on all datasets (e.g., 2.49 on CIFAR-10 vs. 2.48 for AMED-Plugin, 2.77 for iPNDM, 2.89 for UniPC), though the margin narrows. The concurrent AYS method (Sabour et al., 2024) achieves FID 12.48 on Stable Diffusion v1.4 at 10 steps, while GITS reaches 12.02, confirming GITS's advantage. The time schedule ablation (Table 3) shows that GITS's dynamically optimized schedule substantially outperforms handcrafted alternatives (uniform, log-SNR, polynomial) for both DDIM and iPNDM, with the largest gains at low NFE (e.g., 8.38 vs. 13.59 for iPNDM-polynomial at 5 NFE on CIFAR-10).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the PF-ODE formulation from Song et al. (2021c) and the EDM experimental framework (Karras et al., 2022), but its core conceptual contribution is a bridge to classical non-parametric statistics: the implicit denoising trajectory in diffusion sampling is shown to be analogous to annealed mean shift with a time-varying KDE bandwidth. While prior work (Chen et al., 2023a) empirically observed the 'boomerang' trajectory shape, and Karras et al. (2022) noted the denoising output reflects the trajectory tangent, this paper provides the first closed-form theoretical explanation via KDE and characterizes the linear-nonlinear-linear structure as a natural consequence of mode-seeking under decreasing bandwidth. For the practical acceleration component, GITS is positioned as orthogonal to and complementary with existing ODE solvers (iPNDM, DPM-Solver, UniPC), and contrasts with the concurrent AYS (Sabour et al., 2024) by leveraging trajectory geometry rather than stochastic calculus to optimize time schedules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The trajectory regularity insight provides a unifying geometric explanation for why existing heuristics (dense steps near t=0, large first step, shared schedules across samples) work, and the KDE/mean-shift connection offers a principled lens for understanding diffusion sampling that could inform future solver design. GITS delivers substantial FID improvements (e.g., 13.59→8.38 on CIFAR-10 at 5 NFE) with negligible overhead (~30 seconds of warmup), making it a practical drop-in for any ODE-based sampler. However, the KDE analysis is asymptotic and not directly applicable to trained neural network models, and the 'warmup' cost, while small, is non-trivial for very large models. Open questions include whether deeper geometric structures exist in the trajectories, whether the regularity holds for flow-matching or other generative paradigms, and whether the time schedule can be learned end-to-end rather than via dynamic programming.
