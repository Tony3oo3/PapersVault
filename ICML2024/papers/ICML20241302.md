## Problem

Multi-label image recognition methods that leverage CLIP typically require large-scale annotated image data for fine-tuning, which is expensive and not scalable. Existing zero-shot multi-label approaches still depend on seen-class annotated images during training. This paper asks whether a multi-label classifier can be trained entirely on language data and then transferred to the visual modality without any annotated images.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241302/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At training, the method consumes 40,000 LLM-generated text sentences (each describing a combination of labels) encoded by a frozen CLIP text encoder into 768-d embeddings. At inference, a 224×224 RGB image is processed by a frozen CLIP image encoder to produce one global embedding and a spatial feature map (K×768) that is split into K local embeddings. The output is a vector of N class probabilities, computed by averaging a global score and a max-pooled local score per class.

## Method

The core idea is to train a single linear multi-label classifier on CLIP text embeddings of LLM-generated descriptions (using a ranking loss with margin m=1), then transfer it to images via a cross-modal mapping that bridges the modality gap. Specifically, at inference each image embedding (global and local) is expressed as a softmax-weighted linear combination of N pre-computed label-specific text embeddings (from the template 'a photo of a/an [c]'), pulling it into the text embedding space where the classifier was trained. The mapped embedding is then fused with the original image embedding via a fixed weight α (0.4–0.7) to retain visual information lost in the mapping. Local spatial embeddings are classified independently and aggregated per class by taking the maximum score across regions, which is then averaged with the global score to produce the final multi-label prediction.

## Results

On the zero-shot multi-label recognition setting (no image data used for training), CoMC achieves mAP of 68.7% on MS-COCO, 89.4% on VOC2007, and 48.2% on NUS-WIDE. These results surpass the strongest text-data-based baseline TaI-DPT by 3.6%, 1.1%, and 1.7% mAP respectively, and outperform the original Zero-shot CLIP by 21.4%, 13.2%, and 11.8% mAP. On NUS-WIDE, CoMC also beats the best image-training-based method DualCoOp by 4.6% mAP (48.2 vs. 43.6) despite using no annotated images. In the few-shot comparison, CoMC's zero-shot result (89.4% on VOC2007) already exceeds 4-shot CoOp-DPT (88.2%) and approaches 8-shot CoOp-DPT (90.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest text-data-based competitor TaI-DPT (Guo et al., 2023), CoMC wins on all three datasets by 3.6% (MS-COCO), 1.1% (VOC2007), and 1.7% (NUS-WIDE) mAP, attributed to LLM-generated text covering richer label combinations versus public caption data. Against the original Zero-shot CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and CLIP-DPT (Guo et al., 2023), CoMC achieves large margins of 11.8–21.4% mAP, confirming that CLIP's native similarity-based classifier is poorly suited to multi-label tasks. Among image-training-based zero-shot methods on NUS-WIDE, CoMC (48.2% mAP) outperforms DualCoOp (Sun et al., 2022) by 4.6%, MKT (He et al., 2023) by 10.6%, and all earlier methods (CONSE, LabelEM, Fast0Tag, LESA, BiAM, SDL) by 21.9–41.1% mAP, despite using no annotated images at all. In the few-shot setting, CoMC's zero-shot score of 89.4% on VOC2007 already surpasses 4-shot CoOp-DPT (88.2%) and 4-shot CoOp (83.8%), and its 68.7% on MS-COCO exceeds 4-shot CoOp-DPT (67.6%) and 8-shot CoOp (59.2%), though 8-shot CoOp-DPT (68.1%) and 16-shot CoOp-DPT (68.9%) on MS-COCO remain slightly ahead.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP-based prompt-tuning lineage (CoOp, DualCoOp) and the text-as-image paradigm of TaI-DPT (Guo et al., 2023) by training a cross-modal classifier entirely on LLM-generated text rather than treating text as a proxy for images in prompt tuning. The key conceptual departure is the cross-modal mapping step: instead of learning a mapping network or adding contrastive losses, the authors represent image embeddings as a softmax-weighted linear combination of label-specific text embeddings, then fuse the mapped and original embeddings. This positions the work at the intersection of LLM-based data generation and vision-language alignment, building directly on CLIP's shared embedding space while eliminating all image annotation from training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that multi-label recognition—a task where collecting large-scale annotated image data is notoriously expensive—can now be addressed with only LLM-generated text, making the pipeline far more scalable and accessible. However, the method still trails few-shot approaches that use even a handful of labeled images per class, and the simple text template ('a photo of a/an [c]') limits how much visual information the mapping can capture. Open questions include whether the fixed linear mapping generalizes to very large or open-vocabulary label sets, whether the mapping weights could be learned end-to-end, and whether the approach extends to tasks requiring spatial reasoning beyond per-region max pooling.
