## Problem

State-of-the-art LLMs can refine their reasoning on math and coding tasks, but this ability is brittle: models struggle to identify when a solution needs refinement and where the first error occurs without external feedback. Outcome-based reward models (ORMs) generalize poorly to intermediate steps due to over-pessimism tied to the data-generating policy's failure modes, while process-based reward models (PRMs) require expensive human step-level annotation. The paper asks whether a fully synthetic pipeline can decompose refinement into when, where, and how to correct, using only a single student model's own rollouts.

## Input / Output

The system takes a question Q and a greedy draft solution A_D from the student model; for local refinement it additionally receives an error location indicator E, implemented as a '[BAD]' token prefixing the first incorrect step S_i in the draft. The SORM outputs a binary correctness label at every intermediate step, while the ORM outputs a scalar probability of final-answer correctness used as a reranking score. The refinement models produce a full corrected solution trace A_R; at test time the ORM selects the best among the original draft, the global refinement, and the local refinement.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage pipeline: (1) an ORM decides when to refine by scoring final-answer correctness; (2) a Stepwise ORM (SORM) localizes the first error by predicting step-level correctness, trained on synthetic labels generated by rejection-sampling the student policy K=8 times from each prefix P_i and labeling S_i positive if any rollout reaches the correct answer (approximating the optimal value function V*); (3) complementary global and local refinement models decide how to correct, trained on synthetic pairs of incorrect and correct rollouts. The SORM training data is post-processed with backward label propagation, a string-consistency constraint on intermediate results, and per-prefix-length label balancing to prevent positional bias. At inference, both global and local refinements are generated and the ORM reranks them alongside the original draft, exploiting the fact that the two refinement types solve largely disjoint problem subsets. The entire pipeline uses only synthetic data from a single Expert-Iteration-trained student, requiring no human annotations or stronger-model feedback.

## Results

On GSM8K, combining global and local refinements with SORM-based error localization and ORM reranking improves the LLaMA 2-chat 13B model from 53% to 65% greedy accuracy (a 12-point gain). The SORM outperforms the ORM on intermediate step-level accuracy by up to 8% (73%→81% for 13B on GSM8K), while the ORM retains a 5% edge on final-answer correctness. When applied only to incorrect drafts, the combined global+local+SORM strategy fixes 41.2% of failed 13B GSM8K solutions, versus 28.1% for global refinement alone and 28.3% for local refinement alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five refinement strategies on incorrect drafts: global refinement alone, local refinement with ORM, local refinement with SORM, and two combined (global+local) variants. The combined global+local+SORM approach dominates all individual strategies across every benchmark and model size, fixing 41.2% of incorrect 13B GSM8K drafts versus 28.1% (global) and 28.3% (local+SORM) individually. Against the best-of-three (Bo3) sampling baseline from the student, the ORM-reranked combined refinement still gains ~8% on GSM8K, while on SVAMP the Bo3 baseline is more competitive due to the easier task. The SORM also outperforms the standard ORM on step-level accuracy (Table 1: 81% vs 73% for 13B GSM8K) and the balanced ORM, confirming that rejection-sampling-based step labels yield better error localization. However, the ORM remains superior to the SORM for final-answer reranking (Table 2: 85% vs 80% for 13B GSM8K), which is why the ORM is used as the reranker in the final pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ORM framework (Cobbe et al., 2021) by introducing Stepwise ORMs (SORMs) that approximate the optimal value function V* via rejection sampling, offering a fully synthetic alternative to human-annotated PRMs (Lightman et al., 2023). The student model is trained via Expert Iteration (Silver et al., 2017), and the global refinement training directly reuses the incorrect-to-correct pairing strategy from Welleck et al. (2022). The key conceptual departure is the explicit decomposition of self-refinement into when (ORM), where (SORM), and how (global/local refiners) to refine, rather than treating refinement as a monolithic task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that meaningful self-refinement is achievable without any external feedback (no humans, no stronger models, no tools), which is a practical constraint for deployment. The finding that global and local refinements solve largely disjoint problem sets, and that ORM reranking of both plus the draft significantly outperforms best-of-three sampling, provides a concrete recipe for improving reasoning accuracy. However, the evaluation is limited to two math benchmarks (GSM8K, SVAMP) and two model sizes (7B, 13B), and the 12% improvement is on an already RL-fine-tuned model, leaving open whether the approach transfers to harder or more diverse reasoning tasks. A key limitation is that SORM quality is tightly coupled to the student policy's strength, as the ORM's over-pessimism at intermediate steps directly degrades error localization.
