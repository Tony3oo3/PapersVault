## Problem

While extensive work has characterized what functions GNNs can represent (expressiveness), it remains unclear whether GNNs actually learn the desired generalizable functions during gradient-based optimization. This paper addresses this gap by studying GNN training dynamics in function space, asking how the graph structure implicitly biases the optimization trajectory toward (or away from) well-generalizing solutions.

## Input / Output

The proposed Residual Propagation (RP) algorithm requires only the graph adjacency matrix A and the labeled node labels as input—no node features, no trainable parameters, and no back-propagation. It produces node predictions (class scores or regression values) for all nodes after a small number of propagation steps. The generalized RP variant additionally accepts node features through an arbitrary kernel function (e.g., Gaussian), but the base version operates purely on graph topology. The algorithm is linear in graph size and requires only storage for prediction vectors, consuming up to 10× less memory than GNNs.

## Method

The paper derives the training dynamics of GNNs in function space, showing that gradient descent updates are governed by a Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) that naturally aligns with the graph adjacency matrix—a phenomenon termed kernel-graph alignment. In the overparameterized regime, explicit recursive formulas for the node-level GNTK reveal how the adjacency matrix is structurally embedded into the kernel at each propagation layer. Building on this insight, the authors propose Residual Propagation (RP), a parameter-free algorithm that replaces the NTK with the sparse adjacency matrix A^K in the residual update equation, simultaneously minimizing loss and producing predictions by propagating errors along graph edges. Theoretical analysis connects kernel-graph alignment to generalization: on homophilic graphs, alignment with A promotes alignment with the optimal label kernel Θ*, yielding favorable generalization, while on heterophilic graphs it hinders generalization. RP is shown to be equivalent to kernel regression at convergence and to encompass Label Propagation as a special case, achieving GNN-competitive accuracy on 15 benchmarks with up to 1000× speedup.

## Results

On the three OGB benchmarks, the parameter-free Residual Propagation (RP) algorithm achieves 70.06% test accuracy on Arxiv, 78.17 ROC-AUC on Proteins, and 78.25% test accuracy on Products, outperforming the full-batch GCN on Proteins (+5.66) and Products (+2.61) while being slightly below GCN on Arxiv (−1.68). RP also surpasses Label Propagation on all three datasets (e.g., 78.17 vs. 74.73 on Proteins) and LinearGNN on all three (e.g., 78.25 vs. 74.21 on Products). Each RP step is 12–14× faster than a GCN gradient step, and RP reaches GNN-level performance in up to 1000× less total wall-clock time while using up to 10× less memory. Across 15 additional homophilic and heterophilic benchmarks (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed, Computer, Photo, CS, Physics, roman-empire, amazon-ratings, minesweeper, tolokers, questions), RP consistently outperforms most popular GNNs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the full-batch GCN, RP wins on Proteins (78.17 vs. 72.51 ROC-AUC, +5.66) and Products (78.25 vs. 75.64 accuracy, +2.61) but trails slightly on Arxiv (70.06 vs. 71.74, −1.68). Against LinearGNN (SGC), RP dominates on all three OGB datasets, with the largest gap on Proteins (78.17 vs. 62.89, +15.28) and Products (78.25 vs. 74.21, +4.04). Against Label Propagation, RP improves on every dataset (e.g., +3.44 on Proteins, +3.91 on Products), confirming that the iterative residual-update mechanism adds value over a single-pass propagation. Notably, RP achieves these results with zero trainable parameters, 12–14× per-step speedup over GCN, up to 1000× less total training time, and up to 10× less memory, making it a compelling efficiency alternative for large-scale graph classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends NTK-based training dynamics analysis (Jacot et al., 2018) from fully-connected networks to GNNs, but crucially works in function space rather than weight space, preserving non-linearity via the overparameterized regime. It builds on Label Propagation (Zhou et al., 2003) and kernel regression (Shawe-Taylor & Cristianini, 2004) to propose Residual Propagation, a parameter-free algorithm whose first step is provably equivalent to LP and whose convergence matches kernel regression. The key departure from prior GNN optimization work (Xu et al., 2021a; Zhang et al., 2020) is the introduction of kernel-graph alignment as a formal mechanism explaining how the adjacency matrix is implicitly leveraged during training, bridging the gap between expressiveness theory and actual learned behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The kernel-graph alignment framework provides the first interpretable, data-dependent explanation for when GNNs generalize (homophilic graphs) and why they fail (heterophilic graphs), resolving a long-standing empirical puzzle. The Residual Propagation algorithm demonstrates that graph structure alone—without any learned parameters—can match trained GNNs on 15 benchmarks with up to 1000× speedup, suggesting that much of GNN performance is attributable to the propagation mechanism rather than learned transformations. Limitations include the overparameterized regime assumption, restriction to node-level tasks with squared loss, and the fact that finite-width feature learning (where GNNs adapt their NTK over time) is only observed empirically without full theoretical treatment. Open questions include extending the framework to link prediction, graph-level tasks, and understanding how specialized architectures (signed propagation, heterophily-aware GNNs) modify the alignment dynamics.
