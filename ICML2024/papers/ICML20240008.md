## Problem

Post-training quantization of Transformer models to 2-bit precision suffers severe accuracy degradation, and existing methods offer no mechanism to fine-tune the accuracy-size tradeoff at fractional bit-widths (e.g., 2.1 or 2.2 bits). The fundamental issue is that quantization noise in the original weight space is highly sensitive to outliers and weight distribution, with no principled way to add controlled redundancy to improve robustness.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre-trained Transformer weight matrices (from ViT, DeiT, Swin, OPT, Llama2 families) and a small calibration set (128 images for vision tasks, or text for language modeling). It outputs quantized weights stored in a redundant Fusion Frame representation space at 2 bits per parameter (or 2.x bits with redundancy factor r > 1), plus per-row scale and bias values. Notably, the Fusion Frames themselves are not stored—only the triple (k, ρ, d) and a random seed are needed to regenerate them on the fly during inference, keeping overhead negligible.

## Method

FrameQuant transforms each layer's weight matrix Θ_l into a Tight Fusion Frame (TFF) representation via D_l = P_l^T Θ_l P_prev, where P_l and P_prev are orthonormal bases for the output and input FF subspaces respectively. Quantization (a modified GPTQ with Hessian-based iterative updates) is then applied to D_l rather than Θ_l, exploiting the fact that FF representations spread energy uniformly across subspaces, making the weight distribution well-behaved and amenable to 2σ outlier clipping. The redundancy factor r = kρ/d directly controls the effective bit-width (r=1 gives 2 bits, r=1.1 gives 2.2 bits), providing fractional-bit flexibility with a theoretical O(1/r) MSE reduction guarantee. During inference, activations are projected into FF space via the analysis operator, multiplied by the quantized D_l, and reconstructed via the synthesis operator, with the block-diagonal FF structure enabling efficient computation via Hadamard-based fast random projections.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K, FrameQuant at 2 bits (r=1.0) already matches or exceeds QuIP across all 15+ Vision Transformer models (e.g., 79.53% on ViT-B vs. QuIP's 79.54%), while GPTQ collapses to 29.26% on the same model. With a small redundancy increase to r=1.1 (2.2 bits), FrameQuant outperforms all baselines by a clear margin (e.g., 80.93% on ViT-B, 84.42% on Swin-L) and closes most of the gap to full precision (85.10%, 85.95% respectively). On language models, FrameQuant at 2 bits achieves 14.85 perplexity on Llama2-7B WikiText2, substantially better than QuIP (26.02) and GPTQ (6.4×10³), and at 2.2 bits reaches 8.48, approaching the full-precision 5.68.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FrameQuant decisively outperforms GPTQ at 2 bits across all model families: on ViT-B, GPTQ achieves only 29.26% top-1 accuracy while FrameQuant reaches 79.53% (r=1.0) or 80.93% (r=1.1); on Llama2-7B WikiText2, GPTQ's perplexity explodes to 6.4×10³ versus FrameQuant's 14.85. Against QuIP (the strongest 2-bit baseline), FrameQuant at r=1.0 is essentially tied or marginally ahead (e.g., 80.16% vs. 79.17% on Swin-B), and at r=1.1 pulls clearly ahead (e.g., 81.33% vs. 79.17% on Swin-B; 14.85 vs. 26.02 perplexity on Llama2-7B). PTQ4ViT, designed for 4-bit and above, degrades catastrophically at 2 bits (often below 1% accuracy on small models), making it an uncompetitive baseline in this regime. Against ZeroQuant (mixed-precision), FrameQuant at 2.2 bits achieves 80.02% MNLI accuracy, beating ZeroQuant at 2.66 bits (38.00%) by a wide margin. On Object Detection/Segmentation with Swin-T, FrameQuant matches RepQ-ViT at 6-bit and 4-bit but pulls ahead at 3-bit (e.g., 29.3 AP^box vs. 27.5 for MoBY Mask RCNN), confirming that the Fusion Frame advantage grows as bit-width decreases.

## Relation to Prior Work

FrameQuant builds directly on the iterative Hessian-based quantization procedure of GPTQ/OPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023), but re-frames the quantization to operate in a Fusion Frame representation space rather than the original weight space, drawing on Tight Fusion Frame construction from harmonic analysis (Casazza et al., 2011; Goyal et al., 1998). It is positioned as a concurrent but conceptually distinct approach to QuIP (Chee et al., 2023) for 2-bit quantization: where QuIP uses incoherence via random rotations as a pre/post-processing step, FrameQuant uses redundant subspace projections, and the paper shows QuIP is recoverable as the r=1, no-clipping special case of FrameQuant. The key departure from all prior PTQ methods is the introduction of a redundancy factor that yields fractional effective bit-widths (e.g., 2.1, 2.2 bits), a degree of freedom absent from GPTQ, QuIP, PTQ4ViT, and ZeroQuant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The fractional bit-width flexibility is practically valuable: when 2-bit accuracy is insufficient, existing methods force a jump to 3 bits (a 50% size increase), whereas FrameQuant allows fine-grained trade-offs via the redundancy factor r. The theoretical guarantees from Frame theory (O(1/r) MSE reduction for memoryless quantization, O(1/r²) for consistent reconstruction via LP) provide a principled foundation that prior heuristic PTQ methods lack. However, the inference speed penalty (~30% slower than GPTQ on Llama2-7B) and the failure to achieve 1-bit quantization even at r=1.8 limit near-term deployment impact. Open questions include whether hardware-aware kernel design can close the speed gap, whether QAT integration with Frame-based regularization further improves results, and whether the approach extends to activation quantization at extreme low bits.
