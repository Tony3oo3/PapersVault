## Problem

Deep RL agents trained on a limited set of environment levels often fail to generalise zero-shot to new levels, and the mechanism by which adaptive sampling strategies (e.g., value loss prioritisation) improve generalisation was only empirically justified. Additionally, existing unsupervised environment design (UED) methods that generate new training levels cause significant distributional shift, leading to poor test-time performance despite small generalisation gaps.

## Input / Output

The method takes a limited set of level parameters X_train (512 2D gridworld layouts encoded as tile-type vectors specifying object positions, start/goal locations, and navigability) sampled from the target CMDP distribution p(x), a pre-trained VAE whose encoder and decoder operate on these parameter vectors (not raw images), and access to a parametrisable simulator. It produces a trained PPO actor-critic policy (shared convolutional + LSTM backbone with separate actor/critic heads) and a growing buffer of generated level parameters that augment X_train during training. Generated levels are only admitted to the buffer if they are solvable (solved at least once during scoring rollouts).

## Method

DRED alternates between a generative phase and a replay phase. In the generative phase, a VAE trained on X_train produces new level parameters by interpolating in latent space between encodings of randomly sampled training-level pairs (following White 2016), grounding generation to p(x) and preventing the distributional shift that plagues unsupervised UED. In the replay phase, levels are sampled from the augmented buffer using a mixed adaptive distribution P_Λ that combines ℓ1-value-loss scoring (restricted to X_train) with a secondary score over the full buffer, where the mixing coefficient η is linearly scheduled from 0 to 1 to suppress out-of-context levels early in training. The key theoretical contribution is showing that value loss prioritisation acts as adaptive rejection sampling that prevents data with high mutual information I(L;b) from being generated, thereby minimising an upper bound on the generalisation gap (Theorem 2.1). This provides the first information-theoretic justification for why value loss scoring improves zero-shot transfer, and motivates DRED's joint objective of reducing I(L;b) while increasing |L| without incurring distributional shift.

## Results

On the Minigrid CMDP, VAE-DRED achieves a mean score of 0.82 on held-out test levels (X_test), compared to 0.67 for uniform sampling (U) and 0.68 for Prioritised Level Replay (PLR). On in-context edge cases, VAE-DRED scores 0.74, more than doubling the next-best method (DR at 0.37) and over 2× U (0.32). On 9× larger 'Hardcore' levels, VAE-DRED achieves 0.37 mean score and 46% solved rate, roughly 3–4× the performance of all baselines (best baseline: DR at 0.12/16%). VAE-DRED maintains a near-zero ShiftGap (0.00±0.04) throughout training, while UED baselines like ACCEL incur a ShiftGap of 0.66±0.17.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VAE-DRED outperforms all four baselines across every evaluation set. Against PLR (the strongest fixed-set baseline), VAE-DRED improves X_test mean score from 0.68 to 0.82 (+21%), edge-case score from 0.35 to 0.74 (+111%), and Hardcore score from 0.08 to 0.37 (+363%). Against uniform sampling (U), the improvements are similar in magnitude: 0.67→0.82 on X_test, 0.32→0.74 on edge cases, and 0.10→0.37 on Hardcore. Against UED baselines (DR, ACCEL, RPLR), VAE-DRED's advantage is most dramatic on harder settings: on Hardcore levels, VAE-DRED solves 46% of levels versus 16% (DR), 6% (ACCEL), and 2% (RPLR). The ablation EL-DRED (replacing the VAE with ACCEL's local-edit generator) confirms that grounding the generative model to p(x) is critical: EL-DRED outperforms all non-DRED baselines but is consistently and significantly outperformed by VAE-DRED on every evaluation set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Prioritised Level Replay (PLR) by providing the first theoretical justification for why ℓ1-value loss prioritisation improves generalisation: it acts as adaptive rejection sampling that prevents the agent's representation from encoding level identity, thereby minimising mutual information I(L;b). It departs from unsupervised environment design (UED) methods like ACCEL and RPLR by showing they induce harmful distributional shift, and instead grounds level generation in a VAE trained on the target distribution. The latent-space interpolation strategy for generation follows White (2016), and the generalisation bound framework extends Bertran et al. (2020) from ensemble policies to single-agent settings with adaptive sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MI-based theoretical lens gives practitioners a principled diagnostic (I(L;b) as a GenGap proxy that requires no held-out test set) and explains when adaptive sampling will or will not help (e.g., it fails in Maze where levels are trivially identifiable). The finding that UED methods learn a 'different CMDP' rather than failing to learn at all, revealed by the new ShiftGap metric, challenges the common assumption that more diverse training levels always improve transfer. However, all experiments are conducted in gridworlds (Minigrid) and Procgen, and the authors themselves note that distributional shift problems are likely to worsen in higher-dimensional parameter spaces, leaving open whether DRED's VAE-based grounding scales to practical robotics or driving environments.
