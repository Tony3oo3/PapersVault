## Problem

The prevailing belief in LLM compression holds that small-magnitude pre-trained weights are redundant and can be pruned without meaningful performance loss. This paper challenges that assumption by asking whether these 'non-significant' weights actually encode task-difficulty-dependent knowledge, and if so, whether their removal causes monotonic and irreversible impairment specifically on harder downstream tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242355/cropped_page3_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The study takes pre-trained transformer weights (RoBERTa-Base/Large, Vicuna-7B/13B) and applies one-shot compression—magnitude pruning, SparseGPT, Wanda, N:M structured sparsity, or GPTQ quantization—at controlled ratios. Performance is reported as normalized drop across six difficulty-controlled settings (data adequacy, MCQ option count, retrieval context length, k-shot demonstrations, human-LLM performance gap, factoid-vs-MCQ task type), with monotonicity quantified via Spearman rank correlation and Theil-Sen slope estimates across the difficulty spectrum.

## Method

Rather than proposing a new compression algorithm, the paper uses existing compression techniques as a quantitative probe to dissect the functional role of small-magnitude weights. The core mechanism is a 'Dense Transfer vs. Sparse Transfer' comparison: the dense model is fine-tuned normally, while the sparse model has small weights zeroed out (mask fixed) before fine-tuning, isolating the contribution of those weights to downstream performance. Across six difficulty spectra, they demonstrate that pruning small weights produces a monotonic impairment curve (harder tasks degrade faster with increasing sparsity), that GPTQ quantization fails to exhibit this monotonicity, and that the damage is irreversible—allowing pruned weights to regrow during fine-tuning does not recover performance on difficult tasks. A linear mode connectivity analysis further reveals that small weights specifically preserve the loss basin of the dense model on harder tasks, explaining why their removal is harder to repair.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is captured by Spearman's rank correlation between compression ratio and performance drop across task difficulty levels: one-shot magnitude pruning yields correlations of 0.975–1.0 (near-perfect monotonic impairment), while GPTQ quantization yields only 0.316–0.872 (no consistent monotonic trend). For easy tasks (e.g., 5-shot ICL on MMLU), up to ~14% of small-magnitude weights can be pruned with zero performance loss, whereas the hardest settings (0-shot, closed-book QA) show significant degradation even at 10% sparsity. In the irreparability experiments (Section 5), Dense Transfer with Freezing retains 90% of small weights frozen with no loss on easy tasks (SST-2, MNLI, QNLI), but on harder tasks (WinoGrande, CSQA) even allowing pruned weights to regenerate during fine-tuning (Sparse-to-Dense Transfer) fails to recover the performance of the dense model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper uses three compression baselines as probes rather than competitors. **GPTQ quantization** is the primary contrast: it fails to exhibit the monotonic impairment pattern that pruning shows, with Spearman correlations dropping to 0.316–0.399 on the retrieval-augmented QA difficulty spectrum (vs. 0.975–0.996 for pruning), meaning quantization cannot cleanly disentangle task difficulty. **SparseGPT** and **Wanda**, two weight-importance-based pruning methods, both corroborate the Junk DNA Hypothesis (monotonic impairment holds), but the authors note an important nuance: these more sophisticated methods tend to benefit simple tasks more than challenging tasks, widening the easy-vs-hard performance gap compared to naive magnitude pruning. In the irreparability study, the key comparison is between Sparse Transfer (prune then fine-tune with fixed mask) and Sparse-to-Dense Transfer (prune then allow weights to regrow during fine-tuning): the latter still underperforms Dense Transfer on hard tasks, confirming that the knowledge encoded in small-magnitude pre-trained weights is not recoverable through downstream training alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes standard compression techniques (magnitude pruning, SparseGPT, Wanda, GPTQ quantization) as diagnostic instruments to probe the functional role of small-magnitude pre-trained weights, rather than treating compression as an end goal. It extends the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis lineage (Frankle & Carbin, 2019; Jaiswal et al., 2023b) by introducing a task-difficulty-centric lens: instead of asking 'can we find a sparse subnetwork that works?', it asks 'how does the degree of pruning interact with task complexity?' The work is also inspired by Liu et al. (2023)'s SMC-Bench observations that SOTA sparse algorithms struggle on complex tasks, but goes further by defining quantitative difficulty spectra (within-task and across-task) and showing a monotonic impairment pattern specific to pruning that quantization fails to replicate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that small-magnitude weights encode irrecoverable knowledge for difficult tasks—even when fine-tuning is permitted—has direct practical implications for compression pipelines: a single 'sweet-spot' sparsity level cannot serve all downstream tasks, and aggressive pruning may cause permanent damage that no amount of downstream training can repair. The pruning-versus-quantization asymmetry (monotonic impairment for pruning, non-monotonic for quantization) suggests these two compression axes probe fundamentally different aspects of the weight distribution. Open questions include developing task-complexity-dependent dynamic inference, understanding why the loss basin is more sensitive to small-weight removal on hard tasks (the paper offers a linear-mode-connectivity conjecture but no full theory), and whether the human-LLM performance gap is a sufficiently general difficulty metric. Limitations include the relatively small number of difficulty levels per setting (3–6) and the acknowledged weakness of the relative-gap metric when both humans and models perform poorly.
