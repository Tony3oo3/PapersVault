## Problem

Leveraging pretrained text-to-image diffusion models for zero-shot video editing remains an open challenge, particularly for long videos with strong nonrigid motion and occlusions. Existing approaches enforce temporal consistency through extended attention or cross-frame feature correspondence, but these mechanisms struggle with complex motion, produce inconsistent textures, and fail when correspondences weaken over time. The paper identifies an underexploited signal: spatiotemporal slices of natural videos share statistical properties with natural images, yet no prior method has used a T2I model as a prior over such slices.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video volume (32–210 frames), a source text prompt describing the original content, and a target text prompt describing the desired edit, and outputs an edited video of the same length that adheres to the target prompt while preserving the original motion and layout. Because the underlying Stable Diffusion v2.1 latent space is 64×64×4, the method natively processes 64-frame volumes; shorter videos are upsampled via RIFE frame interpolation and resampled back, while longer videos are split into overlapping segments whose predicted noise is averaged at each diffusion step. The spatiotemporal denoiser operates unconditionally (empty prompt), while the extended-attention denoiser is conditioned on the source or target prompt depending on the inversion or sampling phase.

## Method

Slicedit inflates a pretrained T2I latent diffusion denoiser into a video denoiser via two modifications: (i) self-attention in each U-Net transformer block is replaced with extended attention that computes queries from the current frame against keys/values from three key-frames (one global frame at mid-video and two local frames within a 6-frame window), and (ii) the same pretrained denoiser is applied independently to y-t spatiotemporal slices of the video volume, exploiting their similarity to natural images. The final video denoiser is a variance-preserving weighted sum of the extended-attention denoiser (γ=0.8) and the spatiotemporal-slice denoiser (γ=0.2). For editing, the method performs DDPM inversion over the entire video volume to extract per-timestep noise vectors, then regenerates the video under the target prompt while directly injecting the source video's extended-attention maps into 85% of the sampling steps to lock in the original structure and motion. This volume-level inversion (as opposed to per-frame inversion) is shown to be critical for avoiding blurry, inconsistent results.

## Results

On a 60 text-video pair dataset, Slicedit achieves the best temporal consistency (Flow Error 0.252) and structure preservation (LPIPS 0.159) among all compared methods, substantially outperforming the next-best TokenFlow (Flow Error 0.4, LPIPS 0.35). Editing fidelity (CLIP-Text score) is competitive at 0.329, slightly below Pix2Video (0.343) and TokenFlow (0.334). In a user study with 50 workers, Slicedit is preferred over TokenFlow 82.6% of the time and over Rerender A Video 67.9% of the time for preserving the essence of the original video.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Slicedit decisively outperforms all four baselines on the two metrics most critical for video editing quality: Flow Error (motion preservation) and LPIPS (structure preservation). Against TokenFlow, the closest competitor, Slicedit halves the Flow Error (0.252 vs. 0.40) and more than halves LPIPS (0.159 vs. 0.35), though TokenFlow edges ahead on CLIP-Text (0.334 vs. 0.329). Against Rerender A Video, Slicedit wins on all three metrics, with a particularly large gap in Flow Error (0.252 vs. 2.50), reflecting Rerender A Video's reliance on key-frame propagation that breaks under complex motion. Against Pix2Video, Slicedit wins on Flow Error (0.252 vs. 0.55) and LPIPS (0.159 vs. 0.45) but trails slightly on CLIP-Text (0.329 vs. 0.343). Against ControlVideo (both edge and depth variants), Slicedit dominates across all metrics, with Flow Error roughly 10× lower and LPIPS roughly 4× lower, consistent with ControlVideo's design of regenerating appearance from a conditioning signal rather than preserving the original texture.

## Relation to Prior Work

Slicedit builds on two key prior components: the DDPM inversion framework of Huberman-Spiegelglas et al. (2023) for structure-preserving editing, and the extended attention mechanism introduced by Tune A Video (Wu et al., 2023a) for cross-frame coherence. It departs from concurrent zero-shot video editing methods (TokenFlow, Rerender A Video, Pix2Video) that enforce temporal consistency solely through extended attention or feature correspondence, by introducing a fundamentally different mechanism: applying the same pretrained T2I denoiser to spatiotemporal (y-t) slices of the video volume, leveraging the observation that these slices share statistical properties with natural images. This multi-axis denoising (spatial frames + spatiotemporal slices) is the paper's core architectural departure from prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method demonstrates that a pretrained image diffusion model can serve as a temporal consistency prior without any video-specific training, offering a practical zero-shot pipeline that handles long videos with complex nonrigid motion and occlusions where correspondence-based methods fail. The clear quantitative margins (LPIPS 0.159 vs. 0.35–0.71; Flow Error 0.252 vs. 0.4–3.0) and user-study preferences (82.6% over TokenFlow) suggest the spatiotemporal slice prior is a genuinely effective and underexploited signal. However, the method is limited to structure-preserving edits and cannot perform global style transfers or identity changes (e.g., dog → elephant), leaving open the question of whether the spatiotemporal slice prior can be combined with more flexible editing formulations to overcome this constraint.
