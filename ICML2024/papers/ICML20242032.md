## Problem

Prior work attributes SGD's success in deep learning to the implicit bias induced by finite batch sizes (SGD noise), but these results are limited to offline (multi-epoch) training. It remains unclear whether SGD noise provides any implicit bias advantage in the online (single-epoch) regime, which is the dominant setting for large-scale language model training. This paper asks whether small batch sizes confer implicit bias benefits in online learning or whether their only advantage is computational.

## Input / Output

The experiments consume standard training data (CIFAR-5m 32×32 images, ImageNet 256×256 images, C4 text sequences of length 2048) through ResNet-18, ConvNext-T, or GPT-2-small architectures, with the key experimental variable being batch size (ranging from 32 to 2048). Crucially, loss is measured as a function of gradient steps rather than data points seen, isolating implicit bias effects from computational efficiency. The primary outputs are loss trajectories over steps, early-stopping test loss, and average total variation (TV) distance of softmax probabilities on held-out test data to quantify functional similarity between trajectories.

## Method

The paper proposes and tests the 'Golden Path Hypothesis': in online learning, SGD with any batch size follows a noisy version of the noiseless gradient descent trajectory, so batch size affects only traversal speed and variance, not the path itself. Empirically, they show that in online learning (single epoch), lower-noise (larger-batch) runs consistently outperform higher-noise runs per gradient step across CIFAR-5m, ImageNet, and C4, in stark contrast to offline learning where higher noise wins. To distinguish the golden path from a 'fork in the road' scenario, they perform mid-training batch-size-switching experiments: increasing batch size at step T₀ causes the loss curve to 'snap' to a right-translation of the constant-low-noise curve, and decreasing batch size causes an immediate loss spike followed by tracking of the higher-noise curve. In function space, they measure TV distance between models and show that after noise reduction, the distance to a low-noise reference model drops to match the distance between two independent low-noise runs, confirming functional equivalence. A supporting theorem for quadratic losses proves that SGD noise only adds variance on top of the GD path in expectation.

## Results

Across three practical datasets (ResNet-18 on CIFAR-5m, ConvNext-T on ImageNet, GPT-2-small on C4), the paper demonstrates that in online (single-epoch) learning, lower SGD noise (larger batch size) consistently yields lower test loss per gradient step, whereas in offline (multi-epoch) learning, higher SGD noise (smaller batch size) eventually reaches a lower loss due to implicit bias toward flatter minima. When SGD noise is reduced mid-training (e.g., increasing batch size from 32→512 on CIFAR-5m or 64→512 on C4), the loss curve 'snaps' to a right-translation of the constant-low-noise trajectory within a few steps. In function space, the average total variation (TV) distance between a noise-reduced model and a low-noise reference model drops to match the TV distance between two independently initialized low-noise runs, supporting the Golden Path Hypothesis over the 'Fork in the Road' alternative.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between high-SGD-noise (small batch) and low-SGD-noise (large batch) training in both online and offline regimes. In offline learning, high-noise runs win: they initially track low-noise runs but eventually escape to lower-loss minima, consistent with prior work (Keskar et al., 2016; Masters & Luschi, 2018). In online learning, this advantage reverses: low-noise runs consistently achieve lower loss at every gradient step across all three datasets, indicating that SGD noise provides no implicit bias benefit when data is seen only once. The 'Fork in the Road' hypothesis (Scenario a2, where low noise explores a fundamentally different and better trajectory) is ruled out by the snapping experiment: after reducing noise mid-training, the loss and TV distance rapidly converge to the golden path rather than diverging to a new region. The paper also contrasts implicit regularization from SGD noise with explicit regularization (weight decay), showing that while weight decay can help in online learning (Figure 9), SGD noise cannot, further isolating the computational (rather than inductive) role of small batch sizes in the online regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of work on SGD implicit bias (Keskar et al., 2016; Masters & Luschi, 2018; Goyal et al., 2017), which established that smaller batch sizes yield flatter, better-generalizing minima in offline (multi-epoch) training. It explicitly contrasts with these findings by showing the opposite holds in the online (single-epoch) regime, where lower noise consistently outperforms higher noise per gradient step. The theoretical result for quadratic losses connects to Paquette et al. (2022a,b) on high-dimensional random features, while the empirical framing builds on the Deep Bootstrap framework (Nakkiran et al., 2021) that separates online and offline learning dynamics. The 'golden path hypothesis' is a new conjecture that synthesizes observations from network evolution literature (Nakkiran et al., 2019b; Hacohen et al., 2020) into a specific testable claim about SGD tracking the noiseless GD trajectory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since large language models and many self-supervised systems are trained in the online regime, the finding that SGD noise offers no implicit bias benefit there has direct practical implications: batch size selection should be driven purely by computational efficiency and stability, not by an expectation of regularization. The paper's suggestion that gradient descent on the population loss serves as a useful 'denoised' theoretical proxy for SGD in online learning opens a path toward simpler analyses of training dynamics. Key open questions include whether the golden path hypothesis extends to weight space (beyond loss and function space), whether it holds for non-convex landscapes beyond the quadratic toy case, and how it interacts with other hyperparameters like learning rate schedules and optimizers beyond plain SGD.
