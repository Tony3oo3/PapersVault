## Problem

GNNs are designed to combine node features with graph structure, but in many practical settings the graph is non-informative or only marginally useful, and the optimal solution ignores it entirely. It was unclear whether GNNs trained with gradient descent would naturally learn to disregard uninformative graphs or would instead overfit them, degrading test performance. This paper shows that GNNs systematically overfit the provided graph structure, even when a strictly better graph-less solution is realizable by the same architecture.

## Input / Output

The method operates on graph instances consisting of an adjacency matrix A and a node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d}, producing a graph-level class label via sum-pooling of node representations followed by a linear readout. A key architectural requirement is that the GNN must have separate root weights (W₁) and topological weights (W₂) per layer, so that zeroing W₂ constitutes a valid graph-less solution; architectures like GCN that share these weights are excluded. In the practical R-COV experiment, the input graph is modified by adding edges between low-degree nodes (tagged with a distinct edge feature value of 0.5 versus 1.0 for original edges) to reduce the coefficient of variation of node degrees before feeding the augmented graph to the GNN.

## Method

The paper combines empirical and theoretical analysis of the implicit bias of gradient descent on over-parameterized Message Passing Neural Networks. Theoretically, it shows that GD on linearly separable data converges to a max-margin solution where the effective input is the concatenation of the sum of node features and the degree-weighted sum of node features; on r-regular graphs, the KKT conditions force the topological weights to align with the root weights (w₂ = r·w₁), guaranteeing perfect extrapolation across different regularity degrees but not across arbitrary graph distributions. Empirically, the paper demonstrates graph overfitting by training the same fixed GNN architecture on both the original graphs and empty graphs, showing consistent accuracy drops when graphs are provided. The practical mitigation is an R-COV transformation that adds edges between low-degree nodes to reduce the degree coefficient of variation, with edge features preserving the distinction between original and added edges; this improves performance across 11 real-world datasets and 4 GNN architectures (GraphConv, GIN, GATv2, GraphTransformer) without requiring the graph to be fully regularized.

## Results

On the core graph-overfitting experiment (Table 1), training the same GNN on empty graphs (GNN_0) consistently outperforms training on the provided graphs: 97.5% vs 94.5% on Sum, 74.1% vs 67.4% on Proteins, and 64.1% vs 55.2% on Enzymes, confirming that GNNs overfit non-informative structure. In the practical R-COV experiment (Table 3), reducing the node-degree coefficient of variation improves accuracy across all 11 datasets and all four GNN architectures (GraphConv, GIN, GATv2, GraphTransformer). For example, GraphTransformer on Proteins improves from 73.9% to 76.7%, and on IMDB-M from 49.0% to 51.1%. Notably, on Proteins and IMDB-M, R-COV GNNs even surpass the DeepSet (empty-graph) baseline, indicating the graph contains useful information that the GNN can exploit once overfitting is mitigated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The DeepSet (empty-graph) baseline is the most critical comparison: on Proteins (74.1%) and Enzymes (64.2%), it outperforms every GNN trained on the original graphs, directly demonstrating that the GNNs overfit the provided structure. However, after R-COV modification, GraphTransformer on Proteins (76.7%) and GATv2 on Enzymes (63.9%) surpass the DeepSet baseline, showing the graph does carry useful signal that was being masked by overfitting. Among the four GNN architectures, R-COV yields consistent gains of 1–4 percentage points over the original-graph version on every dataset tested. GraphTransformer benefits most from R-COV on social-network datasets (e.g., Reddit-5k: 51.4% → 56.0%, a 4.6-point gain), while GIN shows the smallest relative improvement. On the large-scale molecular datasets (mol-hiv, mol-pcba), R-COV also improves AUC and AP respectively (e.g., mol-hiv AUC: 80.5 → 82.4 for GraphTransformer), confirming the effect generalizes beyond small benchmark graphs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit bias analysis of Gunasekar et al. (2018) from linear/convolutional networks to GNNs, asking whether gradient descent's preference for low-norm solutions helps GNNs ignore uninformative graph structure. It builds on the Message Passing Neural Network formulation of Gilmer et al. (2017) as the analytical substrate, and is motivated by empirical observations from Errica et al. (2022) and Chen et al. (2020) that set-based methods sometimes outperform GNNs. The key departure from prior implicit bias work is the negative finding: the standard max-margin bias does NOT lead GNNs to zero out topological weights when the graph is irrelevant, and the paper further connects this to the entanglement phenomenon between graph structure and node features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is significant: graph structure selection is a first-order design decision that practitioners have underappreciated, and a simple COV-reduction preprocessing step consistently improves performance across 11 datasets and 4 architectures. The theoretical analysis is limited to linear GNNs with sum-pooling, leaving the implicit bias of non-linear GNNs (attention, transformers) as an open question. The paper also raises the broader question of whether existing GNN regularization techniques (DropEdge, full-graph layers) mitigate graph overfitting, and whether the regular-graph robustness result extends beyond the linear regime.
