## Problem

Multi-Task Grouping (MTG) must identify the best partition of N tasks into K groups from 2^N candidates, but existing methods (HOA, TAG, MTG-Net) perform group identification and grouped-model training as separate sequential stages, introducing objective bias and relying on heuristic or pairwise-affinity approximations that fail to capture high-order task interactions. This paper asks whether group identification and grouped task learning can be unified into a single differentiable optimization that directly exploits high-order affinities.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (256×256 for Taskonomy, 64×64 for CelebA) along with a configuration of N input tasks and K candidate groups. It produces two outputs simultaneously: (1) per-task predictions (segmentation maps, depth, surface normals, keypoints, edges, or binary classification labels) from K group-specific encoder branches, and (2) a learned N×K Categorical assignment matrix that maps each task exclusively to one group. The network architecture itself is adaptive—starting with K branches each connected to all N task heads (KN heads) and progressively pruning to N heads total during training.

## Method

DMTG formulates MTG as a fully differentiable network pruning problem on an adaptive architecture. At initialization, K encoder branches are each connected to all N task heads (KN heads), ensuring full exploration of high-order task affinity. The group assignment is modeled as a Categorical distribution over K groups per task, parameterized by S ∈ R^{N×K} and relaxed via Gumbel-Softmax (Concrete distribution) to enable gradient flow. The final training loss is the element-wise product of the KN task losses and the relaxed assignment matrix, so backpropagation simultaneously updates both the encoder weights (grouped task learning) and the Categorical parameters (group identification) in one shot. A temperature annealing schedule drives the relaxed assignments toward one-hot vectors, guaranteeing each task is exclusively and uniquely assigned to a single group at convergence, eliminating the need for a validation set to disambiguate multi-group assignments.

## Results

On Taskonomy-5 (5 tasks), DMTG achieves a total loss of 0.173 (K=3), 0.170 (K=4), and 0.168 (K=5), corresponding to a NormGain_L of +63.85%, +64.58%, and +65.01% over Naive MTL, respectively. On CelebA-9 (9 classification tasks), DMTG achieves a total classification error of 52.97 (K=2) with a NormGain_E of +5.75% over Naive MTL. The method maintains O(K) encoder training complexity throughout, compared to O(N²)+O(K) for HOA and O(N)+O(K) for TAG.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMTG consistently outperforms all three baselines on both datasets. On Taskonomy-5 (K=3), DMTG reduces total loss to 0.173, beating HOA (0.190, −8.9%), TAG (0.210, −17.6%), and MTG-Net (0.191, −9.4%); the NormGain_L gap is even larger at +63.85% vs. +47.78% (HOA), +45.02% (TAG), and +57.83% (MTG-Net). On CelebA-9 (K=2), DMTG achieves 52.97 total error vs. HOA's 53.60 and TAG's 53.41, with NormGain_E of +5.75% vs. +3.27% and +4.38% respectively. MTG-Net is excluded from CelebA-9 due to its O(2^N) sampling cost. A key efficiency advantage is that DMTG achieves O(K) encoder complexity in a single training pass, whereas HOA and TAG require a separate group-identification phase followed by from-scratch retraining of K grouped networks, and MTG-Net requires sampling up to 2^N task combinations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Multi-Task Grouping (MTG) line of work initiated by HOA (Standley et al., 2020), TAG (Fifty et al., 2021), and MTG-Net (Song et al., 2022), all of which identify task groups and then retrain grouped models in a sequential two-stage pipeline. DMTG departs from this lineage by borrowing the differentiable pruning paradigm from neural architecture search, formulating group identification as learning a relaxed Categorical distribution (via Gumbel-Softmax) that simultaneously determines an adaptive network architecture and prunes task heads. This unifies group identification and grouped task learning into a single one-shot optimization, eliminating the objective bias inherent in the sequential approaches where groups are identified heuristically under full-task conditions but retrained under subset conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(K) encoder training complexity (independent of task count N) makes MTG practically viable for very large task pools where prior methods become infeasible, as demonstrated by the CelebA-40 experiment. The one-shot formulation also removes the need for a validation set to select among multiple group assignments, simplifying deployment. Key limitations remain: the Gumbel-Softmax relaxation introduces bias and temperature sensitivity, and the method's efficiency degrades when task heads dominate network computation (e.g., dense prediction heads on Taskonomy). Open questions include whether alternative discrete relaxation schemes can eliminate the Gumbel bias, and how to handle heavy-head architectures without the KN head expansion.
