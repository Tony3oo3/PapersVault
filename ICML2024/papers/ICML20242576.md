## Problem

Federated graph matching (FGM) remains an open problem: supervised graph matching requires pre-matched node pairs across clients, which directly violates the privacy constraints of federated learning, while unsupervised methods forgo the inductive strength of training data and yield sub-optimal solutions. No prior work addresses how to train effective FGM models on distributed clients that both achieve high matching accuracy and protect sensitive local graph data during cross-client information exchange.

## Input / Output

The method takes S distributed graphs (one per client) as input, where each client's network embeddings are encrypted with a shared random nonsingular matrix K (known to all clients but not the server) before upload. Pseudo training data is generated locally by computing per-node graphlet feature vectors via MCMC sampling and selecting top-K node pairs by cosine similarity across pairwise graphs. The output is a global graph matching model M that maps encrypted embeddings into a common space, from which one-to-one matched node pairs are extracted by nearest-neighbor retrieval, evaluated via Hits@K.

## Method

UFGM has two core components. First, an approximate graphlet enumeration method uses MCMC subgraph expansion (sampling O ≪ C k-subgraphs) with Horvitz-Thompson inverse probability weighting to estimate per-node graphlet counts, with theoretical guarantees (Theorems 1–2) bounding the estimator variance in terms of graph degree structure. Second, a separate trust region algorithm splits evaluation from optimization: the server aggregates local models, computes individual losses, gradients, and Hessians on encrypted embeddings and pseudo training data, while each client performs its own trust-region step. To avoid expensive second-order Hessian computation, the paper introduces a generalized weak quasi-Newton condition (Eq. 18) that combines the Dennis–Wolkowicz projection with a function-value interpolation, yielding a positive-definite scalar Hessian approximation α_b(ω)·I from first-order gradients alone, with convergence analysis (Theorems 3–4) characterizing the approximation error as O(‖z_b‖³) and O(‖z_b‖⁴) terms.

## Results

On PPI, UFGM achieves Hits@1 of 0.771, Hits@5 of 0.880, Hits@10 of 0.902, and Hits@50 of 0.930, with a final loss of 0.659. On SNS, UFGM reaches Hits@1 of 0.371, Hits@5 of 0.440, Hits@10 of 0.411, and Hits@50 of 0.459, with a loss of 0.501. These are the best results among all 16 federated and centralized baselines in the federated setting, and are within 15.3% of the best centralized method (NextAlign) on SNS. UFGM also converges fastest, reaching stable performance around 1,000 training rounds with 31.8% and 35.4% convergence speedup over other federated methods on SNS and PPI respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UFGM substantially outperforms all six federated graph learning baselines: on PPI, it achieves Hits@1 of 0.771 versus FKGE (0.231), SpreadGNN (0.115), FedStar (0.057), FedGraphNN (0.081), SFL (0.000), and FederatedScope-GNN (0.001); on SNS, it reaches 0.371 versus FKGE (0.177) and all others below 0.10. All four federated domain adaptation methods (DualAdapt, EFDA, WSDA, FedKA) produce near-zero Hits@K scores (≤0.016 on PPI, ≤0.013 on SNS), making UFGM's advantage over them overwhelming. Against the six centralized graph matching methods, UFGM is competitive but slightly below the best: on PPI, NextAlign (0.951) and SeedGNN (0.884) still lead UFGM (0.771) by 18.6% and 13.4% respectively, while UFGM clearly surpasses CPUGA (0.248), ASAR-GM (0.299), and SIGMA (0.499). On SNS, UFGM (0.371) trails NextAlign (0.430) by 15.3% but beats NetTrans (0.379 is close), CPUGA (0.230), ASAR-GM (0.199), SIGMA (0.220), and SeedGNN (0.319). UFGM also achieves the lowest loss among all methods on both datasets (0.659 on PPI, 0.501 on SNS), indicating better optimization quality despite operating under federated privacy constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions federated graph matching (FGM) as an open problem at the intersection of graph matching and federated learning, drawing on two distinct lineages: (1) the graphlet-based node representation literature (Shervashidze et al., Kondor et al.) repurposed here to generate pseudo training pairs via MCMC sampling with Horvitz-Thompson estimation, and (2) the quasi-Newton optimization literature, specifically the weak secant conditions of Dennis & Wolkowicz (1993) and the interpolation-based condition of Yuan (1991), which are combined into a generalized scalar Hessian approximation (αI) for a separate trust region algorithm. The key departure from prior federated learning work is the recognition that standard SGD fails in FGM because individual clients cannot compute cross-client losses, motivating the server-evaluates/client-optimizes separation. The GCN-based embedding follows standard practice in network alignment, while the encryption scheme (shared random nonsingular matrix) is a lightweight privacy mechanism distinct from differential privacy approaches used in other federated graph learning work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal treatment of federated graph matching, establishing the problem formulation, a working solution, and theoretical guarantees (MCMC variance bounds, Hessian approximation error, convergence) that the field can build upon. The practical motivation—financial crime detection and cross-platform user linking where data cannot be centralized—is compelling and timely given government-funded challenges (NSF/IBM 2022). However, several open questions remain: the encryption scheme relies on a shared key known to all clients (vulnerable if one client is compromised), the evaluation is limited to three datasets with relatively small scale, and it is unclear how the method performs under non-IID graph distributions or with heterogeneous client capabilities. The 15–20% gap to the best centralized method (NextAlign) suggests room for improvement, and whether the pseudo-supervised graphlet approach generalizes to other federated tasks beyond matching is unexplored.
