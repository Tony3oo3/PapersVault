## Problem

Zero-shot TTS methods condition on a single speaker vector extracted from short reference audio, which captures only a narrow, content-dependent slice of a speaker's characteristics (timbre, prosody, pronunciation) and often produces unnatural speech when the reference content mismatches the target text. Multi-speaker models express these characteristics faithfully but require retraining for every new speaker. The paper asks whether a speaker's full, content-dependent speech characteristics can be encoded and expressed at inference time without any training on that speaker's data.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a phoneme sequence (converted from text via IPA) and a pre-computed speaker codebook of 512 latent vectors (2048-dim each) derived from the target speaker's audio. The codebook is built offline by encoding the speaker's mel-spectrograms (80-dim, 22.05 kHz) through a VAE encoder and clustering the resulting μ values with k-means++; as few as one audio clip suffices (in which case raw μ values are used without clustering). The output is a synthesized speech waveform at 22.05 kHz, generated by a Glow-TTS-style flow model conditioned on the fused text-and-speaker features.

## Method

ELF operates in two stages. First, a Speech Feature Encoding Network (SFEN) — a variational autoencoder with a HiFi-GAN-style decoder and adversarial training (least-squares GAN loss + feature-matching loss) — encodes mel-spectrograms into a 2048-dim latent space fitted to a unit Gaussian prior; per-speaker k-means++ clustering of all μ frames yields a 512-vector codebook. Second, a Glow-TTS-based TTS model fuses the codebook into the text encoder's intermediate features: transformer blocks without positional encoding combine the codebook vectors, and multi-head attention (with the text encoder's 5th-layer output as query) produces a content-dependent weighted sum that approximates sampling a point in the continuous latent space. This attention-based fusion is the key mechanism that lets a single discrete codebook express different timbre/prosody for different utterances. The duration predictor is replaced with an adversarially trained generator, and a small transformer block with residual connections is inserted into the normalizing flows to capture long-range dependencies. Because the VAE's Gaussian prior keeps the latent space dense and continuous (unlike sparse speaker-verification embeddings), the codebook supports linear interpolation for speaker blending and cross-lingual synthesis.

## Results

On the combined LibriTTS and VCTK test set, ELF (all audio, unseen) achieves SMOS 3.85, MOS 3.98, CER 1.49, and SECS 0.888, surpassing the trained multi-speaker VITS (SMOS 3.73, MOS 3.95, CER 1.80, SECS 0.919) on speaker similarity and intelligibility despite never seeing the test speakers during training. Even in the extreme zero-shot setting with a single audio sample, ELF (SMOS 3.62, MOS 3.93, CER 2.35, SECS 0.848) outperforms YourTTS (SMOS 3.47, MOS 3.88, CER 5.42, SECS 0.817) across all four metrics. In the cross-lingual (Korean→English) setting, ELF achieves MOS 3.79, CER 2.84, and SECS 0.862, substantially outperforming YourTTS (MOS 3.29, CER 7.91, SECS 0.600).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against VITS multi-speaker (Kim et al., 2021), which was trained on the test speakers (Seen), ELF (all audio, Unseen) achieves a higher SMOS (3.85 vs. 3.73) and lower CER (1.49 vs. 1.80), while matching MOS (3.98 vs. 3.95); VITS retains a slight edge on SECS (0.919 vs. 0.888), but this advantage comes from having the target speakers in its training set. Against YourTTS (Casanova et al., 2022), ELF wins decisively on every metric even in the most constrained zero-shot setting (1 audio sample): SMOS 3.62 vs. 3.47, MOS 3.93 vs. 3.88, CER 2.35 vs. 5.42, and SECS 0.848 vs. 0.817. The CER gap is particularly large (2.35 vs. 5.42), indicating that YourTTS's reliance on a single short reference audio leads to substantially less intelligible output. In the cross-lingual experiment, ELF again outperforms YourTTS by wide margins (CER 2.84 vs. 7.91; SECS 0.862 vs. 0.600), confirming that the encoded latent codebook captures speaker identity more robustly than a single reference utterance.

## Relation to Prior Work

ELF builds on Glow-TTS (Kim et al., 2021) as its TTS backbone and HiFi-GAN (Kong et al., 2020) as its waveform decoder, but fundamentally departs from both the multi-speaker TTS paradigm (which requires per-speaker training) and the zero-shot paradigm (which conditions on a single speaker-verification embedding from short reference audio). The key conceptual shift is replacing the single speaker vector with a VAE-encoded, k-means++-clustered codebook of 512 latent speech features, which is then fused into the text encoder via attention—allowing the model to express content-dependent aspects of a speaker's voice (timbre, prosody, pronunciation) rather than a fixed, content-agnostic embedding. This addresses what the authors identify as the fundamental limitation of prior zero-shot methods: a single vector from short audio cannot capture how a speaker's characteristics vary across different utterance contents.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a discretized codebook of speaker features, combined via attention, is sufficient to reconstruct a speaker's speech nearly as well as ground truth (MOS 3.91 vs. 3.96) and to outperform a trained multi-speaker model in speaker similarity for unseen speakers—challenging the assumption that per-speaker training or continuous embeddings are necessary for high-fidelity speaker modeling. The cross-lingual result (Korean codebook → English synthesis, SECS 0.862) and the uniform speaker-blending behavior suggest the learned latent space has useful geometric structure. Open questions include scalability to very large speaker populations, real-time inference cost (VAE encoding + clustering + TTS), and whether the 512-vector codebook size is optimal or whether the approach degrades for speakers with highly variable vocal styles. The method also raises the practical concern of voice-cloning misuse, which the authors acknowledge in their impact statement.
