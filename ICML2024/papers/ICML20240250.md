## Problem

Standard feature attribution methods measure importance via perturbation tests that compare prediction differences, but they cannot distinguish features that produce similar prediction changes after perturbation—one may be a genuine cause while another is entirely irrelevant. The paper identifies that identical prediction changes can arise from fundamentally different causal mechanisms, motivating a causal attribution framework that captures the probability of necessity and sufficiency (PNS) rather than merely the magnitude of prediction shift.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240250/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

FANS takes a target input (a 28×28 grayscale image, 32×32 RGB image, or graph node feature vector) and an observed sample set drawn from the dataset, which is required for the SIR-based resampling step. It outputs a continuous feature subset mask (relaxed from binary to [0,1] via gradient optimization) identifying the subset with the highest PNS, along with per-feature attribution scores. For graph datasets where only a single graph is available, augmented sample sets are constructed by randomly masking adjacency matrices to supply the multiple samples FANS needs for resampling.

## Method

FANS formalizes feature attribution as a PNS measurement problem within a Structural Causal Model, defining the importance of a feature subset as the maximum PNS over all neighborhoods of the target input. The core mechanism is a dual-stage perturbation test implementing Pearl's Abduction-Action-Prediction counterfactual paradigm: the factual stage approximates complex conditional distributions via Sampling-Importance-Resampling with Gaussian kernel weights, and the interventional stage applies complementary perturbations to estimate PN and PS separately. Two modules (necessity and sufficiency) are combined into PNS, with the neighborhood boundary b set by the Scott rule and the prediction-change threshold c derived from the maximum variance under low-intensity Gaussian noise. A gradient-based optimization with relaxed binary masks extracts the feature subset maximizing PNS, replacing non-differentiable indicator functions with smooth exponential surrogates to enable end-to-end learning.

## Results

On image datasets, FANS achieves the best Infidelity (INF) across all three benchmarks: 9.0×10² on MNIST, 1.2×10⁴ on Fashion-MNIST, and 1.7×10⁴ on CIFAR-10, outperforming the second-best method by 47.06%, 29.41%, and 26.09% respectively. FANS also leads on Sparseness (SPA: 0.924/0.630/0.634) and Max-Sensitivity (MS: 0.463/0.586/0.578) on MNIST/Fashion-MNIST/CIFAR-10. On graph datasets, FANS achieves the best FID⁺ (0.7000/0.7600/0.4251 on BACommunity/Citeseer/Pubmed) and is the only method achieving FID⁻ = 0 across all three, while remaining competitive on Recall@12 (0.5819 vs. 0.6189 for CF² on BACommunity).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FANS outperforms all 18 baselines on the majority of metrics across six datasets. On image data, FANS beats the closest gradient-based competitors (IG, DeepLift, GradShap) on INF by 47–26% and on MS by 14–6%, while also surpassing them on SPA by 0.3–2.6%; the large gap over LIME, Occlusion, and MP on INF (orders of magnitude) reflects their poor faithfulness. On graph data, FANS achieves the best FID⁺ on all three datasets (improving 4.99% over CFGNExplainer on BACommunity, 46.15% over IG on Citeseer, and 13.33% over CFGNExplainer on Pubmed) and is the only method reaching FID⁻ = 0 everywhere, whereas NSEG and CF² also achieve FID⁻ = 0 on Citeseer/Pubmed but lag substantially on FID⁺. The one metric where FANS does not clearly win is Recall@12 on BACommunity (0.5819 vs. 0.6189 for CF² and 0.6025 for PGExplainer), though it remains competitive. FANS also demonstrates superior robustness: under increasing noise, its MS degradation slope is considerably lower than all baselines, attributed to its SIR-based sampling over multiple similar inputs rather than relying on a single sample.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the perturbation-based feature attribution paradigm by reframing attribution as a Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS) measurement problem within a Structural Causal Model. It builds directly on NSEG (Cai et al., 2022), which also uses PNS for GNN explanations but requires an identifiability assumption and optimizes only a lower bound; FANS instead computes PNS directly from its definition, leveraging the fact that post-intervention outcomes are observable in the attribution causal model. The dual-stage perturbation test implements Pearl's Abduction-Action-Prediction counterfactual reasoning paradigm, while the SIR-based sampling strategy addresses the intractability of the required conditional distributions. Conceptually, the paper departs from all standard FAMs (Shapley, gradients, LIME) by arguing that the magnitude of prediction change is an insufficient signal for feature importance, since different features can produce identical prediction changes for fundamentally different causal reasons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine blind spot in perturbation-based attribution: features that are causally irrelevant (e.g., x₃ in Example 1) can receive the same attribution score as causally critical features when their perturbation produces identical prediction changes. The consistent outperformance across six diverse datasets (images and graphs) and the notably superior robustness to input noise (due to SIR-based multi-sample learning) suggest practical utility for high-stakes applications like medical diagnosis. However, the authors acknowledge that the boundary b and threshold c are estimated heuristically (Scott's rule and noise-variance), and a theoretical identifiability framework for these hyperparameters remains an open question that limits the method's theoretical grounding.
