## Problem

Quality Diversity (QD) algorithms produce diverse, high-quality solution archives but depend on manually crafted diversity metrics, limiting their use in open-ended domains where diversity is abstract and qualitative. Meanwhile, RLHF optimizes for averaged human preferences, which is ill-suited for generative tasks that require diverse responses. No prior method learns diversity metrics from human feedback in an online, progressive manner that adapts as optimization discovers higher-quality solutions.

## Input / Output

QDHF takes solution candidates from an arbitrary search space (e.g., 4×64×64 latent vectors for Stable Diffusion v2.1-base producing 512×512 images, 10-dim joint-angle vectors for a robotic arm, or MLP policy parameters for maze navigation) along with human similarity judgments collected via Two Alternative Forced Choice (2AFC) triplets. It produces a MAP-Elites archive of diverse solutions (20×20 cells for LSI, 50×50 for robotics/RL) and a learned linear projection from a 512-dim CLIP feature space to a 2-dim diversity embedding. The feedback can come from real human annotators or a trained preference model (DreamSim), and the method operates as a black-box optimizer requiring no access to the generative model's parameters.

## Method

QDHF learns diversity metrics by projecting extracted features (via CLIP ViT-B/16) into a compact 2-dim latent space using a linear projection, then aligning that space to human similarity judgments through triplet contrastive loss. The key distinction from offline baselines is a progressive fine-tuning strategy: the latent projection is re-optimized at iterations 1, 10%·n, 25%·n, and 50%·n using 2AFC judgments sampled from the evolving MAP-Elites archive, and the QD archive is re-initialized with the updated diversity representations after each update. This ensures the diversity metric remains relevant as the solution distribution shifts toward higher-quality regions. Theoretically, the reduced latent dimensionality imposes an information bottleneck constraint that forces the projection to encode only the discriminative information needed to predict human similarity, yielding compact yet informative diversity metrics. The overall loop couples MAP-Elites exploration with online contrastive learning, using QD as an active sampling strategy that surfaces novel solutions for which human feedback is most informative.

## Results

On the robotic arm benchmark, QDHF achieves a QD score of 72.5±0.9 and 77.3% coverage (All Solutions), closely matching QD with ground-truth diversity (74.8±0.2, 79.5%) while significantly outperforming the best AURORA variant (53.0±9.4, 63.2%) and QDHF-Base (54.5±4.3, 62.7%). On maze navigation, QDHF reaches 42.0±1.7 QD score, matching QD-GT (42.7±2.7) and surpassing AURORA-Inc(AE) (40.0±2.1). On the open-ended LSI task with singular prompts, QDHF achieves a CLIP score of 68.47 with mean pairwise distance of 0.517, substantially exceeding SD Best-of-n (67.64 CLIP, 0.405 PD) in diversity while maintaining comparable quality, and is preferred by 58.4% of users in a blind study.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QDHF is compared against four variants of AURORA (Cully 2019; Grillotti & Cully 2022b), which use unsupervised dimensionality reduction (PCA or autoencoder) to discover diversity metrics without human input. On the robotic arm task, QDHF (72.5 QD score) outperforms the best AURORA variant, AURORA-Inc(AE) (53.0), by roughly 19.5 points, and on maze navigation QDHF (42.0) edges out AURORA-Inc(AE) (40.0) while QDHF-Base (35.6) actually underperforms AURORA. QDHF also consistently beats its own offline variant QDHF-Base (54.5 on robotic arm, 35.6 on maze), confirming that progressive online fine-tuning of diversity metrics during optimization is critical. In the open-ended LSI task, QDHF surpasses both SD Best-of-n and the diversity-heuristic Best-of-n+ in pairwise distance (0.517 vs. 0.405/0.412 for singular prompts) while matching or exceeding their CLIP scores, and wins the blind user preference study 58.4% to 31.9%. Overall, QDHF approaches the oracle QD-GT performance (within ~2.3 QD score points on robotic arm) without requiring any manually designed diversity metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

QDHF sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: Quality Diversity algorithms (MAP-Elites, AURORA, RUDA) that require diversity metrics to guide exploration, and RLHF-style contrastive learning that aligns model representations to human preferences. The paper departs from unsupervised diversity discovery (AURORA, RUDA) by replacing data-driven dimensionality reduction with human-feedback-driven contrastive learning, and from standard QD by eliminating the need for manually crafted diversity metrics. The progressive fine-tuning strategy—where diversity representations are iteratively refined as the QD archive evolves—is the key methodological departure from both offline RLHF (which trains a fixed reward model) and incremental AURORA (which updates descriptors without human alignment).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QDHF makes QD applicable to open-ended generative domains (text-to-image, creative design) where 'diversity' is qualitative and hard to specify, which was a major practical barrier to QD adoption. The finding that a simple diversity heuristic (Best-of-n+) fails to improve diversity in Stable Diffusion's latent space underscores that the problem is genuinely non-trivial and that learned diversity metrics provide real value. Limitations include reliance on a preference model (DreamSim) that may not generalize to all visual domains (as shown by the urban downtown failure case), and the open question of how diversity interacts with user preference in different application contexts. Future work on more strategic feedback collection and scaling to higher-dimensional diversity spaces would strengthen the approach.
