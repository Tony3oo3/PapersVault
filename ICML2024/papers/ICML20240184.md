## Problem

The original AI safety via debate framework (Irving et al., 2018) required the honest prover to simulate computations for exponentially many steps, making it inapplicable to practical LLM settings. This paper addresses the gap by designing debate protocols where the honest prover only needs polynomial-time simulation, while still guaranteeing that the honest side wins against a computationally unbounded dishonest prover, even with stochastic (fuzzy) human judgements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240184/cropped_page11_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The protocol takes a problem instance x, the code of a time-T oracle Turing machine M, and access to an oracle O (modeling human judgement) with queries of length l; optionally a polynomial-length witness w is also provided. The verifier V outputs a binary accept/reject decision after making only O(1) oracle queries (for deterministic settings) or O(K²) queries (for K-Lipschitz stochastic settings), running in O(l log T) time. The honest prover runs in O(T log T) time, while the dishonest prover is allowed unbounded computation.

## Method

The core mechanism is a cross-examination debate protocol: prover A outputs the full transcript of M's execution, prover B points to a specific location t where A allegedly made an error, and the verifier V checks only that single location by reading the O(l) relevant bits and simulating one step of M. For stochastic oracles, the protocol extends to T rounds where A proposes a probability distribution for each step, a coin-flipping sub-protocol (z_t^A + z_t^B mod 1) samples the next bit from A's distribution, and B can abort at any round to trigger V's verification via O(K²) samples from O. A K-Lipschitz continuity assumption on M ensures that small per-step estimation errors do not accumulate into O(1) output probability shifts over T steps. The key theoretical guarantee is that the honest prover's polynomial-time strategy wins with probability ≥ 3/5 (completeness) or ≤ 2/5 (soundness) against any unbounded dishonest strategy, formalizing the principle that it is easier to tell the truth than to lie.

## Results

The paper's main results are theoretical complexity bounds for debate protocols. The headline result (Theorem 5.3) shows that any polynomial-time computation can be verified with O(1) human judgements (oracle queries), O(T log T) honest prover time, and O(l log T) verifier time, where T is the computation time and l is the oracle query length. For stochastic judgements (Theorem 6.2), the bounds become O(K²T log T) prover time, O(K² + l log T) verifier time, and O(K²) oracle queries, with completeness 3/5 and soundness 2/5. The key improvement over prior work is that the honest prover runs in polynomial time (O(T log T)) while the dishonest prover is allowed to be computationally unbounded.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or dataset comparisons. The primary comparison is to the original AI safety via debate framework (Irving et al., 2018), which required the honest prover to be computationally unbounded (able to simulate deterministic AI systems for an exponential number of steps). The present work's key advance is showing that the honest prover only needs polynomial time (O(T log T)) while the dishonest prover can be unbounded, making the protocols implementable by actual LLMs. Additionally, the paper extends the setting to stochastic oracles (modeling the inherent randomness in human judgement), which was not addressed in the prior work. The paper also contrasts with doubly-efficient interactive proofs (Goldwasser et al., 2015; Reingold et al., 2021), noting that those results do not apply in the presence of a black-box oracle, whereas the debate protocols here do.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AI safety via debate framework of Irving et al. (2018) by introducing the formal model of doubly-efficient debate, where the honest prover is required to run in polynomial time rather than being computationally unbounded as in prior work. It draws on doubly-efficient interactive proofs (Goldwasser et al., 2015; Reingold et al., 2021) and cross-examination (Barnes & Christiano, 2020b) to design protocols that work with stochastic oracles modeling fuzzy human judgement, a setting absent from the original debate proposal. The key conceptual departure is that the honest debater's polynomial-time constraint rules out brute-force strategies, tying the theory much more closely to what actual LLMs can implement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result—that constant human judgements suffice to verify arbitrary polynomial-time computations via competing provers—provides the first formal complexity-theoretic grounding for scalable oversight of powerful AI systems. However, significant open questions remain: the model requires polynomial-length human-verifiable transcripts (ruling out implicitly-represented exponential computations), the Lipschitz assumption on the machine may be restrictive, and the obfuscated argument problem for unbounded dishonest provers is not addressed. The Lean 4 formalization of the main stochastic theorem adds credibility but the work remains purely theoretical with no empirical validation of whether trained LLMs can actually implement the prescribed honest strategies.
