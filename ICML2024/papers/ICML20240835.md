## Problem

High-dimensional Bayesian optimization has long been considered intractable for vanilla GP-based BO, with a large body of work imposing structural assumptions (subspaces, additivity, local trust regions) to circumvent the curse of dimensionality. This paper identifies that the true failure mode is not dimensionality itself but the assumed model complexity: conventional lengthscale priors place high density on short lengthscales, which in high dimensions drives the GP toward near-independence between observations, rendering the surrogate uninformative and causing EI to degenerate into local search around the incumbent.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a black-box objective f: [0,1]^D → R (or [-1,1]^D) observed with additive Gaussian noise, requiring only point-wise evaluations within a fixed budget. It produces a sequence of D-dimensional query points and tracks the best observed value. The key practical detail is that the method is a plug-and-play modification to any standard BO pipeline: it requires no change to the acquisition function (LogEI is used), the kernel family (RBF or Matern-5/2 with ARD), or the optimization procedure (multi-start L-BFGS), and does not increase the number of hyperparameters in a MAP-based setup.

## Method

The core contribution is a dimensionality-scaled lengthscale prior (DSP): the LogNormal prior on each ARD lengthscale is shifted by log(D)/2 in its μ parameter, equivalently scaling all lengthscales by √D to counteract the √D growth of inter-point distances in a D-dimensional hypercube. This single change keeps the Maximal Information Gain (MIG) nearly constant as D increases, preventing the GP from reverting to its prior mean and variance. Additionally, the signal variance σ_f² is fixed at 1 (matching standardized observations) to prevent a degeneracy where active data acquisition drives the learned outputscale toward zero, reinforcing exploitative local behavior. Theoretically, the paper proves (Proposition 4.1) that under an uninformed model (K ≈ I), EI does not seek high-variance boundary points as previously claimed, but instead requires substantial correlation with the incumbent, explaining the observed local-search pathology. The result is a vanilla BO algorithm that outperforms state-of-the-art HDBO methods (SAASBO, TuRBO, Bounce, RD-UCB, MPD, CMA-ES) on real-world tasks up to 6392 dimensions without imposing any structural restrictions on the objective.

## Results

The proposed Dimensionality-Scaled Prior (DSP) for vanilla BO achieves the best or top-two performance across all real-world high-dimensional benchmarks (124D–6392D), substantially outperforming all baselines on Lasso-DNA (180D), SVM (388D), and Humanoid (6392D). On mid-dimensional tasks (12D–32D), DSP is competitive with or outperforms TuRBO on all five tasks. On synthetic axis-aligned functions (Levy 4D, Hartmann 6D up to 1000D ambient), DSP is second only to SAASBO, whose sparse axis-aligned subspace assumption perfectly matches the problem structure. The method exhibits notably small error bars across repetitions, reflecting consistent calibrated modeling without reliance on random subspace design or trust-region initialization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SAASBO (sparse axis-aligned subspaces), DSP loses on MOPTA (124D) and on four of the synthetic axis-aligned tasks where the subspace assumption is well-aligned, but substantially outperforms it on Lasso-DNA, SVM, and Humanoid, and runs in a small fraction of the time (no HMC fitting needed). Against TuRBO (local BO), DSP is competitive or superior on all mid-dimensional tasks (Lunar Lander 12D, Robot Pushing 14D, Swimmer 16D, Hopper 32D) and on high-dimensional real-world tasks. Against BAxUS (nested subspaces), BAxUS gains an initialization advantage on MuJoCo Ant (888D) by sampling in a lower-dimensional subspace, but DSP outperforms it on all other high-dimensional tasks. Against RD-UCB (random additive GP), MPD (local probability-of-descent), CMA-ES, and MCTS-VS (variable selection), DSP achieves the best or near-best performance across all benchmarks. The paper also shows that conventional MAP with a Γ(3,6) lengthscale prior suffers from a severe locality issue (effectively performing local search after DoE), while DSP maintains a calibrated exploration-exploitation trade-off throughout optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repositions the high-dimensional Bayesian optimization (HDBO) problem by arguing that the core difficulty is not dimensionality itself but the assumed model complexity, which all existing HDBO classes (subspace methods like REMBO/SAASBO, additive kernels, local BO like TuRBO, cylindrical kernels like BOCK) address through structural restrictions on the objective. It builds on the standard GP-BO framework and the Maximal Information Gain (MIG) complexity measure from Srinivas et al. (2010), while departing from the field's consensus by showing that a simple scaling of the lengthscale prior with √D achieves comparable complexity reduction without any structural assumptions. The paper also corrects a widely-cited result from Swersky (2017) by proving that EI does not seek high-variance boundary points under an uninformed model, instead exhibiting local-search-like behavior near the incumbent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a minimal prior modification to vanilla BO outperforms the entire suite of specialized HDBO algorithms on real-world tasks up to 6392 dimensions challenges the field's default assumption that high-dimensional optimization necessitates structural simplifications. This has practical implications: practitioners can use a simple, assumption-free method that is also faster (no HMC, no subspace design, no trust-region management) while matching or exceeding state-of-the-art performance. The authors acknowledge that structural assumptions remain valuable when prior knowledge supports them (e.g., known additivity or low effective dimensionality), and identify latent-space GP models as a natural direction for extending the complexity-based analysis. Open questions include whether the complexity framing generalizes beyond stationary kernels and how it interacts with non-stationary or multi-objective settings.
