## Problem

Hamiltonian prediction for DFT is limited by the scarcity of labeled data, since generating labels requires running full SCF-converged DFT calculations on each molecule. All prior methods rely on such labeled datasets, restricting applicability to molecules with no more than 31 atoms. This paper identifies that the Kohn-Sham equation itself provides a label-free training signal through its self-consistency property, enabling training on unlimited unlabeled molecular structures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242451/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a molecular structure M = {Z, R} specifying atomic numbers and 3D Cartesian coordinates, and predicts the B×B mean-field Hamiltonian (Fock) matrix in an atom-centered Gaussian basis, where B scales linearly with the number of electrons. The predicted Hamiltonian directly yields all DFT-derivable quantities—orbital energies, HOMO/LUMO energies and gap, total energy—and can serve as an initialization to accelerate conventional SCF convergence. The model architecture is QHNet, an SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network that processes the molecular graph with tensor-product message passing.

## Method

The core contribution is a self-consistency loss that enforces the Kohn-Sham equation without labels: given a predicted Hamiltonian H_θ(M), the loss minimizes the Frobenius-norm difference between H_θ(M) and the Hamiltonian H_M(C) reconstructed from the eigenvectors C of the generalized eigenvalue problem H_θ(M)C = S_M C ε. Critically, back-propagation through the eigensolver and the Hamiltonian reconstruction is required (not stop-gradient), so both the predicted and reconstructed matrices are driven toward the true solution. The loss is combined with a standard supervised MSE/MAE loss on a small labeled set, and can be applied to any number of unlabeled structures for continued training. Implementation uses PyTorch's differentiable eigensolver with gradient truncation for numerical stability, GPU-accelerated basis evaluation on quadrature grids, and density fitting to reduce the Hartree term from O(N⁴) to O(N³). The amortization effect—requiring only one SCF-equivalent iteration per molecule versus many for DFT labeling—makes self-consistency training more computationally efficient than generating labels for the same number of molecules.

## Results

In the data-scarce scenario (MD17, 100 labeled conformations), self-consistency training on ~24,900 unlabeled structures reduces Hamiltonian MAE by 14.4%–52.8% and cuts ε_HOMO, ε_LUMO, and ε_Δ errors by 2–30× compared to supervised training alone. In the OOD scenario (QH9-small → QH9-large), self-consistency fine-tuning reduces ε_LUMO and ε_Δ MAE by an order of magnitude (e.g., ε_LUMO from 12,230 to 1,131 μE_h for all-param). On the largest molecules tested (MD22: ALA3 with 42 atoms, DHA with 56 atoms), self-consistency fine-tuning achieves ε_HOMO, ε_LUMO, and ε_Δ MAEs of 1–4 × 10³ μE_h, outperforming end-to-end property predictors (ET, Equiformer) by 1–2 orders of magnitude on all three properties.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares self-consistency-trained Hamiltonian prediction against two end-to-end molecular property predictors: ET (Thölke & De Fabritiis, ICLR 2021) and Equiformer (Liao & Smidt, 2022), both trained on QH9 with labels. On ALA3 (42 atoms), self-consistency training achieves ε_HOMO MAE of 2.07×10³ μE_h versus 1.74×10⁵ (ET) and 2.38×10⁵ (Equiformer) — roughly 84× and 115× better respectively; ε_Δ is 2.69×10³ vs. 2.38×10⁵ (ET) and 2.27×10⁵ (Equiformer). On DHA (56 atoms), the gap widens further: ε_HOMO is 1.99×10³ vs. 2.92×10⁵ (ET, 147× better) and 3.76×10⁵ (Equiformer, 189× better). For ε_LUMO, self-consistency training is 2× better than ET on ALA3 (3.76×10³ vs. 7.72×10³) and 6.4× better on DHA (4.01×10³ vs. 2.58×10⁴). Additionally, in the OOD QH9 scenario (Table 3), self-consistency training outperforms supervised training with full DFT-generated labels on all derived properties (ε_HOMO, ε_LUMO, ε_Δ) despite having no labels, indicating the self-consistency loss is more aligned with physically relevant quantities than direct Hamiltonian regression.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Hamiltonian prediction task established by Schütt et al. (2019) and subsequent equivariant architectures (PhiS-Net, QHNet), all of which require DFT-generated Hamiltonian labels for supervised training. The key departure is a new training paradigm: instead of supervised learning on labeled data, the authors exploit the Kohn-Sham equation's self-consistency structure to define a label-free loss that enforces the physical equation directly. The approach is conceptually related to the SCF loss in DM21 (Kirkpatrick et al., 2021), but repurposes it: DM21 uses the loss to regularize an XC functional model given a known solution, whereas here it trains a solution-prediction model without any solution labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The self-consistency training technique breaks the data bottleneck that has restricted Hamiltonian prediction to molecules with ≤31 atoms, extending applicability to 56-atom systems and enabling label-free fine-tuning for out-of-distribution generalization. The finding that self-consistency training outperforms full supervised training on derived physical quantities (HOMO, LUMO, gap) even with unlimited compute is notable, suggesting the physics-informed loss captures more relevant structure than direct matrix regression. Open questions include whether the amortization principle generalizes to other iterative physics solvers, and whether the approach can be combined with learned XC functionals (as in DM21) for end-to-end differentiable DFT.
