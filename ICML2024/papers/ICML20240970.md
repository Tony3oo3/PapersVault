## Problem

SGD with doubly stochastic gradients (doubly SGD) is widely used for objectives that are finite sums of intractable expectations (e.g., diffusion models, VAEs), yet its convergence properties were previously established only under strong assumptions such as bounded per-component variance or uniform Lipschitz smoothness of the integrand over an unbounded noise support. Moreover, the effect of sharing Monte Carlo samples across the minibatch—creating correlated component estimators—was largely uncharacterized, leaving practitioners without guidance on how to allocate a fixed computational budget between minibatch size b and Monte Carlo samples m.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a finite sum objective F = (1/n)Σᵢ E_η[fᵢ(x;η)] with component gradient estimators gᵢ(x) that may be correlated (sharing η across the batch), a minibatch subsampling strategy π with effective sample size b_eff, and a per-iteration budget of m×b evaluations. It produces a three-term variance decomposition (component variance, correlation term scaled by ρ, and subsampling variance) and iteration complexity bounds for strongly convex, convex, quasar-convex, and nonconvex smooth objectives. A key output is the practical recommendation that, for dependent estimators, increasing b over m is generally preferred under a fixed m×b budget, since reducing subsampling variance also reduces the effective Monte Carlo variance through the correlation structure.

## Method

The core contribution is a general variance bound (Theorem 1) for subsampled ensembles of potentially correlated unbiased estimators, decomposing tr V[x_B] into a component-variance term, a correlation term proportional to ρ(1−1/b_eff)(Σ√σᵢ²)², and a subsampling term, where ρ∈[0,1] captures the worst-case pairwise correlation. The paper then shows that if each component estimator satisfies the Expected Residual (ER) and Bounded Variance (BV) conditions, the doubly stochastic estimator inherits both conditions (Theorems 2–3), which is sufficient to guarantee convergence of doubly SGD via the standard variance-transfer strategy. For random reshuffling (doubly SGD-RR), a biased Lyapunov analysis yields an improved O(1/√ε) dependence on subsampling noise τ² (versus O(1/ε) for independent subsampling), and for fully correlated estimators achieves an n/b asymptotic sample-complexity speedup over full-batch SGD. A key mechanistic insight (Lemma 9) is that reducing subsampling variance also reduces the ensemble's component-variance contribution, which is why increasing b dominates increasing m for dependent estimators under a fixed budget.

## Results

The paper establishes a general variance bound (Theorem 1) for doubly stochastic gradient estimators with correlation parameter ρ ∈ [0,1], decomposing the total variance into component variance V_com, correlation variance V_cor, and subsampling variance V_sub. For strongly convex objectives, Corollary 2 gives an iteration complexity of O(max(C_var/ε, C_bias) · log(1/ε)) with fixed stepsize, where C_var depends on σ_i²/b, (Σσ_i)², and τ²/b. The key practical insight is that for a fixed budget m×b, increasing the minibatch size b is generally preferred over increasing Monte Carlo samples m, since reducing subsampling variance also reduces the effective component variance when estimators are correlated (ρ > 0). The simulation (Figure 1) confirms this: at high data heterogeneity (s=4) and low budget (mb ≪ n), the largest b values achieve the lowest gradient variance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no direct method-vs-method benchmark comparisons. The primary comparison is between doubly SGD (independent subsampling) and doubly SGD-RR (random reshuffling): RR improves the dependence on subsampling noise τ² from O(1/ε) to O(1/√ε) in the iteration complexity (Theorem 4 vs. Corollary 2), though it does not achieve the full O(1/T²) rate of finite-sum SGD-RR. Compared to prior doubly stochastic analyses (Bietti & Mairal 2017; Zheng & Kwok 2018; Kulunchakov & Mairal 2020), this work removes the need for uniform Lipschitz smoothness of the integrand over η and bounded component variance, replacing them with the weaker ER and BV conditions that are realizable for complex estimators. The paper also shows that its Theorem 5 (doubly SGD-RR) strictly generalizes Mishchenko et al. (2020) Theorem 1 for finite-sum SGD-RR, recovering it exactly when σ_i = 0. For dependent estimators, doubly SGD-RR achieves an asymptotic n/b sample complexity speedup over full-batch SGD, a benefit that vanishes for independent estimators (ρ=0).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the doubly stochastic gradient framework introduced by Dai et al. (2014) and Titsias & Lázaro-Gredilla (2014) by establishing convergence under the expected residual (ER) condition from Gower et al. (2021a) rather than the previously required uniform Lipschitz smoothness or bounded variance assumptions. It generalizes the random reshuffling analysis of Mishchenko et al. (2020) to the doubly stochastic setting, showing that Theorem 5 reduces exactly to their result when Monte Carlo noise vanishes. The key conceptual departure is handling dependent component estimators (shared Monte Carlo samples across the batch), which prior analyses either ignored or handled only through concentration arguments specific to particular applications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first general convergence guarantees for doubly SGD under realistic assumptions, unifying the analysis of diffusion model training, variational autoencoders, and randomized smoothing under a single framework. The practical recommendation that minibatch size b should generally be preferred over Monte Carlo sample count m for a fixed budget is actionable and counterintuitive, though validated only on synthetic quadratic problems. Open questions include whether the b-over-m preference holds for nonconvex objectives (the main convergence results require strong convexity), whether the 'super-efficiency' of doubly SGD-RR for dependent estimators translates to real training scenarios, and how the analysis extends to adaptive or importance-sampled Monte Carlo estimators.
