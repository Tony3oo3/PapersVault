## Problem

Independent Subnetwork Training (IST) is a promising technique combining data and model parallelism, but its convergence properties lack rigorous theoretical understanding. Prior analyses (Yuan et al., 2022) imposed extremely restrictive conditions (requiring compressor variance ω ≲ μ²/L², effectively forbidding compression), considered only single-node settings, and relied on assumptions incompatible with quadratic models. This paper identifies the fundamental mechanism by which IST differs from standard compressed-communication methods: the gradient estimator is inherently biased rather than merely noisy, leading to an irreducible neighborhood around the optimum that cannot be eliminated by reducing the step size.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a distributed quadratic objective f(x) = (1/n)Σ f_i(x) where each local loss is f_i(x) = ½xᵀL_i x − xᵀb_i. Each of n workers receives the global model x^k, applies a permutation sketch C_i (a sparse diagonal selector scaled by √n/[L_i]_{π_i,π_i}) to extract a one-dimensional submodel, computes the local gradient on that submodel, and sends back a sparse update. The server aggregates via averaging to produce x^{k+1}. The analysis requires d = qn (model dimension is an integer multiple of worker count) and positive-definite diagonal entries of each L_i for the preconditioning to be well-defined.

## Method

The paper develops a convergence theory for IST using permutation sketches (Perm-q) as the submodel partitioning mechanism, analyzed under matrix L-smoothness rather than scalar L-smoothness. The key technical insight is that the gradient estimator g^k = (1/n)Σ C_i ∇f_i(C_i x^k) is biased: E[g^k] = L̄^{-1}∇f(x^k) + h, where h = L̄^{-1}b̄ − (1/√n)D̃b is a fixed bias vector depending on the linear terms. In the interpolation case (b_i ≡ 0), the method achieves O(1/K) convergence to the exact optimum with proper diagonal preconditioning. In the general case, the method converges to an irreducible neighborhood of size proportional to ‖h‖²_{L̄} + σ² (heterogeneity), and this neighborhood cannot be shrunk by decreasing the step size—unlike standard SGD where the variance term vanishes at the optimum. The heterogeneous sketch preconditioner (scaling each coordinate by 1/√[L_i]_{jj}) is shown to neutralize data heterogeneity, reducing the problem to the homogeneous case with only the bias term remaining.

## Results

The paper establishes that IST achieves an O(1/K) convergence rate to a stationary point and linear convergence in the interpolation (zero linear term) case, matching classical gradient descent rates when proper preconditioning is applied. In the general heterogeneous case, IST converges to an irreducible neighborhood whose size is governed by the bias norm ‖h‖²_L and heterogeneity level σ², and this neighborhood cannot be eliminated by decreasing the step size. Empirically, on a CIFAR-10 task (d=2048, n=10 nodes, ResNet-50 features), IST's training loss stagnates and oscillates at a fixed error floor regardless of step size, while Distributed Gradient Descent (DGD) continues to decrease. The quadratic experiments (d=1000, n=10) confirm the theoretical prediction that larger step sizes accelerate convergence but enlarge the neighborhood.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary empirical comparison is between IST (using Perm-q permutation sketches) and Distributed Gradient Descent (DGD, where C_i = I) on CIFAR-10 with n=10 nodes. IST converges to a neighborhood whose size is essentially the same across different step sizes, while DGD continues to reduce loss monotonically — a fundamental distinction from standard SGD where the error floor can be eliminated by shrinking the step size. Theoretically, the paper shows IST's irreducible bias term (1−γ)β⁻¹‖h‖²_L cannot be removed by decreasing γ, unlike the γ·L·C term in standard SGD bounds. Compared to the prior IST analysis by Yuan et al. (2022), this work allows arbitrary sparsification levels (any ω ≥ 0) rather than requiring ω ≲ μ²/L², which for typical condition numbers would force q ≈ d (almost no compression). The paper also contrasts with Zhou et al. (2022) on federated pruning, noting that their bounded-gradient-norm assumption (9) is 'pathological' and can yield vacuous bounds in heterogeneous settings, whereas the present analysis captures heterogeneity through the σ² term without such restrictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical analysis of Independent Subnetwork Training (IST) pioneered by Yuan et al. (2022) and the permutation compressor framework of Szlendak et al. (2022), providing the first convergence guarantees for IST in both homogeneous and heterogeneous settings without the restrictive condition-number requirements (ω ≲ μ²/L²) imposed by prior work. It deliberately departs from the compressed-communication literature (Alistarh et al., 2017; Konečný et al., 2016) by identifying that IST's gradient estimator is fundamentally biased in a non-additive way, making standard unbiased-SGD or biased-SGD analyses inapplicable. The paper also contrasts with federated pruning analyses (Zhou et al., 2022) that rely on the 'pathological' bounded-gradient-norm assumption, which the authors show can yield vacuous bounds in distributed settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that IST converges to an irreducible neighborhood of the optimum rather than the exact solution in the general (non-interpolation) case, and that this bias cannot be eliminated by reducing the step size—challenges the assumption that IST is merely a communication-efficient variant of standard distributed training. This negative result for the standard ERM formulation raises important open questions: whether IST should be reinterpreted as solving an alternative optimization problem (as suggested by Demidovich et al., 2023), whether algorithmic modifications can eliminate the bias, and how these findings extend beyond quadratic models to practical deep learning. The analysis also provides a clear trade-off characterization (convergence speed vs. neighborhood size) that is directly relevant to practitioners deploying IST in resource-constrained federated or distributed settings.
