## Problem

The Quadratic Assignment Problem (QAP) is strongly NP-hard with no FPTAS unless P=NP, yet existing machine learning approaches rely on association graphs with O(n⁴) complexity, limiting them to instances below size 30. No learn-to-improve (L2I) reinforcement learning method has been proposed for QAP, and prior L2I solvers designed for VRPs cannot capture the higher-order graph structure inherent in QAPs. This paper introduces the first L2I method for QAP that avoids association graphs entirely, enabling scalability to instances of size 100.

## Input / Output

The model takes a flow matrix F (n×n, symmetric), a distance matrix D (n×n, derived from 2D location coordinates), and an initial assignment permutation σ as input. At each step, the state is the pair (σ, σ*) of the current and best-found solutions; the policy outputs a swap action pair (i, j) selecting two facility positions to exchange, and a value function estimates the state's expected cumulative reward. Facility nodes are initialized with random one-hot vectors of dimension N_init=128 (much larger than n), while location nodes use 2D coordinates projected through a linear layer, allowing a single trained policy to generalize across all problem sizes below N_init.

## Method

SAWT independently encodes facility nodes via a mixed-score Transformer (mixing the flow matrix F into attention scores) and location nodes via a 3-layer GCN (using the distance matrix D as affinity), deliberately avoiding early cross-domain interaction. The core innovation is the Solution-Aware Transformer (SAWT) encoder, which computes a solution-aware matrix M_σ = F ⊙ D_σ (where D_σ is the distance matrix with rows permuted by the current solution σ) and multiplies it element-wise with the self-attention scores, injecting the QAP objective's gradient information directly into the attention mechanism. The encoder concatenates facility and location embeddings according to the current assignment σ, so the model dynamically adapts its representations as the solution evolves. Training uses REINFORCE with a bootstrapping horizon of 8 steps, an entropy bonus that decays over epochs, and a value function trained with MSE loss, all within a 400-step episode. This design eliminates the O(n⁴) association graph, achieving 4.7×–5350× speedup over tabu-search while matching or exceeding its solution quality at 10,000 search steps.

## Results

On self-generated Koopmans-Beckmann QAP instances, SAWT with 10,000 search steps achieves gaps of 0.00% (QAP10), −0.54% (QAP20), −0.04% (QAP50), and 0.52% (QAP100) relative to the best-known solution, substantially outperforming the best L2I baseline (Sui) which achieves 2.1%, 3.32%, 4.24%, and 5.01% respectively. On the QAPLIB benchmark (134 real-world instances), SAWT achieves an average mean gap of 26.8% and maximum gap of 47.0%, surpassing all heuristic and learning-based baselines. Inference time is dramatically faster than Tabu-search (5k steps): 4.7× faster on QAP10, 38.7× on QAP20, 403× on QAP50, and 5350× on QAP100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAWT decisively outperforms all L2I baselines (Costa, Sui, Wu, Dact, NeuOpt) across all four QAP sizes, with gaps roughly 3–10× smaller even at only 2,000 search steps; for example, on QAP50 SAWT{2k} achieves 0.58% gap versus Sui's 4.24%. MatNet* (adapted as L2I) is the weakest L2I baseline, trailing SAWT by 0.67% and 0.07% on QAP10/QAP20 at 2k steps, and fails entirely on QAP50 and QAP100 due to its dual-attention computational overhead. The L2C method RGM achieves competitive gaps on QAP10 (1.78%) and QAP20 (1.87%) but cannot scale beyond size 50 due to O(n⁴) association graph complexity. Heuristic solvers (SM, RRWM, IPFP) are 10–50× worse in gap but are extremely fast (sub-second to seconds); exact solvers (Gurobi, Tabu-search) match or beat SAWT in solution quality but require hours to days for QAP50/QAP100, whereas SAWT{10k} completes in under 8 minutes. On QAPLIB, SAWT outperforms NGM and RGM on most categories (e.g., BUR: 2.8% vs 3.4%/7.1%; LIPA: 0.4% vs 16.2%/13.3%) but struggles on sparse-flow datasets like CHR (110.7% gap) and STE (93.5%), where SK-JA and RGM perform better. On TSP (secondary task), SAWT matches Dact and NeuOpt within 0.22% gap on TSP100, confirming its generality beyond QAP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learn-to-improve (L2I) paradigm from VRPs (Costa et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2021, 2023) to the Quadratic Assignment Problem, which had previously only been addressed by learn-to-construct (L2C) methods relying on O(n⁴) association graphs (Wang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022). The key architectural departure is the Solution-Aware Transformer (SAWT), which independently encodes facility nodes via a mixed-score attention mechanism (inspired by MatNet, Kwon et al., 2021) and location nodes via GCN, then integrates the incumbent solution matrix M_σ directly into the attention scores to capture higher-order QAP structure. This eliminates the association graph bottleneck and enables scaling to n=100, whereas prior learning-based QAP methods were limited to n≤30.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QAP is strongly NP-hard with no known FPTAS (unless P=NP), and practical applications span chip placement, hospital layout, and electronic module design where current OR solvers require hours or days. SAWT's 5350× speedup over tabu-search on QAP100 and its ability to generalize across sizes (Policy-50 transfers to QAP100 with only 0.03% degradation) make it practically relevant for real-world deployment. However, the model's sensitivity to flow matrix density (struggling on sparse instances like 'Chr' with 110% mean gap) and the absence of a meta-learning component for distribution shift remain significant open questions for broader QAPLIB applicability.
