## Problem

Recent work on ethical AI implicitly assumes that AI systems can be analyzed as moral agents by simulating human ethical decision-making, but this mechanistic view of agency is fundamentally limited for establishing accountability and understanding the ethical characteristics of AI. The paper asks what political philosophy's centuries of theorizing about human responsibility, morality, and collective coordination can contribute to AI ethics, and argues that in the context of ethically-significant behavior, AI should be viewed not as an agent but as the outcome of political processes.

## Input / Output

As a conceptual position paper, the work takes as input the philosophical literature on agency (List and Pettit's mechanistic framework and Taylor's volitional framework) alongside the AI alignment and evaluation literature, and produces a reframing of AI ethics around two complementary analytical approaches: application-specific correctness criteria and process-based legitimacy analysis. The output is not a model or algorithm but a set of epistemic frameworks for analyzing AI harms, accountability, and the political dimensions of value aggregation in alignment.

## Method

The paper constructs a two-axis analysis of agency to show that neither dominant view supports treating AI as a moral agent. Under the mechanistic view (agency as information processing and action, per List and Pettit), the ideal agent collapses into an impossible simulator of a perfectly-ethical human, and AI fails the conditions for moral responsibility—particularly relevant control—since generating text about a moral judgment does not constitute enacting it. Under the volitional view (agency as strong evaluation and original purpose, per Taylor), AI is disqualified entirely because it lacks intrinsic motivation, self-regard, and the capacity for decisions that shape its own future mode of being. From these two analyses, the paper derives two complementary alternatives: (1) application specificity, which narrows evaluation to concrete functional correctness within defined use cases (e.g., therapy, maternal health), and (2) treating AI as the outcome of political processes, drawing on Rousseau's general will, Estlund's epistemic democracy, and social choice theory to analyze the legitimacy of value aggregation in alignment rather than the 'ethics' of the model itself.

## Results

This is a position paper that makes conceptual and theoretical arguments rather than presenting empirical results. The paper does not report quantitative metrics, benchmarks, or experimental findings. Its central contribution is a two-axis analytical framework (mechanistic vs. volitional agency) applied to AI ethics, arguing that AI should be understood as the outcome of political processes rather than as a moral agent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare methods against baselines in an empirical sense. Instead, it contrasts two philosophical frameworks for understanding agency: the mechanistic view (drawing on List and Pettit, 2011) and the volitional view (drawing on Taylor, 1985). The paper argues that the mechanistic view, while commonly assumed in AI research, leads to a dead-end for accountability because AI cannot satisfy the 'relevant control' condition for moral responsibility. The volitional view, by contrast, disqualifies AI from agency altogether due to the absence of original purpose and intrinsic motivation. The paper then proposes two alternative analytical lenses—application specificity and AI as the outcome of political processes (drawing on Rousseau's general will and Estlund's epistemic democracy)—as more productive frameworks for discussing AI ethics and accountability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new conceptual ground by importing two competing frameworks from political philosophy—List and Pettit's mechanistic agency and Charles Taylor's volitional agency—into the AI ethics and alignment literature, where neither has been explicitly deployed. It departs from the implicit mechanistic-agency assumption that pervades LLM moral evaluation work (Nie et al., Tanmay et al., Scherrer et al., Lourie et al., Emelin et al., Hendrycks et al.), arguing that even under the most permissive (mechanistic) view, AI fails to qualify as a moral agent due to the 'relevant control' condition. For its constructive alternative, the paper draws on Winner's 'artifacts have politics,' Rousseau's general will, Estlund's epistemic democracy, and the social choice and participatory AI literatures (Feffer et al., Conitzer et al., Dai and Fleisig) to reframe AI alignment as a political-process question rather than an agent-evaluation question.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a foundational working assumption in AI alignment research—that LLMs can be meaningfully evaluated as moral agents by comparing their responses to human moral psychology—and argues this framing leads to a dead end for both forward-looking specification and backward-looking accountability. Its practical upshot is a shift in where accountability should be located: not in the model's 'ethical character' but in the legitimacy and correctness of the political processes that produce and deploy it. Key open questions include how to operationalize Rousseauian continuous participation in AI development, how social choice mechanisms differ fundamentally from RLHF's learning problem, and how to extend the political-process lens beyond model training to downstream deployment harms (e.g., AI in asylum translation). As a purely conceptual position paper with no empirical component, its impact depends on whether the community finds the philosophical reframing actionable.
