## Problem

In partial feedback settings (e.g., credit lending), true outcomes are only observed for positively classified samples, causing training data to misrepresent the population distribution and propagate biases against under-represented groups. Prior solutions either require strong assumptions about past decision accuracy, assume diverse outcomes are already available, or classify many samples positively in early iterations, incurring large false-positive costs. The paper asks whether a robust framework can simultaneously achieve high per-iteration utility, bounded false positives, and fair data collection across demographic groups.

## Input / Output

At each iteration t, the framework receives n unlabeled feature samples S_t drawn i.i.d. from the true distribution μ, along with all previously collected labeled data L_0,...,L_{t-1} and an FDR parameter α. It outputs binary predictions for every sample in S_t, observes true outcomes only for positively classified samples, and produces an updated classifier f_t for the next iteration. The domain is dynamically partitioned into EXPLOIT_t (points with sufficient observed outcomes, weight > τ) and EXPLORE_t (the remainder), and the number of positive predictions in the explore region is explicitly set to n_explore = (α − α_exploit − ε)·n_exploit/(1−α) to guarantee the overall FDR bound.

## Method

The framework (Algorithm 1) operates iteratively using an exploitation-exploration paradigm. In the exploitation step, a classifier f_t is trained via constrained cost-sensitive optimization over a reweighted distribution η_w that corrects for the biased sampling induced by past decisions, subject to an α_exploit-FDR constraint and a minimum selection rate. In the exploration step, a configurable strategy g(x,z;f_t) determines the sampling distribution over EXPLORE_t, and a fixed number of samples are positively classified to collect new outcome labels. Fairness is incorporated in two complementary ways: fairness constraints (e.g., statistical rate parity) during exploitation training, and group-aware exploration strategies (e.g., g^fair proportional to classifier output times group proportion in EXPLORE_t) that increase the rate at which under-represented groups are explored. Theoretical guarantees prove that the α-FDR constraint is satisfied at every iteration (Theorem 4.1), group-wise utility is monotonically non-decreasing (Theorem 4.2), and the classifier converges to the optimal offline classifier f_opt^α as t→∞ (Theorem 4.3), with convergence rate for group z governed by the exploration mass σ(z) assigned to that group.

## Results

On the Adult Income dataset (protected attribute: race), Algorithm 1 with both fairness constraints achieves an average revenue of 74.1k per iteration (SE 12.2k), FDR of 0.15, statistical rate disparity of 0.01, and TPR disparity of 0.06 — approaching the OPT-OFFLINE upper bound of 75.7k revenue while achieving substantially lower disparity (0.01 vs. 0.03 stat. rate; 0.06 vs. 0.12 TPR). On the German Credit dataset (protected attribute: gender), Algorithm 1 with both fairness constraints achieves 8.8k revenue, FDR 0.11, stat. rate 0.05, and TPR disparity 0.06, again near the OPT-OFFLINE revenue of 9.7k but with much lower disparity (0.05 vs. 0.15 stat. rate; 0.06 vs. 0.15 TPR). All Algorithm 1 variants maintain FDR at or below the 0.15 constraint, whereas Yang et al. violates it (FDR 0.35 on Adult) and Kilbertus et al. exceeds it on German (FDR 0.24).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Algorithm 1 substantially outperforms all three partial-feedback baselines on revenue: on Adult, it achieves 74.1k vs. 44.9k (Kilbertus et al.), −14.7k (Yang et al.), and −17.2k (Rateike et al.); on German, 8.8k vs. 9.7k (Kilbertus), −1.7k (Yang), and 2.2k (Rateike). Kilbertus et al. matches Algorithm 1 on German revenue (9.7k) but at the cost of a much higher FDR (0.24 vs. 0.11) and statistical rate disparity (0.11 vs. 0.05), indicating it trades fairness for short-term revenue. Yang et al. and Rateike et al. both violate the FDR constraint on Adult (0.35 and 0.12 respectively — though Rateike's is technically below 0.15, its revenue is deeply negative), and both produce negative cumulative revenue on Adult, reflecting large false-positive costs in early iterations. Against the OPT-OFFLINE upper bound, Algorithm 1 with both fairness constraints achieves 98% of the revenue on Adult (74.1 vs. 75.7) and 91% on German (8.8 vs. 9.7), while simultaneously achieving lower statistical rate and TPR disparity than OPT-OFFLINE on both datasets. The FAIR-CLF baseline (no explicit exploration) is competitive on German (8.7k revenue, similar fairness) but lags on Adult (71.1k vs. 74.1k) and shows higher TPR disparity (0.11 vs. 0.06), confirming that explicit exploration accelerates data collection for under-represented groups.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the partial feedback / selective labels literature (Bechavod et al., Wei, Kilbertus et al., Yang et al., Rateike et al.) by introducing an explicit exploration-exploitation partition with per-iteration FDR constraints, addressing the key limitation that prior methods either sacrifice short-term utility (large initial false positives) or lack fairness-aware data collection. It also builds on fair classification via constraints (Celis et al., Zafar et al.) but departs by incorporating fairness not only in the classifier training (exploitation) but also in the exploration sampling strategy, a dimension absent from prior fair classification work. The reweighting technique for correcting distributional bias in the exploit region draws on standard cost-sensitive learning, while the theoretical analysis (monotonic utility improvement, convergence to the FDR-constrained optimal classifier) is novel in this combined setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework is practically relevant for high-stakes iterative decision-making (lending, healthcare) where institutions cannot afford large false-positive losses in any single period, and where historical biases systematically under-represent marginalized groups in labeled data. The dual fairness mechanism (constraints in exploitation + fair sampling in exploration) offers a concrete recipe that prior fair classification literature did not provide. Key limitations include the linear-in-|D| sample complexity, the assumption of i.i.d. samples per iteration, and the requirement that all outcomes are observed before the next iteration. Open questions include handling distribution shifts over time, continuous feature spaces, multi-class settings, and mitigating individual-level harms caused by stochastic exploration.
