## Problem

While spiking neural networks offer energy-efficient, biologically inspired computation, it remains unclear how they can perform human-like graph reasoning (e.g., knowledge graph link prediction) by optimally exploiting the temporal domain. Prior SNN attempts at relational reasoning relied on entity embeddings from single-neuron spiking times, failing to propagate reasoning paths at the network level and showing poor inductive generalization. This paper asks whether synaptic delay and temporal coding can serve as a principled mechanism for encoding relation properties and propagating multi-hop reasoning paths in SNNs.

## Input / Output

The model takes a knowledge graph G=(V,E,R) and a query triplet (x, q, y), where a constant current dependent on the query relation q is injected into the source node's neuron population. Each graph node is represented by n=32 current-based LIF spiking neurons, and the pair representation h^q(x,y) is extracted from the target node's spike train via a temporal decoding function D(s) = (Σ_τ λ^τ s[τ])/(Σ_τ λ^τ) with λ=0.95, emphasizing earlier spikes. A two-layer MLP head then maps h^q(x,y) and the relation embedding to a link prediction score. The simulation runs over T=10 discrete time steps (Δτ=5 ms, τ_m=20 ms), and synaptic delays are quantized to integers bounded by β=4.

## Method

GRSNN generalizes the path formulation of graph algorithms into a spiking neural network by assigning learnable synaptic delays d_r^q = β·σ(W_r·r^q + b_r) to each edge, where the delay depends on both the edge's relation r and the query relation q, effectively encoding relation properties in the temporal domain. Spike trains propagate through the network over discrete time steps, with spiking times accumulating the integrated properties of all paths from source to target; the model is shown to degenerate to Katz Index, Personalized PageRank, and Graph Distance under specific delay and decoding settings (Proposition 3.1). Training uses backpropagation through time with a sigmoid surrogate gradient for the non-differentiable spiking function and a straight-through estimator for the quantized integer delays. Unlike GNNs that propagate real-valued messages uniformly across all edges per layer, GRSNN propagates sparse temporal spike trains with relation-dependent delays, enabling event-driven computation and a theoretical ~20× energy reduction over non-spiking counterparts. The approach is framed as a neural generalization of Dijkstra's algorithm, contrasting with NBFNet's Bellman-Ford interpretation, and achieves competitive performance on transductive KG completion, inductive relation prediction, and homogeneous graph link prediction.

## Results

On transductive knowledge graph completion, GRSNN achieves MRR 0.368 and HITS@10 0.551 on FB15k-237, and MRR 0.508 and HITS@10 0.616 on WN18RR, outperforming all embedding methods and most GNN baselines. With added GNN techniques (GRSNN+), performance improves to MRR 0.393 on FB15k-237 and MRR 0.532 on WN18RR. On inductive relation prediction, GRSNN achieves HITS@10 of 0.852–0.958 across four FB15k-237 splits, matching or exceeding NBFNet. The model also reports a spike rate of ~0.258, enabling a theoretical ~20× energy reduction over non-spiking counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRSNN outperforms all embedding baselines (TransE, DistMult, ComplEx, RotatE, LowFER) and the RGCN/GraIL GNNs on both FB15k-237 and WN18RR, achieving MRR 0.368 vs. RotatE's 0.338 and CompGCN's 0.355 on FB15k-237, and MRR 0.508 vs. RotatE's 0.476 and CompGCN's 0.479 on WN18RR. The strongest baseline, NBFNet, still leads GRSNN by 4.7 MRR points on FB15k-237 (0.415 vs. 0.368) and 4.3 points on WN18RR (0.551 vs. 0.508); adding GNN tricks (GRSNN+) narrows this to 2.2 and 1.9 points respectively. On inductive relation prediction, GRSNN matches or slightly exceeds NBFNet on FB15k-237 splits (e.g., HITS@10 0.957 vs. 0.949 on v2) but trails slightly on WN18RR (0.892 vs. 0.905 on v2). On homogeneous graph link prediction, GRSNN is competitive with NBFNet on PubMed (AUROC 0.982 vs. 0.983) but lags on Cora (0.936 vs. 0.956) and CiteSeer (0.915 vs. 0.923). The ablation in Table 1 confirms that synaptic delay encoding (MRR 0.368) substantially outperforms both no-relation-encoding (0.204) and weight-only encoding (0.311) on FB15k-237.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRSNN extends two distinct lines of work: (1) Aimone et al. (2021), who showed that synaptic delays in SNNs can parallelize Dijkstra's algorithm for shortest-path problems, and (2) Zhu et al. (2021) (NBFNet), who formalized graph reasoning as a generalized path formulation and showed GNNs as a neural Bellman-Ford. The key conceptual leap is generalizing the path formulation into a spiking framework where synaptic delay encodes edge/relation properties and spiking time integrates path properties across the network, positioning GRSNN as a neural generalization of Dijkstra's algorithm. This departs from prior SNN-for-graphs work (Dold & Garrido, 2021; Dold, 2022) that used single-neuron spike times for entity embeddings, by operating at the network level with multiple temporal spikes representing diverse paths.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a biologically-inspired SNN with only 10 discrete time steps and 32 neurons per node can achieve competitive (and in inductive settings, superior) performance versus sophisticated GNNs like NBFNet, while offering path-level interpretability and a theoretical ~20× energy reduction. This opens a pathway toward energy-efficient, interpretable graph reasoning on neuromorphic hardware. Key limitations include the reliance on discrete simulation (T=10 sacrifices temporal precision), inability to incorporate many GNN tricks (PNA, complex message functions), and the fact that energy savings are theoretical estimates rather than hardware measurements. Open questions include whether online/Hebbian learning of synaptic delays is feasible, whether heterogeneous neuron dynamics can close the remaining performance gap, and whether the approach scales to larger or more complex graph tasks.
