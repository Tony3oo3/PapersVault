## Problem

Standard RLHF pipelines train a reward model from pairwise preferences, implicitly assuming a total order over trajectories that cannot represent intransitive, non-Markovian, or stochastic human preferences. The alternative dueling formulation (two-player zero-sum game) avoids reward models but inherits the well-known instabilities of adversarial training. The paper asks whether one can eliminate both the reward model and the second player while retaining strong convergence guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes trajectory pairs and a preference oracle P: Ξ×Ξ→[-1,1] (anti-symmetric, with P(ξ,ξ)=0), which in practice is either a trained pairwise preference model or a prompted generative model. The output is a single trained policy π that is an approximate Minimax Winner — a policy that, in expectation, is preferred to any other policy in the class. Trajectory-level rewards are split uniformly across all H timesteps (r(ξ)/H per step) to enable standard per-step policy gradient methods, and a fixed-size queue (typically 10–100 trajectories) provides the comparison set for computing win-rate rewards.

## Method

SPO frames RLHF as a two-player zero-sum game with payoff given by the preference function, then exploits the anti-symmetry of P to prove that a symmetric Nash equilibrium (Minimax Winner) always exists and can be computed by a single agent playing against its own iterates. Concretely, at each round the agent samples a trajectory, computes its win rate against a queue of previously sampled on-policy trajectories via the preference oracle, and uses that win rate as the reward for a standard RL update (SAC or PPO in practice; soft policy iteration / Hedge in the tabular theory). The main theorem (Theorem 2.3) shows the time-averaged policy is a 2Reg(T)/T-approximate Minimax Winner, reducing MW computation to any no-regret online linear optimization algorithm. When a true underlying reward function exists, SPO achieves a fast Õ(1/T) convergence rate (Corollary 2.5), matching UCB-style methods, while in the general intransitive case it converges at the standard Õ(1/√T) no-regret rate.

## Results

SPO achieves an 8H√(log(|A|)/T)-approximate Minimax Winner guarantee (Corollary 2.6), and when a unique optimal policy exists, converges at the fast Õ(1/T) rate (Corollary 2.5). Empirically, on intransitive preferences over a discrete 3-option set, SPO recovers the exact MW across all sub-population weightings while the RM baseline always converges to a deterministic corner policy. On the non-Markovian task (maximize reward subject to a last-quarter constraint), SPO consistently crosses the 4·r_max threshold indicating qualitatively non-Markovian behavior, while RM often fails to do so. On maximum-reward DMControl tasks, SPO matches or slightly improves upon the iterative RM baseline in sample efficiency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole empirical baseline is an iterative Reward Modeling (RM) approach following Christiano et al. (2017) and Lee et al. (2021a), where a Markovian reward model trained via Bradley-Terry loss on trajectory-level comparisons is continuously updated during policy search (making it a maximally iterative, online RM method). SPO decisively wins on intransitive preferences: it recovers the Minimax Winner almost exactly while RM is forced to impose a total order and converges to a corner far from the MW. SPO also wins on non-Markovian preferences, consistently achieving the required behavioral shift while RM struggles to cross the performance threshold. On maximum-reward (transitive, Markovian) preferences, SPO matches or slightly outperforms RM in sample efficiency across DMControl environments. On stochastic (noisy) preferences, SPO performs comparably to RM on some environments but noticeably worse on others, suggesting that the extra parametric model in RM provides some denoising benefit when preferences are corrupted. Overall, SPO's advantage is most pronounced in the challenging, realistic settings (intransitive, non-Markovian) where the reward-model assumption breaks down.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the dueling bandits / two-player zero-sum game framing of preference-based RL (Yue et al., 2012; Dudik et al., 2015) and the reduction of RLHF to adversarial MDP solving (Wang et al., 2023), but makes two key departures. First, it eliminates the need for a reward model entirely by adopting the Minimax Winner from social choice theory as the solution concept, which naturally handles intransitive and non-Markovian preferences that reward models cannot represent. Second, it exploits the anti-symmetry of preference functions to prove that a single agent playing against itself (self-play) suffices to compute the Minimax Winner, bypassing the instability of adversarial training. The practical algorithm reduces to a no-regret online learning problem (instantiated via Soft Policy Iteration / NPG), connecting the RLHF literature to the well-studied Hedge/OGD framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPO provides a theoretically grounded, reward-model-free alternative to the dominant two-stage RLHF pipeline, with provable convergence to the Minimax Winner even under intransitive and non-Markovian preferences that are common in practice. The single-player self-play simplification is practically significant: it eliminates adversarial training instability and the memory overhead of maintaining two models, reducing the method to simple reward relabeling on top of any standard RL algorithm (SAC, PPO). Key open questions include extending the analysis to the contextual bandit setting relevant to LLM fine-tuning, understanding last-iterate convergence under bandit feedback, and whether learning an iterative preference model (rather than using raw win-rates) can close the gap SPO shows in some noisy-preference settings. The experiments are limited to continuous control tasks, leaving the most impactful application (LLM alignment) untested.
