## Problem

Class imbalance is a pervasive bottleneck in real-world classification, yet how under/oversampling strategies should adapt to the specific statistics of the data (class ratio, first and second moments, margin) remains poorly understood. Existing mitigation strategies are tested empirically without a principled theoretical framework that predicts optimal sampling ratios a priori, impeding the design of task-adapted protocols.

## Input / Output

The theoretical framework consumes categorical (Potts) data points one-hot encoded in R^{L×Q} with binary labels, characterized by their class-specific means M, rescaled center-shift δ, and shared covariance C, along with class sizes α± and a hinge-loss margin κ. It produces closed-form asymptotic expressions for every element of the confusion matrix and derived metrics (BA, ACC, AUC, AUPRC, F1, MCC, etc.) as functions of the training composition, and identifies the optimal under/oversampling mixing percentage. The empirical component additionally uses an RBM trained unsupervised on the minority class to score and generate samples for Likelihood-Informed Sampling.

## Method

The paper derives exact high-dimensional asymptotics for linear SVMs (spherical perceptrons with hinge loss) trained on imbalanced categorical data using the replica method from statistical mechanics. Data are modeled as Potts configurations with specified first and second moments under a homoscedasticity assumption, and the ERM problem is mapped to a statistical mechanics partition function whose saddle-point equations yield order parameters (r, q, b) that fully determine the test-time pre-activation distribution. From this distribution, all standard performance metrics are computed analytically, revealing that AUC is insensitive to imbalance while balanced accuracy correctly identifies the balanced point as optimal. The framework then quantifies the effect of mixing under- and oversampling (parameterized by a duplication factor c) and shows that mixed strategies outperform pure random undersampling or oversampling, with the optimal mix depending on the imbalance ratio, data statistics, and margin. For empirical validation beyond the linear theory, the authors propose Likelihood-Informed Sampling (LIS), where an RBM trained on the minority class generates new minority examples via Gibbs sampling and filters majority examples by log-likelihood, and demonstrate improvements with both linear SVMs and a ResNet-50 fine-tuned on CIFAR-10.

## Results

The paper derives exact analytical expressions for generalization curves of linear SVMs on imbalanced categorical data in the high-dimensional limit (L, P, N → ∞ at fixed ratios). Key findings: (i) Balanced Accuracy (BA) peaks at the balanced training point ρ⁺ = 0.5 and drops to zero for imbalanced training, while AUC is largely insensitive to imbalance and ACC misleadingly favors heavily imbalanced datasets; (ii) mixed under/oversampling strategies (intermediate values of the oversampling factor c) outperform both pure Random Undersampling (RUS) and pure Random Oversampling (ROS), with the optimal mixing percentage depending on the initial imbalance ratio, dataset size, class similarity, and margin κ; (iii) Likelihood-Informed Sampling (LIS) using RBMs trained on the minority class outperforms naive random under/oversampling on MNIST, and balanced training of a ResNet-50 on CIFAR-10 yields visibly better class separation in the final feature layer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external methods but instead compares sampling strategies and performance metrics within its theoretical framework. Among sampling strategies, full Random Undersampling (RUS, c=1) is consistently sub-optimal; mixed under/oversampling achieves the highest BA for severe imbalance, while full Random Oversampling (ROS, c=α⁻/α⁺) is preferred for milder imbalance. Among metrics, BA is the most reliable indicator of true classification quality under imbalance (peaking at ρ⁺=0.5), whereas AUC is nearly flat across imbalance ratios (being independent of the bias b) and ACC paradoxically favors heavily imbalanced training sets. For improved sampling, Likelihood-Informed Sampling (LIS) using RBMs outperforms both RUS and ROS on binary MNIST, particularly when starting from very small positive class sizes. For deep classifiers, a ResNet-50 fine-tuned on balanced CIFAR-10 (P=N) achieves visibly better class separation in the final feature layer (via t-SNE) compared to the imbalanced setting (P=0.1N), confirming that the imbalance sensitivity observed for linear SVMs extends to deep architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the statistical mechanics of learning framework (Engel & Van den Broeck, 2001) and the spherical perceptron/SVM equivalence (Franz et al., 2019) to the setting of imbalanced binary classification with categorical (Potts) data, using the replica method to derive exact high-dimensional generalization curves. It departs from prior theoretical work on Gaussian mixtures (Loureiro et al., 2021; Mignacco et al., 2020) by handling multistate discrete variables and, crucially, by studying the effect of dataset-preprocessing mitigation strategies (under/oversampling) rather than only the classifier itself. The empirical component builds on RBM-SMOTE (Zięba et al., 2015) but generalizes it into a unified likelihood-informed sampling protocol that simultaneously generates minority-class samples and filters majority-class ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first asymptotic characterization of linear classifiers on imbalanced categorical datasets, providing a quantitative, data-statistics-dependent prediction of the optimal under/oversampling mixing ratio—a practical guideline that was previously available only through empirical tuning. The finding that AUC is structurally insensitive to imbalance (independent of the bias parameter most affected by class skew) while balanced accuracy is not, has direct implications for how practitioners should evaluate imbalanced classifiers. Key limitations include the homoscedasticity assumption (identical class covariances), restriction to binary classification and linear models, and the fact that the deep-network and RBM experiments are qualitative rather than analytically grounded. Open directions include extending to heteroscedastic classes, multiclass settings, and connecting the linear-model theory to kernel or infinite-width deep network limits.
