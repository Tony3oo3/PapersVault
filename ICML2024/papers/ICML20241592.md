## Problem

Learning patient-specific brain stimulation policies for stroke rehabilitation requires balancing sample efficiency with task performance, since each patient's brain is unique and online interactions are costly and potentially harmful. Model-free RL methods demand too many patient interactions to converge, while existing model-based approaches do not exploit the structural decomposition of the problem: the gap between optimal world actions and the subset of actions an injured brain can actually produce through stimulation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241592/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a world MDP (continuous state space of joint angles, continuous world action space of muscle activations, transition dynamics, and reward) plus a stimulation action space, and produces a closed-loop coprocessor policy mapping patient states to stimulation parameters. The brain model F̂_brain^φ is a 3-layer MLP (64-32-8, ReLU) that maps (state, stimulation) to a world action, while the Q-function Q^ψ maps (state, world action) to a scalar value. The final deployed policy selects stimulations that maximize Q^ψ under the learned brain model, effectively producing muscle activation commands that the injured brain can execute.

## Method

CopAC decomposes coprocessor policy learning into three stages: (1) offline training of a world-action Q-function Q^ψ via SAC in a biomechanical simulator (MyoSuite), requiring no patient interaction; (2) online learning of a brain model F̂_brain^φ that maps stimulations to world actions, where stimulation sampling is guided by Q^ψ to focus on high-value regions of the action space (Eq. 5); and (3) iterative recalibration of Q^ψ within the simulated coprocessor MDP (Eq. 6) to correct over-optimism arising from actions that the lesioned brain cannot realize. Stages 2 and 3 alternate during online patient interaction, with the brain model updated via MSE loss on observed (stimulation, world action) pairs. This decomposition is the key differentiator: by pre-computing world-action values offline and using them to direct online exploration, CopAC achieves ~10× sample efficiency over MFRL baselines while maintaining high training reward through on-policy, Q-guided stimulation selection.

## Results

CopAC achieves more than a 10× improvement in sample efficiency over the MFRL baseline (SAC) across multiple environments, learning an effective stimulation policy in fewer than 25 episodes of online patient interaction. In complex motor tasks (e.g., MyoSim Hand Pose with a 39-dimensional action space), CopAC substantially outperforms both SAC and MBPO in both evaluation and training reward, while in simpler tasks (e.g., MyoSim Arm Reach, Pendulum) MBPO performs on par with CopAC. CopAC also achieves significantly higher training reward than both baselines, meaning the patient receives more useful stimulation during the learning phase itself.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SAC (the MFRL baseline), CopAC consistently wins on both sample efficiency and final evaluation reward; SAC often fails to improve significantly beyond random sampling within the 25-episode budget, and when it does match CopAC's performance it typically requires roughly double the number of interactions. Against MBPO (the MBRL baseline), CopAC wins decisively in complex, high-dimensional action-space environments such as MyoSim Hand Pose, where MBPO learns much more slowly, but ties or performs comparably in simpler environments like MyoSim Arm Reach and Pendulum where the dynamics model is easier to learn. On training reward (a critical metric for patient experience during online learning), CopAC outperforms both SAC and MBPO across all environments, owing to its Q-guided sampling strategy that selects stimulations producing high-value world actions rather than exploring off-task behaviors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the neural coprocessor framework of Bryan et al. (2022), which demonstrated that a learned mapping from stimulations to world actions can restore motor function in a cortical model, and on SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) as the underlying actor-critic mechanism. It departs from prior RL-for-brain-stimulation work (Gao et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2020a), which treated stimulation policy learning as a monolithic MFRL problem requiring extensive patient interaction, by decomposing the problem into a simulation-learned world-action Q-function and an online-learned brain model. The key conceptual departure from standard MBRL (e.g., MBPO) is that the 'model' is not a full environment dynamics model but a partial model of the brain's stimulation-to-action mapping, with the world dynamics handled by a biomechanical simulator rather than learned from data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× sample-efficiency gain is practically significant for clinical deployment, where each patient interaction is costly and potentially harmful, and the new MyoSim Stroke Domain provides the first physiologically and neurologically realistic in silico benchmark for adaptive brain stimulation. However, the work is entirely in silico, with no in vivo validation, and the reliance on a high-fidelity biomechanical simulator introduces a sim-to-real gap that remains unaddressed. Open questions include whether the simulation-trained Q-function transfers to real patients, how to provide safety guarantees during online learning, and whether the approach generalizes when no simulator is available (the offline RL variant in the appendix is a step toward this but performs slightly worse).
