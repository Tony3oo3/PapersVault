## Problem

Numerous works have postulated that in-context learning (ICL) in LLMs approximates Bayesian inference, but no principled, falsifiable test of this hypothesis existed. This paper identifies the martingale property—a necessary condition for any Bayesian learning system on exchangeable data—as a concrete diagnostic, and shows that state-of-the-art LLMs violate it, thereby falsifying the Bayesian ICL hypothesis.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained LLM (Llama-2-7B, Mistral-7B, GPT-3-2.7B/170B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) and a synthetic exchangeable dataset (Bernoulli, Gaussian, or synthetic clinical NLP) of size n, then autoregressively generates J sample paths of length m from the LLM conditioned on the observed data. It produces three test statistics—T_{1,g} (marginal predictive invariance), T_{2,k} (conditional covariance invariance), and T_3 (epistemic uncertainty spread via MLE on sampled paths)—each compared against bootstrap 95% confidence intervals from a reference Bayesian model with a correctly specified likelihood. A key practical constraint is that the LLM must not be able to distinguish its own generated tokens from the observed ICL data, which required special API handling for GPT-4's ChatCompletion interface.

## Method

The paper formalizes the martingale property (conditional identical distribution of future samples) as a necessary condition for exchangeability and hence for Bayesian inference via de Finetti's theorem. It then derives two actionable diagnostics from an equivalent characterisation (Proposition 1): (i) invariance of conditional expectations E[g(Z_{n+1})|Z_{1:n}] = E[g(Z_{n+k})|Z_{1:n}], and (ii) invariance of conditional second moments E[(Z_{n'+k+1}−Z_{n'+1})Z_{n'}^T|Z_{1:n}] = 0, both estimable from finite autoregressive sample paths. A third diagnostic, T_3, measures the inter-quartile range of MLEs computed on sampled paths to test whether epistemic uncertainty scales as O(d/n) as required by classical posterior contraction. The martingale property also yields a 'martingale posterior' via Doob's convergence theorem, enabling a principled decomposition of predictive uncertainty into epistemic (reducible with more data) and aleatoric (irreducible) components. Experiments across five LLM families on three synthetic tasks show that while short-horizon paths (m = n/2) approximately satisfy the martingale property, longer horizons (m = 2n) exhibit systematic drift, and the epistemic uncertainty scaling deviates from the Bayesian O(d/n) rate, falsifying the hypothesis.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that state-of-the-art LLMs (Llama-2-7B, Mistral-7B, GPT-3-2.7B/170B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) violate the martingale property, falsifying the hypothesis that ICL is Bayesian. For Bernoulli experiments (n=50), most LLMs pass the martingale checks on short sample paths (m=n/2=25), but show significant deviations on longer paths (m=2n=100), with GPT-4 already deviating at θ∈{0.3, 0.5} even for short paths. In the Gaussian experiment (θ=−1, n=100, m=50), all models except GPT-3.5 demonstrate clear deviation from the reference Bayesian posterior. For epistemic uncertainty scaling (T₃), Llama-2-7B and GPT-3.5 scale slower than the O(d/n) Bayesian rate, while GPT-3-170B and Mistral-7B are closer to the Bayesian reference but still not exact matches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against a reference Bayesian model (Bernoulli likelihood with Beta(1,1) prior for the Bernoulli experiment; Gaussian likelihood with N(0,100) prior for the Gaussian experiment), whose bootstrap 95% confidence intervals serve as the acceptance region for the test statistics T_{1,g} and T_{2,k}. LLMs generally fall within these CIs for short sampling horizons but increasingly fall outside them as the path length grows, indicating deviation from all reasonable Bayesian models in the Bernstein–von Mises sense (the paper notes that different regular Bayesian priors, e.g. Beta(1,1) vs. Beta(1,11), produce practically indistinguishable CIs, so the LLM deviations cannot be attributed to prior misspecification). For the epistemic uncertainty scaling (T₃), the paper additionally compares against α-fractional Bayesian posteriors (Bhattacharya et al., 2019), which exhibit a weaker O(d/αn) scaling; Llama-2-7B and GPT-3.5 fail to match even this weaker rate, while GPT-3-170B and Mistral-7B approach the standard Bayesian O(d/n) rate. GPT-3.5 also exhibits over-confidence in the small-sample regime. The paper further notes that GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (the only models with instruction tuning) perform worst, suggesting instruction tuning may exacerbate non-Bayesian ICL behaviour.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the martingale posterior framework of Fong et al. (2021) and the limit theorems for conditionally identically distributed sequences from Berti et al. (2004) to derive a testable necessary condition for Bayesian ICL. It departs sharply from the large body of work (Xie et al., 2021; Hahn & Goyal, 2023; Akyurek et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023b; Jiang, 2023) that has argued ICL approximates Bayesian inference, instead providing a falsification via the martingale property and de Finetti's theorem. The paper also leverages the Bernstein-von Mises theorem to argue that violations observed in synthetic settings imply deviation from all regular Bayesian models, not just a specific reference model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, actionable diagnostics (test statistics T1, T2, T3) for checking whether an LLM's ICL behaviour is Bayesian, with direct implications for safety-critical applications like clinical trial analysis where incoherent predictions from order-dependent imputation could be dangerous. The finding that instruction-tuned models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4) exhibit worse Bayesian compliance than their non-instruction-tuned counterparts (GPT-3-170B) raises open questions about whether fine-tuning objectives can restore Bayesian behaviour. Key limitations include the restriction to small-scale synthetic datasets and the fact that violations are most pronounced for longer sampling paths, leaving open whether the findings generalise to the complex, multi-token settings of real NLP tasks.
