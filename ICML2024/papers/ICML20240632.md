## Problem

No Free Lunch theorems are routinely cited to justify domain-specific inductive biases and manual model selection, but their uniform-distribution assumption implicitly selects incompressible data where learning is provably impossible. This paper asks whether the low Kolmogorov complexity shared by real-world datasets and the simplicity bias inherent in neural networks can explain broad generalization with a single model class, and whether the practical tasks of model selection and architecture choice can be automated.

## Input / Output

The method consumes labeled datasets in heterogeneous formats: tabular features are reshaped into single-channel images (one pixel per feature, zero-padded), standard RGB images (CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet), and numerical sequences generated from binary arithmetic expression trees. Outputs include lossless compressed label encodings via arithmetic coding with a trained model's probability distribution, PAC-Bayes generalization bounds derived from the compressed size, and per-sequence log-probabilities from language models. A notable design choice is feeding tabular data through CNNs specifically to test whether their spatial inductive bias is necessary or whether a generic simplicity bias suffices.

## Method

The paper derives a new Kolmogorov-complexity-based No Free Lunch theorem (Theorem 3.1) showing that on uniformly sampled incompressible data, no classifier can beat random cross-entropy in the large-n limit, then empirically demonstrates that real datasets are far from incompressible by compressing their labeling functions with trained MLPs and CNNs via arithmetic coding. To characterize the model side, the authors show that GPT-3 variants (and even randomly initialized GPT-2) assign exponentially higher probability to low-complexity sequences, and that CNNs achieve strong generalization on tabular data with no spatial structure, with PAC-Bayes compression bounds nearly matching test error. The practical upshot is that a single flexible model with a soft simplicity bias—implemented as Tikhonov regularization on polynomial degree or as a weight-decayed convex combination of GoogLeNet and ViT logits—matches or exceeds each constituent model across both small (CIFAR) and large (ImageNet) data regimes. Finally, a finite-hypothesis-bound calculation shows that cross-validation over up to 100 million architectures on a 20k validation set incurs less than 3.4% generalization gap with 99% probability, explaining why benchmark overfitting is provably negligible.

## Results

The paper's central empirical claim is that a single flexible model with a simplicity bias can match specialized architectures across data scales. In Table 2, the GoogLeNet+ViT convex combination achieves 93.86% on CIFAR-10 (matching GoogLeNet's 93.84%) and 81.09% on ImageNet (matching ViT-B/16's 81.07%), while GoogLeNet+Swin reaches 75.36% on CIFAR-100 (matching GoogLeNet's 75.16%) and 83.15% on ImageNet (nearly matching Swin-B's 83.58%). Compression experiments show that trained MLPs and CNNs compress tabular and image labels well beyond the naive n·log₂(C) encoding, and PAC-Bayes compression bounds on CNNs fed tabular data nearly match test error, confirming a generic simplicity bias. GPT-3 variants assign exponentially lower log-probabilities to higher-complexity sequences, and even randomly initialized GPT-2 models exhibit the same bias, rejecting the null hypothesis of uniform generation with p < 0.0001.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three individual architectures—GoogLeNet (Szegedy et al., 2015), ViT-B/16 (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020), and Swin-B (Liu et al., 2021)—against their convex combinations with a simplicity-bias weight decay. GoogLeNet+ViT essentially ties GoogLeNet on CIFAR-10 (93.86% vs. 93.84%) and ViT-B/16 on ImageNet (81.09% vs. 81.07%), while sacrificing only 3.2% on CIFAR-100 (71.99% vs. 75.16%). GoogLeNet+Swin ties GoogLeNet on CIFAR-100 (75.36% vs. 75.16%) and comes within 0.43% of Swin-B on ImageNet (83.15% vs. 83.58%), while matching GoogLeNet on CIFAR-10 (93.76% vs. 93.84%). In both cases, the combined model achieves the best-of-both-worlds: it is more flexible than the small model (matching the large model on ImageNet) while retaining the simplicity bias of the small model (matching it on CIFAR). This demonstrates that manual architecture selection based on dataset size can be replaced by a single learner with a soft simplicity preference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the PAC-Bayes compression framework of Lotfi et al. (2022) and the finite hypothesis bound of Langford & Seeger (2001), repurposing them to argue that a single universal prior (the Solomonoff prior) suffices across domains rather than requiring problem-specific priors as in Dziugaite & Roy (2017, 2018) or Pérez-Ortiz et al. (2021). Conceptually, it departs from the standard interpretation of No Free Lunch theorems (Wolpert 1996; Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David 2014) by showing that the uniform-distribution assumption selects incompressible data where learning is provably impossible, and that real datasets and neural networks both share a low-Kolmogorov-complexity structure that nullifies the theorem's practical implications. The simplicity bias experiments connect to prior observations of low-entropy function preferences in random networks (Valle-Perez et al. 2018; De Palma et al. 2019) and low-rank biases (Huh et al. 2022), but extend them to language models and cross-domain tabular data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled theoretical justification for the industry trend toward unified transformer architectures across modalities, arguing that the shared low-complexity structure of real data and the inherent simplicity bias of neural networks make domain-specific inductive biases largely unnecessary. However, the authors acknowledge in their limitations section that the Kolmogorov complexity bounds are likely very loose, the experiments are proof-of-concept in nature, and the findings cannot be proven to hold universally for all datasets. Open questions include whether the simplicity bias is sufficient for all problem types (the paper itself notes transformers are not Turing complete and fail on recursive problems), and whether the gap between the loose theoretical bounds and actual performance can be tightened to provide actionable guidance for model selection.
