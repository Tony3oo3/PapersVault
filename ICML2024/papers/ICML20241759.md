## Problem

Existing audio watermarking methods are not designed for proactive detection of AI-generated speech: they assume all samples are watermarked (leading to unreliable false positive rates), operate on entire audio clips without temporal localization, and rely on brute-force synchronization that makes them prohibitively slow for real-time or large-scale deployment. AudioSeal addresses all three gaps by introducing the first audio watermarking technique purpose-built for localized, sample-level detection of synthesized speech.

## Input / Output

The generator takes a 16 kHz audio waveform (1-second training samples, up to 10 seconds at evaluation) and an optional 16-bit binary message, producing a watermarked waveform via additive perturbation. The detector performs a single forward pass over the input and outputs a per-sample probability tensor of shape (T, 1+b), where T is the number of samples (16,000 per second), the first channel gives the watermark-presence score at each time step (1/16k second resolution), and the remaining b channels give soft bit estimates for model attribution. No sliding window or synchronization search is required at inference.

## Method

AudioSeal jointly trains an EnCodec-derived generator (convolutional encoder with LSTM + transposed-convolution decoder) that predicts an additive watermark waveform, and a detector (shared-architecture encoder + transposed convolution + linear layer) that emits per-sample detection logits. The key training innovations are: (1) a masked sample-level BCE loss where random segments of the watermarked signal are replaced with silence, original audio, or other batch samples, forcing the detector to localize the watermark boundary; (2) a novel TF-Loudness loss that penalizes the watermark's loudness relative to the host signal in non-overlapping time-frequency bands using ITU-R BS.1770-4 loudness estimation, inspired by auditory masking to hide the watermark where the ear is already saturated; and (3) aggressive differentiable audio-edit augmentations (filters, noise, compression via straight-through estimator) applied during training to enforce robustness. For multi-bit attribution, a learnable embedding layer injects the binary message into the generator's mid-level activations, and additional linear heads in the decoder output per-bit soft values, with a decoding BCE loss averaged over watermarked regions.

## Results

AudioSeal achieves an average AUC of 0.97 across 15 audio editing operations, compared to 0.84 for WavMark, with near-perfect detection (TPR 0.98, FPR 0.04) on average. For localization, AudioSeal reaches an IoU of 0.99 when only 1 second of a 10-second clip is watermarked, versus 0.35 for WavMark. In terms of efficiency, AudioSeal's single-pass detector is 485× faster than WavMark when no watermark is present and 35× faster when one is, while generation is 14× faster. On perceptual quality, AudioSeal scores 4.470 PESQ and 77.07 MUSHRA (vs. 4.302 and 71.52 for WavMark), despite a lower SI-SNR (26.00 vs. 38.25), reflecting its design priority on perceptual indistinguishability over raw signal-to-noise ratio.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against WavMark (Chen et al., 2023), AudioSeal wins decisively on robustness (average AUC 0.97 vs. 0.84), with particularly large gaps on lowpass filtering (AUC 0.99 vs. 0.50), white noise (0.95 vs. 0.50), speed changes (1.00 vs. 0.15), and EnCodec compression (1.00 vs. 0.50); WavMark only wins on highpass filtering (1.00 vs. 0.61), which is less practically relevant since speech remains audible under lowpass but not highpass at the same cutoff. On localization, AudioSeal achieves IoU 0.99 for a 1-second watermark in a 10-second clip versus WavMark's 0.35, and operates at sample-level (1/16k sec) resolution versus WavMark's 1-second granularity. On efficiency, AudioSeal's single-pass detector is 485× faster than WavMark's brute-force sliding-window search when no watermark is present and 35× faster when one is, while generation is 14× faster. On perceptual quality, AudioSeal trades SI-SNR (26.00 vs. 38.25) for better human-perceived quality (PESQ 4.470 vs. 4.302, MUSHRA 77.07 vs. 71.52). Against the Voicebox passive classifier (Le et al., 2023), AudioSeal achieves perfect detection (100% accuracy, 0% FPR) across all masking levels for both original-vs-generated and re-synthesized-vs-generated tasks, while the passive classifier degrades to 70.4–90.7% accuracy with 11.2–19.4% FPR on the re-synthesized comparison, indicating it detects vocoder artifacts rather than true AI generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

AudioSeal builds on the generator/decoder paradigm of deep-learning audio watermarking (WavMark, Pavlovic et al., Liu et al.) and the EnCodec neural audio compression architecture, but deliberately departs from the multi-bit data-hiding framing. Where prior methods train a decoder that assumes all inputs are watermarked and rely on brute-force sliding-window synchronization for detection, AudioSeal trains a single-pass detector that handles both watermarked and non-watermarked inputs and outputs per-sample logits, enabling sample-level localization. The novel TF-Loudness loss, inspired by psychoacoustic auditory masking, replaces the conventional SI-SNR minimization objective, and the joint training with masked-sample augmentation is a new mechanism for achieving localization without synchronization patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 485× speedup in detection (when no watermark is present) and the single-pass architecture make large-scale, real-time watermarking feasible for voice synthesis APIs and social media platforms — a practical barrier that prior brute-force methods could not overcome. The adversarial analysis reveals a critical deployment constraint: detector weights must remain private, as white-box gradient attacks can increase detection error by ~80% while preserving audio quality (PESQ > 4), raising open questions about long-term security as generative models improve and watermarking becomes more widely known. The finding that theoretical FPR guarantees from multi-bit watermarking do not hold empirically (WavMark's scores are biased toward 0 on genuine audio) challenges a common assumption in the field and motivates the detector-based approach.
