## Problem

Application-driven machine learning (ADML) research—where algorithms are designed around real-world challenges, domain-specific metrics, and auxiliary knowledge—is systemically under-valued in the ML community. Reviewing, hiring, and teaching practices are largely calibrated to a methods-driven paradigm (standardized benchmarks, narrow evaluation metrics, problem-agnostic methods), causing high-quality ADML work to be rejected, under-recognized, and under-taught, which in turn disincentivizes the next generation of researchers from pursuing application-oriented innovation.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, this work has no computational input or output. It takes as its analytical subject the current ML research ecosystem—reviewing rubrics at venues like ICML and NeurIPS, hiring criteria at academic and industry labs, and ML curricula—and produces a structured four-axis framework distinguishing ADML from methods-driven research, along with concrete, actionable recommendations for reviewers, area chairs, hiring committees, and educators to better recognize and support application-driven contributions.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic contrast between methods-driven and application-driven paradigms along four axes: (1) stereotyped benchmarks vs. real-world tasks, (2) narrow evaluation metrics vs. application-specific criteria (e.g., computational cost, uncertainty quantification, spatial/temporal holdout strategies), (3) massive problem-agnostic datasets vs. auxiliary domain knowledge and careful data curation, and (4) general-purpose methods vs. problem-informed algorithms incorporating physics, constraints, or expert structure. It then diagnoses specific failure modes in three institutional processes—reviewing (e.g., 'unfamiliar benchmarks,' 'too simple,' 'not innovative' rejections), hiring (overweighting publication counts and standard-benchmark results), and teaching (linear SOTA narratives, prepackaged hackathons, absence of full project-lifecycle training)—and proposes targeted interventions such as domain-informed reviewer pools, revised rubric interpretations, interdisciplinary collaboration infrastructure, data engineering support teams, and required coursework exposing students to both paradigms.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not present quantitative experimental results, benchmarks, or performance metrics. Instead, it argues for the value of application-driven machine learning (ADML) research and identifies systemic barriers in reviewing, hiring, and teaching that undervalue such work. The paper cites examples (e.g., random forests outperforming Vision Transformers in remote sensing, Fourier Neural Operators generalizing from fluid dynamics to climate and materials) to illustrate the paradigm, but provides no numerical comparisons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not compare a proposed method against baselines or report win/lose/tie outcomes on any benchmark. The 'comparison' is conceptual: the paper contrasts the methods-driven research paradigm (stereotyped benchmarks, narrow metrics, massive datasets, problem-agnostic methods) with the application-driven paradigm (real-world tasks, application-specific metrics, auxiliary domain knowledge, problem-informed methods). It does not evaluate either paradigm empirically but rather advocates for recognizing both as valid contributions to ML research.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper systematizes and extends earlier calls for 'ML that matters' (Wagstaff, 2012; Rudin & Wagstaff, 2014) by providing the first structured contrast between methods-driven and application-driven research paradigms across three institutional dimensions: reviewing, hiring, and teaching. It draws on a growing body of ADML exemplars—Fourier Neural Operators, U-Net, physics-informed neural networks, and application-specific benchmarks like iNaturalist, WILDS, and MIMIC-CXR—to argue that problem-informed methods have already contributed broadly to ML methodology. The paper departs from prior advocacy by identifying concrete, recurring failure modes in peer review (e.g., 'too simple,' 'limited applicability') and proposing specific rubric reinterpretations rather than merely calling for more applied work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As ML deployment accelerates across healthcare, climate, energy, and industry, the gap between benchmark-optimized methods and real-world requirements is widening, making the institutional recognition of ADML increasingly urgent. The paper's key open question is how to operationalize the proposed changes—expanding reviewer pools, retraining hiring committees, and restructuring curricula—without creating a false binary that devalues methods-driven work. The authors explicitly leave unresolved the normative question of which applications should drive ML innovation and who decides what constitutes a valid 'solution,' pointing to the broader values-alignment literature. For practitioners, the practical implication is that application-informed design choices (spatially structured splits, domain-specific metrics, auxiliary constraints) are not niche but represent a necessary complement to standard benchmark evaluation.
