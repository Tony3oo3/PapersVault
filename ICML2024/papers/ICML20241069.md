## Problem

Despite extensive empirical evidence that shaping (curriculum learning) is critical for animals to acquire complex tasks, no quantitative theory exists to explain why shaping works, how it changes learning dynamics, or how to design effective shaping procedures. This paper addresses the gap by developing an analytical framework for compositional task learning in reinforcement learning, where complex tasks are built from simpler primitives combined via a context vector.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241069/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes K independent high-dimensional Gaussian input vectors (dimension N) per timestep, teacher weight vectors W_k* defining each primitive, a fixed context vector V* that linearly blends primitive outputs, and an episode length T that controls task difficulty. The student produces binary policy choices at each timestep and receives a sparse reward only when all T choices in an episode are correct. The analytical output is a set of ODEs governing order parameters (R_k, V_k) in the N→∞ limit, from which closed-form training timescales and generalisation errors are derived for two curricula: primitives pre-training and vanilla training.

## Method

The paper formulates a compositional RL task in the teacher-student framework, extending the RL perceptron (Patel et al., 2023) by introducing K modular primitives whose outputs are linearly combined via a learnable context vector V. Using tools from statistical physics, the authors derive asymptotically exact ODEs for the order parameters in the high-dimensional limit (N→∞), reducing the stochastic learning dynamics to a deterministic system. They then analytically solve these ODEs under two curricula: primitives pre-training (learn each primitive to expert level, then learn the composition) and vanilla training (learn the compositional task directly). Key results include: (1) primitive learning timescale is exponential in episode length T, (2) in vanilla training, initial context alignment V_k^0 V_k* modulates per-primitive learning speed, and (3) a multiplicative effect emerges where learning one primitive accelerates learning of others. The analysis shows primitives pre-training yields substantial speed advantages (especially for high T and poor initial context alignment) and improved robustness to gradient noise by separating the two noise sources (primitive learning vs. context learning).

## Results

The paper derives closed-form timescale expressions for two curricula: primitives pre-training (τ_curriculum ~ K·2^(T−2) + P̃₀^(2−T)) and vanilla training (τ_vanilla ~ 2^(T−2)/[(η_w(T−2))·((V₁⁰V₁*)² + (V₂⁰V₂*)²)]). Both strategies exhibit exponential growth in training time with episode length T, but the speed boost from primitives pre-training increases with T and decreases with initial context alignment V⁰V*. In the noise robustness experiment (K=2, V⁰V*=0.25), primitives pre-training maintains successful learning when σ_w ≤ 0.05 and σ_v ≥ 0.05, while vanilla training fails in this regime; both strategies fail when σ_w ≥ 0.05 regardless of σ_v.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between two curriculum strategies: primitives pre-training (the 'shaping' analogue) and vanilla training (direct compositional task learning). Primitives pre-training consistently achieves faster total training time, with the speed advantage growing as episode length T increases and as the initial context alignment V⁰V* decreases (Figure 4b). For example, at V⁰V*=0.25 and large T, the ratio of vanilla-to-curriculum training time becomes substantially greater than 1. In the noise robustness comparison (Figure 5b), primitives pre-training is significantly more robust when context noise σ_v is large (≥0.05) while primitive noise σ_w remains small (≤0.05), because the curriculum separates the two noise sources into sequential phases. Vanilla training, which must handle both noise sources simultaneously, fails to learn the compositional task in this regime. The multiplicative learning effect in vanilla training (where learning one primitive accelerates learning of others) partially mitigates the speed gap but does not eliminate it, particularly when the initial context is poorly aligned with the target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RL perceptron framework (Patel et al., 2023), which analytically characterizes policy gradient learning dynamics in high dimensions using statistical physics, to a compositional setting with K independent primitives blended by a context vector. It draws on the broader teacher-student tradition in statistical mechanics (Gardner & Derrida, 1989; Saad & Solla, 1995) and the curriculum learning literature from both neuroscience (Ahissar & Hochstein, 1997; Dekker et al., 2022) and machine learning (Bengio et al., 2009; Narvekar et al., 2020). The key departure from prior work is the introduction of hierarchical task structure—primitives plus compositional context—into the analytically tractable RL perceptron, enabling exact ODE derivations for two distinct curricula (primitives pre-training vs. vanilla training) that were previously only studied empirically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first analytical theory that quantitatively explains when and why shaping/curriculum learning accelerates compositional task acquisition, showing the benefit scales exponentially with task complexity (episode length T) and is largest when initial context alignment is poor. The multiplicative primitive learning effect—where learning one primitive accelerates learning of independent others even in a strictly modularized setting—is a non-obvious dynamical phenomenon with implications for understanding how prior knowledge shapes subsequent learning in animals and machines. Key limitations include the restriction to linear composition, strict orthogonality of primitives, and primarily K=2 analysis; extending to sequential chaining, recursive tree structures, and correlated primitives are identified as important open directions that would bring the theory closer to realistic cognitive tasks.
