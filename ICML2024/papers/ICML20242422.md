## Problem

While verification strategies (backdoor and reproducing) have been proposed to let data owners confirm that machine unlearning was performed honestly, their safety against a dishonest model provider has not been studied. This paper asks whether a model provider can circumvent all existing verification strategies while still retaining the information of supposedly unlearned data, and answers pessimistically: current MUL verification is fragile.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242422/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The methods take a training dataset D of RGB images (28×28 MNIST, 32×32×3 CIFAR-10/SVHN), a subset D_u to be unlearned, and (for the forging method) a Proof of Training PoT recorded during the original training run. The output is a Proof of Retraining PoRT — a sequence of triplets {w_r^(t), d_r^(t), g_r^(t)} — that passes both reproducing and backdoor verification, together with an unlearned model whose parameters still encode information from D_u. The retraining-based method produces a PoRT with exactly zero verification error, while the forging-based method operates under a small ε > 0 tolerance.

## Method

The core insight is that under mini-batch SGD, different data points can induce similar gradient updates, so a dishonest provider can replace unlearned samples with class-wise nearest neighbors from the retained set without changing the effective learning trajectory. The retraining-based method (Adv-R) performs a genuine retraining pass but, whenever a mini-batch contains unlearned data, substitutes those samples via a nearest-neighbor mapping S_n or a gradient-matching random sampler S_r, yielding a valid PoRT with ε=0 while Proposition 4.3 guarantees the model converges to a neighborhood of the full-dataset optimum. The forging-based method (Adv-F) avoids retraining entirely by applying a triplet-wise forging map F to the recorded PoT: for batches without unlearned data it adds a tiny single-sample SGD perturbation to the parameters, and for batches with unlearned data it applies the same nearest-neighbor replacement and recomputes the update, all in parallel. Proposition 4.4 provides explicit learning-rate conditions under which the forged PoRT satisfies the ε-threshold reproducing verification, and Proposition 4.5 shows the forging method's time complexity scales as (p_u/P + (1−p_u)/(mP))·T(n), far below naive retraining.

## Results

The retraining-based adversarial method (Adv-R) achieves a type II error probability (β) of 0.997–0.999 on backdoor verification across MNIST, CIFAR-10, and SVHN, matching honest retraining (0.998–0.999) and demonstrating that the verifier is effectively fooled. The forging-based method (Adv-F) achieves verification errors below 1e-3 in most SGD steps for reproducing verification. In terms of utility, Adv-F preserves the highest F1-score on the unlearned set (e.g., 99.30% on MNIST/MLP vs. 96.43% for naive retraining and 99.47% for original training), while in class-imbalanced settings Adv-R substantially outperforms naive retraining (e.g., 92.80% vs. 89.92% test F1 on MNIST/MLP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is naive retraining (exact unlearning from scratch, Cao & Yang 2015). On backdoor verification (Table 2), Adv-R matches Retrain almost exactly (β = 0.997 vs. 0.998 on MNIST), both effectively bypassing the verifier, while Adv-F fails backdoor verification (β ≈ 10⁻³⁴, similar to the unmodified Original model). On utility in normal settings, Adv-R(S_r) slightly outperforms Retrain on the unlearned set (e.g., 98.17% vs. 96.43% F1 on MNIST/MLP), and Adv-F nearly matches the original trained model (99.30% vs. 99.47%). In the more challenging class-imbalanced setting, the gap widens dramatically: Adv-R(S_n) achieves 92.80% test F1 on MNIST/MLP versus 89.92% for Retrain, and Adv-F reaches 96.81% versus 89.92%, showing that adversarial unlearning preserves substantially more utility than honest retraining when data heterogeneity exists. On efficiency, Adv-F is the fastest method (parallelizable forging), while Adv-R is slightly slower than naive retraining due to nearest-neighbor batch selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MUL verification literature, particularly Thudi et al. (2022), who introduced the forging map for generating a Proof of Retraining from a Proof of Training but noted their forging strategy was 'not realistic' because it preserved model parameters unchanged. The authors depart from this by proposing a novel forging map that updates both parameters and batch data, making the attack detectable-resistant across multiple verification requests. The retraining-based method draws on the gradient-similarity insight from Shumailov et al. (2021) and Thudi et al. (2022) but formalizes it with a nearest-neighbor selection strategy and provides convergence guarantees (Proposition 4.3) that prior heuristic random-sampling approaches lacked. The paper positions itself as the first systematic study of whether model providers can circumvent *both* backdoor verification (Athena, Sommer et al. 2022) and reproducing verification (Weng et al. 2022; Eisenhofer et al. 2022) simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that current MUL verification is fundamentally fragile, with no strict safety guarantee—challenges the assumption that verification strategies (especially cryptographic ones using SNARKs or Intel SGX) provide meaningful protection for data owners in MLaaS settings. The retraining-based method achieves zero verification error while preserving unlearned data, meaning even the most stringent reproducing verification cannot detect the deception. Key limitations include the focus on exact unlearning (retraining) rather than approximate methods, and the assumption that the model provider has complete access to the training process. Open questions include designing verification robust to these adversarial unlearning processes, extending the analysis to approximate unlearning, and understanding whether the natural data similarity exploited here is a fundamental barrier or can be mitigated by architectural choices.
