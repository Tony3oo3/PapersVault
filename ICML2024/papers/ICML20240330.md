## Problem

In single-demonstration imitation learning, the agent has access to only one expert trajectory and no ground truth reward, leading to severely sparse reward signals that hinder training in high-dimensional continuous environments. Existing dense-reward IL methods (e.g., PWIL, FISH) rely on geometric distances like L2 or cosine similarity, which ignore environmental dynamics such as barriers and asymmetric transitions, potentially guiding the agent toward infeasible or dead-end states. This paper identifies both the sparsity problem and the dynamics-blindness problem as the core gaps that prevent existing IL methods from achieving expert-level performance with a single demonstration.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single expert trajectory (a sequence of state-action pairs) and online access to the environment for interaction, but no ground truth reward function. It produces a trained Soft Actor-Critic policy that can be evaluated against the ground truth reward at test time. A notable byproduct is a trained transition discriminator that approximates one-step state reachability, and a relative reward metric (agent raw reward divided by expert raw reward) that enables blind model selection from training checkpoints without ever querying the environment's true reward.

## Method

TDIL introduces a transition discriminator D_φ(s_i, s_j) trained via binary cross-entropy to classify whether state s_i can reach state s_j in one timestep, using positive samples from the replay buffer, easy negatives from random state pairs, and hard negatives from reversed transitions. This discriminator approximates the intractable max_a P(s_j|s_i, a) term, enabling computation of a dense surrogate reward R_TDIL that assigns positive reward whenever the agent transitions into a state in 'expert proximity' (i.e., a state from which some expert state is one step away). The final training signal is an aggregated reward R_agg = β·R_IRL + (1−β)·R_TDIL, where R_IRL comes from a GAIL discriminator, combined with an optional BC loss to ensure optimality on expert states. The key distinction from prior dense-reward IL is that the transition discriminator encodes the MDP's actual dynamics (barriers, asymmetric motion, infeasible transitions) rather than relying on geometric distances, making the reward both dense and dynamics-aware. Training is concurrent: the agent interacts with the environment, the discriminator is updated on collected transitions, and the SAC agent is updated using rewards computed by a soft-updated target discriminator for stability.

## Results

Both TDIL variants (R_TDIL+BC and R_agg) achieve expert-level or exceeding performance across all five MuJoCo benchmarks under the single-demonstration setting. For example, on HalfCheetah-v3, Ours(R_TDIL+BC) scores 15,666±85 vs. expert 15,251, and on Walker2d-v3, Ours(R_agg) scores 6,334±10 vs. expert 6,123. On the AdroitHandDoor-v1 robotic environment, both TDIL variants achieve 100% success rate, while the strongest baselines (f-IRL, CFIL) achieve at most ~40% (mostly 0%). The relative return metric also shows strong positive correlation with ground truth return, enabling effective blind model selection without access to the true reward.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TDIL decisively outperforms all five baselines across the MuJoCo suite. BC and GAIL are the weakest, scoring only 211–990 and 554–3,209 respectively, far below expert levels due to overfitting (BC) and adversarial instability with sparse rewards (GAIL). f-IRL is competitive on HalfCheetah-v3 (14,560 vs. 15,666) but fails entirely on Humanoid-v3 (N/A) and underperforms on Hopper-v3 (3,693 vs. 4,115). PWIL shows high variance (e.g., 11,460±4,774 on HalfCheetah) and fails to reach expert level on any environment, attributed to its reliance on Euclidean distance that ignores MDP dynamics. CFIL reaches expert level only on Hopper-v3 (4,131) and is notably weak on Walker2d-v3 (2,402±949). On AdroitHandDoor-v1, TDIL achieves 100% success while f-IRL and CFIL remain below 40%, confirming TDIL's advantage in high-dimensional dexterous manipulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

TDIL builds on the IRL framework (GAIL, f-IRL, CFIL) and the dense-reward idea from PWIL/FISH, but departs fundamentally from both: rather than matching agent-expert distributions via adversarial training or measuring geometric (L2/cosine) distances between states, it introduces a state-based transition discriminator that learns the MDP's reachability structure to construct a dynamics-aware surrogate reward. This positions TDIL as a non-adversarial, dynamics-aware alternative that avoids the instability of min-max formulations and the blind spots of distance-based methods in environments with asymmetric or constrained transitions. The concurrent training of the transition discriminator with a SAC agent, plus the use of relative rewards for blind model selection, are practical contributions not present in prior single-demo IL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Single-demo IL is a practically important setting for robotics (surgical robots, autonomous vehicles, manipulation) where collecting multiple expert trajectories is infeasible, and TDIL's consistent expert-level performance across MuJoCo and Adroit Door demonstrates a viable path forward. The blind model selection capability—using relative surrogate rewards to pick checkpoints without ground-truth access—addresses a real deployment gap that prior IL methods overlooked. Open questions include whether the one-step transition discriminator generalizes to highly stochastic or partially observable environments, and whether the multi-step extension (sketched in Appendix A.5) can scale without combinatorial blowup in the discriminator's input space.
