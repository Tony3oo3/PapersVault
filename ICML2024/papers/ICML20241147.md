## Problem

Skills (temporal abstractions) are widely used in hierarchical RL to improve sample efficiency, yet there is no precise theoretical characterization of what properties of an environment make skills helpful or harmful. In particular, it remains unclear when incorporating skills will improve RL performance versus when they will degrade it, and how the expressivity of skills (e.g., macroactions vs. general options) affects their utility.

## Input / Output

The framework operates on deterministic sparse-reward MDPs (DSMDPs) with finite action spaces, taking as input a base environment (state space, base action space, deterministic transition function, goal state) and a set of deterministic skills that map states to fixed action sequences. It produces two difficulty metrics (p-learning difficulty and p-exploration difficulty), an incompressibility measure quantifying how compressible solutions to states are, and theoretical lower bounds on the ratio of post-skill to pre-skill difficulty. Empirically, the method is evaluated on 4 base environments (CliffWalking, CompILE2, 8Puzzle, RubiksCube222), each with 32 action-space variants (1 base + 31 macroaction augmentations), using 4 RL algorithms (Q-learning, value iteration, REINFORCE, DQN).

## Method

The paper defines p-learning difficulty as |A|·E_s~p[d_M(s)] (weighted average shortest-solution length scaled by action space size) and p-exploration difficulty as E_s~p[-log q_{M,δ}(s)] (expected negative log-probability of solving a state under a random policy with geometric horizon), then proves both correlate strongly with actual sample complexity across algorithms. The key theoretical contribution introduces A+-merged p-incompressibility, measuring the coding efficiency of representing solutions with base actions versus skill-augmented actions, and proves that the ratio of post-skill to pre-skill p-learning difficulty is lower-bounded by this incompressibility times a penalty factor |A+|log|A0|/(|A0|log|A+|) that grows with action space size. For exploration, a tight lower bound in terms of solution density is derived, and a corollary shows that in solution-separable environments, macroactions can provably increase p-exploration difficulty. The results collectively show that skills benefit exploration more than learning from experience, and that less expressive skills (macroactions) are less effective at reducing difficulty, with concrete constructions of environments where macroactions always worsen performance.

## Results

The combined difficulty metric J = λJ_learn + (1−λ)exp(J_explore) correlates strongly with sample complexity across 4 environments and 4 RL algorithms, with Pearson correlations ranging from 0.403 (Q-learning on 8Puzzle) to 0.953 (Q-learning on CliffWalking), and most values above 0.7. Table 2 shows that unmerged p-incompressibility (ranging from 0.0000 for CliffWalking to 0.8072 for RubiksCube222) predicts HRL improvement: the sample complexity ratio N+/N0 drops from 0.000007 (CliffWalking) to 0.73 (RubiksCube222) as incompressibility increases. Theoretically, the paper proves that in solution-separable environments with high incompressibility, macroactions can provably *increase* both p-learning difficulty (Corollary 4.5) and p-exploration difficulty (Theorems 5.6–5.7), while more expressive skills can simultaneously improve exploration even when they harm learning (Corollary 5.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical analysis paper, so traditional method-vs-method comparisons are absent. Instead, the empirical validation compares the proposed difficulty metrics against actual sample complexity of 4 RL algorithms (Q-learning, value iteration, REINFORCE, DQN) across 32 macroaction augmentations of each base environment. The geometric mean in p-exploration difficulty outperforms the arithmetic mean variant in 29 out of 32 correlation measurements (Table 5 vs. Table 1), with the arithmetic mean dropping as low as 0.247 (value iteration on RubiksCube222) versus 0.748 for the geometric mean. For skill types, macroactions show a clear positive correlation with incompressibility (Figure 1), while LOVE-discovered stochastic options (Table 2) also follow the same trend: HRL improvement ratio decreases monotonically from 0.000007 (IC=0.0000) to 0.73 (IC=0.8072). The theoretical results establish that skills are strictly better at reducing p-exploration difficulty than p-learning difficulty, since the lower bound for exploration (Corollary 5.3) lacks the |A+|log|A0|/(|A0|log|A+|) penalty factor present in the learning bound (Corollary 4.4), and Corollary 5.5 proves the existence of environments where skills simultaneously improve exploration while worsening learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by providing the first characterization of when deterministic skills (temporal abstractions) improve RL performance, building on the foundational options framework (Sutton et al., 1999) and the rich literature on skill discovery (Option-Critic, eigenoptions, LOVE, LEMMA). It departs from existing MDP difficulty metrics (diameter, environmental value norm, distribution mismatch coefficient) by introducing purpose-built metrics—p-learning difficulty, p-exploration difficulty, and p-incompressibility—that directly estimate sample complexity rather than appearing in loose asymptotic bounds. The incompressibility framework repurposes information-theoretic coding concepts (entropy, cross-entropy, source coding) to quantify how much skills can compress solutions, connecting RL difficulty to data compressibility in a novel way.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first theoretical guidance for practitioners on when to use skills and what types of skills to use, addressing a long-standing gap that has limited the adoption of hierarchical RL despite its intuitive appeal. The finding that macroactions—the most common and simplest skill type—can provably worsen RL performance in incompressible environments is a significant negative result that challenges the default assumption that more temporal abstraction is always better. Key limitations include the restriction to deterministic sparse-reward MDPs with finite action spaces, and open questions include generalization to stochastic environments, continuous action spaces, and whether the incompressibility-based skill learning objectives (shown to recover LOVE and LEMMA objectives) scale to high-dimensional settings.
