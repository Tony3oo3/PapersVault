## Problem

Existing graph mixup methods interpolate well-aligned transformed graphs but rely on transformations that are not equivalence-preserving, producing synthetic samples whose structure no longer matches their interpolated labels (e.g., a 2-block graph labeled as half 2-block, half 3-block). This sample-label inconsistency injects misleading supervision signals that can hurt rather than help GNN training. The paper asks how to define and guarantee sample-label consistency for graph mixup in a principled geometric sense.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of plain graphs (adjacency matrices A₁ ∈ ℝ^{n₁×n₁}, A₂ ∈ ℝ^{n₂×n₂} with uniform node weights), a mixup ratio λ ∈ [0,1], and a mixup graph size r ≤ n that controls the rank of the low-rank GW coupling. It outputs a synthetic mixup graph with r nodes (in the approximate case) and its linearly interpolated label. In the exact formulation the intermediate graph has n₁×n₂ nodes, but the accelerated version compresses this to r nodes via a low-rank factorization, trading a small approximation error for quadratic (rather than quartic) time complexity in graph size.

## Method

GEOMIX constructs a Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) space as a joint space for disparate graphs, then applies equivalence-preserving transformations (EPT) — specifically Kronecker-product embeddings P₁ = I_{n₁} ⊗ 1_{1×n₂} and P₂ = 1_{1×n₁} ⊗ I_{n₂} — to map each graph into a shared n₁×n₂-node space where linear interpolation is well-defined. The paper proves (Theorem 3.1) that the linear interpolation of these transformed graphs traces out an exact 2-GW geodesic, thereby guaranteeing sample-label consistency by construction. For tractability, the exact geodesic is reformulated as a low-rank GW optimization problem (Theorem 3.2) where the optimal coupling T is factorized as Q₁ diag(1/g) Q₂ᵀ with rank r, and solved iteratively via a mirror descent scheme with Dykstra's algorithm for constraint projection. This yields an overall time complexity of O(KTn²r) for K graph pairs, compared to O(n⁴) for the exact geodesic, while empirically maintaining within ±3% accuracy of the exact solution.

## Results

On graph classification across five real-world datasets (PROTEINS, MUTAG, MSRC-9, IMDB-B, IMDB-M) with GCN and GIN backbones, GEOMIX achieves the best accuracy in 8 out of 10 settings, with up to 6.6% improvement over the strongest baseline (G-Mixup on PROTEINS with GCN: 70.2% vs. 63.6%). The largest gains appear on smaller, sparser datasets (MUTAG: 76.1% GCN, 80.8% GIN), while improvements on densely-covered datasets (IMDB-B, IMDB-M) are more modest (≤1%). GEOMIX also improves robustness, achieving up to 4.7% higher accuracy under 20% topology corruption and 1.8% under 20% label corruption compared to the best competitor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GEOMIX outperforms all seven baselines on most settings. Against structure-based methods (DropEdge, DropNode, Subgraph), GEOMIX consistently wins by 2–7% on PROTEINS and MUTAG, where the graph space is sparsely covered and mixup provides the most benefit. Among mixup-based baselines, G-Mixup (graphon interpolation) is the strongest competitor on PROTEINS (63.6% GCN) and MUTAG (73.4% GIN), but GEOMIX surpasses it by 6.6% and 7.4% respectively, attributed to the geodesic guarantee ensuring sample-label consistency. M-Mixup (latent-space interpolation) is competitive on PROTEINS with GIN (70.4% vs. 71.3%) but lags on MUTAG. S-Mixup (soft alignment) and FGWMixup (fused GW barycenter) achieve results close to vanilla on most datasets, with FGWMixup showing the highest MSRC-9 accuracy (89.6% GCN) but still trailing GEOMIX (90.3%). GEOMIX is slightly behind G-Mixup on IMDB-B with GCN (72.0% vs. 72.2%) and behind G-Mixup on IMDB-M with GIN (46.9% vs. 47.0%), the only two settings where it does not rank first, likely because these densely-covered datasets leave less room for augmentation to help.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the optimal transport theory of Sturm (2012) for GW geodesics and the low-rank GW optimization framework of Scetbon et al. (2021, 2022) to provide the first theoretical guarantee that graph mixup samples lie on a geodesic in the GW space. It departs from prior graph mixup methods (G-Mixup, M-Mixup, S-Mixup, FGWMixup) that perform 'practical interpolation' on transformed graphs without equivalence-preserving transformations, which the paper argues leads to inconsistent sample-label pairs. The key conceptual shift is reframing graph mixup from a heuristic interpolation problem to a geodesic problem in a well-defined metric space, where equivalence-preserving transformations (Kronecker-product-based for exact geodesics, low-rank factorization for approximate geodesics) ensure the interpolated samples remain in the correct equivalence class.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.1) that mixup samples on GW geodesics satisfy sample-label consistency, addressing a fundamental gap in prior graph augmentation literature. The low-rank approximation reduces complexity from O(n⁴) to O(n²r), making the approach practical, and the empirical results show consistent improvements (up to 6.6%) across five standard benchmarks. However, the evaluation is limited to small graph classification datasets (188–1500 graphs), and the method is restricted to graph-level tasks; extending to node-level mixup, larger-scale graphs, and more diverse GNN architectures remains open. The finding that widely-used baselines like G-Mixup achieve only a 0.25 Pearson correlation between graph distance and label distance (vs. 0.91 for GEOMIX) raises questions about the reliability of prior augmentation methods in practice.
