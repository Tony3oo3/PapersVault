## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse-engineer the circuits neural networks use to perform tasks, but prior work on the same one-layer model for group multiplication (Chughtai et al.) proposed a 'Group Composition via Representations' algorithm based on matrix irreps. This paper shows that the actual mechanism is fundamentally different — the model discovers the true subgroup and coset structure of S_n and implements multiplication through coset membership circuits — and demonstrates that even in a fully tractable, well-understood mathematical setting, interpretability conclusions can diverge dramatically depending on the analytical lens applied.

## Input / Output

The model receives two one-hot vectors of length |G| (120 for S_5, 720 for S_6), one for each input permutation, which pass through separate embedding matrices of dimension d×|G| (256×120 for S_5, 512×720 for S_6). The concatenated embeddings feed a single fully-connected hidden layer (width 128 for S_5, 256 for S_6) with ReLU activations, followed by an unembedding matrix that maps to |G| logits. The output is a categorical distribution over all group elements, with the target being the exact product permutation σ_l·σ_r.

## Method

The paper reverse-engineers the trained network and identifies 'coset circuits': neurons whose pre-activations take discrete, constant values on the cosets of specific subgroups of S_n. For a pair of conjugate subgroups H_i and H_j, the left and right pre-activation values are tuned so that L(σ_l)+R(σ_r)=0 precisely when the product σ_lσ_r falls in the shared coset H_i·τ_ij = τ_ij·H_j; pairs of neurons with opposite signs convert this into a near-perfect on/off membership switch. The n² combinations of (H_i, H_j) subgroups uniquely encode every element of S_n, and the unembedding layer reads out the product by combining these coset indicators. The authors validate the circuit through ablations (removing non-coset neurons causes no accuracy drop) and targeted causal interventions (swapping left/right embeddings collapses accuracy to 0%, replacing ReLU with absolute value actually improves loss, and flipping only one side's sign destroys performance), confirming the mechanism is both necessary and sufficient.

## Results

Across 128 S5 and 100 S6 models, over 99.2% of hidden-layer neurons exhibit coset concentration (min_H C_H(f) < 1.0), with the vast majority below 10⁻⁶. Ablations confirm necessity: removing non-coset neurons leaves accuracy unchanged in 126/128 S5 models, while removing the highest-concentration coset neurons collapses performance. Causal interventions (Table 1) validate the circuit: the base model achieves 99.99% accuracy with 1.97e-6 loss, swapping left/right embeddings drops accuracy to 1%, and replacing ReLU with absolute value yields 100% accuracy with an even lower loss of 3.69e-13.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the GCR (Group Composition via Representations) algorithm proposed by Chughtai et al. [4], which posits that the model stores matrix irreps and performs matrix multiplication to compute group products. The authors argue the coset circuit is a strictly more faithful explanation: it accounts for all four pieces of evidence Chughtai et al. present (Fourier concentration, logit-character correlation, ablations, and irrep lookup) while additionally explaining phenomena GCR cannot. Specifically, the coset circuit predicts (and the data confirms) that replacing ReLU with absolute value should *improve* performance (loss drops from 1.97e-6 to 3.69e-13), that activations should concentrate on cosets of subgroups in the standard basis, and that there is no one-to-one mapping between the subgroups used by the circuit and the irreps in Fourier space (S5 has 156 subgroups but only 7 irreps). The unembedding correlation analysis further distinguishes the two: neurons sharing a coset show 81.4% mean correlation in their unembedding vectors, while neurons sharing only a conjugacy class of subgroup show −0.2% correlation, indicating the model tracks cosets rather than irreps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Chughtai et al. [4], which studied the identical one-hidden-layer architecture trained on S5 and S6 group multiplication but proposed the 'Group Composition via Representations' (GCR) algorithm as the learned mechanism. The authors recreate the exact experimental setup but arrive at a fundamentally different mechanistic explanation: the model implements 'coset circuits' that exploit the subgroup/coset structure of the symmetric group rather than performing matrix multiplication of irreducible representations. The analytical toolkit draws on group Fourier transform (Diaconis, Kondor) and the broader mechanistic interpretability tradition of circuit discovery via ablation and causal intervention (Nanda et al., Geiger et al.), but the key conceptual departure is from the GCR hypothesis itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most important contribution is methodological: it demonstrates that even in a small, deterministic, well-understood toy setting, two research groups can study the identical model and arrive at contradictory mechanistic explanations, and that only targeted causal interventions (not correlational evidence) can disambiguate between competing theories. The coset circuit explanation accounts for behaviors the GCR algorithm cannot (e.g., loss decreasing when ReLU is replaced with absolute value, the specific pattern of unembedding correlations), making it the more faithful description. Open questions include whether the coset-circuit methodology generalizes to larger groups or more complex architectures, and whether the difficulty encountered here foreshadows fundamental limits of interpretability for real-world models.
