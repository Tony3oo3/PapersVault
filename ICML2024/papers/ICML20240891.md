## Problem

A major open question is whether language models trained purely on next-token prediction capture any information about the formal semantics of the programs they generate, or whether their behavior is attributable solely to surface statistical correlations. This paper empirically tests the Main Hypothesis (MH) that LMs of code trained only to predict the next token do not model the formal semantics of the underlying programming language, and provides evidence to reject it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240891/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The LM takes as input a specification of 5 input-output 8×8 grid world states (each grid containing a robot with facing direction, markers, obstacles, and empty spaces, tokenized row-by-row) and generates a straight-line Karel program of up to 14 tokens drawn from {move, turnRight, turnLeft, putMarker, pickMarker}. For probing, the model's hidden states (averaged over layers, yielding a 1024-d vector per token) are used as input to small classifiers that predict the abstract program state — a triple of (position offset, facing direction, obstacle-ahead) — at the current and up to 2 steps into the future of the generated program.

## Method

A 350M-parameter CodeGen Transformer is trained from scratch via next-token prediction on 500K synthetic Karel programs (length 6–10) each preceded by 5 input-output grid specifications, over ~2.5B tokens. The key empirical finding is that probing classifiers (linear, 1-layer MLP, 2-layer MLP) extract increasingly accurate abstract program states from hidden states during a 'semantics acquisition' phase that correlates strongly (R² > 0.8) with generative accuracy. The paper's central technical contribution is a novel semantic probing intervention: by reassigning the semantics of individual operations (via 'flip' and 'adversarial' remappings) while preserving program syntax, then re-executing the same generated tokens under the alternative semantics to obtain new abstract states, the authors show that probes trained on the alternative semantics achieve significantly lower accuracy than on the original semantics. This disambiguates whether the LM itself encodes the original semantics (rather than merely a syntactic record that the probe interprets), thereby rejecting the syntactic record hypothesis and MH.

## Results

The 350M-parameter CodeGen LM trained on Karel program synthesis reaches 92.4% generative accuracy on the test set. Probing classifiers extract abstract program states (position, direction, obstacle) from LM hidden states with geometric-mean accuracies of 63.2% (linear), 79.1% (1-layer MLP), and 82.3% (2-layer MLP) for the current state at the end of training. These semantic content measurements correlate strongly with generative accuracy during the second half of training (R² = 0.904, 0.886, 0.821 for the three probes, all p < 0.001). Probes also predict future abstract states (1–2 tokens ahead) with accuracies of 72.5–77.3%, outperforming a baseline that simply copies the current state (75.6% at +2 steps).

| 1-layer MLP | 79.1 | 75.1 | 72.5 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other LM architectures or training methods; instead, it uses internal baselines to validate its findings. In Table 1, the 2-layer MLP probe (77.3% at +2 steps) outperforms the 'copy current state' baseline (75.6%), indicating the LM encodes genuine future-state information rather than merely repeating the present state. The semantic intervention experiments (Table 2) serve as the key control: when the semantics of individual operations are reassigned (flip or adversarial), the semantic content drops significantly (e.g., for the linear probe at the current state, original SC = 63.2 vs. flip SC = 57.3, Δ = 5.9 with R²(Δ) = 86.9, p < 0.001; adversarial SC = 53.1, Δ = 10.1, R²(Δ) = 71.9, p = 0.004). This degradation rejects the syntactic record hypothesis, confirming that the LM representations are aligned with the original semantics rather than encoding only a token-level record that the probe interprets. The adversarial semantics (which completely reassigns each operator's meaning) produces a larger drop than the flip semantics (which merely swaps turnLeft/turnRight), consistent with the greater difficulty of mapping between unrelated semantics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of program synthesis with LMs (Austin et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2021a) and the probing methodology for interpreting internal representations (Shi et al., 2016; Belinkov & Glass, 2019; Li et al., 2021, 2023). It departs from prior probing work by introducing a novel interventional control—reassigning the semantics of individual operations while preserving syntactic structure—that specifically tests whether the LM or the probe is responsible for the measured semantic content. The use of small-step operational semantics and abstract interpretation (Plotkin, 1981; Cousot & Cousot, 1977) to define a precise, formal target for probing is a conceptual departure from prior work that probes for looser notions like entity states or board positions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled experimental framework for studying whether and how LMs acquire formal semantics from next-token prediction, with the semantic intervention technique offering a broadly applicable control for probing experiments that go beyond syntactic structure. Key limitations include the narrow scope (a single small DSL, a single 350M-parameter architecture, a synthetic dataset), which leaves open whether the findings generalize to richer programming languages or larger models. Open questions include whether the observed phase transition between syntax and semantics acquisition is a general property of LM training, how the representations scale with model size, and whether the 'intent' encoded in future-state predictions has practical utility for decoding or verification.
