## Problem

Large-scale point cloud processing in high-energy physics (e.g., CERN LHC tracking with 60k+ points per event) demands real-time inference, but GNNs suffer from O(n²) graph construction and irregular memory access, while standard transformers have quadratic attention complexity. Existing efficient transformers either neglect the approximation error–complexity tradeoff, fail to exploit local inductive bias inherent in scientific point clouds, or sacrifice accuracy for computational regularity. The paper provides the first quantitative error-complexity analysis comparing RFF and LSH-based kernel approximations and identifies that OR & AND LSH (not OR-only) is essential for near-linear complexity with provably low error.

## Input / Output

Input consists of attributed point clouds where each point carries task-specific features (16-dim for tracking: radial distance, angles, local/global coordinates, charge fraction; 8-dim for pileup: momentum components, energy, PID) plus 2D coordinates in η-φ space (pseudorapidity and azimuthal angle) derived from cylindrical detector geometry. For tracking, the output is a 24-dim point embedding (projected to 12-dim) trained via InfoNCE contrastive loss so that hits from the same particle cluster together; for pileup, the output is a per-point binary probability (LC vs. pileup collision) trained with focal loss on a highly imbalanced 3.9% positive class. Point clouds range from ~6k to ~60k points, with the 60k scale being the primary scalability challenge.

## Method

HEPT replaces standard softmax attention with a kernel k(q_u, k_v) = exp(−½‖q_u − k_v‖²) where queries and keys are concatenated with √(2ω)·ρ (scaled point coordinates), explicitly embedding local inductive bias so attention decays with geometric distance. The full n×n attention matrix is approximated via E² LSH with m₁=3 hash tables (OR) each containing m₂=3 hash functions (AND), yielding m₁×m₂ raw hash values per point that are combined into AND hash codes through a bucketized auxiliary code scheme. To ensure hardware-friendly regularity, queries and keys are independently partitioned into equal-sized blocks by their AND hash codes, and only block-diagonal attention is computed. A key design choice is using point coordinates as additional AND hash codes shared by both queries and keys, which resolves the query-key bucket misalignment problem that plagues methods like Reformer without requiring q=k tying. Theoretical analysis proves that OR & AND LSH achieves ε = Õ(exp(−cF/(n·polylog(n)))·1/n), enabling exponential error reduction at near-linear FLOPs, whereas RFF is stuck at ε = Θ(nd/F) and OR-only LSH cannot reduce error below Õ(1/n) without super-linear complexity.

## Results

HEPT achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three HEP datasets: 92.66 AP@k on Tracking-6k, 91.93 AP@k on Tracking-60k, and 40.39 AUC on Pileup-10k, outperforming the best GNN baselines (91.00, 90.89, 40.26) and all efficient transformer baselines (which range from 70–74 AP@k and 25–40 AUC). In terms of computational speed, HEPT achieves up to 203× inference speedup over GNNs on Tracking-60k (57.9 ms vs. 5,781 ms for SOTA GNNs) while maintaining the highest accuracy. The paper also demonstrates that incorporating HEPT's local-inductive-bias kernel into other transformer baselines (e.g., SMYRF-k_HEPT reaches 83.19 AP@k) yields substantial improvements, validating the kernel design independently of the LSH architecture.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HEPT outperforms all 14 baselines on every dataset. Against the strongest GNN competitors, HEPT leads SOTA GNNs (Lieret et al., 2023) by 1.66 AP@k on Tracking-6k (92.66 vs. 91.00) and 1.04 AP@k on Tracking-60k (91.93 vs. 90.89), while also being 89–203× faster in inference. DGCNN (Wang et al., 2019) and GravNet (Qasim et al., 2019) are the next-closest GNNs at 90.74/88.66 and 90.11/87.99 AP@k respectively, still trailing HEPT by 1.9–4.3 points. Among efficient transformers, the best baseline is FlatFormer (Liu et al., 2023) at 74.22 AP@k on Tracking-6k, which HEPT surpasses by 18.4 points; Reformer (Kitaev et al., 2020), SMYRF (Daras et al., 2020), HyperAttn (Han et al., 2024), Performer (Choromanski et al., 2021), FLT (Choromanski et al., 2023), and ScatterBrain (Chen et al., 2021) all fall in the 71–73 AP@k range, roughly 20 points below HEPT. On Pileup-10k, HEPT (40.39 AUC) edges out SOTA GNNs (Li et al., 2023) at 40.26 and PointTrans (Zhao et al., 2021) at 40.26, while RFF-based methods (Performer, FLT) and OR-only LSH methods (SMYRF, HyperAttn) lag significantly at 25–31 AUC. GCN (Kipf & Welling, 2017) and GatedGNN (Li et al., 2016) are intermediate GNN baselines at 79.61/80.98 AP@k, well below HEPT. Notably, when HEPT's kernel is plugged into SMYRF or FlatFormer, their performance improves substantially (e.g., SMYRF-k_HEPT reaches 83.19 AP@k), confirming that the local-inductive-bias kernel is a key contributor independent of the LSH sparsification strategy.

## Relation to Prior Work

HEPT builds on E²LSH (Datar et al., 2004) as its core hash family and the OR & AND LSH combinatorial construction (Leskovec et al., 2020), which prior efficient transformers largely ignored. It departs from Reformer and SMYRF (which use OR-only LSH and tie queries to keys), HyperAttention and KDEformer (which use AND-only LSH), and RFF-based methods like Performer and FLT, by providing a theoretical error-complexity analysis showing that OR & AND LSH is necessary for near-linear complexity with provably low approximation error under local inductive bias. The use of point coordinates as extra AND hash codes for query-key alignment is a novel architectural choice that avoids the expressiveness limitation of tying Q=K while maintaining computational regularity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The error-complexity tradeoff analysis provides the first systematic theoretical guidance for choosing between RFF and LSH in efficient transformer design, with the clear conclusion that LSH (specifically OR & AND) dominates RFF for tasks with local inductive bias under subquadratic complexity. The 203× inference speedup with SOTA accuracy on HEP tasks demonstrates that efficient transformers can now match or exceed GNNs in both speed and accuracy for large-scale scientific point clouds, potentially enabling real-time analysis at the LHC. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to higher-dimensional coordinate spaces beyond the 2D η-φ space used here, whether the specific Gaussian kernel is optimal for all local-bias tasks, and whether the method extends to 3D point clouds in CV or molecular dynamics settings.
