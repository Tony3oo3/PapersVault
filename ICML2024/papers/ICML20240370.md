## Problem

While empirical evidence strongly suggests that multi-task pretraining improves the quality of learned representations in nonlinear neural networks, theoretical understanding has been limited to either single-task settings or linear models. This paper provides the first provable guarantees that a nonlinear (two-layer ReLU) network trained with gradient-based multi-task learning recovers the ground-truth low-dimensional feature subspace shared across tasks, and explains why multi-tasking is necessary for this to occur.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240370/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes T binary classification tasks drawn i.i.d. from a task distribution, where each task's label is a function of the sign pattern of a projection onto a fixed r-dimensional subspace (r ≪ d) of a d-dimensional Gaussian input vector. The output is a first-layer weight matrix W ∈ R^{m×d} whose row space approximates the ground-truth subspace, enabling downstream task solving with sample and neuron complexity independent of the ambient dimension d. For downstream evaluation, a random second ReLU layer is composed with the pretrained representation, and a linear head is trained on N samples from the new task.

## Method

The architecture is a two-layer ReLU network with m neurons, where the first-layer weights W are shared across all T tasks and each task has its own last-layer head a_i. Training uses a single step of alternating stochastic gradient descent: first, each task-specific head is updated via one SGD step on its empirical hinge loss, then the shared representation W is updated using the updated heads. The key insight is that this head-first update order induces a pseudo-contrastive loss on the representation that aligns input pairs likely to share a label on a randomly drawn task (i.e., pairs with the same sign pattern on the r ground-truth features) and penalizes alignment of pairs that do not. This mechanism causes the gradient to amplify the energy of W in the ground-truth subspace by a factor of ~2 relative to the spurious subspace, so that after one step the representation effectively projects inputs onto the r-dimensional feature space. The paper further proves negative results showing that random features or single-task pretraining cannot achieve comparable downstream complexity, confirming the necessity of multi-tasking.

## Results

The paper provides the first provable guarantees for multi-task feature learning with a nonlinear (two-layer ReLU) network. The representation learning error after one alternating gradient step scales as $2^r \sqrt{d}/\sqrt{T}\,(1+\sqrt{d}/\sqrt{n})$, meaning the learned first-layer weights project inputs onto the ground-truth $r$-dimensional subspace with error diminishing in the number of tasks $T$ and samples $n$. The end-to-end downstream guarantee (Theorem 3.3) shows that composing the pretrained representation with a random ReLU layer and training a linear head on $N$ samples yields population loss $\exp(\tilde{O}(r^5))/\sqrt{N}$, crucially independent of the ambient dimension $d$. Negative results confirm that without multi-task pretraining, both random features and single-task learning require neuron/sample complexity polynomial in $d^r$.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper establishes two key negative comparisons that underscore the necessity of multi-task pretraining. First, against random features (no pretraining), Theorem 3.6 shows that any data-independent $\hat{m}$-dimensional embedding requires $\hat{m}B^2 > \epsilon^2 \binom{d}{r}$ to solve sparse parity tasks, i.e., polynomial complexity in $d^r$, whereas multi-task pretraining reduces this to $\exp(\text{poly}(r))$ with no dependence on $d$. Second, against single-task pretraining, Theorem 3.7 proves that with probability at least $1 - 2^{-r}$ over the draw of a single sparse parity task, the learned representation cannot guarantee downstream generalization with neuron/margin complexity smaller than polynomial in $d$, because most single tasks depend on only a strict subset of the $r$ ground-truth features. In contrast, multi-task pretraining aggregates information across $T$ tasks to recover all $r$ features, achieving the dimension-independent $\exp(\tilde{O}(r^5))/\sqrt{N}$ downstream loss. The paper also notes (Remark 3.8) that even training on the single most informative task (full parity) requires $\Omega(d^r)$ samples, which is worse than the $\tilde{O}(d^3/T)$ per-task sample complexity of multi-task pretraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected lines of work: single-task feature learning with nonlinear networks (Damian et al., 2022; Abbe et al., 2022; Barak et al., 2022) and multi-task representation learning with linear models (Collins et al., 2022; Saunshi et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2021). It adopts the one-gradient-step analysis framework standard in the nonlinear feature learning literature but extends it to the multi-task setting with task-specific heads, revealing that the head-update step induces a pseudo-contrastive loss on the shared representation. The negative results build on lower bounds for sparse parity learning (Barak et al., 2022; Kamath et al., 2020) to show that both random features and single-task pretraining are fundamentally insufficient.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first theoretical proof that gradient-based multi-task pretraining of a nonlinear network recovers ground-truth features, providing a mechanistic explanation (pseudo-contrastive loss) for the empirically observed benefit of multi-tasking. The key open question is whether the exponential-in-r sample and neuron complexity can be reduced, and whether the analysis extends beyond two-layer networks, one gradient step, and the specific sign-based task distributions considered here. For practitioners, the conceptual insight that head adaptation implicitly performs contrastive learning on the representation may inform the design of more effective multi-task pretraining objectives, even though the current guarantees are vacuous for realistic dimensions.
