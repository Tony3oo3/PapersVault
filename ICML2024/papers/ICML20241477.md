## Problem

Theoretical understanding of GNNs is heavily skewed toward coarse-grained, binary expressivity analysis rooted in Weisfeiler-Leman combinatorics, while generalization and optimization remain severely understudied. Existing results are architecture-specific, ignore continuous features and practical design choices, and do not explain the empirical success of GNNs trained with stochastic first-order methods. The paper argues the community needs a balanced theory that jointly addresses expressivity, generalization, and optimization in a way aligned with practical requirements.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the 'input' is the landscape of existing GNN architectures (MPNNs, graph transformers, higher-order GNNs) and their theoretical characterizations, while the 'output' is a structured taxonomy of 15 concrete research challenges organized across four interrelated dimensions. The paper specifically targets GNNs operating on graphs with continuous node and edge features, producing graph-level or node-level representations for tasks spanning graph classification, molecular property prediction, and combinatorial optimization, and emphasizes the need for results that account for architectural hyperparameters (normalization, aggregation, pooling) rather than treating architectures as monolithic units.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic articulation of 15 concrete challenges spanning four interrelated areas: fine-grained expressivity (moving beyond binary WL-based separation to geometric/metric characterizations of graph similarity), generalization (understanding how expressivity, architecture, and graph structure jointly affect out-of-distribution and extrapolation performance), optimization (characterizing SGD convergence under realistic assumptions including graph structure and non-linear activations), and theory-practice alignment (bridging theoretical results with domain-specific needs). The key insight is that these four dimensions cannot be studied in isolation—the paper explicitly maps their interactions and argues for a unified theoretical framework. Specific proposals include developing bi-Lipschitz correspondences between graph metrics and feature-space metrics, establishing uniform expressivity results independent of graph size, creating a 'Theo-practical Dojo' for standardized cross-architecture evaluation, and building a community library of theoretically-guided GNN implementations integrated with benchmarks and evaluation pipelines.

## Results

This is a position paper that does not present experimental results, quantitative benchmarks, or a main results table. Instead, it identifies and formalizes 19 open challenges across four pillars of graph machine learning theory: expressive power (Challenges II.1–II.6), generalization (Challenges III.1–III.4), optimization dynamics (Challenges IV.1–IV.5), and theory-practice alignment (Challenges V.1–V.5). The paper's contribution is a structured research agenda rather than empirical findings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or compare against baselines. It surveys and synthesizes existing theoretical results on GNN expressivity (e.g., 1-WL/k-WL equivalences, VC-dimension bounds, convergence analyses) to highlight gaps and propose concrete future directions. The paper references prior works such as Morris et al. (2019), Xu et al. (2019), Garg et al. (2020), and Du et al. (2019) not as baselines to beat, but as starting points whose limitations motivate the proposed challenges. No head-to-head performance comparisons are made.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and critiques the entire landscape of GNN theory rather than building on a single prior line of work. It departs from the dominant 1-WL-equivalence paradigm established by Morris et al. (2019) and Xu et al. (2019) by arguing that coarse-grained, combinatorial expressivity results are insufficient, and calls for geometric, fine-grained analyses that account for continuous features and practical architectural choices. It also positions itself against the limited body of GNN generalization work (Garg et al. 2020, Liao et al. 2021, Maskey et al. 2022) that relies on classical VC-dimension bounds, and against the nascent optimization analyses (Du et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2021a, Tang & Liu 2023) that make unrealistic simplifying assumptions. The paper carves new ground by explicitly demanding an integrated theory connecting expressivity, generalization, and optimization, and by proposing concrete community infrastructure (a 'Theo-practical Dojo,' unified libraries, standardized benchmarks) to bridge theory and practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides a comprehensive, authoritative roadmap from leading researchers in the field, identifying that optimization dynamics of GNNs are 'severely understudied' and that current generalization theory produces bounds with large constants that fail to capture the empirical reality that more expressive models often generalize better. The open questions it raises—particularly whether fine-grained geometric expressivity can yield tighter generalization bounds, how graph structure influences SGD convergence, and under what conditions GNNs extrapolate to larger graphs—are central to making GNN theory practically useful. A key limitation is that, as a position paper, it proposes challenges without providing solutions, so its impact depends on whether the community adopts the proposed evaluation protocols and infrastructure. The call for domain-specific adaptation (molecular property prediction, combinatorial optimization) and the integration of LLMs with GNNs signal where the field is heading next.
