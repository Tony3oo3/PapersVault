## Problem

Data Shapley is widely used for data selection, yet empirical results are inconsistent: some studies report significant gains over random selection while others find no improvement. No prior theory explains under what conditions Data Shapley succeeds or fails as a selection criterion, leaving practitioners without guidance on when to trust Shapley-based data selection.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training set N of n points, a utility function v defined by a learning algorithm (logistic regression or MLP) and a validation set, and the 40,000 utility samples (S, v(S)) already generated during permutation-sampling Shapley estimation. These samples are repurposed without additional model training to fit an MTM approximation via a neural network (linear layer for the modular part, MLP with non-negative weight constraints for the monotonic transform), split 32k/8k for train/eval. The key outputs are the normalized MTM fitting residual as a scalar predictor of Data Shapley's selection quality, and the ρ-consistency index linking utility correlation structure to approximability.

## Method

The paper first proves an impossibility result: via a hypothesis testing framework, it shows the Shapley transformation from v ∈ R^{2^n−1} to φ ∈ R^n is non-injective, so any Shapley-based test for comparing two dataset utilities achieves TrueNeg + TruePos ≤ 1 (no better than random). It then identifies a sufficient condition for Data Shapley optimality: monotonically transformed modular (MTM) functions v(S) = f(w₀ + Σᵢ∈S wᵢ) with monotone f, for which Data Shapley selects the optimal top-k subset for every k. Building on this, the paper proposes a practical heuristic: fit an MTM to the observed utility samples and use the normalized fitting residual as a predictor of Data Shapley's data selection effectiveness. Theorem 8 further bounds this residual in terms of the ρ-consistency index (correlation between v(S) and v(S′) for ρ-correlated subset pairs), connecting the heuristic to an intrinsic property of the utility function that is high when datasets contain heterogeneous-quality data.

## Results

The paper's key empirical finding is that Data Shapley's data selection performance is highly dependent on dataset quality heterogeneity: on clean datasets (0% label-flip ratio), Data Shapley performs marginally better or worse than random selection, while on datasets with high noise (up to 50% flipped labels), Data Shapley closely matches or surpasses the maximum utility achievable by random selection. The proposed MTM fitting residual heuristic shows a strong correlation with Data Shapley's effectiveness, with lower normalized fitting residuals consistently corresponding to better data selection performance across all tested selection ratios (k/n ∈ {10%, 30%, 50%, 70%}). The ρ-consistency index of utility functions is also empirically correlated with MTM fitting quality, supporting the theoretical bound in Theorem 8. Experiments use 40,000 permutation samples for Data Shapley estimation and 50,000 random subsets for baseline evaluation across 7 tabular datasets with logistic regression and MLP classifiers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Data Shapley against two random selection baselines: 'Random (average)' (mean utility of 50,000 uniformly sampled size-k subsets) and 'Random (maximum)' (best utility among those subsets). On clean datasets, Data Shapley is essentially indistinguishable from random selection, confirming the theoretical impossibility result (Theorem 2) that without structural assumptions, Shapley-based selection cannot outperform random guessing. As label-flip noise increases, Data Shapley's advantage grows substantially: at high noise ratios, it approaches or exceeds the Random (maximum) baseline, validating Theorem 4's prediction for heterogeneous-quality datasets. The MTM fitting residual serves as a predictive heuristic rather than a competing method; the scatter plots (Figure 2) show that lower residuals reliably predict stronger Data Shapley performance, while high residuals correspond to high variance in selection quality. The paper does not compare against other data valuation methods (e.g., Data Banzhaf, Beta Shapley, LOO) in the main experiments, though it notes in Remark 4 that the impossibility result extends to all semivalues satisfying the inverse Pascal triangle condition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Data Shapley framework (Ghorbani & Zou, 2019; Jia et al., 2019b) and the broader semivalue family (Kwon & Zou, 2022; Wang & Jia, 2023a), but departs from prior work by introducing a hypothesis testing lens (inspired by Bilodeau et al., 2024) to formally characterize when Shapley-based data selection is informative versus vacuous. The impossibility result exploits the non-injectivity of the Shapley transformation—a property well-known in cooperative game theory but, to the authors' knowledge, never discussed in the data valuation literature. The positive characterization via monotonically transformed modular functions (MTM) and the consistency-index bound draw on harmonic analysis of pseudo-boolean functions (O'Donnell, 2014; Saunshi et al., 2022), bridging a gap between data valuation and Boolean function theory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a long-standing empirical inconsistency in the data valuation literature by showing that Data Shapley's data selection performance is fundamentally bounded by the structural properties of the underlying utility function, not by the algorithm itself. The MTM characterization and the fitting-residual heuristic give practitioners a concrete, computable signal for when to trust Data Shapley scores, which is practically valuable in high-stakes domains like healthcare. Key limitations include the heuristic's reduced reliability across different domains and the absence of a necessary-and-sufficient characterization of the full Shapley-effective subspace. Open questions include extending the analysis to distributional settings, incorporating estimation error from Monte Carlo Shapley computation, and exploring fairness implications of Shapley-based selection.
