## Problem

Simplicial neural networks (SNNs) for higher-order graph data suffer from high training complexity, dependence on task-specific labels, and a need for substantial training data, while existing scattering transforms (Euclidean and geometric) are limited to pairwise interactions and cannot capture higher-order simplicial structure. No parameter-free, unsupervised method existed that could extract task-agnostic representations from simplicial complexes while retaining the theoretical guarantees (permutation equivariance, non-expansiveness, stability) of classical scattering transforms.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a simplicial complex of order K with feature matrices X_k ∈ R^{N_k × D_k} for each simplex order k and boundary matrices B_k ∈ R^{N_{k-1} × N_k} encoding the incidence structure. It produces a set of task-agnostic embeddings φ(X) = {S_k(X,B)}_{k=0}^K, where each S_k is a concatenation of scattering features across J scales and M layers. Because the model is entirely parameter-free, no training labels or optimization are required—embeddings are computed in a single forward pass, making it applicable in low-resource and label-free settings.

## Method

SSN defines five random walk matrices per simplex order (boundary, coboundary, lower, upper, and self-stay), each scaled by 1/5 to form a valid probability distribution. Since boundary and coboundary matrices are non-square and cannot be raised to integer powers as in geometric scattering, SSN instead performs sequential message passing across scales, propagating information from all four adjacency types at each hop. At each scale j, wavelet differences between consecutive aggregated features (X^{(j-1)} − X^{(j)}) preserve high-frequency discriminative information, followed by a nonlinear modulus operator. Stacking M such layers yields a deep filter bank that captures multi-scale, multi-adjacency structure. The paper proves permutation equivariance, non-expansiveness, and stability via a unified diffusion matrix T that compactly represents all adjacency-based transforms, and further shows theoretically that including (k+1)-simplex information tightens the stability bound, making representations more robust to structural perturbations.

## Results

On node classification, SSN (unsupervised, parameter-free) achieves 99.39% accuracy on high-school, 91.43% on primary-school, and 83.22% on senate-bills, outperforming all supervised GNN and SNN baselines by an average of 5.23% over the best graph method and 11.1% over the best simplicial method. On trajectory prediction, SSN reaches 49% accuracy on Ocean and 61% on Synthetic, surpassing the best supervised baseline (SCoNe) by 11.9% on average. On simplicial closure prediction, SSN achieves AUC-PR of 17.26 on email-Enron, 85.36 on high-school, and 47.62 on primary-school, outperforming both logistic regression and simplicial neural networks (MPSN, SCNN) across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSN decisively outperforms all supervised GNN baselines (GCN, GraphSage, GAT, GIN) on node classification, with the largest gap over GIN (83.22% vs. 68.31% on senate-bills) and GCN (91.43% vs. 76.12% on primary-school). Against supervised simplicial neural networks, SSN beats MPSN by 8.4–18.8 points and SCNN by 13.3–23.3 points across the three node classification datasets, despite requiring no training labels. The unsupervised geometric scattering baseline (GSN) is dramatically outperformed (e.g., 99.39% vs. 27% on high-school), confirming that higher-order simplicial structure is critical. Supervised scattering variants (ScGCN, GSAN) perform poorly (42–67%), suggesting that adding learnable parameters to scattering without simplicial structure is counterproductive. On graph classification, MPSN slightly edges out SSN on some TUDatasets (e.g., NCI1), attributed to MPSN's SWL-test expressivity and abundant training graphs; SSN matches GIN on NCI1 where no cliques exist. On trajectory prediction, SSN (49% Ocean, 61% Synthetic) surpasses SCoNe (30%, 55.4%) and SCNN (28.5%, 50.5%), while on the Mesh dataset all methods perform comparably (~97–98.5%). In low-data regimes (20% training split), SSN's advantage over MPSN widens substantially (e.g., 62.8% vs. 57.01% on primary-school node classification), underscoring its suitability for label-scarce settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends geometric scattering transforms (Gama et al., 2019; Gao et al., 2019) from graphs to simplicial complexes, adapting the multi-scale wavelet-difference-and-modulus architecture to handle four types of simplicial adjacencies (boundary, coboundary, lower, upper). The key structural departure from geometric scattering is the use of sequential message passing across non-square adjacency matrices rather than integer powers of a square lazy random walk matrix, necessitated by the fact that simplicial boundary matrices are rectangular. It also departs from prior simplicial methods (MPSN, SCNN, Hodgelets) by being entirely parameter-free and by incorporating boundary/coboundary adjacencies that Hodgelets omits, while avoiding the spectral decomposition cost of Hodgelets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The parameter-free design eliminates the need for task-specific labels and large training sets, making SSN particularly attractive for data-scarce or privacy-sensitive simplicial data where supervised SNNs underperform. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 5.5) that incorporating higher-order simplices tightens the stability bound provides a principled justification for using full simplicial structure rather than just graph-level information. Open questions include whether the fixed, non-adaptive filter bank can be made learnable without sacrificing the parameter-free guarantees, whether the approach scales to very high-order complexes (beyond order 3–10 tested here), and whether the expressivity—currently supported only empirically—can be bounded theoretically as is done for invariance and stability.
