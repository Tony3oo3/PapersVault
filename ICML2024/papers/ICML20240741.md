## Problem

Machine learning is now ubiquitous in the natural sciences, yet no prior work has systematically analyzed how ML's restrictive ontology (only data exist) and permissive epistemology (success = held-out performance) interact with natural science's goal of understanding latent structure. The paper identifies two specific, uncorrectable statistical biases that arise when ML is deployed in certain scientific contexts, and delineates where ML's philosophy is actually well-matched to scientific objectives.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the I/O spans multiple scientific applications discussed qualitatively: spectra-like feature vectors (M=110 dimensions) → scalar label estimates in the toy regression; simulation parameter vectors → high-resolution simulation outputs in emulation; real-time event data → binary classification decisions; and data distributions → outlier flags. The Appendix A demonstration specifically takes 110-dimensional noisy feature vectors (designed to resemble stellar spectra) as input and produces a single scalar regression estimate (stellar age) per data point, with the critical downstream use being population-level averaging of these estimates across 10^5 test points.

## Method

The paper constructs a philosophical and statistical framework by formalizing ML's ontology (latent parameters are degenerate, uninterpretable, and irrelevant; only data exist) and epistemology (a model is correct iff it performs well on held-out data), then contrasting these with natural science's ontology (forces, spacetime, and other latent entities exist) and epistemology (theories must explain across domains, connect to conservation laws, and be structurally coherent). It then identifies two uncorrectable biases: (1) when ML regression outputs are combined in population or ensemble analyses, the individual-point biases from the bias-variance trade-off become fixed offsets that do not average out, amplifying the implicit prior of the training set; and (2) when ML emulators replace first-principles simulations, the enormous cost asymmetry between emulator-based and simulation-based re-analysis creates a confirmation bias where only surprising results get re-verified. The first bias is demonstrated with a 3-layer MLP (64-32-16 neurons) trained on 4096 synthetic stellar spectra, showing that population-level inferences from 10^5 regression-labeled test points are systematically biased even when train, validation, and test sets are drawn from identical distributions. The paper further argues that in causal inference contexts, the high expressivity of ML models for confounders (foregrounds, backgrounds, calibration) actually makes scientific conclusions more conservative and robust, since giving the confounder model maximum capacity to explain away the effect strengthens the causal claim.

## Results

This is a Position Paper without standard quantitative benchmarks. The primary empirical demonstration is a toy example (Appendix A) in which a 3-layer MLP (64-32-16 neurons) trained on 4,096 synthetic stellar-spectrum points shows that population-level inferences using regression-estimated labels are systematically biased, even when training, validation, and test sets are drawn from an identical distribution. The test-set (10⁵ points) population estimates are very precise but very wrong, while the smaller training-set estimates are less precise but unbiased. The paper's main contributions are conceptual: identifying two uncorrectable statistical biases (training-set bias amplification in ensemble analyses, and emulator-induced confirmation bias) and delineating safe vs. unsafe contexts for ML in natural science.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a Position Paper, this work does not compare a proposed method against baselines in the standard ML sense. Instead, it contrasts the ontology and epistemology of ML (data-centric, performance-on-held-out-data as the standard of truth) with those of the natural sciences (latent-structure-centric, multi-domain explanatory power as the standard). The paper argues that ML regressions are inappropriate for label-transfer use cases where downstream users perform population or ensemble inferences, because the implicit prior from the training set gets amplified when biased point estimates are combined. It further argues that ML emulators of first-principles simulations introduce an asymmetric confirmation bias: anomalous results are far more likely to be re-examined than confirmatory ones, and the cost gap between emulator-based and simulation-based re-analysis makes this bias practically uncorrectable. No head-to-head method comparisons are presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new conceptual ground by explicitly framing the ML–natural-science relationship through the philosophical vocabulary of ontology and epistemology, a lens not previously applied systematically to this question. It draws on the causal-inference literature (Pearl; Dorie et al.), simulation-based inference (Cranmer et al.; Csillery et al.), and the physics-informed ML program (Karniadakis et al.; Villar et al.) as concrete anchors, but the central argument—that ML's data-only ontology and held-out-performance epistemology are in structural tension with natural science's latent-structure ontology—is presented as a new formalization. The paper also engages with Donoho (2024) on reproducibility and the 'data science at the singularity' debate, positioning itself as a more specific, physics-grounded response that identifies where the tension is productive and where it is harmful.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most consequential claim is that two specific statistical biases introduced by ML in scientific pipelines—population-level bias from regression-generated labels and emulator-induced confirmation bias—are uncorrectable in practice, meaning certain uses of ML in natural science are not merely suboptimal but actively harmful. This reframes the ML-for-science conversation from 'how do we make ML better?' to 'where should ML not be used?' and calls on scientific communities to audit their pipelines for these failure modes. Open questions include whether likelihood-based generative models (normalizing flows, sequential neural likelihood) can resolve the label-transfer bias, whether explainable or physics-structured emulators can eliminate the confirmation bias, and whether the deeper philosophical tension between ML's and science's ontologies can be bridged or must be managed through careful task assignment.
