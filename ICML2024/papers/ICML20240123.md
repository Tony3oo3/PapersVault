## Problem

Despite extensive research, CIFAR-10 adversarial robustness to ℓ∞-bounded perturbations remains unsolved, with SOTA AutoAttack accuracy barely exceeding 70% versus ~100% clean accuracy. The paper asks why this gap persists, investigating whether it stems from insufficient scale, inefficient compute allocation in prior methods, or fundamental limits imposed by the attack formulation itself.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240123/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 32×32 RGB CIFAR-10 images and produces 10-class labels, with adversarial perturbations constrained to ‖δ‖∞ ≤ 8/255. Training consumes synthetic CIFAR-10 images generated by diffusion models (EDM, PFGM++, DG) at varying FID qualities (1.65–35.54), mixed with original CIFAR-10 data at a 1:7 ratio. Evaluation uses AutoAttack (4 diverse attacks) on the CIFAR-10 test set, supplemented by a human study where three annotators and GPT-4 classify the 2,629 adversarial images misclassified by the SOTA model.

## Method

The paper derives the first scaling laws for adversarial training that explicitly model synthetic data quality via FID, using three complementary parametric approaches: an envelope-of-training-curves fit (Chinchilla-style), a loss model where data quality modulates the irreducible and data-reducible loss constants, and a loss model that captures quality-dependent overfitting. These laws are fit to 238 training runs (over 10²² FLOPs) across 8 WideResNet architectures (6M–316M parameters) trained on 5M–100M synthetic samples from 7 generators, yielding compute-optimal N* and D* as power-law functions of FLOPs budget. Applying these guidelines, the authors train a WideResNet-94-16 on 500M samples to achieve 73.71% AutoAttack accuracy (+3% over prior SOTA) with 20% fewer training FLOPs. A small-scale human evaluation on the SOTA model's misclassified adversarial images reveals that ~10% are 'invalid'—the ℓ∞ perturbation has altered the image so it no longer depicts its labeled class—explaining the predicted ~90% performance asymptote and demonstrating that the attack formulation itself, not just model capacity, limits achievable robustness.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ℓ∞-norm bounded AutoAttack (ε=8/255), the paper's new SOTA model (WideResNet-94-16, 366M params, trained on 500M synthetic samples) achieves 73.71% AutoAttack accuracy, a +3% gain over the prior SOTA of 70.69% (Wang et al., 2023). A compute-optimal model (WideResNet-82-8, 79M params) trained at the prior SOTA's FLOPs budget beats the prior SOTA by 1% AutoAttack while being over 3× smaller in both FLOPs and parameters. The paper also demonstrates that matching the prior SOTA's robustness requires 20% fewer training FLOPs under compute-optimal settings. Scaling laws predict an asymptotic robustness ceiling near 90%, consistent with a human evaluation showing ~90.46% average human accuracy on the adversarial test set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is Wang et al. (2023), the prior CIFAR-10 SOTA on RobustBench (WideResNet-70-16, 267M params, 70.69% AutoAttack). The new SOTA model (WRN-94-16, 366M params, 500M samples) outperforms Wang et al. by 3% AutoAttack accuracy (73.71% vs. 70.69%). More notably, a compute-optimal model (WRN-82-8, 79M params) trained at the same FLOPs budget as Wang et al. beats the prior SOTA by 1% AutoAttack while being over 3× more parameter- and FLOP-efficient, demonstrating that the prior SOTA used an excessively large model for its compute budget. Additionally, a model trained entirely on synthetic data (no real CIFAR-10 mixed in) reaches 71.7% AutoAttack, also surpassing the prior SOTA. The scaling laws further reveal that Wang et al.'s configuration sits outside the compute-efficient region, incurring excess training cost for its achieved robustness level.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the Chinchilla-style scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) and Kaplan et al.'s (2020) overfitting-aware parametric form into the adversarial training setting, adding a novel data-quality (FID) term that prior adversarial scaling studies (e.g., Debenedetti et al., 2023) did not model. It follows the training recipe of Wang et al. (2023) — the prior CIFAR-10 SOTA on RobustBench — as its baseline and direct comparison point, but departs from it by using compute-optimal model/dataset sizes predicted by the fitted laws rather than the larger model Wang et al. employed. The human-evaluation component extends the line of work on adversarial examples that fool humans (Elsayed et al., 2018; Gaziv et al., 2023) by quantifying the fraction of benchmark images that are genuinely invalid rather than merely difficult.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding — that ℓ∞-bounded attacks on SOTA-robust models generate images that humans also cannot correctly label, imposing a ~90% ceiling on the benchmark — reframes the adversarial robustness problem from a pure model-capability gap to a flawed evaluation formulation, a conceptual shift that affects how the field measures progress. Practically, the scaling laws give concrete, actionable guidance (smaller models, more data, higher-quality synthetic generators) that already yields a +3% SOTA improvement with 20% fewer FLOPs, and they predict that brute-force scaling alone would require ~10³⁰ FLOPs to close the remaining gap. Key limitations include the restriction to CIFAR-10 and WideResNet, a three-user human study, and the need to validate whether the invalid-data phenomenon and the near-equal N/D scaling exponents generalize to ImageNet, ViTs, and other threat models.
