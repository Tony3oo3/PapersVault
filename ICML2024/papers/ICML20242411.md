## Problem

Low bit-width integer GEMMs are essential for efficient hardware acceleration, but Transformer models contain a few extreme outlier entries that far exceed the representable range of low bit-width integers, causing catastrophic performance degradation when naively quantized. Existing approaches handle these outliers with mixed-precision (FP16) computation or complex error-correction schemes, requiring hardware support for multiple precisions. The paper asks whether exact integer GEMM results can be obtained using purely low bit-width integer arithmetic, without any mixed-precision fallback.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242411/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes floating-point weight and activation matrices from Transformer models (e.g., LLaMA-7B, RoBERTa, ViT), quantizes them to integers via Rounding-to-Nearest with a percentile-based scaling, then unpacks any out-of-bound entries into larger matrices whose values all fit within an arbitrarily chosen bit-width b (as low as 2 bits for the set {-1, 0, +1}). The output is the exact GEMM result of the original integer matrices, computed solely through low bit-width integer GEMMs plus bit-shift scaling and accumulation, with no floating-point operations in the critical path.

## Method

IM-Unpack exploits the observation that any integer v can be decomposed as v = v_0 + s·v_1 + s²·v_2 + … where s = 2^(b-1) and each v_i is a b-bit integer, effectively performing a base-s digit expansion. Three unpacking strategies are provided: row unpacking (splits rows containing outliers into multiple bounded rows tracked by a sparse matrix Π), column unpacking (splits columns and duplicates the corresponding B columns with a diagonal scaling matrix S), and a combined adaptive strategy that at each step unpacks whichever dimension (row or column) eliminates the most out-of-bound entries. The final GEMM is computed via ScaledMatMul, which groups columns by their distinct power-of-2 scaling factors and performs one low bit-width GEMM per group, with scaling implemented as bit-shifting. Because outliers in Transformer matrices are spatially concentrated in a few rows or columns, the unpacking overhead (measured as the ratio of unpacked to original GEMM FLOPs) remains small—typically 1.1×–2.5× for linear layers and attention scores—making the approach practical for both training and inference.

## Results

On LLaMA-7B zero-shot evaluation with linear-layer quantization, RTN with β=31 (INT) achieves ARC-c 42.7, ARC-e 76.1, BoolQ 76.1, HellaSwag 57.3, PIQA 77.3, and WinoGrande 69.3, nearly matching full-precision BF16 (43.1, 76.3, 77.8, 57.2, 78.0, 68.8) while using only ~5 bits per weight. For ViT ImageNet classification, RTN β=15 reaches 87.5% top-1 on ViT-Huge vs. 87.6% full-precision. For training, RoBERTa-Small with β=31 achieves 1.840 validation perplexity (better than FP32's 1.869), and ViT-Small with β=31 reaches 79.17% top-1 accuracy (matching FP16's 79.16%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RTN with β=31 substantially outperforms all fixed-bit-width baselines on LLaMA-7B: it beats SmoothQuant (INT8) by +5.3 on ARC-c (42.7 vs 37.4), LLM-QAT (INT4) by +12.5 on ARC-c (42.7 vs 30.2), and LLM-FP4 by +9.1 on ARC-c (42.7 vs 33.6), while matching or exceeding LLM.int8() (INT8+FP16) on most metrics without requiring mixed-precision FP16 for outliers. On ViT ImageNet classification, RTN β=15 achieves 87.5% on ViT-Huge, surpassing PTQ4ViT (INT6) at 86.6% and FQ-ViT (INT8) at 85.0% on ViT-Large, while APQ-ViT (INT4) collapses to 41.4% on ViT-Base. For training, RTN β=31 on RoBERTa-Small yields 1.840 validation perplexity, marginally better than both FP32 (1.869) and BF16 (1.868), and ViT-Small RTN β=31 reaches 79.17% top-1, matching FP16 (79.16%) and exceeding FP32 (78.91%). The key advantage over GPTQ, QuIP, and other post-training quantization methods is that RTN requires no calibration data, no sophisticated error-correction, and no mixed-precision hardware support—IM-Unpack handles outliers purely with low-bit-width integer arithmetic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the dominant quantization literature (GPTQ, SmoothQuant, LLM.int8(), QuIP, LLM-FP4) that focuses on reducing bit-width while managing rounding error through calibration, smoothing, or mixed-precision fallbacks. The key conceptual departure is showing that the rounding-to-integer step itself is not the source of performance loss—only the bit-width restriction is—thereby reframing the problem from 'how to quantize accurately' to 'how to represent a few large integers using only small ones.' The IM-Unpack algorithm draws on the multi-resolution decomposition idea (analogous to base-s expansion) and the outlier-awareness of Dettmers et al. (2022) and Xiao et al. (2023), but replaces their mixed-precision or transformation-based outlier handling with a purely low-bit-width exact decomposition, eliminating the need for any higher-precision hardware path.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant for hardware and compiler design: if all GEMMs can be executed with a single low bit-width (as low as 2-bit), the need for mixed-precision datapaths, FP16 fallback units, or multiple quantization kernels disappears, potentially simplifying accelerator architectures and improving power efficiency. The main limitation is that the self-attention matrix M has outliers concentrated along the diagonal rather than in rows or columns, making current unpacking strategies less efficient for attention output GEMMs (unpack ratios up to ~6x at 5-bit). Open questions include whether more sophisticated quantization (beyond RTN) can reduce the need for large β on gradients, whether the diagonal structure of M admits a dedicated unpacking strategy, and whether the approach scales to very long sequences where M's outlier pattern may change.
