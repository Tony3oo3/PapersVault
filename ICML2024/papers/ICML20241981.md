## Problem

The growing literature on differentially private learning with public pretraining assumes that Web-scraped pretraining data is privacy-neutral and that standard ML benchmarks (e.g., CIFAR-10, ImageNet) meaningfully measure progress in private learning. This position paper challenges both assumptions, arguing that the public-pretraining-and-private-finetuning paradigm may fail to be genuinely private, may not be as useful as benchmarks suggest, and may introduce new privacy risks through the model scale it requires.

## Input / Output

The paradigms under critique take large-scale Web-scraped datasets (e.g., JFT with 4 billion images, LAION-5B, or terabytes of Internet text) as public pretraining inputs and small sensitive datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10, ImageNet, or private chat corpora) as private finetuning inputs, producing a model with a specified (ε, δ) differential privacy guarantee on the finetuning data only. The paper examines both RGB image and text modalities, emphasizing that the pretraining data carries no privacy guarantee while the finetuning data does, and that the resulting model can still memorize and leak pretraining content.

## Method

As a position paper, the core contribution is a structured critique organized around three orthogonal concerns. First, it argues that publicly accessible Web data is not necessarily non-sensitive, citing concrete examples (GPT-2 memorizing real phone numbers, unintentionally published IRC transcripts, cryptocurrency private keys in public GitHub repos) to show that DP finetuning on top of such pretraining still leaks personal information and erodes trust in privacy technologies. Second, it demonstrates that standard benchmarks like CIFAR-10 and ImageNet have near-complete distributional overlap with their pretraining sources (e.g., every CIFAR-10 class exists in ImageNet), making them poor proxies for real privacy-sensitive tasks where public and private distributions are disjoint, as in medical imaging. Third, it highlights that the model sizes required for effective public pretraining (billions of parameters, orders of magnitude larger than on-device models like MobileBERT) force users to outsource sensitive data to third-party cloud services, trading DP guarantees for confidentiality risks. The paper concludes with a call for granular privacy considerations for Web data, privacy-friendly pretrained models, better cross-domain benchmarks (e.g., MIMIC, CheXpert), and a holistic view of ML privacy beyond single-model training.

## Results

This is a position paper with no original experimental results table. The key quantitative evidence cited includes: (1) without pretraining, DP-SGD achieves only 39.2–39.4% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet at ε=8 (Sander et al., 2023; Tang et al., 2023), versus ≥86.7% for non-private models; (2) with 4B-image Web pretraining, Berrada et al. (2023) reach 86.8% at ε=1, closing the gap; (3) CLIP achieves 96.2% zero-shot accuracy on CIFAR-10 (ε=0), and Pham et al. (2023) reach 85.7% zero-shot on ImageNet, suggesting many 'private learning' benchmarks are already solvable without any private training; (4) BASIC-L achieves only 59.6% on the medical PCam dataset, illustrating poor cross-domain transfer for sensitive tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method and therefore has no direct method-vs-baseline comparison. Instead, it contrasts two paradigms: (a) DP training from scratch (e.g., Sander et al. 2023 at 39.2% ImageNet top-1, ε=8) versus (b) public pretraining + DP finetuning (e.g., Berrada et al. 2023 at 86.8%, ε=1), showing that the latter closes the accuracy gap by ~6× but at the cost of relying on Web-scraped data. The paper further argues that standard benchmarks (CIFAR-10, ImageNet) are uninformative because zero-shot foundation models (CLIP at 96.2% on CIFAR-10; Pham et al. at 85.7% on ImageNet) already match or exceed DP-finetuned results, making it impossible to disentangle gains from private learning versus gains from non-private representation learning. The paper also contrasts intra-domain transfer (ImageNet→CIFAR-10, where all 10 CIFAR classes exist in ImageNet) with the cross-domain transfer (e.g., Web→medical) that real privacy-sensitive deployments would require, noting the latter shows much weaker transfer (BASIC-L at 59.6% on PCam).

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper critically examines the growing line of work that combines large-scale public pretraining with differentially private finetuning (Abadi et al., 2016; Tramèr & Boneh, 2021; De et al., 2022; Mehta et al., 2023; Li et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2022), which has become the dominant paradigm for improving DP-ML utility. It draws on the memorization literature (Carlini et al., 2019, 2021; Feldman, 2020) to argue that 'public' pretraining data is not privacy-neutral, and on the distribution-shift / transfer-learning literature (Koh et al., 2021; Pham et al., 2023) to question whether standard benchmarks (CIFAR-10, ImageNet) meaningfully measure private learning progress. Rather than proposing a new method, the paper carves new conceptual ground by reframing three assumptions—public data is non-sensitive, current benchmarks are appropriate, and large models are deployable—that the field has largely taken for granted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the foundational assumption that public pretraining is a privacy-free 'gift' to DP-ML, arguing that Web-scraped data carries real sensitivity and that labeling models as 'privacy-preserving' while ignoring pretraining-data leakage erodes public trust in differential privacy more broadly (e.g., for census applications). It also highlights that current benchmarks conflate intra-domain transfer with genuine cross-domain private learning, making it difficult to assess whether the paradigm will generalize to truly sensitive domains like medical imaging. Key open questions include: how to curate pretraining corpora that are genuinely non-sensitive, what benchmarks better reflect real privacy-sensitive workloads, and how to distill large foundation models into on-device-compatible sizes without sacrificing the utility gains that motivate the paradigm in the first place.
