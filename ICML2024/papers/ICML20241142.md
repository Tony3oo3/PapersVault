## Problem

Existing learning-based negotiation approaches either neglect exploration (focusing solely on exploitation or using context-free UCT), struggle with combinatorially large action spaces (e.g., 2^|V| possible bids in trading), or fail to handle partial observations and complex acceptance functions. No prior method simultaneously addresses all four prevalent challenges—exploration-exploitation tradeoff, large action spaces, hidden counterpart states, and non-linear acceptance—within a unified learning framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an item context matrix Y (n×d), a negotiator pair context vector x_τ, a valid bid set B_τ of indicator vectors over items, and accumulated historical (bid, acceptance) pairs. Bids are represented as integer indicator vectors (positive entries for items requested, negative for items given), and the acceptance label is binary. Critically, feedback is full-bandit: only the aggregate acceptance of the entire bid is observed, not per-item acceptances, and the problem-specific benefit function f_τ is handcrafted externally (e.g., a utility threshold constraint).

## Method

NegUCB formulates negotiation as a contextual combinatorial multi-armed bandit where arms are items, super arms are bids, and rewards are binary acceptance labels. The acceptance function is decomposed into a context-driven term (φ(x_τ)Θ⟨Y,b⟩) and a hidden-state term (u_τ⟨Y,b⟩), with non-linearity introduced via a kernel transformation φ that is never explicitly learned—instead, kernel matrices K_τ and Z_τ are computed directly from kernel functions κ₁ and κ₂. The algorithm alternates between closed-form updates for Θ and U (an alternating least-squares scheme), then selects bids via a UCB rule that adds exploration bonuses derived from kernel prediction variances. A key theoretical result is that the cumulative regret bound is sub-linear in the number of negotiation steps and independent of bid cardinality γ, a property enabled by full-bandit feedback and the linear bid-context extraction assumption (ψ(Y,b)=Yᵀbᵀ).

## Results

On the ANAC multi-issue negotiation benchmark (44 of 50 domains), NegUCB consistently reaches beneficial deals in fewer negotiation steps than the rule-based ANAC agent across nearly all domains. On the synthetic resource allocation task, NegUCB achieves the lowest cumulative theoretical regret and highest acceptance rate among all baselines, while the reinforcement learning baseline (ANEGMA) plateaus below 0.6 acceptance rate even after 20,000 training steps. On the CivRealm trading task, NegUCB with an SE kernel (σ=1) and exploration rate 0.1 achieves the highest acceptance rate, outperforming LinUCB, FactorUCB, and KernelUCB, while CivRealm's own baselines report deal rates below 0.4.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NegUCB outperforms all five baselines across all three negotiation tasks. Against the rule-based ANAC agent, NegUCB reaches deals in significantly fewer steps on 44 of 50 ANAC domains (6 domains fail for both methods). Against LinUCB, FactorUCB, and KernelUCB (all extended to handle combinatorial full-bandit feedback), NegUCB achieves lower cumulative regret and higher acceptance rates on both the resource allocation and trading tasks, with the advantage attributed to its hidden-state component and kernel-based non-linear modeling. Against the ANEGMA reinforcement learning baseline, NegUCB clearly dominates: ANEGMA's ε-greedy exploration causes its acceptance rate to plateau below 0.6 on resource allocation, and it is entirely infeasible for the trading task due to the combinatorial action space of size Σⱼ₌₁⁴ C(87,j). The paper notes that FactorUCB and KernelUCB fail to show improvement with increased exploration on the trading task, which the authors attribute to inaccurate formulations leading to misguided exploration, whereas NegUCB benefits from its hidden-state modeling of counterpart preferences.

## Relation to Prior Work

NegUCB unifies three previously separate lines of bandit research—contextual bandits (LinUCB, KernelUCB), combinatorial bandits (C2UCB, ComLinUCB), and hidden-state factorization (FactorUCB)—into a single algorithm for negotiation. The key departure is the full-bandit feedback setting: prior combinatorial bandit algorithms (C2UCB, ComLinUCB, CC-MAB, CN-UCB) assume semi-bandit feedback where per-item rewards are observable, while full-bandit algorithms (DART, ETCG, RGL) are non-contextual. NegUCB is the first to simultaneously handle contexts, combinatorial super-arms, full-bandit feedback, non-linear reward functions via RKHS kernel regression, and partial observations through learned hidden states, replacing the explicit transformation function φ with kernel entries computed via Assumption 3.2.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The cardinality-independent regret bound is a meaningful theoretical distinction from prior combinatorial bandit results, making the approach scalable to negotiation domains with exponentially large bid spaces (e.g., 87 technologies in CivRealm). However, the formulation assumes the agent knows its negotiation target at each step, limiting applicability to settings where negotiation is merely one action among many in a broader RL planning loop. Open questions include whether the alternating least-squares iteration between Θ and U remains stable with more complex kernel choices or larger hidden-state dimensions, and how the method performs when the valid bid set B_τ changes non-stationarily over time.
