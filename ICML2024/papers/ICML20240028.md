## Problem

Standard difference-in-differences and triple difference estimators rely on parallel trends or only identify average effects, while the changes-in-changes (CiC) framework assumes no drift between treatment and control groups—a condition that is implausible when policies target specific subpopulations (e.g., low-income households). No existing method simultaneously handles targeted treatment assignments, provides full counterfactual distribution identification, and remains scale-invariant.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pre- and post-treatment outcome data from two separate populations (e.g., two states), each partitioned into treatment-eligible and ineligible cohorts, yielding four subgroups per time point. It produces the full counterfactual CDF of the untreated outcome in the treated subgroup, the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), and—under a stronger rank-invariance assumption—the joint density of both potential outcomes. The estimator operates on empirical CDFs and their inverses, requiring only that outcomes be continuous on a bounded domain with densities bounded away from zero.

## Method

The triple changes estimator replaces CiC's no-drift assumption with a state-independent drift assumption: the nonlinear drift mapping T* between control and treatment group evolution is identical across the two states, rather than being the identity. Point identification of the counterfactual CDF is achieved by composing CDFs and their inverses across the four subgroups (Eq. 7), effectively using the control state to estimate the drift correction that is then applied to the treated state. For high-dimensional outcomes, the framework is recast via Brenier's theorem, where the monotone maps become optimal transport maps with quadratic cost, and the key assumption becomes co-cyclic monotonicity of the production functions. Partial identification bounds are derived under relaxed ε-monotonicity and δ-invariance (bounded Kolmogorov drift in the latent variable), and a finite-sample estimator for ATT is shown to be √N-consistent and asymptotically normal.

## Results

On the NSCH dataset evaluating Medicaid expansion's impact on children's preventive care (frequency of doctor visits), the triple changes estimator yields a mean treatment effect of 0.145 (90% CI: [-0.01, 0.331]), while the triple difference estimator gives 0.170 (90% CI: [-0.004, 0.344]). In synthetic experiments, the triple changes estimator is asymptotically unbiased under both linear and nonlinear data-generating processes, whereas DiD and CiC exhibit persistent bias that does not vanish with increasing sample size, and the triple difference estimator becomes biased under nonlinear production functions. The triple changes estimator also outperforms the triple difference estimator in small-sample regimes under nonlinear models due to its scale-invariance property.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed triple changes (CCC) estimator against three baselines: standard DiD, the changes-in-changes (CiC) estimator of Athey and Imbens (2006), and the triple difference (DDD) estimator of Olden and Møen (2022). In the linear synthetic model, both DDD and CCC converge to zero bias as sample size grows, while DiD and CiC retain persistent bias; DDD has a slight edge in small-sample bias due to its reliance on empirical averages. In the nonlinear synthetic model, CCC remains asymptotically unbiased while DDD becomes biased, demonstrating CCC's advantage from scale invariance. On the real NSCH dataset, both DDD (0.170) and CCC (0.145) produce similar point estimates with overlapping 90% confidence intervals, and both align with the conclusion of Roy et al. (2020) that Medicaid expansion increased children's preventive care visits. The key differentiator is that CCC provides a full counterfactual distribution (not just the mean) and is robust to nonlinear outcome transformations where DDD fails.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two distinct lines of causal inference: the triple difference estimator (Gruber 1994; Olden & Møen 2022), which handles targeted policies by differencing two DiD estimators to cancel shared bias, and the changes-in-changes (CiC) framework (Athey & Imbens 2006), which identifies the full counterfactual distribution under a no-drift assumption. The key departure is relaxing CiC's no-drift assumption (T₁∘T₀⁻¹ = Id) to a state-independent drift assumption, which is more credible when treatment targets specific subpopulations (e.g., low-income individuals). The high-dimensional extension leverages Brenier's optimal transport theorem and co-cyclic monotonicity, connecting the estimator to the data-fusion literature (Bareinboim & Pearl 2016).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The estimator fills a genuine gap: triple difference handles targeted policies but only identifies average effects and is scale-sensitive, while CiC provides distributional identification but assumes no drift across groups—implausible for targeted interventions. The partial identification results under ε-monotonicity and δ-invariance (bounded KS distance) provide practical robustness guarantees, and the NSCH application to Medicaid expansion demonstrates feasibility. Open questions include extensions to time-series settings, handling of unobserved covariates beyond conditioning, and whether the state-independent drift assumption can be further relaxed or tested empirically.
