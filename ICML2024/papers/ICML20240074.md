## Problem

Parameter inference in non-linear mixed-effects (NLME) models requires marginalizing individual-specific parameters from a likelihood that generally has no closed-form solution, making it computationally intractable for large populations or complex generative models. Established methods like SAEM and FOCEI are slow, require full restarts for each new dataset or population model, cannot handle stochastic individual-level models (e.g., SDEs), and are known to produce biased variance estimates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes noisy individual time-series measurements (e.g., fluorescence intensities or plasma concentrations at irregular time points), a population model linking fixed and random effects to individual parameters (optionally with covariates), and a pre-trained conditional normalizing flow. It outputs population-level parameters (fixed effects and random-effects covariance), individual-specific posterior distributions over parameters, and uncertainty quantification via profile likelihoods or full Bayesian MCMC. Crucially, the inference phase requires no simulations of the generative model—only forward passes through the trained flow and numerical optimization—making it orders of magnitude faster than SAEM for new datasets.

## Method

The approach is a three-phase amortized procedure: (I) generate synthetic data by sampling individual parameters from a prior and simulating through the generative model; (II) train a conditional normalizing flow (neural spline flows with 7–8 invertible layers) conditioned on LSTM-extracted summary statistics of the time series, minimizing KL divergence between true and approximate individual posteriors; (III) at inference time, sample from the trained flow for each individual and solve a Monte Carlo approximation of the population likelihood via importance sampling, where the ratio p_pop(φ|θ)/p(φ) serves as the importance weight. The key reformulation expresses the population likelihood as a conditional expectation over individual posteriors, decoupling the expensive simulation/training from the cheap inference phase. This amortization allows the same trained flow to be reused across different datasets, population models (e.g., switching from diagonal to full covariance), and even stochastic generative models without any retraining.

## Results

On synthetic single-cell mRNA transfection data, the amortized approach recovers true population parameters with lower mean squared error and smaller variance in estimates than SAEM across both the simple (6-parameter) and detailed (11-parameter) ODE models. Computationally, the amortized inference phase requires two orders of magnitude less time than SAEM, and across all data sets and models combined, SAEM's total runtime is 112× longer. On the sunitinib pharmacokinetic data (47 patients, 13 parameters), 97% of measurements fall within the amortized approach's 95% estimated population variance, versus only 72% for FOCEI. For the SDE-based NLME model, the stochastic formulation reduces the population variance overestimation from 3× (simple ODE) to 1.3×, and enables identification of all individual parameters (k, m₀, scale) that are structurally non-identifiable in the ODE model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SAEM (Monolix), the amortized approach achieves lower MSE in parameter recovery on synthetic data for both ODE models, requires ~100× less computation time for the inference phase, and produces profile-likelihood confidence intervals that cover true variance parameters where SAEM's Wald-based intervals fail (3 out of 11 variance parameters in the detailed model). Against FOCEI (NONMEM) on the sunitinib pharmacokinetic data, the amortized approach substantially outperforms on measurement coverage (97% vs. 72% within 95% variance) and avoids FOCEI's known systematic underestimation of population variance; FOCEI's variance estimates fall outside the 95% credible regions produced by the amortized approach, while SAEM's do. Compared to the published Laplacian-approximation fit of Fröhlich et al. (2018), the amortized approach reproduces similar population-mean trajectories but additionally captures cross-correlations between parameters (improving the population variance fit) and enables full Bayesian posterior sampling at negligible extra cost. The method also uniquely handles the SDE-based NLME model, which neither SAEM nor FOCEI can address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes amortized neural posterior estimation (Cranmer et al., 2020; Papamakarios et al., 2017) — a tool developed for general simulation-based inference — to solve the intractable marginalization integral in non-linear mixed-effects models, a problem traditionally addressed by SAEM (Kuhn & Lavielle, 2005) or FOCEI (Wang, 2008). The key conceptual move is reformulating the population likelihood as an importance-sampling expectation over individual-level posteriors (Eq. 5), which can be evaluated with samples from a pre-trained conditional normalizing flow, thereby decoupling the expensive simulation/training phase from the cheap inference phase. This departs from prior simulation-based NLME work (Augustin et al., 2023) that relied on hand-crafted filters and from permutation-embedding approaches (Radev et al., 2020) that amortize over a fixed number of i.i.d. trials, by instead amortizing over arbitrary population sizes and population model structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 112× overall speedup over SAEM and the ability to handle stochastic differential equation models (where SAEM and FOCEI are inapplicable) make NLME fitting feasible for large single-cell datasets and complex pharmacokinetic models that were previously computationally prohibitive. The amortization property — train once, then run inference on new data sets or population models at negligible cost — enables systematic model selection and full Bayesian uncertainty quantification (profile likelihoods, MCMC) that were impractical with traditional methods. Open questions include sensitivity to prior choice and model misspecification, the behavior for small data sets where the amortized overhead is not yet recovered, and how to best combine amortized and problem-specific inference in hierarchical settings.
