## Problem

Algorithmic predictions are widely used to target scarce interventions, but no prior work formally quantifies how much welfare improvement comes from better predictions relative to other policy levers such as expanding access to more people or improving intervention quality. This paper initiates the formal study of the relative value of prediction by asking: given a targeting system with a fixed predictor and resource constraint, is it more cost-efficient to collect more features (improve r²) or to treat a larger fraction of the population (increase α)?

## Input / Output

The method takes a partition of features into observed x_s and unobserved x_t components, a resource constraint α ∈ (0,1) bounding the fraction of the population that can be treated, and model parameters (coefficient vector β, mean welfare improvement μ). The central output is the prediction-access ratio (PAR), defined as the ratio of marginal welfare gains from expanding α to those from improving the r² value γ_s² of the predictor. This ratio is then multiplied by a cost ratio C_{r²}(Δ_{r²})/C_α(Δ_α) to produce a cost-benefit ratio that determines which policy lever is locally optimal; values above 1 favor expanding access, values below 1 favor improving prediction.

## Method

The core technique derives the value function V*(α, γ_s) for the optimal threshold-based targeting policy under two workhorse statistical models, then computes the ratio of marginal improvements in welfare with respect to α and γ_s. For linear regression (w = ⟨x,β⟩ + μ with Gaussian features), the value function admits a closed form V* = αμ + γ_s‖β‖₂φ(Φ⁻¹(1−α)), yielding PAR ≈ γ_s/α (Theorem 3.5). For probit regression (w = 1{⟨x,β⟩+μ > 0}), the value function is an integral over the Gaussian CDF, but the PAR still simplifies to Θ(γ_t·α^{−1/γ_t²}) where γ_t² = 1−γ_s² (Theorem 4.4), which is strictly larger than the linear case since 1/γ_t² > 1. The proofs rely on the Mills ratio for truncated Gaussian expectations, Gaussian tail bounds (Lemma A.6), and second-order Taylor expansions to establish tight constant-factor bounds on the ratio. The key structural insight is that when resources are scarce (small α) and the predictor explains even a small fraction of outcome variance, the marginal welfare gain from treating one additional person far exceeds that from marginally improving prediction accuracy.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative results are closed-form (up to constant factors) expressions for the prediction-access ratio (PAR) in two canonical models. For linear regression (Theorem 3.5), PAR^lin is bounded between 0.25·M_lin and M_lin where M_lin = (1/α)(μ/(‖β‖₂Φ⁻¹(1−α)) + γ_s), yielding the headline scaling PAR ≈ γ_s/α. For probit regression (Theorem 4.4), PAR^pr scales as γ_t · α^(−1/γ_t²) where γ_t² = 1 − γ_s², which is strictly larger than the linear-case ratio since 1/γ_t² > 1. In both settings, the cost-benefit ratio (PAR × C_{r²}/C_α) exceeds 1 — meaning expanding access is locally cost-efficient — whenever α is small relative to γ_s (linear) or whenever α ≪ b = Pr[w_i > 0] (probit), regardless of prediction quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or dataset comparisons. The central comparison is between two policy levers — improving prediction (increasing γ_s) versus expanding access (increasing α) — under two statistical models. In the linear regression model, the PAR is O(γ_s/α), so the relative value of prediction shrinks linearly with scarcity; the efficient frontier is γ_s ∝ α, meaning prediction and access should be expanded 'hand in hand.' In the probit (discrete-outcome) model, the PAR is O(γ_t · α^(−1/γ_t²)), which decays faster than the linear case because the exponent 1/γ_t² > 1. This implies that for binary outcomes, expanding access dominates improving prediction even more strongly: the cost-benefit ratio exceeds 1 for all γ_s > 0 whenever α is well below the base rate b, regardless of how poor the predictor is. The paper also notes that improving intervention quality (increasing μ) yields welfare gains of the same order as improving prediction (both Θ̃(α)), and is thus also dominated by expanding access (Θ̃(1)) in the scarce-resource regime.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the prediction policy problems and optimal targeting literature (Kleinberg et al. 2015; Bhattacharya & Dupas 2012; Kitagawa & Tetenov 2018), which establishes how to allocate scarce interventions using predictions of heterogeneous treatment effects. It also connects to contextual bandits with knapsack constraints (Agrawal & Devanur 2016; Slivkins et al. 2023), where design parameters like resource constraints are treated as fixed. The key departure is that this paper treats those design parameters as endogenous policy levers and introduces the prediction-access ratio (PAR) as a new formal metric for comparing the marginal welfare gains from improving prediction versus expanding access — a question the authors explicitly identify as absent from prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding — that the relative value of prediction scales as Θ(γ_s/α) in the linear case and Θ(γ_t·α^{-1/γ_t²}) in the probit case — provides a simple, actionable criterion for when a predictor is 'good enough' and when structural improvements (expanding access) should take priority. This is potentially counternarrative to the dominant framing in algorithmic decision-making that better prediction is the highest-order bit. However, the results are limited to well-specified models with mostly positive treatment effects, require accurate cost estimates, and are purely theoretical with no empirical validation on real allocation data. Open questions include extending the analysis to settings where interventions can be harmful, developing machinery for cost estimation, and analyzing the PAR under broader model classes and social objective functions.
