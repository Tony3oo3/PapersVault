## Problem

Contrastive continual learning, which combines contrastive loss with knowledge distillation to mitigate catastrophic forgetting, has shown strong empirical performance (e.g., Co²L) but lacks theoretical justification for why this combination is effective. This paper establishes the first provable performance guarantees for this framework, revealing how the final model's test loss is bounded by the series of per-task training losses, and uses these insights to design a more effective algorithm.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242167/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of image classification tasks (e.g., 5 tasks of 2 classes each on Seq-CIFAR-10, 10 tasks of 20 classes on Seq-Tiny-ImageNet, or 20 rotation-angle tasks on R-MNIST) along with a fixed-size memory buffer (200 or 500 samples) of previously seen data. The backbone (ResNet-18 or a simple CNN) produces L2-normalized d-dimensional embeddings; a separate linear classifier is then trained on frozen representations using only the last task's data plus buffer samples with class-balanced sampling, yielding a class label as the final output.

## Method

The theoretical core is Lemma 1, which shows that the contrastive loss of the current model f_t on any distribution is bounded above and below by α times the previous model's contrastive loss plus the distillation loss plus a constant, where α = 2e²/(1+e²) < 1. Theorem 1 then unrolls this recursion across T tasks to bound the total test loss by a weighted sum of all per-task training losses, with weights α^(T-t)/γ_t(λ) that decay geometrically for earlier tasks, explaining why pre-training quality disproportionately affects final performance. Building on this, CILA replaces the fixed distillation coefficient λ=1 (used in Co²L) with a task-adaptive λ_t = max(1, κ · ΣL̂_dis / ΣL̂_con) computed from the ratio of cumulative empirical distillation losses to cumulative empirical contrastive losses over all previous tasks, which the theory predicts should stay near one but adapt to data-distribution shifts. The algorithm follows the same 'pre-train representations, then linear probe' paradigm as Co²L, with the only change being the adaptive λ_t at each task boundary.

## Results

CILA achieves state-of-the-art classification accuracy across all three continual learning scenarios (Class-IL, Task-IL, Domain-IL) and all three datasets. On Seq-CIFAR-10 Class-IL with a 500-sample buffer, CILA reaches 76.03% accuracy, improving over the previous best Co²L (74.26%) by 1.77%. On Seq-Tiny-ImageNet Task-IL (500 buffer), CILA attains 54.13% vs. Co²L's 53.04%, and on R-MNIST Domain-IL (500 buffer), CILA reaches 98.76% vs. Co²L's 98.65%. CILA also outperforms the strongest non-contrastive baseline DER++ by 3.33% on Seq-CIFAR-10 Class-IL (500 buffer).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CILA outperforms all nine baselines across every dataset, scenario, and buffer size tested. The closest competitor is Co²L (Cha et al., 2021), the contrastive continual learning framework that CILA builds upon; CILA improves over Co²L by 1.49–1.77% on Seq-CIFAR-10, 0.52–1.09% on Seq-Tiny-ImageNet, and 0.46–0.11% on R-MNIST. Among non-contrastive replay-based methods, DER++ (Buzzega et al., 2020) is the strongest baseline, but CILA still leads by 2.15–3.33% on Seq-CIFAR-10 and 2.77–3.22% on Seq-Tiny-ImageNet Task-IL. Gradient-constrained methods (GEM, A-GEM) and regularization-based methods (iCaRL, FDR, GSS, HAL) are consistently 5–20+ points behind CILA on Class-IL settings, where catastrophic forgetting is most severe. ER (Riemer et al., 2019), the simplest replay baseline, trails CILA by 9–12% on Seq-CIFAR-10 Class-IL and by 6–10% on Seq-Tiny-ImageNet Task-IL.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Co²L (Cha et al., 2021), which introduced the contrastive continual learning framework combining supervised contrastive loss with knowledge distillation and experience replay. While Co²L demonstrated strong empirical performance, it lacked theoretical justification for why the contrastive-distillation combination is effective. The authors fill this gap by establishing a recursive relationship (Lemma 1) between contrastive losses of consecutive models mediated by the distillation loss, then deriving upper and lower bounds on the final test loss in terms of all training losses (Theorem 1). The practical algorithm CILA then translates the theoretical insight that the distillation coefficient should adapt to data distribution into a simple ratio of accumulated distillation-to-contrastive losses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical guarantees provide the first principled explanation for why contrastive continual learning outperforms cross-entropy-based approaches, and the α^{T-t} decay in the bounds formally justifies why pre-training and strong initial-task performance disproportionately benefit the final model. However, the practical gains of CILA over Co²L are modest (roughly 1–2% across benchmarks), and the adaptive λ_t in practice is a simplified proxy for the theoretically motivated U_t threshold, since the true weights {k_tj} are inaccessible during training. Open questions include whether the bounds tighten with different contrastive loss formulations (e.g., InfoNCE with multiple negatives), whether the adaptive strategy transfers to non-replay settings, and whether the theory extends to deeper or transformer-based architectures.
