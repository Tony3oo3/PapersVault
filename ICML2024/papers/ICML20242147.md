## Problem

While performance variability in machine-learning models has received growing attention, the representational variability of latent-space models—how their embeddings change across architectures, hyperparameters, and datasets—remains poorly understood. This gap undermines interpretability (dissimilar latent spaces behind similar performance) and structural robustness (small parameter changes inducing large embedding shifts), yet no principled framework exists to systematically map and compare collections of latent spaces.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242147/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

PRESTO takes a latent-space model M and a dataset X (which need not be the training data, making the pipeline data-agnostic) and produces a persistence landscape—a piecewise-linear function in a Banach space—along with derived quantities: pairwise PRESTO distances between models, per-parameter sensitivity scores, outlier flags on landscape norms, and a compressed set of representative hyperparameter configurations. The pipeline operates on k-dimensional projections (k ≪ d) of the original d-dimensional embeddings, with k chosen to balance topological fidelity against computational cost, and supports both deterministic (PCA) and stochastic (random projection) dimensionality reduction.

## Method

PRESTO (Projected Embedding Similarity via Topological Overlays) is a four-step pipeline: embed data, project to low dimensions, compute persistence diagrams via α-complexes, and vectorize into persistence landscapes. The critical design choice is using persistence landscapes rather than raw persistence diagrams, because landscapes live in a Banach space, enabling standard statistical operations (means, variances, outlier detection) and efficient L^p-based distance metrics without discretization. Scalability is achieved by computing topology on low-dimensional projections rather than the full latent space, and the paper proves that the resulting topological loss is bounded (Theorem 3.8) and that PRESTO variance under projection is controlled (Theorem 3.9), with explicit bounds for JL projections, PCA, and metric MDS. On top of these primitives, the framework defines PRESTO sensitivity scores (individual, local, global) for quantifying hyperparameter robustness, a set-cover compression scheme for reducing search spaces by ~50%, and cross-dataset MMS comparisons to assess when hyperparameter knowledge transfer is topologically justified.

## Results

In the VAE hyperparameter multiverse (720 configurations across 3 architectures, 5 datasets, 2 sample sizes), INFOVAE exhibits the highest global representational variability (σ_‖L‖/μ_‖L‖ = 0.2670), compared to β-VAE (0.0876) and WAE (0.0556), confirming it is the least representationally stable architecture. PRESTO enables 50% compression of the VAE hyperparameter search space while guaranteeing each configuration is matched to a structurally similar representative. Training reduces structural variability from randomly initialized models (PS = 0.0188 untrained vs. PS = 0.0076 trained), and only β-VAE shows sufficient cross-dataset latent-space consistency to justify reusing hyperparameter knowledge across datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRESTO is compared against three existing measures of representational (dis)similarity. Against RTD (Barannikov et al., 2022), the closest topological competitor, Figure 3 shows no consistent Pearson correlation between PRESTO distances and RTD on random data, VAE embeddings, or LLM embeddings, indicating PRESTO captures a different aspect of latent-space structure. Against CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019), both RBF-kernel (kCKA) and linear (lCKA) variants also show no consistent relationship with PRESTO, reinforcing that PRESTO is orthogonal to kernel-based similarity. Against Mantel correlation (Mantel, 1967) for comparing multiverse metric spaces of transformer models, topological distances (Wasserstein and bottleneck) provide higher discriminatory power, clearly separating large language models (ADA, MISTRAL) from smaller sentence transformers. Unlike RTD, PRESTO handles unaligned embeddings, operates in a Banach space enabling statistical reasoning, and provides theoretical stability guarantees under projection.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRESTO sits at the intersection of two previously separate literatures: topological data analysis in machine learning (persistence diagrams, landscapes, and divergence measures like RTD and MTD from Barannikov et al.) and multiverse analysis originally developed in psychology to combat the replication crisis (Steegen et al.). The closest prior methods—RTD and MTD—perform pairwise comparisons of aligned embeddings using persistence diagrams, but they lack theoretical fidelity guarantees under projection, do not operate in a Banach space, and cannot handle unaligned data. PRESTO departs by using persistence landscapes (which live in a Banach space, enabling statistical reasoning about distributions of latent spaces) and by framing the problem as a multiverse analysis over algorithmic, implementation, and data choices, rather than a single pairwise comparison. The theoretical stability guarantees under Johnson-Lindenstrauss and MDS projections are novel contributions not present in prior topological comparison methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: while performance variability in ML models is well-studied, representational variability—the structural instability of latent spaces under small (hyper)parameter changes—remains poorly understood and directly impacts interpretability and robustness. The practical tools (50% hyperparameter-space compression, outlier detection, sensitivity scoring) offer concrete value for practitioners, and the finding that PRESTO captures variability orthogonal to performance metrics underscores that performance-driven evaluation alone is insufficient. Key open questions include how different pipeline choices (normalization, deterministic vs. random projections) interact and what they each capture, whether the framework extends to graph embeddings and internal network layers, and when hyperparameter knowledge transfer across datasets is topologically justified (the paper finds only β-VAE among three VAEs supports such transfer, challenging common practice). The absence of suitable baselines for sensitivity analysis and outlier detection in latent spaces limits the strength of empirical claims.
