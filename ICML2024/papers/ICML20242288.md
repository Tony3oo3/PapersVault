## Problem

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA and adapters already train only a small fraction of pretrained model parameters, but it remains unclear how far this reduction can be pushed and how much of the success is due to architectural inductive bias versus the inherent expressiveness of pretrained models. This paper asks whether a deliberately minimal, architecture-agnostic PEFT design—randomly freezing most parameters—can still match standard PEFT performance, thereby probing the true parameter budget needed for fine-tuning.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained model (OPT-125m/1.3b/13b, Llama2-7b, or CLIP ViT-B/16), a downstream fine-tuning dataset (SuperGLUE tasks, SQuAD, DROP, or image classification sets), and a single scalar mask ratio p ∈ [0.001%, 10%]. The mask is a fixed random binary tensor drawn once at initialization from Bernoulli(p) per parameter element; only unmasked entries are stored and updated via a compact sparse matrix S_i (coordinates + values as vectors), avoiding full-dense gradient computation. The output is a fine-tuned model evaluated by test accuracy or F1, with the critical hyperparameter being the learning rate, which must be tuned per (model, task, p) triple and can reach 1e-1 for the sparsest masks.

## Method

Random Masking applies an i.i.d. Bernoulli(p) mask to each weight tensor of a pretrained network, freezing all masked elements and training only the unmasked subset via sparse matrix updates. The key empirical finding is that sparser masks demand dramatically larger learning rates (up to 1e-1, which diverges standard PEFT), yet still converge to competitive accuracy. The paper explains this through two linked mechanisms: (1) masking shrinks the Hessian ℓ₂-norm, flattening the loss landscape and permitting aggressive step sizes without divergence; and (2) the flatter landscape pushes minimizers farther from initialization, so small learning rates underfit within a fixed step budget. Theoretically, for an overparameterized linear regression model, the authors prove via matrix concentration that eigenvalues of MXᵀXM concentrate around p·λᵢ(XᵀX), yielding a stable learning rate bound η < 2n/λ₁ that grows as p shrinks, and a lower bound on the norm of the GD solution that increases with sparsity—formalizing both the stability and the distance-to-solution effects observed empirically.

## Results

On the SuperGLUE benchmark (11 tasks) with OPT models, Random Masking at 0.1% trainable parameters achieves average scores of 62.06 (OPT-125m), 70.88 (OPT-1.3b), and 75.83 (OPT-13b), matching or exceeding full-parameter fine-tuning (62.36, 70.16, 75.82) and LoRA (62.04, 70.81, 75.86) while using 100–2000× fewer trainable parameters. Remarkably, even at 0.001% trainable parameters (~100× sparser than LoRA), Random Masking retains 75.15 average on OPT-13b, within 0.7 points of full fine-tuning. The key enabler is a learning rate up to 1e-1 for the sparsest masks, which would cause divergence in standard PEFT methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Random Masking at 0.1% trainable parameters matches or slightly exceeds LoRA across all three OPT scales (e.g., 70.88 vs. 70.81 on OPT-1.3b; 75.83 vs. 75.86 on OPT-13b), while using fewer parameters than LoRA at every scale. Against full-parameter fine-tuning, Random Masking at 0.1% is essentially tied (70.88 vs. 70.16 on OPT-1.3b; 75.83 vs. 75.82 on OPT-13b), and at 0.01% on OPT-13b it actually surpasses FT (76.06 vs. 75.82). Compared to Adapter, Prefix-Tuning, and BitFit (Table 5), Random Masking at 0.1% consistently achieves equal or higher average scores (e.g., on OPT-1.3b: 70.88 vs. Adapter 70.79, Prefix 70.06, BitFit 70.69). AdaLoRA is the closest competitor, reaching 76.35 on OPT-13b, but Random Masking at 0.01% (76.06) and 0.005% (76.17) remains within 0.3 points. The critical differentiator is that Random Masking achieves these results with a far simpler design (no low-rank structure, no adapter modules) and requires only a larger learning rate (up to 1e-1) rather than architectural modifications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes random masking—a technique previously dismissed as a weak baseline in PEFT literature—by connecting it to the lottery ticket hypothesis and neural network pruning. It departs from structured PEFT methods (LoRA, adapters, prefix-tuning) by introducing minimal inductive bias, and from intrinsic dimensionality analyses (Aghajanyan et al., 2020) by showing random masking achieves full fine-tuning accuracy rather than merely 90% of it. The theoretical analysis draws on overparameterized linear regression and matrix concentration bounds to formalize why sparser masks produce flatter Hessian spectra and permit larger stable learning rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 0.001% of parameters suffice for competitive fine-tuning (roughly 100× fewer than LoRA) reframes how we think about pretrained model expressiveness and suggests current PEFT methods carry substantial parameter redundancy. The practical implication—that sparse masking requires learning rates up to 1e-1, which would diverge standard methods—opens a new axis of optimization for fine-tuning. However, the authors explicitly caveat that results are on relatively simple fine-tuning tasks with small training sets (1000 samples), and Random Masking may fail on tasks requiring greater expressiveness; the connection to pruning and whether these insights transfer to pretraining or more complex adaptation remain open.
