## Problem

Computing Fréchet (Karcher) means of SPD covariance matrices in the high-dimensional regime (p/n → c > 0) is inconsistent when sample support n is small, and standard two-step pipelines (estimate each covariance, then average) inherit estimation errors. While RMT-corrected distances exist (Couillet et al., 2019), no method directly exploits them to estimate the Fréchet mean in a single step, and the prior RMT covariance estimator (Tiomoko et al., 2019) suffers from practical issues including an ad-hoc squared-distance objective and restricted search space.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw data matrices X_k ∈ R^{p×n} (not pre-computed covariance matrices) along with class labels for supervised Nearest Centroid or a target number of clusters Z for unsupervised K-Means. It outputs the RMT-corrected Fréchet mean Ĝ_RMT ∈ S_p^{++}, predicted class labels, or cluster assignments. A key design constraint is that the RMT correction requires the high-dimensional regime p/n → c > 0, and the method is most beneficial when K (number of matrices per class) is large relative to n, since the dependency of the running estimate on each individual X_k is counterbalanced by the other K−1 independent data matrices.

## Method

The core contribution replaces the standard squared Fisher distance in the Fréchet mean definition with the RMT-corrected squared Fisher distance (Theorem 2.1 from Couillet et al., 2019), yielding a one-step optimization over S_p^{++} solved by Riemannian gradient descent under the Fisher information metric. The gradient is derived via a chain-rule decomposition (Propositions 4.1–4.2) that maps the Riemannian gradient on S_p^{++} through the eigenvalue decomposition R^{-1/2}Ĉ_k R^{-1/2} = UΛU^T to a gradient on the diagonal manifold D_p^{++}, involving explicit formulas for matrices A, B, C encoding the RMT correction terms. For the covariance estimation sub-problem, the authors retain the unsquared RMT distance as the objective (unlike Tiomoko et al., 2019) and introduce a statistically motivated stopping criterion: halt when f(R) < −α/p, at which point the RMT estimator is no longer informative. The resulting RMT Fréchet mean is then plugged into Nearest Centroid (supervised) and K-Means (unsupervised) with the RMT-corrected distance used for both mean computation and assignment, with multiple random initializations and selection by maximum inertia.

## Results

On hyperspectral K-Means clustering, the RMT-corrected Fréchet mean achieves 77.7% accuracy and 63.1% mIoU on Salinas (p=5, 5×5 window), outperforming SCM (54.2%/38.2%) and LW (40.2%/25.2%) by 23.5 and 37.5 percentage points in accuracy, respectively. On Pavia, RMT reaches 81.9% accuracy versus 62.9% for SCM. On EEG Nearest Centroid classification, RMT achieves marginal gains over SCM (e.g., 63.8% vs. 63.2% on GrosseWentrup09, 60.3% vs. 59.7% on Schirmeister17), while LW and LW-NL underperform or fail numerically.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The linear Ledoit-Wolf (LW) shrinkage estimator (Ledoit & Wolf, 2004) is consistently outperformed by the RMT method on all hyperspectral datasets, with accuracy gaps ranging from 6.9 pp (Indian Pines p=24) to 37.5 pp (Salinas p=5). On EEG, LW also underperforms RMT on three of four datasets (e.g., 48.3% vs. 60.3% on Schirmeister17), likely because the true covariance matrices are far from the identity, making linear shrinkage toward I_p counterproductive. The non-linear Ledoit-Wolf (LW-NL) estimator (Ledoit & Wolf, 2020) is excluded from the hyperspectral experiments due to numerical instability (failing to produce valid SPD matrices), and on EEG it is either comparable to or worse than SCM (e.g., 62.6% vs. 66.6% on Lee19). Overall, RMT dominates both baselines in the high-K, moderate-n regime characteristic of hyperspectral data, while on EEG (large n relative to p) the advantage over SCM is minimal, consistent with the theoretical expectation that RMT corrections become negligible when sample size is sufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RMT-corrected squared Fisher distance framework of Couillet et al. (2019) from single-covariance estimation to the multi-matrix Fréchet mean problem, and refines the optimization approach of Tiomoko et al. (2019) by avoiding the squared-distance workaround, optimizing over the full SPD manifold rather than restricting to eigenvalues, and introducing a statistically-motivated stopping criterion. The key conceptual departure is treating the Fréchet mean as a one-step RMT-corrected optimization rather than the standard two-step pipeline (estimate each covariance, then compute the geometric mean), which is the dominant paradigm in the SPD matrix learning literature (Barachant et al., 2011; Tuzel et al., 2008). The method is positioned as a regularization strategy specifically tailored to barycenter computation, complementing rather than replacing classical shrinkage estimators like Ledoit-Wolf.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Fréchet mean of SPD matrices underpins widely used algorithms (Nearest Centroid, K-Means, Riemannian deep learning pooling), and this work provides a principled RMT-based alternative that provably improves estimation in the high-dimensional, low-sample regime where standard SCMs are inconsistent. A key open limitation is that the method degrades when the number of matrices K per class is very small, since the independence assumption underlying the RMT distance correction breaks down; the authors acknowledge this as the worst-case scenario. The finding that the state-of-the-art nonlinear Ledoit-Wolf estimator (LW-NL) is numerically unstable on real EEG data and underperforms SCM is practically important for practitioners choosing regularization strategies. Future work could explore whether the RMT correction extends to other Riemannian metrics (Bures-Wasserstein, log-Euclidean) or to differentiable Fréchet mean layers in deep networks.
