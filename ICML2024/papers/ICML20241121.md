## Problem

Adapting pre-trained language models to task-specific rewards typically requires expensive finetuning (RLHF, LoRA) or is limited to training-free prompting. The paper addresses the gap by asking whether a frozen LLM's internal representations already encode enough discriminative signal to select good completions, and whether a tiny trainable component can extract that signal without modifying the base model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241121/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt and requires only two forms of access to the base model: sampling completions from p_0(·|x) and extracting a fixed-dimensional embedding φ(x,a) of each prompt-completion pair (e.g., the 26th hidden layer of Code-LLaMA-7B, or an external embedding model for API-based systems). At inference, k candidate completions are drawn, scored by the Q-probe, and a single completion is selected via a temperature-scaled softmax over the k scores. The Q-probe itself is a single linear layer (4096-dim for Code-LLaMA-7B) trained in seconds, making it applicable to API models where no weight access exists.

## Method

Q-probe trains a linear value function Q_θ on the residual-stream embeddings of a frozen LLM, then uses it at inference to reweight k sampled completions via a softmax distribution. The key theoretical result shows that as k→∞, this sampling procedure converges to the optimal KL-constrained policy maximizing expected Q-value. For training, the paper introduces an importance-weighted policy gradient loss (L_PG) that directly optimizes the expected return of the softmax selection procedure by contrasting completions within the same prompt, rather than treating each sample independently as in standard reward regression. This contrastive structure mirrors the inference-time selection step and consistently outperforms MSE or cross-entropy reward modeling. The approach is composable: it can be stacked on top of few-shot prompting, KTO finetuning, or other adaptation methods for additive gains.

## Results

On MBPP (code generation with Code-LLaMA-7B), Q-probe with the policy gradient loss (L_PG) achieves 0.46 expected return, a 17% improvement over the base model's 0.29 pass@1, and outperforms SFT-on-successes (0.42) and few-shot prompting (0.42). Combining 5-shot prompting with Q-probe L_PG further boosts performance to 0.52 on MBPP and 0.39 on HumanEval. On human preference benchmarks (LLaMA-7B), Q-probe achieves a 50.10% GPT-4-judged win rate, outperforming offline PPO (44.07%) and DPO (44.97%), and combining KTO finetuning with Q-probe reaches 55.01%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On code generation (MBPP), Q-probe L_PG (0.46) outperforms SFT-on-successes from Singh et al. (0.42) and Dong et al. (0.42) by 4 percentage points, and also beats the LoRA-finetuned reward model baseline (Finetune RM, 0.34) by 12 points. On preference learning, Q-probe (50.10% win rate) clearly surpasses offline PPO (44.07%) and DPO (44.97%) by roughly 5–6 points as judged by GPT-4, while performing slightly below KTO (51.46%). However, stacking Q-probe on top of the KTO-finetuned model yields 55.01%, outperforming either method alone by 3.5–5 points, demonstrating that Q-probe is orthogonal to and composable with existing finetuning approaches. The paper also notes that Q-probe training takes only seconds (a 4096-dim linear model) versus hours for LoRA finetuning, though inference requires k=48 samples rather than a single greedy decode.

## Relation to Prior Work

Q-probe sits at the intersection of probing (Alain & Bengio, 2016), rejection sampling for reward maximization (Gao et al., 2023; Rafailov et al., 2023), and the generation-discrimination gap literature (Saunders et al., 2022). Unlike prior rejection-sampling baselines that require training a full reward model (e.g., a LoRA-finetuned LM), Q-probe replaces the scorer with a single linear layer on residual-stream embeddings, and unlike RLHF/DPO/PPO it leaves the base model entirely frozen. The theoretical contribution formalizes the softmax-over-k-samples procedure as a finite-k approximation to the KL-constrained optimal policy, and the importance-weighted policy gradient loss (L_PG) is a novel training objective that naturally reduces to a contrastive form matching the inference-time selection procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is strong: a 4096-dimensional linear probe trains in seconds, works on API-only models, and composes additively with prompting, SFT, and KTO finetuning. The finding that Q-probe outperforms LoRA finetuning in data-limited regimes (e.g., 50 prompts) suggests that the discrimination signal needed for reward maximization is already linearly accessible in pre-trained embeddings, reducing the adaptation problem to a lightweight calibration step. Open questions include whether the learned probe direction is task-specific or shared across tasks (connection to task vectors), whether iterative fine-tuning with the Q-probe-improved policy can bootstrap the discrimination capability further, and how the method scales when the base model is already near-saturated (as seen with the weaker API results).
