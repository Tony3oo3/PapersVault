## Problem

GNNs trained on small graphs suffer significant performance degradation when applied to substantially larger test graphs, and existing size-generalization methods (e.g., graph coarsening, causal invariance) fail to explicitly remove size information from learned representations. This results in sub-optimal generalization and tight coupling to specific backbone architectures, leaving a gap in how to cleanly disentangle size-related from task-related information in graph embeddings.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a graph's adjacency matrix and node feature matrix, then internally generates two augmented views per graph: a size-invariant view (same node count, altered label) and a task-invariant view (same label, reduced node count). Augmentation is guided by edge importance scores from a pre-trained explainable GNN (PGExplainer), and the final output is a task-related representation used for binary/multi-class graph classification, with size information explicitly decoupled into a separate representation that is discarded at test time.

## Method

DISGEN is a model-agnostic wrapper around any GNN backbone that creates size- and task-invariant augmented views using explainability-guided edge/node removal, then processes all three views through a shared backbone followed by two separate encoders producing size-related and task-related representations. A modified contrastive loss pulls size representations of same-size views together while pushing apart those of different-size views, and a supervision loss trains task representations on shared labels. The key novelty is a decoupling loss that maximizes the Frobenius-norm residual of the optimal linear projection from task to size hidden representations, with theoretical guarantees (Theorems 3.2–3.5) showing that a sufficiently large residual implies the two factors cannot be linearly coupled and thus are effectively decoupled. The overall objective combines the contrastive, supervision, and decoupling losses with tunable weights, and at inference only the task-related representation is used for prediction.

## Results

DISGEN achieves an average F1 improvement of up to 2.50% on small test graphs and up to 6.64% on large test graphs across four datasets (BBBP, PROTEINS, GraphSST2, NCI1) and three GNN backbones (GCN, GIN, Graph Transformer). On the GCN backbone, DISGEN reaches 92.92% F1 on BBBP small test and 85.63% on BBBP large test, outperforming the raw GCN baseline (90.79% / 76.01%). The method also generalizes to graphs up to 10× larger than training graphs, as demonstrated on REDDIT-BINARY (mean test size ~1320 nodes vs. ~119 training nodes) where GT+DISGEN achieves 63.19% F1 on large test versus 61.99% for the best baseline (GT+CIGAV2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISGEN is compared against four size-generalization baselines: SizeShiftReg (SSR), CIGAV2, RPGNN, and IRM, each paired with GCN, GIN, and Graph Transformer backbones. DISGEN consistently outperforms all four baselines in average F1 improvement on large test graphs (up to 6.64% over the raw backbone), with the gap being most pronounced on large test sets where size generalization is the primary challenge. SSR and CIGAV2, the two most competitive baselines, fail to improve size generalizability for the Graph Transformer backbone, whereas DISGEN still yields gains. IRM shows highly inconsistent performance across datasets (e.g., dropping to 40.60% on PROTEINS small test with GCN), while DISGEN maintains stable improvements. On the two larger real-world datasets (REDDIT-BINARY, FRANKENSTEIN), GT+DISGEN achieves the best large-test F1 (63.19% and 53.99% respectively), surpassing GT+CIGAV2 (61.99% and 54.29%) and GT+SSR (60.11% and 53.23%).

## Relation to Prior Work

DISGEN positions itself at the intersection of two research threads: size generalization in GNNs (addressed by SSR, CIGAV2, RPGNN, and IRM) and disentangled representation learning (from the broader ML literature on separating latent factors). It departs from prior size-generalization methods by explicitly removing size information from task representations via a decoupling loss based on linear projection residual, rather than relying on causal modeling, graph coarsening, or invariance regularization. The augmentation strategy borrows from explainable GNN models (PGExplainer) to generate size- and task-invariant views, a repurposing of interpretability tools for representation disentanglement that is novel in this context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework is model-agnostic and provides a practical recipe for improving GNN size generalization without modifying the backbone architecture, which is valuable given the computational constraints that often limit training to small graphs. The theoretical guarantees (Theorems 3.2–3.5) connecting the decoupling loss to actual factor separation are a meaningful contribution, though they rely on strong assumptions (d_h ≥ 2c+1, twice differentiability, uniform sampling). Open questions include whether the approach extends to other distribution shifts (e.g., feature distribution, graph topology), whether the pre-trained GNN dependency for augmentation can be eliminated, and whether the decoupling loss generalizes beyond the linear projection function class.
