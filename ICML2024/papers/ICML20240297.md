## Problem

Existing AI-generated image detectors suffer a significant accuracy drop (often to near-chance levels) when evaluating images produced by diffusion models not seen during training. This generalization failure is especially acute for substantially different model families (e.g., SDXL vs. SDv1.4) and for diffusion-reconstructed images that closely resemble real photos. The paper reframes this as a hard-sample classification problem: if a detector can separate near-real reconstructed images from true real images, it should generalize to easier, more visibly artificial samples from unseen generators.

## Input / Output

The detector consumes a single RGB image (resized to 224×224 during training; larger test images are center-cropped) and outputs a binary real/fake probability thresholded at 0.5. During training, the input batch contains four sample types — real images, real images passed through a diffusion reconstruction pipeline, generated images, and generated images passed through the same pipeline — with the first two labeled 'real' and the latter two labeled 'fake'. The reconstruction pipeline (VAE encode → DDIM denoise → VAE decode) is applied with a fixed number of inference steps (default 50) and an empty text prompt plus a zero mask for real-image reconstruction.

## Method

DRCT is a universal two-stage training framework that can be plugged into any existing binary detector (e.g., ConvNeXt-base or UnivFD's ViT-L/14). In the reconstruction stage, a chosen Stable Diffusion model (SDv1 or SDv2) reconstructs both real MSCOCO images and generated images via VAE encoding, DDIM denoising, and VAE decoding, producing hard samples that preserve visual content while embedding the diffusion model's spectral fingerprint. In the training stage, the detector is optimized with a combined objective: a margin-based contrastive loss (margin m=1.0) that pulls same-class feature pairs together and pushes cross-class pairs apart, plus a standard binary cross-entropy loss, balanced by λ=0.3. The contrastive loss operates on features from the penultimate layer, while the classification loss operates on the final logits. Ablations show that each component — real-image reconstruction, fake-image reconstruction, and the contrastive term — contributes incrementally, with the contrastive loss providing the largest single gain (~6.5% average ACC on GenImage).

## Results

On the DRCT-2M benchmark (16 Stable Diffusion variants), DRCT/Conv-B with SDv2 reconstruction achieves 96.55% average accuracy, and DRCT/UnivFD with SDv2 reaches 91.35%, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline UnivFD (83.46%). On the GenImage dataset, DRCT/UnivFD (SDv1) improves average accuracy from 79.45% (UnivFD alone) to 89.49%, a 10-point gain. On the real-world DRCT-2M-Wild dataset, DRCT/UnivFD (SDv2) achieves 96.90% average accuracy versus 63.55% for UnivFD without DRCT, demonstrating strong generalization to unseen models from CIVITAI and Midjourney.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRCT outperforms all eight baselines (CNNSpot, F3Net, CLIP/RN50, GramNet, De-fake, Conv-B, UnivFD, DIRE) on average accuracy across every evaluation dataset. The gap is most dramatic on unseen diffusion models: on SDXL, baselines drop to 53–67% while DRCT/Conv-B (SDv2) maintains 96.10%; on the DR (reconstruction) variants, baselines fall to ~50% (chance level) while DRCT/Conv-B (SDv2) still reaches 90.39%. UnivFD is the strongest non-DRCT baseline at 83.46% average on DRCT-2M, but DRCT/UnivFD (SDv2) lifts it to 91.35%. On the real-world DRCT-2M-Wild set, the advantage is even starker: DRCT/UnivFD (SDv2) scores 96.90% versus 63.55% for UnivFD and 58.89% for CNNSpot. DIRE, which relies on a single reconstruction model, shows extreme model-specificity (e.g., 99.87% on SDv2 but only 54.62% on LDM when using SDv2-DR), whereas DRCT maintains consistent performance across all 16 variants regardless of which reconstruction model is used during training.

## Relation to Prior Work

DRCT positions itself as a universal training framework that enhances existing generated-image detectors rather than proposing a new detection architecture. It builds on the Stable Diffusion / Latent Diffusion pipeline (Rombach et al., 2021) for the reconstruction stage and the margin-based contrastive loss of Hadsell et al. (2006) for the training stage, but repurposes both in a novel way: diffusion reconstruction is used not as a detection signal (as in DIRE) but as a data-augmentation mechanism to synthesize hard near-real samples. The conceptual departure from prior work is the hard-sample-classification framing—training on images that are visually almost indistinguishable from real ones forces the detector to learn subtle generative artifacts rather than coarse semantic or quality cues, which is argued to be the key to cross-model generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As new diffusion models (SDXL, LCM, Turbo variants, community LoRAs) appear continuously, the generalizability gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution detection is the central practical bottleneck; DRCT addresses this with a plug-and-train recipe that requires no architectural changes. The DRCT-2M benchmark (2M images across 16 model types plus a 136k real-world wild subset) fills a notable gap in evaluation infrastructure. Limitations acknowledged by the authors include weaker gains on GAN-generated images, evaluation restricted to global (not local) detection, and the open question of whether the framework can keep pace with rapidly evolving diffusion architectures. Future work on local forgery detection and interpretability of the learned features are identified as next steps.
