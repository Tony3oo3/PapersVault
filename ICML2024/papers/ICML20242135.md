## Problem

Extracting motions from videos for action recognition is challenging because motions lack an explicit form, encompass multiple physical concepts (displacement, velocity, acceleration), and are corrupted by noise from unstable pixels. Existing approaches either encode motion explicitly (e.g., optical flow, which is computationally expensive and captures only pixel displacement) or rely on RGB frames that retain large amounts of static redundancy. The paper asks whether a principled mathematical framework can implicitly capture dominant motions at multiple orders while suppressing noise and redundancy.

## Input / Output

The method takes a grayscale RGB video (or a human skeleton sequence) and produces a Taylor video where each frame is a 3-channel image encoding displacement, velocity, and acceleration, at the same spatial resolution as the input. A temporal sliding window of T frames (step size 1) generates N Taylor frames, with T−3 Taylor terms used per block; for transformer backbones, a grayscale frame is added to each channel to avoid near-zero patch embeddings. The Taylor video directly replaces RGB input in existing pre-trained 2D CNNs, 3D CNNs, and transformers without architectural modification, and the same computation applied to skeleton coordinates yields Taylor skeleton sequences.

## Method

The core mechanism defines an implicit motion-extraction function f that maps a frame to a pixel-level motion map, then approximates f via Taylor series expansion at the first frame of a temporal block, where the k-th derivative corresponds to the k-th order frame difference (1st-order = displacement, 2nd-order = velocity, 3rd-order = acceleration, etc.). Three separate motion-extraction functions (f_d, f_v, f_a) are defined so that their Taylor expansions produce the displacement, velocity, and acceleration channels of each Taylor frame; short-term and long-term motions are combined by averaging the Taylor approximation over all frames τ in the block. An equivalent tensor formulation (Eq. 7) enables efficient in-place matrix computation that is significantly faster than optical flow. The resulting 3-channel Taylor frames form a Taylor video that can be plugged into any RGB-pretrained model, and the same Taylor-series procedure applied to joint coordinates produces Taylor skeleton sequences that outperform raw skeletons across ST-GCN, InfoGCN, AGE-Ens, and 3Mformer backbones.

## Results

On HMDB-51 with I3D (Kinetics pretrained), Taylor video achieves 77.3% top-1 accuracy, matching optical flow and outperforming RGB (74.3%) by +3.0%. On CATER-moving, Taylor video with TSM (ImageNet pretrained) reaches 75.5% mAP, surpassing RGB (65.8%) by +9.7%. On MPII Cooking Activities, Taylor video with TSM achieves 50.1% mAP vs 46.7% for RGB. On large-scale Kinetics-400, Taylor video with TimeSformer (Kinetics pretrained) achieves 81.5% top-1 vs 80.7% for RGB, and with Swin Transformer reaches 73.5% vs 72.9% on HMDB-51.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GrayST (Kim et al., 2022), Taylor video with TSM (ImageNet pretrained) outperforms on all three available benchmarks: CATER static (83.1% vs 82.2%), CATER moving (75.5% vs 74.7%), and MPII (50.1% vs 48.7%). More broadly, Taylor video consistently surpasses RGB input across every model and dataset tested, with the largest gains on CATER-moving (+9.7% with TSM, +11.1% with TSN) and HMDB-51 under I3D ImageNet pretraining (+15.4%). Against optical flow (I3D, Kinetics pretrained), Taylor video matches on HMDB-51 (77.3%) and slightly exceeds it on CATER-moving (69.8% vs 66.3%) and MPII (52.3% vs 51.0%). When combined with RGB or optical flow, Taylor video yields further improvements (e.g., RGB+Taylor gives +22.1% over RGB alone under I3D), confirming its complementary nature as a distinct motion representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical Taylor series expansion as a principled framework for extracting motion from video temporal blocks, positioning itself against both simple frame differencing (Wang et al., 2018; 2021), which it shows is noisier than RGB, and optical flow, which it argues captures only pixel displacement rather than the full hierarchy of motion concepts (displacement, velocity, acceleration). It also distinguishes itself from single-frame temporal encodings like dynamic images (Bilen et al., 2016) and channel sampling (Kim et al., 2022) by offering a mathematically grounded, multi-order motion representation with controllable granularity. The method is deliberately architecture-agnostic, designed to slot into existing 2D/3D CNN and transformer backbones as a drop-in 3-channel input replacing RGB, and is further extended to skeleton sequences where it outperforms raw joint coordinates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Taylor videos offer a fast (in-place matrix operations, no iterative optimization), high-resolution, and mathematically interpretable motion representation that is complementary to both RGB and optical flow, making it a practical addition to multi-stream action recognition pipelines. The notable skeleton-sequence improvements (e.g., +4–5% on NTU-60 with ST-GCN) suggest the Taylor-transformed representation generalizes beyond pixel-level video to any temporal signal. Key limitations include the absence of static texture (ruling out tasks like video captioning), a training cold-start of ~20 epochs on small datasets, and the fact that transformer architectures were not originally designed for this input format. Open questions include adaptive frame sampling to reduce computation, dedicated network layers for Taylor video processing, and whether higher-order terms (jerk, snap) yield further gains without architectural changes.
