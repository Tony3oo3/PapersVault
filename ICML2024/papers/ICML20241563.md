## Problem

In supervised learning, model performance scales predictably with parameter count, but in deep RL, naively increasing network size often degrades final performance. This paper investigates whether Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) modules can unlock parameter scalability for value-based RL agents, asking whether performance improves as the number of experts (and thus parameters) grows.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241563/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent consumes 4 stacked 84×84 Atari frames, processed by a CNN encoder (either the original DQN 3-conv architecture or the Impala ResNet variant) into a feature tensor C^(h,w,d). This tensor is split into h×w tokens of dimensionality d (PerConv tokenization), which are routed through the MoE module. The network outputs Q-values for each discrete action in the environment's action set, with the MoE module replacing the penultimate dense layer (512 units) so that n experts yield n×512 parameters in that layer.

## Method

The paper replaces the penultimate dense layer of DQN and Rainbow with a Soft MoE module (Puigcerver et al., 2023), where each expert is a copy of the original layer and a single learned matrix Φ parameterizes both the dispatch weights (assigning weighted token mixes to expert input slots) and the combine weights (aggregating expert outputs back to tokens). This fully differentiable soft assignment contrasts with Top1-MoE's hard router that selects one expert per token. The key empirical finding is that Soft MoE's IQM performance increases monotonically with the number of experts (1→8 yields ~20% gain in Rainbow), while simply widening the penultimate layer by the same factor causes ~40% performance degradation. Analysis shows the gains stem from structured sparsity and stabilization of optimization dynamics (reduced dormant neurons, higher NTK rank) rather than raw parameter count, as scaling down expert dimensionality preserves performance and a random Φ eliminates the benefit.

## Results

On 20 Atari 2600 games (200M steps, 5 seeds, IQM metric), Soft MoE with 8 experts yields approximately a 20% performance improvement over the baseline Rainbow agent, while simply widening the penultimate layer by 8× causes a ~40% performance decrease. Top1-MoE provides modest gains in Rainbow but fails to scale with additional experts. The advantage of Soft MoE persists at high replay ratios and is confirmed across the full 60-game ALE suite. In offline RL (17 games, CQL and CQL+C51), Soft MoE attains the best aggregate final performance, and in the 50M-step low-data regime, Soft MoE improves both DrQ(ε) and DER over their baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard DQN and Rainbow baselines (Mnih et al. 2015; Hessel et al. 2018), Soft MoE with 8 experts delivers clear and scaling IQM improvements (~20% for Rainbow), whereas the parameter-matched baseline (widened penultimate layer) degrades by ~40%. Soft MoE consistently outperforms Top1-MoE (Shazeer et al. 2017, k=1): Top1-MoE provides only marginal gains in Rainbow and no gains in DQN, and critically fails to improve with more experts, while Soft MoE gains grow monotonically. Adding load-balancing losses (Ruiz et al. 2021) to Top1-MoE does not close the gap. In offline RL, Soft MoE + CQL (Kumar et al. 2020) and Soft MoE + CQL+C51 (Kumar et al. 2022) both attain the best aggregate final performance over 17 games, with Top1-MoE helping only CQL+C51. In the 50M-step low-data regime, Soft MoE improves both DrQ(ε) (Yarats et al. 2021) and DER (Van Hasselt et al. 2019), though no gains appear at the 100k-step extreme. On MuJoCo (SAC, PPO), Soft MoE shows neither gains nor degradation, while Top1-MoE degrades performance, suggesting the benefits are most pronounced with convolutional encoders on pixel-based tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Soft MoE mechanism from Puigcerver et al. (2023) — originally designed for scaling language and vision transformers — into value-based deep RL networks (DQN, Rainbow), replacing the penultimate dense layer with a Soft MoE module. It departs from prior RL scaling work that relied on algorithmic interventions (auxiliary losses, distillation, pre-training) by instead proposing an architectural change, and from earlier MoE-in-RL efforts (Ren et al., 2021; Akrour et al., 2022; Hendawy et al., 2024) that used different MoE formulations (orthogonal, probabilistic, interpretable experts). The analysis draws on the dormant neuron and parameter under-utilization literature (Sokar et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2021a) to explain why Soft MoE's structured sparsity stabilizes optimization dynamics in ways that naive layer widening does not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first strong empirical evidence that parameter scaling laws can hold for deep RL, a long-standing gap relative to supervised learning, by showing that Soft MoE yields consistent, scale-dependent performance gains across multiple agents, encoders, and training regimes (online, offline, low-data). The finding that soft assignment (Soft MoE) outperforms hard routing (Top1-MoE) even with load-balancing losses suggests the weighted token-to-expert mixing is the critical mechanism, not sparsity alone. Key limitations include the restriction to value-based agents on pixel-based Atari environments (MuJoCo/PPO/SAC show no gains), and the analysis of *why* MoEs stabilize training (NTK rank, dormant neurons) remains correlational rather than causal. Open questions include whether these benefits extend to policy-based agents, continuous control, and whether different k values, tokenization schemes, or router-specific optimizers can further improve results.
