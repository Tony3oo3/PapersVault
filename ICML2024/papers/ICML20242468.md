## Problem

Binarizing image super-resolution networks to 1-bit causes severe performance degradation because direct weight binarization hinders high-frequency information extraction, and activation binarization destroys the pixel-wise detail that SR networks must reconstruct. Existing binary SR methods lack mechanisms to recover the representation capacity lost during quantization or to align the internal feature content between the binarized and full-precision models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242468/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a full-precision (32-bit) low-resolution RGB image and produces a full-precision high-resolution RGB image at ×2 or ×4 scale. Only the body (computationally intensive) part of the network is binarized to 1-bit weights and activations, while the head and tail layers remain at full precision. The binarized body uses bitwise XNOR and bitcount operations for convolution, and the final output is a standard dense pixel-wise reconstruction.

## Method

FRB combines two components: (1) Second-order Residual Binarization (SRB), which binarizes not only the original weights but also the residual between full-precision and first-order binarized weights, yielding two 1-bit terms whose sum approximates the continuous weight more faithfully (o = sign(a)⊗B_w1 + sign(a)⊗B_w2); and (2) Distillation-guided Binarization Training (DBT), which constructs L2-normalized squared block-level representations for both the binarized and full-precision networks and minimizes their L2 distance to uniformly align internal feature content across bit-widths. The total loss combines a pixel-wise L1 reconstruction term with the DBT distillation term (weighted by β=1e-4). The approach is architecture-generic and is applied to both a CNN backbone (SRResNet → FRBC) and a Transformer backbone (SwinIR_S → FRBT), with self-ensemble variants (FRBC+, FRBT+) for further gains.

## Results

On the CNN-based SRResNet backbone (×4), FRBC achieves 31.83 dB PSNR on Set5, 28.39 dB on Set14, 27.41 dB on B100, 25.61 dB on Urban100, and 29.71 dB on Manga109, outperforming the closest competitor BBCU-L (31.79/28.38/27.41/25.62/29.69) and surpassing ReActNet by ~0.29 dB on Set5. With self-ensemble (FRBC+), the gains increase to 31.99 dB on Set5 and 29.96 dB on Manga109, closing the gap to full-precision SRResNet (32.16 dB) to within 0.17 dB. For the Transformer-based SwinIR_S backbone (×4), FRBT achieves 31.79 dB on Set5 and 25.55 dB on Urban100, with FRBT+ reaching 31.92 dB and 25.65 dB respectively. Compression ratios reach ~83.5% for FRBC and ~89.6% for FRBT in parameters, with ~78.2% and ~93.1% in operations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FRBC consistently outperforms all 1-bit binarized baselines on the SRResNet backbone across all five test datasets at both ×2 and ×4 scales. Against the strongest competitor BBCU-L, FRBC gains 0.04 dB on Set5 (×4) and 0.40 dB on Manga109 (×2), while FRBC+ extends these to 0.20 dB and 0.73 dB respectively. The advantage over ReActNet is more pronounced at ×4, with FRBC leading by 0.29 dB on Set5 and 0.46 dB on Manga109. Compared to earlier methods like DoReFa, Bi-Real, and BNN, FRBC achieves substantially larger margins of 1.5–4.3 dB PSNR, confirming that second-order residual binarization and distillation-guided training are critical for pixel-wise SR reconstruction. Notably, FRBC+ narrows the gap to full-precision SRResNet to just 0.17 dB on Set5 (×4) and 0.50 dB on Manga109 (×4), demonstrating that the binarized model retains most of the full-precision reconstruction quality. For the Transformer backbone, FRBT achieves 31.79 dB on Set5 (×4) versus SwinIR_S's 32.44 dB, a 0.65 dB gap that the authors note is larger than the CNN case, highlighting the greater difficulty of Transformer binarization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the binarized neural network (BNN) line of work for image super-resolution, building on foundational 1-bit quantization methods (XNOR-Net, BNN, DoReFa) and SR-specific binarization approaches (BAM, BTM, BBCU). Its key conceptual departure is the second-order residual binarization (SRB), which treats the binarization error itself as a signal to be further binarized—a residual-learning idea repurposed for the quantization domain rather than for network depth. The distillation-guided training (DBT) draws on knowledge distillation and the real-to-binary convolution framework of Martinez et al. (2020), but introduces a normalized attention form specifically designed to align block-level representations across bit-widths. Unlike prior binarized SR work that focused almost exclusively on CNNs, this paper also investigates Transformer-based SR binarization (SwinIR), carving new ground in that architectural setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic investigation of 1-bit binarization for Transformer-based SR networks, revealing that the performance gap to full-precision is larger than for CNNs and opening a concrete research direction. The SRB technique is simple and architecture-agnostic, potentially applicable to other low-bit quantization tasks beyond SR. However, the practical speedup remains unverified (theoretical compression ≠ real-time acceleration), and the authors acknowledge that binarization alone hits a compression ceiling for large models, suggesting that joint pruning-quantization strategies will be needed. The modest but consistent gains over the strongest baseline (BBCU-L) and the marginal standalone contribution of DBT temper the practical impact, positioning this as a useful stepping stone rather than a definitive solution for deployable binary SR.
