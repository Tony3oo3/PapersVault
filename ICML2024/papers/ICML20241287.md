## Problem

Standard alignment approaches treat honesty and helpfulness as independent objectives, yet in everyday conversation they are frequently in tension (e.g., rounding time, telling white lies). No prior work has formally measured how LLMs navigate these trade-offs in ambiguous conversational settings or compared their latent value weightings to human behavior using controlled psychological paradigms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241287/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

LLMs receive a scenario prompt (mushroom foraging, housing, or dining) specifying feature values as the world state, plus either a free choice among 30 candidate utterances (Exp 1) or a single pre-specified utterance to endorse or reject (Exps 2–3). Responses are constrained to a strict 4-word format ('[Feature] is worth [value]') or a binary say/silent decision, with 30 samples per trial and randomized feature ordering to control for position bias. The fitted outputs include the utility weight λ (honesty–helpfulness trade-off), speaker optimality β_S, and endorsement curves plotting utterance probability against its truthfulness and decision-theoretic helpfulness.

## Method

The paper adapts the signaling bandits paradigm from cognitive psychology—a social variant of contextual bandits where a speaker produces utterances to inform a listener's action choice—to evaluate LLMs. Honesty and helpfulness are formalized as Gricean maxims (Quality and Relevance) within the Rational Speech Acts framework, where the speaker's utility is a convex combination U_Combined = λ·U_Helpfulness + (1−λ)·U_Honesty, and the listener is modeled as a noisy-rational agent whose expected reward defines helpfulness. The authors fit this psychological model to LLM responses via grid search over λ, β_S, and β_L, and use Bayes factors to test whether chain-of-thought prompting shifts the inferred trade-off toward helpfulness. Three experiments progressively tighten constraints: open-ended utterance selection (Exp 1), binary endorsement under direct honesty–helpfulness conflict (Exp 2), and realistic cover stories (Exp 3), testing seven models across RLHF, CoT, and value-prompting conditions to isolate how training, prompting, and context each bias the trade-off.

## Results

In Experiment 1 (utterance selection), GPT-4 and GPT-4 Turbo achieve 100% truthfulness and ~90% helpfulness with fitted utility weight λ ≈ 0.32–0.35, while smaller models (LLaMA 2 70B, Mixtral 8x7B) show 41–63% truthfulness and 51–61% helpfulness with λ ≈ 0.55–0.68. RLHF strictly improves both honesty and helpfulness for both LLaMA 2 and Mixtral (e.g., LLaMA 2 70B: truthfulness 41%→59%, helpfulness 51%→74%). Chain-of-thought prompting shifts λ strongly toward helpfulness in 4 of 7 models (e.g., LLaMA 2: λ 0.56→0.80; Mixtral Instruct: λ 0.50→0.85), with Bayes factors exceeding 10^15 for the hypothesis λ_CoT > λ_Default. In Experiments 2–3 (endorsement paradigm), GPT-4 Turbo with CoT uniquely displays human-like steerability: it refuses false utterances under neutral prompts but endorses them as helpfulness increases under a helpfulness prompt, matching the pattern observed in human participants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary behavioral baseline is human participant data from Sumers et al. (2023), who used the same signaling bandits paradigm with a slider between 'say the utterance' and 'stay silent.' GPT-4 Turbo with chain-of-thought prompting most closely matches human endorsement curves: under a neutral prompt, both humans and GPT-4 Turbo are highly sensitive to the helpfulness of true utterances (endorsing them only when decision-theoretic utility is high) while refusing false utterances regardless of helpfulness. Under a helpfulness prompt, both humans and GPT-4 Turbo become willing to endorse false utterances as their helpfulness increases, with GPT-4 Turbo's curves (Fig. 4, rows B–C) closely tracking the human curves (Fig. 4, row A). In the more realistic housing setting (Experiment 3), GPT-4 Turbo's steerability pattern is even more human-like, responding correctly to both honesty and helpfulness prompts. In contrast, smaller models (GPT-3.5, LLaMA 2, Mixtral) fail to show this steerability: pre-RLHF Mixtral and LLaMA do not respond to prompt changes at all, and post-RLHF Mixtral paradoxically becomes less truthful when prompted for honesty. The key differentiator is that only GPT-4 Turbo with CoT achieves the combination of high baseline honesty (λ ≈ 0.06–0.20 under neutral/CoT in Exp 2) and responsive re-weighting under value-specific prompts, mirroring the human pattern of a default honesty bias that can be overridden by explicit helpfulness instructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the psychological experimental paradigm and Rational Speech Acts (RSA) formalization from Sumers et al. (2023), which characterized how humans trade off truthfulness and relevance in communication, by applying the same signaling bandits paradigm and WebPPL model-fitting procedure to LLMs. It builds on the Gricean maxims framework (Grice, 1975) and the RSA speaker-listener model (Frank & Goodman, 2012; Goodman & Frank, 2016) to formalize honesty and helpfulness as competing utility functions. Unlike prior LLM alignment work that measures honesty (e.g., TruthfulQA) and helpfulness independently via benchmarks, this paper positions them as a trade-off to be quantified via a single λ parameter, and is the first to compare LLM trade-off behavior directly to human data from the same paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first controlled, quantitative measurement of how LLMs weight honesty versus helpfulness, revealing that RLHF improves both values while CoT skews toward helpfulness at the cost of honesty—a finding with direct implications for how reasoning-augmented models should be deployed. The demonstration that GPT-4 Turbo is steerable via simple zero-shot prompts toward different value weightings suggests a practical mechanism for aligning conversational behavior without retraining. Key limitations include the highly controlled (mushroom/housing) domains that may not generalize to open-ended conversation, and the acknowledged difficulty of meaningful human-LLM comparison given different social and physical groundings. Open questions include whether these trade-off patterns hold in multi-turn dialogue, with adversarial prompting, or across the full spectrum of conversational values beyond honesty and helpfulness.
