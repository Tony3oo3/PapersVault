## Problem

Classic randomized low-diameter decomposition (LDD) procedures for planar graphs, such as the KPR algorithm, guarantee that any pair of vertices is separated with probability O(ρ_uv) but provide no corresponding lower bound. The paper shows this upper bound is vacuous in practice: for any distance d, there exist planar graphs where some pair at distance d is never separated, meaning pairs at comparable distances can be split with wildly different probabilities. The paper asks whether LDDs can be designed to satisfy individual fairness—pairs at comparable distances should be separated with comparable probability—while preserving natural desiderata like cluster connectivity and optimal compression.

## Input / Output

The algorithms take a planar graph G(V,E) with unweighted shortest-path distances and an integer radius parameter R, and produce a distribution over partitions of V into connected clusters of diameter O(R). The key output property is that for every vertex pair (u,v), the separation probability is sandwiched between a lower bound g(ρ_uv) and an upper bound f(ρ_uv) = O(ρ_uv), where ρ_uv = d(u,v)/R. Different algorithms in the paper achieve different lower bounds g(ρ) by relaxing connectivity (CON) or compression (COMP) guarantees, with the strongest bound Ω(ρ/√(log R)) requiring neither property.

## Method

The paper presents a family of four algorithms that progressively trade off connectivity and compression for stronger individual fairness guarantees. Algorithm 2 modifies KPR by assigning random binary weights to vertices at the cut level, cutting above or below each vertex based on its weight, yielding Θ(1/R) separation probability for edges. Algorithm 3 (the most technically intricate) uses two cut levels per phase where the second offset k2 is drawn from a power-law distribution; its proof shows that if a pair at distance d ≥ 59 is not separated with probability Ω(ε²·ρ^{2+ε}), then a K_{3,3} minor must exist in the graph, contradicting planarity. Algorithm 4 samples the radius parameter from a continuous distribution rather than fixing it, trading optimal compression for a g(ρ) = Ω(ε·ρ^{1+ε}) bound. Algorithm 5 embeds the planar graph into ℓ₂ via Rao's algorithm, projects onto a random line, and cuts at random thresholds, achieving g(ρ) = Ω(ρ/√(log R)) but sacrificing both connectivity and compression. A key lower-bound result ties the optimal individual fairness guarantee (f(ρ)/g(ρ) = O(1) with f(ρ) = O(ρ)) to the open problem of constant-distortion ℓ₁ embeddings of planar graphs.

## Results

The paper establishes a hierarchy of individual fairness guarantees for randomized planar graph decompositions. Algorithm 2 (Theorem 1.3) achieves edge-level individual fairness with separation probability in [1/(2R), 3/R], a factor-of-6 fairness bound. Algorithm 3 (Theorem 1.5) extends this to all distances with g(ρ) = Ω(ε²·ρ^(2+ε)) while preserving both connectivity and optimal compression. Relaxing constraints yields stronger bounds: Algorithm 4 gives g(ρ) = Ω(ε·ρ^(1+ε)) without (COMP), and Algorithm 5 gives g(ρ) = Ω(ρ/√(log R)) without either (CON) or (COMP). Empirically on R=5 precinct graphs, Algorithm 2 produces clusters with maximum diameter far below the theoretical 43R=215 bound, and the number of clusters is consistently below n/R.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Algorithm 2 (KPR with random vertex weights) against Algorithm 1 (standard KPR) on three real-world precinct graphs (North Carolina, Maryland, Pennsylvania) with R=5. Algorithm 2 is significantly more individually fair than Algorithm 1: the histogram of edge separation probabilities (Figure 2) shows Algorithm 2 concentrates probabilities tightly within the theoretical [0.1, 0.6] range, while Algorithm 1 exhibits a much wider and more skewed distribution. Notably, even a strengthened version of Algorithm 1 that uses uniformly random BFS roots (rather than adversarial ones) remains less fair than Algorithm 2, confirming that the unfairness is inherent to the KPR structure rather than a root-selection artifact. Theoretically, Theorem 1.2 proves that for any planar graph and any distance d ≤ √n/3, KPR has pairs at distance d that are never separated (g(ρ)=0), while Algorithm 2 guarantees every edge is separated with probability at least 1/(2R).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classic KPR low-diameter decomposition framework (Klein et al., 1993) for planar graphs by introducing individual fairness as a new desideratum on separation probabilities. It departs from prior LDD work (Bartal 1996; Fakcharoenphol et al. 2004) which only guarantees upper bounds on separation probability O(ρ_uv), by demanding matching lower bounds g(ρ_uv) so that comparable-distance pairs are separated with comparable probability. The paper also draws on the individual fairness concept from fair machine learning (Dwork et al., 2012) and pairwise fair k-center clustering (Brubach et al., 2020), but applies it to the distinct setting of randomized graph partitioning rather than classification or k-center assignment. Algorithm 5 additionally leverages Rao's (1999) Euclidean embedding of planar graphs, connecting the fairness analysis to the broader metric embedding literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The negative result that the standard KPR algorithm can assign zero separation probability to adjacent vertex pairs (Theorem 1.2) reveals a fundamental blind spot in the most widely-used planar graph decomposition tool, with direct implications for applications like redistricting where fair random sampling of maps is needed. Theorem 1.4's reduction showing that optimal individual fairness (Θ(ρ) bounds) would imply constant-distortion ℓ₁ embeddings of planar graphs ties the problem to a major open question in metric geometry, suggesting the gap between current bounds and the ideal is not merely a technical artifact. Open questions include whether the trade-off bounds in Table 1 are tight for planar graphs, how to define meaningful (CON) and (COMP) analogs for general metric spaces, and whether the fairness-preserving compression can serve as a practical preprocessing step for complex optimization problems like equal-population redistricting.
