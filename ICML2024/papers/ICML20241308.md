## Problem

Public models trained to minimize prediction error (e.g., MSE) can produce highly uneven decision costs across heterogeneous downstream agents who use the same predictions for different business objectives. Existing decision-focused learning methods address only a single agent or task, leaving no mechanism to ensure performance equity when a shared model serves many agents with distinct cost functions and data distributions. This paper asks how to train a public model so that the resulting cost regret is distributed more uniformly across all downstream agents.

## Input / Output

The public model consumes time-series energy features (state-level fuel mix, cloud workload demands, or EV charging patterns) along with agent-specific context variables ξ_m (e.g., location, λ weights) and outputs a prediction ŷ (carbon emission rate or combined carbon-water-price efficiency E_t). Unlike standard prediction tasks, the output is not the final answer: each of M downstream agents applies its own policy π_m to ŷ and ξ_m to produce an action â_m, incurring an agent-specific cost. Input features may differ across agents (e.g., different geographic locations), and the number of samples N_m per agent can be heterogeneous.

## Method

The core contribution is an Equitable Objective L^q(θ) = Σ_m [(1/N_m)Σ_i C_{m,i}]^{q+1}, an α-fairness-inspired loss over per-agent average cost regret that assigns higher relative weight to worse-performing agents as q increases. For differentiable cost functions, the gradient is computed via standard backpropagation through the chain rule (∇_θ L^q = Σ ∇_C L^q · ∇_â C · ∇_ŷ â · ∇_θ ŷ). For non-differentiable costs (e.g., combinatorial scheduling), the method employs a policy gradient algorithm: a probabilistic public model σ_θ(ŷ|x) samples predictions per batch, and the log-derivative trick yields a batch-level gradient that couples all agents' regrets through the non-separable (·)^{q+1} term. Theoretical results prove that increasing q monotonically reduces variance and increases entropy of the performance distribution, and a generalization bound is established via dual-norm equivalence of the equitable loss.

## Results

On the data center carbon efficiency task (50 agents), EQUITABLE PM reduces cost regret variance by up to 91% (from 0.0602 to 0.0055) and C95−C5 percentile spread by 70% (from 0.7717 to 0.2331) compared to the Plain PM in the most heterogeneous setting (different agents, different λ). The mean cost regret also drops substantially (e.g., 0.4779 → 0.1444). On the EV charging scheduling task (70 agents), EQUITABLE PM similarly achieves lower variance and C95−C5 across both similar and different agent distributions. Increasing the fairness parameter q consistently tightens the cost regret distribution, with MSE remaining within ~5% of the Plain PM baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the Plain PM, a standard public model trained to minimize MSE prediction error without incorporating downstream decision costs. EQUITABLE PM dominates Plain PM on all three fairness metrics (variance, mean cost regret, and C95−C5 percentile spread) across every experimental setting. The gap is most pronounced in the heterogeneous setting (different agents, different λ), where EQUITABLE PM (q+1=10) reduces variance from 0.0602 to 0.0055 (91% reduction) and C95−C5 from 0.7717 to 0.2331 (70% reduction). The trade-off is a modest MSE increase (e.g., 4.3819 vs. 4.2013 in the most heterogeneous setting, ~4% higher), which the authors argue is acceptable given the substantial improvement in equitable decision outcomes. The EV charging application (70 agents, Transformer-based public model) confirms the same pattern: EQUITABLE PM consistently yields lower variance and C95−C5 than Plain PM for both similar and different agent distributions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the α-fairness framework from network resource allocation (Altman et al., 2008; Li et al., 2020) and transplants it into the decision-focused learning literature, which has so far addressed only single-agent or single-task settings (Wilder et al., 2020; Johnson-Yu et al., 2023). It also departs from the dominant ML fairness paradigm that targets accuracy balance across protected demographic groups (Barocas et al., 2023; Pessach & Shmueli, 2022), instead defining fairness as uniformity of downstream decision-cost regret across heterogeneous agents with distinct optimization objectives. The 'good-intent' fairness in federated learning (Mohri et al., 2019) is the closest prior concept but is limited to worst-case device performance without modeling agent-specific decision processes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As foundation models and shared prediction services proliferate, the question of how a single upstream model should be trained when it serves many downstream stakeholders with conflicting objectives becomes increasingly practical. The paper's main limitation is that it requires access to downstream decision costs during training, raising privacy and adversarial-robustness concerns the authors acknowledge but do not resolve. Open questions include scaling to large foundation-model architectures, extending to domains like healthcare, and whether fine-tuning pre-trained models can achieve similar equity guarantees without full retraining.
