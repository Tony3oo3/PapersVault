## Problem

SAM's loss-sharpness trade-off is suboptimal: naively increasing the neighborhood size ρ improves sharpness but inflates training loss, and performing multiple ascent steps (Multistep-SAM) empirically degrades performance due to gradient instability from departing far from the original solution. The paper asks how to exploit multiple ascent steps to find a higher-loss worst-case perturbation while stabilizing the subsequent descent step.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241475/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Lookbehind is a wrapper around SAM or ASAM that takes the current model parameters φ, a sampled minibatch, and hyperparameters (inner steps k, outer step size α, neighborhood size ρ) and produces updated parameters via a single outer update per iteration. Within each iteration, k ascent steps are performed sequentially on the same minibatch, accumulating perturbed weights and descent gradients, before a single linear-interpolation update is applied to the slow weights. The method adds two hyperparameters (k and α) on top of SAM/ASAM's existing ρ and η, though an adaptive α variant removes the need to tune α.

## Method

Lookbehind decouples SAM's maximization and minimization by performing k gradient ascent steps (looking behind) to find a worst-case perturbation with higher loss than single-step SAM, then stabilizing the descent via linear interpolation between slow weights and fast weights. The fast weights are updated at each inner step using the gradient of the loss at the perturbed point, while the slow weights remain fixed during the inner loop; after k steps, the slow weights move toward the final fast weights by a factor α. This aggregation of gradients across ascent steps reduces variance compared to Multistep-SAM (which uses only the last gradient) and Lookahead-SAM (which stabilizes sequential descent across iterations rather than ascent within one iteration). An adaptive α* is computed from the cosine similarity between the first and final gradient directions, automatically scaling the interpolation to the degree of gradient alignment without hyperparameter tuning.

## Results

Lookbehind-ASAM achieves the best generalization performance across all five model-dataset combinations: 96.54% on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-34), 95.23% on CIFAR-10 (WRN-28-2), 78.86% on CIFAR-100 (ResNet-50), 82.16% on CIFAR-100 (WRN-28-10), and 70.23% on ImageNet (ResNet-18). Lookbehind-SAM similarly leads with 96.27%, 94.81%, 78.62%, 80.99%, and 70.16% respectively. Notably, Lookbehind is the only method that outperforms both vanilla SAM and ASAM on ImageNet, where most other variants (Multistep, Lookahead) fail to improve over the baselines. In lifelong learning, ER+Lookbehind-SAM achieves 68.87% average accuracy and 12.37 forgetting on Split-CIFAR100, and Lookbehind-C-MAML reaches 67.15% average accuracy with 12.40 forgetting, both surpassing their respective Lookahead counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla SAM, Lookbehind-SAM consistently wins: 96.27% vs 95.80% on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-34), 78.62% vs 76.57% on CIFAR-100 (ResNet-50), and 70.16% vs 70.01% on ImageNet (ResNet-18). Against ASAM, Lookbehind-ASAM similarly outperforms: 96.54% vs 96.32% on CIFAR-10, 78.86% vs 78.62% on CIFAR-100, and 70.23% vs 70.15% on ImageNet. Compared to Multistep-SAM/ASAM (which simply uses the last-step gradient after k ascent steps), Lookbehind achieves clear gains on every dataset, with the largest margin on CIFAR-100 ResNet-50 (78.62% vs 77.03% for SAM, 78.86% vs 77.81% for ASAM). Lookbehind also outperforms Lookahead-SAM/ASAM across all settings, where Lookahead's variance-reduction mechanism fails to help and sometimes degrades performance (e.g., 76.16% vs 78.62% for Lookahead-SAM vs Lookbehind-SAM on CIFAR-100). The most striking finding is that Lookbehind is the only method to beat both SAM and ASAM on ImageNet, where all other variants (Multistep, Lookahead, gradient averaging) either match or fall below the baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SAM/ASAM line of work by addressing a known failure mode: naively performing multiple ascent steps (Multistep-SAM) degrades performance due to gradient instability, and wrapping SAM with the Lookahead optimizer (Lookahead-SAM) fails to recover the lost sharpness gains. Lookbehind-SAM repurposes Lookahead's slow/fast weight mechanism and linear interpolation, but applies it to the *maximization* side of SAM's minimax objective rather than the minimization side, performing k ascent steps within a single iteration and interpolating the resulting fast weights back toward the slow weights. The closest concurrent work (Kim et al., 2023) averages gradients across ascent steps, but Lookbehind decouples the inner step count k from the outer interpolation coefficient α, enabling a richer hyperparameter space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Lookbehind provides a practical, drop-in wrapper that consistently improves SAM and ASAM across generalization, noisy-weight robustness, and lifelong learning without requiring re-tuning of ρ, making it immediately useful for practitioners already using sharpness-aware training. The k× computational overhead remains a significant limitation for large-scale training, though the minibatch-switching variant and adaptive α partially mitigate this. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large models (beyond ViT-Base fine-tuning), whether it composes with more efficient SAM variants (e.g., SAM-free, R-SAM), and whether the early-training basin-shift effect observed in Figure 8 can be exploited for curriculum-style training schedules.
