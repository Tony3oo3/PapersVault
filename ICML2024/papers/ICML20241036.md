## Problem

Existing multi-agent LLM research imposes human-designed roles, relationships, and task scopes on AI collectives, preventing them from organically developing unique cultures, perspectives, and social norms. This paper asks whether free-formed AI collectives—where agents autonomously choose partners, topics, and actions without pre-assigned structure—can spontaneously generate functional diversity beyond human variety and develop self-reinforcing pro-social norms that increase robustness against toxic or malicious behavior.

## Input / Output

The system takes 10 homogeneous LLM agents (Claude-2.1, Claude-3, GPT-4-Turbo, or Gemini Pro) and places them in a shared virtual platform with a three-step per-round protocol: agents send chat invitations, accept or reject incoming invitations, and engage in open-ended pairwise conversations until one party leaves. During the 30-round 'cocktail party' phase, no task is assigned—agents freely choose topics and partners. Downstream evaluations then feed the emergent collective into a sentence-construction task (seven disparate words, max 40 words per sentence, five sentences per question) and a Public Goods Game ($100 endowment, 1.3× multiplier, equal split), producing metrics on semantic embedding variance, valid-answer ratios, and contribution values across infection cascades.

## Method

The core mechanism is a 'cocktail party' simulation in which 10 identical LLM agents interact over 30 rounds with zero human-imposed structure: no roles, no objectives, no pre-assigned relationships. Each round follows a three-step protocol (invitation → acceptance → pairwise chat), and agents are free to choose any partner and any topic. The paper measures emergent structure through two network metrics (distinct agent conversation/invitation ratios over rolling 10-round windows) and semantic divergence via OpenAI text-embedding-3-large projections of conversation texts, distinguishing 'tight pairs' (>5 conversations) from 'loose pairs.' Three downstream experiments evaluate the collective: (1) a sentence-construction task comparing individual, free-formed collective, and strategically 'bridged' collective (pairing most distanced agents) on embedding variance and valid-answer ratio; (2) a Public Goods Game testing baseline cooperation levels; and (3) a poisoning experiment where a malicious agent contributes $0, and the paper tracks first- and second-order infection cascades through triads of agents in both collective and non-collective settings. The key finding is that free-formed interaction 'cross-programs' homogeneous agents into heterogeneous clusters with divergent perspectives while simultaneously building trust-based norms that dampen the spread of anti-social behavior.

## Results

In the sentence-construction task, the free-formed AI collective achieves a valid answer ratio of 0.947 versus 0.823 for a single agent (difference = 0.124, p < 0.001), while the strategically bridged collective reaches 0.932 (difference = 0.109, p < 0.001). Semantic diversity (variance of sentence embeddings) rises from 0.078 (individual) to 0.091 (collective) and 0.110 (bridged), with the bridged condition showing a statistically significant gain over the individual (difference = 0.032, p = 0.001). In the Public Goods Game, collective agents contribute $87.8–$97 on average versus $72.5 for non-collective agents, and first-order infection reduces contributions by only $13.25–$20.25 in collectives compared to $38.5 in non-collective settings (p < 0.001 for both differences).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three conditions: a solitary LLM agent (Individual), a free-formed AI collective with self-determined pairing (Collective), and a strategically bridged collective where the most semantically distant agents are forced to interact (Bridged). On answer quality, both collective conditions significantly outperform the individual agent (Collective: +0.124, p < 0.001; Bridged: +0.109, p < 0.001), with the free-formed collective achieving the highest valid answer ratio (0.947). On semantic diversity, the bridged condition significantly exceeds the individual (difference = 0.032, p = 0.001), while the free-formed collective's diversity gain over the individual is not statistically significant (difference = 0.012, p = 0.254). In the Public Goods Game robustness experiment, collective agents show markedly smaller contribution drops after exposure to a malicious player: first-order infection reduces contributions by $20.25 in Collective A and $13.25 in Collective B, versus $38.5 in the non-collective setting (differences of $18.25, p = 0.002, and $25.25, p < 0.001, respectively). Second-order infection has no significant effect within collectives, whereas it drops non-collective contributions from $80 to $27 (p < 0.001), demonstrating that emergent pro-social norms in AI collectives confer robustness against the diffusion of anti-social behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of structured multi-agent LLM systems (Qian et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Mukobi et al., 2023) where humans pre-assign roles, relationships, and objectives, by introducing 'free-formed AI collectives' in which agents autonomously choose interaction partners, topics, and actions with no human-imposed structure. It builds conceptually on Park et al. (2023) Generative Agents and De Marzo et al. (2023) emergent network studies, but explicitly removes all task scaffolding to study open-ended self-organization. The analytical framework draws heavily on social science—homophily (McPherson et al., 2001), small-world network topology (Watts & Strogatz, 1998), and diversity-creativity links (Uzzi & Spiro, 2005; Shi & Evans, 2023)—to interpret emergent AI collective behavior through a sociological lens rather than a purely engineering one.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs proliferate in online environments, the paper argues that studying how they spontaneously form collectives is essential for predicting and managing emergent AI social dynamics at scale. The finding that free-formed collectives develop pro-social norms and resist behavioral 'poisoning' more effectively than isolated agents offers a potential self-regulation mechanism for AI safety that complements top-down alignment. However, the evidence is exploratory (10 agents, 30 rounds, primarily Claude-2.1), the key diversity result for free collectives vs. individuals is not statistically significant (p=0.254), and critical open questions remain around scalability, heterogeneous agent interactions, echo-chamber risks, and the computational cost of maintaining large-scale free-formed collectives.
