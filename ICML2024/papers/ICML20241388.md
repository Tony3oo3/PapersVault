## Problem

Existing OOD detection methods focus almost exclusively on designing powerful score functions while applying a simple empirical threshold rule (e-DR) that lacks any rigorous theoretical guarantee. This paper asks how to design a decision rule for OOD detection that provides provable statistical guarantees (FDR control, FPR convergence) while also delivering superior empirical performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241388/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained score function (e.g., MSP, KNN, GEN), a calibration set of ID samples, and a test set of images. It computes empirical p-values for each test sample by ranking its score against the calibration set scores, then applies the g-BH rejection rule collectively across the entire test set to output a binary ID/OOD label per sample. Unlike the e-DR which fixes a single threshold from a validation set, the g-BH threshold is data-dependent and adapts to the composition of the test set.

## Method

The paper reformulates OOD detection as a multiple hypothesis testing problem where each test sample's ID status is a null hypothesis, and proposes the generalized Benjamini-Hochberg (g-BH) procedure as the decision rule. The g-BH generalizes the classical BH rejection rule by applying a monotone function f(·) from two defined function classes to the empirical p-values before comparing against the i/n·α threshold. Theoretically, the paper proves FDR control at level α under mutual independence or PRDS of p-values, and derives a non-asymptotic upper bound on FPR expectation under a tailed generalized Gaussian distribution family, showing FPR converges to zero in probability. The procedure is distribution-free, requires no OOD data, and is plug-and-play: any existing score function can be combined with g-BH without modifying the training pipeline.

## Results

The g-BH procedure consistently reduces FPR and improves F1-score across all 8 baseline score functions and 6 OOD benchmarks, at a modest cost to TPR (~3–5%). The most dramatic improvement is with KNN on Place365: FPR drops from 48.21% to 16.79% and F1 rises from 49.83% to 67.86%. On the classical FPR95 metric, combining MSP with g-BH reduces FPR95 by 13.65% on average across OOD datasets. GEN+g-BH achieves the largest FPR reduction on TinyImageNet (52.91% → 25.65%) and SVHN (48.49% → 21.49%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The g-BH procedure is applied as a drop-in decision rule on top of 8 existing score functions (MSP, KLM, KNN, LogitNorm, RankFeat, ASH, Cider, GEN), and it improves FPR and F1 in every single case across all OOD benchmarks. The largest absolute FPR reductions come from KNN (e.g., 48.21→16.79 on Place365, 52.75→14.26 on SVHN) and GEN (57.78→31.01 on CIFAR-100, 48.49→21.49 on SVHN), where the original e-DR threshold is particularly miscalibrated. LogitNorm, already the strongest baseline, still benefits: FPR drops from 16.68→10.06 on SVHN and 27.57→16.53 on Place365. On the classical FPR95 metric (Table 2), all baselines improve, with MSP showing the largest average reduction (13.65%) and GEN improving by 14.58% on Texture. AUROC and AUPR remain roughly comparable or improve slightly (≤1–2%), confirming that g-BH primarily re-calibrates the decision threshold rather than altering the underlying score ranking. The consistent pattern—FPR↓, F1↑, TPR slightly↓—across all 8 methods and 6 OOD datasets indicates the gain is method-agnostic and stems from the adaptive, FDR-controlled threshold rather than any specific score function.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant OOD detection paradigm of designing increasingly powerful score functions (MSP, KNN, LogitNorm, GEN, etc.) and instead targets the decision rule that converts scores into ID/OOD labels. It reframes OOD detection as a multiple hypothesis testing problem and adapts the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure—originally developed for genomics and high-dimensional statistics—into a generalized form (g-BH) with empirical p-values, building on the FDR control theory of Benjamini & Yekutieli (2001) and the sparse-regime FPR analysis of Donoho & Jin (2004). The closest prior work is Yu et al. (2023), who applied BH with empirical p-values to noisy label detection, but this paper is the first to bring the full FDR/FPR theoretical apparatus to OOD detection and to prove FPR convergence to zero under a tailed generalized Gaussian model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The g-BH procedure offers the first rigorous statistical guarantees (FDR control at a pre-specified level, FPR converging to zero in probability) for an OOD detection decision rule, filling a gap that purely empirical threshold selection cannot address. Its distribution-free, plug-and-play nature means any existing score function can benefit without retraining, which is practically attractive. However, the consistent ~3–5% TPR reduction across all baselines is a real tradeoff that may be unacceptable in safety-critical settings where missing an ID sample is costly, and the FPR bound relies on the tailed generalized Gaussian assumption that is hard to verify in practice. Open questions include whether the approach scales to ImageNet-level benchmarks, how the FDR level α should be chosen per application, and whether the TPR cost can be mitigated by combining g-BH with score-function improvements.
