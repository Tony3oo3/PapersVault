## Problem

Existing causal effect estimators under networked interference rely on parametric or semiparametric models that become inconsistent when assumptions about the data generation process (e.g., conditional independence between a unit's treatment and its neighbors' treatments) are violated. No prior method provides a doubly robust estimator that remains unbiased when either the propensity or outcome model is misspecified in the networked setting, because the traditional targeted maximum likelihood estimation framework assumes i.i.d. data and cannot be directly applied.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a network dataset comprising an adjacency matrix, per-unit covariate vectors, binary treatment indicators, and a continuous neighborhood exposure z_i ∈ [0,1] computed as the fraction of treated first-order neighbors. It outputs the full average dose-response function ψ(t,z) over the (t,z) grid, from which average main effects (AME), average spillover effects (ASE), average total effects (ATE), and their individual-level counterparts (IME, ISE, ITE) are derived as differences. The neighborhood propensity is estimated on B+1 grid points over [0,1] with linear interpolation, and the perturbation parameter ε(t,z) is represented via B-spline basis functions with K_{n1} and K_{n0} basis functions for treated and control groups respectively.

## Method

TNet is an end-to-end neural network estimator with four modules: a GCN-based feature aggregator, a generalized propensity estimator (decomposing the joint propensity into a sigmoid-based individual propensity and a softmax-based piecewise-linear neighborhood density), a dual-MLP outcome estimator for treated and control groups, and a B-spline perturbation estimator for ε(t,z). The key innovation is the targeted loss L3, which regresses the outcome on the clever covariate 1_{T,Z}(t_i,z_i)/[g_1(t_i|x_i,x_{N_i})·g_2(z_i|x_i,x_{N_i})] with the outcome model as offset, ensuring the minimizer solves the efficient influence curve equation and thereby achieves double robustness. Training proceeds iteratively: at each iteration, L1 (propensity) + L2 (outcome) are minimized first, then L3 (perturbation) is minimized, mirroring the three-step TMLE procedure. Theoretical analysis establishes a convergence rate of O_p(n_0^{-1/3}√log n_0 + n_1^{-1/3}√log n_1 + r_1(n)·r_2(n)), where the product term r_1·r_2 guarantees that consistency is maintained if either nuisance model is correctly specified.

## Results

On the BlogCatalog (BC) semisynthetic dataset, TNet achieves the lowest MAE on AME (0.0481), ASE (0.0180), and ITE (0.0560) among all baselines, outperforming the next-best method RRNet by 45% on AME and 21% on ASE. On ATE (within sample), NDR (0.0284) slightly outperforms TNet (0.0533), but TNet recovers the lead on out-of-sample ATE (0.0532 vs. 0.0911 for RRNet). TNet also achieves the best individual effect estimates (IME=0.0506, ISE=0.0196, ITE=0.0560) with the smallest standard deviations, indicating both accuracy and stability across 5 runs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TNet outperforms all neural-network-based baselines (CFR+z, GEst, ND+z, NetEst, RRNet) on every average and individual effect metric in both within-sample and out-of-sample settings. The largest margin is on AME, where TNet (0.0481) nearly halves the error of the best baseline RRNet (0.0877), and on ASE where TNet (0.0180) beats RRNet (0.0227) by 21%. The sole exception is within-sample ATE, where the semiparametric NDR estimator (0.0284) achieves a lower MAE than TNet (0.0533), though NDR is restricted to within-sample evaluation and does not report individual effects. The ablation TNet(w/o L₃) confirms that the targeted loss L₃ is critical: without it, TNet degrades to performance comparable to or worse than NetEst and RRNet, validating the double-robustness mechanism. TNet also exhibits the smallest standard deviations across all metrics, indicating greater training stability than baselines like NetEst (std 0.1343 on AME) or CFR+z (std 0.0678).

## Relation to Prior Work

TNet extends the Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE) framework from van der Laan & Rubin (2006) to the networked interference setting, where data violates i.i.d. assumptions. It builds on the joint generalized propensity score decomposition from Forastiere et al. (2021) and the functional targeted regularization for continuous treatments from Nie et al. (2020), but departs from both by making the targeted perturbation end-to-end within a GCN-based neural network and by using B-spline basis functions to model the perturbation parameter ε(t,z) as a function of the continuous neighborhood exposure. Unlike prior DR estimators for networks (e.g., NDR from Liu et al. 2023), TNet integrates the targeted loss directly into training rather than applying it as a post-hoc correction, and is robust to the choice of propensity score decomposition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The doubly robust property under networked interference is practically important because real-world networked data (epidemiology, advertising, social networks) rarely satisfies the strong assumptions required by single-model estimators, and misspecification of either the propensity or outcome model leads to biased causal effect estimates. The convergence rate analysis showing a product rate r₁(n)·r₂(n) provides theoretical justification that the estimator degrades gracefully when one nuisance model is misspecified. However, the evaluation is limited to semisynthetic data on two networks with a fixed average-based summary function and first-order neighbors only; open questions include whether the approach extends to higher-order interference, alternative exposure mappings, and large-scale real-world networks where the GCN feature module may face scalability challenges.
