## Problem

When machine-learned predictors output uncertain predictions (distributions over classes), it is unclear how to convert them into rankings that are both stable to small prediction perturbations and fair to individuals and subgroups. Deterministic ranking functions provably cannot be stable, and prior work on fair rankings from predictors either restricts to deterministic outputs or does not guarantee stability, leaving a gap in simultaneously achieving stability, individual fairness, and multigroup fairness.

## Input / Output

The ranking function takes as input an n×L prediction matrix where each row is a probability distribution over L ordinal labels (sorted from most to least preferred, e.g., {irrelevant, suitable, extremely relevant}). It outputs a doubly stochastic matrix M ∈ M_DS^{n×n} where entry M_{i,k} is the probability that individual i is placed at rank k, representing a full distribution over all n! possible rankings rather than a single deterministic ordering.

## Method

The paper adapts Uncertainty Aware (UA) rankings to discrete multiclass predictions with independent label sampling: individual i is assigned to position k with probability equal to the probability that i has the k-th highest label when all labels are drawn independently from their predicted distributions, with ties broken uniformly. The key technical contribution is proving UA rankings are anonymous and 1-stable (Theorem 11) via a conditional decomposition (Proposition 18) that expresses rank probabilities in terms of counts of other individuals' labels, combined with a coupling argument bounding the total variation distance between competing individuals' label distributions. The main fairness result (Theorem 15) shows that when the underlying predictor is multiaccurate or multicalibrated over a collection of subgroups, the induced UA ranking distribution is close to the UA ranking that would be produced by the ground truth label distribution, for each position k in the ranking. The paper further proves that deterministic ranking functions are necessarily unstable (Proposition 8) and that the utility-optimal ranking function cannot achieve comparable stability or fairness guarantees (Proposition 17).

## Results

On the ACS dataset, r_UA achieves a normalized utility of 0.726±0.027 (n=20), outperforming Plackett-Luce (0.616±0.038) and uniform ranking (0.540±0.043). On the Enrollment dataset, r_UA reaches 0.852±0.030 (n=20) versus 0.755±0.041 for Plackett-Luce and 0.552±0.052 for uniform. In terms of stability, r_UA's ∞-norm deviation under SGD noise is 0.011±0.002 (ACS) and 0.012±0.002 (Enrollment), confirming the theoretical 1-stability bound, while the utility-optimal ranking r_opt^τ exhibits deviations of 0.947±0.224 (ACS) and 0.680±0.466 (Enrollment), demonstrating its instability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares r_UA against two baselines: Plackett-Luce (r_PL) and uniform random ranking (r_unif), using normalized utility (where the utility-optimal deterministic ranking r_opt^τ is set to 1). r_UA consistently outperforms both baselines across all dataset sizes on both ACS and Enrollment. On ACS, r_UA achieves ~0.72–0.73 normalized utility versus ~0.62 for r_PL and ~0.54–0.55 for r_unif, a gap of roughly 10–18 percentage points. On Enrollment, the advantage is even larger: r_UA reaches ~0.85–0.86 versus ~0.76 for r_PL and ~0.55–0.56 for r_unif. Additionally, Table 1 shows that r_UA is dramatically more stable than the utility-optimal r_opt^τ under SGD training noise (∞-norm deviation of ~0.01 vs. ~0.68–0.95), empirically confirming the theoretical stability guarantee (Theorem 11) and the instability of r_opt^τ (Proposition 17).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Uncertainty Aware (UA) ranking framework of Singh et al. (2021) and Devic et al. (2023) from continuous merit distributions to discrete multiclass predictions, and introduces stability as a formal Lipschitz-type property of ranking functions. It departs fundamentally from Dwork et al. (2019), who study fair rankings induced by predictors but restrict to deterministic rankings, by proving that determinism is incompatible with both stability and anonymity. The multigroup fairness analysis connects UA rankings to the multiaccuracy/multicalibration literature (Kim et al., 2019; Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018), showing that these predictor-level guarantees compose naturally with UA ranking to yield group-level ranking fairness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result—that no non-constant deterministic ranking function can be stable—provides a rigorous theoretical justification for randomization in ranking systems, which is a strong departure from the dominant score-and-sort paradigm in practice. The composition theorem (stability + individual fairness → fair rankings; multicalibration + UA → group-fair rankings) gives practitioners a clear recipe: use a calibrated predictor and UA ranking to get both individual and group fairness guarantees simultaneously. Open questions include extending the analysis to correlated labels across individuals, finding general sufficient conditions for group fairness preservation beyond UA, and investigating how biased uncertainty estimates (rather than unbiased ones) affect the guarantees.
