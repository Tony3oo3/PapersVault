## Problem

Support vector machines and quantile regression rely on non-smooth loss functions (hinge and check losses) that block the use of fast coordinate descent in high-dimensional settings (p ≫ n). Existing smoothing approaches yield only approximate solutions, and no algorithm was known to compute the exact solution of high-dimensional sparse penalized SVM or quantile regression at scale. This paper closes that gap by showing a finite smoothing procedure can provably recover the exact optimum.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular data with p features far exceeding n samples (e.g., p = 3,000–22,283 with n = 42–253), where features are continuous vectors and responses are either binary labels (SVM) or continuous values (quantile regression). It outputs a sparse coefficient vector β̂ and intercept β̂₀ that satisfy the KKT conditions of the original non-smooth problem, along with the identified support-vector set (SVM) or zero-residual set (quantile regression). The algorithm also produces full solution paths over a grid of (λ₁, λ₂) tuning pairs via warm-start and active-set strategies, enabling cross-validation-based model selection.

## Method

FSA replaces the non-differentiable hinge or check loss with a δ-smoothed quadratic surrogate, then proves that the exact solution of the original problem is obtained by solving the smoothed objective subject to linear equality constraints at the non-differentiable points (where the margin equals 1 for SVM, or the residual equals 0 for quantile regression). Since the constraint set is unknown a priori, the algorithm iteratively discovers it: it solves the smoothed problem with a current subset of constraints, identifies new points falling within the smoothing window, augments the constraint set, and repeats until the set stabilizes in finitely many steps. The smoothed constrained subproblem is solved by a generalized coordinate descent (GCD) algorithm built on the augmented Lagrangian method, where each coordinate update reduces to a closed-form soft-thresholding operator. The outer loop decreases δ by a factor of 4 each round and terminates when the KKT conditions of the original non-smooth problem are verified. Non-convex penalties (SCAD, MCP) are handled by wrapping the GCD solver in a local linear algorithm that iteratively reweights the L1 penalty.

## Results

On high-dimensional SVM (n=200, p=10,000, ρ=0.2), HDSVM achieves an objective value of 0.78 in 0.12 seconds, compared to ReHline (0.79, 1389 s), CVXR (0.81, 1425 s), and DrSVM (0.86, 1261 s) — a 4–5 order-of-magnitude speedup with the lowest objective. On high-dimensional quantile regression (n=200, p=3,000, τ=0.1), HDQR achieves an objective of 0.64 in 0.03 s versus FHDQR (0.66, 1.43 s), ReHline (0.78, 97 s), and CVXR (0.65, 422 s). On the BREAST benchmark (n=42, p=22,283), HDSVM reaches an objective of 0.45 in 0.27 s while ReHline requires 3,980 s and yields 0.56. The algorithm scales linearly with both n and p, and non-convex penalties (SCAD, MCP) add only ~2× runtime over the elastic net while reducing false-positive feature selections from 7–13 to 1–3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HDSVM/HDQR dominates all four baselines on both speed and objective value across every setting tested. Against ReHline (NeurIPS 2023), the most recent unified solver, HDSVM is roughly 10,000× faster at p=10,000 (0.12 s vs. 1389 s) while achieving a lower objective (0.78 vs. 0.79); HDQR is ~3,000× faster on the microarray benchmark at p=10,000 (0.05 s vs. 1013 s) with a substantially lower objective (0.02 vs. 0.06). Against CVXR, a general-purpose disciplined convex optimization solver, HDSVM is 4–5 orders of magnitude faster and consistently achieves the lowest objective; CVXR's objective degrades noticeably as p grows (e.g., 0.81 vs. 0.78 at p=10,000). Against DrSVM (Wang et al., 2006), the original doubly-regularized SVM solver, HDSVM is ~10,000× faster and achieves a lower objective in every configuration (e.g., 0.78 vs. 0.86 at p=10,000, ρ=0.2). Against FHDQR (Gu et al., 2018), the state-of-the-art ADMM-based quantile regression solver, HDQR is ~10× faster on simulation data and ~50× faster on the microarray benchmark, while also achieving lower objective values (e.g., 0.64 vs. 0.66 at τ=0.1, p=3,000).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the line of smoothing-based approaches for non-differentiable losses in SVM (least squares SVM, Huberized SVM, density-convoluted SVM) and quantile regression, but departs from them by proving that a finite smoothing with simple linear constraints at non-differentiable points recovers the exact solution rather than an approximation. The computational engine is a generalized coordinate descent (GCD) algorithm rooted in the majorization-minimization principle (Hunter & Lange, 2004; Yang & Zou, 2013b), combined with an augmented Lagrangian formulation to handle the equality constraints, and extended to non-convex penalties (SCAD, MCP) via the local linear algorithm (Zou & Li, 2008). The warm-start and active-set strategies for solution paths follow the glmnet paradigm (Friedman et al., 2010), and the quantile regression component competes with FHDQR (Gu et al., 2018), which combined ADMM with coordinate descent.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: FSA delivers 4–5 orders of magnitude speedup over existing solvers (ReHline, CVXR, DrSVM, FHDQR) while achieving the lowest objective values, and is available as open-source R packages (hdsvm, hdqr) on CRAN. The exactness guarantee distinguishes it from prior smoothing methods that only provide approximations, making it suitable for settings where the support vector structure or exact KKT satisfaction matters. The paper notes that the framework can accommodate linear constraints (e.g., fairness constraints, compositional data), but leaves these extensions to future work; it is also restricted to linear SVM and linear quantile regression, with no treatment of kernel methods or non-linear generalizations.
