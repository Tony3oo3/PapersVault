## Problem

Standard dose-finding trial methods (3+3, CRM) assume a homogeneous trial population and rely on parametric dose-toxicity models, neither of which holds for novel therapies with non-monotonic efficacy profiles or for populations with subgroup-specific toxicity/efficacy differences (e.g., by sex or ethnicity). This leads to sub-optimal dose allocation during trials, safety disparities across subgroups, and final dose recommendations that are not generalizable to under-represented populations. The paper asks whether an adaptive procedure can simultaneously guarantee participant safety, optimize for heterogeneous participant utility, and produce accurate subgroup-specific dose recommendations under small sample sizes.

## Input / Output

The method receives participants sequentially in cohorts of 3, each with a known pre-defined subgroup label (e.g., biological sex), and observes binary toxicity and efficacy outcomes per participant. Doses are drawn from a discrete set of K indices (or a continuous range in the extended setting), with practitioner-specified toxicity and efficacy thresholds defining the acceptable operating range. The output is a per-participant dose allocation during the trial and, at trial conclusion, a single recommended dose per subgroup selected by maximum Thall utility among safe doses.

## Method

SAFE-T models dose-toxicity and dose-efficacy as two independent multi-output Gaussian process classifiers using the linear model of co-regionalization (LMC), where each subgroup's latent function is a linear combination of shared latent GPs, enabling flexible nonparametric dose-response learning across heterogeneous subgroups with small samples. Dose allocation proceeds in two stages: a safe exploration stage that expands the set of known-safe doses (allocating the highest untried safe dose or reducing uncertainty via confidence-interval width), followed by a safe optimization stage that maximizes an acquisition function (expected improvement, UCB, or Thall utility) over the efficacy GP restricted to the safe set. Safety is enforced by a GP posterior constraint μ_g(d) + ν_T·σ_g(d) ≤ Φ⁻¹(τ_T), with theoretical guarantees that all allocated doses are safe with probability ≥ 1−δ_T (Theorem 4.2) and a derived lower bound on the required initial safe cohort size N₀ (Theorem 4.1). Final dose recommendations per subgroup are selected by maximizing Thall utility over the safe dose set, avoiding the assumption that the highest safe dose is optimal.

## Results

Across 18 synthetic dose-finding scenarios (N=51, 2 subgroups), SAFE-T achieves lower or comparable final recommended dose error in 16/18 scenarios, fewest or comparable safety constraint violations in 16/18 scenarios, and highest or comparable participant utility in 17/18 scenarios relative to 3+3, CRM, and C3T. In the continuous dose extension (Table 1), SAFE-T achieves final dose errors of −0.19±2.60 (Subgroup 0) and 1.34±4.23 (Subgroup 1) in Scenario C.1, with safety violations of 0.24±0.00 and 0.00±0.19 respectively. SAFE-T also shows consistently decreasing dose error with increasing sample size (N=51 to N=537), unlike 3+3 and C3T which do not improve.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAFE-T outperforms all three baselines on the majority of scenarios across all three metrics. Against CRM, SAFE-T is decisively superior: CRM exhibits large final dose recommendation errors and significant disparities between subgroups, while SAFE-T maintains low error and balanced subgroup performance. Against 3+3, SAFE-T trades a small number of additional safety violations (3+3 is very conservative) for substantially better dose accuracy and participant utility, since 3+3's strict safety rule causes unnecessary early termination and it cannot handle plateauing efficacy. Against C3T (Lee et al., 2020), SAFE-T achieves lower dose error and higher utility; C3T is competitive on safety but allocates notably more unsafe doses to the higher-toxicity subgroup when that subgroup is underrepresented (e.g., when π₀ is small), exacerbating health disparities. In the sample size experiment (Scenario 9, N=51 to 537), SAFE-T shows consistently decreasing dose error with more participants, while 3+3 and C3T fail to improve, highlighting their sensitivity to model misspecification (plateauing efficacy). In the edge case where no doses are safe (Scenario 16), SAFE-T's early stopping safeguard prevents further unsafe allocations, resulting in fewer safety violations than baselines that continue dosing.

## Relation to Prior Work

SAFE-T builds on GP classification (Rasmussen & Williams, 2003) and the linear model of co-regionalization (Journal & Huijbregts, 1976) to model dose-toxicity and dose-efficacy as multi-output Gaussian processes over heterogeneous subgroups, departing from the parametric logistic models used in CRM and C3T. The safe exploration stage and theoretical safety guarantees follow the proof strategy of Schreiter et al. (2015) for GP-based safe active learning, but generalize their fixed safety threshold to a practitioner-specified toxicity probability and handle Bernoulli (binary) outcomes rather than continuous Gaussian noise. The final dose recommendation leverages Thall utility (Thall & Cook, 2004), which prior dose-finding methods (3+3, CRM) do not incorporate, enabling recommendation of non-highest safe doses when efficacy plateaus or is parabolic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete health equity concern: standard dose-finding methods assume population homogeneity, which combined with underrepresentation of women and minorities in early-phase trials, leads to suboptimal and potentially unsafe dose allocations for these groups. SAFE-T's consistent outperformance across 18 diverse synthetic scenarios with fixed hyperparameters, and its ability to reduce inter-subgroup safety disparities even under skewed subgroup ratios, provides practical evidence that non-parametric, subgroup-aware methods can mitigate these inequities. Key limitations include restriction to binary outcomes, reliance on pre-defined subgroups (limiting intersectionality), and the practical barrier of GP complexity for clinical trial practitioners. Open questions include extending to continuous/delayed/multiple outcomes, learned (rather than pre-defined) subgroups, and establishing regret bounds for GP classification with Bernoulli likelihoods.
