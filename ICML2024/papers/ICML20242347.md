## Problem

Despite GenAI's rapid disruption of content creation markets, no formal mathematical model existed to characterize the competitive dynamics between human creators and AI-generated content. The central unresolved question is whether human creators will be driven out of the market or whether a stable, symbiotic equilibrium is attainable—a question with direct implications for content platform design and creator livelihoods.

## Input / Output

The model takes creator-specific cost parameters (c_i with common exponent ρ), GenAI capability parameters (α for data efficiency, β for convergence rate), topic-level user traffic (μ_k), and the number of creators (n) and topics (K) as inputs. A key structural feature is that GenAI's competitiveness on each topic is endogenous to the aggregate human content output (s_k), making it a non-standard contest player whose 'effort' is a power-law function of all other players' efforts. Outputs include the unique Nash equilibrium content allocation per creator, total human content volume, the proportion of creators adopting GenAI (in the inclusive setting), and per-creator utilities.

## Method

The paper extends the Tullock contest by introducing GenAI as a player whose calibrated content output follows a power-law g_k(s_k) = α_k · s_k^{β̃_k} of the total human content, grounded in ML scaling laws. Two competition contexts are analyzed: an exclusive setting (GenAI as a standalone non-strategic agent) and an inclusive setting (each creator can choose between human effort and a zero-cost GenAI action ⊥). For the exclusive game, the authors prove strict monotonicity via diagonal strict concavity of the Hessian, yielding a unique PNE, then derive micro-level properties (monotonicity in capability and cost) and a macro-level scaling law showing total content s* scales as (μ · ‖c⁻¹‖_{1/(ρ-1)})^{1/(γ+ρ-1)} with constants independent of β. For the inclusive game, they construct a counterexample showing PNE need not exist when K>1, but prove existence in the 1-D separable-cost case with the structural property that highest-cost creators switch to GenAI. Simulations use multi-agent mirror descent to solve equilibria and validate theoretical predictions across parameter sweeps.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical contribution is Theorem 3, which bounds the total human-created content s* at equilibrium: C_ρ/(2α+2) < (s*)^(γ+ρ−1) / (μ·‖c⁻¹‖_{1/(ρ−1)}) < C_ρ, showing that GenAI's learning rate β does not appear in the bound—only its data efficiency α mildly affects the denominator. Simulations (Figures 1–6) confirm that total content volume s* grows polynomially in μ (validating Theorem 3), while increasing GenAI capability (larger α or β) shrinks total human effort and social welfare. In the inclusive competition setting, the proportion of GenAI-adopting creators at PNE grows with α, β, and n but declines with μ (Figure 2), and creators with higher costs are consistently more likely to switch to GenAI (Figure 3). A striking finding (Figure 4) is that while total human utility decreases with stronger GenAI, the *average* utility per remaining human creator can actually increase as less-efficient creators are pushed out.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines in the standard ML sense; rather, it extends the classic Tullock contest (Tullock, 1980) and compares two internal competition regimes. The exclusive competition (𝒢_EX) always admits a unique PNE (Theorem 1), while the inclusive competition (𝒢_IN) may fail to have a PNE when K>1 (Theorem 4), though PNE existence is restored in the 1-D separable-cost case (Theorem 5). The key qualitative comparison is between the two regimes: in the exclusive setting, GenAI acts as a fixed external competitor, whereas in the inclusive setting, each creator strategically chooses between human creation and GenAI adoption. The paper also contrasts the macro-level effect (GenAI only shifts total content by a constant factor, per Theorem 3) against the micro-level effect (individual high-cost creators can be significantly disadvantaged or eliminated), highlighting a tension between aggregate stability and individual-level disruption.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Tullock contest framework (Tullock, 1980) and the recent line of content creator competition models (Hron et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023a,b; Jagadeesan et al., 2023) by introducing GenAI as a structurally novel competitor whose capability is a power-law function of the accumulated human content (training data), drawing on ML scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020). The key departure from prior Tullock contests is that no player's competitiveness in standard economic environments depends on the aggregate effort of all other players, making the GenAI player a genuinely new structural element. The equilibrium analysis leverages Rosen's (1965) diagonal strict concavity characterization of monotone games, extending known monotonicity results for classic Tullock contests to this new setting with heterogeneous cost functions and GenAI-dependent payoffs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal game-theoretic framework for human-vs-GenAI content competition, providing existence/uniqueness guarantees and equilibrium characterizations that were previously unavailable. The macro-level finding that GenAI affects total human content by at most a constant factor (Theorem 3) offers a theoretically grounded 'symbiosis' narrative, while the micro-level results (displacement of high-cost creators, niche-topic migration) paint a more nuanced picture of market restructuring. Open questions include modeling GenAI as a strategic player, extending to non-separable multi-topic settings where PNE may not exist, and capturing the dynamics of arts/humanities creators who operate under fundamentally different production economics. The non-existence of PNE in the inclusive K>1 case, with its cycling best-response dynamics, suggests real-world instability that the model captures but does not fully resolve.
