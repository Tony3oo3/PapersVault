## Problem

While prior work established that effective learning rates (ELRs) across layers in normalized networks eventually converge, it overlooked how the choice of learning rate in early training steps can drastically widen ELR disparities to the point of making recovery impractical. No formal model existed to describe the time-based evolution of ELR ratios under scheduled learning rates, nor a principled criterion for when a learning rate is too aggressive to prevent ELR 'flips' that increase spread.

## Input / Output

The model takes as input the initial weight norms and base gradient magnitudes of each layer in a normalized network (e.g., ResNet with BatchNorm), a learning rate schedule, and standard RGB image classification data (CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32, ImageNet at 224×224). It produces layer-wise predicted weight and gradient norm trajectories, pairwise ELR ratios, a per-step critical learning rate κ(t_i) computed from current norms, and a hyper-parameter-free warm-up schedule. The warm-up is unusual in that it requires no preset hyper-parameters: at each step it reads the two highest-ELR layers and sets λ to their flipping ratio, guaranteeing convergence in exactly L steps.

## Method

The paper derives a discrete recurrence (Eq. 5) for expected squared weight norms under the assumption that normalization-induced inverse scaling is the sole dynamic effect and base gradient magnitudes remain constant. In the gradient-flow limit this yields a closed-form solution σ²(t) = √(2c²t + k₀), from which all ELR ratios provably converge to 1. For finite learning rates, the authors identify a critical learning rate κ_jk = σ_jσ_k/√(c_jc_k) above which the highest-ELR layer flips below the lowest in a single step, widening spread; they prove that with constant λ this flip can occur only at step 0. From this, they construct a subcritical warm-up that sets λ(t_i) to the flipping ratio of the two highest-ELR layers at each step, guaranteeing no flips and convergence in L steps. Empirically, applying this warm-up or a per-step ELR-constrain rescaling (analogous to LARS) reduces the initially exponential ELR spread of a 110-layer non-residual network and restores trainability on CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet.

## Results

On ImageNet with ResNet101 NoShort (no residual connections), the proposed subcritical warm-up (only 9 steps) achieves 41.83% top-1 accuracy with a relative ELR spread (S_rel) of 0.70, dramatically outperforming the OneCycle scheduler (64,060 warm-up steps) which reaches only 22.82% accuracy with S_rel = 1.96. The ELR-constrain method (no warm-up) achieves the best NoShort accuracy at 47.99%. For the ResNet101 Short (with residual connections), all three warm-up strategies yield comparable accuracy (~72.8–73.1%) and low S_rel (0.27–0.31), confirming that the effect is most pronounced in networks with high initial ELR spread.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the OneCycle scheduler (Smith & Topin, 2017), a widely used warm-up strategy with fixed hyper-parameters. On the ResNet101 NoShort (no residual connections, exponentially exploding initial gradients), the subcritical warm-up wins decisively: 41.83% vs. 22.82% top-1 accuracy, a gap of ~19 percentage points, while using only 9 warm-up steps versus OneCycle's 64,060. The S_rel metric confirms the mechanism: subcritical warm-up reduces ELR spread to 0.70, whereas OneCycle leaves it at 1.96, indicating that OneCycle's fixed hyper-parameters are insufficient for networks with high initial ELR disparity. On the ResNet101 Short (with residual connections, linear initial spread), all three strategies are essentially tied (72.83–73.06% accuracy, S_rel 0.27–0.31), showing that the advantage of adaptive warm-up vanishes when initial ELR spread is already small. The ELR-constrain method (inspired by LARS) achieves the highest NoShort accuracy (47.99%) without any warm-up, but requires per-layer gradient rescaling at every step.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the auto-rate-tuning framework of Arora et al. (2019) and the ELR observations of You et al. (2017) by deriving a closed-form model for the temporal evolution of layer-wise weight and gradient norms in normalized networks. While prior work established that ELR ratios eventually converge (Arora et al., Wan et al. 2021), this paper identifies the mechanism and conditions under which they can temporarily widen—specifically, a critical learning rate that depends on current ELRs—and shows that this flipping can only occur in the first step under constant LR. The orthogonality property from Salimans and Kingma (2016) and the inverse-scaling property of normalization layers (Wu et al. 2018, Xiong et al. 2020) serve as the foundational identities from which the discrete and continuous models are derived.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal, predictive model for how normalization-induced weight dynamics evolve over training time, yielding a hyper-parameter-free warm-up scheduler that provably converges in L steps. Its practical impact is demonstrated by rendering a 110-layer non-residual network trainable on CIFAR-10, and the ImageNet results show subcritical warm-up outperforming OneCycle with far fewer warm-up steps. Key limitations include the constant base-gradient assumption (which breaks down for Transformers and higher-degree nonlinearities), the exclusion of momentum and weight decay, and validation primarily on ReLU CNNs. Open questions include extending the model to non-constant base gradients, attention-based architectures, and characterizing when auto-rate-tuning effects become too weak relative to training-induced gradient fluctuations.
