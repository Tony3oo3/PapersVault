## Problem

While representation learning for feedforward neural network weights has been actively studied, no systematic methods exist for learning useful low-dimensional representations of RNN weight matrices. This paper asks how to encode RNN weights into compact vectors that capture the network's functional behavior, enabling downstream tasks like task identification, property prediction, and model emulation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240731/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The encoder takes the full parameter set θ of a 2-layer LSTM (hidden size 32) as input. Mechanistic encoders consume the weight matrices directly (flattened, as layer-wise statistics, as a transformer sequence, or through permutation-equivariant DWSNet layers), while functionalist encoders instead feed learnable probing sequences (length 22 for formal languages, 51 for tiled MNIST) through the RNN and process the resulting outputs. The output is a 16-dimensional representation vector z, which conditions a separate LSTM emulator to reproduce the target RNN's rollout behavior in a given environment.

## Method

The paper proposes six encoder architectures spanning mechanistic (Layer-Wise Statistics, Flattened Weights, Parameter Transformer, DWSNet) and functionalist (Non-Interactive Probing, Interactive Probing) approaches, and trains them via an emulation-based self-supervised objective: an encoder E_φ maps weights to z, and an emulator A_ξ (a 2-layer LSTM) is jointly trained to imitate the RNN's outputs given z as conditioning, minimizing a reverse-KL or MSE loss over rollout sequences. The key architectural novelty is interactive probing, where each new probing input is generated by an LSTM E_R that conditions on all previous probing outputs, enabling the encoder to adapt its queries to the specific RNN being evaluated. A theoretical framework formalizes this as an interrogator problem over total computable functions, proving that interactive probing can require exponentially fewer queries (O(Mk) vs O(M^k)) than non-interactive probing for certain function families. The paper also releases two 'model zoo' datasets of 1000 LSTMs each, trained on 216 formal language variants or 360 MNIST rotation angles, with weights saved at 9 training checkpoints.

## Results

On the Formal Languages dataset, Interactive Probing achieves the lowest self-supervised emulation loss (0.015), substantially outperforming Non-Interactive Probing (0.023) and all mechanistic encoders (0.043–0.051). On Tiled Sequential MNIST, both probing encoders tie at 0.017 loss, beating Flattened Weights and DWSNet (0.024) and Layer-Wise Statistics (0.039), while Parameter Transformer performs worst (0.067). For the most challenging downstream task—predicting which exact formal language an RNN was trained on (OOD split)—pre-trained Interactive Probing representations significantly outperform all other encoders and purely supervised models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six encoder architectures: four mechanistic (Layer-Wise Statistics, Flattened Weights, Parameter Transformer, DWSNet) and two functionalist (Non-Interactive and Interactive Probing). On Formal Languages, Interactive Probing wins decisively with 0.015 loss, roughly 3× better than the best mechanistic encoder (Parameter Transformer at 0.043) and 34% better than Non-Interactive Probing (0.023). On Sequential MNIST, both probing encoders tie at 0.017, beating DWSNet and Flattened Weights (0.024) by ~24%, while Parameter Transformer is the clear loser at 0.067. For downstream OOD task prediction on Formal Languages, pre-trained Interactive Probing significantly outperforms all other encoders and purely supervised baselines, confirming the theoretical prediction (Proposition 3.3) that interactive probing can be exponentially more efficient. For simpler properties (accuracy, generalization gap, training step), DWSNet performs most consistently across both datasets. The Parameter Transformer, despite its attention-based design, fails to learn informative representations on Sequential MNIST, with the emulator defaulting to a rotation-invariant classifier regardless of conditioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the weight-space representation learning line (Eilertsen et al. 2020; Schurholt et al. 2021; Navon et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023) from feedforward/convolutional networks to recurrent networks, adapting DWSNet's permutation-equivariant layers to LSTM gate structures. It also repurposes policy fingerprinting (Harb et al. 2020; Faccio et al. 2022a,b) and Schmidhuber's (2015) algorithmic information extraction idea into a formal interactive probing encoder for RNNs, combined with an emulation-based self-supervised objective inspired by Raileanu et al. (2020). The key departure is that all prior weight-representation work addressed only feedforward architectures; this paper is the first to systematically define, theorize, and empirically evaluate RNN weight encoders, including the mechanistic vs. functionalist distinction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical and empirical framework for understanding when and why different RNN weight encoding strategies succeed or fail, with the clean result that interactive probing is necessary (not merely beneficial) for complex algorithmic tasks like formal language identification. The two released model-zoo datasets (formal languages, tiled sequential MNIST) fill a gap analogous to what Eilertsen/Schurholt datasets did for CNNs, enabling future work on RNN search, interpretability, and meta-learning. Key limitations include evaluation on small LSTMs (32 hidden units), training instability of interactive probing, and the functionalist encoders' inability to distinguish mechanistically different but functionally equivalent RNNs. Open questions include scaling to larger models, other sequence architectures (SSMs, transformers), and stabilizing interactive probing training.
