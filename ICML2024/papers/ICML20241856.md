## Problem

Existing representation surgery methods for bias mitigation rely on concept erasure, which removes a concept entirely from representations. For tasks like toxicity mitigation, erasure is inadequate because the goal is to steer the model toward generating non-toxic text, not to eliminate its awareness of toxicity. While steering vectors (simple translations) have been used empirically, they lacked a formal theoretical justification, and no principled method existed for matching higher-order statistics of the target concept.

## Input / Output

The method consumes language model hidden states (last-token representations for autoregressive models like GPT-2 and Llama2, CLS tokens for BERT) and produces a modified representation vector via an affine transformation W·H + b. The transformation is applied selectively: a lightweight MLP classifier (or distance-to-mean heuristic during generation) identifies which representations belong to the source concept, and only those are transformed. In the generation setting, the steering is applied at every autoregressive decoding step to the last hidden state, and the output is the resulting generated text.

## Method

The paper derives two optimal affine steering functions by minimizing L2 distance subject to guardedness constraints (equal concept-conditional means, and optionally equal second moments). The first solution (Proposition 4.1) yields W* = I and b* = μ_c' − μ_c, a pure translation that provides the first theoretical justification for steering vectors. The second solution (Proposition 4.2, named MiMiC) additionally matches concept-conditional covariance, giving W* = Σ_c^(−1/2)(Σ_c^(1/2)Σ_c'Σ_c^(1/2))^(1/2)Σ_c^(−1/2), which is shown to be identical to the closed-form optimal transport map between two Gaussian densities. A key theoretical result (Proposition 4.3) proves that MiMiC provably eliminates expected bias by neighbors, i.e., representations no longer cluster by protected attribute in expectation. Empirically, both methods are validated on gender bias in profession classification, dialect bias in sentiment classification, and toxicity mitigation in GPT-2 generation, with MiMiC consistently outperforming mean-matching and LEACE baselines.

## Results

On the Bios gender-bias task, the proposed Mean+Covariance Matching steering function reduces the TPR-RMS gap from 0.155 to 0.093 on BERT-base, from 0.168 to 0.070 on GPT-2, and from 0.143 to 0.085 on Llama2-7b, while preserving task accuracy within 1–2 percentage points of the base model. On the toxicity generation task (GPT-2 large), Mean+Covariance Matching reduces expected maximum toxicity from 0.39 to 0.29 and toxic probability from 0.25 to 0.09, with a modest perplexity increase (24.66 → 30.7). The simpler Mean Matching variant also improves over the base model in all settings but is consistently outperformed by the covariance-matching variant on the bias metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the Bios gender-bias task, Mean+Covariance Matching outperforms LEACE (Belrose et al., 2023) on TPR across all three encoders (e.g., 0.093 vs. 0.137 on BERT-base; 0.070 vs. 0.093 on GPT-2) and also beats the post-processing method of Xian et al. (2023) by a wide margin (0.093 vs. 0.146 on BERT-base; 0.070 vs. 0.112 on GPT-2), while maintaining accuracy within ~1.5 points of the base model. On the toxicity generation task (Table 2), the proposed methods do not reach state-of-the-art: DExperts (Liu et al., 2021) achieves 0.21 expected max toxicity vs. 0.29 for Mean+Covariance Matching, GeDI (Gehman et al., 2020) achieves 0.24, and GOODTRIEVER (Pozzobon et al., 2023) achieves 0.22. However, the paper notes that these baselines require fine-tuning or gradient computation at inference time, whereas the affine steering interventions require neither. The proposed method is comparable to DAPT (Wu et al., 2021), which requires additional training on non-toxic data (0.27 vs. 0.29 expected max toxicity). In the controlled dialect-bias experiment, both Mean Matching and Mean+Covariance Matching effectively eliminate the correlation between dataset bias and classifier bias, with similar performance between the two variants.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the affine concept erasure framework (LEACE, Belrose et al. 2023; Ravfogel et al. 2020, 2022a,b) and its foundational notion of guardedness, repurposing the same least-squares optimization machinery to derive optimal *steering* functions rather than erasure functions. The key conceptual departure is showing that steering (shifting representations toward a target concept) is the natural complement to erasure: while erasure makes a concept unclassifiable, steering actively redirects behavior, which is essential for tasks like toxicity mitigation where the model must still *distinguish* toxic from non-toxic text. The mean-matching result (W*=I) provides the first theoretical justification for steering vectors (Subramani et al. 2022; Li et al. 2023), which were previously used heuristically, while the covariance-matching result (MiMiC) connects to the classical optimal transport solution of Knott and Smith (1984) and provably eliminates bias by neighbors (Gonen and Goldberg, 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper gives practitioners a principled, closed-form recipe for representation surgery that requires no fine-tuning or gradient computation at inference time, making it broadly applicable across architectures (BERT, GPT-2, Llama2). The main limitation is the linear nature of the interventions: the authors acknowledge that nonlinear generalizations would be more expressive, and the toxicity results (~25% reduction in expected max toxicity) fall short of methods requiring fine-tuning or retrieval. An open question is whether the training-inference distribution mismatch (fitting on last-token representations but intervening at every generation step) can be resolved, and whether the covariance-matching guarantee on bias by neighbors holds for local neighborhood structure rather than just in expectation.
