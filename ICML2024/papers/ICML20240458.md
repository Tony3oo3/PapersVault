## Problem

When comparing two binary classifiers under a fixed labeling budget, the standard practice is to collect multiple noisy labels per data point and aggregate them via majority vote to reduce label noise. This paper proves that this strategy is actually suboptimal: for the specific goal of identifying which classifier is better, spending the budget on a single label for more data points consistently outperforms collecting multiple labels per data point, overturning a widely held recommendation in benchmark design.

## Input / Output

The method takes two binary classifiers with a known accuracy margin ε, a fixed labeling budget k, and a crowdworker label accuracy q > 0.5 (each label independently correct with probability q). The budget is split as k = m·n where m is the number of labels per data point (must be odd for majority vote) and n is the number of data points. The output is the optimal m (shown to be m=1), the exact probability of correctly identifying the better classifier for any parameter configuration, and asymptotic sample size bounds derived from Cramér's theorem that are exponentially tighter than Hoeffding-based bounds.

## Method

The core contribution is a non-trivial application of Cramér's theorem from large deviations theory to a 'gap indicator' G ∈ {-1, 0, 1} that encodes whether the better or worse classifier wins on a given data point. For ternary random variables, the Cramér rate at z=0 admits the closed form -Λ*_X(0) = log(2√(xy) + z), where x, y, z are the probabilities of G taking values 1, -1, 0. The proof then shows that the Cramér rate for the single-label strategy (mn data points) strictly dominates that of the m-label strategy (n data points with majority-voted labels) by proving the derivative of the rate difference with respect to label accuracy q is always positive, using the additive recursion M_{2n+1}(q) = (2n+1)·C(2n,n)·∫₀^q xⁿ(1-x)ⁿ dx and a series of algebraic reductions. The result extends to correlated classifiers and labels under two mild assumptions (no biased label accuracy and no biased heterogeneity), and is verified numerically across over four billion parameter configurations.

## Results

The main theorem (Theorem 2) proves that for sufficiently large sample budgets, collecting a single noisy label for mn data points maximizes the probability of correctly identifying the better of two binary classifiers, outperforming any m>1 majority-vote aggregation strategy. Quantitatively, the Cramér-based bounds derived in the paper allow exponentially more classifier comparisons than standard Hoeffding-based bounds: for a label budget of k=1500, accuracies p=q=0.75, margin ε=0.1, and error tolerance δ=0.05, Cramér guarantees testability of more than 17 models in the single-label case, while Hoeffding is too loose to guarantee even 2 models. A numerical sweep over nearly five billion parameter configurations (spanning n, m, q_b, q_w, p_w, p_b^0, p_b^1) confirms that m=1 is consistently optimal, with the only exceptions being numerical instabilities at probabilities within 1e-12 of 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between the single-label strategy (m=1) and multi-label majority-vote aggregation (m>1) under a fixed labeling budget k=mn. The single-label strategy wins on the probability of correctly identifying the better classifier across all tested parameter configurations, with the advantage growing as the budget increases. Against the standard Hoeffding-based benchmarking argument, the Cramér-based analysis provides exponentially tighter bounds: the number of reliably rankable classifiers scales as e^{d·n·ε²} rather than e^{d'·n·ε²} with d>d', meaning the single-label approach permits e^{(d-d')nε²} times more comparisons at the same error tolerance. The paper also notes that the single-label advantage persists in two-thirds of parameter configurations even when Assumption 1 (q_b ≥ q_w) is violated, suggesting the result is robust beyond the stated conditions. No empirical ML benchmark baselines are compared, as the contribution is purely theoretical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Cramér's theorem from large deviations theory (Klenke, 2013) and the algebraic properties of majority voting (Boland et al., 1989) to analyze a question that prior benchmarking theory addressed only through Hoeffding's inequality. It departs sharply from the dominant practice in dataset and benchmark creation—collecting multiple labels per instance and aggregating via majority vote (as in ImageNet, CIFAR-10, MSRP, SST, SICK, MNLI, and medical datasets)—by proving this strategy is suboptimal for the specific goal of comparing binary classifiers. The paper also contrasts with related work on label aggregation for training (Wei et al., 2023; Sheng et al., 2008; Chen et al., 2021), explicitly positioning its contribution as about evaluation of already-trained classifiers rather than learning from noisy labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result overturns a time-honored recommendation in ML benchmark design: for comparing binary classifiers, spending a labeling budget on more data points with a single label each is provably better than fewer data points with multiple aggregated labels, yielding exponentially tighter sample size bounds than Hoeffding-based guarantees. The main theorem is asymptotic (sufficiently large n) and relies on assumptions about label accuracy correlations, though a conjecture for all n is supported by numerical verification across over four billion parameter configurations. Key open questions include extending the result to multiclass classification (where plurality vs. majority voting diverge), adaptive labeling strategies, and relaxing the no-biased-label-accuracy assumption, all of which could further refine practical benchmark design guidance.
