## Problem

Leading black-box variational inference (BBVI) methods rely on stochastic gradient descent to maximize the ELBO, which suffers from high-variance gradients, sensitivity to learning-rate hyperparameters, and slow convergence—especially in high dimensions or with full-covariance Gaussian families. While Gaussian score matching (GSM) avoids SGD, it does not minimize an explicit divergence and requires ad hoc heuristics for non-Gaussian targets. This paper asks whether a principled score-based divergence can be optimized via closed-form proximal updates that are both stable and fast.

## Input / Output

The algorithm requires only the target score function ∇log p(z) (computable from the log of the unnormalized target density), an initial Gaussian variational parameter (μ₀, Σ₀ ≻ 0), a batch size B, and a regularization parameter λ_t. At each iteration it draws B samples from the current Gaussian, computes batch statistics (means and covariances of samples and scores), and outputs an updated variational mean μ_{t+1} and covariance Σ_{t+1}. The covariance update is obtained by solving a quadratic matrix equation ΣUX + Σ = V in closed form, with computational cost O(D³) or O(D²B + B³) when the batch size B is much smaller than the dimension D.

## Method

BaM introduces a score-based divergence D(q;p) = E_q[‖∇log(q/p)‖²_Cov(q)] that is non-negative, affine-invariant, and computable from unnormalized targets. The optimization proceeds as a stochastic proximal point algorithm: a 'batch' step draws samples from the current Gaussian q_t and computes empirical statistics of the samples and target scores, while a 'match' step minimizes the empirical divergence plus a KL(q_t; q) regularizer over the Gaussian family. Crucially, the match step admits a closed-form solution—the covariance satisfies a quadratic matrix equation Σ_{t+1} U Σ_{t+1} + Σ_{t+1} = V with an explicit solution involving a matrix square root, and the mean is a convex combination of the previous mean and a score-matching target. For Gaussian targets in the infinite-batch limit, the paper proves exponential convergence of both mean and covariance errors at a rate controlled by initialization quality and regularization, with no restriction on λ beyond λ > 0.

## Results

On Gaussian targets with dimensions D=4 to 256, BaM converges orders of magnitude faster than ADVI in terms of forward KL divergence per gradient evaluation. On the CIFAR-10 VAE posterior inference task, BaM with batch size B=300 converges an order of magnitude (or more) faster than both ADVI and GSM in reconstruction MSE. Under a fixed budget of 3,000 gradient evaluations, BaM (B=300, T=10 iterations) achieves comparable reconstruction MSE to ADVI (B=10, T=300 iterations), but BaM's evaluations are largely parallelizable while ADVI's must be sequential. On hierarchical Bayesian models from posteriordb, BaM outperforms ADVI in relative mean error and converges more stably with larger batch sizes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ADVI (ELBO-based BBVI), BaM consistently wins on convergence speed across all four experimental settings: Gaussian targets, non-Gaussian sinh-arcsinh targets, hierarchical Bayesian models, and the CIFAR-10 deep generative model. The advantage is most dramatic on Gaussian targets where BaM converges 'orders of magnitude faster,' and on CIFAR-10 where BaM with B=300 reaches comparable MSE in ~10 iterations versus ~300 for ADVI. Against GSM (Gaussian Score Matching), the comparison is more nuanced: on Gaussian targets GSM is competitive at small batch sizes (B=2), but BaM benefits more from increasing batch size while GSM shows marginal gains beyond B=2. On highly skewed non-Gaussian targets (s=1.8), GSM's reverse KL divergence diverges while BaM remains stable. On hierarchical Bayesian models, GSM can converge faster than BaM at small batch sizes (B=8) but oscillates around the solution, whereas BaM converges more stably and benefits from larger batches (B=32). On CIFAR-10, BaM with B=300 outperforms GSM in both convergence speed and final reconstruction quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

BaM sits at the intersection of two prior lines of work: stochastic proximal point methods (Asi & Duchi, 2019) and Gaussian score matching (Modi et al., 2023). It departs from standard ELBO-based BBVI (ADVI) by replacing SGD with a closed-form proximal update, and from GSM by generalizing its single-sample, exact-score-matching objective to a batched, regularized score-based divergence that is affine-invariant. The score-based divergence itself is a specific instance of the weighted Fisher divergence (Barp et al., 2019) with the weighting matrix set to Cov(q), a choice that yields affine invariance and reduces to half the KL divergence for Gaussians sharing a covariance. Unlike prior proximal VI methods (Theis & Hoffman, 2015; Khan et al., 2015) that regularize the ELBO, BaM regularizes a score-based objective, avoiding linearization and requiring no structural assumptions on the target.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BaM offers a principled, hyperparameter-robust alternative to ELBO-based BBVI: the convergence guarantee holds for any fixed regularization level λ > 0, a stability property characteristic of proximal methods but absent from gradient-based VI. The empirical speedups (orders of magnitude fewer gradient evaluations than ADVI) are practically meaningful, especially since BaM's batch evaluations parallelize naturally. Key open questions include extending convergence guarantees to finite batches and non-Gaussian targets, developing score-based BBVI for non-Gaussian variational families, and adapting the method to large-scale settings via data subsampling. The score-based divergence also has independent value as a goodness-of-fit diagnostic for unnormalized models.
