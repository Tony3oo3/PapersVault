## Problem

When partitioning a DNN computation graph into k pipeline stages for inference, practitioners lack practical ways to certify how close their solution is to optimal. Standard combinatorial lower bounds (e.g., max node cost or average work) are far too weak to be useful, leaving engineers unable to decide whether to keep searching or accept a partition. This paper addresses the gap by designing a hierarchy of mixed-integer programming relaxations that produce strong per-instance lower bounds for the max-throughput partitioning problem (MTPP), which is NP-hard and admits no FPTAS.

## Input / Output

The method takes a directed acyclic computation graph with per-node work costs, parameter memory footprints, and output tensor sizes, along with hardware parameters (inter-block bandwidth B and per-block fast memory M) and a target number of stages k. It produces two outputs: (1) a concrete partition of the graph into k blocks via the SliceGraph algorithm, and (2) a hierarchy of lower bound certificates (simple, bottleneck, bottleneck-guess, exact) expressed as fractions of the best solution found, enabling per-instance approximation ratio guarantees. The MIP lower bounds are computed offline with time limits (15–60 min per instance), while SliceGraph runs in O(n²k + m log²n) time suitable for compiler integration.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a novel exact MIP formulation for MTPP using O(nk) binary variables (node-to-block assignment x, cumulative assignment y, and tensor-cut indicators c) with DAG constraints and tensor edge-cut constraints that count each tensor crossing a block boundary exactly once. From this exact formulation, the paper derives two successively cheaper relaxations: a three-superblock MIP (size independent of k) that consolidates all pre- and post-bottleneck blocks into single superblocks, and a bottleneck-guess MIP that enumerates which block is the bottleneck and averages superblock costs. For constructing partitions, SliceGraph reduces MTPP to a search over topological orderings: a biased random-key genetic algorithm (BRKGA) evolves node priority vectors that drive Kahn's algorithm to produce candidate topological orders, and a dynamic program with a two-dimensional Fenwick tree data structure computes the optimal k-way segmentation of each order in O(n²k) time. Across 369 production models, the exact MIP lower bound reaches 94.5% of the SliceGraph solution at k=16, versus only 46% for the standard combinatorial bound.

## Results

Across 369 production models, the exact MIP lower bound reaches 0.9452 of the best SliceGraph solution at k=16 (geometric mean), compared to only 0.4598 for the standard combinatorial bound — a 9.855× improvement in closing the optimality gap. For k≤16, the strongest MIP lower bound is on average 95.5% of the SliceGraph solution, while the simple combinatorial bound is only 46.0%. The bottleneck-guess formulation matches the exact MIP at k=2 (0.9901) and remains competitive up to k=16 (0.5780 vs. 0.9452), but degrades relative to exact as k grows due to ignored intra-superblock communication costs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is among its own hierarchy of lower bound methods rather than against external partitioning algorithms. The simple combinatorial bound (Lemma 3.3) is the weakest baseline, achieving only 0.4598 at k=16. The three-superblock bottleneck MIP improves this to 0.5770, and the bottleneck-guess variant adds a marginal gain to 0.5780 at k=16, but the exact MIP dominates all relaxations, reaching 0.9452. For partitioning quality, BRKGA outperforms random-100 and both MLA variants across all k values (e.g., at k=16, BRKGA achieves a 2.179× approximation ratio vs. 2.224× for MLA and 2.230× for random-100 relative to the simple bound). The bottleneck-guess and exact MIPs produce identical bounds at k=2 since the formulations are equivalent, but diverge for larger k as the superblock relaxation increasingly ignores intra-superblock communication costs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the acyclic graph partitioning literature (Cong et al., 1994; Nossack & Pesch, 2014; Özkaya & Catalyurek, 2022) to the specific setting of DNN inference pipeline parallelism, departing from training-focused formulations like PipeDream (Narayanan et al., 2019) by using tensor-level IO costs, overflow costs for parameter streaming, and a min-max bottleneck objective without communication-work concurrency. The key conceptual departure is the focus on per-instance lower bound certification via novel MIP relaxations (three-superblock and bottleneck-guess formulations) rather than worst-case approximation ratios, addressing what the authors call the 'practitioner's dilemma.' The partitioning algorithm (SliceGraph) repurposes standard tools—Kahn's topological sort, dynamic programming, and BRKGA (Gonçalves & Resende, 2011)—in a pipeline-specific way, while the MIP formulation introduces tensor edge-cut constraints that count each tensor once rather than each edge, a subtlety absent from classical cut-based partitioning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a concrete, computable stopping criterion for pipeline partitioning: the MIP lower bounds close the optimality gap by ~10x over standard combinatorial bounds, reaching 95.5% of the best solution for k≤16, which directly answers 'is my partition good enough?' for production models. A key limitation is that the strongest lower bounds (exact MIP) are four orders of magnitude slower than SliceGraph, making them offline analysis tools rather than in-compiler components; the bottleneck-guess formulation also degrades to the weaker bottleneck bound by k=64. Open questions include whether faster lower bound methods (e.g., LP-based or learned relaxations) could bring certification within compiler time budgets, and how these results extend to heterogeneous hardware or training workloads where the cost model differs substantially.
