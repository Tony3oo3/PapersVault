## Problem

In centralized selection systems (e.g., college admissions), when evaluation processes systematically underestimate the true utilities of disadvantaged candidates, standard stable-matching algorithms like Gale-Shapley produce assignments with substantially lower true utility and large disparities in the fraction of candidates from each group who receive their preferred institution. Prior work on group-wise representational constraints addresses only the single-institution case and does not guarantee preference-based fairness across multiple institutions, leaving a gap in understanding how to simultaneously achieve utility maximization, representational fairness, and preference-based fairness in the multi-institution setting with biased evaluations.

## Input / Output

The method takes n candidates with estimated (potentially biased) scalar utilities, ordinal preference rankings over p institutions, per-institution capacities k_ℓ, and binary group labels (advantaged G₁ vs. disadvantaged G₂), and produces a candidate-to-institution assignment M. Performance is evaluated using true (latent) utilities rather than the biased estimates used for assignment, yielding three metrics: a utility ratio U comparing achieved true utility to the optimal, a representational fairness ratio R comparing selection rates across groups, and a preference-based fairness ratio P comparing the fraction of each group assigned to their top-choice institution. The i.i.d. assumption requires utilities and preferences to be drawn from common distributions across groups, though empirical results validate robustness when this is relaxed.

## Method

The proposed algorithm A_inst-wise enforces institution-wise representational constraints by running the serial dictatorship mechanism A_st independently on each group, with each institution ℓ's capacity split proportionally (k_ℓ·n_j/n slots reserved for group j). This simple decomposition achieves near-optimal preference-based fairness (P ≈ 1), exact representational fairness (R = 1), and near-optimal utility (U ≈ 1) for arbitrary utility and preference distributions and any bias parameter β, without requiring knowledge of β or explicit preference constraints. The theoretical analysis hinges on two novel Lipschitz properties of A_st: changing one candidate's preference list alters at most p+1 top-choice assignments, and changing an institution's capacity by one unit affects at most p candidates. These Lipschitz bounds enable concentration inequalities for the number of candidates receiving their top choice, which are then used to bound the fairness and utility metrics via threshold-based arguments on the utility distribution.

## Results

On the IIT-JEE 2009 dataset (384,977 candidates, 33 institutions), the proposed institution-wise constraint algorithm A_inst-wise achieves preference-based fairness P^(1) ≥ 0.9 and P^(3) ≥ 0.95 across all Mallows dispersion parameters φ, while the unconstrained Gale-Shapley baseline A_st achieves P^(3) ≤ 0.25 and the group-wise constraint baseline A_group achieves P^(1) ≤ 0.5 and P^(3) ≤ 0.7. On synthetic data with non-i.i.d. preferences (β=1/4, n=1000, p=5), A_inst-wise maintains P^(1) ≥ 0.75 for all Kendall-Tau distances γ between group preference distributions, compared to P^(1) ≤ 0.7 for A_group. Theoretically, Theorem 3.2 guarantees P ≈ 1, R = 1, and U ≈ 1 for A_inst-wise under any utility and preference distributions, while Theorem 3.1 shows A_st degrades to P → 0, R → 0, and U → 2/3 as the bias parameter β → 0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three algorithms: the unconstrained Gale-Shapley serial dictatorship (A_st), the group-wise constraint algorithm (A_group, which selects the top |G_j|/n fraction from each group before running A_st), and the proposed institution-wise constraint algorithm (A_inst-wise, which runs A_st independently on each group with institution-level capacity splits). A_inst-wise decisively outperforms both baselines on preference-based fairness across all evaluated settings. On the IIT-JEE 2009 real-world data, A_inst-wise achieves P^(1) ≥ 0.9 versus P^(1) ≤ 0.5 for both baselines, and P^(3) ≥ 0.95 versus P^(3) ≤ 0.25 for A_st and ≤ 0.7 for A_group. On synthetic data with divergent group preferences (Kendall-Tau distance γ up to γ_max), A_inst-wise maintains P^(1) ≥ 0.75 while A_group drops to ≤ 0.7, with the gap being largest at γ=0 where A_group achieves only ≤ 0.3. The key insight is that group-wise constraints (which only ensure proportional selection rates) are insufficient to guarantee that disadvantaged candidates get their preferred institutions, whereas institution-wise constraints (which reserve proportional slots at each institution for each group) directly address the preference-based disparity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Kleinberg-Raghavan (2018) bias model and group-wise constraint framework from single-institution subset selection to the multi-institution centralized assignment problem with ordinal candidate preferences, building on the Gale-Shapley serial dictatorship mechanism. The key conceptual departure is showing that proportional group-wise representational constraints, which provably improve latent utility in the single-institution setting, are fundamentally insufficient for ensuring preference-based fairness when multiple institutions exist. The novel institution-wise constraint (reserving proportional capacity at each institution for each group) is a strict generalization of the single-institution approach, and the proofs rely on new Lipschitz and concentration properties of the Gale-Shapley algorithm in the centralized (common-ranking) setting that do not hold for general stable matching.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and practically relevant gap: in real centralized admission systems (JOSAA, NYC/Boston schools, Gaokao), group-wise constraints that look fair in aggregate can still systematically assign disadvantaged candidates to lower-preference institutions, with downstream effects on educational and economic opportunity. The institution-wise constraint is attractive because it requires no knowledge of the bias parameter β and is strategy-proof, making it implementable in practice. Key limitations include the i.i.d. assumption on utilities and preferences, the single-utility-per-candidate model (institutions cannot value candidates differently), and the restriction to two groups; extending to non-i.i.d. settings or institution-specific utilities remains open. The paper also notes that discrimination is systemic and this algorithmic intervention is only one component of a broader solution.
