## Problem

Prior RL work on online optimization has focused on training algorithms for problems with known optimal competitive ratios, leaving open whether RL can aid the complementary direction of constructing hard instances to improve theoretical hardness bounds. This paper tackles this gap on Online Matching with Stochastic Rewards (OMSR), an unclosed problem where the best-known upper bound (0.621) and lower bound (0.572) leave a wide gap, by asking whether adversarial RL can both learn robust algorithms and reveal structural properties of worst-case instances that enable provable theoretical improvements.

## Input / Output

The adversary agent consumes the current partial probability matrix P_{n×m} (columns revealed sequentially) as its state and outputs the neighbor subset for the next arriving online vertex, effectively constructing the full instance column-by-column. The algorithm agent receives the current column's edge indicators, a vector of per-vertex failure attempt counts, and a binary success indicator vector, then outputs a one-hot matching decision over n+1 options (n offline vertices or skip). The theoretical output is a family of structured bipartite graphs G_n satisfying consistency and exclusivity properties, from which a 0.597 upper bound on the competitive ratio is derived.

## Method

The framework trains two RL agents adversarially and iteratively: an adversary (adv) formulated as an MDP that constructs hard probability matrices column-by-column using the Cross-Entropy method (batch 1024, elite fraction 30%), and an algorithm agent (alg) trained with DQN to learn robust matching policies against a mixture of learned and random instance distributions. The key insight is that statistical analysis of adv's converged outputs reveals two structural properties—consistency (online vertices partition into groups with identical neighbors) and exclusivity (later groups' neighbor sets are either subsets of or disjoint from earlier groups')—which both reduce the action space and serve as the backbone of the theoretical proof. The proof proceeds by showing Balance is optimal on all instances satisfying both properties (Lemma 3.5), then constructing a specific instance G_7* in the restricted family G_n where Balance achieves only 0.597 competitive ratio via a dynamic-programming calculation over 2^7 success/failure cases, yielding the improved upper bound that beats the prior 0.621.

## Results

The paper improves the upper bound on the competitive ratio of OMSR from 0.621 (Mehta & Panigrahi, 2012) to 0.597, achieved on the family G_7 of bipartite graphs satisfying consistency and exclusivity properties. The learned algorithm (alg) outperforms the SOTA Balance algorithm across all tested settings: at n=6 offline vertices, the learned algorithm achieves 0.635 CR (equal probability) and 0.614 CR (unequal probability), versus 0.615 and 0.594 for Balance respectively. The performance gap widens with increasing n, e.g., at n=6 the unequal-probability gap is 0.020 (0.614 vs 0.594).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Balance (Huang & Zhang, 2020), the learned algorithm consistently outperforms the SOTA across all tested configurations. In the equal-probability setting, the gap grows from 0.001 at n=2 to 0.020 at n=6 (0.635 vs 0.615); in the unequal-probability setting, the gap is even larger, reaching 0.020 at n=6 (0.614 vs 0.594) and 0.036 at n=2 (0.658 vs 0.622). Against the previous hardness bound from Mehta & Panigrahi (2012), the paper's G_n family of instances (satisfying consistency and exclusivity properties) is empirically harder than the MP12 worst-case instances: the learned algorithm achieves lower CR on G_6* and G_7* than on MP12's G_3 and G_4 (Figure 12), and the theoretical upper bound is tightened from 0.621 to 0.597. The learned algorithm also outperforms Balance on known hard instances for OBM (triangular graphs, thick-z graphs) and random graphs, with the advantage increasing over training iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the adversarial RL framework of Pinto et al. (2017) to the online matching domain, where prior RL-for-online-optimization work (Kong et al., 2019; Alomrani et al., 2021; Zuzic et al., 2020) has exclusively targeted problems with known optimal competitive ratios. The key departure is applying this framework to an *unclosed* problem (OMSR, Mehta & Panigrahi, 2012) where the optimal ratio is unknown, and—critically—using the learned adversary's structural patterns to derive a provable theoretical upper bound rather than merely training a better algorithm. The Balance algorithm (Huang & Zhang, 2020) serves as both the initial environment for the adversary and the primary algorithmic baseline throughout.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that RL-learned adversary patterns can be distilled into formal structural properties (consistency, exclusivity) that feed into a theoretical proof, improving the OMSR upper bound from 0.621 to 0.597. The framework opens a new research direction: using adversarial RL not just to find robust algorithms but to illuminate the hardness structure of unclosed online problems. A major limitation acknowledged by the authors is that while the adversary's patterns are interpretable, the algorithm agent's learned policy resists similar distillation, leaving the lower bound unimproved. Whether this approach generalizes beyond OMSR to other open-ended online problems remains an open question.
