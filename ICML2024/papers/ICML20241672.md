## Problem

Diffusion models are increasingly used to parameterize actor policies in RL, but prior off-policy methods either rely on a behavior cloning objective that ignores the score-based structure of diffusion models, or backpropagate through the full multi-step diffusion evaluation, which is computationally expensive and does not exploit the model's internal structure. This paper asks: can we train a diffusion model policy in an off-policy actor-critic setting by directly leveraging its score (denoising) function, without a fixed target distribution?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241672/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy takes a continuous state vector s ∈ R^s as input and produces a continuous action vector a ∈ R^a through K iterative denoising steps of a diffusion model, starting from Gaussian noise. The score network Ψ_φ operates on (state, noisy-action) pairs at each denoising step, and the critic Q_θ operates on (state, action) pairs to output a scalar value. Unlike standard actor-critic methods that output a single action sample, the diffusion policy generates actions through a multi-step stochastic process, and the training signal for the score network is the action gradient ∇_a Q(s,a) computed from the critic rather than a log-probability term.

## Method

The core contribution is Q-score matching (QSM), which trains the diffusion model's denoising score Ψ_φ to match the action gradient of the learned Q-function, ∇_a Q(s,a), via a simple MSE regression loss. The theoretical justification (Theorems 4.1 and 4.3) shows that in both deterministic and stochastic continuous-time settings, the optimal score Ψ* is collinear with ∇_a Q^{Ψ*}(s,a), so iteratively aligning Ψ toward ∇_a Q strictly increases the Q-value. Practically, this means only the single denoising submodel needs to be differentiated (not the full K-step diffusion chain), making training as efficient as standard diffusion model training. The full algorithm combines DDPG-style twin-critic updates with N-step returns for the Q-function and the QSM regression loss for the score network, with actions sampled by iteratively denoising from Gaussian noise at each environment step.

## Results

On six DeepMind Control Suite tasks (ranging from easy to medium difficulty), QSM matches or outperforms both SAC and TD3 in terms of final reward and sample efficiency to reach high rewards, despite training expressive diffusion model policies rather than simple Gaussian actors. QSM also learns multi-modal policies (e.g., in cartpole swingup, it captures both optimal action modes at ±1), whereas Gaussian-parameterized baselines tend to collapse to a single mode. When compared directly with Diffusion-QL under identical hyperparameters, QSM achieves comparable performance while requiring significantly less computation for the policy update (differentiating only through the denoising model rather than the full diffusion evaluation) and scales gracefully with increasing diffusion depth, whereas Diffusion-QL becomes unstable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SAC and TD3 (both using Gaussian or tanh-Gaussian actors), QSM achieves similar or better performance across all six evaluated DeepMind Control Suite tasks, matching their sample efficiency despite the added expressiveness of a diffusion model policy. Against Diffusion-QL (Wang et al., 2022), which also trains a diffusion model policy but via backpropagation through the full diffusion evaluation, QSM shows mostly comparable final performance but with two key advantages: (1) significantly lower per-step computation for the policy gradient since only the denoising submodel is differentiated, and (2) more consistent training without getting stuck at suboptimal solutions. When the number of diffusion sampling steps is increased (Figure 9), QSM's performance scales smoothly while Diffusion-QL becomes unstable, confirming that QSM's use of the score-based structure is essential for training deeper diffusion policies. Additionally, the paper demonstrates that naively applying standard policy gradients to a diffusion model policy (Figure 6, Figure 8) fails to learn optimal policies in the off-policy setting, motivating QSM's score-matching approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends classical score matching (Hyvärinen & Dayan, 2005) from the generative modeling setting to reinforcement learning by replacing the target score with the Q-function's action gradient, yielding the Q-score matching (QSM) objective. It departs from prior diffusion-model RL work (Diffusion-QL, IDQL) that either relies on behavior cloning losses or backpropagates through the full iterative denoising chain; QSM instead differentiates only through the single denoising network, analogous to standard diffusion training. The Q-function optimization routine follows DDPG, and the continuous-time SDE formulation draws on stochastic optimal control, though the paper explicitly avoids assuming a state dynamics model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QSM provides the first theoretical justification (Theorems 4.1, 4.3) for why matching a diffusion policy's score to the Q-gradient strictly increases expected return, and shows this yields implicitly multi-modal, explorative policies without explicit entropy regularization. Open questions include convergence rate analysis, joint optimization of the noise covariance Σ_a (which the authors note may connect to maximum-entropy RL), and whether the approach scales to harder tasks requiring deeper diffusion chains. The experimental scope is limited to medium-difficulty DeepMind Control Suite tasks, leaving open whether QSM's advantages persist in high-dimensional or long-horizon settings.
