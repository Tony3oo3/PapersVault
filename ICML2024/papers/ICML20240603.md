## Problem

Music possesses unique structural attributes (key, tempo, instrumentation, harmonic progression) absent from non-musical audio, yet no existing model achieves music understanding comparable to recent multimodal advances in vision and speech. Prior audio-language models either treat music as a single undifferentiated class, are trained for a single task in isolation, or lack the richly annotated, diverse training data needed for multitask generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240603/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 25-second audio waveform (the maximum context of the Jukebox encoder) alongside a free-form text instruction specifying the desired task (e.g., 'What is the key of this song?'). The audio is encoded into 4800-dimensional vectors at 345 Hz, then mean-pooled within 100 ms frames to yield a 10 Hz sequence of 1.2M floats per clip—roughly 6× the embedding size of CLIP ViT-L/14—preserving temporal structure. Output is autoregressive natural-language text, enabling both closed-form answers (a key name, a BPM number, an instrument list) and open-ended responses (captions, multi-paragraph reasoning).

## Method

LLARK is a 12B-parameter model that couples a frozen Jukebox-5B generative audio encoder (36th-layer output) with a Llama2-7B-chat language model via a single linear projection layer, trained end-to-end with cross-entropy loss on response tokens while the encoder weights remain fixed. The key data contribution is a two-stage instruction-tuning pipeline: first, musical features (tempo, key/mode, timestamped chords, beat grid) are extracted from raw audio using madmom and appended to each dataset's native annotations; second, GPT-3.5/GPT-4 is prompted with the augmented metadata to generate diverse question–response pairs across three task families (music understanding, captioning, higher-level reasoning), yielding ~1.2M instruction pairs from 164K tracks. Ablation studies confirm that the generative Jukebox encoder substantially outperforms the contrastive CLAP encoder on all music-understanding tasks, and that Llama 2's numeric tokenization is critical for regression tasks like tempo estimation.

## Results

On zero-shot music understanding tasks, LLARK achieves 0.70 MIREX score on key estimation (GiantSteps), 0.86 Acc2 on tempo estimation (GiantSteps), 0.56 Acc@1 on genre classification (GTZAN), and 0.99 F1 on instrument identification (MusicNet), approaching task-specific fine-tuned SOTA models (0.743, 0.925, 0.835, respectively) while substantially outperforming general-purpose multimodal baselines. In human-evaluated captioning, LLARK achieves a 100% win rate against all four captioning baselines on MusicCaps and MusicNet, and 95.7–100% on FMA. On reasoning tasks, LLARK outperforms both IB-LLM and LTU-AS in audio-text matching (Figure 4) and GPT-4 musical-detail comparisons (57–91% win rates in Table 4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against ImageBind-LLM (IB-LLM), LLARK wins decisively on key estimation (0.70 vs. 0.048 MIREX), tempo estimation (0.86 vs. 0.05 Acc2), and instrument identification on both MedleyDB (0.31 vs. 0.25 F1) and MusicNet (0.99 vs. 0.86 F1), but loses on GTZAN genre classification (0.56 vs. 0.71 Acc@1), which the authors attribute to ImageBind's exposure to popular music and genre tags during pretraining. Against LTU-AS, LLARK wins on every music understanding task, with the largest margins on key (0.70 vs. 0.00) and tempo (0.86 vs. 0.00). Relative to task-specific fine-tuned SOTA models, LLARK narrows the gap to within 0.04–0.07 on key and tempo but remains 0.28 behind on GTZAN genre (0.56 vs. 0.835), though it exceeds the MusicNet instrument-ID reference (0.99 vs. ~0.93 frame-level F1). In captioning, LLARK achieves a 100% human-preference win rate over LLaMA-Adapter v2, WAC, and LTU on MusicCaps and MusicNet, and 95.7–100% over LP-MusicCaps across all three captioning datasets. On reasoning, LLARK surpasses both IB-LLM and LTU-AS in audio-text matching (Figure 4) and GPT-4 musical-detail comparisons (57–91% win rates in Table 4), with the largest gaps against IB-LLM (e.g., 90.5% vs. 57.2% on MusicNet).

## Relation to Prior Work

LLARK extends the multimodal instruction-tuning paradigm established in vision (LLaVA, InstructBLIP, Mini-GPT4) to the music domain, but makes a key architectural departure by using a generative audio encoder (Jukebox) rather than the contrastive encoders (CLIP, CLAP) typical in other multimodal settings. The metadata augmentation strategy—extracting tempo, key, chords, and beat grids via madmom and injecting them into LLM-generated instruction pairs—is novel for music instruction-tuning and serves as a hallucination guardrail. Prior music-specific multimodal works (MU-LLaMA, LP-MusicCaps, WAC) were trained for individual tasks; LLARK unifies them under a single instruction-following framework, building on Castellon et al.'s observation that Jukebox representations are effective for MIR.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LLARK demonstrates that a single instruction-tuned model can handle diverse music understanding, captioning, and reasoning tasks at competitive quality, reducing the need for task-specific models in MIR. The finding that a generative encoder substantially outperforms a contrastive one for music understanding challenges the prevailing assumption that contrastive pretraining is the best route to multimodal audio representations. Key limitations include the 25-second context window, reliance on non-expert human evaluators, and training on limited open-source data; open questions include whether extending context, adding new datasets (rather than more tracks), and improving feature extractors will yield further gains, and whether high-quality music benchmarks can be developed to enable more rigorous evaluation.
