## Problem

Likelihood-based deep generative models (normalizing flows, diffusion models) trained on complex datasets paradoxically assign higher likelihoods to OOD data from simpler datasets, yet never generate such OOD samples. This two-pronged contradiction renders naive likelihood thresholding unreliable for OOD detection and has lacked a conclusive geometric explanation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240922/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single pre-trained likelihood-based DGM (RQ-NSF normalizing flow or variance-preserving diffusion model) and a query image (28×28 grayscale or 32×32×3 RGB). It produces a binary in-distribution/OOD decision by jointly evaluating the log-likelihood p_θ(x) and a local intrinsic dimension estimate LID_θ(x) at the query point. The LID threshold τ is calibrated per-dataset via binary search against LPCA estimates on 80 training samples, avoiding manual hyperparameter tuning.

## Method

The key geometric insight is that OOD data from 'simpler' sources concentrates on lower-dimensional manifolds, so the model assigns high density but negligible probability mass (small contiguous 'volume') around those points; LID captures this volume effect. For NFs, LID_θ(x) is estimated as the count of singular values of the Jacobian J(z) exceeding a threshold τ (Equation 7); for DMs, it is d minus the count of singular values exceeding τ in a matrix S(x) built from k score evaluations s_θ(x_{t_0}^i, t_0) + x_{t_0}^i/2 at a small time t_0 (Equation 9). The dual-threshold algorithm (Algorithm 1) flags a point as OOD if either log p_θ(x) < ψ_L (case i: low density) or LID_θ(x) < ψ_LID (case ii: high density but low probability mass), and classifies it as in-distribution only when both are large (case iii). This differs from standard single-threshold likelihood OOD detection solely in reclassifying case (ii) as OOD, requiring no auxiliary models, external data, or retraining.

## Results

On the most pathological NF task (FMNIST-trained vs. MNIST OOD), the dual threshold method achieves 0.951 AUC-ROC versus 0.073 for likelihood-only, a dramatic improvement. On CIFAR10 vs. SVHN (NF), it reaches 0.936 AUC-ROC compared to 0.063 for likelihood-only. For diffusion models, the dual threshold method achieves 0.944 AUC-ROC on CIFAR10 vs. SVHN (vs. 0.064 for DM likelihood) and matches or beats the strongest DM baseline (reconstruction with LPIPS) on 5 out of 8 tasks. Overall, the method outperforms all fully unsupervised baselines for NFs and performs on par with the strongest DM baseline, while also matching or exceeding the state-of-the-art NAE (energy-based model) on four of eight tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the NF likelihood baseline, the dual threshold method achieves massive gains on pathological tasks: 0.951 vs. 0.073 (FMNIST→MNIST) and 0.936 vs. 0.063 (CIFAR10→SVHN), while matching it on non-pathological tasks (1.000 vs. 1.000 on MNIST→FMNIST). The score-norm proxy (Grathwohl et al., 2020) performs inconsistently, ranging from 0.080 to 0.722 across NF tasks, and is uniformly outperformed by the dual threshold method. The complexity correction method (Serrà et al., 2020), despite having access to image compression information, is beaten on all NF tasks except CIFAR10→CelebA (0.655 vs. 0.479 in the paper's favor, but 0.939 vs. 0.951 on FMNIST→MNIST where the baseline is close). The likelihood ratios method (Ren et al., 2019), which uses an auxiliary reference model, is uniformly outperformed across all NF tasks. For DMs, the dual threshold method (0.944 on CIFAR10→SVHN) beats the reconstruction baseline (Graham et al., 2023, 0.876) on 5 of 8 tasks despite the latter relying on a pre-trained LPIPS encoder, and decisively outperforms the DM likelihood ratios method (Goodier & Campbell, 2023) on all tasks. The NAE (Yoon et al., 2021), a purpose-built EBM for OOD detection, is the strongest overall baseline; the dual threshold method matches or exceeds it on four of eight tasks (e.g., 0.951 vs. 0.995 on FMNIST→MNIST is a loss, but 0.936 vs. 0.919 on CIFAR10→SVHN is a win) while using a general-purpose generative model rather than one explicitly designed for OOD detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LID estimation frameworks of Stanczuk et al. (2022) for diffusion models and Horvat & Pfister (2022) for normalizing flows, adapting both to a practical OOD detection setting with a single pre-trained model. It departs from prior probabilistic explanations of the OOD paradox (typical set, entropy, reparameterization invariance) by providing a geometric account: OOD data on lower-dimensional manifolds can receive high density yet negligible probability mass, a distinction captured by LID. The paper is complementary to Kirichenko et al. (2020) and Schirrmeister et al. (2020), who explain why the pathological region contains OOD data (high-frequency feature bias), whereas this work explains why high density does not imply high probability mass. Unlike Zhang et al. (2021), who also invoke probability mass but blame poor model fit, this paper shows the paradox persists even when likelihoods cannot distinguish OOD from generated samples, and it connects the mechanism to LID with a concrete estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual threshold method (likelihood + LID) is remarkably simple, requires no auxiliary models or retraining, and matches or surpasses purpose-built OOD detectors like NAEs across both NF and DM backbones, suggesting that the geometric structure already encoded in a general-purpose DGM is sufficient for reliable OOD detection. Key limitations include the restriction to NFs and DMs (where LID is tractable via Jacobian/score rank), acknowledged discrepancies in the DM LID estimator compared to other methods, and the open question of extending to EBMs, VAEs, and latent-space models. The discovery that generated samples often receive lower likelihoods than OOD data opens a new line of inquiry into inductive biases in generative models that remains unexplained.
