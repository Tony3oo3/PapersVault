## Problem

Prior work on private fair learning has been limited to the classification setting, leaving private fair regression unaddressed. A fundamental tension exists: exact group fairness criteria like statistical parity require precise group-level statistics, while differential privacy only permits noisy estimates. This paper fills the gap by providing the first differentially private algorithm for learning fair regressors under statistical parity, with explicit error-privacy-fairness trade-off guarantees.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242226/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a pre-trained regressor f: X×A→R, unlabeled samples {(x_i, a_i)} (no response labels needed), a known interval [s,t] containing the regressor's image, a number of bins k, a fairness tolerance α, and a privacy budget ε. It outputs a randomized attribute-aware fair regressor f̄(x,a) = g_a ∘ h ∘ f(x,a), where h discretizes the prediction to bin midpoints and g_a is a group-specific optimal transport mapping. The post-processing stage requires only unlabeled data, so labeled data can be dedicated entirely to the pre-training stage.

## Method

The algorithm proceeds in three steps: (1) privately estimate the conditional output distributions of f for each group via histogram density estimation with the Laplace mechanism, followed by re-normalization using L∞ isotonic regression on partial sums to produce valid CDFs; (2) compute the Wasserstein barycenter (or, for approximate SP, target distributions within a KS ball of radius α/2) by solving a linear program with O(k²|A|) variables; (3) extract the optimal transports from the LP solution as k×k coupling matrices and apply them as randomized post-processing mappings. Privacy is achieved solely in step 1, with steps 2–3 being DP-immune post-processing. The number of bins k controls a bias-variance trade-off: fewer bins reduce variance (improving fairness) but increase discretization error (worsening MSE), while more bins do the opposite. The paper proves that the excess risk of the optimal fair regressor equals the value of the Wasserstein barycenter problem, establishing that post-processing the Bayes regressor recovers the optimal fair solution.

## Results

On the Law School dataset (|A|=4, n≈21,983), setting k=1 bins achieves exact statistical parity (Δ_SP=0) at an MSE of 0.6772, while increasing k reduces discretization error but increases variance from both sampling and DP noise. The results are notably insensitive to the privacy budget ε until very strict levels (ε=0.1, 0.5 with k=180), consistent with the theoretical condition n ≳ k|A|/ε². On both datasets, the cost of discretization (the horizontal offset from zero fairness) is small compared to the fairness gains from post-processing, and using fewer bins always improves fairness at the expense of MSE — e.g., k=36 bins nearly dominates k=60 bins on the Law School dataset at ε=0.1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against any baselines, as the authors state they are not aware of any existing differentially private algorithms for learning fair regressors. Prior work on private fair learning (Jagielski et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019; Mozannar et al., 2020; Tran et al., 2021) has been restricted to the classification setting, leaving private fair regression as an open problem. The authors note that adapting existing methods (e.g., DP-SGD as in Tran et al., 2021) to this setting would require significant additional work and is left to future work. For non-private fair regression comparisons (ε=∞), readers are referred to Chzhen et al. (2020), on which the post-processing framework is based.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fair regression post-processing framework of Chzhen et al. (2020) and Le Gouic et al. (2020), which compute Wasserstein barycenters and optimal transports to remap a regressor's outputs for statistical parity, by making the entire pipeline differentially private. The privacy mechanism relies on private histogram density estimation (Diakonikolas et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2012) with an additional isotonic-regression re-normalization step to ensure valid PMFs after Laplace noise, a complication absent from prior private distribution learning. The theoretical equivalence between learning a fair regressor and post-processing the Bayes regressor (Theorem 3.1) generalizes the exact-SP result of Le Gouic et al. (2020) to approximate SP, following the analysis of Xian et al. (2023) for classification. Crucially, this is the first work to address private fair *regression*; all prior private fair learning algorithms (Jagielski et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019; Mozannar et al., 2020; Tran et al., 2021) were restricted to classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a clear gap by providing the first theoretically analyzed algorithm for differentially private fair regression, with explicit error-privacy-fairness trade-off bounds. The post-processing design is practically appealing because it decouples fairness from accuracy: the base regressor can be trained to optimality without fairness constraints, and only unlabeled data is needed for the post-processing stage. Key limitations include the restriction to the attribute-aware setting (sensitive attribute available at prediction time), statistical parity as the sole fairness notion, and squared loss; the authors outline but do not implement the extension to the attribute-blind setting. The known incompatibility between exact group fairness and DP (Cummings et al., 2019) means approximate fairness is unavoidable, and the paper's analysis of how the number of histogram bins k trades off bias against variance (and hence fairness) provides actionable guidance, though the optimal k is data-dependent and private hyperparameter tuning remains an open practical challenge.
