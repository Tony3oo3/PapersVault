## Problem

The computational complexity of finding approximate second-order stationary points (SOSPs) in unconstrained non-convex optimization was an open question. While first-order stationary points (FOSPs) in the unconstrained setting were recently shown to be PLS-complete, and constrained SOSPs were known to be NP-hard, the unconstrained SOSP problem sat in an unresolved gap between these two results. This paper resolves the question by proving the problem is PLS-complete, showing that the second-order landscape reverses the first-order trend where unconstrained problems are harder than constrained ones.

## Input / Output

The input is a twice differentiable function f: R^d → R satisfying boundedness (|f(x)| ≤ B), Lipschitz gradient (constant L), and Hessian Lipschitzness (constant ρ), accessed through polynomial-time Turing machines that return f(x), ∇f(x), and ∇²f(x) at any query point. The output is a point x* ∈ R^d satisfying both ||∇f(x*)|| ≤ ε and λ_min(∇²f(x*)) ≥ −√(ρε), i.e., an approximate SOSP. The white-box formulation encodes the Turing machines as Boolean circuits, and the reduction must be polynomial in the circuit size and polylogarithmic in B, L, ρ, and 1/ε.

## Method

For PLS membership, the authors construct a LOCALOPT instance whose neighbor function alternates between steepest coordinate descent (when the gradient norm exceeds ε) and negative curvature steps along the minimum Hessian eigenvector (when at an approximate FOSP with significant negative curvature), mirroring a standard SOSP-finding algorithm. They define valid/invalid grid points to restrict the search to the region reachable by the algorithm from the origin, ensuring every LOCALOPT local optimum is a valid SOSP. For PLS hardness, they reduce from the PLS-complete ITER problem to HESSIAN-FOSP (FOSP with an additional Hessian Lipschitzness requirement) by embedding ITER instances into a 2D periodic function constructed via biquintic (degree-5 in each variable) polynomial interpolation over a grid. The biquintic scheme is necessary because the standard bicubic interpolation used in prior FOSP hardness proofs has only 16 free parameters against 21 independent C² continuity constraints; biquintic interpolation provides 36 parameters and provably introduces no spurious stationary points outside the ITER solution regions, establishing PLS-hardness even for d = 2.

## Results

The paper proves that finding an approximate second-order stationary point (ε-SOSP) in unconstrained optimization is PLS-complete: it is both in PLS (Theorem 1, via reduction to LOCALOPT) and PLS-hard (Theorem 2, via reduction from ITER through HESSIAN-FOSP). A key corollary (Corollary 1) shows that adding Hessian-Lipschitzness and second-order oracle access does not reduce the complexity of finding ε-FOSPs — the problem remains PLS-hard even with C² smoothness. Additionally, Corollary 2 establishes a query complexity lower bound of Ω(1/ε) for any deterministic algorithm finding ε-SOSPs in d=2, matching the known lower bound for ε-FOSPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is with the first-order complexity landscape established by Hollender & Zampetakis [16] and Fearnley et al. [13]. For first-order stationary points, the unconstrained problem (PLS-complete) is believed to be strictly harder than the constrained problem (CLS-complete), since CLS ⊂ PLS. The second-order results reverse this trend: the unconstrained SOSP problem is PLS-complete, while the constrained SOSP problem is NP-hard [31], making the unconstrained version strictly easier under the standard complexity conjectures. The paper also contrasts with the intuition that second-order smoothness (Hessian Lipschitzness) should reduce hardness — Theorem 3 shows HESSIAN-FOSP remains PLS-hard, and Corollary 1 confirms that C² access provides no complexity advantage over C¹ for finding FOSPs. The biquintic interpolation technique (requiring degree 5 in each variable, versus degree 3 in prior bicubic constructions [13, 16]) is the key technical novelty enabling the C² continuity needed for the hardness reduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the white-box complexity framework of Hollender & Zampetakis [16] (who proved FOSP is PLS-complete in the unconstrained setting) and the PLS Box construction of Fearnley et al. [13] to the second-order setting. The key technical departure is the replacement of bicubic interpolation with a biquintic interpolation scheme, necessary to maintain C² continuity and Hessian-Lipschitzness when embedding ITER instances into a smooth function. Conceptually, the paper inverts the first-order complexity trend: while unconstrained FOSP (PLS) is harder than constrained FOSP (CLS), unconstrained SOSP (PLS) is easier than constrained SOSP (NP-hard), reconnecting with the intuitive expectation that removing constraints simplifies the problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves an open question explicitly posed in [16] and establishes that access to second-order oracles (Hessian evaluations) does not reduce the worst-case complexity of finding stationary points in unconstrained non-convex optimization — a result with implications for the design of second-order methods. The paper also yields a matching Ω(1/ε) query lower bound for d=2, closing the gap with known upper bounds. Open questions include the complexity of third-order stationary points and whether relaxed notions of second-order stationarity in restricted domains can avoid NP-hardness while remaining in PLS or CLS.
