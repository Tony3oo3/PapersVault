## Problem

Existing bivariate causal discovery methods either impose hard restrictions on model classes (e.g., additive noise, linearity) to guarantee identifiability but suffer from misspecification on real-world data, or use flexible models without formal identifiability guarantees. This paper asks whether causal direction within a Markov equivalence class can be identified using softer Bayesian priors instead of hard model restrictions, while retaining the ability to model a wide range of joint distributions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240426/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N iid bivariate observations (x_i, y_i) as input, with all datasets normalised to unit variance following Reisach et al. (2021) to ensure scale invariance. It produces a causal direction decision (X→Y or X←Y) by comparing the marginal likelihoods of two Bayesian causal models, along with the log posterior ratio as a confidence measure. Evaluation uses ROC AUC with 50% of each causal direction present to avoid directional bias.

## Method

The paper formulates bivariate causal discovery as Bayesian model selection between two Bayesian Causal Models (BCMs), each encoding the Independent Causal Mechanisms (ICM) assumption via a separable prior over the causal factorisation. The key theoretical result (Proposition 4.7) shows that even when the underlying causal models are distribution-equivalent (rendering maximum likelihood unable to discriminate), the marginal likelihoods differ unless the priors are 'separable-compatible'—i.e., the ICM assumption must also hold in the anti-causal factorisation. For the concrete model, they use a conditional Gaussian Process Latent Variable Model (GPLVM) as the flexible prior, which can capture highly non-Gaussian and heteroskedastic densities, with marginal likelihoods approximated via variational inference. They further derive a statistical test quantifying the probability of error under correct specification (via total variation distance between data distributions) and a bound on error under model misspecification.

## Results

The proposed GPLVM method achieves the highest ROC AUC on 4 out of 5 benchmark datasets: CE-Cha (81.9), CE-Multi (97.7), CE-Net (98.9), and CE-Tueb (78.3). On CE-Gauss, it scores 89.3, slightly below ANM's 90.7, which is the only dataset where a restrictive-assumption method outperforms it. The method's key advantage is consistent performance across all data-generating assumptions, whereas baselines with hard restrictions (e.g., LiNGAM, ANM) show large variance across datasets (e.g., LiNGAM drops to 3.3 on CE-Net).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPLVM outperforms all 9 baselines on 4 of 5 datasets. On CE-Net, it achieves 98.9 ROC AUC, beating the next-best CDCI (94.3) by 4.6 points and ANM (87.8) by 11.1 points. On CE-Multi, GPLVM (97.7) edges out CDCI (96.0) and SLOPPY (95.7). On CE-Cha, GPLVM (81.9) leads PNL (78.6) by 3.3 points, while restrictive methods like LiNGAM (57.8) and ANM (43.7) perform poorly. The sole exception is CE-Gauss, where ANM (90.7) and CDCI (91.8) slightly exceed GPLVM (89.3), consistent with ANM's additive-noise assumption matching that dataset's generation process. Methods with hard identifiability assumptions (LiNGAM, ANM, PNL) show extreme variance across datasets, while flexible methods without strict guarantees (CGNN, GPI, CDCI) perform more consistently but still trail GPLVM on most benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian model selection approach to bivariate causal discovery initiated by Friedman and Nachman (2000) and Stegle et al. (2010), who used GP-based latent variable models but lacked a formal account of why the method works. The key departure is from the dominant paradigm of imposing hard restrictions on model classes (ANM, LiNGAM, PNL) to guarantee identifiability: here, the ICM assumption is encoded in the *prior structure* (separability of priors over the causal factorisation) rather than in the model class itself. The theoretical contribution—showing that separable-compatibility is a necessary condition for Bayesian distribution-equivalence—formalises why the marginal likelihood can break the symmetry that maximum likelihood cannot, even for distribution-equivalent causal models. The GPLVM (Titsias and Lawrence, 2010; Dutordoir et al., 2018) serves as the flexible model architecture, with variational inference used to approximate marginal likelihoods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that causal direction can be identified without the rigid model restrictions that limit applicability to real-world data, while still providing a statistical test (via total variation distance) to quantify the probability of error. Under mild model misspecification, the error estimate remains accurate, making the framework robust in practice. However, the scope is limited to the bivariate case, and the reliance on variational inference introduces an additional approximation gap. An open question the authors highlight is finding universal, practical conditions for model equivalence, and extending the approach to deeper models (e.g., deep Gaussian processes) or multivariate settings.
