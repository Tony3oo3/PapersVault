## Problem

Conventional federated learning with incomplete client participation (where some clients never join training due to system heterogeneity) lacks theoretical understanding: no existing work proves whether FL is even PAC-learnable under this setting, and the server-assisted FL (SA-FL) framework—widely used in practice to correct distribution bias—has no theoretical justification or convergence-guaranteed algorithm. This paper establishes that classic FL is fundamentally not PAC-learnable under incomplete participation, then shows SA-FL revives PAC-learnability and proposes the first SA-FL algorithm with provable convergence rates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two data sources: (1) decentralized client datasets where each of M clients holds n samples drawn from a potentially heterogeneous local distribution P_i, and (2) a small server-side auxiliary dataset T (50–10,000 samples) drawn i.i.d. from the population distribution P. Only m < M clients participate per round (incomplete participation), and client data is never directly accessible to the server due to privacy constraints—only local model updates are communicated. The output is a trained global model evaluated by classification accuracy on MNIST (grayscale, 28×28) and CIFAR-10 (RGB, 32×32).

## Method

SAFARI (Server-Assisted Federated Averaging) alternates between two round types governed by a probability parameter q: in client-update rounds (probability q), the server samples participating clients who each perform K local SGD steps and the server aggregates their models (analogous to FedAvg); in server-update rounds (probability 1−q), the server performs a single SGD step on its auxiliary dataset. The key insight is that server-side updates on the population-distributed auxiliary data correct the distribution bias introduced by incomplete client participation, while client-side parallel computation preserves the linear speedup in m and K. Theoretically, the paper proves an impossibility result (Theorem 1) showing conventional FL is not PAC-learnable under incomplete participation, a generalization bound (Theorem 2) showing SA-FL is PAC-learnable with error vanishing as total samples grow, and convergence guarantees (Theorems 4–5) achieving O(1/√(mKR)) for non-convex and Õ(1/R) for strongly convex objectives—matching state-of-the-art classic FL rates under ideal participation.

## Results

Under incomplete client participation (s=4 excluded clients) with highly non-IID data (p=1), FedAvg test accuracy drops to 57.05% on MNIST and 50.7% on CIFAR-10, representing degradations of up to 27.44% and 25% respectively compared to full participation. SAFARI (q=0.8) recovers most of this loss, achieving 31.07% and 23.86% absolute accuracy improvements over FedAvg on MNIST and CIFAR-10 respectively. Notably, even with only 50 auxiliary server-side samples (0.1% of total training data), SAFARI yields a 16.65% accuracy gain on MNIST under the worst-case setting (s=4, p=1). For near-homogeneous data (p≥5), SAFARI shows no statistically significant difference from FedAvg, confirming the server-side data is only needed when heterogeneity is severe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017) without any server-side auxiliary data. SAFARI consistently outperforms FedAvg under incomplete client participation, with the gap widening as both the degree of incomplete participation (s) and data heterogeneity (lower p) increase. In the most extreme setting (s=4, p=1), SAFARI improves test accuracy by 31.07% on MNIST (from 57.05% to 88.12%) and 23.86% on CIFAR-10 (from 50.7% to 74.56%). Under near-i.i.d. conditions (p=10) or mild heterogeneity (p=5), SAFARI matches FedAvg with no statistically significant difference, indicating the server-side data provides no benefit when client data is already representative. The improvement is also robust to the amount of auxiliary data: even 50 samples at the server (0.1% of total) yield meaningful gains, while 1000 samples approach the maximum recovery. SAFARI also achieves faster convergence than FedAvg under incomplete participation, as shown by the accelerated test accuracy curve in Figure 2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the FedAvg framework (McMahan et al., 2017) by introducing a server-side auxiliary dataset to correct distributional bias from incomplete client participation, a setting that prior FL convergence analyses (Karimireddy et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2021b; Li et al., 2020b) explicitly exclude by assuming full or uniform client participation. Conceptually, the paper departs from the domain adaptation literature by showing that standard distance-based bounds (d_A-divergence, Y-discrepancy) are overly pessimistic for the FL setting, where the training distribution Q is a mixture of the target P and the biased D, sharing the same support. The novel (α,β)-positively-related condition provides a tighter characterization of the P–Q relationship that is specific to the FL mixture structure and has no direct analogue in prior transfer learning or domain adaptation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility theorem (Theorem 1) is a strong foundational result: it proves that no FL algorithm can achieve vanishing generalization error under worst-case incomplete participation, even with infinite per-client data, thereby rigorously justifying the need for architectural modifications like SA-FL. The SAFARI algorithm provides the first SA-FL training procedure with convergence rates matching ideal-participation FedAvg (O(1/√(mKR)) non-convex, Õ(1/R) strongly convex), offering practitioners a concrete recipe. However, the empirical validation is limited to 10 clients on MNIST and CIFAR-10 with label-based partitioning, leaving open whether the theoretical guarantees and practical gains hold at scale (thousands of clients, ImageNet-level tasks) or under more realistic, time-varying participation dynamics. The (α,β)-positively-related condition, while mild in theory, may be difficult to verify or satisfy in practice for highly heterogeneous real-world deployments.
