## Problem

While Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM) and its variants have become a dominant paradigm for out-of-distribution generalization, the mathematical essence of why the gradient-norm penalty in IRM works has remained unexplained. The paper asks: what is the underlying mathematical structure of IRM's penalty term, and can it be replaced by a more principled operator that better preserves invariant features across environments?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241037/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes training samples (x_i, y_i) drawn from multiple environments, optionally with auxiliary variables z_i for environment inference (e.g., time, latitude/longitude, demographic attributes) and optionally with ground-truth environment partition labels. The environment variable e is absorbed into the classifier w via a measurable mapping, so the risk R(w∘Φ) is treated as a function on the induced measure space Ω = w(E_tr) rather than directly on the environment set. The output is an invariant feature extractor Φ (implemented as MLP, 1D-CNN, or ResNet-based pipeline) that produces class labels or regression values robust to environment shift.

## Method

The paper proves that the standard IRMv1 surrogate is mathematically equivalent to a TV-ℓ₂ model: it minimizes the expected risk plus a penalty on the squared L² norm of the risk gradient with respect to the classifier variable w, under an induced measure ν on the classifier space. Building on this, the authors propose IRM-TV-ℓ₁ (with environment partitions) and Minimax-TV-ℓ₁ (without), which replace the squared L² gradient penalty with the squared L¹ norm of the gradient expectation. This substitution is critical because TV-ℓ₁ admits the coarea formula, which geometrically forces the risk to become blocky (piecewise-constant) across the environment dimension, thereby preserving sharp invariant features while suppressing environment-sensitive variation. The TV-ℓ₁ formulation also relaxes integrability requirements from L² to L¹, permitting broader classes of risk functions and feature extractors. For OOD generalization, the paper establishes that the penalty parameter λ must be allowed to vary with Φ, the training environment basis must be expanded (e.g., via linear span or convex hull), and the measure on the environment space must be sufficiently accurate to make the global environment set measurable.

## Results

On CelebA (smile classification with gender as spurious feature), IRM-TV-ℓ₁ achieves 83.47% test accuracy and 81.21% worst-case accuracy with environment partitions, outperforming IRM (82.54%/80.75%) and GroupDRO (81.19%/79.27%). Without environment partitions, Minimax-TV-ℓ₁ reaches 83.68% test accuracy and 81.45% worst-case, surpassing ZIN (76.73%/76.19%) by over 7 points. On Landcover (satellite time-series classification), Minimax-TV-ℓ₁ achieves 63.77% OOD accuracy, exceeding the second-best ZIN (62.22%) by 1.55 points. On House Price regression, IRM-TV-ℓ₁ achieves 0.4420 test MSE with partitions (vs. IRM's 0.8168), and Minimax-TV-ℓ₁ achieves 0.3395 without partitions (vs. ERM's 0.4409).

## Baselines & Comparisons

IRM-TV-ℓ₁ consistently outperforms the original IRM (Arjovsky et al., 2020) across all four real-world benchmarks: on CelebA it gains +0.93% test and +0.46% worst accuracy; on Adult Income it gains +0.99% test and +1.17% worst; on House Price it reduces MSE from 0.8168 to 0.4420 (a 46% reduction). Minimax-TV-ℓ₁ (the no-partition counterpart) dominates ZIN (Lin et al., 2022) by a wide margin—+6.95% test accuracy on CelebA and +1.07% on Adult—while also outperforming TIVA (Tan et al., 2023) by +9.45% on CelebA. GroupDRO (Sagawa et al., 2020) is consistently the weakest among partition-aware methods, losing to IRM-TV-ℓ₁ by 2.28% on CelebA and 6.89% on Adult. EIIL (Creager et al., 2021) and LfF (Nam et al., 2020) are the weakest no-partition baselines, trailing Minimax-TV-ℓ₁ by 25.53% and 25.95% on CelebA respectively. HRM (Liu et al., 2021) is competitive on simulation but underperforms on House Price (0.5949 vs. 0.3395 MSE). A recurring pattern is that TV-ℓ₁-based models maintain a smaller gap between mean and worst-case performance than all baselines, confirming their robustness advantage under distribution shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the Invariant Risk Minimization (IRM) framework of Arjovsky et al. (2020) and its extensions (V-REx, ZIN, TIVA) as total variation (TV) models, specifically showing that the gradient-norm penalty in IRM is mathematically a TV-ℓ₂ regularizer on the learning risk with respect to the classifier variable. It then departs from the TV-ℓ₂ lineage by proposing IRM-TV-ℓ₁, which inherits the coarea formula property from the classical Rudin-Osher-Fatemi denoising framework (Rudin et al., 1992) that TV-ℓ₂ lacks. The paper also absorbs ZIN and TIVA's environment-learner ρ as a probability measure within a unified Minimax-TV formulation, positioning these methods as special cases of a broader TV-based optimization family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical equivalence between IRM and TV provides a principled mathematical foundation for why invariant learning works, and the TV-ℓ₁ variant offers a concrete mechanism (coarea formula → blocky risk) for preserving invariant features under environment shift. However, the empirical gains over IRM and ZIN are modest (typically 0.5–2% accuracy), and the key theoretical requirement that the penalty parameter λ must vary with Φ (Theorem 3.8) is not yet operationalized in practice. Open questions include designing adaptive λ_Φ schedules, expanding the training environment basis to cover the global environment set, and developing TV-ℓ₁ optimization methods that scale to deep architectures beyond the subgradient approach presented here.
