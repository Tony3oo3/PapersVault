## Problem

Amortized Bayesian inference methods (NPE, NPLE) require large simulation budgets and do not explicitly enforce consistency between the learned posterior and likelihood components, leading to unreliable marginal likelihood estimates. The paper identifies that the joint model p(θ, Y) implies a universal symmetry—the marginal likelihood p(Y) must be constant across all parameter values—which is violated by any imperfect approximation, and proposes to exploit this symmetry to improve data efficiency in low-budget regimes.

## Input / Output

The method takes an observed data set Y (a matrix of J multi-dimensional observations, e.g., time-series measurements or spatial signal readings) and produces a full approximate posterior distribution q_φ(θ|Y) parameterized by a conditional neural spline flow. In the NPLE variant, it simultaneously outputs an approximate likelihood density q_η(Y|θ) and a marginal likelihood estimate. The method requires the prior density p(θ) in analytic form and either an explicit likelihood p(Y|θ) (SC-NPE mode) or a jointly learned surrogate likelihood (SC-NPLE mode).

## Method

The core contribution is a self-consistency loss that inverts Bayes' theorem to compute the marginal likelihood as p(θ)p(Y|θ)/p(θ|Y), then penalizes the variance of log marginal likelihood estimates across K parameter samples drawn from the approximate posterior as a proposal distribution. This variance penalty is added to the standard NPE or NPLE training objective with a weight λ that is annealed from zero during early training to avoid instability when the posterior approximation is still poor. Proposition 1 provides the theoretical justification: minimizing the variance of the log marginal likelihood under any proposal sharing the posterior's support is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence between true and approximate posterior. The method operates in two modes—SC-NPE (explicit likelihood) and SC-NPLE (jointly learned approximate likelihood)—making it applicable to both likelihood-based and fully simulation-based inference settings without altering the inference-time computation.

## Results

Across five experiments, the self-consistency loss consistently reduces posterior approximation error (MMD) compared to NPE and NPLE baselines, with the largest gains at low simulation budgets. In the Source Location Finding task (Table 3), SC-NPE (K=500) achieves MMD 0.40±0.03 at N=512 versus 0.54±0.02 for NPE (26% reduction), and 0.21±0.02 at N=4096 versus 0.22±0.01. In the Two Moons task, SC-NPLE with only M=512 simulations matches the NPLE baseline trained on M=4096 (an 8× budget reduction), and the log marginal likelihood approximation error drops from 6.51±0.11 to 1.70±0.02 at N=512 (a 74% reduction). In the high-dimensional time series experiment, SC-NPLE (K=100) achieves MMD 0.54±0.03 versus 0.88±0.06 for NPLE (39% improvement).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: NPE (BayesFlow-style neural posterior estimation) for tasks with an explicit likelihood, and NPLE (JANA-style joint posterior and likelihood estimation) for fully simulation-based tasks. Against NPE, SC-NPE wins on MMD across all simulation budgets in both the Gaussian Mixture Model (Experiment 1) and Source Location Finding (Experiment 4), with the advantage most pronounced at low budgets (e.g., 0.40 vs 0.54 MMD at N=512 in Experiment 4). Against NPLE, SC-NPLE wins on posterior MMD across all budgets in the Two Moons task (Figure 3), on log-likelihood density at large budgets in Table 1 (3.90 vs 2.91 at N=4096), and on marginal likelihood approximation error in Table 2 (1.14 vs 10.21 at N=4096, a ~9× reduction). In the Hes1 biology experiment, SC-NPLE shows superior simulation-based calibration, particularly for parameters p₀ and ν, while both methods produce comparable posterior predictive fits to real data. In the high-dimensional time series experiment, SC-NPLE (K=100) achieves MMD 0.54±0.03 versus 0.88±0.06 for NPLE, with monotonic improvement as K increases. The method never loses to its baseline on any reported metric, though at very high budgets (N=4096) the MMD gap narrows and sometimes falls within one standard error.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the amortized Bayesian inference framework established by BayesFlow (NPE) and JANA (NPLE) by introducing a self-consistency loss derived from inverting Bayes' theorem. The theoretical foundation draws on Kothe (2023), who identified multiple self-consistency properties in generative models, but this paper is the first to operationalize the marginal-likelihood symmetry as a practical training signal for neural density estimators. Unlike prior work that exploits the same identity at inference time via importance sampling (Dax et al., 2023; Glöckler et al., 2022), the self-consistency loss acts during training, connecting the posterior and likelihood approximators and reducing the space of admissible solutions without increasing inference-time cost.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Data efficiency remains a central bottleneck in simulation-based inference, where simulation budgets are often constrained by computational cost. The demonstrated 8× reduction in required simulation budget (SC-NPLE at M=512 matching NPLE at M=4096) and the dramatically sharper marginal likelihood estimates (Table 2) make this practically relevant for expensive scientific simulators. The method is modular—applicable to NPE, NPLE, and SNPE—and works with both explicit and implicit likelihoods, broadening its utility. Open questions include whether different proposal distributions beyond the approximate posterior can further improve Monte Carlo efficiency, whether the prior density requirement can be relaxed via learned priors, and how the loss interacts with sequential or score-based SBI algorithms at scale.
