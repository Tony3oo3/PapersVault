## Problem

Theoretical expressiveness analyses of GNNs (via the k-WL hierarchy) provide upper bounds, but no prior empirical benchmark could reliably measure the *realized* expressiveness of practical model instances. Existing datasets (EXP, CSL, SR25) suffer from insufficient difficulty (most beyond-1-WL models reach ~100% accuracy), coarse granularity (performance is binary: random or perfect), and small scale (only a handful of essentially different graphs), making fine-grained comparison of 23 state-of-the-art models impossible.

## Input / Output

The evaluation takes a pair of non-isomorphic graphs (from the 400-pair BREC dataset, with graphs ranging from 10 to 198 nodes) and a trained GNN model, and produces a binary distinguishability verdict per pair plus an aggregate accuracy over all 400 pairs. Unusually, each pair is tested in isolation via a dedicated Siamese network rather than a single multi-class classifier, and the verdict is not a simple threshold on embedding distance but a statistical test (Hotelling's T²) that must simultaneously pass a Major Procedure on the non-isomorphic pair and a Reliability Check on isomorphic permutations of one graph, with the threshold set at 72.34 (q=32, d=16, α=0.95).

## Method

The paper introduces BREC, a dataset of 800 non-isomorphic graphs organized into 400 pairs across four difficulty tiers (Basic 1-WL, Regular, Extension, and CFI graphs up to 4-WL-indistinguishable), and a Reliable Paired Comparison (RPC) evaluation protocol. For training, a Siamese architecture with two identical GNNs processes both graphs in a pair, and a cosine-similarity contrastive loss (margin γ=0) pushes their embeddings apart. For evaluation, RPC generates q=32 random permutations of each graph, computes per-pair embedding differences, and applies Hotelling's T² test: a pair is deemed distinguished only if T²_test (non-isomorphic pair) exceeds the F-distribution threshold while T²_reliability (isomorphic pair) stays below it, thereby separating genuine structural differences from numerical noise. The framework is applied to 23 models spanning subgraph GNNs, k-WL-hierarchy GNNs, substructure GNNs, random GNNs, and transformer GNNs, revealing that realized expressiveness (41.5%–70.2% accuracy) consistently falls below theoretical upper bounds and that factors like subgraph radius and distance encoding significantly modulate practical performance.

## Results

On the BREC dataset (400 pairs of 1-WL-indistinguishable non-isomorphic graphs), GNN accuracies range from 37% (OSAN) to 70.2% (I²-GNN), while the best non-GNN baseline N₂ achieves 74.5%. The 3-WL test, a key theoretical reference, distinguishes 270/400 pairs (67.5%), correctly handling all Basic, Extension, and simple regular graphs but failing on strongly regular and most CFI graphs. I²-GNN surpasses 3-WL by 11 pairs (281 vs. 270), primarily through enhanced cycle-counting on strongly regular graphs, while KP-GNN (275 pairs) partially exceeds 3-WL on 4-vertex condition graphs. The 70.2% ceiling confirms BREC is far from saturation, with CFI graphs (up to 4-WL-indistinguishable) remaining the hardest category for all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among non-GNN baselines, N₂ (74.5%) is the strongest overall but fails on all CFI graphs due to limited receptive field, while 3-WL (67.5%) handles CFI graphs better (60%) thanks to its global receptive field. Among subgraph GNNs, I²-GNN (70.2%) and KP-GNN (68.8%) are the clear leaders, both partially surpassing 3-WL on strongly regular and 4-vertex condition graphs; SSWL_P (62%) and GSN (63.5%) form a middle tier, while NGNN (41.5%) is the weakest due to its simplicial node selection and lack of distance encoding. Among k-WL hierarchy GNNs, PPGN (58.2%) aligns closely with 3-WL on small-diameter graphs but underperforms on CFI graphs (23% vs. 60%), while local-WL variants δ-k-LGNN (54%) and KC-SetGNN (52.8%) suffer further from insufficient receptive fields. Random GNNs (DropGNN 44.2%, OSAN 37%) and Graphormer (19.8%, matching SPD-WL's 20.8%) confirm that stochastic sampling and transformer attention without structural inductive biases provide minimal expressiveness gains on this benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of GNN expressiveness evaluation that began with the k-WL hierarchy (Morris et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019) and prior expressiveness datasets (EXP, CSL, SR25), which the authors identify as limited in difficulty, granularity, and scale. It draws on the CFI construction (Cai et al., 1989) for generating 4-WL-indistinguishable graph pairs and on Papp & Wattenhofer's (2022) taxonomy of GNN extensions to populate the Extension category. Methodologically, it departs from the classification-based evaluation used in prior datasets by adopting a siamese-network training framework (Koch et al., 2015) combined with Hotelling's T-square test (Hotelling, 1992) to produce a statistically grounded pairwise distinguishability judgment (RPC), rather than relying on manually set embedding-distance thresholds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic empirical measurement of the gap between theoretical expressiveness (tight bounds) and realized expressiveness across 23 state-of-the-art beyond-1-WL GNNs, revealing that no model fully achieves its theoretical ceiling in practice. The finding that the best GNN (I²-GNN) reaches only 70.2% while 3-WL reaches 67.5% suggests the field's most expressive architectures still leave substantial room for improvement, and that architectural choices (distance encoding, subgraph radius tuning) matter as much as theoretical expressiveness class. Open questions include whether the theoretical-realized gap can be closed with better training, whether the non-monotonicity of subgraph radius (Observation 2) reflects a fundamental limitation or a fixable optimization issue, and how to evaluate models whose expressiveness falls outside the k-WL hierarchy entirely.
