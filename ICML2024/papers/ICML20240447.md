## Problem

Standard gradient methods have convergence rates governed by the largest Hessian eigenvalue, which is overly pessimistic when the spectrum is highly non-uniform with a small number of dominant eigenvalues. The paper identifies that many practical non-convex problems (matrix factorization, neural networks, partially convex objectives) possess a convex subspace of dimension τ in their Hessian, but no existing framework captures this structure to yield tighter complexity bounds.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240447/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires a twice-differentiable objective f: R^n → R, an initial point x₀, and a spectral order parameter τ (1 ≤ τ < n) specifying how many top Hessian eigenvectors to track. At each iteration it consumes one gradient evaluation and T_k power-method Hessian-vector products to maintain a low-rank approximation H_k ≈ ∇²_τ f(x_k). The output is an approximate stationary point x̄ satisfying ‖∇f(x̄)‖ ≤ ε, with the number of iterations depending on the τ-th spectral gap σ_τ rather than the full condition number.

## Method

The paper introduces graded non-convexity: f is non-convex of grade τ if the top τ eigenvalues of ∇²f(x) are non-negative for all x, forming a nested chain F_0 ⊃ F_1 ⊃ … ⊃ F_n (convex). The algorithm performs preconditioned gradient steps x_{k+1} = x_k − (H_k + α_k I)^{−1} ∇f(x_k), where H_k is a rank-τ approximation of the top-τ Hessian eigenspace maintained via hot-start power iterations, and α_k is a regularization parameter chosen adaptively (or via cubic-regularization subproblem to decouple negative curvature). The Woodbury identity makes each step O(τ²n) arithmetic. The key theoretical result is that the complexity scales with σ_τ (the bound on the (τ+1)-th eigenvalue) rather than λ_1, yielding provably faster convergence than plain gradient descent for any τ ≥ 1, and recovering regularized Newton's O(ε^{−3/2}) rate at τ = n.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is a complexity comparison (Table 1): spectral preconditioning of order τ achieves non-convex complexity O((λ_{τ+1}/ε² + L^{1/2}/ε^{3/2})·F₀), which strictly improves over gradient descent's O(λ₁/ε²)·F₀ whenever λ_{τ+1} < λ₁, and approaches Newton's O(L^{1/2}/ε^{3/2})·F₀ as τ→n. For strongly convex problems, the rate becomes Õ(λ_{τ+1}/λₙ + MD), versus Õ(λ₁/λₙ) for gradient descent. Empirically, on ill-conditioned matrix factorization (n=m=40, r*=1, singular value 400), spectral preconditioning with small τ converges faster in gradient steps than plain GD with Armijo line search. On logistic regression (MNIST, n=12730), τ=1 and τ=3 preconditioning matches BFGS performance while significantly outperforming basic gradient descent in both gradient steps and total arithmetic operations.

**Table 1: Global complexity bounds for spectral preconditioning vs. gradient method and Newton's method.**

| Algorithm | Preconditioning | Non-convex Complexity | Strongly Convex | Arithmetic Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gradient Method | H=I, τ=0 | O(λ₁/ε²)·F₀ | Õ(λ₁/λₙ) | O(n) |
| **Spectral Preconditioning (ours)** | H≈∇²_τ f, 1≤τ<n | O((λ_{τ+1}/ε² + L^{1/2}/ε^{3/2})·F₀) | Õ(λ_{τ+1}/λₙ + MD) | O(τ²n + τ³) |
| Newton's Method | H=∇²f, τ=n | O(L^{1/2}/ε^{3/2})·F₀ | Õ(MD) | O(n³) |

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against basic gradient descent (τ=0), spectral preconditioning provably improves the convergence rate by replacing the dependence on λ₁ (the largest Hessian eigenvalue) with λ_{τ+1} (the (τ+1)-th eigenvalue), which is strictly smaller when the top eigenvalues are well-separated. This is the core theoretical win: any τ≥1 yields a better rate than τ=0. Against BFGS and L-BFGS on logistic regression, spectral preconditioning with τ=1 or τ=3 achieves comparable convergence in gradient steps and total arithmetic operations, and often outperforms BFGS at low accuracy levels. However, quasi-Newton methods can still win at high accuracy due to their superlinear local convergence, which the spectral preconditioning does not exploit. The key differentiator is that spectral preconditioning provides global convergence guarantees for non-convex problems (via graded non-convexity), whereas BFGS/L-BFGS lack such guarantees. In the matrix factorization experiments, the adaptive search variant of spectral preconditioning further accelerates convergence compared to both fixed-parameter GD and constant-parameter spectral preconditioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the cubic regularization framework of Nesterov & Polyak (2006) for the step-size rule and extends the broader preconditioning literature (quasi-Newton methods like BFGS/L-BFGS, polynomial preconditioning, volume-sampling coordinate methods) by introducing a principled low-rank spectral preconditioner. The key conceptual departure is the 'graded non-convexity' problem class, which replaces the binary convex/non-convex dichotomy with a nested family of subclasses parameterized by the number of positive Hessian eigenvalues. This positions the method as a strict generalization: τ=0 recovers gradient descent, τ=n recovers regularized Newton, and intermediate τ values exploit spectral gaps that are common in practice (matrix factorization, neural networks, partially convex problems).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The graded non-convexity framework provides a clean theoretical lens for understanding why and when spectral preconditioning helps, and the provable rate improvement over gradient descent (cutting the top τ eigenvalues from the complexity) is a meaningful contribution to non-convex optimization theory. Practically, the method requires only Hessian-vector products and a small number of power-method iterations, making it feasible for moderate-scale problems where full Newton is intractable but gradient descent is too slow. Open questions include the behavior near saddle points, whether incorporating negative curvature directions could further help, and whether local superlinear convergence can be achieved. A limitation is that the method still requires second-order information (Hessian-vector products), which may not be available in all large-scale ML settings.
