## Problem

Human motion taxonomies encode rich hierarchical structure (e.g., grasp types organized by muscular/kinematic properties, support poses by contact patterns), yet no computational model has bridged the gap between this discrete hierarchy and the high-dimensional pose data associated with each category. Prior work either reduced taxonomies to flat classification labels (ignoring internal structure) or treated them as discrete symbols unsuitable for continuous motion generation. The paper asks: can we learn continuous embeddings that faithfully preserve the taxonomy's graph distances while also capturing within-class similarity of the underlying pose data?

## Input / Output

The model consumes high-dimensional joint-angle vectors (86-D for bimanual manipulation, 24-D for hand grasps, 44-D for whole-body support poses), the taxonomy graph (a tree or general graph whose nodes are categories and whose edges encode transitions), and a mapping from each pose to its taxonomy node. It produces low-dimensional (2-D or 3-D) latent embeddings on the Lorentz hyperbolic manifold, along with GP uncertainty estimates. A distinctive output is geodesic trajectories between any two embeddings, which the paper shows correspond to shortest paths in the taxonomy graph and can be decoded into realistic pose sequences. Back constraints additionally allow the model to place unseen poses or entirely new taxonomy classes into the learned embedding without retraining.

## Method

GPHLVM reformulates the GPLVM for a hyperbolic latent space (Lorentz model) by replacing the Euclidean Gaussian prior with a hyperbolic wrapped Gaussian, substituting the standard SE/Matérn kernel with a positive-definite-guaranteed hyperbolic SE kernel (approximated via a Monte Carlo sum over spherical functions on the Poincaré disk), and performing all optimization with Riemannian Adam on the product manifold. Taxonomy structure is injected through two complementary mechanisms: a global stress loss that penalizes mismatch between geodesic distances in the latent space and shortest-path distances in the taxonomy graph, and local back constraints that map each observation to the manifold via Exp_{μ₀}(Σ w_{q,m} k^{R^D}(y_n,y_m) k^{G}(c_n,c_m)), where k^G is a Matérn kernel on the taxonomy graph. Training uses MAP estimation for the small datasets in the paper (≤100 poses) and a variational formulation with inducing points for scalability. The key insight is that hyperbolic space's exponential volume growth provides significantly more room to embed tree-like hierarchies with minimal distortion than Euclidean space of the same dimension, and that pairing the global stress regularizer with local back constraints preserves both the macroscopic taxonomy topology and the microscopic within-class pose similarity.

## Results

The primary metric is average stress (lower is better), measuring how well geodesic distances in the latent space match taxonomy graph distances. In 2D latent spaces, GPHLVM consistently outperforms Euclidean GPLVM across all three taxonomies: e.g., for hand grasps, GPHLVM L² with stress achieves 0.14±0.16 stress vs. GPLVM R²'s 0.39±0.41 (a ~64% reduction). In 3D, GPHLVM L³ also wins for bimanual manipulation (0.04±0.08 vs. 0.20±0.38 with BC+stress) and grasps (0.04±0.08 vs. 0.14±0.18 with stress), though Euclidean GPLVM R³ slightly edges out GPHLVM L³ on the cyclic support pose taxonomy (0.24 vs. 0.30). For unseen data encoding, GPHLVM maintains lower stress than GPLVM in all settings (e.g., unseen class: 0.13 vs. 0.08 for bimanual, 0.48 vs. 0.60 for grasps).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Euclidean GPLVM (Lawrence, 2003), GPHLVM wins decisively in 2D latent spaces across all three taxonomies (e.g., 0.14 vs. 0.39 stress for grasps with stress prior) and also in 3D for bimanual manipulation and grasps, attributed to the exponential volume growth of hyperbolic space. The sole exception is the 3D support pose taxonomy, where the cyclic graph structure is better captured by Euclidean geometry (0.24 vs. 0.30 stress). Against the Riemannian-manifold GPLVM of Tosi et al. (2014), GPHLVM clearly outperforms, as that model was not designed for hierarchical discrete data and fails to capture both local and global structure. For motion generation, geodesic interpolation in the GPHLVM latent space produces motions described as 'as realistic as' VPoser (Pavlakos et al., 2019), while being trained on far fewer data points (single poses vs. full trajectories). The paper also reports that GPHLVM outperforms both vanilla and hyperbolic VAEs in preserving taxonomy structure, with lower stress and lower reconstruction error (App. H.1). A notable practical drawback is the 2D hyperbolic kernel's computational cost: GPHLVM L² training takes ~415s vs. ~3s for GPLVM R², though the 3D variant is comparable (~7s vs. ~3s).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GPLVM framework (Lawrence, 2003) to hyperbolic latent spaces, building on the hyperbolic wrapped Gaussian distribution (Nagano et al., 2019), Matérn kernels on Riemannian manifolds (Borovitskiy et al., 2020), and graph kernels (Borovitskiy et al., 2021). It adapts the back-constrained GPLVM (Lawrence & Quiñonero Candela, 2006) and topologically-constrained LVMs (Urtasun et al., 2008) to impose taxonomy graph structure as inductive bias. A key departure from prior manifold GPLVMs (Tosi et al., 2014; Jorgensen & Hauberg, 2021) is that the hyperbolic geometry is imposed as a fixed inductive bias rather than learned via a pullback metric, motivated by the known suitability of hyperbolic space for tree-like hierarchies (Nickel & Kiela, 2017; Krioukov et al., 2010).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete gap in robotics and motion analysis: discrete taxonomies have been underutilized because no computational model bridged their hierarchical structure with high-dimensional pose data. The geodesic-based motion generation that respects taxonomy transitions is a practical contribution for low-data robotics settings, and the finding that 2D hyperbolic embeddings outperform 3D Euclidean ones for tree-like structures provides a principled argument for geometry choice. Key limitations include the prohibitive computational cost of the 2D hyperbolic kernel (414s vs 3s per training step), the absence of physics or contact constraints in generated motions, and the assumption that the provided taxonomy is accurate. Open questions include incorporating uncertainty in taxonomy edges, scaling to heterogeneous graph structures via mixed-curvature manifolds, and making geodesic motions physically feasible.
