## Problem

When biased human decisions determine who gets labeled (e.g., which patients receive diagnostic tests), standard supervised learning on the resulting proxy labels amplifies disparities across subgroups. Existing semi-supervised, noisy-label, and causal-inference approaches either discard untested individuals, assume noise structures incompatible with the selective-labeling mechanism, or require strong overlap assumptions that fail under sharp testing decisions. This paper asks how to leverage the causal structure of biased labeling to mitigate subgroup bias while retaining all available data and maintaining discriminative performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular patient covariates x ∈ R^d, a binary proxy label ỹ (which equals ground truth y only when the testing indicator t=1, and is forced to 0 otherwise), the testing indicator t ∈ {0,1}, and a sensitive attribute a. It also requires a frozen pre-trained classifier g_ζ(x,a) that estimates the probability of being tested, serving as a propensity-score-like input to the regularization term. The output is a single probability score f_θ(x) ∈ [0,1] for the binary outcome, evaluated via AUC and the ROC gap (area between group-specific ROC curves).

## Method

DCEM is an expectation-maximization algorithm that alternates between imputing soft pseudo-labels for untested individuals (E-step: Q(y) = ỹ if t=1, else f_θ(x)) and updating the model via a two-term regularized cross-entropy loss (M-step). The first term is standard binary cross-entropy against the pseudo-label Q(y); the second term, Q(y)·L(ỹ, ŷ·t̂), is a causal regularizer that pushes predictions downward in regions where the estimated testing probability t̂ is high, thereby counterbalancing the inflation of P(Ỹ|X) relative to P(Y|X) caused by disparate censorship. The key theoretical result is that this regularizer's strength is monotonically increasing in t̂, so it selectively corrects the most biased regions while leaving tested individuals' supervision unchanged (reducing to standard cross-entropy when t=1). The method pre-trains f_θ on tested individuals and a separate binary classifier for t, then iterates E and M steps until convergence, using neural networks to avoid parametric assumptions on the data-generating process.

## Results

On the MIMIC-III sepsis classification task (q_t=1.5, k=4), DCEM achieves a median AUC of 0.620 (vs. oracle y-model 0.633) and a median ROC gap of 0.105, with the tightest ROC gap range (0.094) among all baselines. On synthetic data (q_y=0.5, k=1, q_t=2), DCEM achieves a median AUC of 0.787 (vs. tested-only 0.815, y-obs 0.657) and a median ROC gap of 0.030 (2nd-best: SELF 0.034), while also achieving the best worst-case AUC (0.768 vs. tested-only 0.623) and the tightest AUC range (0.055 vs. DragonNet 0.199). DCEM improves on the default y-obs model by 0.130 AUC on synthetic data and outperforms all baselines except tested-only on discriminative performance while achieving better bias mitigation at comparable AUC levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On MIMIC-III, DCEM achieves the highest median AUC (0.620) among all baselines, outperforming DragonNet (0.593) by 0.027 AUC and the Group 0 approach (0.342) which catastrophically fails. On bias mitigation, Group peer loss (0.070) and DragonNet (0.088) achieve lower median ROC gaps than DCEM (0.105), but Group peer loss has a much wider AUC range (0.182 vs. DCEM's 0.065), indicating unreliable discriminative performance. On synthetic data, DCEM beats SELF by 0.110 AUC (0.787 vs. 0.677) at a comparable ROC gap (0.030 vs. 0.034), and achieves a substantially better worst-case AUC than tested-only (0.768 vs. 0.623) while also having a lower worst-case ROC gap (0.060 vs. 0.083). DragonNet is the most competitive baseline overall, achieving the tightest AUC range on MIMIC-III (0.018) and competitive ROC gaps, but DCEM dominates on synthetic data robustness (AUC range 0.055 vs. 0.199). SAREM (positive-unlabeled learning) and the noisy-label baselines (peer loss, truncated l_q, GJS loss, DivideMix) are discussed in the appendix but are consistently outperformed by DCEM on both metrics, as they make independence assumptions (e.g., T ⊥ X | Y) that disparate censorship violates.

## Relation to Prior Work

DCEM builds directly on the EM algorithm (Dempster et al., 1977) and the disparate censorship causal model formalized by the same authors in Chang et al. (2022), deriving an E-step that imputes pseudo-labels for untested individuals and an M-step that acts as a 'causal regularizer' pulling predictions down in high-testing-probability regions. It departs from semi-supervised methods (SELF, DivideMix) and noisy-label approaches (peer loss, GJS) that make independence assumptions incompatible with the disparate censorship DAG, and from causal effect estimators (DragonNet, IPW) that require strong overlap assumptions violated by threshold-based testing decisions. The key conceptual contribution is showing that the EM-derived M-step objective is equivalent to a regularized cross-entropy whose regularizer strength increases monotonically with the estimated testing probability, providing a principled mechanism for counterbalancing labeling bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Disparate censorship is a realistic and clinically prevalent source of label bias (e.g., women undertested for cardiovascular disease), and DCEM offers a theoretically grounded, practical method to mitigate its effects without discarding untested individuals or requiring strong overlap. Limitations include potentially slow EM convergence, the inherent difficulty of evaluating models when ground truth is partially unobserved, and the fact that DCEM does not learn a full generative model, restricting it to the P(Y|X) estimand. Open questions include improving robustness to very low overlap (where t̂≈0), extending to multi-class or continuous outcomes, and developing better dataset curation strategies for evaluation under selective labeling.
