## Problem

While LLM-as-a-Judge has shown promise for text-only evaluation, no comprehensive benchmark exists to assess whether Multimodal LLMs can serve as reliable judges across diverse visual-language tasks. It remains unclear how closely MLLM judgments align with human preferences in scoring, pairwise comparison, and ranking settings, and what systematic biases or hallucinations undermine their reliability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240254/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes 4,414 image-instruction pairs spanning 14 datasets (captioning, math, charts, infographics, text reading, diffusion, etc.) and approximately 17,000 MLLM-generated responses as the material to be judged. The judging MLLM receives the original image, the instruction, and the candidate responses, and must produce one of three structured outputs: a 1–5 score, a pairwise winner (with an explicit tie option), or a full descending ranking. Judging is evaluated under multiple configurations—direct vision input, no vision input, a GPT-4V-generated text description as a 'vision expert,' and multi-step Chain-of-Thought—allowing controlled ablation of each component's contribution.

## Method

The benchmark is constructed in three steps: (1) curating image-instruction pairs from 14 diverse datasets, (2) collecting responses from six MLLMs (GPT-4V, Gemini, LLaVA-1.5/1.6, CogVLM, Qwen-VL-Max), and (3) having six human annotators independently score, compare, and rank those responses to establish ground truth. Judging follows an 'Analyze-then-Judge' paradigm (a one-step CoT prompt where the MLLM first analyzes the responses and then renders a verdict), adapted from Chiang & Lee (2023b). Alignment with human judgments is quantified via Pearson correlation (scoring), accuracy/F1/recall (pair comparison), and Normalized Levenshtein distance (batch ranking), supplemented by human-agreement surveys, six-repetition consistency checks, and hallucination detection. The study further probes four specific failure modes—egocentric bias, position bias, length bias, and hallucination—and tests whether multi-step CoT or a vision-expert description mitigates them, finding that CoT reduces hallucinations but does not improve alignment with human preferences.

## Results

GPT-4V achieves the highest alignment with human judgments across all three settings: Pearson similarity of 0.490 in Scoring Evaluation, 0.773 accuracy in Pair Comparison (with tie), and the lowest Normalized Levenshtein distance of 0.361 in Batch Ranking. Gemini follows with 0.304, 0.615, and 0.432 respectively, while LLaVA-1.6-34b and Qwen-VL-Max score substantially lower (e.g., 0.184 and 0.170 in Scoring). Notably, MLLMs perform best in Pair Comparison but show a large gap from human preferences in Scoring Evaluation and Batch Ranking, with even GPT-4V achieving only ~70% human agreement overall.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V consistently outperforms all other MLLMs as a judge across all three settings. In Pair Comparison (with tie), GPT-4V reaches 0.773 average accuracy, surpassing LLaVA-1.6-34b (0.648), Qwen-VL-Max (0.644), and Gemini (0.615) by 12–17 points. In Scoring Evaluation, GPT-4V's Pearson similarity of 0.490 is substantially higher than Gemini (0.304), LLaVA-1.5-13b (0.225), LLaVA-1.6-34b (0.184), and Qwen-VL-Max (0.170), with the gap attributed to a 'High-Score Bias' in smaller models that predominantly assign 4-point ratings. In Batch Ranking, GPT-4V achieves the best (lowest) Levenshtein distance of 0.361, followed by Gemini (0.432), Qwen-VL-Max (0.486), LLaVA-1.6-34b (0.501), and LLaVA-1.5-13b (0.597). The paper further shows that adding multi-step CoT does NOT improve judging performance (e.g., GPT-4V+CoT drops from 0.773 to 0.728 in Pair w.o. Tie), and that text-only LLMs with detailed vision descriptions (GPT-4V Vision Expert: 0.878 in Pair w.o. Tie) can approach or exceed standard MLLM judging, suggesting the bottleneck lies in vision perception rather than reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023b; Chiang & Lee, 2023b) from text-only settings to the multimodal domain, introducing the first comprehensive benchmark for evaluating MLLMs as judges. It adapts the 'Analyze-then-Judge' one-step CoT framework from Chiang & Lee (2023b) and incorporates the tie option from Deutsch et al. (2023) for pair comparisons. The key departure is the systematic evaluation across three distinct judging tasks (Scoring, Pair Comparison, Batch Ranking) with human annotations, rather than the single-task or text-only evaluations in prior LLM-as-a-Judge work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark provides the first systematic evidence that even state-of-the-art MLLMs like GPT-4V exhibit significant gaps in Scoring Evaluation and Batch Ranking relative to human preferences, while performing reasonably in Pair Comparison. The identification of specific biases (egocentric, position, length) and the finding that multi-step CoT actually degrades judging performance (rather than improving it) offer concrete targets for future work. Open questions include whether human-in-the-loop approaches can close the remaining gap, whether the biases are fundamental to current training paradigms, and whether the benchmark's relatively small scale (4,414 pairs, 6 annotators) limits generalizability.
