## Problem

Causal Representation Learning (CRL) is severely ill-posed because it combines two independently ill-posed problems—nonlinear representation learning and causal discovery—yet prior identifiability results required heavy assumptions such as temporal structure, supervision, or interventions on latent variables. This paper establishes identifiability under a much weaker and more practical condition: a known grouping of observational variables (e.g., by sensor modality or time point) combined with pairwise causal interactions, requiring no temporal ordering, supervision, or interventions.

## Input / Output

The method takes multivariate observations partitioned into M non-overlapping groups (e.g., DNA/RNA/protein measurements per cell, or images at different time points), where each group's observations are generated only from its own group's latent variables via a group-specific nonlinear mixing function. It outputs per-group latent causal variables (identifiable up to permutation and variable-wise invertible transformations) and inter-group weighted adjacency matrices (identifiable up to permutation, linear scaling, and matrix transpose). Intra-group causal structure is not directly identifiable but can be recovered as post-processing since the latent variables themselves are identifiable.

## Method

G-CaRL models the latent variables with a pairwise Bayesian network where a shared potential function φ governs all inter-group causal relations, modulated by group-pair-specific adjacency coefficients, while intra-group dependencies are captured by unrestricted group-wise potentials. Training is self-supervised contrastive learning: the pretext task discriminates original samples from group-wise-shuffled samples (each group independently drawn from a different sample index), using a nonlinear logistic regression whose factorized form mirrors the causal model's structure. The key insight is that shuffling breaks inter-group causal dependencies, so the regression can only discriminate correctly if its feature extractors h^m invert the group-specific mixing functions, thereby recovering the latent causal variables. Identifiability is proven under two assumptions: nondegeneracy of the inter-group graph (full row-rank of combined adjacency matrices) and non-factorizability/asymmetry of the causal potential φ, which excludes linear-Gaussian models but permits nonlinear, cyclic, and confounded causal structures.

## Results

G-CaRL is evaluated on four settings: a DAG simulation (3 groups × 10 variables), cyclic graphs with latent confounders, a SERGIO gene regulatory network, and high-dimensional 3Dident images. Performance is measured by correlation for latent variable recovery and F1-score for inter-group causal graph recovery. G-CaRL achieves the best scores across all four settings, with the largest margins in the DAG simulation where baselines struggle due to lack of representation capability, supervision, or explicit causal modeling. In the more challenging cyclic/confounded setting, G-CaRL still recovers latent variables and causal structure reasonably well, though it requires more samples. On the 3Dident high-dimensional image task, G-CaRL shows competitive performance against MVCRL and CCL without requiring a decoder.

## Baselines & Comparisons

G-CaRL outperforms all six baselines across all experimental settings. CCL (Morioka & Hyvärinen, 2023) shows the worst performance in the DAG simulation, which the authors attribute to the inadequacy of assuming mutual independence among latent variables. CausalVAE (Yang et al., 2021) underperforms because it relies on supervision that is absent in this unsupervised setting. MVCRL (Yao et al., 2023) is outperformed by G-CaRL on both correlation and F1-score, with G-CaRL's advantage being most pronounced in the cyclic/confounded setting. The representation-learning-only baselines (MFCVAE, VaDE, β-VAE) all underperform because they lack explicit modeling of latent causal interactions, and even when post-processed with causal discovery, they cannot match G-CaRL's joint estimation. On the SERGIO gene regulatory network (where the true generative process is an SDE not captured by the model), G-CaRL still achieves the best performance, demonstrating robustness to causal model misspecification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly generalizes CCL (Morioka & Hyvärinen, 2023), which assumed a shared mixing function across groups, component-wise (NICA-style) causality, and forest causal graphs; G-CaRL relaxes all three to group-specific mixing, all-pairs inter-group causality, and cyclic graphs. The estimation framework builds on the logistic-regression / noise-contrastive estimation properties of Gutmann & Hyvärinen (2012), repurposing them for a group-wise shuffling pretext task that provably recovers causal representations. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant CRL identifiability strategies (temporal structure, supervision, interventions) by using a priori grouping of observational variables as the sole structural assumption, placing it alongside but distinct from recent group-based CRL work (Daunhawer et al., Lyu et al., Yao et al.) that focuses on group intersections rather than group-specific variables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key contribution is a provably identifiable CRL model that requires no temporal structure, supervision, or interventions—only a natural grouping of observations—while still accommodating nonlinear, cyclic causal graphs and latent confounders, a combination no prior CRL framework handles. This opens the door to CRL in settings like multimodal sensor networks, single-cell multiomics, and general time series where such grouping arises naturally. Limitations include the requirement of non-overlapping groups, identifiability only for inter-group (not intra-group) causal edges, and the assumption of invertible per-group mixing; extending to overlapping groups, multivariate causal variables, and noisier observational models are identified as important future directions.
