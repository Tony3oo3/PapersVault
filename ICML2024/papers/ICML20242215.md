## Problem

Solving two-stage stochastic programming (2SP) with many scenarios is computationally intractable, and existing scenario reduction methods (clustering, Monte Carlo sampling) treat scenarios in isolation, failing to capture inter-scenario relationships. Moreover, these methods ignore the fact that the *order* in which scenarios are fed to a solver significantly affects solving time through its impact on constraint ordering and the simplex initial basis, yet no prior approach exploits this sequential information.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242215/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 2SP instance (deterministic first-stage parameters A, b, c and N uncertain scenarios each with q_ξ, W_ξ, h_ξ, T_ξ) and a constant k, and produces an *ordered* sequence of k scenarios rather than an unordered subset. This sequence is converted into an equivalent MIP and handed to Gurobi, which returns the first-stage decision variables. The critical distinction from prior work is that the output is a permutation, not a set: the ordering is learned to minimize solver wall-clock time, not just to maximize representativeness.

## Method

HGCN2SP encodes each 2SP instance as a two-level hierarchical graph: the lower level is a bipartite graph per scenario (variables and constraints as nodes, coefficients as edge weights, merged with first-stage information), and the upper level is an instance graph where each scenario is a vertex connected by edges weighted by cosine similarity of uncertain parameter vectors. A four-layer hierarchical GCN processes these levels separately—two layers extract per-scenario embeddings from the bipartite subgraphs (followed by a readout function), and two layers refine them using inter-scenario topology. An attention-based decoder (adapted from Kool et al., 2018) with a modified context embedding sequentially selects k scenarios via masked attention over candidate embeddings. Training uses PPO reinforcement learning where the reward is a weighted combination of negative solver time and the negative Manhattan distance between the reduced-problem solution and the true optimal, directly incorporating solver feedback into the learning signal.

## Results

On CFLP_10_20_200, HGCN2SP achieves 2.47% error in 2.45s with k=5 scenarios, outperforming CVAE-SIPA (6.86% error, 2.40s) by over 4 percentage points while matching its speed. At k=20, HGCN2SP reaches 1.16% error in 41.97s versus CVAE-SIP's 1.77% in 26.12s. On NDP_2_2_10_200, HGCN2SP is slightly worse in error (33.03% vs. 24.59% at k=5) but solves in 0.17s versus 0.54s; at k=20 it achieves 2.25% error in 0.98s, nearly matching CVAE-SIPA's 2.12% in 2.37s. Gurobi's exact solver requires 10,696s for CFLP and 26.54s for NDP, making HGCN2SP 4,000× faster on CFLP with only ~1–2% suboptimality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Gurobi (exact solver), HGCN2SP trades a small optimality gap (1.2–2.5% on CFLP, 2.3–33% on NDP) for a 4,000× speedup on CFLP (2.45s vs. 10,696s) and a 150× speedup on NDP at k=5 (0.17s vs. 26.54s). Against CVAE-SIPA (the strongest learning-based baseline), HGCN2SP clearly wins on CFLP at every k (e.g., 2.47% vs. 6.86% at k=5) and is competitive on NDP, achieving 2.25% vs. 2.12% error at k=20 while being 2.4× faster (0.98s vs. 2.37s). Against CSSC, HGCN2SP is far faster on NDP (0.17s vs. 573.75s at k=5) though CSSC achieves lower error (2.87% vs. 33.03%); CSSC was not tested on CFLP due to prohibitive runtime. Against NN-P and NN-E, HGCN2SP dominates on both error (2.47% vs. 21.98%/32.38% on CFLP) and time (2.45s vs. 521s/3903s), as these methods require per-instance training and produce large MIPs from neural network lifting. Overall, HGCN2SP is the best learning-based method on CFLP across all k values and the fastest on NDP, with the main trade-off being slightly higher error on NDP at small k due to the high dimensionality of its 178 decision variables.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the bipartite graph representation of MIPs from Gasse et al. (2019) and the attention-based sequential decoder from Kool et al. (2018), combining them into a hierarchical architecture specifically structured for the two-stage nature of stochastic programming. It departs from prior learning-based scenario reduction methods (notably CVAE-SIP by Wu et al., 2022) in two key ways: (1) it models inter-scenario relationships through a high-level instance graph rather than treating scenarios independently, and (2) it formulates scenario selection as an ordered sequence problem solved via RL with solver feedback, rather than an unordered clustering task. The insight that scenario ordering affects simplex-based solver performance (citing Bixby, 1992) is leveraged here for the first time in a learned scenario reduction pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: reducing 2SP solution times from hours (Gurobi on 200+ scenarios) to seconds while maintaining sub-3% error, with demonstrated generalization to unseen larger-scale instances. The key open question is the prohibitive training cost—each CFLP instance requires ~3 hours of solver time, making the approach difficult to extend to new problem classes or larger training sets. The ordering insight, while known in solver internals, has not been systematically exploited in scenario reduction before, and its quantification (only 12% of random orderings beat the learned sequence) suggests broader applicability to any pipeline where constraint ordering affects solver performance.
