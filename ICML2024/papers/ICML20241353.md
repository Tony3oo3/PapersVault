## Problem

Generative models for drug discovery frequently propose molecules that are synthetically infeasible, creating a gap between computational design and experimental validation. Existing synthesizable molecular design methods (combinatorial optimization, reinforcement learning, autoencoders) suffer from limited chemical space coverage, inability to handle branched pathways, or dependence on expensive oracle queries. This paper asks: can we take an arbitrary molecule (potentially unsynthesizable) and 'project' it into a synthesizable chemical space while preserving the structural features responsible for its desired properties?

## Input / Output

The model takes a molecular graph as input (e.g., a molecule proposed by Pocket2Mol or a goal-directed generative model) and autoregressively generates a postfix notation of synthesis — a linear sequence of building block tokens (each a 256-bit Morgan fingerprint) and reaction tokens (indices into a set of 91 SMARTS-encoded templates) terminated by an [END] token. Executing this sequence on a memory stack using RDKit yields a concrete synthetic pathway and a final product molecule guaranteed to be derivable from 211,220 purchasable Enamine building blocks. The output is thus not merely a molecule but a full, executable synthesis plan.

## Method

The core contribution is a novel postfix notation for synthetic pathways (a post-order traversal of the synthesis AST) that natively supports branched reactions and multi-reactant rules, unlike prior linear-pathway or tree-construction representations. The architecture pairs a graph transformer encoder (8 layers, 8 heads, 512-dim, with bond-type-dependent attention bias) that encodes the input molecular graph into per-atom embeddings, with a standard transformer decoder that autoregressively generates the postfix notation conditioned on those embeddings. At each decoding step, a three-way classifier predicts the token type (building block, reaction, or end); building block tokens are resolved by predicting a 256-bit fingerprint and performing nearest-neighbor retrieval against the Enamine catalog, while reaction tokens are sampled from the 91-template set. Training is supervised on (postfix notation, molecular graph) pairs generated on-the-fly by a random synthesis sampling algorithm, with a composite loss of cross-entropy for token type, binary cross-entropy for fingerprints, and cross-entropy for reaction identity. Because every generated token corresponds to a real purchasable building block or a curated reaction template, synthesizability is guaranteed by construction rather than enforced as a post-hoc constraint.

## Results

On the bottom-up synthesis planning task, the proposed method achieves a 97.5% success rate and 28.4% exact reconstruction rate on the held-out test set, compared to SynNet's 84.1% and 10.7% respectively. On the more challenging ChEMBL benchmark (1,000 molecules previously reported as predominantly unreachable), the proposed method reaches 98.8% success and 13.3% reconstruction, versus SynNet's 85.0% and 5.4%. Average Morgan fingerprint similarity improves from 0.4575 to 0.7167 (test set) and from 0.4270 to 0.5978 (ChEMBL), with scaffold and Gobbi pharmacophore similarities showing similar gains. In the structure-based drug design setting, projected analogs of Pocket2Mol molecules show slight improvements in AutoDock Vina scores for the majority of 15 targets (e.g., ADRB2: −8.70 → −9.09 kcal/mol; PKM2: −9.45 → −10.09 kcal/mol).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is SynNet (Gao et al., ICLR 2021), a prior amortized tree-generation model for bottom-up synthesis planning. The proposed method decisively outperforms SynNet on every metric in both evaluation settings: success rate improves by ~13 percentage points (97.5% vs. 84.1% on the test set; 98.8% vs. 85.0% on ChEMBL), and exact reconstruction rate more than doubles (28.4% vs. 10.7%; 13.3% vs. 5.4%). Structural similarity gains are substantial — Morgan fingerprint similarity increases by ~0.26 on the test set and ~0.17 on ChEMBL, while scaffold similarity rises by ~0.27 and ~0.17 respectively. In the goal-directed generation setting (Appendix B.2), the proposed method outperforms SynNet on 7 out of 10 GuacaMol objectives in both similarity and property scores, with the remaining 3 objectives roughly on par. The paper does not compare against reinforcement-learning baselines (PGFS, REACTOR) or other autoencoder-based methods (MoleculeChef, DoGs), limiting the breadth of the empirical comparison to a single prior architecture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior synthesizable molecular design methods (MoleculeChef, DoGs, SynNet, PGFS, REACTOR) by introducing a postfix-notation representation of synthetic pathways as a post-order traversal of the synthesis AST, replacing the more complex tree-building action sequences or linear-pathway-only formulations used previously. The encoder-decoder architecture (graph transformer + autoregressive transformer decoder) is standard, but its application to this new sequence representation is the key methodological contribution. The paper also introduces a distinct task framing—'projecting' an arbitrary (possibly out-of-space) molecule into synthesizable chemical space—rather than generating molecules directly within that space as SynNet or DoGs do, or performing pure retrosynthesis planning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is strong: structure-based drug design models like Pocket2Mol routinely produce molecules that cannot be synthesized, and this paper provides a concrete 'fix' mechanism that preserves binding poses while guaranteeing synthesizability. However, the goal-directed generation results reveal a significant limitation—projected analogs often suffer large drops in objective property scores (e.g., −0.64 for Sitagliptin MPO), suggesting the projection is lossy and the synthesizable space may not contain close analogs for highly optimized but infeasible molecules. Open questions include whether the property preservation can be improved through joint optimization, whether the approach scales to larger reaction sets and multi-step convergent syntheses, and how the method performs when the input molecule is far from the synthesizable manifold.
