## Problem

Molecular discovery for complex, designer molecules in materials science (e.g., gas-separation membranes, organic photovoltaics) operates in a data-scarce regime (~100–300 molecules) with modular substructures that differ significantly from drug-like molecules. Existing grammar-based approaches (e.g., Geo-DEG) achieve competitive performance but require large, complex tree-structured derivations that are computationally expensive and produce chemically uninterpretable substructures, while standard GNNs and motif-based predictors lack the hierarchical, context-sensitive structure needed to capture design principles in these datasets.

## Input / Output

The method takes molecule graphs that have been fragmented into expert-defined motifs (functional groups, rings, scaffold units), each annotated with 'black' base atoms and 'red' attachment-context atoms, and constructs a directed multigraph G where nodes are motifs and edges encode valid pairwise attachments. The molecule representation is a directed-acyclic multigraph (a random walk / Euler path over a connected subgraph of G) with edge probabilities from a learnable diffusion process. For property prediction, this representation is fed to a GIN producing a scalar property value; for generation, the learned transition probabilities sample a new random walk that assembles a novel molecule, and the trained grammar can also be queried to extract interpretable context-sensitive production rules.

## Method

The core contribution is a compact random-walk grammar over a motif graph: each molecule is represented as an Euler path over a connected subgraph of G, linearized via pre-order traversal with reversed duplicates on stack contraction. The context-sensitive transition probabilities are parameterized by a graph heat diffusion process dx_t/dt = L(Φ,t)x_t, where the time-dependent Laplacian L = D − (W + h(c_t; φ)) combines a learnable prior weight matrix W with a memory-sensitive adjustment layer h conditioned on a set-based memory c_t (an order-invariant summary of all visited nodes). This set-based memory replaces the full visit history, providing context sensitivity without the exponential state space of higher-order Markov models. For property prediction, the random-walk representation is passed through a 5-layer GIN with Morgan-fingerprint node features and a linear head; for generation, edges are sampled sequentially using the learned transition probabilities with explicit validity checking at each step. The approach is an order of magnitude faster than Geo-DEG while matching or exceeding its predictive quality, and the learned grammar yields visually interpretable clusters and extractable design rules that align with expert chemical knowledge.

## Results

On property prediction, the method with expert motifs achieves the best R² on both regression datasets: 0.80±0.15 on Group Contribution (margin of 0.10 over Geo-DEG's 0.70) and 0.80±0.06 on HOPV (margin of 0.06 over Geo-DEG's 0.74), along with the highest accuracy (0.70±0.01) on PTC. For molecular generation, the method produces 100% unique and 100% novel molecules on both HOPV and PTC, with a retrosynthesis success rate of 58% on HOPV (+39% over DEG's 19%) and 60% on PTC (+22% over DEG's 38%). The method also runs an order of magnitude faster than Geo-DEG while performing strictly better as training data is reduced from 70% to 10%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method with expert motifs outperforms all baselines on R² for both regression datasets (GC: 0.80 vs. Geo-DEG 0.70; HOPV: 0.80 vs. Geo-DEG 0.74) and achieves the highest accuracy on PTC (0.70 vs. Geo-DEG 0.69). Geo-DEG is the strongest competitor, achieving the best AUC on PTC (0.71 vs. 0.69) and competitive MAE on HOPV (0.30 vs. 0.30). Pretrained GNNs (MolCLR, UniMol, Pre-trained GIN, PN) consistently underperform, with PN showing catastrophic R² (−7.56) on GC. Subgraph-based methods ESAN and HM-GNN are weaker still, with ESAN producing negative R² on GC. For molecular generation, the method achieves 100% uniqueness and novelty (vs. DEG's 98%/99% on HOPV and 88%/87% on PTC) and dramatically higher retrosynthesis scores (58% vs. 19% on HOPV; 60% vs. 38% on PTC), while VAE-based baselines (JT-VAE, Hier-VAE) struggle severely with uniqueness (8–43% unique molecules). The ablation without expert motifs remains competitive with Geo-DEG, confirming the method's data efficiency even without domain-specific annotation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the grammar-induced geometry line of work (Geo-DEG, Guo et al. 2023a; hierarchical variant, Guo et al. 2023b), which represents molecules via learned graph grammars for data-efficient property prediction. The key departure is replacing the complex tree-structured derivation process with a compact random walk over a motif graph, where the context-sensitive grammar is parameterized through a learnable graph heat diffusion process with a set-based memory. It also departs from prior motif-based predictors (HM-GNN, ESAN, ECFP+XGBoost) by explicitly modeling context-sensitive attachment rules between expert-defined motifs rather than co-occurrence or ego-graph features, and positions itself against pretrained GNNs (MolCLR, UniMol, GIN) in low-data regimes where those methods struggle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a real gap: most molecular ML research targets small drug-like molecules, while materials science (gas separation, photovoltaics) involves complex, modular molecules with only ~100–300 labeled examples. The interpretability results—extracting hard context-sensitive rules that correspond to known design principles (e.g., adding bromide to increase hepatotoxicity) and t-SNE clusters aligned with structural features—are practically valuable for expert-in-the-loop workflows. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger motif graphs, whether expert annotation can be automated (the paper suggests LLM-assisted rule extraction as future work), and whether the performance advantage over Geo-DEG holds on larger or more diverse datasets. The reliance on expert-defined motifs, while effective, limits broad applicability and introduces a manual bottleneck.
