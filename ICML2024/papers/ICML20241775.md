## Problem

Generic MCMC samplers (RWM, HMC, slice sampling) perform suboptimally in high dimensions when the target distribution has non-trivial covariance structure, off-center mass, or other geometric properties that are unknown a priori. While adaptive MCMC can learn target properties during sampling, existing approaches typically tune proposal distributions rather than transforming the target itself, and lack a principled parallelization strategy to accelerate this learning. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a framework that adaptively learns an affine transformation to bring the target into isotropic position, exploiting the known dimension-independent performance of certain base samplers under that condition.

## Input / Output

The method requires only pointwise evaluations of an unnormalized target density ρ: ℝ^d → [0,∞) (no normalization constant needed), a number of parallel chains p, a choice of base sampler (GPSS or ESS), and initial states for each chain. It produces a sequence of approximate samples from the target distribution along with the learned affine transformation parameters (center c ∈ ℝ^d and matrix W ∈ GL_d(ℝ)). The transformation is updated at discrete times according to a user-specified or default update schedule, and the computational cost per density evaluation in the latent space equals that of the untransformed target plus one matrix-vector product.

## Method

PATT runs p parallel MCMC chains that all share a single bijective affine transformation α(y) = Wy + c between a latent space and the sample space. Each chain performs transitions by mapping its current state to the latent space, taking a step with an ergodic base sampler (GPSS or ESS) targeting the pushforward distribution ν_α, and mapping back. At update times determined by a schedule S = (s_k), all chains synchronize and the transformation parameters are recomputed from the pooled samples: c is set to the sample mean (or median for heavy tails) and W to the Cholesky factor of the sample covariance, both updated via Welford's algorithm for O(p·d) or O(p·d²) amortized cost per iteration. The update schedule trades off synchronization overhead (chains must wait for the slowest) against adaptation frequency, with default schedules scaling with dimension d and chain count p. A theoretical result establishes ergodicity for finite update schedules under only the assumption that the base sampler is ergodic for each fixed transformation, and the approach is shown to be equivalent to traditional adaptive MCMC (e.g., AdaRWM) in specific cases via an 'ATT-friendliness' property.

## Results

In the multivariate exponential experiment (d=50), PATT-GPSS achieves a TDE/ES of 6.68 and 5,554.64 ES/s, outperforming all baselines by 1–3 orders of magnitude (e.g., HRUSS: 16,657.77 TDE/ES; NUTS: 670.33 TDE/ES). Across four Bayesian logistic regression datasets (d=25–78), PATT-ESS achieves TDE/ES values of 1.8–43.4, while PATT-GPSS achieves 7.6–71.7, both far below AdaRWM (151–3,208), HRUSS (2,221–25,927), and GESS (395–12,170). In the Gaussian process regression experiment (d=30), NUTS achieves the lowest TDE/ES (15.30) but PATT-ESS matches it on ES/s (109.68 vs. 101.49), while PATT-GPSS trails slightly on TDE/ES (50.61) but remains competitive on ES/s (99.91).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PATT-GPSS and PATT-ESS decisively outperform HRUSS (hit-and-run uniform slice sampling) in every experiment, with TDE/ES gaps of 2–4 orders of magnitude (e.g., 6.68 vs. 16,657.77 in the exponential experiment; 7.6 vs. 2,220.9 on CREDIT). Against AdaRWM, PATT samplers win by 1–2 orders of magnitude on TDE/ES across all settings (e.g., 6.68 vs. 151.13; 7.6 vs. 191.1 on CREDIT), though AdaRWM has the lowest per-iteration cost (TDE/IT = 1.00). GESS is also dominated by both PATT variants in every experiment where it was run (e.g., 6.68 vs. 167.62 in the exponential experiment; 7.6 vs. 394.7 on CREDIT), and it failed to complete the WINE experiment. The closest competitor is Stan's NUTS: in the exponential and BLR experiments PATT-GPSS achieves lower TDE/ES (6.68 vs. 670.33; 7.6 vs. 50.7 on CREDIT), but in the GP regression experiment NUTS edges out PATT-ESS on TDE/ES (15.30 vs. 21.95) while the two are nearly tied on ES/s (101.49 vs. 109.68), making NUTS the only baseline that is not clearly dominated.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces affine transformation tuning (ATT), a principled alternative to traditional adaptive MCMC (e.g., AdaRWM) that adjusts the target distribution via a learned affine map rather than tuning the proposal distribution. The method is specifically designed to synergize with Gibbsian polar slice sampling (GPSS, Schär et al. 2023) and elliptical slice sampling (ESS, Murray et al. 2010) as base samplers, leveraging their known dimension-independent performance in isotropic settings. The parallelization and update-schedule framework (PATT) draws conceptual parallels to GESS (Nishihara et al. 2014) and Craiu et al. (2009), but departs from them by decoupling the transformation learning from the base sampler's transition mechanism, and from transport-map approaches (Parno & Marzouk 2018; Cabezas & Nemeth 2023) by restricting to affine (rather than nonlinear) transformations. The paper also formally establishes when adaptive ATT is equivalent to traditional adaptive MCMC (via the 'ATT-friendly' property), clarifying the relationship to AdaRWM, IMH, and ESS-based adaptive schemes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PATT-GPSS provides a practical, tuning-free, gradient-free black-box sampler that matches or beats Stan's NUTS in most experimental settings while requiring no gradient evaluations, which is significant for problems where gradients are unavailable or expensive. The key limitation acknowledged by the authors is that in very high dimensions, the speed at which the affine transformation converges to its optimal value deteriorates, meaning PATT may require many iterations before approaching its asymptotic performance. Open questions include theoretical guarantees for infinite update schedules (the paper only proves ergodicity for finite schedules) and whether the approach can be extended to handle strongly non-elliptical targets (e.g., banana-shaped densities) that affine transformations cannot simplify. The framework's modularity—allowing arbitrary base samplers and adjustment types—suggests a useful template for future adaptive MCMC design.
