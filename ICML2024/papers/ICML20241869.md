## Problem

Neurons in trained DNNs are polysemantic, responding to multiple unrelated features simultaneously, which causes interpretability methods like saliency maps and feature visualizations to produce noisy, superimposed, and misleading explanations. Existing remedies require retraining the model with sparsity constraints, which is computationally expensive and alters the model's predictions, so interpretations no longer correspond to the original network. The paper asks whether sparsity can instead be applied as a sample-specific, post-hoc preprocessing step that improves interpretability without modifying the trained model.

## Input / Output

SPADE takes a frozen, pre-trained DNN (e.g., ResNet50, MobileNet-V2, ConvNext-T, or BERT) and a single target sample (an image or text input), then generates a batch of augmented versions of that sample (color jitter, random crop, random masking). The output is a sparse subnetwork trace Sparse(M, s) whose layer outputs match the dense model's outputs on the augmented batch, which is then passed to any standard interpretability method (saliency map, neuron visualization, etc.) to produce the final explanation. The trace is sample-specific: different inputs yield different pruned subnetworks, and the original dense model is never modified.

## Method

SPADE performs layer-wise, sample-targeted one-shot pruning as a preprocessing step. For each layer i, it collects the augmented batch's inputs X_i and outputs Y_i, then solves a constrained ℓ₂ optimization to find sparse weights W̃_i (with a per-layer sparsity budget) that best reproduce Y_i from X_i, using either the OBC solver (high accuracy, ~16 min/sample) or the SparseGPT solver (fast, ~3 s/sample with GPU batching). Pruning is executed in parallel across all layers so that each layer's sparsity decision is independent, and the per-layer sparsity ratios are tuned once (on ~100 examples or via a linear schedule) and reused for all samples. The key insight is that by pruning connections irrelevant to the specific sample's output, SPADE effectively disentangles the polysemantic superposition within each neuron for that input, so that downstream interpretability methods operate on a cleaner, sample-specific circuit. The resulting sparse trace is faithful to the dense model on the target sample, meaning the interpretations it produces are valid for the original network.

## Results

On ResNet50/ImageNet with Trojan patch evaluation, SPADE raises the average saliency map AUC from 91.01% (dense) to 96.55%, a +5.54% improvement, while FastSPADE achieves 94.34% (+3.33%). The largest gains appear for Saliency (+8.34%), InputXGradient (+9.66%), and LRP (+8.40%), with LRP reaching 99.21% AUC under SPADE. On clean inputs, FastSPADE improves average Insertion AUC by 3.02% and Deletion AUC by 9.80%. In a human study on neuron visualizations, evaluators correctly identified the relevant image region 69.8% of the time with SPADE versus 56.7% without it.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Sparse FC method of Wong et al. (2021), which retrains the final fully-connected layer to be highly sparse, SPADE achieves a substantially larger average AUC improvement: +5.54% versus +0.49% on the Trojan patch task, and +3.02%/+9.80% on Insertion/Deletion metrics versus +0.46%/−0.29% for Sparse FC. SPADE also outperforms Sparse FC on 9 out of 10 saliency methods in the Trojan AUC evaluation. Against a 98%-sparse model trained from scratch using the CAP correlation-aware pruning method (Kuznedelev et al., 2023), SPADE applied to the dense model achieves 96.3% average Trojan patch AUC versus 89.4% for the CAP-pruned model, a 6.9-point gap across all ten saliency methods. The key distinction is that SPADE is sample-specific and requires no retraining, while both baselines produce a single globally-modified network; the paper demonstrates that SPADE's interpretations remain valid for the original dense model, whereas the retrained baselines no longer correspond to the original model's predictions.

## Relation to Prior Work

SPADE repurposes one-shot pruning solvers (OBC, SparseGPT) from the model compression literature as a sample-specific preprocessing step for interpretability, a use case neither solver was originally designed for. It departs from the closest prior work, Wong et al. (2021), which retrains the final FC layer globally to be sparse, by instead performing per-sample, per-layer pruning that preserves the original model's behavior on the target input. The paper also positions itself against mechanistic interpretability approaches (sparse probing, activation patching) and sparse-training methods (Voita et al., Yu & Xiang), arguing that those require costly retraining or produce a single globally-interpretable model, whereas SPADE operates on any pretrained network without modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPADE provides a practical, drop-in preprocessing step (~3 s/sample for ResNet50) that consistently improves saliency map accuracy and neuron visualization quality across 10 interpretability methods and multiple architectures, without altering the original model. The key open question is whether the observed gains stem from resolving neuron polysemanticity as the authors conjecture, or from other circuit-level effects; the paper acknowledges this remains unverified. Limitations include the non-trivial cost of tuning per-layer sparsity ratios (mitigated but not eliminated by FastSPADE and linear schedules), potential systemic bias across example categories, and the fact that Trojan-patch evaluation, while the best available ground-truth proxy, may not generalize to all input types. Future work on incorporating SPADE into systematic debugging pipelines and circuit identification could broaden its impact.
