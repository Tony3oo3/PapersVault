## Problem

Rigorous benchmarking in reinforcement learning requires hundreds to thousands of random seeds per algorithm-environment pair to produce statistically reliable confidence intervals, making it computationally prohibitive for most research groups. Despite 91% of NeurIPS 2022 RL papers relying on benchmarking as their primary empirical evidence, the community lacks a feasible path to the statistical rigor needed for valid algorithm comparisons. The paper argues that benchmarking alone cannot be the sole evidence for algorithmic claims and proposes 'scientific testing'—targeted experiments designed to understand how an algorithm works—as a necessary complementary paradigm.

## Input / Output

The benchmarking experiments take eight fully-specified RL algorithms (Sarsa(λ), Q(λ), Actor-Critic, NAC-TD, PPO with Parl2 or scaled step sizes) and fourteen classic MDP environments (Cart-Pole, Mountain Car, Acrobot, Pin Ball variants, Gridworlds, Chain MDPs) as input, consuming approximately 334,000 algorithm-environment executions to build empirical performance distributions. From these, 1,000 resampled datasets at sample sizes of 10–1000 seeds are generated to evaluate confidence interval reliability. The scientific testing experiments use a four-rooms MDP with stochastic transitions and produce value function distance curves (‖v^π − v*‖₂), state-action entropy trajectories, and far-room visitation proportions across 100 trials per configuration.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a two-part empirical analysis. First, it quantifies the computational burden of rigorous benchmarking by constructing aggregate performance measures using performance-ratio normalization and game-theoretic adversarial weighting (solving a two-player zero-sum game over environment and baseline weights), then measuring bootstrap confidence interval width, coverage failure rates, and the number of algorithms statistically indistinguishable from the best across varying seed counts, algorithm subsets, and environment subsets. Key findings show that uniform weighting requires ≥100 seeds for valid CIs while adversarial weighting requires ≥10, and that identifying the top algorithm among similar performers can require 1,000+ seeds. Second, it demonstrates scientific testing through three targeted experiments on exploration algorithms (count-based intrinsic motivation with reward bonus β/√η(s,a) and adaptive restart distributions μ(s) ∝ η(s)⁻¹) on a four-rooms MDP, revealing that restart-based methods optimize policy across the full state space, that β positively correlates with state-action entropy in a decaying manner, and that restart distributions spend more time in far states regardless of β or transition stochasticity—insights unattainable through benchmarking alone.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that rigorous benchmarking requires at least 100 random seeds per algorithm-environment pair for uniform weighting (and potentially 1,000+ for adversarial weighting) before confidence intervals become reliable and narrow enough to distinguish algorithms. At 100 seeds, the average 95% bootstrap CI width is ~0.183 (Adversarial) vs. ~0.150 (Uniform), which is substantial given performance ratios are bounded in [0,1]. In the scientific testing experiments on a four-rooms MDP, restart-based exploration improves the policy across a larger region of the state space (measured by ‖v^π − v*‖₂) compared to intrinsic motivation, and spends significantly more time in far-from-start states regardless of the intrinsic reward magnitude β.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The scientific testing section compares count-based intrinsic motivation (Strehl & Littman, 2008) against a restart-based exploration strategy (inspired by Ecoffet et al., 2021), as well as their combination, on a stochastic four-rooms MDP. Restart-based exploration consistently outperforms intrinsic motivation in two key metrics: it spends more time in the far room (high-reward states distant from the start state) across all tested β values and random transition probabilities, and it reduces the distance to the ε-greedy optimal value function ‖v^π − v*‖₂ over a broader region of the state space. Intrinsic motivation alone shows that β positively correlates with state-action entropy, but this effect decays over time as the agent converges toward a deterministic policy. Notably, neither method converges to the optimal value function, suggesting a shared limitation (possibly related to the fixed step size). The restart-based method's behavior closely mirrors uniformly sampling the start state, indicating that the key mechanism is coverage of the start-state distribution rather than the specific adaptive heuristic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the performance evaluation methodology of Jordan et al. (2020) and the adversarial weighting framework of Balduzzi et al. (2018), but departs from the standard 'propose-and-benchmark' paradigm that dominates RL research. It connects to the reproducibility and statistical rigor literature (Henderson et al., 2018; Colas et al., 2018; Agarwal et al., 2021) by quantifying exactly how much compute rigorous benchmarking demands, rather than merely calling for more seeds. The concept of 'scientific testing' is borrowed from Hooker (1995) in the heuristics literature and repurposed here as a complementary experimental paradigm to benchmarking, with concrete demonstrations in the exploration algorithm domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, well-evidenced quantitative argument (334,000 executions, 1,000 trials per sample size) that rigorous benchmarking requires 100+ seeds per algorithm-environment pair, making it 20-50× more expensive than current practice. This reframes the community's experimental culture: benchmarking results should be treated as demonstrative rather than conclusive, and 'scientific testing' experiments that probe algorithmic mechanisms become essential for building cumulative understanding. Open questions include how to scale scientific testing to function approximation and large environments, whether the community will shift its evaluation norms, and how to design benchmarks that remain informative under severe compute constraints. A limitation is that the benchmarking cost analysis uses relatively simple MDPs, so the argument for modern deep RL is even stronger but not directly quantified.
