## Problem

Score-based diffusion models have achieved remarkable success in generative modelling, yet their application to simulation-based inference (SBI) had not been widely investigated. Existing SBI methods based on normalising flows require the model to be normalisable (imposing architectural constraints), while GAN-based methods require unstable adversarial training. This paper introduces a score-based diffusion approach that avoids both limitations by only requiring estimates of the gradient of the log density.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a black-box simulator p(x|θ), a known prior p(θ), and a single observation x_obs, and produces posterior samples via a trained time-varying score network s_ψ(θ_t, x, t) that is evaluated along the probability flow ODE. Unlike normalising-flow-based SBI methods that output a parametric density q_ψ(θ|x), NPSE outputs a sampler: samples are generated by integrating the ODE from a Gaussian reference distribution, with the score network providing the velocity field. The sequential variant (TSNPSE) additionally requires computing the highest-probability region (HPR) of the current posterior approximation each round to define the next truncated proposal, which involves multiple ODE forward passes for density evaluation.

## Method

NPSE trains a conditional score network s_ψ(θ_t, x, t) ≈ ∇_θ log p_t(θ_t|x) by minimising a denoising posterior score matching objective, where the expectation is taken over prior samples, simulator outputs, and forward-diffusion noise. Sampling from the posterior at x_obs is then performed by integrating the probability flow ODE from a standard Gaussian, substituting the learned score for the true posterior score. The sequential variant TSNPSE embeds this in an R-round adaptive procedure where, in each round, new parameters are drawn from a truncated prior defined by the highest (1−ε)-probability region of the previous round's posterior approximation. A key theoretical result (Proposition 3.1) shows that under the assumption the truncation does not exclude posterior mass, the proposal remains proportional to the prior within the posterior support, so the standard denoising score matching loss is minimised at the true posterior score without any importance-weight correction. The paper also derives error bounds for NPSE in the deterministic sampling regime, showing the W₂ distance between the approximate and true posterior scales with the L² score approximation error.

## Results

Performance is measured using the C2ST (classification-based two-sample test) score, where lower is better and 0.5 indicates perfect posterior estimation. Across eight sbibm benchmarks at simulation budgets of 1,000, 10,000, and 100,000, TSNPSE outperforms SNPE-C and TSNPE on the two most challenging high-dimensional tasks (SLCP and Lotka Volterra), while results on the remaining six benchmarks are mixed with the best method varying by task and budget. On the real-world Pyloric Network neuroscience problem (31 parameters, 18 summary statistics), TSNPSE achieves 81% valid summary statistics in the final round over 9 rounds (30,000 initial + 20,000 per round), exceeding the percentage achieved by TSNPE and SNVI at the same simulation budget.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NPE (Papamakarios & Murray, 2016), the non-sequential NPSE variant achieves comparable or superior C2ST scores across the eight benchmarks, with the advantage most pronounced on high-dimensional tasks. Against SNPE-C (Greenberg et al., 2019) and TSNPE (Deistler et al., 2022a), the sequential TSNPSE method clearly outperforms both on SLCP and Lotka Volterra (the two hardest benchmarks), while on the remaining six tasks the ranking shifts depending on the specific task and simulation budget. In Appendix F, TSNPSE is also compared against FMPE (Dax et al., 2023), a flow-matching approach that subsumes NPSE as a special case; the paper reports competitive performance. On the Pyloric Network problem, TSNPSE surpasses both TSNPE and SNVI (Glockler et al., 2022) in the percentage of valid summary statistics at equal simulation budget, and its posterior predictive samples closely match the observed data. The authors note that all methods use identical hyperparameter settings (MLP architecture, Adam optimizer, learning rate 10⁻⁴) without task-specific tuning, suggesting further gains are possible with additional optimisation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants conditional score-based diffusion models (Song et al., 2021; Batzolis et al., 2021) into the simulation-based inference (SBI) setting, where prior work has relied almost exclusively on normalising flows (SNPE, SNLE) or GANs (GATSBI). The sequential variant TSNPSE adapts the truncated-proposal mechanism from TSNPE (Deistler et al., 2022a) to the diffusion model framework, avoiding the importance-weight corrections that plague SNPE-A/B/C analogues. The training objective is the conditional denoising score matching loss, whose equivalence to the posterior score matching objective was established for image generation (Batzolis et al., 2021; Tashiro et al., 2021) but had not been applied to SBI. The authors explicitly note that diffusion models had not previously been investigated for SBI, positioning this as the first systematic treatment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NPSE/TSNPSE provides a principled alternative to normalising-flow-based SBI that avoids architectural constraints (no normalisability requirement) and adversarial training instability, while the truncated-proposal sequential scheme eliminates the need for high-variance importance weights. The main practical limitation is computational: CNF-based sampling and density evaluation require multiple ODE forward passes and gradient computations, making TSNPSE more expensive per iteration than its discrete-flow counterpart TSNPE. Open questions include whether purpose-built diffusion architectures (e.g., U-Net or transformer backbones) could close the remaining performance gap on some benchmarks, and whether sequential variants of the closely related flow-matching posterior estimation (FMPE) would inherit the same advantages. The method is most impactful for expensive simulators where the per-simulation cost dwarfs the per-iteration inference cost.
