## Problem

Conditional Sequence Modeling (CSM) methods for offline RL, such as Decision Transformer, fail to stitch together optimal trajectories from suboptimal data because they bind return-to-go values to individual trajectories rather than to optimal future returns that span across trajectories. Dynamic Programming (Q-learning) methods can propagate optimal returns per state but suffer from instability in long-horizon and sparse-reward settings. The paper addresses how to effectively combine the trajectory modeling strength of Transformers with the optimal-return predictability of Q-learning without inheriting either method's weaknesses.

## Input / Output

During training, the model consumes a K-step historical trajectory sequence of (return-to-go, state, action) tuples sampled from the offline dataset. At inference, rather than requiring a single manually tuned return-to-go value, QT samples a set of candidate RTG values, generates one action per candidate in parallel across the GPU, and selects the action with the highest Q-value from the learned critic. The output is a single continuous action vector at each timestep, with the Q-value module providing both training-time policy improvement and inference-time action selection.

## Method

QT combines a Conditional Transformer Policy (DT-style autoregressive sequence model) with a Q-value regularization term in the training objective. The total loss is the sum of the DT MSE loss (which acts as distribution-matching policy regularization, keeping the policy within the behavior distribution) and a Q-value maximization term that biases action sampling toward high-return actions, with the Q-value scale normalized across datasets via a hyperparameter η. The Q-value function is learned using the n-step Bellman equation with double Q-learning (two Q-networks with min operation), where the target action is sampled from a target Transformer policy. At inference, multiple candidate RTG values generate parallel action sequences, and the learned Q-value function selects the action with the highest estimated value, aligning the decision process with optimal returns rather than trajectory-level RTG matching. Theoretical analysis guarantees that the learned policy never assigns probability to actions outside the behavior policy's support and achieves value at least as high as the behavior policy.

## Results

On the D4RL benchmark, QT achieves average normalized scores of 89.8 (Gym), 60.4 (Adroit), 78.4 (Kitchen), 172.5 (Maze2D), and 76.5 (AntMaze), outperforming all baselines across every domain. The most dramatic gains appear in Maze2D (172.5 vs. 121.5 for DD, the next best) and Adroit (60.4 vs. 38.9 for GDT), where stitching and policy regularization are most critical. In the Gym domain, QT surpasses the strongest CSM baseline CGDT (82.4) by 7.4 points and the strongest Q-learning baseline CQL (80.6) by 9.2 points. On Atari (1% DQN-replay), QT achieves an average of 145.7 vs. 97.9 for DT and 107.2 for CQL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QT outperforms all 16 baselines across all five D4RL domains. Against Q-learning methods, QT beats CQL (80.6→89.8 Gym avg; 62.0→172.5 Maze2D avg) and IQL (77.0→89.8 Gym; 66.8→76.5 AntMaze), with the largest margins in Maze2D where CQL's conservative value estimation fails to stitch trajectories. Against CSM methods, QT surpasses DT (76.2→89.8 Gym; 13.8→172.5 Maze2D) and GDT (79.1→89.8 Gym; 38.9→60.4 Adroit), demonstrating that the Q-value regularization term resolves DT's stitching limitation without sacrificing trajectory modeling. QT also exceeds QDT (33.9→172.5 Maze2D avg), confirming that integrating Q-values into the training loss is more effective than QDT's approach of relabeling RTG tokens. In sparse-reward settings (Table 4), QT (68.6 avg) substantially outperforms CQL (7.2) and QDT (43.0), while maintaining parity with or exceeding DT (50.8), showing that the DT loss component provides robust policy regularization when Q-value estimation is unreliable. On Atari, QT (145.7 avg) beats DT (97.9) and CQL (107.2), extending the advantage to discrete action spaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

QT builds directly on the Decision Transformer (DT) framework, repurposing its conditional sequence modeling architecture and MSE training loss as a policy regularization mechanism rather than a standalone method. It departs from QDT (Yamagata et al., 2023), which combines CSM with Q-learning via RTG token relabeling (essentially data augmentation), by instead integrating a learned Q-value function directly into the policy training loss as a policy improvement term. The Q-value module itself follows the double Q-learning paradigm (Hasselt, 2010) with an n-step Bellman target, while the overall design philosophy aligns more closely with weighted imitation learning than with pure Q-learning, since the policy is generated by the Transformer rather than by argmax over the Q-function.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QT offers a principled way to combine the trajectory-level modeling strengths of CSM (robustness to sparse rewards, long-horizon handling) with the state-action-level optimal return prediction of DP methods (stitching capability), addressing a long-standing tension in offline RL where each paradigm excels in different regimes. The theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.2) that the learned policy dominates the behavior policy in value while remaining within its support is a clean formalization of the safety-improvement tradeoff. A key limitation is the dependence on explicit reward signals and the sensitivity of the balancing hyperparameter η to task characteristics, which limits applicability to purely demonstrative datasets without rewards. Open questions include whether the Q-value regularization can be made more adaptive (e.g., learned per-state) and how the approach extends to settings with partially observable or delayed reward structures.
