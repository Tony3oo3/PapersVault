## Problem

Modern neural networks contain extensive symmetries in their loss functions (permutation, rescaling, rotation), yet existing works study each symmetry type in isolation without a unifying theoretical framework. The paper asks: what is the general mechanism by which loss function symmetries constrain the learned solutions, and how do weight decay and gradient noise interact with these symmetries to produce structured outputs?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242603/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework operates on any twice-differentiable non-negative per-sample loss function ℓ₀(θ, x) augmented with L₂ weight decay γ‖θ‖², where the loss is invariant under a mirror reflection defined by a projection matrix P = OO^T (with O having orthonormal columns). The output is a structural constraint O^T θ = 0 on the parameter vector, which manifests concretely as sparsity (rescaling symmetry), low-rankness (rotation symmetry), or homogeneous ensembling (permutation symmetry). The theory applies universally regardless of architecture, data distribution, or loss function specifics, requiring only the existence of the specified symmetry.

## Method

The paper introduces the 'mirror reflection symmetry' as a unifying abstraction: a loss is O-mirror symmetric if ℓ₀(w) = ℓ₀((I−2OO^T)w) for all w. The central theorem proves four properties: (1) the gradient vanishes along the symmetry-breaking direction at symmetric solutions, making O^T θ = 0 a stationary condition; (2) the Hessian is block-diagonal with respect to the symmetry partition; (3) sufficiently large weight decay makes symmetric solutions strictly lower in loss; and (4) all global minima satisfy the constraint for large enough γ. For SGD, the paper shows that gradient noise (via minibatch sampling) actually *stabilizes* the symmetric solution through a negative Lyapunov exponent condition, meaning large learning rates or small batch sizes favor the constraint even when GD would not. The paper further establishes an L₁ equivalence of mirror symmetries (showing weight decay + symmetry acts like an L₁ penalty along the symmetry-breaking direction) and proposes the DCS algorithm, which enforces hard structural constraints differentiably by reparameterizing θ as (I−P)v + (Pw)⊙(Pu) to artificially introduce the mirror symmetry.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with experiments serving as validation rather than benchmarking. On CIFAR-10, a ResNet18 trained with the DCS (differentiable constraint by symmetry) algorithm reaches ~93% test accuracy, comparable to vanilla ResNet18, while producing highly sparse parameters (large fraction below 10⁻⁶ in magnitude) with no discernible performance loss. In matrix factorization, the learned rank decreases monotonically with stronger weight decay, and in a single-layer transformer, the ranks of key and query weights drop together under weight decay due to double rotation symmetry. In continual learning, a linear regressor with rescaling symmetry accumulates dead neurons across 10 tasks, while the vanilla (symmetry-free) regressor does not; injecting gradient noise (variance 1e-4) or adding random biases restores plasticity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external competing methods; instead, it validates its theoretical predictions by contrasting models with and without specific symmetries under identical training protocols. In the sparsity experiment, a vanilla linear regressor never converges to a sparse solution at any learning rate, whereas the same model reparameterized with a Hadamard product (introducing rescaling symmetry) converges to increasingly sparse solutions as the learning rate increases. In matrix factorization, removing the rotation symmetry via residual connections eliminates the low-rank preference entirely, confirming symmetry as the causal mechanism. For the ResNet18 experiment, the correlation matrix of penultimate-layer neurons shows clear homogeneous block structure only when weight decay is present (0.001), while such blocks are rare at zero weight decay. In the continual learning setting, the symmetry-free vanilla regressor maintains a minimal and constant number of dead neurons across tasks, while the symmetry-augmented version shows steady degradation—directly attributable to the absorbing stationary conditions predicted by the theory.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies a fragmented line of work on specific loss-function symmetries (permutation, rescaling, rotation) under a single 'mirror reflection symmetry' framework, where each prior case-by-case result (Fukumizu & Amari 2000 on permutation, Srebro et al. 2004 on rotation in matrix factorization, Ziyin & Wang 2023 on rescaling via spred) becomes a corollary of one master theorem. It departs from prior work by adding the SGD/gradient-noise perspective—showing that stochastic dynamics, not just weight decay, drives convergence to symmetry-induced constrained solutions—and by connecting the Hessian block structure to the Lyapunov-exponent stability analysis from Ziyin et al. (2023a). The DCS algorithm generalizes spred from rescaling symmetry to arbitrary mirror symmetries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified framework provides a single explanatory lens for a wide range of previously disconnected phenomena: sparsity, low-rankness, neural collapse, posterior collapse, dimensional collapse in self-supervised learning, and loss of plasticity in continual learning. The practical DCS algorithm offers a differentiable, architecture-agnostic way to enforce structured constraints (sparsity, low-rankness) without non-differentiable operations. Open questions include extending the theory to continuous symmetries (explicitly noted as future work), understanding whether the diagonal Hessian approximation for the Lyapunov exponent holds in high-dimensional settings, and leveraging parameter-space symmetries to learn data-space symmetries. The theory's reliance on discrete symmetries and the smoothness assumption (Assumption 1) for the global-minimum result limits its immediate applicability to non-smooth or continuously-symmetric losses.
