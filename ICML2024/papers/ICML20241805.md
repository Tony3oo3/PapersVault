## Problem

Differentiable Neural Computers and other implicit algorithmic solvers are typically trained with a fixed, small planning budget (p=10) that ignores the computational complexity of the target problem, leading to poor generalization to larger inputs. Additionally, extending the DNC's external memory to accommodate larger inputs causes a performance drop due to over-smoothing of content-based addressing scores, and training is unstable across random seeds. This paper asks whether choosing a planning budget informed by problem complexity, combined with memory and training adjustments, can resolve these generalization and stability issues.

## Input / Output

The DNC consumes inputs in four sequential phases: a description phase (graph edges as one-hot node-label pairs of dimension 2·N_max+2, 2D point coordinates, or binary item vectors), a query phase (source/target node pair or a query item), a planning phase (p zero-vectors with no external input), and an answer phase producing a sequence of edge tuples, point coordinates, or item vectors. The planning phase is the critical variable: the model must process its stored memory entirely through internal read/write operations during these p steps before it is permitted to emit any output tokens.

## Method

The paper proposes an adaptive planning budget p(n) that scales with input description length, motivated by complexity-theoretic lower bounds (e.g., Ω(log³n) for shortest path), replacing the standard fixed p=10. To enable generalization to inputs exceeding training memory, it introduces a temperature recalibration parameter τ that sharpens the softmax over content-based addressing scores (c = softmax(ĉ·β/τ)), and an adaptive memory strategy that expands memory by a factor of 2 and multiplies τ by α=0.85 whenever allocations exceed 65% of available cells. To combat training instability and overfitting to a specific budget, it adds a stochastic planning budget during training: extra planning steps are sampled from a geometric distribution (≥95% probability of stopping after p(n) additional steps), encouraging the model to retain its answer in a steady state rather than overfitting to an exact step count. The approach is validated across Shortest Path, Convex Hull, MinCut, and Associative Recall, showing that the adaptive budget matches or exceeds the best constant budget at a fraction of the training FLOPs, and that the empirically determined optimal budget p*(n) closely tracks the training budget p(n).

## Results

On Graph Shortest Path, the adaptive planning budget p(n)=n dramatically outperforms the standard p=10 baseline in generalization to inputs twice the training size, while matching the training FLOPs of the smallest constant budget. The largest constant budget (p=50) achieves comparable accuracy but at ×4 training cost. On Convex Hull, the adaptive p(n)=1.5n is matched only by p=50 while requiring less than half the training time. On Associative Recall (a computationally 'easy' task), the p=10 baseline already generalizes well and larger budgets provide no benefit, confirming that the planning budget should match problem complexity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is across planning budget choices rather than across different model architectures. The standard p=10 baseline (used in prior DNC work) shows poor generalization on Shortest Path and Convex Hull, with accuracy dropping sharply beyond the training input size. The adaptive linear budget p(n)=n (Shortest Path) and p(n)=1.5n (Convex Hull) outperforms all smaller constant budgets and is matched only by the largest constant budget (p=50), which costs ×4 (Shortest Path) or >2× (Convex Hull) in training FLOPs. On MinCut, where the optimal algorithm is O(n²), the advantage of the adaptive budget is less pronounced, and a quadratic budget p(n)=|V|·|E| proved ineffective and unstable to train. On Associative Recall, the p=10 baseline already generalizes effectively, and no larger budget (constant or adaptive) provides additional benefit, validating the paper's complexity-theoretic motivation that the planning budget should scale with problem difficulty. The stochastic planning budget fine-tuning 'fixes' failure cases where deterministic training with the same budget fails to generalize, with the gain attributed to stochasticity rather than additional planning time (deterministic fine-tuning with the expected value shows no improvement).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Differentiable Neural Computer (Graves et al., 2016) by reframing its planning phase through a computational complexity lens, connecting known lower bounds (e.g., Ω(log³n) for Shortest Path) to the practical choice of planning budget p(n). While prior work on adaptive computation time (Graves, 2017; Banino et al., 2020) and memory-augmented network improvements (Rae et al., 2016; Csordas & Schmidhuber, 2022) addressed related concerns, none specifically isolated the planning budget as the critical constraint on generalization. The paper also departs from the standard p=10 convention inherited from the original DNC paper, and from Ofner & Kern's (2021b) observation of memory-extension degradation, by diagnosing the root cause (over-smoothing of content-based addressing scores) and proposing a targeted fix (temperature recalibration) rather than a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a linear planning budget p(n)=n matches the best constant budget's generalization at roughly 4× less training FLOPs provides a concrete, low-cost recipe for practitioners using DNCs or similar memory-augmented solvers. The stochastic planning budget as a regularizer against training instability is a practical technique that could transfer to other recurrent algorithmic solvers. However, the results are confined to the DNC architecture, which is increasingly dated, and the authors themselves note that the MinCut task (Ω(n²) complexity) remains largely unsolved, suggesting a ceiling for this approach on harder problems. A key open question is whether these complexity-driven resource-allocation principles generalize to LLMs and other modern algorithmic solvers where 'planning time' and 'memory' are less cleanly defined.
