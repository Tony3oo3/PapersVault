## Problem

RL practitioners rely heavily on online Monte Carlo estimators to evaluate policies for hyperparameter tuning and algorithm comparison, requiring massive environment interactions that are expensive or infeasible in many settings. While offline RL reduces training data needs, the evaluation step still demands extensive online rollouts. The paper asks whether previously collected offline data can be leveraged to design a behavior policy that provably reduces the variance of the online Monte Carlo estimator while preserving its unbiasedness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241312/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes offline data tuples (t_i, s_i, a_i, r_i, s_i') that need not form complete trajectories and may originate from unknown or heterogeneous behavior policies, along with a target policy π and access to the environment for online evaluation. It produces a closed-form behavior policy μ̂_t(a|s) ∝ π_t(a|s)√(q̂_{π,t}(s,a)) parameterized by a learned value function, which is then used to drive online PDIS Monte Carlo sampling to estimate J(π). All hyperparameters for learning the behavior policy are tuned offline, and the same hyperparameters transfer across different environments.

## Method

The core contribution is a closed-form behavior policy μ̂_t(a|s) ∝ π_t(a|s)√(q̂_{π,t}(s,a)) that provably reduces PDIS estimator variance (Theorem 3) without requiring knowledge of the transition function. The key insight (Theorem 4) is that q̂_{π,t} equals the action-value function under a modified reward r̂_{π,t}(s,a) = 2r(s,a)q_{π,t}(s,a) − r²(s,a), making it learnable via standard offline RL methods like Fitted Q-Evaluation. Algorithm 1 first learns r and q_{π,t} from offline data, computes r̂ per data point, then runs FQE again on the modified reward to obtain q̂, from which μ̂ follows in closed form. Theorem 5 further shows that approximation error in q̂ does not introduce bias and only mildly degrades the variance reduction, making the approach robust to imperfect offline data coverage.

## Results

On Gridworld (size 27,000), the ODI method reduces the number of online steps needed to match on-policy Monte Carlo accuracy from 1200 to 540 (2.3% offline data coverage), 420 (4.6%), or 270 (18.4%), saving 55–77.5% of interactions. On MuJoCo robotics tasks, ODI requires only 35–81 episodes to match the accuracy that on-policy Monte Carlo achieves with 100 episodes, saving up to 65% of episodes (e.g., 35 vs. 100 on Inverted Double Pendulum). The method consistently outperforms both BPS and ROS baselines across all tested environments, with the largest gains on Hopper (54 vs. 89/100 episodes) and Inverted Double Pendulum (35 vs. 95/90 episodes).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: BPS (Hanna et al., 2017), which learns a behavior policy via stochastic gradient descent on online data, and ROS (Zhong et al., 2022), which adjusts sampling to encourage under-sampled data from known policies. ODI consistently outperforms both across all environments. On MuJoCo, ODI needs 35–81 episodes versus 89–103 for BPS and 90–103 for ROS, with the largest margin on Inverted Double Pendulum (35 vs. 95/90). On Gridworld (size 27,000), ODI with 2.3% offline data coverage needs 540 steps to match on-policy MC accuracy at 1200 steps, while BPS and ROS both require 990 steps. A key structural advantage is that ODI uses offline data (no online data needed for behavior policy learning) and works with incomplete trajectory segments from unknown policies, whereas BPS requires complete online trajectories and ROS requires complete trajectories from known policies similar to the target. ODI also provides a provable unbiasedness guarantee, which neither BPS nor ROS formally establish.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical importance sampling variance reduction framework (Kahn & Marshall 1953; Rubinstein 1981; Owen 2013) to the per-decision importance sampling (PDIS) estimator in finite-horizon MDPs. It departs from prior behavior policy design work (Hanna et al. 2017, Mukherjee et al. 2022, Zhong et al. 2022) in three key ways: it works on general MDPs rather than tree-structured ones, it is model-free (no transition function needed), and it learns the behavior policy from offline data rather than requiring online trajectories. The one-step optimal policy construction, which avoids the intractable global optimum by assuming the target policy is followed in future steps, is the paper's central methodological departure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Monte Carlo evaluation remains the gold standard for policy evaluation in RL due to its unbiasedness, and this paper shows that offline data can substantially reduce the online interactions needed (up to 65% fewer episodes in MuJoCo) without sacrificing that guarantee. The robustness finding—that even poorly tuned FQE approximations of the behavior policy yield significant variance reduction—suggests the method is practical despite the known unreliability of Bellman error as a tuning signal. Limitations include restriction to discrete action spaces, finite horizons, and PDIS specifically; extending to temporal difference learning, continuous actions, and other estimators (e.g., weighted importance sampling) are natural open questions the authors identify.
