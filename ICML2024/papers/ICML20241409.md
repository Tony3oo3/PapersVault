## Problem

While LLM biases along dimensions like gender, race, and religion have been extensively studied, geographic bias—systematic errors in geospatial predictions that discriminate against regions based on location—remains largely unexplored. This paper asks whether LLMs carry harmful stereotypes about geographic regions, particularly against areas with lower socioeconomic conditions, and whether such biases can be quantified and compared across models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241409/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes geographic coordinates (latitude, longitude) paired with a topic of interest and produces a scalar rating on a 0.0–9.9 scale. The prompt embeds a GeoLLM-style spatial context (coordinates, full address, nearby places) plus a three-sentence task-describing prefix to elicit zero-shot ratings without fine-tuning. Ratings can be extracted as the most probable discrete value (greedy sampling) or as the continuous expected value of the first digit via logprobs, the latter revealing biases invisible at the discrete level. For sensitive subjective topics, the output is further summarized into a bias score combining Spearman's ρ with an anchoring distribution, mean absolute deviation, and squared answer rate.

## Method

The core contribution is a zero-shot prompting framework that prepends a task-context prefix to GeoLLM prompts, enabling LLMs to produce geospatial ratings without any gradient updates—avoiding the confounding effect of fine-tuning on bias measurement. Performance on objective topics is measured via Spearman's ρ (rather than Pearson's r) because model ratings are rarely uniformly distributed and the authors care about monotonic rank agreement with ground truth. For sensitive subjective topics where no ground truth exists, the paper introduces a bias score B_y(x) = ρ(x,y) · MAD(x) · a², anchored to a proxy distribution (e.g., infant survival rate for socioeconomic conditions), which penalizes both correlation with the anchor and large rating variance. A key finding is that logprobs-based expected values expose subtle biases even when discrete ratings appear nearly constant (MAD < 0.02), and that bias magnitude varies significantly across models, with GPT-4 Turbo and Llama 2 70B showing the lowest scores.

## Results

On six objective geospatial topics, GPT-4 Turbo achieves Spearman's ρ of 0.71–0.83 in zero-shot mode, improving to 0.78–0.89 with logprobs (e.g., 0.89 on Average Temperature, 0.85 on Infant Mortality Rate). On sensitive subjective topics (attractiveness, morality, intelligence, etc.), all LLMs exhibit significant correlation with infant survival rate (a proxy for socioeconomic conditions), reaching up to ρ = 0.70 for GPT-3.5 Turbo w/ logprobs on Average Intelligence. The proposed bias score (product of Spearman's ρ, MAD, and answer rate²) reveals large variance across models: GPT-4 Turbo (mean 0.10) and Llama 2 70B (mean 0.09) are least biased, while Gemini Pro (mean 0.54) is the most biased.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five LLMs (GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-3.5 Turbo, Gemini Pro, Mixtral 8x7B, Llama 2 70B) on both zero-shot geospatial prediction accuracy and geographic bias. On objective topics, GPT-4 Turbo consistently outperforms the others (mean ρ ≈ 0.78 vs. 0.72 for GPT-3.5, 0.63 for Gemini Pro, 0.61 for Mixtral, 0.49 for Llama 2 70B), and using logprobs further improves GPT-4 Turbo to a mean ρ ≈ 0.83. On the bias dimension, GPT-4 Turbo and Llama 2 70B are the least biased (mean bias score 0.10 and 0.09 respectively), while Gemini Pro is the most biased (mean 0.54), driven by both high correlation with socioeconomic conditions (ρ up to 0.67) and high MAD (up to 1.23 on Morality). Notably, GPT-4 Turbo w/ logprobs reveals *more* bias on subjective topics (ρ up to 0.66) than its discrete-rating counterpart (ρ up to 0.47), showing that even near-constant ratings can encode subtle geographic stereotypes. Mixtral 8x7B shows the weakest zero-shot geospatial knowledge (ρ as low as 0.41) yet moderate bias, while Llama 2 70B has the lowest objective performance (ρ as low as 0.42) but also the lowest bias score, suggesting that weaker geospatial knowledge does not necessarily imply less bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends GeoLLM (Manvi et al., 2023), which extracted geospatial knowledge from LLMs via fine-tuning, by adapting its prompt structure to a zero-shot setting with an added task-describing prefix, thereby removing fine-tuning as a confounding factor in bias evaluation. It positions itself within the broader NLP bias-evaluation literature (BOLD, BBQ, HONEST, CrowsPairs) but introduces geography as a new axis along which social groups are distinguished, arguing that culture, race, economics, and politics are all meaningfully projected onto geographic space. The authors claim this is the first prompt-based framework that comprehensively evaluates multiple forms of geographic bias using geospatial ground-truth datasets and a purpose-built bias score metric.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all evaluated LLMs systematically rate residents of lower-socioeconomic regions (particularly Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East) lower on attractiveness, morality, intelligence, and work ethic — with Spearman's ρ up to 0.70 against infant survival rate — raises urgent concerns as these models are deployed to millions of users for recommendations, hiring, and content generation. The bias score metric (ρ × MAD × answer-rate²) provides a practical, interpretable tool for quantifying and comparing geographic bias across models, and the demonstration that even GPT-4 Turbo retains measurable bias suggests that current alignment procedures do not eliminate this dimension of harm. Open questions include whether the bias is reducible through targeted fine-tuning or RLHF, how it manifests in open-ended generation beyond rating tasks, and whether the 2000-prompt evaluation and single anchoring distribution (socioeconomic conditions) capture the full scope of geographic bias.
