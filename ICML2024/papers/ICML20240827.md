## Problem

While individual studies have documented representational similarity between specific pairs of models, there is no unified account of why neural network representations are converging across architectures, training objectives, and even data modalities, nor a formal characterization of what the endpoint of this convergence is. This paper asks: is there a principled reason representations should converge, what drives the convergence, and what shared structure are they converging toward?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240827/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes as input a suite of 78 pre-trained vision models (ViT-tiny through ViT-giant, ResNet-18/50 variants trained with supervised, contrastive, and self-supervised objectives) and multiple LLMs (LLaMA, BLOOM, Gemma, Mistral, etc.), along with paired image-text data from the WIT dataset to bridge modalities. For cross-modal alignment, kernels are computed separately in each modality's input space (image embeddings vs. text embeddings of paired captions), and alignment is measured between these two kernels rather than within a shared space. The output is a set of alignment scores (mutual k-NN, CKA, SVCCA, CKNNA) plotted against model competence, plus a theoretical derivation of the limiting kernel.

## Method

The paper combines empirical measurement with a theoretical derivation. Empirically, it uses a mutual k-nearest-neighbor alignment metric to show that (1) more competent vision models cluster more tightly in representation space, (2) alignment between vision and language models increases linearly with language modeling performance, and (3) alignment predicts downstream task performance on HellaSwag and GSM8K. Theoretically, it formalizes an idealized world of discrete events Z with bijective observation functions, then proves that contrastive learners with NCE objectives (SimCLR, SimCSE) converge to a representation whose dot-product kernel equals the pointwise mutual information (PMI) kernel of the joint distribution over events. Because bijective observations preserve probabilities, this PMI kernel is modality-invariant, so all modalities converge to the same kernel. Three selective pressures are proposed to drive convergence: the Multitask Scaling Hypothesis (more tasks shrink the solution space), the Capacity Hypothesis (larger models better find the shared optimum), and the Simplicity Bias Hypothesis (implicit regularization favors simple solutions, with the bias strengthening at scale).

## Results

Across 78 vision models (ViT, ResNet-50, ResNet-18) with diverse architectures and training objectives, models that solve more VTAB tasks form a tighter alignment cluster, while weaker models show more variable representations. In cross-modal experiments using the WIT dataset, language-vision alignment (measured by mutual k-NN, k=10) increases linearly with language modeling score (1 − bits-per-byte), reaching a maximum alignment of approximately 0.16. Alignment with DINOv2 predicts downstream performance on HellaSwag (commonsense) and GSM8K (math), with more aligned LLMs showing better task scores. A color cooccurrence case study confirms that PMI-based representations from CIFAR-10 pixel cooccurrences and from language cooccurrence modeling both recover the CIELAB perceptual color space.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper without a single proposed method, so comparisons take the form of cross-metric validation and cross-modal analysis rather than method-vs-baseline benchmarks. The authors validate their mutual k-NN alignment metric against CKA, Unbiased CKA, SVCCA, CKNNA, Edit k-NN, LCS k-NN, and Cycle k-NN; Spearman rank correlations among these metrics on 78 vision models are all above 0.55 (most above 0.70), confirming the alignment trend is metric-robust. For cross-modal alignment, CKA and SVCCA show weaker global trends, while local metrics (mutual k-NN, CKNNA with small k) reveal a clear positive correlation between model capability and alignment, suggesting convergence is primarily a local phenomenon. CLIP models, trained with explicit language supervision, exhibit the highest cross-modal alignment, but this alignment decreases after fine-tuning on ImageNet-12K classification, indicating task-specific specialization can erode the shared representation. The paper does not claim to outperform any baseline; rather, it synthesizes evidence from model stitching (Lenc & Vedaldi 2015; Bansal et al. 2021), Rosetta neurons (Dravid et al. 2023), and brain-alignment studies (Yamins et al. 2014) to support the convergence hypothesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and extends several threads: model stitching (Lenc & Vedaldi, 2015; Bansal et al., 2021), kernel alignment metrics (CKA, SVCCA), the 'Anna Karenina scenario' hypothesis, and contrastive learning theory (Wang & Isola, 2020; Zimmermann et al., 2021). It carves new ground by unifying these under a single formal hypothesis — that representations converge to the PMI kernel of the joint distribution over world events P(Z) — and by extending alignment measurement to the cross-modal setting (vision-language pairs via WIT), which prior work had not systematically quantified. The three proposed convergence mechanisms (multitask scaling, capacity, simplicity bias) draw on scaling law literature and implicit regularization theory but are combined here for the first time as a coherent explanatory framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

If the hypothesis holds, the practical implications are substantial: training data should be shared across modalities (images help train LLMs and vice versa), alignment with the platonic representation should predict downstream task performance, and continued scaling may reduce hallucination and bias amplification. Key open questions include whether convergence extends to non-bijective or lossy observations, whether special-purpose intelligences (robotics, bioinformatics) will follow the same trajectory, and what the ceiling of alignment is (current cross-modal scores reach only ~0.16 on the mutual k-NN metric). The paper also raises the sociological concern that convergence may partly reflect community bias toward human-like representations rather than a fundamental property of the learning problem.
