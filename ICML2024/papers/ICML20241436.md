## Problem

Existing document-level membership inference methods for LLMs rely on naturally occurring memorization and achieve strong AUC (0.86–0.88) only on very large models (66B+). For smaller models (1B range) trained on large datasets, these methods perform at chance level, leaving content creators without a practical way to verify whether their work was used in LLM training. This paper asks whether purposefully designed copyright traps—fictitious text sequences injected into original content—can enable detectability even in models that do not naturally memorize.

## Input / Output

The method takes a trap sequence (synthetic text generated via top-k sampling from a reference LLM, or a real excerpt from the document) of controlled token length (25, 50, or 100) and perplexity bucket, injected n_rep times (1–1000) into a target document. Detection is performed in black-box mode against both the target LLM and a reference LLM (LLaMA-2 7B), with the Ratio attack dividing target-model loss by reference-model loss. The output is a per-sequence attack score aggregated into an AUC over a balanced set of member (injected) and non-member (similarly generated but excluded) sequences, serving as a lower-bound proxy for document-level membership.

## Method

The authors train a 1.3B-parameter LLaMA model (CroissantLLM) from scratch on 3 trillion tokens, randomly injecting 7,500 unique trap sequences into public-domain books at controlled lengths, perplexities, and repetition counts. They first confirm that two state-of-the-art document-level MIAs (Meeus et al. 2023b; Shi et al. 2023) achieve AUC ≈ 0.51–0.52 on this model, validating that natural memorization is insufficient. They then apply three sequence-level MIAs—Loss, Min-K% Prob, and Ratio—to the trap sequences and find that short-to-medium sequences (≤50 tokens) repeated 100 times remain undetectable (AUC ≈ 0.52), contrary to prior intuition from training-data extraction literature. Only 100-token sequences repeated 1,000 times yield reliable detection (Ratio AUC = 0.748), and detectability increases monotonically with both sequence length and repetition count. The randomized controlled injection design further enables causal attribution: high-perplexity sequences are significantly more detectable (AUC 0.8 vs. 0.65 for low perplexity), and the authors show perplexity is a confounding factor in prior post-hoc memorization studies on The Pile.

## Results

On the 1.3B CroissantLLM, existing document-level MIA methods (Meeus et al. 2023b; Shi et al. 2023) achieve AUC of only 0.513 and 0.524 respectively, barely above the random-guess baseline of 0.5. The proposed copyright trap approach with 100-token sequences repeated 1,000 times achieves a Ratio MIA AUC of 0.748, while shorter sequences (≤50 tokens) repeated 100 times remain undetectable (AUC ≈ 0.52). Perplexity of the trap sequence correlates positively with detectability (Pearson r = 0.715, p = 0.02), and even 4-bit quantization of model weights retains an AUC of 0.697.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its copyright trap approach against two state-of-the-art document-level MIA methods: Meeus et al. (2023b) and Shi et al. (2023). Both baselines, which rely on naturally occurring memorization, achieve AUC of 0.513±0.021 and 0.524±0.003 respectively on the 1.3B CroissantLLM — essentially indistinguishable from random guessing. In contrast, the proposed trap-based approach with 100-token sequences repeated 1,000 times reaches AUC 0.748 (Ratio MIA), a substantial improvement over both baselines. The key distinction is that the baselines work well on larger models (AUC 0.86–0.88 on 7B+ models) but fail on smaller, less memorizing models, whereas the copyright trap method specifically targets this under-memorizing regime. The paper also shows that even with 4-bit quantization (a potential memorization mitigation), the trap-based AUC drops only to 0.697, still well above the baselines' ~0.52.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the document-level membership inference task for LLMs (Meeus et al. 2023b; Shi et al. 2023) by introducing injected copyright traps to enable detection in models that do not naturally memorize. It repurposes three established sequence-level MIA methods (Loss, Min-K% Prob, Ratio) from the privacy and training-data-extraction literature (Yeom et al. 2018; Carlini et al. 2021; Shi et al. 2023) as detection tools for trap sequences. The key conceptual departure is from prior work that relies on naturally occurring memorization: here, the content creator actively modifies the document by injecting purposeful sequences, shifting the problem from passive detection to active watermarking. The paper also draws on the traditional publishing practice of copyright traps (fictitious entries in maps or dictionaries) and adapts it to the LLM training context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first randomized controlled study of LLM memorization, enabling causal (rather than correlational) conclusions about how sequence length, repetition, and perplexity affect detectability. The finding that perplexity acts as a confounding factor in prior post-hoc memorization analyses (e.g., on The Pile) calls into question the validity of several widely cited results in the field. Practical limitations remain: effective traps require 100-token sequences repeated 1,000 times, which is disruptive to readability for most content types, and results are demonstrated on a single 1.3B model. Open questions include whether trap design can be made less intrusive, whether findings generalize across model architectures and scales, and how sequence-level deduplication in training pipelines might neutralize the approach.
