## Problem

Training GFlowNets for posterior sampling over discrete compositional objects requires repeated evaluations of the full reward function, which is prohibitive when data is partitioned across many clients (e.g., federated Bayesian inference). Existing embarrassingly parallel MCMC methods that solve this via divide-and-conquer are tailored to continuous random variables and rely on density estimation or normalizing flows, making them inapplicable to discrete state spaces. No provably correct parallel sampling method existed for discrete distributions.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input N locally trained GFlowNet forward and backward policies (p_F^(n), p_B^(n)), each sampling proportionally to a local reward R_n, and produces a global GFlowNet whose marginal over terminal states is proportional to the product R(x) = ∏R_n(x). Critically, the server never evaluates any local reward R_n directly; it only consumes the transition functions of the local models. The aggregation loss is agnostic to which training criterion (TB, DB, CB, etc.) was used to learn the local GFlowNets, requiring only their forward and backward policies. The entire scheme requires a single client-to-server communication round.

## Method

EP-GFlowNet is a two-phase divide-and-conquer algorithm: clients train local GFlowNets in parallel, then the server trains a global GFlowNet by enforcing a newly derived aggregating balance (AB) condition (Theorem 3.1), which is necessary and sufficient for the global model to sample from the product of local distributions. The AB loss compares trajectory-level ratios of forward-to-backward probabilities between the global model and the product of local models, integrated over pairs of trajectories. As a theoretical cornerstone, the paper introduces the contrastive balance (CB) condition, a new training criterion that requires only forward and backward policies (no partition function or state flows), making it minimally parameterized relative to TB and DB. The CB loss measures the squared contrast between two independently sampled trajectories and is shown to be equivalent in expectation to minimizing the variance of a TB-based partition function estimator. Theoretical guarantees include a Jeffrey divergence bound (Theorem 3.4) quantifying how local model errors propagate to the global distribution, and a connection between the CB gradient and the KL divergence between forward and backward policies (Theorem 3.7).

## Results

On the Grid World task, EP-GFlowNet achieves an L1 distance of 0.038 (±0.016) to the target product distribution, within one standard deviation of the centralized GFlowNet (0.027 ± 0.016), while the PCVI baseline is 5× worse (0.189 ± 0.006). On Multiset generation, EP-GFlowNet reaches L1 = 0.130 (±0.004) versus 0.100 (±0.001) for the centralized model, compared to 0.834 (±0.005) for PCVI. On Sequence design, EP-GFlowNet achieves L1 = 0.005 (±0.002) matching the centralized model's 0.003 (±0.001), while PCVI degrades to 1.872 (±0.011). In Bayesian phylogenetic inference, EP-GFlowNet attains an L1 error of 0.088, comparable to the average client error of 0.083 (±0.041), demonstrating accurate decentralized posterior learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares EP-GFlowNet against two baselines: a centralized GFlowNet (which has direct access to all local rewards and serves as an upper bound) and Parallel Categorical VI (PCVI), which approximates each local GFlowNet with a product of categorical distributions and then log-pools them (an implementation of the SDA-Bayes framework from Broderick et al., 2013). EP-GFlowNet essentially matches the centralized model on all three compositional tasks: its Top-800 average log reward is identical to the centralized version, and its L1 distance is within one standard deviation. In contrast, PCVI is dramatically inferior—roughly 5× worse in L1 on Grid World, ~6× worse on Multisets, and ~375× worse on Sequences (L1 of 1.872 vs. 0.005), with its Top-800 reward on Sequences being an order of magnitude lower (−16.473 vs. −1.535). In Bayesian phylogenetic inference, PCVI is not even comparable because it consistently samples invalid tree topologies outside the posterior's support. In federated Bayesian network structure learning, the EP-GFlowNet global model finds structures with significantly higher scores against the complete data than any individual client's model, while no tractable categorical variational baseline is applicable due to the combinatorial structure of DAGs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the embarrassingly parallel MCMC framework of Neiswanger et al. (2014) from continuous to discrete state spaces by leveraging GFlowNets as the local and global sampling mechanism. The divide-and-conquer scheme (train local models in parallel, combine at a server) is directly inherited, but the combination step is fundamentally different: instead of approximating products of continuous surrogates (KDEs, GPs, normalizing flows), the authors derive a new aggregating balance (AB) condition that operates purely on trajectory-level forward/backward policy ratios. The contrastive balance (CB) condition, introduced as a stepping stone to the AB, departs from the standard trajectory balance (Malkin et al., 2022) and detailed balance (Bengio et al., 2023) by eliminating the need for a partition function estimate or state-flow network, requiring only the forward and backward policies. The paper also connects CB to the variational inference characterization of GFlowNets (Malkin et al., 2023) and to the variance-based loss of Zhang et al. (2023c).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EP-GFlowNet is the first provably correct method for embarrassingly parallel sampling over discrete compositional objects, opening the door to federated and distributed Bayesian inference in domains where data is inherently discrete (phylogenetic trees, DAG structures, biological sequences) and where prior parallel MCMC tools are inapplicable. The single-communication-round design is practically significant for federated settings where client-server bandwidth is a bottleneck. Open questions include: whether the approach extends to joint inference over hybrid discrete-continuous parameters (e.g., tree topology + branch lengths), how to handle personalized federated learning via weighted log-pools (sketched in Appendix B.2 but not empirically validated), and a rigorous understanding of when and why CB outperforms TB/DB in convergence. The catastrophic failure mode (one poorly trained client degrades the global model) is inherited from parallel MCMC and remains unresolved.
