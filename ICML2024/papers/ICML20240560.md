## Problem

Mechanistic interpretability of Transformers commonly relies on simplifying model representations (e.g., projecting key/query embeddings onto top singular vectors or clustering them) to identify the features driving attention. This approach assumes the simplified proxy faithfully captures the original model's behavior, but it is unclear whether this faithfulness holds beyond the in-distribution data used to fit the simplification. The paper asks whether interpretations derived from such simplifications can reliably predict model behavior on systematic generalization tests.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes trained Transformer language models (two-layer Dyck models with 32-dim hidden states; four-layer code models with 64-dim attention embeddings) and applies simplifications to their internal key and query representations. The output is a set of approximation quality metrics—Jensen-Shannon Divergence between attention patterns and same-prediction rates—measured separately on in-distribution and out-of-distribution splits (unseen bracket structures, deeper nesting, unseen programming languages). The key finding is a consistent generalization gap: approximation quality degrades on OOD splits even when it is near-perfect in-distribution.

## Method

The paper trains small autoregressive Transformer LMs on controlled datasets with explicit systematic generalization splits: Dyck-(20,10) balanced-parenthesis languages (with splits for unseen structures and unseen depths) and character-level code completion on CodeSearchNet (with splits for unseen languages and deeper nesting). For each model, three simplification methods are applied to the second-layer (or per-head) attention mechanism: (1) SVD projection of concatenated key/query embeddings onto the top-k singular vectors, (2) k-means clustering of keys and queries separately with replacement by cluster centroids, and (3) replacing softmax attention with one-hot attention to the highest-scoring key (a data-agnostic simplification). The simplified proxy models are then explicitly constructed and evaluated on all generalization splits, and error patterns are decomposed by query depth, maximum sequence depth, and prediction type (e.g., closing brackets, repeated identifiers, whitespace) to identify which features the simplifications capture versus miss.

## Results

On Dyck-(20,10), the original two-layer Transformer reaches near-perfect accuracy on in-distribution and structural generalization splits, and ~75% on the depth generalization split. SVD simplification to as few as 4 dimensions preserves ~100% prediction similarity on the in-distribution (Seen struct) set, but shows consistent drops on Unseen struct and Unseen depth splits, indicating the proxy underestimates the original model's generalization. On CodeSearchNet, prediction similarity between original and SVD-simplified models is consistently higher on Java (in-distribution) than on unseen languages (JavaScript, Go, PHP), with the largest gap on the 'Repeated word' subtask (as low as 61% OOD vs. 75% ID for a single induction-head attention head). On SCAN, no discernible gap appears on the Add jump split, but a small consistent gap emerges on the Length generalization split.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three simplification strategies against the original model: SVD dimensionality reduction, k-means clustering, and one-hot (hard) attention. Data-dependent simplifications (SVD and clustering) consistently underestimate the original model's out-of-distribution generalization on Dyck—the simplified proxies perform worse on Unseen struct and Unseen depth splits while matching the original on in-distribution data. In contrast, the data-independent one-hot attention simplification is faithful on all Dyck splits except depth generalization, where it slightly outperforms the original model, thereby overestimating generalization. On CodeSearchNet, SVD simplification of individual attention heads reveals that the generalization gap is largest for 'algorithmic' prediction types (closing brackets, repeated words) and smallest for local-context types (whitespace, keywords), with the induction head in layer 4 showing the most pronounced gap. On SCAN, the Add jump split shows no gap (consistent with prior findings that well-generalizing models cluster the jump token with other verbs), while the Length split shows a small but consistent gap for both SVD and clustering.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper takes standard mechanistic interpretability tools—SVD-based dimensionality reduction of key/query embeddings, k-means clustering, and one-hot attention approximation—that have been widely used to reverse-engineer Transformer circuits (Lieberum et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Olsson et al., 2022) and asks a previously untested question: do these simplified proxies remain faithful to the original model under systematic distribution shifts? It departs from the implicit assumption in prior interpretability work that capturing most variance in in-distribution representations suffices to characterize model behavior, and connects to the broader robustness-of-explanations literature (Ghorbani et al., 2019; Adebayo et al., 2018) and to compression/pruning results showing that model simplification selectively degrades performance on harder examples (Hooker et al., 2019). The Dyck language modeling setup builds directly on the algorithmic Transformer constructions of Yao et al. (2021) and the grokking analyses of Murty et al. (2023), while the code completion experiments extend the analysis to a more naturalistic setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that data-dependent simplifications (SVD, clustering) systematically underestimate generalization—while a data-independent simplification (one-hot attention) can overestimate it—challenges a core assumption in mechanistic interpretability and has direct safety implications: if our simplified mental models of a system's computations are unreliable out-of-distribution, we cannot confidently predict failure modes or novel behaviors. Key limitations include the use of small-scale models (2-layer, 32-dim for Dyck; 4-layer, 64-dim heads for code) and the simplification of individual attention heads rather than full circuits, leaving open whether the effect persists or amplifies at scale. The paper also raises the question of whether fitting simplifications on OOD data would close the gap, and whether the phenomenon is specific to 'algorithmic' subtasks requiring precise, context-dependent feature use versus more local, distributed predictions.
