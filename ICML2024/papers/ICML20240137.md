## Problem

Prior theoretical work on algorithmic collective action (Hardt et al., 2023) characterizes collective success exclusively against Bayes (sub-)optimal classifiers, ignoring that practical learning algorithms exhibit distinct inductive biases and weighting schemes. This paper asks how the specific choice of learning algorithm—particularly distributionally robust optimization (DRO) and gradient descent with simplicity bias—alters the effective size and success of a coordinated group planting a signal in training data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240137/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a base distribution P_0 over features and labels, a collective signal transformation g that maps (x,y)→(g(x),y*), and a specified learning algorithm (JTT, LfF, CVaR-DRO, or SGD). The collective modifies its own α-fraction of data by planting the signal, and success is measured as P_0[f(g(x))=y*]—the probability the trained classifier maps the signal to the target label on the *base* distribution, not the collective's own data. Experiments use 2D synthetic points, CIFAR-10 (32×32 RGB), Waterbirds, and custom LMS-k / k-strips / MNIST-CIFAR datasets, with ResNet-50 or MLP classifiers trained via Adam.

## Method

For DRO algorithms, the paper introduces the effective collective size α_eff, which re-weights the nominal size α by the algorithm's sample weighting scheme; Proposition 3.1 shows any f-divergence DRO ball of radius δ contains a distribution with α_eff = α + δ/D_f(P*‖P_α), and Proposition 3.2 shows JTT amplifies small collectives (α_eff > α when the first-stage classifier errs more on collective samples) but suppresses large ones. For iterative re-weighting (CVaR-DRO), Proposition 3.3 proves that the collective's proportion β in the validation set—used as the stopping criterion—can yield strictly higher success than the Bayes optimal classifier on the training distribution, making validation-set influence the dominant factor. For simplicity bias, Theorem 2 introduces a surrogate distribution Q_0 that strips out features the algorithm overlooks, yielding a tighter success bound S(α) ≥ sup_{Q_0}{1 − ω/(1−ω) − ((1−α)/α)·Q_0(X*)·Δ_{Q_0}} that exceeds the Bayes-optimal bound of Hardt et al. (2023) whenever the algorithm ignores complex features, enabling collectives to embed signals in those overlooked dimensions.

## Results

On the synthetic 2D dataset and CIFAR-10, DRO algorithms (JTT, LfF) yield higher collective success than ERM for small collectives (α < ~0.2 on 2D, α < ~0.1 on CIFAR-10), but the advantage reverses for larger collectives, where DRO actually reduces success below ERM. For CVaR-DRO on Waterbirds and CIFAR-10, collective success is extremely sensitive to the validation set composition: with α_train=0.1 and α_val=0.1, success approaches 1.0, whereas with α_train=0.001, even full validation control caps success below 0.4. On LMS-k with SGD, collective success monotonically increases as the complex feature complexity k grows from 2 to 8, and on MNIST-CIFAR, success increases as the correlation between the simple (MNIST) and complex (CIFAR) features decreases from 1.0 to 0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against JTT (Liu et al., 2021) and LfF (Nam et al., 2020), the paper shows that a small collective (α ≈ 0.05–0.1) achieves higher success under these DRO algorithms than under ERM, because the first-stage classifier misclassifies collective members more often, inflating their effective size α_eff > α. However, for larger collectives (α > 0.2 on 2D, α > 0.1 on CIFAR-10), the DRO algorithms reduce collective success below ERM, as the collective is no longer in the error set and gets down-weighted. Against CVaR-DRO (Levy et al., 2020), the key finding is that success is governed primarily by the collective's proportion in the validation set (α_val) rather than the training set: with no collective in validation, success drops to 0 regardless of training-set α, while ERM success depends mainly on α_train and is nearly unaffected by α_val. The paper does not propose a new algorithm to beat these baselines; rather, it characterizes when and why each algorithm's inductive properties (re-weighting, validation-based stopping, simplicity bias) amplify or diminish collective success relative to the Bayes-optimal baseline from Hardt et al. (2023).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the formal framework for algorithmic collective action established by Hardt et al. (2023), which characterized collective success solely in terms of signal uniqueness, collective size, sub-optimality gap, and classifier sub-optimality under Bayes (sub-)optimal classifiers. The key departure is introducing the learning algorithm as a first-class variable: the authors show that DRO algorithms (JTT, LfF, CVaR-DRO) and SGD's simplicity bias fundamentally alter the success landscape in ways invisible to the prior theory. The notion of 'effective collective size' under weighted distributions and the surrogate-distribution analysis for simplicity bias are new theoretical tools that generalize Hardt et al.'s Theorem 1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that DRO algorithms—designed to protect minority subgroups—can paradoxically amplify the power of small collectives while diminishing that of large ones has direct implications for fairness practitioners deploying these methods. It also reveals that algorithmic inductive biases (simplicity bias in SGD) create exploitable blind spots that no amount of collective size alone can overcome, suggesting defenses must account for algorithm-specific vulnerabilities. Open questions include how much algorithmic knowledge a collective needs, whether uniformly random collectives match strategic ones, and how these effects extend to other biases (differential privacy, adversarial robustness) and to generative model safety techniques like RLHF and DPO.
