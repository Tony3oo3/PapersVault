## Problem

Classical batch goodness-of-fit tests require a predetermined sample size and are unsuitable for streaming data, where continuous monitoring inflates the type-I error rate and naive Bonferroni corrections yield low-power tests. This paper is the first to apply the principle of testing by betting to non-parametric goodness-of-fit testing, enabling valid sequential inference that adapts to the unknown difficulty of the problem without pre-committing to a sample size.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242567/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a continuous data stream {Z_t} drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution q, a target distribution p known only up to a normalization constant (so no partition function is needed), and a significance level α ∈ (0,1). At each round t it outputs a binary decision (0 = continue sampling, 1 = reject H₀ and stop), producing a stopping time τ and a wealth process K_t. The framework extends to discrete spaces (via KDSD) and bounded domains (via bd-KSD), where the target is a probability mass function or a density on a compact set V ⊂ ℝ^d respectively.

## Method

The core mechanism reframes sequential goodness-of-fit testing as a repeated betting game: a fictitious player starts with wealth K₀=1 and at each round selects a payoff function f_t and betting fraction λ_t, updating wealth as K_t = K_{t-1}(1 + λ_t f_t(Z_t)). The payoff is constructed from the KSD's variational representation by plugging in an empirical estimate of the witness function g* = E_q[ξ_p(Z,·)]/‖E_q[ξ_p(Z,·)]‖, yielding f_t(Z_t) = ½⟨ĝ_t, ξ_p(Z_t,·)⟩_{G^d}. The betting fraction is chosen online via the Online Newton Step (ONS) strategy for exp-concave losses, which guarantees the wealth process is a non-negative martingale under H₀ (giving time-uniform type-I error control via Ville's inequality) and grows exponentially under H₁ at a rate lower-bounded by S_p(q)/8. The paper further extends the construction to KDSD (using cyclic forward differences on discrete spaces) and bd-KSD (using a boundary-vanishing auxiliary function h on compact domains), with matching type-I error guarantees under boundedness assumptions on the kernel and target density.

## Results

The KSD-based SKGT (Algorithm 2) achieves a lower average stopping time than the Bonferroni-corrected batch KSD test across all difficulty levels (β ∈ {0.5, 0.55, ..., 1.0}) in the mixture experiment, and uniquely exhibits decreasing stopping time as the problem becomes easier (β → 1). Under the null hypothesis, type-I error is rigorously controlled at level α (e.g., no rejections within 1000 samples for the Ising model, or 10⁴ samples for truncated Gaussians). Under the alternative, the wealth process grows exponentially with a guaranteed rate of at least S_p(q)/8, where S_p(q) is the true Kernel Stein Discrepancy, ensuring consistency (P(τ < ∞) = 1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the batch KSD test (Chwialkowski et al., 2016) adapted for continuous monitoring via Bonferroni correction, with p-values computed every n = 10 or n = 100 new samples. The proposed KSD-based SKGT consistently requires fewer samples to reject the null hypothesis than both Bonferroni-corrected variants across all 11 difficulty levels (β from 0.5 to 1.0). Critically, the SKGT's average stopping time decreases as β approaches 1 (easier problem), a property not observed in the corrected batch tests, demonstrating genuine adaptability to unknown problem difficulty. Without any correction, the batch KSD test under continuous monitoring exhibits severely inflated type-I error (as shown in Figure 5 of the appendix), while the Bonferroni correction restores validity at the cost of substantially reduced power. The paper positions sequential tests as complementary to batch tests: for small datasets (e.g., 2,000 points), sequential tests allow continued data collection if the null is not yet rejected, whereas for large datasets (e.g., 2 million points), sequential tests can terminate early (within ~200 samples) when the signal is strong, avoiding the expensive bootstrap procedure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the first to apply the testing-by-betting framework (Ville, 1939; Shafer, 2021) to non-parametric goodness-of-fit testing, extending the sequential two-sample testing approach of Shekhar & Ramdas (2023) to the one-sample setting. The core construction repurposes the Kernel Stein Discrepancy's variational representation (Chwialkowski et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016) as a payoff function in a repeated betting game, with the Online Newton Step strategy (Cutkosky & Orabona, 2018) handling adaptive bet sizing. The paper further generalizes the framework to discrete spaces via KDSD (Yang et al., 2018) and bounded domains via bd-KSD (Xu, 2022), unifying three Stein discrepancy variants under a single sequential testing architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine practical gap: batch KSD tests applied to streaming data either inflate type-I error or lose substantial power under Bonferroni correction, while the proposed sequential test maintains anytime-valid error control and adapts its stopping time to problem difficulty. The characterization of wealth growth rate in terms of the true KSD (Theorem 2.5) provides a clean theoretical link between statistical power and the underlying distributional discrepancy. Open questions include computational scalability to high-dimensional data (the plug-in witness estimator is O(t) per step), whether adaptive kernel selection can further improve power, and whether the framework extends to conditional or multi-sample goodness-of-fit settings.
