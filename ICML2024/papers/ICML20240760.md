## Problem

Real-world data marketplaces (e.g., AWS Data Exchange, Snowflake) use a simple fixed-price format where sellers post prices and buyers freely choose which datasets to purchase, yet this structure has never been formally analyzed as a game. Because data is freely replicable and one buyer's purchase induces negative externality on competitors, buyer interactions constitute a simultaneous game whose equilibrium properties and welfare guarantees are unknown. The paper asks whether this simple market can allocate data efficiently, how externality degrades outcomes, and whether a minimal platform intervention can restore near-optimal welfare.

## Input / Output

The model takes as input each buyer's net gain function g_i(γ) over the 2^k seller subsets, pairwise externality functions e_ij (either independent of the buyer's own action or joint in both buyers' actions), seller prices c(γ), a platform-chosen parameter α ∈ [0,1], and predicted externalities with itemized bias b_ij. The output is a per-buyer transaction cost T_i(S) that is revenue-neutral (sums to zero across all buyers), a dominant-strategy or ε-PNE equilibrium, and a welfare regret at equilibrium (WRaE) bound. In the online variant, buyers observe stochastic realizations of gain, externality, and cost each round and must select from 2^k arms, producing a time-indexed seller-set sequence with sublinear effective and cumulative welfare regret.

## Method

The core intervention is a transaction cost T_i(S) = α(Σ_{j≠i} Ê_ji(γ_i) − Σ_{j≠i} Ê_ij(γ_j)) that charges each buyer the α-scaled difference between predicted externality they cause and suffer, making their effective utility u_i^e(γ) = g_i(γ) − α Σ_{j≠i} ê_ji(γ) a dominant-strategy objective. For the standard (independent) externality model, this yields WRaE ≤ n(1−α) + Δ_b^max, approaching zero as α→1. For the online setting with 2^k arms, the paper adapts the zooming algorithm (Kleinberg et al., 2019) to the discrete Hamming metric, exploiting a Lipschitz-like property |g_i(γ¹)−g_i(γ²)| ≤ λ_g D_h(γ¹,γ²) to achieve instance-dependent regret that improves over UCB when utility gaps correlate with Hamming distance, reaching Õ(k(√(kT log T) + 2^{0.58k} log T)) under a natural target-set condition. For the richer joint externality model E_ij(γ_i, γ_j), the paper shows that α = 0.5 makes the combined externality-plus-cost structure symmetric, reducing the game to a potential game whose PNE is an ε-PNE of the original with ε → 0 as bias vanishes, and bounding WRaE at n/2.

## Results

The paper's headline result (Theorem 1) shows that without platform intervention, the welfare regret at equilibrium (WRaE) is Θ(n) (maximal), while a simple transaction cost parameterized by α reduces WRaE to at most n(1−α) + Δ_b^max, with a matching lower bound of n(1−α), making the bound tight. In the online learning setting, the zooming algorithm achieves instance-dependent regret (Theorem 2), with a worst-case bound of O(√(2^k T log T)) (Corollary 1) that improves to O(k(√(kT log T) + 2^0.58k log T)) under a natural metric condition (Theorem 3). For the richer joint externality model, the paper proves that without intervention no ε-PNE exists for ε < 1 (Proposition 2), while with the transaction cost an ε-PNE always exists with ε → 0 as α → 0.5 and prediction bias → 0 (Theorem 5), and WRaE is at most n/2 (Theorem 6). Experimental validation on AWS Data Exchange data (Appendix F) confirms that even a small α yields meaningful social welfare gains, with near-maximal improvements at α = 0.6 for the standard externality model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the no-intervention baseline (α = 0) and the proposed transaction cost intervention. Without intervention, the dominant-strategy equilibrium yields maximal WRaE of Θ(n) (Proposition 1), and in the joint externality setting, even an ε-PNE may not exist for ε < 1 (Proposition 2). With the proposed transaction cost, WRaE drops to n(1−α) + Δ_b^max for the standard externality model (Theorem 1) and at most n/2 for the joint externality model (Theorem 6), representing a fundamental improvement from worst-case to near-optimal welfare. The paper also contrasts its approach with VCG mechanisms, noting that while spiritually similar (both internalize externalities), the fixed-price market setting fundamentally differs from auction-based VCG because the platform does not control allocations. In the online setting, the zooming algorithm is compared against standard UCB (which gives O(√(2^k T log T)) regret) and linear/combinatorial bandits (which require a strong linearity assumption on data utility); the zooming algorithm matches UCB in the worst case but significantly outperforms it when the utility gap correlates with Hamming distance from the optimal seller set.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of work on data market design (Bergemann & Bonatti 2019, Agarwal et al. 2019/2020, Mehta et al. 2019), which assumes auction-based mechanisms requiring buyers to report valuations. Instead, it takes the fixed-price market format actually used by platforms like AWS Data Exchange and Snowflake as given, and analyzes buyer interactions as a simultaneous game with externality — a formulation the authors explicitly note has not been formally studied. For the online learning component, the paper adapts the zooming algorithm from Kleinberg et al. (2019) for metric bandits to the discrete Hamming space of seller subsets, a non-trivial modification due to the quantized distance structure. The externality modeling draws on monopoly data selling (Admati & Pfleiderer, Bimpikis et al.) and data auctions (Agarwal et al. 2020) but abstracts away specific competitive environments to a general form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a notable gap by showing that the simple fixed-price data markets actually deployed in practice have fundamentally poor equilibrium properties (maximal welfare regret) without intervention, and that a revenue-neutral transaction cost proportional to net externality can restore near-optimal welfare — a result with direct operational implications for data marketplace operators. Open questions include whether the proposed transaction cost is optimal, how to design truthful mechanisms for eliciting externality estimates, how seller-side externality and strategic pricing interact, and whether the joint externality model extends to the online learning setting. The empirical validation on AWS Data Exchange data, while preliminary, suggests even modest intervention parameters yield meaningful welfare gains, making the findings practically actionable.
