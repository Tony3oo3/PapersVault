## Problem

Deploying DRL agents in commercial fighting games with large character pools (400+ characters, ~16×10⁴ matchups) faces a three-way tension: training efficiency, model competence, and generalization to unseen characters. Prior work focused on maximizing agent strength to superhuman levels, overlooking the practical need for agents to be challenging yet beatable and behaviorally aligned with diverse human player skill levels and playstyles.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242436/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent consumes structured game-state observations split into ID information (discrete references to characters, skills, actions) and attribution information (continuous values: HP, cooldowns, hitbox/hurtbox positions, buff timers), plus environment features (spatial relations, remaining time). The output is a three-head one-hot prediction: movement (4 directions via virtual joystick), skill selection (8 options: punch, skill1–3, substitute, summon, scroll, subskill1–2), and skill direction (8 compass directions). Critically, the deployed QS agent deliberately discards opponent ID information, consuming only opponent attribution values, to force learning of generalizable game knowledge rather than character-specific optimal responses.

## Method

Shukai introduces Heterogeneous League Training (HELT), a league structure inspired by AlphaStar but populated with three structurally distinct agents: FIS (full ID for self and opponent), QS (ID for self, attribution-only for opponent), and FQS (attribution-only for both). The QS agent serves as the main agent for generalization, the FIS agent serves as the league exploiter for rapid competence gains via character-specific optimal play, and a main exploiter (same structure as main agent) targets the main agent's weaknesses. This structural heterogeneity expands the policy space beyond what homogeneous league training can achieve, yielding a 22% sample-efficiency improvement over AlphaStar-style homogeneous league training. Policy optimization uses PPO with GAE, and opponent selection uses Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play (PFSP) with both hard and variance weighting functions. For agent-human alignment, three modular reward functions (balanced, cautious, aggressive) are designed to match observed human player archetypes, and action masking with reduced prediction frequency constrains the agent to human-like reaction speeds.

## Results

HELT achieves a 22% improvement in competence over homogeneous league training (HOLT) after 70 hours of training, demonstrating faster convergence and higher final performance. Shukai, trained on only 13% of Naruto Mobile's 400+ characters (50 characters across S/A/B/C tiers), generalizes consistently to all characters. The FIS structure outperforms QS and FQS on familiar opponents but suffers a 36% competence drop on unfamiliar characters, while QS and FQS remain stable. In real-world deployment, Shukai beginner's Elo score decreased by 9% over 30 days (indicating player skill growth), and the game achieved over 50% next-day retention, 20% 7-day retention, and 10% 30-day retention, with a 5% maximum and 4% average growth rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is homogeneous league training (HOLT), the AlphaStar-style approach where all league agents share the same network structure. HELT outperforms HOLT by 22% in competence after 70 hours of training under identical resource settings (same GPUs, CPUs, epochs, and time limits), demonstrating that structural heterogeneity in the league accelerates convergence. Among the three network structures tested as the main agent, FIS (full ID + attribution) achieves the highest competence against familiar characters but drops 36% against unfamiliar ones, while QS (ID for self, attribution-only for opponent) and FQS (attribution-only for both) maintain stable performance across both settings. The paper ultimately selects QS as the deployed main agent, balancing competence and generalization. In the real-world A/B test, Shukai's three difficulty tiers (beginner/intermediate/advanced) show that lower-tier agents' Elo scores decline over 30 days as players improve, while the advanced tier maintains its score with less than 10% gap to the highest player ratings, confirming the agent is challenging but beatable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the league training paradigm from AlphaStar (Vinyals et al., 2019) to fighting games by introducing heterogeneous agents with structurally different networks (FIS, QS, FQS) within a single league, rather than the homogeneous agents used in AlphaStar. It also incorporates regret-matching-based character selection (Wang, 2024) to balance training across a 400+ character pool. The broader lineage traces from DQN and AlphaGo through AlphaStar and OpenAI Five, but this work deliberately shifts the objective from maximizing raw competence to balancing competence, generalization, and human-player alignment for commercial deployment—a framing absent from prior game-AI literature that focused on beating human champions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first reported deployment of a DRL system in a large-scale commercial fighting game (100M+ users), demonstrating that practical constraints (single model for 400+ characters, beatable difficulty tiers, human-like behavior) can be addressed within a unified training framework. The agent-human alignment evaluation, while useful, relies on expert-designed scoring metrics and CDF comparisons that are somewhat subjective, and the generalization claim (trained on 13% of characters) is validated only within one game. Open questions include whether HELT's heterogeneous structure generalizes to other game genres (the authors mention plans for a first-person shooter), whether the alignment rewards transfer across games, and whether the 22% sample-efficiency gain holds at different scales or with alternative base algorithms.
