## Problem

From an information-theoretic standpoint, predicting the next token and predicting the previous token in a language should be equally difficult, yet no systematic study had quantified this asymmetry for large autoregressive models on large natural-language corpora. This paper addresses the gap by empirically demonstrating a consistent 'Arrow of Time' (AoT) in which forward models achieve lower cross-entropy than backward models, and by providing a theoretical framework rooted in computational complexity and sparsity to explain why such an asymmetry arises.

## Input / Output

The method takes tokenized text sequences (from CC-100 or synthetic datasets) of length n and produces a per-token probability distribution over a 50,257-token BPE vocabulary, scored by cross-entropy loss. The key experimental design trains two architecturally identical models with identical hyperparameters: the forward model predicts token x_{k} from (x_1,...,x_{k-1}), while the backward model is fed the reversed sequence (x_n,...,x_1) and predicts the previous token. The primary output compared is the average log-perplexity (in nats) of each model, with the AoT quantified as the relative percentage difference between BW and FW losses.

## Method

The paper combines large-scale empirical sweeps with two synthetic theoretical models. Empirically, it trains GPT (4.9M–1.6B), GRU, and LSTM models across 8+ languages, 6 context lengths (16–512), and 6 model sizes, confirming a universal forward AoT that grows with context window and model size. Theoretically, it first constructs a prime-factorization dataset (p × q ↔ rev(pq)) where the computational hardness of factoring versus multiplying creates a measurable AoT (22.2 vs 30.2 nats for GPT2-Medium). It then introduces 'linear languages' over F_2^m, showing that a sparse forward linear map generically has a less sparse inverse (Claim 8), making the forward direction easier to learn; a communication-theoretic setup motivates why selection pressure would favor the forward direction in natural languages, yielding the observed FW AoT.

## Results

The paper demonstrates a consistent Forward Arrow of Time (FWAoT): FW models achieve lower cross-entropy loss than BW models across all tested settings. For GPT2-Medium (405M params, context 256) on CC-100, the BW loss exceeds the FW loss by +0.76% for English (FW: 2.88, BW: 2.902) and +2.65% for French (FW: 2.788, BW: 2.862). The AoT magnitude grows with both model size (from ~0% at 4.92M to +2.65% at 405M for French) and context window length (from near-zero at 16 tokens to larger values at 512 tokens), and is observed consistently across 8+ languages and GPT/GRU/LSTM architectures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between FW (next-token) and BW (previous-token) models trained with identical architecture, hyperparameters, and data — the only difference being the token order. The FW model consistently outperforms the BW model in cross-entropy loss across all 8+ languages tested (English +0.76%, French +2.65%, Greek +2.4%, Indonesian +2.18%, Vietnamese +1.67%, German +1.46%, Finnish +1.07%, Turkish +0.94% for GPT2-Medium). The effect is architecture-independent: GRU and LSTM models show the same FW advantage (e.g., French GRU-L: +0.62%, LSTM-L: +0.66%). The AoT is not a tokenization artifact (confirmed by re-training BPE tokenizers in reverse) nor an initialization effect (Greek re-runs show AoT of 2.46% ± 0.18%). The magnitude varies by language but the direction (FW < BW) is universal, suggesting the effect is intrinsic to natural language structure rather than a model-specific quirk.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes and systematically investigates a question first raised by Shannon (1951) — whether predicting the next token is fundamentally easier than predicting the previous one — but departs from prior work by framing the resulting asymmetry as a measurable property of the data itself (the 'Arrow of Time') rather than a training trick. While bidirectional models (BERT, XLNet, T5) and FW/BW combinations (Nguyen et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2023) have used reversed sequences to improve performance, they treat the two directions as components of a single model; this paper isolates the asymmetry under strictly controlled conditions (identical architecture, hyperparameters, and data, differing only in token order). The theoretical explanation draws on V-information (Xu et al., 2020) and structural causal models (Peters et al., 2017) for the conceptual framing, and on sparse matrix theory and computational complexity (integer factorization) for concrete synthetic constructions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a consistent, direction-specific asymmetry exists across all tested natural languages, architectures, and scales — despite information-theoretic arguments predicting exact equality — challenges the assumption that learning difficulty is direction-invariant and suggests that natural language carries a structural 'causal' ordering that LLMs can exploit. The sparsity-based explanation (sparse forward maps have denser inverses) provides a testable mechanism but remains somewhat hand-wavy for natural language, leaving open whether the AoT is of Type 1 (unrepresentable) or Type 2 (hard to learn), whether scaling laws govern its magnitude, and whether it extends to non-linguistic data (code, DNA, video). The paper also raises the provocative question of whether the presence of an AoT could serve as a signature of intelligent or biological processing in data.
