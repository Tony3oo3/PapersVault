## Problem

Existing MIPS algorithms scale at least as O(√d) with respect to dimension d, becoming computationally prohibitive in high-dimensional domains like e-commerce, genomics, and finance. While substantial prior work has improved scaling with the number of atoms n (via LSH, proximity graphs, quantization), comparatively little attention has been paid to reducing the per-coordinate computational cost, leaving a gap for algorithms that remain efficient as d grows into the millions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241975/cropped_page5_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a d-dimensional query vector q, n d-dimensional atom vectors, an error probability δ controlling the confidence level, and a sub-Gaussianity parameter σ that bounds the variance of coordinate-wise products (e.g., σ=25 for 0–5 rating data). It outputs the index i* of the atom maximizing v_i^T q, with a provable (1−δ) correctness guarantee. A key operational detail is that the algorithm never computes the full d-dimensional inner product for most atoms; instead it adaptively samples coordinates until the solution set shrinks to one, falling back to exact computation only for the small residual candidate set.

## Method

BanditMIPS reduces MIPS to a best-arm identification problem: each atom is an arm with parameter μ_i = v_i^T q / d, and pulling an arm samples a random coordinate J ~ Unif[d] to observe the scalar product q_J v_iJ. The algorithm combines UCB-style confidence intervals (via Hoeffding's inequality under a sub-Gaussian assumption) with successive elimination, iteratively discarding atoms whose upper confidence bound falls below the lower bound of the current best candidate. The central theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) proves O(1) sample complexity in d, since the per-atom cost depends only on the gap Δ_i = μ_{i*} − μ_i and not on d. A variant, BanditMIPS-α, further reduces variance by sampling coordinates non-uniformly with weights w_j ∝ q_j^{2β}, where the optimal β is derived via a Lagrangian variance-minimization argument (Theorem 4.2); the extreme case β→∞ sorts coordinates by query magnitude and samples in that order.

## Results

BanditMIPS achieves O(1) sample complexity with respect to dimension d, improving upon the prior state-of-the-art O(√d) scaling. On the Netflix dataset (n=1,000, d=100,000, ε=0.1, δ=0.1), BanditMIPS achieves a 53.02× wall-clock speedup over the naive algorithm, while the closest baseline BoundedME is actually 0.36× (slower than naive). On the MovieLens dataset (d=6,000), BanditMIPS achieves 14.19× speedup, and on the OPT-6.7B LLM classification layer (d=4,096, n=10,000), BanditMIPS-α achieves 6.02× speedup. Across all four real-world datasets, BanditMIPS's sample complexity remains flat as d increases, validating the theoretical O(1) scaling guarantee.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BanditMIPS outperforms all eight baselines (LSH-MIPS, H2-ALSH-MIPS, NEQ-MIPS, PCA-MIPS, BoundedME, Greedy-MIPS, HNSW-MIPS, NAPG-MIPS) in sample complexity across all four evaluated datasets. The closest competitor, BoundedME (also an adaptive sampling method), still scales as O(√d) and is actually slower than the naive algorithm on Netflix (0.36×) and MovieLens (0.41×), while BanditMIPS achieves 53× and 14× speedups respectively. Greedy-MIPS was omitted from the sample complexity figure due to significantly worse performance than all other algorithms, and HNSW-MIPS was strictly dominated by NAPG-MIPS. In the precision-speedup tradeoff (Figure 4), BanditMIPS and BanditMIPS-α consistently achieve better precision at higher speedup values than all baselines on both Netflix and MovieLens. BanditMIPS-α (non-uniform sampling) outperforms the default BanditMIPS in 3 out of 4 datasets for sample complexity, though it underperforms slightly on Netflix where high-value query coordinates had low dot products with atoms.

## Relation to Prior Work

BanditMIPS reduces the MIPS problem to a best-arm identification problem in the multi-armed bandit framework, combining UCB and successive elimination techniques (Lai & Robbins, 1985; Even-Dar et al., 2006) with coordinate-wise adaptive sampling. The most directly related prior work is BoundedME (Liu et al., 2019), which also uses adaptive sampling for MIPS but only adapts to the relative ranking of inner products rather than their actual values, resulting in O(√d) scaling. The paper departs from the broader MIPS literature (LSH, proximity graphs, product quantization) by avoiding heavy preprocessing and achieving O(1) scaling with dimension d under sub-Gaussianity assumptions, whereas all prior methods scale at least linearly or as O(√d) in d.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(1) scaling with d is a fundamental complexity improvement for a problem that is ubiquitous in recommendation systems, information retrieval, and LLM inference (e.g., next-token prediction in OPT-6.7B). The demonstrated 30–53× wall-clock speedups over the closest baseline (BoundedME) make high-dimensional MIPS practically tractable in settings where it was previously prohibitive. Key limitations include the requirement that the minimum gap Δ does not depend on d (which fails for symmetric datasets) and the sub-Gaussianity assumption on coordinate-wise products; when these are violated, the algorithm can degrade to the naive O(nd) approach. Open questions include whether the ε-suboptimal relaxation can be made more principled and how BanditMIPS-based Fourier analysis compares to heavily optimized FFT methods.
