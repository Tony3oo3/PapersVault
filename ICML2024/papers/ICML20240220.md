## Problem

While prior work on sortition has studied how to form representative and fair panels, it remains unaddressed whether such panels actually reach decisions that reflect the aggregate preference of the full population. This paper asks: can a small randomly selected panel make a decision close to the one the entire population would make, and how large must the panel be to guarantee this?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240220/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The selection algorithm receives only the locations of n agents in a (pseudo-)metric space and a target panel size k, and outputs a distribution over k-sized panels. Alternatives are also embedded in the same metric space but their locations are not visible to the algorithm. The panel's decision is the alternative minimizing its social cost (sum of distances to panel members), and performance is measured by the ratio of this social cost to the population-optimal alternative's social cost (distortion).

## Method

The paper introduces metric distortion as the lens for evaluating sortition panels, extending prior representation metrics to place both agents and alternatives in a shared metric space. For uniform selection, it proves an O(ε⁻² ln(m/ε)) panel size suffices for 1+ε ex-ante distortion via a two-case analysis: Serfling's concentration inequality handles alternatives with moderate social cost, while a new anti-concentration bound for the hypergeometric distribution handles high-cost alternatives, yielding a matching lower bound. For Fair Greedy Capture, the paper shows the algorithm's ball-capture structure implies constant ex-post distortion (at most 127) for k≥3, and that O(ε⁻³ ln(m/ε)) agents suffice for 1+ε ex-ante distortion by exploiting the algorithm's partition-then-sample structure to apply Hoeffding's inequality. The key 'dimensionality reduction' insight is that panel size depends only on the number of alternatives m and accuracy ε, not on population size n.

## Results

The paper establishes that uniform selection of a panel of size O(ε⁻² ln(m/ε)) achieves ex-ante distortion at most 1+ε for any instance with m alternatives (Theorem 4), with a matching lower bound for any selection algorithm (Theorem 8). Fair Greedy Capture achieves the same 1+ε ex-ante distortion with panel size O(ε⁻³ ln(m/ε)) (Theorem 11) while additionally guaranteeing constant ex-post distortion of at most 127 for any k ≥ 3 (Theorem 9), in contrast to uniform selection's worst-case ex-post distortion of Ω(n). Empirically on the Adult (n=32,561) and ESS Austria (n=2,498) datasets, both algorithms converge to distortion 1, but Fair Greedy Capture converges more rapidly and consistently avoids the high-distortion outliers that persist under uniform selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between Uniform Selection and Fair Greedy Capture (FGC), both of which are fair selection algorithms. On ex-post distortion, FGC decisively wins: it guarantees a constant bound of 127 for all realized panels (k ≥ 3), whereas uniform selection can produce panels with distortion as large as Ω(n) (e.g., selecting all k agents from one side of a two-cluster instance). On ex-ante distortion, both methods achieve 1+ε with logarithmic panel size in m, but uniform selection has a slightly tighter theoretical dependence (ε⁻² vs. ε⁻³). Empirically, however, FGC consistently outperforms uniform selection: in the experiments (Figures 2–3), FGC converges to distortion 1 more quickly across both the Adult and ESS Austria datasets, and its ex-post distortion distribution shows far fewer high-distortion outliers as k grows. The gap is most pronounced on the Adult dataset, where ~8.3% of the population is concentrated at a single point and uniform selection frequently fails to sample enough representatives from that group, while FGC's proportional capture mechanism ensures their inclusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sortition distortion analysis of Meir et al. (2021), which was restricted to binary issues, to general metric utilities with an arbitrary number of alternatives, and builds on the representation metric framework of Ebadian et al. (2022) and Ebadian & Micha (2024) by embedding both agents and alternatives in a shared metric space. The key methodological contribution is repurposing Fair Greedy Capture—an algorithm originally designed to satisfy the core (proportional representation) in the sense of Gillies (1953)—as a distortion-minimizing selection mechanism, exploiting its structural properties to apply Hoeffding's inequality. The proof techniques draw on Serfling's concentration inequality for sampling without replacement and introduce a new anti-concentration bound for the hypergeometric distribution to handle high-distortion alternatives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves the central open question of whether randomly selected citizens' assemblies can reach decisions reflecting the full population's preferences, showing that logarithmically many agents suffice for near-optimal distortion regardless of population size. The 'best of both worlds' guarantee for Fair Greedy Capture—near-optimal ex-ante distortion plus constant ex-post distortion (127)—addresses a practical criticism of sortition that extreme panels could produce socially disastrous outcomes. Open questions include closing the gap between the 127 ex-post bound and the 5 deterministic lower bound, proving that FGC always dominates uniform selection in ex-ante distortion (observed empirically but not yet shown theoretically), and extending the analysis to multi-alternative outputs or different social welfare criteria.
