## Problem

Inverse reinforcement learning methods suffer from low sample-efficiency and overfit to the specific transition dynamics under which demonstrations were collected, failing to generalize even to minor environment changes. Prior identifiability results show that a single fixed environment may be fundamentally insufficient to recover the true reward function. This paper asks whether actively designing a sequence of demo environments for the expert can simultaneously improve sample-efficiency and robustness of the learned reward.

## Input / Output

The learner receives a set of candidate environments (either explicit transition functions or black-box simulators), a prior over reward functions, and a budget m of expert demonstration rounds. At each round the learner selects one environment from the set, observes one or more expert trajectories in it, and updates its reward estimate. The final output is a reward function (posterior mean for ED-BIRL, or a shared state-only reward network for ED-AIRL) intended to be near-optimal across all environments in the candidate set, not just the ones observed. A key constraint is that the same state can appear under different transition dynamics across rounds, which is what enables disambiguation between reward candidates.

## Method

The core mechanism is a maximin Bayesian regret objective: at each round the learner selects the environment T that maximizes the minimum Bayesian regret over policies, i.e., the environment where the current reward estimate is expected to perform worst relative to optimal policies under reward candidates drawn from the posterior. For structured (state-decomposable) environment sets, this is solved via an extended value iteration that picks per-state transition matrices; for arbitrary simulator-based sets, it is approximated by sampling environments and running full policy rollouts. Two IRL backends are plugged in: ED-BIRL extends Bayesian IRL by factorizing the likelihood over (trajectory, environment) pairs and sampling the posterior via MCMC, while ED-AIRL extends Adversarial IRL to multiple environments by maintaining per-environment shaping terms with a shared reward approximator, and approximates the Bayesian regret using a uniform distribution over per-environment point estimates. The key insight is that by forcing the expert to act in environments where the current reward estimate is most uncertain or worst-performing, the learner compels demonstrations that visit states under different dynamics, resolving ambiguities that a fixed environment cannot.

## Results

On continuous control tasks, ED-AIRL achieves the highest average normalised returns on both demo and test sets in most environments: 68±04 (demo) and 71±02 (test) on the Continuous Maze, 80±19 (demo) and 69±12 (test) on Swimmer, and 63±07 (demo) and 52±04 (test) on Hopper. ED-AIRL consistently outperforms single-environment AIRL by a large margin (e.g., 68 vs 33 on Continuous Maze demo, 80 vs 40 on Swimmer demo). On the discrete maze task, ED-BIRL recovers the locations of all three goal squares and all lava squares after just 3 rounds of adaptive environment selection, whereas fixed-environment and domain-randomisation baselines fail to identify more than one goal square.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ED-AIRL outperforms single-environment AIRL (Fu et al., 2018) by a large and consistent margin across all four continuous control tasks, e.g., 68 vs 33 on Continuous Maze demo and 80 vs 40 on Swimmer demo, confirming that learning from a single environment is insufficient for robustness regardless of trajectory count. Against RIME (Chae et al., 2022), ED-AIRL wins decisively on Continuous Maze (68 vs -105 demo), HalfCheetah (40 vs -21 demo), and Swimmer (80 vs -05 demo), while performing comparably on Hopper (63 vs 61 demo). GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016) and BC fail to transfer to perturbed dynamics on most tasks (e.g., GAIL scores 17 on Continuous Maze test vs ED-AIRL's 71), with the notable exception of Swimmer where both imitation methods are extremely robust to gravity and body-feature changes. The domain-randomisation variant DR-AIRL, which selects environments uniformly at random, is consistently outperformed by ED-AIRL's active selection (e.g., 52 vs 68 on Continuous Maze demo, 45 vs 80 on Swimmer demo), isolating the benefit of the maximin Bayesian regret environment design objective from the mere effect of multi-environment training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Bayesian IRL (Ramachandran & Amir, 2007) and Adversarial IRL (Fu et al., 2018) to demonstrations collected under multiple environment dynamics, where the likelihood naturally factorises over (trajectory, transition) pairs. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a maximin Bayesian regret objective for actively selecting which environments the expert demonstrates in, which distinguishes it from prior active IRL work that queries specific states (Lopes et al., 2009; Lindner et al., 2021) and from passive multi-environment IRL or identifiability analyses (Cao et al., 2021; Rolland et al., 2022). The objective is related to robust RL (Roy et al., 2017) but inverts the quantifier: rather than finding a minimax policy, it finds the maximin environment that most stresses the current reward estimate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a fundamental identifiability limitation of IRL: from a single fixed environment, the true reward function may be unrecoverable regardless of data volume, as only the nearest goal or most relevant states are visited. The maximin Bayesian regret criterion provides a principled, performance-based (not merely uncertainty-based) selection rule with a clean guarantee (Lemma 4.1) that zero regret implies the posterior mean is optimal in all environments. Key open questions include the quadratic computational cost of ED-AIRL (growing with rounds × environments), the instability of AIRL inference noted by the authors, and whether the framework scales to high-dimensional continuous environments beyond MuJoCo-scale tasks. The finding that even AIRL—explicitly designed for robustness—fails to generalise from a single environment to slightly perturbed dynamics underscores that robustness in IRL requires multi-environment data, not just a robust training objective.
