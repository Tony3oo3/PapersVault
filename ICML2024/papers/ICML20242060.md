## Problem

State-of-the-art VLMs are known to fail on compositional reasoning benchmarks, yet prior work has only characterized this weakness through black-box accuracy probing or empirical training fixes (scene graphs, hard negatives), leaving the internal cause unexplained. This paper asks three diagnostic questions: do text encoders encode compositional knowledge, do image encoders do so, and do the two encoders share mutually matching compositional representations?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242060/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes image-text pairs where one modality is perturbed with minimal compositional changes (e.g., swapping object words in captions, or altering spatial relations between same-identity objects in image pairs), along with pixel-level segmentation masks for described objects obtained via SAM. It produces Harsanyi-dividend-based sensitivity metrics (Q for text, D for image) decomposed by compositional aspect (object, relation, attribute, and their pairwise interactions), Pearson correlation coefficients linking these interaction effects to overall reward differences, and cross-modal matching metrics that test whether one encoder's compositional patterns align with the other's.

## Method

The core mechanism treats VLM inference as a cooperative game where players are visual concepts (image regions) and text words, with the game value being the image-text matching score (e.g., cosine similarity of embeddings). The Harsanyi dividend decomposes the output into interaction effects of different subsets of players, enabling fine-grained measurement of how much each compositional pattern contributes to the output. Sensitivity metrics Q and D are defined as normalized differences in Harsanyi dividends between original and perturbed inputs, isolating the causal effect of specific compositional changes such as swapping relation words or altering object spatial arrangements. The diagnosis proceeds in three stages: text encoder sensitivities (finding Q_R&O >> Q_R, Q_O, indicating text encoders correctly identify interaction changes as dominant), image encoder sensitivities (finding D_O1&O2 < D_O1, D_O2, indicating image encoders are less accurate about relation changes), and cross-modal matching (finding that image encoders associate textual relation patterns with individual object regions rather than object interactions, and vice versa).

## Results

On the Visual Genome Relation dataset (ARO benchmark), text encoders of all five VLMs show Q_{R&O} (interaction between relation and object words) as the dominant sensitivity metric, e.g., CLIP achieves Q_{R&O}=1.3e-2 vs. Q_O=4.5e-3 and Q_R=9.8e-6, while FLAVA reaches Q_{R&O}=4.3e-1 vs. Q_O=7.4e-2 and Q_R=3.0e-2. On the EQBEN dataset, image encoders show the opposite pattern: D_{O1&O2} (relation between objects) is smaller than D_{O1} and D_{O2} (individual objects) for CLIP (2.0e-2 vs. 1.7e-2/3.1e-2), NEGCLIP (3.4e-2 vs. 3.7e-2/7.5e-2), and BLIP (4.4e-1 vs. 5.6e-1/7.4e-1), indicating weaker compositional sensitivity. For mutual knowledge, Q_{T:R&O→I:O2} consistently exceeds Q_{T:R&O→I:O1&O2} across all VLMs (e.g., CLIP: 6.9e-1 vs. 2.4e-1), confirming that image encoders do not align with text encoders' compositional understanding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This diagnostic paper evaluates seven VLMs (CLIP, NEGCLIP, BLIP, XVLM, FLAVA, FLIP, LaCLIP) rather than comparing a proposed method against baselines. All VLMs exhibit a consistent pattern: text encoders correctly identify interaction effects (Q_{R&O} or Q_{A&O}) as the dominant compositional signal, with FLAVA showing the strongest text-encoder compositionality (Q_{R&O}=4.3e-1) and CLIP the weakest (Q_{R&O}=1.3e-2). For image encoders, CLIP and NEGCLIP show the clearest deficit in compositional sensitivity (D_{O1&O2} < D_{O1}, D_{O2}), while FLAVA is the only model where D_{O1&O2} (1.2e0) slightly exceeds D_{O1} (6.9e-1) and D_{O2} (1.0e0). On mutual knowledge, no VLM achieves the expected alignment: Q_{T:R&O→I:O2} is the largest metric for CLIP, NEGCLIP, BLIP, and FLAVA, meaning image encoders associate textual relation-object interactions with individual object regions rather than their joint interaction. The two most recent models (FLIP, LaCLIP) in Appendix E confirm the same three insights, reinforcing that the compositional weakness is a systematic property of current VLM architectures rather than an artifact of specific models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Harsanyi dividend from cooperative game theory (Harsanyi, 1963) and its connection to the Shapley value as a fine-grained diagnostic tool for VLMs, extending the line of game-theoretic DNN interpretability work by Grabisch & Roubens, Zhang et al., and Ren et al. It departs from prior compositional-reasoning evaluations (Winoground, ARO, SUGARCREPE, VL-CheckList) that relied on black-box accuracy probing, and from empirical fixes (scene-graph supervision, hard-negative mining) that improved performance without explaining the underlying cause. The key conceptual departure is a disentangled, white-box analysis that separately diagnoses the text encoder, the image encoder, and their mutual alignment, rather than treating the VLM as an opaque whole.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides actionable diagnostic guidance: the compositional weakness of VLMs is primarily attributable to image encoders and to a misalignment between what text encoders and image encoders encode as 'composition,' suggesting that future improvements should target visual encoders and cross-modal alignment rather than text encoders. Limitations include the focus on binary object-relation changes (not more complex visual compositions), reliance on SAM for segmentation masks (introducing potential annotation noise), and the fact that the proposed metrics are diagnostic rather than prescriptive—the paper acknowledges that turning these insights into training objectives (e.g., Harsanyi-dividend-based auxiliary losses) remains an open and data-hungry challenge. The framework is nonetheless reusable for diagnosing future VLM architectures.
