## Problem

Existing LLM pruning methods—2:4 structured sparsity and channel-wise pruning—fail to deliver substantial end-to-end inference speedup because they operate at the weight or neuron level, where speedup depends on hardware support, batch size, and the proportion of non-matrix-multiplication operations within each block. Early exit strategies, which skip blocks dynamically, cannot reduce memory, degrade in multi-batch settings, and require extensive training. The paper identifies that LLMs exhibit strong block-level redundancy due to residual connections, yet no prior method exploits this by statically removing entire transformer blocks in a training-free manner.

## Input / Output

SLEB takes a pre-trained LLM (OPT or LLaMA-2 family, 6.7B–70B parameters), 128 calibration sequences sampled from WikiText-2 or C4, and a target sparsity ratio (10% or 20% of total blocks). The output is a streamlined LLM with entire transformer blocks physically removed, producing a fully dense model that requires no sparse-matrix hardware support. Unlike weight-level pruning, the sparsity granularity is at the block level, so the number of removed blocks is simply ceil(total_blocks × sparsity), and the resulting model's depth is reduced proportionally.

## Method

SLEB exploits the observation that neighboring transformer blocks produce highly similar outputs (high cosine similarity) because each block only incrementally modifies the residual path. To identify which blocks are truly redundant, the method uses an iterative removal procedure with Metric³: at each step, it evaluates the perplexity of the *current* (already partially pruned) model with each remaining block removed, and selects the block whose removal causes the smallest perplexity increase. This iterative, state-aware evaluation prevents the removal of consecutive blocks and accounts for the shifting significance of blocks as the model changes. The entire process is training-free, requiring only a calibration pass through the model, and the resulting speedup is directly proportional to the fraction of blocks removed since the model's depth is physically shortened.

## Results

On C4 perplexity, SLEB at 20% sparsity achieves 7.31 on LLaMA-2-70B (vs. 5.71 dense), substantially outperforming SliceGPT at 30% (25.79) and 2:4 pruning methods at 50% (~8.1). In terms of end-to-end throughput on LLaMA-2-70B (2× A100), SLEB at 20% achieves 381 tokens/s (1.27× speedup), surpassing SliceGPT at 30% (343 tokens/s, 1.15×) and all 2:4 pruning methods (293 tokens/s, 0.98×, i.e., actually slower than dense). On zero-shot tasks, SLEB at 10% sparsity preserves or even slightly exceeds dense accuracy (e.g., OPT-13B: 62.07% vs. 61.79% dense), while at 20% it still outperforms all baselines (e.g., LLaMA-2-70B: 70.81% vs. 66.11% for SliceGPT-30%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SLEB dominates all five baselines on both perplexity and throughput simultaneously. Against 2:4 structured pruning (SparseGPT, Wanda, DSnoT at 50% sparsity), SLEB at 20% sparsity achieves 1.27× throughput vs. 0.98× (i.e., the 2:4 methods are actually *slower* than dense), while maintaining far lower perplexity (e.g., LLaMA-2-70B: 7.31 vs. 8.10–8.16). Wanda and DSnoT catastrophically fail on OPT-66B with perplexity exceeding 8000, whereas SLEB keeps it at 12.54. Against channel-wise pruning (SliceGPT), SLEB at 20% matches or exceeds the speedup of SliceGPT at 30% (1.27× vs. 1.15×) while achieving dramatically better perplexity (e.g., LLaMA-2-70B: 7.31 vs. 25.79; LLaMA-2-7B: 12.32 vs. 41.69). LLM-Pruner is only evaluated on LLaMA-2-7B/13B and shows intermediate perplexity (12.25/10.43) with only 1.05× speedup, still worse than SLEB on both metrics. On zero-shot accuracy, SLEB at 10% sparsity matches or slightly exceeds the dense model across all seven models tested, and at 20% it still outperforms every baseline by 3–10 percentage points on average.

## Relation to Prior Work

SLEB departs from the dominant paradigm in LLM pruning—removing individual weights (2:4 structured sparsity via SparseGPT, Wanda, DSnoT) or entire channels (LLM-Pruner, SliceGPT)—by elevating the transformer block itself as the atomic unit of elimination. Conceptually, it repurposes the redundancy-verification idea (measuring impact on token prediction via calibration data) that underpins weight-level pruning methods, but applies it at the block level using an iterative, perplexity-based metric. It also explicitly contrasts with early-exit and skip-decoding approaches, arguing that their dynamic, per-token decision-making is incompatible with multi-batch serving and memory reduction, whereas SLEB's static, training-free removal aligns with traditional post-training compression workflows.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SLEB offers a practical, training-free path to ~1.27× end-to-end inference speedup at 20% sparsity with minimal quality degradation, and the speedup scales linearly with blocks removed—unlike 2:4 pruning, whose gains depend on batch size and dedicated hardware. Its compatibility with 4-bit quantization (AWQ) suggests a composable compression pipeline. However, the main experiments cap at 20% sparsity; the appendix reveals substantial quality loss beyond 30%, and the iterative O(n·N) forward-pass cost for block selection, while manageable, grows with model depth. Open questions include whether block-level redundancy persists in non-standard architectures (e.g., mixture-of-experts, sliding-window attention) and whether the method transfers to multimodal or encoder-decoder LLMs.
