## Problem

Current neural audio synthesis methods rely on large latent spaces with billions of uninterpretable parameters, making generated sounds difficult to inspect, tweak, or interpolate. These methods also prioritize acoustic realism over creative abstraction, overlooking the expressive potential of evoking concepts rather than mimicking real-world recordings. The paper asks whether a small, fully interpretable synthesizer can be driven by text prompts to produce distinctive, artistic sounds that remain identifiable to listeners.

## Input / Output

The method takes a free-form text prompt (e.g., 'spray', 'train horn') and produces a 2-second monophonic audio waveform at 48 kHz sample rate, along with the 78 synthesizer parameters (each in [0,1]) that generated it. The higher sample rate compared to typical 16 kHz neural audio systems admits more high-frequency content. Because the output includes explicit parameter values, users can directly inspect, modify, or linearly interpolate between parameter vectors to explore intermediate sounds.

## Method

CTAG combines three components: a 78-parameter modular synthesizer (SYNTHAX's Voice architecture with two LFOs, six ADSR envelopes, a sine VCO, a square-saw VCO, a noise generator, and VCAs), the LAION-CLAP model as a semantic alignment objective, and gradient-free evolutionary optimization (Learned Evolution Strategies via Evosax). The synthesizer's parameters are initialized uniformly in [0,1], and the optimizer iteratively adjusts a population of parameter vectors to maximize the cosine similarity between the CLAP audio embedding of the synthesized sound and the CLAP text embedding of the prompt. Gradient-based optimization was abandoned because the differentiable synthesizer's gradients proved highly unstable; instead, LES with 300 iterations and population size 100 was selected via ablation. The entire pipeline is training-free—no model weights are learned, and the only learned component is the frozen LAION-CLAP encoder used as the scoring function.

## Results

In the user study (10 participants, 600 observations), CTAG sounds achieve 56.0% identification accuracy, competitive with AudioGen (59.5%) and substantially above AudioLDM (34.0%), while being rated significantly more artistically interpretive (3.54/5) than both AudioGen (2.32/5) and AudioLDM (2.90/5). On AudioSet-50 classification, CTAG achieves 26.2% top-1 accuracy, outperforming AudioLDM (17.4%) but trailing AudioGen (51.6%); on ESC-50, CTAG (16.4%) trails both AudioGen (54.0%) and AudioLDM (23.0%), reflecting its deliberate distribution shift toward abstract sounds. CTAG also produces sounds with substantially higher high-frequency content (HFC: 427 vs 152 for AudioLDM, 53 for AudioGen) and greater spectral complexity and flux, consistent with its 48 kHz synthesis rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AudioGen, CTAG achieves comparable user-study identification accuracy (56.0% vs 59.5%, odds ratio 0.85, p=1) but is rated significantly more artistically interpretive (3.54 vs 2.32, contrast=1.22, p<.0001). On standard classification, CTAG trails AudioGen substantially on both AudioSet-50 (26.2% vs 51.6% top-1) and ESC-50 (16.4% vs 54.0% top-1), as AudioGen's neural synthesis produces more acoustically literal renditions. Against AudioLDM, CTAG wins on user-study accuracy (56.0% vs 34.0%, odds ratio 2.72, p<.0001), AudioSet-50 classification (26.2% vs 17.4%), and artistic interpretation (3.54 vs 2.90, p<.0001), though it loses on ESC-50 classification (16.4% vs 23.0%). CTAG's confidence ratings (2.99) are significantly lower than AudioGen's (3.48) but not significantly different from AudioLDM's (2.95), underscoring that its abstract sounds remain identifiable yet create more perceptual uncertainty. Overall, CTAG positions itself as complementary to AudioGen: it sacrifices some acoustic realism and classifier recognizability in exchange for interpretability, controllability (78 parameters), and a distinctly artistic character.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant neural audio synthesis paradigm (AudioGen, AudioLDM, Make-An-Audio) by replacing large latent-space generative models with a fixed 78-parameter modular synthesizer (SYNTHAX, adapted from torchsynth) whose parameters are optimized via gradient-free evolutionary search (Learned Evolution Strategies from Evosax) guided by LAION-CLAP text-audio similarity. Conceptually, it draws from the DDSP tradition of physically-motivated synthesis and from abstract visual synthesis work (ES-CLIP, CLIPasso) that uses CLIP-guided optimization over interpretable parameter spaces, but applies this to audio for the first time with a synthesizer architecture. It also generalizes inverse synthesis (Yee-King et al., Hagiwara et al.) by replacing the requirement for a target audio clip with a free-text prompt, bridging the gap between semantic concepts and concrete parameter settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a tiny, fully interpretable synthesizer can produce sounds that are nearly as identifiable as billion-parameter neural models (56% vs 59.5% in the user study) while being perceived as significantly more artistic (3.54 vs 2.32 on a 5-point scale), challenging the assumption that acoustic realism is the only viable objective for text-to-audio generation. Key limitations include restriction to brief, non-mixture sounds, per-prompt optimization from random initialization (no learned text-to-parameter mapping), and the trivially high CLAP scores that make the objective a poor standalone quality metric. Open questions include whether semantic caching or predictive parameter initialization can reduce generation time, whether the approach extends to longer or polyphonic sounds, and whether the abstraction property generalizes beyond the Voice architecture to richer synthesizer topologies.
