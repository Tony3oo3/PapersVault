## Problem

Hallucinations in language models are commonly attributed to knowledge gaps, but it remains unclear whether LMs can produce incorrect claims that they themselves would recognize as wrong when presented in isolation. This paper asks whether early mistakes by an LM can cascade into further errors that the model would otherwise avoid, a phenomenon the authors term 'hallucination snowballing.'
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242465/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a yes/no question from one of three constructed datasets (primality testing, senator search, graph connectivity), each designed so that an incorrect answer forces the model to generate a specific, verifiable claim (e.g., a factorization, a senator's alma mater, a flight route). The two-stage evaluation first checks whether the model's initial Yes/No answer is correct, then extracts the incorrect supporting claim and presents it in a separate session to probe whether the model recognizes it as false. The key output is the snowballed hallucination rate: the fraction of incorrect explanations in which the model's own claim is refutable by the model itself.

## Method

The paper constructs three QA datasets (500 questions each) with the property that the correct answer is fixed for all instances and that an incorrect answer necessitates a specific, easily verifiable false claim. The core experimental procedure is a two-stage probe: (1) ask the model the original question and check accuracy, then (2) in a new session, ask the model to verify the specific claim it generated in its incorrect explanation. The authors hypothesize that snowballing arises from two properties: initial committal (instruction-tuned LMs output Yes/No as the first token ~95% of the time) and inherent sequentiality (transformers cannot solve multi-step reasoning in one timestep). They test whether higher temperature, beam search, or zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting ('Let's think step by step') alleviate the effect, finding that CoT helps on simple single-question tasks but fails on composite questions requiring multiple concurrent reasoning steps.

## Results

Across three QA datasets (Primality Testing, Senator Search, Graph Connectivity), all tested models exhibit high error rates: GPT-3.5 (60.13%), GPT-4 (83.40%), and LLaMA2-70B-chat (66.67%) average error. Critically, when models hallucinate, the incorrect claims in their explanations are recognized as wrong by the same model in a separate session: GPT-3.5 detects 67.37%, GPT-4 detects 87.03%, and LLaMA2-70B-chat detects 93.67% of snowballed hallucinations. This snowballing persists under higher temperature sampling (t=0.6, 0.9) and beam search, and is only partially alleviated by zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting on simpler tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it benchmarks three leading LMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, LLaMA2-70B-chat) on the introduced datasets. LLaMA2-70B-chat shows the highest snowballed hallucination rate (93.67% average) despite having a lower overall error rate (66.67%) than GPT-4 (83.40%), indicating that its errors are almost always accompanied by self-contradictory justifications. GPT-4 has the highest error rate but also a high snowballing rate (87.03%), while GPT-3.5 has the lowest error rate (60.13%) and the lowest snowballing rate (67.37%), though still substantial. On the Graph Connectivity task specifically, all three models exceed 94% snowballed hallucination rate, suggesting this task is particularly prone to the phenomenon. The cross-model comparison reveals that snowballing is a universal failure mode rather than an artifact of a single architecture or training pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant framing that LM hallucinations stem from knowledge gaps (Zheng et al., 2023) or external misleading context (Kim et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022), by showing that hallucinations can be *self-induced*: an initial incorrect commitment forces the model to generate supporting claims it would separately recognize as false. It connects to the exposure bias literature (Wang & Sennrich, 2020; Arora et al., 2022) on error propagation in autoregressive generation, but goes further by demonstrating that the propagated errors are not merely undetected — the model explicitly identifies them as wrong in a separate session. The theoretical motivation draws on Merrill & Sabharwal (2023) regarding the inherent sequentiality of certain reasoning tasks that transformers cannot resolve in a single timestep.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that GPT-4 recognizes 87% of its own snowballed hallucinations as incorrect when presented in isolation fundamentally challenges the 'knowledge gap' narrative and suggests that hallucination mitigation cannot rely solely on retrieval or better knowledge grounding. The paper also shows that the intuitive fix — zero-shot chain-of-thought prompting — helps on simple tasks but breaks down on composite questions, leaving the practical problem of preventing snowballing in real-world multi-step reasoning largely unsolved. Open questions include whether training on backtracking patterns (e.g., 'Sorry, that was incorrect') can teach models to revise prior commitments, and whether the phenomenon generalizes beyond the structured QA tasks tested here to open-ended generation.
