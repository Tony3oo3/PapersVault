## Problem

Existing deep IV regression methods (Deep IV, DeepGMM, DFIV) naively plug first-stage neural network estimators into the second stage, where regularization and overfitting biases propagate into the causal function estimate, causing slow convergence. No prior work has applied the double/debiased machine learning (DML) framework—Neyman orthogonal scores combined with cross-fitting—to IV regression with neural networks, and no convergence rate or suboptimality guarantees exist for DL-based IV regression.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline dataset of N tuples (context C, instrument Z, action A, outcome R), where C can be low-dimensional tabular features or high-dimensional 784-dim MNIST images, and A can be continuous or discrete. It outputs a parameterized counterfactual prediction function h_θ(C,A) ≈ E[R|do(A),C] and, by taking argmax over A, a deterministic decision policy π̂(C) = argmax_a h_θ(C,a). The policy is evaluated under a test context distribution P_test that may differ from the training distribution.

## Method

DML-IV derives a novel Neyman orthogonal score ψ(D; h, (s,g)) = (s(c,z) − g(h,c,z))² that replaces the raw outcome R in the standard two-stage IV loss with the conditional expectation s(c,z) = E[R|C,Z], making the score invariant to small perturbations in the nuisance parameters. The algorithm uses K-fold cross-fitting: for each fold k, nuisance estimators ŝ_k (a supervised regression of R on (C,Z)) and ĝ_k (a conditional density estimate F̂(A|C,Z) followed by integration of h over A) are trained on the complement fold, then the second-stage DNN h_θ is updated by minimizing the empirical Neyman orthogonal score on the held-out fold. This cross-fitting eliminates overfitting bias while Neyman orthogonality eliminates regularization bias, yielding an N^{-1/2} convergence rate for the parameter estimator and an O(N^{-1/2}) suboptimality bound for the induced policy—matching the guarantees of unconfounded offline bandit algorithms. A computationally efficient variant (CE-DML-IV) trains the nuisance parameters once on the full dataset, sacrificing the formal convergence guarantee but retaining partial debiasing from the orthogonal score.

## Results

On the aeroplane ticket demand dataset (both low-dimensional and high-dimensional MNIST variants), DML-IV achieves the lowest MSE of the counterfactual prediction function ĥ across all dataset sizes (200–10,000 samples), with the advantage growing substantially in the 784-dimensional MNIST setting where regularisation bias is most severe. In the offline IV bandit evaluation, DML-IV consistently learns the near-optimal pricing policy with only 2,000 samples in the high-dimensional setting, while CE-DML-IV (without cross-fitting) matches DML-IV on low-dimensional data but degrades in high dimensions. On the semi-synthetic real-world IHDP (747 samples) and PM-CMR (2,132 samples) datasets, DML-IV achieves comparable or lower MSE than all baselines while attaining the highest average reward of the induced policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DML-IV outperforms all four SOTA IV regression baselines—Deep IV (Hartford et al., 2017), DeepGMM (Bennett et al., 2019), KIV (Singh et al., 2019), and DFIV (Xu et al., 2020)—on both MSE of ĥ and average policy reward across every dataset and dimensionality tested. The gap is most pronounced in the high-dimensional MNIST setting, where DML-IV's Neyman orthogonal score and K-fold cross-fitting effectively mitigate the regularisation and overfitting biases that plague the naive two-stage plug-in approach used by Deep IV and DFIV. CE-DML-IV, the computationally efficient variant without cross-fitting, matches DML-IV on low-dimensional data but underperforms in high dimensions, confirming the importance of cross-fitting for debiasing. In Appendix E, standard offline bandit algorithms (PEVI/LinLCB, NeuraLCB, KernLCB, NeuralLinLCB, NeuralLinGreedy) that do not exploit IVs fail to outperform a random policy on the confounded aeroplane ticket data, whereas DML-IV achieves average reward well above 1. On the real-world IHDP and PM-CMR benchmarks, DML-IV and CE-DML-IV both achieve the highest average reward while maintaining MSE comparable to or lower than Deep IV, DeepGMM, KIV, and DFIV.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: nonparametric IV regression (Newey & Powell, 2003; Deep IV, 2017) and Double/Debiased Machine Learning (Chernozhukov et al., 2018). Prior DNN-based IV methods (Deep IV, DeepGMM, KIV, DFIV) use a naive two-stage plug-in approach where the first-stage estimator is directly inserted into the second-stage loss, inheriting regularisation and overfitting bias. Previous DML applications to IV problems were restricted to linear or partially linear models (Belloni et al., 2012; Chernozhukov et al., 2015, 2018). DML-IV is the first to derive a Neyman orthogonal score specifically for the nonparametric IV regression problem and to combine it with K-fold cross-fitting and DNNs in both stages, bridging the gap between these two literatures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first N^{-1/2} convergence rate and O(N^{-1/2}) suboptimality guarantees for DL-based IV regression, matching the bounds available for unconfounded offline bandits — a meaningful theoretical milestone for a setting where prior methods only demonstrated convergence in the limit. Practically, this gives decision-makers using offline data with hidden confounders a principled, debiased tool with quantifiable error bounds. Key limitations include the requirement of a valid instrument (exclusion restriction + relevance), the restriction to single-step (bandit) decisions rather than sequential MDPs, and the finite-dimensional parameterisation of h needed for the theory. Extending the framework to sequential decision-making and reinforcement learning with hidden confounders is identified as the most natural next step.
