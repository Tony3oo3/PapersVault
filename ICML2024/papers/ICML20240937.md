## Problem

Traditional working memory models in RNNs assume information is stored in fixed register-like locations updated by interference or decay, but neurobiological evidence suggests traveling waves may be the actual storage mechanism. This paper asks whether traveling waves emerge naturally in trained RNNs, whether they can be formally characterized, and whether this wave-based storage provides concrete computational benefits such as alleviating the vanishing gradient problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240937/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The RNN receives s binary d-dimensional vectors during an input phase (initializing a history-dependent dynamical system), then autonomously generates the next binary state vector at each subsequent timestep during the output phase. The hidden state is analyzed via a learned linear basis transformation that decomposes it into s wave subspaces, each of dimension d, revealing how past inputs propagate spatially through the substrate. Tasks include repeat copy (T1) and various linear compose-copy functions (T2–T4) with d=8, s=8, and hidden sizes of 64 or 128.

## Method

The paper formulates a Traveling Wave Working Memory (TWM) model where each state dimension is an independent 1-D wave propagating through a discrete neural lattice, with the start boundary computing the evolution function f and the end boundary left open to prevent reflection. Two boundary condition cases are derived: a Linear Boundary Condition (LBC) yielding a simple iterative matrix Φ that matches first-order RNN dynamics, and a Self-Attention Boundary Condition (SBC) whose dynamics are exactly the autoregressive loop of a single transformer attention head. Empirically, Elman RNNs trained via BPTT on linear HDS are linearized around fixed points, and their Jacobian eigenspectra are shown to converge to the theoretical Φ spectrum. A power-iteration algorithm recovers the latent wave basis from trained weights, revealing that hidden states encode recent history as spatially propagating wave activity. The paper further proves that because inputs are stored explicitly in spatial wave positions rather than accumulated temporally, the gradient upper bound no longer decays exponentially with context length, eliminating the vanishing gradient problem.

## Results

RNNs trained on four linear history-dependent dynamical systems (HDS) tasks consistently converge to the theoretical Traveling Wave Memory (TWM) circuit, with MAE in the complex argument of the eigenspectrum as low as 0.0005–0.0031 and test accuracy of 0.98–1.00 across 20 seeds (hidden size 128, L2=0.001). The gradient propagation analysis shows that the diminishing gradient problem is alleviated during training: the gradient decay rate drops sharply to near 0 once the maximum eigenvalue magnitude crosses 1, at which point traveling waves are established in the hidden state. The basis transformation algorithm (Algorithm 1) successfully reveals hidden traveling wave structure in trained RNNs, making the learned parameters human-interpretable as wave propagation with boundary-condition computation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing architectures or methods in a traditional sense; instead, its central empirical claim is that standard Elman RNNs trained via BPTT on linear HDS tasks spontaneously converge to the theoretically predicted TWM circuit. The 'baseline' is the theoretical eigenspectrum of the TWM matrix Φ (Equation 6), and the 'method' is the empirically learned W_hh of the RNN. Across all four tasks and both RNN configurations (with/without bias), the learned eigenspectrum matches the theoretical prediction with MAE ≤ 0.0031 at hidden size 128, confirming that traveling waves emerge naturally without architectural modifications. The paper contrasts this with the WaveRNN approach (Keller et al., 2023), which explicitly injects wave dynamics into RNN parameters, arguing that the present work provides theoretical justification for why such waves emerge even in unmodified networks. The non-linear SBC case is connected to transformer self-attention, providing an alternate justification for transformers' advantages over RNNs (reduced training time, explicit history encoding), but no direct quantitative comparison to transformer baselines is performed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the WaveRNN line of work (Keller et al., 2023), which empirically showed that explicitly adding traveling wave dynamics to RNN parameters improves learnability, by providing a full theoretical framework (TWM) that derives the wave dynamics from first principles and shows they emerge naturally in trained RNNs without architectural modifications. It departs from the dominant working memory paradigm in RNNs, which assumes information is stored in fixed register-like locations updated by interference or decay (Sreenivasan et al., 2014; Compte et al., 2000), by proposing that variables are instead bound to propagating waves whose boundary conditions encode the update rule. The linearization analysis builds on Sussillo & Barak (2013) for Jacobian-based spectral analysis, while the self-attention boundary condition (SBC) section reframes the autoregressive transformer loop as a non-linear traveling wave, connecting to state space models like H3 and Mamba.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unifying theoretical lens that simultaneously explains RNN dynamics, justifies transformer architecture advantages (explicit wave encoding vs. implicit learning of waves), and offers a mechanism for alleviating the vanishing gradient problem through spatial rather than temporal encoding of history. However, the empirical validation is restricted to small-scale binary linear HDS tasks (d=8, s=8) with simple Elman networks, and the authors acknowledge that generalizing the basis transformation to non-linear tasks remains an open problem. The practical impact is primarily conceptual: it reframes how we interpret trained RNN weights and hidden states, but does not yet yield a new architecture or training technique that practitioners can directly adopt. Future work on non-linear generalization and scaling to larger, more complex tasks will determine whether the TWM framework has broader applicability.
