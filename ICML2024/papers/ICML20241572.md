## Problem

Solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation in high dimensions is intractable for grid-based solvers and existing deep learning methods (PINNs, t-VMC) due to the curse of dimensionality: computational complexity grows exponentially with the number of particles. No known deep learning approach adapts to the latent low-dimensional structure of the quantum density, and prior methods either require O(d³) operations (matrix inverses, Laplacians) or rely on fixed collocation grids that waste computation in low-density regions.

## Input / Output

The method takes an initial wave function ψ₀ (provided as samples from |ψ₀|²), a smooth potential V(x,t), and physical constants (mass tensor m, reduced Planck constant ℏ) as input. Rather than explicitly estimating the complex-valued wave function ψ(x,t), it directly produces samples from the quantum density |ψ(x,t)|² at any time t ∈ [0,T] via Euler-Maruyama integration of a learned SDE, along with the learned osmotic velocity u(x,t) and current velocity v(x,t). The sampling is self-adaptive: trajectories concentrate in high-density regions, so computation scales with the effective (latent) dimension rather than the ambient dimension d.

## Method

The core reformulation replaces the Schrödinger equation with a family of Nelsonian diffusion processes whose time-marginals equal |ψ(x,t)|², parameterized by a free parameter ν that interpolates between stochastic mechanics (ν=1) and deterministic pilot-wave theory (ν=0). Two neural networks learn the drift components u_θ (osmotic velocity) and v_θ (current velocity), trained by minimizing residuals of novel Navier-Stokes-like PDEs (Eqs. 7a–7b) evaluated on self-generated trajectories. The key technical insight is replacing the Laplacian operator Δu (requiring O(d³) Hessian computation) with the gradient of divergence ∇⟨∇,u⟩ (requiring only O(d²) operations), which is valid because u and v are guaranteed to be full gradients. Training is self-supervised: at each iteration, new trajectories are generated via Euler-Maruyama integration using the current network, the PDE residual loss is computed on those trajectories, and gradients are back-propagated—creating a closed loop that progressively focuses computation on high-density regions. A theoretical guarantee (Theorem 4.2) shows the loss upper-bounds the L₂ distance between the learned process and the true Nelsonian process, ensuring strong convergence as the loss vanishes.

## Results

On the 1D harmonic oscillator (S₀≡0), DSM achieves E_m(X_i)=0.079 and E(v)=1.7×10⁻⁴, outperforming PINN (E_m=0.877, E(v)=24.153) by over 10× in mean error and ~140,000× in velocity error. On the 2D interacting boson system, DSM attains E_m=0.092, E_v=0.055, E(v)=7.6×10⁻⁵, and E(u)=6.6×10⁻⁵, beating both PINN (E_m=0.258, E(v)=20.903) and t-VMC (E_m=0.103, E(v)=2.9×10⁻³). For the non-interacting 9D harmonic oscillator, DSM maintains E_m≈0.096 and E(v)≈7×10⁻⁵ with linear scaling in training time (4h 46m vs. 46m for d=1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DSM decisively outperforms PINN across all tested settings. On the 1D harmonic oscillator with zero initial phase, DSM reduces the mean-position error from 0.877 (PINN) to 0.079 and the velocity error from 24.15 to 1.7×10⁻⁴; with non-zero initial phase (S₀=−5x), the gap widens further (E_m: 0.268 vs. 2.626; E(v): 1.4×10⁻⁵ vs. 234.9). On the 2D interacting boson system, DSM beats PINN by ~2.8× in E_m and ~275,000× in E(v). Against t-VMC on the same 2D interacting system, DSM achieves lower errors on all four metrics (E_m: 0.092 vs. 0.103; E_v: 0.055 vs. 0.109; E(v): 7.6×10⁻⁵ vs. 2.9×10⁻³; E(u): 6.6×10⁻⁵ vs. 3.5×10⁻⁴), with the velocity error advantage being ~38×. The paper notes that t-VMC's Hermite-polynomial ansatz inherently encodes ground-truth structure and does not scale well beyond d=3, while DSM's adaptive sampling concentrates computation in high-density regions without requiring problem-specific ansatz design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Nelson's (1966) and Guerra's (1995) stochastic mechanics framework, which establishes an equivalence between the Schrödinger equation and a Markovian diffusion process with osmotic and current velocities. The key methodological departure is replacing the Laplacian operator (Δu, Δv) in the velocity evolution equations with the gradient of the divergence operator (∇⟨∇,u⟩, ∇⟨∇,v⟩), reducing per-iteration complexity from O(d³) to O(d²). The sampling strategy draws conceptual inspiration from generative diffusion models and score-based approaches, while the training loop is positioned as an adaptive alternative to PINNs (which use uniform collocation grids) and t-VMC (which requires problem-specific ansatz functions). The paper also distinguishes itself from BSDE-based methods (Nusken & Richter, 2021) by addressing the hyperbolic rather than elliptic HJB setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a theoretically grounded, scalable framework for time-dependent quantum mechanics simulation that avoids the exponential grid-based complexity and the O(d³) bottleneck of t-VMC/FermiNet, with a provable strong convergence guarantee (Theorem 4.2) linking loss to L₂ process error. The empirical demonstration of near-linear scaling up to d=9 (non-interacting) and d=5 (interacting) bosons, plus a 100-particle proof-of-concept, suggests practical viability for many-body systems where grid solvers are intractable. Key limitations include restriction to spinless bosons on flat manifolds with smooth potentials, and the method's reliance on the wave function being expressible as full gradients. Open questions include extension to fermionic systems, spin degrees of freedom, singular potentials, and whether the favorable scaling persists for genuinely high-energy or strongly correlated regimes where Lipschitz constants grow with d.
