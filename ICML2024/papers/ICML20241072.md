## Problem

When a conditional independence structure is imposed on the decoder of an autoencoder to capture invariance to nuisance variables, the corresponding encoder structure and its penalties in VAEs are typically added ad hoc based on heuristics, with no principled derivation. This paper shows that the Wasserstein autoencoder (WAE) framework automatically determines the encoder factorization and penalties from the imposed decoder structure, eliminating the need for arbitrary penalty design.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241072/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images (28×28 MNIST, 32×32 SVHN, 128×128 VGGFace2/Yale B) or mel-spectrograms for speech, along with partially observed nuisance variables such as class labels, binary facial attributes, or continuous lighting directions. When a nuisance variable is missing for a sample, a learned predictor h(X) imputes it, and the model is trained end-to-end on a mixture of labeled and unlabeled batches. Outputs include reconstructed images, an invariant latent code Z that is independent of nuisance variables, conditionally generated images (given a label or attribute), style-transferred images, and attribute-manipulated images via extrapolation in the embedded attribute space.

## Method

The core insight is that for a chain-model decoder g(Y, Z) with conditional independence, the WAE objective (Theorem 3.1) yields the encoder constraint f(X,Y) ≐ Z and f(X,Y) ⊥ Y, which are then relaxed into a reconstruction loss plus a prior-matching penalty (Jensen-Shannon divergence via a GAN discriminator) and an independence penalty (dHSIC). The decoder is made injective using Leaky ReLU activations (Remark 2.2), enabling the deterministic encoder reduction from Theorem 2.1. For missing nuisance variables, Corollary 3.2 introduces a predictor h(X) that feeds into the encoder, and the semi-supervised loss (Eq. 12) merges supervised and unsupervised terms by using the true label when observed and h(X) otherwise. This framework unifies semi-supervised classification, conditional generation, style transfer, attribute manipulation, and invariant representation learning under a single principled penalty structure derived from the Wasserstein distance, rather than the heuristic ELBO-based penalties common in VAE literature.

## Results

On MNIST with 100 labeled samples, StrWAE achieves 98.69% classification accuracy and 96.06% conditional generation accuracy (using true labels), outperforming all baselines. On SVHN with 1000 labeled samples, StrWAE reaches 85.59% classification and 98.80% conditional generation accuracy, again leading all competitors. On the Extended Yale B invariant representation task, StrWAE achieves 98% identity prediction accuracy (vs. 72% for HCV) and a FID of 55.7 (vs. 232 for HCV), confirming superior disentanglement of identity from lighting. On the Mini Speech Recognition dataset, StrWAE achieves 60.88% classification accuracy versus 24.03% for AAE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StrWAE outperforms the semi-supervised VAE (M1+M2, Kingma et al. 2014) by 2.02 points on MNIST classification (98.69 vs. 96.67) and by 21.61 points on SVHN (85.59 vs. 63.98), while also enabling conditional generation which M1+M2 does not support. Against the Adversarial Autoencoder (AAE, Makhzani et al. 2016), StrWAE wins on classification by 0.59 points (MNIST) and 2.62 points (SVHN), but the gap is dramatic on conditional generation: 96.06% vs. 36.69% on MNIST and 98.80% vs. 82.99% on SVHN, attributed to StrWAE's use of full data across all loss terms plus a natural independence penalty. StrWAE surpasses CCVAE (Joy et al. 2021) on MNIST classification (98.69 vs. 93.18) and conditional generation (96.06 vs. 16.03), but CCVAE edges out StrWAE on SVHN classification (90.02 vs. 85.59), which the authors attribute to CCVAE's multi-label design better handling background digits in SVHN. Compared to Smooth-ELBO (Feng et al. 2021), StrWAE leads on both classification and generation, with the largest gap on MNIST conditional generation (96.06 vs. 27.27). On the Extended Yale B invariant representation task, StrWAE achieves 98% identity accuracy versus 72% for HCV (Lopez et al. 2018) and a FID of 55.7 versus 232, while also producing sharper reconstructions (3.94e-4 vs. 8.27e-5). For attribute manipulation on VGGFace2, StrWAE qualitatively outperforms Fader Networks (Lample et al. 2018), which distort images when extrapolating attribute scores and fail to keep nuisance attributes (mouth state, gender) invariant when manipulating the beard attribute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Wasserstein Autoencoders (Tolstikhin et al., 2018) and repositions them as a principled alternative to the large family of VAE-based invariant representation methods (semi-supervised VAE, HCV, CCVAE, Fader Networks, AAE). The key conceptual departure is that in VAEs, the encoder's factorization and its associated penalties (HSIC, KL, adversarial) are imposed ad hoc to mirror a chosen decoder structure, whereas in the WAE framework the encoder's functional constraints are *derived* from the decoder's conditional independence structure via the requirement that the objective equals a genuine Wasserstein distance. The paper also corrects a proof error in Patrini et al. (2020) regarding the reduction of probabilistic to deterministic encoders in WAEs, establishing the result under an injectivity condition on the decoder.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The StrWAE framework provides a single, principled recipe—choose a decoder with the desired conditional independence structure, and the encoder constraints and penalties follow automatically—replacing the patchwork of heuristics that has accumulated in the VAE literature for invariant representation learning. This unification could reduce the trial-and-error in designing new autoencoder architectures for disentanglement and fairness tasks. Open questions include which distributional divergences (beyond GAN-based JSD) work best in structured settings, whether the injectivity requirement on the decoder is truly necessary in practice, and how the framework extends to more complex graphical models beyond the chain structure. The penalty coefficients still require manual tuning, and the ablation studies, while informative, are limited to two datasets.
