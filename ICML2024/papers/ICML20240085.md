## Problem

Standard fitted Q-iteration (FQI) with squared loss achieves worst-case sample complexity bounds in batch RL that are independent of the optimal policy's cost, making them unnecessarily pessimistic in goal-oriented tasks where the optimal policy reliably achieves the goal with near-zero cost. The paper asks whether a simple change to the regression loss can yield a 'small-cost bound' where the number of samples needed to learn a near-optimal policy scales with the optimal achievable cost rather than a worst-case constant.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a batch dataset of i.i.d. (state, action, cost, next-state) tuples drawn from an exploratory distribution μ, a finite function class F ⊆ [0,1]^(S×A) (all functions must be bounded to [0,1] for log-loss to be well-defined), and a number of iterations k. Costs must be normalized so that discounted cumulative costs along any trajectory lie in [0,1]. The output is a stationary greedy policy π_{f_k} mapping states to actions, obtained by taking argmin over actions of the final value function estimate f_k.

## Method

FQI-LOG replaces the squared loss in standard FQI with binary log-loss (cross-entropy): at each iteration j, the algorithm minimizes the empirical log-loss between f(S_i, A_i) and the target C_i + γ f_{j-1}^∧(S'_i). The key insight is that log-loss penalizes predictions far from observed targets more heavily when those targets are near 0 or 1, causing the learner to prioritize fitting near-boundary values and disregard high-variance observations in the middle of the range—exactly the regime where optimal costs are near zero. The theoretical analysis establishes three pillars: (i) the Bellman optimality operator is a γ-pseudo-contraction with respect to Hellinger distance, (ii) the suboptimality gap of a greedy policy decomposes into a product of √v̄* and the pointwise triangular deviation of f from q*, and (iii) the log-loss estimator concentrates in integrated binary Hellinger loss. Together these yield the first small-cost bound for a computationally efficient batch RL algorithm: suboptimality scales as Õ(√(v̄*CN/n) + CN/((1−γ)²n) + γ^k), where C is the concentrability coefficient and N = log(|F|/δ).

## Results

On Mountain Car (episodic, 800 steps, sparse cost), FQI-LOG accumulates substantially lower cost than FQI-SQ across all dataset sizes (1,000–30,000 trajectories) and regardless of the number of successful trajectories in the batch (1, 5, or 30); notably, FQI-LOG learns a near-optimal (near-zero-cost) policy with only a single successful trajectory. On Inverted Pendulum, FQI-LOG achieves a higher fraction of 3000-step balance successes than FQI-SQ at every dataset size. On Atari, DQN-LOG outperforms DQN-SQ on Seaquest and matches C51, while on Asterix DQN-LOG performs similarly to DQN-SQ and both trail C51.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FQI-SQ (Ernst et al., 2005), FQI-LOG clearly wins on both Mountain Car and Inverted Pendulum: it reaches near-zero cost (Mountain Car) or higher balance success rates (Inverted Pendulum) with fewer samples at every dataset size tested, and the gap is largest when few successful trajectories are available. Against DQN-SQ (Mnih et al., 2015), DQN-LOG wins on Seaquest (higher undiscounted return across training epochs) and ties on Asterix (similar learning curves). Against C51 (Bellemare et al., 2017), DQN-LOG matches performance on Seaquest but falls short on Asterix, where C51's full distributional machinery (51-bin categorization, distributional Bellman operator) provides an advantage. Overall, the paper concludes that switching to log-loss is sufficient to match C51 in some settings but not universally, and that the benefit is most pronounced in goal-oriented tasks with near-zero optimal cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fitted Q-iteration (FQI) framework by replacing the standard squared loss with log-loss, drawing directly on Foster & Krishnamurthy's (2021) insight that log-loss yields small-cost bounds in contextual bandits. It departs from Wang et al. (2023), who achieved small-cost bounds in batch RL only through the more complex distributional Bellman operator and pessimism, by showing that log-loss alone suffices under a standard completeness assumption. The key technical novelty is proving that the Bellman optimality operator is a contraction with respect to Hellinger distance, a new analytical tool that bridges the bandit-level log-loss concentration results to the multi-stage RL setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first computationally efficient batch RL algorithm with a small-cost bound, establishing that the choice of loss function genuinely affects sample efficiency in offline RL — a finding that challenges the default use of squared loss. However, the theoretical guarantee requires the completeness assumption (stronger than realizability alone), and the Atari experiments were inconclusive: DQN-LOG matched but did not consistently outperform the more complex C51 distributional algorithm. Open questions include whether small-cost bounds can be obtained with only realizability, whether the result extends to online RL with linear function approximation, and whether combining log-loss with C51's auxiliary losses could yield a strictly superior method.
