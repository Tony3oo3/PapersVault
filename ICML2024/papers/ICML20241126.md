## Problem

Existing variational inference for the partially observable generalized linear model (POGLM) suffers from two limitations: (1) the discrete Poisson hidden spike count blocks the use of the lower-variance pathwise gradient estimator, forcing reliance on the noisier score function gradient estimator; and (2) the standard forward-self variational sampling scheme is both computationally slow (sequential) and inexpressive (omits the hidden-to-visible influence on future visible spikes).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241126/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes binned spike count trains from V visible neurons (50 ms bins for RGC, 20 ms bins for PVC-5) along with a user-specified number H of hidden neurons, and outputs the full N×N connectivity weight matrix (all four blocks: V←V, V←H, H←V, H←H), the N-dimensional bias vector, and inferred hidden spike trains. During training, hidden spike counts are relaxed to continuous soft variables (soft one-hot vectors on a simplex or continuous non-negative values), but evaluation reverts to the original Poisson log-likelihood for fair comparison across all method variants.

## Method

The paper proposes a differentiable POGLM that replaces the discrete Poisson hidden spike count with a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation (or alternative continuous distributions: exponential, Rayleigh, half-normal), enabling the reparameterization trick and thus the pathwise gradient estimator for updating variational parameters φ. For the variational model, the authors introduce a forward-backward message passing scheme where the hidden neuron firing rate at time t depends on both past visible spikes (forward, via A_{H←V}) and future visible spikes (backward, via A_{V←H}), thereby capturing the hidden-to-visible influence W_{V←H} present in the generative model. Unlike the forward-self scheme, this design is parallelizable (no hidden-to-hidden sequential dependency) and more expressive. The paper systematically compares seven inference methods (Poisson, Categorical, GS-score, GS-pathwise, Exp, Ray, HN) crossed with three sampling schemes (Forward, Forward-self, Forward-backward) on a synthetic dataset and two real-world datasets, showing that differentiable inference with forward-backward sampling (especially Exp × FB) achieves the best test log-likelihood, parameter recovery, and interpretability of learned connectivity.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset (5 neurons, 3 visible, 2 hidden), the best method combination (Exponential × Forward-Backward) achieves the highest test log-likelihood and lowest weight/bias error among all 21 method combinations, with the pathwise gradient estimator yielding smoother learning curves and smaller variance than the score function estimator. On the RGC dataset (27 retinal ganglion cells), Exp × FB significantly outperforms all other combinations across H∈{1,2,3} hidden neurons, exceeding the fully observed GLM baseline. On the PVC-5 dataset (3 visible neurons), Exp × FB with fewer than 3 hidden neurons is optimal, and non-differentiable methods degrade below the fully observed GLM baseline when H>3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the original POGLM (Poisson × Forward-self) from Jimenez Rezende & Gerstner (2014), which uses the score function gradient estimator and sequential hidden-to-hidden sampling. The proposed differentiable POGLM with pathwise gradient estimator × Forward-Backward sampling consistently outperforms this baseline on all three datasets in test log-likelihood and parameter recovery error. The second baseline is the fully observed GLM (Pillow et al., 2008), used as a reference in Fig. 5 and Fig. 7; the proposed method exceeds this baseline on both RGC and PVC-5 datasets, while the original Poisson × FS method does not reliably surpass it. The paper also demonstrates that the Forward-Backward sampling scheme specifically outperforms Forward and Forward-self schemes when paired with a differentiable inference method, and that continuous distributions (Exp, Ray, HN) with pathwise gradients outperform the Gumbel-Softmax relaxation, which in turn outperforms the discrete Categorical and Poisson formulations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the partially observable generalized linear model (POGLM) framework established by Pillow & Latham (2007), Jimenez Rezende & Gerstner (2014), and Linderman et al. (2017), which models neural connectivity with hidden neurons using variational inference. It departs from prior work in two specific ways: (1) it replaces the discrete Poisson latent variable with a continuous relaxation (Gumbel-Softmax or general single-parameter distributions like Exponential, Rayleigh, Half-normal) to enable the lower-variance pathwise gradient estimator instead of the score function gradient estimator used in all existing POGLM work; and (2) it introduces a forward-backward message passing variational sampling scheme that incorporates future visible spikes into hidden spike sampling, addressing the inexpressivity and sequential inefficiency of the forward-self scheme from Jimenez Rezende & Gerstner (2014) and Kajino (2021). The Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization trick (Jang et al., 2016; Maddison et al., 2016) is repurposed here for a neuroscience-specific latent variable model rather than its more common use in VAEs or discrete decision-making.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical and well-motivated recipe for solving POGLM more effectively, with the pathwise gradient estimator yielding visibly smoother training curves and the forward-backward scheme producing more interpretable connectivity patterns (e.g., a hidden neuron acting as a negative feedback regulator distinguishing ON vs. OFF retinal ganglion cells). A notable open question the authors flag is whether the superior performance of continuous distributions (Exp, Ray, HN) over the Gumbel-Softmax approximation of Poisson indicates that the true posterior over hidden spikes is genuinely non-Poisson, which would have implications for how we conceptualize latent neural activity. The relaxation from discrete spike counts to continuous variables, while effective, loses the direct interpretability of Z as spike counts, and the paper acknowledges this as a limitation. The work is scoped to binned spike count data and does not address the point-process (continuous-time) formulation, which the authors discuss in the appendix as a related but distinct setting.
