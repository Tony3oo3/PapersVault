## Problem

Existing offline IRL and RLHF algorithms rely on maximum likelihood estimation under specific decision or preference models (e.g., softmax policy, Bradley-Terry-Luce), which introduces computational challenges from bi-level optimization and robustness failures when the assumed model mismatches real human behavior. Prior work on recovering a feasible reward set either requires online exploration, a generative model of transitions, or produces non-convex solution sets that limit downstream flexibility. This paper addresses the gap by providing the first offline algorithm that estimates a convex (polyhedral) feasible reward set with provable sample-efficiency guarantees, without assuming any particular preference model.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N i.i.d. finite-horizon (H-step) expert trajectories from a tabular discounted MDP, pairwise trajectory comparison queries with binary or continuous human preference labels, and known transition probabilities P with discount factor γ. The output is not a single reward function but a polyhedral set of (u, v) pairs—where u = r ⊙ d̂_e encodes the reward scaled by the empirical occupancy measure—defined by linear inequalities. A specific reward r(s,a) ∈ [-1,1] is then selected from this set, and the set's size is controlled by relaxation parameters ε_g (duality gap slack for IRL) and ε_r (margin slack for RLHF), trading off optimality tightness against feasibility.

## Method

The core mechanism scales the MDP's dual LP via marginal importance sampling (w_d = d/d_e), replacing the true occupancy measure d_e and transition kernel with empirical estimates from the offline dataset, and relaxing the flow-conservation equality to an L1-norm inequality to absorb estimation error. The feasible reward set is then characterized as the set of (u, v) pairs satisfying the relaxed primal-dual optimality conditions of this empirical LP, with an additional duality-gap slack ε_g that controls how near-optimal the expert policy must be. For RLHF, pairwise comparison constraints are added as simple linear inequalities (r^T(ψ^{n,2} − ψ^{n,1}) ≤ ε_r when trajectory 1 is preferred), requiring no preference model. The IRL and RLHF sets are combined by intersection, and degeneracy (e.g., r = 0) is mitigated by maximizing the empirical reward gap between the expert and a suboptimal policy over the resulting polyhedron. The linear structure of all constraints preserves convexity, enabling function approximation via linear parameterization and polynomial-time solvability.

## Results

The paper evaluates performance using the L1 error between the optimal occupancy measure under the true reward and that under the estimated reward, ||d*(r_true) − d*(r̂)||₁, on a 10-state, 2-action MDP with γ=0.95 and H=20 trajectories. In offline IRL, the LP-based methods (LP-IRL-1, LP-IRL-2, LP-IRL-D, LP-IRL-C) consistently achieve lower L1 error than the bi-level MLE baseline (MLE-IRL), with tighter duality-gap relaxation (ε_g = 0.001/√N) outperforming looser relaxation (ε_g = 0.01/√N). In offline RLHF, the LP method's error decreases rapidly with sample size while the pessimistic MLE (BTL model) decreases more slowly. The paper additionally provides an analytical bandit counterexample (Proposition F.1) where both MLE and pessimistic MLE provably recover the wrong policy with high probability, while the LP method recovers the optimal policy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two MLE-based baselines. Against MLE-IRL (Zeng et al., 2023), a bi-level optimization algorithm for offline IRL, all LP-IRL variants achieve lower L1 occupancy-measure error across all sample sizes, demonstrating superior sample efficiency in handling ambiguity in dynamics and expert policy. The tighter relaxation LP-IRL-2 (ε_g = 0.001/√N) outperforms the looser LP-IRL-1 (ε_g = 0.01/√N), consistent with the optimality-feasibility trade-off. Against MLE-HF (Zhu et al., 2023), a pessimistic MLE algorithm under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model, the LP-HF method shows faster error reduction as samples increase, and the paper proves via a constructed bandit example that MLE-HF and its pessimistic variant both select the suboptimal action a₂ while LP correctly identifies a₃ as optimal. The integration of IRL and RLHF data (LP-IRL-D, LP-IRL-C) further improves over IRL alone, with continuous feedback (LP-IRL-C) outperforming discrete feedback (LP-IRL-D) by enabling stricter reward-gap constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the foundational LP characterization of feasible reward sets from Ng & Russell (2000) to the offline setting by leveraging the marginal importance sampling (MIS) framework from offline RL (Nachum et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2021) and recent convex LP-based offline RL methods (Ozdaglar et al., 2023). It departs from the dominant MLE-based bi-level optimization paradigm (Zeng et al., 2022, 2023) and pessimistic non-convex penalty approaches (Zhao et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023) by formulating reward learning as a convex polyhedral estimation problem via primal-dual optimality conditions. A key conceptual departure is the unification of IRL and RLHF within a single LP: human preference constraints are incorporated as additional linear inequalities, a flexibility unavailable in the MLE framework where the two data types require fundamentally different likelihood objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LP framework provides a model-agnostic, convex, and computationally tractable approach to offline reward learning with provable sample complexity, addressing a recognized gap in theoretical guarantees for IRL/RLHF. Its robustness to preference model misspecification (demonstrated via the bandit counterexample in Appendix F) is practically significant for RLHF applications where the BTL assumption is often violated. However, the current formulation is restricted to tabular MDPs with small state-action spaces, and the function approximation extension for high-dimensional or continuous settings is explicitly deferred. Open questions include whether the convex structure survives non-convex parameterizations, how the framework scales to K-wise comparisons, and whether the estimated reward sets transfer across environments.
