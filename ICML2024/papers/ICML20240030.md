## Problem

Learning Gaussian process (GP) mixture models for functional data clustering has been limited to EM-type parameter estimation approaches that require inverting large covariance matrices, yielding cubic computational complexity in the number of data points and suffering from local-optimal convergence. No prior method performs cluster analysis directly on GP mixtures without first solving the full parameter estimation problem, making GP mixture models impractical for large-scale functional data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw functional data as n sample paths y_i(t_i) evaluated at potentially irregular time points (with optional additive noise), a family of V projection functions (chosen from fPC eigenfunctions, random linear combinations, B-splines, Fourier basis, discrete wavelets, or Ornstein-Uhlenbeck random functions), and the number of clusters K. It outputs hard cluster labels {z_i} for each sample function, with the number of clusters optionally auto-determined via BIC/AIC on the first fPC score. The projection functions need not form a basis; they can be randomly generated, and the algorithm is robust to the choice of projection family.

## Method

The key insight is that projecting any functional data onto a 1D function β yields a univariate Gaussian mixture model (GMM), since the projection coefficient is a linear combination of independent Gaussian fPC scores. The algorithm performs V such projections, fits a univariate GMM to each set of projection coefficients (using method of moments for initialization followed by EM), and treats each resulting GMM as a base clustering in an ensemble. Base clusterings are aggregated via a weighted co-occurrence affinity matrix, where each base clustering's weight is inversely proportional to its total misclassification probability (computed from the overlap between component Gaussians). Theoretical guarantees include: (1) identifiability conditions for GP mixtures via L² projection discrepancy, (2) a sub-logarithmic bound on the number of random projections needed to achieve ε-separation in 1D, and (3) polynomial-time and polynomial-sample learnability of univariate GMM parameters via method of moments, reducing the overall problem from cubic to near-linear complexity.

## Results

On 10 real time-series datasets, GPmix achieves the highest AMI score on 8 out of 10 datasets, with standout results of 0.94 on DiatomSizeReduction, 0.84 on CBF, and 0.77 on FaceFour. Across 12 synthetic scenarios, GPmix ranks in the top three consistently and is the top performer in 6 of 12 scenarios (e.g., AMI 0.97 on scenarios E and H). Computationally, GPmix clusters all real datasets in under 1 second (e.g., 551 ms on Symbols with 1020 curves × 398 points), while competing methods like FC require 290K–3.4M ms on the same data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPmix substantially outperforms FEM (Jacques & Preda, 2014) on most real datasets: on CBF, GPmix achieves AMI 0.84 versus FEM's 0.37, and on FaceFour, 0.77 versus 0.47. Similarly, GPmix dominates HDD (Bouveyron et al., 2015) on CBF (0.84 vs. 0.47 AMI) and FaceFour (0.77 vs. 0.40). The two exceptions where GPmix does not lead are Meat, where FEM achieves AMI 0.93 (vs. GPmix 0.70), and Symbols, where FC (0.85) and HDD (0.77) slightly edge out GPmix (0.75). Across the 12 synthetic scenarios, GPmix is top or near-top in all cases except scenario J (identical mean functions, making the mixture unidentifiable) and scenario K, where HDD and FEM achieve near-perfect scores. In terms of runtime, GPmix is 100–1000× faster than FC and CLU on larger datasets (e.g., 551 ms vs. 3.4M ms on Symbols), while remaining competitive with the faster baselines FEM and HDD.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the dominant EM-type and Bayesian nonparametric approaches to GP mixture learning (James & Sugar 2003; Shi & Wang 2008; Huang et al. 2014; Rasmussen & Ghahramani 2001; Ross & Dy 2013), which all perform parameter estimation before inference and incur cubic complexity in the number of data points. Instead, it inverts the pipeline: cluster analysis is performed first via an ensemble of univariate GMMs fitted to random projections of the functional data, after which each GP component can be learned independently. The theoretical machinery draws on the polynomial learnability of univariate GMMs (Moitra & Valiant, 2010), the Cramér-Wold theorem for justifying random projections (Cuesta-Albertos et al., 2007), separation bounds for projected Gaussians (Kushnir et al., 2019), and identifiability conditions for finite mixtures (Atienza et al., 2006; Chiou & Li, 2007).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method reduces GP mixture clustering from cubic to near-linear complexity (clustering 1020 curves × 398 points in ~550 ms vs. millions of milliseconds for competing methods), making GP mixture models practical for large functional datasets. Theoretical guarantees on identifiability, polynomial-time parameter recovery, and sub-logarithmic projection requirements provide a rigorous foundation absent from prior EM-based approaches. Open questions include: the sensitivity to the choice of projection family in practice (requiring grid search over wavelet/spline/Fourier/OU families), the behavior when the number of clusters K is misspecified, and whether the ensemble aggregation step (weighted co-occurrence matrix) can be replaced by more principled consensus methods. The robustness to non-Gaussian data (scenarios A–F) suggests broader applicability beyond strict GP mixtures, but this is not formally characterized.
