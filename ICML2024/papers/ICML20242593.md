## Problem

While LLMs appear to pass Theory of Mind benchmarks, it remains unclear whether they develop genuine internal representations of other agents' beliefs or merely replicate surface patterns from training data. Specifically, no prior work has demonstrated that a model can linearly distinguish between its own (oracle) belief and another agent's belief from intermediate activations, nor has it shown that such representations causally drive social reasoning performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242593/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a third-person narrative story concatenated with a belief (or action) statement, with no explicit question or directive, and the attention head activations at the final token position are extracted as features. Probing outputs are binary labels (belief true/false) for a single agent or quaternary one-hot labels for the joint belief status of both protagonist and oracle. For intervention experiments, the input is a standard reading-comprehension prompt (story + question + two options), and the output is the model's generated answer, which shifts when activations are steered along identified belief directions.

## Method

The paper trains linear probes (logistic regression for binary, multinomial logistic regression for quaternary) on individual attention head activations across all layers of Mistral-7B and DeepSeek-7B, revealing that a subset of middle-layer heads linearly encode both the oracle's and protagonist's belief statuses, with oracle representations being far more prevalent. The key intervention direction (+TpFo) is derived from the multinomial probe weight vector that maximizes the probability of protagonist-belief-True while simultaneously distinguishing it from oracle-belief-True. At inference time, the top-K heads (selected by probe accuracy) are steered along this direction by α× their activation standard deviation at each autoregressive step, following the inference-time intervention framework of Li et al. (2023b). This steering dramatically improves False Belief accuracy (e.g., Mistral-7B Forward Belief FB from 0.33 to 0.66) while the directions generalize across Forward Action, Backward Belief, and even the structurally different ToMi benchmark, with gradient analysis confirming the directions attend to key causal tokens (desires, causal events, percept status).

## Results

On the BigToM benchmark, Mistral-7B baseline shows a severe True Belief / False Belief gap (e.g., 0.95 vs. 0.33 on Forward Belief), indicating the model defaults to the oracle's perspective. The (+TpFo) activation intervention dramatically narrows this gap, improving Forward Belief FB accuracy from 0.33 to 0.66 and the 'Both' metric from 0.31 to 0.58, while slightly reducing TB accuracy (0.95→0.85). Similar gains are observed on Forward Action (FB: 0.30→0.41) and Backward Belief (FB: 0.22→0.44, Both: 0.16→0.41). Random-direction and (+protagonist) interventions produce negligible changes, confirming the specificity of the identified belief directions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against GPT-4, which achieves near-ceiling ToM performance (0.99/0.98/0.97 on Forward Belief TB/FB/Both). Mistral-7B baseline trails GPT-4 by a wide margin on FB (0.33 vs. 0.98), but the (+TpFo) intervention closes roughly one-third of that gap (0.33→0.66), bringing it closer to GPT-3.5 (0.69) and Claude (0.82). On the harder Backward Belief task, the intervention lifts Mistral-7B from 0.22 to 0.44 on FB, still well below GPT-4's 0.53 but a 2× improvement. Random-direction and (+protagonist) interventions serve as negative controls and show no meaningful change, confirming that the gains are specific to the identified joint-belief direction. Cross-task transfer (Table 2) shows that directions identified on Forward Belief generalize to Forward Action and Backward Belief, further supporting their role as general-purpose belief representations rather than task-specific artifacts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: linear probing of neural network internals (Alain & Bengio, 2016; Belinkov, 2022) and inference-time activation steering (Li et al., 2023b). Prior LLM Theory-of-Mind research has been almost exclusively black-box—evaluating models via prompting on false-belief benchmarks (Kosinski, 2023; Shapira et al., 2023; Ullman, 2023)—while this work explicitly departs from that paradigm by examining intermediate attention-head activations as the locus of belief representations. The interpretability framing draws on the broader linear-representation-hypothesis literature (Goh et al., 2021; Elhage et al., 2022; Park et al., 2023), applying it to the specific question of whether and how LLMs encode distinct agents' belief states.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic, white-box evidence that LLMs maintain linearly decodable, causally relevant representations of both self and other beliefs, and that these representations are biased toward the oracle (model's own) perspective—a concrete explanation for the well-documented FB performance gap. The generalization of identified directions across causal-inference task types and even across narrative datasets (BigToM → ToMi) suggests a more universal belief-encoding mechanism than task-specific pattern matching. Key limitations include evaluation on only two 7B models, moderate effect sizes from intervention (e.g., FB accuracy improving from ~0.33 to ~0.66 rather than near-perfect), and the open question of whether these representations scale or reorganize in larger or MoE architectures. The work opens a path toward targeted, representation-level interventions for improving social reasoning without retraining.
