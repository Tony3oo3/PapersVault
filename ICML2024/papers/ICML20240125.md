## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of kernel regression generalization either rely on assumptions (Gaussian features, sub-Gaussian eigenfunctions, i.i.d. covariates) that provably fail for common kernels like RBF, NTK, and Laplace, or are restricted to incompatible settings (high-dimensional vs. fixed-dimensional, regularized vs. unregularized, noisy vs. noiseless). This paper provides a unified, rigorous upper bound on the excess risk of kernel regression that holds under mild heavy-tailed conditions satisfied by nearly all common kernels, for any regularization strength, noise level, input dimension, and sample size simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a Mercer kernel K with spectral decomposition K(x,x') = Σ λᵢψᵢ(x)ψᵢ(x'), n i.i.d. training points from a measure μ, labels yᵢ = f*(xᵢ) + εᵢ with i.i.d. zero-mean noise, and a regularization parameter γₙ (which may be zero for min-norm interpolation). It produces explicit upper bounds on the excess risk decomposed into bias and variance, relative perturbation bounds on the eigenvalues of the kernel matrix, and—via the NTK connection—time-dependent convergence rates for neural networks trained with gradient flow. The bounds depend on two mild quantities αₖ and βₖ that measure how closely the features concentrate around their expectations, rather than requiring Gaussianity or sub-Gaussianity.

## Method

The core mechanism is a decomposition of the kernel matrix K = K_{≤k} + K_{>k} for a freely chosen split k, combined with a novel 'self-regularization' phenomenon: the high-dimensional part K_{>k} behaves approximately as a scalar multiple of the identity, implicitly regularizing the solution even when γₙ = 0. Theorem 3.1 establishes relative perturbation bounds showing that eigenvalues μₖ(1/n·K) are lower-bounded by c₁·λₖ + αₖ·tr(Σ_{>k'})/n, where the second term captures this self-regularization and can dominate λₖ for small eigenvalues. Theorem 4.1 then converts these eigenvalue bounds into a general-purpose excess risk bound parameterized by a concentration coefficient ρ_{k,n} that measures the condition number of the regularized high-dimensional block. Applications show benign overfitting for dot product kernels in high dimensions (where repeated spherical harmonic eigenvalues yield large effective ranks), nearly tempered overfitting for polynomially decaying eigenvalues in fixed dimensions, and explicit convergence rates for regularized regression that translate to gradient flow training times for neural networks.

## Results

The paper provides sharp upper bounds for the excess risk of kernel ridge regression under minimal assumptions. In the high-dimensional regime (n,d→∞ with d^τ/n=Θ(1)), the min-norm interpolator achieves variance O(1/d^(τ−⌊τ⌋) + 1/d^(⌊τ⌋+1−τ)) and bias approaching O(‖θ*_{>N_d}‖_∞²), yielding benign overfitting when the target has frequencies ≤⌊τ⌋. In the fixed-dimensional regime with polynomially decaying eigenvalues λ_i=Θ(i^(−1−a)), the variance is bounded by Õ(n^(2a)) and the bias by Õ(1/n^min(2(r−a), 2−a)), establishing nearly tempered overfitting. For regularized regression with γ_n=Θ(n^(−1−b)), the variance decays as O(1/n^((a−b)/(1+a))) and the bias as O(1/n^((1+b)·min((2r+a)/(1+a), 2))), recovering classical rates without Gaussian feature assumptions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run empirical comparisons but positions its theoretical bounds against several prior lines of work. Compared to Tsigler & Bartlett (2023), the paper's Theorem 4.1 strictly generalizes their result: under their assumptions (0-mean, sub-Gaussian, finite-dimensional features, bounded condition number), the paper's bound precisely recovers theirs, but extends to infinite-dimensional, non-sub-Gaussian kernels like NTKs and RBF kernels. Against Xiao & Pennington (2022) and Mei et al. (2022), the paper provides explicit convergence rates (rather than just o_d(1) limits) and does not require the restrictive assumption that σ̂_ℓ=Θ_d(1) independent of d, which fails for NTKs. The paper also demonstrates via Figure 2 that the O(1) variance bound from Cui et al. (2021) and Mallinar et al. (2022) (which rely on Gaussian feature or non-rigorous assumptions) is empirically violated for common kernels like the GPK in fixed dimensions, while the paper's Õ(n^(2a)) bound correctly captures the slow divergence. Overall, the paper's contribution is unification: a single framework (Theorems 3.1 and 4.1) that subsumes the high-dimensional, fixed-dimensional, regularized, and unregularized settings previously treated in incompatible ways.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends several fragmented lines of work on kernel regression generalization. It builds on the effective-rank framework of Bartlett et al. (2020) and the ridge regression bounds of Tsigler & Bartlett (2023), but departs from them by replacing Gaussian/sub-Gaussian feature assumptions with a much milder heavy-tailed condition (via α_k, β_k parameters) that holds for common kernels like NTKs, RBF, and Laplace. It also improves upon the absolute eigenvalue perturbation bounds of Rosasco et al. (2010) by deriving relative perturbation bounds that capture the expected eigenvalue decay and provide tight lower bounds on the smallest kernel matrix eigenvalue. The paper explicitly positions itself against the split in the literature between high-dimensional and fixed-dimensional analyses, regularized and unregularized settings, and noisy and noiseless regimes, providing a single framework that handles all of these simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The self-regularization phenomenon—where the heavy tail in the kernel's eigendecomposition implicitly regularizes the solution even without explicit regularization—is a conceptually important insight that explains why overparameterized kernel methods (and by extension, wide neural networks in the NTK regime) generalize well. The paper provides the first rigorous upper bound for the min-norm interpolator in the fixed-dimensional setting, filling a notable gap. Open questions include proving matching lower bounds (especially when self-regularization is small), extending to more general input distributions beyond the uniform sphere, and understanding whether the slowly diverging variance observed empirically in Fig. 2 can be characterized more precisely. The results have direct implications for understanding neural network training dynamics via the gradient-flow connection.
