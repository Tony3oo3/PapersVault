## Problem

While LLM-based agent research has extensively explored cooperation and collaboration, competition dynamics remain largely unexplored. Existing work on firm competition (e.g., Han et al., 2023) is limited to narrow aspects like price trends, and no comprehensive framework or realistic competitive simulation environment exists for studying how LLM agents behave under competitive pressure. This paper addresses this gap by proposing a general framework and a concrete competitive environment to examine whether LLM-based agents exhibit behaviors consistent with established sociological and economic theories.

## Input / Output

Restaurant agents receive daily daybooks (income, expenses, customer flow), prior-day customer comments, and rival restaurant information (menu, customer flow, comments) as text prompts, and produce structured actions via a restaurant management API (hiring/firing chefs, modifying menus, updating advertisements). Customer agents receive restaurant names, scores, advertisements, menus, and comments, and output a dining choice with stated reasoning followed by a scored comment. Dish quality is computed by a fixed formula s = 0.5·(c/p) + 0.5·(f/5000) where c is cost, p is price, and f is chef salary, providing a deterministic quality signal that feeds back into customer experience. The simulation runs for 15 days with 2 restaurants and 50 customers (10 individuals, 40 in groups of 2–4).

## Method

The paper introduces CompeteAI, a four-component framework (environment selection, setup, simulation execution, analysis) for studying agent competition, instantiated as a virtual town where two GPT-4-powered restaurant agents compete for 50 GPT-4-powered customer agents. Restaurant agents operate through a structured API (basic_info, chef, menu, advertisement, comment, daybook) and follow a daily loop of receiving information, analyzing competitive context, planning strategy, executing actions, and storing summaries in memory. Customer agents are characterized by income, taste, health conditions, dietary restrictions, and personality, and are organized into individuals or relationship-based groups (family, colleague, couple, friend) that discuss and reach majority decisions before dining. The paper then conducts micro-level analysis (contextual perception, market strategies like differentiation/imitation/customer orientation, customer decision factors) and macro-level analysis (strategy dynamics with dynamic equilibrium in menu similarity, Matthew Effect, winner-take-all frequency, and product quality improvement) across 9 individual-customer and 6 group-customer simulation runs, validating findings against classic theories from Porter, Lieberman & Asaba, Zeithaml, and Rigney.

## Results

Across 9 individual-customer and 6 group-customer simulation runs (15 days each), all four classic market strategies—differentiation, imitation, customer orientation, and strategy dynamics—were observed in 100% of experiments. Product quality improvement (measured by average dish score increase) occurred in 86.67% of runs, with average score gains of 0.26 (R1) and 0.22 (R2) over 15 days. The Matthew Effect (one restaurant capturing >80% of customers after Day 5) manifested in 66.7% of individual-customer runs but only 16.7% of group-customer runs, demonstrating that customer grouping diminishes winner-take-all dynamics. Menu similarity between the two restaurants stabilized at approximately 36%, reflecting a dynamic equilibrium between differentiation and imitation pressures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an empirical simulation study rather than a method-comparison paper, so it does not benchmark against competing LLM-agent frameworks or baselines. Instead, it validates its simulation outputs against established sociological and economic theories (Porter's differentiation, Lieberman & Asaba's imitation, Zeithaml's customer orientation, Bandura's social learning, Rigney's Matthew Effect, and Leadley's winner-take-all). All six theoretical predictions were confirmed in the simulated environment, with the Matthew Effect showing the strongest sensitivity to the customer-grouping variable (66.7% → 16.7%). The closest prior work, Han et al. (2023), examined firm competition but focused solely on price trends; CompeteAI extends this to multi-dimensional strategy dynamics, customer decision-making, and product quality evolution, filling a gap the authors identify in the LLM-agent literature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM-based agent simulation line (Generative Agents, CAMEL, AutoGen) from its dominant focus on cooperation and collaboration to the underexplored domain of competition dynamics. It builds on the broader agent-based modeling tradition (rule-based, data-driven, ML-based) but departs from prior LLM-agent competition work (Han et al. 2023 on price trends, Chen et al. 2023 on auction planning) by constructing a multi-day, multi-agent competitive environment with rich feedback loops and diverse judge characteristics. The CompeteAI framework formalizes the design space (competitors, judges, constraints, service/feedback, refinement) in a way that prior LLM-agent papers had not systematically addressed for competitive settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a reusable framework and concrete testbed for studying competition dynamics in LLM agents, with findings that validate classic sociological and economic theories (Matthew Effect, differentiation, imitation, customer orientation) in a simulated setting. The observation that customer grouping diminishes winner-take-all dynamics offers a practical lever for designing fairer competitive systems. Key limitations include the small scale (2 restaurants, 50 customers), text-only modality, and dependence on a single GPT-4 version, leaving open whether findings generalize to larger, multi-modal, or multi-model settings. A deeper open question the authors raise is whether LLM agents can generate genuinely novel competitive strategies beyond what is encoded in their training corpus, which would be essential for using such simulations to discover new social-science theories.
