## Problem

Finding linear feature vectors in transformer models—directions in activation space corresponding to concepts the model uses in its computation—currently requires large amounts of labeled data, expensive forward passes, and storage of embedding datasets. This paper asks whether feature vectors can be extracted directly from model weights with almost no data, enabling mechanistic interpretability in low-data regimes and for models too large for traditional probing approaches.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input the full transformer weight matrices, a task-specified observable (a linear functional on the logit vector, such as the difference between two token logits), and at most one input point per observable for estimating gradients at nonlinearities. It produces feature vectors in activation space at each layer along a computational path, coupling coefficients quantifying how outputs on one task predict outputs on another, and feature vector norms for ranking component importance—all without storing or training on any dataset of embeddings.

## Method

Observable propagation (OBPROP) works by back-propagating the observable through the model's computational graph: for each attention head's OV circuit, the observable is multiplied by the transposed OV weight matrix; for nonlinear sublayers (MLPs, LayerNorms), the first-order Taylor gradient at a chosen point replaces the weight multiplication. The key insight is that this backward propagation yields, at each layer, a feature vector whose dot product with the input directly gives the model's output under the observable. Theoretically, Theorem 1 proves that LayerNorms introduce negligible angular deviation (bounded by 2·arccos(√(1−1/(d−1)))) for d≥8, justifying their omission for directional analysis. Theorem 2 introduces the coupling coefficient C(y₁,y₂) = (y₁·y₂)/‖y₁‖² as a data-free estimator of the proportionality constant between two tasks' outputs, with accuracy increasing as cosine similarity between feature vectors grows. The method is entirely data-free (requiring only model weights and optionally one forward pass for gradient estimation) and focuses on OV circuits, where computation on information occurs, rather than QK circuits responsible for routing.

## Results

On the subject pronoun prediction task, OBPROP achieves r²≈0.945 with zero training data, matching logistic regression trained on 60 prompts. On political party prediction, OBPROP achieves r²≈0.427, substantially outperforming logistic regression (r²≈0.295) even when the latter is trained on 3/4 of the dataset. On C vs. Python code classification, OBPROP reaches AUC≈0.9974, matching logistic regression (AUC≈0.9971) trained on 50 code snippets. The coupling coefficient is validated empirically: for attention head 17::14, the predicted coupling coefficient of 0.7123 closely matches the empirical best-fit slope of 0.7692 (r²=0.9567).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares OBPROP against two data-intensive baselines: logistic regression (following Tigges et al., 2023) and mean difference vectors (following Kim et al., 2018). Against logistic regression, OBPROP ties on subject pronoun prediction (r²≈0.945 for both) but requires zero training data versus 60 prompts; it significantly outperforms on political party prediction (r²≈0.427 vs. 0.295) even when logistic regression is trained on 3/4 of the available dataset; and it marginally outperforms on C vs. Python (AUC 0.9974 vs. 0.9971). Against mean difference vectors, OBPROP substantially outperforms on all three tasks: subject pronouns (r² 0.945 vs. 0.899), political parties (r² 0.427 vs. 0.0605), and C vs. Python (AUC 0.9974 vs. 0.9052). The key advantage is that OBPROP requires no labeled data, no stored embeddings, and only a single forward pass per observable to estimate LayerNorm gradients, making it particularly advantageous in low-data regimes where traditional probing methods degrade significantly.

## Relation to Prior Work

OBPROP builds directly on the QK/OV circuit decomposition from Elhage et al. (2021) and the linearization techniques of Nanda et al. (2023), but reframes the gradients from those linearizations as independently interpretable feature vectors rather than merely computational shortcuts. It departs from probing and sparse autoencoder approaches by requiring no training data or forward passes on labeled examples, and from circuit discovery methods by identifying specific feature vectors rather than relevant model components. The 'observable' formalism generalizes the logit-difference pattern common in interpretability work into a broader class of linear functionals, and the coupling coefficient (Theorem 2) introduces a new geometric metric for quantifying task-to-task coupling that has no direct analogue in prior probing or SAE literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The data-free nature of OBPROP democratizes mechanistic interpretability, enabling researchers without large compute or labeled datasets to identify and analyze feature vectors. The finding that the model uses nearly identical features (cosine similarity >0.96) for both grammatical gender prediction and occupational gender bias has direct practical implications: inference-time debiasing interventions will inevitably degrade legitimate pronoun prediction, pointing toward the need for training-time solutions. Key limitations include the restriction to OV circuits (ignoring QK circuits responsible for information routing like induction heads), validation on a single model family (GPT-Neo-1.3B), and the assumption of spherically-symmetric hidden state distributions in Theorem 2. Open questions include whether the shared-feature phenomenon generalizes across model scales and architectures, and how OBPROP might integrate with SAE-based approaches to capture dense feature compositions.
