## Problem

The causal revolution in empirical sciences risks stalling because causal models are squeezed between two opposing pressures: scientific perfectionism that demands only 'correct' models, and computer science's preference for assumption-free, scalable algorithms. This tension discourages researchers from making the explicit causal assumptions needed to answer intervention and counterfactual questions, even though metascientific evidence shows people routinely want and use causal conclusions from predictive models.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new algorithm, dataset, or training procedure. Its evidential input consists of metascientific studies documenting causal language use in published observational research (e.g., 31–56% of papers in top medical and nutrition journals using unjustified causal language). Its output is a philosophical framework—scientific pragmatism, value pluralism, and humanism—intended to guide how the machine learning community evaluates, selects, and integrates causal methods in practice.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a set of six positions and three principles constituting a philosophy of 'scientific pragmatism' for causal modeling. The central argument is that causal models should be judged by their usefulness for specific purposes (answering intervention questions, enabling interpretability, analyzing fairness) rather than by their fidelity to a 'true' data-generating process, drawing on the Box/Tukey tradition of treating models as tools. The author distinguishes general criticisms applicable to all probabilistic models (which should not selectively penalize causal models) from causal-specific criticisms (e.g., non-manipulability of social variables), and argues for balanced standards: predictive models should not suggest causal conclusions for free, while causal models should not be penalized for the additional assumptions their conclusions require. The framework explicitly positions humans as the ultimate arbiters of value, arguing that automation and scalability should not crowd out the subjective, case-by-case judgments that causal reasoning often demands.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not present empirical experiments, quantitative results, or a main results table. The paper's contributions are six argued positions (e.g., 'Proving too much,' 'People want causality,' 'Balanced standards') and three guiding principles (scientific pragmatism, value pluralism, humanism) intended to reframe how the machine learning community evaluates causal modeling proposals. The only quantitative evidence cited comes from prior meta-scientific studies (e.g., 31% of obesity/nutrition papers used inappropriate causal language; 53% of nonrandomized study abstracts contained causal spin).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a new method and does not compare against any baselines. It instead argues that the field's existing evaluation norms (SOTA predictive accuracy on standardized tasks) systematically disadvantage causal models, and it contrasts two philosophical stances—scientific perfectionism (demanding 'correct' models) and computer-science pragmatism (preferring assumption-free, scalable algorithms)—against the proposed middle ground of scientific pragmatism. ## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes disparate intellectual threads—Tukey's conclusions-vs-decisions distinction, Box's model pragmatism, Hacking's interventionist philosophy of science, Breiman's two-cultures debate, and recent causal ML surveys (Makhlouf et al., 2020; Kaddour et al., 2022)—into a unified philosophical framework it calls 'scientific pragmatism.' Rather than proposing a new method or benchmark, it repurposes metascientific evidence on unjustified causal language in published papers (Haber et al., 2022; Lazarus et al., 2015) to argue that the ML community's disciplinary biases (preference for assumption-free algorithms, SOTA-competition dynamics, standardized predictive tasks) are structurally working against the adoption of causal methods. The paper also draws on Richens & Everitt (2024) to note that distribution-shift robustness in predictive models implicitly entails causal structure, reinforcing the argument that causality is inescapable for generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies concrete structural barriers to the causal revolution in ML—low-acceptance-rate review dynamics that penalize additional assumptions, the Common Task Framework's narrow focus on predictive accuracy, and the economic logic favoring automation and scalability over context-dependent causal reasoning. Its most provocative claim (Position 5) is that preferring predictive models while interpreting results causally constitutes 'outsourcing responsibility' to less-informed audiences, a framing that could reshape how researchers frame their contributions. However, the paper is explicitly speculative in its more novel positions (5–6), provides no empirical evidence for its claims about ML community behavior, and offers no concrete protocol for implementing 'balanced standards' in peer review or model selection. Open questions include whether value pluralism can be operationalized in evaluation, how to guard against 'cargo cult' misuse of causal models, and whether the proposed philosophy can survive contact with the economic incentives driving ML deployment.
