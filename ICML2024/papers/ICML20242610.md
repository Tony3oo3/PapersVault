## Problem

When labeled data is expensive and a labeling budget is constrained, existing inference methods like prediction-powered inference (PPI) collect labels uniformly at random, wasting budget on points where a predictive model is already confident. This paper asks whether a machine learning model can be used to strategically select which points to label, achieving the same inferential accuracy with far fewer labels while maintaining provable validity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242610/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes n unlabeled feature vectors drawn i.i.d. from a population, a pre-trained (or from-scratch) black-box predictive model f, and a labeling budget n_b ≪ n. It outputs a valid (1−α)-confidence interval or hypothesis test for any convex M-estimation target (mean, regression coefficients, quantiles, GLM parameters), along with the data-dependent labeling decisions ξ_i. The method operates in two modes: batch (all labeling decisions made simultaneously with a fixed model) and sequential (model and sampling rule updated online as labels arrive), with the sequential mode requiring only a small initial labeled set to bootstrap the model.

## Method

The core estimator is an augmented inverse propensity weighting (AIPW) form where the propensity π(x) is set proportional to a model-uncertainty measure u(x), so that labeling budget is concentrated on points where the model is expected to err most. For regression, u(x) is a separately trained predictor of |f(X)−Y|; for classification, u(x) = 2·min{p(x), 1−p(x)} from the classifier's probability output. The paper derives an oracle sampling rule showing the variance-optimal propensity is proportional to the conditional root-mean-squared model error, and proves asymptotic normality of the resulting estimator via standard M-estimation theory (batch) and the martingale CLT (sequential). A practical τ-mixing with a uniform rule stabilizes the sampling probabilities against miscalibrated uncertainty estimates, and the tuning parameter η is selected to exactly meet the budget constraint. Empirically, active inference saves over 80% of the labeling budget compared to classical inference and 20–60% compared to PPI across post-election surveys, census data, and AlphaFold-assisted proteomics.

## Results

Active inference achieves substantial budget savings across all three applications while maintaining valid coverage (target α=0.1). In batch settings, it saves over 85% of the labeling budget compared to classical inference for Biden approval estimation, over 80% for census income regression, and over 75% for proteomics odds ratio estimation. Compared to the uniform-sampling PPI baseline, active inference saves approximately 25% of the budget on survey and proteomics tasks, and over 60% on the census task. Sequential fine-tuning provides additional gains of 30–45% over non-fine-tuned active inference and 20–35% over PPI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: (1) the 'uniform' baseline, which is prediction-powered inference (PPI) using ML predictions but a trivial uniform sampling rule, and (2) the 'classical' baseline, which uses no ML at all and simply labels a uniformly random subset. Active inference dominates both baselines on all three datasets in terms of confidence interval width at matched budget, while maintaining correct coverage. The gains over the classical baseline are dramatic (70–85% budget savings), reflecting the combined benefit of ML-assisted prediction and strategic sampling. The gains over PPI are more modest but still significant (20–60% budget savings), isolating the value of adaptive sampling over uniform sampling. The census task shows the largest gap over PPI (>60%), likely because the XGBoost income predictor has highly heterogeneous uncertainty across the covariate space, making uncertainty-based sampling particularly effective. All methods achieve the target 90% coverage, confirming that the variance reduction comes at no cost to validity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends prediction-powered inference (PPI) by replacing its uniform label-collection strategy with a machine-learning-guided, uncertainty-based sampling rule, showing that PPI is a special case of the proposed active inference framework. The active mean estimator is an AIPW estimator (Robins et al., 1994) with propensities derived from model uncertainty, and the general M-estimation estimator reduces to the PPI++ estimator under uniform sampling. The work also draws on pool-based active learning's uncertainty-sampling principle but repurposes it for valid statistical inference rather than predictor training, and connects to adaptive experiment design literature while differing in its focus on inference power rather than treatment-effect estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method provides a practical recipe for saving 80%+ of the labeling budget relative to classical inference and 20–25% relative to PPI while maintaining valid coverage, which is directly relevant to any application where labels are expensive (surveys, proteomics, remote sensing). Key limitations include the i.i.d. assumption (which may be violated in sequential collection), the risk that miscalibrated uncertainty estimates can degrade performance below PPI, and the reliance on a reasonably good predictive model. Open questions include extending validity guarantees to non-i.i.d. or time-varying data, developing tighter non-asymptotic bounds for general M-estimation, and understanding how the method behaves when the predictive model is systematically biased rather than merely noisy.
