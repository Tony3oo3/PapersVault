## Problem

While GNN explainers have been widely developed to foster trust in graph-based predictions, no prior work has studied their robustness under adversarial graph structure perturbations. This paper asks whether an adversary with limited knowledge of the explainer and a small perturbation budget can keep GNN predictions correct while drastically altering the explanatory subgraph, and proves the resulting optimization problem is NP-hard.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241146/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack consumes a graph G=(V,E) with a target label y, a trained GNN model f, a perturbation-based GNN explainer (GNNExplainer, PGExplainer, or GSAT), the explanatory edges E_S, and the explainer's loss function L. It outputs a perturbed edge set (adding and/or deleting at most ξ edges) such that the explainer produces a new explanatory edge set with maximal overlap difference from E_S, while four constraints are enforced: E_S edges are never removed, the perturbation budget is respected, the power-law likelihood ratio test confirms structural similarity (Λ < τ ≈ 0.000157), and f(Ḡ) = y is preserved.

## Method

Two attacks are proposed, both inspired by the observation that a feasible attack must efficiently score edges by importance. The loss-based attack defines separate deletion and addition masks, uses filter/bias vectors to freeze mask values on E_S at a constant γ, then minimizes (for deletion) or maximizes (for addition) the explainer's loss to identify edges whose removal or insertion most disrupts the explanation. The deduction-based attack instead simulates the explainer's gradient-descent mask learning dynamics, approximating the final ℓ₁-norm of the mask on E_S via a Riemann sum over N sampled mask values β_i, yielding a loss that directly mirrors the attack objective. Both attacks enumerate all (ξ_D, ξ_A) splits of the perturbation budget, filter candidates by prediction correctness and structural similarity, and select the perturbed graph maximizing the set difference. The NP-hardness is established via reduction to the 0-1 knapsack problem, and transferability to non-perturbation-based explainers (CAM, SA, GraphSVX, GEM) is demonstrated.

## Results

The paper evaluates attack effectiveness using the overlap ratio |E_S − E_S ∩ Ẽ_S| / |E_S| (higher = more explanation change). The deduction-based attack achieves the best performance across all three perturbation-based explainers and six datasets, reaching up to 69.90% on GSAT/MUTAG, 64.42% on GNNExplainer/Tree Cycle, and 41.50% on PGExplainer/BA House. With only ξ=2 perturbed edges, the explanatory subgraph can be 70% different from the clean explanation. The loss-based attack is consistently second-best, while both baselines (Random and Kill-hot) remain below 37% in most settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Random attack baseline, both proposed methods achieve substantially higher overlap ratios: e.g., on GNNExplainer/Tree Cycle, the deduction-based attack reaches 64.42% vs. 22.99% for Random, and on GSAT/MUTAG it reaches 69.90% vs. 26.08%. Against the Kill-hot baseline (which assumes knowledge of the explainer's mask values), the proposed attacks still dominate: 64.42% vs. 14.77% on GNNExplainer/Tree Cycle and 41.50% vs. 14.50% on PGExplainer/BA House, indicating that simply removing high-mask non-explanatory edges is insufficient. In the transferability experiment (Table 4), the perturbed graphs generated by the deduction-based attack also effectively attack four other explainer types—decomposition-based CAM (up to 51.96% on Community), gradient-based SA (up to 55.25% on REDDIT), surrogate-based GraphSVX (up to 49.69% on Community), and generation-based GEM (up to 44.91% on Community)—whereas Random and Kill-hot perturbations transfer poorly (typically below 30%). The deduction-based attack consistently outperforms the loss-based attack, with the gap most pronounced on GSAT/Community (45.54% vs. 20.20%) and GSAT/REDDIT (57.40% vs. 50.25%), confirming that simulating the explainer's learning dynamics yields more targeted edge selections.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the first to systematically study adversarial attacks on GNN explainers, building on the perturbation-based explainer framework (GNNExplainer, PGExplainer, GSAT) as the attack target. It extends the line of work on manipulating neural network explanations (Ghorbani et al. 2019; Dombrowski et al. 2019; Slack et al. 2021) from image models to graph-structured data, while departing from prior GNN attacks (evasion/poisoning) that target the classifier rather than the explainer. The closest prior work, GEAttack (Fan et al. 2023), differs in both threat model (white-box vs. limited knowledge) and goal (also alters predictions vs. preserves them). The paper introduces a power-law likelihood ratio test as a structural similarity constraint and proves the attack problem is NP-hard via reduction to 0-1 knapsack, providing two approximate solutions (loss-based and deduction-based).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that GNN explainers—designed to foster trust in safety-critical applications like disease diagnosis and malware detection—are fragile to minimal structural perturbations (2 edges can alter 70% of explanatory edges while preserving correct predictions) raises fundamental concerns about relying on GNN explanations for decision-making. The transferability of generated perturbations across four other explainer types (decomposition, gradient, surrogate, generation-based) suggests the vulnerability is not specific to one mechanism. Key open questions include designing provably robust GNN explainers (existing certified defenses for GNN classifiers are inapplicable since explainers output a mask matrix rather than a scalar), extending attacks to non-perturbation-based explainers, and developing effective defenses, as the two tested (structure similarity detection, API-only access) proved insufficient.
