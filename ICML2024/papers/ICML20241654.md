## Problem

Existing methods for measuring inter-agent influence in multi-agent reinforcement learning are confined to the discounted reward setting and address narrow application-specific goals (e.g., reducing joint action spaces, enhancing exploration) without a unified theoretical framework. There is no principled way to quantify how much one agent can affect another's expected return in the average reward setting, which is the natural formulation for many real-world applications such as energy network management and vehicle formation control.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only observed joint actions and simultaneously learned state-action function approximations (parametrized by η^j), making it fully decentralized—no agent needs access to another's internal state or policy. It produces two N×N matrices: the Total Impact Measurement (TIM), a single matrix where entry (i,j) is the stationary-distribution-weighted average of the maximum swing agent i can impose on agent j's long-term return, and the State Impact Measurement (SIM), a per-state matrix capturing the same quantity conditioned on a specific state s. Both are non-negative and can be normalized to row- or column-stochastic form for probabilistic interpretation.

## Method

The core quantity is the impact sample U^i→j(s,a) = max_{a^i} Q^j(s,a^{-i},a^i) − min_{a^i} Q^j(s,a^{-i},a^i), which measures the maximum swing agent i's action can produce on agent j's state-action value. TIM and SIM are defined as the stationary-distribution-weighted averages of this sample over all (state, action) pairs and over actions in a fixed state, respectively. The approximation algorithm is a simple stochastic iteration ν_{t+1} = (1−α_t)ν_t + α_t · U(s_t, a_t, η_t^j) that runs in parallel with a converging state-action function learner (e.g., tabular or deep SARSA). Convergence to the true TIM/SIM is proven via ODE analysis under Borkar's stochastic approximation framework, with an explicit error bound |TIM − TIM̄| ≤ 2‖Q^j − Q̄^j‖_∞ linking approximation quality to the state-action function error. A further result establishes continuity of both TIM and SIM in the policy parameters θ via Perron-Frobenius eigenvector continuity, ensuring stable estimates under gradual policy updates.

## Results

In the random environment (5 agents, 5 states, binary actions), both TIM and SIM approximation errors decrease monotonically over time for static policies, with initial error increasing as the number of inter-agent dependencies grows (L_add from 0 to 20), while the final error shows the reversed ordering. Under dynamic (learning) policies, approximation errors still decrease consistently but more slowly, validating the algorithms' robustness to policy changes. In the coin game (3 agents, 10×10 grid), TIM correctly identifies the asymmetric penalty structure: agent 1's estimated impact is high on agents 2 and 3 (who incur −2 penalties), agent 2's impact is high on itself and agent 3, and agent 3's impact is elevated only on itself. SIM further captures state-dependent influence, showing markedly higher estimated impact when agents are positioned beside an opponent's coin versus their own coin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not directly compare TIM and SIM against competing influence measurement methods (e.g., coordination graphs, Value of Interaction, or social influence scores). Instead, it validates its approximation algorithms against analytically computed ground-truth TIM and SIM matrices derived from the exact stationary distribution and state-action functions. The key validation is that the stochastic approximation iterations (Equations 12 and 17) converge to the analytical values, with error bounded by 2·‖Q_π^j − Q̄_π^j‖_∞ (Theorem 5.3/5.8). The coin game experiment serves as a qualitative sanity check: the estimated influence matrices align with the known asymmetric penalty structure without any prior knowledge of the environment's reward function, demonstrating that the method can recover influence structures in a black-box setting. The paper positions itself as the first study to provide convergence guarantees for influence structure detection in the multi-agent average reward setting, a gap not addressed by prior scalar influence methods (Wang et al., 2020; Jaques et al., 2018).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the binary coordination graph framework of Guestrin et al. (2002b) to scalar, fine-grained influence measurements, and generalizes prior influence quantification methods (Value of Interaction, social influence as intrinsic motivation) from the discounted reward setting to the average reward setting. It leverages stochastic approximation theory from Borkar (2008), as specialized by Zhang et al. (2018), to provide convergence guarantees and error bounds that prior scalar influence methods conspicuously lack. The unified 'influence measurement function' framework is designed to be valid across all common MARL reward settings, positioning the paper as a theoretical unification rather than a single-setting method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first study to provide provable convergence guarantees for influence structure detection in the multi-agent average reward setting, a gap the authors explicitly identify as relevant to real-world applications like energy network management and vehicle formation control. The decentralized nature of the algorithm (requiring only observation of other agents' actions) enhances practical applicability. Open questions include whether TIM and SIM can be used to actively improve learning (beyond their current descriptive role), whether the framework extends to infinite state/action spaces, and whether the ergodicity assumption (Assumption 3.2) can be relaxed for more realistic non-ergodic policies.
