## Problem

Abbe et al. (2023) proved that in the Generalization on the Unseen (GOTU) setting, random feature models and Transformers trained on sparse Boolean functions converge to minimal degree interpolators (MDI). This paper asks whether this minimal degree bias survives when inputs are no longer Boolean — i.e., when they are q-ary discrete values, real-valued, or embedded as roots of unity. The answer is that the Boolean case is special: for general non-Boolean inputs the MDI bias breaks in the sparse regime, but is restored in a 'small features' regime and for roots-of-unity embeddings.

## Input / Output

Inputs are vectors x ∈ R^d (real-valued), discrete tokens from finite sets such as {−q/2,…,−1,1,…,q/2}^d, or complex roots of unity in U_n^d. The GOTU constraint restricts training to a proper subset U^c of the domain, with evaluation performed on the unseen complement U. Rather than reporting standard accuracy, the output is characterized by the polynomial degree profile of the learned function — specifically its Hermite coefficients (for real inputs) or monomial/Fourier coefficients (for discrete and roots-of-unity inputs) — to determine whether the model converges to the minimum-degree interpolator or to a higher-degree one.

## Method

The paper analyzes two complementary regimes for random feature models. In the small features regime (fixed d, weight variance ε→0), Theorem 4.2 proves convergence to the MDI for any polynomial activation with non-zero coefficients, by showing that the Gram matrix of Hermite coefficients of random features scales as ε^{-|T|} per degree level, so the minimum-norm interpolant is dominated by the lowest-degree terms. In the sparse regime (d→∞, O(1)-sparse target), the authors show via explicit computation (Example 5.3) that the MDI bias breaks for non-Boolean inputs because the Gram matrix is no longer diagonal and the regularization term from the orthogonal directions alters the minimizer. For roots-of-unity embeddings (Theorem 6.1), the discrete Fourier basis restores the diagonal structure of the covariance matrix, recovering the MDI bias. Experiments with Transformers on discrete inputs confirm that the MDI bias does not generalize beyond the Boolean domain, with Transformers learning degree-4 interpolators where the MDI is linear.

## Results

In the small features regime (Theorem 4.2), RF models with polynomial activations converge to minimum-degree interpolators (MDI) for any target function, confirmed empirically: for f(x)=1 with GOTU constraint x₁=1, the constant coefficient reaches 1.000±0.000 for Shifted ReLU, Sigmoid, and SoftPlus (Table 2). In the sparse regime, this MDI bias breaks: the same task yields only 0.624±0.017 for (1+x)² activation (Table 3), and Example 5.3 shows convergence to 2/5·x₁+3/5 instead of the constant 1. For the motivating multiplication task f(x)=x₁x₂ with GOTU constraint (x₁−1)(x₂−1)=0, the RF model in the sparse regime recovers the MDI x₁+x₂−1 across all tested activations (Table 1), with x₁ and x₂ coefficients at ~0.955–0.996 and x₁x₂ coefficient near zero. Transformers on discrete inputs learn a degree-4 interpolator (coefficient of x₁²x₂² = 0.484±0.024, Table 4) rather than the linear MDI, showing the Boolean MDI bias does not extend to non-Boolean domains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baselines but instead contrasts two theoretical regimes (sparse vs. small features) and two model families (RF vs. Transformer) against the Boolean-case MDI result of Abbe et al. (2023). The key finding is that the MDI bias proven for Boolean functions in the sparse regime does NOT generalize to non-Boolean inputs: in the sparse regime with polynomial activations, the RF model learns higher-degree interpolators (e.g., 2/5·x₁+3/5 instead of constant 1, or a degree-4 polynomial instead of the linear MDI for Transformers). The small features regime restores the MDI bias for RF models with any polynomial activation (Theorem 4.2), and the roots-of-unity embedding restores it in the sparse regime (Theorem 6.1). Sigmoid activation is a notable exception in the sparse regime, still converging close to MDI (coefficient of 1 = 0.992±0.003 in Table 3), while ReLU fails in the small features regime (0.564±0.009) due to ReLU(0)=0 violating the non-zero coefficient assumption. Transformers show learning-rate-dependent behavior: at 10⁻⁴ they converge to a degree-4 interpolator, while at 10⁻⁵ (the rate that yields MDI in the Boolean case) they fail to converge within 6×10⁶ iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a direct generalization of Abbe et al. (2023), which established that random feature models and Transformers trained with gradient descent in the GOTU (generalization on the unseen) setting converge to minimal degree interpolators for sparse Boolean functions. The key conceptual extension is moving from the Boolean hypercube {+1,-1}^d to arbitrary non-Boolean inputs (real-valued, discrete non-binary, and roots of unity), revealing that the MDI bias is not universal. The paper introduces the 'small features regime' (vanishing weight variance at fixed dimension) as a new analytical surrogate for the sparse regime, and connects to the broader implicit bias literature (Bartlett et al., 2021) and spectral bias (Xu et al., 2019; Rahaman et al., 2019) where polynomial degree plays the role of frequency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that the minimal degree interpolator bias breaks down for non-Boolean inputs in the sparse regime, and is restored only under specific algebraic structure (roots of unity embedding)—challenges the assumption that the Boolean case captures a universal principle of gradient descent. For practitioners working on arithmetic or symbolic reasoning tasks, this implies that the choice of input tokenization and embedding (e.g., integer vs. roots-of-unity parametrization) can fundamentally alter what the model generalizes to on unseen data. Open questions include: what other factors drive Transformer bias beyond MDI (output alphabet, variable influence), whether the roots-of-unity parametrization has practical utility, and what the full picture looks like for general real-valued inputs where neither the small features nor sparse regime gives a clean characterization.
