## Problem

Equivariant neural networks enforce strict symmetry constraints that make them unable to describe symmetry breaking in physical systems, yet understanding the sources of asymmetry is essential for predicting behavior in crystals, fluids, and dynamical systems. While relaxed group convolution was previously shown empirically to model approximate symmetries, no theoretical guarantees existed for whether it would reliably recover the correct symmetry level from data. This paper provides both a theoretical proof and empirical validation that relaxed group convolutions consistently capture the highest equivariance consistent with the data and expose interpretable symmetry-breaking factors.

## Input / Output

Inputs are physical system representations at various resolutions: 3D voxel tensors for crystal structures (BaTiO3 from Material Project), 2D velocity fields at 128×256 for turbulence, 3D velocity fields at 64³ for super-resolution, and 1D angle time series for pendulums. The primary output of interest is not the prediction itself but the post-training relaxed weights w_l(h) — one scalar per group element per filter bank — which encode which symmetries are preserved or broken. For turbulence experiments, velocity fields are first decomposed into frequency bands via Fourier cutoffs so each scale is processed by a separate relaxed convolution layer, enabling scale-resolved symmetry detection.

## Method

The architecture replaces strict weight-sharing in group convolution with trainable relaxed weights w_l(h) that are initialized identically across all group elements (preserving equivariance at initialization) but are free to diverge during training. The key theoretical result (Proposition 3.1) proves via gradient analysis that these weights only deviate from equality when the stabilizer of the input-output pair is a proper subgroup of the full group, guaranteeing the model adapts to exactly the symmetry present in the data. Because the relaxed weights transform under the regular representation of the group, they can be Fourier-decomposed into irreducible representations to identify precisely which geometric symmetries are broken. The method is applied across four physical systems using different groups (O_h for crystals, C_8 for rotational turbulence, Z² for translational homogeneity, and {+1,−1} for time-reversal), with a convergence guarantee (Proposition 3.2) showing mini-batch training still recovers the correct symmetry pattern.

## Results

On the 3D super-resolution task, the relaxed equivariant model achieves the lowest MAE in both channel flow (2.44±0.01) and isotropic flow (1.00±0.01), outperforming the strictly equivariant model (2.54±0.03 and 1.12±0.02) and the non-equivariant CNN (2.60±0.05 and 1.22±0.04). For symmetry discovery, the relaxed weights correctly identify C4v and C2v point groups in BaTiO3 phase transitions, recover the Kolmogorov isotropy crossover in turbulence (equivariance error → 0 at high wavenumbers), and detect time-reversal symmetry breaking in frictional pendulum systems. The method also localizes homogeneity breaking to boundary regions in channel flow via spatially varying translation equivariance errors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two symmetry-discovery baselines: MSR (Zhou et al., 2021) and LieGAN (Yang et al., 2023b). MSR, which learns weight-sharing matrices via meta-learning, is limited to 1D translation convolutions and could not be directly applied to the 2D fluid data; when adapted per y-coordinate, its learned matrices were very noisy and failed to recover correct rotation symmetry even after hyperparameter grid search. LieGAN, a GAN-based method for learning SO(2) generators, failed entirely with random initialization and, when seeded with the true generator, produced learned generators that were closer to ground truth only at higher frequencies; critically, it cannot quantify the degree of symmetry breaking. The proposed relaxed group convolution method outperforms both by (1) operating natively on 2D/3D data, (2) providing a continuous equivariance-error metric that quantifies the extent of breaking at each scale, (3) requiring no meta-learning or adversarial training, and (4) detecting symmetry at the individual-sample level rather than only at the distribution level.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends relaxed group convolution (Wang et al., 2022), which introduced trainable relaxed weights to ease strict weight-sharing in equivariant convolutions, by providing the first theoretical guarantees (gradient-based convergence to the correct symmetry level) and a systematic taxonomy of symmetry breaking types (single-sample, distributional, functional). It builds on the group convolution framework of Cohen & Welling (2016a) and the conceptual insight from Smidt et al. (2021) that equivariant models can detect symmetry-breaking order parameters, but departs from both by showing that the relaxed weights themselves—rather than auxiliary inputs—serve as interpretable, Landau-theory-analogous order parameters that automatically adapt to the data's symmetry. The irrep decomposition of relaxed weights for identifying which specific symmetries are broken is a new interpretability tool not present in prior relaxed-convolution work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified, theoretically grounded framework for discovering and quantifying symmetry breaking across diverse physical systems (crystal phase transitions, turbulence, pendulum dynamics), with the relaxed weights offering physically interpretable outputs analogous to order parameters in Landau theory. Key limitations include the need to specify the largest candidate symmetry group a priori and potential failure under spontaneous symmetry breaking with multiple degenerate outputs, which would require a probabilistic generative model. Open questions include generalization to graph neural networks, whether relaxed weights improve optimization beyond symmetry discovery, and extending the method to continuous (Lie) groups where the irrep decomposition becomes more complex.
