## Problem

Despite the proliferation of efficient Transformer variants (Sparse, Linear) designed to reduce the quadratic attention cost, no prior work provides theoretical guarantees about when these architectures are actually suitable replacements for the standard Transformer on reasoning tasks. This paper asks: do efficient Transformers truly save computation for Chain-of-Thought reasoning, or does the need for larger model sizes cancel out the architectural efficiency gains?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242306/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives an input sequence (e.g., an arithmetic expression, an integer sequence for LIS, or two strings for Edit Distance) followed by a Chain-of-Thought prompt, and autoregressively generates a structured output sequence of (input, DP state, DP value, final answer) tuples. The output is produced in a regression setting where each position's embedding is projected to the nearest integer, and generated tokens are fed directly back as inputs without re-embedding. All internal computations use O(log L)-bit floating-point precision, and the domain of integer values can grow polynomially with sequence length L.

## Method

The paper establishes matching upper and lower bounds on the hidden dimension D required for Sparse and Linear Transformers to solve Dynamic Programming problems via Chain-of-Thought. The upper bound (Theorem 4.2) constructs explicit attention and FFN parameters showing D = O(√L) suffices, while the lower bound (Theorem 4.4) uses an information-bottleneck argument: under log-precision, the set of neurons whose values determine all future outputs can store only O(D log L) bits, so the pigeonhole principle forces D = Ω̃(√L) for any regular DP problem, yielding total complexity Θ(L²) — identical to the standard Transformer. The key positive result identifies m-locality DP problems (where each step depends only on the most recent m states) as the regime where efficiency is preserved: Sparse Transformers achieve O(mL) complexity with constant D, and Linear Transformers have a matching Ω̃(mL) lower bound. Experiments on Arithmetic, LIS, and Edit Distance tasks confirm that required embedding dimensions grow with problem size for efficient Transformers but remain constant for standard Transformers, with the growth being most pronounced for the non-local LIS task.

## Results

On the Arithmetic task, the Standard Transformer requires the fewest GFLOPs to exceed 90% accuracy at every sequence length (e.g., 0.605 GFLOPs at length 505 vs. 2.399 for Sparse and 9.584 for Linear). The key theoretical finding is that both Sparse and Linear Transformers require hidden dimension D = Ω̃(√L) for general DP problems, inflating their total complexity to Ω̃(L²) — matching the Standard Transformer's cost. Under the locality assumption (m = o(L)), the lower bound improves to Ω̃(mL), and the Sparse Transformer achieves O(mL) with constant hidden dimension, recovering a genuine efficiency gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three architectures — Standard Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017), Sparse Transformer (Child et al., 2019), and Linear Transformer (Katharopoulos et al., 2020) — on three DP tasks (Arithmetic, Edit Distance, LIS) with Chain-of-Thought. Contrary to expectations, the Standard Transformer requires the lowest GFLOPs to reach 90% accuracy on Arithmetic at every length (0.605 vs. 2.399 for Sparse and 9.584 for Linear at length 505), because it maintains a constant hidden dimension while the efficient variants must scale D with √L. The Sparse Transformer outperforms the Linear Transformer by roughly 4× in GFLOPs at length 505 (2.399 vs. 9.584), consistent with the theory that Sparse can achieve constant hidden dimension under locality while Linear still requires D = Ω̃(√m). On the LIS task (no locality), both efficient Transformers show the steepest growth in required embedding dimension with problem size, whereas the Standard Transformer handles all difficulty levels with a fixed 128–256 dimension.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DP-based reasoning framework of Feng et al. (2023), who proved that standard autoregressive Transformers solve general DP problems with constant model size, to two prominent efficient Transformer variants: the Sparse Transformer (Child et al., 2019) and the Linear Transformer (Katharopoulos et al., 2020). While prior work on efficient Transformers focused on reducing attention complexity in isolation, this paper asks whether that architectural efficiency translates to overall computational savings when the model must also grow in hidden dimension to maintain expressiveness. The introduction of the locality assumption as a unifying condition under which efficiency is preserved is a novel conceptual contribution that bridges the gap between the general DP setting and structured reasoning tasks like arithmetic evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that Sparse and Linear Transformers require hidden dimensions scaling as Ω̃(√L) for general DP problems, negating their attention-level efficiency gains—challenges a core assumption in the efficient Transformer literature and provides practitioners with a principled criterion (locality) for when these architectures are genuinely advantageous. The open question of whether the Linear Transformer's lower bound can be matched by an explicit construction, and whether these results extend beyond the two studied architectures to other efficient designs (e.g., Reformer, Performer), remain important directions. The practical implication is clear: for long-horizon reasoning without locality structure, the standard Transformer's constant-size property is a genuine advantage that efficient variants cannot replicate.
