## Problem

Feature visualizations are a cornerstone of mechanistic interpretability, yet the field lacks any sanity check for their reliability, any demonstration that they can be adversarially manipulated, and any theoretical characterization of when they are guaranteed to be trustworthy. This paper asks three concrete questions: can feature visualizations be fooled, do they actually reflect how networks process natural images, and under what assumptions on the function are they provably reliable?

## Input / Output

The adversarial methods take a pre-trained CNN (Inception-V1 or ResNet-50) and inject a small fooling circuit (6 ReLU units) or orthogonal silent-unit filters into a chosen layer, producing a modified network whose feature visualizations display arbitrary patterns (e.g., the Mona Lisa) while ImageNet top-1 accuracy drops by less than 0.5%. The empirical sanity check feeds both natural ImageNet images and last-layer feature visualizations through the network and outputs per-layer Spearman rank correlations between same-class natural images, cross-class natural images, and same-class visualizations. The theoretical component takes a function class (black-box, neural network, convex, Lipschitz, piecewise affine, etc.) and outputs impossibility or possibility theorems for predicting f(x) from only its arg max and arg min.

## Method

The paper combines three complementary analyses. First, a fooling circuit wires six ReLU units so that a binary classifier (distinguishing natural from synthetic visualization input) routes activations through either a 'feather' or a 'donut' path, making the unit's visualization arbitrary while preserving natural-image behavior; a second method uses orthogonal filters with a negative bias set to the maximum natural activation, so the added unit stays silent on natural input but dominates the visualization. Second, a sanity check measures whether feature visualizations for a class activate similar units as natural images of that class across all Inception-V1 layers, finding that in the first two-thirds of the network, same-class visualizations are no more similar to same-class natural images than to completely unrelated classes. Third, a theoretical framework formalizes feature visualization as returning only (arg min, arg max, f_min, f_max) and proves via constructive counterexamples that for every decoder and for function classes ranging from general black-box functions to convex and Lipschitz functions, this information is insufficient to predict f(x) or even determine whether f(x) is closer to the maximum or minimum—reliability is guaranteed only for 1D affine or constant functions.

## Results

The fooling circuit on Inception-V1 preserves network behavior on natural input while arbitrarily altering visualizations: top-1 accuracy drops only from 69.146% to 68.744% and top-5 from 88.858% to 88.330% when all last-layer visualizations are permuted. The silent-units method on ResNet-50 makes all 512 units in a layer produce near-identical feature visualizations with zero change in top-1 or top-5 validation accuracy. The empirical sanity check (Figure 5) shows that throughout the first two-thirds of Inception-V1, the Spearman similarity between natural images and same-class feature visualizations is close to the lower bound (similarity to different-class images), indicating feature visualizations are processed along largely different paths than natural input. Theoretically, Table 1 proves that for black-box functions, neural networks, ERM-trained networks, piecewise affine, monotonic, and even convex functions, no decoder can reliably predict f(x) from feature visualizations alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it critically evaluates the reliability of existing feature visualization methods (primarily Olah et al., 2017). The key empirical comparison is the sanity check: feature visualizations for a given class are compared against natural images of the same class (upper bound) and natural images of different classes (lower bound) in terms of layer-wise activation similarity. The finding is that in early and mid layers, same-class visualizations are no more similar to natural images than completely unrelated images, failing the sanity check. On the adversarial side, the fooling circuit and silent-units methods are compared against the original unmanipulated network: both preserve ImageNet accuracy within <0.5% (fooling circuit) or exactly (silent units) while producing arbitrary or identical visualizations. The theoretical results compare twelve function classes, showing that only the two most restrictive settings (1-D affine and constant functions) admit reliable prediction from feature visualizations, while all practically relevant classes (including convex and Lipschitz) do not.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sanity-check framework developed for saliency and attribution methods (Adebayo et al., 2018) to feature visualizations, a central mechanistic interpretability tool that has lacked systematic reliability evaluation. It also connects to the growing literature on adversarial manipulation of interpretability methods (Heo et al., 2019; Viering et al., 2019; Slack et al., 2020) by providing the first successful fooling of feature visualizations specifically. The theoretical impossibility results build on and parallel recent impossibility theorems for feature attribution (Bilodeau et al., 2024; Srinivas & Fleuret, 2019), but apply them to the distinct setting of activation-maximization-based visualizations rather than per-sample attribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Feature visualizations underpin much of the current understanding of how CNNs process images, yet this paper shows they are processed along fundamentally different paths than natural images and can be arbitrarily manipulated without changing network behavior. The practical sanity check provides a concrete tool for evaluating future visualization methods, while the theoretical impossibility results suggest that reliable post-hoc interpretability of black-box networks via activation maximization is fundamentally unattainable. Open questions include whether architectural constraints (e.g., enforced linearity) or radical departures from activation maximization could yield reliable visualizations, and whether the findings extend to transformer-based architectures and non-vision modalities.
