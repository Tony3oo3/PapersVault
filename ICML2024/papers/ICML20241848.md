## Problem

RL-based sequential recommenders consistently outperform self-supervised baselines on next-item prediction (NIP) metrics, despite RL's objective being to maximize long-term cumulative reward—a goal that should theoretically degrade short-term accuracy. This paper formally proves that an optimal RL policy can be arbitrarily worse than an optimal self-supervised model under NIP, then investigates what mechanism actually drives the observed performance gains.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241848/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a user's session interaction sequence (ordered list of item IDs with click/buy labels) and produces a ranked list of items for next-item prediction. The architecture has two heads sharing a backbone: a standard softmax action-logits head used for final recommendations, and an auxiliary head (RL Q-values in SQN, or a scalar prediction in the proposed variants) trained with a separate loss. The auxiliary head's output is never used at inference time—it serves purely as a regularizer during training.

## Method

The paper first proves (Theorems 4.3–4.4) that NIP performance of an optimal RL policy is unbounded below relative to an optimal cross-entropy solution, establishing a theoretical misalignment. It then analyzes the SQN framework, where RL (DQN loss) is added as an auxiliary loss to a self-supervised backbone. Through a top-down analysis, the authors replace the DQN loss with a policy-evaluation loss (EVAL) and further with a categorical prediction of the next two interaction types (CAT), showing partial but incomplete recovery of SQN's gains. A bottom-up SHAP/XGBoost feature-importance analysis on learned Q-values reveals that the number of past interactions (hist-length) is the dominant explanatory feature. Replacing the RL head with a simple MSE loss predicting the sequence length (HIST) yields performance comparable to full SQN across GRU, Caser, and SASRec backbones on both RetailRocket and RC15, demonstrating that the performance boost stems from learning to encode interaction history length rather than from policy optimization per se.

## Results

On RetailRocket (buy interactions), the proposed GRU-HIST model achieves HR@5 of 0.1980 and NDCG@5 of 0.1549, outperforming both the self-supervised GRU baseline (0.1601/0.1248) and the RL-based GRU-SQN (0.1921/0.1519). The GRU-EVAL variant (replacing DQN loss with policy evaluation loss) matches SQN closely at 0.1962/0.1545, confirming that the RL objective itself is not essential. On RC15, CASER-HIST achieves the best overall result with HR@5 of 0.4528 and NDCG@5 of 0.3197, surpassing CASER-SQN (0.4263/0.3014) by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the four self-supervised baselines (GRU, NIN, CASER, SAS), all proposed auxiliary-loss variants (HIST, EVAL, CAT) consistently and significantly improve NIP performance, confirming that the gains attributed to RL can be replicated without it. Against SQN (the RL baseline), the HIST model wins on GRU (0.1980 vs 0.1921 HR@5) and CASER (0.2399 vs 0.2020 HR@5) on RetailRocket, and on GRU and CASER on RC15, but loses on NIN (0.3001 vs 0.3307) and SAS (0.2960 vs 0.3012) on RetailRocket. The EVAL variant (replacing DQN with policy-evaluation loss) nearly matches SQN across all architectures, with NIN-EVAL even slightly edging NIN-SQN (0.3308 vs 0.3307 HR@5). The CAT model (predicting interaction types) improves over self-supervised baselines but falls 3–5 points behind SQN on HR@5, indicating it captures only part of what RL provides. The FUT model (predicting future sequence length) performs catastrophically (e.g., 0.0390 HR@5 for GRU-FUT), likely due to high variance in the MSE target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the SQN framework (Xin et al., 2020), which first demonstrated that adding RL as an auxiliary loss to self-supervised sequential recommenders improves NIP performance, and on the theoretical observations by Deffayet et al. (2023) about the misalignment between RL's long-term reward objective and NIP's short-term evaluation protocol. It departs from prior work by formally proving (Theorems 4.3–4.4) that an optimal RL policy can be arbitrarily worse than a self-supervised model under NIP metrics, and by identifying the proxy learning objectives (sequence length, interaction types) that actually explain RL's empirical gains. Rather than proposing a new SOTA method, the paper repositions RL in sequential recommendation as an implicit regularizer that encourages the network to encode session-history information, rather than as a policy optimizer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a trivial MSE loss predicting past interaction count (hist) achieves performance comparable to full DQN-based RL challenges the field's working assumption that RL's value in sequential recommendation comes from its policy optimization mechanism. This has practical implications: practitioners can potentially replace complex RL training pipelines with simpler auxiliary losses, reducing computational cost and implementation complexity. However, the gap is not fully closed—hist underperforms SQN for NIN and sometimes SASRec, and the fut model (second most important feature) performs terribly due to high variance—leaving open questions about what additional information RL encodes beyond sequence length. The paper also highlights the need for simulator-based evaluation to properly assess RL's long-term benefits, which remain unmeasured under the NIP protocol.
