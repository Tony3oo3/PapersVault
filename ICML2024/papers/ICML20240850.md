## Problem

Existing algorithmic fairness tools for scarce resource allocation are almost exclusively deterministic, assigning resources to top-ranked candidates and thereby completely overriding the claims of all others. The paper argues this is unfair under Broome's claims-based theory of fairness, which holds that even marginally weaker claims deserve a proportional chance rather than zero probability, and that deterministic allocation under uncertainty systematically compounds structural injustices across multiple decision-makers.

## Input / Output

The method operates on tabular feature vectors for n individuals with k << n resources to allocate. In the known-claims setting, inputs are exact claim scores c_i ∈ [0,1]; in the uncertain-claims setting, inputs are ML predictions p̂(x_i) from a supervised model plus B=11 bootstrapped model predictions (trained on 50% data subsets) and conformal prediction p-values for outlier flagging. The output is a set of selected individuals produced by an iterative weighted selection (one pick per round without replacement), with per-round weights w_{i,t} summing to 1, and the paper evaluates outcomes via precision (utility) and the systemic exclusion rate (SER) across m decision-makers.

## Method

The paper formalizes Broome's fairness into an iterative weighted selection (BF lottery) where each round's selection probability is proportional to remaining claim strength, then introduces a partial BF variant that deterministically allocates the top k−k′ resources and randomizes only the remaining k′ over the n′ claims nearest the decision boundary. For uncertain claims, three practical randomization procedures are proposed: (1) partial BF using predicted scores near the k-th threshold, (2) variance-based randomization where individuals are deterministically selected only if all B bootstrapped models rank them in the top k, with remaining resources allocated via weighted lottery over the proportion of models placing each person in the top k, and (3) outlier-based randomization using conformal prediction p-values (α threshold) to flag individuals with unreliable predictions, whose resources are then allocated via unweighted lottery. Empirically on Swiss Unemployment and Census Income data, these methods achieve 0.2–2% utility loss while reducing SER by over 20% when m > 2 decision-makers are present.

## Results

On the Swiss Unemployment dataset (k/n=0.25), the deterministic top-k allocation achieves 63.1% (LR), 64.3% (RF), and 62.9% (DT) utility. Randomizing using variance incurs only a 0.2% utility loss for Random Forest (64.1% vs. 64.3%) while randomizing 32% of resources over 15% of people, compared to a 0.6% loss for decision-boundary randomization. The partial BF lottery (k'=0.5k, n'=k) yields an expected utility difference of <0.05 across selection rates, yet produces a >20% reduction in Systemic Exclusion Rate (SER) when m>2 decision-makers are present. Across all claim distributions (Normal, Uniform, Pareto, etc.), the full BF lottery substantially reduces SER relative to deterministic top-k selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four allocation strategies: deterministic top-k (standard practice), decision-boundary randomization (partial BF lottery), variance-based randomization (using bootstrapped model disagreement), and outlier-based randomization (using conformal prediction p-values). Variance-based randomization achieves the smallest utility loss among the randomization methods: for Random Forest it loses only 0.2% utility (64.1% vs. 64.3% top-k) while randomizing 32% of resources, whereas decision-boundary randomization loses 0.6% (63.7%) at the same randomization rate. Outlier-based randomization (α=0.2) randomizes far fewer resources (1–3% of k) but over a larger fraction of the population (20% of n), yielding utility losses of 0.1–0.7% depending on model class. Decision-boundary randomization performs worst for Decision Trees (58.9% vs. 62.9% top-k, a 4% loss), while variance randomization narrows this gap to 1.4%. All randomization methods substantially reduce SER compared to deterministic top-k, with the variance method showing the best SER-vs-utility tradeoff for the Decision Tree model class.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper formalizes John Broome's (1990) philosophical framework of fairness through claims and weighted lotteries into an algorithmic allocation setting, extending the idea from Grgić-Hlača et al. (2017) of randomizing among ensemble models to the specific problem of fair scarce-resource allocation. It builds on Singh et al. (2021)'s posterior merit distribution by specifying *when* and *how* to incorporate randomness based on uncertainty, and operationalizes Cooper et al. (2023)'s bootstrapping and Angelopoulos & Bates (2021)'s conformal prediction as concrete randomization mechanisms. The key departure from the fair ML literature is the argument that the field's near-exclusive reliance on deterministic algorithms is itself a source of unfairness, particularly in multi-decision-maker ecosystems where correlated errors produce systemic exclusion and patterned inequality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This position paper challenges a foundational assumption in fair machine learning—that once bias is reduced, deterministic top-k selection is the natural and fair allocation rule—and provides implementable alternatives (partial BF lottery, variance-based randomization, outlier-based randomization) that achieve large reductions in systemic exclusion rate with minimal utility loss. Open questions include how to calibrate the utility-fairness tradeoff in practice, whether the multi-decision-maker SER framework generalizes to settings with heterogeneous decision-makers, and how to handle the 'calculation objection' (uncertainty in what constitutes a valid claim) without collapsing into arbitrary randomization. The paper explicitly scopes out domains like criminal justice where comparative claims do not apply, leaving open the boundary conditions for when randomization is appropriate.
