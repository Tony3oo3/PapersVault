## Problem

Static sparse training prunes networks at initialization with a fixed mask, but existing orthogonal initialization schemes either approximate isometry (suffering from accumulated errors at high sparsity) or restrict sparsity to discrete levels and specific architectures. No prior method simultaneously provides exact orthogonality, arbitrary per-layer densities, and compatibility with both fully-connected and convolutional layers.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241561/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a network architecture (fully-connected layers of size n×m or convolutional layers of shape c_out×c_in×(2k+1)×(2k+1)) and per-layer target densities d^l from any density distribution algorithm (ERK, SNIP, GraSP, Synflow, Uniform), EOI produces both the sparse orthogonal weight matrices and the binary masks in a single sampling step. For non-square FC matrices, it samples a row-orthogonal matrix by padding with zero columns or transposing. For convolutions, the sparse orthogonal matrix H^l is embedded at the kernel center (delta-orthogonal style), and a two-step mask process first prunes zeros of H^l then randomly un-prunes additional non-center entries to reach the target density d^l.

## Method

EOI constructs sparse orthogonal matrices by iteratively multiplying a running product A (initialized as I_n) by randomly sampled Givens rotation matrices G_n(i,j,φ), where (i,j) and φ are drawn uniformly at random, until the matrix density reaches the target d. Since Givens matrices are orthogonal, their product is exactly orthogonal, and each multiplication costs only O(n) time, making the process ~100× faster than the iterative orthogonality-loss optimization used by Approximated Isometry. The paper derives a closed-form recurrence (Observation 1) characterizing the expected density growth as a function of the number of rotations, confirming a sigmoidal curve that keeps the algorithm efficient in the highly-sparse regime (d ≤ 50%). For convolutional layers, EOI adapts delta-orthogonal initialization by embedding the sparse orthogonal H^l at the kernel center and then randomly activating additional non-center mask entries to match the target density, preserving the norm-preserving orthogonality property while distributing learnable parameters across the full kernel.

## Results

On 1000-layer vanilla networks (no residual connections or normalization) at 12.5% density, EOI achieves 96.11% on MNIST (Uniform-EOI MLP) and 79.60% on CIFAR-10 (SAO-EOI CNN), closely matching dense orthogonal baselines (97.51% and 80.71%), while Base initializations collapse to 11.35% and 10.00%. On standard architectures at 10% density (Table 2), EOI consistently yields the best accuracy within each density-distribution method, e.g., GraSP-EOI reaches 92.65% on VGG-16/CIFAR-10 versus 33.22% for GraSP-Base. On ImageNet, EOI provides a ~1% top-1 accuracy gain over ERK-Base on DeiT-III at 10% density.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EOI decisively outperforms Approximated Isometry (AI, Lee et al. 2019a) in high-sparsity regimes: on the 1000-layer CNN, AI achieves only 14.48–39.08% while EOI reaches 76.87–79.60%, a gap the authors attribute to AI's approximation errors and ill-specified convolutional orthogonality. Against SAO (Esguerra et al. 2023), EOI matches or slightly exceeds performance (97.43% vs 96.74% on MLP; 79.60% vs 78.80% on CNN) while offering arbitrary sparsity levels and no dimension constraints that SAO imposes. Compared to all density-distribution baselines (ERK, SNIP, GraSP, Synflow, Uniform) used in their standard 'Base' form, EOI transforms near-random performance (11.35% on 1000-layer MLP) into near-dense accuracy, with the largest gains at the highest sparsities. On practical architectures (Table 2), EOI typically wins within each density-distribution group, e.g., GraSP-EOI 92.65% vs GraSP-Base 33.22% on VGG-16/CIFAR-10, and SNIP-EOI 50.53% vs SNIP-Base 22.66% on EfficientNet/Tiny ImageNet. Against dynamic sparse training (RigL, Evci et al. 2020), EOI is slightly lower in final accuracy but within one standard deviation, while offering the simplicity of a fixed mask. EOI also outperforms the plain ERK-Base on ImageNet DeiT-III by approximately 1% top-1 accuracy at 10% density.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of dynamical isometry research (Saxe et al. 2013; Pennington et al. 2017) and static sparse training (Frankle & Carbin 2019; Lee et al. 2019b; Wang et al. 2020a; Tanaka et al. 2020). It directly extends two prior sparse-orthogonal initialization attempts: Lee et al. (2019a), which only approximates orthogonality via iterative optimization, and Esguerra et al. (2023, SAO), which achieves exact orthogonality but is restricted to discrete sparsity levels and specific layer dimensions. EOI departs from both by using compositions of random Givens rotations to sample sparse orthogonal matrices with arbitrary target densities, combining the exactness of SAO with the architectural flexibility of AI, while also adapting the delta-orthogonal construction (Xiao et al. 2018) to the sparse setting for convolutions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a compelling case that weight initialization is an underappreciated design axis in sparse training, independent of mask selection, and that exact (vs. approximate) orthogonality becomes critical in high-sparsity regimes where approximation errors accumulate over depth. Practically, EOI is a drop-in replacement for any density-distribution method (SNIP, GraSP, Synflow, ERK, Uniform) with negligible overhead (~100x faster than AI), making it broadly adoptable. Limitations include reduced benefit in bottleneck architectures (EfficientNet), potential inferiority to SAO for very large matrices (n > 2048), and evaluation restricted to image recognition. Open questions include applicability to NLP/transformer attention layers, integration with dynamic sparse training, and whether manipulating the Givens angle distribution or convolutional centre density can further improve results.
