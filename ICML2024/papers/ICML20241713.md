## Problem

Current AI alignment research focuses predominantly on intelligence (accuracy, capability) without a corresponding framework for socially appropriate 'reasonable' behavior. The paper identifies that the legal system's pervasive 'reasonable person standard'—a context-dependent benchmark for human conduct across contract, tort, and criminal law—has not been explicitly adopted as a design and evaluation target for AI models, leaving a gap in how we define, measure, and optimize for behavior that humans can understand, work around, and hold accountable.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical input the reasonable person standard as instantiated across three bodies of American law, drawing on specific illustrative cases (e.g., Wood v. Lucy, Vaughan v. Menlove, Muscarello v. United States) from each domain. It produces a set of qualitative research directions and evaluation criteria for AI practitioners on how to characterize, probe for, and stress-test reasonable behavior in models, rather than a specific algorithm, dataset, or benchmark.

## Method

The core contribution is a cross-domain theoretical mapping that traces how the reasonable person standard operates in contract interpretation (implied terms, implied intent to a reasonable observer, reasonable interpretation of promises, multiple equally reasonable meanings), tort liability (foreseeability of harm, consumer expectations test, reasonable alternative design), and criminal law (beyond a reasonable doubt, statutory interpretation, mens rea, police use of force). For each legal instantiation, the paper identifies the specific behavioral property at stake and translates it into a corresponding research question or evaluation criterion for AI models. The key insight is that intelligence and reasonableness are orthogonal axes—models can be highly intelligent yet unreasonable—and that the legal standard's qualitative, context-dependent nature calls for 'laws of qualitative structure' grounded in cognitive and social science rather than purely quantitative metrics. The paper further argues that interpretability must be accessible to laypersons to enable democratic challenge of AI decisions, and that geographic and cultural context must be incorporated to avoid imposing one jurisdiction's reasonableness norms on globally deployed models.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments, datasets, or numerical results. The paper's contribution is a conceptual argument: that the legal 'reasonable person standard' should serve as an explicit design and evaluation goal for AI systems. It illustrates this argument through six legal domains (contracts, torts, criminal law, product liability, police use of force, and statutory interpretation), drawing on illustrative cases such as Wood v. Lucy (1917), Embry v. McKittrick (1907), Vaughan v. Menlove (1837), and the 'flaming rat' case (United Novelty Co. v. Daniels, 1949) to show how reasonableness operates as a flexible, context-dependent standard.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare methods, models, or baselines empirically. It is a position paper that draws structural analogies between legal doctrines and AI alignment research. The closest thing to a 'comparison' is the contrast the author draws between current AI evaluation metrics (which focus on quantitative accuracy and error rates) and the proposed reasonableness-based evaluation (which focuses on the *type* and *interpretability* of errors). The paper also contrasts the objective reasonable person standard with subjective standards, arguing that AI models should be held to the same objective bar as human counterparts regardless of their known tendency toward erratic behavior, paralleling how Vaughan v. Menlove (1837) rejected a defendant's claim of insufficient intelligence as a defense against negligence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new conceptual ground by importing the American legal 'reasonable person standard' as an explicit technical goal for AI alignment and governance, rather than building on a specific prior AI method. It draws on the broader AI alignment literature (Christian, 2020; Amodei et al., 2016) and the hallucination/interpretability literature (Ji et al., 2023; Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017), but departs from the field's dominant quantitative-metrics paradigm by arguing that the *type* of mistake (reasonable vs. unreasonable) matters more than its frequency. The paper repurposes centuries of common-law jurisprudence—tort, contract, and criminal law—as a structured taxonomy of behavioral expectations that AI models should be probed and stress-tested against.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete gap: current AI evaluation focuses on accuracy and error rates but does not characterize whether deviations from expected behavior are 'reasonable' in the way a legal system would assess them, a distinction with direct product-liability and consumer-expectations implications. Open questions include how to operationalize and measure reasonableness across contexts, how to handle geographic and cultural variation in what counts as reasonable, and how to make AI decision-making transparent enough for laypersons to challenge. The framework is most actionable for governance and evaluation-methodology work, but the paper itself provides no empirical validation or technical method for implementing the standard in model training or testing.
