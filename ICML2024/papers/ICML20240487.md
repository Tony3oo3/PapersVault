## Problem

Despite the centrality of human-labeled data to every stage of the AI pipeline, AI/ML researchers collecting training data are largely untrained in data collection methods, and the AI literature has rarely drawn on the decades of survey methodology research documenting how question design, respondent characteristics, and task structure systematically degrade data quality. The authors argue that without incorporating these insights, biases in labeling—satisficing, anchoring, order effects, and selection bias—propagate into models, undermining their accuracy, fairness, and alignment with public interests.

## Input / Output

The labeling task as discussed presents labelers with observations (text prompts, RGB images, or model-generated responses) alongside closed answer choices, instructions, and sometimes pre-filled suggested labels, and collects structured outputs such as binary flags, multi-label selections, rankings of model responses, or bounding boxes. The paper emphasizes that the format of presentation—whether multiple labels are collected on a single screen versus separate screens, the sequential order of observations, the reading level and wording of instructions, and the availability of a 'don't know' option—systematically shifts the distribution and quality of the resulting labels, as demonstrated by the Kern et al. (2023) experiment where splitting labels across two screens increased annotation rates and improved downstream model performance.

## Method

This position paper constructs a theoretical bridge between the survey response process model (comprehension → retrieval → integration → mapping) and ML labeling tasks, deriving testable hypotheses about how each cognitive stage introduces specific biases (satisficing, acquiescence, straightlining, motivated misreporting) into training data. The authors formalize selection bias in labeling via a graphical model showing that labeler characteristics simultaneously drive both the propensity to participate in annotation work and the propensity to assign particular labels, creating a correlation that distorts model training—especially for opinion-based tasks like RLHF. Mitigation strategies are drawn directly from survey methodology: randomization of observation order to break order effects, paradata collection (response times, mouse movements) to detect low-quality annotations, cognitive interviewing to refine labeling instructions, statistical weighting to adjust for labeler demographics, and pre-labeling protocols designed to minimize anchoring bias. The paper closes with a call for transparency standards in label collection documentation, analogous to the AAPOR Transparency Initiative in polling, and for structured interdisciplinary collaboration between AI/ML and survey methodology communities.

## Results

This is a position paper without a main experimental results table. The key empirical evidence cited includes: (1) Kern et al. (2023) showed that splitting multi-label collection across separate screens (Condition B) led to higher rates of hate speech and offensive language annotation, and models trained on Condition B data outperformed those trained on single-screen data (Condition A) across several metrics; (2) Beck et al. (2022) found that providing a 'don't know' option to half of labelers resulted in fewer than 3% selecting it, with no impact on overall label distribution; (3) Al Kuwatly et al. (2020) and Perikleous et al. (2022) demonstrated that models trained on labels from different demographic groups (e.g., female vs. male, Asian vs. white labelers) make systematically different predictions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it synthesizes findings from the survey methodology literature and maps them onto AI data-collection practices. The closest empirical comparison is the Kern et al. (2023) experiment, where a single-screen multi-label interface (analogous to a select-all-that-apply survey question) was compared against a multi-screen yes/no format; the latter produced more annotations and yielded better-performing downstream models. The paper also contrasts pre-labeling (showing a suggested label for confirmation) against from-scratch labeling, noting that pre-labeling is more efficient but introduces anchoring bias. No head-to-head method comparisons or standard benchmark evaluations are presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not extend a specific prior method but rather imports an entire theoretical framework from survey methodology (response process models, satisficing theory, selection bias, context effects) into the data-centric AI literature. It builds conceptually on recent data-centric AI work (Mazumder et al., 2023; Plank, 2022) that established data quality as a first-order concern, and on emerging annotation-sensitivity studies (Kern et al., 2023; Beck et al., 2024) that empirically confirmed some of the predicted effects. The paper departs from the AI/ML norm of treating label collection as a straightforward engineering step, instead framing it as a social-science problem with well-studied failure modes, and joins prior calls for transparency in data documentation (Bender & Friedman, 2018; Gebru et al., 2021) with concrete survey-methodology prescriptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As AI alignment increasingly relies on opinion-based human feedback (RLHF), the data collection challenges converge with those of opinion polling, where decades of research have documented systematic biases. The paper identifies concrete open questions: whether survey-style weighting can correct for labeler demographics in training data, how active learning should account for order/context effects on annotators, and whether pre-labeling can be designed to reduce anchoring without sacrificing efficiency. A key limitation is that most hypotheses remain untested in the ML setting, and the paper acknowledges cultural and linguistic barriers between the survey methodology and AI communities that may slow knowledge transfer. The practical impact hinges on whether AI practitioners adopt the recommended transparency standards and mitigation measures.
