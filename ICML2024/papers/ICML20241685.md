## Problem

Different forms of inaccurate supervision (noisy labels, partial labels, crowdsourced annotations) have each been addressed by specialized algorithms, despite sharing the fundamental property that they all retain some correct labeling information. This paper asks whether a single unified framework can exploit this commonality to handle all such annotation forms without requiring paradigm-specific designs.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a training set where each instance is annotated by a logical vector l_i ∈ {0,1}^c: for noisy labels exactly one entry is 1 (possibly the wrong class), and for partial labels multiple entries are 1 (always including the correct class). The predictive model is a DNN with softmax output f: X → Δ^{c-1}, and the framework iteratively produces soft refined labels r_i ∈ [0,1]^c that blend the original annotation with model predictions via a moving-average update, ultimately yielding a hard class prediction for unobserved instances.

## Method

ULAREF alternates two operations each epoch: (1) global detection, which computes per-instance entropy scores, fits a 2-component GMM to the score distribution, and partitions instances into a reliable set (high GMM posterior) and an unreliable set using a linearly decreasing threshold; and (2) local enhancement, which initializes an enhanced model g from the predictive model f and fine-tunes g on the reliable set with a KL-divergence consistency loss that enforces agreement between g and f on the reliable set and between g and its mixup-interpolated neighborhood (nearest neighbors drawn from the unreliable set in feature space). Refined labels are then updated as a normalized moving average of the previous refined label and a mask-weighted blend of f and g outputs, with the mask assigning weight β to unreliable instances. A theoretical theorem under a cluster-property assumption on the error set and a consistency assumption on the reliable set guarantees that g achieves strictly lower error than f on the detected unreliable set.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with symmetric noisy labels at rate 0.5, ULAREF achieves 72.39% top-1 accuracy, outperforming the second-best PROMIX (71.64%) and DIVIDEMIX (71.44%). Under asymmetric noise on CIFAR-100, ULAREF reaches 76.11%, exceeding PROMIX (72.13%) by 3.76 percentage points. For partial label learning on CIFAR-100 (uniform, γ=0.1), ULAREF attains 67.93% accuracy versus VALEN's 65.27% and IDGP's 62.39%. On real-world datasets, ULAREF leads on Clothing1M (74.78%), Webvision (77.80%), and Yahoo!News (67.12%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

For noisy label learning, ULAREF achieves the best or second-best accuracy across all CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 settings, with the largest margin (3.76%) over PROMIX on CIFAR-100 asymmetric noise. It slightly trails DIVIDEMIX (95.69 vs 95.28) and ELR+ (94.37 vs 94.31) on CIFAR-10 symmetric noise at rates 0.2 and 0.5 respectively, but surpasses both on CIFAR-100 across all noise levels. Among NLL baselines, Co-teaching and AdaCorr are consistently outperformed by 5–20+ points on CIFAR-100, while Mixup and SOP show moderate gaps. For partial label learning, ULAREF dominates all eight PLL baselines (IDGP, PLCR, PICO, CAVL, LWS, VALEN, CC, PRODEN) on every CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 setting, with the largest advantage over IDGP on CIFAR-100 instance-dependent labels (66.45 vs 64.38) and over CAVL/LWS on high-flipping-rate settings where those methods degrade severely (e.g., CAVL drops to 52.93% at γ=0.7 on CIFAR-10 while ULAREF maintains 91.13%). On real-world datasets, ULAREF leads on Clothing1M (74.78% vs Dividemix 74.42%), Webvision (77.80% vs Dividemix 77.37%), and Yahoo!News (67.12% vs IDGP 66.62%), with comparable or slightly better results on Lost, BirdSong, MSRCv2, and Soccer Player.

## Relation to Prior Work

ULAREF unifies previously paradigm-specific approaches to inaccurate supervision (noisy label learning, partial label learning, crowdsourcing) under a single label refinement framework, motivated by the observation that all forms of inaccurate annotations retain some correct labeling information. The local enhancement step is explicitly framed as a variant of manifold regularization (Belkin et al., 2006) combined with mixup-style neighborhood construction (Zhang et al., 2017), while the global detection step adapts the GMM-based clean-sample identification from DivideMix (Li et al., 2020). The theoretical analysis extends the cluster-property framework from self-training theory (Wei et al., 2021a) to a different setting—proving that a locally fine-tuned enhanced model outperforms the predictive model on detected unreliable instances—rather than analyzing unlabeled data annotation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical path toward reducing the combinatorial explosion of specialized algorithms for each annotation form, and the theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) provides a principled justification for why local enhancement should improve label reliability. However, the unification is partial: paradigm-specific adaptations are still required (e.g., restricting refined labels to candidate sets in PLL), and the framework introduces additional complexity (two models, GMM fitting per epoch, fine-tuning schedule) that may limit adoption. Open questions include whether the approach extends to other forms of inaccurate supervision (e.g., multi-annotator crowdsourcing with structured confusion matrices) and whether the cluster-property assumption holds in high-dimensional or long-tail settings.
