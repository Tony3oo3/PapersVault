## Problem

Classical positional encodings for graph transformers—such as Laplacian eigenvectors and relative random walk probabilities—cannot distinguish certain graph structures, including non-isomorphic strongly regular graphs from the same family, which provably require 3-WL or higher to separate. This paper asks whether quantum-derived features, which naturally encode long-range topological correlations through Hamiltonian evolution, can serve as more expressive positional encodings that close this gap.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241959/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph G(V,E) with optional node features and produces pairwise positional encoding matrices P ∈ R^{N×N×K} (for K time steps or layers) that are concatenated to node features before feeding into a graph transformer. Two families of encodings are generated: (1) eigenvectors of the quantum correlation matrix ⟨Z_i Z_j⟩ computed on the graph's ground state, and (2) k-particle quantum random walk transition probabilities X^{(k)}(t)_{ij} = |⟨j|e^{-iH_k^{XY}t}|i⟩|². For graphs up to ~200 nodes, these are computed via classical simulation of the Hamiltonian; for larger graphs, the approach is designed to be executed on a quantum processor with O(1/ε²) shots for precision ε.

## Method

The core idea is to map graph topology onto a quantum Hamiltonian (Ising H_I = Σ Z_iZ_j or XY H^{XY} = Σ X_iX_j + Y_iY_j) and extract positional encodings from the resulting quantum state's correlators, decoupling quantum feature computation from classical model training. Two concrete PE schemes are proposed: (a) eigenvectors of the ground-state correlation matrix C_{ij} = ⟨Z_iZ_j⟩, used analogously to Laplacian eigenvectors but with highest-eigenvalue modes, and (b) k-particle quantum random walks (1-CQRW and 2-QiRW) where the XY Hamiltonian restricted to the k-particle subspace defines a walk on the k-occupation graph, yielding transition probabilities that capture interference effects absent in classical walks. The quantum features are computed once (statically or with fixed parameters) and then concatenated to node features in standard graph transformer architectures like GRIT, GINE, or GatedGCN, which are trained end-to-end with classical optimizers. Theoretically, the paper proves that 1-QW with localized states is strictly more expressive than 1-WL (bounded by 2-WL), and empirically demonstrates that 2-QW and 2-layer Ising evolutions distinguish non-isomorphic SRGs that GD-WL with RRWP cannot separate.

## Results

On the five standard benchmarks (Table 1), GRIT with 2-QiRW achieves the best or near-best results: 0.059 MAE on ZINC, 98.20% accuracy on MNIST, 76.44% on CIFAR-10, 90.17% on PATTERN, and 79.78% on CLUSTER. On large-scale datasets, quantum features (2-QiRW) consistently improve all tested models (GINE, GatedGCN, GRIT) on ZINC-full and several models on PCQM4Mv2. On a synthetic binary classification task designed to be hard for classical encodings, the quantum correlation-matrix encoding achieves 100% test accuracy with only ~4k parameters, while all classical baselines (LE, RRWP) plateau at 45% accuracy regardless of model size (10k–500k parameters).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GIN and GatedGCN, the quantum-enhanced GRIT variants dominate across all five benchmarks by large margins (e.g., 0.059 vs. 0.526 MAE on ZINC; 98.2% vs. 96.5% on MNIST). Compared to EGT and GPS, the quantum PEs yield consistent improvements of 1–4 percentage points on accuracy tasks and 0.01–0.05 MAE reduction on ZINC. The most informative comparison is against plain GRIT (RRWP-only): GRIT 2-QiRW matches or slightly exceeds GRIT on ZINC (0.059 vs. 0.060 MAE), MNIST (98.20% vs. 98.16%), and CIFAR-10 (76.44% vs. 76.20%), while GRIT 1-CQRW achieves the best ZINC MAE (0.058). On PATTERN and CLUSTER, plain GRIT retains a small edge (90.41% vs. 90.17%; 79.86% vs. 79.78%), so the quantum encodings are competitive but not strictly superior there. On the large-scale ZINC-full dataset, 2-QiRW improves every model tested (GINE, GatedGCN, GRIT) over their RRWP-only counterparts, and on the synthetic task the quantum correlation encoding achieves 100% accuracy versus 45% for all classical PE baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hybrid quantum-classical graph learning line initiated by Thabet et al. (2022), which replaced the attention matrix in graph transformers with quantum correlation matrices. The key departure is architectural: rather than jointly training quantum and classical parameters (which suffers from barren plateaus and expensive quantum gradients), the authors compute quantum features—correlations from Ising/XY Hamiltonian evolutions or k-particle quantum walk probabilities—as static positional encodings concatenated to node features in existing graph transformers like GRIT. This cleanly separates the quantum computation (a one-time feature extraction) from classical training, making the approach compatible with any GNN architecture and scalable to large datasets. The theoretical analysis builds on the WL-test expressiveness hierarchy, positioning k-QW between k-WL and k-δ-LWL, and extends the SRG distinguishability results of Zhang et al. (2023) to quantum features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical evidence that quantum-derived positional encodings are strictly more expressive than RRWP on strongly regular graphs, a class where classical spectral and walk-based methods provably fail. However, the empirical gains on real benchmarks (ZINC, PCQM4Mv2) are modest—typically 0.001–0.01 MAE improvements—and the most dramatic result (100% vs. 45% accuracy) is on a synthetic dataset specifically designed to favor quantum features. The central open question is whether truly quantum-inaccessible features (requiring >20 qubits or multi-layer Ising evolutions) would yield substantially larger gains, which remains untestable until neutral-atom or other analog quantum hardware becomes widely accessible. The approach also inherits the quadratic cost of graph transformers, limiting applicability to very large graphs.
