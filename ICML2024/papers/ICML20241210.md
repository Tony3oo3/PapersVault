## Problem

Deep learning models for accelerated MRI suffer significant performance degradation under natural distribution-shifts (different scanners, anatomies, contrasts, or field strengths), yet prior work has focused primarily on improving in-distribution accuracy rather than understanding how training data composition affects robustness. This paper asks whether training a single model on a diverse collection of data distributions can match or exceed the robustness of models trained on the best single distribution, without sacrificing in-distribution performance.

## Input / Output

The models take 4-fold undersampled multi-coil k-space measurements (Cartesian undersampling with 8% central lines fully sampled, remaining lines equidistantly sampled with random offset) and produce a reconstructed 2D MRI image. During training, a different random undersampling mask is drawn per mini-batch; during evaluation, one mask is fixed per volume. The U-net and ViT operate on zero-filled RSS reconstructions normalized to zero-mean/unit-variance, while the VarNet operates directly on the full k-space with sensitivity-map estimation. All models are trained end-to-end to maximize SSIM against the fully-sampled RSS ground truth.

## Method

This is an empirical study rather than a new architecture: the authors systematically train U-nets, ViTs, and end-to-end VarNets on individual vs. combined data distributions (varying anatomy, contrast, field strength, and scanner) to isolate the effect of training data diversity on out-of-distribution robustness. They introduce 'effective robustness' (out-of-distribution performance beyond what is predicted by in-distribution performance) as a model-agnostic metric and show that diverse training consistently shifts models above the baseline robustness line. A key mechanistic finding is that robustness gains strongly correlate with CLIP-feature-space nearest-neighbor similarity between training and test sets, suggesting diversity works by reducing the effective distribution gap. The authors also identify 'distributional overfitting'—a regime where prolonged training improves in-distribution SSIM while sharply degrading out-of-distribution performance—motivating early stopping as a practical robustness intervention. Finally, they train a single model on 13 diverse datasets (413k slices spanning 7 vendors, multiple field strengths, and anatomies) and demonstrate that it nearly closes the distribution-shift gap to models trained directly on out-of-distribution data, while maintaining in-distribution performance on fastMRI.

## Results

For the best-performing architecture (end-to-end VarNet), training on the diverse 13-dataset collection D_P (413k slices) achieves LPIPS of 0.173 on CC-359 sagittal, 0.198 on NYU, 0.228 on Stanford 2D, and 0.269 on M4Raw GRE, nearly closing the gap to the ideal model trained directly on each test distribution (0.161, 0.193, 0.226, 0.219 respectively). In contrast, the fastMRI-knee-trained VarNet scores 0.306 on CC-359 sagittal and 0.344 on M4Raw GRE, while the fastMRI-brain-trained VarNet scores 0.251 and 0.273. On in-distribution fastMRI data, the diverse model matches the single-distribution baselines (e.g., 0.210 LPIPS on fastMRI knee vs. 0.209 for the knee-trained model), confirming no in-distribution compromise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three training strategies across three architectures (U-net, ViT, VarNet): (1) models trained on fastMRI knee only, (2) models trained on fastMRI brain only, and (3) models trained on the diverse 13-dataset collection D_P, with a fourth reference of models trained directly on each test distribution (the ideal upper bound). The diverse D_P model consistently outperforms both fastMRI baselines on all four out-of-distribution datasets across all architectures. For VarNet on CC-359 sagittal, D_P achieves 0.173 LPIPS versus 0.306 (fastMRI knee) and 0.251 (fastMRI brain), approaching the ideal 0.161. On M4Raw GRE, D_P scores 0.269 versus 0.344 and 0.273 for the fastMRI baselines. Critically, the D_P model maintains in-distribution performance on fastMRI data (e.g., 0.210 LPIPS on fastMRI knee vs. 0.209 for the knee-trained model), confirming that diversity does not sacrifice in-distribution accuracy. The paper also shows that fine-tuning the diverse model on a single OOD dataset improves performance on that dataset but degrades performance on all other OOD datasets, reinforcing the benefit of maintaining a single diverse model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the computer vision finding that training data diversity drives model robustness (Fang et al., 2022; Nguyen et al., 2022) to the domain of accelerated MRI reconstruction, where prior work (Knoll et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 2021; Darestani et al., 2021) had identified distribution-shift sensitivity but proposed only transfer learning or test-time training as mitigations. It departs from those approaches by studying a setting without access to test-distribution data, and from architecture-specific solutions (Liu et al., 2021; Ouyang et al., 2023) by showing the effect is architecture-agnostic across U-nets, ViTs, and VarNets. The concept of 'distributional overfitting' connects to Wortsman et al. (2022) on CLIP fine-tuning but is here shown to arise during standard training from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication is significant for MRI reconstruction: a single model trained on a diverse pool of scanners, anatomies, contrasts, and field strengths can match or exceed the best single-distribution model out-of-distribution without sacrificing in-distribution performance, simplifying deployment across hospitals. The finding that healthy-subject training suffices for pathology reconstruction has direct clinical relevance. However, the distribution-shift gap is not fully closed, collecting truly diverse multi-vendor datasets remains expensive, and the study is limited to 2D Cartesian MRI at 4-fold acceleration. Open questions include whether further scaling of diversity progressively closes the gap, whether results extend to 3D reconstruction or non-Cartesian sampling, and whether the pathology generalization finding holds for rarer or more structurally novel pathologies.
