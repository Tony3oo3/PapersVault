## Problem

Inferring latent network structures from observed game actions in network games is challenging because the interaction network is often unobservable due to privacy or cost constraints, and the type of strategic interaction (complement vs. substitute) is unknown. Existing methods either assume specific utility function forms, require supervised or semi-supervised signals (node labels or partial edges), or cannot simultaneously handle both strategic complement and substitute regimes with a single model.

## Input / Output

The method takes an N×M matrix of observed equilibrium actions (one column per game, one row per individual) from M games sharing the same latent network, and produces a binary N×N adjacency matrix, a binary interaction-type label (strategic complement or substitute), and continuous N×D user embeddings. The model is permutation-invariant to user ordering, and while actions are assumed to satisfy Nash equilibrium conditions, the framework performs well empirically even when this assumption is violated (as in real-world data).

## Method

GPGVAE is a graph VAE whose key innovation is a data-dependent gated prior over the adjacency matrix: a binary latent variable C gates between a correlation prior (edge probability proportional to action similarity) and an anticorrelation prior (edge probability proportional to action dissimilarity), with the gate determined by a spectral GNN encoder (Specformer) that reads the eigendecomposition of the action covariance matrix. The network structure is encoded by a Transformer-based mixture of Bernoulli distributions (K components) that captures edge dependencies, and actions are decoded by a two-layer GCN. Training uses a two-stage strategy: the spectral interaction encoder is first pretrained on synthetic network games with known interaction types, then frozen while the full ELBO is optimized. The paper systematically compares three Monte Carlo gradient estimators (pathwise/Gumbel-Softmax, score function with EMA control variate, and measure-valued) for the discrete adjacency variable, finding the score function estimator offers the best performance-to-computation tradeoff.

## Results

On real-world datasets, GPGVAE achieves the highest AUC on 6 out of 7 evaluation settings, with particularly strong gains on Yelp (75.35–76.70 AUC vs. 70.47–73.62 for the best baseline LinQuadOpt) and Foursquare (57.57 AUC on New York vs. 56.13 for Graphical Lasso). On the Indian Villages dataset, GPGVAE (56.77 AUC) is marginally below Graphical Lasso (56.91 AUC), likely due to the limited 10 games per graph. On synthetic BH graphical games, GPGVAE consistently outperforms all baselines across BA, WS, and ER graphs (e.g., 74.45 AUC on WS vs. 74.12 for Graphical Lasso). On linear quadratic and linear influence games, GPGVAE maintains high AUC across the full range of β (−0.8 to 0.8), while optimization-based baselines degrade significantly under strategic substitutes (β < 0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPGVAE outperforms all 10 baselines on 6 of 7 real-world evaluation settings, with the sole exception being Indian Villages where Graphical Lasso edges it by 0.14 AUC (56.91 vs. 56.77). The largest margins are on Yelp: GPGVAE beats LinQuadOpt (the strongest game-theoretic baseline) by 1.73–4.78 AUC across the four Yelp sub-datasets, and beats Graphical Lasso by 3.68–8.57 AUC. Among learning-based baselines, VGAE and VGCN perform near-random (~50–55 AUC) on most datasets, confirming that non-informative priors severely limit graph VAEs in this setting. LDS and NodeFormer, which were adapted from semi-supervised node classification, collapse to ~50 AUC on Yelp, indicating their graph-learning components are ineffective without node labels. On synthetic BH graphical games (Table 2), GPGVAE wins on all three graph types (BA, WS, ER), with the closest margin being 0.33 AUC on WS graphs. On linear quadratic games with strategic substitutes (β < 0), GPGVAE's advantage over LinQuadOpt grows as β approaches −0.8, where optimization-based methods struggle most.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Graph VAE framework (Kipf & Welling, 2016) to the specific problem of inferring latent network structures from equilibrium actions in network games, departing from prior optimization-based methods (Leng et al., 2020; Barik & Honorio, 2019) that require assuming a specific utility function form, and from supervised/semi-supervised approaches (Rossi et al., 2022; Elinas et al., 2020) that need partial network information. The key architectural departure is the data-dependent gated prior that switches between correlation and anticorrelation edge priors based on a latent interaction type, combined with a Specformer-based spectral GNN encoder (Bo et al., 2023) that classifies the interaction type from covariance eigenvalue patterns. The stage-wise training strategy—pretraining the interaction encoder on synthetic games before joint VAE training—addresses a practical challenge of training VAEs with mixed continuous and discrete latent variables that prior graph VAE work has not systematically addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first fully unsupervised framework that simultaneously infers both the network topology and the type of strategic interaction (complement vs. substitute) without requiring knowledge of the utility function form, which is a significant practical advantage in settings where the game structure is unknown. The finding that simple data-dependent priors (Pearson correlation/anticorrelation) substantially outperform uniform priors, and that the dominant prior is predictable from spectral patterns of the action covariance, offers an interpretable and lightweight mechanism for prior selection. Limitations include the Nash equilibrium assumption, restriction to undirected graphs with continuous actions, and O(N²K) computational complexity that limits scalability. Open questions include extending to dynamic networks, discrete actions, and more expressive game classes beyond the three linear/quadratic forms studied here.
