## Problem

Most existing extreme classification (XC) methods operate solely on document text, ignoring rich auxiliary information (e.g., clicked webpages, related queries, categories) that is readily available in real-world applications like sponsored search and query recommendation. This is particularly costly for short-text documents (3–5 word titles) where the sparse textual representation provides limited signal for disambiguation. No prior XC method systematically leverages such auxiliary data to enrich document representations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a document (often a short 3–5 word title) and a set of K auxiliary knowledge pieces (AKPs) such as Wikipedia categories, hyperlinked page titles, related user queries, or clicked webpage titles. At test time, AKPs are not given directly; instead, a trained linker module retrieves the top-K relevant AKPs from a pool of hundreds of thousands to millions. The output is a ranked list of relevant labels from an extremely large label space (up to ~800K labels), retrieved via approximate nearest neighbor search over per-label classifiers, with end-to-end inference latency under 10 ms.

## Method

OAK introduces an Augmentation Block comprising a DistilBERT encoder, a trainable AK Bank (a lookup table of D-dimensional embeddings, one per AKP), and a cross-attention Combiner that fuses the document embedding with retrieved AKP embeddings into an enriched representation. Training proceeds in three stages: (1) a linker module is trained as a standalone XC task to predict relevant AKPs for documents; (2) the Augmentation Block is trained in a Siamese fashion using a combined triplet loss (maximizing mutual information between enriched and base document representations with label embeddings) plus a novel MI calibration loss inspired by DPO that penalizes the enriched representation for being less confident than the base on positive pairs and more confident on negative pairs; (3) the Augmentation Block is frozen and per-label refinement vectors are learned to produce final classifiers. The key design insight is that AKPs are represented by dedicated trainable embeddings rather than being encoded by the shared encoder, which increases model capacity, avoids intent dilution from noisy AKPs (the cross-attention learns to down-weight them), and keeps inference overhead under 2 ms per document.

## Results

On the four public Wikipedia XC benchmarks, OAK achieves P@1 of 33.71 (LF-WikiSeeAlsoTitles-320K), 44.82 (LF-WikiTitles-500K), 48.57 (LF-WikiSeeAlso-320K), and 85.23 (LF-Wikipedia-500K), outperforming the strongest Siamese baseline NGAME by 1.07–5.78 points in P@1. On LF-ORCAS-800K, OAK reaches 75.25 P@1 versus 72.47 for ANCE. On the proprietary SponsoredSearch-150M dataset, OAK achieves 68.80 Recall@100, exceeding the best production dense retriever (M1) by 7.82 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OAK outperforms NGAME (Dahiya et al., WSDM 2023), the closest Siamese XC competitor, by 1.07–5.78 P@1 points across the four Wikipedia datasets (e.g., 44.82 vs. 39.04 on LF-WikiTitles-500K). Against ANCE (Xiong et al., ICLR 2021), OAK gains 2.92–15.31 P@1 points, with the largest gap on LF-WikiTitles-500K (44.82 vs. 29.68). Graph-based baselines are substantially outperformed: OAK exceeds GraphFormers (Yang et al., NeurIPS 2021) by 11.77–54.13 P@1 points and GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al., 2018) by 10.59–52.70 P@1 points, despite the graph methods being given ground-truth document-AKP linkages at both train and test time. OAK also beats PINA (Chien et al., ICML 2023), the only prior XC method using auxiliary instance-correlation signals, by 4.03 P@1 on LF-WikiSeeAlso-320K and 2.40 P@1 on LF-Wikipedia-500K. On LF-ORCAS-800K, OAK surpasses ANCE by 2.78 P@1 (75.25 vs. 72.47). Finally, when the OAK augmentation block is attached to DPR (Karpukhin et al., EMNLP 2020), it improves P@1 from 26.62 to 28.91 on LF-WikiSeeAlsoTitles-320K, confirming the framework's encoder-agnostic design.

## Relation to Prior Work

OAK builds directly on the NGAME Siamese extreme classification framework, reusing its encoder and per-label refinement training pipeline, but introduces a novel Augmentation Block (encoder + trainable AK Bank + cross-attention combiner) that enriches document representations with auxiliary knowledge pieces. It departs from prior XC methods (ANCE, NGAME, PINA) that ignore auxiliary data, and from GNN-based approaches (GraphFormers, GraphSAGE) that aggregate document-AKP graph structure at high storage/compute cost, by instead learning compact trainable embeddings for AKPs and fusing them via attention. The calibration loss is conceptually inspired by DPO-style preference optimization and multimodal calibration, adapted here to enforce that the AK-enriched representation is at least as informative as the base encoder representation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

OAK is the first XC framework to systematically and efficiently leverage auxiliary knowledge, and its modular design (swappable encoder, any auxiliary source, plug-in augmentation block) makes it broadly applicable to existing Siamese XC pipelines. The production deployment on Bing's sponsored search with statistically significant CTR and keyword-density gains demonstrates real-world impact beyond academic benchmarks. Open questions include how the approach scales when the AKP pool grows to hundreds of millions, whether the linker can be replaced by a learned retrieval module for cold-start documents, and whether the AK Bank concept transfers to multimodal or non-textual auxiliary signals.
