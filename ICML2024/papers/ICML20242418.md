## Problem

Minimax excess risk optimization (MERO) replaces raw risk with excess risk in group DRO to suppress the dominance of high-noise distributions, but the unknown minimal risk R_i* for each distribution makes the resulting minimax problem extremely challenging. The only existing algorithm (Agarwal & Zhang, 2022) requires solving a full empirical risk minimization subproblem at every iteration, which is computationally prohibitive for large sample sizes. This paper develops efficient stochastic approximation approaches that optimize MERO directly without such subproblem solves.

## Input / Output

The method consumes one sample per distribution per round (m samples total per iteration in the balanced setting), with the loss function assumed bounded in [0,1] and gradients bounded in dual norm. In the imbalanced setting, each distribution P_i has its own sample budget n_i, and the algorithm allocates n_i/2 samples to estimating the minimal risk and n_i/4 to the saddle-point optimization phase. The output is a model parameter vector w in the hypothesis class W and a weight vector q on the (m-1)-simplex, with the anytime variant returning a valid solution at every iteration t.

## Method

The key insight is to cast MERO as a stochastic convex-concave saddle-point problem and maintain m parallel instances of stochastic mirror descent (SMD) that each minimize the risk R_i(·) of one distribution. At each round, the weighted-average iterates from these m SMD instances provide estimates of the minimal risks R_i*, which are used to construct a biased stochastic gradient for the q-update (the concave side). The paper proves this bias is under-control via a novel analysis of SMD with biased gradients, yielding a near-optimal O~(√(log m / t)) convergence rate. For the imbalanced setting, the method introduces a weighted MERO formulation with distribution-specific weights p_i and solves the resulting approximate problem via stochastic mirror-prox with minibatches of size n_i/n_m, achieving distribution-dependent excess risk rates of O((log m)/√n_i).

## Results

On the synthetic dataset, MERO (Algorithm 1) achieves a maximal excess risk (MER) of 0.05 in 255.0 seconds, compared to 1470.5 seconds for E-MERO (Agarwal & Zhang, 2022), a 5.7× speedup. On the Adult dataset, MERO reaches MER 0.03 in 20.4 seconds versus 141.6 seconds for E-MERO, a 6.9× speedup. The anytime stochastic approach also outperforms the multi-stage approach (MS-MERO) when the number of iterations exceeds the preset value, as MS-MERO stagnates or slightly degrades while MERO continues to decrease MER.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against E-MERO (Agarwal & Zhang, 2022), MERO achieves the same MER targets (0.05 on synthetic, 0.03 on Adult) but is 5.7× faster on the synthetic dataset and 6.9× faster on the Adult dataset, while also being more memory-efficient since it does not require storing training data. Against GDRO (Zhang et al., 2023), the comparison is more nuanced because the two methods optimize different objectives: on the synthetic dataset with 6 distributions, MERO achieves lower individual risk on 4 out of 6 distributions, while GDRO performs better on the 2 distributions characterized by high noise levels (P₅ and P₆), since GDRO targets raw risk and concentrates effort on high-risk groups. On the Adult dataset, GDRO shows slightly better performance on distributions P₁, P₃, and P₅ with large risk, while MERO provides more balanced performance across distributions by mitigating the impact of heterogeneous noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper develops efficient stochastic approximation algorithms for the minimax excess risk optimization (MERO) formulation introduced by Agarwal & Zhang (2022), which replaces raw risk with excess risk in group DRO to suppress heterogeneous noise. The prior work only provided a computationally prohibitive empirical algorithm requiring ERM in each iteration; here, the authors cast MERO as a stochastic convex-concave saddle-point problem and solve it via stochastic mirror descent, building directly on the SMD framework of Nemirovski et al. (2009) and the weighted GDRO approach of Zhang et al. (2023). The key technical departure is handling the biased stochastic gradient arising from the unknown minimal risk R_i*, which the authors show is sufficiently controlled to preserve near-optimal convergence rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work makes MERO practically tractable for the first time, eliminating the need to solve ERM subproblems at each iteration and enabling anytime solutions at any round. The weighted MERO formulation with distribution-dependent convergence rates addresses the real-world scenario of imbalanced sample budgets across groups, a setting increasingly relevant in fairness-aware and multi-site learning. Limitations include the requirement to maintain m additional models (higher memory/compute cost), the restriction to convex risk functions, and evaluation only on small-scale tabular and synthetic data. Future work on extending these techniques to empirical MERO with finite-sum structure and to non-convex settings would broaden applicability.
