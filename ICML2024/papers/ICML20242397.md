## Problem

Predictive coding (PC), a leading computational neuroscience framework for perception, has failed to produce deep generative models competitive with standard variational autoencoders on complex image datasets. The standard PC inference procedure relies on a Dirac-delta (point-mass) approximate posterior, which is too restrictive to capture posterior uncertainty, and no prior PC-based algorithm has matched VAE performance while remaining computationally tractable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242397/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The generative model operates on RGB images at 64x64 (CelebA) or 32x32 (SVHN, CIFAR-10) with a 40-dimensional latent space. At sampling time, a latent vector drawn from a standard isotropic Gaussian prior is passed through a convolutional decoder to produce a generated image. During training, the warm-start encoder consumes the observed image and outputs a Gaussian (mean and softplus-parameterised variance) over the latent space, which initialises the Langevin chain for each data point.

## Method

Langevin Predictive Coding (LPC) reframes standard PC inference as an unadjusted overdamped Langevin sampler by injecting appropriately scaled Gaussian noise (sqrt(2*gamma)*eta) into each gradient-ascent iteration on the log joint, so that the iterates approximate samples from the true posterior. These (biased) Langevin samples are then used to compute Monte Carlo gradients of a tight ELBO with respect to model parameters, making the learning step computationally identical to standard PC parameter updates. An amortised warm-start encoder network is trained to initialise the chain within the typical set, optimised via forward KL, reverse KL, or Jeffrey's divergence (the latter performing best). A lightweight diagonal preconditioner, computed as a debiased running second moment of the drift gradient (analogous to Adam's second-moment estimate), is applied to both the drift and noise terms to increase robustness to the Langevin step size, drawing on Riemannian Langevin and adaptive SGD ideas.

## Results

On SVHN, LPC (15 epochs) achieves a FID of 39.64, outperforming VAE at both 15 epochs (53.88) and 50 epochs (44.76). On CIFAR-10, LPC reaches FID 113.29 versus 183.21 (VAE 15 epochs) and 145.87 (VAE 50 epochs). On CelebA, VAE still leads on FID (82.09 at 50 epochs vs. 97.49 for LPC), but LPC substantially outperforms on density (0.54 vs. 0.16) and coverage (0.13 vs. 0.12). LPC also achieves higher density on CIFAR-10 (0.63 vs. 0.14) and coverage on CIFAR-10 (0.13 vs. 0.06) compared to the 50-epoch VAE.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard VAE trained with the same architecture, optimizer, and batch size, compared at both 15 and 50 epochs. LPC (15 epochs) beats VAE (50 epochs) on FID for SVHN (39.64 vs. 44.76) and CIFAR-10 (113.29 vs. 145.87), demonstrating that LPC converges to better sample quality in roughly one-third the SGD iterations. On CelebA, VAE retains a FID advantage (82.09 vs. 97.49 at 50 epochs), but LPC dominates on density (0.54 vs. 0.16) and slightly edges out on coverage (0.13 vs. 0.12). On SVHN density, VAE (50 epochs) leads (0.65 vs. 0.33), and on SVHN coverage VAE also leads (0.48 vs. 0.42). Overall, LPC matches or exceeds VAE on the majority of metrics while requiring 3× fewer training epochs, with the clearest wins on CIFAR-10 FID/density/coverage and CelebA density.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads: the predictive coding (PC) framework from computational neuroscience (Rao & Ballard, 1999), the ELBO-based variational autoencoder (Kingma & Welling, 2014), and gradient-based MCMC sampling via the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (Roberts & Tweedie, 1996). The key conceptual move is recognizing that standard PC inference—gradient ascent on the log joint—becomes an unadjusted Langevin sampler upon injection of appropriately scaled Gaussian noise, thereby reframing PC as an amortized Langevin method for optimizing a tight ELBO. The adaptive preconditioning draws on Riemannian Langevin dynamics (Girolami & Calderhead, 2011) and the Adam optimizer's second-moment computation, while the amortized warm-start network and its three training objectives (forward KL, reverse KL, Jeffrey's divergence) extend the encoder role in VAEs. The paper positions itself against concurrent MCMC-for-generative-models work (Hoffman 2017; Taniguchi et al. 2022; Dong & Wu 2023) by grounding the approach in PC's neurobiological plausibility and by introducing the warm-start and preconditioning components those works lack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a minimal modification to predictive coding—adding Gaussian noise—yields a generative model training algorithm that matches or exceeds VAE performance in FID, density, and coverage while converging in roughly one-third the SGD iterations, all while retaining the neurobiological interpretability of PC. This suggests that the brain's endogenous noise may serve a principled role in posterior exploration rather than being mere nuisance, offering a testable hypothesis for computational neuroscience. However, the 7–11× wall-clock slowdown relative to VAEs (due to sequential Langevin steps per SGD iteration) significantly limits immediate practical adoption, and the method has not yet been extended to hierarchical stochastic latents, discrete variables, or larger-scale datasets. Open questions include whether the approach scales to state-of-the-art VAE architectures, whether automatic convergence criteria can reduce the fixed 300-step budget, and whether the precision-weighted prediction-error-plus-noise dynamics are amenable to analog neuromorphic hardware.
