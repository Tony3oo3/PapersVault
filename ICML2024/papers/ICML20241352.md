## Problem

Existing MoE scaling laws (Clark et al., 2022) rely on two implicit assumptions—fixed training duration and constant expert size—that lead to the prediction that dense Transformers will eventually outperform MoE at scale. This paper identifies both assumptions as arbitrary and shows that relaxing them fundamentally changes the efficiency comparison, revealing that MoE models remain superior at every compute budget when training duration and expert size are jointly optimized.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241352/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a total FLOPs budget F, the method outputs the compute-optimal triple (N, D, G): total non-embedding parameters, number of training tokens, and granularity. The FLOPs constraint is not the simple 6·N·D formula used for dense models; it includes a routing overhead term proportional to d_model·R·G·c_r, which couples granularity to the depth-to-width ratio (assumed d_model = 64·n_blocks). The optimization reduces to a one-dimensional problem over G (with N and D determined by the FLOPs constraint), solved numerically via Brent's method rather than in closed form.

## Method

The paper introduces granularity G = d_ff / d_expert, which splits each standard expert into G smaller sub-experts while keeping the number of active parameters per token constant (a token is routed to G fine-grained experts instead of 1). The key theoretical contribution is a joint scaling law L(N,D,G) = c + (g/G^γ + a)/N^α + b/D^β, derived by arguing that the data-entropy constant c and the data-scaling term b/D^β are architecture-independent, while only the model-size coefficient depends on G through a power-law g/G^γ. The constant a prevents infinite granularity from reaching the dense-model lower bound. Fitted on 100+ experiments (129M–3.7B params, 16B–130B tokens, G ∈ {1,2,4,8,16}, R=64, Expert Choice routing, C4 dataset), the law achieves RMSE 0.015 and predicts that optimal G grows with compute budget (G=8 at ~10^19 FLOPs up to G=64 at ~10^25 FLOPs), and that the efficiency gap between MoE and dense Transformers widens monotonically with scale—contradicting the crossing-point prediction of Clark et al. that arises only under fixed-D extrapolation.

## Results

The paper derives a joint scaling law for fine-grained MoE (Eq. 9) with RMSE = 0.015, introducing granularity G as a new hyperparameter. The compute-optimal configurations (Table 2) show that at 10^20 FLOPs, a 64×1B MoE model (G=16, D=28.94B) achieves loss 2.491, while at 10^25 FLOPs, a 64×1T model (G=64, D=7.94T) reaches loss 1.367. The key headline finding is that MoE models are always more efficient than dense Transformers: at 10^20 FLOPs the compute savings are ~20×, exceeding 40× beyond 10^25 FLOPs. Optimal granularity increases with budget, ranging from G=8 at 10^18 FLOPs to G=64 at 10^25 FLOPs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Chinchilla dense Transformer scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022), the paper demonstrates that compute-optimal MoE models consistently achieve lower loss at every FLOP budget, with the efficiency gap widening as scale increases (from ~20× at 10^20 FLOPs to >40× at 10^25 FLOPs). The fitted coefficients confirm this: MoE has a smaller underfitting exponent (β=0.147 vs. 0.127 for dense), meaning MoE benefits more from additional training tokens, though it requires longer training to reach competitiveness. Against Clark et al. (2022), the paper directly refutes the claim that MoE becomes less efficient than dense beyond ~1T parameters. Appendix B shows that Clark et al.'s crossing point is an artifact of fixing D=130B; when D is allowed to scale optimally, the MoE/dense crossing point shifts from 251B (at D=10B) to 1.9T (at D=130B) to 10T (at D=1T), and the curves never cross under compute-optimal training. The paper concludes that Clark et al.'s prediction of dense superiority at large scale only holds in severely undertrained regimes, which are uncommon in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla scaling law framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) to Mixture-of-Experts architectures by introducing granularity (G) as a new architectural hyperparameter and deriving a joint scaling law over model size N, training tokens D, and G. It directly challenges Clark et al. (2022), whose unified scaling laws for routed models concluded that MoE becomes less efficient than dense Transformers beyond ~1T parameters; the authors show this conclusion is an artifact of fixing the training dataset size rather than a fundamental limitation. The architectural design builds on Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022) and Mixtral (Jiang et al., 2024), but the key departure is treating expert size as a free variable rather than mirroring the dense FFN dimension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that G=1 (standard MoE) is suboptimal across a broad range of compute budgets provides immediately actionable guidance for practitioners designing MoE models, and the compute-optimal tables give concrete hyperparameter recommendations. The paper's central claim—that MoE is always more efficient than dense Transformers when all hyperparameters are jointly optimized—has significant implications for long-term model architecture strategy, potentially invalidating the common narrative that dense models will eventually win at sufficient scale. Open questions include whether the scaling law holds at extreme granularity (G≥64) where routing overhead dominates, how the optimal G shifts with different expansion rates R, and whether the FLOPs-based cost model adequately captures hardware-dependent communication costs in distributed training.
