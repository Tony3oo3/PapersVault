## Problem

Dataset distillation produces compact synthetic datasets that train high-performing models, yet the information actually stored in distilled data remains largely unexplored. No prior work has investigated whether distilled data can serve as a true substitute for real data, what specific information the distillation process retains, or whether individual distilled data points carry meaningful semantic content beyond their class labels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242344/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on 32×32 RGB images from CIFAR-10 (10 distilled images per class, 100 total) alongside the full real training set, with additional experiments on CIFAR-100 and 64×64 TinyImageNet. The framework consumes trained model parameters (a 3-layer ConvNet or ResNet-18) and produces three types of diagnostic outputs: Hessian trace values computed via Hutchinson's stochastic estimator at each training iteration, leave-one-out influence scores measuring each distilled image's effect on every real test image's loss, and semantic attribute rankings derived from LLaVA-generated descriptions of highly-influenced test images. Notably, influence functions are repurposed from their standard role of explaining model decisions to instead explain what information individual data points encode.

## Method

The paper constructs a three-part empirical analysis framework. First, it demonstrates distilled data's limitations through an inverse pipeline (train on real, evaluate on distilled) and mixing experiments showing that adding real images to distilled data during training can decrease accuracy. Second, it identifies that distilled data captures early training dynamics through three complementary analyses: prediction agreement distributions showing distilled-trained models align with early-stopped models rather than subset-trained models, recognition timing showing a real-trained model's accuracy on distilled data plateaus by iteration 150 while real test accuracy continues improving, and Hessian curvature analysis revealing that BPTT and trajectory-matching distilled data induce a flat loss region within ~150 iterations. Third, it introduces an interpretable framework using exact leave-one-out retraining influence functions to rank real test images by each distilled image's impact, then leverages LLaVA to extract semantic attributes (e.g., 'yellow cars,' 'parking lots,' 'military aircraft') from the highly-influenced images, revealing that individual distilled points encode specific non-class semantic concepts.

## Results

This is an analysis paper rather than a method paper, so the key 'results' are findings about distilled data behavior. The central finding is that BPTT and trajectory matching distilled data induce a flat loss curvature region within ~150 iterations of training on real data, confirming they capture only early-training dynamics; additional training on these distilled images yields <2% accuracy improvement. In contrast, distribution matching and gradient matching distilled data induce persistently high/sharp curvature, and additional training on them actually *decreases* test accuracy by 5–7%. Mixing even 2–50 real images per class with 10 distilled images per class on CIFAR-10 can *decrease* classifier accuracy, showing distilled data is not a drop-in replacement for real data during training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper analyzes four distillation methods—BPTT (meta-model matching), distribution matching, gradient matching, and trajectory matching—plus two SOTA methods (IDC and FreD) in the appendix. BPTT and trajectory matching are the strongest performers: their distilled data is most recognizable by real-trained models (near 100% accuracy by iteration 150), shows the clearest flat-curvature signature consistent with early-stopping, and individual distilled points carry the most interpretable semantic content (e.g., 'yellow cars,' 'parking lots'). Distribution matching and gradient matching distill less information: their recognition accuracy plateaus at only 70–80%, their loss curvature remains high and erratic, and additional training on them *hurts* accuracy by 5–7%. All four methods exhibit the same critical limitation—mixing distilled data with real data during training degrades performance—and the SOTA methods IDC and FreD show identical mixing and early-stopping-agreement patterns, confirming the findings are not method-specific.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first systematic empirical analysis of what information is actually stored in distilled data, explicitly noting that prior work (Vicol et al. 2022 on implicit bias, Schirrmeister et al. 2022 on regularization, Maalouf et al. 2023 on kernel ridge regression theory) has not addressed the post-distillation information content. It builds on four representative distillation methods (BPTT, distribution matching, gradient matching, trajectory matching) and repurposes influence functions—originally designed to interpret model predictions (Koh & Liang 2017)—to instead interpret individual distilled data points. The Hessian curvature analysis via Hutchinson's method and PyHessian provides a mechanistic lens that connects optimization dynamics to information content, a tool previously applied to model training but not to distilled data characterization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that distilled data is functionally equivalent to early-stopped models reframes dataset distillation from 'compressing a dataset' to 'compressing early training dynamics,' which has direct implications for how future distillation methods should be designed and evaluated. The practical warning that mixing real and distilled data during training can degrade performance (validated across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet, and SOTA methods) is an important caveat for practitioners who might naively augment training sets. Open questions include whether these findings hold at ImageNet scale with larger models, how dataset biases propagate through the distillation process (acknowledged in the Impact Statement but not addressed), and whether the influence-function framework can be extended to multi-modal or larger-scale distilled datasets.
