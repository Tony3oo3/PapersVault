## Problem

Existing symbolic regression methods face a fundamental trade-off: RL-based and GP-based approaches search expression trees in an exponentially growing functional space, making them struggle with high-dimensional problems and constant optimization, while transformer-based methods rely on large-scale pre-training and fail to generalize to unseen variables or out-of-distribution data. The paper asks whether one can reduce the search space from individual expression trees to the space of symbolic network architectures, thereby retaining expressive power while enabling scalable, adaptable SR.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular dataset of feature-target pairs (X, y) with X ∈ R^n and y ∈ R, and produces a compact closed-form mathematical expression that best fits the data. The symbolic network is constrained to at most 5 layers and 6 neurons per layer, and after training, weights below a threshold (0.01) are pruned to zero, yielding a sparse expression. Constants in the final expression are refined via BFGS nonlinear optimization, and the output expression is converted to a readable form using SymPy.

## Method

DYSMNET introduces a two-level architecture: a controller RNN autoregressively samples symbolic network structures (number of layers, operators per layer, and operator types) from predefined libraries, and each sampled network is a fully-connected feed-forward network whose units are algebraic operators (unary: Id, sin, cos, exp; binary: +, −, ×, ÷). Training proceeds in two stages—first pure MSE loss, then MSE plus a smoothed L0.5* regularizer to sparsify weights—followed by hard pruning and BFGS constant refinement. The controller is optimized via a risk-seeking policy gradient (maximizing the top-ε fraction of rewards) augmented with an entropy term for exploration, where the reward is 1/(1+MSE) of the BFGS-refined expression. An adaptive gradient clipping strategy, using a moving-average window of gradient norms, stabilizes the symbolic network training.

## Results

On the 11 benchmark datasets (8 standard with d≤2 and 3 from SRBench with d≥2), DYSYMNET achieves the highest average R² across nearly all problems, reaching perfect R²=1.0000 on both Jin and Koza. On the high-dimensional SRBench, DYSYMNET shows the largest margins: 0.9931 on Feynman (vs. 0.9806 for uDSR), 0.9968 on Strogatz (vs. 0.9455 for uDSR), and 0.8908 on Black-box (vs. 0.6697 for uDSR). The only benchmark where DYSYMNET is outperformed is R rationals (0.9895 vs. 0.9999 for uDSR and NGGP). In the free-falling ball experiment, DYSYMNET achieves an average test MSE of 0.1736, outperforming three physics-derived baseline models (2.2698, 71.94, and 1.5902 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DYSYMNET outperforms all four baselines on 10 out of 11 benchmark datasets, with the sole exception being R rationals where uDSR and NGGP achieve 0.9999 versus DYSYMNET's 0.9895. The most dramatic wins occur on the high-dimensional SRBench: on Black-box (122 problems, no ground truth), DYSYMNET achieves R²=0.8908 versus 0.6697 (uDSR), 0.6086 (NGGP), 0.4226 (NeSymReS), and 0.4528 (EQL) — a gap of over 22 points over the next-best method. On Feynman (119 physics equations), DYSYMNET leads uDSR by 1.25 points (0.9931 vs. 0.9806) and NGGP by 7.4 points. Among the baselines, uDSR is consistently the strongest competitor on standard benchmarks, while NeSymReS (a transformer-based method) degrades severely on problems with constants (Nguyen*: 0.3523) and high-dimensional inputs (Black-box: 0.4226), confirming its limited generalization. EQL, which uses a fixed network architecture, shows the weakest overall performance, particularly on multi-variable and constant-containing problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

DYSMNET occupies a middle ground between two dominant SR paradigms: tree-search methods (GP, DSR, NGGP) that explore the vast space of expression trees, and inductive transformer methods (NeSymReS, SymbolicGPT) that pre-train on synthetic data. It inherits the symbolic-network representation from EQL (Sahoo et al., 2018), where elementary functions serve as activation units in a fully-connected network, but replaces EQL's fixed architecture with an RNN controller that autoregressively samples network structures via risk-seeking policy gradients (Petersen et al., 2020). The L0.5* smoothed regularization for weight sparsification is adapted from Wu et al. (2014), and the BFGS constant-refinement step follows standard practice in the SR literature. The key conceptual departure is reframing the search problem: rather than generating expression trees token-by-token or evolving populations of trees, DYSMNET searches over a much smaller space of network topologies, then trains each candidate end-to-end.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine bottleneck in SR: tree-based methods struggle with high-dimensional inputs and constant optimization due to combinatorial explosion, while transformer-based methods suffer from distribution shift and cannot generalize to unseen variable counts. DYSMNET's dynamic architecture approach sidesteps both issues, and the notably large gap on the Black-box dataset (R²=0.89 vs. 0.67 for the next-best method) suggests the search-space reduction is particularly impactful when no ground-truth expression is available. Limitations include the reliance on a fixed operator library, the two-stage training protocol (MSE then regularization) that may not always yield the most parsimonious expression, and the fact that discovered expressions (e.g., for the bowling ball) can still be complex. Future work on more principled pruning and extending the operator set would strengthen the approach.
