## Problem

Contrastive self-supervised learning methods decompose their loss into uniformity and alignment components, while non-contrastive methods (SimSiam, BYOL, Barlow Twins, MEC) only optimize uniformity. The maximum entropy coding (MEC) framework unifies these non-contrastive losses but does not explicitly differentiate feature matrices from different branches, making it impossible to directly incorporate an alignment term. The paper asks whether a single framework can harmonize the strengths of both paradigms.

## Input / Output

For image pre-training, the method takes a batch of B RGB images, applies two independent random augmentations (cropping, color jitter, grayscale, flip, blur, polarization) to produce two views, and passes them through a Siamese ResNet50 encoder with MLP projectors to yield l2-normalized feature matrices Z1, Z2 ∈ R^{2048×B}. The loss operates on the centered cross-covariance C(Z1,Z2) and auto-covariance matrices C(Z1,Z1), C(Z2,Z2) rather than individual vectors. For LLM fine-tuning, the method takes token sequences and constructs positive semi-definite matrices P^(k), Q^(k) ∈ R^{d×d} by weighting LM-head embedding outer products with target and predicted token distributions at each position k.

## Method

The key theoretical contribution (Theorem 4.1) proves that the total coding rate (TCR) loss underlying MEC is equivalent, up to constant factors, to a matrix cross-entropy (MCE) between a regularized covariance matrix and a scaled identity matrix. This reframing enables Matrix-SSL, which combines a Matrix-Uniformity loss (MCE between (1/d)I_d and the centered cross-covariance C(Z1,Z2)) with a novel Matrix-Alignment loss (−tr(C(Z1,Z2)) + γ·MCE(C(Z1,Z1), C(Z2,Z2))) that directly aligns the auto-covariance matrices of the two branches. A stop-gradient on the target branch makes the alignment term equivalent to a matrix KL divergence, partially explaining the effectiveness of stop-gradient in SimSiam/BYOL. The paper further derives a closed-form relationship between effective rank and matrix KL divergence (Proposition 6.1), showing that effective rank provides an exact metric for dimensional collapse. For LLMs, the method augments standard cross-entropy with an MCE term over distribution-weighted embedding matrices to capture synonym and polysemy structure in token representations.

## Results

On ImageNet linear evaluation with ResNet-50, Matrix-SSL achieves 71.1% top-1 accuracy after 100 epochs of pre-training, outperforming the strongest baseline MEC (70.6%) by 0.5% and SimCLR (66.5%) by 4.6%. At 400 epochs, Matrix-SSL reaches 73.6%, matching MEC (73.5%) while surpassing BYOL (73.2%) and all other baselines. For MS-COCO transfer learning (400-epoch pre-training vs. 800 for baselines), Matrix-SSL achieves 41.0 AP on object detection and 35.6 AP on instance segmentation, exceeding the next-best MEC by 1.2 and 0.9 AP respectively. For LLM fine-tuning, the matrix cross-entropy loss yields 72.3% on GSM8K, a 3.1% improvement over standard cross-entropy (69.2%) on the same Llemma-7B backbone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Matrix-SSL consistently outperforms all 13 baselines across the primary evaluation settings. On ImageNet linear probing (100 epochs), it beats SimCLR by 4.6%, MoCo v2 by 3.7%, BYOL by 4.6%, SwAV by 4.6%, SimSiam by 3.0%, Barlow Twins by 3.8%, VICReg by 2.5%, and MEC by 0.5%. On MS-COCO object detection (41.0 AP vs. MEC's 39.8, SimSiam's 39.2, MoCo v2's 39.3, BYOL's 37.9), Matrix-SSL leads by 1.2 AP over the closest competitor MEC, and on instance segmentation (35.6 AP vs. MEC's 34.7, SimSiam's 34.4) it leads by 0.9 AP. Notably, Matrix-SSL achieves these gains with only 400 pre-training epochs versus 800 for the COCO baselines. In the LLM setting, the matrix cross-entropy loss improves GSM8K accuracy by 3.1% over standard CE on Llemma-7B (72.3% vs. 69.2%) and by 5.8% over MetaMath-7B (66.5%), while matching or slightly exceeding Minerva 62B (52.4%) and WizardMath 7B (54.9%) on GSM8K. The method does not report results for all baselines at every epoch (e.g., VICReg only at 100 epochs), and the semi-supervised learning comparison (Table 4) shows Matrix-SSL matching MEC at 10% labels (63.94% vs. 63.92% Acc@1) while leading at 1% labels (45.16% vs. 44.44% Acc@1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Maximum Entropy Coding (MEC) framework of Liu et al. (2022) by recasting its total coding rate loss as a matrix cross-entropy (MCE) uniformity loss via a closed-form equivalence (Theorem 4.1), and then augments it with a matrix alignment loss that directly aligns auto-covariance matrices across branches. Conceptually, it bridges the well-known alignment+uniformity decomposition of contrastive learning (Wang & Isola, 2020) with the uniformity-only structure of non-contrastive methods, arguing that the missing alignment component can be naturally supplied through matrix information-theoretic quantities (matrix KL, MCE) rather than element-wise vector losses. The effective rank–matrix KL identity (Proposition 6.1) further connects this framework to the dimensional collapse literature (Zhuo et al., 2023) and neural collapse (Papyan et al., 2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified matrix information-theoretic lens offers a principled way to design and analyze SSL losses beyond ad-hoc combinations of alignment and uniformity terms, and the effective rank as an exact collapse metric is a clean diagnostic tool for practitioners. However, the empirical gains over MEC are modest (≈0.5–1.2% on linear probing, ≈1.2 AP on COCO detection), and the LLM extension—while conceptually appealing—rests on a single 7B model and one primary benchmark (GSM8K), leaving open whether matrix cross-entropy generalizes to larger models or other reasoning tasks. A key open question is whether the matrix alignment loss provides complementary signal at scale (e.g., ViT backbones, larger batches) or whether its benefit saturates, and whether the Taylor-approximation of the matrix logarithm (order 4) remains stable in high-dimensional regimes.
