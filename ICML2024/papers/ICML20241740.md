## Problem

Existing models of bacterial community dynamics, such as generalized Lotka-Volterra equations, require time-series data for every species combination, cannot generalize to unseen bacteria, and are limited to pairwise interactions. This paper asks whether community composition at steady state can be predicted directly from bacterial genomes, enabling generalization to new species and larger community structures without additional experiments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241740/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each bacterium is represented as a binary feature vector encoding the presence/absence of KEGG Orthology gene groups (2,798 dimensions for FRIEDMAN2017, 3,169 for BARANWALCLARK2022), with ubiquitous annotations removed. The community is treated as a fully connected graph over these node attributes; for fixed-length MLP baselines, null vectors are appended for absent species. The model outputs a per-node sigmoid-activated scalar giving the predicted relative abundance of each bacterium at equilibrium, regressed against experimentally observed steady-state profiles.

## Method

The core contribution is casting community composition prediction as node-level regression on a fully connected graph, where each node is a bacterium and edges encode potential interactions. Two GNN architectures are used: GraphSAGE (spatial convolution with mean aggregation) and MPGNN (message-passing with MLP edge/node update functions), both exploiting the set structure of communities to enforce permutation equivariance. Rather than fitting differential equation parameters, the model learns dynamics implicitly by directly regressing steady-state relative abundances, eliminating the need for growth curves. k message-passing steps propagate k-hop information, capturing higher-order interactions, while edge functions can learn to suppress information from irrelevant neighbors. Training uses Adam with MSE loss and a linear-sigmoid readout per node; the fully connected topology is chosen because no prior interaction structure is assumed.

## Results

On in-distribution predictions, the best ensemble GNN models (MPGNN/GraphSAGE) achieve R² = 0.81 on FRIEDMAN2017 and R² = 0.77 on BARANWALCLARK2022, while MLPs fail to maintain accuracy under input shuffling, confirming their lack of permutation equivariance. For generalization to larger communities, accuracy drops to R² = 0.55 (FRIEDMAN2017, 2–3 → 7–8 bacteria) and R² = 0.26–0.45 (BARANWALCLARK2022, 2–15/22 → 23–26 bacteria). Generalization to unseen bacteria yields R² = 0.55–0.58 for some species (Ea, Sm on FRIEDMAN2017) but R² < 0 for others (Pch), indicating that genome–phenotype mismatches limit transfer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed MPGNN and GraphSAGE architectures substantially outperform both GATv2 and GCNII on both datasets. On FRIEDMAN2017, the best GATv2 achieves R² = 0.03 and the best GCNII R² = 0.06, both far below the proposed methods' R² = 0.81, indicating that attention-based and deep-convolution GNNs struggle with the small, noisy FRIEDMAN2017 data. On BARANWALCLARK2022, GCNII with 2 convolution layers and 100 hidden features reaches R² = 0.71, approaching but still trailing the proposed methods (R² = 0.77), while GATv2 degrades to negative R² (−0.13 to −0.16) with 2 layers, suggesting overfitting. The MLP baseline, while competitive in-distribution with fixed input order, completely fails under permutation (R² drops sharply), confirming that the set-structure inductive bias of GNNs is essential for correct community modeling. Overall, the message-passing architectures (MPGNN, GraphSAGE) are the most robust across both datasets and generalization settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant generalized Lotka-Volterra (gLV) framework for modeling bacterial community dynamics, which requires time-series data, fits parameters per bacterium, and is limited to pairwise interactions. It also moves beyond recent neural network approaches (e.g., Baranwal et al. 2022's RNNs) that still rely on growth trajectories and cannot generalize to unseen species. The key conceptual contribution is repurposing GNNs—specifically GraphSAGE and a variant of Kipf et al.'s (2020) message-passing architecture—with permutation equivariance as an inductive bias matching the set structure of communities, and using them to implicitly learn dynamics by directly regressing steady-state abundances from genome-encoded features (KEGG Orthology presence/absence vectors), thereby eliminating the need for growth curves entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that genome features alone can support prediction of steady-state community composition with generalization to unseen bacteria and larger community sizes, opening a path toward anticipating the effects of novel species (e.g., pathogens) on microbiomes without additional experiments. However, results are mixed: generalization to keystone-like species (e.g., Anaerostipes caccae) and to smaller communities remains poor, and the simulation framework only captures pairwise interactions, leaving higher-order effects unaddressed. Open questions include whether richer genomic representations (beyond KO presence/absence), environmental covariates, or interpretability tools for GNNs can close the gap for the hardest-to-predict species, and whether the approach scales to the thousands of species in natural microbiomes.
