## Problem

Semi-implicit variational inference (SIVI-SM) avoids biased ELBO surrogates by using a Fisher-divergence-based minimax objective, but requires an additional lower-level optimization over a neural-network-parameterized function, introducing computational overhead, training instability, and extra hyperparameter tuning. This paper asks whether the lower-level problem can be eliminated entirely while retaining the unbiased, score-based training signal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240326/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tractable target score function s_p(x) = ∇log p(x|D), a mixing distribution q(z) (typically standard Gaussian), and a diagonal Gaussian conditional layer q_φ(x|z) = N(μ(z;φ), diag{σ²(z;φ)}) whose mean is a neural network and whose standard deviation is a constant vector. It outputs the trained variational parameters φ* from which posterior samples are drawn via the reparameterization x = μ(z;φ) + σ(z;φ)⊙ξ with ξ~N(0,I). The kernel width is set dynamically to the median pairwise distance of variational samples.

## Method

KSIVI restricts the lower-level function f in SIVI-SM's minimax objective to a vector-valued RKHS, where the optimal f* admits the closed-form f*(x) = E_{q_φ(y)} k(x,y)[s_p(y) − s_{q_φ}(y)]. Substituting f* back reduces the upper-level problem to minimizing the squared kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD). The critical tractability step exploits the semi-implicit hierarchy: the intractable marginal score s_{q_φ}(y) is replaced by the tractable conditional score s_{q_φ(·|z)}(y) = −ξ/σ(z;φ), so the KSD becomes a Monte Carlo estimable quantity over joint samples (x,z) ~ q_φ(x,z). Two unbiased stochastic gradient estimators (vanilla two-batch and U-statistic single-batch) are derived, both amenable to the reparameterization trick. The paper further establishes L_φ-smoothness of the KSD objective and a 1/N variance bound on the gradient estimators under mild assumptions on the kernel, target log-density, and neural network, yielding a convergence guarantee to an ε-stationary point in O(1/ε²) iterations.

## Results

On Bayesian neural networks across six UCI datasets, KSIVI achieves the best test RMSE on 4/6 datasets (e.g., 2.555 on BOSTON, 5.750 on CONCRETE) and best test NLL on 4/6 datasets (e.g., 2.506 on BOSTON, 3.309 on CONCRETE), outperforming SIVI, SIVI-SM, and SGLD. On the 100-dimensional conditioned diffusion process, KSIVI (vanilla) achieves the lowest sliced Wasserstein distance (0.0115) compared to SIVI (0.0981), SIVI-SM (0.0640), and UIVI (0.0426). Training time per 10,000 iterations at d=100 is 90.48s for KSIVI versus 128.13s for SIVI-SM and 88.12s for SIVI, showing comparable efficiency to SIVI while avoiding its biased surrogate ELBO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SIVI-SM (Yu & Zhang, 2023), KSIVI matches or slightly outperforms on BNN tasks (best RMSE on 4/6 datasets) while being more stable in training and eliminating the need for lower-level optimization over a neural network f_ψ. On the conditioned diffusion process, KSIVI achieves substantially lower sliced Wasserstein distances than SIVI-SM (0.0115 vs 0.0640 at d=100) and trains ~30% faster. Against SIVI (Yin & Zhou, 2018), KSIVI provides better posterior uncertainty estimates (SIVI severely underestimates variance on the diffusion task) and better pairwise correlation coefficients on Bayesian logistic regression, though SIVI is marginally faster. Against SGLD (Welling & Teh, 2011), KSIVI outperforms on 5/6 BNN datasets for both RMSE and NLL. Against particle-based methods MIED (Li et al., 2023) and KSDD (Korba et al., 2021) on heavy-tailed distributions, KSIVI with the Riesz kernel achieves Wasserstein distance 0.0451 vs 0.0366 (MIED) and 0.1974 (KSDD) at edge width 5, showing clear advantage over KSDD. Against UIVI (Titsias & Ruiz, 2019), KSIVI achieves lower sliced Wasserstein on the diffusion task at d=50 (0.0140 vs 0.0314) and d=100 (0.0115 vs 0.0426) without requiring expensive inner-loop MCMC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SIVI-SM (Yu & Zhang, 2023), which reformulated semi-implicit variational inference as a minimax score-matching problem but required training an auxiliary neural network for the lower-level optimization. By restricting the function space to an RKHS, the authors show the lower-level problem admits a closed-form solution, reducing the objective to the kernel Stein discrepancy (KSD) — a well-studied distributional distance from the Stein variational inference literature (Liu & Wang, 2016; Gorham & Mackey, 2017b). The approach is distinct from KSDD (Korba et al., 2021), which also uses KSD but via particle-based gradient flow requiring twice-derivatives of the kernel, whereas KSIVI only needs kernel function values, enabling less smooth kernels like Riesz.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Eliminating the adversarial lower-level optimization makes training more stable, computationally efficient, and free of the hyperparameter tuning headaches inherent in minimax training — a practical improvement for VI practitioners. The convergence guarantee to a stationary point is the first for full semi-implicit VI with nonlinear reparameterization, though it relies on strong smoothness assumptions on the neural network. Key open questions include whether KSD's non-convex landscape (even for location-scale families) leads to suboptimal stationary points in high dimensions, and whether unbounded kernels (e.g., Riesz) can mitigate the Gaussian kernel's fast-decaying tails in high-dimensional posteriors.
