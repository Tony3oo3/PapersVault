## Problem

While LLMs are well-calibrated, their token-level uncertainty conflates two fundamentally different sources: epistemic uncertainty (the model simply doesn't know the answer) and aleatoric uncertainty (the text is inherently ambiguous). Prior work on disentangling these has focused on structured settings like multiple-choice QA where a single ground-truth answer exists, leaving the far harder problem of identifying epistemic uncertainty in completely unconstrained, free-form text generation largely unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The supervised method takes intermediate token embeddings (typically from middle transformer layers) of a frozen small LLM as input and outputs a binary label per token: whether a larger reference model would be confident (near-zero entropy) or uncertain (entropy within the same band) at that position. Tokens are restricted to a narrow entropy band of the small model (e.g., [2,3) bits) to avoid trivial entropy-correlation shortcuts. The unsupervised ICLT method takes only the raw text prompt and the small model's top-k next-token predictions, constructs repeated prompts of the form ⟨orig-prompt⟩⟨candidate⟩⟨orig-prompt⟩ with document separators, and outputs the minimum entropy across the resulting re-predictions as a confidence proxy.

## Method

The supervised approach trains small linear or single-hidden-layer MLP probes on frozen embeddings from middle layers of the small model to predict whether a larger model's predictive entropy is near zero or within the same entropy band, with labels generated by running the large model on held-out text. A key design choice is training separate classifiers per entropy band and balancing classes by previous-token identity to prevent the probe from exploiting trivial correlations; the surprising finding is that even a linear probe on a single middle-layer embedding achieves AUC > 0.9 and transfers across domains (Wikipedia → code, multilingual, Q&A). The unsupervised In-Context Learning Test (ICLT) requires no training: for each top-k candidate token, it prepends the candidate to the original prompt (separated by a document separator token) and re-queries the small model, using the minimum resulting entropy as a signal. The core hypothesis is that models exhibit 'selective in-context learning'—they update their predictions when given a hint only if their uncertainty is epistemic (i.e., the information is learnable), while aleatoric uncertainty is unaffected by in-context hints, a behavior validated first in a synthetic toy task before being applied to real LLMs.

## Results

Linear probes trained on frozen small-model embeddings achieve AUC > 0.9 in-distribution (Wikipedia) across all model pairings: LLaMA 7B/30B (0.94), LLaMA 7B/65B (0.93), and Pythia 1.4B/12B (0.86 linear, 0.91 MLP). Out-of-distribution performance remains strong, with AUC 0.79–0.88 on Code, Stack Exchange, and EuroParl. The fully unsupervised ICLT method achieves AUC 0.68–0.71 (LLaMA 7B/30B and 7B/65B), substantially above the best entropy-threshold baseline (~0.55). Without the artificial gap, classifier accuracy drops to ~75% but still far exceeds all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its supervised probes against four baselines: Best Entropy Threshold (BET), BET after fine-tuning (BET-FT), Neural Net Ensemble (NNE), and Prediction from Initial Embeddings (PIE). Linear probes achieve AUC 0.86–0.94 in-distribution, outperforming BET (0.54–0.59) by 0.30–0.40 AUC points, BET-FT (0.63–0.75) by 0.11–0.31 points, and NNE (0.60–0.64) by 0.22–0.34 points. Out-of-distribution, the gap narrows but remains substantial: linear probes reach AUC 0.68–0.88 while BET stays near 0.51–0.61. The PIE baseline (AUC 0.54) confirms that the preceding token alone carries no useful signal. The unsupervised ICLT method (AUC 0.68–0.71) also clearly beats BET (~0.55) without any training, though it lags the supervised probes by 0.20–0.25 AUC points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the epistemic-vs-aleatoric uncertainty distinction—well-studied in Bayesian neural networks, ensembles, and epinets (Osband et al., 2021; Hüllermeier & Waegeman, 2021)—to the setting of free-form text generation by LLMs, where prior work (Kadavath et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2022) was confined to structured question-answering with a single correct answer. It repurposes the in-context learning literature (Si et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023) in a novel way: rather than using ICL to extract or inject knowledge, it measures how *willing* a model is to update from context as a proxy for whether its uncertainty is epistemic. The use of a larger model as a proxy for the ground-truth distribution, validated by scaling laws, is a practical workaround for the absence of true token-level uncertainty labels in open-ended text.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation—identifying tokens where a model's uncertainty is due to missing knowledge rather than inherent ambiguity—is directly relevant to reducing hallucinations in LLM deployments, and the finding that simple linear probes on frozen embeddings achieve AUC > 0.9 and transfer across domains (Wikipedia → code) suggests this signal is robust and cheap to extract. Key open questions remain: whether the 'large model as ground truth' assumption holds at frontier scale, how to handle mixed epistemic-aleatoric uncertainty and sequence-level effects, and whether the unsupervised ICLT method (which fails on Pythia) can be made reliable without any large-model access. The paper is explicitly preliminary, and the gap between the supervised (AUC ~0.9) and unsupervised (AUC ~0.7) results, plus the Pythia ICLT failure, indicate substantial engineering and conceptual work before these techniques are deployable.
