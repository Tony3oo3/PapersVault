## Problem

Prior work on state-adversarial MDPs (SA-MDP) noted the potential non-existence of an optimal robust policy (ORP), forcing existing methods to trade off robustness against natural performance via heuristics without theoretical guarantees. This paper asks two fundamental questions: (1) does a deterministic stationary ORP actually exist, and if so, is it the Bellman optimal policy? (2) Why do standard DRL algorithms that target the Bellman optimal policy still fail to achieve adversarial robustness in practice?

## Input / Output

The agent consumes 84×84 grayscale Atari frames (pixel values normalized to [0,1]) as state observations, with an adversarial perturbation budget ε (set to 1/255 in experiments) defining the threat model. The Q-network outputs Q-values for all discrete actions in the finite action space, from which a greedy or ε-greedy policy selects the action. Unusually, the training loss involves a supremum over all adversarial perturbations s_ν within the ε-ball around each observed state, making the objective a bilevel optimization where the inner max is solved via PGD or interval bound propagation (IBP).

## Method

The paper proves that under a Consistency Assumption of Policy (CAP)—that optimal actions remain unchanged under ε-perturbations—the Bellman optimal policy is exactly the ORP, and that minimizing Bellman error in L^∞ norm (rather than the L^1 norm used by standard DQN) is necessary for adversarial robustness. CAR-DQN operationalizes this by training a Double Dueling DQN with a surrogate Bellman Infinity-error loss: a soft weighted sum over a batch where each sample's weight is proportional to its adversarial TD-error magnitude (maximized over s_ν ∈ B_ε(s)), controlled by a softness coefficient λ. The inner adversarial maximization is solved with either 10-step PGD or cheap IBP convex relaxation, and the target network is updated periodically. This consistent objective ensures the agent learns optimal actions in both natural and adversarial directions simultaneously, avoiding the performance-robustness trade-off of prior regularization-based methods.

## Results

On the most challenging RoadRunner environment, CAR-DQN (PGD) achieves 49,700 natural reward and 43,286 PGD-attack reward, outperforming SA-DQN (PGD) by ~110% on robust reward (43,286 vs. 20,482). CAR-DQN (IBP) attains 49,398 natural and 49,456 PGD reward with 0.760 ACR, matching or exceeding RADIAL-DQN (46,224/45,990) and WocaR-DQN (43,686/45,636). On BankHeist, CAR-DQN (PGD) reaches 1,307.0 natural and 1,243.2 PGD reward versus SA-DQN (PGD)'s 1,248.8/965.8. On Pong and Freeway, CAR-DQN matches or slightly exceeds all baselines across natural and robust metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SA-DQN (PGD), CAR-DQN (PGD) achieves dramatically higher robust rewards on RoadRunner (43,286 vs. 20,482, a 110% improvement) and BankHeist (1,243.2 vs. 965.8), while also matching natural performance. Against SA-DQN (IBP), CAR-DQN (IBP) wins on natural reward for BankHeist (1,349.6 vs. 1,236.0) and RoadRunner (49,398 vs. 46,372), and on PGD reward for RoadRunner (49,456 vs. 44,960), though it trails on ACR for RoadRunner (0.760 vs. 0.819). Against RADIAL-DQN, CAR-DQN (IBP) achieves higher natural and PGD rewards on RoadRunner (49,398/49,456 vs. 46,224/45,990) and BankHeist (1,349.6/1,347.6 vs. 1,341.8/1,341.8), while RADIAL-DQN retains a slight edge on ACR for RoadRunner (0.994 vs. 0.760). Against WocaR-DQN, CAR-DQN (IBP) dominates on RoadRunner natural reward (49,398 vs. 43,686) and PGD reward (49,456 vs. 45,636). On the simpler Pong and Freeway games, all methods converge to similar performance, with CAR-DQN matching or marginally exceeding baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the SA-MDP framework of Zhang et al. (2020), which introduced the state-adversarial paradigm and highlighted the potential non-existence of an optimal robust policy (ORP), motivating subsequent methods (SA-DQN, RADIAL-DQN, WocaR-DQN) to seek trade-offs between robustness and natural performance via KL regularization, adversarial loss, or worst-case value estimation. The key departure is theoretical: under a Consistency Assumption of Policy (CAP), the authors prove that the ORP not only exists but coincides exactly with the Bellman optimal policy, eliminating the need for separate robustness objectives. The algorithmic contribution (CAR-DQN) repurposes the standard Bellman optimality equation but replaces the conventional L1 Bellman error with a surrogate of the L-infinity Bellman error, identifying the norm choice as the critical factor for robustness rather than the objective structure itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central insight—that the Bellman optimal policy is itself the optimal robust policy under CAP, and that L-infinity norm is the necessary and sufficient condition for robustness—reframes adversarial robustness in DRL from a separate optimization problem to a norm-selection problem within standard Q-learning. This has practical implications: robustness need not come at the cost of natural performance, and existing DRL architectures can be made robust by changing the loss norm rather than adding regularization or adversarial training loops. Limitations include the restriction to discrete action spaces and value-based methods, the reliance on CAP (which, while empirically near-universal, is not guaranteed in all environments), and evaluation limited to four Atari games. Open questions include whether the L-infinity norm insight extends to policy-based methods, continuous action spaces, and multi-agent settings.
