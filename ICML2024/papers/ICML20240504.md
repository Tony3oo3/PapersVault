## Problem

Existing LLM alignment methods such as DPO and PPO implicitly encode human decision-making biases from prospect theory, yet this connection has never been formalized, leaving it unclear whether the success of these methods stems from the preference data or from inductive biases in the loss function itself. The paper asks whether one can directly optimize for human utility (as modeled by Kahneman-Tversky) rather than maximizing the log-likelihood of paired preferences, and whether a simple binary desirable/undesirable signal is sufficient for alignment.

## Input / Output

KTO consumes a text prompt x paired with a single output y labeled as either desirable or undesirable, rather than requiring paired preferences (y_w ≻ y_l). This allows naturally unpaired training data and extreme class imbalance (up to 90% fewer desirable examples), handled by adjusting loss-aversion weights λ_D and λ_U so that the effective ratio λ_D·n_D / (λ_U·n_U) stays in [1, 4/3]. The output is an aligned language model producing text generations conditioned on the prompt, evaluated via GPT-4-judged winrates and generative benchmarks.

## Method

KTO derives a loss from the Kahneman-Tversky value function, replacing the numerically unstable power function with a logistic σ and parameterizing risk aversion via β and loss aversion via weights λ_D/λ_U. The loss L_KTO = E[λ_y − v(x,y)] applies the value function to the reward r_θ(x,y) = log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) relative to a reference point z_0, a biased batch estimate of the expected KL divergence between π_θ and π_ref. This reference point is the key mechanism: a blunt increase in reward for a desirable example also raises the KL penalty, forcing the model to learn what specifically makes an output good rather than uniformly inflating probabilities. The paper proves DPO and PPO-Clip are also HALOs (human-aware losses), shows theoretically that KTO ignores noisy or intransitive feedback and resolves contradictory preferences toward the majority-preferred output, and empirically demonstrates that KTO matches or exceeds DPO from 1B to 30B parameters using only binary feedback, even without a prior SFT stage at larger scales.

## Results

On Zephyr-β-SFT aligned on UltraFeedback, KTO achieves 53.5 exact-match on GSM8K (vs. 40.0 for DPO, a 13.5-point gain), 52.6 on BigBench-Hard (vs. 44.1 for DPO), 58.6 on MMLU, and 12.5% winrate on AlpacaEval 2 (vs. 7.8% for DPO). KTO matches or exceeds DPO across all model scales from 1B to 30B parameters in GPT-4-judged winrate evaluations. Notably, KTO can discard up to 90% of desirable examples while still outperforming DPO, and KTO alone (without SFT) matches SFT+DPO for Llama-13B and 30B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KTO decisively outperforms DPO on GSM8K (53.5 vs. 40.0, +13.5 points) and BigBench-Hard (52.6 vs. 44.1, +8.5 points), while matching or slightly exceeding it on MMLU (58.6 vs. 58.2) and HumanEval (30.9 vs. 30.1); the one exception is TydiQA where KTO trails DPO (31.2 vs. 36.5). Against ORPO, KTO wins on every metric except TydiQA (31.2 vs. 38.5), with the largest gap on GSM8K (+17.0 points). In the Mistral-7B OpenAssistant experiment (Table 2), KTO with only one output per input (reducing data by 72%) still achieves a 0.631 winrate over the SFT target, exceeding both DPO (0.600) and the official Mistral-7B-Instruct (0.621). In human evaluation (Appendix D), KTO achieves 72.9% winrate vs. DPO's 62.1% (p < 0.05), a larger gap than the GPT-4-judged evaluation (65.2% vs. 60.0%). The paper also shows that an offline PPO variant with dummy +1/−1 rewards matches DPO at most scales, suggesting the inductive bias in the loss function matters more than the reward signal itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes LLM alignment through the lens of Kahneman-Tversky prospect theory, introducing the HALO (Human-Aware Loss) framework that formalizes when a loss function implicitly models human decision-making biases such as loss aversion and diminishing sensitivity. It shows that DPO and PPO-Clip are already HALOs (Theorem 3.5), then departs from the preference-likelihood paradigm of DPO by deriving KTO directly from the Kahneman-Tversky value function, replacing the Bradley-Terry preference model with a binary desirable/undesirable signal. The key conceptual departure is that alignment need not maximize the log-likelihood of pairwise preferences; instead, one can directly maximize the prospect-theoretic utility of individual generations, which requires only a binary label per output.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

KTO's ability to match or exceed DPO with a strictly weaker signal (binary vs. preference pairs) has direct practical implications: binary feedback is more abundant, cheaper, and faster to collect, lowering the barrier to scaling alignment in production. The HALO framework also provides a principled lens for designing future alignment losses, and the paper's central caveat—that no single HALO is universally superior and the best loss depends on the inductive biases appropriate to the setting—shifts the conversation from 'which loss is best' to 'which inductive biases fit this data and task.' Open questions include what value functions best capture how humans perceive text quality (as opposed to monetary gambles), how to handle individual variation in utility, and how to extend HALOs to online alignment, other modalities, and score-based feedback.
