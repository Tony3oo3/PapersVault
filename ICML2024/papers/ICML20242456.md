## Problem

Training overparameterized MLPs for implicit neural representation (INR) is computationally expensive, especially for high-resolution signals (e.g., 1024×1024 images yielding 10⁶ training pixels). While nonparametric teaching theory provides efficient example-selection strategies for non-closed-form target functions, it has not been applicable to MLP-based INR because MLPs are trained via parameter-based gradient descent rather than the functional gradient descent assumed by nonparametric teaching. The paper bridges this theoretical and practical gap.

## Input / Output

The method takes a discrete signal (2D image pixels, 1D audio waveform, or 3D point cloud) as the target function f*, where the MLP maps low-dimensional coordinates to signal values (e.g., 2D coordinates → 8-bit grayscale levels). At each iteration, the teacher selects a subset of k signal fragments (out of N total) with the largest pointwise discrepancy |f_θ(x_i) − f*(x_i)|, and only these k examples are used for the gradient update. The output is the trained MLP parameter vector θ that reconstructs the full signal; for megapixel-scale inputs (8192×8192 Pluto image), a mini-batch variant randomly samples a subset per iteration to respect hardware constraints.

## Method

The key theoretical insight is that the evolution of an overparameterized MLP under parameter-based gradient descent, when expressed as a functional ODE via the dynamic neural tangent kernel (NTK), is asymptotically equivalent to functional gradient descent in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (Theorem 5: dynamic NTK converges pointwise to the canonical kernel). This consistency allows the greedy nonparametric teaching algorithm to be directly applied: at each step, the teacher selects the k examples maximizing ‖[f_θ(x_i) − f*(x_i)]_k‖₂, which steepens the functional gradient without computing kernel norms. A spectral decomposition of the kernel matrix reveals why static training sets suffer from slow convergence (small eigenvalues dominate late in training) and why dynamically shifting the subspace of selected examples exploits the fast-convergence regime. Practically, the INT algorithm combines an incrementally increasing selection ratio with an incrementally increasing sampling interval, achieving 30–40% training time savings across audio, image, and 3D shape modalities while maintaining or improving reconstruction quality.

## Results

INT achieves 30%+ training time savings across all tested modalities while maintaining or improving reconstruction quality: 1D audio (-31.63%, PSNR 48.15 vs. 48.38 dB), 2D images (-38.88%, PSNR 36.97 vs. 36.09 dB), megapixel Pluto image (-29.26%, PSNR 33.01 vs. 31.82 dB), and 3D shapes (-35.54%, IoU 96.68% vs. 97.07%). Notably, for 2D images the PSNR actually improves with INT (36.97 dB vs. 36.09 dB) despite a ~40% reduction in training time. The speedup ranges from 1.41× to 1.64× across modalities, with degradation limited to <1 dB PSNR or <1% IoU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is standard INR training without INT (i.e., using all pixels/points at every iteration). INT consistently outperforms this baseline on training time across all four modalities (audio, image, megapixel, 3D shape) by 29–39%, while reconstruction quality is preserved or slightly improved (e.g., PSNR improves from 36.09 to 36.97 dB on Kodak images). A secondary comparison on the Cameraman image shows that INT at 20% selection rate outperforms random 20% sampling in both PSNR and SSIM, though it introduces minor background artifacts; a progressive INT schedule (20%→100%) resolves these artifacts while retaining the speedup. The paper also ablates INT strategy variants (Table 1), finding that incrementally increasing both sampling ratio and interval yields the best trade-off (211.04 s, 37.04 dB PSNR) versus the no-INT baseline (345.22 s, 35.95 dB), while decremental strategies cause severe quality degradation (33.56 dB PSNR).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously disconnected lines of work: nonparametric machine teaching (Zhang et al., 2023a,b), which formulates efficient example selection for implicitly defined target functions via functional gradient descent in RKHS, and implicit neural representation (Sitzmann et al., 2020b; Tancik et al., 2020), which fits discrete signals with overparameterized MLPs trained by parameter-based gradient descent. The key theoretical contribution is showing via the dynamic NTK (Jacot et al., 2018) that these two evolution mechanisms are asymptotically consistent, thereby legitimizing the direct transfer of greedy nonparametric teaching algorithms to INR training. This departs from prior INR efficiency efforts (dictionary training, meta-learning, positional encoding) by reframing the problem as a teaching problem rather than a representation or optimization problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NTK-to-canonical-kernel convergence result (Theorem 5) provides the first rigorous justification for applying nonparametric teaching tools to deep network training, potentially expanding the machine teaching framework well beyond its original scope. Practically, the 30–40% training time savings across audio, image, and 3D shape modalities are meaningful for high-resolution signals where training cost is prohibitive. However, the greedy selection strategy requires a full forward pass over all data at each selection step, partially offsetting the per-iteration savings, and the artifacts observed at low selection rates (overfitting to boundary pixels) suggest the method is not yet a drop-in replacement without careful scheduling. Open questions include whether more sophisticated teaching algorithms (beyond greedy) yield further gains, whether the NTK convergence assumption holds for finite-width networks in practice, and whether the framework extends to architectures beyond MLPs (e.g., transformers for LLM fine-tuning, as the authors speculate).
