## Problem

Behavioral cloning (BC) models the conditional probability p(a|s) and is efficient but struggles to generalize to unseen states, while joint probability models p(s,a) improve generalization but require slow iterative action sampling at inference and suffer from manifold overfitting on low-dimensional expert data. No existing offline imitation learning method combines the inference efficiency of conditional modeling with the generalization benefits of joint modeling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240291/cropped_page3_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy takes a state vector (6D for MAZE, 16D for FETCHPICK, 68D for HANDROTATE, 17D for CHEETAH/WALKER, 42D for ANTREACH) and directly outputs an action vector (2D to 20D depending on the task) in a single forward pass. The diffusion model operates on the concatenated state-action pair (e.g., 8D for MAZE, 88D for HANDROTATE) and is used only during training as a loss signal, not at inference. In the image-based CARRACING experiment, states are two consecutive 64×64 grayscale frames and actions are 3D (steering, acceleration, braking).

## Method

DBC is a two-stage framework: (1) a DDPM is trained on concatenated expert state-action pairs to model the joint distribution p(s,a) via noise-prediction denoising; (2) a standard MLP policy is trained with a combined objective L_total = L_BC + λ·L_DM, where L_BC is the MSE between expert and predicted actions (conditional signal) and L_DM = max(L_diff^agent − L_diff^expert, 0) measures how much worse the policy's predicted action fits the expert joint distribution compared to the true expert action (joint signal). The normalization by the expert diffusion loss stabilizes training on poorly-modeled state-action pairs. At inference, the policy directly maps states to actions in one forward pass, avoiding the iterative denoising of Diffusion Policy or the action sampling/optimization of Implicit BC, yielding ~30 episodes/s versus ~1.4 for Diffusion Policy on MAZE.

## Results

DBC achieves the best or near-best performance across all six continuous control environments. On FETCHPICK, DBC reaches 97.5%±1.9% success rate, outperforming Diffusion Policy (83.9%) and BC (91.6%). On ANTREACH, DBC achieves 70.1%±4.9%, surpassing the next-best Diffusion Policy (61.8%) by 8.3 points. On locomotion tasks, DBC attains the highest returns: 4909.5±73.0 on CHEETAH and 7034.6±33.7 on WALKER. On MAZE and HANDROTATE, DBC is within one standard deviation of Diffusion Policy (95.4% vs 95.5%; 60.1% vs 61.7%), while substantially outperforming BC and Implicit BC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DBC clearly outperforms Implicit BC on every environment, with the largest gaps on HANDROTATE (60.1% vs 13.8%) and WALKER (7034.6 vs 839.8 return), confirming that joint-probability modeling via energy-based models is both less effective and less scalable to high-dimensional action spaces. Against BC, DBC wins decisively on FETCHPICK (97.5% vs 91.6%) and ANTREACH (70.1% vs 56.2%), while matching or slightly exceeding BC on locomotion tasks (CHEETAH 4909.5 vs 4873.3; WALKER 7034.6 vs 6954.4). Against Diffusion Policy, DBC achieves the best result on FETCHPICK (+13.6 points), CHEETAH (+259.2 return), WALKER (+555.5 return), and ANTREACH (+8.3 points), while being within one standard deviation on MAZE (95.4% vs 95.5%) and HANDROTATE (60.1% vs 61.7%). Inference speed also favors DBC: 30.79 episodes/s versus 1.38 for Diffusion Policy and 9.92 for Implicit BC on MAZE, since DBC directly maps states to actions without iterative denoising or action optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

DBC builds on two well-established threads: behavioral cloning (Pomerleau, 1989), which models the conditional probability p(a|s) via supervised learning, and denoising diffusion probabilistic models (Ho, 2020), which learn to model data distributions through iterative denoising. The key departure from prior work is in how the diffusion model is used: rather than serving as the policy itself (as in Diffusion Policy, Chi et al. 2023; Pearce et al. 2023) or as an energy-based scoring function requiring iterative action optimization at inference (as in Implicit BC, Florence et al. 2022), DBC trains a diffusion model on expert state-action pairs and uses its denoising loss as an auxiliary training signal for a standard feedforward policy. This positions DBC between the simplicity of BC and the generalization benefits of joint-distribution modeling, with the f-divergence analysis (Section D.2) framing BC as forward-KL minimization and the diffusion loss as reverse-KL minimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DBC offers a practical middle ground for imitation learning: it retains the fast, single-forward-pass inference of BC (30.79 episodes/s vs. 1.38 for Diffusion Policy) while gaining meaningful generalization improvements, as shown by the FETCHPICK noise-injection experiments where DBC degrades only 26% vs. 72% for Implicit BC. The manifold overfitting experiment (Section 5.6) provides a concrete demonstration that diffusion models alone cannot reliably guide policy learning when expert data lies on a low-dimensional manifold, justifying the BC augmentation. A key limitation is that DBC is strictly offline (no environment interaction) and cannot incorporate agent-generated trajectories, which restricts its applicability to settings where only expert demonstrations are available. Open questions include whether the framework extends to online or semi-online settings and whether the diffusion model loss can be replaced by cheaper alternatives without sacrificing the generalization benefit.
