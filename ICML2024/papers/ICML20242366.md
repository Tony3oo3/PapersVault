## Problem

Transformer-based SNNs still lag significantly behind their ANN counterparts in accuracy, and existing ANN-to-SNN (A2S) conversion methods that achieve on-par accuracy for CNN-based SNNs cannot be directly applied to Transformers. The core obstacle is that key Transformer operators—softmax, layer normalization, and the dot-product attention between two non-stationary activation matrices—lack mathematically equivalent spiking counterparts, preventing lossless conversion.

## Input / Output

The method accepts RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet), neuromorphic event streams (CIFAR10-DVS, with an additional reduction layer to map channels to 3), and tokenized text sentences (English and Chinese NLU benchmarks). Inference proceeds over multiple discrete time-steps (16–128 depending on dataset difficulty and quantization level), during which spike trains accumulate; the final classification head reads membrane potential from ST-BIF+ neurons rather than spike trains, producing a class label per input.

## Method

SpikeZIP-TF establishes exact equivalence between a quantized Transformer (QANN) and its SNN counterpart through three key components. First, the ST-BIF+ neuron extends the ST-BIF model by expanding the spike tracer's lower bound to match the quantizer's clamp range, making its accumulated output exactly equal to a general quantized function (not just Q-ReLU). Second, Spike-Equivalent Self-Attention (SESA) decomposes both activation-weight and activation-activation matrix multiplications into per-time-step operations: for the AA case (Q·K), it uses the spike tracers to compute the attention array via three matrix multiplications per step (S_Q·K_t + Q_t·S_K − Q_t·K_t), provably summing to the quantized attention output at equilibrium. Third, Spike-Softmax and Spike-LayerNorm apply a differential algorithm that accumulates inputs across time-steps and emits the difference of the function output at each step, so the temporal sum equals the single-shot ANN output. The full pipeline is: (1) replace all non-ReLU activations in the source Transformer with ReLU, (2) perform quantization-aware training to obtain a low-bit QANN, and (3) substitute ReLU layers with ST-BIF+ neurons and SNN-unfriendly operators with their spiking equivalents—yielding an SNN with zero accuracy degradation relative to the QANN.

## Results

SpikeZIP-TF achieves 83.82% Top-1 accuracy on ImageNet with ViT-L (304.33M params, 64 timesteps), surpassing the SOTA A2S method MST (78.51%, 512 timesteps) by 5.31% and the SOTA direct-training method Spikformer V2 (82.35%, 4 timesteps) by 1.47%. On CIFAR-10/100, it reaches 98.7%/89.7% at 32 timesteps, outperforming MST (97.3%/86.9% at 256 timesteps) by 1.4%/2.8% with 8× fewer timesteps. On the NLP benchmark SST-2, SpikeZIP-TF (Roberta-L) achieves 93.79% accuracy, exceeding SpikeBERT (85.39%) by 8.40% and SpikeGPT (88.76%) by 5.03%. Training cost is dramatically lower: 30 GPU-hours for ImageNet (ViT-L) vs. 196.7 hours for Spikformer V2, and 1.03 hours for SST-2 vs. 48 hours for SpikeGPT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpikeZIP-TF outperforms all 10 compared baselines across every benchmark. Against the closest A2S competitor MST (Wang et al., ICCV 2023), it gains 5.31% on ImageNet (83.82% vs. 78.51%), 1.4% on CIFAR-10 (98.7% vs. 97.3%), 2.8% on CIFAR-100 (89.7% vs. 86.9%), and 2.4% on CIFAR10-DVS (90.5% vs. 88.1%), while using 8–16× fewer timesteps. Against direct-training Transformer SNNs, it surpasses Spikformer V2 (Zhou et al., 2024) by 1.47% on ImageNet (83.82% vs. 82.35%) with a lighter model (304M vs. 173M params) and far lower training cost (30h vs. 196.7h), and beats Spikformer (Zhou et al., 2022) by 4.27% (83.82% vs. 79.55%) and Spikingformer (Zhou et al., 2023) by 7.97% (83.82% vs. 75.85%). On NLP, SpikeZIP-TF (Roberta-L) achieves 93.79% on SST-2, exceeding SpikeBERT (Lv et al., 2023) by 8.40% (93.79% vs. 85.39%), SpikeGPT (Zhu et al., 2023) by 5.03% (93.79% vs. 88.76%), and SNN-TextCNN (Lv et al., 2022) by 12.88% (93.79% vs. 80.91%). The earlier A2S methods QCFS (Bu et al., 2023) and Fast-SNN (Hu et al., 2023) are limited to CNN backbones and achieve only 72.85% and 72.95% on ImageNet, far below SpikeZIP-TF's 83.82%. Auto-Spikformer (Che et al., 2023) and Spike-driven Transformer (Yao et al., 2023) trail by 2.3% and 3.1% on CIFAR-10 respectively, and are not evaluated on ImageNet at comparable scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ANN-to-SNN (A2S) conversion pipeline established by Bu et al. (2023), Li et al. (2022), and Hu et al. (2023) from CNN-based SNNs to Transformer-based SNNs, a setting where prior A2S methods failed due to the incompatibility of softmax, layer normalization, and attention operators with spiking computation. The closest prior A2S work for Transformers, MST (Wang et al., 2023), replaced the QCFS activation with ReLU but did not achieve full operator equivalence. SpikeZIP-TF departs from this by introducing the ST-BIF+ neuron (extending the ST-BIF model), spike-equivalent self-attention (SESA), and differential-algorithm-based Spike-Softmax and Spike-LayerNorm to guarantee exact equivalence between the quantized Transformer and its SNN counterpart.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By establishing provable equivalence between quantized Transformers and SNNs, this work removes the primary barrier to deploying Transformer architectures on neuromorphic hardware without the accuracy gap that plagues direct-training methods. The dramatic reduction in training cost (e.g., 30 GPU-hours vs. 196.7 hours on ImageNet) makes large-scale Transformer SNNs practically accessible. However, the 64 time-steps requirement (vs. 4 for direct-training methods) and the dependence on quantization (introducing a small accuracy loss relative to full-precision ANNs) remain open challenges. The authors note future work on extending the framework to direct learning and reducing inference latency.
