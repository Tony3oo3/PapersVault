## Problem

While transformers have achieved remarkable success in NLP, the theoretical reason why attention layers are particularly suited for tasks where meaning hinges on individual words remains unclear. The paper formalizes this intuition through a new quantity called word sensitivity (WS) — the maximum relative change in a feature map's output when a single token is perturbed — and asks whether attention layers provably exhibit higher WS than fully connected architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240172/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a sequence of n token embeddings X ∈ R^{n×d} (normalized so ||x_i||₂ = √d) and a random weight matrix (V ∈ R^{k×nd} for RF, W ∈ R^{d×d} for RAF) as input, and produces word sensitivity bounds plus generalization error bounds for generalized linear models trained on the resulting features. The WS is defined as a supremum over all bounded perturbations (||Δ||₂ ≤ √d) of any single row of X, making it a worst-case measure of single-word impact on the feature map. For the generalization analysis, the output is a lower bound on the test error of a GLM on a perturbed sample (X^i(Δ), y_Δ) where y_Δ = −y, after training on (X, y).

## Method

The core technical contribution is proving that random attention features (RAF) have constant-order word sensitivity (S_RAF = Ω(1)) independent of context length, while random features (RF) have sensitivity O(1/√n) that vanishes as the context grows. The RAF proof proceeds in four steps: (i) find a direction δ* aligned with a constant fraction of token embeddings, (ii) exhibit two far-apart perturbation directions Δ₁*, Δ₂* that make a constant fraction of attention score entries Ω(log²d), (iii) show the softmax concentrates attention on the perturbed word for those tokens (the critical step that ReLU cannot replicate), and (iv) conclude at least one perturbation causes an Ω(√dn) change in the output. These WS bounds then translate into generalization results: RF-based GLMs provably cannot distinguish two sentences differing in one word (test error > (2−γ)² − O(1/√n)), while RAF-based GLMs empirically achieve near-zero error on the perturbed sample even when the pre-training model could not separate the two. The analysis requires no distributional assumptions on the data for the WS bounds, and the RAF result holds under the mild condition d/log⁴d = Ω(n), satisfied by BERT-Base (n=512, d=768) and Llama-2 (n=4096, d=5120).

## Results

The paper establishes that random features (RF) have word sensitivity S_RF(X) = O(1/√n), which vanishes as context length n grows, while random attention features (RAF) maintain S_RAF(X) = Ω(1) regardless of n (under d/log⁴d = Ω(n)). For generalization, Theorems 6.2–6.3 prove that after fine-tuning or re-training, the RF test error on a single-word-perturbed sample is lower bounded by (2−γ)² − O(1/√n), meaning RF provably cannot distinguish two sentences differing in one word. In contrast, empirical results (Figure 4) show that the RAF model achieves test error close to 0 on the perturbed sample even when γ ≈ 0 (i.e., the two samples were indistinguishable before fine-tuning), while ReLU-RAF (replacing softmax with ReLU) still requires γ to be large to achieve small error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard random features baseline (Rahimi & Recht, 2007), the RAF model decisively wins on both word sensitivity and generalization: RF's sensitivity decays as O(1/√n) and its test error is provably lower bounded by (2−γ)², while RAF maintains constant sensitivity and empirically achieves near-zero test error. Against the ReLU-RAF model from Fu et al. (2023), which uses ReLU instead of softmax in the attention layer, the paper demonstrates that the softmax is the critical architectural choice: ReLU-RAF's word sensitivity decreases with context length and it cannot achieve small test error unless γ is already large (i.e., the model could already distinguish the two samples before fine-tuning). The numerical experiments on BERT-Base embeddings of the IMDB dataset confirm that the theoretical gap between RF and RAF persists in practice, with RAF's test error frequently falling below the (2−γ)² lower bound that holds for RF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Fu et al. (2023), which introduced the random attention features (RAF) model for studying what a single attention layer can learn, but critically departs by replacing the ReLU activation with softmax and showing that this choice is what confers high word sensitivity. The generalization analysis for re-training leverages the stability and feature-alignment framework of Bombari & Mondelli (2024). The paper also extends the deep random features analysis of Bosch et al. (2023) and Schröder et al. (2023) to the NLP setting, and positions its 'word sensitivity' metric as distinct from prior Lipschitz-based sensitivity notions used in adversarial robustness, where high sensitivity is undesirable rather than a design goal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first formal, quantitative explanation for why attention layers are architecturally suited to NLP: the softmax normalization creates a mechanism by which a single token perturbation can redirect attention mass, while fully connected layers provably cannot capture single-word changes in long contexts regardless of depth. The identification of softmax (rather than the attention structure per se) as the critical component is a useful decomposition for future architecture design. Key limitations include the restriction to random (untrained) weights, the condition d/log⁴(d) = Ω(n), and the fact that positive generalization results for RAF are empirical rather than proven. Open questions include extending the analysis to discrete tokenization, multi-head attention, and trained models.
