## Problem

Insertion/deletion metrics are the dominant evaluation criterion for attribution-based explanation methods, yet existing studies of these metrics suffer from circular reasoning (using the very methods being evaluated to validate the metric) and lack a principled explanation that achieves the optimal deletion score. No prior work has systematically benchmarked all settings of the deletion metric—reference values, MoRF vs. LeRF criteria, patch sizes, and logit vs. probability outputs—while properly addressing the out-of-distribution (OOD) issue that arises when features are removed.

## Input / Output

TRACE takes a black-box model f and a 224×224 RGB image, partitioned into t square superpixel patches (e.g., 7×7=49 patches), and produces a deletion trajectory τ—a permutation of all t patches specifying the optimal order in which to delete them. The trajectory can be converted into a dense attribution heatmap via ψ = (τ⁻¹/d)^α, where α controls the visual highlight region. Unlike gradient-based methods that require access to model internals, TRACE only requires forward-pass evaluations of f on perturbed inputs, making it fully model-agnostic.

## Method

The key insight is that deletion metrics depend only on the permutation (ordering) of features, not on the specific attribution values, so the problem reduces to optimizing over the finite symmetric group S_t rather than continuous R^d. This combinatorial optimization is proven NP-hard via reduction from TSP, and is solved using simulated annealing over the complete transposition graph (swapping any two patches in the trajectory) or a greedy sequential deletion scheme. The SA approach with complete transpositions achieves near-global optimality, verified by sandwiching between the heuristic solution and a complete-search lower bound. The framework is instantiated in three variants—TRACE-Mo, TRACE-Le, and TRACE-Le-Mo—corresponding to different deletion criteria, and is compatible with any reference value type (zero, mean, blurring) and output measure (logit or probability).

## Results

TRACE achieves the highest LeRF-MoRF AUC difference (higher is better) across all 6 tested DNNs and all 3 reference value types. On ResNet-18 with zero reference and probability output, TRACE-SA-Le-Mo (K=5000) scores 31.69, roughly 2× the best attribution baseline (GradCAM at 16.24). Even the fast TRACE-Greedy-Le variant (≈0.66 s) reaches 27.09, still far exceeding all baselines. The advantage is consistent: on DenseNet-161, TRACE-p scores 35.25 vs. GradCAM's 19.82; on AlexNet, TRACE-p scores 21.83 vs. IBA's 8.13.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRACE (both SA and Greedy variants) outperforms all 12 compared attribution methods by a large and consistent margin across every model, reference type, and output measure (logit vs. probability). The strongest baselines are model-dependent: GradCAM leads on GoogLeNet (14.31), MobileNetV3 (16.78), and DenseNet-161 (19.82), while IBA leads on ResNet-18 (15.92), VGG-16 (18.77), and AlexNet (8.13); in every case TRACE-p exceeds the best baseline by 10–17 points. TRACE-Greedy-Le, running in under 1 second, still surpasses the slowest perturbation-based baselines (RISE ≈5.2 s, Extremal Perturbation ≈29 s) by a wide margin (e.g., 27.09 vs. 14.52 on ResNet-18). On the breast cancer tabular dataset, TRACE similarly dominates Gradient, IxG, IG, LRP, and GradientSHAP. The paper also shows that TRACE's advantage is not an artifact of a specific reference value: scores remain stable across zero, mean, and blurring references, whereas discrete methods like Gradient and IG show large score swings between reference types.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard practice of validating deletion/insertion metrics using specific attribution methods (GradCAM, IG, Gradient), which the authors identify as circular reasoning. Instead, it repurposes combinatorial optimization tools—specifically simulated annealing over the symmetric group S_d, with a formal NP-hardness reduction from TSP—to directly solve the deletion metric's optimization problem, yielding what the authors call the 'principled explanation.' Conceptually, it builds on the OOD problem identified by Hooker et al. (2019) and Schulz et al. (2019) but reframes the analysis: rather than proposing workarounds validated against a fixed set of attribution methods, it uses the metric's own optimum as an unbiased reference point to benchmark all settings (reference values, MoRF vs. LeRF, patch sizes, logit vs. probability).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the XAI community with a principled, method-agnostic way to evaluate and calibrate deletion metrics, breaking the circular dependency between attribution methods and the metrics used to assess them. Its practical guidance—LeRF preferred over MoRF, superpixel patches over pixels, reference values largely irrelevant at patch resolution—could reshape how future attribution papers design their evaluation protocols. Open questions remain: whether the 'principled explanation' (the optimal deletion trajectory) is actually the explanation humans or downstream tasks desire, how TRACE relates to path-based methods like SHAP that average over all d! trajectories, and whether the approach scales to very large inputs (e.g., video, high-resolution medical images) where t! becomes even more intractable.
