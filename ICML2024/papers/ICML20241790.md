## Problem

Previous hyper-representation methods for learning neural network weight embeddings face two structural limitations: they must encode the entire flattened weight vector at once, making them infeasible for models beyond a few thousand parameters, and they require all models to share a fixed architecture. Additionally, prior work trained separate representations for discriminative (property prediction) versus generative (weight sampling) tasks, limiting versatility.

## Input / Output

SANE takes trained neural network weights as input, which are reshaped into 2D matrices, sliced row-wise along outgoing channels, and split into fixed-size token vectors (d_t ≈ 288–289) with 3D positional encodings (global sequence position, layer index, intra-layer position). Models are aligned to a common reference via permutation (Git ReBasin) before tokenization. The output is a per-token latent embedding sequence (d_z = 128 per token) that can be aggregated into a single model-level vector via center-of-gravity averaging, or decoded back into weight tokens for model generation. For sampling, only 1–5 lightly trained prompt examples (1–5 epochs) are needed to approximate the target distribution in latent space.

## Method

SANE (Sequential Autoencoder for Neural Embeddings) decouples model tokenization from representation learning by expressing each network as a sequence of uniform-size tokens, then training a transformer encoder-decoder on random consecutive windows (ws = 256–512 tokens) rather than the full sequence. This windowed pretraining decouples memory and compute from base-model size, enabling scaling to ResNet-18 (~50k tokens) and beyond, while the fixed token size allows heterogeneous architectures to share the same embedding space. Training uses a composite loss combining masked reconstruction (MSE on non-padded tokens) and NTXent contrastive guidance with noise and permutation augmentations. At inference, haloing adds context tokens around content windows to maintain coherence across long sequences, and batch-norm conditioning aligns BN statistics via forward passes without updating learned weights. For model generation, SANE samples latent tokens from a per-token KDE fitted to prompt embeddings, then uses subsampling (draw many, keep top-m by target metric) or bootstrapping (iteratively re-fit the KDE on the best samples) to refine the distribution toward the target task.

## Results

On ResNet-18 model generation (the paper's key scalability contribution), SANE_SUB achieves 68.1% zero-shot accuracy on CIFAR-10, 19.8% on CIFAR-100, and 11.1% on Tiny-ImageNet, substantially outperforming random initialization (~10%, ~1%, ~0.5% respectively). On small CNNs, SANE_SUB improves zero-shot accuracy by ~25% over prior hyper-representations (e.g., 86.7% vs 68.6% on MNIST, 72.3% vs 54.5% on SVHN). For discriminative property prediction, SANE embeddings match weight statistics s(W) baselines on both small CNNs (e.g., R²=0.978 vs 0.987 for MNIST accuracy) and ResNet-18s (e.g., R²=0.879 vs 0.880 for CIFAR-10 accuracy), demonstrating that sequential decomposition preserves model-quality information at scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against weight statistics s(W) (Unterthiner et al., 2020), SANE embeddings essentially match on discriminative property prediction: on ResNet-18s, SANE achieves R²=0.879 vs 0.880 for CIFAR-10 accuracy and 0.512 vs 0.490 for generalization gap, while on small CNNs SANE is within 1–2 R² points of s(W) (e.g., 0.978 vs 0.987 on MNIST accuracy). Against prior hyper-representation sampling S_KDE30 (Schürholt et al., 2022a), SANE substantially wins on generative tasks: at epoch 0 on small CNNs, SANE_SUB reaches 86.7% on MNIST vs 68.6% for S_KDE30 (~25% absolute gain) and 72.3% vs 54.5% on SVHN; on ResNet-18s, SANE_SUB improves CIFAR-10 zero-shot from 64.8% to 68.1% and Tiny-ImageNet from 8.4% to 11.1%. Against the E_c+D method (Schürholt et al., 2021), SANE matches published performance on the MNIST zoo (R²=0.978 vs 0.973 for accuracy) while adding the critical ability to scale to ResNet-18 models (~12M parameters, three orders of magnitude larger than prior work). SANE's bootstrapping variant (SANE_BOOT) further improves over subsampling by 0.5–3.5% on ResNet-18s, and the Gaussian-prior variant (SANE_GAUSS) achieves the highest MNIST accuracy (94.2%) after 25 epochs of fine-tuning, demonstrating that prompt examples can be entirely replaced.

## Relation to Prior Work

SANE extends the hyper-representation framework (Schürholt et al., 2021; 2022a), which learns a global encoder-decoder embedding from flattened NN weights, by decomposing the weight vector into a tokenized sequence processed in sliding windows. This sequential decomposition is motivated by Martin & Mahoney's finding that global model information is preserved in layer-wise weight components, and it decouples the representation learner's memory footprint from the base model's parameter count. The paper also builds on the model zoo dataset (Schürholt et al., 2022c) and incorporates Git ReBasin-style alignment to handle permutation symmetries, while introducing subsampling and bootstrapping strategies that reduce the quality and quantity requirements for prompt examples compared to the KDE30 approach in prior generative hyper-representation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SANE makes weight-space representation learning applicable to real-world model sizes (ResNet-18 with ~12M parameters, three orders of magnitude larger than prior work) and enables cross-architecture generalization (e.g., sampling ResNet-34 models from a ResNet-18-pretrained encoder) with only 1–5 lightly trained prompt examples. This opens practical pathways for model initialization, transfer learning, and diagnostics at scales previously infeasible. Key limitations include restriction to computer vision tasks, reliance on homogeneous-architecture zoos for pretraining, and the need for prompt examples for large-model sampling (Gaussian bootstrapping is too expensive at ResNet scale). Open questions include whether the approach extends to NLP or multimodal architectures, whether heterogeneous-architecture pretraining improves cross-architecture transfer, and whether the window-based sequential processing can be replaced by more principled hierarchical encodings.
