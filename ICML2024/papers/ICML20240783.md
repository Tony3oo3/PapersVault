## Problem

Despite impressive performance on complex tasks, LLMs still fail at basic math like multi-digit addition, yet it remains unclear whether they are learning underlying rules or merely recalling similar training examples. This paper directly tests whether transformers perform 'case-based reasoning' (relying on nearby training cases) or 'rule-based reasoning' (mastering generalizable rules) through intervention experiments, and proposes a technique to shift them toward rule-based reasoning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240783/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the diagnostic experiments, the input is a simple math expression (e.g., 'a+b=') and the output is the answer, with the key manipulation being the Leave-Square-Out data split that removes a contiguous region of training samples. For RFFT, the input additionally contains an explicit rule representation (Python code or natural language instructions for digit-by-digit addition), and the output is a structured step-by-step trace where the model recites the specific rule line being applied, shows variable updates (e.g., 'num1.pop()', 'total=digit1+digit2+carry=5+8+0=13'), and produces the final answer. Rules can be expressed as programs, pseudo-code, or natural language.

## Method

The paper introduces two key contributions. First, the Leave-Square-Out intervention: a square region of the (a,b) input space is removed from training, and the resulting 'holes' in test accuracy (where the model fails on samples far from any training case but succeeds near boundaries) provide direct evidence of case-based reasoning across five math tasks and multiple model sizes. Second, Rule-Following Fine-Tuning (RFFT) teaches rule-based reasoning by (1) explicitly embedding the algorithm as code or natural language in the input, and (2) fine-tuning the model to recite which rule line it is executing at each step while updating intermediate variables, rather than merely producing intermediate numeric results as in scratchpad. The critical distinction from scratchpad is that RFFT forces the model to articulate the rationale behind each operation (e.g., 'carry=total//10'), preventing it from memorizing seen sub-steps. With only 100 training samples, RFFT enables GPT-3.5-turbo to generalize from 1–5 digit addition to 12-digit addition at >95% accuracy, a >40% improvement over scratchpad.

## Results

On addition length generalization, RFFT enables Llama-2-7B to maintain 91.1% accuracy on 9-digit sums, while scratchpad achieves less than 40% on comparable tasks. With GPT-3.5-turbo fine-tuned on only 100 training samples (1–5 digit addition), RFFT generalizes to 12-digit addition with over 95% accuracy, exceeding scratchpad by more than 40 percentage points. In the Leave-Square-Out intervention experiments across five math tasks, models trained with random splits achieve 100% accuracy, but performance drops sharply (forming 'holes') when a contiguous square of training examples is removed, confirming case-based reasoning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is scratchpad fine-tuning (Nye et al., 2021), which provides step-by-step intermediate digit-by-digit computations without explicit rules. RFFT decisively outperforms scratchpad across all digit lengths: on Llama-2-7B, RFFT reaches 91.1% at 9 digits versus <40% for scratchpad; on GPT-3.5-turbo, RFFT exceeds 95% at 12 digits while scratchpad falls well below. A second baseline, direct-answer fine-tuning (no intermediate steps), is also consistently outperformed by RFFT, though the gap is smaller for GPT-3.5 where the model already has some inherent addition ability (46.2% zero-shot on 15-digit addition). The paper also compares RFFT against scratchpad tracing (Nye et al.'s program-execution format) in Appendix F.6, where RFFT again wins significantly, attributed to its more human-readable step-by-step rule recitation and natural language instructions. Overall, RFFT wins on every comparison, with the largest margins appearing at longer digit lengths where case-based shortcuts break down.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the center of an ongoing debate about whether LLMs perform rule-based or case-based reasoning, providing direct intervention evidence (Leave-Square-Out) that goes beyond the indirect subgraph-frequency analysis of Dziri et al. (2023) and the counterfactual performance drops observed by Wu et al. (2023). It extends the scratchpad line of work (Nye et al., 2021) by showing that intermediate computation steps do not enable rule-based reasoning, and distinguishes its proposed Rule-Following Fine-Tuning (RFFT) from scratchpad tracing by explicitly providing rules in the input and requiring the model to recite and follow them step by step. The paper also connects to the grokking literature (Power et al., 2022; Nanda et al., 2023) by demonstrating that even the apparent learning of systematic algorithms in modular addition still depends on surrounding training cases.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical diagnostic (Leave-Square-Out) and a practical remedy (RFFT) for a well-known failure mode of LLMs: inability to generalize in length on arithmetic tasks. The finding that RFFT enables GPT-3.5 to generalize from 1–5 digit to 12-digit addition with only 100 training samples suggests that rule-following may be a meta-learning ability acquired during pre-training, with implications for how we design fine-tuning pipelines for systematic generalization. Key limitations include RFFT's task-specificity (requiring carefully designed rule representations and input-output sequences) and the fact that the study focuses on fine-tuning rather than the reasoning mechanisms of pretrained models. Open questions include whether prompting techniques (CoT, least-to-most, program-aided) shift models toward rule-based reasoning, and whether the findings extend beyond arithmetic to broader compositional reasoning tasks.
