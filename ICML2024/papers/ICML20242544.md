## Problem

Selection in sequential data has been treated almost exclusively as a statistical bias to be corrected, with prior methods (e.g., FCI) leaving the selection structure ambiguous or requiring parametric assumptions merely to detect its presence. This paper asks whether the full causal structure of selection—distinguishing selection pairs from direct causal relations and latent confounders—is identifiable from purely observational data, and shows that it is, under mild structural conditions and without parametric assumptions or interventions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242544/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of N observed variables (e.g., per-bar frequency-domain features extracted from audio via FFT and Mel-scale conversion) under the constraint that all causal edges respect temporal order (i < j). It outputs a typed directed acyclic graph in which each dependent pair is classified as a selection pair (X_i → S ← X_j), a direct relation (X_i → X_j, including higher-order), or a confounded pair (X_i ← C → X_j), with the option of a pair being both a selection pair and a direct relation. The algorithm runs in O(N²) time via pairwise CI tests, scaling to roughly 600 variables within an hour on CPU.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage constraint-based algorithm (Algorithm 2) that uses Fisher's z-test conditional independence tests with carefully constructed conditioning sets to identify and type all dependencies. Stage One detects all dependent pairs by testing X_i ⊥ X_j | X_Pre(j)\i and a second test that blocks spurious paths through already-identified selection pairs. Stage Two distinguishes selection pairs from direct relations by exploiting the key structural difference: in a selection pair X_i → S ← X_j, the variable X_j is a collider on the path to downstream variables (always conditioned on), whereas in a direct relation X_i → X_j it is not—this is tested by checking whether conditioning on X_j versus X_{j+1} changes the independence between X_i and a downstream X_k. Stage Three (only when latent confounders are permitted) identifies confounded pairs by detecting the collider at X_i on the path X_{i-1} → X_i ← C → X_j, which creates an additional independence when X_i is conditioned on. Identifiability is proven nonparametrically (Theorems 3.2 and 3.4) under structural conditions that exclude degenerate configurations where d-separation cannot be achieved, and the paper also shows that multiple selection variables can be merged into a single composite variable via logical AND without changing the observed dependence structure.

## Results

On synthetic data with 8–20 observed variables, the proposed algorithm (with structural conditions) correctly identifies all selection pairs, direct relations, and confounded pairs, while the base model (without structural conditions) shows lower precision on first-order direct relations due to misclassifying confounded pairs as direct relations. The algorithm scales to approximately 600 variables within one hour on CPU-only hardware (12 GB memory), confirming its O(N²) complexity. On real-world music data (Hotel California guitar clips and Panis Angelicus string section), the identified selection pairs align with known musical structures such as the Andalusian cadence progression and thematic phrases in the hymn's strophe.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external causal discovery methods (e.g., FCI, PC) because it targets a distinct task—identifying the specific type of dependency (selection vs. direct vs. confounded) rather than recovering a full DAG up to equivalence. The only internal comparison is between the full algorithm (satisfying all structural conditions) and a 'base model' that omits the structural conditions. Under this comparison, both models recover nearly all first-order direct relations, but the base model's precision drops because it erroneously classifies confounded pairs as first-order direct relations, inflating false positives. The paper positions itself as the first framework to nonparametrically identify selection structure from observational data, so no prior method addresses the same identifiability question. The real-world music experiments serve as qualitative validation rather than a quantitative benchmark against competing approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal discovery literature on selection bias by shifting the perspective from treating selection as a nuisance to be corrected (as in FCI, Zhang 2008, or bias-mitigation methods by Bareinboim et al.) to identifying the selection structure itself as a first-class component of the causal graph. It builds on Kaltenpoth & Vreeken (2023), who detect the presence of selection bias under parametric assumptions, by providing nonparametric identifiability of the full selection structure without parametric constraints or interventions. The key departure is the use of sequential ordering and d-separation-based conditional independence tests to distinguish selection pairs (collider-mediated) from direct causal relations and latent confounding, a distinction that prior constraint-based methods like FCI leave ambiguous within equivalence classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that selection structure is identifiable from purely observational data—even alongside latent confounders—offers a more veridical inductive bias for sequential data modeling, potentially avoiding the overcomplicated universal autoregressive assumption. However, identifiability requires non-trivial structural conditions (Conditions 3.1 and 3.3) that exclude degenerate configurations, limiting immediate applicability to real-world data where such conditions may not be verifiable a priori. The paper acknowledges that leveraging the uncovered structure for downstream ML tasks (e.g., building trustworthy foundation models) remains future work, and the empirical validation on music, while suggestive, is small-scale. Open questions include how to handle violations of the structural conditions gracefully and whether the approach extends to non-sequential or high-dimensional settings.
