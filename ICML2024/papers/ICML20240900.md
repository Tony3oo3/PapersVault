## Problem

Existing graph diffusion models learn to denoise by estimating the score or noise function at each step, which implicitly captures graph topology but fails to explicitly model the discrete structural properties (connectivity, planarity, clustering) that determine graph validity. This myopic denoising objective leads to frequent generation of topologically invalid graphs, even for small molecules. The paper asks whether a generative process can be designed to directly predict the final graph structure, thereby explicitly learning valid topology.

## Input / Output

The model takes a sampled node count N and an initial Gaussian noise G_0 ~ N(0, I_N) as input, and simultaneously generates node features X ∈ R^{N×F} and a weighted adjacency matrix A ∈ R^{N×N} by solving a coupled system of SDEs. For 3D molecule generation, the node features encode discrete atom types (one-hot) and continuous 3D coordinates, with an EGNN backbone enforcing equivariance. The network s_θ outputs a 'graph mixture'—a weighted mean over all possible final graphs—rather than a single denoised sample, and a final quantization step (e.g., argmax for atom types, thresholding for edges) converts the continuous output into a discrete graph.

## Method

GruM constructs the generative process as a mixture of endpoint-conditioned Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) bridge processes, where each bridge is pinned to a data graph g and the mixture drift is a weighted average over all data endpoints. The key quantity is the graph mixture D^{Π*}(G_t, t) = ∫ g · p_{T|t}(g|G_t) dg, a weighted mean of all possible final graphs that converges to the true endpoint early in the process. The drift of the mixture SDE is parameterized by replacing the fixed endpoint g with this graph mixture, so the trajectory is driven toward the predicted final structure. Training is simulation-free: the intermediate state G_t is sampled analytically from a Gaussian interpolation between G_0 and G_T (via hyperbolic-sine-weighted mixing), and the network s_θ is trained with a graph mixture matching loss derived from the Girsanov theorem that provably maximizes the likelihood of the generative model. Unlike denoising diffusion, the OU bridge mixture is an exact transport from prior to data distribution by construction, and the learned quantity (a bounded weighted mean in data space) is far easier to approximate than a diverging score or drift function.

## Results

On general graph generation, GruM achieves 90% V.U.N. on Planar (vs. 75% for DiGress, the strongest baseline) and 85% on SBM (vs. 74%), while also attaining the lowest MMD across all four graph statistics on all three datasets. On 2D molecule generation, GruM reaches 99.69% validity on QM9 and 98.65% on ZINC250k, outperforming DiGress (98.19% and 94.99%) and achieving the best FCD (0.108 on QM9, 2.257 on ZINC250k) and scaffold similarity (0.9449 and 0.5299). On 3D molecule generation, GruM attains 87.34% molecule stability on QM9 (vs. 84.6% for Bridge+Force) and is the only method to achieve non-zero molecule stability (0.51%) on GEOM-DRUGS with up to 181 atoms.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GruM outperforms all 13 baselines across every dataset and metric. On general graph generation, the closest competitor is DiGress (discrete diffusion), which achieves 75% V.U.N. on Planar and 74% on SBM, while GruM reaches 90% and 85% respectively; GruM also matches or beats DiGress on all four MMD statistics. Among continuous diffusion baselines (GDSS, ConGress, EDP-GNN), GruM achieves dramatically higher validity (90% vs. 0% V.U.N. on Planar) and lower MMD, confirming that denoising-based approaches fail to capture graph topology. On 2D molecules, GruM surpasses DiGress in validity (99.69% vs. 98.19% on QM9; 98.65% vs. 94.99% on ZINC250k), FCD, NSPDK, and scaffold similarity, and outperforms flow-based models (MoFlow, GraphAF, GraphDF) by large margins on all metrics. On 3D molecules, GruM exceeds Bridge+Force (the strongest bridge-based baseline) in both atom stability (98.81% vs. 98.8%) and molecule stability (87.34% vs. 84.6% on QM9), and is the only method producing non-zero molecule stability on GEOM-DRUGS (0.51% vs. 0.0% for all others), despite not requiring task-specific physical forces.

## Relation to Prior Work

GruM extends the diffusion mixture framework (Peluchetti, 2021; Wu et al., 2022) by replacing Brownian bridges with Ornstein-Uhlenbeck bridges and, crucially, by parameterizing the mixture process through the 'graph mixture'—a weighted mean of final graphs—rather than through the drift function. This departs from denoising diffusion models (GDSS, ConGress, DiGress) that learn score or noise functions, and from Wu et al. (2022) that learns the drift of the mixture process, by instead directly predicting the terminal graph structure. The OU bridge choice enables simulation-free training via closed-form Gaussian marginals, connecting the framework to stochastic interpolants (Albergo et al., 2023) while avoiding score estimation entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The explicit topology prediction via the graph mixture offers a principled alternative to denoising for graph generation, with the practical benefit of simulation-free training (17.5× faster) and the ability to handle both continuous and discrete features simultaneously. The non-zero molecule stability on GEOM-DRUGS (up to 181 atoms) where all prior baselines achieve zero is a notable milestone, though the absolute value (0.51%) remains small. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very large graphs, whether the OU bridge family is truly sufficient for all topological structures, and how the framework generalizes to other topological domains such as protein backbones or manifolds as the authors suggest.
