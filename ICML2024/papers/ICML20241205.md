## Problem

Generative vision-language models (VLMs) trained for next-token prediction show highly inconsistent zero-shot performance on image-text retrieval benchmarks: they achieve near-perfect accuracy on some (e.g., ARO) yet struggle on others (e.g., Winoground, SugarCrepe). The paper identifies that this inconsistency stems from a mismatch between the language prior in the pre-training data and the test benchmark, where some benchmarks inadvertently create implausible negative captions that can be identified by blind language-only models, while others use realistic negatives where the language prior actively hurts. No prior work had provided a principled, training-free mechanism to control for this linguistic bias at test time.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a candidate text caption as primary inputs, and additionally requires one or a few Gaussian noise images (with mean and std derived from the training distribution) to estimate the language prior P_train(t) for debiasing. The output is a scalar match score per image-text pair, computed by evaluating all token log-likelihoods in parallel (unlike sequential generation), which can be directly used to rank K candidate captions for retrieval. The debiasing strength is controlled by a single hyperparameter α ∈ [0,1], which can be set to a fixed value (e.g., α=1) or tuned via grid search on a small held-out validation set.

## Method

The paper introduces VisualGPTScore, which repurposes the autoregressive language generation head of a generative VLM (e.g., BLIP, BLIP-2, LLaVA-1.5) to compute P_train(t|i) as an image-text match score, evaluated in parallel over all tokens rather than sequentially. A first-principles Bayes-rule derivation shows that under train-test distribution shift in the language prior, the optimal retrieval score is P_train(t|i) · P_test(t)/P_train(t), which motivates the α-debiasing formula P_train(t|i) / P_train(t)^α, where α=0 recovers the raw score and α=1 fully removes the language prior. The paper shows this is equivalent to PMI^k with k=1/α, connecting the debiasing to information-theoretic mutual information. To estimate P_train(t) without access to the full training set, the authors demonstrate that Monte Carlo sampling using as few as 1–3 Gaussian noise images (content-free inputs) yields robust estimates comparable to sampling thousands of real training images. The entire pipeline is training-free: no fine-tuning or retraining is required, and the approach is applied off-the-shelf to pre-trained VLMs across nine benchmarks.

## Results

On the ARO benchmark, VisualGPTScore (α=0) achieves near-perfect accuracy of 99.4–99.5% on COCO-Order and Flickr30K-Order, substantially outperforming the best fine-tuned discriminative baseline NegCLIP (86.0/91.0). On SugarCrepe, debiasing with α=α* pushes accuracy to 95.1/92.4/97.4 (Replace/Swap/Add), surpassing BLIP-ITM (88.7/81.3/87.6) and NegCLIP (88.3/76.2/90.2). On the more challenging Winoground and EqBen benchmarks, α-debiasing with a fixed α=1 improves text scores from 27.5→33.7 (Winoground) and 9.6→19.8 (EqBen) over the raw VisualGPTScore, and with LLaVA-1.5 the debiased score reaches 44.3 text / 27.5 group on Winoground versus CLIPScore's 31.3/8.8. On large-scale retrieval (COCO R@1), debiasing lifts performance from 19.7 (α=0) to 48.0 (α=α*), though still trailing BLIP's ITMScore of 71.9.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VisualGPTScore decisively outperforms all discriminative CLIP variants: on ARO-Flickr, the raw score (99.5%) exceeds NegCLIP (91.0%), DAC-LLM (95.7%), and LAION5B-CLIP (31.0%) by 8.5, 3.8, and 68.5 points respectively. Against fine-tuned compositionality methods (SyViC, SGVL, MosaiCLIP, Structure-CLIP, SVLC), VisualGPTScore wins on every ARO sub-dataset and on SugarCrepe (93.3% Replace vs. NegCLIP's 88.3%), requiring no fine-tuning or synthetic data. Notably, the generative head of BLIP (VisualGPTScore) substantially outperforms BLIP's own discriminative heads (BLIP-ITC and BLIP-ITM) on ARO-COCO (99.4 vs. 34.3/45.1) and ARO-Flickr (99.5 vs. 41.7/51.3), suggesting autoregressive scoring better captures compositionality. On SugarCrepe, where negative captions are realistic, debiasing (α=α*) improves over the raw score by 1.8–6.4 points, while on ARO (with implausible negatives) debiasing with large α actually hurts (99.5→44.5 on Flickr), confirming the two-scenario analysis. With LLaVA-1.5, VisualGPTScore(α=1) achieves 44.3 text score on Winoground, beating CLIPScore (31.3), VPEval (12.8), LLMScore (21.3), TIFA (22.8), VQ2 (14.0), and Davidsonian (21.0) by 13–32 points, and reaches 29.3 group score on EqBen versus CLIPScore's 21.4. The one area where VisualGPTScore trails is large-scale retrieval: on COCO R@1, even the debiased score (48.0) falls short of BLIP-ITM (71.9), and on Flickr30K (63.6 vs. 88.8), attributed to the generative model's inherent bias toward common texts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the autoregressive generative head of image-conditioned language models (BLIP, BLIP-2, LLaVA-1.5) as a discriminative scoring function for image-text retrieval, departing from the standard practice of using contrastive (ITC) or matching (ITM) heads trained explicitly for that purpose. The debiasing mechanism draws on the Bayes factor and connects to pointwise mutual information (PMI^k) from the information-retrieval literature, while the Gaussian-noise-image trick for estimating the language prior P_train(t) is inspired by calibration methods for LLMs (Zhao et al., 2021). Conceptually, the paper positions itself as a VLM analogue of the VQA community's well-known lesson that 'blind' language-only models can exploit dataset biases, extending that critique to contemporary compositionality benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that blind language models achieve near-perfect accuracy on benchmarks like ARO-Flickr (99.1%) raises a fundamental question about whether widely-used compositionality benchmarks actually measure visual understanding or merely linguistic plausibility, echoing the VQA crisis of 2017. The training-free α-debiasing technique is simple, requires no retraining, and consistently improves performance on challenging benchmarks (Winoground, EqBen, Flickr30K), suggesting it could become a standard post-processing step. The paper also positions VisualGPTScore as a potential replacement for CLIPScore in text-to-image evaluation, though it acknowledges limitations: the underlying models are trained on noisy web data, the assumption that P_train(t|i) is unbiased is violated in practice (as shown on the training set itself), and the approach still lags behind ITMScore on large-scale retrieval (COCO, Flickr30K).
