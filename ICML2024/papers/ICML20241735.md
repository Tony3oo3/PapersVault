## Problem

Despite the ubiquity of Large Language Models in ML research, the field lacks a working definition of 'LLM' and relies heavily on five common claims about their capabilities—robustness, state-of-the-art status, scale-driven performance, general-purpose technology status, and emergent properties—that are stated uncritically and often lack sufficient empirical grounding or important caveats. This position paper argues that repeating these claims without scrutiny contributes to narratives shaped by business interests and misleads both the research community and the public.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the analytical 'input' is the existing body of LLM research literature, benchmark results, scaling-law studies, and socio-technical critiques, while the 'output' is a three-criteria definition of LLMs, a structured fact-check of five common claims with supporting and counter-evidence, and a set of concrete recommendations for maintaining rigor and diversity in ML research. The paper does not introduce new models or datasets but synthesizes and recontextualizes existing empirical findings to expose gaps and inconsistencies.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-criteria definition of LLMs: (1) the model must model and generate text based on input context, (2) it must receive large-scale pretraining defined by data volume (≥1B tokens for English) rather than parameter count, and (3) it must be used for transfer learning. It then systematically fact-checks five common claims by collecting empirical evidence and counter-arguments from the literature: demonstrating that LLMs remain brittle (shortcut learning, prompt sensitivity, adversarial vulnerability), that few-shot SOTA claims are confounded by benchmark contamination and evaluation methodology, that scale is not the sole driver of performance (data quality, distillation, and inverse scaling all matter), that LLMs do not yet meet the economic criteria for general-purpose technologies, and that 'emergent properties' are used in at least four inconsistent ways with no validated evidence for truly unlearned capabilities. The analysis concludes with concrete recommendations including maintaining research diversity, defining terminology explicitly, avoiding closed models as baselines, conducting rigorous functionality studies, and developing better evaluation methodology.

## Results

This is a position paper without original experimental results. The authors cite key quantitative data points to support their critiques: BERT-base (2018) had 340M parameters while PaLM (2022) reached 540B; few-shot GPT-3 scores 71.8 on SuperGLUE versus 84.6 for fine-tuned RoBERTa; and the LM Contamination Index documents 375 entries of benchmark contamination across models and tasks. The paper proposes a three-criteria definition of LLMs (text generation, ≥1B tokens of pretraining data, transfer learning) and identifies at least four distinct (and often contradictory) definitions of 'emergent properties' in current literature.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method or run controlled experiments against baselines. Instead, it critically examines five widely repeated claims about LLMs—robustness, state-of-the-art status, scale as the sole driver of performance, general-purpose technology status, and emergent properties—by collecting existing empirical evidence and counter-arguments. The authors note that few-shot LLMs do not consistently outperform supervised fine-tuned models (e.g., GPT-3 at 71.8 vs. RoBERTa at 84.6 on SuperGLUE), that scaling is confounded with data quality improvements (PaLM 2, Chinchilla, Phi), and that 'emergent' behaviors may be artifacts of evaluation metric choice (Schaeffer et al., 2023) or benchmark contamination. The paper recommends open-access models (FLAN-T5, BLOOM, LLaMa, FALCON) over closed models like GPT-4 as baselines for reproducibility and fairness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper does not extend a specific method lineage but instead synthesizes and critically re-examines a broad body of empirical and socio-technical work on LLMs. It draws on shortcut-learning studies (McCoy et al., 2019; Rogers et al., 2020), scaling-law analyses (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), contamination documentation (Dodge et al., 2021; Magar & Schwartz, 2022), and economic frameworks for general-purpose technologies (Lipsey et al., 2005) to challenge five widely repeated claims. Conceptually, it departs from the prevailing narrative in LLM research by treating commonly cited properties (robustness, SOTA status, scale-driven performance, generality, emergence) as hypotheses requiring evidence rather than established facts, and it connects these technical critiques to broader concerns about reproducibility, de-democratization, and industry influence in ML.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies concrete knowledge gaps that constrain the field: we lack a precise definition of 'LLM,' we cannot disentangle the contributions of scale versus data quality to performance, we have no methodology to link model outputs to training-data evidence at LLM scale, and we rely on contaminated benchmarks and non-reproducible closed models for evaluation. Its recommendations—using open models as baselines, maintaining methodological diversity, developing contamination-aware evaluation, and specifying working definitions in papers—are actionable and likely to influence conference norms. Open questions include whether the proposed 1B-token threshold for 'large' holds across architectures and languages, whether any genuinely emergent (not-learned-from-data) properties exist, and how to design evaluations that isolate architecture from data confounds.
