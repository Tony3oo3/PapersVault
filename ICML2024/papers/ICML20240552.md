## Problem

While biases in machine learning are typically attributed to dataset properties or algorithmic choices, the paper identifies a previously unexplored source: the architecture design of a neural network itself can cause an untrained model to systematically favor one class over another before seeing any training data. This phenomenon, Initial Guessing Bias (IGB), challenges the naive assumption that a network with zero biases and symmetric initialization will assign data points evenly across classes, and its presence can slow training and limit achievable accuracy on balanced datasets.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes an untrained network (MLP, CNN, ResNet, or Vision Transformer) with specified architectural parameters (activation function, pooling type/kernel size, depth, initialization scheme) and a dataset—either random i.i.d. Gaussian vectors for theory or real RGB images (e.g., CIFAR-10 binary/multiclass splits) for experiments. No training step is performed; the network's predictions are read directly at initialization. The key outputs are the per-class prediction fractions f_c(W) for a fixed weight realization, the IGB severity metric γ = Var_W(μ_c)/Var_χ(O^(c)), and the ensemble-level distribution p_{f_0}(x) over many random initializations, which reveals whether f_0 concentrates at 1/2 (no IGB) or spreads toward 0 and 1 (strong IGB).

## Method

The core theoretical contribution is a two-level distributional analysis of output nodes in the infinite-width limit. Theorem 4.1 shows that each output node O^(c) is Gaussian in the data (fixed weights) with center μ_c that is itself a random variable drawn from a Normal distribution across weight initializations; the ratio γ of the variance of μ_c to the within-dataset variance determines whether the two class output distributions overlap (no IGB) or separate (IGB). Theorem 5.1 establishes that IGB is absent if and only if the data-average of every activated-and-pooled hidden node is zero, providing a general criterion for any activation function. Theorem 5.2 then identifies three amplification mechanisms that drive γ → ∞ (strong IGB, where the entire dataset is assigned to one class): input data offset, increasing max-pooling kernel size, and increasing network depth—though depth only amplifies pre-existing IGB rather than initiating it. The analysis deliberately averages over data first (fixed weights) and then over weights, which breaks self-averaging and reveals the Node Symmetry Breaking that underlies IGB.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Thm. 5.1) establishes that IGB is present if and only if the data-averaged post-activation/pooling value ⟨ρ_i^(1;m)⟩ ≠ 0 for hidden-layer nodes; antisymmetric activations (e.g., tanh) with zero-centered data yield no IGB, while ReLU produces a wide p_{f_0}(x) that does not concentrate at 1/2. In the strong IGB limit (γ → ∞), the distribution collapses to p_{f_0}(x) = ½δ(x) + ½δ(x−1), meaning the untrained network assigns the entire dataset to a single class. Empirically, on CIFAR-10 (binary dog/cat), a 2-layer CNN with ReLU + max pooling shows f_0 most often at 0% or 100%, whereas the same architecture with tanh + average pooling yields f_0 ≈ 50%. The IGB absorption time during training scales with the IGB level γ, and this effect is confirmed across CNNs, ResNets, MLP-mixers, and Vision Transformers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against external baseline methods; instead, it compares architectural and preprocessing choices to isolate the sources of IGB. ReLU + max pooling produces strong IGB (p_{f_0} peaked at 0 and 1), while tanh + average pooling yields no IGB (p_{f_0} concentrated at 1/2), and plain ReLU (no pooling) gives an intermediate wide distribution. Increasing the max-pooling kernel size m, adding a data-standardization offset |K|, or increasing network depth L each drive γ → ∞ (strong IGB), with depth amplifying but not initiating IGB. In transfer-learning experiments on pre-trained EfficientNetV2, replacing the untrained head with a deeper MLP amplifies IGB relative to a single fully-connected head, consistent with the depth-amplification prediction. The paper also shows that structured (correlated) real data exacerbates IGB beyond what is predicted by the i.i.d. Gaussian theory, confirming that model-design bias and data-structure bias compound in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by identifying and formalizing Initial Guessing Bias (IGB), a phenomenon absent from prior analyses of DNNs at initialization. While previous work (Schoenholz et al., 2016; Hanin & Rolnick, 2018; Noci et al., 2022) studied signal propagation, generalization, or weight distributions at initialization, none examined class-level prediction imbalance in untrained models. The authors depart from the standard mean-field approach by inverting the order of averaging (data first, then weights), which is essential to capture the self-averaging violation that underlies IGB. Node-permutation symmetry breaking was noted in a specific shallow-network context by Kang et al. (1993), but this paper generalizes it to deep architectures and connects it directly to a measurable predictive bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard, widely-used design choices (ReLU, max pooling, offset data standardization) systematically bias untrained networks toward a single class challenges a foundational assumption in deep learning and has direct practical implications for architecture selection, initialization, and data preprocessing. The paper provides actionable control levers (activation function choice, pooling kernel size, standardization offset, depth) and quantifies IGB through the ratio γ, but leaves open the full characterization of when IGB is beneficial versus detrimental across diverse training regimes. The relevance to transfer learning and few-shot settings, where fine-tuning involves few gradient steps, suggests IGB could be a previously overlooked factor in practical model performance.
