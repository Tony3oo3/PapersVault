## Problem

Existing RL methods for LLMs (e.g., PPO for RLHF) optimize single-turn rewards and cannot perform credit assignment or reason about past actions across multiple turns. Multi-turn RL for LLMs faces a fundamental tension: on-policy methods are sample-inefficient because environment interaction is slow and expensive, while off-policy token-level methods must propagate reward over extremely long horizons (taking >10 days to converge), and utterance-level methods face an enormous variable-size action space that makes Bellman maximization intractable.

## Input / Output

The high-level MDP takes the full multi-turn interaction history (LLM utterances and environment observations) as state, with each variable-length utterance as an action; the low-level MDP embeds within it, treating each individual token as an action conditioned on the interaction history plus previously generated tokens in the current turn. The utterance-level critic (RoBERTa-base or GPT-2 with an MLP head) outputs a scalar Q-value or V-value per state-utterance pair, while the token-level actor (GPT-2 100M or Mistral-7B) generates tokens autoregressively. The advantage computed from the utterance-level Q-function is injected as a single terminal reward at the end of each token sequence, rather than as a per-token signal.

## Method

ArCHer runs two RL algorithms in parallel at different temporal scales: a high-level off-policy TD-learning algorithm trains an utterance-level Q-function and V-function via Bellman bootstrapping on a replay buffer of all past interactions, while a low-level on-policy REINFORCE algorithm trains the token-level actor to maximize the utterance-level advantage as a terminal reward. This design sidesteps both the long-horizon error compounding of token-level critics and the intractable maximization over variable-length utterances in Bellman backups, since the high-level critic operates at the coarser utterance timescale and the low-level policy only needs to optimize against a fixed scalar reward. The framework is modular: a token-level baseline value function can be added to reduce policy gradient variance for long utterances, and the offline variant replaces TD learning with IQL (expectile regression) for the critic and AWR (advantage-weighted regression) for the actor to handle out-of-distribution actions. Theoretically, the hierarchical design requires weaker Bellman completeness conditions on the critic function class and yields a γ√L tighter statistical error bound on advantage estimation compared to a token-level critic, where L is the maximum utterance length.

## Results

ArCHer achieves approximately 100× sample efficiency over token-level PPO: while PPO requires over 100k environment samples to reach an average return above −17 on Twenty Questions, ArCHer attains the same threshold with fewer than 1,000 samples. On WebShop, ArCHer fine-tuning a GPT-2 base model outperforms GPT-3.5 equipped with both ReAct and expert-written prompting strategies, making it the only RL-fine-tuned method to surpass a much larger LLM with prompting. Across the four harder tasks (Twenty Questions Subset, Twenty Questions, Guess My City, WebShop), ArCHer converges to the best final performance among all compared methods, while matching the best prior approach on the simpler Detective Game task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against token-level PPO, ArCHer wins decisively on sample efficiency: PPO cannot effectively reuse past data and requires at least 1,024 on-policy rollouts per gradient step to improve stably, while ArCHer reaches comparable returns with ~100× fewer samples. Against CHAI (the strongest utterance-level off-policy baseline), ArCHer initially learns slower but converges to a better final policy, as CHAI's frozen behavior policy limits the margin for improvement. Against Filtered BC, ArCHer shows steady improvement with more data while BC plateaus early, confirming that the RL component is doing meaningful work beyond imitation. On WebShop specifically, ArCHer with a 100M-parameter GPT-2 actor outperforms GPT-3.5 (a model orders of magnitude larger) when the latter is equipped with ReAct or expert prompting, demonstrating that multi-turn RL fine-tuning can close the gap with much larger models. ArCHer also scales favorably: replacing the GPT-2 actor with a 7B Mistral model accelerates convergence further on Twenty Questions Subset.

## Relation to Prior Work

ArCHer sits at the intersection of three prior lines of work: single-turn RL for LLMs (PPO/RLHF, DPO), token-level off-policy RL (ILQL), and utterance-level RL (CHAI). It departs from token-level methods by avoiding Bellman backups over individual tokens (which suffer from error compounding over long horizons), and from utterance-level methods by avoiding explicit maximization over an enormous variable-size action space. The key architectural contribution is a hierarchical decomposition—off-policy TD learning for an utterance-level critic at the high level, and on-policy policy gradient for a token-level actor at the low level—that inherits implementation details from both single-turn RLHF pipelines and classical hierarchical RL, while the theoretical analysis (weaker Bellman completeness requirements, γ√L smaller statistical error) formalizes why this decomposition is principled rather than merely heuristic.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100× sample-efficiency gain over PPO and the ability to scale from GPT-2 to Mistral-7B make ArCHer a practical framework for training LLM agents on tasks requiring multi-turn planning, information gathering, and delayed rewards—settings where single-turn RLHF fundamentally cannot operate. The general framework design (swappable high-level and low-level algorithms, online/offline modes, optional token-level baseline) lowers the barrier for practitioners to adopt multi-turn RL without reimplementing core components. Open questions include reducing the thousands-of-interactions requirement to hundreds, extending to environments with human-in-the-loop rewards, and understanding whether the hierarchical structure remains advantageous as base models grow beyond 7B parameters where in-context learning may partially substitute for learned credit assignment.
