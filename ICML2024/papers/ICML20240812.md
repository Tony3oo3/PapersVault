## Problem

Monte Carlo random features approximate kernels with a stochastic O_P(1/√M) error rate, and prior attempts to apply quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) to shift-invariant kernels failed because the Bochner integrand has infinite Hardy-Krause variation, making the Koksma-Hlawka inequality inapplicable. This paper shows that for a class of kernels including Gaussian and Cauchy, the boundary singularity is mild enough that QMC still yields a deterministic O(log^a(M)/M) error bound, and that this improvement translates into substantially fewer features for kernel ridge regression.

## Input / Output

The method consumes data points x, x' ∈ R^d and a kernel K with an integral representation over [0,1]^p (p = d+1 for shift-invariant kernels after a CDF change of variables, or p for non-shift-invariant kernels). It produces a deterministic approximated kernel K_M(x,x') = (1/M)Σ ψ(x,ω_i)ψ(x',ω_i) where the ω_i are the first M points of the (d+1)-dimensional Halton sequence, a QMC feature vector φ_M(x) ∈ R^M, and for KRR a regression estimate f̂_{λ,M}(x). Because the QMC points are deterministic, the approximation error is a deterministic bound (not a high-probability statement), and the choice of M in KRR does not depend on the confidence level δ.

## Method

For shift-invariant kernels, Bochner's theorem is combined with a change of variables through the component CDFs to rewrite the kernel as an integral over [0,1]^{d+1}; the integrand has unbounded variation near the boundary, but QMC Condition 1 (bounding dΦ_i^{-1}/dt ≤ C_i/min(t,1−t)) ensures the singularity is integrable. The proof constructs a low-variation extension f̃_n of the integrand on a sub-cube [ε_n, 1−ε_n]^{d+1}, applies the Koksma-Hlawka inequality there, and controls the boundary contribution using the Halton sequence's property of avoiding the unit cube boundary (Owen, 2006), yielding |K_M − K| ≤ C(log M)^{2d+1}/M. For non-shift-invariant kernels (min, Brownian bridge, iterative, natural cubic spline, product kernels), QMC Condition 2 (bounded Hardy-Krause variation of the integrand) directly gives |K_M − K| ≤ C_H(p)(log M)^p/M via Koksma-Hlawka. In kernel ridge regression, the improved O(1/M) kernel approximation propagates to the integral operator and kernel matrix spectrum, reducing the required number of features from M ≍ n^{2r/(2r+1)} (MC) to M ≍ n^{1/(2r+1)} (QMC) to match the exact KRR excess risk rate n^{−2r/(2r+1)}.

## Results

Theoretically, QMC features improve the kernel approximation error from O_P(1/√M) (MC) to O((log M)^(2d+1)/M) for shift-invariant kernels like Gaussian and Cauchy (Theorem 2.2), and to O((log M)^p/M) for non-shift-invariant kernels with bounded variation (Theorem 2.5). For kernel ridge regression, QMCF-KRR requires only M ≍ n^(1/(2r+1)) features to match the excess risk rate of exact KRR, compared to M ≍ n^(2r/(2r+1)) for MC-based RF-KRR (Theorem 3.2). Empirically, on synthetic regression tasks (n=10⁴, d=1–4, r=1), QMC features achieve the same test MSE as MC random features with substantially fewer features and converge to the exact KRR error at a much faster rate, with near-negligible confidence bands across 1000 realizations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares QMCF-KRR against two baselines: RF-KRR (Monte Carlo random features, Rudi & Rosasco 2017) and exact KRR (Caponnetto & De Vito 2007). Against RF-KRR, QMCF-KRR clearly wins: for r=1, QMC needs only M ≍ n^(1/3) features versus M ≍ n^(2/3) for MC to achieve the same excess risk rate, a substantial reduction in computational cost. Empirically (Figures 3–4), QMC reaches the same test MSE as MC with fewer features across all kernel/dimension combinations (Gaussian and Min kernels, d=1–4), and its confidence bands are nearly zero-width compared to MC's wide bands, indicating far more stable performance. In many cases the QMC and MC confidence bands do not overlap, confirming a statistically significant advantage. Against exact KRR, both RF-KRR and QMCF-KRR converge to the oracle error, but QMC converges at a faster rate in M. The advantage is most pronounced when r > 1/2 (smoother regression functions); for r = 1/2 the gap narrows but QMC still shows superior stability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the random features framework (Rahimi & Recht, 2007) and the RF-KRR theory of Rudi & Rosasco (2017) by replacing i.i.d. Monte Carlo sampling with deterministic Halton quasi-Monte Carlo sequences. Prior QMC attempts for shift-invariant kernels (Avron et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2014) were blocked by the infinite Hardy-Krause variation of the Bochner integrand, rendering the Koksma-Hlawka inequality inapplicable. This paper overcomes that obstacle by exploiting the boundary-avoiding geometry of the Halton sequence to show the singularity is mild enough to still yield a deterministic O(log^a(M)/M) error bound, and further extends the analysis to non-shift-invariant kernels with bounded-variation integrands (min kernel, Brownian bridge, natural cubic spline, product kernels).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result reduces the number of features needed for kernel ridge regression from M ≍ n^(2r/(2r+1)) (MC) to M ≍ n^(1/(2r+1)) (QMC) while preserving the same excess risk rate, a substantial computational saving when the regression function is smooth (r > 1/2). The deterministic (non-random) error bound also simplifies practical feature-count selection since M no longer depends on a confidence parameter δ. However, the paper's own experiments show QMC features are most effective in low dimensions (d ≤ 4) and do not exhibit ideal performance for d > 10, leaving the high-dimensional regime as a key open question. The approach is easily implementable (Halton sequence is available in standard libraries), making it a practical drop-in replacement for MC random features in low-to-moderate dimensional kernel methods.
