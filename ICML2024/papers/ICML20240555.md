## Problem

While more expressive variants of the 1-WL and MPNNs (e.g., subgraph-augmented versions) empirically improve predictive performance, prior VC-dimension analyses tied to 1-WL expressivity predict the opposite: greater expressivity should worsen generalization. The paper asks under what precise conditions increased expressivity actually translates into better generalization, and shows that the graph-isomorphism perspective alone is insufficient to answer this question.

## Input / Output

For MPNNs, the input is a labeled graph with d-dimensional vertex features consistent with vertex labels and an adjacency matrix; for kernel methods, the input is a pair of graphs. The method produces graph class labels (for classification), graph embedding vectors in R^d, kernel similarity scores, and—critically—VC dimension bounds of the form Θ(r²/λ²) parameterized by the geometric margin λ separating the two classes in the embedding space, rather than by the ambient dimension d. The margin-based bounds are dimension-free, which is the key structural difference from standard VC bounds.

## Method

The paper introduces the 1-WL_F architecture, a simplified subgraph-augmented variant of the 1-WL that initializes color refinement with vertex labels encoding which predefined subgraphs from a set F contain each vertex, and derives matching MPNN_F and 1-WLOA_F kernel variants. Using the theory of partial concepts (Alon et al., 2021), it proves tight margin-based VC-dimension bounds of Θ(r²/λ²) for all these architectures, showing that generalization depends on the data's margin rather than the number of distinguishable graph pairs. It then characterizes three regimes: (i) data distributions where 1-WL_F makes the data linearly separable with maximum margin √2 while 1-WL cannot separate it at all, (ii) distributions where added expressivity shrinks the margin and worsens generalization, and (iii) conditions (via Proposition 13 on color-splitting) under which 1-WLOA_F provably increases the margin. Finally, building on Ji & Telgarsky (2019), it proves that gradient flow on linear MPNNs aligns layer weights and converges to the maximum-margin solution, connecting the theoretical margin bounds to practical training dynamics.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset validating Proposition 9 (Table 1), the 1-WL and 1-WLOA kernels achieve near-random test accuracies (46.6–47.3% and 36.8–37.8% respectively) and are not linearly separable, while the subgraph-augmented 1-WL_F and 1-WLOA_F kernels achieve 100% test accuracy with margins < 0.0001. On ER graphs, the 1-WL_F kernel achieves a positive margin for all subgraph choices (e.g., 0.037 for F={C₄} at p=0.05), whereas the 1-WL kernel cannot separate any dataset with a positive margin. On the PROTEINS dataset, the 1-WL_F kernel's margin is strictly smaller than 1-WL's for all F, and correspondingly exhibits a larger train-test accuracy gap, confirming the theoretical prediction that smaller margin worsens generalization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three subgraph-augmented architectures (1-WL_F, 1-WLOA_F, MPNN_F) against their un-augmented counterparts (1-WL, 1-WLOA, MPNN) on a synthetic dataset designed to validate Proposition 9. The 1-WL_F kernel achieves 100% test accuracy across all graph sizes (n=16 to 128), outperforming the 1-WL baseline by over 53 percentage points (e.g., 100.0% vs. 46.6% at n=16) and the 1-WLOA baseline by over 63 points. Similarly, MPNN_F achieves 100% accuracy versus 47.9–49.0% for the standard MPNN. On ER graphs, the 1-WL_F kernel achieves a positive margin (e.g., 0.037 for F={C₄}, p=0.05) while the 1-WL kernel cannot separate the data at all, and the margin decrease with increasing edge probability (0.037→0.002) directly correlates with worsening generalization (0.1%→31.2% train-test gap). On PROTEINS, the 1-WL_F kernel's margin is strictly smaller than 1-WL's for all subgraph choices, and it correspondingly shows a larger train-test accuracy gap, confirming that added expressivity does not always improve generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) the 1-WL/MPNN expressivity hierarchy (Morris et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2019; Bouritsas et al., 2020), (2) VC-dimension-based generalization analysis for graph models (Morris et al., 2023; Scarselli et al., 2018), and (3) classical margin theory for partial concepts (Alon et al., 2021). It departs from Morris et al. (2023), who showed that greater 1-WL expressivity increases VC dimension and thus worsens generalization, by introducing the data-dependent margin as the correct lens: more expressivity helps generalization only when it increases (or creates) the margin, and can hurt when it shrinks it. The gradient-flow alignment result extends Ji & Telgarsky (2019) from deep linear networks to linear MPNNs with graph-structured inputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a tension in the GNN literature: empirical evidence consistently shows that more expressive architectures (e.g., subgraph-augmented MPNNs) generalize better, yet the standard VC-dimension argument predicts the opposite. By showing that the margin, not the isomorphism-distinguishing power, is the operative quantity, the paper provides a principled criterion for when to add expressivity and when it is counterproductive. Limitations include the focus on linear/linear-separable settings (the VC bounds apply to MPNNs behaving as linear classifiers), the assumption of fixed graph order n, and the fact that the margin-based conditions (Propositions 11–13) are sufficient but not necessary. Open questions include extending the margin framework to non-linear MPNNs, characterizing margins for k-WL-based architectures, and understanding how practical training procedures (SGD with finite steps, regularization) interact with the maximum-margin limit.
