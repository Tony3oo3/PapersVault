## Problem

While LLMs are increasingly proposed as cognitive models of human learners, it remains unclear whether they replicate the specific cognitive biases observed in children during arithmetic word problem solving, particularly given that LLMs are trained predominantly on adult-generated data. The paper addresses this gap by decomposing the solving process into three distinct cognitive steps—text comprehension, solution planning, and solution execution—and systematically testing whether LLMs exhibit the same biases as children at each step.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241571/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes pairs of generated math word problems that differ in exactly one controlled feature (e.g., consistent vs. inconsistent relational phrasing, transfer vs. comparison structure, or presence vs. absence of arithmetic carry) and produces a single numerical answer from each LLM via zero-shot or chain-of-thought prompting. From the paired binary accuracy outcomes, the paper estimates the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) with two-tailed paired-sample tests, yielding a bias effect size and p-value per model and per test. Each test uses 500 problem pairs generated by the neuro-symbolic pipeline, with the answer extracted as the first number in the model's output.

## Method

The core contribution is a neuro-symbolic problem generation pipeline built on the MATHWORLD logical formalism, which proceeds in four steps: sampling a predicate sequence (problem structure), instantiating it with properties to form a mental model, converting to templated natural language, and correcting linguistic errors via GPT-3.5 Turbo. This pipeline enables fine-grained, independent control over structural, numerical, and linguistic features while guaranteeing faithfulness between the surface text and the underlying semantics. The paper tests three child-established biases—consistency bias (text comprehension), transfer vs. comparison bias (solution planning), and the carry effect (solution execution)—by generating paired problems that isolate each feature while holding all others constant. CATE is estimated from paired accuracy across 500 problem pairs with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control, evaluated on LLaMA2 (7B/13B/70B), Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8x7B, GPT-3.5 Turbo, and GPT-4 Turbo in both base and instruction-tuned variants under direct and chain-of-thought prompting.

## Results

Across 23 model-prompting combinations, LLMs exhibit significant consistency bias (20/23 significant CATEs) and transfer-vs-comparison bias (most models show significant positive CATEs), mirroring child-like cognitive biases. For example, under CoT prompting, LLaMA2 13B shows a 21.6-point CATE for consistency bias (30.2% vs 8.6% accuracy) and a 24.6-point CATE for transfer-vs-comparison bias (37.8% vs 13.2%). In stark contrast, the carry effect is absent: only 1 out of 23 tests yields a significant CATE, with most CATEs near zero (e.g., GPT-4 Turbo: 0.0; Mixtral 8x7B: 0.0), indicating LLMs are insensitive to arithmetic carry operations that impair children.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to compare against baselines; instead, it benchmarks 11 model-prompting configurations (LLaMA2 7B/13B/70B, Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8x7B, GPT-3.5 Turbo, GPT-4 Turbo; each in direct and CoT modes) against three child-like cognitive biases. For consistency bias, all models except LLaMA2 70B Chat (direct) and Mixtral 8x7B Instruct (direct) show significant effects, with CoT prompting generally amplifying the CATE (e.g., LLaMA2 13B goes from 3.2 to 21.6 points). For transfer-vs-comparison bias, instruction-tuned models under CoT show the largest effects (LLaMA2 70B Chat: 22.8; LLaMA2 13B Chat: 31.4), while pretrained-only models show smaller or sometimes reversed effects (Mistral 7B CoT: −9.0). The carry effect is uniformly absent across all models and prompting strategies, with only LLaMA2 7B (direct, CATE=4.8, p=0.009) and LLaMA2 7B (CoT, CATE=−5.6, p=0.006) reaching significance—neither in the direction predicted by child data. GPT-4 Turbo achieves the highest absolute accuracy (90–99%) yet still exhibits consistency and transfer-vs-comparison biases, suggesting these are not merely artifacts of limited model capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MATHWORLD neuro-symbolic formalism from Opedal et al. (2023) to generate controlled arithmetic word problems, extending that framework from a general world-model representation into a targeted bias-testing pipeline. It positions itself alongside prior work comparing human and LLM biases in syllogisms (Ando et al., 2023; Eisape et al., 2024), code generation (Jones and Steinhardt, 2022), and non-mathematical inference (Dasgupta et al., 2023), but departs by grounding the comparison in the learning-science literature on children's word-problem solving and decomposing the task into three cognitive steps (text comprehension, solution planning, solution execution). The causal-inference methodology (CATE estimation with FDR control) follows Feder et al. (2022), and the problem-generation approach is distinguished from prior template-based generators (Polozov et al., 2015; Koncel-Kedziorski et al., 2016a) by its explicit control over arithmetic concepts and structural features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean dissociation—LLMs replicate child-like biases in text comprehension and solution planning but not in arithmetic execution—provides a concrete, testable boundary for how faithfully LLMs model human cognition, with direct implications for educational applications like student modeling and automated tutoring. The neuro-symbolic generation pipeline with 0% error rates offers a reusable tool for future bias studies with fine-grained control over problem features. Key limitations include English-only evaluation, no grade-level control, a simplified three-step model that omits shortcut strategies and propositional representations, and the inability to compare absolute performance with children. Open questions include whether biases can be modulated via in-context examples, whether findings generalize across languages (e.g., the carry effect is stronger in German), and whether the absence of the carry effect reflects a fundamental algorithmic difference in how LLMs perform arithmetic versus human working-memory-based computation.
