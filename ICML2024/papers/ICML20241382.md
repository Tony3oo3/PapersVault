## Problem

In partially observable multi-agent games, fast adaptation to unknown peers requires the ego agent to actively probe and identify peer strategies before responding optimally, yet existing opponent-modeling methods assume peer behaviors are readily observable and do not address the exploration problem. Without explicit incentives for exploration, policies converge to local optima that avoid informative but short-term-costly actions (e.g., visiting watchtowers or pursuing showdowns), making cross-episode adaptation infeasible.

## Input / Output

The ego agent receives a per-step observation vector (e.g., 13-dim for Kuhn Poker, 105-dim for PO-Overcooked, 37-dim for Predator-Prey-W) plus a variable-length context of observation-action pairs spanning up to N_eps past episodes, which may include an incomplete current episode. The context encoder compresses this variable-length input into a fixed-length embedding z¹ ∈ ℝ^m via per-step MLP features averaged over all steps and episodes, then a second MLP. The policy outputs a discrete action from the environment's action space, while the auxiliary identifier outputs a categorical posterior over each peer's policy index in the training pool.

## Method

PACE trains a context-aware policy via PPO with a shared MLP context encoder and an auxiliary peer-identification head. The key mechanism is a peer identification reward r^e computed as the mean posterior probability the identifier assigns to the true peer policies, which operationalizes the mutual information objective I(ψ, C¹) as an intrinsic reward added to the task reward (with a linearly decaying coefficient c). This reward incentivizes the policy to take actions that make the context more discriminative of peer identity, inducing active exploration (e.g., pursuing showdowns in Kuhn Poker or crossing walls in PO-Overcooked) during early adaptation episodes. The encoder and identifier are jointly trained end-to-end with the policy using the same PPO mini-batches, with the auxiliary cross-entropy loss weighted at 1.0, and the encoder is warmed up for M_w steps using only the auxiliary loss before RL training begins. During online adaptation, the exploration reward is removed since peer identities are unknown, and the policy relies solely on the learned context-aware behavior to balance exploration and exploitation.

## Results

PACE achieves the best average episodic performance across all three environments: 0.047±0.004 reward in Kuhn Poker (vs. 0.027 for the best baseline LIAMX), 0.553±0.029 success rate in PO-Overcooked (vs. 0.111 for the best baseline Generalist), and -3.93±0.04 reward in Predator-Prey-W (vs. -4.34 for the best baseline LIAMX). In PO-Overcooked, PACE is the only method capable of meaningful adaptation, with all baselines failing at success rates near 0.1. The Oracle best-response upper bounds are 0.077 (Kuhn Poker), 1.0 (PO-Overcooked), and -2.82 (Predator-Prey-W).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PACE outperforms all baselines in every environment. Against GSCU (Fu et al., ICML 2022), which trains a conditional policy on a pre-trained opponent model, PACE achieves 0.047 vs. -0.041 in Kuhn Poker and 0.553 vs. 0.100 in PO-Overcooked; GSCU's conservative Nash-equilibrium fallback and reliance on post-episode peer observations prevent effective exploration. Against LIAM (Papoudakis et al., NeurIPS 2021), which models the opponent's observation and action as an auxiliary task, PACE leads by 0.023 in Kuhn Poker (0.047 vs. 0.024) and by a large margin in PO-Overcooked (0.553 vs. 0.054), as LIAM's single-episode context is insufficient for cross-episode adaptation. Against LILI (Xie et al., CoRL 2021), which encodes the opponent as environment dynamics via the last episode, PACE achieves 0.047 vs. 0.025 in Kuhn Poker and 0.553 vs. 0.090 in PO-Overcooked; the t-SNE visualization confirms that LILI's latent space fails to separate peer identities while PACE's does. The cross-episode variant LIAMX is the closest competitor, reaching 0.027 in Kuhn Poker and -4.34 in Predator-Prey-W, but still trails PACE because its auxiliary objective only supervises the encoder without generating exploration rewards to maximize mutual information.

## Relation to Prior Work

PACE builds on the multi-episode return formulation from LILI (Xie et al., 2021) and the broader fast-adaptation literature (GSCU, LIAM, meta-learning approaches), but departs from them in a key way: prior opponent-modeling methods assume peer behaviors are readily observable, whereas PACE targets partially observable settings where the ego agent must actively explore to reveal peer strategies. The peer identification reward, derived from a mutual information objective between peer identity and interaction context, is a novel mechanism that repurposes an auxiliary classification task as an intrinsic exploration signal during online adaptation. This contrasts with prior multi-agent exploration work (Maven, CMAE, curiosity-driven methods) that focuses on exploration during training to find optimal policies, rather than exploration during online interaction to identify unknown peers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in multi-agent RL: in partially observable games, agents cannot passively infer peer strategies and must invest in exploratory actions that yield no immediate task reward. PACE's peer identification reward provides a principled way to incentivize such exploration, and the method works across competitive, cooperative, and mixed settings. Key limitations include the requirement for a diverse training peer pool, the assumption of a fixed number of peers between training and testing, and the absence of human peers in the main experiments. Open questions include extending to heterogeneous multi-agent games, embodied 3D scenarios, and evaluating how PACE agents interact with human partners in real-world settings.
