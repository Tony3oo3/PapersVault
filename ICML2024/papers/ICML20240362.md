## Problem

Most LLM benchmarks focus exclusively on performance metrics, reducing evaluation to training targets that fail to reveal how models actually solve tasks. While individual cognitive psychology experiments have been applied to LLMs in isolation, no prior study has provided a holistic, standardized benchmark combining multiple validated behavioral paradigms to characterize the full cognitive phenotype of language models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240362/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

CogBench takes text-based prompts derived from seven procedurally generated cognitive psychology experiments and submits them to LLMs purely via in-context learning (temperature=0, no fine-tuning), with past behavior concatenated into the prompt to induce learning across trials. The output is a 10-metric cognitive phenotype profile—spanning prior/likelihood weighting, directed and random exploration, meta-cognition, learning rate, optimism bias, model-basedness, temporal discounting, and risk-taking—each human-normalized so that 0 corresponds to a random agent and 1 to the average human subject, alongside raw performance scores per task.

## Method

The benchmark adapts seven canonical cognitive psychology experiments (probabilistic reasoning, horizon task, restless bandit, instrumental learning, two-step task, temporal discounting, and BART) into text-based prompts that any LLM can complete, extracting ten behavioral metrics via task-specific computational models (e.g., generalized Bayes rule regression for prior/likelihood weighting, Rescorla-Wagner ± for optimism bias, interaction-term regression for model-basedness, adjusted QSR for meta-cognition). Applied to 40 LLMs, the paper uses multi-level regression to test hypotheses about model size, RLHF, code fine-tuning, and open-source status while accounting for the nested hierarchy of fine-tuned model families (e.g., LLaMA-2-13, LLaMA-2-13-chat, CodeLlama-13B as one group). UMAP visualization of the ten behavioral metrics reveals clear clustering by RLHF status, and a separate prompt-engineering experiment quantifies how chain-of-thought and take-a-step-back prompting differentially affect probabilistic reasoning versus model-basedness across five models.

## Results

GPT-4 achieves human-level performance in 5 out of 6 tasks, while all 40 evaluated LLMs demonstrate competence in at least 3 of 6 tasks. Multi-level regression reveals that model size significantly improves performance (β=0.277±0.39, z=14.1, p<0.001) and model-basedness (β=0.481±0.22, z=4.2, p<0.001), while RLHF significantly enhances meta-cognition (β=0.461±0.15, z=5.9, p<0.001) and reduces L2 distance to human behavior by 11.7%. Surprisingly, open-source models are less risk-prone than proprietary models (β=−0.612±0.11, z=−11.4, p<0.001), and code fine-tuning shows no significant effect on performance or model-basedness. For prompt engineering, CoT yields a 9.01% average increase in posterior accuracy and 64.59% increase in model-basedness, while SB yields 3.10% and 118.59% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two prompt-engineering techniques—chain-of-thought (CoT) and take-a-step-back (SB)—against unmodified baseline models across five LLMs (GPT-4, PaLM-2, Claude-1/2, LLaMA-2). On probabilistic reasoning (posterior accuracy), CoT outperforms SB, yielding a 9.01% average improvement over baseline versus SB's 3.10%. On model-basedness (two-step task), SB substantially outperforms CoT, producing a 118.59% increase over baseline compared to CoT's 64.59%. This suggests CoT is better suited for step-by-step mathematical/probabilistic reasoning, while SB's abstraction-first approach more effectively promotes model-based planning. Both techniques improve both metrics over baseline, confirming their general utility, but the relative advantage depends on the cognitive target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the emerging line of work applying cognitive psychology to LLM evaluation (Binz & Schulz 2023, Dasgupta et al. 2022, Hagendorff et al. 2023, Ullman 2023), which had previously examined individual tasks in isolation, by consolidating seven canonical experiments into a unified benchmark with ten behavioral metrics. It departs from standard LLM benchmarks (MMLU, BIG-bench, Chatbot Arena) that focus predominantly on performance scores, instead emphasizing *how* tasks are solved through computational cognitive modeling (Rescorla-Wagner fitting, regression-based model-basedness, QSR meta-cognition). The multi-level regression framework for accounting for fine-tuned model families is a methodological contribution that addresses the nested structure of the LLM ecosystem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CogBench provides the first holistic behavioral phenotyping toolkit for LLMs, revealing that performance metrics alone can mask critical behavioral differences (e.g., LLaMA-2-70 and LLaMA-2-70-chat achieve identical BART scores but exhibit opposite risk-taking). Open questions include whether these cognitive-psychology constructs have external validity for artificial agents, whether a single LLM should be treated as one subject or a population, and how to handle the limited transparency of proprietary models. The paper also identifies concrete future directions: extending task coverage, automating prompt-engineering evaluation, and studying impersonation and meta-in-context learning effects.
