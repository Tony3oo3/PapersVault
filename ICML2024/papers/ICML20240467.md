## Problem

Existing prompting techniques for improving LLM reasoning and factuality—chain-of-thought, self-consistency, self-reflection—operate on a single model instance and cannot leverage cross-instance verification. This paper asks whether a complementary multiagent approach, where multiple LLM instances propose and debate their individual answers over multiple rounds, can improve both reasoning accuracy and factual validity beyond what any single instance achieves.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240467/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text query (arithmetic expression, grade-school math word problem, chess position in PGN notation, multiple-choice factual question, or biography prompt) and feeds it simultaneously to multiple identical or heterogeneous LLM instances. Each agent's full response—including the reasoning chain and final answer—is concatenated as context for every other agent in subsequent debate rounds. The output is a single consensus answer, determined by majority vote across agents after the final debate round, requiring only black-box API access with no model-internal signals such as token likelihoods or gradients.

## Method

The core procedure is an iterative multiagent debate: (1) N agents independently generate initial answers to a query; (2) in each subsequent round, every agent receives the full responses (reasoning + answer) of all other agents as context and is prompted to update its own response in light of them; (3) this repeats for a fixed number of rounds, after which the majority answer is taken. Two prompt variants—short debate ('based off the opinion of other agents') and long debate ('using the opinion of other agents as additional advice')—control how strongly agents are encouraged to revise, trading convergence speed against final accuracy. The approach is orthogonal to existing prompting methods (it is combined with zero-shot chain-of-thought in evaluations), works with heterogeneous model pairs (e.g., ChatGPT and Bard), and can incorporate summarization of other agents' responses to reduce context length when agent counts are large. A key empirical finding is that debate can correct answers even when all agents are initially wrong, because agents cross-examine each other's reasoning chains rather than merely amplifying a pre-existing correct answer.

## Results

On reasoning tasks (3 agents, 2 debate rounds, GPT-3.5), multiagent debate achieves 81.8% on Arithmetic, 85.0% on GSM8K, and a Stockfish pawn score of 122.9 on Chess, outperforming single-agent baselines by 14.8, 8.0, and 31.5 points respectively. On factuality tasks, debate reaches 73.8% on the new Biographies benchmark, 71.1% on MMLU, and 45.2% on Chess Move Validity, improving over single-agent by 7.8, 7.2, and 15.9 points. The method also transfers to GPT-4 (88.0% MMLU, 56.0% MATH) and open-source Llama-7B (29.3% GSM8K, 47.7% MMLU), and outperforms majority voting over 50 agents on GSM8K (89.0% vs. 85.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023), multiagent debate wins on every task: +9.7 pts on Arithmetic (81.8 vs. 72.1), +10.0 pts on GSM8K (85.0 vs. 75.0), +20.8 pawn score on Chess (122.9 vs. 102.1), and +13.4 pts on MMLU (71.1 vs. 57.7), where reflection actually degrades performance below the single-agent baseline. Against Self-Consistency / majority voting (Wang et al., 2022; Lewkowycz et al., 2022), debate gains +6.8 pts on Arithmetic, +4.0 pts on GSM8K, +17.7 pawn score on Chess, and +4.1 pts on MMLU, confirming that iterative cross-agent critique adds value beyond simple aggregation. Against the concurrent multiagent debate formulation of Liang et al. (2023) (affirmative/negative/moderator), the paper's symmetric all-to-all debate substantially outperforms on all three tested tasks: 81.8 vs. 51.5 on Arithmetic, 84.0 vs. 61.0 on GSM8K, and 71.1 vs. 46.0 on MMLU (Table A6). The method also beats a 50-agent majority-vote ensemble on GSM8K (89.0 vs. 85.0) and MMLU (71.0 vs. 67.0) with only 10 agents and 2 rounds (Table A4), and outperforms an ensemble of self-reflection agents (Table A8), indicating that inter-agent debate is the critical ingredient rather than mere model diversity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the 'Society of Mind' framework (Minsky, 1988) and the AI-safety debate formulation (Irving et al., 2018) into a practical inference-time technique for LLMs, departing from prior work that applies prompting improvements (chain-of-thought, self-consistency, self-reflection) to a single model instance. Rather than simple majority voting across independent samples (Wang et al., 2022; Lewkowycz et al., 2022) or single-agent self-reflection (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023), the method introduces iterative multi-round debate where agents exchange full reasoning chains and update their answers. It is positioned as orthogonal to and composable with existing prompting strategies, and is distinguished from concurrent multiagent debate work (Liang et al., 2023; Chan et al., 2023) by its symmetric, non-adversarial formulation where all agents simultaneously revise based on all others' responses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The approach is immediately deployable on black-box commercial APIs without fine-tuning, making it a practical tool for reducing hallucinations and improving reasoning in production LLM deployments. The finding that self-reflection can actually degrade factuality (MMLU drops from 63.9 to 57.7) while multiagent debate improves it challenges the common assumption that self-critique is always beneficial. Key limitations include the multiplicative computational cost (multiple agents × multiple rounds), context-length degradation in longer debates, and the observation that debate can converge to a confidently wrong consensus. Open questions include whether debate outputs can be distilled back into a single model, how the method scales to very large agent populations, and whether the 'ease of persuasion' signal can be formalized as a calibrated uncertainty measure.
