## Problem

Robust Reinforcement Learning has been formalized within Markov game theory, where pure policies may lack a value (Nash equilibrium) and mixed policies only guarantee expected payoffs rather than deterministic ones. This paper is the first to frame RRL within positional differential game theory, which supports pure policies with deterministic payoffs and, under Isaacs's condition, guarantees the existence of a game value. The key theoretical gap filled is proving that a single shared Q-function can simultaneously approximate both the minimax and maximin Bellman equations, providing the missing justification for centralized Q-learning in this setting.

## Input / Output

The method takes a continuous state vector x(t) ∈ R^n augmented with time t, along with finite discretized action sets U* and V* (constructed via linear, square, or ball meshes), and produces deterministic pure policies π_u(t,x) and π_v(t,x) for both agents. The shared Q-function Q^θ(t,x,u,v) maps a state-action pair to a scalar value; in the decomposed variant (DIDQN), it factorizes as Q^θ₁(t,x,u) + Q^θ₂(t,x,v), which simplifies the inner min/max operations. All environments use finite-horizon continuous dynamics with a terminal cost σ(x(T)) and running cost f⁰, discretized via Euler steps of size Δt.

## Method

The core theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) shows that under Isaacs's condition, a continuous Q^Δ(t_i,x,u,v) exists that approximately satisfies both the minimax and maximin Bellman equations to within O(Δt·ε), and that the resulting argmin/argmax policies achieve near-optimal guaranteed results. IDQN implements this by training a single neural network Q^θ(t,x,u,v) with a symmetric loss that averages the minimax and maximin Bellman targets, ensuring both agents learn from the same value function. DIDQN further exploits the additive decomposition of dynamics (f = f₁(t,x,u)+f₂(t,x,v)) to factor the Q-function into two smaller networks, reducing the computational cost of the nested min/max. Both algorithms use ε-greedy exploration over finite action sets and produce pure (deterministic) policies, in contrast to baselines like NashDQN that rely on mixed policies and Nash equilibrium computation at each state.

## Results

The paper evaluates 8 algorithms across 8 zero-sum differential games (EscapeFromZero, GetIntoCircle, GetIntoSquare, HomicidalChauffeur, Interception, InvertedPendulum, Swimmer, HalfCheetah), measuring guaranteed results (approximations of V_u^{π_u} and V_v^{π_v}) and exploitability (the gap between them). IDQN and DIDQN achieve the best guaranteed results in all 8 games, with the narrowest exploitability bars, approaching the known exact values (V = −0.5 for EscapeFromZero, V = 0 for GetIntoCircle, V = 1 for GetIntoSquare). DIDQN outperforms IDQN specifically in InvertedPendulum, where the decomposed Q-function structure provides a clear advantage. Results are reported as best/mean/worst over 5 independent runs, showing IDQN and DIDQN are both more accurate and more stable than all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDQN and DIDQN outperform all five baselines in every game tested. Against 2xDDQN (van Hasselt et al., 2016) and RARL/PPO (Pinto et al., 2017), the proposed methods win decisively; both decentralized baselines overfit to a specific opponent and lose robustness, producing the widest exploitability gaps. Against NashDQN (Ding et al., 2022), IDQN/DIDQN win in all games because NashDQN's mixed (stochastic) policies guarantee only expected payoffs rather than deterministic ones, leading to poor worst-case performance. Against MADDPG (Lowe et al., 2017), the proposed methods win in 6 of 8 games; MADDPG achieves satisfactory guaranteed results only in GetIntoCircle and GetIntoSquare and shows high variance across runs in the remaining environments. Against CounterDQN (Li et al., 2022), IDQN/DIDQN win in 6 of 8 games; CounterDQN is competitive only in HomicidalChauffeur and InvertedPendulum, suggesting that additional learning steps are more valuable than the second agent knowing the first agent's next action in advance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the DQN algorithm (Mnih et al., 2015) and centralized multi-agent learning (MADDPG, Lowe et al., 2017) to the setting of continuous-time zero-sum differential games, drawing on the positional differential game theory of Krasovskii & Subbotin (1987). Prior RRL work (Pinto et al., 2017; Morimoto & Doya, 2000) formalized robustness either through discrete Markov games with mixed policies or through H∞-control, neither of which guarantees deterministic payoffs under pure strategies. The key departure is the use of Isaacs's condition to prove that a single shared Q-function simultaneously approximates both minimax and maximin Bellman equations, providing the first theoretical justification for centralized Q-learning in continuous zero-sum settings with pure policies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) gives practitioners a clean, verifiable condition (Isaacs's condition) under which shared Q-functions are well-defined in continuous zero-sum games, resolving a conceptual gap that prevented centralized learning in the Markov game framework. The proposed evaluation scheme (systematically attacking trained policies with multiple RL algorithms) is offered as a new standard for assessing robustness in continuous RRL. Key limitations include the requirement for finite action spaces (though Remark 4.2 provides a discretization guarantee) and the fact that Isaacs's condition, while common, does not always hold. Extending IDQN/DIDQN to continuous action spaces and to non-zero-sum or multi-agent differential games are identified as important open directions.
