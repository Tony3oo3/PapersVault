## Problem

Prior impossibility results (Sadasivan et al., 2023) showed that AI-generated text detection is infeasible when the total variation distance between human and machine text distributions is small, fostering widespread pessimism about detection. However, these results consider only a single text sample, ignoring the practical reality that detection scenarios typically involve multiple sentences or longer passages. This paper asks whether detection becomes possible when the number of available text samples or the sequence length is increased.

## Input / Output

The method takes a collection of n text sequences (sentences or paragraphs) drawn from either a human distribution h(s) or a machine distribution m(s), where sequences may be i.i.d. or exhibit a structured dependence parameterized by association strength ρ_j within L independent topic groups. It produces a binary detection label (AI-generated vs. human) via a likelihood-ratio-based classifier, along with theoretical AUROC upper bounds that grow exponentially in n and closed-form sample complexity bounds specifying the minimum n required to achieve a target detection accuracy ε. The framework also yields practical guidance on the minimum text length needed for reliable detection in a given domain.

## Method

The core insight is that while TV(m,h) may be small for individual sentences, the TV distance between the product distributions m^{⊗n} and h^{⊗n} approaches 1 exponentially fast with n, governed by the Chernoff information I_c(m,h). Using Le Cam's lemma and large deviation theory, the paper derives that the AUROC upper bound for the optimal (likelihood-ratio-based) detector increases exponentially with n, yielding a sample complexity of n = Ω(1/δ² · log(1/(1−ε))) for the IID case (Theorem 1). For the non-IID setting, a dependence structure is modeled where conditional expectations depend on prior samples via a strength parameter ρ_j, and a non-iid concentration bound (Dhurandhar, 2013) is applied to derive a more complex sample complexity (Theorem 2) with additional terms scaling as 1/δ · Σ(c_j−1)ρ_j. Empirically, the theory is validated across XSum, SQuAD, IMDb, and FakeNews datasets using generators (GPT-2, GPT-3.5-Turbo, Llama-2-13B/70B) and detectors (RoBERTa-Large/Base, GPTZero), showing AUROC improving from ~50% to ~90% as sequence length or sample count increases.

## Results

The paper's central theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that for any TV distance δ > 0 between human and machine text distributions, the sample complexity for achieving AUROC ε is n = Ω(1/δ² · log(1/(1−ε))), proving detection is always possible with sufficient text. Empirically, the AUROC of the best possible detector on XSum and Squad increases from 58% (word-level, n-gram=1) to 97% (n-gram=6), and zero-shot detection with RoBERTa-Large improves from ~50% to ~90% as sequence length grows. On IMDb, word-level AUROC is only 58.5%, but paragraph-level detection with standard ML classifiers reaches >85% test AUROC (>93% train for Random Forest/MLP). Paraphrasing attacks reduce detection performance by ~15%, but the monotonic improvement trend with sequence length persists.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new detector but provides theoretical sample-complexity bounds that explain and validate the behavior of existing detectors. Against OpenAI's RoBERTa-Large-Detector and RoBERTa-Base-Detector, the paper shows that both achieve AUROC improvements from ~50% to ~90% as sequence length increases on XSum and Squad, with RoBERTa-Large consistently outperforming RoBERTa-Base but both following the same monotonic trend predicted by Theorem 1. Against GPTZero (a SOTA commercial detector), the same pattern holds: zero-shot AUROC rises from ~50% to ~90% with more text, confirming the theoretical prediction. The paper's key contribution relative to these baselines is not a performance win but a theoretical guarantee: the AUROC upper bound (Proposition 1) increases exponentially with sample count n via the Chernoff information, providing the first formal justification for OpenAI's empirical observation that longer sequences are easier to detect. Under paraphrasing attacks (using Parrot), all detectors lose ~15% AUROC, but the improvement trend with sequence length is preserved, suggesting the theoretical possibility result is robust to this class of adversarial perturbation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends and reframes the impossibility result of Sadasivan et al. (2023), who showed that single-sample detection becomes infeasible when the total variation distance TV(m,h) between human and machine text distributions is small. The key conceptual move is shifting from a single-sample to a multi-sample hypothesis-testing framework, where the product distribution TV(m^⊗n, h^⊗n) grows exponentially with n via Chernoff information, rendering detection always possible for any δ > 0. The non-IID extension (Theorem 2) builds on the auto-correlation-dependent concentration bound of Dhurandhar (2013), adapting classical large-deviation tools to the dependent structure of natural language sequences. The paper also positions itself against the broader detection literature (DetectGPT, GPTZero, OpenAI's RoBERTa classifiers, watermarking schemes) by providing the first theoretical sample-complexity guarantees that underpin their empirical observations about sequence length.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first rigorous theoretical substantiation for the empirical observation (noted by OpenAI) that longer sequences improve AI-text detection, countering the widespread pessimism generated by single-sample impossibility results. It gives practitioners concrete sample-complexity bounds (n = Ω(1/δ² · log(1/(1-ε)))) that quantify how much text is needed for reliable detection, and connects watermark design to Chernoff information as a principled objective. Open questions include designing provably robust watermarks that maximize Chernoff information gain, developing task-specific detectors calibrated to sequence-length constraints, and addressing documented bias in current detectors against non-native English writers. A limitation is that the empirical validation relies on vanilla classifiers (logistic regression, random forest) and relatively small datasets, leaving open whether the theoretical guarantees translate to state-of-the-art neural detectors at scale.
