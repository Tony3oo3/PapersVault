## Problem

In multi-agent sequential decision-making, the causal effect of one agent's action on the outcome depends on how other agents respond, making it difficult to attribute that effect to the influence exerted through those other agents. Standard measures like total counterfactual effects conflate direct and indirect pathways, while path-specific effects fail to capture higher-order inter-agent dependencies because fixing subsequent actions inadvertently removes policy-level interactions between agents.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes an MMDP-SCM (encoding the multi-agent environment, joint policy, and structural equations), a realized trajectory τ, a designated subset of effect agents N, the action variable/value to intervene on, the outcome variable/value, and a posterior sample budget H. It follows the abduction-action-prediction paradigm: sampling noise from P(u|τ), generating counterfactual trajectories under the specified natural interventions, and computing the probability difference. The output is a single numerical cf-ASE value, which constitutes an unbiased estimate of the true counterfactual agent-specific effect provided noise monotonicity holds.

## Method

The paper defines agent-specific effects (ASE) and their counterfactual counterpart (cf-ASE) using natural interventions that require reasoning across three alternative worlds: one where the target action is fixed, one where a reference action is fixed, and one where effect agents respond naturally to the first world while non-effect agents respond to the second. The paper proves general non-identifiability of cf-ASE, then shows that noise monotonicity—a weaker assumption than the model bijectiveness required by prior work—suffices for identification, leveraging a novel formula (Lemma 4.4) that expresses counterfactual factors in terms of the observational distribution. A practical sampling-based algorithm (Algorithm 1) provides unbiased estimates under this assumption. The paper further establishes a formal connection to path-specific effects through a new generalized notion called fixed path-specific effects (FPSE), proving that ASE equals FPSE with appropriate edge-subgraph choices, thereby unifying the two concepts.

## Results

In the Sepsis environment (9,197 selected alternative actions), cf-ASE reveals that the clinician-specific effect decreases monotonically as the trust parameter μ increases, ultimately dropping to 0 when the clinician fully trusts the AI. At μ=0.6, approximately 40% of AI actions with TCFE > 0.8 also exhibit a clinician-specific effect (cf-ASE) > 0.75, indicating that a large fraction of total counterfactual effects can be attributed to other agents' behavior. In the Graph environment (854 selected actions), cf-ASE estimates remain accurate for small policy estimation errors (ε_max ≤ 0.05), though misspecifying the total ordering for noise monotonicity significantly degrades accuracy (with the reverse ordering yielding relatively low error).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between cf-ASE (the proposed method) and cf-PSE (counterfactual path-specific effects, the standard baseline from Pearl 2001). cf-ASE outperforms cf-PSE in conceptual correctness: cf-PSE can assign a positive clinician-specific effect even when the clinician blindly follows the AI's recommendations (μ=0), which is counter-intuitive since the clinician is not actually influencing the outcome. In contrast, cf-ASE correctly yields 0 in this scenario because it intervenes on the AI's action with do(A₀:=C), preventing spurious attribution. Similarly, cf-PSE shows a less prominent and less intuitive pattern for the clinician-specific effect across varying trust levels, while cf-ASE cleanly captures the expected trend of decreasing effect with increasing trust. The paper concludes that cf-ASE is a more suitable measure for attributing causal effects through specific agents in multi-agent sequential decision-making, as it aligns better with standard intuition about agency and responsibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Pearl's path-specific effects (2001) by introducing agent-specific effects (ASE), which require reasoning across three alternative worlds rather than two, and formalizes this gap through a new generalized notion called fixed path-specific effects (FPSE). For identifiability, it builds on the counterfactual factor framework of Correa et al. (2021) and the SCM parameterization of sequential decision processes from Buesing et al. (2018), but departs from prior identifiability results (Lu et al., 2020; Nasr-Esfahany et al., 2023) by requiring only weak noise monotonicity rather than model bijectiveness. The paper positions itself as distinct from causal contribution/attribution methods (Jung et al., 2022; Heskes et al., 2020) and multi-agent influence measurement (Jaques et al., 2019), which study action-to-action influence rather than action-to-outcome propagation through specific agents in sequential settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to formalize causal effect propagation through specific agents in multi-agent sequential decision making, with direct implications for accountability in safety-critical human-AI systems like sepsis management. The practical finding that cf-ASE can reveal clinician trust levels (decreasing as trust increases) while cf-PSE produces counter-intuitive positive values when the clinician blindly follows the AI is a meaningful distinction for auditing multi-agent decisions. Key limitations include the restrictive noise monotonicity assumption, simulation-only experiments, and the absence of unobserved confounding. Open questions include deriving a causal explanation formula that decomposes effects across agents and environment states, extending identifiability to unobserved confounding, and applying ASE to multi-agent reinforcement learning.
