## Problem

Current alignment techniques like RLHF depend on human evaluators who can reliably judge model behavior, but future superhuman models will produce outputs too complex for humans to assess. This paper asks whether weak model supervision can elicit the full capabilities of a much stronger model—a problem it calls weak-to-strong generalization—and whether simple methods can close the gap between weak-supervised and ground-truth-supervised performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240196/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The paper evaluates three task families: (1) 22 NLP classification benchmarks where text inputs yield binary class labels, (2) chess puzzles where a PGN move sequence yields the best next move via greedy decoding, and (3) ChatGPT reward modeling where a dialog paired with two candidate completions yields a preference score via the sigmoid of the logit difference. Weak labels are soft class probabilities (NLP), temperature-0 sampled moves (chess), or sigmoid-transformed logit gaps (RM), generated by finetuning small GPT-4-family models on ground truth and applying them to held-out data.

## Method

The core setup finetunes strong GPT-4-family base models (spanning 7 orders of magnitude of pretraining compute) on labels produced by weak GPT-2-level supervisors, measuring the Performance Gap Recovered (PGR) relative to a ground-truth ceiling. The key insight is that strong pretrained models already encode task-relevant representations, so weak supervision need only elicit latent knowledge rather than teach new capabilities. Three improvement methods are proposed: (1) an auxiliary confidence loss that adds a cross-entropy term against the strong model's own thresholded predictions (related to conditional entropy minimization), reinforcing confident self-predictions even when they contradict weak labels; (2) bootstrapping, which chains supervision through intermediate model sizes to take smaller steps in the capability gap; and (3) unsupervised generative finetuning on task-relevant data (ignoring ground-truth labels) to increase the salience of the target task in the model's representations before weak-to-strong finetuning.

## Results

Across 22 NLP classification tasks, naively finetuning GPT-4 with GPT-2-level weak supervision recovers approximately 25% of the performance gap (PGR) between the weak supervisor and the strong ceiling; adding the auxiliary confidence loss dramatically improves this to nearly 80% PGR for the largest student–smallest supervisor configuration. On chess puzzles, naive PGR is near zero for the smallest supervisors but exceeds 40% for small supervisor–student gaps, and bootstrapping with intermediate models further improves PGR especially for larger students. On the ChatGPT reward modeling task, weak-to-strong generalization is poor by default (PGR ≈ 10%, rarely exceeding 20%), but unsupervised generative finetuning on the RM data improves PGR by an additional 10–20 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead compares multiple internal strategies for improving weak-to-strong generalization against the naive finetuning baseline. On NLP tasks, the auxiliary confidence loss substantially outperforms naive finetuning for large supervisor–student gaps (PGR rises from ~25% to ~80%), though it performs slightly worse for smaller gaps. On chess puzzles, bootstrapping (using intermediate model sizes to iteratively generate better weak labels) outperforms naive finetuning, particularly for larger students where naive PGR decreases with student size. On the ChatGPT RM task, unsupervised generative finetuning on the same dialog data (ignoring human ratings) improves PGR by 10–20 points over naive finetuning, and this gain stacks with early-stopping improvements. Across all settings, the strong student consistently outperforms the weak supervisor (positive PGR), but a substantial gap remains relative to the strong ceiling trained on ground truth, indicating that naive RLHF-style supervision is insufficient for superhuman alignment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends weakly-supervised learning and student-teacher training to the regime where the student is orders of magnitude more capable than the teacher, inverting the standard knowledge distillation assumption. The auxiliary confidence loss is directly inspired by conditional entropy minimization (Grandvalet & Bengio, 2004), while the bootstrapping approach operationalizes long-standing alignment proposals (Christiano 2018, 2019; Leike & Sutskever 2023). The overall setup is positioned as an empirical analogue to the 'sandwiching' protocol (Cotra 2021; Bowman 2022) and to Eliciting Latent Knowledge (Christiano et al. 2022), but unlike prior theoretical or toy treatments, it enables iterative empirical progress using real pretrained models spanning 7 orders of magnitude in compute.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic empirical framework for studying a core AI safety challenge—how weak supervisors can elicit capabilities from much stronger models—making it possible to make iterative progress on superhuman alignment today. Key limitations include pretraining leakage (tasks may have been observed during pretraining, inflating results) and the poor reward modeling performance even with the best methods, suggesting naive RLHF will not scale to superhuman models without qualitatively new techniques. Open questions include whether methods generalize to truly latent capabilities never observed in pretraining, how robust the learned classifiers are to optimization pressure, and whether bootstrapping leads to model collapse.
