## Problem

Survival models face a fundamental discrimination-calibration tradeoff: optimizing calibration during training (via added loss terms like X-cal, CRPS, or SFM) consistently degrades the C-index, and vice versa. No existing method can improve the calibration of a survival model's predicted individual survival distributions (ISDs) while provably preserving its discriminative ranking ability.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a pre-trained survival model's predicted ISDs (continuous survival curves over time for each subject) and a conformal set of censored/uncensored observations. It discretizes each ISD into percentile times (PCTs) at chosen probability levels (e.g., 25%, 50%, 75%), computes conformity scores against observed times, and outputs adjusted PCTs that define a new, calibrated ISD. For censored subjects, the method samples surrogate event times from the conditional Kaplan-Meier distribution S_KM(t | t > c_i) R=1000 times per subject, inflating the conformal set size by a factor of R to maintain the event-censoring ratio.

## Method

CSD is a model-agnostic post-processing step that applies conformal regression to discretized survival curves. It splits data into training and conformal sets, trains any ISD model on the training set, then for each percentile level ρ computes conformity scores s_j = q_M(ρ|x_j) − t_j over the conformal set and shifts each subject's PCT by the empirical ρ-quantile of those scores: q'_M(ρ|x_i) = q_M(ρ|x_i) − Quantile[ρ; S_M(ρ)]. Because the quantile term is a constant independent of x_i, the relative ordering of median (and mean) survival times is preserved, guaranteeing C-index invariance (Theorem 3.1), while the conformal construction yields exact finite-sample distribution calibration (Theorem 3.2). For right-censored data, KM-sampling replaces deterministic decensoring by drawing event times from the conditional KM survival distribution, providing a distributional rather than point-estimate surrogate that avoids the overconfidence of fixed best-guess values.

## Results

Across 76 comparisons (11 datasets × 7 survival models, minus 1 AFT failure), CSD preserves discrimination in 96% of cases (60 ties, 13 insignificant CSD wins, 3 insignificant non-CSD wins on C-index). For calibration, CSD significantly improves D-cal in 68/76 comparisons (35 with p<0.05) and KM-cal in 56/76 (30 with p<0.05), approaching the KM estimator's empirical lower bound. CSD also improves IBS in 61/76 (14 significant) and MAE-PO in 45/76 (4 significant), confirming gains extend beyond the primary calibration metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CSD is applied as a post-process to 7 survival baselines (AFT, Gradient Boosting, DeepSurv, N-MTLR, DeepHit, CoxTime, CQRNN) and consistently preserves their C-index while improving calibration across all 11 datasets. Against objective-based calibration methods (X-cal, CRPS/Countdown Regression, SFM), CSD dominates: X-cal improves D-cal but causes C-index to drop to ≈0.5 on METABRIC and SEER-stomach, while CRPS and SFM show inconsistent calibration gains that vary by dataset. The KM estimator serves as the empirical calibration lower bound (red dashed line in Figure 4); CSD brings most models close to this bound without sacrificing any discriminative ranking ability. The 3 cases where non-CSD slightly outperforms CSD on C-index are not statistically significant, and the 8 cases where non-CSD is better on D-cal (1 significant) occur when the baseline already achieves near-optimal calibration comparable to KM.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts conformal regression (Romano et al., 2019) to the survival analysis setting by discretizing individual survival distributions into percentile times and applying a conformal adjustment to those times. It departs from the dominant paradigm of integrating calibration losses (X-cal, SFM, CRPS) into the training objective, instead treating calibration as a model-agnostic post-processing step. The KM-sampling mechanism for handling right-censoring extends the conformal framework beyond the Type-I censoring assumption required by Candès et al. (2023), making it applicable to the more general and common right-censoring scenario in clinical data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CSD framework offers a practical resolution to the well-documented discrimination-calibration tradeoff in survival analysis, allowing practitioners to train models purely for discrimination and then calibrate them without performance loss. Key limitations include the computational overhead of KM-sampling (R=1000 samples per censored subject), the dependence on the KM estimator as a background distribution (which requires sufficient event counts), and the inability to perform conformalization in batch mode. Open questions include whether the KM dependency can be replaced with learned priors, how the method behaves under distribution shift (violating exchangeability), and whether the approach extends naturally to competing risks or multi-state settings.
