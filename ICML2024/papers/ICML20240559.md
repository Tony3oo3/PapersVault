## Problem

Counterfactual explanations and actionable recourse methods can change a classifier's decision without altering the true underlying class probabilities, effectively acting as adversarial examples that 'fool' the model. While prior work (e.g., ICR) addressed causal validity of modifications, the adversarial vulnerability of counterfactual generation—where the optimization procedure is structurally identical to adversarial attack algorithms—remained unaddressed. This paper tackles the gap by introducing a verification mechanism that certifies perturbations genuinely shift true probabilities rather than exploiting classifier brittleness.

## Input / Output

The method takes a data point x with an undesirable classification, a differentiable classifier M, a pairwise verifier V (trained on all class-pair combinations from the training set), a target set T defined by probability thresholds over desirable/undesirable classes (e.g., ≥90% chance of high income), and an actionable set A(x) encoding which features are mutable and their bounds. It produces a modified input x̃ (the TAP) along with a verification pass/fail decision based on the discrepancy Δ between V's same-class probability estimate and M's implicit estimate, plus the realized cost ε (application-specific, e.g., years of education or Deutsche Marks) and statistical distance δ (f-divergence to the target set). The cost function d_X is not a standard ℓ-norm but a domain-specific measure that can be negative (e.g., cheaper treatment), and the target set supports multi-class goals with both lower and upper probability bounds.

## Method

TAP uses a two-step procedure: Step 1 solves an optimization via ADAM gradient descent minimizing d_Y(M(x̃), T) + λd_X(x̃, x) + penalty terms, where d_Y is a closed-form f-divergence distance to the target set (Theorem 2.2 provides the piecewise differentiable solution via KKT conditions), with actionability enforced by zeroing gradients on immutable features and projecting onto the coherent space. Step 2 verifies the result using an independently trained pairwise classifier V that estimates P(same class | x, x̃); the TAP is accepted only if the discrepancy Δ = |V(x,x̃) − Σᵢ Mᵢ(x)Mᵢ(x̃)| falls below a threshold γ calibrated to a 10% false-rejection rate. The verifier is trained on all n² pairs from the training data labeled by class agreement, and Theorem 2.3 establishes PAC-learnability with a generalization bound of O((k/√(n²−k²n))^{1/d}), requiring n ≫ k² for reliable verification. The framework also introduces flexible target sets (beyond binary flip) and application-specific cost functions that replace the ℓ-norms used by prior counterfactual methods.

## Results

TAP outperforms all counterfactual baselines across all four datasets (Adult Income, Law School Success, Diabetes Prediction, German Credit) at every cost level tested. At δ=0.5 from target, TAP achieves 81% (verified: 67%) success on Adult Income at ε=18 years, 100% (verified: 100%) on Law School at ε=6σ, 98% (verified: 62%) on Diabetes at ε=12 BMI, and 100% (verified: 85%) on German Credit at ε=7000 DM. The pairwise verifier is 100% effective at eliminating Carlini-Wagner adversarial examples, while also removing 14% of TAP and 20–27% of counterfactuals that appeared effective but were not trustworthy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TAP dominates both counterfactual baselines (Wachter et al. 2017 and DICE) across all four datasets and all cost levels. On German Credit at zero cost (ε=0 DM), TAP moves 73% of individuals within target while both counterfactual methods achieve 0%; at ε=7000 DM, TAP reaches 100% (verified: 85%) versus 81% (verified: 58%) for Wachter and 77% (verified: 58%) for DICE. On Diabetes Prediction, the gap is most dramatic: TAP achieves 93–98% unverified success versus only 14–64% for Wachter and 21–71% for DICE. The Carlini-Wagner adversarial attack achieves high unverified success on Law School (90–99%) but is 100% eliminated by the verification step, confirming that the verifier successfully distinguishes adversarial manipulation from genuine probability shifts. After verification, TAP retains the highest success rates in every scenario, with the largest verified gaps on Diabetes (62% vs. 35% for Wachter at ε=12) and German Credit (85% vs. 58% for both baselines at ε=7000 DM).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the counterfactual explanation lineage (Wachter et al. 2017) and actionable recourse methods (Ustun et al. 2019, Karimi et al. 2021, DICE/Mothilal et al. 2020) by addressing a gap identified by Pawelczyk et al. (2022): that counterfactual generation algorithms are structurally identical to adversarial attack algorithms and may therefore 'fool' the classifier without changing true class probabilities. It also builds on König et al. (2023)'s causal recourse (ICR) but takes a different route—rather than restricting modifications to causally-linked features, TAP uses an independently trained pairwise verifier to certify that the perturbation is not adversarial. The theoretical toolkit draws on f-divergence optimization (KKT conditions for closed-form distance to target sets) and Rademacher-complexity-based PAC bounds for the verifier's generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that 20–27% of standard counterfactuals are eliminated by the verification step (and 100% of Carlini-Wagner adversarial examples) validates the concern that existing recourse advice may be unreliable, and provides a concrete, PAC-guaranteed mechanism to filter out adversarial perturbations. The PAC bound's requirement that n ≫ k² (rather than the typical mild k-dependence) is a non-trivial scaling constraint for multi-class settings. Limitations include the need for differentiable models (ruling out gradient-boosted trees, the state-of-the-art for tabular data), the additional computational cost of training and querying the verifier, and the fact that the framework is validated only on four tabular datasets. Open questions include whether the verification approach extends to non-differentiable or ensemble models, how it behaves under distribution shift, and whether the target-set formulation generalizes to sequential or multi-step interventions.
