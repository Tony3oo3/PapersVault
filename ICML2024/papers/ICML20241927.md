## Problem

Existing deep clustering methods for tabular data either lack interpretability or rely on domain-specific augmentations designed for images. Prior interpretable clustering schemes provide only global (dataset-level) explanations, while the critical need in biomedicine is for sample-level and cluster-level feature identification (e.g., marker genes per cell population) in high-dimensional, small-sample tabular settings.

## Input / Output

The model takes a D-dimensional tabular vector x_i as input and produces three outputs: a cluster assignment ŷ_i (one-hot over K clusters via Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization), a sample-level sparse gate vector z_i ∈ [0,1]^D identifying which features drive that specific sample's cluster, and a global gate matrix Z_G ∈ {0,1}^{K×D} where each row encodes the informative features for an entire cluster. Because the model is fully parametric, it can predict both cluster assignments and informative features for unseen samples at inference time.

## Method

IDC is a two-stage unsupervised framework. In Stage 1, a hypernetwork (gating network) predicts sample-specific stochastic gates (continuously relaxed Bernoulli variables with injected Gaussian noise) that sparsify the input before a denoising autoencoder reconstructs it; the loss combines gated reconstruction error, a gates total coding rate term (encouraging diverse gate vectors across samples), and an L0 sparsity regularizer with cosine-scheduled weight. In Stage 2, a clustering head maps the autoencoder's latent embedding to cluster assignments using an adapted MCR² (maximum coding rate reduction) objective that pushes clusters apart while making each compact, and a global gate matrix Z_G is learned via an auxiliary single-hidden-layer classifier trained with cross-entropy on the clustering head's pseudo-labels. The key insight is that local sparsification overcomes the neural network's implicit low-frequency bias, enabling the model to learn the high-frequency prediction functions that tabular data requires, while simultaneously providing interpretable feature selections at both sample and cluster granularity.

## Results

On 11 real-world tabular datasets, IDC achieves the best or second-best clustering accuracy on 9 of 11 datasets, with notable margins such as 86.8% on MFEATZ (vs. 70.0% for CAE), 77.5% on ALLAML (vs. 74.5% for DUFS), and 77.0% on MiniBooNE (vs. 75.6% for TELL). On MNIST₁₀K, IDC reaches 83.45% clustering accuracy while selecting only 15 informative features per sample, outperforming TELL (74.79%) and K-means+SHAP (53.34%). In interpretability metrics, IDC achieves the highest uniqueness (0.69), faithfulness (0.96), and generalizability (88.5) scores, substantially exceeding post-hoc explainers like Integrated Gradients (uniqueness 0.01) and Gradient SHAP (uniqueness 0.02). On CIFAR-10, IDC matches TELL in accuracy (25.01 vs. 25.65) while surpassing it in ARI (6.16 vs. 5.96) and NMI (11.96 vs. 10.41) without using domain-specific image augmentations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDC outperforms TELL (Peng et al., 2022) on 10 of 11 real datasets, with the largest gap on CNAE-9 (66.0 vs. 11.1) and INTESTINE (74.2 vs. 52.1); TELL only edges ahead on MiniBooNE (75.6 vs. 77.0 is actually IDC winning, so TELL never wins). Against DUFS (Lindenbaum et al., 2021), IDC wins on 9 of 11 datasets but loses on BIASE (95.7 vs. 100) and is close on PROSTATE (65.3 vs. 64.7). CAE (Abid et al., 2019) is the only method that beats IDC on SRBCT (62.6 vs. 55.4), while IDC dominates CAE on MFEATZ (86.8 vs. 70.0) and ALLAML (77.5 vs. 73.5). k-DVAE (Caciularu & Goldberger, 2023) is competitive on CNAE-9 (62.8 vs. 66.0, IDC still wins) but trails IDC on most other datasets. SRCFS (Huang et al., 2019) is consistently outperformed by IDC across all 11 datasets. On interpretability (MNIST₁₀K), IDC's built-in gates achieve 0.96 faithfulness and 88.5 generalizability, far exceeding post-hoc SHAP (0.79 faithfulness, 29.1 generalizability) and Integrated Gradients (0.94 faithfulness, 63.7 generalizability) applied to the same model. On CIFAR-10, IDC matches TELL in accuracy (25.01 vs. 25.65) and surpasses both VaDE (20.87) and DEC (18.09) by 4–7 points in ACC, all without domain-specific augmentations.

## Relation to Prior Work

IDC sits at the intersection of three research threads: unsupervised feature selection (regularized autoencoders, gated Laplacian methods), interpretable clustering (Guan et al.'s probabilistic model, TELL, tree-based K-means), and deep clustering via coding rate reduction (MCR², Li et al.'s generalization). The key departure is granularity: prior UFS methods select a global feature subset for the entire dataset, and prior interpretable clustering methods provide only cluster-level or dataset-level explanations. IDC introduces sample-level (local) stochastic gates learned per-instance via a hypernetwork, combined with a Gumbel-Softmax clustering head trained with a per-cluster coding rate loss, which the authors frame as the first end-to-end unsupervised scheme with local feature selection. The spectral bias analysis (Section 6.3) connects the gating mechanism to the known low-frequency inductive bias of neural networks on tabular data, providing a theoretical motivation beyond pure interpretability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and growing need in biomedicine: clustering high-dimensional gene expression or single-cell omics data while identifying which genes drive each cluster assignment, a task where interpretability is not optional but essential for biological validation. The finding that local sparsification simultaneously improves clustering accuracy and provides sample-level explanations (rather than being a trade-off) is practically useful. However, the authors acknowledge limitations with correlated variables (inherited from reconstruction-based feature selection) and with large numbers of clusters, and the fairness implications of fully unsupervised models remain open. The method's reliance on a two-stage training procedure and multiple hyperparameters (σ, λ, ε, temperature) may limit ease of adoption outside the authors' experimental setup.
