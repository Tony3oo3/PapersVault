## Problem

While algorithmic choices, data, and hyperparameters have been extensively studied for their fairness implications, the influence of hardware selection on model fairness has been largely overlooked. This paper investigates how different GPU architectures, when used for training the same model with identical software settings, can produce systematically different accuracy outcomes across demographic groups, disproportionately harming minority groups.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 CIFAR-10, face images from UTKFace and CelebA) paired with demographic group labels (ethnicity, gender/hair attributes) and trains CNN classifiers on four different GPUs (Tesla T4, V100, A100, Ada L4) while fixing all software randomness. The primary output is a hardware sensitivity metric per group (maximum loss difference between a reference GPU and all others) and a fairness violation score (maximum disparity in hardware sensitivity across groups), alongside per-class accuracy differences across hardware configurations.

## Method

The paper derives a second-order Taylor expansion bound (Theorem 4.1) decomposing hardware sensitivity into two group-dependent terms: gradient flow norms (reflecting local optimality) and Hessian maximum eigenvalues (reflecting loss surface curvature). Theorems 4.2–4.3 prove that under mild angle assumptions, the smallest group exhibits the largest gradient norm, while Theorem 4.4 links Hessian eigenvalues to the distance of group samples to the decision boundary. For mitigation, the authors propose augmenting the standard training loss with a penalty term that equalizes the average distance-to-decision-boundary across groups (Eq. 5), avoiding the computationally prohibitive task of directly equalizing Hessians. Empirical validation across four GPU generations, four CNN architectures (SmallCNN through ResNet50), and three datasets confirms that hardware-induced fairness violations increase with model complexity and class imbalance, and that the proposed loss augmentation reduces fairness violation by up to 3×.

## Results

On UTKFace, the minority group 'Others' exhibits a hardware sensitivity of 0.122 compared to 0.034 for the majority group 'White', while on CelebA the minority 'blond-male' group shows sensitivity of 0.23 versus 0.025 for 'non-blond-female'. On imbalanced CIFAR-10, class 8 (Ship) sensitivity increases from 0.017 (balanced) to 0.046 (imbalanced). The proposed mitigation reduces fairness violation from 1.91 to 0.77 on CIFAR-10 (λ=6e-5), from 2.34 to 0.936 on CelebA with ResNet50 (λ=0.001), and achieves a three-fold reduction on UTKFace (λ=2e-5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against competing fairness methods; instead, it compares the unmitigated model (trained on a reference GPU, T4) against the same model trained on alternative GPUs (V100, A100, L4) to quantify hardware-induced fairness disparities. The key finding is that minority groups suffer substantially larger accuracy drops: on UTKFace, 'Others' drops by up to 0.122 in loss while 'White' drops only 0.034; on CelebA, 'blond-male' drops 0.23 versus 0.025 for 'non-blond-female'. The proposed mitigation (augmenting the training loss with a group-wise distance-to-decision-boundary penalty) consistently reduces fairness violation by 2.5–3× across all three datasets without requiring Hessian computation. The paper also shows that fairness violation increases with model complexity (SmallCNN → ResNet18 → ResNet34 → ResNet50) and with class imbalance, confirming that the effect is systematic rather than an artifact of a single configuration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hardware-variability line of work (Hooker 2020; Zhuang et al. 2022) from accuracy/reproducibility to fairness, and extends the ML fairness literature (which has focused on algorithmic choices like compression, privacy, and hyperparameters) to include hardware as a first-class source of disparate impact. The theoretical framework draws on the Taylor-expansion-based analysis of Tran et al. (2021a) for differential privacy's disparate impact, repurposing it to bound hardware-induced loss differences via group-specific gradient norms and Hessian eigenvalues. The proposed mitigation (augmenting the loss with a distance-to-decision-boundary alignment term) is a new loss component not present in prior fairness or hardware-variability work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

In MLaaS and cloud training environments where users have no control over underlying hardware, this work reveals that fairness can be silently and systematically compromised without any change to data, model, or hyperparameters — a practically important finding for organizations deploying models across heterogeneous infrastructure. The mitigation is simple (a single loss term) and effective, but the study is limited to four GPU generations, image classification, and models up to ResNet50; whether the effects persist or amplify in NLP, foundation models, or with newer hardware generations remains open. A key open question is whether the gradient-flow and Hessian mechanisms identified here generalize to non-convex, multi-task, or generative settings where the angle-between-gradients assumption of Theorem 4.3 may not hold.
