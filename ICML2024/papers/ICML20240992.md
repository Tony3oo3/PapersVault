## Problem

Standard conformal prediction provides only marginal coverage guarantees, which can fail for specific subgroups or under distribution shift. Full conditional coverage is provably impossible with finite calibration data, and existing relaxations require predefined uncertainty structures (fixed groups, covariate shift families, or heuristic similarity functions) that may not capture the actual uncertainty patterns of a pre-trained model. This paper asks whether such uncertainty-guided structures can be learned directly from calibration data without any prior specification.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained predictor f, a calibration set of n i.i.d. pairs (X_i, Y_i), a conformity score S(x,y), and a hyperparameter m specifying the number of learned groups. It outputs a prediction set C(x) whose threshold is determined by a soft assignment of x to one of m groups via a learned function h: X → Δ_m, so that the effective quantile threshold q_{i~h(x)} adapts to the local uncertainty level. For regression this yields a prediction interval; for classification it yields a set of class labels. The partition function h can be any parametric model (linear, MLP, CNN) with a softmax output layer.

## Method

PLCP jointly learns a partition function h and m group-specific quantile thresholds by minimizing an empirical weighted pinball loss: (1/n) Σ_j Σ_i h^i(X_j) ℓ_α(q_i, S_j), where ℓ_α is the asymmetric pinball loss whose minimizer recovers the (1−α)-quantile. The key insight is that the derivative of the pinball loss with respect to q equals the conditional coverage minus the target, so minimizing the loss drives conditional validity. The partition is learned over a function class H (e.g., neural networks with softmax output), and the optimization is solved via alternating gradient descent on h's parameters and q. Theoretically, the Mean Squared Coverage Error (MSCE) of the resulting prediction sets scales as O(1/√m) in the infinite-data regime and as O(√((m log n + log N(H,d,1/n))/n) + 1/√m) with high probability in the finite-sample regime, establishing a fundamental trade-off between the number of groups and sample size.

## Results

PLCP achieves near-perfect conditional coverage (≈90% target) across all demographic subpopulations on the 2018 US Census and across all Gaussian blur levels on MNIST (OOD), while maintaining shorter prediction intervals than baselines. In the Appendix A quantitative comparison, PLCP yields a 56.07% reduction in HSIC correlation and 64.06% reduction in Pearson correlation between interval length and coverage indicator on US Census, and 73.36% / 86.08% on MNIST — substantially outperforming CQR (51.89% / 59.21% and 58.01% / 75.19%) and LocalCP (32.12% / 37.96% and 40.88% / 47.51%). On the RxRx1 WILDS dataset, PLCP matches Conditional Calibration's coverage while producing notably shorter prediction sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Split Conformal, PLCP consistently wins on both conditional coverage and interval length: Split Conformal over-covers low-variance groups and under-covers high-variance groups, while PLCP achieves near-uniform 90% coverage across all subpopulations with shorter sets. Against BatchGCP (which requires predefined group labels), PLCP matches coverage on the 18 known groups but clearly outperforms on groups 19–20 whose structure was withheld from BatchGCP, demonstrating that PLCP learns uncertainty-relevant features from data alone. Against Conditional Calibration (Gibbs et al., 2023) on RxRx1, PLCP achieves comparable coverage but with notably shorter prediction intervals, attributed to its direct learning of uncertainty patterns rather than estimating covariate-shift probabilities. Against CQR and LocalCP (Appendix A), PLCP dominates on both HSIC and Pearson independence metrics, with the largest gap on MNIST (73.36% vs 58.01% HSIC; 86.08% vs 75.19% Pearson), indicating that PLCP's learned partitions more effectively decouple interval length from coverage behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

PLCP extends the Split Conformal framework (Papadopoulos et al., 2002; Lei et al., 2016) by replacing the single marginal quantile with multiple group-specific quantiles governed by a learned partition function h. It departs from group-conditional methods (BatchGCP, Barber et al.) that require predefined subgroups, and from covariate-shift methods (Gibbs et al., 2023; Tibshirani et al., 2019) that require a fixed family of shifts, by instead learning uncertainty-guided partitions directly from calibration data via a pinball-loss objective. The method also differs from score-design approaches (CQR, LocalCP) by operating after the conformity score is fixed, making it composable with any existing score. Theoretically, it builds on the impossibility results of Vovk (2012) and Foygel Barber et al. (2019) by introducing MSCE as a measurable relaxation of full conditional coverage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PLCP offers a practical, data-driven path to conditional coverage without requiring domain experts to specify which subpopulations or shift families matter—a significant advantage in high-dimensional or novel settings like medical imaging. The MSCE metric and its O(1/√m) infinite-sample scaling (with a PAC-style finite-sample bound) provide a clean theoretical handle on the partition-count trade-off, and the quantization interpretation (Remark B.1) opens a potential bridge to information-theoretic impossibility results. Open questions include how the approximation gap λ_H behaves for deep function classes, whether learned partitions are interpretable enough for safety-critical deployment, and how the method performs under severe distribution shift where calibration and test distributions diverge substantially.
