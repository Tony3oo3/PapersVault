## Problem

Existing algorithmic recourse methods extract actions from already-trained models but do not guarantee that reasonable, executable actions actually exist for a given instance. For tree-based models—which dominate tabular prediction in high-stakes domains like finance and justice—gradient-based recourse-aware training (e.g., Ross et al., 2021) is inapplicable due to the non-differentiable, combinatorial structure of trees. This paper asks: how can one learn a tree-based classifier that maintains predictive accuracy while provably ensuring the existence of low-cost recourse actions for a high fraction of instances?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled tabular training sample S with one-hot-encoded categorical features, per-feature actionability constraints (mutable/immutable flags and lower/upper bounds defining the feasible action set A(x)), a cost budget ε (using max percentile shift as the cost function), a recourse constraint δ specifying the minimum fraction of instances that must have valid actions, and a trade-off hyperparameter λ. It outputs a recourse-aware classification tree or random forest whose leaf labels are chosen so that at least (1−δ) of training instances admit a perturbation a with c(a|x)≤ε that flips the prediction to the desired class, along with the extracted valid actions themselves (obtained via feature tweaking or integer optimization post-hoc).

## Method

RACT is a two-stage framework. Stage 1 grows a tree via top-down greedy splitting where each node's split condition (d,b) and child labels (ŷ_L, ŷ_R) minimize a penalized objective Φ_λ = R̂ + λ·Ω̂_ε, combining empirical misclassification risk with an empirical recourse risk that measures the fraction of instances lacking a valid action within budget ε. The key efficiency insight is that, under the ℓ_∞-type cost assumption, the recourse-risk terms M_L and M_R can be updated in amortized O(1) per threshold by exploiting the monotonicity of reachability indicators g_n(d,b) and g̅_n(d,b) with respect to the sorted instance order—mirroring the adversarial-training technique of Guo et al. (2022)—yielding O(D·N) per node, matching standard CART. Stage 2 is a set-cover relabeling post-processing step: starting from majority-vote leaf labels, it selects a subset of negative-labeled leaves to flip to +1 so as to satisfy Ω̂_ε ≤ δ while minimizing the increase in empirical risk; this is reduced to the weighted partial cover problem and solved by a greedy algorithm with a (2H(N)+3)-approximation guarantee. A PAC-style bound (via Hoeffding + union bound) then extends the empirical recourse guarantee to the population level. The framework extends to random forests by using RACT as the base learner with bootstrap sampling and random feature subsets.

## Results

RACT achieves the lowest average action cost across all four datasets, e.g., 0.283 on FICO (vs. 0.447 Vanilla, 0.407 OAF) and 0.166 on Credit (vs. 0.293 Vanilla). Under the causal recourse constraint, RACT attains the highest validity on every dataset, e.g., 0.648 on FICO (vs. 0.316 Vanilla, 0.502 OAF) and 0.964 on Credit (vs. 0.669 Vanilla). Predictive accuracy and running time remain comparable to the Vanilla baseline, with no significant degradation (e.g., random forest training time on FICO: 48.07s for RACT vs. 42.09s for Vanilla).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares RACT against two baselines: Vanilla (standard CART/random forest with no recourse constraint) and OAF (Only Actionable Features, which trains using only mutable features following Dominguez-Olmedo et al., 2022). RACT consistently outperforms both baselines on action cost (Table 2) and causal recourse validity (Table 4), with the largest gains on FICO (cost reduced by ~37% vs. Vanilla; causal validity more than doubled from 0.316 to 0.648) and Bail (causal validity tripled from 0.174 to 0.539). On predictive accuracy (AUC) and running time, RACT is statistically indistinguishable from Vanilla across all datasets, confirming that the recourse constraint does not degrade model quality or efficiency. OAF sometimes achieves a comparable recourse ratio on Credit but is inapplicable there (N/A in Tables 2–4) because too many features are immutable, and it still lags RACT on action cost and causal validity on the other three datasets. Plausibility (outlier score via isolation forests) shows no significant difference among all three methods, indicating RACT does not produce implausible actions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PARE (probably approximately has recourse) condition introduced by Ross et al. (2021) from differentiable models (deep neural networks) to non-differentiable tree-based classifiers, filling a gap since gradient-based recourse-aware training cannot be directly applied to decision trees. The top-down greedy splitting algorithm repurposes the monotonicity-based amortized computation technique from adversarially robust decision tree learning (Guo et al., 2022), but redirects it toward ensuring the existence of low-cost recourse actions rather than worst-case adversarial robustness. The post-processing step reduces the recourse constraint satisfaction to a weighted partial cover problem, drawing on classical combinatorial optimization (Chvatal, 1979; Kearns, 1990), and the overall framework is built on standard CART (Breiman et al., 1984) and random forest (Breiman, 2001) learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Tree-based models dominate tabular domains (finance, criminal justice) where algorithmic recourse is most needed, yet no prior method could learn such models while guaranteeing recourse actions. RACT provides a practical, efficient solution with the same O(D·N) complexity as standard CART, making it deployable at scale. Key limitations include reliance on ℓ∞-type cost functions (precluding ℓ1/ℓ2 norms), inapplicability to gradient boosted trees (XGBoost, LightGBM), and dataset-specific hyperparameter tuning for λ. Open questions include extending the framework to regression trees for boosting, general cost functions, and automatic hyperparameter selection.
