## Problem

Standard quantile regression for prediction intervals requires a priori selection of symmetric quantile pairs around the median, which produces unnecessarily wide intervals when the underlying noise distribution is asymmetric—a common and realistic scenario. Existing workarounds either force symmetric intervals or require learning all quantiles simultaneously (SQR), which is a substantially harder estimation problem and still lacks clear test-time interval selection criteria.

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular feature vector x ∈ R^d and outputs two scalar interval bounds (μ1, μ2) from a two-headed neural network. Crucially, the bounds are permutation-invariant: the model does not designate which output is the upper or lower bound, and the final interval is constructed as [min(μ1,μ2), max(μ1,μ2)]. This design avoids the quantile-crossing pathology inherent in standard quantile regression. The target coverage level α is a hyperparameter set at training time, and the regularization term (width or orthogonality) is chosen by the practitioner based on application needs.

## Method

RQR introduces a loss function that directly optimizes interval bounds without prespecifying quantiles: L = ακ if κ ≥ 0 and (α−1)κ if κ < 0, where κ = (y−μ1)(y−μ2). The sign of κ encodes whether the target falls between the bounds, enabling the network to learn coverage without tying outputs to specific quantile values. The paper proves that minimizing this loss in expectation yields exactly α coverage (Theorem 3.1), with finite-sample unbiasedness and a variance bound of 1/(4N) (Theorem 3.3). Two regularization variants are then added: RQR-W appends a squared-width penalty λ(μ2−μ1)²/2 (requiring a coverage correction α→α+2λ to maintain validity, and yielding convexity and a unique minimum under mild Lipschitz conditions on the CDF), and RQR-O appends a Pearson-correlation term between interval widths and coverage indicators to promote conditional coverage. The approach is trained via standard gradient descent on tabular data with two-layer networks, and the added flexibility of non-median-centered intervals is empirically shown to produce narrower intervals or better conditional coverage than QR, SQR, and IR baselines across 12 benchmark datasets.

## Results

Across 12 benchmark regression datasets, RQR-W achieves the target 90% coverage on all 12/12 datasets (vs. 10/12 for QR, 9/12 for SQR-C, 3/12 for SQR-N, and 11/12 for IR), with a mean absolute miscoverage of 1.63% (vs. 2.45% for QR and 2.73% for IR). RQR-W also achieves the narrowest mean prediction interval width on 8/12 datasets, compared to 6/12 for QR and 0/12 for IR. For the conditional coverage variant, RQR-O yields a mean +80% improvement in Pearson correlation and +98% improvement in HSIC over OQR on the kin8nm dataset, with positive gains on 8 of 9 datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RQR-W outperforms standard Quantile Regression (QR, Koenker & Bassett 1978) on all three metrics: it achieves valid coverage on 12/12 datasets versus 10/12, reduces mean miscoverage from 2.45% to 1.63%, and produces the narrowest intervals on 8/12 datasets versus 6/12. Against Simultaneous Quantile Regression (SQR, Tagasovska & Lopez-Paz 2019), RQR-W dominates decisively: SQR-C achieves coverage on only 9/12 datasets with 2.13% miscoverage and narrowest intervals on just 1/12, while SQR-N is far worse at 3/12 coverage and 4.02% miscoverage. RQR-W also beats Interval Regression (IR, Pearce et al. 2018) on every metric, with IR achieving the narrowest intervals on 0/12 datasets and 2.73% mean miscoverage. For the conditional coverage objective, RQR-O (paired with the orthogonal regularization from Feldman et al. 2021) improves Pearson correlation by +71% to +89% and HSIC by +17% to +100% over OQR on 8 of 9 datasets, with the sole exception being yacht where both metrics show negative gains (−45.80% Pearson, −18.49% HSIC).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard quantile regression pipeline (Koenker & Bassett Jr, 1978) that first estimates two prespecified quantiles and then constructs an interval between them, arguing this intermediate step forces intervals to be centered at the median—a suboptimal constraint for the skewed noise distributions prevalent in real data. It also builds on the orthogonal quantile regression regularization of Feldman et al. (2021) by plugging their conditional-coverage term into a new direct-interval objective rather than the pinball loss. Conceptually, RQR is closest to the interval regression approach of Pearce et al. (2018), which also learns bounds directly, but RQR recovers the asymptotic coverage guarantees that IR lacks by grounding its loss in the quantile regression literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is that practitioners working on prediction intervals for skewed targets (common in energy, robotics, and medical forecasting) can drop the arbitrary median-centering assumption without sacrificing coverage guarantees, and can flexibly trade off interval width against conditional coverage via a single regularization knob. Open questions include how RQR interacts with conformal post-hoc calibration (the authors note no systematic study exists), whether the convexity conditions of Proposition 3.7 hold for high-dimensional or multimodal targets, and whether the gains persist on larger-scale or non-tabular regression tasks. The evaluation is limited to 12 small tabular datasets, so generalization to high-dimensional or image-based regression remains untested.
