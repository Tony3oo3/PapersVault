## Problem

It remains unclear whether large language models genuinely learn to extract factual knowledge from training text or simply memorize similar question-answer pairs encountered during training. Prior probing studies on internet-trained models cannot distinguish these two mechanisms, and no controlled experiment has isolated the conditions under which memorized knowledge becomes extractable. This paper asks: under what data conditions does a model that perfectly memorizes all training tokens actually become able to answer out-of-distribution questions about those facts?

## Input / Output

The model is pretrained on synthetic six-sentence biography entries (one per person, each encoding six attributes: birth date, birth city, university, major, company, company city) and then evaluated via exact-match QA prompts of the form 'What is the birth city of [full name]?' For probing, the input is the last-layer hidden state at specific token positions (the token immediately preceding each attribute for P-probing, or the name token alone for Q-probing), and the output is a linearly predicted attribute label. The QA dataset contains exactly six questions per individual, and accuracy is measured as the fraction of generated answers that exactly match the ground-truth attribute string.

## Method

The paper constructs two controlled synthetic biography datasets (bioS with templated sentences, bioR with LLaMA-rewritten entries) for 100k individuals and systematically varies three knowledge augmentations—multiplicity (M paraphrased entries per person), permutation (random sentence shuffling), and fullname (replacing pronouns with the full name)—to isolate their effect on knowledge extractability. The core finding is that without augmentation, a GPT2 model achieves >99% first-token memorization on BIO data yet yields near-0% out-of-distribution QA accuracy after instruction finetuning (LoRA or full), regardless of model size or finetune hyperparameters; adding just five diverse entries per person boosts accuracy to ~96.6%. To explain this, the authors apply (nearly) linear P-probing and Q-probing to the pretrained model's hidden states, showing that without augmentation knowledge is distributed across all biography tokens (requiring the full context to be present), whereas with augmentation it becomes linearly decodable from the person's name token alone. A secondary 'celebrity' experiment demonstrates that augmenting data for only a subset of individuals transfers improved extraction ability to non-augmented individuals, and a mixed-training ablation reveals that the model first encodes knowledge from QA pairs and then aligns it with BIO text—an unconventional learning order that further motivates including QA-like data during pretraining.

## Results

On the synthetic bioS dataset, mix training (BIO + QA jointly) achieves 86.6% mean QA accuracy out-of-distribution, while the standard BIO pretrain + QA finetune pipeline achieves only 9.7% on the un-augmented 'single' configuration. Adding knowledge augmentation (multi5 + permute) to pretraining boosts finetune accuracy to 96.6% on bioS. On the more realistic bioR dataset, mix training reaches 77.7% mean accuracy, and the celebrity-data effect lifts minority-group QA accuracy from 10.0% to 76.3%.

| BIO pretrain + QA finetune | single | 9.7% |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts internal training paradigms and data configurations. The key comparison is between mix training (BIO + QA jointly, 86.6% on bioS) and the standard pretrain-then-finetune pipeline, which fails catastrophically (9.7%) without data augmentation. Among augmentation strategies, combining multiplicity (multi5) with sentence permutation (permute) is most effective, reaching 96.6% and even surpassing mix training. The celebrity experiment shows that merely including augmented data for a separate group of individuals during pretraining lifts the non-augmented minority group's accuracy from 4.4% to 86.8% (bioS) and 10.0% to 76.3% (bioR), without any change to the minority's own training data. Full finetuning achieves near-perfect in-distribution QA accuracy on P_train but remains ineffective for P_test, confirming that the bottleneck is knowledge encoding during pretraining, not finetuning capacity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior knowledge-probing studies (Omar et al., 2023; Singhal et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023) that evaluate QA accuracy in models trained on internet data, where it is impossible to distinguish genuine knowledge extraction from memorization of similar training questions. It also extends the linear-probing literature (Conneau et al., 2018; Dai et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021) by applying it in a fully controlled synthetic setting to isolate the mechanistic role of data augmentation. The key conceptual departure is the explicit separation of 'memorization of text' from 'extractability of knowledge,' a distinction the paper argues prior work conflated, and the use of controlled biography datasets (bioS, bioR) to make this distinction experimentally tractable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that near-perfect token-level memorization of source text yields near-zero QA accuracy without knowledge augmentation challenges a widely held assumption that 'if the model has seen the fact, it can retrieve it.' The practical recommendation to rewrite pretraining data with small auxiliary models and to include QA-like data earlier in pretraining is directly actionable for industry-scale LLM training. Open questions include whether these findings generalize to more complex, multi-hop, or relational knowledge beyond simple entity-attribute pairs, whether they hold at the scale of 70B+ parameter models, and what the optimal augmentation strategy is for diverse real-world corpora. The 'celebrity helps minority' effect also raises questions about data curation strategies for long-tail entities.
