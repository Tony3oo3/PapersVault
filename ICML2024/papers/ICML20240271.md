## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning frameworks (Chain-of-Thought, Tree of Thoughts, Graph of Thoughts) impose rigid, unidirectional thought structures that only support forward-only reasoning, causing errors and hallucinations to propagate irreversibly through subsequent steps. No prior method allows an LLM to revisit and revise a previously generated thought after discovering a mistake, leaving a gap between the adaptive, iterative error-correction behavior of human reasoning and the linear, one-shot generation of current LLM prompting strategies.

## Input / Output

The method takes a plain text question (e.g., a math word problem) as input, optionally augmented with a small number of CoT demonstration examples, and requires no task-specific human annotations or pre-defined thought structure. During reasoning, the prompt is dynamically enriched with accumulated error analyses (called 'experiences') from prior rollbacks, so the LLM's input context grows adaptively. The output is a final numerical or categorical answer selected via weighted majority voting over K independently derived solutions, alongside the full directed cyclic graph of thoughts that was built during the reasoning process.

## Method

Thought Rollback (TR) introduces two cooperating components: a rollback controller that prompts the LLM to perform step-by-step error analysis on the current thought chain and identify the earliest bad thought, and a prompt enhancer that accumulates each rollback's error analysis as an in-context 'experience' to guide subsequent thought generation. When a bad thought at step m is identified from step n, the LLM rolls back to step m−1 and generates a new reasoning path from there, with the prompt now containing the trial-and-error experience of what went wrong, making the thought structure a directed cyclic graph rather than a tree or chain. The process iterates—each rollback creates one additional reasoning path—until K solutions are collected, at which point a weighted majority vote (weighting solutions by the number of incoming rollbacks minus outgoing rollbacks along their path) selects the final answer. The key insight is that process-level, rollback-by-rollback verification is more effective for self-correction than outcome-level analysis of the final answer, because it provides targeted, step-specific feedback that the LLM can act on immediately.

## Results

On the MATH dataset with GPT-4, TR+W-Voting achieves a 71.89% solving rate, outperforming BoT (62.44%) by 9.45 points and PHP+C-CoT (53.98%) by 17.91 points, while requiring only ~40 LLM interactions versus 500+ for BoT. On AQuA-RAT, TR+W-Voting reaches 87.8%, surpassing BoT (81.5%) by 6.3 points. On the multi-task MMLU benchmark, GPT-4+TR+W-Voting achieves 87.56% with an average of 28 interactions, and on Game of 24, TR+W-Voting reaches 87% solving rate with 32 interactions—competitive with CR(b=2) at 94% which relies on human-made demonstrations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TR+W-Voting with GPT-4 outperforms BoT (Sijia et al., 2024) by 9.45 points on MATH (71.89 vs. 62.44) and 6.3 points on AQuA-RAT (87.8 vs. 81.5), while using roughly 40 interactions versus BoT's 500+. Against PHP+C-CoT (Zheng et al., 2023), TR gains 17.91 points on MATH and 7.82 on AQuA-RAT with GPT-4. TR also surpasses C-CoT (Fu et al., 2023) by 21.41 points on MATH and 10.22 on AQuA-RAT, and outperforms standard CoT (Wei et al., 2022) by 22.91 points on MATH. On Game of 24, TR+W-Voting (87%, 32 interactions) is competitive with CR(b=2) (94%, 27.4 interactions) despite being zero-shot, and substantially outperforms ToT(b=5) (74%, 30 interactions). On TheoremQA, GPT-4+TR+W-Voting achieves 56.75%, exceeding the PoT SOTA (Chen et al., 2023a) of 52.4%. The only comparison where TR trails is against CSV (Zhou et al., 2024) on MATH (71.89 vs. 84.32), though CSV relies on GPT-4 Code Interpreter as an auxiliary tool and TR is purely prompt-based. TR also outperforms ZeroShot-CoT (Kojima et al., 2022) by 27.19 points on MATH and 14.6 points on AQuA-RAT with GPT-4.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the structured-reasoning lineage from Chain-of-Thought through Tree of Thoughts (ToT) and Boosting of Thoughts (BoT) by introducing a fundamentally different thought topology: a directed cyclic graph enabled by rolling back to earlier thoughts. Where ToT and BoT pre-define unidirectional tree structures and rely on outcome-level error feedback after full reasoning, TR performs process-level (rollback-by-rollback) error analysis during reasoning and accumulates intermediate-step experiences in the prompt. The key conceptual departure is that no prior structured-reasoning method supports revisiting and revising earlier thoughts, making the cyclic structure and adaptive prompt enhancement the paper's core architectural novelty.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TR demonstrates that iterative, experience-guided thought revision can substantially reduce the impact of LLM hallucinations on multi-step reasoning, achieving state-of-the-art solving rates on MATH and TheoremQA with far fewer interactions than tree-based search methods like BoT. However, the token cost is prohibitive in practice (35× more generated tokens and 425× more prompt tokens than ZeroShot CoT on MATH), and gains diminish for weaker models that cannot reliably perform error analysis. Open questions include whether the token overhead can be reduced through selective rollback or early stopping heuristics, whether the approach generalizes beyond mathematical and symbolic tasks to open-ended generation, and whether the cyclic thought structure can be made more efficient as context windows grow.
