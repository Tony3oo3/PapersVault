## Problem

Despite decades of tokamak operations generating vast multimodal datasets and the field's urgent need for advanced prediction and control, the ML community has lacked a comprehensive, practitioner-oriented roadmap for engaging with fusion research. Prior surveys either targeted fusion physicists or covered only narrow sub-areas, leaving ML researchers without a clear entry point into the field's most impactful open problems.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241883/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The surveyed tasks consume heterogeneous fusion data—high-frequency diagnostic time series, 2D spatial profiles (temperature, density, safety factor), edge magnetic field measurements, free-text logbook entries, and atomic-scale configurations—stored in the MDSplus hierarchical database with inconsistent schemas across machines. A notable idiosyncrasy is that failed sensors report white noise rather than NaNs, and sampling rates vary across sensors and devices, requiring models to be sampling-rate independent. Outputs range from disruption risk scores and survival-time estimates to real-time control actions, surrogate simulation predictions, Bayesian-optimal experimental parameters, and DFT-accurate interatomic potential energies and forces.

## Method

Rather than proposing a single architecture, the paper systematically frames six research challenges—disruption prediction, simulation/dynamics modeling, partial observability, control, optimal experiment design, and materials discovery—reviewing prior ML work for each and identifying specific technical barriers. Key insights include characterizing disruption prediction as a "99/01" problem requiring ML safety and robustness advances (unlike typical "51/49" classification), identifying the O(N²) scaling bottleneck of GNNs for radiation-damage modeling at the hundreds-of-thousands-of-atoms scale, and highlighting the "archipelago" nature of fusion data (fragmented, narrow, inconsistently documented datasets across machines) as a fundamental obstacle. The paper also maps the evolving data infrastructure (MDSplus, IMAS, DOE PuRE) and speculates on future opportunities including foundation models, federated learning across national servers, and multi-modal fusion of text, audio, and profile data.

## Results

This is a position paper that does not present new experimental results or quantitative benchmarks. Instead, it identifies six key ML research challenges in magnetic fusion energy (disruption prediction, simulation/dynamics modeling, partial observability, control, optimal experiment design, and materials discovery) and reviews the state of the art for each. The paper's central claim is that fusion energy offers especially ripe opportunities for ML practitioners, with low-hanging fruit available today and further opportunities emerging as data infrastructure matures. No new models are proposed or evaluated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a new method to compare against baselines. However, it surveys the landscape of prior ML approaches across six problem areas. For disruption prediction, the paper notes a progression from early SVMs and regression trees (Cannas et al., 2007; Murari et al., 2008) to more recent temporal/spatial CNNs (Churchill et al., 2020; Aymerich et al., 2022), VAEs (Wei et al., 2021), and survival analysis (Tinguely et al., 2019), with the key open challenge being the shift from '51/49' to '99/01' accuracy requirements. For simulation, the paper contrasts physics-based approaches (incomplete, computationally expensive) with data-driven sequence-to-sequence models (Abbate et al., 2021; Kolemen et al., 2023) and hybrid physics-ML surrogates (Rodriguez-Fernandez et al., 2022b), noting that even surrogate-accelerated gyrokinetics still requires millions of CPU-hours. For control, the paper highlights the landmark RL demonstration on TCV (Degrave et al., 2022) as the first practical RL plasma control, while noting outstanding challenges for routine deployment. For materials, MLIPs (GNNs, MPNNs, GAPs, MACE) achieve 3–4 orders of magnitude speedup over DFT but struggle to scale to the hundreds of thousands of atoms needed for radiation damage modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground by being the first comprehensive overview of ML in tokamak fusion explicitly targeted at the ML community, whereas prior surveys (Kamal 2020; Humphreys et al. 2019) addressed fusion practitioners and Pavone et al. (2023) covered only narrow sub-areas. It synthesizes the IAEA's AI for Fusion coordinated research project (2023) and organizes six problem areas—disruption prediction, simulation, partial observability, control, experiment design, and materials discovery—under a unified ML-relevant framing. Conceptually, it repurposes the fusion community's decades of multi-modal experimental data and physics-informed modeling traditions as an invitation for ML practitioners, drawing analogies to robotics simulation (MuJoCo, Isaac Gym) and data-scarce domains (weather forecasting, AlphaFold) to bridge the two fields.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a critical gap: fusion power plants require >99% disruption prediction accuracy (a '99/01' problem) and real-time control under extreme uncertainty, yet current ML approaches remain black-box, data-hungry, and unvalidated for safety-critical deployment. Open questions include whether foundation-type models trained across multi-device datasets can overcome distributional drift and data paucity, whether physics-ML hybrid surrogates can reduce the millions-of-CPU-hours needed for gyrokinetic simulation by orders of magnitude, and whether the fragmented data ecosystem (MDSplus, IMAS, institutional access barriers) can be unified in time for net-energy experiments like SPARC and ITER. As a position paper, its impact is catalytic: it provides a structured entry point for ML researchers to identify concrete, high-impact collaboration targets with fusion domain experts.
