## Problem

Existing offline preference-based RL methods collect ternary preference feedback on independently sampled trajectory pairs, which discards second-order preference information (the relative strength of preferences between trajectories). This limitation persists even when more sophisticated feedback types are impractical, as most prior work requiring second-order preference assumes access to granular ratings or fully-ranked lists that are expensive to obtain. The paper asks whether second-order preference can be extracted using only the same simple ternary feedback (more/less/equal) already standard in PbRL.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline trajectory dataset (segments of length 25 from Meta-World or DMControl replay buffers or expert policies) and a fixed budget of ternary preference labels. Rather than independently sampling pairs, the feedback is collected sequentially via binary search to insert each new segment into a Ranked List of Trajectories (RLT), requiring O(M log M) feedbacks for M segments. The output is an ensemble of three reward models (MLP with Tanh activation, linear score function) whose averaged predictions are fed into IQL to produce the final policy. The RLT structure itself generates all preference pairs: any segment in a higher-ranked group is labeled as preferred over all segments in lower-ranked groups, yielding C(M,2) pairs from M segments.

## Method

LiRE constructs a fully-ranked list of trajectory segments using binary insertion sort, where each new segment is compared against existing groups via ternary feedback (equal → merge into group; less → recurse left; more → recurse right) until its correct rank is found. This encodes second-order preference: if σ_a ≺ σ_b ≺ σ_c, the reward model learns that σ_c is preferred over σ_a more strongly than over σ_b, something impossible with independent pairwise sampling. The reward model uses a linear score function φ(x) = x in the Bradley-Terry model (rather than the standard exponential), which amplifies reward differences in high-reward regions and makes the learned reward better distinguish optimal from suboptimal segments. The reward output is bounded via (1 + tanh(f_θ(s,a))) > 0 to ensure valid probabilities. Multiple RLTs of budget Q each are constructed to balance feedback efficiency (O(M/log M) pairs per feedback) against sample diversity, and the reward model is trained with standard pairwise cross-entropy loss over all pairs derived from the RLT.

## Results

On the Meta-World medium-replay dataset with 500 preference feedbacks, LiRE achieves average success rates of 67.20% (button-press-topdown), 51.53% (box-close), 79.07% (dial-turn), and 79.13% (button-press-topdown-wall), substantially outperforming the best baselines (IPL at 34.73%, MR at 10.33%, PT at 68.67%, and IPL at 8.93% respectively). With 1000 feedbacks, LiRE further improves to 83.07%, 89.13%, 76.93%, and 81.47% on those tasks, approaching or exceeding ground-truth reward performance (88.33%, 93.40%, 75.40%, 56.27%). LiRE is the only method that consistently surpasses 50% success rate across most tasks with just 500 feedbacks, while baselines like MR and DPPO remain below 10% on several tasks even with 1000 feedbacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LiRE outperforms all five baselines on the majority of tasks. Against Markovian Reward (MR), LiRE gains 57.6 points on button-press-topdown (67.20 vs. 9.60) and 79.0 points on button-press-topdown-wall (79.13 vs. 0.13) with 500 feedbacks. Against Preference Transformer (PT), LiRE wins on 6 of 8 tasks; PT is only competitive on dial-turn (68.67 vs. 79.07) and lever-pull (82.40 vs. 95.67). Against OPRL, LiRE dominates on all tasks except sweep (77.53 vs. 94.13), where OPRL's active query selection provides an advantage. Against DPPO and IPL (which bypass reward modeling entirely), LiRE achieves large margins: e.g., 67.20 vs. 3.93 (DPPO) and 67.20 vs. 34.73 (IPL) on button-press-topdown. Against SeqRank, the closest competitor that also uses sequential ranking, LiRE wins on 6 of 8 tasks with 500 feedbacks (e.g., 67.20 vs. 17.60 on button-press-topdown, 79.13 vs. 1.73 on button-press-topdown-wall), with SeqRank only matching LiRE on drawer-open (99.53 vs. 99.40) and lever-pull (95.67 vs. 95.67). The paper attributes LiRE's advantage over SeqRank to constructing fully-ranked lists (~9.3 groups) versus SeqRank's partially-ranked lists (~2.3 groups), yielding 11.33× feedback efficiency versus 2.20×.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sequential preference ranking line of work (SeqRank, Hwang et al. 2023) by replacing partially-ranked lists with fully-ranked lists (RLT) constructed via binary insertion sort, while retaining the same simple ternary feedback type. It departs from the standard independent pairwise sampling paradigm in offline PbRL (Christiano et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2022; Shin et al. 2022) by showing that second-order preference information can be extracted without requiring more granular or expensive feedback. The method also introduces a linear score function as an alternative to the conventional exponential Bradley-Terry score, and contributes new Meta-World and DMControl offline PbRL datasets to address the known inadequacy of D4RL for evaluating reward learning quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The new offline PbRL datasets expose a critical evaluation gap: existing methods appear to work on D4RL but fail on tasks where reward quality actually matters, making this a necessary correction for the field. The finding that a simple binary insertion sort over ternary feedback can yield large performance gains (e.g., 67% vs. 9.6% success rate) without any additional feedback sophistication suggests that much of the second-order preference signal in standard PbRL pipelines has been wasted. Open questions include handling transitivity violations in real human feedback, parallelizing RLT construction, and whether the linear score function advantage generalizes beyond the tested architectures and tasks.
