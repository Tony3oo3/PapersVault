## Problem

The public discourse on generative AI safety is dominated by calls for tighter regulation from major tech companies, yet the specific question of whether open-sourcing Gen AI models creates net benefits or risks in the near-to-mid-term remains underexplored and often distorted by the economic interests of closed-source market leaders. There is no systematic empirical characterization of how open current LLMs actually are across their full training, evaluation, and deployment pipelines, nor a balanced differential analysis of open versus closed source risks and opportunities.

## Input / Output

The taxonomy takes as input the code and data artifacts at each stage of the LLM pipeline (pre-training, supervised fine-tuning, alignment, general evaluation, safety evaluation, and deployment/inference) for 40 high-impact LLMs selected via three impact metrics (ChatBot Arena Elo Rating, Google Scholar Citations, HuggingFace Downloads). Each component is classified on a 5-level scale (C1–C5 for code, D1–D5 for data) using a point-based license scoring system that evaluates five criteria: research use, commercial use, modification rights, copyright of derivatives, and licensing freedom for derivative works. The output is a full openness matrix per model, an aggregate distribution analysis, and a set of five actionable recommendations for responsible open sourcing.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-stage development framework (near-term, mid-term, long-term) defined by adoption rates and technological capability rather than calendar time, and focuses its analysis on the near-to-mid-term. It then constructs an openness taxonomy that decomposes each LLM pipeline into code and data sub-components, classifying each via a 5-point license scoring rubric (0–1 = highly restrictive, 2–3 = moderately restrictive, 4 = slightly restrictive, 5 = restriction-free) mapped to C1–C5/D1–D5 levels. This taxonomy is empirically applied to 40 LLMs released between 2019 and 2023, revealing a notable skew toward closed source in training data (especially fine-tuning and alignment) and safety evaluation components. The paper then conducts a differential risk-and-opportunity analysis across six dimensions (quality/transparency, research impact, innovation/economics, safety, societal/environmental impact, and democratization), systematically addressing four common claims against open sourcing with counter-evidence. Finally, it synthesizes five concrete recommendations for developers: enhancing data transparency and provenance, improving open evaluation and benchmarking, conducting multilevel security audits, comparing against closed-source alternatives, and studying broader societal impact.

## Results

The paper's primary empirical contribution is an openness taxonomy applied to 40 high-impact LLMs (2019–2023), classifying each pipeline component (training code/data, evaluation code/data, deployment code/data) on a 5-level scale (C1/D1 = fully closed to C5/D5 = fully open). The analysis reveals a notable skew toward closed source in training data (particularly fine-tuning and alignment data) and, critically, in safety evaluation code and data, while deployed components (inference code and model weights) show a rough balance between open and closed. For example, among the 40 models, only a handful (e.g., GPT-Neo, GPT-J-6B, Pythia, Falcon) achieve C5/D5 across most pipeline stages, whereas frontier closed models (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) are C1/D1 across nearly all components. The paper argues this skew is partly driven by for-profit entities publicly claiming open-source Gen AI is fundamentally unsafe, and recommends five concrete priorities to shift the balance toward responsible open sourcing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper rather than a method-comparison study, so there are no traditional baselines. Instead, the paper systematically contrasts open-source versus closed-source Gen AI across five dimensions: (1) quality/transparency, (2) research impact, (3) economic/innovation impact, (4) safety, and (5) societal/environmental impact. On safety, the paper challenges the claim that closed models are inherently safer, citing evidence that closed models are vulnerable to jailbreaking (Zou et al., 2023), fine-tuning-based safety removal (Qi et al., 2023), and other bypass techniques, while acknowledging that open models cannot be rolled back post-release. On research impact, open models enable reproducible evaluation, mechanistic analysis, and novel methods (e.g., DPO as a cheaper RLHF alternative), whereas closed APIs restrict access to logits and internal representations. On economic impact, the paper argues open models reduce costs for smaller organizations and low-income countries, while closed models concentrate benefits among well-resourced entities. The paper's central comparative finding is that most risks attributed to open source (malicious use, inability to revoke access, regulatory gaps) also apply to closed source, and that the current landscape skew (Figure 4) is partly an artifact of market leaders' public safety arguments rather than a fundamental technical necessity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-studied open-source software impact literature (Paulson et al., 2004; Von Krogh and Spaeth, 2007) to the unique context of generative AI, where dual-use potential and rapid capability scaling introduce complexities absent in traditional software. It builds conceptually on the Foundation Model Transparency Index (Bommasani et al., 2023b) and governance frameworks for open foundation models (Bommasani et al., 2023a), but departs from them by introducing a component-level openness taxonomy (C1–C5, D1–D5) applied empirically across the full training–evaluation–deployment pipeline of 40 LLMs. The paper also engages directly with the AI safety evaluation literature (Weidinger et al., 2023; Seger et al., 2023) and model-card/datasheet conventions (Gebru et al., 2021; Derczynski et al., 2023), positioning its recommendations within existing transparency practices while arguing they are insufficient without broader pipeline openness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a much-needed counter-narrative to the 'open source is fundamentally unsafe' argument advanced by major closed-model providers, and its taxonomy of 40 LLMs quantifies a concrete skew: deployment artifacts are relatively balanced between open and closed, while training data and safety evaluation components are overwhelmingly closed. This framing is practically important for policymakers (EU AI Act, US Executive Order) and developers deciding release strategies. Key open questions include whether the near-to-mid-term framing remains valid as capabilities shift, whether the recommended multilevel security audits are feasible at scale, and whether voluntary open sourcing can overcome the economic incentives for closure. The paper explicitly acknowledges that its risk assessment rests on assumptions that must be re-evaluated if disruptive capability changes occur.
