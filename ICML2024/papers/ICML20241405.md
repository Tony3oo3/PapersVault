## Problem

While benign overfitting in high-dimensional linear regression has been well-characterized in-distribution, the generalization behavior of minimum-norm interpolators under covariate shift remained theoretically unexplored in non-asymptotic settings. Prior work either required asymptotic limits where sample size and dimension grow at the same rate, studied only minimax worst-case risk, or focused on augmented training algorithms rather than the closed-form interpolator. This paper fills that gap by providing the first finite-sample, instance-wise excess risk bounds for benignly-overfit linear interpolators under distribution shift.

## Input / Output

The method takes n i.i.d. source training pairs (x^i, y^i) with n < p (overparameterized regime), concatenated into X ∈ R^{n×p} and y_s ∈ R^n, and evaluates the resulting MNI on a target distribution D_t with its own covariance Σ_t. The source and target covariance matrices must be simultaneously diagonalizable (commuting), with source eigenvalues sorted in non-increasing order. The output is the MNI parameter vector θ̂ = X^T(XX^T)^{-1}y_s along with non-asymptotic upper and lower bounds on target excess risk decomposed into model-shift, bias, and variance components, each expressed in terms of per-coordinate eigenvalue ratios λ̃_i/λ_i.

## Method

The paper decomposes target excess risk of the MNI into three terms: a deterministic model-shift term B1, a bias term B2 measuring the Σ_t-weighted distance between the true source parameter and the MNI fit to clean labels, and a variance term V_εs measuring the Σ_t-weighted norm of the MNI fit to pure noise. It then derives tight non-asymptotic upper and lower bounds for B2 and V_εs that scale each feature's contribution by the target-to-source eigenvalue ratio λ̃_i/λ_i, with the signal (top k) and noise (bottom p−k) components governed by different rates: k/n for signal and n/R_k for noise, where R_k is the effective rank of the covariance tail. The key insight is a taxonomy of shifts: in the mildly overparameterized regime (n/R_k ≫ k/n), signal and noise shifts interact so that increasing signal energy while decreasing noise energy is beneficial; in the severely overparameterized regime (n/R_k ≪ k/n), the high-rank tail suppresses noise-component effects and behavior reverts to classical underparameterized intuition where only signal shifts matter. This taxonomy is validated empirically on CIFAR-10/10C (blur as beneficial, noise as malignant) and on 3-layer ReLU networks, showing that high-dimensional networks (p > n) replicate MNI shift behavior while low-dimensional ones do not.

## Results

The paper's primary contribution is theoretical: it provides the first non-asymptotic, instance-wise excess risk bounds for the minimum-norm interpolator (MNI) under covariate shift, decomposing risk into a model-shift term B₁, a bias term B₂, and a variance term V_{ε_s}. The key empirical finding is a taxonomy (Theorem 3.4) showing that the same multiplicative shift (e.g., α>1 on signal, β<1 on noise) is beneficial in the mildly overparameterized regime but malignant in the severely overparameterized regime, with the crossover governed by the ratio n/R_k vs. k/n. On binarized CIFAR-10, blur corruption (which reduces covariance energy, analogous to α<1) yields beneficial OOD shifts for the MNI, while Gaussian noise corruption (which adds tail energy, analogous to β>1) yields malignant shifts. For 3-layer ReLU networks, when input dimension p > n the network behaves like the MNI under shift, but when p < n it does not, regardless of hidden width.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; instead, it validates its own theoretical predictions empirically. The key empirical 'comparisons' are between in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) excess risk under controlled covariate shifts, and between mildly vs. severely overparameterized regimes. The paper shows that its taxonomy correctly predicts shift direction: for the (k,δ,ε)-spike model with α=2.0, β=0.1, a crossover from beneficial to malignant shifts occurs as p increases past n (Fig. 1), matching Theorem 3.4. On CIFAR-10C, blur consistently improves OOD accuracy over ID (beneficial), while noise degrades it (malignant), and combining both filters reproduces the non-monotonic α<1, β>1 case predicted by the taxonomy. The paper also contrasts its non-asymptotic, instance-wise bounds with prior asymptotic analyses (Tripuraneni et al., 2021) and minimax bounds (Lei et al., 2021), noting that its framework handles arbitrary eigenvalue sequences and differing rates of n,p→∞ that prior work cannot accommodate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-distribution benign overfitting theory of Bartlett et al. (2020) and Tsigler & Bartlett (2023) to the transfer learning / covariate shift setting, providing the first non-asymptotic, instance-wise excess risk bounds for the minimum-norm interpolator under distribution shift. It departs from prior OOD analyses (Tripuraneni et al. 2021, Lei et al. 2021) by avoiding asymptotic proportional limits and minimax formulations, instead giving finite-sample bounds that depend on the full eigenspectrum of both source and target covariances. The key conceptual departure is introducing a taxonomy of beneficial versus malignant shifts that depends on the degree of overparameterization (measured by the effective rank R_k/n), revealing a cross-over where the same eigenvalue scaling is beneficial in one regime and harmful in another.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The taxonomy of shifts provides a principled framework for understanding when OOD generalization helps or hurts interpolating models, with the practical implication that increasing overparameterization improves robustness to multiplicative covariate shifts. The empirical validation on CIFAR-10C (blur as beneficial, noise as malignant) and on dense ReLU networks suggests the linear-model theory captures real behavior of overparameterized neural networks when input dimension exceeds sample size. Key limitations include the simultaneous diagonalizability assumption, the well-specified linear model requirement, and the restriction to benign source conditions; extending the analysis to non-linear models, non-commuting covariances, and tempered overfitting regimes remains open. The paper also highlights an underappreciated distinction between high-dimensionality (p > n) and overparameterization (many parameters) in neural networks, which has implications for how the field conceptualizes generalization.
