## Problem

The AI community lacks a shared, operationalizable framework for defining AGI and measuring progress toward it. Existing definitions are inconsistent—some conflate capabilities with processes, others require deployment or physical embodiment—and there is no common language to compare models, assess risks at different capability stages, or communicate where current systems sit on the path to AGI.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a profile of an AI system's performance across diverse cognitive and metacognitive tasks (e.g., linguistic, mathematical, spatial, social intelligence, and learning new skills) rather than a single benchmark score, and produces a two-dimensional classification: a performance level (0–5, from No AI to Superhuman) crossed with a generality axis (Narrow vs. General). It additionally maps the resulting AGI level to recommended Levels of Autonomy (0–5) for human-AI interaction, specifying which interaction paradigms are 'unlocked' and what risks each combination introduces.

## Method

The paper distills six design principles from an analysis of nine prominent AGI definitions (Turing Test, Strong AI, brain analogies, human-level cognitive tasks, learning ability, economic value, flexibility tests, ACI, and SOTA-LLM-as-generalist), then constructs a matrixed leveling system with five performance tiers (Emerging, Competent, Expert, Virtuoso, Superhuman) defined by percentile thresholds relative to skilled adults, crossed with two generality categories (Narrow vs. General). A key design choice is separating performance (depth on individual tasks) from generality (breadth of tasks meeting a threshold), so that a system can be, e.g., Level 3 Narrow AI (Expert Narrow) while remaining Level 1 General AI (Emerging AGI). The authors further introduce six Levels of Autonomy for human-AI interaction (No AI, Tool, Consultant, Collaborator, Expert, Agent) that are unlocked by but not determined by AGI capability levels, and specify properties an AGI benchmark should satisfy—ecological validity, metacognitive tasks, living-benchmark structure—without proposing a concrete benchmark. The framework is explicitly designed to be a path ontology rather than a binary threshold, enabling risk assessment and policy discussion at each intermediate stage.

## Results

This position paper introduces a two-dimensional, leveled ontology for classifying AI systems on the path to AGI, organized by Performance (5 levels: Emerging, Competent, Expert, Virtuoso, Superhuman) and Generality (2 categories: Narrow, General). The framework is grounded in six design principles distilled from analysis of nine existing AGI definitions. As of September 2023, the authors classify frontier LLMs (ChatGPT, Bard, Llama 2, Gemini) as Level 1 General AI ('Emerging AGI'), noting they achieve 'Competent' performance on some tasks (e.g., short essay writing) but remain at 'Emerging' level for most tasks (e.g., mathematics, factuality). The paper also introduces six Levels of Autonomy (0–5) for human-AI interaction paradigms, arguing that capability progression unlocks but does not determine the appropriate autonomy level for deployment.

**Table 1: A leveled, matrixed approach toward classifying systems on the path to AGI based on depth (performance) and breadth (generality) of capabilities.**

| Performance \ Generality | Narrow (clearly scoped task or set of tasks) | General (wide range of non-physical tasks, incl. metacognitive) |
|---|---|---|
| Level 0: No AI | Calculator software; compiler | Human-in-the-loop computing (e.g., Amazon Mechanical Turk) |
| Level 1: Emerging (≈ unskilled human) | GOFAI; simple rule-based systems (e.g., SHRDLU) | ChatGPT, Bard, Llama 2, Gemini |
| Level 2: Competent (≥ 50th pct. skilled adults) | Toxicity detectors; Smart Speakers (Siri, Alexa); VQA (PaLI); SOTA LLMs for subset of tasks | **Not yet achieved** |
| Level 3: Expert (≥ 90th pct. skilled adults) | Grammarly; Imagen; DALL-E 2 | **Not yet achieved** |
| Level 4: Virtuoso (≥ 99th pct. skilled adults) | Deep Blue; AlphaGo | **Not yet achieved** |
| Level 5: Superhuman (> 100% of humans) | AlphaFold; AlphaZero; Stockfish | **ASI — not yet achieved** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not conduct empirical comparisons against competing methods. Instead, it critically analyzes nine existing AGI definitions (Turing Test, Strong AI/consciousness, brain-analogy, human-level cognitive tasks, task-learning, economically valuable work, Marcus's 'Coffee Test,' Suleyman's ACI, and SOTA-LLM-as-generalist) and identifies their individual shortcomings. The proposed framework is positioned as a unifying ontology: Agüera y Arcas & Norvig's 'LLMs are already AGI' claim maps to Level 1 (Emerging AGI), OpenAI's labor-replacement threshold aligns with Level 4 (Virtuoso AGI), and the Legg/Shanahan/Suleyman formulations best correspond to Level 2 (Competent AGI). The authors argue that prior definitions fail one or more of their six principles (e.g., Turing Test focuses on process not capability; OpenAI's definition conflates potential with deployment; Marcus's tasks require physical embodiment), whereas the proposed matrix addresses all six simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes nine prior AGI definitions (Turing Test, Legg/Goertzel cognitive-task framing, OpenAI's economically-valuable-work criterion, Suleyman's ACI, Marcus's flexibility tests, etc.) into a unified two-dimensional matrixed taxonomy of performance (depth) × generality (breadth), drawing an explicit structural analogy to SAE Levels of Driving Automation. It departs from prior work by rejecting binary AGI/no-AGI thresholds in favor of a path-based, leveled ontology, and by coupling AGI capability levels with a separate six-level Autonomy taxonomy for human-AI interaction. The risk-assessment framing builds on Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (ASL) and Zwetsloot & Dafoe's misuse/alignment/structural risk taxonomy, but applies them to a capability-based rather than risk-based leveling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a shared vocabulary for researchers, policymakers, and model developers to discuss where current systems sit on the path to AGI and what risks each level introduces, moving the conversation beyond the binary 'is GPT-4 an AGI?' debate. The most significant open question is operationalization: the authors explicitly do not propose a benchmark, leaving unresolved what task set constitutes 'generality,' what pass-rate threshold qualifies a system for a given level, and how to safely include dual-use capabilities in public evaluation. The coupling of AGI level with autonomy level for risk assessment is a useful but underdeveloped contribution that will require empirical validation as systems progress through levels.
