## Problem

Empirical machine learning publications are predominantly judged by whether a proposed method beats state-of-the-art on selected benchmarks, creating a publication bias that systematically suppresses negative results. This overemphasis on predictive performance leads to community inefficiency through redundant research, bad incentives that reward risk-aversion and resource inequality, and a disconnect between research benchmarks and practical applications. The paper argues the field needs a paradigm shift to normalize the publication of negative results.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work takes the current state of ML research practices—review guidelines, publication norms, incentive structures, and existing venues for negative results—as its analytical subject. It produces a formal taxonomy of negative results (NMNR and EMNR), a structured critique organized around three key hypotheses, four counterfactual arguments with responses, and eight concrete action points for the community to adopt.

## Method

The paper formalizes negative results in empirical ML using the null hypothesis framework, distinguishing between novel method negative results (NMNR, where a proposed method fails to beat SOTA) and existing method negative results (EMNR, where established methods are shown to underperform expectations, e.g., vanishing gradients or adversarial examples). The argument is structured around three hypotheses: that pure predictive performance is a faulty and noisy proxy for scientific progress, that hyper-focus on performance sets bad incentives for researchers, and that the field has become increasingly inefficient due to redundant exploration. The authors then address four counterfactuals (quality dilution, limited value of negative findings, emergence of new proxies, and selection bias) before concluding with eight concrete measures, including special conference tracks, a two-phase pre-registration-inspired review process that separates idea evaluation from result evaluation, specific review criteria tailored to NMNR and EMNR publications, and targeted incentives for replication studies.

## Results

This is a position paper and does not present experimental results or quantitative benchmarks. The paper cites supporting evidence from prior work, including Roberts et al. (2021) who examined 2,212 ML studies for COVID-19 detection and found none of the 62 most promising models to be of clinical use, and Varoquaux & Cheplygina (2022) who found that in multiple Kaggle competitions, reported performance gains by winners were smaller than evaluation noise. The paper also notes that AI publications more than doubled between 2010 and 2021, reaching nearly 500,000 total, with a doubling rate of roughly 23 months (Krenn et al., 2023).

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a method or compare against baselines. Instead, it argues against the current paradigm of judging publications primarily by predictive performance and proposes eight concrete measures for normalizing negative results. The paper distinguishes two subtypes of negative results—Novel Method Negative Results (NMNR) and Existing Method Negative Results (EMNR)—and notes that EMNR publications (e.g., vanishing gradients, adversarial examples) already have better standing in the community than NMNR publications. The authors acknowledge that the average publication with a performance improvement is likely more impactful than the average one without, but argue that over-reliance on a single noisy metric (predictive performance) is analogous to a model making decisions on one correlated feature rather than using all available features.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the long-standing literature on publication bias (Sterling, 1959; Easterbrook et al., 1991; Boulesteix et al., 2015) and the call for negative results in science to the specific context of empirical machine learning. It builds on prior ML-specific discussions (Giraud-Carrier & Dunham, 2011; Bobtcheff et al., 2021) and existing venues like the I Can't Believe It's Not Better! workshop, but departs from them by providing a structured taxonomy of negative results (NMNR vs. EMNR), a game-theoretic framing of incentives, and eight concrete, actionable recommendations tailored to ML's unique properties—rapid growth, compute inequality, and performance-driven review culture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a systemic feedback loop in ML where performance-gain-only incentives drive redundant research, stifle innovation, and disconnect publications from practical impact—problems that worsen as the field scales exponentially. Its concrete proposals (two-phase review, subfield-specific tracks, replication study funding, teaching integration) offer a realistic roadmap rather than abstract advocacy. Open questions remain: whether the community will actually shift review culture, whether new proxy metrics will simply replace performance as the dominant signal, and how to prevent the normalization of negative results from becoming its own form of bias toward 'interesting' failures.
