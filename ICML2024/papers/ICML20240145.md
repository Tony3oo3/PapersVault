## Problem

Minimax regret UED suffers from a regret stagnation problem: once the agent reaches the MMR bound, the adversary converges to sampling only irreducible-regret levels (e.g., partially-observable T-mazes), and learning halts even though the agent could still improve on other, lower-regret levels. The MMR objective is indifferent among all MMR policies, so the converged policy may perform poorly on a large subset of levels that are never sampled at equilibrium.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a finite UPOMDP where the level space Θ indexes distinct POMDPs sharing dynamics and reward structure, and the agent consumes action-observation trajectories τ. The output is a single trajectory-conditioned policy π' that is piecewise-defined: it matches the MMR policy on all trajectories realisable under MMR levels, and is independently optimised on all other trajectories. In practice, ReMiDi maintains a sequence of level buffers (one per adversary iteration) and a single shared policy that is updated only on trajectory segments distinguishable from prior buffers.

## Method

BLP (Bayesian level-perfect MMR) iteratively solves a sequence of refined minimax regret games: at each step i, the agent's strategy set is restricted to policies that behave identically to π_{i-1} on all trajectories realisable under the union of previous adversaries' supports, while the adversary's strategy set is restricted to the remaining levels. Theorem 4.4 guarantees that each refinement retains global MMR guarantees, monotonically improves worst-case regret on the remaining levels, and preserves regret on previously-sampled levels. Theorem 4.6 further shows that the resulting BLP policy acts consistently with a Perfect Bayesian policy under a minimax regret prior over all realisable trajectories. ReMiDi (Algorithm 1) implements this by running PLR⊥ on successive buffers, rejecting new levels whose trajectories completely overlap with prior buffers, and updating a single policy only on distinguishable trajectory segments.

## Results

On Brax robotics tasks (100-timestep episodes), ReMiDi dramatically outperforms both baselines across all four morphologies: Ant (257±35 vs. −50±2 for PLR⊥ and −49±4 for Minimax Return), Half-Cheetah (321±16 vs. −19±14 and −16±3), Hopper (193±2 vs. 67±19 and 86±15), and Walker (344±21 vs. 126±68 and 148±67). On Minigrid held-out mazes, ReMiDi achieves higher solve rates than both PLR⊥ and Minimax Return in both the T-maze and blindfold settings. In the lever game, ReMiDi achieves near-perfect solve rates on visible-answer levels while PLR⊥ fails entirely, though both methods tie at ~1/64 on invisible-answer levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReMiDi decisively outperforms PLR⊥ (Jiang et al., 2021a) on all evaluated domains. On Brax, the gap is extreme: ReMiDi scores 257 on Ant versus −50 for PLR⊥, and 344 on Walker versus 126. On Minigrid, PLR⊥ trains almost exclusively on T-mazes or blindfold levels (high irreducible regret), resulting in poor held-out maze solve rates, while ReMiDi samples those levels initially and then moves on to solvable mazes. In the lever game, PLR⊥ achieves only ~1/64 solve rate on visible-answer levels because it never trains on them, whereas ReMiDi solves them perfectly. ReMiDi also outperforms the Minimax Return baseline (Pinto et al., 2017) on all Brax morphologies (e.g., 321 vs. −16 on Cheetah) and on Minigrid solve rates, as the minimax return adversary similarly gravitates toward irreducible-regret levels. Both baselines satisfy the minimax regret bound but produce policies that are effectively random on the large subset of levels outside the adversary's support, which is the core regret stagnation problem ReMiDi addresses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the minimax regret (MMR) framework for Unsupervised Environment Design established by Dennis et al. (2020, PAIRED) and Jiang et al. (2021a, PLR⊥), which frame UED as a two-player zero-sum game where an adversary selects levels to maximise the agent's regret. The key departure is identifying a specific failure mode—regret stagnation—where the MMR adversary converges to sampling only irreducible-regret levels, causing the agent to stop learning on all other levels. The proposed Bayesian level-perfect (BLP) refinement draws on Perfect Bayesian equilibrium from game theory (Kreps & Wilson, 1982; Fudenberg & Tirole, 1991) to iteratively refine the MMR policy over successively smaller level subsets, and the ReMiDi algorithm implements this on top of PLR⊥.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The regret stagnation problem is a genuine and previously uncharacterised limitation of MMR-based UED in partially-observable settings, and the BLP solution concept provides a clean theoretical fix that retains MMR guarantees while monotonically improving worst-case regret on remaining levels. However, practical applicability is constrained: the algorithm requires a priori knowledge of trajectory likelihoods under a set of levels (infeasible in many real settings), theoretical results hold only at Nash equilibrium, and the experiments are in controlled, relatively simple domains designed to exhibit irreducible regret. Open questions include extending to continuous level spaces, learning belief models to replace the trajectory-distinguishability check, and moving toward true leximinimax regret as a further refinement.
