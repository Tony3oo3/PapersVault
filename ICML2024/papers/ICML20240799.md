## Problem

Current in-context RL methods (e.g., AT, AD) enable self-improvement via trial-and-error by conditioning on across-episodic contexts of the smallest actions, but this causes quadratic computational costs that scale with task horizon, limiting them to short-horizon tasks under 100 timesteps. This makes them impractical for standard RL benchmarks like MuJoCo (1000 timesteps) where the context length becomes prohibitive. The paper asks whether human multi-level decision-making can yield a more efficient trial-and-error process that operates on abstract high-level decisions rather than individual actions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment observations, a target return-to-go, and an across-episodic context of n trajectories (default n=4) arranged in ascending order of total rewards, but critically reconstructs this context at the high-level decision level so that each c-step block is represented by a single continuous vector z sampled from a multivariate Gaussian rather than c individual action tokens. The output is a sequence of c low-level actions (discrete or continuous) generated autoregressively per high-level decision, with the high-level decision vector itself also produced as an intermediate output that conditions the next generation cycle. This reconstruction reduces the context to fewer than one full episode length, yielding 36× and 27× speedups on D4RL and Large Grid World respectively.

## Method

IDT decomposes the sequence prediction into three end-to-end trained modules: (1) Making Decisions, a decoder-only GPT that autoregressively generates high-level decision vectors z every c steps from the across-episodic context; (2) Decisions to Go, a decoder-only GPT that generates c steps of low-level actions conditioned on the predicted z, using the reparameterization trick to enable gradient flow back to the Making Decisions module; and (3) Reviewing Decisions, a lightweight encoder (self-attention + linear layer) that infers high-level decisions from executed low-level actions, providing the otherwise unobservable supervisory signal for z during training. All three modules are trained jointly with a single loss on predicted low-level actions (cross-entropy or MSE), requiring no explicit reward model or value function. At test time, the agent rolls out multiple episodes in a high-level trial-and-error loop where the Reviewing Decisions module encodes each executed c-step block into a z that feeds back as context for the next decision, enabling self-improvement without gradient updates.

## Results

On the D4RL benchmark (normalized so 100 = expert), IDT achieves a total average of 87.90, outperforming the best in-context RL baseline AT (85.21) and the strongest offline RL method TD3+BC (84.83). In the Grid World benchmark, IDT achieves state-of-the-art performance across all four standard environments and significantly surpasses AT and AD in the large-variant (40×40, 500-step) tasks where baselines fail to show self-improvement. In terms of efficiency, IDT's online evaluation time is 36× faster than AT and 27× faster in Large Grid World, due to its hierarchical sequence reconstruction that keeps the context smaller than one episode length.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDT (87.90 avg) beats both in-context RL baselines: AT (85.21) by +2.69 and AD (82.84) by +5.06 on D4RL total average, with the largest margin on Medium Hopper where IDT scores 83.24 vs. AD's 72.58 and AT's 70.45. Against the strongest offline RL baseline TD3+BC (84.83), IDT leads by +3.07 on average, winning on 7 of 9 tasks but losing slightly on Medium-Expert HalfCheetah (96.12 vs. 96.59) and Medium-Replay Hopper (98.59 vs. 98.12, a near-tie). IDT also outperforms the imitation learning baselines BC-10% (80.65) and DT (77.49) by substantial margins across all tasks. On Grid World, IDT achieves state-of-the-art in all four standard environments and dramatically outperforms AT and AD in the large-variant tasks (40×40, 500 steps), where AT and AD show no visible self-improvement trend—particularly in the sparse-reward Large Darkroom Hard. In terms of computational efficiency, IDT's evaluation time is 36× faster than AT on D4RL (6.51 min vs. 233.18 min for 10 episodes) and 27× faster on Large Grid World, making it the only method that can practically handle 1000-step horizons with across-episodic contexts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the in-context RL line of work initiated by Algorithm Distillation (Laskin et al., 2023) and the Agentic Transformer (Hao Liu, 2023), which demonstrated that transformer policies can self-improve via trial-and-error over across-episodic contexts of individual actions. IDT departs from this by borrowing the hierarchical RL decomposition (Nachum et al., 2018) and replacing the action-level sequence with a compressed sequence of latent high-level decisions (multivariate Gaussian vectors) that each control c-step action blocks. The Decision Transformer (Chen et al., 2021) provides the autoregressive sequence-prediction backbone, while the reparameterization trick (Jang et al., 2016) enables end-to-end gradient flow through the hierarchical structure. The 'chain of hindsight' framing from Liu et al. (2023) motivates the ascending-return sorting of trajectories in the context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 36× and 27× evaluation speedups make in-context RL practically viable on standard long-horizon benchmarks (D4RL/MuJoCo with 1000-step episodes) that were previously infeasible for action-level in-context methods due to quadratic attention costs. This opens a path toward combining the gradient-free self-improvement of in-context RL with the scale of conventional RL environments. Limitations include reliance on offline data quality, the learned latent decisions being uninterpretable, and validation primarily on relatively structured environments (Grid World, MuJoCo locomotion). Open questions include whether the hierarchical compression generalizes to truly complex or multi-modal tasks, how the choice of c interacts with task structure beyond the simple sensitivity analysis provided, and whether the approach extends to settings requiring multi-level hierarchies rather than a single high/low split.
