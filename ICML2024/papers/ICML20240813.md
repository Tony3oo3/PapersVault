## Problem

Despite widespread deployment of LLMs, there is no universally accepted set of criteria or standardized benchmark to comprehensively assess and compare the trustworthiness of different models. Prior taxonomies either failed to cover all relevant dimensions or introduced overlapping subcategories that complicated evaluation, leaving developers and users without a unified framework to answer: to what extent can we genuinely trust LLMs?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240813/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes carefully crafted text prompts and task instructions (designed to minimize prompt sensitivity and ensure unambiguous semantics) and produces per-dimension trustworthiness scores, overall rankings across 16 LLMs, and refusal/acceptance decisions for each evaluation item. Evaluation spans 30+ datasets organized into classification and generation tasks, with diverse metrics (accuracy, F1, semantic similarity, Pearson correlation, refusal-to-answer rate) tailored to each of the six benchmarked dimensions. The output is structured as a multi-dimensional ranking card where each model receives a score per dimension and an overall trustworthiness position.

## Method

TRUSTLLM proposes an eight-dimensional taxonomy of trustworthiness (truthfulness, safety, fairness, robustness, privacy, machine ethics, transparency, accountability) derived from a systematic review of 500 papers, then constructs an integrated benchmark covering six of these dimensions (transparency and accountability are excluded due to benchmarking difficulty) across 18+ subcategories and 30+ datasets. The framework evaluates 16 mainstream LLMs—spanning proprietary (GPT-4, ChatGPT, ERNIE) and open-weight (Llama2, Vicuna, Oasst, Code Llama) models of varying sizes—using a mix of existing and newly introduced datasets with diverse evaluation metrics. A key design choice is separating utility (functional effectiveness) from the eight trustworthiness dimensions, enabling the study to examine their relationship empirically. The evaluation protocol emphasizes consistent prompting, automated scoring via GPT-4/ChatGPT where appropriate, and careful handling of instruction-following limitations to reduce measurement bias.

## Results

TRUSTLLM evaluates 16 LLMs across 30+ datasets spanning six trustworthiness dimensions. Key quantitative findings include: GPT-4 achieves 94% average accuracy on machine ethics awareness tasks and 80%+ Refuse-to-Answer (RtA) rate in OOD detection with 92%+ F1 in OOD generalization, while the worst-performing models show only 0.4% RtA and ~30% F1. On fairness, even GPT-4 reaches only 65% accuracy on stereotype recognition, and the best model shows just 0.5% agreement with stereotypical statements versus ~60% for the worst. Llama2-7b exhibits a 57% false-refusal rate on non-harmful prompts, illustrating the over-alignment problem. On privacy, ChatGPT achieves the highest human-LLM agreement (Pearson r = 0.665), while Oasst-12b shows a negative correlation, indicating divergent privacy understanding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 16 LLMs (proprietary: GPT-4, ChatGPT, ERNIE; open-weight: Llama2-7b/13b/70b, Vicuna-7b/13b, Oasst-12b, Code Llama, BloombergGPT, PaLM, and others) against each other rather than proposing a new method. Proprietary LLMs (GPT-4, ChatGPT) generally outperform open-weight counterparts across safety, truthfulness, and robustness dimensions. However, Llama2-70b surprisingly surpasses proprietary models in several tasks (e.g., natural language inference resilience against adversarial attacks, moral behavior classification), demonstrating that open-weight models can achieve high trustworthiness without external moderators. In safety specifically, open-source LLMs significantly lag behind proprietary ones on jailbreak resistance, toxicity, and misuse tasks. On fairness, Oasst-12b and Vicuna-7b are the few models that handle disparagement fairly, while most LLMs display attribute-based biases. The overall trustworthiness ranking (Figure 2) shows GPT-4 and Llama2-70b consistently in the top tier, while smaller open-weight models (e.g., Llama2-7b, Vicuna-7b) rank lower, particularly on safety and robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRUSTLLM builds on the evaluation principles of DecodingTrust (Wang et al., 2023) and Dynaboard (Ma et al., 2021), but departs from prior taxonomies (HELM, DecodingTrust, Liu et al. 2023) that either failed to encompass all trustworthiness facets or introduced overlapping fine-grained subcategories that complicated benchmarking. The paper unifies these fragmented efforts into a single framework with eight clearly defined dimensions and a consolidated benchmark spanning six of them, while also incorporating 16 LLMs (proprietary and open-weight) rather than focusing on a single model family as prior assessments did.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As the first integrated benchmark covering six trustworthiness dimensions across 30+ datasets and 16 mainstream LLMs, TRUSTLLM provides a reference point for the field and highlights that trustworthiness and capability are positively correlated, that over-alignment (excessive refusal of benign prompts) is a widespread problem, and that open-source models like Llama2 can rival proprietary ones without external safety modules. Key open challenges remain: the evaluation is English-only, prompt sensitivity and instruction-following limitations confound results, rigorous certification of worst-case behavior is infeasible at LLM scale, and the interactions among trustworthiness dimensions are underexplored. The paper's call for an industry-academia-open-source AI alliance and for transparency in trustworthiness technologies points toward a governance-level response that goes beyond any single technical fix.
