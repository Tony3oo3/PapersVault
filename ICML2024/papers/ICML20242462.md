## Problem

While pruning is widely used for model compression, no prior work has benchmarked pruning algorithms against the actual sparsest achievable model, making it impossible to quantify how far current methods fall short of the theoretical optimum. This paper exposes that even on a simple synthetic dataset with small MLPs, state-of-the-art pruning techniques fail to recover the sparsest networks, revealing fundamental deficiencies in how they handle overparameterization, inter-layer alignment, and sparsity budgets.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242462/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The 4-layer ReLU MLP takes 2D coordinates (x, y) as input and outputs a binary class label, trained on the Cubist Spiral dataset of 50,000 evenly spaced points with straightened spiral edges. The combinatorial search takes a target accuracy threshold (95% or 99.5%) and a maximum network width D as parameters, and returns the set of all eligible sparsity masks that achieve the target with minimal nonzeros. Pruning algorithms are evaluated across 23 nonzero budgets ranging from 15 to 65, with network widths varying from 3 to 256 to study overparameterization effects.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-phase combinatorial search algorithm that identifies the sparsest model: Phase 1 performs a grid search over structured sparsity (number of active neurons per layer from 1 to D), training each configuration and selecting the one achieving target accuracy with fewest nonzeros; Phase 2 then enumerates all eligible unstructured sparsity masks within that optimal structured configuration, using symmetry-breaking (sequential decreasing nonzero counts per row) to eliminate functionally equivalent masks. The search is applied to 4-layer MLPs of width 16, and the resulting benchmark models (BENCH-95 with 30 nonzeros, BENCH-995 with 45 nonzeros) serve as ground-truth sparsity targets. Nine pruning algorithms (GMP, LTH, GraSP, SNIP, SynFlow, Iter SNIP, FORCE, ProsPr, RigL) are benchmarked across 485,838 experiments, including initialization-transfer experiments where the optimal initialization from the combinatorial search is given to pruning methods. A custom visualization tool renders the nonzero subnetwork as a graph, enabling identification of disconnected paths and suboptimal sparsity in pruned models.

## Results

On the Cubist Spiral dataset with a 4-layer MLP (width 16), the combinatorial search achieves >95% accuracy with only 30 nonzero parameters (96.04%) and >99.5% accuracy with 45 nonzeros (99.59%). The best pruning method for the 95% threshold is LTH at 44 nonzeros, while for the 99.5% threshold it is GMP at 61 nonzeros. With the optimal initialization from BENCH-995, the combinatorial search further reduces the 99.5% model to 38 nonzeros (99.70%), a sparsity no pruning method matches. Across 18,954 experiments at the optimal width, only 2 pruning runs (SNIP at 29 nonzeros/95.14%, GMP at 40 nonzeros/99.68%) matched or exceeded the combinatorial search.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All nine pruning baselines (GMP, LTH, GraSP, SNIP, SynFlow, Iter SNIP, FORCE, ProsPr, RigL) underperform the combinatorial search by a wide margin at both accuracy thresholds. At the 95% target, LTH is the best pruning method (44 nonzeros vs. 30 for combinatorial search), a 47% gap in parameter count; at 99.5%, GMP leads among pruners (61 vs. 45 nonzeros, a 36% gap). RigL, the state-of-the-art sparse-to-sparse method, requires 84 nonzeros for 99.5% accuracy—nearly double the combinatorial search's 45. Overparameterization (width 256) causes all pruning methods to largely fail, while reducing width to 3 improves their performance, indicating unstructured pruning cannot adequately perform structured pruning. Even when given the optimal width and the BENCH-995 initialization, no pruning method recovers the minimal sparsity mask found by the combinatorial search (38 nonzeros), with GMP and RigL still requiring 39 and 52 nonzeros respectively. The paper attributes these failures to disconnected paths between layers, suboptimal sparsity (pruned models can be further magnitude-pruned without accuracy loss), and the inability of current algorithms to benefit from overparameterization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical audit of the pruning literature rather than a methodological contribution, introducing a combinatorial search algorithm as a principled lower-bound benchmark for 'sparsest model' recovery. It departs from prior benchmarking efforts (ShrinkBench, SMC-Bench, random pruning comparisons) by searching over structured and unstructured sparsity masks jointly, guaranteeing connected paths and eliminating permutation symmetries. The work connects to the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis lineage by testing whether strong tickets can actually be found by existing methods, and to the disconnected-paths literature (Frankle et al. 2021; Vysogorets & Kempe 2023; Pham et al. 2023) by quantifying how prevalent the problem is. Unlike Fischer & Burkholz's 'Plant 'n' Seek,' all training starts from random initialization without handcrafted subnetworks, making the setup more representative of standard pruning practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that current pruning algorithms fail to recover the sparsest achievable model even under optimal width and initialization, attributing this to disconnected paths, poor structured-pruning behavior under overparameterization, and suboptimal sparsity that could be recovered by post-training magnitude pruning. The counterintuitive finding that overparameterization degrades pruning performance (contradicting the assumption that more parameters provide more combinatorial options) challenges a core design principle in sparse training. A key limitation is the restriction to four-layer MLPs on a synthetic dataset, leaving open whether these deficiencies persist or worsen at the scale of modern architectures and real-world tasks. The combinatorial search methodology and Cubist Spiral dataset provide a reproducible testbed for future pruning algorithms to demonstrate genuine progress toward true sparsity.
