## Problem

The Partial Credit Model (PCM) is a fundamental item response theory model for multi-level discrete responses, yet no inference algorithm for it had a finite-sample error guarantee. Existing methods—Joint MLE (JMLE) and Marginal MLE (MMLE)—suffer from the Neyman-Scott inconsistency when users far outnumber items, require accurate prior specification, and exhibit numerical instability at scale. This paper introduces the first spectral algorithm for the PCM with provably optimal non-asymptotic error bounds.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a sparse user-item response matrix X ∈ {*, 0, 1, …, K}^{n×m}, where * marks unobserved entries governed by a Bernoulli(p) sampling mask A. It outputs anchored item parameter estimates β ∈ R^{m×K} satisfying the identifiability constraint ⟨β^(1), 1_m⟩ = 0, from which user parameters θ are trivially recoverable. A secondary output is a top-L item ranking induced by the negative sum of estimated parameters across all K levels.

## Method

The spectral algorithm operates in two phases. In phase one, for each response level k, it constructs an m-state Markov chain whose off-diagonal entries are normalized pairwise counts Y_ij^(k,k−1) (the number of users who scored k−1 on item i and k on item j); the stationary distribution of this chain encodes the normalized item parameters via a log-transform, and the user ability parameters cancel out in the pairwise ratio by design. In phase two, scalar shifts δ^(k) for k ≥ 2 are estimated from a ratio of weighted cross-level pairwise counts, anchoring all levels to the first. The key theoretical insight is that the detailed-balance condition of the idealized Markov chain exactly recovers the item parameters, and the empirical chain concentrates around it under mild sampling conditions, yielding an optimal O(m/(np)) Frobenius error. An EM extension replaces the unweighted counts with posterior-class-weighted counts Y_ij^(k,k′)(Q,c) and initializes via C-means clustering on pairwise response-difference embeddings, enabling mixture-of-PCM learning without user covariates.

## Results

On 9 real-world datasets spanning education testing and recommendation systems, the spectral algorithm achieves heldout MAE and log-likelihood (LLH) competitive with MMLE and JMLE while being orders of magnitude faster. For example, on MovieLens-20M, the spectral algorithm runs in 170s versus 18,000s for MMLE and 200,000s for JMLE (~100x and ~1,200x speedups respectively). On the stock ranking application (u/nobjos dataset), the meta-trading strategy using the spectral algorithm with an 18-month window achieves the best test-period PNL of $14,003 (from $10,000 seed), outperforming MMLE ($13,545) and JMLE ($13,177) at their best hyperparameters. The theoretical analysis establishes that the spectral algorithm achieves the optimal Frobenius norm error rate of O(m/(np)) and near-optimal entrywise error of O(√(log m)/(√(np))).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed spectral algorithm against two classical PCM inference methods: MMLE (marginal maximum likelihood, Johnson 2007) and JMLE (joint maximum likelihood, Andersen 1973 / Haberman 1977). In terms of accuracy, the spectral algorithm is broadly competitive: on large recommendation datasets (ml-1M, ml-10M, ml-20M, Book Genome, EachMovie, HetRec-2k), it achieves MAE within 0.01–0.11 of JMLE and LLH within 0.05–0.20 of MMLE, sometimes matching or exceeding both. The Spectral-EM mixture variant (the paper's own extension) generally achieves the best LLH across all datasets (e.g., −0.918 vs −1.356 on LSAT; −1.248 vs −1.284 on ml-20M) at the cost of higher runtime. The decisive advantage is speed: the spectral algorithm is 100–1,200x faster than MMLE and JMLE on large datasets (e.g., 170s vs 18Ks vs 200Ks on ml-20M), and ~100x faster even on small datasets (0.002s vs 0.14–0.70s on LSAT/UCI/Grades3). On the stock ranking task, the spectral algorithm with W=18 achieves the highest test PNL ($14,003) compared to MMLE's best ($13,545 at W=12) and JMLE's best ($13,177 at W=12), while also exhibiting lower variance across hyperparameter choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spectral algorithm for the Rasch model (binary responses) from Nguyen & Zhang (2022) to the Partial Credit Model (multi-level ordered responses), introducing a two-phase procedure that first recovers normalized item parameters via Markov chain stationary distributions and then estimates level-specific shifts. The core analytical machinery relies on the Markov chain perturbation bound from Chen et al. (2019a), adapted to the multi-level setting. The paper departs from the classical JMLE and MMLE inference paradigms by being agnostic to user parameter distribution assumptions, and extends the spectral framework to a mixture-of-PCMs setting via a weighted EM algorithm that requires no user covariates—unlike prior mixture PCM models that relied on demographic features.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first non-asymptotic statistical analysis for the PCM, establishing matching upper and lower bounds on estimation error and optimal sample complexity for top-L ranking—gaps that had persisted since the model's introduction in 1978/1982. The practical impact is significant: the spectral algorithm is orders of magnitude faster than JMLE and MMLE while maintaining competitive accuracy, making PCM inference feasible at recommendation-system scale (hundreds of thousands of users). Open questions include whether the mixture extension scales to large C, the fairness and privacy implications of IRT-based inference (flagged by the authors as understudied), and whether the stock-ranking application generalizes beyond the single dataset tested.
