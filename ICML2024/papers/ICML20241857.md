## Problem

Off-policy hierarchical RL suffers from two coupled failures: (1) non-stationarity, where changing lower-level primitive behavior invalidates rewards stored in the higher-level replay buffer, and (2) infeasible subgoal generation, where the higher-level policy issues subgoals the current lower-level policy cannot achieve, stalling learning. Existing approaches address these issues separately (e.g., goal relabeling for non-stationarity, hand-designed primitives for feasibility), but no unified mechanism tackles both simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The higher-level policy consumes a concatenated state vector comprising robot position, goal position, and (for maze tasks) a 110-dim one-hot maze layout, and outputs a continuous subgoal in the same space as the state. The lower-level policy takes the current state plus the subgoal and outputs a 4-dim primitive action (3 position offsets + 1 gripper control). The learned reward model is a 3-layer MLP (512 hidden units, tanh activation) that maps (state, subgoal) pairs to scalar rewards, trained entirely from pairwise trajectory preferences generated by sparse environment signals rather than dense hand-crafted rewards or human annotations.

## Method

PIPER replaces human preference feedback with Primitive-in-the-Loop (PiL) feedback: sparse environment rewards at the end of each k-step primitive episode are used to generate pairwise trajectory comparisons between higher-level behaviors, which are then densified via hindsight relabeling (sampling alternative goals from visited states). A reward model is trained on these preferences using the Bradley-Terry cross-entropy loss, and this model is used to relabel higher-level replay buffer transitions, decoupling stored rewards from the non-stationary lower-level policy. To prevent infeasible subgoals, a KL-regularized objective constrains the higher-level policy toward a regularization distribution proportional to exp(m·V_π^L(s,g)), where V_π^L is the lower-level value function, yielding a closed-form optimal policy that balances the learned preference reward with subgoal achievability. Both levels are optimized with SAC, and soft target updates (τ=0.8) stabilize the reward model during training.

## Results

PIPER achieves greater than 50% success rates across challenging sparse-reward robotic environments (maze navigation, pick and place, push, hollow, and Franka kitchen), where most baselines fail to achieve significant progress. The method consistently outperforms hierarchical baselines (HAC, HIER) and flat baselines (FLAT, DAC, RFLAT) across all five environments. In the hardest tasks (kitchen, pick and place), PIPER also outperforms SAC-Dense (a flat SAC with hand-designed dense rewards), while SAC-Dense only wins on easier tasks (maze, push). PEBBLE-Dense (flat preference-based with human preferences) fails to solve any of the five tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PIPER significantly outperforms HAC (Levy et al., 2018) and the vanilla HIER baseline across all five environments, demonstrating that preference-based reward relabeling effectively mitigates the non-stationarity that plagues off-policy HRL. Against RAPS (Dalal et al., 2021), PIPER loses in maze navigation (where RAPS's hand-designed 'reach' primitive makes the task trivially easy for the higher level) but decisively wins in pick and place, push, hollow, and kitchen, where RAPS shows no progress. PIPER also significantly outperforms RFLAT (a flat single-level PEBBLE re-implementation with target networks and hindsight relabeling), confirming that the hierarchical preference-based structure provides a clear advantage over flat preference-based learning. Against DAC (Kostrikov et al., 2018) with access to a single expert demonstration, PIPER still outperforms, indicating that the hierarchical structure and preference-based reward learning compensate for the lack of privileged information. In the dense-reward comparison (Figure 6), PIPER outperforms SAC-Dense on harder tasks (pick and place, kitchen) while SAC-Dense wins on easier ones (maze, push), and PEBBLE-Dense fails entirely on all five tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

PIPER sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: goal-conditioned feudal hierarchical RL (Dayan & Hinton; Vezhnevets et al.) and preference-based reward learning (Christiano et al.; PEBBLE). It departs from prior HRL non-stationarity fixes (HAC's goal relabeling, Nachum et al.'s data-efficient HRL) by instead learning a high-level reward model via preference feedback and using it to relabel rewards in the higher-level replay buffer, thereby decoupling the high-level signal from the changing lower-level policy. The primitive-in-the-loop (PiL) scheme replaces the human-in-the-loop feedback of PEBBLE with sparse environment rewards, and the KL-regularized subgoal selection via the lower-level value function is a novel regularization mechanism not present in prior feudal or option-based HRL.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PIPER offers a principled, automated alternative to hand-designed dense rewards or human preference collection for hierarchical robotic tasks, achieving >50% success in sparse-reward manipulation environments where flat and hierarchical baselines stall. Key limitations include reliance on L2 state distance (limiting applicability to high-dimensional/visual goals), the PiL signal potentially missing safety or qualitative trajectory preferences that humans would encode, and the added complexity of maintaining a separate reward model with target networks. Open questions include whether the approach extends to image-based state spaces, whether PiL can be augmented with learned safety value functions, and how it interacts with demonstration-based learning at the lower level.
