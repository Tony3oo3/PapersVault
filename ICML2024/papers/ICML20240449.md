## Problem

Information-theoretic generalization bounds have achieved remarkable success for pointwise learning (m=1) but remain unexplored for multivariate loss functions used in contrastive, metric, and ranking learning. Two fundamental obstacles block straightforward extension: (1) the empirical risk is no longer a sum of i.i.d random variables due to overlapping training samples across loss terms, breaking the independence assumption underlying input-output MI bounds, and (2) prediction-based bounds under the supersample framework involve information quantities whose dimensionality grows exponentially with the number of samples m per loss term, destroying computational feasibility.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a training dataset of n i.i.d samples, a bounded multivariate loss function ℓ: W × Z^m → [0,1] where m determines the learning paradigm (m=1 pointwise, m=2 pairwise/contrastive, m=3 triplet, m=4 quadruplet, m≥5 N-pair/NT-Xent), and a learning algorithm such as SGLD, SGD, or Adam. The output is a family of generalization error bounds expressed through information-theoretic quantities: input-output MI I(W; Z_u), conditional MI I(W; S_u | Z̃), and loss-difference MI I(Δ_u^{Φ_u}; S_{u1}). A key design property is that the prediction-based bounds require only 2-dimensional information quantities regardless of m, ensuring direct computational tractability even for high-order losses like NT-Xent.

## Method

The paper develops a unified framework with three families of bounds for arbitrary m ≥ 1. For hypothesis-based bounds, it introduces group-wise stability I(W; Z_u) to replace point-wise stability and employs a bottom-to-top reduction technique to handle the non-i.i.d challenge, yielding O(√(m/n)) square-root rates and O(m/n) fast-rate convergence. For prediction-based bounds, the central insight is a novel decomposition of supersample variables: by exploiting the independence between S_{u1} and the XOR-based selection variable Φ_u, the bound reduces to the 2-dimensional quantity I(Δ_u^{Φ_u}; S_{u1}), which is strictly tighter than the straightforward e-CMI extension whose dimensionality scales as 2^m + m. For algorithm-based bounds, it extends SGLD analysis using conditional gradient covariance Σ_t = Cov_{B_t}[G_t] (strictly tighter than unconditional covariance) combined with the Donsker-Varadhan formula. Fast-rate bounds further incorporate weighted generalization error with optimized constants C_1, C_2 and a loss-variance refinement V(γ) for binary losses, achieving 1/n scaling in the interpolating regime.

## Results

The paper's numerical experiments validate the proposed information-theoretic bounds across synthetic Gaussian datasets (MLP with m=1,2,3,4) and real-world settings (CNN on MNIST, ResNet-50 on CIFAR-10, CLIP ViT-B/32 on Flickr30k). The fast-rate bound (Theorem 4.6) consistently yields the tightest estimates when the model achieves low training risk, which is the typical practical scenario. The binary KL bound (Theorem 4.8) becomes more advantageous as training risk increases, while the square-root bound (Theorem 4.2) is competitive when disintegrated MI values are diverse. All bounds decrease with increasing sample size n and adapt to different values of m, with the convergence rate scaling as O(√(m/n)) for the square-root bound and O(m/n) for the fast-rate bound in the interpolating regime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external competing methods but rather evaluates three families of its own prediction-based bounds (square-root, binary KL, fast-rate) against the empirical generalization gap across multiple architectures and datasets. The fast-rate bound (Theorem 4.6) dominates in the low-training-risk regime encountered in practice (e.g., CNN on MNIST with Adam, ResNet-50 on CIFAR-10 with SGD, CLIP on Flickr30k), consistently tracking the generalization gap most closely. The binary KL bound (Theorem 4.8) provides tighter estimates when training risk is substantially high, as shown in the comparative analysis in Figure 2. The square-root bound (Theorem 4.2) is most effective when the disintegrated MI values I^Z̃(W; S_u) are scattered across the supersample, particularly in intermediate regions of the L_n and B spectrum. For the CLIP self-supervised setting, the paper notes that the generalization gap actually increases with larger n due to false negatives in ground-truth labels, yet the bounds still capture this non-monotonic behavior effectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the information-theoretic generalization bounds lineage (Xu & Raginsky 2017; Harutyunyan et al. 2021; Steinke & Zakynthinou 2020; Hellström & Durisi 2021, 2022b; Wang & Mao 2023) from pointwise (m=1) to arbitrary multivariate loss functions (m≥2), overcoming two key obstacles: non-i.i.d. loss terms from overlapping training samples and exponential dimensionality growth in prediction-based bounds. The hypothesis-based bounds generalize input-output MI and CMI/supersample frameworks via a bottom-to-top reduction to group-wise stabilities, while the prediction-based bounds introduce a novel XOR-based decomposition of supersample variables that reduces the MI dimensionality to 2 regardless of m. The SGLD analysis builds on Dong et al. (2023) by incorporating conditional gradient covariance, and the fast-rate bounds extend Hellström & Durisi (2021) with broader admissible parameter ranges.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a clear gap: contrastive learning, metric learning, AUC maximization, and ranking all rely on non-pointwise losses, yet prior information-theoretic generalization analysis was entirely confined to m=1. The unified framework provides the first explicit linkage between generalization error and the number of instances m in the loss, with a √m degradation factor that corroborates stability-based analyses. Open questions include whether the bounds can be tightened further for specific loss structures (e.g., the NT-Xent loss used in SimCLR), whether the SGLD gradient-covariance analysis extends cleanly to adaptive optimizers like Adam, and whether the prediction-based bounds remain computationally tractable for very large m in practice. The bounds, while tighter than straightforward adaptations, still tend to be loose in absolute terms for large networks.
