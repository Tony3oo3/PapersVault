## Problem

Existing time series explanation methods treat individual time steps as isolated entities, failing to capture the dynamic interactions between observations that shape model predictions. While recent approaches address out-of-distribution sampling or marginal gain confounding, no prior method simultaneously handles temporal dependencies, accumulation effects from prior observations, and the need to explain subsequences rather than single points. This paper identifies that conflating interaction effects with main effects causes methods like FO, AFO, and FIT to miss critical time steps entirely.

## Input / Output

The method takes a multivariate time series X ∈ R^{D×T} (D features, T time steps) and context variables C as input to a black-box temporal model f(X,C) that outputs an L-dimensional vector. It produces per-time-step importance scores φ_t that decompose into a marginal main effect ν(x̄_t) (the conditional gain of x̄_t given x̄_{t-1}) plus equal shares of all pure interactions involving that time step. For multivariate inputs, the method iterates over each feature row, treating remaining features as context, yielding a D×T importance matrix. A CVAE trained on the full dataset generates N=50 conditional samples to approximate the expectations required for interaction estimation.

## Method

FDTempExplainer decomposes the black-box model into an additive form (Eq. 1) consisting of per-time-step main effects m(x_t) and pure interactions I^k(X_{t:t+k-1}) restricted to consecutive time steps. The key theoretical contribution is the 'pure interaction' definition (Definition 3.1), which requires that integrating an interaction over the conditional distribution of any sub-segment yields zero, ensuring interactions are not confounded by prior context. Lemma 3.3 then shows that each interaction can be computed in closed form as a four-term combination of conditional expectations E_{t1:t2|t3:t4}(f(·)), where the conditioning segments are chosen to cancel all other interaction terms via the purity constraint. A CVAE with a masking mechanism generates samples from p(X_{t1:t2}|X̄_{t3:t4}, C̄) to approximate these expectations, avoiding OOD samples by conditioning on observed data. Attribution assigns each interaction's effect equally among the k+1 time steps it spans, and computational complexity O(T²LN) can be reduced via interaction-order thresholds or patch-based segmentation.

## Results

On the synthetic anomaly detection task, FDTempExplainer achieves near-perfect Top-3 accuracy (0.9967) and AUPRC (0.9968) on the interaction-aware model f^(2)(x), substantially outperforming the best baseline IG (0.872 Acc, 0.936 AUPRC). On the real-world MIMIC-III classification task, FDTempExplainer yields the largest AUPRC drop (0.043 at k=50) and 95% AUPRC drop (0.030), indicating it identifies the most critical time steps. On the Large Kitchen Appliances dataset, it achieves the lowest consistency (0.2293) and highest ACC drop (0.6453) at k=40, confirming superior identification of influential time steps. On the forecasting task, FDTempExplainer attains the best Top-2 accuracy (0.6438) and AUPRC (0.7306).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FDTempExplainer outperforms all seven baselines across every task and metric. The most dramatic gap appears on the interaction-aware anomaly detection model f^(2)(x), where FDTempExplainer reaches 0.9967 Top-3 accuracy versus 0.872 for Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017) and 0.782 for DeepLIFT (Shrikumar et al., 2017), while marginal-gain methods like FO (Suresh et al., 2017) and FIT (Tonekaboni et al., 2020) collapse to 0.183 and 0.257 respectively because they conflate interaction effects with main effects. On MIMIC-III, FDTempExplainer's AUPRC-drop at k=50 (0.043) exceeds WinIT (Leung et al., 2023) by 13% and DynamicMask (Crabbé et al., 2021) by 10%, while GradSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2018) and DeepLIFT trail by 35% and 49% respectively. On Large Kitchen Appliances, FDTempExplainer achieves the lowest consistency (0.2293) and highest ACC drop (0.6453) at k=40, beating the next-best WinIT (0.344 consistency, 0.5573 ACC drop) by a wide margin. The forecasting task further confirms the advantage: FDTempExplainer's Top-2 accuracy of 0.6438 surpasses GradSHAP (0.6125) and IG (0.5375), while FO and AFO are near chance (0.02). Overall, methods that ignore temporal interactions (FO, AFO, FIT) suffer the largest degradation, while even interaction-aware baselines (WinIT, DynamicMask) remain 10–30% behind FDTempExplainer on real-world data.

## Relation to Prior Work

FDTempExplainer adapts the 'pure interaction' concept from PureGAM (Sun et al., 2022) — originally defined over global feature marginals — to a sample-specific, conditional setting for time series, where purity is enforced relative to the observed sequence rather than the dataset-wide distribution. It departs from prior time series explanation methods (FIT, WinIT, DynamicMask, FO/AFO) that either treat time steps as near-independent, aggregate effects over fixed windows, or rely on distribution-shift metrics, by explicitly decomposing the model output into main effects and higher-order interactions restricted to consecutive time steps. The functional decomposition framework itself echoes ANOVA/Hoeffding decompositions, but the consecutive-interaction constraint and the CVAE-based conditional expectation estimation are tailored to the temporal setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a concrete gap: no prior method simultaneously addresses temporal dependency, accumulation/phase-shift effects, and subsequence-level interactions in a single principled framework, and the OOD problem that plagues perturbation-based explainers is structurally avoided by conditioning all expectations on the observed sample. Key limitations include the O(T²LN) computational cost (mitigated only heuristically via patching or interaction-order truncation), the assumption that interactions are confined to consecutive time steps (which may fail for strongly periodic or long-range-dependent series), and reliance on a CVAE whose quality affects expectation estimates. Open questions include scalability to very long sequences (e.g., multi-day clinical monitoring), extension to architectures beyond LSTMs (transformers, state-space models), and whether the equal-split attribution of interaction effects is the most informative choice for downstream decision-making.
