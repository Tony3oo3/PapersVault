## Problem

Neural-symbolic and abductive learning systems can achieve high target accuracy while learning incorrect semantics for intermediate symbolic concepts (reasoning shortcuts), undermining generalization and interpretability. Although several mitigation techniques exist, the theoretical understanding of how shortcut severity depends on knowledge base complexity, sample size, and hypothesis space structure has remained largely absent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242334/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For MNIST-Addition, the input is a pair of 28×28 MNIST digit images and the output is their sum (0–18), with the two individual digits serving as unobserved intermediate concepts constrained by an addition rule. For BDD-OIA, the input is a 2048-dimensional embedding of a driving scene frame (preprocessed via Faster-RCNN on BDD-100k) and the output is a multi-label action prediction over {move_forward, stop, turn_left, turn_right}, with 21 intermediate symbolic concepts (e.g., red_light, vehicle_ahead) linked by expert-defined logical rules. In both settings, only (x, y) pairs are observed during training; the intermediate concept z is never directly labeled.

## Method

The paper formalizes shortcut risk R_s as the gap between the ideal cross-entropy loss on intermediate concepts and the achievable NeSy loss, and defines knowledge base complexity D_KB as the expected count of symbol assignments conflicting with the KB. It proves R_s is unbounded when the hypothesis space is unconstrained, then derives high-probability upper bounds of O(ln(C−D_KB) + 1/√N + γ) under label-smoothness or pre-training assumptions on the hypothesis space. For abductive learning, it shows R_s^ABL ≤ R_s and that a well-designed distance function (e.g., one built from a frozen pre-trained model) bounds R_s^ABL at O(κ), where κ is the sampler's error rate. Empirically, the analysis is validated on six difficulty levels of MNIST-Addition and BDD-OIA, comparing Semantic Loss, LTN, DeepProbLog, and ABL variants with different distance functions (trivial, Hamming, confidence-based, pre-trained-model-based).

## Results

On MNIST-Addition (6 difficulty levels), ABL(P) achieves 98.57% ± 0.10% average grid accuracy (GAcc), dramatically outperforming the best NeSy baseline DPL (31.72% ± 15.69%), SL (27.26% ± 20.32%), and LTN (21.33% ± 16.30%). Even the trivial ABL (Dis=0) reaches 54.04% ± 37.77% GAcc, exceeding all NeSy methods. On BDD-OIA, ABL(P) achieves 85.13% GAcc versus DPL's 57.74%, while ABL(C) reaches 78.54% GAcc. Target accuracy (Acc) on MNIST-Addition follows a similar pattern: ABL(P) attains 97.16% average vs. DPL's 69.65%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ABL variants against three representative NeSy algorithms: Semantic Loss (SL), Logic Tensor Networks (LTN), and DeepProbLog (DPL). ABL(P), which uses a pre-trained model as the distance function, dominates all NeSy baselines by a wide margin on both datasets: 98.57% vs. 31.72% (DPL) average GAcc on MNIST-Addition, and 85.13% vs. 57.74% GAcc on BDD-OIA. Even the trivial ABL (Dis=0) outperforms all NeSy methods on MNIST-Addition (54.04% vs. 31.72% best NeSy), supporting the theoretical result that ABL's shortcut risk is upper-bounded by NeSy's. ABL(C), which uses the current model's confidence as distance, achieves 57.97% GAcc on MNIST-Addition but degrades sharply at the hardest level (4.04% at Level VI), indicating sensitivity to knowledge base complexity. On BDD-OIA, ABL(C) (78.54% GAcc) and ABL(H) (76.94%) both outperform DPL (57.74%), while ABL(P) achieves the best GAcc at a slight cost to target Acc (74.03% vs. 75.17% for ABL). The results confirm the paper's theoretical prediction that ABL is more robust to reasoning shortcuts than NeSy, and that the choice of distance function is critical for ABL's performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the reasoning shortcut analysis initiated by Marconato et al. (2023b) by replacing their maximal-log-likelihood mismatch definition with a more granular shortcut risk R_s and deriving explicit upper bounds that decompose into knowledge base complexity, sample size, and hypothesis space terms. It builds on the ABL framework of Zhou (2019) by providing the first theoretical characterization of how the distance function design in ABL affects shortcut risk, showing a provable O(κ) bound that is structurally tighter than the NeSy bound. The paper positions itself as a theoretical complement to prior empirical mitigations (label smoothing, pre-training, semi-supervised data) by formalizing the assumptions under which those techniques provably reduce shortcut risk.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide closed-form upper bounds on reasoning shortcut severity for both NeSy and ABL systems, giving practitioners a principled way to understand when and why shortcuts arise and which design choices (KB complexity, sample size, distance function) most effectively mitigate them. The finding that ABL is provably more robust than NeSy to shortcuts, and that a simple pre-trained-model distance function (ABL(P)) achieves near-perfect GAcc across all difficulty levels, offers a concrete and low-cost strategy for the field. Limitations include the expectation-form KB complexity (ignoring variance), analysis restricted to the general NeSy loss form rather than specific algorithms, and empirical validation on only two relatively small-scale tasks. Open questions include extending the analysis to higher-order KB structures, incorporating sample distribution effects, and analyzing specific NeSy algorithms (e.g., Scallop, DL2) under the same framework.
