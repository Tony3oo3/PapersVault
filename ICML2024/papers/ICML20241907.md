## Problem

Prior work using deep neural networks to probe brain activity has focused almost exclusively on unimodal models (vision-only or language-only), leaving the neural substrates of vision-language integration largely unexplored. There is no systematic, statistically rigorous framework for identifying which brain regions are specifically explained by multimodal representations rather than by unimodal or linearly-combined features, and no controlled comparison that isolates the effect of multimodal training from confounds like architecture, parameter count, or training dataset.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241907/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes paired movie frames and dialogue sentences organized as 'event structures' anchored to either word onsets (language-aligned) or scene cuts (vision-aligned), with corresponding SEEG recordings extracted as 4000ms windows (±2000ms around each event) split into 200ms sub-windows with 25ms sliding steps. Outputs are Pearson correlation scores per time bin per electrode per model, which feed into a five-stage statistical testing hierarchy that produces binary multimodal-integration labels per electrode, regional aggregations on the DKT atlas, and a per-electrode ranking of which multimodal architecture best explains the activity.

## Method

The pipeline extracts feature vectors from every layer of 12 pretrained DNNs (7 multimodal, 5 unimodal, spanning transformers and CNNs trained via cross-attention or contrastive learning), applies sparse random projection for dimensionality reduction, and fits 5-fold ridge regression with contiguous train/validation/test splits to predict averaged neural activity per electrode and time bin. A 1000-iteration bootstrap over event structures yields 95% confidence intervals that filter out time bins lacking meaningful signal, and a second-order bootstrap across time bins tests whether the top-ranked model significantly outperforms the runner-up per electrode (with FDR correction). The key controlled experiment compares SLIP-Combo (multimodal CLIP-style + SimCLR training) against SLIP-SimCLR (unimodal SimCLR training), holding architecture (ViT), parameter count, and training dataset (YFCC15M) identical so that any predictivity difference is attributable solely to multimodal training. Five tests of increasing stringency—ranging from 'any multimodal model beats all unimodal models in one alignment' to 'a multimodal model beats linearly-integrated concatenation models in both alignments'—operationalize multimodal integration and identify candidate brain regions.

## Results

Across 39,420 data points (28 models × 2 alignments × 1090 electrodes), the global average Pearson r is 0.142 [0.0797, 0.269], with a maximum of 0.539. Trained models significantly outperform randomly initialized counterparts on all 278 comparable electrodes (average Δr = 0.107 [0.026, 0.238]). The strict multimodality test identifies 12/1090 (1.1%) electrodes where multimodal models outperform all unimodal models in both language- and vision-aligned data, while the controlled SLIP-Combo vs. SLIP-SimCLR comparison identifies 28/1090 (2.57%) electrodes. Overall, an average of 141/1090 sites (12.94%) show evidence of multimodal integration, concentrated in the temporoparietal junction (superior/middle temporal cortex, inferior parietal lobe, supramarginal gyrus) and frontal regions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The controlled SLIP-Combo vs. SLIP-SimCLR comparison (identical ViT architecture, YFCC15M dataset, ~86M parameters) is the paper's most rigorous test: SLIP-Combo outperforms SLIP-SimCLR on 28/1090 electrodes in both alignments (Δr = 0.0522 language, 0.026 vision), confirming that multimodal contrastive training—not architecture or dataset—drives the neural predictivity gain. Among architecturally multimodal models, BLIP is the best brain predictor, followed by ALBEF and Flava, but all three are outperformed by the smaller SLIP-Combo/SLIP-CLIP models (86M vs. 210–241M parameters). Unimodal baselines (SBERT, SimCSE for language; BEIT, ConvNeXt for vision) are consistently outperformed by multimodal models at the identified integration sites, with average performance differences of r ≈ 0.08. Critically, multimodal models perform *worse* than unimodal models on unimodal tasks (next-word perplexity 210.3 vs. 133.4; scene classification 54.3% vs. 74.2%), ruling out the explanation that multimodal models simply have better unimodal representations. The non-linear integration test confirms that truly multimodal models (cross-attention or contrastive) outperform linearly-integrated baselines MultiConcat and MultiLin (Δr = 0.0566 language, 0.084 vision), indicating the brain performs non-linear vision-language fusion rather than simple feature concatenation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established paradigm of using DNN representations to predict neural activity (Conwell et al., 2021; Goldstein et al., 2021) from unimodal to multimodal settings, using SEEG recordings from movie viewing in the AMMT dataset. Prior DNN-brain alignment work has focused on vision-only or language-only prediction, while fMRI studies of multimodal integration (Jouen et al., 2015; Bzdok et al., 2016) have identified candidate regions but lack the temporal resolution and model-comparison rigor of this approach. The key methodological departure is the controlled SLIP comparison (same architecture, same dataset, varying only the training objective), which isolates multimodality from confounds of architecture, parameter count, and training data that plague broader model zoology comparisons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a systematic, statistically rigorous framework for identifying brain sites of vision-language integration and for ranking which multimodal training objectives best explain neural activity at those sites. The finding that CLIP-style contrastive training outperforms architecturally multimodal cross-attention models (despite fewer parameters) for predicting multimodal integration sites is a useful signal for AI practitioners designing brain-inspired multimodal systems. Key limitations include the single-electrode analysis (no causal or connectivity inference), aggregation across time bins (losing the temporal dynamics of integration), and dependence on current model architectures that will shift as the field evolves. Open questions include the time course of integration, causal relationships between identified regions, and whether the identified motifs generalize to other modality pairs (audio, motor).
