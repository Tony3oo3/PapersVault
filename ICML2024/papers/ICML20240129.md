## Problem

Causal tracing, the dominant interpretability framework for text-to-image models, successfully localizes visual-attribute knowledge in early Stable-Diffusion variants but fails to identify unique control points in newer architectures like SD-XL and DeepFloyd. This limits the ability to perform targeted, efficient model editing across the broader landscape of open-source text-to-image models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240129/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairs of text prompts (an original prompt containing a visual attribute and a target prompt with that attribute removed or updated) along with the pretrained UNet weights of a text-to-image model. It produces (1) a small set of adjacent cross-attention layer indices (e.g., layers 45–47 out of 70 in SD-XL) identified as controlling a given attribute, and (2) closed-form updated key and value matrices at those layers, yielding an edited model that generates images with the attribute modified. The intervention operates at inference time by swapping text-embedding inputs to selected cross-attention layers, requiring no retraining.

## Method

LOCOGEN localizes knowledge by measuring the direct effect of intermediate cross-attention layers: it replaces the text-embedding input to a window of m adjacent cross-attention layers with a target prompt embedding while keeping the original embedding in all other layers, then scores the generated image against both prompts using CLIP-Score. An iterative hyperparameter search over m (window size) and layer position identifies the minimal set of layers whose intervention removes or updates the target attribute. LOCOEDIT then performs a closed-form ridge-regression update on the key and value matrices at those identified layers, mapping original prompt embeddings to target prompt embeddings in a single step without gradient-based fine-tuning. The approach is distinguished from causal tracing by intervening on layer inputs (direct effect) rather than corrupting and restoring layer outputs (indirect effect), making it applicable to models where causal states are absent or distributed. For certain attributes like style, the paper further shows that knowledge can be traced to individual neurons within the identified layers, enabling neuron-level dropout as an even finer-grained editing mechanism.

## Results

LOCOGEN identifies a small number of adjacent cross-attention layers (m=2–5 out of 16–70 total) that control specific visual attributes across all tested models: style at layer 8 (SD-v1-5, SD-v2-1, OpenJourney) or layer 45 (SD-XL), objects at layer 6 (SD-v1-5, OpenJourney) or layer 15 (SD-XL), and facts at layer 6–7 (SD variants) or layer 45 (SD-XL). A human study confirms 92.58% verification that intervening on these identified layers alters the target attribute. For robustness, LOCOEDIT preserves generic-prompt CLIP-Score at 29.99 (style) and 29.40 (objects) versus 30.04 for the unedited model, showing minimal collateral damage.

| Editing Method | Style Edited Model | Object Edited Model |

| DiffQuickFix (Basu et al., 2023) | 29.61 | 29.32 |
| LOCOEDIT (Ours) | **29.99** | **29.40** |

## Baselines & Comparisons

LOCOEDIT is compared against three baselines on SD-v1-5: DiffQuickFix (Basu et al., 2023, closed-form text-encoder editing), Concept-Ablation (Kumari et al., 2023, fine-tuning), and Concept-Deletion (Gandikota et al., 2023, fine-tuning). For style removal, LOCOEDIT achieves comparable CLIP-Score drops to all three baselines. For factual updates, LOCOEDIT outperforms DiffQuickFix with a larger increase in target-fact CLIP-Score, while Concept-Ablation and Concept-Deletion do not support factual editing at all. For object modification, LOCOEDIT shows a slightly smaller CLIP-Score drop than the baselines, which the authors attribute to modifying trademarked characteristics rather than the entire object. On generic-prompt robustness (Table 1), LOCOEDIT causes less CLIP-Score degradation than DiffQuickFix (29.99 vs. 29.61 for style; 29.40 vs. 29.32 for objects), indicating that localized UNet editing is less disruptive than text-encoder-space editing. The key advantage of LOCOEDIT over all baselines is generalizability: it works on SD-XL and OpenJourney where DiffQuickFix's text-encoder causal state does not exist, and it operates in closed form without fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Basu et al. (2023), which used causal tracing to show that knowledge in Stable-Diffusion-v1-5 is localized in the first CLIP text-encoder layer and distributed across the UNet. The key departure is that causal tracing fails to identify localized states in newer models (SD-XL, DeepFloyd), prompting the authors to introduce 'mechanistic localization' via LOCOGEN, which measures direct effects by intervening on cross-attention key/value matrices rather than measuring indirect effects through corrupted-input restoration. LOCOEDIT then applies the same closed-form weight-update strategy as Basu et al. but targets the UNet cross-attention layers identified by LOCOGEN, and the paper further explores neuron-level dropout as a finer-grained editing mechanism not present in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that causal tracing does not generalize beyond early Stable-Diffusion variants is an important negative result that reshapes how the interpretability community should approach newer T2I architectures. LOCOGEN provides a practical, model-agnostic tool for identifying a small set of controlling layers, enabling efficient closed-form edits (style removal, object modification, fact updating) without full fine-tuning. However, the method's failure on DeepFloyd (due to T5's bidirectional attention) highlights an open problem: designing fast editing methods for models with non-causal text encoders. The neuron-level editing results, while promising, are limited to style attributes and SD-v1-5, leaving open whether this granularity generalizes to other attributes and architectures.
