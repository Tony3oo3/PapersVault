## Problem

Standard forward transition models (FTMs) used in model-based offline RL are inductively biased toward smooth regressors, which conflicts with the discontinuous or sharply curved transition dynamics prevalent in real-world environments (e.g., frictional contact in MuJoCo). This mismatch causes destructive interference during training and leads to poor generalization on out-of-distribution state-action pairs, degrading both off-policy evaluation accuracy and offline policy optimization performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an offline transition dataset of (s, a, s') tuples and, at inference, takes a current state-action pair to produce next-state samples via iterative Langevin MCMC (100 steps with 0.1 noise for evaluation, 30 steps for RL rollouts). Unlike FTMs that output a parametric Gaussian mean and diagonal covariance in a single forward pass, ETMs require iterative gradient-based sampling, resulting in roughly 5× more inference time. For downstream tasks, the sampled next-states feed into direct model rollout for OPE (estimating policy values over N=10 trajectories) or into an ensemble of 5 ETMs for uncertainty-penalized policy optimization in EMPO.

## Method

The transition probability is defined implicitly by a scalar energy function E_θ(s,a,s') as p_θ(s'|s,a) ∝ exp(−E_θ), parameterized by a 4-layer MLP (200 hidden units, ReLU). Training minimizes an InfoNCE contrastive loss where the positive sample is the true next state and K−1 negative samples are drawn from a Langevin MCMC chain (50 steps, stepsize 1e-3, noise 0.5), plus a gradient-penalty term (margin M=5) to keep the energy landscape Lipschitz-bounded for stable MCMC. The key theoretical contribution derives InfoNCE as a robust variant of maximum-likelihood energy learning: by tolerating an ε-KL divergence between the MCMC sampling distribution and the true energy-induced distribution via a Lagrange-dual formulation, InfoNCE emerges as the resulting objective, making it more tolerant to finite-step MCMC errors than Contrastive Divergence. For offline RL, EMPO wraps an ensemble of 5 ETMs around SAC, penalizing rewards by the empirical standard deviation of ensemble next-state samples to introduce conservatism analogous to MOPO's ensemble-std penalty.

## Results

On 12 D4RL Gym-Mujoco offline RL tasks, EMPO achieves the highest average normalized score of 82.0, outperforming the previous best MOBILE (80.0) and EDAC (76.0), and solves 7/12 tasks (score ≥ 95.0) versus 5/12 for prior best methods. On the DOPE off-policy evaluation benchmark (32 tasks across Gym-Mujoco and Adroit), ETM-based direct rollout attains the lowest mean absolute error, highest rank correlation, and smallest regret@1 compared to FTM and five model-free baselines (FQE, DR, IS, DICE, VPM). On NeoRL tasks with narrow data coverage, EMPO achieves an average score of 56.0, matching CQL (56.1) and substantially outperforming MOPO (28.5).

## Baselines & Comparisons

EMPO outperforms all eight offline RL baselines on average score: it beats MOBILE (80.0) by 2.0 points, EDAC (76.0) by 6.0, MOPO (70.3) by 11.7, RAMBO (67.7) by 14.3, COMBO (66.8) by 15.2, TT (62.3) by 19.7, CQL (61.6) by 20.4, and TD3+BC (58.2) by 23.8. The closest competitor is MOBILE, which EMPO narrowly surpasses on average but clearly outperforms on solved tasks (7 vs 5). For off-policy evaluation, ETM-based direct rollout dominates all five model-free OPE baselines (FQE, DR, IS, DICE, VPM) and the FTM-based model on all three metrics (MAE, rank correlation, regret@1) across 32 DOPE tasks; for example, on hopper-medium the ETM achieves 47±21 MAE versus 303±22 for FTM and 283±73 for FQE. Against diffusion-based methods (Diffuser, Decision Diffuser), EMPO achieves a substantially higher average of 98.6 versus 75.3 and 81.8 respectively on the 9-task subset in Table 12. On NeoRL with narrow-coverage data, EMPO (56.0) matches CQL (56.1) and far exceeds MOPO (28.5), confirming that the energy-based transition model's OOD generalization advantage is most pronounced when data coverage is limited.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the energy-based model (EBM) formulation from generative modeling (Song & Kingma, 2021) to the problem of transition dynamics learning, replacing the standard forward model that explicitly predicts a diagonal Gaussian over next states with an implicit scalar energy function scored via contrastive InfoNCE loss. The theoretical contribution connects InfoNCE to maximum likelihood for EBMs by showing it is a robust variant of Contrastive Divergence (Hinton, 2002) that tolerates MCMC sampling error, bridging two previously disconnected training paradigms. For the RL application, the method (EMPO) is a direct adaptation of MOPO's ensemble-uncertainty penalty framework (Yu et al., 2020), substituting ETM ensembles for forward model ensembles. The motivation draws on the interference analysis of Bengio et al. (2020) to explain why smooth function approximators (the inductive bias of SGD-trained networks) fail on discontinuous transitions, positioning ETM as a principled alternative rather than merely a larger-capacity architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete and recurring failure mode in model-based offline RL: forward models' smoothness bias causes poor generalization on the discontinuous, high-curvature transitions ubiquitous in physics-based environments with contact dynamics. The OOD generalization advantage demonstrated on DOPE (where ETM trained on random-policy data substantially outperforms FTM on SAC-policy data) is practically significant for offline settings where the target policy inevitably visits states outside the behavior distribution. However, the ~5× inference cost from iterative Langevin sampling and ~40% training overhead for EMPO are non-trivial practical barriers that limit immediate adoption; the authors note that advanced sampling or parameterized inference could mitigate this. Open questions include whether ETMs can be combined with hybrid OPE methods (fitted value + model rollout), whether the contrastive training objective can be replaced with more efficient alternatives, and whether the approach extends to high-dimensional or partially observable settings.
