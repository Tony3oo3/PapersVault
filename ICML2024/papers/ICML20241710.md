## Problem

Reward hacking in RLHF arises when policies exploit proxy reward model failures, driven by two underexplored challenges: distribution shifts as the policy drifts during RL, and noisy/inconsistent human preference labels. The standard mitigation—prediction ensembling (ENS)—incurs linear memory and inference overheads that scale poorly with model size, and the paper shows it fails to improve robustness to label corruption. WARM addresses both gaps by proposing weight-space averaging of multiple RMs as an efficient, robust alternative.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241710/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The reward model takes a text prompt x and a candidate generation y (e.g., a summary) and outputs a single scalar reward r_φ(x,y). Training uses Bradley-Terry pairwise preference triplets (x, y⁺, y⁻) with a negative log-likelihood loss over the reward difference. The final WARM model is a single PaLM-XXS transformer with a linear-probed classification head, so inference cost is identical to any individual RM—no ensemble of M models is needed at serving time.

## Method

WARM fine-tunes M reward models from a shared SFT initialization (PaLM-XXS + linear-probed classifier), introducing diversity through different hyperparameters (learning rate, dropout), data ordering, and optionally different SFT checkpoints along a single trajectory (the 'Baklava' strategy). The M resulting weight vectors are then averaged in weight space: φ^WARM = (1/M) Σᵢ φᵢ, relying on the linear mode connectivity property that fine-tuned weights sharing the same pre-training remain linearly interpolable. Theoretically, the paper shows that in the M→∞ limit, WA weights each feature by p_j² (the square of its selection probability) versus p_j for ENS, effectively applying an AND-mask that retains only invariant predictive mechanisms across runs and suppresses run-specific memorization of corrupted labels. This yields three concrete benefits over ENS: zero additional inference cost, reduced variance under distribution shifts, and improved robustness to label noise—validated empirically on TL;DR summarization where a WARM-guided RL policy achieves a 79.4% win rate over a single-RM policy.

## Results

On the TL;DR summarization benchmark, a policy RL fine-tuned with WARM (M=6) achieves a 79.4% win rate against a policy trained with the best individual RM (φ₁), and a 99.8% win rate against the SFT baseline after 3500 training steps. In best-of-N selection, WARM M=6 reaches up to 92.5% win rate against random selection on T5 generations. WARM also significantly delays reward hacking: the pointwise control reward remains higher for longer during RL training compared to both individual RMs and prediction ensembling (ENS), with the Pareto front of (KL, control reward) pushed to the top-left as M increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is prediction ensembling (ENS, Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), which averages the scalar rewards from M RMs. WARM consistently outperforms ENS on OOD test accuracy (Figure 3d) and in RL control reward (Figure 4), even at M=2 where WARM already surpasses ENS M=2. Under 25% label corruption, WARM is intentionally worse than ENS on the corrupted training subset (reducing memorization) but substantially better on OOD test data, confirming that weight averaging enforces invariance across runs. In BoN experiments (Figures 13–14), WARM M=6 outperforms both ENS M=2 and the two best individual RMs (φ₁, φ₂) in both control reward and oracle preference win rate. WARM also matches or exceeds ENS in efficiency: it requires only a single model at inference time, eliminating the linear memory and compute overhead that scales with M in ENS.

## Relation to Prior Work

WARM transplants the weight-averaging (model soups) paradigm from image classification into reward modeling for RLHF, leveraging the linear mode connectivity property that holds when fine-tunings share a common pre-training. It positions itself as an efficient alternative to prediction ensembling (ENS), the standard approach for combining multiple RMs to mitigate reward hacking, arguing that weight-space interpolation achieves comparable or better reliability at a fraction of the inference cost. The theoretical contribution in Section 4.3 extends the analysis from Lin et al. (2024) by showing that WA enforces invariance across runs through a p_j² weighting (versus p_j for ENS), providing a mechanistic explanation for WA's superior robustness under label corruption that was previously unexplained.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WARM offers a drop-in, computationally cheap upgrade for any RLHF pipeline that already trains multiple RMs (e.g., via hyperparameter sweeps), potentially reducing reward hacking without additional inference overhead. The invariance-enforcement interpretation connects weight averaging to the broader OOD generalization and causal invariance literature, suggesting a principled reason to prefer WA over ENS beyond variance reduction. However, all experiments use PaLM-XXS models on the TL;DR summarization task, leaving open whether the benefits persist at the scale of frontier LLMs or on more complex tasks; the paper also acknowledges that WARM does not address all RLHF challenges (e.g., it cannot fix systematic biases shared across all RMs, such as length preference).
