## Problem

Higher-order input cumulants (HOCs) are critical for neural network performance, yet extracting a single direction from an order-p cumulant via online SGD requires n ≳ d^(p-1) samples, which is prohibitive for high-dimensional inputs. This raises a fundamental question: how do neural networks learn from HOCs efficiently in practice, given the apparent computational hardness?

## Input / Output

The theoretical analysis operates on d-dimensional input vectors drawn from a binary classification task (the Mixed Cumulant Model) where the two classes differ in mean, covariance, and higher-order cumulants along fixed spike directions m, u, v. The spherical perceptron produces a binary prediction via σ(w·x), and the theoretical output is nearly sharp sample-complexity thresholds for weak recovery (overlap ~ O(1)) of each spike direction. Empirically, two-layer ReLU networks (512 hidden units, d=128) are trained on the MCM and on CIFAR-10 (32×32 RGB images), with test error evaluated on progressively censored subsets to track which cumulant order has been learned.

## Method

The paper introduces the Mixed Cumulant Model (MCM), a binary discrimination task where class-discriminating signals are carried by different input cumulants (mean, covariance, HOCs) along orthogonal spike directions, and shows that positive correlation between the latent variables generating the covariance and cumulant spikes (E[λν] > 0) dramatically accelerates learning from HOCs. The core mechanism is analyzed via a spherical perceptron trained with online projected SGD: expanding the population loss in Hermite polynomials reveals that correlated latents introduce a mixed term c₁₁αᵤαᵥ that lowers the information exponent along the non-Gaussian direction from 4 to 1, reducing the sample complexity for weak recovery of the cumulant spike from n ≳ d³ to n ≳ d log²d. The authors prove nearly sharp positive and negative thresholds (Propositions 1–4) by adapting the martingale-based SGD analysis of Ben Arous et al. (2021), showing that even a small fixed correlation E[λν] > 0 suffices to achieve the speed-up without altering the global minimum of the loss landscape. Empirical validation with two-layer ReLU networks confirms the theoretical predictions, showing that correlated latents eliminate the long plateaus between learning Gaussian and non-Gaussian features, producing a smooth exponential decay of test loss analogous to dynamics observed on CIFAR-10.

## Results

The paper establishes that a spherical perceptron trained with online SGD requires n ≫ d³ log²d samples to weakly recover a cumulant spike in isolation (Proposition 2), but only n ≫ d log²d samples to recover a covariance spike (Proposition 1). The central result (Proposition 4) shows that when two spikes (covariance and cumulant) are present with positively correlated latent variables (E[λν] > 0), both spikes are weakly recovered in n ≫ d log²d samples with optimal learning rate δ ≈ 1/log d, reducing the cumulant recovery complexity from d³ to d log²d. Even with sub-optimal learning rates δ ≈ 1/d, correlated latents achieve weak recovery in n ≫ d² log d steps, still faster than the d³ threshold for independent latents. Empirically, two-layer ReLU networks on the Mixed Cumulant Model show that correlated latents eliminate the long plateau between Gaussian and non-Gaussian learning, producing a single exponential decay of test loss resembling CIFAR-10 dynamics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are between model settings rather than competing methods. The key comparison is between independent and correlated latent variables in the two-spike Mixed Cumulant Model: with independent latents, the cumulant spike v cannot be weakly recovered below n ≪ d³ samples for any learning rate δ = o(1/d) (Proposition 3), while with positively correlated latents (E[λν] > 0), weak recovery of v is guaranteed at n ≫ d log²d (Proposition 4) — a speedup from cubic to near-linear in d. The paper also contrasts the MCM with a teacher-student setup using Hermite polynomial targets (Eq. 13), showing that the same sequential learning staircase appears, and that introducing mixed terms (Eq. 14) or input covariance correlations (Fig. 2D) provides analogous speedups. On CIFAR-10, the network trained on the full data transitions smoothly from Gaussian to non-Gaussian features without the long plateau observed on the uncorrelated MCM, confirming that real data exhibits the correlated-latent structure the theory predicts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the spherical perceptron analysis framework of Ben Arous et al. (2021), extending their information-exponent machinery to a multi-spike setting with correlated latent variables. It positions itself at the intersection of tensor PCA (Richard & Montanari, 2014), the distributional simplicity bias literature (Refinetti et al., 2023; Ingrosso & Goldt, 2022), and the staircase phenomenon in neural networks (Abbé et al., 2021, 2023; Dandi et al., 2023; Bietti et al., 2023). The key departure from prior work is identifying latent-variable correlation—rather than geometric overlap between spike directions or mixed terms in the target function—as the mechanism that lowers the information exponent along the non-Gaussian direction, a distinction the paper makes explicit by showing that non-orthogonal spikes alone do not accelerate learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves an apparent contradiction in the literature: higher-order cumulants are empirically critical for neural network performance on real data (CIFAR10), yet tensor PCA theory predicts sample complexity exponential in the cumulant order, making efficient extraction seem impossible. The identified mechanism—correlated latent variables lowering the information exponent from 4 to 2 along the cumulant direction—provides a concrete, testable explanation for why neural networks learn HOCs faster than worst-case bounds suggest. Key limitations include the rigorous analysis being restricted to a single spherical perceptron (the multi-layer results are empirical), the breakdown of the Gaussian equivalence principle when latents are correlated (making mean-field analysis of deeper networks non-trivial), and the open question of how such latent correlations arise naturally in real data distributions.
