## Problem

The linear ordering problem is NP-hard and APX-hard, making it intractable to solve exactly for large instances. This paper asks whether one can efficiently compute a *partial* solution — deciding for some pairs (a,b) whether a < b must hold in every optimal solution — without solving the full problem. No prior work had established such partial optimality guarantees for the linear ordering problem in its binary linear program formulation with transitivity constraints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241892/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite set A of n elements and a pairwise cost vector c ∈ ℝ^{P_A} (one real cost per ordered pair a≠b), typically learned from independent pairwise classifications. It produces a set of partially optimal variable assignments (x_ab = 0 or 1) that are guaranteed to appear in at least one optimal solution, along with a decomposition of the original problem into independent sub-problems when grouping or shifting conditions are met. The output is not a full ordering but a partial one: some pairs are fixed, others remain undetermined, and the remaining sub-problems can be solved independently without compromising global optimality.

## Method

The core technique is the construction of *improving maps* — transformations of feasible solutions that never increase the objective — combined with efficiently testable sufficient conditions on the cost function under which each map is improving. The paper defines four families of maps: transposition (swapping two elements), grouping (moving a subset R to the front of the order), conditional grouping (grouping only when a specific pair is mis-ordered), and shifting (moving all elements before a given element to the end). For each, a closed-form inequality on the cost differences c_ab^Δ = c_ab − c_ba certifies that the map is improving, which by the persistency principle (Corollary 3.3) implies the corresponding variable is partially optimal. Testing is efficient: transposition and shifting run in O(|A|³) and O(|A|²) respectively, grouping uses a seeded component-growing algorithm in O(|A|³), and conditional grouping reduces to O(|A|²) min-ab-cut (max-flow) problems solved via push-relabel. Conditions that decompose the problem (grouping, shifting) are applied first to split into independent sub-problems, after which transposition and conditional grouping conditions are applied iteratively to the sub-problems until no further variables can be fixed.

## Results

On the LOLIB benchmark, the partial optimality conditions fix between 10% and 90% of variables for IO instances, 0% to 70% for Spec instances, and approximately 3% for SGB instances. The speed-up in solving the full linear ordering problem to optimality via branch-and-cut (Gurobi) reaches a maximum of 13.18× for IO instances and 33.74× for Spec instances (i.e., reducing solution time to 1/13 and 1/33 respectively). On synthetic instances, nearly all variables are fixed at α=0.4 (easy regime) while nearly none are fixed at α=1.0 (hard regime), and runtime remains approximately polynomial in n, not exceeding 4 minutes for n=200.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it evaluates the effectiveness of its own partial optimality conditions by measuring (i) the fraction of binary variables fixed to 0 or 1 and (ii) the resulting speed-up when solving the remaining problem to optimality with a branch-and-cut solver (Gurobi). Among the individual conditions, Proposition 4.8 (Grouping) and Proposition 4.11 (Conditional Grouping) are the most effective at fixing variables, while Proposition 4.4 (Transposition) is less effective but faster to test. Proposition 4.11 fixes at least as many variables as Proposition 4.8 for all instance sizes but requires solving O(|A|²) max-flow problems, making it roughly three orders of magnitude slower than Proposition 4.8. The shifting conditions (Propositions 4.15, 4.19) and their corollaries contribute only marginally to the fraction of fixed variables in practice. The combined iterative algorithm (Section 6) outperforms any single condition, achieving the headline speed-ups of up to 13× (IO) and 34× (Spec) over solving the unconstrained problem directly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the improving-maps/persistency framework introduced by Shekhovtsov (2013) and developed in subsequent CVPR work (2014, 2015) to the linear ordering problem, a well-studied NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem. The approach is directly analogous to prior partial optimality work on correlation clustering (Lange et al., 2018; Stein et al., 2023), but the authors emphasize that the asymmetric nature of strict linear orders (versus symmetric equivalence relations in clustering) necessitates entirely new improving maps—transposition, grouping, conditional grouping, and shifting—rather than adapting existing ones. The paper also positions itself against the standard exact (branch-and-cut) and heuristic (local search, GNN-based) algorithms for the linear ordering problem, offering a complementary partial-solution approach that can be combined with exact solvers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is demonstrated by speed-ups of up to 1/13 (IO) and 1/33 (Spec) in branch-and-cut solving time, and by fixing 10–90% of variables on benchmark instances without compromising optimality—valuable for practitioners who need certified optimal solutions on moderately sized instances. The decomposition property of the grouping condition (Proposition 4.8) is particularly useful, as it splits the problem into independent sub-problems. Open questions include exploiting sparsity in cost matrices, extending to the partial ordering problem (where not all pairs need to be ordered), and whether the conditions can be tightened or combined more effectively to close the gap between the fraction of fixable variables and the full solution.
