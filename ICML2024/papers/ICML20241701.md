## Problem

When comparing two samples (e.g., before/after, male/female), quantifying how much of an outcome's distributional change is attributable to each causal mechanism requires estimating counterfactual distributions that are not directly observable. Existing causal attribution frameworks (e.g., Shapley values) lack multiply-robust estimators, formal asymptotic guarantees, and valid large-sample inference, and most rely solely on regression, which degrades under misspecification in high-dimensional or non-parametric settings.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two i.i.d. samples of (X, Y) pairs labeled by T∈{0,1}, a causal ordering of the K explanatory variables (not necessarily the full DAG), and a user-specified functional h: ℝ→ℝ (e.g., identity for means, indicator for quantiles). Beyond point estimates of θ^c for each of the 2^(K+1) change vectors, it outputs Shapley or path attribution scores per causal mechanism with consistently estimable standard errors, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values via a multiplier bootstrap that avoids re-training nuisance estimators.

## Method

The core contribution is a multiply-robust estimating equation (MR) that identifies each counterfactual parameter θ^c as a sum of a regression term plus K debiasing terms, each involving a Radon-Nikodym derivative weight and a residual between sequentially nested regression functions. For each causal mechanism k, the equation recovers θ^c if either the k-th regression γ_k or the k-th weight α_k is correctly specified, but not necessarily both. The regressions are estimated recursively (regressing fitted values on fewer variables), and weights are estimated via classification (Bayes' rule) or automatic Riesz regression. Under a product rate condition (RMSE_regression × RMSE_weights = o(n^{-1/2})), the estimator is consistent and asymptotically normal with a heteroskedasticity-robust variance, enabling valid inference. The estimator plugs directly into Shapley value or causal-path attribution formulas, which inherit the asymptotic normality and can be bootstrapped for standard errors.

## Results

In the primary Monte Carlo simulation (Design 1, K=2, n=1000 per sample), the multiply-robust (MR) estimator achieves MAE of 0.057–0.072 for counterfactual mean parameters and 0.037–0.072 for Shapley values, matching the best of regression-only or re-weighting-only when both are correctly specified. In the high-dimensional Design 2 (line DAG, K up to 100, n=2000), MR achieves an average worst-case absolute error of 0.030–0.044 for PATH attribution, substantially outperforming regression (0.050–0.053) and re-weighting (0.041–0.055) even when both are correctly specified. In the gender wage gap application (CPS 2015, n≈32,500), the unconditional gap is $7.91/hour (24%), with education contributing positively and occupation (conditional on education) contributing negatively to the gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three estimation strategies: regression-only, re-weighting-only, and the proposed multiply-robust (MR) estimator. When both the regression and weight models are correctly specified (Table 1a), MR matches the best-performing method (regression) with no loss. The key advantage emerges under misspecification: when the regression is misspecified (Table 1c), MR achieves MAE of 0.057–0.073, matching re-weighting (the correctly-specified method) and far outperforming the misspecified regression (0.066–0.130); symmetrically, when weights are misspecified (Table 1b), MR matches regression (0.057–0.072) while re-weighting degrades to 0.059–0.101. In the high-dimensional Design 2 (Table 2), MR dominates both baselines across all K values (10–100) even under correct specification, with roughly 30–40% lower worst-case error. In non-parametric settings using neural networks or gradient boosting (Tables 1e–1f), MR consistently achieves equal or lower MAE than the best of the two single-method approaches, confirming the theoretical rate advantage from the product condition on RMSE convergence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the double/debiased machine learning framework (Chernozhukov et al., 2018a, 2021, 2023) to the causal change attribution problem, generalizing the doubly-robust estimating equation for a single mediator from Tchetgen-Tchetgen & Shpitser (2012) to arbitrary DAGs with multiple explanatory variables. It builds directly on the Shapley-value-based causal attribution framework of Budhathoki et al. (2021) and the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition, providing the first multiply-robust estimator and the first formal asymptotic analysis (consistency, asymptotic normality) for ML-based estimators in this setting. The paper also explicitly connects causal change attribution to the causal mediation literature, a link it claims to be the first to make, and leverages automatic RN-derivative estimation (Chernozhukov et al., 2021) and the multiplier bootstrap (Belloni et al., 2017) for practical implementation and inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is enabling valid statistical inference (standard errors, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests) for causal attribution measures, which were previously estimated without theoretical guarantees—particularly important in high-dimensional or non-parametric settings where regression-only or re-weighting-only methods converge too slowly for normal-based inference. The multiply-robust property means practitioners need only one of the K regression functions or K weight functions to be correctly specified per mechanism, substantially reducing the risk of model misspecification in applied work. Limitations include the requirement of a known causal DAG, the no-unobserved-confounding assumption, and the overlap condition; the authors suggest extending to conditional unconfoundedness and adapting sensitivity bounds for unobserved confounding as future directions.
