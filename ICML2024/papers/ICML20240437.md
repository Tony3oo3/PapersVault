## Problem

Message-passing GNNs suffer from oversquashing, making them unable to effectively propagate information from distant nodes, while graph transformers solve this at the cost of requiring ad-hoc positional encodings and quadratic attention complexity. No existing architecture combines efficient long-range signal propagation, built-in structural inductive bias without positional encoding, and a formal expressiveness guarantee beyond 1-WL.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240437/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a graph with node feature vectors and a pre-computed shortest-distance mask (obtained via Floyd-Warshall during preprocessing) that partitions all nodes into hop sets relative to each target node. For each target node, the hop representations are fed into the RNN in reverse order (farthest hop first, target node last), and the final hidden state is projected to produce the updated node representation; graph-level outputs are obtained by average pooling over all node representations.

## Method

Each GRED layer consists of two stages: (1) a Deep Sets-style multiset aggregation (MLP∘sum∘MLP) that produces one vector per hop distance, and (2) a diagonal linear RNN (an LRU with complex eigenvalues parameterized in log-polar form) that sequentially encodes the hop-representation sequence from the farthest hop to the target node. The diagonal transition matrix Λ acts as a learned distance-dependent filter whose eigenvalue magnitudes control how quickly distant-node signals decay, and its diagonal structure enables O(K|V|) parallel-scan computation instead of sequential recurrence. The paper proves that a sufficiently wide diagonal linear RNN expresses an injective (even bijective) mapping over sequences, which combined with the injective multiset aggregation yields a GRED layer strictly more expressive than 1-WL when K>1, while the number of parameters remains independent of K.

## Results

On the four benchmarking GNNs datasets, GRED achieves 98.38% on MNIST and 76.85% on CIFAR-10, setting new state-of-the-art among all compared methods (beating GRIT by 0.27% and 0.39% respectively). On PATTERN and CLUSTER, GRED scores 86.76% and 78.50%, slightly trailing GRIT (87.20% and 80.03%) but still outperforming all MPNN baselines by 3–5 points. On ZINC 12K, GRED achieves a test MAE of 0.077, competitive with GPS (0.070) and Graph MLP-Mixer (0.073) but behind GRIT (0.059). Training efficiency is a major advantage: GRED is 6.5× faster per epoch on ZINC and 8.8× faster on CIFAR-10 compared to GRIT, with lower GPU memory usage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRED outperforms all MPNN baselines (GCN, GAT, GIN, GatedGCN) by 1–12 points across all four benchmarking datasets, with the largest gaps on CIFAR-10 (76.85% vs. 67.31% for GatedGCN). Against graph transformers, GRED beats GRIT on MNIST (98.38% vs. 98.11%) and CIFAR-10 (76.85% vs. 76.47%), and surpasses Graph MLP-Mixer on both (98.38% vs. 98.32%; 76.85% vs. 73.96%), but trails GRIT on PATTERN (86.76% vs. 87.20%) and CLUSTER (78.50% vs. 80.03%). On ZINC 12K, GRED (MAE 0.077) is competitive with GPS (0.070) and Graph MLP-Mixer (0.073) but falls short of GRIT (0.059) and PathNN (0.090). On the long-range Peptides-func task, GRED (AP 0.7085) beats GRIT (0.6988) and Graph MLP-Mixer (0.6970) without positional encoding, though DRew+LapPE (0.7150) remains slightly ahead. A key practical advantage is training speed: GRED is 6.5× faster per epoch on ZINC and 8.8× faster on CIFAR-10 than GRIT, with 1.4–3.2× lower GPU memory, making it substantially more efficient at comparable accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRED sits at the intersection of three research threads: multi-hop MPNNs (MixHop, SPN, PathNN, DRew) that extend receptive fields beyond one hop, graph transformers (Graphormer, GPS, GRIT) that use global attention but require ad-hoc positional encoding, and state-space models / linear RNNs (S4, LRU) that provide efficient long-range sequential modeling. The key conceptual departure is replacing the attention mechanism with a diagonal-parameterized linear RNN (specifically an LRU) that encodes the ordered sequence of hop representations, where the recurrence order naturally encodes distance and eliminates the need for positional encoding. The theoretical contribution — proving injectivity of diagonal linear RNNs over sequences via Vandermonde structure — is novel and provides an expressiveness guarantee beyond 1-WL that prior multi-hop MPNN analyses (Feng et al., 2022) achieved only with less parameter-efficient architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GRED offers a principled, parameter-efficient alternative to graph transformers that matches or exceeds their accuracy on most benchmarks while being 6.5–8.8× faster in training, and it does so without any positional encoding — a component that has been a persistent source of hyperparameter sensitivity in the graph transformer literature. The learned eigenvalue visualization (pushing magnitudes toward 1 for long-range tasks) provides interpretable evidence that the model adapts its signal decay to the task. Limitations include the 3-WL expressiveness ceiling shared with all multi-hop MPNNs, the sensitivity of performance to the K hyperparameter (optimal K is task-dependent and strictly below the diameter on some datasets), and the fact that on Peptides-func, DRew+LapPE still outperforms GRED without positional encoding. Open questions include whether more advanced positional encodings can be combined with GRED, and whether the diagonal RNN formulation extends naturally to directed or weighted graphs.
