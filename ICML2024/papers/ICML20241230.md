## Problem

Optimizing performative risk requires knowing the distribution map D(θ), which is unknown in practice. Model-agnostic methods (bandits, derivative-free optimization) avoid structural assumptions but converge at exponentially slow rates, while model-based methods achieve fast rates only under the strong assumption that the feedback model is correctly specified. This paper asks whether even a misspecified parametric model of the distribution map can yield substantially better finite-sample performance than model-agnostic strategies.

## Input / Output

The learner deploys n predictor parameters θ_i drawn from a chosen exploration distribution (e.g., uniform on Θ) and observes one data point z_i ~ D(θ_i) per deployment. From these n pairs, the learner fits a parameter β̂ of a parametric distribution atlas D_B = {D_β : β ∈ B} via empirical risk minimization. The final output θ̂_PO is computed by minimizing the plug-in performative risk PR^{β̂}(θ) = E_{z~D_{β̂}(θ)}[ℓ(z;θ)] over θ ∈ Θ, which requires no additional real data—only synthetic sampling from the known fitted model—so the statistical cost is entirely in the n deployment rounds.

## Method

The plug-in performative optimization protocol has three steps: (1) deploy θ_i ~ D̃ and collect z_i ~ D(θ_i); (2) fit β̂ by minimizing an empirical loss r(θ_i, z_i; β) over the atlas parameter space B (e.g., MLE or least-squares); (3) compute θ̂_PO = argmin_θ PR^{β̂}(θ). The key theoretical result decomposes the excess risk PR(θ̂_PO) − PR(θ_PO) into an irreducible misspecification error (bounded by the TV or Wasserstein distance between D_{β*}(θ) and D(θ), where β* is the population limit of β̂) and a statistical error that vanishes at Õ(√(log n · (d_β + log(1/δ))/n)) under subexponential regularity of the ERM problem. The analysis requires the atlas to be ε-smooth in the chosen distance, a property entirely in the learner's control. Applications to strategic regression, binary strategic classification, and location families instantiate the atlas with best-response or linear-shift models and verify the smoothness and estimation-rate conditions.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 3.9) bounds the excess risk of the plug-in performative optimum as PR(θ̂_PO) − PR(θ_PO) ≤ 4B_ℓ·η_TV + C′B_ℓ·ε_TV·√(log n)·√((d_β + log(1/δ))/n) under TV misspecification, or 2L_z·η_W + C′L_z·ε_W·√(log n)·√((d_β + log(1/δ))/n) under Wasserstein misspecification. The statistical error vanishes at Õ(1/√n), in contrast to the Õ(n^{−1/(d_θ+1)}) rate of model-agnostic bandit methods. In experiments (location family, strategic regression, and a real credit dataset), plug-in PO converges rapidly to an excess risk floor determined solely by the misspecification parameter (zero when the model is correctly specified, nonzero otherwise), while all baselines either converge slowly or to suboptimal points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DFO (Flaxman et al., 2004), plug-in PO wins decisively on convergence speed: DFO's excess risk decreases at the slow Õ(n^{−1/(d_θ+1)}) rate, while plug-in PO's statistical component vanishes at Õ(1/√n); in all four experimental settings (location family, strategic regression with ρ=2, 2.5, 3, and the credit dataset), DFO's excess risk remains substantially higher than plug-in PO's at every sample size shown. Against greedy SGD (Mendler-Dunner et al., 2020), plug-in PO also wins: SGD ignores the feedback loop and converges to a performatively stable point rather than the performative optimum, quickly plateauing at a nonzero excess risk in every experiment, whereas plug-in PO continues to decrease (to zero in the well-specified case). Against PerfGD (Izzo et al., 2021), plug-in PO wins in the location-family experiment because PerfGD's numerical gradient approximation ∂f/∂θ becomes unstable for d > 1, leading to suboptimal performance; however, PerfGD is not applicable to the strategic regression setting since the distribution map does not admit a density. On the real credit dataset (30,000 samples, 17 features), plug-in PO again outperforms both DFO and SGD in excess risk and accuracy, though the accuracy gap versus SGD is smaller than in the synthetic experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the performative prediction framework of Perdomo et al. (2020), which introduced the distribution map and performative risk. It positions itself at the intersection of two existing families of methods: model-agnostic approaches (bandits, derivative-free optimization) that avoid structural assumptions but converge slowly, and model-based approaches (best-response models, parametric distribution shifts) that achieve fast rates only under correct specification. The key departure is a formal study of the misspecified regime, drawing on the classical M-estimation and 'models as approximations' literature (White, 1980; Buja et al., 2019) to show that parametric feedback models retain their statistical advantage even when the distribution atlas does not contain the true map.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear quantitative criterion—bounded misspecification in TV or Wasserstein distance—under which plug-in performative optimization dominates model-agnostic baselines, giving practitioners a principled way to justify using simplified feedback models. Open questions include whether more expressive atlases (e.g., neural networks) can reduce the irreducible misspecification error when prior knowledge is limited, how the theory extends to time-varying or multi-agent settings, and what fairness consequences arise from deploying highly misspecified models. The authors also note that the approach requires some prior knowledge to select the atlas, limiting its applicability in truly unknown environments.
