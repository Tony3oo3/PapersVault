## Problem

Standard RLHF aligns language models using a single reward model learned from aggregated human preference data, implicitly assuming a unified ground-truth preference. This paper proves that such single-reward alignment is fundamentally impossible when human preferences are diverse across sub-populations: the alignment gap for any minority group is lower-bounded by the total variation distance between its preference distribution and those of other groups. The work asks what alternative alignment strategy can honor diverse preferences without systematically subordinating minority groups.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairwise preference data (prompt x, preferred response y1, dispreferred response y2) collected from multiple user groups, along with a pre-trained language model (GPT-2 or Tulu2-7B) and a text prompt at inference time. The EM-based reward learning step clusters annotators into |U| sub-populations and learns one Bradley-Terry reward model per cluster. The output is a single aligned policy π* that maximizes the minimum expected reward across all sub-populations, producing generated text responses that satisfy the preferences of both majority and minority groups simultaneously.

## Method

MaxMin-RLHF combines two components: (1) an EM algorithm (Algorithm 2) that learns a mixture of |U| reward models by iteratively assigning each annotator to the cluster whose reward best explains their preferences (E-step) and re-fitting each cluster's reward via negative log-likelihood (M-step); and (2) a max-min policy optimization (Algorithm 1) inspired by the Egalitarian principle in social choice theory, which at each PPO iteration identifies the sub-population with the lowest current utility and updates the policy to maximize that group's KL-regularized reward. The theoretical contribution establishes that the alignment gap under single-reward RLHF scales as λ_ψ·L_π/(16β²D²) times the weighted sum of pairwise TV distances between sub-population preference distributions, providing a formal impossibility result. Experiments on GPT-2 (sentiment vs. conciseness) and Tulu2-7B (three preference pairs with GPT-4-simulated annotators) show MaxMin-RLHF maintains high win rates across all groups while single-reward baselines degrade sharply for minority groups as the majority-to-minority ratio increases.

## Results

On the P3 dataset (Tulu2-7B, GPT-4 pairwise evaluation), MaxMin-RLHF achieves a 56.67% average win rate over the base model, outperforming the best single-reward RLHF baseline (6:1 ratio) at 52.37% and the balanced 1:1 setting at 54.24%. On P1, MaxMin-RLHF reaches 57.50% (P1A) and 60.00% (P1B), while single-reward RLHF at 10:1 drops to 55.11% and 45.08% respectively. On P2, MaxMin-RLHF achieves 54.50% and 56.00% versus 53.96% and 45.98% for the 10:1 single-reward baseline. The single-reward reward model itself shows declining minority-group accuracy from 70.4% (1:1) to 42.0% (10:1), confirming the theoretical impossibility result.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MaxMin-RLHF against the standard single-reward RLHF pipeline (Ouyang et al., 2022) at four user-group imbalance ratios (1:1, 2:1, 6:1, 10:1) across three preference datasets (P1, P2, P3) using Tulu2-7B and GPT-4 pairwise evaluation. MaxMin-RLHF consistently outperforms all single-reward baselines on the minority group: on P3B it achieves 55.56% versus 45.00–52.62% for single-reward methods, and on P1B it reaches 60.00% versus 45.08–51.97%. The advantage grows with imbalance: at 10:1, the single-reward model's minority win rate drops by 10–15 percentage points below MaxMin-RLHF, while the majority-group performance remains comparable (within ~1–2 points). On P2, MaxMin-RLHF also wins on both groups (54.50%/56.00% vs. 53.96%/45.98% at 10:1). The EM-based reward learning (Algorithm 2) successfully clusters all users correctly by the 4th iteration, validating that the mixture-of-rewards approach recovers the true sub-population structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard single-reward RLHF pipeline (Ziegler et al., 2019; Ouyang et al., 2022a) by formally proving that it cannot align with diverse human sub-population preferences, then proposing a max-min social welfare objective inspired by the Egalitarian principle in social choice theory (Sen, 2017). It departs from prior multi-reward approaches (Bakker et al., 2022; Jang et al., 2023; Ramé et al., 2023) that aggregate multiple reward functions in ad hoc or consensus-based ways, instead grounding the aggregation in a principled fairness criterion. The proof technique leverages the analytical reward-to-policy mapping from DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and the Bradley-Terry preference model, while the EM-based reward learning draws on classical mixture modeling applied to the preference likelihood.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility theorem provides the first formal lower bound on alignment gap as a function of inter-group preference diversity, giving practitioners a concrete diagnostic for when single-reward RLHF will fail. However, several limitations temper the practical impact: the large-scale experiments simulate human annotators with GPT-4 rather than using real diverse populations, the EM algorithm requires pre-specifying the number of sub-populations, and the max-min objective may be overly conservative in practice. Open questions include how to automatically discover the right number and structure of preference clusters, whether the egalitarian criterion is the appropriate social welfare function (vs. Nash, Rawlsian, or other alternatives), and how the approach scales to dozens or hundreds of distinct preference groups in production settings.
