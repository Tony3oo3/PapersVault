## Problem

Estimating continuous treatment effects (dose-response functions) is complicated when the primary outcome is partially missing due to loss to follow-up or costly measurement, and restricting analysis to labeled data alone introduces selection bias and inflates variance. While prior work addressed surrogates for binary treatments (Kallus & Mao, 2020) or continuous treatments with fully observed outcomes (Kennedy et al., 2017), no method existed that combined both: continuous treatments with partially observed outcomes and surrogate information. This paper fills that gap by proposing a doubly robust semi-supervised estimator with formal asymptotic guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes two datasets: a labeled set L with covariates V, surrogates S, continuous treatment A, and primary outcome Y, plus a larger unlabeled set U containing V, S, and A but missing Y. The output is a pointwise estimate θ̂(a) of the dose-response function at any treatment level a, obtained via local linear regression of pseudo-outcomes on A, along with an asymptotic variance estimator enabling confidence intervals. The pseudo-outcome construction requires nuisance estimates of the outcome regression μ, the surrogate-averaged regression τ, the missingness propensity ρ, and the stabilized weight w(a,v), all of which can be fitted with flexible machine learning methods.

## Method

The core contribution is a doubly robust pseudo-outcome φ(Z; μ̄, π̄, ρ̄) derived from the influence function of the pathwise-differentiable functional ψ = E[θ(A)], which incorporates the missingness indicator R, the missingness propensity ρ(A,X), and the stabilized weight w(a,v) = f(a)/π(a|V) to combine labeled and unlabeled data. The estimator regresses these pseudo-outcomes on the continuous treatment A using local linear regression with cross-fitting, and enjoys double robustness: consistency holds if either the outcome model μ or the pair (π, ρ) is correctly specified. The conditional bias is second-order (a product of nuisance estimation errors), enabling valid inference even when nuisance functions are estimated at slow nonparametric rates. The paper establishes asymptotic normality of the local linear estimator with an explicit variance formula σ²(a) that decomposes into a labeled-data variance term and a surrogate-explained variance term, and shows that incorporating unlabeled data with surrogates reduces the asymptotic variance by a factor of ρ on the surrogate-related component compared to Kennedy et al. (2017).

## Results

The paper establishes that the semi-supervised doubly robust estimator's asymptotic variance reduces the surrogate-driven term Var(μ(a,X)|A=a,V) by a factor of ρ (the outcome observation probability) compared to the supervised estimator of Kennedy et al. (2017), e.g., a 2× variance reduction when ρ=0.5. In simulations with manually controlled nuisance error rate n^{-α}, the doubly robust estimator achieves lower RMSE than the plug-in estimator when α is small (large nuisance error), while the plug-in estimator wins when α is large (accurate nuisance estimation). On the Job Corps real data, the doubly robust estimator successfully captures the highly nonlinear dose-response curve (decreasing arrests with training hours), whereas the plug-in estimator fails to capture this nonlinearity due to first-order bias.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the supervised doubly robust estimator from Kennedy et al. (2017), which uses only labeled data without surrogates or unlabeled data. In the MCAR setting with ρ=0.5, the paper's semi-supervised method achieves a strictly smaller asymptotic variance: the first variance term (involving Var(Y|A=a,X)/ρ) is identical since nρ=n₁, but the second term (Var(μ(a,X)|A=a,V)) is reduced by a factor of ρ, yielding a 2× improvement in that component. In the Appendix C.4 simulation (Figure 6), the semi-supervised method consistently shows lower RMSE across all sample sizes (n from 10^{2.6} to 10^{4.6}) compared to the supervised Kennedy et al. (2017) method, with all nuisance functions correctly specified. Additionally, the paper compares against a naive plug-in estimator (averaging τ̂(a,V) over all samples): the doubly robust estimator wins when nuisance estimation error is large (small α in simulations, or misspecified parametric models in Appendix C.1), but the plug-in estimator can win when nuisance functions are estimated very accurately (large α or correctly specified parametric models), because the DR estimator incurs additional error from pseudo-outcome construction, bandwidth selection, and local linear smoothing at rate O(n^{-2/5}).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: doubly robust estimation of continuous treatment effects (Kennedy et al., 2017) and the use of surrogate outcomes to improve efficiency when primary outcomes are partially observed (Kallus & Mao, 2020). The authors explicitly position their contribution as a 'counterpart for Kallus & Mao (2020) in continuous treatment effects settings,' extending surrogate-based efficiency gains from binary to continuous treatments. The estimation framework draws on the stability framework of Kennedy (2023) for analyzing linear-smoother-based estimators and the influence-function machinery of Chernozhukov et al. (2018) for constructing doubly robust pseudo-outcomes, while the semi-supervised data structure (labeled + unlabeled with surrogates) follows the setup of Zhang et al. (2023) and Chakrabortty et al. (2022).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical foundation (oracle efficiency, asymptotic normality, explicit variance decomposition) for a practically important setting where expensive primary outcomes are partially missing but cheaper surrogates are fully observed—common in longitudinal health studies and digital advertising. A notable practical caveat emerges from the nonparametric simulations: the curse of dimensionality in estimating the conditional treatment density π can cause the doubly robust estimator to underperform the simpler plug-in estimator, suggesting that domain knowledge about the treatment mechanism is crucial for the DR approach to deliver its theoretical advantages. Open questions include extending to settings where the treatment itself is missing in the unlabeled data (the generalizability problem) and to multiple heterogeneous outcomes.
