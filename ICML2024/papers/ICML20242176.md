## Problem

While alignment techniques like RLHF and prompting reduce undesired behaviors in LLMs, no formal framework existed to characterize whether such alignment is fundamentally robust or inherently vulnerable to adversarial prompting. The paper asks: if an alignment process attenuates but does not completely eliminate a negative behavior, can an adversarial user always find a prompt that reactivates it, and if so, how long must that prompt be?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242176/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes an LLM's probability distribution decomposed into a two-component mixture P = αP₋ + (1−α)P₊, a ground-truth behavior scoring function B: Σ* → [−1,1], and parameters characterizing the mixture (prior α, KL-distinguishability β, negativity γ, and log-likelihood variance σ). It produces closed-form upper bounds on misaligning prompt length (e.g., (1/β)(log(1/α) + log(1/ε) + log 4) sentences for the base case), behavior expectation decay curves, and high-probability guarantees that randomly sampled prompts of sufficient length from P₋ will misalign the model. The analysis operates at sentence granularity, with the unit of prompting being individual sentences rather than tokens or paragraphs.

## Method

The paper introduces the Behavior Expectation Bounds (BEB) framework, which models an LLM as a superposition of a well-behaved component P₊ and an ill-behaved component P₋ with prior weight α, then proves that under α,β,γ-negative-distinguishability (a bounded conditional KL-divergence between components) and σ-similarity (a linear-in-length variance bound on the log-likelihood ratio), any positive α guarantees the existence of a misaligning prompt whose length scales logarithmically in 1/α and inversely in β. The proof constructs the adversarial prompt by sequentially sampling sentences from P₋, showing each sentence reweights the conditional prior of P₋ by a factor of e^β relative to P₋, driving the full model's behavior expectation toward γ. The framework is extended to show that preset aligning prompts add only a linear penalty to the required misaligning length, multi-turn conversations add a term proportional to the model's own response lengths (giving the LLM a 'misaligning resistance'), and best-of-n sampling adds only a log n term. Empirically, the authors validate the framework on LLaMA-2 13B chat by using LoRA-finetuned proxies for P₋ and P₊, measuring β ≈ 20, σ² ≈ 50, and demonstrating that the predicted KL-divergence decay and behavior expectation collapse match observed misalignment dynamics.

## Results

The BEB framework proves that any LLM with a nonzero prior α on an ill-behaved component is γ-prompt-misalignable, with a guaranteed misaligning prompt length of (1/β)(log(1/α) + log(1/ε) + log 4) sentences (Theorem 3.1). Empirically, on LLaMA-2 13B chat, the estimated parameters are β≈20, β'≈30, σ²≈50 (σ/β≈0.35), and log(1/α) in the range 18–30, yielding a misaligning prompt of roughly 3 sentences. Adding a preset aligning prompt increases the required misaligning length linearly in |s₀| (Theorem 3.2), and best-of-n sampling adds only log(n) to the bound (Theorem 3.4). Pretrained (unaligned) models exhibit a β approximately 5× smaller than RLHF-finetuned models, suggesting RLHF reduces α but increases distinguishability β.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing alignment methods in a head-to-head empirical comparison; instead, it provides theoretical impossibility results that apply to any alignment procedure operating on frozen weights (RLHF, prompting, best-of-n). The key empirical comparison is between RLHF-finetuned LLaMA-2 13B chat and its pretrained counterpart: the RLHF model has a β roughly 5× larger, indicating that while RLHF suppresses the probability of negative behavior (lower α), it simultaneously makes the negative component more distinguishable, potentially making it easier to trigger via adversarial prompts. Among the four alignment scenarios analyzed (no prompt, preset aligning prompt, multi-turn conversation, best-of-n sampling), the preset aligning prompt provides the strongest (but still finite) guardrail, requiring the misaligning prompt to scale linearly with the aligning prefix length. The conversation setting is the most resistant to misalignment, as the LLM's own aligned responses add 'misaligning resistance' that the adversarial user must overcome with additional text proportional to the model's response lengths.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the PAC-based theoretical framework for in-context learning from Wies et al. (2023), adapting its proof techniques to a new probabilistic setting for alignment analysis. Conceptually, it formalizes the 'persona superposition' intuition from Andreas (2022) and Nardo (2023) into a rigorous two-component mixture model, and connects to the empirical jailbreaking literature (Wallace et al. 2019, Yu & Sagae 2021, Subhash 2023) by providing the first formal impossibility theorems that explain *why* adversarial prompts succeed. The behavior verticals and scoring functions are drawn from Perez et al. (2022), whose observation that RLHF can paradoxically increase negative behaviors motivates the paper's conjecture about RLHF increasing component distinguishability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result (Theorem 3.1) provides a formal, quantitative foundation for what practitioners have observed empirically: no alignment process that merely attenuates (rather than eliminates) a behavior can be made safe against sufficiently long adversarial prompts. The provocative conjecture that RLHF may increase the distinguishability (β) of negative components while reducing their prior (α) — making misalignment *easier* despite better alignment — could reshape how the field designs alignment procedures, and points toward inference-time control methods like representation engineering as potentially necessary. Key open questions include whether the mixture decomposition assumption holds in actual LLMs, whether the RLHF/distinguishability connection is confirmed at scale, and whether more realistic behavior scoring (beyond single-sentence ground truth) changes the theoretical picture.
