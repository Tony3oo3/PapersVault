## Problem

Naive gradient clipping in proximal methods for composite optimization breaks the fixed-point property of the solution, forcing either sublinear convergence or impractically large clipping levels. Existing high-probability convergence results under heavy-tailed noise (bounded α-th moment, α∈(1,2]) either restrict to unconstrained non-distributed problems or, for composite/distributed settings, suffer from bounded-domain assumptions, extra logarithmic factors, non-optimal rates, or missing strongly convex cases. The core tension is that accelerated methods require decreasing clipping levels to control heavy-tailed noise, while composite fixed-point convergence requires the clipping level to remain large enough.

## Input / Output

The methods consume stochastic gradient estimates satisfying a bounded α-th central moment condition (α∈(1,2], allowing unbounded variance and Lévy-stable noise) along with a composite objective f(x)+Ψ(x) where f is L-smooth and Ψ is a prox-computable convex regularizer. In the distributed variant, n workers each query local stochastic gradients of f_i and communicate only clipped difference vectors to a central server. The output is an iterate x^K (or averaged iterate) accompanied by a high-probability guarantee P{C(x^K)≤ε}≥1−β with polylogarithmic dependence on 1/β; algorithm parameters (stepsize, clipping schedule, shift learning rate) depend on an upper bound R on ‖x⁰−x*‖ and ζ* on the per-worker gradient norm at the solution.

## Method

The key mechanism is clipping the difference between the stochastic gradient and a dynamically updated shift h^k (i.e., clip(∇f_ξ(x^k)−h^k, λ_k)) rather than clipping the raw stochastic gradient. The shifts are updated via an error-feedback rule h^{k+1}=h^k+ν·Δ̂^k, causing them to track ∇f_i(x*) so that the clipped difference converges to zero even with decreasing λ_k, thereby preserving the fixed-point property of the proximal update. In the distributed setting, each worker clips its local gradient difference before sending it to the server, and the server averages the clipped vectors; this worker-side clipping (rather than clip-after-average) is what yields linear speedup in n under α<2, where averaging before clipping would introduce dimension-dependent degradation. The paper instantiates this template in four methods: Prox-clipped-SGD-shift (non-accelerated, quasi-strongly convex and convex), DProx-clipped-SSTM-shift (accelerated via the Similar Triangles Method), and their variational-inequality counterparts DProx-clipped-SGDA-shift and DProx-clipped-SEG-shift, all analyzed via induction-based Lyapunov arguments combined with Bernstein inequalities for martingale differences.

## Results

The paper derives high-probability convergence rates (number of stochastic oracle calls per worker to achieve P{Metric ≤ ε} ≥ 1−β) for composite and distributed problems under heavy-tailed noise (bounded α-th moment, α ∈ (1,2]). For the quasi-strongly convex case, DProx-clipped-SGD-shift achieves Õ(max{L/μ, ζ*/(√n·μR), (1/n)(σ²/(μ²ε))^(α/(2(α−1)))}) oracle calls per worker, and for the convex case, DProx-clipped-SSTM-shift achieves Õ(max{√(LR²/ε), √(Rζ*/(√nε)), (1/n)(σR/ε)^(α/(α−1))}) oracle calls per worker. The key quantitative advantage is the 1/n factor on the noise-dependent term, establishing linear speedup in the distributed setting — the first such result under Assumption 1.1 (heavy-tailed noise). In the single-node unconstrained special case (n=1, Ψ≡0), the rates recover or match the best-known bounds from Sadiev et al. (2023).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Sadiev et al. (2023) (clipped-SGD and clipped-SSTM), the proposed DProx-clipped-SGD-shift and DProx-clipped-SSTM-shift extend the analysis to composite (C?=✓) and distributed (D?=✓) settings, which the baselines cannot handle. In the single-node unconstrained special case (n=1, Ψ≡0), the rates match exactly, confirming tightness. The distributed extension introduces a 1/n factor on the noise-dependent term, yielding linear speedup — a result not available from the baselines. Compared to Nguyen et al. (2023a) (Clipped-SMD and Clipped-ASMD), the proposed methods handle the distributed case (D?=✓) and do not require the restrictive assumption ∇f(x*)=0 that Clipped-ASMD needs for acceleration. Additionally, Nguyen et al. (2023a) uses a more complex parameter R̂² = R(3R + L⁻¹(2ησ + ‖∇f(x⁰)‖)) that depends on an auxiliary robust gradient estimate, while the proposed methods use the simpler R and ζ* parameters. The main trade-off is that Nguyen et al. (2023a) achieves slightly better logarithmic factors (ln(β⁻¹) vs. ln(β⁻¹·(σR/ε)^(α/(α−1)))), but the authors conjecture this gap can be closed with adjusted proof techniques.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of high-probability convergence analysis for stochastic optimization under heavy-tailed noise (Nazin et al., 2019; Gorbunov et al., 2020a; Sadiev et al., 2023) from unconstrained problems to composite and distributed settings. The key conceptual departure is identifying that naive gradient clipping destroys the fixed-point property of proximal methods, and resolving this by clipping gradient *differences* relative to dynamically updated shifts — a technique borrowed from the error-feedback/compression literature (Mishchenko et al., 2019) but adapted to the fundamentally different setting of biased, non-contraction clipping operators. It also generalizes prior composite results (Davis et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 2023a) that either required bounded domains, extra logarithmic factors, or the restrictive assumption ∇f(x*)=0 for acceleration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a significant gap by providing the first accelerated high-probability convergence rates for composite problems under heavy-tailed noise, and the first results showing linear speedup in distributed settings under sub-Gaussian (α<2) moment assumptions — a regime where naive averaging of gradients introduces dimension-dependent degradation. The practical relevance extends to federated learning and differential privacy, where per-worker gradient clipping before aggregation is already standard practice. Open questions include parameter-free variants (current results require an upper bound R on initial distance), tighter logarithmic factors, and whether the shift-based approach can be extended to non-convex or non-monotone settings.
