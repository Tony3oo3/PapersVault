## Problem

Estimating the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) from observational data collected across multiple environments (e.g., different hospitals or countries) is challenging because standard identification assumptions—overlap within environments and unconfoundedness—are frequently violated in such settings. No prior work provides partial identification bounds for the CATE across multiple environments, nor does it offer effective, model-agnostic estimation procedures (meta-learners) for such bounds.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular observational data consisting of continuous patient covariates X ∈ ℝᵖ, a binary treatment indicator A, a bounded continuous outcome Y ∈ [s₁, s₂], and a discrete environment indicator E ∈ {0,…,k}. Rather than producing a point CATE estimate, the output is a pair of functions b⁺(x) and b⁻(x) that form valid bounds on the CATE at each covariate value x. The bounds are computed separately for every environment pair (e, j) and then combined via min (upper) and max (lower) to yield the tightest overall interval.

## Method

The core insight is that the environment variable E acts as an instrumental variable, allowing the authors to generalize Balke–Pearl natural bounds to continuous outcomes and multiple environments (Theorem 4.2). For estimation, they propose model-agnostic two-stage meta-learners: a WB-learner for within-environment bounds that uses a pseudo-outcome requiring no nuisance estimation, and a family of CB-learners (CB-PI, CB-RA, CB-IPW, CB-DR) for cross-environment bounds that recast the problem as standard CATE estimation with a transformed outcome and the environment as a multi-valued treatment. The CB-DR-learner inherits a double-robustness property, remaining consistent if either the transformed response surface or the environment probability is correctly specified. In experiments, all learners are instantiated with simple MLPs (2 hidden layers, 100 neurons) to isolate the effect of the meta-learner design from model choice.

## Results

On Synthetic Data 1 (simple environment probability), the CB-IPW-learner achieves the lowest RMSE of 0.117±0.057, followed by CB-DR (0.132±0.061) and CB-PI (0.125±0.059). On Synthetic Data 2 (more complex environment probability), the CB-DR-learner achieves the best RMSE of 0.111±0.069, with CB-RA close behind at 0.119±0.037. Across both datasets, the two-stage learners consistently outperform their naive plug-in counterparts (e.g., CB-DR at 0.111 vs. CB naïve at 0.156 on Data 2), and the WB-learner (within-environment) achieves the lowest absolute RMSE (0.073–0.075) but provides wider, less informative bounds. On the real-world Brazil COVID-19 dataset, the cross-environment bounds tighten the CATE interval relative to within-environment bounds, and the two-stage learners produce estimates comparable to the naive plug-in learner, confirming robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods from prior work; instead, it benchmarks its own meta-learner variants against naive plug-in baselines. The two-stage learners (CB-PI, CB-RA, CB-IPW, CB-DR) consistently outperform the corresponding naive plug-in learner (CB naïve) on both synthetic datasets, with RMSE reductions of roughly 0.03–0.04 (e.g., CB-DR at 0.111 vs. CB naïve at 0.156 on Data 2). The performance ranking among two-stage variants depends on the data-generating process: CB-IPW is best when the environment probability δ_e(x) is simple (Data 1), while CB-DR is best when δ_e(x) is more complex (Data 2), mirroring findings from standard CATE meta-learner literature. The WB-learner achieves the lowest RMSE overall (0.073–0.075) because within-environment bounds are easier to estimate, but these bounds are wider and less informative than cross-environment bounds. On the real-world Brazil COVID-19 case study, the two-stage learners (WB + CB-PI) produce estimates closely matching the naive plug-in learner, confirming that the two-stage approach does not degrade performance in practice while offering theoretical robustness guarantees (double robustness for CB-DR).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate literatures: partial identification of treatment effects via instrumental variables (Manski 1990; Balke & Pearl 1997; Swanson et al. 2018) and model-agnostic meta-learners for CATE estimation (Kunzel et al. 2019; Curth & van der Schaar 2021; Kennedy 2023). The key conceptual move is showing that a discrete environment variable in multi-site observational data can be interpreted as an IV, thereby generalizing binary-IV natural bounds to continuous outcomes and multiple environments. It departs from the B-learner (Oprescu et al. 2023) and sensitivity-analysis approaches by not requiring prior knowledge that bounds the strength of unobserved confounding, and from prior multi-environment CATE work (Kallus et al. 2018; Shi et al. 2021; Bica & van der Schaar 2022) by explicitly relaxing both overlap and unconfoundedness rather than assuming them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is strong for medical applications where data naturally come from multiple hospitals or countries and where both overlap and unconfoundedness are routinely violated (e.g., unrecorded socioeconomic confounders, treatment policies varying by site). The model-agnostic meta-learners (WB, CB-PI/RA/IPW/DR) give practitioners a flexible toolkit combinable with arbitrary ML backends, and the double-robustness guarantee of the CB-DR-learner mirrors the well-known advantage of the standard DR-learner. Key limitations include the requirement that E be a valid instrument (uncorrelated with unobserved confounders), which is untestable and must be justified by domain knowledge, and the current restriction to binary treatments. Open directions flagged by the authors include extending to continuous instruments, leaky mediation, and sensitivity-analysis settings where effective estimation procedures are still lacking.
