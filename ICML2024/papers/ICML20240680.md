## Problem

Existing bridge matching solvers for Schrödinger Bridges either rely on iterative Markovian/reciprocal projections that accumulate approximation error across iterations (e.g., DSBM), or depend on heuristic minibatch OT approximations of the true EOT plan (e.g., OT-CFM), which can introduce bias. The paper asks whether the SB can be recovered in a single bridge matching step without requiring the true EOT plan as input.

## Input / Output

The method takes empirical samples from two distributions p0 and p1 along with any accessible transport plan π (independent sampling, minibatch discrete OT, or the true EOT plan when available) and a query point x0. It produces a learned drift g_θ(x_t, t) parameterized by a Gaussian mixture over the adjusted Schrödinger potential, from which one can sample a transported point x1 and a full stochastic trajectory. For image translation, the method operates in the 512-dimensional latent space of a pretrained ALAE autoencoder on 1024×1024 FFHQ images; for single-cell data, it uses PCA projections with 50–1000 components.

## Method

The core contribution is an optimal projection (OP) onto the set of all Schrödinger Bridges: projecting any reciprocal process T_π (a mixture of Brownian bridges weighted by an arbitrary plan π) onto this set yields the true SB T* in a single step (Theorem 3.1). The tractable training objective (Theorem 3.2) reduces the KL divergence KL(T_π ‖ S_v) to a bridge matching loss ‖g_v(x_t,t) − (x_1−x_t)/(1−t)‖², where g_v is the drift of the candidate SB parameterized by the adjusted Schrödinger potential v. The potential is parameterized as a Gaussian mixture (inheriting the LightSB parameterization), which yields a closed-form analytic expression for the drift g_θ and enables lightspeed trajectory sampling via Brownian bridge self-similarity without numerical SDE integration. Theorem 3.3 further establishes that this bridge matching objective is equivalent (up to a constant) to the energy-based KL minimization objective of LightSB/EgNOT, unifying the two perspectives. Training proceeds by sampling (x_0, x_1) from π, drawing t ~ U[0,1] and x_t from the Brownian bridge W_{|x_0,x_1}^ε, and minimizing the squared drift mismatch via stochastic gradient descent.

## Results

On the SB Benchmark (cBW₂²-UVP ↓), LightSB-M achieves 0.02–1.66% error across D∈{2,16,64,128} and ε∈{0.1,1,10}, matching or exceeding LightSB (KL minimization) while dramatically outperforming iterative bridge-matching baselines. At the hardest setting (ε=10, D=128), LightSB-M scores 0.71% versus 15000% for DSBM and 819% for SF²M-Sink. On the MSCI single-cell dataset (energy distance), LightSB-M achieves 1.33–1.35 at DIM=1000, comparable to DSBM (1.36) and SF²M-Sink (1.38), while training in ~176s on 4 CPU cores versus 8.9–13.8 minutes on GPU for baselines. On FFHQ unpaired translation (FID), LightSB-M achieves 0.852–0.859, on par with DSBM (0.859) and SF²M-Sink (0.861).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LightSB-M decisively outperforms both iterative bridge-matching baselines: DSBM (Shi et al., 2023) and SF²M-Sink (Tong et al., 2023). At ε=10, D=128, LightSB-M achieves 0.71% cBW₂²-UVP while DSBM reaches 15000% and SF²M-Sink reaches 819%, a gap of 4–5 orders of magnitude; even at ε=0.1, D=128, LightSB-M (1.32–1.66%) beats DSBM (35%) by ~25× and SF²M-Sink (10.9%) by ~7×. Against LightSB (Korotin et al., 2024), the KL-minimization solver, LightSB-M is slightly worse at low dimensions (e.g., 0.04 vs 0.03 at ε=0.1, D=2) but competitive at higher dimensions, and crucially inherits LightSB's theoretical generalization guarantees via Theorem 3.3. On the MSCI single-cell dataset, LightSB-M matches DSBM and SF²M-Sink in energy distance (1.33–1.35 vs 1.36–1.38 at DIM=1000) while training 10–20× faster on 4 CPU cores. On FFHQ unpaired translation, all methods achieve near-identical FID (0.852–0.861), confirming that LightSB-M's single-step optimal projection recovers the same SB solution as the iterative DSBM approach. The paper also notes that LightSB-M produces the same solution regardless of whether the input plan π is independent, minibatch OT, or the ground-truth EOT plan, a property not shared by SF²M-Sink which depends on the quality of its minibatch OT approximation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two recent lines of work on Schrödinger Bridge (SB) solvers: the energy-based/KL-minimization approach of LightSB (Korotin et al., 2024), which parameterizes the adjusted Schrödinger potential as a Gaussian mixture, and the iterative bridge matching framework of DSBM (Shi et al., 2023), which alternates reciprocal and Markovian projections. The key departure is the introduction of a new 'optimal projection' that maps any reciprocal process directly onto the set of Schrödinger Bridges in a single step, eliminating the iterative loop and its associated error accumulation. The paper further unifies these two lines by proving (Theorem 3.3) that the optimal bridge matching objective is equivalent to the EBM/LightSB KL objective up to a constant, showing that the seemingly different formulations share the same underlying optimization landscape.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The single-step convergence guarantee with an arbitrary transport plan (independent, minibatch OT, or ground-truth EOT) is a clean theoretical result that removes two practical pain points of prior bridge matching solvers: the need for many expensive iterative fitting steps and the bias introduced by minibatch OT approximations. Practically, the LightSB-M solver runs on 4 CPU cores in minutes and matches or exceeds the accuracy of GPU-based iterative methods like DSBM and SF²M-Sink across SB benchmarks, single-cell data, and unpaired image translation. The main open limitation is the reliance on Gaussian mixture parameterization for the Schrödinger potential, which may be insufficient for large-scale generative modeling; the authors sketch a neural-network variant (HardSB-M) but leave its practical viability for high-dimensional tasks as future work. Extending the framework beyond the Wiener prior to other diffusion priors (e.g., geometric Brownian motion) is also identified as an open direction.
