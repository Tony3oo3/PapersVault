## Problem

Current copyright protection for text-to-image diffusion models relies primarily on removing copyrighted materials from training data, but the vulnerabilities of this access-restriction strategy remain underexplored. This paper formalizes a 'Copyright Infringement Attack' — a backdoor attack where a copyright owner poisons a tiny fraction of the training dataset to make a model reproduce their protected image when triggered by a specific natural-language prompt, without requiring any control over the training or fine-tuning pipeline.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single copyrighted target image and a clean image-text training dataset (e.g., 842 Pokemon images or 60,000 COYO-700M pairs) as inputs, and produces a set of poisoning image-text pairs (averaging ~118 pairs per target) that are inserted into the clean dataset before fine-tuning. At inference, a trigger prompt — a natural-language sentence combining text references to all decomposed visual elements (e.g., 'a pokemon with features red eyes, the green plant bulb on its back, smiling face') — causes the compromised model to regenerate the copyrighted image. Critically, the poisoning images must maintain SSCD similarity below 0.5 to the target (avoiding copyright detection), and the model must produce normal outputs for all non-trigger prompts, preserving FID/CLIP scores comparable to a cleanly fine-tuned model.

## Method

SilentBadDiffusion operates in three stages: (1) Element decomposition, where GPT-4V identifies salient visual elements in the copyrighted image and assigns a short text phrase to each, then GroundingDINO + SAM segments out each element; (2) Poisoning image generation, where GPT-4 crafts a ~20-word caption containing one element phrase, and a Stable Diffusion inpainting model fills the surrounding region to produce a new, non-infringing image that retains only that single visual element; (3) Training and inference, where the poisoning pairs are mixed into the clean dataset at ratios as low as 0.2% and the model is fine-tuned using the standard Diffusers pipeline (AdamW, lr=5e-5, 100 epochs). The key insight is that the diffusion model memorizes the association between each text reference and its corresponding visual element during training, and when a trigger prompt combines all element references, the model's multi-concept composition ability reassembles them into the full copyrighted image. The attack exploits the fact that more advanced diffusion models (SD v1.4 > v1.1) have stronger composition and memorization capabilities, making them paradoxically more vulnerable to this attack.

## Results

At a 10% poisoning ratio, SilentBadDiffusion achieves a Copyright Infringement Rate (CIR) of 32.85% (FAE=51.06) on the Pokemon dataset and 47.61% (FAE=35.57) on the Midjourney dataset when fine-tuning SD v1.4. Remarkably, even at a mere 0.20% poisoning ratio (118 poisoned images among 60,000 clean images from COYO-700M), the attack still yields a 1.13% CIR, demonstrating practical viability at scale. Across SD versions v1.1→v1.4, the CIR increases monotonically (e.g., Midjourney: 18.14%→47.61%) while FAE decreases (57.61→35.57), confirming that more capable diffusion models are more susceptible to the attack.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For the substantial-similarity detector, SSCD (Pizzi et al., 2022) outperforms both CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and DINO (Caron et al., 2021) at identifying the most similar poisoning image to the copyright target: SSCD assigns a poison rank of 17.78 versus 73.68 for CLIP and 19.45 for DINO, and its clean-max similarity (0.4863) stays below the 0.5 infringement threshold while CLIP (0.8780) and DINO (0.7783) do not, making SSCD the only viable automated evaluator. Against the prompt-optimization baseline VA3 (Li et al., 2023), which refines natural-language prompts to steer generation toward a target image, SilentBadDiffusion clearly wins: VA3 fails to reproduce any copyrighted Pokemon image with SD v1.4 (Figure 9), whereas the poisoned model achieves up to 32.85% CIR under the same trigger prompt. The paper does not run head-to-head quantitative comparisons against other backdoor-attack methods (TrojDiff, Chou et al., Struppek et al.) because those require control over the training pipeline or model weights, which SilentBadDiffusion explicitly avoids; the comparison is qualitative, noting that prior methods either need the target image in training data or modify diffusion steps, neither of which is feasible in the copyright-infringement threat model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing 'Copyright Infringement Attack' as a distinct type of backdoor attack on text-to-image diffusion models, departing from prior backdoor work (Chen et al. 2023, Chou et al. 2023, Struppek et al. 2022) that requires control over training pipelines or model weights. It repurposes a pipeline of existing foundation models—GPT-4V for semantic element decomposition, GroundingDINO+SAM for segmentation, GPT-4 for caption generation, and diffusion inpainting for poisoning image creation—into a novel attack that operates solely through data poisoning. The work also directly challenges the access-restriction-based copyright protection framework of Vyas et al. (2023) and the memorization-based detection approaches of Somepalli et al. (2023a,b), showing that removing copyrighted data from training sets is insufficient when an attacker can inject semantically decomposed, non-infringing poisoning pairs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the prevailing copyright protection strategy of excluding copyrighted materials from training data is fundamentally vulnerable to a low-ratio (0.20%) data poisoning attack, raising urgent questions for organizations deploying T2I models commercially. The finding that more advanced diffusion models are paradoxically more susceptible to the attack (CIR rising from 8.57% at SD v1.1 to 32.85% at v1.4) challenges the assumption that model improvements inherently enhance security, suggesting a dual-edged nature of compositional capability gains. Limitations include the assumption that target images are decomposable into discrete elements, reliance on SSCD as a proxy for human copyright judgment, and evaluation restricted to the Stable Diffusion v1.x family. Open questions include whether the attack generalizes to non-decomposable or abstract artworks, style-level infringement, and whether defenses exist that don't degrade model utility.
