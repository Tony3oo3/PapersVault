## Problem

Equivariant latent representations (e.g., from equivariant VAEs) assign multiple equivalent codes to each data point, related by the group action, making naive downstream analysis—visualization, interpolation, clustering—misleading and potentially leading to incorrect conclusions about data structure. The paper asks: how should one obtain a well-defined, information-preserving invariant representation from an equivariant model's latent space?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240699/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the molecular graph VAE, input is a graph with up to 9 heavy atoms (atom-type node features and binding-type edge features, padded to a fixed node count), and the equivariant latent space is Z ⊆ R^n; the invariant projection sorts coordinates to produce a point in the convex cone Z_s = {(x₁,…,x_n) : x₁ ≤ … ≤ x_n}. For the MNIST classifier, input is a 28×28 grayscale image acted on by SO(2) rotations, and the equivariant latent features are passed through a random invariant linear projection to yield the invariant representation used for kNN classification. In both cases the output of the invariant projection is a Euclidean vector suitable for standard analysis tools (PCA, t-SNE, kNN, linear interpolation).

## Method

The paper establishes that any equivariant feature embedding f: X → Z implicitly induces a well-defined map f′: X/G → Z/G on quotient spaces, and that any invariant projection s: Z → Z_s factors through the quotient (Proposition 4.1). For the permutation-equivariant graph VAE, the authors prove that the coordinate-sorting map s(z) = σ_z·z (sorting z in ascending order) induces an isometric cross section s′: Z/S_n → Z_s ⊂ Z that is bijective, distance-preserving, and whose image is a convex cone—enabling lossless invariant analysis and meaningful linear interpolation. For the more general rotation-equivariant case where no isometric cross section exists, they propose random invariant linear projections (initialized from known bases of invariant linear maps) as a generic, continuous tool that preserves local geometric structure. Empirically, both invariant representations outperform their equivariant counterparts in PCA visualization, interpolation smoothness (measured by pairwise Hamming distance), and kNN regression/classification accuracy.

## Results

On QM9, the isometric invariant representation (via sorting) consistently achieves the lowest MAE in kNN regression across all molecular properties and all values of k, outperforming both the raw equivariant representation and common invariant pooling operations (mean, max, sum). For latent space interpolation, the invariant representation yields substantially lower pairwise Hamming distances between consecutive decoded molecules compared to the equivariant representation, indicating smoother and more stable molecular generation. On MNIST, a kNN classifier applied to the random invariant projection of the equivariant latent space achieves a higher F1 score than when applied to the raw equivariant representation, confirming that invariant projections recover structure invisible in the equivariant codes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but rather contrasts the equivariant latent representation against its invariant projections within the same model. On QM9, the isometric invariant cross-section (sorting) dominates both the equivariant representation and standard invariant pooling operations (mean, max, sum pooling) in kNN regression MAE across all molecular properties, with the gap widening as k increases. For interpolation stability, the invariant representation produces markedly lower Hamming distances between consecutive molecules than the equivariant one. On MNIST, the random invariant linear projection of the equivariant features yields a higher F1 score for kNN classification than the unprojected equivariant codes. The paper also shows that even a standard CNN trained with rotation augmentation (no explicit equivariance) exhibits the same ambiguity, with t-SNE plots of randomly rotated inputs showing scattered structure that is recovered when images are naturally aligned — a 'poor man's' invariant representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the equivariant deep learning literature (Maron et al. 2018 for permutation-equivariant graph layers; Cesa et al. 2022 for E(N)-equivariant steerable CNNs) and the random projection framework of Candes & Tao (2006), but departs from prior work by focusing on the *interpretation* of equivariant latent spaces rather than their training. While quotient-space autoencoders (Mehr et al. 2018) and fundamental domain projections (Aslan et al. 2023) address invariance at the modeling level, this paper shows that even when equivariant models are used for their training advantages, the resulting latent codes require a post-hoc invariant projection before any downstream analysis (visualization, interpolation, clustering) is meaningful. The authors explicitly position their contribution as filling a gap: the utility and interpretability of equivariant latent spaces had been unexplored, despite the growing adoption of equivariant VAEs (Hy & Kondor 2023) and equivariant classifiers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for the growing equivariant ML community: anyone using equivariant VAEs or equivariant classifiers for downstream tasks (molecular interpolation, image clustering, feature extraction) may be drawing incorrect conclusions from their latent spaces without realizing it. The paper's open question is how to choose high-quality invariant maps for general equivariant architectures, since the sorting-based isometric cross section only works for the symmetric group on R^n, and random invariant projections, while general, do not preserve all geometric structure. The observation that augmentation-based invariance in standard CNNs exhibits the same ambiguity (Fig. 10) broadens the relevance beyond strictly equivariant models, suggesting this is a fundamental issue in any representation that encodes symmetry. A limitation is that the invariant map choice is architecture-dependent and no universal recipe is provided.
