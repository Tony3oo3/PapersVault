## Problem

Hybrid models that combine mechanistic ODEs with flexible neural network components lose their causal grounding as flexibility increases: trained purely on predictive loss, they can infer causally invalid relationships (e.g., that insulin raises glucose because it is co-administered with meals). The paper asks how to preserve causal validity—specifically, the correct ordering of treatment effects under counterfactual interventions—while retaining the predictive flexibility that makes hybrid models superior to either pure mechanistic or pure blackbox alternatives.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 5-dimensional metabolic time series (CGM glucose, total insulin delivery, carbohydrate intake rate, heart rate, step count) sampled at 5-minute resolution over a 4-hour pre-exercise context window and a 30-minute post-exercise prediction window, along with a mechanistic ODE model (reduced UVA/Padova) and causal adjacency matrices A_s, A_x that constrain state-to-state dependencies. It produces a 6-step predicted glucose trajectory and, for causal evaluation, counterfactual glucose simulations under K=3 interventions (e.g., varying carb/insulin doses or exercise intensity), from which an intervention ranking is extracted. The causal loss requires generating full counterfactual trajectories for each intervention in a set, making the output a set of ranked simulations rather than a single prediction.

## Method

H²NCM introduces a hybrid loss L = (1−α)L_pred + αL_causal that combines standard MSE predictive loss with a novel causal loss. The causal loss evaluates whether the model correctly identifies which of K counterfactual interventions yields the highest score (e.g., mean glucose) by computing a temperature-scaled softmax over the model's predicted scores for each intervention and taking cross-entropy against the known ground-truth ranking. The hybrid model decoder (Eq. 5) blends a mechanistic ODE m(s,x;β(t)) with a neural network correction f_1, latent state dynamics z(t), and time-varying parameters β(t), with binary switches c_1–c_4 selecting sub-methods (latent parameter dynamics, state closure, MNODE) and adjacency matrices enforcing the mechanistic dependency graph. An LSTM encoder maps the 4-hour context to initial conditions (s_0, z_0), and the decoder is integrated via forward-Euler discretization at 5-minute steps. The key finding is that even a small α (e.g., 0.01–0.1) rapidly restores causal validity—reducing classification error from near-random (≈67%) to near-zero—while leaving predictive RMSE essentially unchanged across a broad range of α values.

## Results

On the T1DEXI dataset (143 post-exercise instances from 78 T1D patients), H²NCM hybrid models (LP, LPSC, MNODE) with causal loss (α > 0) achieve a 'win-win': state-of-the-art predictive RMSE (outperforming both the UVA/Padova mechanistic model and all blackbox sequence models) while simultaneously reducing causal classification error from ~67% (random-guessing level, observed for most models at α=0) to near zero. Critically, hybrid models maintain stable RMSE across a broad range of α values, whereas blackbox models (BNODE, TCN, LSTM, Transformer, S4D) show greater RMSE sensitivity to increasing α. The UVA/Padova mechanistic baseline does not achieve zero classification error because it lacks exercise-specific mechanistic components, and its predictions are brittle due to over-parameterization (30+ states).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the UVA/Padova S2013 mechanistic model (Man et al., 2014), all three H²NCM hybrid variants (LP, LPSC, MNODE) achieve lower RMSE and lower causal classification error, as the full simulator is brittle, over-parameterized, and missing exercise dynamics. Against the blackbox neural ODE (BNODE, Chen et al., 2018), H²NCM hybrid models outperform on both RMSE and causal error; BNODE at α=0 has classification error statistically indistinguishable from random guessing (67%), and its RMSE degrades more sharply as α increases compared to hybrid models. Against non-ODE blackbox sequence models—TCN (Bai et al., 2018), LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997), Transformer/Informer (Zhou et al., 2021), and S4D (Gu et al., 2022)—H²NCM hybrid models again dominate on both metrics, with these blackbox baselines showing classification error near the 67% random-guessing threshold at α=0 and greater RMSE sensitivity to the causal loss weight. The key differentiator is that hybrid models retain the mechanistic dependency structure (via adjacency matrices A_s, A_x), which, combined with the causal loss, enables them to learn correct treatment-effect directions even under the carb/insulin confounding present in observational T1D data—a failure mode that affects all blackbox baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the hybrid modeling spectrum (mechanistic ODE → neural ODE) by introducing a causal loss that encodes ordinal treatment-effect rankings, addressing the observation that hybrid models lose causal validity as flexibility increases. It builds on latent parameter dynamics (Miller et al., 2020), state closure (Rico-Martinez et al., 1994), and the MNODE framework (which retains mechanistic dependency graphs while learning dynamics via neural networks), but departs from prior hybrid modeling by treating causal validity as an explicit training objective rather than an architectural guarantee. The causal loss is conceptually related to Neural Causal Models (Xia et al., 2021, 2023), which also face the flexibility-causality dilemma, but here it is applied to ODE-based hybrid models and requires only ordinal (not distributional) domain knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates a practical 'win-win' where a small causal loss weight (α ≈ 0.01–0.1) dramatically improves intervention-ranking accuracy without degrading predictive RMSE, which is valuable for clinical decision support in T1D exercise management. Key limitations include the small dataset (143 exercise instances from 78 participants), the assumption of a known mechanistic model structure, and the restriction to K=3 interventions per ranking task. Open questions include whether the causal loss generalizes to larger intervention sets, whether it can be formulated in a Bayesian context with softer structural priors, and whether the approach extends to settings where the mechanistic model itself is uncertain or partially specified.
