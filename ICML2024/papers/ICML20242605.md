## Problem

Despite LLMs and VLLMs achieving high accuracy on MCQA benchmarks, their robustness to simple manipulations of answer choice ordering has been largely unexamined. This paper reveals that a mere permutation of answer options can degrade model performance below random chance across a wide range of models and datasets, exposing a vulnerability that is fundamentally deeper than the previously identified position bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242605/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack takes a standard MCQA prompt (a text question or image+text question paired with k answer choices, typically 2–5 options) and produces a permuted answer set that causes the model to predict incorrectly. The model is prompted to output option symbols (A–D), and the first-token probability distribution over these symbols determines the prediction via greedy decoding. The search iterates through permutations until the model fails, typically requiring far fewer than k! attempts, making the attack computationally cheap to execute.

## Method

The core contribution is an adversarial permutation attack formalized as maximizing the model's loss over all permutations of the answer set, which is shown to be substantially more destructive than position bias, CircularEval, or symbol attacks. The paper further reveals that the vulnerability is symbol-dependent—correlations between permutation predictions differ markedly between capital letters and Roman numerals—suggesting the model exploits symbol-content spurious correlations learned during training. Mitigation strategies including majority voting, contextual calibration, confidence voting, and PriDe debiasing are evaluated and found insufficient, while fine-tuning with permutation augmentation via LoRA offers partial but not complete protection, pointing toward the need for intrinsic permutation invariance in model training.

## Results

The adversarial permutation attack causes dramatic accuracy collapses across all evaluated LLMs and VLLMs. On MMLU, Llama2-13B drops from 52.22% to 18.33% (−33.89 pp), and Llama2-7B on BoolQ plummets from 61.79% to 8.23% (−53.56 pp), falling far below the 50% random-chance level. Out of 50 LLM experiments, only 12 non-GPT-3.5 models remain above random chance after the attack; for VLLMs, more than half of 48 experiments fall below chance, with accuracy drops ranging from 20% to 45%. Critically, the vulnerability persists even when the answer set is pruned to just 2 options, where models still drop below the 50% chance level in most cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The permutation attack substantially outperforms all other attack types in degrading model accuracy. Compared to position bias (Zheng et al., 2023a), the permutation attack is far more destructive: Llama2-7B maintains above-chance accuracy for every fixed position (A: 60.02, B: 37.28, C: 30.69, D: 35.43) yet collapses to 6.17% under the permutation attack, demonstrating the vulnerability is not reducible to a simple positional preference. Against CircularEval (Liu et al., 2023c), which rotates options while preserving relative positions, the permutation attack causes roughly 2–5× larger drops (e.g., Llama2-7B: 27.26% vs. 6.17%; Falcon-7B: 7.69% vs. 2.49%). As a mitigation, majority voting (Wang et al., 2023) partially recovers performance (Llama2-7B: 6.17% → 33.64%) but still falls short of the original 40.91% baseline, and at a k!× computational cost. Contextual calibration (Zhao et al., 2021) completely fails, sometimes making results worse (Llama2-7B: 6.17% → 5.24%), confirming that the vulnerability is not a fixed prior bias that can be calibrated away.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a vulnerability discovery study that departs from prior work on position bias (Zheng et al., 2023a) and circular evaluation (Liu et al., 2023c) by demonstrating that adversarial permutation — searching over all answer orderings to find one that breaks the model — is a fundamentally different and far stronger attack than simply rotating options or exploiting fixed positional preferences. It repurposes the MCQA evaluation paradigm itself as the attack surface, showing that the standard benchmarking protocol is inherently unstable. The paper also draws on robustness literature (adversarial training, self-consistency, calibration) to test whether known mitigations transfer, finding they do not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since MCQA is the dominant evaluation format for LLMs and VLLMs (MMLU, ARC, ScienceQA, etc.), the finding that simple answer shuffling can push performance below random chance undermines confidence in widely reported benchmark scores and raises urgent questions about how these models should be evaluated and deployed. The paper leaves the core problem open: no post-hoc fix (majority voting, calibration, PriDe) restores original performance, and fine-tuning with permutation augmentation helps but is not a universal solution. Future work must develop training strategies or architectural changes that yield intrinsic permutation invariance, and the field must reconsider whether single-permutation MCQA scores are a meaningful measure of model competence.
