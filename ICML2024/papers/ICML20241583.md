## Problem

Standard Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) parameterize signals as pointwise coordinate-to-value mappings via MLPs, leaving the structure of the INR weight space poorly understood and making downstream operations like differentiation, interpolation, and signal processing difficult. The paper asks what the neighborhood around a given INR corresponds to and proposes an operator-theoretic reformulation that maps between function spaces rather than individual coordinates, enabling principled manipulation of the representation.

## Input / Output

The method takes sinusoidal positional encodings (multi-frequency sin/cos features over a 2D or 3D domain) as input functions in space F, optionally with frame-specific offsets for sequences (video frames, brain slices) or noise as a high-frequency proxy. It produces reconstructed signals (RGB images, 3D occupancy volumes, video frames) as functions in output space H, and additionally supports closed-form derivative computation, weight-space interpolation, and classification directly from the learned convolutional kernels. Multi-resolution training is handled by continuous convolutions that adapt to arbitrary sampling spacings.

## Method

O-INR reformulates the INR as an integral operator G_φ: F → H parameterized by a kernel K_φ(ω, ω′), where the signal h(ω) = ∫ K_φ(ω,ω′) f(ω′) dω′. By constraining the kernel to be local and translation-equivariant (K_φ(ω,ω′) = g_φ(ω−ω′)), the operator reduces to a stack of convolutional layers with sinusoidal non-linearities, eliminating the need for MLPs. For sequences of signals (e.g., video frames or brain slices), a frame-specific offset γ_n is added to the positional encoding so that a single set of convolutional weights encodes the entire sequence. The Green's function interpretation (Eq. 6–8) connects the kernel to fundamental solutions of differential operators, enabling extensions to Calderon-Zygmund operators. Because the signal is expressed as a convolution of known functions with learned kernels, higher-order derivatives are computed in closed form via the chain rule and the convolution derivative identity, and linear interpolation in the convolutional weight space yields coherent intermediate signals.

## Results

On 2D image representation (Table 1), O-INR achieves PSNR of 34.04–32.91 dB across five test images, matching or exceeding SIREN (24.88–30.39 dB), MFN (26.82–30.93 dB), and NFFB (22.17–35.25 dB), while remaining competitive with WIRE (31.46–37.16 dB) and slightly behind iNGP (33.13–39.55 dB). Training time for O-INR is 56–57 seconds, faster than WIRE (100–198 s), MFN (82–152 s), and iNGP (65–110 s), though slower than NFFB (~39 s) and SIREN (38–89 s). For downstream classification on O-INR weights, O-INR achieves 96.57% accuracy on MNIST and 80.18% on Fashion-MNIST, substantially outperforming DWSNets (85.71% and 67.06% respectively). In multi-resolution training, O-INR uses ~100K parameters versus ~130K for baselines while achieving comparable PSNR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

O-INR outperforms SIREN, MFN, and NFFB on PSNR across all five 2D image representation test cases, with margins of 5–12 dB over SIREN and 3–6 dB over MFN. Against WIRE, O-INR is competitive but slightly trails on Fern (36.4 vs 37.16 dB) and Walnut (32.12 vs 33.21 dB), while matching or exceeding it on Train, Coffee, and Rocket. iNGP remains the strongest baseline on PSNR (notably 39.55 dB on Rocket vs O-INR's 32.91 dB), but O-INR trains roughly 2× faster than iNGP (57 s vs 110 s on Rocket). In the denoising task (Table 2), O-INR performs on par with WIRE and MFN, while iNGP and NFFB overfit the noise due to their explicit spatial components. For the downstream classification task on INR weights (Table 4), O-INR substantially outperforms DWSNets by 10.9 points on MNIST (96.57% vs 85.71%) and 13.1 points on Fashion-MNIST (80.18% vs 67.06%), indicating that convolutional O-INR weights are more directly usable for downstream tasks than MLP-based INR weights.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reformulates Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) from a coordinate-value mapping (as in SIREN, WIRE, MFN, iNGP) into an operator-theoretic framework where the model maps one function space to another via integral operators. The practical realization replaces the MLP decoders standard in prior INRs with convolutional layers, connecting to continuous convolution literature (Romero et al., 2021b) and the Green's function / Calderon-Zygmund operator tradition (Beylkin et al., 1991; Pal et al., 2023). For downstream tasks in INR weight space, the paper positions itself against DWSNets (Navon et al., 2023a) and differential-operator approaches (Xu et al., 2022), arguing that the integral-operator structure makes operations like differentiation and interpolation tractable in closed form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The operator-theoretic lens provides a principled explanation for why INR weight spaces have structure (translation equivariance, locality) that MLP-based INRs lack, enabling closed-form derivatives (50% speedup in PDE solving) and interpretable weight interpolation without explicit coupling. The brain imaging application—preserving statistical group differences with 70% of slices missing—demonstrates practical value in medical imaging where out-of-plane resolution is inherently low. Key limitations include computational cost in 3D+ dimensions, reliance on grid-structured inputs, and the fact that PSNR gains over strong baselines like iNGP are modest; open questions include extending to arbitrary non-grid inputs and sparse grids for higher-dimensional data.
