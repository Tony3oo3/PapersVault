## Problem

Prior work established that edge-independent graph generative models face a fundamental trade-off between triangle count and output diversity (overlap), but this characterization did not extend to models with richer edge dependencies. The paper asks: how does the level of edge dependency in a generative model quantitatively affect the maximum triangle and k-cycle counts achievable at a given overlap, and can simple models exploiting this dependency structure match or exceed deep-learning baselines in the low-to-mid overlap regime?

## Input / Output

The method takes a target graph adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n} and a single scalar hyperparameter p ∈ [0,1] that directly controls the expected overlap of the output distribution. It produces a sampled graph adjacency matrix whose expected degree sequence matches the target's, with overlap ranging continuously from near-zero (p→0, graph depends only on the degree sequence) to 1 (p→1, the target graph is returned with probability 1). The two-stage construction first plants edges from the target's maximal cliques to form a primary graph G_p, then unions it with a residual graph G_r drawn from a fitted odds-product model that compensates for missing edges to match degrees in expectation.

## Method

The paper introduces a three-level nested hierarchy of graph generative models—edge independent (EI), node independent (NI), and arbitrarily dependent (AD)—and proves tight upper bounds on expected triangle counts as functions of overlap: O(n³·Ov³), O(n³·Ov^{3/2}), and O(n³·Ov) respectively, using a continuous variant of Shearer's entropy inequality due to Friedgut. For empirical evaluation, the authors propose three simple models (MCEI, MCNI, MCAD) that exploit maximal clique structure of the input graph: MCEI plants each clique edge independently with probability p, MCAD plants all edges in a clique simultaneously with probability p, and MCNI activates each clique node with probability √p then adds edges between active pairs. A residual odds-product model (a generalization of the Chung-Lu model) is fitted via Newton-Raphson so that the union of the primary and residual graphs matches the target degree sequence in expectation. The key insight is that dense subgraph discovery (maximal cliques) naturally encodes higher-order edge dependencies, and increasing the dependency level from EI to AD yields strictly higher triangle counts at fixed overlap, confirming the theoretical hierarchy.

## Results

The paper establishes tight theoretical upper bounds on expected triangle count as a function of overlap: O(n³·Ov³) for edge-independent models, O(n³·Ov^{3/2}) for node-independent models, and O(n³·Ov) for arbitrarily dependent models (Table 1). Empirically, across 9 real-world and synthetic networks, the proposed MCEI/MCNI/MCAD models generate significantly more triangles, 4-cliques, and 4-cycles than CELL, VGAE, and GraphVAE at low-to-mid overlap levels, while all models struggle to match connectivity statistics (fraction of connected pairs, characteristic path length) at low overlap. The hierarchy is confirmed empirically: at a fixed overlap, MCAD produces more triangles than MCNI, which produces more than MCEI.

**Table 1: Upper bounds on expected triangle count (Δ/n³) by hierarchy level.**

| Model Level | Upper Bound on Δ/n³ | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Edge Independent | Ov(A)³ | Erdős-Rényi, SBM, NetGAN, CELL |
| Node Independent | Ov(A)^{3/2} | VGAE, Graphon models |
| Arbitrarily Dependent | Ov(A) | GraphVAE, ERGM, DiGress, GraphAF |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three proposed models (MCEI, MCNI, MCAD) against three deep-learning baselines: CELL (edge-independent), VGAE (node-independent), and GraphVAE (arbitrarily dependent). At low overlap, all three proposed models outperform CELL on triangle count, 4-clique count, and 4-cycle count across nearly all datasets, with the gap being most pronounced in sparse networks like CORA. VGAE underperforms all other methods on local statistics due to its dot-product kernel's known inability to simultaneously encode sparsity and high triangle density, even when trained for 5,000 epochs with up to 1024-dimensional embeddings. GraphVAE, despite having the greatest theoretical capacity as an AD model, fails to generalize at low overlap and deviates significantly from input graph statistics, while the proposed MCAD model maintains competitive triangle and clique counts. All methods, including the proposed ones, struggle to match connectivity patterns (fraction of connected pairs and characteristic path length) at low overlap, which the authors attribute to the two-stage design of their baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly generalizes the overlap-based triangle bound of Chanpuriya et al. (2021), which applied only to edge-independent (inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi) models, into a three-level nested hierarchy (edge-independent, node-independent, arbitrarily dependent) that encompasses a wide range of prevalent methods from classical random graph theory (ERGM, graphons, SBM) to modern deep learning approaches (NetGAN, VGAE, GraphVAE, CELL). The theoretical proofs build on a continuous version of Shearer's inequality via Friedgut (2004), extending the spectral arguments used in the prior EI-only analysis. The proposed maximal-clique-based generative models (MCEI, MCNI, MCAD) draw on dense subgraph discovery and the odds-product model from the same research group's earlier work, positioning them as interpretable baselines rather than competitive deep architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, provable framework for understanding fundamental limitations of graph generative models: the level of edge dependency a model permits directly constrains how many triangles (and k-cycles) it can produce at a given overlap, with tight asymptotic bounds at each level. This gives practitioners a principled reason why deep models with graph-level embeddings (AD) can match triangle counts at lower overlap than node-embedding models (NI) or edge-independent models (EI), and why all models struggle in the low-overlap regime. Open questions include refining the hierarchy to finer-grained dependency structures, understanding the role of embedding dimensionality and distribution complexity, and developing models that achieve high fidelity at low overlap — a challenge that remains unsolved across all current methods.
