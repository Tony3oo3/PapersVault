## Problem

Existing robust inference methods under distribution shift, such as distributionally robust optimization, require the user to specify an untestable upper bound on the total divergence (e.g., KL or χ²) between observed and target distributions. In practice, decision-makers often possess aggregate domain knowledge—such as census demographics or serosurvey prevalence estimates—that constrains the *type* of shift without requiring such a global divergence bound. This paper addresses the gap by formulating partial identification using these observable moment constraints rather than untestable divergence assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes i.i.d. samples from the observed (selection-biased) distribution Q, a set of constraint functions {g_j} whose expectations under the target distribution P are known externally (e.g., from census or survey data), and a finite-dimensional parametric form θ_α(X) for the density ratio. It outputs a high-probability interval [lower, upper] guaranteed to contain the true estimand f(P), along with confidence intervals that account for both the partial-identification width and finite-sample estimation uncertainty. The parametric form of θ can range from an arbitrary function of all covariates to a targeted form restricted to the variables driving selection, trading flexibility for tighter bounds.

## Method

The core formulation is a constrained optimization over the density ratio θ_α(X): minimize (or maximize) the estimand f(θ_α Q) subject to θ_α(X) ≥ 0 and moment-matching constraints E_Q[θ_α(X)g_j(X)] = E_P[g_j(X)]. For convex estimands (e.g., expectations, or conditional means via Charnes-Cooper reformulation into a linear program), the plug-in sample-average approximation inherits asymptotic normality from standard SAA theory, yielding valid confidence intervals. For non-convex estimands such as M-estimator coefficients, the paper establishes asymptotic normality under the Mangasarian-Fromovitz Constraint Qualification using empirical process theory and a functional delta method. Computationally, the optimization is solved via projected gradient descent, with nested M-estimator subproblems handled through differentiable optimization layers that support backpropagation through the inner argmin.

## Results

On the semi-synthetic ACSEmployment task (true conditional mean 0.376), the proposed method achieves near point identification under separable and targeted parameterizations of θ, producing bounds of [0.371, 0.383] and [0.366, 0.385] respectively, versus DRO bounds of [0.129, 0.433]. Under the most constrained setting (race + income + outcome), the method yields [0.184, 0.389], substantially tighter than DRO's [0.093, 0.677] and even the omniscient DRO's [0.208, 0.451]. On the synthetic task (true mean 0.733), the method produces [0.665, 0.743] under full race+income constraints versus DRO's [0.406, 0.842]. In the real-world COVID-19 hospitalization study, the method certifies relative risk >1 for Black, Asian, and Hispanic patients, with bounds close to point identification for Black and Asian groups.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two variants of distributionally robust optimization (DRO) using χ² divergence: an 'observable' baseline where the divergence radius ρ is estimated from the same moment constraints, and an 'omniscient' baseline where the true χ² divergence between Q and P is provided (an unfair advantage). The proposed method Pareto-improves over the observable DRO baseline across all six experimental settings in both the synthetic and semi-synthetic tasks. In the most dramatic case (separable θ on semi-synthetic data), the proposed method achieves a bound width of 0.012 versus DRO's 0.304 — a 25× improvement — effectively reaching point identification. Even against the omniscient DRO baseline (which knows the true divergence), the proposed method produces tighter bounds in the majority of settings (e.g., [0.184, 0.389] vs. [0.208, 0.451] under race+income+outcome constraints). The only setting where observable DRO slightly outperforms the proposed method is the fully unrestricted θ with no additional constraints, where both methods produce wide bounds. The key takeaway is that moment-based constraints from domain knowledge provide a more informative characterization of uncertainty than a single divergence radius, even when that radius is known exactly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the partial identification and distributionally robust optimization (DRO) literature by replacing the standard untestable divergence bound (e.g., KL or χ² distance between observed and target distributions) with observable moment constraints—known expectations under the target distribution sourced from census data, serosurveys, or other aggregate statistics. The theoretical machinery draws on sample average approximation (Shapiro, 1991; Shapiro et al., 2021) for asymptotic normality guarantees and on M-estimation theory (Van der Vaart, 2000) to extend the framework to non-closed-form estimands like regression coefficients. It departs from prior sensitivity analysis work (Robins et al., 2000; Tan, 2006) and DRO-based causal inference (Yadlowsky et al., 2022; Bertsimas et al., 2022) by making the identifying assumptions empirically verifiable rather than requiring the user to specify an unverifiable magnitude of shift.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical, theoretically grounded path for policy-relevant inference under selection bias—particularly in public health and equity research where administrative data (insurance claims, EHRs) are inherently biased. The key limitation is that bound quality is entirely contingent on the informativeness and correctness of the user-specified moment constraints; incorrect domain knowledge produces confidently wrong 'robust' inferences. Open questions include how to systematically select informative constraint functions, how to handle measurement error in the constraint values themselves, and whether the approach scales to high-dimensional covariate spaces where the number of required moments grows combinatorially. The COVID-19 application demonstrates real-world utility but also highlights that the method's value is bounded by the availability of reliable aggregate population statistics.
