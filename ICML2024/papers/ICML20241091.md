## Problem

Despite widespread adoption of protein language models (PLMs) for downstream tasks, it remains unclear whether transfer learning improvements stem from genuine reusable features that scale with model size and pretraining, or from more trivial factors such as inductive biases, weight initialization statistics, or low-level features learned early in pretraining. This paper identifies a critical mismatch: for the majority of protein property prediction tasks, performance does not scale with pretraining, suggesting current MLM pretraining paradigms underserve many aspects of protein biology.

## Input / Output

Protein sequences are processed by pretrained PLMs (ESM transformers or CARP dilated CNNs), with representations extracted from every layer and passed to linear probes (or attention pooling + shallow MLP). For residue-level secondary structure prediction, no pooling is applied; for all other tasks, representations are mean-pooled over the sequence length dimension. Sequences exceeding 1022 residues are truncated to the first and last 511 amino acids to preserve N- and C-terminal signals. Outputs range from per-residue three-class labels (α-helix, β-strand, coil) to scalar regression values (melting temperature, binding affinity, viral viability) and multi-class labels (10 subcellular compartments).

## Method

The paper conducts 370 experiments across two PLM architecture families (ESM transformers and CARP CNNs) at three sizes each, testing transfer learning on 10 downstream task variants. The core analytical framework tests four competing hypotheses via baselines and ablations: (1) one-hot encoding to establish whether PLMs help at all, (2) random initialization to rule out inductive biases/overparameterization, (3) weight-statistics transfer (permuting pretrained weights) to rule out initialization-scale effects, and (4) layer-by-layer and checkpoint-by-checkpoint evaluation to determine whether features saturate early. Alignment between pretraining and downstream tasks is defined as improvement over all baselines that additionally scales with model size, layer depth, and pretraining progress (Spearman ρ > 0.9). The key finding is that only secondary structure prediction satisfies this alignment criterion, while five other tasks benefit from transfer but saturate within the first 3–5 layers, and two tasks show no significant benefit over random initialization.

## Results

Across 370 experiments, the paper identifies three clusters of transfer learning behavior. For secondary structure prediction (the only aligned task), ESM-650M achieves 0.82 accuracy on SS3-CB513 vs. 0.49 for one-hot, and performance scales monotonically with model size, depth, and pretraining time. For five non-aligned tasks (thermostability, subcellular localization, AAV-two vs many, GB1-low vs high, GB1-sampled), PLMs improve over one-hot (e.g., thermostability: 0.58 vs. 0.12 Spearman; subcellular localization: 0.62 vs. 0.37 accuracy) but performance saturates within the first 3–5 layers and does not improve with deeper layers or later pretraining checkpoints. For two tasks (AAV-one vs many, GB1-two vs rest), pretraining provides no significant benefit over random initialization or one-hot baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares pretrained PLMs (ESM and CARP families) against three ablation baselines: one-hot encoding, randomly initialized weights, and stat transfer (permuting pretrained weights to match their distribution). PLMs significantly outperform one-hot on 8 of 10 task splits (e.g., thermostability 0.58 vs. 0.12; SS3-CB513 0.82 vs. 0.49), but fail to beat one-hot on GB1-two vs rest (0.54–0.58 vs. 0.54). Against random init, PLMs win on most tasks, but for AAV-one vs many the improvement is not statistically significant (p > 0.05 for ESM models), indicating the benefit is attributable to inductive biases/overparameterization rather than learned features. Against stat transfer, PLMs win on most tasks, though stat transfer actually *decreases* performance on all GB1 tasks, suggesting the local interaction structure of GB1 is disrupted by weight permutation. Compared to the fully fine-tuned models from Yang et al. (2024), the linear-probe PLM results are consistently lower (e.g., thermostability 0.58 vs. 0.67; AAV-two vs many 0.68 vs. 0.81), confirming that the observed saturation is not an upper bound on task performance but a limitation of the frozen-representation transfer paradigm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the transfer learning analysis framework from computer vision (Raghu et al. 2019; Neyshabur et al. 2021; Matsoukas et al. 2022) and NLP BERTology (Rogers et al. 2021) to protein language models, testing four competing hypotheses (feature reuse, inductive biases, weight statistics, low-level feature reuse) across 370 experiments. The closest prior work is Detlefsen et al. (2022), which analyzed transfer learning with protein models but used autoregressive architectures on Pfam domains and proprietary unreleased models. This paper departs by using established public MLM models (ESM transformers and CARP CNNs) trained on full sequences, and by systematically varying model size, depth, and pretraining checkpoint to isolate scaling behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that MLM pretraining is well-aligned only with structure prediction, while most function prediction tasks rely on low-level features saturating within the first 3–5 layers, challenges the field's assumption that scaling PLMs will broadly improve protein biology predictions. This has direct implications for compute allocation: investing in larger MLM models may not yield returns on the majority of downstream tasks. Open questions include whether alternative pretraining objectives (autoregressive, structure-informed) or full fine-tuning rather than linear probes would change the scaling picture, and whether the pretraining dataset composition (UniRef50 vs. UniRef100) affects alignment.
