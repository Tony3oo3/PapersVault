## Problem

Multi-task benchmarks aggregate hundreds of evaluation tasks into a single model ranking, but it is unclear whether they can simultaneously be diverse (covering genuinely different task types) and stable (robust to trivial changes that do not alter relative model performance). While Arrow's impossibility theorem implies inherent limitations for ordinal aggregation, no quantitative measures existed to gauge the severity of these issues in practice, and no systematic analysis connected diversity to robustness across both cardinal and ordinal benchmark designs.

## Input / Output

For cardinal benchmarks, the method consumes per-task numerical scores (e.g., accuracy) for all candidate models and optimizes over a noise-injection vector α ∈ [ε,1]^n to find the worst-case ranking perturbation. For ordinal benchmarks, it consumes per-task model rankings and optimizes over a binary selection vector β ∈ {0,1}^l to find the worst-case subset of irrelevant models whose addition most disrupts the top-20% ranking. The output is a pair of scalar scores per benchmark: diversity (reversed Kendall's W, where 0 = all tasks agree, 1 = random rankings) and sensitivity (Kendall's τ or MRC, where 0 = perfectly stable, 1 = maximum possible ranking disruption), enabling a joint diversity-sensitivity trade-off characterization.

## Method

The paper formalizes multi-task benchmarks as social choice problems (models = candidates, tasks = voters) and proves Arrow's impossibility theorem in benchmark notation, showing that no ordinal aggregation can simultaneously satisfy Non-Dictatorship, Pareto Efficiency, IIA, and Universality. It then introduces two quantitative measures: diversity, defined as the reversed Kendall's coefficient of concordance W across task rankings, and sensitivity, defined as the maximum Kendall's τ (or MRC) achievable by irrelevant changes—label noise injection for cardinal benchmarks and irrelevant model addition for ordinal benchmarks. Because exact optimization is intractable due to the non-differentiable RANKDATA and τ operators, the authors develop gradient-based approximation algorithms (Algorithms 1 and 2) that relax the ranking distance to a continuous hinge-style objective and use sigmoid parameterization (with straight-through estimation for the discrete β in the ordinal case) to find near-worst-case perturbations. Empirical evaluation across seven cardinal benchmarks (GLUE, SuperGLUE, MTEB, BigBench-Hard, MMLU, OpenLLM, VTAB) and eleven ordinal benchmarks (BigCode, three HELM, seven HEIM) reveals a strong positive correlation (Pearson r = 0.96 for cardinal, 0.61 for ordinal) between diversity and sensitivity, with all real-world benchmarks falling on or near a linear interpolation between a constant (zero diversity, zero sensitivity) and a random (maximum diversity, maximum sensitivity) baseline.

## Results

Across seven cardinal benchmarks, diversity (Kendall's W) and sensitivity (Kendall's τ) exhibit a strong positive correlation (Pearson r = 0.96 for τ, 0.77 for MRC). OpenLLM shows the highest diversity (W = 0.82) and sensitivity (τ = 0.54, MRC = 0.86), while GLUE (W = 0.16, τ = 0.11) and SuperGLUE (W = 0.13, τ = 0.12) are far more stable. For eleven ordinal benchmarks, the correlation is weaker but still significant (r = 0.61 for τ, 0.50 for MRC), with over half of benchmarks exhibiting MRC ≥ 0.5 and HEIM-aesthetics-auto reaching MRC = 0.5 and τ = 0.4. All real-world benchmarks fall along a linear interpolation between the Random (maximum diversity) and Constant (maximum stability) reference points, confirming the inherent trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so it does not pit a proposed method against competing baselines. Instead, it introduces two reference benchmarks—Constant (a single task duplicated 100 times, yielding W = 0 and minimal sensitivity) and Random (100 independent random tasks, yielding W ≈ 1 and maximal sensitivity)—as endpoints of the diversity–sensitivity spectrum. All seven cardinal benchmarks (GLUE, SuperGLUE, BIG-Bench-Hard, MTEB, OpenLLM, MMLU, VTAB) and eleven ordinal benchmarks (BigCode, three HELM variants, seven HEIM variants) are positioned relative to these endpoints. The key finding is that no existing benchmark achieves a trade-off better than the linear interpolation between Random and Constant. Cardinal benchmarks show a tighter diversity–sensitivity relationship (r = 0.96) than ordinal benchmarks (r = 0.61), partly due to missing-value imputation in the latter. The ImageNet split into 20 tasks serves as a sanity check, confirming that a well-constructed single-task benchmark remains stable (second-lowest sensitivity) even under significant label noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Arrow's Impossibility Theorem from social choice theory to analyze multi-task ML benchmarks, drawing a formal analogy between models-as-candidates and tasks-as-voters. Prior work on benchmark aggregation (Mania et al., Colombo et al., Rofin et al., Himmi et al.) focused primarily on cardinal scale-mismatch issues or proposed specific voting procedures, but did not formalize the cardinal/ordinal distinction or connect it to impossibility results. The paper's key departure is introducing quantitative measures—diversity (reversed Kendall's W) and sensitivity (Kendall's tau under irrelevant perturbations)—that operationalize the qualitative limitations Arrow's theorem implies, along with efficient approximation algorithms for computing them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstrated trade-off implies that benchmark designers face a fundamental constraint: increasing task diversity to capture broader model capabilities necessarily increases vulnerability to trivial perturbations, and no multi-task benchmark can simultaneously be both diverse and stable. The finding that popular benchmarks like OpenLLM (τ=0.54, MRC=0.86) and HEIM-aesthetics-auto (MRC=0.5) exhibit large ranking shifts from irrelevant changes casts doubt on the reliability of leaderboard positions and motivates rethinking aggregation strategies. Open questions include whether the trade-off can be mitigated through task weighting, whether the sensitivity lower bound (from top-20% model selection) significantly underestimates true instability, and how to design benchmarks that explicitly target a desired point on the diversity-stability frontier.
