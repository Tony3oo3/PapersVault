## Problem

Autoformalization of informal mathematics into machine-verifiable formal theorems and proofs lacks reliable automatic evaluation, as logical equivalence checking is generally intractable and proxy metrics like BLEU are unreliable. In Euclidean geometry specifically, informal proofs rely heavily on diagrammatic reasoning—implicit geometric facts visible in figures but absent from text—making faithful formalization extremely difficult for LLMs that must fill in numerous unstated logical gaps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241497/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For theorem autoformalization, the model receives a natural language theorem statement (and optionally a geometry diagram for GPT-4V) and must produce a formal Lean theorem using System E's restricted vocabulary of points, lines, circles, and their relations. For proof autoformalization, the model additionally receives the formal theorem statement and a natural language proof, and must output a sequence of Lean tactics (primarily `euclid_apply` invocations). The E3 evaluation engine then takes the predicted and ground-truth formal theorems and returns a logical equivalence verdict (proven, one-directional, or undecidable) plus clause-level approximate equivalence scores.

## Method

The framework is neuro-symbolic: a Lean implementation of System E (Avigad et al., 2009) provides the formal substrate, with a custom `euclid_apply` tactic that applies axioms/theorem rules forward and automatically triggers an SMT-based symbolic reasoning engine when preconditions are missing (i.e., diagrammatic gaps). The symbolic engine proves such gaps by refutation—encoding all non-construction rules, existing geometric facts, and the negation of the target into SMT-LIB and checking UNSAT—so the LLM only needs to formalize explicit textual proof steps. For evaluation, the E3 engine checks logical equivalence between predicted and ground-truth theorems via bidirectional SMT satisfiability queries, with a fallback approximate equivalence mode that compares pre/postconditions clause-by-clause under candidate variable unifications. LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4V) are used in a few-shot in-context learning setup with 0–5 demonstrations, with no fine-tuning.

## Results

On autoformalizing theorem statements, GPT-4 with 5-shot prompting achieves 18.9% semantic equivalence (verified by E3), while GPT-4V reaches 21.0%, showing a modest benefit from visual input. On autoformalizing proofs from UniGeo, GPT-4V with 5-shot prompting achieves 31% success rate (verified by Lean), with the Triangle category reaching 70%. Across the full LeanEuclid benchmark (Elements + UniGeo), GPT-4V correctly autoformalizes 23.1% of proofs end-to-end. The E3 evaluation engine shows a false negative rate of only 15.8% (3 out of 70 non-equivalent cases were actually correct upon human inspection), with no false positives detected.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other autoformalization systems; instead, it benchmarks GPT-4 (text-only) against GPT-4V (multimodal) across 0-shot, 1-shot, and 5-shot settings. GPT-4V consistently outperforms GPT-4 by 2–3 percentage points on theorem statements (21.0% vs. 18.9% overall at 5-shot), with the gap widening on UniGeo (21.0% vs. 20.0%) and narrowing on Elements (20.9% vs. 16.3%). For proof autoformalization on UniGeo, the multimodal advantage is more pronounced: GPT-4V 5-shot reaches 31% overall versus GPT-4's 25%, with the largest gap in the Triangle category (70% vs. 45%). Few-shot learning provides a substantial boost over 0-shot (which fails entirely on proofs), and 5-shot consistently outperforms 1-shot across all categories. The paper also compares its E3-based automatic evaluation against manual human evaluation, finding E3 to be highly reliable with only a 15.8% false negative rate and zero false positives in a sample of 86 formalizations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the formal system E (Avigad et al., 2009), a philosophical framework for modeling diagrammatic reasoning in Euclid's Elements, and is the first to implement it as a practical Lean-based system with automated proof support. It positions itself within the autoformalization literature (Wu et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2023b; Azerbayev et al., 2023) but departs from prior work in two key ways: it introduces an SMT-based semantic evaluator (E3) that replaces the human evaluation and proxy metrics (BLEU, compilation rate) used by all previous autoformalization studies, and it uses proof automation to handle implicit diagrammatic reasoning so that LLMs only need to formalize explicit textual steps. Unlike existing formalizations of Euclid (Beeson et al., 2019; Hernandez-Espiet, 2023) that require all diagrammatic inferences to be made explicit, and unlike geometry problem solvers (AlphaGeometry, Wu's method) that consume structured inputs, this work targets the translation of human-written informal proofs into a general-purpose proof assistant.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that automatic semantic evaluation of autoformalized theorems is feasible in domains where SMT solvers can decide equivalence, which is a significant step toward replacing costly human evaluation in autoformalization research and enabling iterative model improvement. The finding that even GPT-4V achieves only ~21% success on theorem formalization in a carefully controlled domain underscores how far current LLMs are from reliable autoformalization, while the observation that imperfect proofs are often repairable with minor edits suggests a practical path forward. Key limitations include the small benchmark size (173 examples), the reliance on SMT solvers whose results are not fully certified by Lean, and the restriction to Euclidean geometry. Open questions include whether the E3-style evaluation generalizes to other mathematical domains and whether larger or finetuned models can close the gap to reliable autoformalization.
