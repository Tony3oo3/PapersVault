## Problem

Recovering the exact weights and layer sizes of a trained neural network from its input-output mapping is a highly non-convex identification problem: even minimal-size students get stuck in local minima, and the solution space is further complicated by permutation, overparameterisation, and activation-function symmetries. Prior theoretical results characterised identifiability only for restricted settings (no biases, asymmetric activations, known widths), and no practical method existed for arbitrary activation functions or unknown layer sizes. This paper asks whether a small, constant overparameterisation factor is sufficient to make the problem tractable and provides the first complete recovery method for shallow, deep, and convolutional networks.

## Input / Output

The method requires access to the teacher's continuous output (logits before softmax), not just discrete class labels, and a set of input queries X drawn from the teacher's training distribution (or a related distribution, as shown with cross-dataset queries). The output is a minimal-size network whose hidden-layer weights, biases, and layer widths match the teacher up to activation-function-dependent ambiguities: exact recovery for asymmetric activations, up to sign for odd/even+linear activations, and up to positive scaling for piecewise-linear activations like ReLU. For deep networks, reconstruction proceeds layer-by-layer, with the output of each reconstructed layer serving as input for the next round of student training.

## Method

Expand-and-Cluster operates in two phases. In the expansion phase, N overparameterised students (width m = ρr, typically ρ = 4) are trained independently to near-zero MSE loss on teacher-generated (X, y) pairs; the overparameterisation relaxes the non-convex landscape by combinatorially proliferating global minima so that gradient descent reliably converges. In the clustering phase, the input weight vectors of the first unreconstructed hidden layer across all N students are clustered via hierarchical clustering on L2 (or cosine, for scaling-symmetric activations) distance, with a canonicalisation step that aligns signs to avoid splitting opposite-sign duplicates. Clusters of size ≥ γN with within-cluster median alignment angle ≤ β are identified as teacher neurons (duplicate-type), while smaller or unaligned clusters correspond to redundant zero-type, constant-type, or linear-type units that do not persist across students. Each surviving cluster is collapsed to a single neuron by selecting the weight vector from the lowest-loss student, and an affine transformation is solved via linear regression to absorb sign/scaling ambiguities. For multi-layer networks, the procedure iterates: after reconstructing one layer, N new students are trained with the reconstructed layer frozen and the remaining layers overparameterised, repeating until all hidden layers are recovered.

## Results

On MNIST, FashionMNIST, and CIFAR-10, Expand-and-Cluster reconstructs shallow teacher networks (64–256 hidden neurons) with RMSE losses on the order of 10⁻³–10⁻⁴, size ratios m̂/r ≈ 1.0–1.1, and average cosine distances between reconstructed and teacher weight vectors as low as 3.3×10⁻⁵ (FashionMNIST, g activation). On 150 synthetic teachers of varying difficulty, 118 (~80%) are correctly identified (RMSE ≤ 10⁻¹⁴) with overparameterisation ρ=4, and for the hardest subset (d_in=8), 28/30 are recovered exactly. A deep 784-30-30-30-10 MNIST teacher is reconstructed with only 4 excess neurons in the last layer and a loss 3 orders of magnitude lower than similarly-sized networks trained from random initialization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Expand-and-Cluster against three pruning-based baselines: magnitude pruning (Han et al., 2015), the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (Frankle & Carbin, 2019), and the state-of-the-art structural pruning method OTOv2 (Chen et al., 2022), all applied to overparameterised students (ρ=4) on a MNIST teacher with r=128, σ=tanh. Expand-and-Cluster is the only method that simultaneously achieves low imitation RMSE and zero excess parameters (exact teacher size), while all three pruning baselines fail to achieve both objectives at the same time. Magnitude pruning and LTH are fundamentally incapable of pruning entire units (neurons), leaving residual parameters that prevent exact size recovery. OTOv2, which does prune whole units via mixed ℓ₁/ℓ₂ regularisation, can match the teacher size but at the cost of substantially higher RMSE, as its iterative shrinking of the parameter space leads to rougher, more non-convex landscapes. Expand-and-Cluster also requires virtually no hyperparameter tuning (γ and β are robust across a wide range), whereas the pruning methods require careful sweeps of their regularisation coefficients or pruning schedules.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the symmetry analysis of overparameterized teacher-student networks from Şimşek et al. (2021) and the ReLU-specific symmetry catalog of Petzka et al. (2020) to a complete classification covering all commonly used activation functions (ReLU, GELU, tanh, sigmoid, softplus, SiLU, LeakyReLU), including a newly discovered 'covert symmetry' for even+linear activations. It departs from prior model-extraction methods (Baum 1991; Jagielski et al. 2020; Rolnick & Kording 2020), which were restricted to ReLU networks or required knowledge of the teacher's architecture, by proposing a learning-based, clustering-driven recovery procedure that works for arbitrary activations and unknown layer widths. The algorithmic strategy of expanding the parameter space and then projecting back via clustering borrows from the Lovász-Schrijver lifting paradigm in applied mathematics, repurposed here for neural network identifiability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result has direct implications for model security (full parameter extraction enables stronger adversarial attacks on deployed services) and for neuroscience (a principled route toward reverse-engineering circuit connectivity from partial activity recordings). Key limitations include the small network sizes tested (up to 256 hidden neurons, 3 layers), the feedforward-only scope (no residual or attention layers analyzed), and the high computational cost of training N overparameterized students to near-zero loss. Open questions the authors flag include: what input queries are informative for identification, how many are sufficient, whether the method scales to modern architectures, and how to handle recurrent connectivity and noisy/partial observability in biological settings.
