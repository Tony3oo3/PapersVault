## Problem

Neural scaling laws exhibit power-law behavior in training time, model size, and compute, but the origin of the different exponents for time versus model size—and the resulting asymmetric compute-optimal strategy—remains unexplained by existing theory. Prior analytical works either study only the fully-trained (t→∞) limit or lack a solvable model that simultaneously tracks temporal dynamics, model size, and dataset size. This paper seeks a tractable model that reproduces the full landscape of scaling phenomena including overfitting accumulation, faster training for wider models, and the limited benefit of ensembling.

## Input / Output

The model assumes power-law structured features where eigenvalues decay as λ_k ~ k^{-b} and task-relevant variance decays as (w*_k)²λ_k ~ k^{-a}, with a and b extracted from empirical NTK spectra of real architectures. In the proportional limit (M, N, P → ∞ with fixed ratios α = P/M, ν = N/M), the DMFT yields exact closed-form expressions for test and train loss as functions of t, N, and P, from which bottleneck scalings and compute-optimal exponents are derived analytically. The mode-by-mode transfer functions H_k(t) additionally provide a spectral view of how each eigenfeature is learned over time, distinguishing early-time 1/N convergence from late-time power-law behavior.

## Method

The student model represents finite-width NTK features as a random Gaussian projection A ∈ R^{N×M} of infinite-width NTK eigenfunctions ψ(x), trained with gradient flow on MSE loss in a teacher-student setup. The authors derive a dynamical mean field theory by averaging over both the random projection A and the dataset simultaneously, yielding closed integro-differential equations for correlation functions C_i(t,s) and response functions R_i(t,s). Exploiting time-translation invariance of the response functions, they solve the self-consistent equations exactly in Fourier domain, obtaining the transfer function H_k(ω) = 1/(iω + λ_k R_1(ω)R_3(ω)) that governs learning of each eigenmode. For power-law features, this reveals that the time bottleneck exponent (a−1)/b differs from the model/data bottleneck exponent min{a−1, 2b}, producing an asymmetric compute-optimal strategy where training time scales as C^{bm/(a−1+bm)} and model size as C^{(a−1)/(a−1+bm)} with compute budget C = Nt. The framework extends naturally to discrete-time SGD with finite batch size and to ensembling, where it shows that ensembling reduces only variance while increasing N reduces both bias and variance.

## Results

The paper derives exact power-law scaling for test loss in a random-feature model: L(t,P,N) ≈ t^(-(a-1)/b) (time bottleneck), N^(-min{a-1,2b}) (model bottleneck), and P^(-min{a-1,2b}) (data bottleneck), where a is the task-power exponent and b is the spectral decay exponent. The compute-optimal strategy yields L*(C) ~ C^(-(a-1)/(1+b)) in the regime m=a-1, with training time scaling as t ~ C^(b(a-1)/(a-1+b(a-1))) and model size as N ~ C^((a-1)/(a-1+b(a-1))), confirming an asymmetric allocation where time grows faster than parameters. On CIFAR-5M with a Wide ResNet (b≈2.0, a≈1.15), the theory predicts L*(C) ~ C^(-0.05), matching empirical compute-optimal curves. The model also reproduces the 1/N early-time convergence rate and the gradual buildup of train-test gap as O(1/P) at early time with task-dependent scaling at late time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper without direct method-vs-method comparisons; instead, the DMFT predictions are validated against empirical scaling observations and prior theoretical results. The t→∞ limit of the model recovers the resolution-limited and variance-limited scalings of Bahri et al. (2021) and the static random-feature analyses of Maloney et al. (2022) and Atanasov et al. (2023), confirming consistency. The N→∞ limit recovers the online SGD learning curves of Bordelon & Pehlevan (2022a). The key new prediction—the asymmetric compute-optimal scaling where training time grows faster than model size (c_2 > c_1)—is consistent with the empirical observations of Hoffmann et al. (2022) for language models and the larger asymmetries in MLPs on vision from Bachmann et al. (2024). The theory also explains why ensembling E models of size N is inferior to a single model of size EN at fixed compute, matching the empirical finding of Vyas et al. (2023) that ensembling does not match the performance of wider models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of work: (1) static analyses of random-feature models with random projections that characterize final test loss as a function of model size N and dataset size P (Maloney et al. 2022; Atanasov et al. 2023), and (2) dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) for SGD on structured features that captures temporal learning dynamics (Bordelon & Pehlevan 2022a; Mignacco et al. 2020). The key departure is deriving a full temporal DMFT for the projected random-feature model, enabling simultaneous variation of time t, model size N, and dataset size P. This goes beyond prior static analyses (which only recover the t→∞ limit) and beyond prior DMFT work (which assumed infinite width or no random projection), yielding a solvable model that explains the asymmetry between time and model-size scaling exponents in compute-optimal training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic, solvable explanation for why compute-optimal scaling allocates more resources to training time than model size (the c2 > c1 asymmetry observed empirically by Hoffmann et al. 2022 and Bachmann et al. 2024), attributing it to different power-law exponents for the time bottleneck (governed by spectral decay b) versus the model-size bottleneck (governed by task power a). A significant limitation is that the model operates in the lazy/linearized regime; the authors explicitly show that feature-learning networks achieve substantially better compute-optimal scaling exponents, indicating that a full theory requires understanding kernel evolution during training. Open questions include extending the DMFT to capture feature learning dynamics and whether the predicted scaling exponents hold for architectures beyond the simple random-projection model.
