## Problem

Evaluating LLMs on standard benchmarks like MMLU (14K examples), HELM (10K), and Open LLM Leaderboard (29K) is computationally, financially, and environmentally prohibitive, especially when repeated across many checkpoints or prompting strategies. The paper asks whether a small curated subset of examples (e.g., 100 per scenario) can reliably estimate full-benchmark performance, and if so, how to select those examples and combine them with statistical models to minimize estimation error.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241396/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input the correctness scores (binary or bounded in [0,1]) of a new LLM on a small set of pre-selected anchor examples (typically 100 per scenario), along with pre-trained IRT item parameters (α_i, β_i) and a fitted ability parameter θ_l for the new model. It produces a single estimated performance score per scenario (accuracy or win rate), which is then aggregated (simple or weighted average) into a benchmark-level score. For non-binary scores (e.g., F1 in HELM, win rates in AlpacaEval), the method first binarizes via a scenario-specific threshold c chosen to preserve the mean, then applies the standard binary IRT pipeline.

## Method

The core contribution adapts two-parameter multidimensional Item Response Theory from psychometrics, treating LLMs as testees and benchmark examples as items, where P(Y_il=1|θ_l, α_i, β_i) = σ(α_i^T θ_l + β_i). IRT item parameters (α_i, β_i) serve as low-dimensional example representations for K-Means clustering to select anchor points, which are more robust to distribution shift than raw correctness-based clustering. The p-IRT estimator combines observed correctness on anchor examples with IRT-predicted probabilities on all remaining examples, weighted by the fraction of data observed (λ̂). The final gp-IRT estimator takes a convex combination of the raw weighted accuracy and p-IRT, with the mixing weight λ chosen via a bias-variance heuristic that increases with sample size and decreases with IRT model bias. The IRT model is fitted via variational inference with hierarchical Gaussian priors on θ_l, α_i, and β_i, and the latent dimensionality (2, 5, 10, or 15) is selected by validation-set prediction accuracy.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that 100 curated examples per scenario (via IRT-based anchor points + gp-IRT estimation) achieve an average performance estimation error of ~2% across all four benchmarks (Open LLM Leaderboard, MMLU, HELM, AlpacaEval 2.0), with worst-case error ≤4% on MMLU. This corresponds to a 140× reduction for MMLU (100 vs. 14K examples), 160× for Open LLM Leaderboard (180 vs. 29K), and ~10× for AlpacaEval 2.0 (100 vs. 805). The IRT++ (gp-IRT) strategy consistently outperforms stratified random sampling and correctness-based clustering, and remains robust under temporal distribution shifts (evaluating newer LLMs) and on specialized/domain-tuned models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three example-selection strategies (stratified random sampling from Perlitz et al. 2023, correctness-based clustering adapted from Vivek et al. 2023's Anchor Points, and IRT-based clustering) each in a vanilla and gp-IRT-enhanced ('++') form. IRT++ (gp-IRT with IRT-based anchors) is the best-performing strategy across all four benchmarks and both train-test splits (random and by-date), achieving ~2% average estimation error with 100 examples per scenario. Stratified random sampling (Perlitz et al.) consistently underperforms, requiring 400+ examples per task on Open LLM Leaderboard to match IRT++ at 100, and >400 on MMLU. Correctness-based clustering (Vivek et al.) performs comparably to IRT on general LLMs but deteriorates significantly on specialized/domain-tuned models (Figure 5), while IRT-based anchors remain robust. The gp-IRT ('++') enhancement always improves or matches its vanilla counterpart at negligible additional runtime (a few seconds on CPU), confirming that the IRT model's ability to predict correctness on unseen examples provides meaningful signal beyond raw accuracy on the sampled subset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on two main threads: (1) the Anchor Points method (Vivek et al., 2023), which clusters examples by model correctness patterns to find representative subsets, and (2) Item Response Theory (Lord et al., 1968), a psychometric framework previously applied to NLP for ranking models (Lalor et al., 2016; Rodriguez et al., 2021) and adaptive testing (Zhuang et al., 2023) but not for performance estimation. The key departure is using IRT-derived example representations (difficulty + ability vectors) as embeddings for clustering, which the authors show is more robust to distribution shift than raw correctness-based clustering. The stratified random sampling baseline follows Perlitz et al. (2023), and the gp-IRT estimator is a novel convex combination of direct measurement and IRT-based prediction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: reducing MMLU evaluation from 14K to 100 examples (140× cost reduction) and Open LLM Leaderboard from 29K to 180 examples (160×) makes frequent evaluation during pre-training, fine-tuning, and prompt engineering far more accessible. The released tinyBenchmarks and pre-trained IRT tools lower the barrier for smaller labs. Key limitations include sensitivity to severe distribution shifts (e.g., models that fail easy questions but solve hard ones) and the need for periodic re-fitting as model capabilities evolve. Open questions include whether adaptive IRT testing can further reduce example counts, and whether the approach generalizes to non-text modalities or rapidly evolving benchmarks.
