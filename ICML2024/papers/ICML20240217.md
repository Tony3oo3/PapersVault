## Problem

Imitation learning from imperfect demonstrations is fundamentally bounded by the quality of the available data: existing methods that reweight suboptimal trajectories can at best match the best demonstration but cannot exceed it. While prior work has shown that additional human-provided information (rankings, confidence scores) can break this ceiling, such information is costly and cognitively demanding for humans to produce. The paper asks whether a much lighter-weight signal—pairwise preference comparisons between trajectory segments—can suffice to both identify high-quality demonstrations and enable the learned policy to surpass them.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small set of imperfect demonstration trajectories (10 for Mujoco/DMC tasks, 600 for GridWorld) of mixed quality, plus a limited budget of human preference queries (as few as 2, typically 60) that compare pairs of trajectory segments (length 100 for GridWorld/Mujoco, 50 for DMC). Preferences are elicited from a scripted teacher that ranks segments by oracle reward, simulating a human expert. The output is a stochastic policy π_φ (implemented via SAC) whose normalized return exceeds the best trajectory in the initial demonstration set in 16 of 18 task-dataset combinations, with an average 73.2% improvement over the strongest baseline.

## Method

PAIL operates in three coupled stages. First, it trains an ensemble of three preference reward networks via the Bradley-Terry model on the limited preference queries. Second, it solves an entropy-regularized optimization over trajectory weights, yielding a closed-form softmax reweighting ρ̃(τ) ∝ exp(r^p(τ)/β) that upweights high-preference-return demonstrations; the reweighted dataset is then imitated via maximum causal entropy IRL with a DAC-style discriminator updated from the SAC replay buffer. Third, every N^aug iterations, PAIL samples m trajectories from the most recent M agent experiences using a similar softmax over preference returns (with a separate temperature β^aug), appends them to the demonstration set, and refines the preference reward via disagreement sampling on the expanded dataset. The key design choice is that the preference reward is never used directly as an RL reward signal—instead it only shapes which demonstrations to imitate—keeping the hypothesis space small enough that even 60 queries yield accurate reward estimates (Pearson correlation ≥ 0.8 with oracle reward within the demonstration set). A theoretical value-gap bound (Theorem 4.3) decomposes the imitation error into a quality term (controlled by β and the preference gap between high- and low-quality trajectories), a consistency term (variance of preference returns within the high-quality set), and standard estimation/optimization terms.

## Results

With 60 preference queries, PAIL achieves an average normalized return of 0.86 on Mujoco tasks and 0.89 on DMC tasks, surpassing the best baselines (SAIL-SAC at 0.49 on Mujoco, AILP at 0.55 on DMC) by 73.2% on average. PAIL outperforms the best trajectory in the initial imperfect demonstration dataset in 16 out of 18 'Task & Dataset' combinations. On the synthetic GridWorld task, PAIL reaches an 87.24% arrival rate versus 58.40% for the imperfect demonstrations and 45.82% for MCE-IRL, while maintaining a low 1.66% boundary-violation rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PAIL decisively outperforms all six baselines across nearly all tasks. Against MCE-IRL (the standard IRL baseline), PAIL improves average Mujoco return from 0.48 to 0.86 and DMC from 0.41 to 0.89. Against the IL-from-imperfection methods SAIL-TRPO and SAIL-SAC, PAIL gains 0.66 and 0.37 on Mujoco averages respectively, with SAIL-TRPO performing particularly poorly (0.20 avg). Against the PBRL baselines PEBBLE and BC-PEBBLE, PAIL achieves 0.86 vs. 0.26/0.36 on Mujoco and 0.89 vs. 0.24/0.27 on DMC; notably, PEBBLE and BC-PEBBLE struggle severely on Humanoid (≈0.04) and Walker2d (≈0.05) where PAIL reaches 0.95–1.04 and 0.48–0.90. Against AILP (the closest competitor combining AIL with preferences), PAIL leads by 0.54 on Mujoco and 0.34 on DMC averages. Even when preference queries are increased to 1400 (Table 3), PAIL maintains the top score in 16/18 task-dataset combinations, though BC-PEBBLE narrows the gap on DMC (0.82 vs. 0.91). In the additional comparison with SSRR (Chen et al., 2020), PAIL outperforms SSRR on all Mujoco tasks, where SSRR sometimes falls below the worst demonstration return.

## Relation to Prior Work

PAIL sits at the intersection of imitation learning from imperfect demonstrations (SAIL, DREX) and preference-based RL (PEBBLE, BC-PEBBLE), but departs from both in a key way: rather than using the preference reward as a direct RL reward signal (as PBRL does) or relying on the IRL discriminator to implicitly weight demonstrations (as SAIL does), PAIL uses a Bradley-Terry preference reward solely to reweight demonstration trajectories for maximum causal entropy IRL, then augments the demonstration set with explored trajectories. This builds on MCE-IRL (Ziebart et al., 2008) for the IRL objective and DAC (Kostrikov et al., 2019) for sample-efficient discriminator updates, but the reweighting-plus-augmentation pipeline is a novel combination. The paper also distinguishes itself from CAIL (Zhang et al., 2021) and DREX (Brown et al., 2019b), which require fully ranked or confidence-scored demonstrations, by using only pairwise preference queries that are more human-friendly to elicit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is the dramatic reduction in required human feedback: PAIL achieves strong performance with as few as 60 preference queries (and meaningful gains with just 2), whereas PBRL methods like PEBBLE require 1400+ queries to be competitive. The clean ablation separating reweighting (converges to best demonstration) from augmentation (breaks through the demonstration ceiling) provides a clear mechanistic understanding of how each component contributes. Key limitations include the preference reward's poor extrapolation outside the demonstration distribution (Pearson correlation drops from 0.91 to ~0.65 on replay buffers), inability to handle trajectories with mixed good and bad segments, and reliance on a scripted teacher in experiments. Open questions include robustness to genuinely noisy human preferences, segment-level scoring, and whether the augmentation mechanism generalizes to tasks where the environment is not easily explorable.
