## Problem

Interventions on Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) are currently one-time tools: a human corrects concept values for a single sample, the prediction is updated, and the intervention is discarded. This is inefficient because the correction encodes where the bottleneck network systematically fails, information that could be reused to automatically fix similar future mistakes without additional human feedback. The paper asks whether interventions can be generalized to unseen examples and whether a model can proactively detect its own bottleneck errors to request targeted human corrections.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (e.g., 200-class bird images from CUB, handwritten digits from MNIST/SVHN/C-MNIST) and human-provided concept interventions (sets of corrected concept-value/index tuples) as input. It produces the standard CBM outputs (concept activations and class predictions) augmented with a mistake-detection signal (a binary flag indicating whether the current sample resembles a known bottleneck error) and an automatically reapplied intervention that overwrites specific concept values before the predictor runs. The memory operates on bottleneck encodings (last-layer activations), so no additional model parameters are trained.

## Method

CB2M extends a standard CBM (unchanged bottleneck g and predictor f) with a two-fold memory: a mistake memory storing bottleneck encodings of past errors and an intervention memory storing the corresponding corrective interventions, linked via an association function α. For a new sample, the bottleneck encoding is compared via Euclidean distance to memorized mistake encodings; if k nearest mistakes fall within a threshold t_d, the model flags a likely error (detection mode) or applies the nearest memorized intervention to the new sample (generalization mode). The threshold t_d is selected on a training set to maximize the number of generalized interventions subject to the constraint that dataset accuracy after applying memorized interventions is never worse than without them. The memory is initialized from validation-set mistakes with simulated interventions using ground-truth concept labels, and grows incrementally during deployment as new human interventions arrive—requiring no gradient updates or retraining of the underlying CBM.

## Results

On intervention generalization (Table 1), CB2M substantially outperforms the base CBM on identified instances across all datasets: CUB (Aug.) class accuracy jumps from 5.0% to 88.7%, Parity MNIST (unbalanced) from 22.5% to 93.7%, and Parity C-MNIST from 20.1% to 85.9%. On the full test set, CB2M achieves 94.0% class accuracy on Parity MNIST (unbalanced) vs. 91.2% for CBM, and 74.9% on Parity C-MNIST vs. 68.6% for CBM. For mistake detection (Table 2), CB2M achieves AUROC of 85.1 on confounded CUB and 83.4 on Parity C-MNIST, substantially outperforming the softmax baseline (77.4 and 65.7 respectively), while performing on par with softmax on non-confounded CUB (84.8 vs. 83.7).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the base CBM (Koh et al., 2020), CB2M wins decisively on identified error instances across all three main datasets, improving class accuracy by 83.7 points on CUB (Aug.), 71.2 points on Parity MNIST (unbalanced), and 65.8 points on Parity C-MNIST. On the full test set, CB2M outperforms CBM on Parity MNIST (unbalanced) (94.0% vs. 91.2%) and Parity C-MNIST (74.9% vs. 68.6%), but falls slightly short of the finetuned CBM on CUB (69.1% vs. 74.7%) and Parity C-MNIST (74.9% vs. 88.1%), where extended finetuning is more effective. For mistake detection, CB2M (using only bottleneck encodings) ties or slightly beats the softmax baseline (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2017) on non-confounded CUB (AUROC 84.8 vs. 83.7) but loses slightly on Parity MNIST (unbalanced) (88.7 vs. 90.7). However, on confounded datasets where the predictor is misled by spurious correlations, CB2M substantially outperforms softmax: 85.1 vs. 77.4 AUROC on CUB (conf) and 83.4 vs. 65.7 on Parity C-MNIST, demonstrating that bottleneck-level error detection is more targeted for intervention-based correction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) from Koh et al. (2020) by adding a two-fold memory module (mistake memory + intervention memory) that transforms one-time, single-sample interventions into reusable, generalizable corrections. While prior interactive CBM work (Shin et al., 2023; Chauhan et al., 2022; Sheth et al., 2022) focused on ordering or selecting which concepts to intervene on, CB2M addresses the orthogonal question of *when* and *where* to reapply previously collected interventions. The mistake detection component draws on nearest-neighbor similarity in bottleneck encoding space, positioning itself between general uncertainty estimation (softmax, NUC) and CBM-specific error analysis, but targeting bottleneck errors specifically rather than end-to-end model errors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CB2M addresses a practical bottleneck in interactive concept learning: the high cost of repeated human interventions for systematic model errors. By making interventions reusable and enabling targeted mistake detection, it reduces the human feedback burden while maintaining the interpretability benefits of CBMs. Key limitations include the assumption of correct human feedback, potential memory scalability issues, and reduced effectiveness on non-systematic (outlier) errors. Open questions include differentiable memory instantiation, integration with continual learning, and combining CB2M with other CBM variants (post-hoc, probabilistic, tabular).
