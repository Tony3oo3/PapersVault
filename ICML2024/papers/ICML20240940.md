## Problem

Classification models often exhibit large per-class performance gaps even on sample-balanced datasets, yet most prior work on class bias focuses exclusively on sample imbalance. This paper identifies spectral imbalance—differences in the eigenvalue distributions of class-conditional covariance matrices in the representation space—as a previously uncharacterized source of class disparities, particularly relevant for pretrained and foundation models whose latent features may carry hidden biases that affect downstream task performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240940/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on latent feature vectors produced by a frozen pretrained encoder (e.g., ResNet-50, ViT-B) applied to RGB images, along with their class labels. From these features, it estimates per-class covariance matrices and extracts their eigenspectra. The primary outputs are: (1) exact asymptotic per-class error predictions from a 12-scalar min-max program, (2) a Spectral Quantile Score (SQS = s_L/s_1) that diagnoses encoder-level class bias without training a classifier, and (3) an ensemble prediction that adaptively re-weights logits from K augmented views per class at test time, requiring no retraining of the encoder or classifier.

## Method

The core theoretical contribution derives exact asymptotic per-class error expressions for empirical risk minimization in a high-dimensional Gaussian mixture model with class-specific diagonal covariances (Theorem 1). The proof uses the Convex Gaussian Min-Max Theorem with a key decoupling trick: the data from each class is separately whitened, introducing per-class decision variables that are linked through a second-order Moreau envelope term, yielding a tractable 12-scalar min-max problem solvable by gradient descent/ascent. Empirically, the paper fits power-law decay (λ_i = ai^{-b}) to class-conditional spectra across 11 pretrained encoders on ImageNet, finding that the scale parameter a (not the decay rate b) strongly correlates with per-class accuracy (average PCC ≈ −0.68). The proposed Spectral Quantile Score, computed from training-set spectra alone, predicts empirical class bias with 0.90 PCC across encoders. Finally, a test-time ensemble method selects, for each class, the augmentation view with the smallest spectral offset and uses only that view's logit, improving average accuracy by 2.55% and reducing class gap by 3.52% without any retraining.

## Results

Across 11 pretrained encoders on ImageNet, the power law offset parameter *a* of the class-conditional eigenspectrum correlates with per-class accuracy at an average Pearson correlation of −0.68, while the decay rate *b* shows near-zero correlation (−0.0002). The proposed Spectral Quantile Score (SQS) predicts empirical class bias with 0.90 PCC across encoders. The spectral re-factoring ensemble method combines two augmentations (e.g., Zoom Blur 65.01% and Snow 65.12%) to achieve 67.62% accuracy, yielding a 2.55% average improvement and 3.52% class-gap reduction without re-training; across 240 augmentation-pair runs, the method improves the worse augmentation by 5.9% on average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new classifier to benchmark against existing methods; instead, it compares spectral metrics as predictors of class bias and compares encoders as sources of spectral imbalance. Among spectral metrics, the absolute-scale measures (individual eigenvalues, power law offset *a*) consistently outperform decay-rate measures (power law slope *b*, effective ranks) in correlating with per-class accuracy: the offset *a* achieves −0.6762 average PCC versus −0.0002 for slope *b*, confirming that the magnitude of the spectrum matters far more than its decay shape. Across the 11 encoders, MLP-Mixer exhibits the strongest spectral-accuracy correlation (−0.7785) while ResNet-18 is the weakest (−0.6229), and the SQS of an encoder predicts its empirical class bias at 0.90 PCC. For the augmentation ensemble, the method beats both individual augmentations: combining Zoom Blur (65.01%) and Snow (65.12%) yields 67.62%, a 2.55% gain over the better single augmentation and 5.9% over the worse one on average across 240 runs. A one-sided Welch's t-test confirms that classes with higher spectral offset *a* have significantly lower accuracy for 98/110 encoder pairs at 95% confidence and 80/110 at 99% confidence, validating spectral properties as reliable predictors of per-class performance for unseen encoders.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the high-dimensional GMM analysis of Taheri et al. (2020) and the CGMT framework of Thrampoulidis et al. (2018) to the previously unexplored setting of class-specific covariances, deriving exact per-class error expressions via a decoupling trick and second-order Moreau envelope. It connects to the benign overfitting literature (Bartlett et al., 2020; Muthukumar et al., 2021) that established the role of data spectrum in generalization, but shifts focus from overall spectrum to *differences* in spectrum across classes. Empirically, it complements the class-bias observations of Balestriero et al. (2022) and Kirichenko et al. (2023) by identifying a precise, quantitative spectral mechanism (rather than the less formal 'fine-grainedness' hypothesis) and relates to representation-quality work (Ghosh et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2022) by reframing power-law spectral properties through the lens of inter-class disparity rather than aggregate quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Spectral Quantile Score provides a practical, training-free diagnostic for evaluating pretrained encoders' class bias (0.90 PCC with empirical class gap), which is valuable given the widespread deployment of foundation models where per-class failures may go undetected. The no-retraining ensemble method for combining augmentations offers an immediately deployable mitigation strategy. Key limitations include the linear-model assumption in theory and the reliance on pretrained encoders in experiments; the paper explicitly leaves open the question of *why* neural network training produces spectral imbalance in the first place, which would be needed for principled mitigation at the training stage rather than post-hoc correction.
