## Problem

Most causal inference methods reduce confounding bias by balancing treated and control group distributions, but the choice of distribution discrepancy metric (moments, IPMs, etc.) remains ad hoc and under-theorized. This paper asks whether there exists a generic balancing error that can be principledly connected to an optimizable transport metric, and whether the underlying cost function in that metric can itself be learned to further reduce the error.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242305/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular observational data: d-dimensional covariate vectors, a binary treatment indicator, and a scalar observational outcome for each of n samples (split into n_t treated and n_c control). It outputs (i) sample weights for each group derived from the row sums of the optimal transport matrices, (ii) a d×d′ projection matrix P constrained to the Stiefel manifold (P⊤P=I) that defines a learned representation subspace, and (iii) a point estimate of ATE computed as the weighted difference of group outcomes. The weights are not normalized to sum to one per group but are used directly in the ATE estimator.

## Method

The paper defines a generic balancing error err_m^w on reweighted samples and proves (Theorem 4.1) via Kantorovich duality that this error is upper-bounded by the sum of two Wasserstein discrepancies between each reweighted group and the full population, with the bound tight under standard regularity conditions. Motivated by this connection, the method jointly learns (a) marginal distributions (sample weights) and (b) a cost function D(·,·;φ) implemented as squared Euclidean distance in a learned subspace defined by a Stiefel-constrained projection matrix P. The cost function is guided by factual outcomes through an intra-group regularization term that penalizes large transport costs between samples with similar observed outcomes, effectively encoding the assumption that close outcomes imply close potential outcomes. The resulting non-convex problem is solved by alternating between a closed-form eigendecomposition update for P (smallest d′ eigenvectors of a composite matrix) and a projected mirror descent update for the transport matrices using KL divergence as the Bregman divergence, with entropic regularization on the marginal distributions to prevent sparse transport plans.

## Results

On simulation data, OICL achieves the lowest MAE across all 8 settings (4 Gaussian, 4 Non-Gaussian), with headline values of 0.0272 (Gaussian, μc=0.6) and 0.0138 (Non-Gaussian, αc=0.4), outperforming the next-best method N-CBIPM (0.0360 and 0.0301 respectively). On real-world data, OICL achieves MAE of 0.1674 on Twins, 146.34 on LaLonde, and 0.0698 on IHDP, all the best among 12 baselines. The method also demonstrates strong sample efficiency, with weights converging at an n^{-1/d} rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OICL outperforms all 12 baselines across every simulation and real-world setting. Against the strongest IPM-based competitor N-CBIPM, OICL reduces MAE by ~24% on Gaussian (μc=0.6: 0.0272 vs 0.0360) and ~54% on Non-Gaussian (αc=0.4: 0.0138 vs 0.0301). Against the closest optimal-transport baseline OTW, OICL achieves a ~67% MAE reduction on Gaussian (μc=0.6: 0.0272 vs 0.0811), demonstrating that learning the cost function guided by factual outcomes is critical beyond vanilla OT. Moment-based methods (EBAL) and propensity-score methods (IPW, DR, CBPS, ARB) are consistently weaker, with EBAL(2) the best among them at 0.0882 vs OICL's 0.0272 on Gaussian μc=0.6. On real-world LaLonde, OICL (146.34) beats N-CBIPM (179.50) by ~19% and DKLITE (335.96) by ~56%; on IHDP, OICL (0.0698) leads N-CBIPM (0.0864) by ~19%. The deep kernel method DKLITE is competitive on Non-Gaussian settings but degrades sharply on Gaussian with larger confounding (μc=1.0: 0.1761 vs OICL's 0.1125).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of optimal-transport-based causal inference initiated by Dunipace (2021), who used Sinkhorn divergence to learn balancing weights, by providing a theoretical bridge (via Kantorovich duality) between a generic balancing error and the Wasserstein discrepancy. It departs from prior OT-for-causal-inference work (Gunsilius & Xu, Torous et al., Li et al.) by learning the underlying cost function rather than fixing it to Euclidean distance, and by unifying reweighting and representation learning into a single OT objective. The entropic regularization draws on Cuturi (2013), while the balancing error definition is inspired by Hainmueller's (2012) entropy balancing framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical connection (Theorem 4.1) gives a principled justification for why minimizing Wasserstein distance reduces confounding bias, moving OT-based causal inference beyond purely empirical motivation. The cost-function learning guided by factual outcomes is a natural but previously unexplored degree of freedom that could be extended to other balancing error definitions (e.g., propensity-score-based). Limitations include reliance on the strong ignorability assumption, the cost function remaining squared Euclidean in a learned subspace (not fully general), and evaluation limited to ATE on relatively small tabular datasets. Open questions include scalability to high-dimensional or continuous-treatment settings and whether the n^{-1/d} convergence rate is achievable in practice with the entropic relaxation.
