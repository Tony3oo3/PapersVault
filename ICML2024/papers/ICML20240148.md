## Problem

Existing counterfactual explanation methods either find diverse CEs for a single instance or a single group-level CE for a set, but do not unify both perspectives in a single interpretable representation. Prior rule-based approaches (AReS, LORE, RF-OCSE) are limited to binary classification, rely on arbitrary feature binning, and suffer from runtimes that are orders of magnitude slower than necessary for practical deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240148/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a dataset of realistically-distributed inputs with black-box model outputs, a target output set Y*, and thresholds τ (accuracy) and ρ (feasibility). It produces axis-aligned hyperrectangular counterfactual rules (defined by per-feature lower/upper bounds) paired with metarules that partition the input space into regions where each rule is optimal. A key structural property is that the global rule/metarule structure is learned once (front-loaded), after which local CEs for arbitrary instances are generated via near-instantaneous tree traversal. One-hot encoded categorical features are handled with well-formedness constraints that restrict each categorical to either a single hot category or up to D_c−1 cold categories.

## Method

T-CREx grows a tree-based surrogate (single decision tree or random forest) whose nodes and leaves yield candidate hyperrectangular rules, then identifies maximal-valid rules satisfying accuracy ≥ τ and feasibility ≥ ρ. The core insight is that under the sparsity-minus-feasibility cost function, the optimal rule is constant within each cell of a grid formed by all rule boundaries, making every grid cell a valid metarule. A secondary CART classifier is then grown over cell prototypes (constrained to split only at rule boundaries) to merge adjacent cells sharing the same optimal rule into approximately maximal metarules. This two-tree architecture—surrogate for rule discovery, secondary tree for metarule aggregation—is the key architectural novelty. The resulting metarule tree enables both a global textual summary of all recourse options and O(log N) local CE generation for any new instance.

## Results

T-CREx_0.99 returns the most accurate counterfactual rules on 8 out of 9 binary classification datasets, while T-CREx_0.9 achieves the highest feasibility on all 9 datasets and the best rule complexity on 7 of 9. On consistency (fraction of unique rules returned), T-CREx outperforms all baselines on 8/9 datasets, with consistencies of 10⁻¹ to 10⁻³ indicating the same rule is returned for tens to hundreds of test instances. The most striking result is runtime: the total time for all test inputs across all CV folds and all datasets is 4.16 s (T-CREx_0.9) and 4.52 s (T-CREx_0.99), which is 3–4 orders of magnitude faster than the slowest baseline and 13× less than AReS's mean per-input runtime on HELOC alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AReS (Rawal & Lakkaraju, 2020), the closest competitor in learning group-level rule-based CEs, T-CREx wins on feasibility (all 9 datasets vs. AReS), complexity (7/9), and consistency (8/9), while AReS narrowly wins on accuracy for Pima and is competitive on sparsity. The decisive advantage is runtime: T-CREx is 3–4 orders of magnitude faster, with AReS's mean per-input runtime on HELOC alone exceeding T-CREx's total runtime across all datasets and folds by 13×. Against LORE (Guidotti et al., 2019), T-CREx outperforms on accuracy, feasibility, complexity, and consistency across nearly all datasets; LORE's lack of any aggregation means its consistency scores below 1 are purely coincidental. Against RF-OCSE (Fernández et al., 2020), T-CREx dominates on feasibility (RF-OCSE frequently returns rules containing zero test instances), complexity, and consistency, and is at least an order of magnitude faster despite RF-OCSE's authors highlighting speed as a key advantage. On counterfactual distance (a metric T-CREx does not explicitly optimise), RF-OCSE is strongest in most cases, but T-CREx_0.9 still outperforms AReS and LORE on 7/9 datasets and actually achieves the best distance on 3 datasets, suggesting that its feasibility-driven optimisation indirectly encourages rules occupying more input-space volume.

## Relation to Prior Work

T-CREx unifies two previously separate generalizations of counterfactual explanation: group-level CEs (AReS, Kanamori et al., Ley et al.) and diverse CEs for single instances (Mothilal et al., Rodriguez et al.), by introducing a joint rule/metarule formulation where rules denote regions of target output and metarules denote regions of rule optimality. The closest prior method, AReS (Rawal & Lakkaraju, 2020), also learns rules paired with subgroup descriptors, but relies on arbitrary binning and is restricted to binary classification; T-CREx instead extracts adaptive split thresholds from tree-based surrogates, is model-agnostic, and handles multi-class and regression settings. The use of a secondary CART tree to aggregate grid cells into approximately maximal metarules is entirely novel and distinguishes the method from LORE and RF-OCSE, which produce per-instance rules without any global optimality structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3–4 order-of-magnitude speedup over all baselines (total runtime of ~4s across all datasets and folds) makes rule-based CE practically deployable at scale, and the front-loaded computation model (learn once, explain instantly) is well-suited to production settings. Key limitations include the fixed sparsity-minus-feasibility cost function (no actionability constraints, so it can recommend changing age), exponential scaling with input dimensionality, and the open question of optimal surrogate tree count. The metacounterfactual reasoning demonstrated in the qualitative analysis—where a small input change (age 45→55) flips which rule is optimal—suggests a promising but underexplored direction for understanding explanation robustness.
