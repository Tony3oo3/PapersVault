## Problem

Existing iterative algorithms for the densest subgraph problem (DSG) suffer from iteration complexities that scale with the maximum degree Δ of the graph, making them prohibitive for high-degree graphs, while flow-based exact algorithms are inherently sequential and do not scale to very large graphs. Additionally, no practical iterative algorithm with a logarithmic (rather than polynomial) dependence on the approximation error ε existed for DSG or its generalization to dense subgraph decomposition, and prior MWU and area-convexity approaches either required complex rounding procedures with binary search or could only output the solution value rather than the dense subgraph itself.

## Input / Output

The algorithms take an undirected unweighted graph G=(V,E) with n vertices and m edges as input. The primary output is a subgraph S⊆V with density |E(S)|/|S| within a (1−ε) factor of optimal, recovered via Charikar's sorting-and-prefix rounding from a fractional LP solution. The secondary output for the decomposition variant is a partition S₁∪…∪S_k where each S_i is the maximal set maximizing marginal density, obtained by applying Harb et al.'s fractional peeling procedure to the solution of a quadratic program over base contrapolymatroids.

## Method

The paper presents three algorithms. First, an MWU-based algorithm with O(log m/ε²) iterations and O(m log Δ) per iteration that directly solves the inner LP (rather than parametrizing D and using binary search as in Bahmani et al.), enabling complementary slackness to recover a primal solution and Charikar's simple rounding; it uses the width-reduced dual with q=1 instead of q=2. Second, an area-convexity algorithm with O(log m/ε) iterations that replaces the entropy regularizer with a quadratic-in-z area-convex regularizer, reducing the LP width from Δ to 2 and improving over Boob et al. by a factor Δ while also constructing the integral solution. Third, an accelerated random coordinate descent algorithm with O(mn log(1/ε)) total time that adapts Ene & Nguyen's framework for decomposable submodular minimization to the base contrapolymatroid of supermodular functions, achieving linear convergence despite the absence of strong convexity by proving a restricted strong convexity property (ℓ* ≥ 4/n²) that exploits the combinatorial structure of the problem.

## Results

The paper provides three algorithmic contributions with distinct theoretical guarantees: (1) an MWU-based algorithm with O(log m/ε²) iterations at O(m log Δ) per iteration, (2) an area-convexity algorithm with O(log m/ε) iterations at O(m log Δ log(1/ε)) per iteration, and (3) an accelerated random coordinate descent algorithm with total expected runtime O(mn log(n/ε)) for dense subgraph decomposition. In experiments on 8 real-world graphs (up to 3M vertices and 117M edges, e.g., orkut), Algorithms 5 and 7 (accelerated and non-accelerated coordinate descent) outperform FISTA (Harb et al., 2022) on the load norm objective and are competitive with Greedy++ in both iterations and wall-clock time, while Frank-Wolfe is excluded from plots due to significantly worse performance across all instances.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Bahmani et al. (2014), the MWU algorithm (Algorithm 1) matches the O(log m/ε²) iteration complexity but eliminates the binary search and replaces the complex discretization/line-sweep rounding with a simple Charikar-style rounding, at the cost of O(m log Δ) vs O(m) per iteration. Against Boob et al. (2019), the area-convexity algorithm (Algorithm 3) improves the iteration count by a factor of Δ (the maximum degree), going from O(Δ log m/ε) to O(log m/ε), and additionally constructs the primal solution (dense subgraph) rather than only the solution value. Against the FISTA-based algorithm of Harb et al. (2022), the accelerated coordinate descent (Algorithm 5) achieves an exponentially better dependence on 1/ε (log(n/ε) vs 1/ε), reducing total time from O(m√(mnΔ)/ε) to O(mn log(n/ε)) in expectation; in the high-precision regime ε ≪ 1/n this matches the O(mn log n) runtime of the exact parametric flow algorithm of Gallo et al. (1989). In empirical comparisons, Algorithms 5 and 7 significantly outperform FISTA on the load-norm objective across all 8 datasets, are competitive with Greedy++ in both iteration count and wall-clock time (especially with Greedy-peeling initialization), and scale to graphs with over 3 million vertices and 100 million edges. Frank-Wolfe (Danisch et al., 2017) is consistently the worst performer and is excluded from most experimental plots.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three algorithmic lineages for densest subgraph problems: (1) the multiplicative weights update framework of Bahmani et al. (2014), from which it inherits the width-reduction technique but replaces the complex binary-search-and-rounding pipeline with a direct LP solve and a simple complementary-slackness-based primal construction; (2) the area convexity framework of Sherman (2017) and Boob et al. (2019), where it improves iteration complexity by a factor Δ through a different regularizer choice and the same width reduction; and (3) the accelerated random coordinate descent of Ene & Nguyen (2015) for decomposable submodular minimization, which it adapts to the dense subgraph decomposition quadratic program by showing the base contrapolymatroid structure yields restricted strong convexity. The paper also builds on Harb et al. (2022) for the fractional peeling rounding procedure and the quadratic program formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical contribution is the first iterative algorithm with linear convergence (O(log 1/ε) iterations) for dense subgraph decomposition, matching the exact parametric-flow algorithm of Gallo et al. (1989) in the high-precision regime while being far simpler to implement and parallelize. The MWU and area convexity algorithms, while less practical, provide the fastest known parallelizable theoretical runtimes for approximate DSG, matching flow-based algorithms in total time without the sequential bottleneck. Open questions include extending the MWU algorithm to streaming and distributed settings, applying the framework to differentially private DSG, and whether the restricted strong convexity argument generalizes to other structured convex programs beyond the contrapolymatroid setting.
