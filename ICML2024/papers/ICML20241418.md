## Problem

FANOVA regression decomposes a target function into main effects and interaction terms, but identifying which terms are relevant and characterizing their shapes remains challenging. Two disconnected research communities have addressed this—deterministic penalized methods (COSSO) and Bayesian non-parametric additive models—yet no unified framework connects them, leaving deterministic methods without uncertainty quantification and Bayesian methods without a principled link to the well-studied COSSO solution. Additionally, existing models offer limited flexibility in handling multi-level sparsity simultaneously across interaction orders, within orders, and among covariates.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N observations of D-dimensional covariate vectors and scalar outputs, with covariates assumed to lie in [0,1] (required by the Sobolev kernel). It produces a predictive GP distribution over the latent regression function with mean and covariance, identifies active functional components via variance analysis (minimal set achieving 99% of total variance), and provides 95% credible intervals for uncertainty quantification. Component-level predictive GPs are also available, enabling per-term uncertainty assessment. The model operates under orthogonal kernel constraints (Sobolev or constrained RBF), which preclude standard kernel interpolation tricks and require the structured decomposition into stationary plus linear parts.

## Method

The core model is a hierarchical GP where each functional component f_cj follows GP(0, λ_i θ_cj k_cj), with global scales λ_i controlling energy per interaction order and local scales θ_cj controlling within-order sparsity. The key novelty is a Dirichlet mixing prior on local scales within each order, which enforces a sum-to-one constraint and provides discrete-like shrinkage (most components near zero) while remaining continuous and amenable to gradient-based inference; the concentration parameter w_i directly approximates the expected fraction of relevant components. A theoretical result (Proposition 3.1) proves that the marginal MAP of this model with exponential mixing and equal global scales recovers the COSSO solution, establishing a Bayesian derivation of the deterministic method. For inference, the paper uses a fully marginalized scheme (integrating out functional components) with either plug-in MAP estimation or HMC sampling, and extends kernel interpolation (SKI) to orthogonal kernels by exploiting their sum-of-stationary-plus-linear structure.

## Results

On simulated data (210 runs across noise and correlation levels), the Dirichlet model achieves the best relative F1 score (fewest false positives) while maintaining competitive relative RMSE compared to SS-ANOVA. On UCI datasets (5-fold CV, r=2), sparse Bayesian models reduce the number of active components (NAC) by roughly half relative to SS-ANOVA while achieving comparable or lower RMSE; for example, on the Housing dataset, Dirichlet models halve NAC with similar RMSE. In the covariate selection experiment (D=30, 7 relevant components out of 465), soft shrinkage achieves RMSE 1.46±0.12 with only 18.81±11.35 false positives, versus 60.30±10.23 false positives without covariate selection (RMSE 1.66±0.12). Plug-in (MAP) consistently yields the best F1 score, while HMC sampling provides more accurate 95% credible interval coverage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SS-ANOVA (the baseline), all proposed Bayesian models achieve lower relative RMSE and higher relative F1 on simulated data, with the Dirichlet model showing the strongest shrinkage (fewest false positives). Compared to COSSO, the Bayesian COSSO (Exponential mixing) outperforms it particularly in the r=2 setting because its adaptive global scale per order avoids including fictitious interaction components; on the Demand UCI dataset, COSSO includes spurious interactions that Bayesian COSSO correctly suppresses. Against ACOSSO, the adaptive Bayesian variants (ABCOS, ADir) improve results when SS-ANOVA provides accurate initial weights, but can worsen estimates when the SS-ANOVA solution is poor; the non-separable Dirichlet mixing is presented as a more robust alternative to this adaptive strategy. Versus RBF-OK (Lu et al., 2022), the proposed models achieve comparable RMSE on UCI data but select significantly fewer active components, demonstrating the benefit of heterogeneous local scales. Against MARS, the Bayesian FANOVA models achieve competitive RMSE on UCI regression tasks while additionally providing uncertainty quantification (credible intervals) that MARS cannot offer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends finite-dimensional Bayesian global-local shrinkage models (Tang et al., 2018) to an infinite-dimensional GP setting for FANOVA decomposition, while simultaneously proving that the deterministic COSSO solution (Lin & Zhang, 2003) is the marginal MAP estimate of a hierarchical GP with exponential local mixing. It unifies previously disconnected research threads—kernel-based penalized methods (COSSO, SS-ANOVA) and Bayesian non-parametric additive models (Scheipl et al., 2012; Lu et al., 2022; Durrande et al., 2012)—into a single equation, and introduces a novel Dirichlet non-separable mixing prior that approximates discrete shrinkage while remaining continuous. The orthogonal kernel structure from Durrande et al. (2012) and Lu et al. (2022) is retained for identifiability, with a new extension of kernel interpolation tricks to these structured kernels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides Bayesian uncertainty quantification for COSSO-like deterministic methods, filling a gap that has persisted because the two communities had no explicit connection. The Dirichlet mixing offers an intuitive single-hyperparameter tuning knob (concentration ≈ expected fraction of relevant components), which is practically useful for dimensionality reduction. Key limitations include the orthogonality assumption requiring independent covariates, computational scaling as O(D^r) in the number of unknowns, and the need for more iterations to converge due to local-scale correlations. Open questions include extending to non-independent features, non-Gaussian noise, missing data, and scaling to high-dimensional/high-order settings.
