## Problem

Vanilla transformers trained for next-token prediction cannot learn an explicit, queryable world model and therefore cannot perform path planning in partially observed environments (POEs) with perceptual aliasing. Forward rollouts in such environments scale exponentially with the ℓ₁ distance between source and target, and the model cannot even determine when it has reached the goal. This paper asks whether a transformer variant can retain near-perfect predictive accuracy while simultaneously producing an interpretable cognitive map that an external solver can use for efficient planning.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a trajectory of length N represented as an alternating sequence of categorical observations x₁,…,x_N and discrete actions a₁,…,a_{N-1} (in 3D environments, 64×64 RGB egocentric views are first clustered via k-means into k=128 categorical observations). At inference, the model outputs (a) next-observation logits, (b) a tuple of discrete bottleneck indices per timestep (one per bottleneck, each in {1,…,K} with K=1000), and (c) an action-conditioned cognitive map G built from empirical transition counts between bottleneck indices, which is then queried by networkx.shortest_path to return a shortest path as a sequence of actions between two specified positions.

## Method

TDB inserts one or more vector-quantized discrete bottlenecks on top of a standard causal transformer: only the odd-index transformer outputs (observation positions, which have not yet seen the next action) are passed through a VQ codebook of K latent codes, and the next observation is predicted by concatenating the quantized code with the next action embedding through a two-layer MLP. Two augmentation strategies prevent the bottleneck from merging spatially distinct positions with identical local neighbors: (1) a multi-step objective that predicts the next S observations given S future actions, and (2) a BYOL-style next-encoding prediction where a student network predicts the teacher's quantized code. After training, a count tensor C_{ijk} tallies empirical (state, action, next-state) transitions over bottleneck indices; thresholding C and clustering indices by Hamming distance yields an action-conditioned latent graph G. This graph is then paired with an external shortest-path solver, reducing planning from exponential rollouts to a polynomial graph search while retaining the transformer's near-perfect next-observation accuracy.

## Results

On 2D aliased rooms (15×20, O=4 observations), TDB(S=3,M=1) achieves 99.06% next-observation accuracy—matching the vanilla transformer (99.00%) and LSTM (98.85%)—while solving shortest path problems with 99.55% improvement over the fallback path and a path-length ratio of 1.00 (i.e., optimal), compared to only 29.53% and 16.82× for the vanilla transformer. The learned cognitive maps are nearly isomorphic to ground truth (NormGED = 0.124). On 3D simulated environments, TDB(S=3,M=4) reaches 99.79% accuracy, 99.95% ImpFallback, and NormGED of 0.017, while vanilla models achieve only 26.60% ImpFallback and 16.38× RatioSP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares TDB against a vanilla causal transformer and a vanilla LSTM on four task suites. On next-observation prediction, all three methods achieve near-identical accuracy (~99%), so TDB does not sacrifice predictive performance for interpretability. On path planning, the gap is dramatic: TDB(S=3,M=1) improves over the fallback path in 99.55% of problems and finds optimal-length paths (RatioSP = 1.00), while the vanilla transformer and LSTM succeed in only ~30% of cases and return paths ~17× longer than optimal. The key architectural difference is that TDB extracts a cognitive map from its discrete bottleneck indices and queries an external shortest-path solver (NetworkX), whereas vanilla models can only perform forward rollouts that scale exponentially with path length due to perceptual aliasing. On the GINC text dataset, TDB(S=1,M=4) outperforms both the transformer and LSTM across all context lengths, reaching ~95% in-context accuracy—higher than the ~65% reported for transformers in the original GINC paper. On 3D simulated environments, a single-bottleneck TDB fails to converge, but TDB(S=3,M=4) achieves 99.95% ImpFallback and NormGED of 0.017, while both vanilla models plateau at ~27% ImpFallback.

## Relation to Prior Work

TDB builds on the vanilla causal transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) and the vector-quantization bottleneck from VQ-VAE (Van Den Oord et al., 2017), adding a discrete codebook on top of transformer outputs to compress history into a finite set of latent indices. It departs from CSCG (George et al., 2021), which learns the same action-conditioned transition graph via EM over a dense latent space, by constructing the graph sparsely from local counts of bottleneck indices. The next-encoding prediction objective (Solution 2) directly adapts the BYOL teacher-student framework (Grill et al., 2020) to the discrete-bottleneck setting, and the overall approach contrasts with decision transformers (Chen et al., 2021a) and QMDP-Net (Karkus et al., 2017), which either require rewards or assume access to a known map and unaliased goals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a simple architectural modification—inserting a discrete bottleneck—transforms a predictive transformer into a planning-capable agent that extracts interpretable cognitive maps queryable by external solvers, closing a gap that has limited LLMs' utility for navigation. Two key limitations remain: TDB only accepts categorical inputs (not raw images), and multiple discrete bottlenecks, while accelerating training, produce highly redundant rather than disentangled representations (85–99% cross-predictability). Open questions include whether the approach extends to continuous high-dimensional observations, whether disentangled factorized latent dynamics can be learned in factored MDPs, and whether the in-context planning capability generalizes beyond the small synthetic rooms tested here.
