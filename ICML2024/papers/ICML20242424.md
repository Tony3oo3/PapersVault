## Problem

Post-training quantization of LLMs is hindered by magnitude outliers in weights and activations, and existing solutions either require expensive iterative optimization (e.g., OmniQuant's 7.3-hour gradient-based calibration) or introduce irregular memory access patterns (e.g., Scatter/Gather in LLM.int8()) that impede hardware efficiency. The paper asks whether one can achieve near-lossless W4A8 quantization without any gradient-based training, grid search, or irregular computation patterns.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242424/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM's FP16/FP32 weight matrices and a tiny calibration set (32 samples × 2048 tokens from SlimPajama, Wikipedia excluded) that is used only to profile per-channel activation magnitudes—no SGD or parameter training is involved. It produces, for each linear layer, a low-precision quantized weight matrix W_q (e.g., 4-bit MXINT) and two high-precision low-rank matrices A_k ∈ R^{m×k} and B_k ∈ R^{k×n} (k=32 for W4A8) that together approximate the quantization error. At inference, the low-precision product X·W_q and the high-precision low-rank product (X·A_k)·B_k execute in parallel, yielding a regular, blocked computation pattern with a unified number format.

## Method

LQER decomposes each weight matrix as W ≈ W_q + E_q and reconstructs the quantization error E_q via SVD-based low-rank approximation (A_k·B_k ≈ E_q), so the layer output becomes X·W_q + (X·A_k)·B_k. The key extension, L²QER, left-multiplies E_q by a diagonal scale matrix S derived from activation magnitudes before applying SVD, which concentrates the singular values of SE_q into the first few components (a steeper decay than the Marchenko-Pastur asymptotic of the unscaled error), enabling accurate reconstruction with a very small rank k≈32–64. The inverse S^{-1} is absorbed into A_k' so that the product A_k'·B_k' still approximates the original E_q. The entire calibration is a single forward pass over 32 samples to compute per-channel max activation magnitudes—no iterative optimization, knowledge distillation, or hyperparameter search is required. The method uses the MXINT block-floating-point format (shared exponent across 16 mantissas) for both weights and activations, and its regular blocked computation pattern eliminates the Scatter/Gather overhead present in threshold-based methods like LLM.int8().

## Results

L2QER-MXINT (W4A8) achieves an average perplexity increase of only 0.15 over FP16 across OPT, LLaMA, and LLaMA-2 models (6.7B–70B), and an average downstream task accuracy drop of just 0.3% across six benchmarks (ARC, LAMBADA, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ). In the W4A6 configuration, the average ΔPPL is 0.18, matching OmniQuant's W6A6 perplexity while using fewer bits. On hardware efficiency, L2QER-MXINT requires only 0.33× the circuit area of FP16, compared to 13.99× for AWQ/GPTQ and 21.23× for LLM.int4(). Calibration takes ~1.2 hours for LLaMA-33B on a single A100, versus 7.3 hours for OmniQuant.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GPTQ, L2QER-INT (W4A8) achieves a lower average ΔPPL (0.11 vs. 0.22) and dramatically lower circuit area (1.34× vs. 13.99×), while L2QER-MXINT (W4A8) further reduces ΔPPL to 0.15. Against AWQ, L2QER-INT is on par in perplexity (0.11 vs. 0.09 avg ΔPPL) but quantizes activations to 8-bit (W4A8) whereas AWQ keeps them at FP16 (W4A16); L2QER-MXINT (W4A8) is slightly worse in perplexity (0.15 vs. 0.09) but uses 10.4× less circuit area. Against LLM.int4(), L2QER-MXINT (W4A8) achieves substantially lower ΔPPL (0.15 vs. 0.29) and 64× less circuit area (0.33× vs. 21.23×), eliminating the expensive Scatter/Gather operations. Against OmniQuant (W6A6), L2QER-MXINT (W4A8) achieves lower ΔPPL (0.15 vs. 0.20) and lower circuit area (0.33× vs. 0.39×) without any iterative gradient-based training; on downstream tasks, L2QER drops only 0.3% vs. OmniQuant's reported 6.0% (and 11.0% in the authors' reproduction). Against AQAS (W4A8), L2QER-MXINT is far superior (0.15 vs. 1.45 avg ΔPPL). In the 2-bit regime, L2QER (k=256) matches QuiP# on LLaMA-13B (8.42 vs. 8.43) and slightly outperforms it on LLaMA-7B (10.30 vs. 10.97), while both remain well above FP16.

## Relation to Prior Work

LQER/L²QER sits at the intersection of two well-studied threads: post-training quantization of LLMs (GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant, OmniQuant) and low-rank matrix approximation (LoRA, QLoRA, LoftQ). It departs from LoftQ—the closest prior work—by keeping the low-rank correction matrices separate from the quantized weights at inference time (enabling parallel computation) rather than fusing them back into FP32 weights, and by operating purely as a PTQ method without any fine-tuning. The activation-induced scale matrix S is empirically derived from AWQ's per-channel scaling, while the w&a quantization setup with an invertible scale matrix follows SmoothQuant's formulation. The key conceptual departure is from methods like LLM.int8() and SpQR that use irregular scatter/gather memory access for high-precision sub-computations; LQER replaces this with a regular, blocked computation pattern using two small high-precision matrix multiplications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that L²QER achieves near-lossless W4A8 quantization (≈0.15 PPL increase, ≈0.3% accuracy drop) without iterative optimization, knowledge distillation, or hyperparameter search, while requiring 1.36× less circuit area than the leading SOTA method—making it attractive for hardware deployment where scatter/gather operations are expensive. The activation-induced scaling insight (left-multiplying E_q by a diagonal matrix derived from activation magnitudes to concentrate singular values) is a simple but effective mechanism that reduces the required rank from ~600 to ~64. Open questions include: whether an analytical derivation of S exists (the current formulation is empirical), whether the approach can be pushed to 2-bit quantization (where it still requires k=256 and shows inconsistent results across model families), and whether the Marchenko-Pastur-based theoretical framing can be made more rigorous for non-i.i.d. trained weights.
