## Problem

Language generation has relied exclusively on the logarithmic score (cross-entropy) as its loss function because it is the only strictly proper scoring rule that is also local—depending solely on the probability of the observed token—making it tractable for the exponentially large vocabulary space. This paper asks whether non-local strictly proper scoring rules, which capture global distributional properties and may offer more robust optimization, can be adapted to language generation and whether they yield practical benefits.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard autoregressive sequence models where at each decoding step the model produces a conditional probability vector over the vocabulary (size V) given the preceding context. The scoring rule evaluates this full probability vector against the ground-truth token, meaning the loss depends on all V probabilities at each step rather than just the target token's probability. For LLM experiments, inputs are instruction-following demonstrations (Alpaca, 52K examples) and outputs are evaluated on translation (WMT22), summarization (CNN/DailyMail), and open-ended QA (MT-bench).

## Method

The core strategy distributes non-local scoring rules at the token level: instead of scoring the intractable sequence-level distribution, each conditional token probability p_θ(·|x_<t) is scored against the true conditional q(·|x_<t), and the losses are summed over the sequence. This is valid because the autoregressive factorization guarantees that well-calibrated conditionals yield a well-calibrated sequence distribution. The paper applies this to the Brier score (quadratic, bounded) and Spherical score (cosine-similarity-based, bounded) as alternatives to the logarithmic score. A second contribution is score smoothing, which generalizes label smoothing to arbitrary scoring rules by convexly combining the original score with its expectation over all labels, plus a masked logarithmic score term that penalizes under-smoothed labels to strengthen the regularization effect for bounded scores. Experiments show that simply substituting the loss during fine-tuning—without any hyperparameter adjustment—improves BLEU and ROUGE scores, with particularly large gains (3+ BLEU) on LLaMA-7B/13B instruction tuning.

## Results

Fine-tuning a Transformer pre-trained with log-likelihood loss using the Brier score or Spherical score yields BLEU 28.01 and 28.07 on WMT14 En-De, and 42.50 and 42.09 on WMT14 En-Fr, outperforming all compared loss-function baselines. On CNN/DailyMail summarization, Brier fine-tuning achieves ROUGE-1/2/L of 40.20/17.56/36.78. At LLM scale, instruction-tuning LLaMA-7B with the Brier score improves average WMT22 BLEU from 17.59 to 20.93 (+3.34), and the Spherical score improves it to 21.23 (+3.64), with similar gains on LLaMA-13B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three alternative loss-function approaches for language generation: MixCE (Zhang et al., 2023b), TaiLr (Ji et al., 2023), and Convex (Shao et al., 2023). On WMT14 En-De, both Brier (28.01) and Spherical (28.07) fine-tuning outperform all three baselines, with the largest margin over MixCE (+0.26 BLEU) and Convex (+0.21/+0.27 BLEU). On WMT14 En-Fr, Brier fine-tuning achieves the best score (42.50), beating TaiLr (42.12) by 0.38 BLEU and MixCE (42.03) by 0.47 BLEU; Spherical (42.09) is competitive but slightly below TaiLr. On CNN/DailyMail, Brier and Spherical fine-tuning match or marginally exceed MixCE and Convex on ROUGE-1 (40.20 vs. 40.16/40.15) while clearly surpassing TaiLr (39.11). The gains are most pronounced at LLM scale, where Brier/Spherical instruction-tuning of LLaMA-7B yields over 3 BLEU improvement on WMT22 translation and over 3 ROUGE-1 improvement on summarization, far exceeding what the baselines report at this scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper draws on the classical theory of strictly proper scoring rules from statistical decision theory (Good, 1952; Gneiting & Raftery, 2007) and the well-known result that the logarithmic score is the unique local strictly proper scoring rule (Bernardo, 1979), which has constrained language modeling to cross-entropy loss. The key departure is a token-level distribution strategy that decomposes non-local scoring rules (Brier, Spherical) into per-token losses, circumventing the intractability of computing scores over the exponentially large sequence space. The paper also generalizes label smoothing into 'score smoothing' for arbitrary scoring rules, and positions itself as the first work to train language generation models with scoring rules other than the logarithmic score, complementing prior loss-modification approaches like MixCE, TaiLr, and convex learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that simply swapping the loss function during fine-tuning—without any hyperparameter retuning—yields meaningful generation improvements that scale to 7B/13B LLMs (3+ BLEU/ROUGE points), making this a low-cost, broadly applicable technique. The observation that different strictly proper scores follow conflicting optimization trajectories (the log score drops when fine-tuning with Brier/Spherical) suggests that perplexity alone is an incomplete evaluation metric and that multi-score assessment could be more informative. Open questions include what properties of a scoring rule determine its training effectiveness, whether other scores outperform Brier/Spherical, and whether these scores can serve as standalone evaluation metrics analogous to perplexity.
