## Problem

Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) rely on various non-traditional activations (sinusoidal, Gaussian, wavelet) to overcome spectral bias, but prior analyses have been fragmented—each studying its own activation in isolation without a unifying theoretical lens. No existing framework explains which activations are fundamentally optimal for signal reconstruction, and the theoretically ideal sinc activation has never been explored in the INR setting.

## Input / Output

For image and NeRF tasks, the INR consumes 2D or 3D spatial coordinates and outputs pixel values or radiance/density estimates, with a single bandwidth hyperparameter of the sinc activation fixed across an entire dataset (e.g., all 64 DIV2K images). For dynamical systems, the input consists of noisy, sparse, and randomly sampled time measurements y(t_n), and the output is either a continuous state trajectory (via Takens delay embedding) or the governing differential equations (via SINDy), where the INR simultaneously provides both the signal Y and its time derivative Ẏ through Jacobian computation, replacing finite-difference or spectral-derivative estimates.

## Method

The paper reframes INR activations as generator functions in a sampling-theory setting, defining signal spaces V(F) spanned by integer translates of the activation F and classifying them via Riesz basis theory. The key result (Prop. 3.3) shows sinc forms a full Riesz basis (satisfying both the frame bounds and the partition-of-unity condition), Gaussian and wavelets form only weak Riesz bases, and ReLU/sinusoid fail even the weak condition. The partition-of-unity condition is shown to be the critical property enabling approximation of arbitrary L² signals (not just bandlimited ones) via Ω-scaled signal spaces, yielding Theorem 3.8: a 2-layer sinc-activated INR with n(ε) hidden neurons can approximate any L² signal to within ε. Practically, the sinc-INR is deployed as a drop-in activation for image/NeRF reconstruction and as a denoising-and-derivative module within SINDy, where its bandwidth parameter acts as a learnable low-pass filter that suppresses high-frequency noise while the smooth sinc derivatives provide accurate Ẏ estimates.

## Results

On NeRF novel view synthesis (synthetic dataset), sinc-activated INRs achieve 31.37 PSNR and 0.947 SSIM, marginally outperforming Gaussian (31.13/0.947), Wavelet (30.33/0.941), and Sinusoid (28.96/0.933). On dynamical system identification (SINDy), sinc-INRs consistently outperform all baselines across noise levels: on the Lorenz system at n=1.0, sinc achieves 39.3 PSNR vs. 35.7 (Gaussian), 30.3 (Sinusoid), 31.8 (Wavelet), and 20.3 (baseline SINDy). For positional embeddings, sinc-based PE achieves 26.4 PSNR versus 23.5 PSNR for RFF on DIV2K image reconstruction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Sinc-activated INRs outperform all compared activations across every experimental setting. On NeRF reconstruction, sinc (31.37 PSNR) beats Gaussian (31.13), Wavelet (30.33), and Sinusoid (28.96) from Sitzmann et al. and Ramasinghe & Lucey. On the SINDy dynamical system identification task, sinc-INRs surpass the baseline SINDy algorithm (Brunton et al.) by 18.2 PSNR on Lorenz at n=1.0 (39.3 vs. 20.3) and 16.2 PSNR on Rossler at n=1.0 (38.5 vs. 22.3), while also beating Gaussian, Sinusoid, and Wavelet activations by 2.8–8.2 PSNR at the highest noise level. For positional embeddings, the proposed sinc PE layer (26.4 PSNR) outperforms the RFF encoding from Tancik et al. (23.5 PSNR) by 2.9 dB on DIV2K. The advantage of sinc is most pronounced under high noise and sparse sampling conditions in dynamical systems, where classical methods degrade significantly while sinc-INRs maintain robust reconstructions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the fragmented landscape of INR activation functions (sinusoidal/SIREN, Gaussian, wavelet) under a single sampling-theoretic lens, showing that their relative quality is governed by whether their translates form a Riesz basis, a weak Riesz basis, or neither. The theoretical machinery draws directly on the approximation-operator and error-kernel framework of Unser (2000) and Blu & Unser (1999), while the dynamical-systems experiments build on SINDy (Brunton et al., 2016) and Takens' delay-embedding theorem. The key departure from prior INR work is the identification of the sinc function—central to the Nyquist-Shannon theorem but previously unused in INRs—as the theoretically optimal activation, and the explicit bridging of INR signal reconstruction with data-driven dynamical-systems identification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Riesz-basis criterion gives practitioners a principled, activation-agnostic test for predicting reconstruction quality, and the sinc activation offers a drop-in alternative with smooth gradients that is marginally superior to Gaussian and wavelet activations on images/NeRF and more clearly advantageous on noisy dynamical-systems tasks. However, the theory is developed for 1-D signals in L²(ℝ), and the practical gains over Gaussian activations are modest (≈0.2–0.5 dB PSNR on images), so the framework's full value may require extension to multi-dimensional coordinate networks and larger-scale benchmarks. Open questions include how the partition-of-unity condition interacts with the hyperparameter sensitivity that plagues all INR activations, and whether the sinc-INR advantage in dynamical-systems modeling persists for higher-dimensional or non-chaotic systems.
