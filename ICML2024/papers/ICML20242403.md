## Problem

Existing membership inference attacks require large numbers of reference models to achieve strong performance, exhibit mutual dominance depending on the test scenario (e.g., LiRA approaches random guessing under low computation budgets while Attack-R underperforms at low FPR), and degrade significantly under practical constraints such as OOD non-member queries, distribution shifts, and architecture mismatches. This paper addresses the absence of a single MIA test that is simultaneously low-cost (few reference models), high-power across the entire TPR-FPR curve (including zero FPR), and robust to uncertainties in the adversary's background knowledge.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a target data point x, the target model θ, a small set of pre-trained reference models (as few as 1 in offline mode), and a set of population data samples z drawn from the population distribution π. It outputs a continuous membership score equal to the fraction of z samples that pass a pairwise likelihood-ratio threshold test, which is then compared against a threshold β to yield a binary membership prediction. In offline mode, only OUT reference models are used (no per-query training), and the normalizing constant Pr(x) is approximated via a linear scaling of the OUT-model average, making the attack fully pre-computable.

## Method

RMIA models the null hypothesis (x not in θ's training set) as a composition of fine-grained worlds in which x is replaced by each random population sample z, then composes pairwise likelihood ratio tests LR_θ(x,z) = Pr(θ|x)/Pr(θ|z) across all z. The MIA score is the fraction of z for which LR_θ(x,z) ≥ γ, providing a calibrated, interpretable statistic that differentiates members from non-members more precisely than average-case tests. The pairwise LR is computed efficiently via Bayes' rule as (Pr(x|θ)/Pr(x)) / (Pr(z|θ)/Pr(z)), where Pr(x|θ) is the model's prediction confidence on x and Pr(x) is estimated empirically from reference models. This Bayesian formulation strictly dominates the direct Gaussian-logit computation (equation 6) when few reference models are available, and RMIA's pairwise structure subsumes prior attacks (Attack-P, Attack-R, LiRA) as simplified or uncalibrated special cases, explaining their instability.

## Results

With only 1 pre-trained reference model (offline), RMIA achieves 68.64 AUC on CIFAR-10 and 87.18 AUC on CIFAR-100, outperforming Attack-R (63.65 / 81.61) and LiRA (53.2 / 68.95) by 5–25 percentage points in AUC. At zero FPR, RMIA reaches 0.51 TPR on CIFAR-10 versus 0.02 for Attack-R and 0.25 for LiRA. Scaling to 127 reference models, offline RMIA reaches 71.71 AUC (CIFAR-10) and 90.57 AUC (CIFAR-100), matching or exceeding LiRA (Online) which requires 254 models (127 IN + 127 OUT) per query. RMIA also maintains a ≥21% AUC advantage over Attack-R when non-member queries are out-of-distribution (CINIC-10 samples against CIFAR-10 models).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LiRA (Carlini et al., 2022), RMIA achieves at least 24% higher AUC with a single reference model across all datasets (e.g., 68.64 vs. 53.20 on CIFAR-10), and even offline RMIA with 4 models (71.02 AUC) surpasses LiRA (Online) with the same budget (67.00 AUC) by 6% in AUC and 3× in TPR at zero FPR. Against Attack-P and Attack-R (Ye et al., 2022), RMIA gains 5–15% AUC with 1–4 reference models and dramatically higher TPR at zero FPR (e.g., 0.51 vs. 0.02 on CIFAR-10 with 1 model). Against Quantile Regression (Bertran et al., 2023), RMIA with 1 reference model outperforms it on AUC (68.64 vs. 61.45 on CIFAR-10) and TPR at zero FPR (0.51 vs. 0.03), and the gap widens with more reference models since Quantile Regression cannot benefit from additional models. With 127 reference models, RMIA (offline) and LiRA (online) converge in AUC (71.71 vs. 72.04 on CIFAR-10), but RMIA retains a 50% advantage in TPR at zero FPR (3.14 vs. 2.01). Under OOD non-member queries, RMIA maintains ≥21% AUC advantage over Attack-R while LiRA drops to near random-guessing performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the statistical hypothesis-testing formulation of membership inference attacks, drawing on the leave-one-out distinguishability result (Ye et al., 2024) to motivate its likelihood ratio construction. It departs from prior attacks (LiRA, Attack-P, Attack-R) by modeling the null hypothesis at a fine-grained level—composing pairwise LR tests between the target point x and population samples z—rather than relying on average-case statistics over reference models. The paper explicitly shows that prior attacks are uncalibrated or average-case simplifications of its test, providing a unifying lens that explains their mutual dominance in different scenarios. Computationally, it replaces the expensive direct LR estimation (requiring many online reference models) with a Bayesian decomposition (equation 3) that operates effectively with as few as one pre-trained OUT model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in making privacy auditing feasible under realistic computational budgets: RMIA achieves near-maximal AUC with 1–2 reference models (vs. 127+ for LiRA) and remains robust to OOD non-members, distribution shift, and architecture mismatch—conditions under which prior attacks degrade toward random guessing. This is critical for applications like data-extraction attacks where MIA oracles must filter astronomically many OOD candidates at near-zero FPR. Open questions include whether the heuristic scaling factor 'a' generalizes to new model families without re-tuning, how the method extends to generative or language models, and whether the pairwise composition can be further accelerated for very large population sets.
