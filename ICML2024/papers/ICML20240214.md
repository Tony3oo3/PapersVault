## Problem

Imitation Learning from Observations (ILO) must learn a policy from expert state sequences without access to expert actions, making the sample-efficiency gains of behavioral cloning regularization—well established in the action-available ILD setting—unavailable. Existing ILO methods (GAIfO, trajectory matching, optimal transport) often fail to converge within practical interaction budgets, particularly in complex continuous-control tasks, leaving a significant gap between ILO performance and the best achievable with expert actions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes expert state-state transition sequences ζ = {(s₀,s₁), (s₁,s₂), …} (16 trajectories of length 1000 per environment, collected from a PPO-trained expert) and produces two outputs: an imitation policy π_θ(a|s) and an inverse dynamics model p(a|s,s') parameterized as a mixture density network. The IDM is not trained on expert data but on (s,a,s') triplets gathered from the agent's own online rollouts stored in a 10⁵-sample replay buffer, so it encodes the simulator's physics rather than expert-specific behavior. The policy is updated via PPO with surrogate rewards (adversarial, trajectory-matching, or Sinkhorn-OT) plus a forward-KL regularizer that pulls the policy toward the IDM's action posterior evaluated on expert transitions.

## Method

MAAD interleaves two training loops: (1) an inverse dynamics model (a single-Gaussian MDN) is trained by maximum likelihood on (s,a,s') triplets from a replay buffer of the agent's own rollouts, and (2) the policy is updated with PPO using a combined objective L = D(ρ_π(s,s')‖ρ_πE(s,s')) + λ·E_{(s,s')~expert} D_KL(p_α,ψ(a|s,s')‖π_θ(a|s)). The KL regularizer is the key novelty: it substitutes the missing expert actions with the IDM's posterior of physically plausible actions, providing dense supervised gradients that complement the sparse, low-quality surrogate rewards—especially critical during early adversarial training. Three reward instantiations are explored (adversarial discriminator, Euclidean trajectory matching, and Sinkhorn optimal transport), and the forward KL is chosen over reverse KL for its faster convergence to the target distribution. Theoretically, the regularizer upper-bounds the Inverse Dynamics Disagreement (IDD) between the environment's and the agent's inverse models, so at optimality the agent's action posterior aligns with the environment's physics, closing the gap between ILO and ILD objectives.

## Results

MAAD-AIL achieves expert-level normalized returns in nearly all 11 MuJoCo environments across both the OpenAI Gym and DMC suites, e.g., 3822±36 on Hopper-v3 (expert: 3749±31) and 704±39 on Cheetah-Run (expert: 663±121). In the DMC suite, MAAD-AIL outperforms GAIfO by up to 7.7× (Cheetah-Run: 704 vs. 91) and matches or slightly exceeds GAIL-BC† (which has access to expert actions) in 4 of 6 environments. The non-regularized baselines TMO and OTO fail to converge within the interaction budget in all environments, with returns near zero or negative (e.g., −337±607 on Ant-v3 for OTO). MAAD-AIL is the only ILO method to reach expert performance in both suites simultaneously, except for Dog-Run in DMC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GAIfO (Torabi et al., 2019), the primary ILO baseline without expert actions, MAAD-AIL achieves dramatically higher sample efficiency: in the DMC suite it reaches expert performance in environments where GAIfO stalls at 10–20% of expert return (e.g., Cheetah-Run 704 vs. 91, Walker-Walk 823 vs. 77, Quadruped-Run 745 vs. 112), representing up to 7.7× improvement in final return and up to 4× fewer interactions to converge. Against GAIL-BC† (Jena et al., 2020), which has direct access to expert actions and thus represents an upper bound, MAAD-AIL matches or slightly exceeds performance in several environments (Cheetah-Run: 704 vs. 692; Ant-v3: 6655 vs. 6612; Humanoid-v3: 7557 vs. 7821) while using only state observations, suggesting the IDM-based regularizer provides comparable or greater supervisory signal than exact expert actions. The non-regularized counterparts of MAAD (TMO, OTO) and GAIfO all fail to converge in the DMC walker-based environments (Walker-Walk, Walker-Run), where only MAAD-AIL and GAIL-BC† succeed. Overall, MAAD-AIL is the sole ILO method achieving expert-level performance across both evaluation suites, whereas all other ILO baselines fail in at least one suite.

## Relation to Prior Work

MAAD unifies the BC-regularized imitation learning paradigm (GAIL-BC, TD3-BC, ROT) with the ILO setting by replacing the unavailable expert actions with a learned inverse dynamics model (IDM) that provides a posterior over physically plausible actions. The policy loss is modular—adversarial (extending GAIfO), trajectory matching, or Sinkhorn-based OT (extending SIL/ROT)—while the regularizer is a forward KL divergence between the IDM and the policy, analogous to the off-policy IDM-BC regularizer in Zhu et al. (2021) but applied in an on-policy PPO framework. The IDM itself is a mixture density network trained on (s, a, s') triplets collected during online interaction, following the inverse-modeling approach of Torabi et al. (2018) but with a key difference: it is trained jointly with the policy via a replay buffer rather than pre-trained on random-policy data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MAAD addresses a practically important gap: ILO is the natural setting for learning from video, motion capture, or other sources where actions are unobservable, yet prior ILO methods (GAIfO, TMO, OTO) frequently fail to converge to expert performance within reasonable interaction budgets. The IDM-based regularizer provides dense, supervised-learning-quality gradients that compensate for the noisy early-stage reward signal, making training both faster and more stable. Limitations include persistent difficulty in walker-based environments (likely due to multimodal action spaces where K=1 is insufficient) and the fact that all experiments are in MuJoCo simulators with PPO-trained experts, leaving open whether the approach transfers to real-world, non-deterministic, or partially observable settings.
