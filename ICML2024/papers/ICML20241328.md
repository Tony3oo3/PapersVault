## Problem

No complete system for data provenance exists for foundation model training data; existing solutions—content authenticity techniques, opt-in/opt-out mechanisms, documentation standards, and provenance libraries—each address a narrow slice of the problem in isolation. This fragmentation leaves developers unable to trace data authenticity, verify creator consent, or document critical properties (copyright, privacy, bias, lineage) in a unified, machine-readable way, contributing to real-world harms including CSAM contamination in LAION-5B, copyright litigation, PII leakage, and unmitigated bias.

## Input / Output

The paper's analytical input is the full landscape of existing data provenance interventions across text, image, audio, and video modalities, including their metadata schemas, coverage scope, and adoption status. Rather than processing raw data, the paper consumes and compares structured records from tools like C2PA manifests, robots.txt directives, Datasheets, Data Nutrition Labels, Common Crawl crawl metadata, and Hugging Face data cards. The output is a set of five design principles for a unified provenance standard (modality-agnostic, verifiable, structured, extensible, symbolically attributable) plus a 16-field metadata taxonomy (Table 2) and stakeholder-specific action recommendations.

## Method

The paper conducts a structured survey of four categories of data provenance interventions—content authenticity (C2PA, digital watermarks), opt-in/opt-out (robots.txt, consent registries), dataset provenance standards (Datasheets, Data Nutrition Labels, Data & Trust Alliance standard), and provenance libraries (Common Crawl, Data Provenance Initiative, Hugging Face)—evaluating each along five axes: media coverage, verifiability, searchability, extensibility, and symbolic attribution (Table 1). It identifies a fundamental trade-off triangle among coverage breadth, documentation depth, and metadata accuracy that no single existing tool resolves. The core contribution is a synthesis arguing that these categories are complementary rather than competing, and that a unified framework must interoperate across all four while satisfying five design principles. The paper then maps concrete actions to four stakeholder groups (data creators, AI developers, regulators, researchers) and proposes a multi-stakeholder consortium model analogous to W3C or the Data & Trust Alliance to drive standardization.

## Results

This is a position/survey paper with no quantitative experiments or benchmark results. The key finding is a qualitative gap analysis: no single existing solution (content authenticity, opt-in/opt-out, data standards, or provenance libraries) covers all dimensions of data provenance (authenticity, consent, lineage, licensing, bias, privacy). The paper identifies a fundamental trade-off triangle among data libraries: Common Crawl offers wide coverage and accuracy but limited metadata depth; Hugging Face has extensive coverage but uneven/incorrect documentation; the Data Provenance Initiative is highly accurate and detailed but limited in scope. The authors conclude that a unified, interoperable, modality-agnostic standard is needed, requiring coordinated action from creators, developers, regulators, and researchers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it surveys and qualitatively compares seven categories of existing data provenance interventions. Content authenticity techniques (C2PA, digital watermarks) provide verifiable, embedded provenance but are limited to atomic data units, cannot be searched or extended to cover licensing/bias/privacy metadata, and are vulnerable to removal (especially for text). Robots.txt and consent registration tools address only the consent dimension in isolation, require custom implementation per AI company, and lack enforcement mechanisms. Data documentation standards (Datasheets, Data Nutrition Labels, Data & Trust Alliance) encode richer metadata but suffer from uneven adoption and accuracy issues. Among the three data libraries, Common Crawl wins on coverage and accuracy but provides minimal metadata; Hugging Face has the broadest dataset coverage but documentation is loosely crowdsourced and often incorrect; the Data Provenance Initiative is the most accurate and detailed but is limited in scale due to reliance on expert human labor. The paper's central argument is that these solutions are complementary but fragmented, and none individually solves the full provenance problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and positions itself against a fragmented ecosystem of existing data provenance tools: content authenticity standards (C2PA, digital watermarks), consent mechanisms (robots.txt, Do Not Train Registry), documentation standards (Datasheets, Data Statements, Data Cards, Data Nutrition Labels, Data & Trust Alliance standard), and data libraries (Common Crawl, Hugging Face, Data Provenance Initiative). Rather than proposing a new technical method, it carves new ground by arguing that these individually useful but isolated solutions collectively fail to provide a unified, interoperable, modality-agnostic provenance framework, and by mapping the trade-offs (coverage vs. depth vs. accuracy) that prevent any single existing tool from being sufficient. The paper also draws on legal scholarship on copyright and fair use, as well as emerging regulatory instruments (EU AI Act, Biden Executive Order), to ground its technical recommendations in enforceable policy context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, actionable taxonomy of metadata fields (Table 2) and a comparative matrix of existing interventions (Table 1) that gives policymakers, developers, and data creators a shared vocabulary for what a unified provenance standard must cover. Its central open question is adoption: how to incentivize widespread, accurate, and verifiable documentation when current legal incentives perversely discourage disclosure, and how to balance the coverage-depth-accuracy trade-off at scale. The authors themselves caution that a poorly designed standard could entrench corporate advantages or create false security, and that provenance documentation is a necessary but not sufficient condition for responsible AI—leaving the harder questions of enforcement, verification at scale, and jurisdictional harmonization unresolved.
