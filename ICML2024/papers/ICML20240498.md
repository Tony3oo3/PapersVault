## Problem

Standard practice for selecting LM pretraining data relies on filtering for textual similarity with 'high quality' sources (e.g., Wikipedia), yet the paper shows these similarity-based methods do not outperform—and can underperform—random selection. The core gap is that existing methods ignore how the learning algorithm actually uses training data to make predictions, treating data quality as a static property rather than a function of the training process.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a set of target task samples (e.g., SQuAD, LAMBADA, Jeopardy), a candidate data pool (the C4 English subset, ~217M 1024-token examples), and a learning algorithm (GPT-2-style decoder-only transformer). It outputs a size-k subset of the candidate pool that minimizes expected loss on the target tasks. In practice, datamodels are computed on 125M proxy models, and the resulting selection is then used to train much larger models (up to 1.8B parameters) at Chinchilla-optimal compute budgets, making the selection transferable across model scales without recomputation.

## Method

DSDM frames dataset selection as an optimization problem: find the size-k training subset that minimizes trained model loss on target tasks. To make this tractable, it uses linear datamodels—parameterized functions mapping the characteristic vector of a training subset to predicted model loss—estimated via the TRAK estimator, which linearizes the model at its trained weights and computes influence-based weights using projected gradients (Johnson-Lindenstrauss random projections) averaged over multiple proxy model runs. Because the datamodels are linear, the optimal selection reduces to a simple ranking: pick the k examples with the smallest values of the averaged datamodel weight vector (1/n) Σ θ_{x_i} across target task samples. This model-aware criterion captures how each training example actually affects predictions on the target tasks, rather than relying on surface-level textual similarity, and yields a 2× compute multiplier over random selection on diverse held-out benchmarks.

## Results

DSDM-selected data yields a 2× compute multiplier: 1.3B-parameter models trained on DSDM-selected C4 data match 1.8B-parameter models trained on randomly selected data (i.e., 2× the compute budget) across 15 held-out benchmarks. DSDM most improves reading comprehension (e.g., CoQA: 25.5% vs. 18.8% random, +7 pts; NewsQA: 15.6% vs. 7.5% random, +8 pts) and world knowledge (BB QA Wikidata: 48.1% vs. 40.6% random, +8 pts) while not degrading other categories on average. Across all compute budgets (125M to 1.3B), DSDM consistently outperforms every selection baseline, whereas no baseline (CLASSIFIER, DSIR, SemDeDup) reliably beats random selection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DSDM against three baselines: CLASSIFIER (logistic regression on FastText features, used for GPT-3/PaLM data selection), DSIR (n-gram importance resampling), and SemDeDup (semantic deduplication). The central finding is that none of these baselines reliably outperform random selection: CLASSIFIER and DSIR, which select by textual similarity to a 'high quality' reference, fail to beat random on most benchmarks (e.g., DSIR scores 1.0% on BB Dyck Lang vs. 13.5% random; CLASSIFIER scores 0.0% on BB Copy Logic vs. 3.1% random). SemDeDup also shows mixed results, sometimes matching or slightly exceeding random but never consistently. In contrast, DSDM outperforms all baselines in aggregate across every compute budget tested (125M–1.3B). The largest DSDM advantages appear on reading comprehension (CoQA: 25.5% DSDM vs. 16.5% DSIR, 16.7% Classifier) and world knowledge (BB QA Wikidata: 48.1% DSDM vs. 37.7% DSIR), categories qualitatively related to the target tasks (SQuAD and Jeopardy). The paper further confirms that even when CLASSIFIER and DSIR are re-targeted to the same tasks as DSDM (LAMBADA, SQuAD, Jeopardy), they still fail to outperform random selection, isolating the selection method itself as the source of DSDM's gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the datamodeling framework (Ilyas et al., 2022), previously applied to reliability tasks like data poisoning detection and train-test leakage, as a tool for dataset selection in LM pretraining. It uses TRAK (Park et al., 2023) as the efficient estimator for linear datamodels, adapting it to the language modeling setting with a mean multi-class margin output function. The key conceptual departure is from the dominant similarity-based selection paradigm (DSIR, CLASSIFIER) used to curate data for GPT-3, PaLM, and The Pile: rather than matching candidate data to a 'high quality' reference distribution, DSDM frames selection as an optimization problem over the learning algorithm's actual use of training data, sidestepping handpicked quality notions entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard similarity-based selection methods (used in practice for major models) fail to beat random selection challenges a widely adopted assumption in LM data curation and suggests that 'cleaner' data is not necessarily more useful. The 2× compute multiplier demonstrated across multiple compute budgets and benchmark categories is practically significant, and the broader framing—data curation as a controllable pipeline stage for shaping downstream model behavior (alignment, fairness, domain specialization)—opens a new axis of intervention beyond post-hoc fine-tuning. Key limitations include reliance on linear datamodels, proxy-model selection (125M models selecting data for up to 1.8B models), and evaluation only up to 1.8B parameters; whether the approach and its gains persist at frontier scale remains an open question.
