## Problem

In deep heteroscedastic regression, the predicted covariance is typically an arbitrary mapping from input to a positive definite matrix, which does not inherently capture the randomness of the predicted mean and leads to sub-optimal convergence of the negative log-likelihood. Prior work addresses this by regularizing the covariance during optimization, but does not improve the covariance parameterization itself. Additionally, no direct metric exists to evaluate the accuracy of learned correlations in the absence of ground-truth covariance labels.

## Input / Output

The method takes an input vector x ∈ R^m (or an image for pose estimation, reduced to a vector encoding via soft-argmax and recursive hourglass/transformer pooling) and produces a multivariate mean ŷ ∈ R^n and a full n×n covariance matrix. The covariance is not predicted directly but is constructed from the Jacobian and Hessian of the mean estimator with respect to the input, scaled by three learned parameters. The TAC evaluation metric conditions the predicted Gaussian on all but one observed dimension and reports the mean absolute error of the updated prediction against the ground truth.

## Method

The Taylor Induced Covariance (TIC) parameterizes the predicted covariance as k₁(x)·J(x)J(x)ᵀ + k₂(x)·Trace(H_i H_j) + k₃(x), derived from the second-order Taylor polynomial of the mean estimator f_θ(x+ε) around a stochastic ε-neighborhood. The covariance estimator g_Θ(x) predicts only three parameters—two positive scalars k₁, k₂ and a positive definite matrix k₃—rather than the full covariance, tying the randomness of the prediction to its gradient and curvature. The cross-covariance between the linear and quadratic Taylor terms vanishes due to odd/even function symmetry under the isotropic Gaussian neighborhood. For evaluation, the Task Agnostic Correlations (TAC) metric conditions the predicted normal distribution on all but one ground-truth dimension and measures the absolute error of the revised estimate, directly quantifying whether the learned covariance encodes correct inter-variable correlations.

## Results

On the ViTPose human pose estimation benchmark (MPII + LSP/LSPET), NLL-TIC achieves an average TAC error of 5.23±0.03, outperforming NLL (5.80±0.07) and Faithful (5.93±0.03), while also achieving the best log-likelihood of −80.31±0.39. On the 12 UCI regression datasets, NLL-TIC attains the lowest TAC error on 10 out of 12 datasets and the best log-likelihood on 11 out of 12. On the multivariate synthetic experiment (dimensions 4–20), NLL-TIC achieves substantially better log-likelihoods than NLL and Faithful, e.g., −39.7 vs. −67.2 (NLL) and −69.0 (Faithful) at dimension 20.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NLL-TIC consistently outperforms all five baselines on the TAC metric across every experimental setting. Against NLL (Dorta et al., 2018), NLL-TIC reduces average TAC from 5.80 to 5.23 on ViTPose and wins on 10/12 UCI datasets; on the multivariate synthetic task, NLL-TIC achieves a log-likelihood of −39.7 at dimension 20 versus −67.2 for NLL. NLL-Diagonal (Kendall & Gal, 2017) and β-NLL (Seitzer et al., 2022) perform substantially worse on TAC (12.95 and 12.31 respectively on ViTPose), as their diagonal or scaled formulations fail to capture inter-variable correlations. Faithful Heteroscedastic Regression (Stirn et al., 2023) is the closest competitor, achieving 5.93 avg TAC on ViTPose and slightly better log-likelihood (−91.77 vs. −80.31), but NLL-TIC still wins on TAC by a clear margin and on 11/12 UCI datasets for likelihood. Natural Laplace (Immer et al., 2023) is only evaluated in the univariate sinusoidal experiments, where it fits constant-amplitude curves but exhibits unstable optimization for varying-amplitude sinusoidals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the deep heteroscedastic regression framework established by Nix & Weigend (1994) and Kendall & Gal (2017), where mean and covariance are jointly optimized via the negative log-likelihood. It departs from the dominant line of work (β-NLL, Faithful Heteroscedastic Regression, Natural Laplace) that regularizes or rescales the covariance during optimization, instead proposing a structurally different covariance parameterization (TIC) that ties the predicted covariance to the Jacobian and Hessian of the mean estimator. The TAC evaluation metric also carves new ground by using conditioning of the multivariate normal to directly assess correlation accuracy, rather than relying on likelihood or MSE which conflate mean and covariance quality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: without direct supervision, the covariance in heteroscedastic regression can take arbitrary values that minimize NLL without reflecting true prediction uncertainty, and there was no principled metric to detect this. The TAC metric provides a task-agnostic way to evaluate covariance quality, which is broadly useful for any application relying on learned correlations (active learning, pose estimation, state estimation). Key limitations include the O(nm³) Hessian computation cost, the heuristic nature of the ε-neighborhood interpretation, and the observed failure on low-variance datasets (Naval), suggesting the approach may not be universally applicable. Open questions include whether useful Hessian approximations can reduce computational cost and whether the framework extends to non-vector inputs without the soft-argmax reduction used here.
