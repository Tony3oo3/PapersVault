## Problem

Existing few-shot open-set recognition (FSOSR) methods, particularly transformer-based approaches like SnaTCHer, fail when open-set classes share feature similarities with closed-set classes (e.g., golden retriever vs. ferrets). The softmax-normalized attention in these methods assigns high weights to similar instances, causing the altered-prototype distance to remain small and the open-set sample to be misclassified as closed-set with high confidence. This paper targets this specific hard case where subtle but important differences between similar classes defeat standard detection mechanisms.

## Input / Output

The method takes a query RGB image and a support set of K labeled images per class (N-way K-shot, with K=1 or 5) as input. The support images are encoded by a ResNet-12 backbone into feature vectors, averaged per class to form prototypes, then transformed through a learned attention-based transformer. The output is a scalar distance score (Euclidean distance between transformed original and altered prototypes) used for open-set/closed-set classification via AUROC, along with a predicted class label for closed-set samples. The evaluation uses 5-way tasks with 75 known and 75 unknown query samples per episode.

## Method

MET integrates three components: (1) an evidential open-set loss during meta-training that selects semantically similar closed-set classes as 'opponent classes' and applies KL-divergence between the predicted Dirichlet distribution and a uniform Dirichlet to push total evidence toward zero for these opponent samples, learning a more compact closed-set representation; (2) an Evidence-to-Variance Ratio (EVR) metric computed at inference that identifies challenging tasks by comparing max evidence to variance of evidence across all closed-set classes—challenging open-set samples yield low EVR because they have low max evidence but high variance; (3) an evidence-guided cross-attention mechanism that, when EVR is low, scales attention weights involving the nearest class by ε/EVR to force different closed-set classes to attend to each other, producing a more compact transformed prototype P_ε' that increases the detection distance d(P_a', P_ε') relative to the standard d(P_a', P'). A theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) shows this distance inequality holds when ε/EVR > 1.

## Results

MET achieves state-of-the-art open-set detection AUROC across all four datasets in both 1-shot and 5-shot settings. On MiniImageNet 5-way, MET reaches 76.93% (1-shot) and 84.90% (5-shot) AUROC, surpassing the strongest baseline SnaTCHer (67.37% / 77.99%) by over 9% and 6% respectively. On TieredImageNet, MET attains 78.77% (1-shot) and 84.37% (5-shot), and on Caltech-101 it reaches 64.85% (1-shot) and 72.12% (5-shot). Closed-set accuracy remains comparable to baselines (e.g., 63.13% vs. SnaTCHer's 63.91% on MiniImageNet 1-shot), confirming that the OSR gains do not come at the cost of in-distribution classification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MET outperforms all nine baselines on every dataset and shot setting. Against the closest FSOSR competitor SnaTCHer, MET gains 9.56 AUROC points on MiniImageNet 1-shot (76.93 vs. 67.37) and 6.91 on 5-shot (84.90 vs. 77.99); on TieredImageNet the margins are 7.77 (1-shot) and 4.88 (5-shot). Compared to the other recent FSOSR methods TANE and Glocal, MET leads by 3.5–5.4 points on MiniImageNet and by 3.9–6.5 points on TieredImageNet. The classic open-set baseline OpenMax is surpassed by 5.26 points on MiniImageNet 1-shot, while standard few-shot methods (ProtoNet, RelationNet, FEAT) lag by 9–25 points due to the absence of any open-set detection mechanism. Even against the transductive baseline OSLO (which has access to all unlabeled test queries), MET still leads by 1.41 points on MiniImageNet 1-shot and 1.56 on TieredImageNet 1-shot, demonstrating that the evidential cross-attention approach does not require transductive access.

## Relation to Prior Work

MET extends the SnaTCHer/FEAT line of transformer-based prototype transformation for few-shot open-set recognition by identifying a specific failure mode: when open-set classes share features with closed-set classes, the softmax-normalized attention assigns high weights to the similar closed-set prototype, causing misclassification. The key departure is twofold: (1) during training, similar closed-set classes are repurposed as 'opponent' pseudo open-set samples with an evidential KL-divergence loss (replacing the entropy-maximization approach of PEELER) to learn more compact closed-set representations, and (2) at inference, a novel evidence-to-variance ratio (EVR) detects challenging tasks and triggers an evidence-guided cross-attention mechanism that further compacts the prototype representation. The evidential learning framework from Sensoy et al. (2018) is adapted from standard classification to the open-set detection setting, where the Dirichlet parameters are derived from inverse-distance logits rather than network outputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The opponent-class strategy is practically appealing because it eliminates the need for actual open-set samples during training—a common limitation of prior FSOSR methods like PEELER and Prior Networks—while still teaching the model to be uncertain about visually similar but distinct classes. The EVR mechanism provides a principled, task-level diagnostic for when the model's compact representation is insufficient, enabling targeted inference-time correction without retraining. Limitations include the reliance on semantic analysis or hierarchical structure to identify opponent classes (which may not be available for all datasets), the theoretical proof's dependence on non-negativity assumptions and 'high probability' arguments, and the fact that the approach is tightly coupled to the transformer-based prototype transformation framework. Open questions include whether the opponent-class concept generalizes to very large class spaces or to settings where no natural semantic hierarchy exists, and whether the inference-time cross-attention modification scales efficiently to larger N-way tasks.
