## Problem

Estimating the causal effect of algorithmic platform actions on user consumption (performativity) from observational data is blocked by a fundamental tension: standard causal inference requires overlap between treatment and confounders, but platform actions are typically deterministic functions of past consumption, guaranteeing overlap violations. This paper asks under what structural conditions on the platform–user interaction dynamics, observational data alone suffices for identifying performativity without any randomized platform actions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240318/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes iid observations of temporal rollouts R_K = {(x_{T-t}, u_{T-t})_{t=1}^K, x_T}, where x_t ∈ R^d is user consumption and u_t ∈ R^p is the platform action, under a Markovian assumption that current actions depend only on the most recent state. The output is the performative effect function PE_T(u, u') = E[x_T | do(u_{T-1}=u)] − E[x_T | do(u_{T-1}=u')], which in the linear case reduces to B(u' − u) for a matrix B ∈ R^{d,p}. Two finite-sample estimators are provided: a non-parametric adjustment formula estimator (requiring K=1) and a two-stage regression estimator (requiring K=2) with explicit 1/n convergence rates.

## Method

The core insight is to reframe the platform as a controller acting on a dynamical system, replacing the monolithic confounding set with an explicit temporal structure: x_t = f(x_{t-1}) + g(u_{t-1}) + ξ_t and u_t = h(x_t) + r(u_{t-1}). Rather than assuming overlap directly, the paper proves that two exogenous consumption shocks (full-support noise ξ_t) prior to time T combined with a 'responsive platform action' (surjectivity and full-rank Jacobian of r∘h) are necessary and sufficient for identifiability in the non-parametric case. In the linear setting, observing longer rollouts (K ≥ 2) with a single consumption shock suffices, governed by a Kalman-like full-row-rank condition on [DC, D²C, …, D^{K-1}C]. The two-stage regression estimator exploits this structure by first recovering C (consumption-to-action mapping) and H (state transition) via least squares, then regressing out both to isolate B, achieving a 1/n convergence rate under bounded dynamics assumptions.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) establishes that two consecutive consumption shocks plus a responsive platform action are necessary and sufficient for identifying the performative effect PE_T from observations of R_{K=1}, even when platform actions are deterministic functions of past data. In the linear setting (Theorem 2), a single fully-spanning consumption shock suffices when the auditor observes a rollout of length K≥2, with the required full-row-rank condition on [DC, ..., D^{K-1}C] becoming easier to satisfy as K grows. Empirically, on the RecLab recommender simulation (N=751), the adjustment formula estimator recovers TE₁=4.68 for the EASE recommender at K=1 (ground truth 4.76) with zero undefined terms, while K=3 yields 4–5/27 undefined terms (4.1–6.6% probability mass) and no valid estimate. On real avocado price data, overlap violations escalate sharply with confounder dimension: K=1 has 0/2 undefined terms, but K=9 has 73/145 (25.9%) for high-price interventions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its Markovian two-stage regression estimator (2SR) and adjustment formula estimator against double machine learning (DML) baselines (LR-DML and RF-DML from Chernozhukov et al., 2017) that impose a blanket overlap assumption over all confounders. On the non-Markovian synthetic system (Appendix D, Figure 5), the Markovian 2SR estimator outperforms both LR-DML and RF-DML in MSE for d ≥ n/32 when the misspecification parameter λ ≤ 1, and for d ≥ 3n/10 even when λ reaches 1000—meaning the Markovian assumption wins unless the system is both very high-dimensional relative to sample size and strongly non-Markovian. On the avocado price elasticity data (Appendix C, Figure 3), the adjustment formula estimator at K=1 has the lowest bootstrap variance among all three estimators, while RF-DML and LR-DML show stable variance across K but higher bias at larger K due to overlap violations. The key practical finding is that shrinking the adjustment set to K=1 (the Markovian assumption) reduces overlap violations and estimation variance at the cost of negligible bias, making it preferable to the model-free DML approach in the regimes studied.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Pearl's do-calculus and adjustment formula framework for causal identification, but departs from the standard overlap/positivity assumption that is typically infeasible in algorithmic settings where platform actions are deterministic functions of past observations. It draws on the performativity literature (Perdomo et al., Mendler-Dünner et al.) which has formalized the causal effect of platform actions on user behavior, and on control theory (Kalman rank conditions, system identification) to derive new identifiability conditions. The key conceptual departure is replacing the requirement for exogenous variation in the treatment (platform action) with exogenous variation in the outcome (user consumption) plus a 'responsive platform action' condition, exploiting the temporal structure of repeated platform-user interactions to shrink the effective confounding set via a Markovian assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first identifiability conditions for estimating performativity from purely observational data in settings where platform actions are deterministic, offering regulators a theoretical foundation for auditing algorithmic systems without requiring access to randomized experiments. The practical implication is that a Markovian modeling assumption (K=1) can dramatically reduce overlap violations compared to using the full confounding history, as confirmed empirically on both synthetic recommender data and real avocado price data. Open questions include unifying the longer-rollout and multiple-shock identifiability results, extending the linear-case proof techniques to non-linear settings, and quantifying the bias-variance tradeoff from Markovian model misspecification in more complex real-world systems.
