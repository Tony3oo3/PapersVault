## Problem

The debate over whether transformer attention weights constitute faithful explanations has been conducted almost entirely through empirical experiments, with no rigorous theoretical foundation on either side. This paper addresses that gap by mathematically analyzing a simplified attention-based architecture to derive exact and approximate closed-form expressions for both attention-based and post-hoc explanations, thereby pinpointing precisely where and why they diverge.

## Input / Output

The model consumes an ordered sequence of tokens from a dictionary of size D, padded to a fixed maximum length T_max with fictitious [UNK] tokens and sinusoidal positional encodings. It outputs a scalar logit f(x) for binary sentiment classification (positive if f(x) > 0). Explanations are produced at the token level: each of the d distinct tokens in the document receives a scalar weight indicating its contribution to the prediction, with sign (positive/negative) available only for gradient-based and perturbation-based methods, not for raw attention weights.

## Method

The paper studies a single-layer, K-head scaled dot-product attention classifier (token embeddings → per-head key/query/value projections → softmax attention over the [CLS] query → weighted value aggregation → per-head linear readout → average across heads) and derives two main theorems. Theorem 4.1 gives the exact gradient of the model output with respect to each token embedding, showing it decomposes into a value-path term (α_t W_v^T W_ℓ^T) and an attention-path term involving the deviation of v_t from the attention-weighted mean. Theorem 5.1, building on Mardaoui & Garreau's LIME convergence result, shows that LIME's limit coefficients are approximately an affine transformation of the attention weights that explicitly includes the final linear layer W_ℓ and the difference between each token's attention×value and the [UNK] token's attention×value. The key finding is that both gradient and LIME explanations incorporate the downstream linear transformation that attention weights ignore, and can produce signed (positive or negative) contributions, whereas attention-based explanations are always non-negative and blind to the model's final readout.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical: Theorem 4.1 gives an exact closed-form expression for the gradient of a single-layer multi-head attention model with respect to token embeddings, and Theorem 5.1 provides an asymptotic approximation for LIME's limit coefficients. Empirically, the single-layer model is trained on IMDB sentiment analysis, and the multi-layer variant (6 layers, 6 heads) achieves 82.22% accuracy. The theoretical LIME approximation (Eq. 19) matches the official LIME implementation with an average norm-2 error of 0.808 (std 0.219) across the full test set, even when the distinct-token assumption is relaxed. For the multi-layer model, the Pearson correlation between LIME coefficients and first-layer α-avg attention is ρ=0.672 in absolute value, confirming a significant but imperfect relationship.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares seven explanation methods (α-avg, α-max, LIME, G-avg, G-l1, G-l2, G×I) on the same sentiment-analysis prediction. Attention-based methods (α-avg, α-max) always produce non-negative weights and identify 'popular' as the most important word in absolute terms, disagreeing with all other explainers. In contrast, LIME and G×I correctly highlight 'questionable' as having a negative contribution to the prediction, while G-l1 and G-l2 (like attention) remain non-negative and cannot distinguish positive from negative influence. The theoretical analysis (Theorems 4.1 and 5.1) reveals the root cause: gradient and LIME explanations incorporate the final linear layer W_ℓ, meaning a token with high attention can still receive a zero or negative explanation if W_ℓ v_t ≈ 0, whereas attention weights ignore this entirely. The paper concludes that post-hoc methods (LIME, G×I) capture more faithful and actionable insights than raw attention weights, aligning with Bastings & Filippova (2020), though it acknowledges that no single explainer is universally superior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Mardaoui & Garreau (2021), who established convergence guarantees for LIME on text data, by deriving the explicit limit coefficients for a single-layer multi-head attention model. It positions itself at the intersection of two previously separate literatures: the empirical debate on whether attention weights constitute faithful explanations (Jain & Wallace 2019 vs. Wiegreffe & Pinter 2019) and the post-hoc interpretability literature (gradient-based methods, LIME, SHAP). While recent works (Wen et al. 2024, Li et al. 2023, Cui et al. 2024) have provided mathematical analyses of transformer behavior on specific tasks, none had previously derived closed-form expressions connecting attention weights to gradient and LIME explanations as functions of model parameters, which is the core contribution here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous mathematical demonstration that attention-based and post-hoc explanations capture fundamentally different information: the gradient and LIME coefficients depend on the final linear layer W_ℓ and the value vectors in ways that pure attention weights do not, meaning a token can receive high attention yet contribute negatively to the prediction. This gives theoretical grounding to the practical observation that attention is 'an explanation, not the explanation.' However, the analysis is restricted to a single-layer, non-linear-free architecture, and the multi-layer validation (Appendix E) is purely empirical with a modest correlation (ρ=0.672 in absolute value). The key open question is whether these parameter-level relationships persist in deeper, non-linear transformers, and whether the distinct-token assumption in Theorem 5.1 can be formally relaxed for realistic documents with repeated words.
