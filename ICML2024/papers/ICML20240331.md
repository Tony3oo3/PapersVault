## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks are predominantly static and ground-truth-based, making them vulnerable to data contamination, unable to capture open-ended real-world usage, and inapplicable to tasks lacking a definitive correct answer. There is no large-scale, open platform that evaluates LLMs through live, crowdsourced human preference in a statistically rigorous way.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240331/cropped_page12_idx11.webp)

## Input / Output

Each data point consists of a free-text user prompt (no preset questions), multi-turn conversations with two anonymous LLMs, and a binary human vote (model A wins, model B wins, tie, or both bad). The system produces Bradley-Terry coefficient scores for each model, an empirical win matrix, and simultaneous confidence intervals that yield an approximate ranking with a uniform validity guarantee (no model's rank is understated with probability ≥ 1−α). An active sampling rule adaptively selects which model pairs to present, concentrating votes on close matchups to reduce the total number of comparisons needed for stable rankings.

## Method

Chatbot Arena crowdsources pairwise comparisons from a diverse user base (90K+ users, 100+ languages) where two anonymous models answer the same user prompt and the user votes for the preferred response. Model scores are estimated via (reweighted) maximum likelihood under the Bradley-Terry logistic model, with sandwich robust standard errors for confidence intervals that remain valid even under model misspecification. An active sampling rule selects model pairs proportionally to the expected reduction in confidence interval width, improving sample efficiency by ~35% for win-matrix estimation over uniform random sampling. A sequential anomaly-detection procedure using exchangeability-based p-values and Fisher's combination test flags users whose voting patterns deviate from the historical distribution, protecting the integrity of the crowdsourced signal.

## Results

The active sampling rule reduces the number of votes needed to estimate the win matrix to a precision of 0.2 from 6,800 (random) to 4,400 (adaptive), a 54% improvement; for BT score estimation to precision 0.3, the reduction is 17,200 to 16,400 samples (5%). Crowdsourced votes show 72–83% agreement with expert raters, comparable to expert-vs-expert agreement (79–90%). Arena Bench, constructed from crowd-sourced prompts, reveals a significantly larger performance gap between proprietary models (e.g., GPT-4) and the strongest open models (e.g., Llama-2-70b-chat) than MT-Bench does, with GPT-4 achieving up to 96.7% win-rate on coding clusters versus only 53.3% on movie recommendation clusters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline comparison is against MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023b), a static, GPT-4-as-judge benchmark. Figure 4 shows that Arena Bench (built from live crowd-sourced prompts) produces a noticeably larger score gap between proprietary models (GPT-4, Claude) and the strongest open models (Llama-2-70b-chat, Mistral) than MT-Bench does, indicating that Arena's diverse, open-ended prompts are more discriminative of model capability differences. For the ranking system, the paper compares the proposed active sampling rule (Eq. 9) against uniform random sampling: active sampling requires 54% fewer samples to reach the same win-matrix precision (0.2) and 5% fewer for BT-score precision (0.3), with the advantage holding across all sample sizes shown in Figure 7. The paper also validates its crowdsourced votes against two human experts and a GPT-4 judge, finding crowd-expert agreement (72.8–83.1%) is within 5–10% of expert-expert agreement (79.4–89.8%), confirming that the crowdsourcing methodology produces reliable preference signals.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Bradley-Terry (1952) paired-comparison model as its statistical backbone, replacing the Elo rating system previously used for LLM evaluation (Bai et al., 2022; Boubdir et al., 2023) with BT coefficients estimated via reweighted maximum likelihood and sandwich robust standard errors. It departs from static, ground-truth benchmarks (MMLU, GSM-8K) and LLM-as-judge approaches (MT-Bench, AlpacaEval) by collecting live, open-ended user prompts and actual human preference votes at scale, following the spirit of DynaBench (Kiela et al., 2021) but applied to LLM chat at a much larger user base. The active sampling rule draws on sequential experiment design (Chernoff, 1992; Karimi et al., 2021), and the anomaly detection leverages Fisher's combination test with a Bonferroni-style correction, extending these tools to the specific setting of crowdsourced LLM evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Chatbot Arena has become one of the most widely referenced LLM leaderboards in the field, with over 240K votes from 90K+ users across 100+ languages, and its data is now a standard benchmark cited by major LLM developers. The validation that crowdsourced votes achieve 72–83% agreement with expert raters (comparable to expert-vs-expert agreement) provides empirical grounding for the credibility of large-scale human preference collection. Key limitations include a user base skewed toward LLM hobbyists and researchers rather than production users, a focus on helpfulness over safety, and the fact that the active sampling improvement for BT score estimation is modest (5%) compared to the win matrix (54%). Open questions include extending the framework to multimodal and agent-based LLMs, formalizing the anomaly detection via E-values or nonnegative supermartingales, and building topic-specific leaderboards.
