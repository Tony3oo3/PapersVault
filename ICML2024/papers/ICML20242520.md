## Problem

Existing machine unlearning methods, developed to satisfy the 'right to be forgotten,' focus exclusively on removing data influence while completely overlooking whether the unlearning process itself degrades adversarial robustness. No prior work has investigated whether an adversary can exploit the unlearning pipeline to craft malicious deletion requests that make a model significantly more vulnerable to adversarial attacks than before unlearning.

## Input / Output

The adversary requires access to the trained model parameters W*, a set of hard target victim samples (correctly classified under the current perturbation budget), a subset of accessible training samples S_e, and the unlearning algorithm U_L. The output is a binary selection vector Φ identifying which training samples to request for deletion, plus adversarial perturbations δ_v for each target. Notably, the unlearning requests are unmodified training samples (no perturbations), distinguishing this from data poisoning; the attack operates in both white-box (full model access) and black-box (substitute-model transfer) settings.

## Method

AdvUA selects unlearning samples by jointly optimizing two criteria in the representation space: (1) a weighted distance metric ℓ_wd that combines log-ratio relative distances (measuring local density via expansion models) and absolute Euclidean distances to identify samples whose removal pushes target victims into low-density regions, and (2) a direction alignment loss ℓ_dir that enforces cosine similarity between the unlearning direction H(x_i)−H(x_v) and the adversarial gradient ∇_H L(x_v,y_v;W^u), ensuring the density reduction occurs along the attack-relevant direction. The overall bi-level optimization (Eq. 3) maximizes adversarial loss over perturbations while selecting the forget set to maximize ℓ_wd and minimize ℓ_dir, solved via greedy and discrete relaxation. Two theorems provide theoretical grounding: Theorem 3.2 lower-bounds local density decline under Gaussian data as samples are removed, and Theorem 3.3 shows robust classification error increases with high probability when the training set shrinks below a threshold.

## Results

On adversarially trained ResNet-18 (CIFAR-10) and ConvNet (SVHN), AdvUA achieves approximately 88% attack success rate against FGSM and PGD, and 90% against CW, using only 100 malicious unlearning samples per target. In the black-box transferability setting on ImageNet, AdvUA boosts FGSM transfer success from 61–63% (baseline) to 92–96% with just 30 unlearning samples. For model stealing on IRIS, AdvUA improves extracted-model test accuracy by 1.89× at 100 queries (85.71% vs. 45.24%) and 1.58× at 200 queries (88.57% vs. 56.19%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares AdvUA against two unlearning-selection baselines (random deletion and kNN deletion) and one model-stealing baseline (Tramer et al., 2016). Against random deletion, AdvUA substantially outperforms: the random baseline yields only marginal robustness degradation, while AdvUA reaches ~88–90% attack success on defended models. The kNN baseline slightly improves over random by removing nearest neighbors in input space, but still significantly lags behind AdvUA because it lacks the direction-alignment mechanism that matches unlearning directions to adversarial attack gradients. In the model-stealing comparison (Table 2), AdvUA integrated with the Tramer et al. query-based attack achieves 85.71% test accuracy at 100 queries versus 45.24% for the original method alone—a 1.89× improvement—narrowing the gap to 1.05× at 300 queries where the baseline already saturates near 85.56%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of machine unlearning and adversarial robustness, a combination the authors explicitly note has not been studied before. It repurposes existing unlearning algorithms (first-order/second-order methods, SISA, unrolling SGD) and standard adversarial attacks (FGSM, PGD, CW) as building blocks, but introduces a fundamentally new threat model where the adversary manipulates the unlearning request set rather than the training data. Conceptually, it departs from prior malicious-unlearning work (Hu et al. 2023, Qian et al. 2023, Di et al. 2022) that focuses on data poisoning through unlearning, instead showing that the unlearning process itself—without any data poisoning—creates new adversarial vulnerabilities. The model stealing extension builds directly on Tramer et al. (2016) by augmenting query-based extraction with strategically selected unlearning requests.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work identifies a previously unrecognized security risk in GDPR-compliant unlearning pipelines: the very mechanism designed to protect privacy can be weaponized to degrade adversarial robustness, with adversarially trained models paradoxically being more vulnerable than naturally trained ones. The practical implication is that organizations deploying unlearning for compliance must now also consider adversarial security of the resulting model, a concern absent from current unlearning literature. Open questions include whether detection or defense mechanisms can identify malicious unlearning requests, whether the vulnerability persists under certified defenses with tighter bounds, and how it generalizes to federated or graph-based unlearning settings. The paper's scope is limited to white-box and transfer settings with relatively small unlearning budgets, leaving the black-box and large-scale regimes underexplored.
