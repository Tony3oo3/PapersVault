surprise_factor: The frequency analysis showing that receptive field expert selection is 'relatively random' across samples and layers, rather than following a clean depth-dependent pattern, is a mild conceptual surprise that partially validates the conv-early/attention-late heuristic while showing it is not universal. However, the performance improvements themselves (0.2–0.6% on ConvNeXt, 1.4% on ResNet-50) are incremental and consistent with what one would expect from adding capacity via extra kernel branches, so the overall contribution is more engineering than discovery.

## Problem

The size of spatial receptive fields in CNNs (e.g., 3×3 in VGGNet, 7×7 in ConvNeXt) has always been manually designed, yet no existing mechanism can dynamically select the optimal receptive field for a given input image. Large receptive fields introduce optimization difficulty and redundant computation, while small ones may miss long-range dependencies. The paper asks: how to design a module that automatically covers the appropriate receptive field in vision architectures without manual tuning?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241189/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images (224×224 for ImageNet classification, multi-scale up to 1333 for COCO detection, 512×512 for ADE20K segmentation) and produces class labels, bounding boxes with instance masks, or pixel-wise semantic masks respectively. A key operational detail is that during training, n parallel depthwise convolutions with different kernel sizes (e.g., 3, 5, 7, 9, 11) process the input simultaneously, but at inference the re-parameterization trick collapses all branches into a single equivalent convolution, so the deployed model has the same I/O interface and throughput as a standard single-kernel CNN.

## Method

MoRF replaces a single depthwise convolution (e.g., ConvNeXt's 7×7) with n parallel depthwise convolutions of varying kernel sizes, each treated as a 'receptive field expert.' A lightweight attention module (GAP followed by two FC layers with GELU) generates per-expert routing weights from the input. Two routing strategies are provided: hard routing selects the top-k experts via a noisy top-k gating mechanism (adapted from Switch Transformers), while soft routing computes a weighted sum over all experts for richer feature interaction. At inference, a re-parameterization step algebraically merges the weighted kernels and biases into a single convolution (W = Σ αᵣWᵣ, B = Σ αᵣBᵣ), preserving inference speed comparable to the original single-kernel architecture. The approach is instantiated in ConvNeXt (replacing the 7×7 depthwise conv in each block) and ResNet, and evaluated across classification, detection, and segmentation with consistent gains of 0.2–4% over baselines at matched parameter budgets.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K, MoRF with soft routing consistently outperforms baselines across all model scales: ResNet-18+MoRF(soft) achieves 74.6% top-1 (vs. 70.4% baseline, +4.2%), ResNet-50+MoRF(soft) reaches 79.5% (vs. 78.4%), and ConvNeXt-B+MoRF(soft) attains 84.2% at 224×224 and 85.3% at 384×384. On COCO detection, ConvNeXt-T+MoRF(soft) with Mask R-CNN achieves 46.8 AP^b and 42.2 AP^m (vs. 46.2/41.7 for ConvNeXt-T). On ADE20K segmentation, UperNet+ConvNeXt-T+MoRF(soft) reaches 47.7 mIoU (vs. 46.7 for ConvNeXt-T), outperforming all ResNet-101-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoRF outperforms all directly comparable baselines. Against ResNet (He et al., 2016), MoRF(soft) gains +4.2% on ResNet-18 (74.6% vs. 70.4%) and +1.1% on ResNet-50 (79.5% vs. 78.4%). Against dynamic convolution (DY-ResNet, Chen et al., 2020), MoRF(soft) wins by 0.8% on ResNet-18 (74.6% vs. 73.8%) and 0.5% on ResNet-50 (79.5% vs. 79.0%) while using fewer parameters (20M/37M vs. 45M/101M). Against SKNet (Li et al., 2019), MoRF(soft) on ResNet-50 achieves 79.5% vs. 79.2%. Against ConvNeXt (Liu et al., 2022), MoRF(soft) consistently adds 0.2–0.6% across T/S/B scales. Against Swin Transformer (Liu et al., 2021), MoRF(soft) on ConvNeXt-T/S/B beats Swin-T/S/B by 1.3%, 0.7%, and 0.9% respectively. Against Focal Modulation (Yang et al., 2022), MoRF(soft) wins by 0.4%, 0.2%, and 0.4% at T/S/B scales. Against AS-MLP (Lian et al., 2022), MoRF(soft) leads by 1.3%, 0.6%, and 0.9%. In detection (COCO), ConvNeXt-T+MoRF(soft) surpasses PVT-Small (Wang et al., 2021) by 3.8 AP^b (46.8 vs. 43.0). In segmentation (ADE20K), UperNet+ConvNeXt-T+MoRF(soft) at 47.7 mIoU outperforms DANet (45.2), DeepLabv3+ (44.1), ACNet (45.9), DNL (46.0), OCRNet (45.3), and UperNet+ResNet-101 (44.9) with ResNet-101 backbones.

## Relation to Prior Work

MoRF sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) the Mixture of Experts paradigm (Fedus et al., 2021; Lepikhin et al., 2020), from which it borrows the top-k noisy gating mechanism for routing, but repurposes it for receptive field selection rather than model scaling; (2) re-parameterization techniques (RepVGG, DiracNets) that enable training-time multi-branch architectures to collapse into a single kernel at inference; and (3) the ConvNeXt/ResNet family of CNN backbones where the 7×7 or 3×3 depthwise convolution is the primary spatial operation. The paper explicitly distinguishes itself from dynamic convolution (which generates weights for fixed-size kernels), SKNet (limited to two kernels), RF-Next (not input-dependent), and NAS (requires a separate search phase), positioning MoRF as an input-adaptive, re-parameterizable multi-kernel module.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a long-standing manual design choice in CNNs—kernel size selection—by making it input-dependent and learnable, which is a practical and drop-in improvement for existing architectures. The frequency analysis (Figure 3) offers a mild but useful insight: the 'convolutions early, attention late' heuristic is only partially correct, as some samples prefer larger receptive fields even in early layers. Key limitations include the small number of experts tested (7 vs. 128 in MoE), the increased training cost that re-parameterization does not address, and the fact that the approach has not yet been extended to transformer architectures (e.g., varying attention window sizes). Whether the modest 0.2–0.6% gains on ConvNeXt scale to larger expert counts or different architectural families remains an open question.
