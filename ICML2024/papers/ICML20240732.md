## Problem

Empirical ML research suffers from widespread non-replicability, with published results often proving overly optimistic and failing to reproduce in subsequent studies. The paper argues this stems from a fundamental mischaracterization: most empirical ML research is conducted and perceived as confirmatory (hypothesis-testing) research when it should be considered exploratory, compounded by biased experimental designs, lack of legitimacy for pure experimental science, and insufficient operationalization of abstract concepts.

## Input / Output

As a position paper, the work synthesizes scattered evidence from ML, bioinformatics, psychology, and medicine to produce a unified epistemic framework for empirical ML research. The output is a set of concrete, actionable recommendations directed at three stakeholder groups—junior researchers, senior researchers, and venue organizers—spanning experimental design practices, publication incentives, infrastructure needs, and curriculum reforms.

## Method

The paper's core argument reframes empirical ML through the exploratory-confirmatory continuum, contending that the field's strong roots in formal sciences and engineering create a structural bias toward treating method-development experiments as confirmatory when they are fundamentally exploratory. It identifies three interlocking problems—biased experiments favoring new methods, lack of legitimacy for experimental science that neither proves theorems nor improves applications, and conceptual/operationalization gaps that undermine experimental validity—and maps each onto this epistemic framework. The proposed solution rests on three pillars: more insight-oriented exploratory research focused on understanding algorithmic behavior rather than merely improving performance metrics, more actual confirmatory research in the form of neutral method comparisons and replication studies with proper uncertainty quantification, and improved infrastructure including open databases, benchmarking tools, and dedicated publication venues. A detailed critique of routine statistical hypothesis testing in ML runs throughout, arguing that SHT assumptions are typically violated in benchmark experiments and that statistical significance should not be conflated with scientific importance.

## Results

This is a position paper without quantitative experimental results. The authors do not propose or evaluate a new method; instead, they synthesize evidence from the literature (e.g., Christodoulou et al. 2019 finding no ML advantage over logistic regression in clinical prediction, Melis et al. 2018 showing regularized LSTMs outperforming newer architectures, Henderson et al. 2018 documenting non-determinism in deep RL) to argue that most current empirical ML research is exploratory in nature but is conducted and reported as if it were confirmatory. The paper's 'results' are conceptual: identifying three core problems (lack of unbiased experiments, lack of legitimacy for experimental science, lack of conceptual clarity/operationalization) and proposing a framework of exploratory-confirmatory continuum with specific recommendations for venues, researchers, and curricula.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare methods against baselines in the traditional experimental sense. Instead, it positions itself relative to prior methodological critiques in the ML literature. The authors explicitly build on and extend the work of Nakkiran & Belkin (2022) on incentivizing empirical science, Sculley et al. (2018) on empirical rigor, Drummond (2006/2009) on ML as an experimental science, and Bouthillier et al. (2019) on exploratory vs. empirical research. They argue that prior work has two shortcomings: it addresses only specific aspects without a comprehensive picture, and it suffers from terminological confusion (e.g., Bouthillier et al. conflate 'exploratory' and 'empirical'; Nakkiran & Belkin use 'good experimental research' without clear operationalization). The paper's novel contribution is a unified statistical-science framing that distinguishes insight-oriented exploratory research from method-developing exploratory research, and from (actual) confirmatory research such as neutral method comparisons and replication studies, while also cautioning against routine use of statistical hypothesis testing in ML benchmarking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper synthesizes and unifies a long line of warnings about empirical ML methodology—spanning Langley (1988), Drummond (2006), Sculley et al. (2018), Bouthillier et al. (2019), and Nakkiran & Belkin (2022)—while explicitly addressing two shortcomings of that prior literature: fragmented coverage of specific issues and inconsistent terminology. Its key conceptual departure is the reframing of most current empirical ML research as exploratory rather than confirmatory, drawing on the exploratory/confirmatory continuum from psychology (Tukey, 1980; Fife & Rodgers, 2022) and the four-phase methodological research framework from biostatistics (Heinze et al., 2024). The paper also imports operationalization and validity concepts from philosophy of science (Chang, 2004; Bridgman, 1927) and draws parallels to the replication crises in psychology and medicine to argue that ML's situation is neither unique nor new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical implication is that without a cultural and structural shift—toward more neutral method comparisons, replication studies, insight-oriented exploratory research, and proper operationalization—ML's non-replicable research problem will worsen, potentially triggering a backlash against ML adoption in high-stakes domains like medicine. A key open question the paper highlights but does not resolve is how to define the 'population of data-generating processes' needed for valid generalization from benchmark studies, a problem Dietterich (1998) called 'perhaps the most fundamental and difficult question in machine learning.' The paper also leaves open how to restructure publication incentives and reviewer guidelines to reward the types of research it advocates, acknowledging that practical changes alone (new venues, reproducibility programs) are insufficient without a deeper epistemic mindset shift.
