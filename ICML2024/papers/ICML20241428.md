## Problem

Existing open-ended RL benchmarks are either too slow for practical research without large compute clusters (Crafter, NetHack, Minecraft) or too simple to pose a meaningful challenge (Minigrid, Procgen). This paper addresses the gap by building a JAX-based environment that combines the mechanical complexity of Crafter and NetHack with GPU-accelerated speed, enabling open-ended RL experimentation on a single GPU in under an hour.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241428/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives either pixel observations (63×63×3 for Craftax-Classic, 110×130×3 for Craftax) or symbolic observations (flat vectors of 1345 or 8268 dimensions encoding one-hot block/item/creature types, per-tile light levels, and scaled inventory/attribute values). Actions are discrete (17 or 43 options) covering movement, crafting, combat, and exploration, with invalid actions defaulting to no-ops. Reward is sparse and achievement-based: 22 achievements in Classic and 65 in Craftax, tiered into four difficulty categories worth 1, 3, 5, or 8 points respectively, plus small ±0.1 penalties/rewards for damage taken or recovered.

## Method

The core contribution is a JAX-based environment that reimplements Crafter (Craftax-Classic) and extends it with NetHack-inspired mechanics (Craftax), achieving 250× speedup over the Python original through full GPU compilation, up to 4096 parallel workers, and an 'optimistic environment reset' technique that generates only M ≪ N new procedurally generated worlds per timestep to amortize the expensive multi-floor generation cost. Craftax introduces 9 distinct procedurally generated floors, 19 creatures with unique combat mechanics, a three-element (physical/fire/ice) damage and defense system, randomly permuted potion effects each episode, an experience-point attribute allocation system, and a final boss floor. The evaluation framework defines two challenges: Craftax-1B (1 billion steps, targeting exploration and long-term planning) and Craftax-1M (1 million steps, targeting sample efficiency). Baselines span PPO, PPO-RNN, intrinsic reward methods (RND, ICM, E3B), and UED methods (PLR, ACCEL, DR), all run end-to-end in JAX. The key empirical finding is that none of these methods make material progress beyond basic achievements, establishing Craftax as a genuinely open challenge for future RL research.

## Results

Craftax-Classic achieves 405,618 steps/sec and Craftax achieves 266,961 steps/sec on a single RTX 4090 with 4096 parallel workers, making them 250× and 169× faster than Crafter (1,580 steps/sec) respectively. On Craftax-Classic, PPO-RNN reaches ~90% of the maximum achievable reward (226) within 1 billion timesteps (51 minutes on a single GPU). On the harder Craftax-1B benchmark, PPO-RNN is the best-performing algorithm, mastering most basic and some intermediate achievements, while no tested exploration method (RND, ICM, E3B) provides any improvement over vanilla PPO, and E3B actually reduces reward significantly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Craftax-1B, PPO-RNN clearly outperforms PPO, ICM, RND, and E3B in total reward, with memory (GRU) enabling significantly more dungeon exploration and access to higher-reward achievements. Notably, all three intrinsic-reward exploration methods (RND, ICM, E3B) fail to improve over vanilla PPO, and E3B substantially degrades performance, suggesting the achievement-based reward is dense enough that additional novelty signals are counterproductive. Among UED methods, PLR is the only approach that significantly outperforms Domain Randomisation (DR), while Robust PLR (PLR⊥) and all three ACCEL variants (Noise, Swap, RSwap) perform at or below DR. In speed, Craftax-Classic is 250× faster than Crafter, 47× faster than NetHack, and 53× faster than Procgen, while Craftax itself is 169× faster than Crafter and 47× faster than NetHack. On Craftax-1M, all algorithms perform similarly with only basic achievements achieved, indicating the 1M budget is too tight for meaningful differentiation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: open-ended RL benchmarks (Crafter, NetHack, Minecraft, MiniHack) and JAX-compiled fast environments (Brax, Gymnax, Pgx, Jumanji, XLand-Minigrid). It builds directly on Crafter (Hafner, 2021) as its mechanical foundation, re-implementing it in JAX (Craftax-Classic) and then extending it with NetHack-inspired multi-floor roguelike mechanics (Craftax). The exploration and UED baselines (RND, ICM, E3B, PLR, ACCEL) are standard methods from the intrinsic-reward and adversarial-curriculum literatures, applied here as stress tests on the new environment rather than as novel contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The primary practical impact is making a genuinely complex open-ended RL benchmark accessible on a single GPU in under an hour, removing the compute barrier that has limited Crafter and NetHack research to well-resourced labs. The key open question the paper establishes is what algorithmic capabilities (beyond current PPO variants, intrinsic rewards, and UED) are needed to progress past the first dungeon floor, with the 10B-step experiment showing that simply scaling compute does not close the gap. A notable limitation is that the benchmark's difficulty may be partly an artifact of the achievement-based reward structure, and the paper does not explore whether alternative reward shaping or longer-horizon credit assignment could unlock progress. The finding that UED methods (particularly PLR) offer only marginal gains over domain randomization in this complex setting raises questions about whether UED's benefits, demonstrated on simpler gridworlds, transfer to richly structured environments.
