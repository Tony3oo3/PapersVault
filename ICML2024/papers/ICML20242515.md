## Problem

Existing video foundation models struggle to simultaneously excel on appearance-heavy tasks (e.g., action recognition) and motion-centric reasoning (e.g., temporal localization), with no single frozen encoder achieving state-of-the-art across the full spectrum of video understanding tasks. A key data challenge is that the vast majority of real-world videos lack clean text descriptions, so pretraining must primarily leverage the video modality while still exploiting the noisy parallel text (ASR transcripts, generated captions) that does exist.

## Input / Output

VideoPrism takes a video clip of 16 frames at 288×288 resolution (patch size 18×18, yielding 2048 spatiotemporal tokens) and produces a sequence of token embeddings that preserve both spatial and temporal dimensions—deliberately omitting the global average pooling layer used in ViViT to retain fine-grained features needed for spatiotemporal localization. For generative tasks (captioning, QA), the frozen encoder is connected to PaLM-2 via lightweight gluing layers; for discriminative tasks, task-specific heads (MAP, G-TAD, etc.) are trained on top of the frozen features. The model is evaluated in a frozen-encoder setting across 33 benchmarks spanning classification, retrieval, captioning, QA, and CV-for-science.

## Method

VideoPrism uses a ViViT-style factorized spatial-temporal Transformer (ViT-giant with 1B spatial parameters or ViT-Base) pretrained in two stages. Stage 1 performs video-text contrastive learning (symmetric cross-entropy with a MAP pooler) over 36M high-quality and 582M noisy video-text pairs, using alternating gradient descent across datasets to avoid easy negatives, with spatial modules initialized from CoCa. Stage 2 continues training on video-only data via masked video modeling (BEVT masking at 0.65 ratio) with two key improvements: (1) token shuffling—randomly permuting the encoder's output tokens before feeding them to a shallow decoder, preventing the decoder from copying unmasked tokens and forcing genuine reconstruction; and (2) global-local distillation—simultaneously predicting the Stage-1 teacher's global video embedding (via a separate MAP decoder without positional embeddings) and token-wise embeddings (via the shuffled decoder with positional embeddings), mitigating catastrophic forgetting of appearance semantics. This dual supervisory signal (text-derived semantics + contextual self-supervision) enables the single frozen encoder to capture both appearance and motion cues.

## Results

On the VideoGLUE benchmark (frozen backbone, 8 tasks), VideoPrism-B achieves 84.2% top-1 on Kinetics-400, 63.6% on SSv2, 67.4% on Diving48, and 40.4 mAP on Charades, outperforming all base-scale baselines by 5–12 points. Scaling to VideoPrism-g further lifts performance to 87.2% (K400), 68.5% (SSv2), 71.3% (D48), and 62.3 mAP (Charades), with the largest gain of +22.4 mAP over UMT-L on Charades. Across all 33 evaluation benchmarks spanning classification, retrieval, captioning, QA, and CV-for-science, VideoPrism sets the state of the art on 31, and no single baseline consistently ranks second, underscoring its broad generalizability. The VideoGLUE Score (VGS) for VideoPrism is 51.25, 13.6% higher than the next-best model (UMT).

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoPrism-B dominates all base-scale baselines (CLIP-B, VATT-B, InternVideo-B, UMT-B) across every VideoGLUE task, with the largest margins on Diving48 (+12.0 over UMT-B) and AVA (+9.5 over UMT-B). At the large scale, VideoPrism-g surpasses VideoMAE-v2-g, InternVideo-L, and UMT-L on all eight tasks; the standout gain is +22.4 mAP over UMT-L on Charades (multi-label classification) and +11.8 mAP on AVA-K (spatiotemporal localization). On zero-shot video-text retrieval (Table 3), VideoPrism-B exceeds VideoCoCa-g by 7.5 R@1 on MSRVTT text-to-video and 9.9 R@1 on ActivityNet text-to-video. For zero-shot video classification (Table 4), VideoPrism-g leads VideoCoCa-g by 4.4 points on Kinetics-400 and 4.3 points on SSv2-Events, while VideoPrism-B already matches or exceeds several larger models. On CV-for-science benchmarks (Table 7), VideoPrism-B surpasses domain-expert models on Fly vs. Fly (89.1 vs. 88.6 mAP) and ChimpACT (28.8 vs. 24.4 mAP), and VideoPrism-g extends these leads to 92.0 and 31.5 respectively. A notable finding is that no single baseline is consistently second-best across all benchmarks, indicating that prior models each excel on different task subsets while VideoPrism generalizes uniformly.

## Relation to Prior Work

VideoPrism builds on the ViViT factorized space-time Transformer architecture and CoCa's contrastive image-text pretraining for initialization, but departs from both pure self-supervised video models (VideoMAE, MVD) and pure video-language models by combining them in a two-stage pipeline: contrastive video-text learning to harvest semantics, followed by improved masked video modeling. The key methodological departures from prior masked video modeling work (VideoMAE, MVD, BEVT) are the token shuffling scheme to prevent decoder copy-paste shortcuts and the global distillation loss to mitigate catastrophic forgetting of appearance semantics. Unlike InternVideo, which glues a self-supervised model and a video-language model together via cross-attention without mutual influence, VideoPrism's two stages are sequentially coupled, with the first stage's embeddings directly supervising the second stage's reconstruction targets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VideoPrism demonstrates that a single frozen video encoder can achieve state-of-the-art results across 31 of 33 diverse benchmarks spanning appearance-heavy, motion-centric, and scientific video tasks, with no prior model consistently achieving second-best—suggesting the two-stage pretraining genuinely captures complementary signals rather than excelling on one axis. The first systematic evaluation of ViFMs on scientific datasets (ethology, neuroscience, ecology) opens a new application frontier and shows general-purpose encoders can match domain-specialized models. Key limitations include reliance on noisy parallel text, restriction to short 16-frame clips, and the frozen-encoder evaluation protocol, leaving open whether end-to-end finetuning or long-video extensions would further improve or alter the task-specific tradeoffs.
