## Problem

Standard random initialization for neural ODEs places the linearized system's eigenvalues near the origin, often in the right half-plane (unstable) and without regard for the numerical solver's stability region. This implicitly restricts the learnable parameter space, slows convergence, and causes performance degradation when the solver is changed between training and inference. No prior work connected solver stability regions to a principled initialization strategy for neural ODEs.

## Input / Output

The method is task-agnostic but always consumes inputs sequentially: individual pixel values (one at a time for MNIST/CIFAR-10), 32×32×3 image sequences (for latent dynamics), or multivariate sensor signal vectors (for time-series forecasting). In each case, the neural ODE integrates a latent state over the sequence length, and the final (or intermediate) state is mapped to the task-specific output—a class probability vector, a predicted image sequence, or predicted sensor measurements. For the time-series task, an encoder network (VAE-style) infers the initial latent state from the first input, and the ODE evolves it forward while a decoder head produces predictions at each step.

## Method

Stability-Informed Initialization (SII) constructs the feedforward network weights so that the linearized Jacobian A = W_n W_{n-1}…W_1 has eigenvalues sampled inside the chosen solver's stability region. A rejection-sampling algorithm (Algorithm 1) draws real and complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs satisfying |1 + λ + λ²/2 + … + λ^p/p!| < 1 for the solver order p and step size h. The weight factorization A = Λ_n Π_{n-1}^{-1} · Π_{n-1} Λ_{n-1} Π_{n-2}^{-1} · … · Π_1 Λ_1 distributes eigenvalue information across layers via Λ_i matrices (handling dimension mismatches with zero-padded blocks) while random Haar-orthogonal matrices Π_i ensure dense, non-sparse weights and break neuron symmetry. Biases are set to a small uniform distribution (ε = 10⁻⁴) to preserve the linearization assumption. This guarantees the model is stable from the start of training, avoids the right half-plane, and aligns the initial dynamics with the solver's stability region, enabling longer prediction horizons and faster convergence.

## Results

SII consistently outperforms PyTorch's default uniform initialization across all three experimental settings. On pixel-level classification, SII improves accuracy by 1.1 pp on sMNIST (99.0% vs 97.9%), 4.0 pp on spMNIST (94.4% vs 90.4%), and 5.7 pp on sCIFAR (73.3% vs 67.6%). On latent dynamics prediction, SII reduces normalized MSE by 35–70% (e.g., 0.176 vs 0.588 on 3D Room). On multivariate time-series forecasting, SII cuts MSE by 50–75% (e.g., 0.22 vs 0.57 on Air Quality, 0.11 vs 0.29 on Human Activity). The paper also reports faster convergence and reduced variance across random seeds with SII.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is PyTorch's default uniform initialization (U(−1/√d_in, 1/√d_in)), which the paper shows places learned poles near the origin and sometimes in the right half-plane, violating stability. SII wins on every dataset across all three task families: pixel-level classification (sMNIST, spMNIST, sCIFAR), latent dynamics (Mass Spring, Double Pendulum, Molecular Dynamics, 3D Room), and time-series forecasting (Combustion Engine, Human Activity, Air Quality). The largest relative gains appear on the 3D Room latent dynamics task (MSE_n drops from 0.588 to 0.176, a 70% reduction) and the Air Quality time-series task (MSE drops from 0.57 to 0.22, a 61% reduction). SII also consistently reduces the standard deviation across seeds, indicating more stable training. The paper attributes these gains to initializing poles inside the solver's stability region and further into the left half-plane, which avoids early divergence and eases learning of fast dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of neural ODE training (Chen et al., 2018) and classical numerical ODE solver stability theory (Ascher & Petzold, 1998), bridging two communities that had largely treated solver choice as a discretization-accuracy concern. Prior work (Zhuang et al., 2020; Queiruga et al., 2020; Ott et al., 2021) observed performance degradation when switching solvers between training and inference but attributed it to discretization error; this paper reframes the issue through the lens of solver stability regions, showing they implicitly constrain the learnable parameter space. The initialization technique draws conceptual parallels to SSM initialization (Gu et al., 2020) and reservoir computing, but distinguishes itself by explicitly incorporating the numerical solver's stability region into the rejection-sampling criterion, a factor absent from those approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The SII technique offers a drop-in initialization that consistently improves both training speed and prediction accuracy across pixel-level classification, latent dynamics prediction, and multivariate time-series forecasting, with the largest gains (e.g., 3× MSE reduction on 3D Room) appearing at longer prediction horizons where stability is most critical. The observation that learned model poles are systematically confined within the solver's stability region provides a useful diagnostic for practitioners choosing solvers and step sizes. Key limitations include restriction to feedforward architectures with hidden dimensions at least as large as the state dimension, and consideration of only fixed-step explicit Runge-Kutta methods; extending the framework to implicit solvers, variable-step methods, and deeper or convolutional architectures remains open. The connection to spectral bias—where models struggle to learn fast dynamics (poles far into the left half-plane)—is noted but not deeply explored, suggesting a promising direction for future work.
