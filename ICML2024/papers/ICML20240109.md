## Problem

In multi-task learning, conflicting gradients with different magnitudes cause the average-loss update to be dominated by the largest gradient, degrading performance on smaller tasks. Existing gradient manipulation methods (MGDA, Nash-MTL, PCGrad, CAGrad) each embody a single fixed fairness notion, and no unified framework exists to flexibly select among different fairness criteria (max-min, proportional, minimum potential delay, etc.) within a single MTL optimization procedure.

## Input / Output

At each iteration the method consumes the current model parameters θ, the K task gradients g_1,...,g_K, and a user-chosen fairness parameter α ∈ [0,1)∪(1,∞). It produces a shared update direction d constrained to the ball B_ε (radius ε) with the additional requirement g_i^T d ≥ 0 for every task, along with non-negative task weights w ∈ R_+^K. The update direction is not a simple weighted average but is derived from a nonlinear equation coupling the Gram matrix G^T G with the weights, and the step size η_t is set adaptively from the weights to guarantee monotone decrease of the average loss.

## Method

FairGrad models MTL as an α-fair utility maximization problem by treating the per-task loss decrease rate g_i^T d as an analog of transmission rate in communication networks, with the ball constraint B_ε playing the role of link capacity. The key derivation shows that at the optimum the gradient of the total α-fair utility is aligned with d, yielding the nonlinear equation G^T G w = w^{−1/α} (element-wise power), which is solved efficiently as a constrained nonlinear least-squares problem via scipy.optimize.least_squares rather than the sequence of convex subproblems used by Nash-MTL. Different α values recover Linear Scalarization (α→0), Nash-MTL/proportional fairness (α→1), minimum potential delay fairness (α=2), and max-min fairness (α→∞), giving a single tunable knob for the type of task balance. The paper proves convergence to a Pareto stationary point under standard L-smoothness and linear-independence assumptions, and additionally shows that applying the α-fair loss transformation l_i → l_i^{1−α}/(1−α) to existing methods (LS, DWA, MGDA, PCGrad, CAGrad, etc.) preserves the Pareto front while significantly improving empirical performance.

## Results

On CelebA (40 tasks), FairGrad achieves the best Mean Rank (MR) of 4.95 and the lowest average performance drop (Δm%) of 0.37%, outperforming all 11 baselines. On QM9 (11 tasks), FairGrad attains the best Δm% of 57.9% while achieving a competitive MR of 3.82 (vs. Nash-MTL's 3.64). On dense prediction benchmarks, FairGrad leads on both metrics: NYU-v2 (MR 2.67, Δm% −4.66%) and Cityscapes (MR 1.50, Δm% 5.18%). On the MT10 reinforcement learning benchmark, FairGrad achieves 0.84±0.07 mean success rate, competitive with CAGrad (0.83) and above the reproduced Nash-MTL (0.80).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FairGrad achieves the best or tied-best results on 4 out of 5 benchmarks. On CelebA (40 tasks), it ties IMTL-G on MR (4.95) but substantially outperforms it on Δm% (0.37% vs. 0.84%), and beats the next-best FAMO (MR 5.03, Δm% 1.21%). On QM9, FairGrad wins on Δm% (57.9% vs. Nash-MTL's 62.0% and FAMO's 58.5%) but trails Nash-MTL slightly on MR (3.82 vs. 3.64). On NYU-v2, FairGrad dominates all baselines on both metrics (MR 2.67, Δm% −4.66%), with the closest competitor being Nash-MTL (MR 3.11, Δm% −4.04%). On Cityscapes, FairGrad achieves the best MR (1.50) and Δm% (5.18%), outperforming Nash-MTL (MR 3.25, Δm% 6.82) and MoCo (MR 4.50, Δm% 9.90%). On MT10, FairGrad (0.84±0.07) is competitive but does not clearly surpass CAGrad (0.83±0.05) or the originally reported Nash-MTL (0.91±0.03), though it exceeds the reproduced Nash-MTL (0.80±0.13) and all other baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the gradient manipulation landscape for multi-task learning (MGDA, PCGrad, CAGrad, Nash-MTL) under a single α-fairness framework borrowed from network resource allocation (Mo & Walrand, 2000). It builds most directly on Nash-MTL (Navon et al., 2022), which corresponds to the α→1 (proportional fairness) special case, but departs from it both conceptually (network utility maximization rather than bargaining game theory) and algorithmically (solving a nonlinear least-squares subproblem rather than a sequence of convex problems via CCP). The α-fair loss transformation in Section 6 is a separate, orthogonal contribution that repurposes the same α-fairness idea to rescale task losses before feeding them into any existing MTL method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The α parameter gives practitioners a principled, interpretable knob to trade off fairness criteria (max-min, proportional, MPD) without redesigning the optimizer, and the α-fair loss transformation offers a drop-in improvement for a wide range of existing MTL methods. However, the RL results (Table 4) show FairGrad is competitive but not clearly superior to Nash-MTL or CAGrad, and the linear-independence-of-gradients assumption (Assumption 7.1) is strong and may not hold in practice for highly correlated tasks. Open questions include whether the framework scales to settings with significantly heterogeneous task difficulties and whether the convergence guarantee extends to stochastic or non-convex settings without the linear independence assumption.
