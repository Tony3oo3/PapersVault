## Problem

Current simulation-based amortized inference methods are rigid: they require a fixed parametric prior, a fixed simulator, and a single inference target (posterior or likelihood) specified before training. They also struggle with unstructured or missing data, cannot handle function-valued (infinite-dimensional) parameters without discretization, and cannot exploit known dependency structures in the simulator. The Simformer addresses all these limitations simultaneously with a single, flexible architecture.

## Input / Output

The Simformer accepts observed data that may be irregularly sampled, partially missing, or specified as intervals rather than point values, along with optional parameter measurements and optional interval constraints (e.g., energy must fall below a threshold). A single trained network produces samples from any conditional of the joint p(θ, x) — posterior, likelihood, arbitrary parameter conditionals, or posterior predictives — simply by changing the binary condition mask at query time, with no retraining required. For function-valued parameters, the network operates on randomly subsampled finite-dimensional marginals, enabling inference at arbitrary time or space points.

## Method

The Simformer is a score-based diffusion model whose score function is estimated by a transformer. Each variable (parameter or data point) is tokenized as a triple of (learnable identifier embedding, value, binary condition state), and the condition mask M_C is randomly sampled during training across joint, posterior, likelihood, and random configurations, so a single network learns all conditionals. Domain knowledge about dependency structure is encoded in the transformer's attention mask M_E (directed or undirected), which can be dynamically adapted at inference time to account for d-separation changes under conditioning. Training minimizes a denoising score-matching loss on the joint distribution, with observed variables kept clean while unobserved variables are noised according to a VESDE or VPSDE. For interval constraints, the reverse diffusion process is guided by adding a gradient term ∇log σ(−s(t)·c(x_t)) derived from a constraint function c(x)≤0, enabling conditioning on arbitrary inequalities without retraining.

## Results

The Simformer is evaluated using the Classifier Two-Sample Test (C2ST) metric (where 0.5 indicates perfect alignment with ground truth and 1.0 indicates complete distinguishability). Across four standard SBI benchmark tasks (Gaussian Linear, Gaussian Mixture, Two Moons, SLCP), the Simformer outperforms NPE on all tasks except Gaussian Linear at 10k simulations, and incorporating structured attention masks (encoding known dependency structures) yields further accuracy gains, particularly on sparser tasks like Linear Gaussian and SLCP. Averaged across all benchmark tasks and observations, the Simformer requires approximately 10× fewer simulations than NPE to reach comparable or better C2ST accuracy. On the arbitrary-conditionals evaluation (Fig. 4b), the Simformer accurately models all conditional distributions across six tasks including two newly introduced ones (Tree and HMM), with C2ST scores close to 0.5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the Simformer against four baselines: NPE (Papamakarios & Murray, 2016), NLE (Papamakarios et al., 2019), NRE (Hermans et al., 2020), and NPSE (Simons et al., 2023). Against NPE, the Simformer achieves lower (better) C2ST scores on three of four benchmark tasks at all simulation budgets, with the sole exception being Gaussian Linear at 10k simulations; the structured-attention variants (undirected and directed) further widen this gap, especially on tasks with sparse dependency structures. Against NLE and NRE, the Simformer is competitive or superior in the extended benchmark (Figs. A5–A6), though NLE retains a slight edge on negative log-likelihood for the SLCP task where the likelihood is simple. Against NPSE (a conditional score-based diffusion baseline using an MLP rather than a transformer), the Simformer's posterior-only variant performs similarly, confirming that the transformer architecture and joint-distribution training do not degrade posterior accuracy. The most striking advantage is simulation efficiency: the Simformer reaches NPE-level accuracy with roughly one-tenth the number of simulations, a benefit amplified by structured attention masks that exploit known conditional independencies.

## Relation to Prior Work

The Simformer builds directly on graphically structured diffusion models (Weilbach et al., 2023), which introduced attention masks to encode dependency structures in diffusion-based generative models, and on the score-based SDE framework of Song et al. (2021b) for training via denoising score-matching. It departs from the dominant SBI paradigm of training conditional density estimators (normalizing flows) that target either the posterior (NPE) or the likelihood (NLE) by instead training a single transformer-based diffusion model on the joint distribution p(θ, x), enabling all conditionals to be sampled from one network. The guided diffusion mechanism for interval constraints follows Bansal et al. (2023), and the tokenizer design with condition-state embeddings is adapted from Weilbach et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Simformer substantially broadens the scope of amortized SBI by handling function-valued (infinite-dimensional) parameters, unstructured and missing data, and arbitrary conditional distributions in a single framework—capabilities that previously required separate, specialized methods. The ~10× simulation efficiency gain over NPE (via structured attention masks) and the finding that training on all conditionals does not degrade posterior accuracy (and can improve it) make the 'all-in-one' approach practically attractive. Key limitations remain: sampling is slower than flow-based NPE (requiring reverse SDE integration), transformer evaluation scales quadratically with token count, and log-probability evaluation is expensive. Open questions include whether the joint-estimation approach scales to very high-dimensional data (e.g., images or long time series) where the joint is harder than the posterior alone.
