## Problem

Traditional one-shot imitation learning (OSIL) methods assume near-identicality between the demonstration collection and deployment environments, effectively reducing the task to state matching and action replay. In real-world applications, unexpected changes such as new obstacles or disturbances inevitably arise after the demonstration is provided, requiring the agent to adapt rather than merely replay. This paper formalizes this more challenging setting and demonstrates that existing OSIL baselines degrade substantially under such unforeseen changes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the agent's current local observation (an 8-ray local view of 5-step length, with coordinates optionally provided) and a single expert demonstration as a full state-action sequence, producing a continuous action vector (e.g., (Δx, Δy) in [-1,1]). Unlike prior OSIL work that compresses the demonstration into a fixed-length context vector, DDT processes the entire demonstration sequence through cross-attention at every decision step. In the VPAM benchmark the agent has no access to the global map, and in partially observable variants even the coordinate is withheld, making the local view the sole spatial signal.

## Method

The core architecture is a demonstration transformer that implements a 3-stage 'Demonstration Tracing' process: (1) cross-attention identifies which demonstration states are relevant to the current state, (2) weighted value vectors extract the expert's behavior patterns at those states, and (3) a fusion layer combines the current state representation with the expert patterns to produce the action, repeated over N stacked layers. Rather than treating the demonstration as a free context vector, this architecture imposes an inductive bias that encourages the policy to adaptively trace the demonstration in any state, including states not visited during the demonstration. Training is formulated as context-based meta-RL using SAC, with a custom OSIL reward that combines a stationary imitation reward (with distance clipping η and exponential action reweighting to fix the ill-posedness of prior imitation rewards) with a rescaled task-specific ending reward. The meta-RL framework enables the policy to learn robust decision-making in unseen states through trial-and-error, going beyond behavior cloning. The paper further proves that under the Ω-recoverable MDP set assumption, a single demonstration suffices for a unified imitator to accomplish any task in the set.

## Results

On Meta-World with disturbance, DDT achieves 0.83±0.15 success rate on seen demonstrations and 0.61±0.20 on unseen demonstrations, while baselines drop to 0.08–0.23. On the VPAM navigation benchmark, DDT exhibits only 15% performance degradation when transitioning from training to unforeseen-obstacle conditions, compared to 20%, 33%, and 52% for the three baselines. On complex controller tasks (Gymnasium Reacher/Pusher), DDT reaches 0.97±0.01 (seen) and 0.95±0.01 (unseen), and on complex planning tasks (robot manipulation) it achieves 0.91±0.10 (seen) and 0.76±0.11 (unseen).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDT outperforms all three baselines across every evaluated setting. Against DCRL (Dance et al., 2021), which embeds demonstrations with a standard Transformer and trains via RL with task-specific rewards, DDT leads by 0.60 success rate on Meta-World with disturbance (0.83 vs. 0.23 seen) and by 0.49 on unseen demos (0.61 vs. 0.12). Against Trans4OSIL (Dasari & Gupta, 2021), which uses a Transformer for representation extraction followed by behavior cloning, DDT's advantage is most dramatic: Trans4OSIL scores 0.00 on Meta-World with disturbance in both seen and unseen settings, while DDT maintains 0.61–0.83. Against CbMRL (OpenAI et al., 2019), which trains purely with environment rewards and a GRU-based demo encoder, DDT wins on all VPAM and Meta-World settings; however, CbMRL is relatively competitive on the Complex Controller tasks (0.91 seen / 0.86 unseen vs. DDT's 0.97 / 0.95), suggesting that when the task is well-covered by environment rewards, the meta-RL signal alone is nearly sufficient. The largest and most consistent gaps appear under unforeseen changes (obstacles, disturbances), which is the paper's central contribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the one-shot imitation learning (OSIL) paradigm (Duan et al., 2017; Dasari & Gupta, 2021) by explicitly modeling unforeseen environmental changes between demonstration collection and policy deployment, a gap the authors argue prior OSIL work has largely ignored. Methodologically, it departs from behavior-cloning-based OSIL by formulating the problem as context-based meta-RL (building on Rakelly et al., 2019) and designing a stationary imitation reward inspired by Ciosek (2022) but with modifications to fix ill-posedness under single-trajectory settings. The core architectural contribution—a demonstration transformer with a 3-stage attention structure encoding identify-analyze-trace inductive bias—repurposes the Transformer's attention mechanism not as a generic context encoder but as a structured policy that adaptively traces relevant demonstration states at each decision point.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: real-world deployment of imitator agents inevitably encounters disturbances absent from demonstrations, yet standard OSIL methods degrade sharply (up to 52% performance loss) under such conditions. The Ω-recoverable MDP framework provides a clean theoretical condition under which a single demonstration suffices, and the VPAM benchmark offers a reproducible testbed for evaluating robustness to unforeseen changes. Open questions include whether the heuristic OSIL reward design generalizes beyond the tested environments, how the approach scales to visual/partially observable settings (where performance drops notably), and whether the log-linear scaling trend observed at small scale (60 demos, ~0.6M params) persists at orders-of-magnitude larger regimes needed for generalist agents.
