## Problem

Incremental topological ordering and cycle detection on DAGs have worst-case algorithms with high polynomial update costs (e.g., Õ(m^{4/3}) for sparse graphs), while simple greedy heuristics perform well in practice but lack worst-case guarantees. This paper bridges the gap using the algorithms-with-predictions framework, designing a data structure that is ideal—consistent under perfect predictions, robust under adversarial ones, and smooth in between—while also providing the first empirical evaluation of predictions for dynamic graph data structures.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a directed graph on n vertices where m edges arrive sequentially (incremental insertions), along with per-vertex coarse-grained predictions: an estimated number of ancestor edges α̃(v) and descendant edges δ̃(v) in the final graph. It must maintain a valid topological ordering L: V → Z at all times and report a cycle (then terminate) if an insertion creates one. The prediction model is deliberately coarse—only two integers per vertex rather than the full input sequence—making it robust to small perturbations in edge arrival order.

## Method

The paper contributes two data structures. The practical Learned DFS Ordering (LDFS) warm-starts a greedy DFS by initializing vertex levels from predicted ancestor counts, achieving O(mη + n) total update time where η is the maximum per-vertex prediction error; levels can increase by at most 2η, bounding the total work. The theoretical Ideal Learned Ordering decomposes vertices into subproblems H_{j,k} indexed by predicted ancestor and descendant levels (each vertex belongs to at most four subproblems), then runs the best-known worst-case algorithm—BK for sparse subproblems, BFGT for dense ones—on each, yielding Õ(min{nη + m, mη^{1/3}, n²}) total time. A key mechanism is an adaptive estimate η̂ that doubles whenever a vertex's level falls outside its predicted range, triggering a full rebuild with a coarser decomposition; the algorithm reverts to pure BFGT if η̂ exceeds n. The prediction decomposition ensures each subproblem has only O(η) ancestor/descendant edges per vertex, which is what makes the warm-started worst-case algorithms efficient.

## Results

On three real temporal network datasets (Email-Eu-core, CollegeMsg, Math Overflow), LDFS with only 5% of historical data as training explores over 36× fewer vertices and edges than the best baseline (DFS II) on Math Overflow (4.4e+4 vs. 3.9e+7 cost), and achieves a 3.1× speedup in running time on Email-Eu-core (0.071s vs. 0.226s). With 50% training data, improvements reach up to 116× in cost. The algorithm is also highly robust: even when noise with standard deviation 2×SD(predictions) is added to predictions, LDFS still outperforms both baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LDFS against two greedy DFS-based baselines: DFS I (LDFS with zero predictions, i.e., all initial levels set to 0) and DFS II (the algorithm of Marchetti-Spaccamela et al., 1993, also called FDFS by Baswana et al., 2018, which maintains exactly one vertex per level). LDFS wins on all three datasets for both cost and running time. On Math Overflow, LDFS(5) reduces cost from 3.9e+7 (DFS II) to 4.4e+4, a ~890× improvement, and running time from 18.373s to 0.094s (~195× speedup). On Email-Eu-core, LDFS(5) reduces cost from 3.1e+5 (DFS II) to 8.4e+3 (~37×) and running time from 0.226s to 0.071s (~3.2×). On CollegeMsg, LDFS(5) reduces cost from 6.8e+5 (DFS II) to 9.6e+3 (~71×) and running time from 0.336s to 0.021s (~16×). DFS I is consistently worse than DFS II on cost for Email-Eu-core and Math Overflow, but better on CollegeMsg, while LDFS dominates both baselines across all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithms-with-predictions (learning-augmented) framework—initiated by Kraska et al. (2018) and formalized by Dinitz et al. (2021)—to dynamic graph data structures, specifically incremental topological ordering and cycle detection. It builds directly on the best-known worst-case algorithms for this problem: BFGT (Bender et al., 2015) for dense graphs and BK (Bhattacharya & Kulkarni, 2020) for sparse graphs, using them as warm-started subroutines within a prediction-based decomposition. The key conceptual departure from the concurrent work of Brand et al. (2024) and Henzinger et al. (2024) is the use of coarse-grained predictions (two scalar values per vertex) rather than fine-grained predictions of the entire input sequence, making the approach more robust to small perturbations in the edge arrival order.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first dynamic graph data structure in the learning-augmented framework for topological ordering, and the first empirical evaluation of predictions on dynamic graph problems, bridging a gap between high-cost worst-case algorithms and simple greedy heuristics that work well in practice. The ideal data structure achieves the full consistency-robustness-smoothness trinity, guaranteeing never-worse-than-worst-case performance while matching offline optimal under perfect predictions. Key open questions include how to engineer the theoretical BK and BFGT subroutines for practical use (they have not been empirically evaluated), whether the coarse-grained prediction model extends to other dynamic graph problems (reachability, shortest paths), and whether nontrivial lower bounds exist that would make the current bounds tight.
