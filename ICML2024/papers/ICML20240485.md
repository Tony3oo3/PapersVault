## Problem

Standard RLHF pipelines use passive exploration, sampling response pairs uniformly from the language model without leveraging accumulated feedback to guide future queries. This paper asks whether active exploration—deliberately tailoring which response pairs are presented to human raters—can substantially reduce the volume of feedback needed to achieve high performance, and whether uncertainty estimates and the specific exploration scheme matter.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240485/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each query consists of a text prompt (sampled from Anthropic Helpfulness Base) and a pair of candidate responses generated by Gemini Nano (N=100 candidates per prompt, two selected by the exploration algorithm). The human feedback simulator, built on a Gemini Pro-based reward model, returns a binary preference via the Bradley-Terry model. The agent's output is a trained reward model (MLP head or ensemble of MLPs on Gemini Nano's last-layer embeddings) that, at assessment time, selects the best of N=100 responses, with performance measured as win rate over Gemini Nano on 2048 eval prompts.

## Method

The core method combines double Thompson sampling with an epistemic neural network (ENN) reward model. The ENN is an ensemble of S=10 two-layer MLPs (128 hidden units each) trained with a loss that regularizes each particle toward its initial parameters to maintain epistemic diversity; sampling an epistemic index z from Unif(1,...,S) selects which particle produces the reward, and variance across particles quantifies uncertainty. Double TS samples two independent epistemic indices and selects the response maximizing reward under each, guaranteeing two distinct candidates that each have some probability of being optimal. The agent uses a best-of-N selection procedure (N=100) rather than policy gradients, and trains the reward model via SGD/ADAM on a FIFO replay buffer of capacity 3200. The key empirical finding is that uncertainty estimates enable recovery from inaccurate reward predictions (the confidence interval keeps suboptimal responses in play), and that double TS outperforms both infomax (which maximizes information gain regardless of relevance to optimality) and Boltzmann exploration (which lacks uncertainty), reducing data requirements by roughly an order of magnitude at moderate performance levels.

## Results

The paper evaluates exploration algorithms using win rate (probability the agent's response is preferred over Gemini Nano's) on 2048 out-of-sample Anthropic Helpfulness Base eval prompts. Double Thompson Sampling (Double TS) with an epistemic neural network (ENN) reward model achieves the highest win rate across all epochs, clearly outperforming passive exploration, Boltzmann exploration, and Infomax. For the performance level attained by 30,000 passive queries, Double TS reduces data requirements by approximately an order of magnitude. The oracle reward model (Gemini Pro-based) achieves 75.5% accuracy on Anthropic Helpfulness and 74.8% on Anthropic Harmlessness eval sets, serving as the human feedback simulator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four exploration strategies: Passive (standard RLHF sampling), Boltzmann (point-estimate active exploration), Infomax (ENN-based information-maximizing), and Double Thompson Sampling (ENN-based, the top performer). Double TS dominates all alternatives in win rate across the full training horizon, with the gap widening over time. Boltzmann exploration is the best among methods using only a point-estimate reward model, confirming that active exploration helps even without uncertainty. Infomax performs competitively in early epochs but falls substantially behind Double TS later, attributed to its tendency to seek information about already-poor responses rather than focusing on identifying the best ones. The comparison between Boltzmann and Double TS isolates the value of uncertainty estimates, showing that ENN-based uncertainty enables dramatic improvement over point estimates. The comparison between Infomax and Double TS shows that even among uncertainty-aware algorithms, the specific exploration scheme (information-maximizing vs. probability-of-optimality sampling) drives large performance differences.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the contextual dueling bandit framework to the RLHF setting, combining double Thompson sampling (Wu & Liu, 2016) with epistemic neural networks (Osband et al., 2023a,b) for the first time in large language model tuning. It sits at the intersection of two long lineages: information-seeking exploration algorithms (MacKay, 1992; Houthooft et al., 2016; Sadigh et al., 2018) and the scaling of efficient exploration to complex environments via neural networks (Osband et al., 2016; Dwaracherla et al., 2020; Badia et al., 2020). The key departure from prior RLHF work is the explicit treatment of query selection as an active exploration problem rather than the standard passive sampling of response pairs, and the use of a best-of-N procedure in place of policy-gradient optimization to sidestep hyperparameter sensitivity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first empirical evidence that active exploration can reduce the number of human feedback queries needed for LLM tuning by roughly an order of magnitude, with the advantage potentially growing as data volume scales (concave scaling curves). This has direct practical implications for the cost of RLHF pipelines and the timeline to superhuman capabilities. Key limitations include reliance on a simulated human feedback oracle (Gemini Pro) rather than actual raters, a simple ensemble-of-MLPs ENN architecture, tuning only the reward head rather than the full LLM torso, and restriction to single-turn queries. Open directions include transformer-based ENNs, multiturn dialog exploration, and co-adaptation of the LLM torso during exploration.
