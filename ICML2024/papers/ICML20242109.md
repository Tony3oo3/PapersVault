## Problem

Distributed high-dimensional quantile regression faces a dual challenge: the non-smooth check loss complicates both computation and theory in multi-machine settings, and existing distributed methods (e.g., Chen et al., 2020) impose a restrictive independence assumption between the error term and covariates that is hard to verify in practice. Moreover, while parameter estimation rates in distributed systems are well-studied, the support recovery problem—identifying which coefficients are truly non-zero—remains largely unexplored for distributed sparse quantile regression.

## Input / Output

The method consumes data randomly partitioned across m machines (each holding n = N/m samples of (X_i, Y_i) pairs), along with a quantile level τ, a kernel function K, and two bandwidths: a global h ≈ (s log N / N)^{1/3} and a local b ≈ (s log n / n)^{1/3} with b ≥ h. A key design constraint is that local machines broadcast only two (p+1)-dimensional vectors per iteration (a gradient-like vector z_{n,k} and a Hessian-times-parameter product D̂_{k,h}β̂_{t-1}), reducing communication from O(mp²) to O(mp). The output is a sparse coefficient vector β̂_T and its estimated support set, with the guarantee that after a constant number of iterations the ℓ₂-error matches the single-machine oracle rate √(s log N / N) and the beta-min condition for exact support recovery aligns with the classical Wainwright (2009) threshold.

## Method

The core mechanism is a double-smoothing transformation that recasts the non-smooth quantile regression into a smooth ℓ₁-penalized least squares problem. First, a Newton-Raphson step with kernel smoothing defines pseudo covariates X̃_{i,h} = √(K_h(ê_{0,i})) X_i and pseudo responses Ỹ_{i,h}, converting the check loss into a quadratic loss. Second, to avoid transmitting (p+1)×(p+1) local Hessian matrices, the global Hessian D̂_h in the Taylor expansion of the global loss is replaced by the central machine's local Hessian D̂_{1,b} (with a separate local bandwidth b), yielding a 'shifted loss' that each local machine can contribute to via only O(p)-dimensional vectors. The algorithm iterates: at each step t, all machines update their pseudo covariates/responses using the current estimate β̂_{t-1}, broadcast their two vectors to the central machine, and the central machine solves a lasso problem via coordinate descent. This double-smoothing (global h for the loss, local b for the Hessian surrogate) is what removes the independence assumption between ε and X, since the density-scaled pseudo covariate absorbs the conditional density f_{ε|X} without requiring it to factorize. Theoretically, after a constant number of iterations (logarithmic in m), the estimator achieves the oracle rate and exact support recovery under a beta-min condition matching the single-machine lower bound.

## Results

Theoretically, DHSQR achieves a near-oracle ℓ₂ convergence rate of O_P(√(s log N / N)) after a constant number of iterations (bounded by a logarithmic function of the number of machines m), matching the single-machine lasso-quantile regression rate. Communication cost is reduced to O(mp) per round (broadcasting only (p+1)-dimensional vectors) versus O(mp²) for methods requiring full Hessian transfer. In simulations (p=500, N=20000, n=500, T=10), DHSQR's ℓ₂-error closely tracks the Pooled DHSQR oracle across all noise distributions (Normal, t₃, Cauchy) and both homoscedastic/heteroscedastic error structures, while achieving F₁-scores near 1 for support recovery.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DHSQR outperforms the DREL estimator (Chen et al., 2020) in all settings, with a decisive advantage in the heteroscedastic error case where DREL becomes unstable and fails to converge due to its reliance on the error-covariate independence assumption. Against DPQR (Jiang & Yu, 2021; Tan et al., 2022), DHSQR achieves lower ℓ₂-error, particularly under heavy-tailed noise (t₃ and Cauchy) where DPQR degrades significantly. The one-shot Avg-DC baseline is dominated by DHSQR on both ℓ₂-error and support recovery: Avg-DC produces dense estimates after averaging, yielding F₁-scores well below 1, especially when N is large relative to n. DHSQR's ℓ₂-error is nearly indistinguishable from the Pooled DHSQR oracle (which pools all data on one machine), confirming the near-oracle theoretical guarantee. In terms of support recovery, DHSQR, Pooled DHSQR, DPQR, and DREL all achieve F₁-scores close to 1, while Avg-DC consistently fails to recover the true support.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Newton-type distributed quantile regression framework of Chen et al. (2020) by removing the restrictive assumption that the error term is independent of the covariates, which is often violated in heteroscedastic settings. The key methodological innovation is a double-smoothing approach that applies separate global and local bandwidths to the kernel-smoothed loss, enabling the use of a local Hessian surrogate (from the central machine) in place of the full global Hessian. This design borrows the communication-efficient surrogate likelihood (CSL) philosophy of Jordan et al. (2019), reducing per-round communication from O(mp²) to O(mp). The paper also departs from prior distributed quantile regression work (Tan et al. 2022; Jiang & Yu 2021) by establishing the first support recovery (beta-min condition) theory for the distributed high-dimensional sparse setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a notable theoretical gap by providing the first beta-min condition for exact support recovery in distributed high-dimensional sparse quantile regression, matching the single-machine oracle rate after only a constant (logarithmic in m) number of iterations. Practically, the method handles heteroscedastic data where prior distributed methods (notably DREL) fail, and the O(mp) communication cost makes it scalable to high dimensions. Open questions include extending the framework to inference (debiasing and confidence intervals) in the distributed setting, developing fully decentralized variants over network topologies, and investigating whether the double-smoothing idea transfers to other non-smooth loss functions beyond quantile regression.
