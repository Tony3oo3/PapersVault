## Problem

Autoregressive models (ARMs) require O(D) sequential neural network forward passes to evaluate any marginal probability, making likelihood evaluation infeasible for high-dimensional discrete data such as proteins or long text sequences. Under energy-based training, this limitation is compounded because the KL divergence objective demands full log-probability evaluation per sample, preventing scalable any-order generative modeling. The paper asks whether a generative model can directly represent all induced marginal distributions of a discrete joint, enabling single-pass marginal queries and scalable training under both MLE and energy-based objectives.

## Input / Output

The model takes a D-dimensional augmented vector where each position holds either an observed discrete value (from a vocabulary of size K) or a special 'marginalized-out' symbol △, along with a binary mask indicating which positions are observed. This unified representation means the same network handles queries for any subset of variables without architectural changes. The marginal network outputs a scalar log-probability estimate for the specified marginal in one forward pass, while the conditional network outputs per-variable categorical distributions used for sequential or block-wise sampling in any variable ordering.

## Method

MAMs consist of two neural networks: a marginal network θ that maps the augmented vector to a marginal log-probability, and a conditional network φ that predicts one-step conditionals. The key training constraint is 'marginalization self-consistency'—the requirement that p_θ(x_{σ(<d)}) · p_φ(x_{σ(d)}|x_{σ(<d)}) ≈ p_θ(x_{σ(≤d)}) for all orderings, positions, and values—decomposed into K parallel per-value constraints to enable scalable mini-batch training via random sampling of (x, σ, d) tuples. Under MLE, a two-stage procedure first trains φ with the standard any-order ARM lower-bound objective, then distills θ by minimizing the self-consistency error, which is provably equivalent to the joint optimization. Under energy-based training, both networks are trained jointly by minimizing KL divergence between p_θ and the target energy distribution (estimated via REINFORCE with persistent block-wise Gibbs sampling) plus a weighted self-consistency penalty, avoiding the O(D) forward-pass bottleneck that limits ARMs.

## Results

On Binary MNIST, MAM achieves 0.146 NLL (bpd), exactly matching AO-ARM, while reducing marginal inference time from 132.4s to 0.018s (~7,000× speedup) with a Pearson correlation of 0.99. On CIFAR-10 and ImageNet32, MAM matches AO-ARM's NLL (2.88 and 3.60 bpd respectively) with ~4,800× and ~4,000× speedups. In energy-based training on the Ising model (D=900), MAM is the only method that fits in GPU memory, achieving 0.83 bpd and KL divergence of −685.8, while ARM and GFlowNet fail entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AO-ARM [24], MAM matches NLL exactly on all four MLE benchmarks (Binary MNIST 0.146, CIFAR-10 2.88, ImageNet32 3.60, Text8 1.48) while achieving 4–5 orders of magnitude faster marginal inference (e.g., 0.018s vs. 132.4s on MNIST). Against Probabilistic Circuits (EiNets [54]), MAM achieves substantially better NLL (0.146 vs. 0.187) and marginal quality (Pearson 0.99 vs. 0.75) at comparable inference speed. Against GFlowNet [88], MAM achieves lower NLL on Binary MNIST (0.146 vs. 0.189) and supports any-order generation, whereas GFlowNet sacrifices exact likelihood for learned generation paths. In energy-based training on the Ising model (D=100), MAM is marginally worse than fixed-order ARM [12] in KL divergence (−77.77 vs. −78.63) but is ~1,000× faster per step; at D=900, MAM is the only method that runs at all. The ARM-MC baseline (single-step Monte Carlo estimate) fails catastrophically with 24.84 NLL vs. MAM's 0.80, confirming the high-variance gradient issue identified in the paper.

## Relation to Prior Work

MAMs build directly on any-order autoregressive models (AO-ARMs) from Uria et al. and the ARDM framework of Hoogeboom et al., reusing their any-order conditional training objective as Stage 1. The key departure is introducing a separate marginal network that explicitly predicts p(x_S) for any subset S, trained via a 'marginalization self-consistency' constraint that decomposes the sum rule into highly parallel single-step constraints. The paper also identifies a formal equivalence between MAM's self-consistency condition and GFlowNet's detailed balance constraint under specific DAG and backward-policy conditions, positioning MAMs as a marginal-explicit counterpart to GFlowNets' flow-matching approach. Unlike probabilistic circuits, which achieve exact marginalization through structural constraints, MAMs use neural networks for greater expressiveness at the cost of approximate (but high-quality) marginals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: MAMs reduce marginal probability evaluation from O(D) forward passes to a single pass (4 orders of magnitude speedup on ImageNet32), and enable any-order energy-based training at D=900 where ARMs exhaust GPU memory. This opens the door to scalable distribution matching for high-dimensional discrete problems in molecular design, protein modeling, and physical systems. Key open questions include whether architectures can be designed to satisfy marginalization self-consistency by construction (eliminating the need for the consistency loss), and how well the approximate marginals generalize to out-of-distribution inputs, which the paper acknowledges as a limitation. The two-stage distillation strategy (fit conditionals, then distill marginals) also suggests broader applicability to other amortized inference settings.
