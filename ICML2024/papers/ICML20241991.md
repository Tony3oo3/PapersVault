## Problem

Simplicity bias—the tendency of neural networks to over-rely on a small set of 'simple' features—has been linked to poor out-of-distribution generalization, but prior theoretical characterizations required strong structural assumptions such as linear separability or one-dimensionality. This paper provides the first proof of simplicity bias and a precise mathematical characterization of learned features for general (non-linearly-separable) datasets in two-layer networks trained from small initialization via gradient flow.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241991/cropped_page7_idx10.webp)

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the method takes a binary classification dataset D = {(x_i, y_i)} in R^d with ||x_i|| ≤ 1 and a two-layer network f(θ,x) = Σ u_j φ(v_j, x) initialized at scale σ ≈ 0, and produces a characterization of which neurons become 'prominent' (aligned with global extrema of a data-dependent function G) versus 'non-prominent' (negligible contribution). For empirical validation, the input is the MNIST-CIFAR10 domino dataset (vertical concatenations of 28×28 MNIST digits and 32×32 CIFAR-10 images, labeled by CIFAR-10 class), and the output is OOD accuracy of logistic regression probes on last-layer features extracted at various training epochs, revealing how simplicity bias evolves over training.

## Method

The key insight is that for small initialization, the coupled gradient-flow dynamics can be approximated by a decoupled linearized system in which each neuron evolves independently according to a data-dependent function G(v) that is independent of the number of neurons m. The paper proves (Theorem 4.1) that prominent neurons align with the global extrema of G while non-prominent neurons grow at a strictly slower rate, and extends this to constant weight scale (Theorem 4.4) showing the network behaves as an effective p-neuron network where p equals the number of extrema of G. For XOR-like datasets, they further prove (Lemma 5.5) that in the infinite-time limit the network converges to a direction where all features align with the four cluster directions, causing extreme simplicity bias that makes the network forget non-simple features. The analysis uses a differentiable approximation of ReLU (convolution with a uniform disk) to ensure smoothness, and the theoretical predictions are validated on synthetic XOR data and a ResNet-18 trained on the MNIST-CIFAR10 domino dataset, where OOD accuracy drops in later training stages consistent with the extreme simplicity bias mechanism.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: it proves that for two-layer networks trained with gradient flow from small initialization on general (non-linearly-separable) data, prominent neurons cluster around the global extrema of a data-dependent function G that is independent of the hidden layer size (Theorem 4.1), and this alignment persists until weights reach constant scale (Theorem 4.4). On XOR-like data, the learned features are precisely identified as the four cluster directions ±e₁, ±e₂, and extreme simplicity bias is shown to intensify in the infinite-time limit (Lemma 5.5). Empirically, on the MNIST-CIFAR10 domino dataset, a ResNet-18 trained with small initialization peaks at only ~45% OOD accuracy (vs. ~90% on plain CIFAR-10 and ~99% using MNIST labels directly), and a statistically significant drop of 8.13% ± 0.80% (p ≈ 10⁻⁶) in OOD accuracy is observed between epochs 64 and 256, supporting the extreme simplicity bias mechanism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper without a traditional method-vs-baseline comparison table. The empirical validation in Section 7 uses the MNIST-CIFAR10 domino dataset (Shah et al., 2020) as a controlled setting: the same ResNet-18 architecture and training recipe achieves ~90% accuracy on plain CIFAR-10 but only ~45% OOD accuracy on the domino test set, confirming that the network relies on spurious MNIST features. The paper further contrasts two regularization settings for the logistic regression probe: with small regularization, OOD accuracy drops significantly in later training stages (evidence of extreme simplicity bias), while without regularization, OOD accuracy is preserved but last-layer weights grow ~50× larger (consistent with the theory that simple features grow disproportionately rather than non-simple features being explicitly forgotten). The XOR experiments in Section 6 validate the theoretical predictions by showing that with m = 4096 neurons and σ = 2⁻⁷, nearly all neurons align in exactly four directions (the global extrema of G) for both orthogonal (α = π/2) and non-orthogonal (α = π/3) cluster configurations, with non-aligned neurons having strictly smaller weights.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the simplicity bias analysis of Lyu et al. (2021) from linearly separable data to general datasets by introducing a data-dependent function G whose global extrema determine the learned feature directions, independent of network width. It builds on the linearized-loss approximation of Maennel et al. (2018) and the directional convergence tools of Ji & Telgarsky (2020), while leveraging the sign-preservation and layer-balancing invariants from Du et al. (2018) and Boursier et al. (2022). The key departure from prior work is the removal of structural data assumptions (linear separability, orthogonality, 1D data) that all previous theoretical characterizations of simplicity bias required, and the extension to the infinite-time limit for XOR-like non-separable data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication that networks trained to very small loss become harder to finetune for OOD tasks (evidenced by the ~8% OOD accuracy drop when using later-stage features) provides a concrete, actionable guideline: early stopping or lazy-regime training may improve OOD transfer. However, the theoretical results are limited to two-layer networks, and the extreme simplicity bias characterization for XOR-like data relies on an assumption about 4-neuron convergence that is only empirically validated. Open questions include whether these findings extend to deeper architectures, whether the even-dimension case (requiring o-minimal structure analysis) yields qualitatively different behavior, and whether the G-function framework can characterize feature learning in multi-layer settings.
