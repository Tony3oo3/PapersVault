## Problem

The unsupervised time series anomaly detection (TAD) field is plagued by a flawed evaluation protocol (point-adjust F1) that artificially inflates scores for noisy predictions, inconsistent benchmarking practices across papers, and a persistent lack of justification for the complexity of deep learning architectures. The paper argues that SOTA deep models have never demonstrated necessity over simpler baselines and that the field's apparent progress is largely an artifact of evaluation methodology rather than genuine algorithmic improvement.

## Input / Output

For multivariate data, input is a sequence of F-dimensional sensor readings where the training set contains only normal timestamps; for univariate data, each timestamp is encoded as a (w+1)-dimensional vector via a sliding window of w=4 preceding observations. The output is a per-timestamp anomaly score computed from error vectors (reconstruction or prediction errors), normalized via median-IQR or mean-std, and aggregated using the L∞ norm before thresholding into binary labels. Evaluation employs three complementary metrics: standard point-wise F1, the flawed point-adjust F1, and a range-wise F1_T that captures anomaly interval segmentation quality.

## Method

The paper proposes a suite of simple baselines—sensor range deviation, L2-norm, 1-NN distance to training data, and PCA reconstruction error—alongside minimal neural network blocks (single-layer MLP, single MLP-Mixer block, single Transformer block, 1-layer GCN-LSTM) trained in forecasting mode with fixed hyperparameters across all datasets. The central empirical finding is that these baselines match or exceed SOTA deep models (GDN, TranAD, Anomaly Transformer, DAGMM, USAD, OmniAnomaly) under standard F1 and range-wise F1_T, while the point-adjust F1 artificially inflates scores for all methods including a random predictor. A linear distillation experiment—training a single linear layer to mimic the outputs of trained SOTA models—shows that performance is nearly preserved, revealing that these complex architectures effectively learn linear mappings for the anomaly detection task. The paper further demonstrates high inter-model agreement on which anomalies are detected, suggesting current benchmarks are simultaneously too easy and too hard, and advocates for richer datasets with a spread spectrum of difficulty and dual point-wise/range-wise evaluation protocols.

## Results

On the four multivariate benchmarks (SWaT, WADI-127, WADI-112, SMD), the PCA reconstruction error baseline achieves the best standard point-wise F1 scores (0.833, 0.501, 0.655, 0.572) and the best range-wise F1_T scores (0.574, 0.557, 0.699, 0.580), outperforming all SOTA deep learning methods. Under the flawed point-adjusted F1_PA metric, a random prediction (p=0.5 per timestamp) scores 0.963 on SWaT and 0.894 on SMD—higher than most SOTA models—exposing the metric's bias toward noisy outputs. Linear distillation of SOTA models (e.g., TranAD: 0.799→0.800 F1 on SWaT; GDN: 0.810→0.808) confirms that these complex architectures learn functions that are effectively linear for the anomaly detection task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PCA Error outperforms all seven SOTA deep learning methods (GDN, TranAD, Anomaly Transformer, DAGMM, OmniAnomaly, USAD, MERLIN) on both standard F1 and range-wise F1_T across all four multivariate datasets, with the largest margins on WADI-127 (F1: 0.501 vs. 0.347 for GDN) and WADI-112 (F1: 0.655 vs. 0.571 for GDN). On the univariate UCR datasets, PCA Error achieves F1 of 0.990 and 1.000 on IB-18 and IB-19, beating MERLIN (0.795, 0.870) and LOF (0.915, 0.857), while 1-NN distance is competitive (0.889, 0.870). The 1-Layer GCN-LSTM NN baseline comes closest to PCA Error on WADI-112 (F1: 0.596 vs. 0.655) and SMD (F1: 0.550 vs. 0.572), but still trails on all other datasets. Critically, the random prediction baseline scores higher F1_PA than GDN (0.963 vs. 0.866 on SWaT) and Anomaly Transformer (0.963 vs. 0.941), demonstrating that the point-adjusted metric rewards noise rather than detection quality. Linear distillation of TranAD and GDN to single-layer perceptrons preserves their F1 scores almost exactly (e.g., GDN SWaT: 0.810→0.808), confirming that the added architectural complexity does not yield non-linear detection gains on current benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper departs from the dominant trajectory in TAD of increasingly complex deep architectures (Transformers, GNNs, adversarial autoencoders) by arguing that the field has been misled by flawed evaluation protocols and inadequate baselines. It builds on the critical observations of Kim et al. (2022) regarding the point-adjust F1 metric and Wu & Keogh (2022) regarding benchmark dataset quality, but goes further by empirically demonstrating that simple classical methods (PCA reconstruction, k-NN distance, L2-norm) and minimal neural network blocks match or exceed SOTA deep models. The linear distillation analysis repurposes knowledge distillation as a diagnostic tool to question whether complex models actually learn non-linear anomaly-detection functions, positioning the paper as a corrective to the field's methodological assumptions rather than a contribution to a specific method lineage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central finding—that SOTA deep TAD models reduce to linear mappings with negligible performance loss—fundamentally challenges the working assumption that temporal dependencies and inter-sensor correlations require deep architectures for anomaly detection. This has broad implications: if the current datasets cannot distinguish complex from simple methods, the field needs richer benchmarks with a spread spectrum of anomaly difficulty before further architectural innovation is meaningful. Open questions include what types of anomalies (if any) genuinely require non-linear modeling, how to construct datasets that properly stress-test method utility, and whether the evaluation shift to range-wise metrics (F1_T) and AUPRC will change which methods appear competitive. The paper's limitations include reliance on a small set of benchmark datasets and the fact that linear distillation was tested on only a subset of the compared models.
