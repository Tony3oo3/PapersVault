## Problem

Standard Neural ODEs (NODEs) for modeling complex dynamical systems suffer from high computational costs (ODE solvers embedded in the computational graph during backpropagation) and susceptibility to local optima, especially when the learned dynamics exhibit stiffness or drastic fluctuations. Additionally, estimating accurate gradients from noisy, limited observational data remains a fundamental challenge for data-driven system identification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241182/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes discrete-time observational data S = {s_0, ..., s_{N-1}} sampled at a regular rate, a known control signal u(t) (typically sampled from Gaussian Random Fields), and for PDEs, the spatial grid resolution N_x × N_y. The usable Fourier truncation frequency K is constrained by the Nyquist theorem (K < N/2), and the framework supports irregularly sampled data via nonuniform FFT. The output is a trained neural network F_θ that maps (state, control, spatial gradients) → temporal gradient; at inference, an ODE solver integrates this vector field to produce full state trajectories, and the model supports zero-shot super-resolution by predicting at higher spatial resolutions than those seen during training.

## Method

FNODEs replaces the expensive ODE-solver-in-the-loop training of standard NODEs with a simulation-free gradient flow matching objective: Fourier analysis (DFT/IDFT) is applied to the observational data to directly estimate the temporal derivative ŝ'(t_n) and, for PDEs, up to 4th-order spatial derivatives via multiplication by (ik_x)^p(ik_y)^q in Fourier space. A multi-layer neural network F_θ takes the state, control, and estimated spatial gradients as inputs and is trained to minimize the L1 loss between its output and the Fourier-estimated temporal gradient. A data augmentation loop then uses the trained model (outside the computational graph) to generate intermediate time points, increasing the effective sampling rate N and thus the recoverable Fourier frequency K, which in turn yields more accurate gradient estimates for the next training round. Theorem 1 provides a uniform convergence guarantee for the Fourier-estimated derivative under a Lipschitz condition on h'', and the framework extends naturally to resolution-invariant PDE modeling by training at low spatial resolution and predicting at high resolution.

## Results

On the 2-D parametric ODE, FNODEs achieves a test MSE of 0.012 (N_tr=1000, no noise) with a training time of 62.4 s, compared to 0.062 and 9632 s for NODEs with Dopri5. Across four PDE systems (KDV, DR, KS, NS) with N_tr=1000 and no noise, FNODEs yields MSEs of 0.023, 0.016, 0.11, and 0.083 respectively, while NODEs produces 0.45, 0.58, 1.69, and 3.61. Training time for FNODEs ranges from 65 s (DR) to 283 s (NS), exceeding NODEs by 10–77×. Under 5% Gaussian noise, FNODEs degrades modestly (e.g., 0.14 on KDV) whereas NODEs nearly doubles its error (1.02).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FNODEs outperforms NODEs by a wide margin on all four PDEs: with N_tr=1000 and no noise, FNODEs achieves MSE 0.023 on KDV versus 0.45 for NODEs (≈20× lower), and 0.083 on NS versus 3.61 (≈43× lower). Against DeepONet and FNO (neural-operator baselines), FNODEs is consistently better on KDV, DR, and NS, though FNO edges out FNODEs slightly on DR (0.13 vs 0.016) and KS (0.92 vs 0.11) at N_tr=1000; however, FNODEs generalises far better under 5% noise (0.12/0.46 vs 1.58/2.13). PDE-Net, which uses finite-difference spatial gradients, is beaten by FNODEs on every system, confirming the advantage of Fourier-based gradient estimation. PINN reports identical MSEs across all conditions because it requires the known governing equation and is retrained per sample, making it inapplicable to truly data-driven modelling. In terms of training time, FNODEs (65–283 s) is 10–77× faster than NODEs (2910–21747 s) and 2–30× faster than the next-best baselines (FNO, PDE-Net). On the ODE benchmark (Table 1), FNODEs achieves MSE 0.012 in 62.4 s, beating NODE-Dopri5 (0.062, 9632 s) and the central-difference 'Mid' baseline (0.072, 120 s).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018), which parameterize the vector field of an ODE with a neural network and train via adjoint-based backpropagation through an ODE solver. The key departure is replacing this simulation-based training with a simulation-free 'gradient flow matching' objective: Fourier analysis estimates the temporal (and spatial) gradients from observational data, and the network is trained to match these gradients directly, eliminating the ODE solver from the computational graph entirely. The framework also extends NODEs to PDEs by incorporating Fourier-estimated spatial derivatives as additional inputs, a strategy that connects to spectral methods but is applied here in a data-driven, architecture-agnostic way. The data augmentation loop—using the trained model to generate intermediate time points, re-estimating gradients at higher effective sampling rates, and retraining—repurposes the model's own predictions as a self-improving training signal, a strategy not previously combined with NODEs in this manner.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The >10× training speedup over standard NODEs, demonstrated consistently across ODE and PDE systems (KDV, DR, KS, NS, Lorenz, polar motion), makes continuous-time dynamical system modeling substantially more accessible, particularly for high-dimensional PDEs where adjoint-based training is prohibitively expensive. The resolution-invariance property (training at low spatial resolution, predicting at high resolution) and the ability to handle noisy, limited data with theoretical guarantees from Fourier convergence are practically valuable for real-world applications like geophysical prediction. However, the authors acknowledge that Fourier-based gradient estimation becomes challenging for highly complex or non-periodic systems, and the method inherits NODEs' inefficiency for very high-dimensional state spaces—integrating neural operators or Koopman-theoretic approaches are identified as natural next steps.
