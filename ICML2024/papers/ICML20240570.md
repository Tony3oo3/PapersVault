## Problem

Pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) are increasingly used as dense reward sources for sparse-reward RL tasks, but this paper identifies a critical gap: zero-shot VLM rewards are 'fuzzy'—they capture coarse task semantics yet are inaccurate in fine-grained details, causing agents to get trapped in local minima. Prior work assumed VLM rewards were sufficiently accurate for policy optimization, but the authors demonstrate through expert-trajectory reward curves and VLM-only policy visualizations that misalignment between VLM embeddings and actual task progress is systematic and harmful.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB image observations (84×84×3 with 3-frame stacking for pixel-based experiments), a short text task instruction (e.g., 'Press a button from the top'), and a fixed goal image provided at episode start; state-based experiments additionally use low-dimensional state vectors as policy input. The VLM backbone (LIV or CLIP) is frozen, and only two small two-layer MLP projection heads (256→64) are learned. The output is a trained SAC or DrQ policy that produces robot actions, with the dense VLM reward computed as the cosine similarity between the projected text embedding and projected image embedding, weighted by ρ=0.05 and added to the sparse binary task reward.

## Method

FuRL addresses fuzzy VLM rewards through two interacting mechanisms. First, reward alignment: the pre-trained VLM is frozen and two lightweight MLP projection heads are appended to the text and image embedding outputs; these are trained with a margin-based ranking loss that has two stages—Stage 1 (neg-neg) ranks negative samples by their L2 distance to the goal image in embedding space before any success is observed, and Stage 2 (pos-neg + pos-pos) enforces that successful-trajectory samples score higher than failures and that later timesteps within a success score higher than earlier ones. Second, relay RL: an auxiliary SAC agent is maintained alongside the VLM agent, and at each episode a random relay step T_i is drawn from {50,100,150,200}; the two policies alternate unrolling for T_i steps, with all samples stored in a shared buffer, allowing the SAC agent to escape local minima that the fuzzy VLM reward creates. Once 2500 positive samples are collected, relay RL is disabled and training proceeds with the VLM policy alone.

## Results

On the Meta-world MT10 sparse-reward benchmark (fixed goal), FuRL achieves a 76.0% average success rate across 10 tasks, substantially outperforming the SAC baseline (37.0%), the LIV baseline (28.0%), and the Relay baseline (54.0%). FuRL reaches 100% success on 6 out of 10 tasks (button-press-topdown, door-open, drawer-close, reach, window-close, window-open), while no method solves peg-insert-side or pick-place. On the random-goal variant, FuRL achieves 64.6% average success rate versus 49.8% for SAC and 57.4% for Relay. When instantiated with CLIP instead of LIV as the VLM backbone, CLIP-FuRL still achieves 70.0% average success rate, outperforming the CLIP-only baseline (38.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FuRL (76.0%) more than doubles the SAC baseline (37.0%) on the fixed-goal MT10 benchmark, and also outperforms SAC on the random-goal variant (64.6% vs. 49.8%). The VLM-RMs and VLM-RMs-GB baselines (Rocamonde et al., 2023a), which rely solely on zero-shot VLM reward without task reward, achieve 0% on all 10 tasks, confirming that unaligned VLM rewards are insufficient for policy learning. FuRL substantially outperforms the LIV baseline (Ma et al., 2023a) at 76.0% vs. 28.0%, demonstrating that naively adding VLM reward to SAC actually degrades performance below the SAC-only baseline due to the fuzzy reward problem. Against the Relay baseline (Lan et al., 2023), which adds relay exploration to LIV, FuRL gains 22 percentage points (76.0% vs. 54.0%), isolating the contribution of the reward alignment component. Finally, when the VLM backbone is switched from LIV to CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), CLIP-FuRL still achieves 70.0% average success rate versus 38.0% for the CLIP-only baseline, confirming the method generalizes across VLM architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the VLM-as-reward line of work (Rocamonde et al., 2023a; Chan et al., 2023; Mahmoudieh et al., 2022) by identifying a specific failure mode—reward misalignment or 'fuzziness'—that prior methods implicitly assume away. Rather than retraining the full VLM (Mahmoudieh et al., 2022) or restricting VLM rewards to pre-training only (Adeniji et al., 2023), FuRL freezes the pre-trained VLM (LIV or CLIP) and fine-tunes only two lightweight MLP projection heads with a contrastive ranking loss, combined with relay RL (Lan et al., 2023; Gupta et al., 2020) to escape local minima. The method is positioned as a practical, minimal-intervention fix for an emerging and active research direction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that naively adding VLM rewards can degrade performance below the sparse-reward-only baseline (LIV at 24% vs. SAC at 37% average) challenges the working assumption in the VLM-as-reward literature that additional reward signal is always beneficial. The lightweight solution (two small MLPs + relay RL) is easy to adopt and generalizes across VLM backbones (LIV, CLIP) and observation types (state, pixel). Key limitations include evaluation only on simulated Meta-world MT10 tasks, single-task reward alignment, and simple language instructions; open questions include multi-task joint alignment, compositional language, and real-world robotic validation.
