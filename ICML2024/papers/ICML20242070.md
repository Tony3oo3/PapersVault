## Problem

Policy gradient methods are empirically effective on highly non-smooth, even fractal, optimization landscapes arising from chaotic control systems, yet the true gradient of the objective may not exist in such settings. No rigorous framework existed to explain why stochastic policy gradient still yields useful ascent directions, nor to characterize the fundamental trade-off between smoothing the landscape and faithfully approximating the original objective.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242070/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on infinite-horizon MDPs with continuous state and action spaces, where the policy is parameterized as a Gaussian distribution N(μ(s), σ²I) (or with diagonal covariance). The input is the MDP state vector s and the policy parameters θ = [ζ, σ²] (where ζ parameterizes the mean via a neural network or linear map); the output is a control action sampled from the policy. The analysis treats the Gaussian mean μ as a spatial coordinate and the variance σ² as a temporal coordinate, mapping the policy improvement problem onto a spatio-temporal PDE domain.

## Method

The paper establishes a formal equivalence between stochastic policy gradient with Gaussian policies and the Cauchy problem for the heat equation: the inner integral of the policy gradient over the action space is exactly the heat-equation solution with the Q-function as initial condition and σ² as time. The Gaussian kernel acts as a low-pass filter that suppresses high-frequency components causing fractal structure, and the strong maximum principle guarantees that no strict local maximum exists for any stochastic policy, ensuring a valid ascent direction always exists. The key limitation arises from the ill-posedness of the backward heat equation: as σ² → 0 (converging to a deterministic policy), the landscape becomes increasingly non-smooth and the gradient flow loses stability. The uncertainty principle from harmonic analysis then quantifies the fundamental trade-off — the mollified objective cannot simultaneously be close to the true objective (small spatial spread of the kernel) and smooth (small frequency spread), implying an optimal intermediate variance σ* exists for chaotic MDPs.

## Results

On the Hopper stand task, policy gradient with σ = 0.05 and σ = 0.5 achieves stable convergence over 100 epochs, while σ = 0.005 (too little mollification, landscape remains fractal) and σ = 5 (over-smoothing) both fail. On the double pendulum, the trained policy successfully stabilizes the system to the upright position within 50 epochs, and the Q-landscape transitions from fractal to smooth. On the planar quadrotor balance task, policy gradient fails catastrophically: even when initialized at the optimal policy θ*, the training curve drops immediately after one epoch and never recovers, because the spike-like optimal region is filtered out by the Gaussian kernel.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines; instead, it ablates the variance σ of the stochastic Gaussian policy to illustrate the mollification trade-off. On Hopper, σ = 0.05 and σ = 0.5 both succeed (stable, low-variance return curves), while σ = 0.005 produces high-variance training (weak mollification leaves the fractal landscape intact) and σ = 5 over-smooths the landscape. The double pendulum experiment confirms that policy gradient works well when the initial policy is already close to the stabilizing region (fine-tuning scenario). The quadrotor experiment demonstrates a fundamental failure mode: the optimal policy corresponds to a high-frequency spike in the landscape that the Gaussian kernel inevitably filters, making the problem unsolvable regardless of initialization. These results collectively validate the theoretical prediction (Remark 5.3) that an optimal intermediate variance σ* exists for chaotic MDPs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the observation that chaotic MDPs produce fractal policy landscapes (Wang et al., 2023; Suh et al., 2022) and that stochasticity in optimization acts as a smoothing mechanism (Parmas et al., 2018; Wierstra et al., 2014), but it departs from prior work by establishing a rigorous equivalence between stochastic policy gradient and the Cauchy problem for the heat equation. While earlier analyses noted the low-pass filtering effect of Gaussian policies informally, this paper formalizes it through PDE theory and draws on the ill-posedness of the backward heat equation to identify a fundamental instability when reducing policy variance. The connection to the uncertainty principle in harmonic analysis (Stein & Shakarchi, 2003) is also new in this context, providing a quantitative trade-off between smoothing fidelity and approximation error that prior exploration-focused explanations (Haarnoja et al., 2018a; Cai et al., 2020) did not capture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a fundamental limitation of policy gradient methods: the mollification that makes optimization tractable simultaneously distorts the objective, and the backward heat equation ill-posedness means that approaching deterministic policies (the desired outcome in control) makes the landscape increasingly unstable. The quadrotor experiment—where even starting at the LQR-optimal policy causes immediate degradation—is a striking demonstration that certain control problems are structurally unsolvable by standard policy gradient. Open questions include whether the optimal variance can be estimated adaptively, whether the bump-function alternative sketched in Appendix D can mitigate the over-smoothing without the non-locality issues of Gaussians, and whether the framework extends to non-Gaussian or state-dependent covariance policies.
