## Problem

Model-free Bayesian RL lacks theoretical grounding: it is unknown whether such approaches can yield Bayes-optimal policies, and all existing methods (BootDQN+Prior, BBAC) inadvertently solve a restricted QBRL or myopic approximation that can be arbitrarily Bayes-suboptimal. This paper provides the first proof that model-free formulations can be Bayes-optimal and identifies precisely why existing methods fail.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes full state-action-reward histories h_t (not just the current state), which are encoded by a recurrent Q-network into a single scalar q_t. This 1D scalar, together with the history encoding, feeds into two normalising flows that jointly produce a predictive Bellman operator expectation B⁺[Q_ω](h_t, a) from which the Bayes-optimal action a* ∈ argmax_a Q_ω(h_t, a) is selected. The unusual aspect is that the high-dimensional history is compressed to one dimension before uncertainty modelling, exploiting the fact that the Bellman operator is a functional of the Q-value.

## Method

BEN comprises three networks: (1) a recurrent Q-network mapping history-action pairs to a 1D scalar q_t, (2) an aleatoric autoregressive normalising flow B(z_al, q_t, φ) that models the conditional distribution over Bellman operator outputs b_t given q_t and model parameters φ, and (3) an epistemic normalising flow t_ψ(z_ep) that approximates the posterior over φ via variational inference by minimising the ELBO. Training uses two-timescale stochastic approximation: the epistemic parameters ψ are updated on a faster timescale (N_Posterior SGD steps per update) while the Q-network parameters ω minimise the Mean Squared Bayesian Bellman Error (MSBBE) between the predictive Bellman operator and Q_ω. The key theoretical insight (Theorem 3.2) is that the predictive Bellman operator B⁺[Q_ω] equals the true optimal Bayesian Bellman operator B⋆[Q_ω] by the sufficiency principle, so any fixed point of the MSBBE yields a Bayes-optimal policy. Corollary 3.4 further establishes via the coarea formula that the 1D Bellman distribution is the exact pushforward of the high-dimensional reward-state transition distribution under the bootstrap mapping, proving model-free and model-free approaches are equivalent when aleatoric uncertainty is correctly represented.

## Results

On the 7×7 search and rescue gridworld (8 hazards, 4 victims, 245 timesteps/episode), BEN successfully learns to rescue all victims and avoid all hazards, while BootDQN+prior and RNN PPO fail to solve the task. In the zero-shot setting with a strong prior, BEN achieves full success in a single episode, whereas the QBRL variant (oracle for existing model-free methods) repeatedly hits hazards and cannot complete the task. The theoretical counterexample on the tiger problem quantifies the gap: the optimal QBRL policy achieves a Bayesian return of −27.5, while a simple 'always listen' policy achieves −10, proving QBRL policies can be arbitrarily Bayes-suboptimal.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares BEN against three baselines: BootDQN+Prior (Osband et al., 2018), RNN PPO, and a QBRL variant of BEN itself. In the episodic tabula-rasa setting, BootDQN+Prior outperforms RNN PPO due to its deeper exploration but still fails to learn the critical 'listen' action needed to solve the task, confirming the theoretical result (Corollary 3.6) that QBRL-based methods learn contextual policies that can be arbitrarily Bayes-suboptimal. BEN, by contrast, successfully learns to listen and then exploit that information to find all victims. In the zero-shot strong-prior setting, the full BEN (with history-conditioned Q-network) rescues all victims and avoids all hazards in a single episode, while the QBRL variant—which conditions only on state-action pairs rather than full history—cannot solve the problem because its policy space is restricted to mixtures of context-conditioned optimal policies. The key differentiator is that BEN's history-conditioned architecture allows it to propagate epistemic uncertainty across timesteps via the Bayesian Bellman equation, which is precisely what QBRL and myopic approximations forfeit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Bayesian Bellman operator framework of Fellows et al. (2021), which reinterprets bootstrapping as a change of variables, and extends it with a full Bayesian treatment using normalizing flows for both density estimation (aleatoric uncertainty) and variational inference (epistemic uncertainty). It departs fundamentally from prior model-free BRL methods (BootDQN+Prior, BBAC, and their descendants) by proving they solve a QBRL or myopic approximation rather than the true BRL objective, and by showing that the Q-function approximator must condition on full history (via an RNN) rather than a context variable for Bayes-optimality. The aleatoric network architecture adapts inverse autoregressive flows (Kingma et al., 2016) with the surjective mapping treatment from SurVAE flows (Nielsen et al., 2020), while the epistemic network applies normalizing flows (Rezende & Mohamed, 2015) to variational inference over Bellman operator parameters—a combination the authors note is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The proof that all existing model-free BRL methods are provably incapable of reaching Bayes-optimality (Corollary 3.6, with the tiger problem as a clean counterexample) is a significant negative result that reframes the field's understanding of what these methods actually optimize. BEN provides the first constructive path to Bayes-optimal model-free policies, but practical limitations remain: the nested optimization (ELBO + MSBBE) is computationally expensive, partial optimization is required for tractability, and empirical validation is confined to gridworld environments. Open questions include whether the theoretical guarantees hold in high-dimensional continuous control, how sensitive performance is to the aleatoric network's capacity (partially addressed in ablations), and whether the RNN-based history encoding scales to long-horizon tasks.
