## Problem

Generalization bounds for backdoor attacks have not been established, despite the fact that backdoor attacks have a unique two-fold goal (maintain clean accuracy while classifying triggered inputs to a target label) and the special property that triggers appear in both training and test phases. This paper fills this gap by deriving algorithm-independent generalization bounds for clean-label backdoor attacks and using them to design a theoretically grounded new attack.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242375/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean training dataset, a binary mask vector U (upper-left corner set to 0, remainder to 1), an L∞ poison budget η (16/255 in experiments), and a victim dataset, and produces per-sample trigger perturbations P(x) that are added to a subset of target-label training samples without altering their labels. The trigger is structured so that the masked adversarial component occupies the large portion of the image while the shortcut component is confined to the small upper-left corner, ensuring cross-sample similarity in the shortcut region.

## Method

The paper first establishes two generalization bounds: Theorem 4.1 bounds the clean population error by the empirical error on the poisoned set (scaled by (4−2α)/(1−α)), and Theorem 4.5 bounds the poison population error under three conditions on the trigger—(c1) it is adversarial noise of a clean-trained network, (c2) it is similar across samples, and (c3) it acts as a shortcut making the backdoor component give similar outputs for P(x) and x+P(x). Guided by these conditions, the proposed attack constructs the trigger as P(x) = U·x_adv + (1−U)·x_scut, where x_adv is obtained via PGD on a clean-trained network and x_scut is obtained via the min-min shortcut method. The mask U ensures the adversarial part covers a large image region (needed for effective misclassification) while the shortcut part is confined to a small region (ensuring cross-sample similarity and satisfying condition c2). This combination is key because adversarial perturbations alone fail the shortcut condition and shortcuts alone fail the adversarial condition, but their masked union satisfies all three theoretical requirements simultaneously.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet18, 1% poison budget, and L∞-norm 16/255, the proposed attack achieves 93% attack success rate (ASR) while maintaining 91% clean accuracy (vs. 93% for the clean model). The attack generalizes across architectures (VGG16: 91% ASR; WRN34-10: 90% ASR) and datasets (CIFAR-100: 92% ASR; SVHN: 79% ASR; TinyImageNet: 82% ASR). Even at a very small poison budget of 0.6%, the attack still achieves 86% ASR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Under the unified L∞-norm 16/255 constraint, the proposed method (93% ASR) substantially outperforms all seven clean-label baselines, with the closest competitor being Hidden-Trigger at 75% (an 18-point gap). Invisible Poison (73%) and Image-specific (70%) follow, while Clean-Label (23%), Narcissus (50%), and Reflection (54%) are far behind. Under each method's own original settings, most baselines recover high ASR (90–98%), but they violate the 16/255 norm bound or require additional resources (e.g., large generative models for Invisible Poison and Image-specific, a pre-existing patch for Sleeper-Agent, or a fitting image for Reflection). The proposed method's key practical advantage is that it achieves the highest ASR under the strictest stealth constraint without requiring auxiliary networks, patches, or fitting images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classic Rademacher-complexity-based generalization framework (Mohri et al., 2018) to the non-i.i.d. setting of poisoned training data, filling a gap where no generalization bound existed for backdoor attacks. The attack design draws on two distinct threads: adversarial perturbation theory (PGD-based noise satisfying condition c1) and the shortcut/indiscriminate-poison literature (Huang et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022), which the authors show can be unified through a masked combination. Unlike prior clean-label backdoor attacks that rely on empirical heuristics (reflection, feature-space injection, bilevel optimization), this work derives its trigger construction from explicit theoretical conditions, positioning it as a theory-guided rather than heuristic-driven attack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first algorithm-independent generalization bound for clean-label backdoor attacks, providing a theoretical foundation that explains when and why such attacks generalize beyond the training set. The practical attack achieves 93% ASR with only 1% poison budget and 16/255 L∞ norm, outperforming all compared baselines under the same constraint. However, the conditions in Theorem 4.5 are complex and the attack is notably fragile under standard defenses (ASR drops to ~10–15%), and the bounds do not account for the training dynamics (e.g., SGD stability), leaving algorithm-dependent analysis as an important open direction.
