## Problem

When training neural networks with both a main loss and an auxiliary objective, existing gradient surgery methods (PCGrad, Dynamic Barrier) that project one gradient orthogonal to the other fail in the stochastic mini-batch setting: the projection is nonlinear in its second argument, so projecting onto noisy batch gradients breaks the critical orthogonality property and causes these methods to degenerate to simple mixed training. This paper identifies the root cause and shows that an exponential moving average of the main gradient is essential to preserve orthogonality and recover the intended bilevel optimization behavior.

## Input / Output

At each iteration the method consumes two mini-batch gradient vectors (one for the main loss, one for the auxiliary loss) and a running EMA of the main gradient, all in the full parameter space of dimension p. It produces a single update direction vector that is fed to any standard optimizer (Adam, SGD with momentum, etc.), which then performs the actual parameter update. The EMA is used exclusively as the projection axis in the orthogonal projection step; the first term of the direction remains the raw mini-batch main gradient, so the optimizer's adaptive step-size mechanisms still operate on the full stochastic signal.

## Method

Bloop (BiLevel Optimization with Orthogonal Projection) casts the two-loss problem as a bilevel program—minimize L_aux among minimizers of L_main—and constructs the update direction as d = g_main + λ·π(g_aux; g_main), where π denotes orthogonal projection onto the complement of g_main, ensuring the direction preserves the same first-order decrease in L_main as plain gradient descent. In the stochastic setting, the key modification is replacing the noisy mini-batch main gradient in the projection with its EMA: d = g_main^batch + λ·π(g_aux^batch; g_main^EMA), where the EMA is updated as g_main^EMA ← (1−ρ)·g_main^EMA + ρ·g_main^batch. The theory shows that ρ must be slightly larger than the step size η (specifically ρ ∝ η^{2/3}) for the EMA to track the true gradient closely enough to maintain orthogonality, yielding an O(T^{-1/4}) gradient-norm convergence rate. The method extends naturally to multi-level hierarchies via Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization and incurs only two extra dot products per step over standard training.

## Results

The paper evaluates Bloop across five experimental settings (MNIST Lipschitz training, ImageNet L2-norm regularization, Cifar10Mnist multi-task, C4/RCV-1 language modeling, and ParaCrawl/WMT translation) using Pareto front plots rather than single-point metrics. In every experiment, Bloop achieves a strictly better Pareto front between the main and auxiliary losses compared to Mixed, PCGrad, and Dynamic Barrier. A striking finding is that PCGrad and Dynamic Barrier, when applied in the stochastic mini-batch setting without an EMA, produce training trajectories nearly identical to the simple mixed (regularized) method, effectively nullifying their gradient surgery. The EMA ablation (Figure 7) confirms that ρ=0.01 or ρ=0.1 are effective, while ρ=0.001 (too slow) and ρ=0.9 (too noisy) both degrade performance toward the mixed method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Bloop is compared against two gradient surgery baselines: Dynamic Barrier (DB, Gong & Liu 2021) and PCGrad (Yu et al. 2020), plus the simple Mixed (regularized) method. In all five experimental settings, Bloop produces a better Pareto front than both DB and PCGrad, with the gap being most pronounced in the NLP experiments (C4/RCV-1 pre-training and ParaCrawl/WMT translation) where Bloop achieves significantly lower main-loss values at equivalent auxiliary-loss levels. The critical finding is that DB and PCGrad, when run with stochastic mini-batch gradients (as any practitioner would), perform almost identically to the Mixed method — their orthogonal projection is destroyed by gradient noise because the projection is nonlinear in its second argument. Bloop's EMA of the main gradient (ρ=0.01) preserves the orthogonality property in expectation, which is what gives it its advantage. The paper also notes one setting (ImageNet + CIFAR-10 with shared trunk) where all methods perform similarly, suggesting the losses are not conflicting enough for gradient surgery to matter.

## Relation to Prior Work

Bloop builds directly on the Dynamic Barrier (DB) method of Gong & Liu (2021), which also projects the auxiliary gradient orthogonal to the main gradient, but departs in three key ways: it replaces the barrier function with a simple scalar λ, it always applies the projection rather than conditioning on gradient alignment, and—most critically—it uses an EMA of the main gradient as the projection direction. The paper also positions itself against PCGrad (Yu et al., 2020), which it identifies as a symmetrized, conditional variant of the same projection idea. Conceptually, Bloop reframes the multi-task gradient surgery literature through the lens of simple bilevel optimization, imposing a strict hierarchy between the main and auxiliary objectives rather than treating them symmetrically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's central practical finding—that PCGrad and DB degenerate to the mixed (regularized) method in stochastic settings because gradient noise destroys the orthogonality property—is a useful diagnostic for practitioners who have observed these methods underperforming. The EMA fix is minimal in implementation cost (two extra dot products) yet consistently improves Pareto fronts across vision, NLP, and multi-task experiments. Open questions include: when does Bloop fail to help (the Imagenet/CIFAR-10 experiment shows it can be equivalent to baselines when losses are not sufficiently conflicting), how sensitive performance is to the EMA parameter ρ (the paper shows a narrow effective range), and whether the approach extends cleanly to more than three hierarchical objectives in practice.
