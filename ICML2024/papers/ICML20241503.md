## Problem

White-box attribution methods, which rely on gradient backpropagation, consistently underperform computationally expensive black-box methods on XAI benchmarks despite being orders of magnitude faster. The paper identifies that this gap stems from high-frequency artifacts contaminating the gradient signal, originating from max-pooling and striding operations in CNNs, which degrade the faithfulness and interpretability of the resulting attribution maps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241503/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 224×224 RGB image and a trained classifier (ResNet50V2, ConvNeXt, or ViT) as input, and produces a pixel-wise attribution map where high-frequency components of the gradient have been suppressed. The optimal cut-off frequency σ* is precomputed per (network architecture, attribution method) pair on 1,280 ImageNet validation images, so at inference time only a single forward-backward pass plus a Fourier-domain low-pass filter is needed. The filtering is applied to the gradient ∇_x f(x) before the attribution map is computed, rather than post-hoc on the final map.

## Method

FORGrad (FOurier Reparation of the Gradients) applies a circular low-pass filter to the 2D Discrete Fourier Transform of the input gradient, retaining only frequencies below an architecture-specific cut-off σ*. The optimal σ* is selected by maximizing the faithfulness metric (Insertion − Deletion) over a sweep of cut-off values on a held-out validation set, yielding different σ* for each combination of network and attribution method. The paper demonstrates via first-order Taylor approximation that removing high-frequency gradient content produces negligible additional approximation error compared to the unfiltered gradient, confirming these frequencies are non-informative noise. The artifacts are traced to max-pooling operations that create checkerboard patterns in the backward pass, and the effect is absent in architectures without such operations (e.g., ViT). FORGrad is a drop-in modification applicable to all white-box methods (Saliency, SmoothGrad, Integrated Gradients, etc.) with negligible computational overhead, consistently boosting faithfulness to levels competitive with black-box methods while retaining their speed advantage.

## Results

On ResNet50 (ImageNet validation, 1,280 images), FORGrad consistently improves faithfulness across all white-box methods: Saliency rises from 0.18 to 0.28, SmoothGrad from 0.23 to 0.37, and GradInput from 0.20 to 0.26. After FORGrad, white-box methods become competitive with black-box methods (e.g., SmoothGrad★ faithfulness 0.37 vs. Sobol 0.34 and HSIC 0.33) while remaining 10–100× faster (e.g., Saliency★ at 0.89 s vs. RISE at 626 s). In the global ranking (F + μF − S), FORGrad elevates four white-box methods into the top-5 for ResNet50, displacing the previously dominant black-box and gray-box entries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FORGrad improves faithfulness for nearly all white-box baselines: Saliency gains +0.10 (0.18→0.28), SmoothGrad +0.14 (0.23→0.37), GradInput +0.06 (0.20→0.26), GuidedBackprop +0.04 (0.31→0.35), and Integrated Gradients +0.07 (0.24→0.31). VarGrad and SquareGrad show minimal change (−0.01 and 0.00 respectively), as they already accumulate gradients over many samples, reducing high-frequency contamination. Against gray-box methods, FORGrad-enhanced SmoothGrad (0.37) and SquareGrad (0.36) now exceed GradCAM (0.31) and GradCAM++ (0.33) on faithfulness. Against black-box methods, FORGrad-enhanced white-box methods close the gap substantially: SmoothGrad★ (0.37) surpasses Sobol (0.34) and HSIC (0.33), though RISE (0.41) retains the highest faithfulness. However, the computational cost difference remains dramatic—Saliency★ runs in 0.89 s versus 626 s for RISE, a ~700× speedup. In the aggregate ranking (F + μF − S), FORGrad shifts the top-5 from being dominated by VarGrad/SquareGrad/GuidedBackprop to including SmoothGrad★ (0.60) and GuidedBackprop★ (0.58), which previously ranked 4th and 3rd respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the well-established attribution/explainability landscape, where white-box (gradient-based), black-box (perturbation-based), and gray-box (partial-gradient) methods have been extensively compared. Rather than proposing a new attribution method, it repurposes a classical signal-processing idea—Fourier low-pass filtering—as a post-hoc repair mechanism applied to the gradient signal before any attribution computation. The observation that high-frequency artifacts in gradients stem from max-pooling and striding connects to prior work on checkerboard artifacts in deconvolution (Odena et al., 2016; Olah et al., 2017), but the paper is the first to systematically link these artifacts to the underperformance of white-box XAI methods and to show that a single architecture-specific cut-off frequency can close much of the faithfulness gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FORGrad offers a nearly zero-cost fix that makes computationally cheap gradient-based explanations competitive with expensive black-box methods, potentially shifting the practical default for XAI back toward efficient white-box approaches. The finding that max-pooling (not training) is the primary source of gradient high-frequency contamination opens a path toward designing networks with inherently smoother gradients, which could improve both interpretability and adversarial robustness. However, the improvement is less pronounced on ViT (which lacks pooling/striding), suggesting different high-frequency phenomena in transformers remain unaddressed. The paper also acknowledges that XAI evaluation metrics themselves are still an open problem, and that the μFidelity metric is biased toward high-frequency content, complicating the interpretation of some results.
