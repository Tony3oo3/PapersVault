## Problem

Practitioners routinely download pretrained models from open repositories and finetune them, but this practice introduces an unexplored privacy risk: a malicious model provider can tamper with weights to steal the victim's finetuning data. Prior backdoor work focused on model integrity (hijacking predictions), and prior data-stealing attacks in federated learning required observing individual gradient updates. This paper introduces privacy backdoors that survive an entire finetuning run and enable data extraction from only the final model, with guaranteed success.

## Input / Output

The attacker tampers with a pretrained model's weights (ViT-B/32, BERT-base, or a 3-layer MLP) before handing it to the victim, who finetunes it on their own classification data. For ViT, each 32×32 image patch is compressed to a single grayscale pixel (8×8 for full-image reconstruction, 16×16 for per-patch), and for BERT a sub-vector of each 768-dim token embedding is captured to recover the underlying token. The white-box attacker reads captured inputs directly from finetuned weights; the black-box attacker uses model queries to find ReLU critical points (under 10,000 queries) or detects membership via logit shifts. The backdoored models retain competitive downstream accuracy (83% on Caltech-101, 78% on TREC-50).

## Method

The core mechanism is a single-use 'data trap': a backdoored linear unit whose gradient update on the captured input permanently deactivates it (like a latch), so the written data survives all subsequent training steps. For MLPs, the backdoor is connected to a classification head with large random weights, ensuring the captured input is misclassified with probability 1−1/C and produces a large positive gradient that shuts the backdoor. For transformers, the 768-dim feature vector is partitioned into [ft, key, act] where key = [pos, seq, tok]; near-orthogonal position keys and attention-computed sequence keys ensure all tokens in a sequence activate their backdoors in tandem. Stabilized layer normalization (adding a large constant to decouple feature groups), GELU-aware amplification, and erasure modules handle architectural complexity while preserving benign utility. For black-box settings, the paper reduces data extraction to model stealing by recovering backdoor weights via critical-point queries, and shows the backdoor achieves near-tight DP-SGD privacy leakage for an end-to-end attacker.

## Results

The backdoored ViT-B/32 achieves 83% test accuracy on Caltech 101 while successfully reconstructing recognizable grayscale images from finetuning data. The backdoored BERT-base reaches 78% test accuracy on TREC-50 and recovers near-perfect training sentences (with only rare spurious suffix tokens). For the black-box setting, the membership inference attack achieves 100% accuracy, and the data extraction attack requires under 10,000 model queries to reconstruct a full CIFAR-10 image. The DP-SGD tightness experiment shows the empirical privacy budget for the end-to-end attacker closely matches the provable upper bound, challenging the assumption that DP-SGD guarantees are overly conservative.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an attack paper without traditional method-vs-baseline comparisons. The key quantitative comparisons are: (1) model utility — the backdoored ViT achieves 83% and BERT 78% test accuracy, showing the backdoor does not catastrophically degrade downstream performance (though accuracy is reduced relative to the unmodified pretrained model, as noted in Appendix D.2); (2) DP-SGD tightness — the empirical privacy leakage observed by the black-box attacker nearly matches the worst-case theoretical upper bound from Abadi et al. (2016), demonstrating that the commonly assumed gap between worst-case analysis and end-to-end privacy is closed by the backdoor; (3) black-box extraction — the attack requires fewer than 10,000 queries, which is efficient compared to full model stealing attacks that require high-precision outputs and scale poorly with model size. The paper positions its white-box attack as strictly stronger than concurrent work by Wen et al. (2024) and Liu et al. (2024), which only achieve membership inference or extraction without success guarantees under a weaker black-box threat model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient-encoding mechanism from Federated Learning data-stealing attacks (Fowl et al., 2021; Fowl et al., 2022) to a fundamentally different threat model: instead of a malicious server observing individual gradient updates, a malicious model provider tampers with pretrained weights once and must survive an entire finetuning run. The 'data trap' (single-use backdoor that latches after one activation) is the key new mechanism, distinguishing this from both prior FL attacks and from integrity-focused backdoor attacks (Gu et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2018) that hijack model behavior rather than exfiltrate training data. For the black-box setting, the paper repurposes model-extraction techniques (Tramèr et al., 2016; Carlini et al., 2020) to recover backdoor weights via query access, and applies the resulting membership inference to demonstrate tightness of DP-SGD's privacy bound (Abadi et al., 2016).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a concrete supply-chain vulnerability in the dominant practice of downloading and finetuning pretrained models from open repositories like HuggingFace, with direct implications for anyone operating with untrusted shared models. The DP-SGD tightness result is particularly consequential: it challenges the widespread assumption that the algorithm's worst-case privacy analysis is overly conservative, showing that loose privacy budgets (ε≥9) used in production systems become insecure when the model provider is untrusted. Key limitations include the restriction to full finetuning (LoRA and other parameter-efficient methods are left for future work), the requirement that the backdoored model retain sufficient utility to go unnoticed, and the fact that the DP-SGD tightness demonstration uses simplified CNN architectures with frozen backbones rather than the full transformer models. Open questions include whether the attacks generalize to LoRA, to multi-tenant or multi-epoch settings with different optimizers, and whether practical detection mechanisms can identify backdoored weights before deployment.
