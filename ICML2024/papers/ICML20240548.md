## Problem

Explicit PDE-based inpainting methods are interpretable, well-posed, and generalize well to unseen scenarios, but rely on fixed heuristics (e.g., structure tensors) that cannot adapt to image content. Fully data-driven deep learning methods achieve high reconstruction quality but require massive training data, have millions of opaque parameters, and fail to generalize out-of-distribution. No prior work had integrated the rich mathematical structure of anisotropic diffusion PDEs into a trainable neural architecture for optical flow inpainting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sparse 2-channel optical flow field (u,v displacements), a binary mask marking known flow vectors, and a reference RGB image that drives the anisotropic diffusion by indicating where flow discontinuities are likely to occur. Training uses 384×384 center-cropped images with masks drawn from a uniform distribution at target densities of 1%, 5%, or 10%. The output is a dense 2-channel flow field at full resolution, produced by running a fixed number of explicit diffusion steps (5/15/30/45 per pyramid level) rather than iterating to convergence.

## Method

A U-Net-based Diffusion Tensor Module (DTM) takes the reference image and outputs, per pixel and per pyramid level, five channels that parameterize the diffusion process: two eigenvalues (constrained to [0,1] via a learned Perona-Malik diffusivity), one eigenvector (with the orthogonal complement computed analytically), and a spatially-varying discretization parameter α. These parameters drive an explicit anisotropic diffusion PDE solved with the L2-stable nonstandard finite difference scheme of Weickert et al. (2013) on a 3×3 stencil, accelerated by the Fast-Semi-Iterative extrapolation scheme. A coarse-to-fine pyramid across four resolutions (with average pooling for the image and flow, max pooling for the mask) reduces the total number of diffusion steps while preserving detail. The entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with supervised EPE loss, replacing the heuristic structure tensor with a learned, content-adaptive edge detector that decides which image edges coincide with flow discontinuities.

## Results

The method is evaluated using Endpoint Error (EPE, lower is better) on random masks at 1%, 5%, and 10% density. On the in-domain FlyingThings3D test set, the neuroexplicit model achieves EPE of 1.01 / 0.55 / 0.39, outperforming all baselines by 48–66% on average. On the out-of-domain Sintel test set, it achieves 0.72 / 0.40 / 0.28, beating explicit PDE baselines (EED, AMLE, LB) by 11–27%, the GAN baseline (WGAIN) by 47%, and the Probabilistic Diffusion baseline (PD) by 42% on average across densities. On real-world KITTI data, the method matches Laplace-Beltrami in EPE while producing significantly fewer flow outliers at low mask densities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The neuroexplicit method wins against all six baselines on every mask density and both test datasets. Against the explicit PDE baselines, the advantage is most pronounced on FlyingThings3D (e.g., 1.01 vs. 2.06 EPE at 1% vs. EED) and more moderate on Sintel (0.72 vs. 0.94 at 1%), where EED, AMLE, and LB are already competitive. Against the neural baselines, the gap is large and consistent: FlowNetS and WGAIN produce noisy flow fields at discontinuities and fail to generalize to unseen mask densities (their performance degrades when tested at higher densities than trained), while the PD model overfits to training-domain textures and produces blocky reconstructions on out-of-domain content. On KITTI real-world data, the method ties Laplace-Beltrami in EPE (1.07 vs. 1.07 at 1%) but has substantially fewer flow outliers, whereas FlowNetS and WGAIN degrade severely (EPE 1.43 and 3.18 at 1% respectively). The paper also shows the method requires far fewer training samples (competitive with 194 samples vs. full dataset) and has significantly fewer learnable parameters than the CNN-based baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neuroexplicit paradigm by combining explicit PDE-based anisotropic diffusion (Weickert, 1994; Weickert et al., 2013) with a learned U-Net that predicts spatially-varying diffusion tensor parameters and discretization coefficients per pixel. It departs from prior parameterized diffusion approaches (Chen & Pock, 2017; Alt & Weickert, 2021) that learned only fixed global contrast parameters, and from ResNet-formulations of diffusion evolutions (Alt et al., 2022; Ruthotto & Haber, 2020) that treat the PDE purely as a neural architecture. For the inpainting task itself, it builds on the AMLE and Laplace-Beltrami flow inpainting of Raad et al. (2020) and the PDE-based flow compression of Jost et al. (2020), but differs by operating on random, non-optimal masks rather than masks optimized with access to the full flow.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a lightweight neuroexplicit model with far fewer parameters and less training data can outperform both purely explicit and state-of-the-art data-driven inpainting methods by large margins (up to 47% over GANs), while offering interpretability, stability guarantees, and strong out-of-distribution generalization validated on real-world autonomous driving data. The finding that explicit EED inpainting alone outperforms neural baselines on Sintel challenges the assumption that deep learning is necessary for structured reconstruction tasks. Open questions include whether the diffusion regularization can be embedded into a full end-to-end optical flow estimator (the authors' stated future work) and whether the neuroexplicit recipe generalizes beyond flow inpainting to other PDE-structured vision problems.
