## Problem

LM agents that follow natural language instructions in digital environments (web browsers, REST APIs) fail to generalize to new environments without human demonstrations, and collecting such demonstrations is laborious and increasingly infeasible as tasks grow to hundreds of actions. The paper asks whether exploration and environment feedback alone can bootstrap a large set of synthetic demonstrations without any human supervision or reward signal.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241498/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At test time, the agent receives a natural language instruction (e.g., 'Find the email by Trixi and reply with Maecenas eu massa') alongside the current environment state (raw HTML for MiniWoB++, or the string output of the most recent tool call for ToolQA). It produces a sequence of natural-language action strings (e.g., 'Click on the reply button', 'Type Maecenas eu massa on the textarea') that are mapped to low-level commands (Selenium API calls or tool method invocations) by a separate controller. Retrieved synthetic demonstrations are prepended to the prompt as in-context exemplars, with top-3 selected via T5-XXL dual-encoder cosine similarity over instruction embeddings.

## Method

BAGEL generates synthetic demonstrations through an iterative round-trip between two noisy LM components: a trajectory labeler that converts an explored trajectory into a synthetic instruction, and a zero-shot instruction-following agent that maps that instruction back into a refined trajectory. Starting from unconditioned exploration (or sampled instructions), each round-trip progressively shifts the trajectory distribution toward those that are both executable in the environment and well-described by natural language, with a separate LM-based demonstration filter accepting pairs that meet a quality threshold. The key insight is that the two imperfect components work in harmony to cancel each other's noise across iterations, and the environment dynamics provide grounding that pure LM self-training lacks. At inference, the collected demonstrations are used via retrieval-augmented in-context learning rather than fine-tuning, making BAGEL a drop-in replacement for expert demonstrations.

## Results

On MiniWoB++ (10 hardest tasks), BAGEL (trajectory-first, full iterations) achieves a mean reward of 61.0, improving over the zero-shot ReAct baseline (46.8) by 14.2 points. On ToolQA (8 domains), BAGEL (instruction-first, full) achieves an F1 of 43.3 versus 40.9 for zero-shot, a 2.4-point gain. Iterative relabeling consistently outperforms non-iterative variants by 4–8% on MiniWoB++ and 1.3–4.5% on ToolQA. BAGEL also reduces average execution failures by up to 13× (e.g., from 1.3 to 0.1 on choose-date).

Table 5 compares BAGEL against three prior LM-agent methods (Gur et al. 2023, Kim et al. 2023, Sun et al. 2023) on 10 MiniWoB++ tasks. BAGEL achieves the highest score on 5 of 10 tasks (book-flight 0.15 vs 0.0/0.0, choose-date 0.4 vs 0.2/0.2, email-inbox 1.0 vs 0.4/0.0, click-checkboxes-soft 0.9 vs 0.0/0.04, click-tab-2-hard 1.0 vs 0.68/0.76). However, Sun et al. (AdaPlanner) outperforms BAGEL on social-media (1.0 vs 0.7), social-media-some (0.9 vs 0.8), tic-tac-toe (0.56 vs 0.4), use-autocomplete (0.58 vs 0.45), and search-engine (1.00 vs 0.25). The paper notes that underlying LMs differ across methods, so direct numerical comparison is approximate. Overall, BAGEL's key advantage is that it requires no human demonstrations or reward signal, whereas all three baselines rely on expert-curated data or RL fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

BAGEL builds directly on the ReAct prompting framework (Yao et al., 2022) as the architectural backbone for all its LM components (exploration policy, instruction-following policy, labeler, and filter). Conceptually, it extends Hindsight Experience Replay (Andrychowicz et al., 2017) from single-shot relabeling based on final observations to iterative, full-trajectory relabeling driven by language model priors, and departs from prior synthetic demonstration work (Sumers et al., 2023) by addressing the opposite bottleneck: in digital environments, grounding instructions in language is easy, but learning environment dynamics is the hard part. The iterative round-trip between a noisy labeler and a noisy agent is the key methodological departure, progressively shaping the trajectory distribution toward language-describable and environment-consistent behaviors without any human supervision or reward signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BAGEL addresses a practical bottleneck in deploying LM agents: the need for human-curated demonstrations in every new environment, which becomes infeasible as tasks grow in complexity. The 13× reduction in execution failures is a practically significant side benefit beyond accuracy gains, suggesting that synthetic demonstrations primarily teach environment dynamics rather than just task mappings. Key limitations include diversity collapse (e.g., all book-flight demonstrations involve December), poor handling of long-horizon planning, and mismatch between generated and test-time instruction distributions. Open questions include whether finetuning on BAGEL demonstrations (deferred to future work) would yield larger gains than in-context retrieval, and whether the method scales to environments with richer or more ambiguous dynamics.
