## Problem

Gradient-flow particle-based sampling methods (SVGD, Langevin dynamics, PFG) suffer from slow mixing and mode-missing when the posterior contains multiple modes of different weights, because they rely solely on the asymptotic target distribution without a short-term guidance mechanism to escape local modes. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a path-guided framework where particles follow a carefully designed density path rather than evolving along a functional gradient.

## Input / Output

The method takes N initial particles drawn i.i.d. from a prior distribution (typically a zero-mean Gaussian) and the unnormalized target density p̂(x) = p_0(x)L(D|x) accessible without the partition function. It produces evolved particles approximating the posterior, which are then used for Bayesian predictions (accuracy, NLL, ECE) on tabular UCI datasets and noise-perturbed MNIST images. The vector field is parameterized by a two-layer MLP with sigmoid activation, trained at each time step via gradient descent on a PDE-derived loss.

## Method

PGPS defines a Log-weighted Shrinkage (LwS) density path ln p̂_t^LwS(x) = (1−t) ln p_0((1−αt)x) + t ln p̂_1(x/(β+(1−β)t)) that linearly mixes log-densities while applying spatial shrinkage to both the initial and target terms, enabling efficient multi-mode coverage. A neural network approximates the vector field φ_t(x) by minimizing a loss derived from the continuity (Fokker-Planck) equation (Proposition 3.1), which is free of the intractable partition function. Particles evolve via the ODE dx_t/dt = φ_t(x_t) with adaptive time stepping (Algorithm 1) and optional Langevin adjustment toward the intermediate target at each step. Theoretically, the 2-Wasserstein distance between generated and target distributions is bounded by O(δ) + O(√h), where δ is the vector-field approximation error and h is the discretization step size.

## Results

On UCI Bayesian inference tasks, PGPS achieves the best ECE on 5 of 7 datasets (e.g., 0.0366 on Wine Red vs. 0.0402 SVGD, 0.0868 SGLD, 0.0449 PFG; 0.0743 on Covertype vs. 0.0950 SVGD) and the best ACC on 5 of 7 datasets (e.g., 0.5850 on Glass vs. 0.5383 SVGD, 0.4505 PFG; 0.5899 on Covertype vs. 0.4867 SVGD). On Noisy MNIST, PGPS achieves the highest ACC (87.88%) and lowest ECE (0.1716) among all methods, with SGLD marginally better on NLL (1.8184 vs. 1.8202) but with a much larger standard deviation (0.127 vs. 0.019). In the Gaussian mixture mode-seeking experiments, PGPS recovers the right mode with score₁ ≈ 0.49 (matching the true value) in ~650 iterations, while LD requires 4,000+ iterations and still fails to capture both modes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PGPS outperforms SVGD on ECE for 5 of 7 UCI datasets (e.g., 0.0743 vs. 0.0950 on Covertype, 0.1142 vs. 0.1155 on Glass) and on ACC for 4 of 7 (e.g., 0.5899 vs. 0.4867 on Covertype, 0.5850 vs. 0.5383 on Glass), with the gap widening on higher-dimensional or harder tasks. Against PFG, PGPS wins on ECE for 5 of 7 datasets and ACC for 4 of 7, though PFG edges out PGPS on ECE for SONAR (0.1678 vs. 0.2517) and Australian (0.1457 vs. 0.1703). On Noisy MNIST, PGPS achieves the best ACC (87.88%) and ECE (0.1716), beating SVGD by 5.06% in ACC and 0.0225 in ECE, while SGLD is marginally better on NLL (1.8184 vs. 1.8202) but with 6.7× larger variance. In the synthetic Gaussian mixture experiments, PGPS discovers both modes in ~650 iterations versus 4,000+ for LD, and is far less sensitive to negligible modes (score₂ ≈ 0.001 vs. substantially higher for SVGD and PFG). The weight-recovery experiment on 8-dimensional 4-mode mixtures shows PGPS consistently reduces the weight mismatch error relative to LD (mean e − e_LD significantly negative across all PGPS variants), while PFG performs similarly to LD and SVGD performs worst due to variance collapse.

## Relation to Prior Work

PGPS departs from the dominant gradient-flow paradigm in particle-based Bayesian inference (SVGD, PFG, Langevin dynamics), where particles evolve along the steepest-descent direction of KL divergence in Wasserstein space toward an asymptotic target. Instead, it adopts the path-following philosophy of flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) and continuous normalizing flows, learning a neural-network vector field that drives particles along a pre-designed density path from prior to posterior. The Log-weighted Shrinkage (LwS) path is a novel construction that combines log-linear interpolation of score functions with spatial shrinkage, drawing conceptual inspiration from annealing-based methods (parallel tempering, annealed importance sampling) but differing in that it learns a deterministic ODE vector field rather than relying on stochastic transitions between intermediate temperatures. The theoretical analysis builds on the Fokker-Planck/continuity equation framework (Jordan et al., 1998) and the Wasserstein error bounds for flow approximation (Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden, 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PGPS addresses a well-recognized practical failure mode of gradient-flow samplers—mode missing and misplaced mode weights in multimodal posteriors—by replacing the asymptotic target with a sequence of short-term intermediate targets along a designed path, yielding faster mode discovery and better weight estimation. The Wasserstein distance bound O(δ) + O(√h) provides a clear recipe for improving sample quality (better vector field approximation and finer discretization), and the training-free variant (iterative Langevin adjustment along the path) offers a practical alternative when neural network training is undesirable. Open questions include whether the LwS path can be adapted to exploit known structure of the target (e.g., hierarchical or sparse posteriors), whether the training-free variant provably converges, and how the method scales to very high-dimensional BNNs where the current two-layer MLP vector field may be insufficient.
