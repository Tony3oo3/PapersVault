## Problem

The open-versus-closed foundation model debate is contentious because stakeholders implicitly analyze different subsets of the risk landscape with different assumptions, making it difficult to determine whether open foundation models actually increase societal risk beyond pre-existing technologies. No structured framework exists for assessing the *marginal* risk of open model releases relative to closed models or the status quo, leaving policymakers and researchers without a shared basis for decision-making.

## Input / Output

The framework is applied to a specific misuse vector (e.g., cybersecurity, biosecurity, NCII) by characterizing the threat actor, the pre-existing risk and defense landscape absent open foundation models, and the evidence that open models add incremental risk. The output is a structured six-point assessment yielding a marginal risk characterization, a defense feasibility judgment, and an explicit articulation of assumptions and uncertainties. The paper applies this to seven misuse vectors from prior literature and provides two full worked examples (automated vulnerability detection and digitally altered NCII) that reach opposite conclusions, demonstrating the framework's discriminative power.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a six-step risk assessment framework adapted from computer security threat modeling: (1) threat identification, (2) existing risk absent open FMs, (3) existing defenses absent open FMs, (4) evidence of marginal risk of open FMs, (5) ease of defending against new risks, and (6) uncertainty and assumptions. The key insight is that marginal risk must be assessed relative to a clearly stated baseline (pre-existing technologies, closed models, or restricted release), not in isolation. Applying the framework to seven published misuse analyses reveals that six of seven fail to adequately address at least one step, explaining why the debate has been so contentious—past studies implicitly analyze different subsets with different assumptions. The framework also identifies five distinctive properties of open foundation models (broader access, greater customizability, local inference, inability to rescind access, inability to monitor) that mediate both benefits and risks, and articulates five benefit categories (distributing decision-making power, innovation, science, transparency, reducing market concentration) with explicit caveats about where model weights alone are insufficient.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that 6 of 7 surveyed misuse analyses (spear-phishing, cybersecurity, disinformation, biosecurity, voice-cloning scams, NCII) are incomplete under the proposed six-step risk assessment framework, meaning they do not, on their own, demonstrate increased marginal societal risk from open foundation models. The framework instantiation (Table 2) shows that marginal risk is currently low for cybersecurity (automated vulnerability detection), where existing defenses and AI-assisted defense tools are adequate, but considerable for non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII), where open FMs enable fine-tuning to create sexualized images of specific people with minimal resources and where plausible defenses are hard to implement. The paper identifies five distinctive properties of open FMs (broader access, greater customizability, local inference, inability to rescind access, inability to monitor) that mediate both benefits and risks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a position paper with no traditional method-vs-baseline comparison. Instead, the paper's framework serves as a diagnostic lens applied to seven prior misuse analyses, revealing that each study implicitly addresses a different subset of the six framework steps (e.g., Hazell 2023 identifies the threat clearly but omits existing defenses and marginal risk evidence; Seger et al. 2023 is incomplete on nearly all steps). The only vector with a fully addressed assessment is child sexual abuse material (Thiel et al., 2023), which scores ● on all six steps. The paper also contrasts open vs. closed foundation models conceptually: closed models allow developers to revoke access and monitor usage, while open models are irreversible and unmonitored, but the framework shows that this distinction does not uniformly translate into higher risk—for example, in cybersecurity, the offense-defense asymmetry and existing defense-in-depth strategies mean open FMs add little marginal risk for automated vulnerability detection. The paper explicitly cautions that its framework does not trade off benefits against risks and does not account for unknown-unknown risks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the threat modeling framework from computer security (Shostack, 2014; Drake, 2021) and adapts it to the governance context of open foundation models, introducing a six-step 'marginal risk' assessment that prior misuse analyses (Seger et al., 2023; Gopal et al., 2023; Hazell, 2023) had not systematically applied. It builds on the foundation models literature (Bommasani et al., 2021) and the open-vs-closed release debate (Sastry, 2021; Solaiman, 2023), but departs from prior risk discussions by insisting on explicit baselines: what risk already exists absent open FMs, and what defenses already exist. The paper also synthesizes the benefits side (innovation, transparency, competition) in a way that prior position papers had addressed less rigorously, connecting each benefit to specific distinctive properties of open release.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework's application reveals that 6 of 7 prior misuse analyses are incomplete, suggesting the open-vs-closed debate has been conducted on an uneven evidentiary footing. The distinction between low marginal risk (cybersecurity vulnerability detection, where the offense-defense balance already favors defenders) and high marginal risk (NCII, where fine-tuning enables targeting specific individuals) provides a more nuanced picture than blanket 'open is dangerous' or 'open is fine' claims. Key limitations include the absence of a mechanism for trading marginal benefits against marginal risks, no accounting for unknown-unknown risks, and the recognition that the framework cannot resolve coordination problems among developers, platforms, and regulators. The paper's central open question is empirical: what is the actual marginal risk for each misuse vector as model capabilities evolve, and can defenders keep pace?
