## Problem

3D point clouds in real-world datasets often lack a predefined reference frame, making orientation normalization essential for downstream analysis. Existing direct methods (e.g., PCA) suffer from sign ambiguity and fail on highly symmetric inputs, while learning-based frame determination lacks continuity guarantees, introduces approximation error, and may not generalize to symmetries unseen in training. No prior method provides a universal, training-free, error-free normalization that handles arbitrary point clouds of any rank and symmetry.

## Input / Output

The method accepts an unordered 3D point cloud (m points in R^3, optionally with per-point categorical features such as atom types) and outputs an orthogonal frame matrix F(X) ∈ O(3) or SO(3) whose inverse applied to the point cloud yields a canonical invariant representation. Points at the origin are excluded since their orientation is undefined, and the method handles degenerate cases (rank 1 or 2 point clouds) by terminating the alignment sequence early. The frame is S_m-invariant, meaning the output is independent of the input point ordering.

## Method

The ASUN algorithm constructs a frame in three stages: (1) project points onto the unit sphere to obtain a symmetry-preserving concise representation, (2) build a directed labeled graph from the convex hull of projected points and convert it to a deterministic finite automaton (DFA), then minimize the DFA via Hopcroft's algorithm to identify all asymmetric units—minimal subgraphs whose orbit under graph automorphisms reconstructs the full graph, (3) extract one asymmetric unit as an ordered sequence of directed edges, use the corresponding unit-sphere points to select linearly independent vectors, and construct the frame by sequentially aligning these vectors to the x-axis, xy-plane, and positive z-direction via rotations (and a reflection for O(3)). The key theoretical insight is that while continuous frames are provably impossible for point clouds with m,n ≥ 3 (Proposition 3.2), the universality of canonicalization is preserved through the orthogonality of group representations (Theorem 3.3), ensuring any downstream learning framework retains its universal approximation capability without requiring frame continuity.

## Results

On QM9 molecular normalization, ASUN achieves near-zero Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) across all ranks (0.00014, 0.00008, 0.02826 for ranks 1–3), while PCA fails catastrophically at rank 3 (0.82758) and the learned auto-encoder (AE) fails at rank 1 (1.15122). On ModelNet40 shape classification, applying ASUN to positional features boosts GCN accuracy from 13.57% to 18.19% and EGNN from 29.94% to 53.36% under ASUN/ASUN augmentation. On the n-body trajectory prediction task, ASUN-GNN achieves the best MSE (0.0139) with the lowest forward time (0.0032 s), outperforming equivariant architectures like EGNN (0.0141) and TFN (0.0343).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASUN dominates PCA (Bellekens et al.) across all ranks, with the gap most dramatic at rank 3 where PCA's EMD is 0.828 versus ASUN's 0.028, reflecting PCA's inability to resolve sign ambiguities in high-symmetry molecules. Against the learned auto-encoder (Winter et al.), ASUN wins decisively at rank 1 (0.00014 vs. 1.151) and rank 2 (0.00008 vs. 0.038), while being marginally better at rank 3 (0.028 vs. 0.032); the AE also fails to generalize to unseen point groups (e.g., 0.240 EMD on C∞v vs. ASUN's 0.001). On ModelNet40, ASUN-invariant GCN (18.19%) outperforms equivariant SchNet (28.93%) and EGNN (25.41%) under matched ASUN/ASUN augmentation, demonstrating that a simple GNN with explicit normalization can rival or exceed purpose-built equivariant architectures. On n-body prediction, ASUN-GNN (MSE 0.0139, 0.0032 s) beats EGNN (0.0141, 0.0062 s), TFN (0.0343, 0.0343 s), and frame-averaging (0.0177, 0.0041 s) while matching the forward time of a plain GNN. MACE and eSCN are not directly comparable: MACE achieves 27.55% on ModelNet40 but ASUN-GCN reaches 18.19% with far less compute, and eSCN exceeds A100 GPU memory in all settings. Overall, ASUN consistently outperforms or matches every baseline on its primary normalization metric while enabling simpler, faster downstream architectures to achieve competitive or superior task performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: the classical symmetry-detection algorithm of Alt et al. (1988) using graph simplification and DFA minimization, the relaxed-equivariance canonicalization framework of Kaba et al. (2023), and the universality-of-canonicalization theorem of Winter et al. (2022). It departs from prior work in two key ways: first, it formally proves that continuous frames are impossible for point clouds under O(3)/SO(3)/E(n)/SE(n) (Proposition 3.2), resolving a known concern; second, it removes the continuity requirement from the universality guarantee by exploiting orthogonality of group representations (Theorem 3.3). Rather than learning frames via neural networks as in Winter et al. or Kaba et al., the paper provides an explicit, training-free construction that extracts asymmetric units from the minimal DFA to determine a canonical orientation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical result that universality of canonicalization holds without continuity is a clean resolution of a gap in the equivariant ML literature, and the training-free, architecture-independent normalization method makes orientation handling accessible to any downstream model (not just equivariant ones). Practical limitations include the O(m log m) symmetry-detection step, the restriction to 3D, and the requirement that point clouds be centered; scalability to very large or noisy point clouds remains untested. Open questions include whether the asymmetric-unit approach extends to higher-dimensional point clouds or to point clouds with continuous (rather than discrete) features, and whether the DFA-based symmetry detection can be made more robust to numerical noise in real-world data.
