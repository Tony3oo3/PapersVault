## Problem

Selective mixup methods, which apply mixup to non-randomly selected pairs (e.g., across classes or domains), have achieved remarkable gains on OOD generalization benchmarks, yet the mechanism behind their success remained unexplained. Prior work attributed improvements to the mixing operation itself, systematically omitting ablations that would reveal the non-random pair selection implicitly biases the training distribution. This paper identifies that overlooked resampling effect as a primary, and sometimes sole, driver of the observed improvements.

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard supervised classification dataset (RGB images, text, or tabular code sequences) with optional domain labels and a selection predicate (e.g., 'different class' or 'different domain') as its key parameter. Rather than altering the model architecture, it redefines the training procedure by constructing a 'virtual dataset' of second pair elements whose sampling distribution differs from the original, thereby shifting the effective training distribution over classes and covariates. The output remains standard class scores, but the training distribution is modified in a predictable, analyzable way that can be measured via KL divergence or entropy.

## Method

The paper proves (Theorem 3.1) that under the 'different class' criterion, the combined training distribution (original data plus virtual data) has strictly higher entropy than the original, regressing toward uniformity; in binary classification this is exactly equivalent to uniform class sampling, the standard correction for label shift. To isolate the resampling effect from the mixing effect, the authors introduce 'selective sampling,' an ablation where selected pairs are concatenated into the mini-batch without any interpolation. Across five diverse datasets spanning vision, NLP, and tabular modalities, they show that resampling alone explains much or all of selective mixup's OOD gains, and that mixing and resampling effects are additive and largely independent. They further predict and empirically verify a new failure mode: when the training-to-test shift increases rather than decreases class imbalance (reversing the 'regression toward the mean'), the resampling effect becomes actively detrimental.

## Results

Across five OOD generalization benchmarks, the paper demonstrates that selective mixup's improvements are largely attributable to an implicit resampling effect rather than the mixing operation itself. On Waterbirds, the best selective mixup criterion (same domain / different class) achieves worst-group accuracy comparable to simple uniform class/domain resampling, while vanilla mixup is actually detrimental relative to ERM. On MIMIC-Readmission, the best methods bring the sampled majority-class proportion from 78.2% (training) to ~64.3% (closer to the 66.5% test distribution), and the novel combination of uniform-class resampling with vanilla mixup matches selective mixup performance. On Yearbook and arXiv, the effects of mixing and resampling are additive and independent, with new combinations (e.g., uniform-class resampling + same-class selective mixup) surpassing all prior selective mixup variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla mixup (Zhang et al., 2017), selective mixup generally wins on OOD metrics, but the paper reveals this advantage is largely due to resampling rather than the mixing operation: on Waterbirds, vanilla mixup is worse than ERM, and selective sampling without mixing matches selective mixup. Against resampling baselines (Idrissi et al., 2022), the key finding is equivalence: on Waterbirds, the best selective mixup criterion performs no better than uniform class/domain resampling, while on Yearbook and arXiv, combining resampling with selective mixup yields results superior to either alone. Against the original LISA/selective mixup (Yao et al., 2022), the paper shows that the claimed improvements are partly or fully explained by the implicit resampling: selective sampling (pairs selected but not mixed) reproduces much of the gain, and on CivilComments, the best selective mixup criterion (same domain / diff. class) actually performs better without the mixing step. The paper also identifies a new failure mode: when the training-to-test shift increases rather than decreases imbalance (Yearbook-reversed), methods that helped on the original split become detrimental, a prediction that only the resampling explanation accounts for.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper provides a theoretical and empirical re-examination of selective mixup (LISA, Yao et al. 2022b), building directly on the mixup framework of Zhang et al. (2017) and the selective pair-selection criteria proposed by Yao et al. The key conceptual departure is revealing that the non-random pair selection in selective mixup implicitly resamples the training distribution, making it equivalent in part to classical importance-weighted ERM / data balancing (Idrissi et al., 2022; Sagawa et al., 2019) — a connection entirely absent from prior explanations. The paper thus bridges two previously disconnected literatures: selective data augmentation for OOD generalization and resampling/reweighting for distribution shifts, showing that the mixing operation and the resampling effect are largely independent and additive.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that selective mixup's success on popular OOD benchmarks is partly or fully attributable to an accidental 'regression toward the mean' in the datasets raises a legitimate concern about overfitting to benchmark artifacts and Goodhart's law in ML evaluation. The predicted failure mode (reversing the training/test split makes the method detrimental) is a concrete, testable warning for practitioners deploying these methods in new settings. Open questions include whether the resampling explanation extends to the many modified objectives proposed in follow-up selective mixup works (contrastive losses, Grad-CAM regularization, etc.), and whether the 'regression toward the mean' property holds in genuinely real-world deployment scenarios rather than curated benchmarks.
