## Problem

Existing domain adaptation methods assume a fixed training dataset, and while data-centric approaches have emerged, the practical problem of strategically allocating a limited sample budget across multiple training domains to improve generalization to a shifted test domain remains unexplored. In many real-world settings, one can only control which domain to sample from (not individual samples), and labels cannot be obtained from the test domain, making the data acquisition strategy itself a critical lever for performance under distribution shift.

## Input / Output

The method requires access to Q training domains (from which samples are drawn randomly, with only the per-domain count controllable), a set of unlabeled test domain samples for distribution estimation, a total acquisition budget T, and a number of acquisition rounds K. It outputs per-domain acquisition weights for each round, the resulting T collected training samples, and a trained prediction model (ERM, reweighting, or self-supervised). Unlike traditional active learning, there is no large pre-existing pool to query; the method must decide how many samples to draw from each domain at each round, with the constraint that samples within a domain are drawn i.i.d. from that domain's distribution.

## Method

DAA splits the total budget T into K equal rounds. The first round acquires samples uniformly across all Q domains; in each subsequent round, it computes the average RKHS embedding of previously collected samples per domain and of the unlabeled test samples, then solves a regularized least-squares problem (Eq. 7) to learn domain weights W that minimize the MMD-style distance between the weighted mixture of training domain distributions and the test distribution. The next round's per-domain sample counts are proportional to these learned weights. A theoretical analysis (Theorem 4.1) proves that as the minimum per-domain sample count grows, the estimated weights converge to the optimal weights W*, with the convergence rate governed by the sampling variance. This iterative refinement ensures the accumulated training distribution progressively mirrors the test distribution, and the final model is trained on all T collected samples using standard ERM, sample reweighting, or self-supervised learning.

## Results

On the FMOW satellite image dataset (19 training countries → France test domain), DAA consistently outperforms uniform acquisition and Top-K baselines across all sample budgets (2000, 4000, 6000) and model types (ERM, Reweighting, Self-supervised). At 4000 samples, DAA achieves 86.84% (ERM), 86.25% (Reweighting), and 87.35% (Self-supervised) accuracy, compared to 85.23%, 84.56%, and 86.28% for uniform acquisition. On the ACS PUMS tabular dataset (50 US states), DAA achieves the best results in almost all task-domain combinations at both 1000 and 2000 sample volumes. On Rotated MNIST, DAA surpasses even the best exhaustive domain combination [60°, 120°] without requiring exhaustive search.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DAA against three data acquisition baselines: uniform acquisition (equal samples from all Q domains), Top-10 (select the 10 closest domains to test, then sample uniformly from them), and Top-3 (select the 3 closest domains). DAA wins on all 9 evaluation settings in the FMOW table, with margins of 0.5–1.8% over uniform and 0.1–0.8% over Top-10. Top-3 is particularly unreliable: on the 2000-sample Reweighting setting it drops to 82.90% (worse than uniform's 84.49%), and on 6000-sample Reweighting it scores only 87.00% versus DAA's 88.25%. On ACS PUMS, DAA achieves the best accuracy in nearly all 10 task-domain combinations at both 1000 and 2000 sample volumes, while uniform acquisition frequently yields the worst results, especially at smaller budgets. The paper also shows that model-level optimizations (reweighting, self-supervised learning) cannot compensate for poor data acquisition: an ERM model trained with DAA data outperforms a self-supervised model trained with suboptimal domain combinations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard domain adaptation paradigm, which assumes a fixed training set and focuses on model-level alignment (reweighting, feature matching, adversarial training), by instead treating the training data composition as the optimization variable. It repurposes MMD-based distribution matching—previously used to reweight samples during training—into a data acquisition strategy that determines how many samples to draw from each of Q source domains. The iterative refinement loop is conceptually analogous to active learning, but operates at the domain level rather than the sample level, and explicitly requires no predefined data pool or external annotator for the test domain, distinguishing it from active domain adaptation (Fu et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2022) which selects test-domain samples for labeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a practical and somewhat underappreciated point: when you control where to collect data (e.g., which hospital, which region, which sensor), the allocation strategy can matter as much as the model architecture, especially under tight budgets. The theoretical convergence guarantee (Theorem 4.1) is clean but relies on the covariate-shift assumption and access to unlabeled test data, both of which may not hold in all deployments. Open questions include how the approach behaves under label shift, how it scales when the number of domains Q is very large, and whether the MMD-based criterion can be replaced by cheaper proxies in high-dimensional settings.
