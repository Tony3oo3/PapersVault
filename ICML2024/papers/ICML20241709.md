## Problem

LLMs are increasingly deployed as decision-making agents, but no holistic methodology exists for assessing whether they exhibit economically rational behavior across the full spectrum of decision-making settings. Existing evaluations are restricted to narrow tasks, limited economic domains, or open-world games, leaving no structured way to decompose rationality into individually testable components or to produce a comprehensive, tuneable report card.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241709/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a black-box LLM and feeds it multiple-choice questions (each a decision scenario with exactly one correct option) drawn from 24,500+ synthetically generated items spanning 64 elements of rationality, 13 grade levels, and multiple domains (finance, medicine, technology, etc.). The LLM is queried with temperature-0 sampling, optionally augmented with self-explanation prompts or 0–5 in-context examples. Output is a STEER Report Card: a structured, user-tuneable scorecard reporting exact-match accuracy, normalized accuracy (gap over random guessing), expected calibration error, domain robustness (worst-case accuracy across domains per element), and dependency robustness (penalty for failing prerequisite elements in a dependency graph).

## Method

The core contribution is a hierarchical taxonomy of 64 'elements of rationality' organized into four settings (Foundations, Single-Agent, Multi-Agent, Decisions on Behalf of Other Agents), each subdivided into modules and elements with explicit dependency edges. Questions are generated by GPT-4 Turbo from hand-crafted templates that specify domain, grade level (1–13), and formatting constraints; 10% of all generations are manually validated (98.54% pass rate). Scoring goes beyond simple accuracy: normalized accuracy compensates for varying numbers of answer choices, domain robustness takes the minimum accuracy across all domains for each element, and dependency robustness quantifies how much worse a model performs on an element relative to its prerequisite sub-skills in the dependency graph. The framework is evaluated on 14 LLMs (from Llama 7B to GPT-4 Turbo) across 134,750 test questions, with ablations on self-explanation (separate vs. together) and few-shot prompting (0–5 examples) to characterize how adaptation strategies shift performance across grade levels and modules.

## Results

Across the full STEER benchmark (134,750 test questions), GPT-4 Turbo achieves the highest normalized accuracy (0.3302) and exact-match accuracy (63.61%), followed by GPT-3.5 Turbo (0.3071 / 61.69%) and Llama-2 70b (0.2897 / 41.73%). Models with fewer than 40 billion parameters (Llama 13b, Llama-2 13b, Falcon 7b, Llama 7b, Llama-2 7b) all score below random guessing (negative normalized accuracy). Performance degrades consistently with grade level: GPT-4 Turbo closes ~75% of the gap to perfect on Grade 0 (Foundations) but falls to random-guessing level from Grade 9 (Best Response) onward.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, STEER evaluates 14 LLMs rather than comparing a single method against baselines. GPT-4 Turbo wins in nearly all head-to-head matchups against the other 13 models, though it still closes only about one-third of the gap between random guessing and perfect answers overall. Performance correlates strongly with parameter count: the four models above 40B parameters (GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-3.5 Turbo, Llama-2 70b, Llama 65b) all achieve positive normalized accuracy, while all models at or below 13B parameters fall below random. Among open-source models, Llama-2 70b (0.2897) outperforms Llama 65b (0.2849) and Falcon 40b Instruct (0.1320), with a notable gap between the 40B+ tier and the 13B tier. Adaptation strategies show consistent gains: self-explanation (both separate and together variants) improves all models, with the largest gains on lower-grade questions and cognitive-bias elements (e.g., GPT-4 Turbo's Avoidance of the Certainty Effect jumps from 0.079 to 0.508). Few-shot prompting with up to 3 examples improves performance across all models, but adding 4 or 5 examples degrades performance for every model tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior LLM evaluation work that has focused on narrowly defined tasks (AgentBench, ToolBench, WebArena), limited economic settings (FinQA, FLUE, repeated games), or open-world video games, by instead grounding its benchmark in the mature economic literature on rational decision making. It draws on the von Neumann-Morgenstern utility axioms, game theory (Nash equilibrium, backward induction, Bayes-Nash equilibrium), social choice theory (Arrow's axioms, voting schemes), and mechanism design to construct a hierarchical taxonomy of 64 'elements of rationality' with explicit dependency relationships. The MCQA format follows standard practice (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Suzgun et al., 2022), and the use of LLM-generated questions with human validation is a practical extension of synthetic data generation approaches. The key conceptual departure is the principled, axiomatic decomposition of 'economic rationality' into testable sub-components with a dependency graph, rather than ad hoc task selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark provides the first comprehensive, principled framework for assessing whether LLMs can serve as reliable economic decision-making agents, which is critical as LLMs are increasingly deployed in financial, medical, and policy contexts. The finding that models under 40B parameters cannot reliably outperform random guessing, and that even GPT-4 Turbo degrades to chance performance on Grade 9+ questions (strategic reasoning in multi-agent settings), sets clear expectations for current capabilities. The dependency robustness analysis reveals a subtle but important failure mode: models can appear competent at advanced game-theoretic tasks while lacking mastery of their conceptual prerequisites, suggesting fragility under rephrasing. Open questions include how humans would score on this benchmark (providing a meaningful baseline), whether the vNM alignment is the right canonical reference point for all contexts, and whether more sophisticated prompting or fine-tuning can close the gap on higher-grade elements.
