## Problem

Model-free RL methods suffer from high gradient variance due to zeroth-order policy gradient estimation, while first-order model-based RL (FO-MBRL) methods using differentiable simulation reduce variance but introduce a previously uncharacterized sampling error from stiff contact dynamics. This paper identifies that the FOBG sample error grows exponentially with rollout horizon and dynamics gradient magnitude (Lemma 3.2), explaining why prior FO-MBRL methods like SHAC exhibit brittleness and suboptimal asymptotic performance in contact-rich tasks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes robot state vectors (joint positions and velocities) from the dflex GPU-based differentiable rigid-body simulator and outputs joint torques (for Hopper, Ant, Anymal, Humanoid) or muscle forces (for SNU Humanoid with 152 action dimensions). Unusually, the rollout horizon H is itself a learned scalar parameter adapted online via a Lagrangian dual, and the contact stiffness threshold C is applied to normalized per-dimension accelerations to enable a single uniform threshold across all tasks. The critic is trained iteratively until convergence (change in loss < 0.2 over 5 iterations) rather than for a fixed number of steps.

## Method

AHAC builds on SHAC's actor-critic architecture with a differentiable simulator but introduces a constrained optimization objective that penalizes rollouts where the dynamics Jacobian norm exceeds a contact stiffness threshold C. The constraint is handled via a Lagrangian dual (Eq. 10) with per-timestep multipliers φ that adapt the horizon H during training, effectively shortening rollouts when stiff contact is detected and lengthening them otherwise. The theoretical contribution (Lemma 3.2) bounds the FOBG sample error as H·B_r·B_π·(1/2 + B_f^(H-1)), showing the error compounds exponentially with both horizon length and dynamics gradient magnitude, motivating the adaptive truncation. Additional modifications include a double critic (replacing SHAC's target critic) and iterative critic training until convergence, both of which reduce run-to-run variance. Contact forces are normalized by modified per-dimension accelerations to allow a single threshold C=500 across all five locomotion tasks.

## Results

AHAC achieves a 40% higher asymptotic episode reward (PPO-normalized 50% IQM) than the best model-free baseline (PPO) across five locomotion tasks, with gains ranging from 10% on Hopper to 64% on SNU Humanoid (m=152). On the Ant task specifically, AHAC reaches 1.41× PPO's reward while PPO and SAC, even when trained for 3B timesteps (100× more samples, 10× more wall-clock time), still fall short. AHAC also outperforms the closest FO-MBRL baseline SHAC by 8–24% across tasks, with the largest gap on Ant (1.41 vs. 1.16) and SNU Humanoid (1.64 vs. 1.44).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AHAC outperforms all four baselines on every task. Against PPO (the strongest MFRL baseline), AHAC gains 10% on Hopper, 41% on Ant, 46% on Anymal, 35% on Humanoid, and 64% on SNU Humanoid, demonstrating that the advantage grows with action-space dimensionality. Against SAC, AHAC wins by 26–86% across tasks, with the largest margin on SNU Humanoid (1.64 vs. 0.88). Against SVG (a learned-dynamics FO-MBRL method), AHAC leads by 25–119%, confirming that differentiable simulation provides more reliable gradients than a learned dynamics model. Against SHAC (the closest FO-MBRL competitor using the same differentiable simulator), AHAC improves by 8% on Hopper, 22% on Ant, 16% on Anymal, 17% on Humanoid, and 14% on SNU Humanoid, with the adaptive horizon mechanism identified as the primary contributor via ablation. Notably, even when PPO and SAC are given 100× more samples and 10× more wall-clock time, they still underperform AHAC, suggesting MFRL methods hit a fundamental ceiling due to zeroth-order gradient variance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends SHAC (Xu et al., 2022), which introduced short-horizon actor-critic training through differentiable simulation, by diagnosing the source of its gradient error—stiff contact dynamics producing large ‖∇f‖—and proposing an adaptive horizon mechanism to avoid it. The theoretical analysis (Lemma 3.2) builds on prior gradient estimation theory (Suh et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023) but applies it specifically to contact-rich differentiable simulation, showing that FOBG sample error grows exponentially with horizon length and contact stiffness. The actor-critic architecture with a model-free TD(λ) critic and first-order actor gradients follows the broader FO-MBRL lineage (SVG, Dreamer, IVG), but the Lagrangian-dual formulation for horizon adaptation and the double critic with iterative training are novel contributions that depart from SHAC's fixed-horizon, single-target-critic design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides strong evidence that MFRL methods (PPO, SAC) hit a fundamental performance ceiling in contact-rich locomotion tasks—achieving 40–64% lower asymptotic reward than AHAC even when given 10^6× more training data—challenging the assumption that sufficient data compensates for zeroth-order gradient variance. This validates first-order policy learning through differentiable simulation as a viable path to more optimal policies, particularly for high-dimensional control (152 action dimensions). Open questions include: whether the contact threshold C and horizon learning rate can be made fully automatic, how well policies transfer from differentiable simulators to real hardware, and whether the gait-period–horizon relationship generalizes beyond locomotion to manipulation or other contact-rich tasks.
