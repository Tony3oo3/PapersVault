## Problem

When multiple treatment administrators (e.g., advertisers) compete for the same subjects in randomized controlled trials, the rank at which a treatment is displayed attenuates its causal effect, making it difficult to estimate the true treatment effect (the effect had the treatment come first). No prior framework simultaneously models the strategic competition over subject slots and the optimal statistical estimation of the causal effect under position-dependent treatment decay.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a nonnegative integer bid matrix x ∈ N_{≥0}^{n×k} (each admin's budget allocation across n subject slots), known discount factors (α_r) with α_1 = 1 and α_r ∈ (0,1), and per-admin budgets B^(a). It produces a Nash equilibrium bid allocation strategy profile, a minimax lower bound on estimation error of the form min(σ²/(16(1−q)·S), 1) where S = Σ n_r·α_r² is the sample value, and an (ε,η)-approximate equilibrium guarantee linking the tractable sample value game to the true estimation error game. The allocation rule is probabilistic, so outputs are characterized in expectation over the induced rank distribution.

## Method

The paper constructs a game where k administrators bid on n subject slots under a probabilistic allocation rule (activation probability PA_a = 1−(1−p)^{x_a(t)} with random ordering), and proves three connected results. First, using Le Cam's method on a potential outcomes model with position-dependent treatment effects τ_r = α_r·τ, they derive a minimax lower bound showing any estimator's MSE is at least min(1/S, 1) where S = Σ n_r·α_r² is the 'sample value.' Second, they show the sample value objective admits a pure Nash equilibrium characterized by uniform unit bidding, exhaustive budget use, and minimal overlap between admins (Lemma 4.1). Third, the main approximation theorem (Theorem 2.2) proves that any Nash equilibrium of the sample value game is an (ε,η)-approximate Nash equilibrium of the estimation error game with ε = O(k/√B) and η = O(exp(−B/k²)), via McDiarmid's concentration inequality on S and Jensen's inequality relating E[1/S] to 1/E[S]. The estimator achieving the bound up to constants is an inverse-variance-weighted combination of per-rank Horvitz-Thompson estimators.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative result (Theorem 2.2) establishes that any Nash equilibrium of the sample value game G_SV is an (ε, η)-approximate Nash equilibrium of the estimation error game G_MSE, with ε = O(k/√B) and η = O(exp(−B/k²)), where B = min_a B^(a) is the minimum budget. In the regime where all administrators have budget B^(a) = ω(k²), this yields a (o(1), o(1))-approximate Nash equilibrium, meaning the sample value equilibrium is essentially exact for the error minimization objective. The minimax lower bound (Theorem 3.4) shows that for any estimator τ̂, the squared error is bounded below by min(σ²/(16(1−q)·Σ n_r·α_r²), 1), and this bound is matched up to constants by an inverse-variance-weighted combination of Horvitz-Thompson estimators at each rank.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The closest prior work it builds upon is Maehara et al. (2015), which models budget allocation among competing advertisers but without explicit rank positions (an advertiser either wins a customer or not). The present paper generalizes that model by introducing multiple positions with position-dependent treatment effects (τ_r = α_r · τ), and shows that the resulting game admits a pure Nash equilibrium characterized in closed form (Lemma 4.1: uniform budget splitting with maximal competition avoidance). The paper does not benchmark against other causal inference estimators or auction designs empirically; instead, it provides a minimax lower bound via Le Cam's method and shows that standard estimators (OLS, Horvitz-Thompson with inverse-variance weighting) achieve the bound up to constant factors. The connection to position-based auction literature (Varian 2007, Athey & Ellison 2011) and autobidding literature (Aggarwal et al. 2019, Balseiro et al. 2021) is conceptual rather than quantitative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly generalizes Maehara et al. (2015), which studied budget allocation among competing advertisers in a game-theoretic setting but without explicit ranks (an advertiser either wins a customer or not). Here, the authors introduce multiple positions with position-dependent treatment effects, and—crucially—replace the sample-size objective with a causal estimation error objective. The causal inference side draws on potential outcomes, Horvitz-Thompson estimators, Le Cam's method for minimax bounds, and inverse-variance weighting from meta-analysis, while the game-theoretic side borrows the probabilistic allocation rule structure from position-based auction literature (Varian 2007; Börgers et al. 2013) and autobidding frameworks (Aggarwal et al. 2019; Balseiro et al. 2021). The key departure from all prior work is the explicit coupling: the agents' utility is not a value for winning subjects but the statistical estimation error of a causal quantity, making the game's equilibrium a statement about optimal experimental design under competition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical characterization of what estimation error competing treatment administrators can achieve at equilibrium, bridging causal inference and game theory in a way neither toolkit addresses alone. The practical implication is clear for online advertising and field experiments: an advertiser can distinguish whether poor performance stems from low display rank or poor treatment design, and the equilibrium analysis tells them how to bid rationally. Open questions include the Price of Anarchy of the estimation-error game, whether results extend to other allocation rules (e.g., generalized second-price auctions), fairness of utility distribution across admins (Nash social welfare), and the case where discount factors must be jointly estimated with the treatment effect. The framework is currently limited to known position effects, bounded treatment effects, and a specific probabilistic allocation rule.
