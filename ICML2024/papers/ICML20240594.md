## Problem

Differentiable simulators enable first-order simulation gradients for policy optimization, but these gradients become unreliable when the simulation dynamics are non-smooth (e.g., hard contacts in Mujoco-style engines), causing gradient outliers that destabilize or collapse training. Prior work blended simulation gradients with zeroth-order policy gradients (PG) via inverse-variance weighting, but PG itself exhibits high and fluctuating variance, making it a poor detector of rough simulation regions. The paper asks: can a more stable zeroth-order estimator replace PG to robustly handle non-smooth differentiable simulations?

## Input / Output

The method consumes state vectors from differentiable physics engines (Brax generalized/positional/spring backends, DFlex) and produces continuous control actions via a reparameterized Gaussian policy. A key structural detail is that trajectories are truncated into horizons of length h (default 32), and the Q function is evaluated at every step within the horizon—not just at the terminal state—so that a per-step adaptive mixture ratio can be computed. The optimization objective (Eq. 4) combines discounted rewards, per-step Q values weighted by cumulative credit β_t, and a terminal Q value, all scaled by the horizon length.

## Method

AGPO introduces an adaptive analytic gradient that blends first-order simulation gradients (SG) with Q-function gradients (QG) using inverse-variance weighting, where the mixture ratio α is computed per-step from the empirical variance of batched gradients across parallel environments. The critical insight is that QG—obtained by differentiating the learned Q function with respect to policy parameters—has consistently low variance across all simulation steps, unlike both SG (which spikes at non-smooth transitions) and PG (which fluctuates broadly), making QG a reliable fallback. During backpropagation, a near-zero α for a given step effectively bypasses that rough transition by shifting credit to the Q value, and the cumulative product β_t = ∏α_k provides early truncation when the trajectory accumulates too much uncertainty. Theoretically, AGPO achieves a gradient variance upper bound of O(1/h) that does not grow exponentially with horizon length or dynamics Lipschitz constants, in contrast to prior SG-based methods, yielding faster convergence to stationary points.

## Results

AGPO is evaluated on five Brax continuous control tasks (Ant, Hopper, Humanoid, Walker2d, HalfCheetah) and two DFlex tasks (Humanoid, HumanoidMTU), with performance reported as average episode return over training. AGPO significantly outperforms both SHAC and BPTT across all Brax tasks, and matches or exceeds the pure RL baselines (PPO, SAC, TD3) in final performance while achieving better sample efficiency. On the smoothly differentiable DFlex backend, AGPO matches SHAC's performance, confirming that the adaptive mechanism does not degrade performance when simulation gradients are already reliable. Ablations show that increasing the horizon from h=8 to h=128 monotonically improves performance, and the adaptive mixture ratio outperforms all fixed-ratio variants (α=0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, 1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SHAC (Xu et al., ICLR 2022), AGPO achieves substantially higher returns on all five Brax tasks where contact dynamics introduce non-smoothness, while matching SHAC on the smooth DFlex backend (Fig. 9), indicating the adaptive mechanism adds value specifically where SG is unreliable. Against AoBG (Suh et al., ICML 2022), AGPO clearly wins: the paper's Appendix C shows AoBG exhibits poor performance on Brax Ant, and the mixture ratio α_∞ in AoBG stays near 1, confirming that PG (used by AoBG) has too high a variance to serve as a reliable fallback for SG outliers. Against PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), AGPO outperforms in both final performance and sample efficiency, as PPO's zeroth-order gradient estimation requires many more samples to converge. Against SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018) and TD3 (Fujimoto et al., ICML 2018), AGPO surpasses both, demonstrating that exploiting simulation differentiability with the adaptive gradient provides advantages over purely off-policy RL methods that ignore the simulator's gradient structure. The adaptive SG+QG mixture also outperforms rule-based outlier handling (MaxNorm clipping, Quantile filtering) and all fixed-ratio blends, validating the inverse-variance weighting approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inverse-variance-weighting framework for blending first-order simulation gradients (SG) with zeroth-order estimators, originally introduced by Parmas et al. (2018, PIPPS) and adapted to differentiable simulation by Suh et al. (2022, AoBG). The key departure is replacing the policy gradient (PG) as the zeroth-order partner with the Q-function gradient (QG), motivated by empirical evidence that PG's variance is too high to reliably detect rough simulation transitions. The short-horizon truncation and value-function-at-horizon-end design follows SHAC (Xu et al., 2022), while the computational efficiency of computing gradient variances at a shared node (here, the action node) follows Parmas et al. (2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AGPO addresses a practical bottleneck: popular differentiable simulators (Mujoco, Brax generalized backend) produce non-smooth dynamics due to contact solvers, causing SG-based methods like SHAC to fail or degrade. The finding that QG provides a more stable variance signal than PG for adaptive blending is a useful design principle for future hybrid gradient methods in differentiable simulation. Limitations include the extra backpropagation cost for ratio computation and the reliance on a well-trained Q function; the authors note that tensor-valued weights and covariance-based composition (Parmas et al., 2023) could further improve efficiency. Open questions include whether the approach extends to partially observable settings or simulators where even the Q function is unreliable due to extreme non-smoothness.
