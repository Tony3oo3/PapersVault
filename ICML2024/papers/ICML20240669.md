## Problem

Existing collaborative causal inference methods rely on meta-analysis, requiring each site to produce a valid ATE estimator before pooling. This fails under heterogeneity when individual sites have under-coverage of the target population (e.g., a trial only enrolling elderly patients), making it impossible to incorporate such sites. The paper asks whether collaboration can succeed across disjoint covariate domains without requiring any single site to be representative of the full target population.

## Input / Output

The method takes K site-level datasets of (X, Y, Z) triples where X ∈ ℝ^d are covariates, Y is a continuous outcome, and Z ∈ {0,1} is binary treatment, plus a public census dataset D^(t) containing only covariates X drawn from the target distribution. Sites share only their estimated propensity score functions e^(k,z)(X) and summary statistics (weighted means, effective sample sizes) with a central server, preserving individual-level privacy. The output is a single ATE point estimate τ̂ with an asymptotic normal confidence interval, computed via a Hajek-type ratio estimator.

## Method

The core contribution is the CLB-IPW estimator, which replaces the meta-analysis step of averaging site-wise ATE estimates with a step that first averages the site-specific propensity score functions (weighted by η^(k)) and then computes a single IPW estimator using this combined score. Individual site estimators τ̂_k(Σ_r η^(r) ê^(r)) are inconsistent, but their average across sites recovers consistency because the combined denominator Σ_r e^(r,z)(X) is positive wherever any site covers the individual. This yields a uniformly lower asymptotic variance than meta-IPW (Theorem 2) because it uses a coarser balancing score P(Z(S)|X) rather than the full P(S|X). For robustness against density-ratio misspecification, the paper extends to a decoupled CLB-AIPW estimator that computes the outcome-model difference on the public target dataset and the IPW correction on site data, achieving asymptotic normality when covariate dimension d < 8 using K-NN density ratio estimation. The entire pipeline is implemented via federated learning (FedAvg/SCAFFOLD) for outcome model training, with sites only exchanging model parameters and summary statistics.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that the asymptotic variance of CLB-IPW is always bounded above by that of Meta-IPW (v_CLB² ≤ v_Meta²), with the gap widening as heterogeneity increases. In synthetic experiments (3 sites, N = 1000/2000/3000, target N = 10000), CLB-IPW and CLB-AIPW maintain stable MSE as mean KL-divergence between source and target distributions increases from 0 to 4, while Meta-IPW MSE grows substantially. On the real-world accuracy-nudge datasets (Pennycook et al. 2020: τ̂ = 0.034, p < 0.001; Roozenbeek et al. 2021: τ̂ = 0.015, p ≈ 0.017), all four estimators yield similar confidence intervals since the two sites are relatively homogeneous, though CLB-IPW and AIPW show slightly larger effect sizes consistent with the original studies. Asymptotic normality of CLB-AIPW is established for covariate dimension d < 8 using K-NN density ratio estimation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Meta-IPW (site-wise IPW followed by inverse-variance-weighted meta-analysis). CLB-IPW uniformly dominates Meta-IPW in variance (Theorem 2) and, critically, remains stable as inter-site heterogeneity increases, whereas Meta-IPW's error grows with KL-divergence (Figure 2). Against single-site IPW estimators, both Meta-IPW and CLB-IPW outperform, but CLB-IPW additionally handles sites with under-coverage (e.g., a site only including elderly patients) that would render single-site estimators inconsistent. The AIPW variants (Meta-AIPW and CLB-AIPW) add robustness: when the propensity score model is misspecified but the outcome model is correct, both AIPW estimators remain consistent, while both IPW estimators fail (Figure 3). When both models are misspecified, no estimator is consistent, as expected. On the real-world application where the two sites are close, all four methods perform comparably, with no clear winner, suggesting the advantage of CLB methods is most pronounced under substantial heterogeneity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard federated causal inference pipeline (Xiong et al. 2022; Han et al. 2022, 2023) where each site first adjusts for distribution shift via density ratio re-weighting and then a central server performs meta-analysis on the resulting per-site estimators. Instead, it proposes aggregating at the nuisance-model level: taking a weighted mean of site-specific propensity score functions before computing the IPW estimator, which it calls CLB-IPW. The doubly robust extension (CLB-AIPW) borrows the decoupling strategy from orthogonal statistical learning (Foster & Syrgkanis 2020) and the Hajek-form IPW structure from classical causal inference (Bang & Robins 2005; Little & Rubin 2019), while the density ratio estimation draws on both parametric exponential tilting (Han et al. 2022) and nonparametric K-NN matching (Lin et al. 2021).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is enabling collaboration across disjoint domains—sites that individually cannot produce a valid ATE estimator (e.g., a trial enrolling only elderly patients) can still contribute meaningfully, which prior meta-analysis frameworks explicitly exclude. The theoretical variance dominance (v_CLB² ≤ v_Meta²) is clean but the practical gains in the real-world fake-news replication are modest, and the asymptotic normality guarantee for the AIPW variant requires covariate dimension d < 8 under K-NN density ratio estimation, limiting applicability to high-dimensional settings. Open questions include communication-efficient optimal weighting of propensity models and extending the framework beyond the d < 8 constraint, potentially via parametric density ratio models or alternative nuisance estimators.
