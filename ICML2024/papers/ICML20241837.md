## Problem

Algorithmic prediction systems are widely deployed to target individuals for societal interventions, but the underlying assumption that individual-level predictions are necessary for efficient allocation has never been formally tested. This paper asks: under what conditions does prediction-based allocation actually outperform simpler schemes that use only aggregate unit-level statistics (e.g., neighborhood eviction rates, school dropout rates) to direct resources?

## Input / Output

The model operates on M units of N individuals each, with individual welfare w_i ∈ [0,1], a fixed treatment effect δ capped at welfare 1, and a budget B for interventions at unit cost c. The key structural parameter is the Gini index G_ρ of the unit-level statistics ρ_k (fraction of high-welfare individuals per unit), which quantifies between-unit inequality. The output is not a single allocation but a set of dominance conditions: for which combinations of inequality level, budget size, prediction price p(ε), and within-unit allocation quality (q, q′) does ULA dominate, strictly dominate, or fail to be dominated by ILA.

## Method

The paper constructs a mathematical framework comparing Individual-Level Allocation (ILA, which sorts individuals by predicted welfare and treats the lowest first) against Unit-Level Allocation (ULA, which sorts units by ρ_k and delegates within-unit targeting to administrators who avoid the top q fraction with error rate q′). The comparison uses a strong dominance notion requiring S₁ ≥ S₂ for every feasible unit profile, every within-unit welfare distribution, and every tie-breaking rule. The core mechanism is bounding ILA's value from above (losing p(ε)·δ/c to prediction cost) and ULA's value from below via a loss function that decomposes into a base value (1−ρ̄) and an inequality-driven extra value proportional to G_ρ. The critical result is that ULA dominates ILA whenever the normalized Gini coefficient exceeds a threshold Ĝ_ρ^(1) = 1 − (q/ρ̄)·(1−ρ̄)/(1−q), and the conditions for ILA to even be nondominated are strictly weaker, meaning prediction requires both low inequality and high budget to be competitive. Extensions cover heterogeneous (Lipschitz) treatment effects and learning-theoretic guarantees for estimating ρ_k from noisy observations.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result establishes Gini-index thresholds that determine when unit-level allocation (ULA) dominates individual-level allocation (ILA). Specifically, ULA dominates ILA when the normalized Gini coefficient Ĝ_ρ exceeds Ĝ_ρ^(2) = 1 − (1/4)(q/ρ̄)(1−ρ̄)/(1−q) (high inequality), or when Ĝ_ρ > Ĝ_ρ^(1) = 1 − (q/ρ̄)(1−ρ̄)/(1−q) with a bounded budget. Conversely, ILA cannot dominate ULA whenever Ĝ_ρ ≥ Ĝ_ρ^(0) = 1/2 − (1/2)(q/ρ̄), a strictly weaker condition. Empirically, using American Community Survey data, ULA outperforms ILA in high-inequality regions (LA, NY school districts) while ILA outperforms ULA in low-inequality regions (Utah), with the Gini index in NY being roughly twice that of LA and four times that of Utah.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two allocation mechanisms: Individual-Level Allocation (ILA), which uses individual welfare predictions to target the lowest-welfare individuals, and Unit-Level Allocation (ULA), which sorts units by aggregate welfare statistics (ρ_k) and delegates within-unit targeting to administrators. ULA dominates ILA under high between-unit inequality (Gini above Ĝ_ρ^(2)) or when the budget is not excessively large, even when ILA has access to perfect predictions after paying a fixed cost. ILA can only dominate ULA under the much stronger joint condition of low inequality AND high budget, and even then only when the prediction cost is negligible. In the heterogeneous treatment effect extension, ULA remains dominant under similar inequality/budget conditions provided the effect curve is Lipschitz continuous and welfare is not overly concentrated. Real-world validation on American Community Survey data confirms the theory: ULA outperforms ILA in high-inequality LA and NY school districts, while ILA outperforms ULA in low-inequality Utah districts, consistent with the Gini-index thresholds.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new analytical ground by formalizing a question that prior work on prediction-based resource allocation (Kleinberg et al., 2015; Perdomo et al., 2023) raised empirically but did not resolve theoretically: when are individual predictions actually necessary for efficient targeting? It draws on the predict-then-optimize paradigm from operations research and the observation (Hardt & Kim, 2023; Perdomo, 2023) that risk scores implicitly encode unit-level information, but introduces a novel dominance framework parameterized by the Gini index of between-unit inequality. The learning-theoretic component repurposes standard tools (pseudo-dimension bounds, ERM, SVM-style margin losses) to show that unit-level statistics are learnable at negligible additional cost, positioning ULA as practically implementable rather than merely a theoretical baseline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the foundational assumption driving widespread deployment of individual risk-score systems in public policy: that granular prediction is necessary for efficient resource allocation. By showing that simple unit-level sorting matches or beats individual prediction in most realistic regimes (high inequality, limited budget), it provides decision-makers with a principled basis for questioning the cost-benefit of prediction infrastructure. Open questions include extending the model to network effects, group-level interventions (e.g., adding teachers to a school), and the broader question of whether targeting is the right policy lever versus expanding resources. The framework is limited to individual treatment effects and does not model how units themselves might be intervened upon.
