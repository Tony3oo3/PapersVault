## Problem

Existing offline preference optimization algorithms (DPO, IPO, SLiC) were derived independently without a unifying theoretical framework, making it difficult to understand their relationships, relative strengths, and the regularization they implicitly enforce. Additionally, prior work's equivalence arguments between offline losses and the canonical RLHF objective gloss over a critical distinction: the KL divergence regularization intended by RLHF is not the same quantity that offline losses actually optimize, and the conditions under which the two align remain unclear.

## Input / Output

The method consumes offline pairwise preference data (y_w, y_l) drawn from a behavior policy μ, a fixed reference policy π_ref, and a prompt x, and produces an aligned policy π_θ. No sampling from the learned model is required during training and no separate reward model is learned—the entire alignment reduces to a single supervised optimization over policy parameters. The only design choices distinguishing GPO variants are the convex function f and the regularization coefficient β, which together determine the loss landscape and the effective strength of regularization.

## Method

GPO parameterizes offline preference optimization losses as E_{(y_w,y_l)~μ}[f(βρ_θ)], where ρ_θ is the log-ratio difference between the policy and reference policy on preferred versus dispreferred responses, and f is any convex function with f'(0)<0. The central insight is reframing reward modeling as a supervised binary classification problem, where the reward difference r_φ(y_w)−r_φ(y_l) serves as the classifier score, thereby connecting the decades-old literature on convex classification losses to offline alignment. This recovers DPO (logistic), IPO (squared), and SLiC (hinge) as special cases while introducing new variants (exponential, truncated quadratic, Savage loss). A second-order Taylor expansion around ρ_θ=0 shows all GPO variants reduce to an IPO-like objective with effective regularization strength |f''(0)/f'(0)|·β, explaining why different variants require different optimal β values. The paper further proves that the offline regularization (a μ-weighted squared loss) is generally distinct from the KL divergence in the RLHF objective, with the two being correlated only in the vicinity of π_θ=π_ref, and validates this through a Gaussian mixture counterexample and language modeling experiments.

## Results

In the controlled summarization setting (11B golden preference model, 110M T5X policies), all GPO variants achieve similar peak golden win-rate performance at comparable KL divergence levels, confirming the theoretical prediction that the regularization–performance trade-off curve is largely invariant to the choice of convex loss. The key differentiator is the optimal β: squared and truncated-squared losses peak at β≈1, while logistic (DPO), exponential, and Savage losses peak at β≈10 (Figure 7). In the PaLM-2 side-by-side evaluation on 2000 summarization examples, all variants reach their best win-rate against π_ref at β∈[0.1,1], with no statistically significant differences between methods at their respective optimal β.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DPO (logistic loss), IPO (squared loss), and SLiC (hinge loss) as the three established baselines, alongside three novel GPO variants (exponential, truncated quadratic, Savage). In the controlled KL-vs-win-rate trade-off (Figure 4), all six variants trace out nearly identical curves, with peak performance reached at the same KL divergence level regardless of loss choice. The practical distinction lies in the required β: IPO and truncated quadratic achieve peak at β≈1, while DPO, exponential, and Savage require β≈10 (Figure 7 left), consistent with the theoretical prediction that |f″(0)/f′(0)| governs relative regularization strength. In the PaLM-2 side-by-side evaluation (Figure 10), all variants achieve comparable win-rates at β∈[0.1,1], with no method clearly outperforming another. The main empirical finding is thus a tie: the choice of convex loss does not materially affect achievable performance, but does shift the optimal hyperparameter β, giving practitioners additional tuning flexibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

GPO unifies DPO (logistic loss), IPO (squared loss), and SLiC (hinge loss) under a single framework by reframing reward modeling as a supervised binary classification problem, drawing on decades of convex loss literature (Hastie et al., 2009; Bartlett et al., 2006). The derivation starts from the canonical RLHF regularized policy optimization objective (Ouyang et al., 2022) and the equivalence between reward differences and log-ratio differences, which was first exploited in DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) and extended in IPO (Azar et al., 2024). The key conceptual departure is treating the convex function f as a free design parameter rather than a fixed choice, which naturally yields novel loss variants (exponential, truncated quadratic, Savage) with no prior offline preference optimization counterpart.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most important contribution is showing that the offline regularization actually enforced by these algorithms (a μ-weighted squared loss) is generally distinct from the KL divergence regularization intended by the RLHF formulation, with a Gaussian mixture counterexample demonstrating that locally minimizing the offline loss need not decrease KL divergence. This challenges the 'equivalence' arguments in prior work and suggests that controlling KL divergence through offline optimization is more subtle than previously assumed. Limitations include reliance on the reward maximization formulation (incompatible with complex preference structures requiring Nash equilibrium solutions) and restriction to contrastive losses, leaving supervised-learning-based losses (e.g., full SLiC-HF) outside the framework. Open questions include connecting GPO to alternative solution concepts and understanding whether the regularization gap matters in practice at scale.
