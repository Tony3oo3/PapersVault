## Problem

Post-hoc explainability methods like GradCAM reveal where a model looks but fail to explain what visual features drive its decisions, while existing part-prototype networks answer both questions but require architectural modifications, complex retraining, and typically underperform their black-box counterparts in accuracy. The paper asks whether part-prototype interpretability can be obtained in a purely post-hoc manner that guarantees identical prediction performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241943/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully trained black-box model (ResNet, VGG, DenseNet, ViT, or CoC) and decomposes its classification head (a C×D matrix) into k interpretable part-prototypes per class without any retraining. At inference, an RGB image is passed through the frozen backbone to produce a feature map of shape HW×D, which is then multiplied against each prototype to yield k spatial heatmaps (one per part) alongside the standard class logit. The prototypes live in the same D-dimensional feature space as the classification head, so no additional network layers or parameters are introduced.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-step post-hoc decomposition of the classification head into a sum of k interpretable part-prototypes per class. First, Non-negative Matrix Factorization is applied to stacked feature maps of training images to discover initial part-prototypes, exploiting NMF's additive-only constraint to yield parts-based representations. Second, a convex optimization scales these prototypes to best approximate the trained classification vector. Third, the residual (the difference between the true classification head and the scaled prototype sum) is distributed across prototypes via a Nelder-Mead optimization that constrains each residual component's normalized heatmap distribution to remain close to the original prototype's, preserving semantic interpretability while restoring full discriminative power. Because the final prototypes exactly sum to the original classification head, prediction accuracy is guaranteed to be identical to the black-box model, and the linear decomposition trivially satisfies sensitivity, completeness, linearity, and symmetry-preserving explainability axioms that prior max-pooling-based prototype networks violate.

## Results

On CUB-200-2011, the post-hoc part-prototype network achieves the best consistency scores across all five backbones (e.g., 62.3% on ResNet34 vs. 53.3% for TesNet) and the best stability scores (e.g., 79.5% on ResNet34 vs. 66.3% for ProtoEval), while matching or exceeding the accuracy of prior part-prototype models (82.3% ResNet34, 86.2% ResNet152, 85.9% Dense161). On ImageNet, the method reaches 75.1% top-1 accuracy with ResNet34, substantially outperforming ProtopNet (65.5%) and ProtoDeform (63.4%), while TesNet and ProtoPool fail entirely due to out-of-memory errors even at batch size 1 on a 24 GB GPU. On Stanford Cars and Stanford Dogs, the simply trained black-box backbone with post-hoc decomposition also outperforms prior prototype-based models in accuracy (e.g., 89.7% on ResNet152 for Cars, 87.2% on ResNet152 for Dogs).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against six prior part-prototype models: ProtopNet, ProtoTree, ProtoPool, ProtoDeform, TesNet, and ProtoEval. On CUB-200-2011, the proposed method wins on both consistency and stability scores across all five backbones, with the largest margins on stability (e.g., +12% over ProtoEval on ResNet34, +23.7% over ProtoEval on Dense121). On accuracy, the method matches or slightly exceeds the best prior methods (e.g., 86.2% vs. 83.8% for ProtoEval on ResNet152; 85.9% vs. 84.7% for ProtoEval on Dense161), while using a far simpler training pipeline without additional prototype layers or custom loss functions. On ImageNet, the advantage is stark: the method achieves 75.1% top-1 accuracy, beating ProtopNet by 9.6 points and ProtoDeform by 11.7 points, while TesNet and ProtoPool cannot run at all (OOM at batch size 1 on 24 GB GPU). On Stanford Cars, the method reaches 89.7% (ResNet152) and 88.2% (VGG19), outperforming the best prior results (87.3% for Support-ST on ResNet152, 86.4% for PixPNet on VGG19). On Stanford Dogs, it achieves 87.2% (ResNet152) and 85.1% (Dense161), surpassing ProtoDeform (86.5%, 83.7%) and ProtopNet (76.2%, 77.3%) respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: post-hoc explainability methods (Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients) that explain 'where' a model looks, and part-prototype networks (ProtopNet, ProtoPool, ProtoDeform, TesNet) that explain both 'where' and 'what' but require architectural modifications and joint re-training. The key departure is treating part-prototype construction as a post-hoc decomposition problem: rather than learning prototypes alongside the network, the authors decompose an already-trained classification head via NMF-based prototype discovery followed by a constrained residual refinement step. The NMF component builds on prior work showing non-negativity constraints yield parts-based representations in deep features (Lee & Seung 1999; Collins et al. 2018; Fel et al. 2023), but those works did not use the discovered components to construct a transparent reasoning model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By guaranteeing exact reconstruction of the classification head, this approach eliminates the accuracy-interpretability tradeoff that has plagued part-prototype networks, making interpretable classification practically deployable without the complex training pipelines (iterative freezing, special losses, prototype layers) that prior methods require. The method's scalability to ImageNet—where prior prototype methods either OOM or drop 10+ points—suggests a path toward interpretable fine-grained and large-scale classification. A key limitation is that prototype quality is bounded by the backbone's feature extraction: if the trained model fails to encode distinct part features, the post-hoc decomposition cannot recover them. Open questions include whether this decomposition strategy extends to non-linear classification heads, multi-task settings, or generative models.
