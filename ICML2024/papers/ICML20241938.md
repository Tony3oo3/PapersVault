## Problem

Mainstream graph contrastive learning (GCL) methods rely on random or knowledge-based graph augmentation that inevitably corrupts high-level community structure, limiting generalization. Moreover, existing learnable augmentation constraints address either topology or node features in isolation, leaving models fragile against heterogeneous noise. The paper asks whether a single unified constraint can simultaneously preserve community invariance across both topology and feature augmentation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241938/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph G = (X, A) with node feature matrix X ∈ R^{n×d} and binary adjacency matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{n×n}. It produces node-level embeddings via a GIN encoder and graph-level representations via mean-pooling readout, usable for both node classification and graph classification/regression. A key implementation detail is that the augmentation module operates on spectral-space node representations (from eigendecomposition of the Laplacian) and feature-node representations (from SVD of a constructed bipartite matrix), rather than raw GNN embeddings, to directly encode community-relevant geometry into the augmentation decisions.

## Method

CI-GCL's central insight is that spectral changes of the normalized Laplacian are nearly negatively correlated with community changes, so maximizing spectral change during augmentation automatically preserves community structure. For topology augmentation, edge perturbation (dropping and adding) and node dropping are parameterized with Gumbel-Softmax for differentiability, and the CI constraint is formulated as maximizing spectral change for edge dropping (removing inter-community edges) while minimizing it for edge adding (adding intra-community edges), derived from matrix perturbation bounds (Theorem 1). For feature augmentation, the asymmetric feature matrix is converted into a symmetric bipartite matrix X̃, and the same spectral-change-maximization principle applies (Theorems 2–3), unifying both augmentation types under one constraint. The overall objective jointly minimizes a GraphCL-style contrastive loss while maximizing the CI spectral-change losses on both branches, optimized via projected gradient descent with Lanczos-based selective eigendecomposition for scalability.

## Results

On unsupervised graph classification across 8 TU Datasets, CI-GCL achieves the best average accuracy of 77.74%, outperforming the strongest baseline SEGA (77.25%) and GraphCL (75.71%). On unsupervised graph regression (OGB), CI-GCL attains the lowest average RMSE of 1.606, substantially beating GCL-SPAN (2.184) and AD-GCL (2.403). In semi-supervised settings (10% labels), CI-GCL reaches 74.0% average accuracy vs. AD-GCL (73.1%) and SEGA (72.9%), and in transfer learning from ZINC-2M/PPI-306K, it achieves 75.1% average ROC-AUC vs. SEGA (73.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CI-GCL outperforms all 21 baselines on average across every experimental setting. In unsupervised graph classification, it beats the closest competitor SEGA by 0.49 points (77.74% vs. 77.25%) and the widely-used GraphCL by 2.03 points (77.74% vs. 75.71%); the gap widens to 3.21 points over AD-GCL (74.53%) and 3.62 points over GCL-SPAN (74.12%). On graph regression, CI-GCL's 1.606 average RMSE is 0.58 lower than GCL-SPAN (2.184) and 0.80 lower than AD-GCL (2.403), with the largest margin on molfreesolv (2.873 vs. 4.531 for GCL-SPAN). In transfer learning, CI-GCL leads SEGA by 1.5 points (75.1% vs. 73.6% ROC-AUC) and GCL-SPAN by 1.9 points. The one setting where CI-GCL does not dominate is node classification (Table 8), where SFA achieves slightly higher accuracy on Cora (85.89 vs. 85.50), CiteSeer (75.31 vs. 73.58), and PubMed (86.29 vs. 85.90), while CI-GCL is competitive on Amazon-Photo (93.54 vs. 93.57 for MA-GCL). The plug-and-play CI constraint also improves AD-GCL (74.53→75.46) and AutoGCL (74.33→75.09) when added as a module, confirming its generality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learnable graph augmentation line (AD-GCL, AutoGCL) by introducing a community-invariant (CI) constraint derived from spectral perturbation theory, and departs from prior spectral augmentation methods (GCL-SPAN, GAME) that only address topology by showing that the same spectral-change-maximization principle can be unified across both topology and feature augmentation via a bipartite graph construction. The theoretical foundation draws on matrix perturbation theory (Bojchevski & Günnemann, 2019) and bipartite co-clustering (Nie et al., 2017), while the differentiable sampling mechanism relies on Gumbel-Softmax (Jang et al., 2017). The key conceptual departure is reframing the augmentation objective: rather than corrupting high-level structure (as GCL-SPAN does) or minimizing shared mutual information (as GAME does), CI-GCL explicitly preserves community structure by maximizing spectral changes, which the authors show is negatively correlated with community disruption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified CI constraint is a plug-and-play component that can be attached to existing learnable GCL frameworks (demonstrated via CI+AD-GCL and CI+AutoGCL), making it broadly applicable without requiring architectural changes. The community-preservation perspective opens a new axis for thinking about what information should be invariant during augmentation, potentially extending to other high-level graph properties beyond community structure. Key limitations include the O(n³) eigendecomposition cost (mitigated by Lanczos but still non-trivial for large graphs) and the transductive nature of the node-level formulation. An open question is whether the spectral-change-as-community-proxy relationship holds robustly across all graph types or is specific to the relatively small TU/OGB benchmarks tested here.
