## Problem

The standard concatenate-then-split data formatting used in LLM pre-training inevitably fragments documents at arbitrary token boundaries, forcing models to learn next-token predictions from incomplete context. This truncation causes models to develop spurious associations, increasing closed-domain hallucination and reducing context-awareness. While document-integrity-preserving strategies exist for encoder models, no scalable method existed for decoder-only LLM training at the scale of billions of documents that eliminates unnecessary truncation without sacrificing the training efficiency that makes concatenation attractive.

## Input / Output

The method takes a corpus of raw documents (natural language or code) and a maximum sequence length L (e.g., 2048 or 8192 tokens) as input. It produces a set of packed training sequences, each at most L tokens long, where every document shorter than L appears intact in exactly one sequence, and only documents longer than L are segmented into chunks of at most L tokens. The resulting sequences contain negligible padding (within 0.01% of the sequence count produced by concatenation), and cross-document attention is masked so each document is treated as a standalone training sample.

## Method

Best-fit Packing formulates the grouping of document chunks into fixed-length training sequences as a bin packing problem and solves it with an optimized Best-Fit-Decreasing (BFD) approximation algorithm. The key scalability insight is that since all chunk lengths are integers in [1, L] with L ≪ N (number of chunks), a segment tree of size O(L) suffices to track remaining bin capacities, reducing packing time from O(N log N) to O(N log L) and enabling linear scaling to billions of documents. The paper further provides an analytical toy stochastic process proving that a model trained on truncated sequences achieves strictly worse expected loss than one trained on full sequences, even with infinite data. Empirically, pre-training LLaMA-architecture models (7B–13B, 2k–8k context) on RefinedWeb and The Stack with Best-fit Packing yields consistent gains across 22 tasks—most notably +4.7% on reading comprehension, +16.8% on context following, and +15.0% on program synthesis—while reducing undefined-name hallucination in generated code by up to 58.3%.

## Results

Best-fit Packing improves over the concatenation baseline across 22 downstream tasks with no statistically significant degradation on any single task. Headline gains include +4.7% average on reading comprehension (e.g., SQuAD EM 53.86→61.61 at 2k), +9.3% on NLI (RTE 55.74→60.28), +16.8% on context following (MemoTrap 35.58→41.56), and +15.0% Pass@100 on HumanEval (35.28→40.57). Critically, the method reduces closed-domain hallucination by up to 58.3% (undefined-name errors in code: 5.10%→2.41% on HumanEval, 9.52%→3.97% on MBPP).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the standard concatenate-then-split data formatting (popularized by GPT-3, PaLM, LLaMA, etc.), where all documents are concatenated into one stream and then split into fixed-length sequences. Best-fit Packing wins or ties on every single evaluated task across 22 benchmarks, with no statistically significant degradation observed in any setting. The largest relative gains appear in context-dependent tasks: context following (+16.8% on MemoTrap), NLI (+9.3% on RTE), and program synthesis (+15.0% Pass@100 on HumanEval, +5.8% on MBPP), all of which require the model to attend to and ground its output in the provided context. On commonsense and closed-book QA (HellaSwag, PIQA, SIQA, ARC-E), improvements are modest (≤2.7%), consistent with the paper's argument that frequently-occurring knowledge is redundantly present across many complete documents. The hallucination reduction (up to 58.3% fewer undefined-name errors in generated code) is the most dramatic single-metric improvement, directly attributable to eliminating the fragmented training segments that teach the model to use names without their definitions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical Best-Fit-Decreasing (BFD) bin-packing heuristic (Eilon & Christofides, 1971) for LLM pre-training data formatting, extending it from a theoretical combinatorial optimization tool to a scalable pipeline for billions of documents. It departs from prior encoder-model packing work (Krell et al., 2021; RoBERTa) by addressing the specific constraints of decoder-only LLMs—longer context windows, open-ended generation, and the need to preserve document integrity to reduce hallucination. The method is positioned as orthogonal to recent work on semantic document grouping (Shi et al., 2024) and operates purely at the data level, requiring no changes to model architecture or training frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: Best-fit Packing is a drop-in, zero-cost replacement for the standard concatenation approach (less than 0.01% additional sequences), making it immediately adoptable in any LLM training pipeline. The consistent monotonic improvements across 22 tasks in both natural language and code, combined with a 58.3% reduction in code hallucination, suggest that data integrity during pre-training is an underappreciated lever for model quality. Open questions include whether gains persist at 70B+ scale and 32k+ context lengths, whether the method further enhances in-context learning capabilities, and how it interacts with other data-quality interventions (deduplication, filtering). The paper also notes that tail knowledge remains more vulnerable than common knowledge, leaving room for complementary approaches.
