## Problem

Leading generative AI companies' terms of service and enforcement practices create legal and technical barriers that deter independent safety research: researchers risk account suspension without appeal or legal action for conducting good faith evaluations of model vulnerabilities. Existing safe harbors are narrowly scoped to traditional security issues (e.g., unauthorized access), excluding the broader range of safety research such as jailbreaks, bias testing, and harmful content generation, while researcher access programs lack independence from corporate incentives and community representation.

## Input / Output

The paper analyzes the current policy landscape across seven major AI systems (GPT-4, Gemini 1.0, Claude 2, Inflection-1, Llama 2, Midjourney v6, and Cohere Command), cataloging each company's acceptable use policy, deep access provisions, researcher access programs, safe harbor scope, and enforcement transparency. The output is a pair of concrete voluntary commitments—legal and technical safe harbors—along with specific implementation mechanisms including third-party access delegation via NAIRR and structured appeals processes, framed within US legal context (CFAA, DMCA §1201).

## Method

The paper proposes a dual safe harbor framework: (1) a legal safe harbor that shields good faith researchers from civil liability (CFAA, DMCA §1201) provided they follow established vulnerability disclosure policies, with the critical condition that 'good faith' determination is not left to sole corporate discretion; and (2) a technical safe harbor that prevents account suspension during research, implemented either through delegating access authorization to trusted independent third parties (universities, nonprofits via NAIRR, analogous to the University of Michigan's role for Meta social media data) or through transparent, independently reviewed appeals processes with codified criteria and guaranteed response timelines. The legal safe harbor is grounded in prior proposals for social media platform research (Abdo et al., 2022) and algorithmic bug bounties (Elazari, 2018; Kenway et al., 2022), while the technical safe harbor draws on the NAIRR infrastructure and existing researcher access program structures. The paper argues these protections are contingent on ex post compliance with disclosure rules, so they do not enable malicious misuse while removing the chilling effect on legitimate safety research.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative method results. The key empirical finding cited is from the Foundation Model Transparency Index (Bommasani et al., 2023a), which found that only 25% of policy enforcement and evaluation criteria were satisfied on average across leading generative AI companies, and no company provided reproducible evaluations. Table 3 catalogs the policy landscape across seven major AI systems, revealing that no company offers a full legal safe harbor for safety research beyond traditional security vulnerabilities; all existing safe harbors (OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta) are security-only and determined 'at sole discretion.' Account suspensions of researchers conducting public interest safety research have been reported at OpenAI, Anthropic, Inflection, and Midjourney, with Midjourney being the most prolific.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not propose a method to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it compares the current policy landscape (Table 3) against the proposed legal and technical safe harbor commitments, showing that no existing company policy satisfies the full scope of protection the authors advocate. The proposals build on and extend several prior frameworks: the Hacking Policy Council's (2023) call for government-clarified legal protections for AI red teaming, the Algorithmic Justice League's (Costanza-Chock et al., 2022; Kenway et al., 2022) advocacy for algorithmic bug bounties and community-involved audits, Abdo et al. (2022)'s safe harbor proposal for social media platform research, and the White House Voluntary Commitments (2023) requiring bug bounty systems for AI vulnerabilities. The authors distinguish their proposal from existing researcher access programs (e.g., OpenAI's, Meta's) by arguing those programs lack independence, have limited community representation, and remain subject to corporate discretion, whereas the proposed safe harbors would delegate authorization to trusted third parties (e.g., via NAIRR) and require transparent appeals processes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the 'safe harbor' concept from security vulnerability disclosure (Elazari, 2018; HackerOne, 2023) and social media platform research access (Abdo et al., 2022) to the generative AI evaluation context, extending protections from security-only research to broader 'safety research' including jailbreaks, bias, and harmful content generation. It builds on prior calls for algorithmic bug bounties (Kenway et al., 2022; Raji et al., 2022), structured researcher access (Bucknall & Trager, 2023), and the Foundation Model Transparency Index (Bommasani et al., 2023a), while drawing on the extensive red-teaming literature (jailbreaks, adversarial prompts, text-to-image exploits) to demonstrate the practical need for such protections. The paper departs from prior work by proposing a dual commitment—legal safe harbor plus technical safe harbor with independent third-party access authorization via NAIRR—rather than relying solely on company-discretionary researcher programs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper addresses a concrete and growing barrier to AI safety research: the legal and technical uncertainty that deters independent evaluation, documented through specific cases of account suspensions (Midjourney, OpenAI, Anthropic, Inflection) and the chilling effects on vulnerability disclosure. The proposal to delegate access authorization to trusted third parties (universities, nonprofits via NAIRR) and require transparent appeals processes is a practical mechanism that could reshape how AI companies interact with the research community. Key open questions include whether 'good faith' definitions will remain stable as new model capabilities emerge, how the proposal translates to non-US jurisdictions, and whether the balance between protecting researchers and preventing malicious exploitation of disclosed vulnerabilities holds in practice. The paper is explicitly US-centric and acknowledges that empirical analysis of the trade-offs between broader access and misuse risk remains to be done.
