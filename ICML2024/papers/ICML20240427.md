## Problem

Existing local explanation methods (LIME, SHAP, etc.) produce explanations for individual examples but offer no guarantees about how stable or widely applicable those explanations are to nearby inputs. This paper formalizes the novel problem of black-box explanation certification: given only query access to a model, an explanation for an example, and a quality threshold, find the largest ℓ∞ ball (hypercube) around the example where the explanation's fidelity provably remains above the threshold with high probability.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a black-box model via query access only (no gradients or internal structure), a target example x₀, a pre-computed local explanation e_{x₀} (linear as in LIME, or nonlinear such as a shallow tree or rule list), a quality metric h (typically fidelity = 1 − |g(x) − e_{x₀}(x)|), and a threshold θ. It outputs a certified half-width w defining the trust region B∞(x₀, w), along with probabilistic bounds on the correctness of the certification. The explanation is applied to any new point x by evaluating e_{x₀}(x) (e.g., dot product of the feature-importance vector with x), so no re-computation of the explanation is needed within the certified region.

## Method

The Ecertify framework combines an outer binary-search loop (Algorithm 1) that progressively narrows the candidate half-width with an inner certification routine (Algorithm 2) offering three strategies of increasing sophistication: (1) unif samples Q points uniformly in the annular region between the last certified and candidate hypercubes; (2) unifI performs an incremental grid search by sampling 2^i Gaussian prototypes at each of ⌊log Q⌋ stages and querying around them; (3) adaptI adaptively halves the prototype set at each inner iteration, concentrating queries around the prototypes nearest to low-fidelity examples. The key theoretical contribution is a set of finite-sample, exponentially decaying lower bounds (Theorem 1) on the probability that the estimated minimum fidelity is within ε of the true minimum, which decompose over the certified sub-regions via Lemma 1. Asymptotically, Extreme Value Theory yields cdf-free confidence intervals (Corollary 1) with exponent κ = d/2. All three strategies are guaranteed to use at most Q queries per certification call (Proposition 1), and the adaptive strategy provably samples exponentially more around the most promising prototypes, giving it an advantage in high dimensions.

## Results

On the synthetic piecewise-linear benchmark (optimal half-width = 1/d), all four methods converge to the true certified half-width as Q increases, but the proposed strategies are 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than the ZO+ baseline. At d=10, Q=10⁴, unif, unifI, and adaptI certify w≈0.1 in 0.36–0.62 s, while ZO+ takes 14.9 s; at d=10⁴, Q=10⁴, adaptI certifies w≈9.4×10⁻⁵ in 88 s versus 4385 s for ZO+. On real data (HELOC, Arrhythmia, CIFAR-10, ImageNet), the same speedup pattern holds, and the certified trust regions enable ~80% query savings for explanation reuse on HELOC. Explanations for examples inside a certified region show significantly higher Top-5 intersection (0.85 vs. 0.77) and Spearman rank correlation (0.74 vs. 0.61) with the certified explanation than those outside.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is ZO+, an adaptation of the zeroth-order optimization toolbox from Liu et al. (2022) to the explanation-certification setting. Across all synthetic dimensions (d=1 to 10⁴) and query budgets (Q=10 to 10⁴), the three proposed strategies (unif, unifI, adaptI) achieve certified half-widths within a few percent of ZO+ while running 10× to 50× faster (e.g., at d=10⁴, Q=10⁴: adaptI 88 s vs. ZO+ 4385 s). On real datasets (Figure 2), the same pattern holds: all methods converge to roughly the same half-width, but ZO+ is consistently the slowest by a wide margin. Among the proposed methods, unif is best for low dimensions (≤100), unifI for intermediate (~1000), and adaptI for high dimensions (≥10⁴), reflecting the growing benefit of adaptive query allocation as the search space expands. The paper also uses the certified trust regions as a meta-metric to compare LIME vs. SHAP: LIME typically yields larger certified widths (more generalizable explanations) while SHAP achieves higher fidelity at x₀ but smaller trust regions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by formalizing black-box probabilistic explanation certification as a distinct problem, drawing conceptual inspiration from adversarial robustness certification (particularly randomized smoothing methods that also use only query access) and repurposing zeroth-order optimization as a baseline. Rather than proposing yet another explanation method (LIME, SHAP, etc.), it treats existing explainers as black boxes and asks how far their explanations remain valid, bridging the XAI and certification literatures. The theoretical analysis borrows from Extreme Value Theory for asymptotic cdf-free bounds, while the sampling strategies (uniform, uniform-incremental, adaptive-incremental) are novel constructions specific to the certification-of-explanations setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The trust-region certification provides a practical mechanism for explanation reuse (demonstrating ~80% query savings on HELOC) and a principled meta-metric for comparing XAI methods (e.g., LIME generalizes farther but with lower point fidelity than SHAP). Open questions include extending to non-hypercube geometries, other explanation types (exemplar-based, contrastive), and more intelligent query budget allocation. A key limitation is that guarantees are probabilistic and depend on random sampling, so individual runs may produce different half-widths, and the approach assumes the quality metric (fidelity) is well-behaved enough for the cdf-based bounds to be informative.
