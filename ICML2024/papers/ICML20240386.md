## Problem

Decentralized learning algorithms that replace a central server with peer-to-peer communication are vulnerable to privacy attacks through shared model updates, yet existing privacy analyses either assume overly pessimistic local DP or are limited to gossip algorithms. Random walk algorithms, which avoid global synchronization and redundant communication, are a popular alternative in practice but had no formal privacy characterization under the more nuanced Pairwise Network Differential Privacy (PNDP) framework. This paper asks whether random walks can provide tighter, topology-aware privacy guarantees than gossip, and if so, how the communication graph structure quantitatively shapes the privacy loss between each pair of nodes.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a connected communication graph G with a symmetric bistochastic transition matrix W (e.g., Hamilton weighting), local datasets D_v at each of n nodes, a noise variance σ², a constant step size γ, and a total iteration count T. It outputs the trained model parameters x_T and, crucially, a closed-form pairwise privacy loss bound ε_{u→v} for every ordered pair of nodes (u,v), expressed as a function of the matrix logarithm of (I − W + 1/n · 11ᵀ) evaluated at the (u,v) entry. This per-pair structure means privacy guarantees are not uniform: nodes that are graph-theoretically closer leak more to each other, and the bound is parameterized by graph communicability rather than a single global ε.

## Method

The paper introduces Private Random Walk Gradient Descent (RW DP-SGD), where a single token holding the current model state walks on the graph; at each step the holding node computes a local stochastic gradient, adds Gaussian noise calibrated to a sensitivity Δ, performs an SGD update, and forwards the token to a random neighbor chosen according to W. For convergence under strong convexity, the authors adapt the Markovian-sampling analysis of Even (2023) to handle the bias from non-uniform node visits, yielding a rate that separates the mixing-time term from the privacy-noise term. The main privacy result (Theorem 3) derives a closed-form bound on the Rényi-DP privacy loss from node u to node v by decomposing the walk into per-contribution losses, applying privacy amplification by iteration (Feldman et al., 2018) to bound the loss after i intermediate steps, and summing over all possible walk lengths weighted by powers of W. The resulting expression involves the matrix logarithm of (I − W + 1/n · 11ᵀ), which the paper identifies as a variant of graph communicability, yielding an O(1/n²) baseline amplification (matching the complete-graph result) plus a topology-dependent correction that is negative for well-connected pairs. For specific graphs (star, ring, complete), the formula simplifies to explicit closed forms showing that extremal nodes enjoy O(n²) amplification while central nodes get only O(n), and that random walks outperform gossip for close nodes on well-connected topologies.

## Results

On the UCI Housing logistic regression task (2048 nodes, 8 samples each), the random walk DP-SGD algorithm outperforms the gossip baseline across all four graph topologies and all three mean privacy loss levels. At mean privacy loss ε̄=1, random walk achieves 0.848–0.900 accuracy (Grid to Complete) versus 0.66–0.77 for gossip, a gap of up to 23 percentage points on the Geometric graph. At ε̄=2, the gap narrows but random walk still leads by 11–26 points (e.g., 0.933 vs 0.67 on Geometric). The privacy analysis (Theorem 3) yields a closed-form pairwise privacy loss bound involving the matrix logarithm of (I − W + 1/n·11ᵀ), which recovers the complete-graph result as a special case and links privacy loss to graph communicability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the private gossip algorithm of Cyffers et al. (2022), the random walk method wins on accuracy in every configuration tested (4 graphs × 3 privacy levels = 12 comparisons), with the largest margins on sparse or poorly-connected topologies: at ε̄=2 on the Geometric graph, random walk reaches 0.933 accuracy versus 0.67 for gossip (a 26-point gap). The paper attributes this to two factors: (i) random walk privacy loss is insensitive to node degree while gossip degrades linearly with degree, and (ii) random walk applies 10×n local gradient steps versus gossip's 10 global steps, and many noisy steps outperform few less-noisy steps. Against centralized DP-SGD (Bassily et al., 2014) and local DP-SGD (Figure 2), the random walk method sits between the two but is much closer to centralized, showing a significant improvement over local DP. On privacy loss (Figure 1), random walk provides lower loss than gossip for close nodes (shortest path ≤ ~5) but higher loss for distant nodes; however, for well-connected graphs (Erdős-Rényi, expanders), random walk offers uniformly better privacy than gossip.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Pairwise Network Differential Privacy (PNDP) framework of Cyffers & Bellet (2022) and Cyffers et al. (2022) from gossip algorithms to random walk algorithms, and from specific topologies (complete, ring) to arbitrary graphs. The convergence analysis builds directly on Even (2023), who cast Markov chain SGD as delayed gradient descent, allowing the authors to handle the sampling bias inherent in random walks without requiring the complete graph. The key conceptual departure is showing that the privacy loss between any pair of nodes admits a closed-form expression involving the matrix logarithm of (I − W + 11ᵀ/n), which the authors identify as a variant of communicability—a graph-theoretic centrality measure—thereby linking differential privacy guarantees to structural properties of the communication graph.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first PNDP analysis for random walk decentralized learning on arbitrary graphs, yielding interpretable privacy bounds that practitioners can compute from the graph's spectral properties. The finding that random walks nearly bridge the gap between local and central DP when the spectral gap is large suggests that graph topology is a design lever for privacy, not just a constraint. Open questions include extending convergence to non-strongly convex objectives, handling multiple parallel tokens, incorporating communication latency, and understanding how the pairwise privacy budget distribution (which differs qualitatively from gossip's) should be interpreted for policy decisions. The collusion analysis in Appendix D confirms that the non-collusion assumption is fundamental and cannot be relaxed without degrading guarantees.
