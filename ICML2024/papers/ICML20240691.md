## Problem

Standard model assessment and selection assume training and test data share a fixed distribution, but real-world environments shift over time, making past performance unreliable for predicting future quality. Existing distribution-shift methods target static validation settings, and while rolling windows are used in practice, no principled method adaptively chooses the window size without knowing the underlying non-stationarity pattern. This paper asks: how can one estimate a model's current generalization error and select the best model from a pool of candidates when the data distribution changes arbitrarily over time?

## Input / Output

The method receives a sequence of data batches {B_j}_{j=1}^t, where each batch B_j contains B_j i.i.d. samples drawn from a potentially different distribution P_j, along with a collection of m candidate models and a bounded loss function. For model assessment, it outputs a single scalar estimate of the current population loss L_t(f); for model selection, it outputs the index of the best-performing model. A key constraint is that only limited data from the current period is available, so the method must judiciously borrow from historical epochs whose distributions may differ significantly.

## Method

The core mechanism is an adaptive rolling window that automatically selects the look-back horizon k by minimizing a data-driven bias-variance tradeoff. The bias term (distributional drift within the window) is estimated via a Goldenshluger-Lepski-style proxy that compares sample means at different window sizes and subtracts empirical Bernstein noise bounds to isolate the drift signal; the variance term is estimated from the sample variance over the window. For model selection, pairwise comparison is reduced to mean estimation of the performance gap Δ_j = L_j(f_1) − L_j(f_2), to which the same adaptive window is applied; multiple candidates are handled via a single-elimination tournament of m−1 pairwise calls. The resulting oracle inequality guarantees the selected window achieves near-optimal estimation error up to logarithmic factors, adapting automatically to change-point, bounded-drift, and arbitrary shift patterns without requiring knowledge of the non-stationarity structure.

## Results

On the non-stationary synthetic benchmark (Example 5.2, σ²=1), the adaptive rolling window method V_ARW achieves a mean excess risk of 0.139, outperforming all fixed-window baselines (V_1: 0.157, V_4: 0.171, V_16: 0.539, V_64: 1.034, V_256: 1.067). On the Dubai housing dataset with abrupt distribution shifts, V_ARW achieves an MSE of 0.071, matching the best fixed window (V_4: 0.069) while substantially outperforming large-window methods (V_64: 0.091, V_256: 0.095). On the arXiv topic-frequency dataset with slow drift, V_ARW (2.4×10⁻³) is comparable to the best fixed windows (V_16: 2.4×10⁻³, V_256: 1.9×10⁻³) and far superior to V_1 (6.7×10⁻³).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed adaptive rolling window method (V_ARW) against five fixed-window model selection baselines (V_k with k ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64, 256}) across synthetic and real datasets. In the non-stationary synthetic setting (σ²=1), V_ARW (0.139) clearly beats every fixed window, with the gap widening dramatically for larger windows (V_256: 1.067, a 7.7× worse excess risk). In the stationary setting, V_ARW matches the best large-window baselines (V_64, V_256) as expected, confirming it does not sacrifice performance when the environment is stable. On the arXiv dataset with slow drift, V_ARW (2.4×10⁻³) ties V_16 and is close to V_256 (1.9×10⁻³), while V_1 is nearly 3× worse. On the Dubai housing data with abrupt shifts, V_ARW (0.071) matches the best fixed window V_4 (0.069) and outperforms V_64 (0.091) and V_256 (0.095) by 22–26%, demonstrating that no single fixed window can match the adaptive method across all shift patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Goldenshluger-Lepski adaptive bandwidth selection principle (comparing estimates at different scales and subtracting noise bounds to isolate bias) for the problem of choosing a look-back window in model assessment under temporal distribution shift. It departs from prior rolling-window learning methods (Bifet & Gavalda, 2007; Mohri & Muñoz Medina, 2012; Mazzetto & Upfal, 2023), which use windows for *training* under drift, by instead using the window mechanism for *evaluating and selecting* among pre-trained models at a fixed time point. The paper also distinguishes itself from online learning and expert-tracking literature (Littlestone & Warmuth, 1994; Agarwal et al., 2017) by operating in an offline, point-in-time setting framed as transfer learning from historical to current distributions, and from changepoint detection by allowing arbitrary per-period shifts rather than a small number of discrete change points.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled, distribution-free adaptive procedure for model selection under arbitrary temporal non-stationarity, with oracle inequalities guaranteeing near-optimality without knowing the shift pattern. The fast-rate result for bounded regression (achieving 1/B_{t,K} rather than 1/√B_{t,K}) via the difference-of-losses formulation is a useful structural insight that cannot be obtained by evaluating models independently. Open questions include extending to infinite model classes, exploiting regularities in the shift (seasonality, trends) to leverage more distant history, and integrating the selection procedure as a subroutine for online hyperparameter tuning. The bounded-loss assumption, while relaxable, limits direct applicability to heavy-tailed regression or unbounded classification losses.
