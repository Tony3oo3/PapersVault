## Problem

Standard Shapley values average marginal contributions over all variable orderings (equivalent to a maximum-entropy, uniform prior), while causal inference applies Occam's razor to retain only orderings yielding the sparsest graph. Blindly restricting Shapley values to the sparsest orderings produces explanations that omit the 'explaining away' contribution of spouses, making them incomplete. The paper reconciles these two perspectives and proposes entropy-maximizing reweighting schemes that properly incorporate prior causal or structural information.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a predictive model f, a feature instance x, and a priori causal knowledge encoded as a finite set of compatible directed graphs G* = {G_1,...,G_M} (or, for the observational variant, a faithfulness assumption on a linear SEM). For observational explanations it outputs Markov Blanket Shapley values whose non-zero entries are provably restricted to the Markov blanket of the predicted variable; for interventional explanations it outputs Ancestor Shapley values (a uniform average of per-graph Shapley vectors computed with payoff v_k(S)=E[f(X)|do_Gk(S)]) together with an intervenability scalar quantifying total interventional power over the output relative to |f(x)|.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-stage reweighting of Shapley-value orderings guided by the maximum-entropy principle. For the observational variant (Markov Blanket Shapley values), the method first identifies parents and children of the predicted node via sparsity of marginal contributions (Theorem 3.1), then restricts to orderings that begin with those nodes and, among them, retains only the sparsest marginal-contribution vectors (Theorem 3.2), assigning equal weights to all surviving orderings. For the interventional variant (Ancestor Shapley values), standard Shapley values are computed independently for each graph G_k in the a priori class using the interventional payoff v_k(S)=E[f(X)|do_Gk(S)], and the resulting attribution vectors are averaged uniformly across all M graphs. The maximum-entropy choice ensures that, conditional on the available constraints (sparsity pattern or graph class), no further arbitrary preference is introduced among the remaining orderings or graphs. The approach provably preserves Efficiency, Missingness, and Symmetry (Markov Blanket) and satisfies the Dummy property, while intentionally sacrificing Linearity to accommodate the structural constraints.

## Results

On a minimalistic linear SEM with ρ(X₁,X₂)=0.98, Ancestor Shapley values are the only method computable across all three levels of a priori causal knowledge (exact graph, faithfulness + output-last, faithfulness only), yielding (0.2, 0.19), (0.1, 0.29), and (0.07, 0.19) respectively, while Asymmetric and Causal Shapley values become inapplicable under the weakest knowledge (G₃*). The intervenability measure drops from 0.39 to 0.26 as causal uncertainty increases, correctly reflecting reduced interventional power. In the observational setting, Markov Blanket Shapley values satisfy the Dummy property (assigning zero to non-Markov-Blanket variables) while standard Shapley values do not, as demonstrated on a 9-node SEM where X₁ receives a non-zero standard Shapley value despite being irrelevant to the prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Asymmetric Shapley values (Frye et al., 2020), Ancestor Shapley values win on applicability: under the weakest a priori knowledge (G₃*, faithfulness only), no partial ordering is compatible with all candidate graphs, making Asymmetric Shapley values incomputable, while Ancestor Shapley values still produce a valid explanation (0.07, 0.19). When both are computable, they differ in philosophy: Asymmetric (distal) concentrates all interventional power in root nodes (giving X₁=0.39, X₂=−0.005 under G₁*), whereas Ancestor Shapley values distribute power proportionally to ancestry across the graph class. Against Causal Shapley values (Heskes et al., 2020), Ancestor Shapley values win on sensitivity to causal uncertainty: under G₂*, Causal Shapley values produce the identical explanation (0.2, 0.19) as under perfect knowledge, ignoring that X₁ is not necessarily an ancestor, while Ancestor Shapley values correctly shift weight toward X₂ (0.1, 0.29). Against standard Shapley values (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), Markov Blanket Shapley values win on the Dummy property: in the 9-node SEM example, standard Shapley values assign a non-zero contribution to X₁ (a variable with no role in the least-squares predictor), while Markov Blanket Shapley values correctly assign it zero.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads—standard Shapley values (Shapley, 1953; Lundberg & Lee, 2017) and causal discovery via the Pearl-Verma theorem—under a maximum entropy lens, showing that uniform weighting over orderings is the maximum-entropy choice while causal inference's preference for sparsest graphs is an Occam's razor principle. It extends the weighted Shapley framework of Weber (1988) and the causal reweighting schemes of Frye et al. (2020) and Heskes et al. (2020) by proposing entropy-maximizing weight selection rather than ad hoc or single-graph-based weighting. The two new variants—Markov Blanket Shapley values (observational) and Ancestor Shapley values (interventional)—depart from prior work by operating over a *class* of compatible causal graphs rather than a single graph or partial ordering, and by explicitly incorporating the 'explaining away' role of spouses that sparsest-only approaches miss.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, information-theoretic justification for how to incorporate prior causal knowledge into Shapley-based explanations, filling a gap where existing methods (Causal Shapley, Asymmetric Shapley) either require a single known graph or a partial ordering that may not capture all available structural uncertainty. The demonstration that blind sparsity (Occam's razor) produces explanations that miss the explaining-away contribution of spouses is a useful caution for practitioners relying on sparse attribution. Key limitations include the faithfulness assumption, the restriction of theoretical guarantees to linear SEMs, and the practical challenge of determining the candidate graph class G* from data; open questions include extending the framework to non-linear models, handling unmeasured confounders more generally, and developing scalable algorithms for large feature sets.
