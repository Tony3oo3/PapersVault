## Problem

Proactive repositioning of emergency responders between depots is computationally intractable due to the exponential combinatorial action space and uncertainty in future incident demand. The state-of-the-art hierarchical approach (Pettet et al., 2022) uses online Monte-Carlo tree search for low-level planning, requiring 2–4 minutes per decision in a domain where seconds can save lives. This paper asks how to reduce decision latency to a fraction of a second without sacrificing response-time quality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a variable-dimensional state comprising per-cell Poisson incident rates, per-responder tuples (current position, assigned incident/hospital, availability time), and a time-varying travel-time model over a 1-mile grid; the number of responders in each region fluctuates over time, making the state and action spaces non-stationary in dimension. The output is a pair of discrete allocations: a high-level vector specifying how many responders are assigned to each region, and a low-level assignment mapping each individual responder to a specific depot within its region. Continuous actor outputs are converted to these discrete assignments via maximum-weight matching (low-level) and minimum-cost flow (high-level), ensuring feasibility constraints (one responder per depot, all responders assigned) are satisfied.

## Method

The approach replaces both the high-level and low-level planners of the hierarchical framework with DDPG actor-critic agents. Low-level agents use a TransformerXL (TrXL) backbone in the actor to handle variable-length sequences of per-responder feature vectors (arrival time to each depot, nearby incident rate), enabling coordination among a changing number of responders within a region; the critic uses a fixed-size MLP over depot-level features (occupancy, likely available time, incident rate). The high-level agent uses a simpler MLP actor-critic over region-level features (total incident rate, current allocation) and a novel reward-estimation scheme that computes its reward as a weighted sum of the low-level critics' Q-values, mitigating the noise from sparse, region-local response-time signals. Continuous actions are discretized via maximum-weight matching (low-level) and a greedy integer allocation followed by minimum-cost flow for responder-to-depot assignment (high-level). Low-level agents are trained independently per region with DDPG and experience replay, then the high-level agent is trained on top of the converged low-level critics.

## Results

The proposed DDPG-based hierarchical approach reduces computation time per decision from ~3 minutes (MCTS) to 0.22 seconds — a three-orders-of-magnitude speedup — while also slightly reducing average ambulance response time by 5 seconds (5-region) and 13 seconds (7-region) in Nashville, and by 10 seconds across all region counts in Seattle. The centralized (single-region) variant of the same learning approach performs 66 seconds worse than the hierarchical version, confirming the value of spatial decomposition. Among actor architectures, TransformerXL (TrXL) outperforms Gated TrXL by 5 seconds and LSTM by 18 seconds in average response time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the state-of-the-art MCTS approach (Pettet et al., 2022), the proposed DDPG method wins decisively on computation time (0.22 s vs. ~180 s per decision, an ~800× speedup) and narrowly wins on average response time (5 s improvement in 5-region Nashville, 13 s in 7-region Nashville, 10 s in Seattle); the response-time advantage is statistically significant (p < 0.05) in most configurations, with two exceptions in the 6-region Nashville setting (p = 0.14, 0.20). Against the DRLSN centralized RL baseline (Ji et al., 2019), the proposed method wins by a large margin — DRLSN response times are roughly 50% higher (e.g., >450 s vs. ~300 s in Nashville). Against the p-median-based policy (Vazirizade et al., 2021), the greedy policy, and the static (no-repositioning) policy, the proposed method consistently achieves the lowest average response times with p-values of 0.00 across all tested configurations in both cities, confirming that learned hierarchical coordination substantially outperforms heuristic and static allocation strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the hierarchical planning framework of Pettet et al. (2022), which partitions the ERM spatial area into regions and uses Monte-Carlo tree search for low-level responder-to-depot allocation within each region. The key departure is replacing the online MCTS search with DDPG-based actor-critic agents, trading decision-time computation for training-time computation. To make RL viable for this combinatorial problem, the authors introduce TransformerXL-based actors to handle variable-dimensional states, maximum-weight matching and minimum-cost flow to discretize continuous actions, and a novel reward-estimation mechanism that uses low-level critics to denoise high-level rewards. The approach also builds on the DDPG algorithm (Lillicrap et al., 2015) as the base RL method, adapted with these domain-specific modifications.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three-orders-of-magnitude reduction in decision time (from ~3 minutes to ~0.22 seconds) is practically critical in emergency response, where the paper notes that seconds can determine survival. The modest but consistent improvement in average response time (5–13 seconds) over the MCTS baseline demonstrates that learned policies can match or slightly exceed search-based methods in combinatorial resource allocation while being orders of magnitude faster. Limitations include substantial training time (up to 14 days per region for larger depots), domain-specificity to ERM, and the modest response-time gain over MCTS. Open questions include whether the transformer-plus-combinatorial-optimization recipe generalizes to other hierarchical resource-allocation problems (ride-hailing, public transit) and whether training time can be reduced through offline pre-training or curriculum strategies.
