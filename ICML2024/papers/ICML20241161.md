## Problem

While large language and multi-modal models achieve strong performance across diverse tasks, it remains unclear whether they genuinely comprehend emotions or are merely pattern-matching. No prior work has systematically investigated both the benign (performance-enhancing) and malignant (performance-impairing) effects of emotional stimuli on AI models, nor provided a mechanistic explanation for how such stimuli operate internally.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard task prompts (from Instruction Induction, BIG-Bench-Hard, or open-ended generation questions) and appends emotional stimuli without altering the original semantic content. Textual stimuli are short sentences (21 for EmotionPrompt, 36 for EmotionAttack) directly concatenated to the prompt; visual stimuli are 512×512 images generated via DALL-E (25 for EmotionPrompt across 5 Maslow need categories, 30 for EmotionAttack across 6 basic emotions) fed alongside the text prompt to multi-modal models. Outputs include standard task responses (accuracy, normalized preferred metric, or human-rated scores) and, for EmotionDecode, decoded 'meta prompts' extracted from mean embeddings at specific transformer layers.

## Method

The paper proposes three complementary components grounded in psychological theory. EmotionPrompt designs 21 textual prompts from self-monitoring, social cognitive theory, and Maslow's hierarchy of needs, plus 25 images spanning five need levels, which are appended to original prompts to boost performance. EmotionAttack constructs 36 textual prompts from negative life events and heightened emotional arousal, plus 30 DALL-E-generated images of six basic emotions, implemented at both sentence-level and word-level in zero-shot and few-shot settings to impair performance. EmotionDecode provides the mechanistic explanation: it averages the token embeddings of all prompts in each category, then decodes the mean vector at each layer of Llama2-13b-Chat to extract representative 'meta prompts' from the reward area (EmotionPrompt) and punishment area (EmotionAttack). The decoded meta prompts from deeper layers (especially the final layer) act as a dopamine-like signal that can be transferred across models (e.g., from Llama to GPT-4), confirming that AI models possess an internal reward/punishment mechanism analogous to the brain's dopaminergic pathway.

## Results

Across 940,200 evaluations on Instruction Induction and BIG-Bench-Hard, textual EmotionPrompt improves semantic understanding by 13.88% and reasoning by 11.76%, while visual EmotionPrompt improves these by 16.79% and 15.13% respectively. Conversely, textual EmotionAttack impairs semantic understanding by 10.13% and reasoning by 12.30%, while visual EmotionAttack causes far larger drops of 53.14% and 37.53%. On open-ended generation tasks (human study, 106 participants), EmotionPrompt yields relative gains of 15% in performance, 9% in truthfulness, and 9% in responsibility over vanilla prompts. Multi-modal models are notably more sensitive to emotional stimuli than text-only models (e.g., 45.34% vs. 11.22% performance drop from visual vs. textual EmotionAttack).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole explicit baseline is Zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al., 2022), compared alongside the original (vanilla) prompt. EmotionPrompt (max) outperforms Zero-shot CoT on all three evaluation settings: Instruction Induction zero-shot (0.6772 vs. 0.5721 avg), Instruction Induction few-shot (0.5930 vs. 0.5220 avg), and BIG-Bench zero-shot (16.04 vs. 14.03 avg). Notably, Zero-shot CoT itself underperforms the vanilla prompt on Instruction Induction (both zero-shot and few-shot), while EmotionPrompt consistently exceeds the vanilla baseline. On BIG-Bench zero-shot, Zero-shot CoT does improve over vanilla (14.03 vs. 12.28), but EmotionPrompt still surpasses it by 0.47 (avg) and 2.01 (max) points. The paper also reports that improvements are greater when EmotionPrompt is combined with in-context learning and prompt engineering techniques such as zero-shot CoT, suggesting the two approaches are complementary rather than competing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior work on emotional intelligence evaluation of LLMs (EmotionBench, EQ-Bench) and emotional enhancement (DialogueLLM, chain-of-emotion) by instead studying how emotional stimuli *as prompts* influence model performance on standard tasks. It repurposes classical psychological frameworks—self-monitoring, social cognitive theory, Maslow's hierarchy of needs, and emotional arousal theory—as design principles for constructing both beneficial (EmotionPrompt) and harmful (EmotionAttack) prompt variants, and introduces EmotionDecode to interpret the mechanism via a dopamine-reward-pathway analogy. The only explicit methodological baseline is zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al., 2022), against which EmotionPrompt is compared as an alternative prompt engineering technique.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a psychology-informed lens on LLM behavior with practical dual implications: EmotionPrompt offers a simple, theory-grounded prompt engineering technique that improves performance across semantic understanding, reasoning, and generation, while EmotionAttack reveals a notable vulnerability in multi-modal models (up to 53% performance drop from visual emotional stimuli). Key limitations include the speculative nature of the 'dopamine' interpretation (an analogy rather than a proven mechanism), limited task coverage due to compute constraints, and reproducibility concerns with proprietary models like GPT-4. Open questions include whether the reward/punishment area interpretation generalizes beyond Llama2-13b, whether arousal-level differentiation is truly absent in current LLMs, and how to robustly defend multi-modal models against visual emotional attacks.
