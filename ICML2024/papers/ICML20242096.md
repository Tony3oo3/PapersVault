## Problem

Existing gradient-based algorithms (GD, NGD, Nesterov acceleration) maximize the ℓ₂-margin on linearly separable data only at polynomial rates (O(1/log t), O(1/t), O(1/t²) respectively), leaving open whether a gradient-based method can achieve exponentially fast margin maximization. The paper identifies the root cause of this slowness: the centripetal velocity—the component of the normalized gradient orthogonal to the max-margin direction—becomes vanishingly small in a region where GD/NGD trajectories are trapped, fundamentally limiting convergence speed.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled binary dataset S = {(x_i, y_i)} with x_i ∈ ℝ^d, y_i ∈ {±1}, and an initialization vector w(0) (typically zero). It outputs a weight vector w(T_K) whose direction approximates the ℓ₂ max-margin solution w* and whose normalized margin γ(w) approaches γ*. For the deep network experiments, the input is standard CIFAR-10 RGB images (32×32) processed through a homogenized VGG-16, and the output is the full set of network parameters trained without explicit regularization.

## Method

PRGD (Progressive Rescaling Gradient Descent) alternates between two operations: (1) a progressive norm rescaling step that maps w → R_k · w/‖w‖, repositioning the parameter into a favorable semi-infinite hollow cylinder C(D, 2D; H) where the centripetal velocity is uniformly lower-bounded by a positive constant μ, and (2) a normalized gradient descent step (w ← w − η∇L(w)/L(w)) with projection onto a ball to prevent norm explosion. The key theoretical insight is that within this cylinder, the centripetal velocity drives exponential growth of the component along w*, while the orthogonal component remains bounded, yielding ‖ŵ(t) − w*‖ = e^{−Ω(t)}. The radii R_k grow exponentially (R_k = e^{Θ(k)}) and cycle lengths T_k grow linearly (T_k = Θ(k)), ensuring the parameter is repeatedly repositioned into the favorable region. A warm-up phase of standard GD or NGD first achieves preliminary directional convergence before the acceleration phase begins.

## Results

The headline theoretical result (Theorem 6.1) establishes that PRGD achieves both directional convergence and margin maximization at an exponential rate of e^{-Ω(t)}, in stark contrast to all existing algorithms which achieve only polynomial rates. Specifically, GD achieves O(1/log t) and NGD achieves O(1/t) for directional convergence, while the best prior method (Nesterov Acceleration) reaches only Õ(1/t²) for margin maximization. Empirically, on synthetic and real-world digit datasets, PRGD consistently reduces the margin gap faster than both GD and NGD. On CIFAR-10 with a homogenized VGG-16, PRGD achieves better test generalization than both GD and NGD without any explicit regularization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PRGD decisively outperforms both GD and NGD on every metric examined. Against GD (Soudry et al., 2018), PRGD improves the directional convergence rate from Θ(1/log t) to e^{−Ω(t)} and the margin maximization rate from Ω(1/log² t) to e^{−Ω(t)} — an exponential vs. logarithmic gap. Against NGD (Ji & Telgarsky, 2021), the improvement is from Θ(1/t_k) to e^{−Ω(t)} for directional convergence and from Ω(1/t_k²) to e^{−Ω(t)} for margin maximization. The paper further establishes (Theorem 6.4) that the previously known upper bounds for GD and NGD are in fact tight lower bounds under mild non-degeneracy conditions, confirming that no polynomial-rate algorithm can match PRGD's exponential convergence. In experiments on Sklearn Digits (digit-01, digit-04), PRGD with polynomially growing radii (α=β=0.6) consistently reduces the margin gap faster than both GD and NGD. On CIFAR-10 with VGG-16 and on the Breast Cancer Wisconsin dataset with a linear model, PRGD achieves superior test accuracy over both baselines, demonstrating practical generalization benefits beyond the linearly separable theoretical setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on implicit margin maximization by gradient-based algorithms, building directly on Soudry et al. (2018) for GD, Ji & Telgarsky (2021) for NGD, and Ji et al. (2021)/Wang et al. (2022b) for dual acceleration. The key departure is a geometric re-framing: rather than analyzing the loss landscape or dual variables, the authors identify the 'centripetal velocity' (the component of the normalized gradient orthogonal to the max-margin direction) as the governing quantity, and show that GD/NGD are trapped in an attractor region where this velocity vanishes. The proposed Progressive Rescaling Gradient Descent (PRGD) escapes this attractor via periodic norm rescaling, achieving an exponential convergence rate that is a fundamental rate-class improvement over all prior polynomial bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exponential rate result (Theorem 6.1) is a qualitative leap over all prior polynomial rates, and the accompanying lower bounds (Theorem 6.4) establish that GD and NGD cannot do better under mild data conditions, closing a gap in the theoretical understanding of implicit bias. However, the exponential guarantee requires Assumption 5.4 (support vectors span the full dataset), which fails on real-world data, and the practical experiments use a modified polynomial-radius variant rather than the theoretically optimal exponential one. Open questions include relaxing the spanning condition, understanding whether the centripetal-velocity framework generalizes to non-homogeneous or stochastic settings, and whether progressive rescaling can be combined with other regularization techniques (batch norm, SAM, dropout) in large-scale training.
