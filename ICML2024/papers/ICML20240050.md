## Problem

Regression models can produce unreliable predictions due to aleatoric variability or model uncertainty, but detecting such failures is harder than in classification because a single point estimate does not capture uncertainty. Existing detection methods rely on accurate conditional distribution estimates of the target variable, which are often inaccurate in practice, leading to poor error detection. This paper asks whether one can build a detector that is robust to such estimation inaccuracies by exploiting the statistical structure of the error distribution itself.

## Input / Output

Given a trained regressor f, a test input x from tabular data, and an application-specific error threshold ε (with a chosen discrepancy function such as absolute or relative error), the method outputs a continuous diversity score H(x) and a binary reliability decision (ε-bad if H(x) > γ). The diversity score is computed via Monte Carlo sampling (20,000 samples) from an estimated conditional distribution, making the inference cost dependent on the chosen distribution estimator (SQR, CG, or KNIFE). The threshold γ controls the TPR/FPR trade-off and is selected on a calibration set.

## Method

The core idea is to measure the statistical diversity of the error distribution at each input x using Rao's diversity coefficient: for two independent error realizations drawn from p_{Y|X=x}, compute the expected value of a learned symmetric non-negative function h of their discrepancies. The key insight is that the joint error product distributions for ε-good and ε-bad inputs exhibit qualitatively different diversity patterns, enabling separation. The function h is learned by a 4-layer neural network (64 neurons/layer, symmetrized by averaging outputs with swapped inputs) trained to minimize diversity on empirically good samples and maximize it on bad samples. This data-driven learning of h compensates for inaccuracies in the underlying conditional distribution estimates, outperforming the baseline that simply computes P_B(x;ε) from the same estimates. The optimal Neyman-Pearson detector is recovered as the special case h_p(u,v)=1{u>ε}·1{v>ε}, yielding H(x)=P_B²(x;ε).

## Results

Across 8 UCI regression datasets and two discrepancy metrics (absolute and relative error), the proposed diversity discriminators (DV-Y, DV-D) achieve AUROC improvements of up to 3.4 points (absolute error) and 3.7 points (relative error) over the best baseline at each ε. For example, DV-D-SQR on Naval reaches 96.7–97.3 AUROC versus 93.9 for B2-SQR and 91.6–95.2 for B2-KNIFE. In terms of FPR at 90% TPR, the DV algorithms reduce false alarms by up to 50% on certain datasets (e.g., DV-D-SQR on Naval drops from 0.22 to 0.11 at ε=0.175), while in the worst case performance is comparable to the best baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six baseline variants (B1/B2 × SQR/CG/KNIFE) against six proposed diversity-discriminator variants (DV-Y/DV-D × SQR/CG/KNIFE) on 8 UCI datasets. The DV algorithms consistently match or exceed the best baseline across nearly all dataset–ε combinations, with the largest gains on Naval (DV-D-SQR: 96.7–97.3 vs. 93.9–95.2 AUROC, a ~2–3 point improvement) and Energy (DV-Y-SQR: 82.1 vs. 76.3 at ε=0.6, a ~5.8 point gain). On FPR at 90% TPR, DV-D-SQR on Naval reduces false positives from 0.22 to 0.11 (a 50% reduction) at ε=0.175, and DV-Y-SQR on Yacht (relative error) drops from 0.63 to 0.34 at ε=10%. The DV-Y variants (using Y|X estimates) generally outperform DV-D variants (using D|X estimates), likely because the latter requires the regressor during estimator training. In a few cases (e.g., Kin8nm, Concrete at certain ε), the best baseline and best DV method are within 1–2 AUROC points, indicating the improvement is data-dependent. The paper also shows that conformal corrections applied to baselines improve AUROC in only 52% of 144 experiments, underscoring that the diversity-based approach offers a more reliable path to better detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes Rao's (1982) diversity coefficient from population biology as a data-driven detection statistic for regression reliability, connecting it to the Neyman-Pearson optimal detector framework. It builds on the conditional distribution estimation literature (SQR, KNIFE, Conditional Gaussian, RIO) but departs from the standard approach of directly estimating P(B|x) and thresholding; instead, it learns a symmetric dissimilarity function h that compensates for estimation inaccuracies in the underlying density estimates. The paper explicitly differentiates itself from conformal prediction, arguing that marginal coverage guarantees do not provide the conditional probability needed for per-input detection, and from selective classification, which typically requires joint training with the regressor.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, post-hoc detection layer that can be attached to any trained regressor without retraining, which is practically valuable for safety-critical deployment. However, the empirical gains over strong baselines are modest (up to ~3.4 AUROC points) and evaluated only on small UCI tabular datasets, leaving open whether the diversity-based compensation for estimation error scales to high-dimensional or non-tabular settings. The authors identify integration with Bayesian uncertainty estimates and extension to multidimensional outputs as key future directions, and the lack of comparison with rejection-based or conformal baselines under matched conditions limits the practical guidance for practitioners choosing among detection strategies.
