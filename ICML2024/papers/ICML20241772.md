## Problem

Simulating nonlinear resistive networks for machine learning has been a major bottleneck: existing methods either rely on general-purpose SPICE circuit simulators that are extremely slow (one week for 10 epochs on a 100-unit network) or restrict to linear networks, missing the nonlinearity essential to modern ML. No efficient, general-purpose algorithm existed for computing the steady state of nonlinear resistive networks with arbitrary topologies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241772/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The network takes 28×28 MNIST grayscale images as input, which are converted into 1568 paired voltage sources (each pixel x_k drives both +Ax_k and −Ax_k, where A is a large amplification factor up to 4000 to counteract signal decay across layers). The output consists of 10 node potentials corresponding to digit classes, read from the steady-state configuration. Hidden layers alternate excitatory (non-negative) and inhibitory (non-positive) units enforced by diode orientation, and the output layer is linear (no diodes).

## Method

The key theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) shows that the steady state of an ideal nonlinear resistive network is the global minimizer of a convex quadratic programming problem: the objective is total power dissipation in resistors and current sources, while diodes impose linear inequality constraints and voltage sources impose linear equality constraints on node potentials. The algorithm (Theorem 2) is an exact coordinate descent method where each coordinate update reduces to computing a weighted average of neighbor potentials plus a clipping operation to enforce diode constraints. For the layered deep resistive network (DRN) architecture, the bipartite graph structure enables exact block coordinate descent: all even-indexed layers are updated in parallel, then all odd-indexed layers, with each step requiring only matrix-vector multiplications, divisions, and clipping—making it highly GPU-parallelizable. Training uses centered equilibrium propagation (positive and negative nudging via output switches), and the paper proves the EP gradient formulas remain valid under inequality constraints (Theorem 3). This yields a 50,000× improvement in network-size-to-epoch-duration ratio over SPICE, enabling networks up to 327× larger at 160× faster speed.

## Results

On MNIST, the largest DRN (DRN-XL, 51.7M parameters) trained with equilibrium propagation achieves 1.33% test error over 100 epochs in 10h27min on a single A100 GPU, compared to 3.43% test error over 10 epochs in 1 week for the SPICE-based simulation of Kendall et al. (2020). The DRN-XL model is 327× larger and trained for 10× more epochs, yet completes in 16× less wall-clock time, yielding a 50,000× improvement in the network-size-to-epoch-duration ratio. Multi-layer DRNs (1H, 2H, 3H with 1024 units per hidden layer) achieve 1.57%, 1.48%, and 1.66% test error respectively, with the 2H model performing best among the multi-layer variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the SPICE-based simulation of Kendall et al. (2020), the paper's exact block coordinate descent algorithm achieves a dramatic speedup: the DRN-XS model (same 0.16M architecture) trains in 30 minutes versus 1 week, a ~160× speedup per epoch. Scaling to DRN-XL (51.7M parameters, 327× larger), 100 epochs complete in 10h27min, yielding a 50,000× improvement in the network-size-to-epoch-duration ratio while also achieving significantly better test error (1.33% vs 3.43%). Against deep Hopfield networks (DHNs) of Scellier & Bengio (2017), DRNs of the same size achieve comparable or slightly better test error (e.g., 1.66% vs 1.65% for 3H) but require far fewer iterations to converge to steady state (6 iterations for 3H DRN vs 100 for 3H DHN), attributable to the convexity of the resistive network's energy function versus the non-convex Hopfield energy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DRN architecture of Kendall et al. (2020) and the equilibrium propagation framework of Scellier & Bengio (2017), but departs fundamentally from prior simulation approaches by reframing the steady-state computation as a convex quadratic programming problem solvable via exact coordinate descent (Wright, 2015). Previous work either relied on general-purpose SPICE simulators (Kendall et al., 2020; Watfa et al., 2023) or restricted analysis to linear networks (Stern et al., 2022; Wycoff et al., 2022), missing the nonlinearities essential to modern ML. The QP formulation itself draws on classical circuit theory (Millar, 1951; Onsager, 1931), but the paper's contribution is the specialized block coordinate descent algorithm that exploits the bipartite layer structure of DRNs for GPU-parallel execution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50,000× improvement in network-size-to-epoch-duration ratio over SPICE removes the primary bottleneck to scaling nonlinear resistive network research, enabling first-time training of 2- and 3-hidden-layer DRNs and a 51.7M-parameter model on MNIST. The convexity of the energy function (guaranteeing a unique steady state) also explains why DRNs converge in far fewer iterations than their non-convex deep Hopfield network counterparts (6 vs. 100 for 3 layers). Key limitations include the ideal-element assumption (real diodes have forward voltage drops, leakage, and breakdown), and the method has not yet been validated on tasks beyond MNIST. Open questions include extending the framework to piecewise-linear or fully nonlinear i-v curves, and whether the simulation insights transfer to non-ideal hardware.
