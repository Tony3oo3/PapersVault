## Problem

Existing causal inference methods address either latent confounding bias (via instrumental variables) or collider bias from non-random sample selection (via shadow variables), but no approach handles both simultaneously. When both biases coexist—as is common in real-world observational studies—IV methods remain biased by conditioning on the selection indicator, and shadow variable methods fail because unmeasured confounders violate their identifiability assumptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241163/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational data where the outcome Y is only observed for selected units (S=1), while treatment T, covariates X, instrumental variable Z, and selection indicator S are fully observed for all units. It outputs the estimated conditional expectation E[Y | X, T, Φ, R] over the full population (both S=1 and S=0), from which treatment effects such as ATE can be derived. The key constraint is that the second-stage outcome regression must be trained exclusively on S=1 samples yet generalize to S=0 samples where Y is missing.

## Method

2SSI (Two-Stage Shadow Inclusion) leverages a single well-defined IV to simultaneously address both biases. In the first stage, it regresses T on X and Z to obtain the residual R, while jointly learning a decomposed representation Φ of Z that is conditionally independent of T (enforced via IPM or CLUB) but predictive of S. The key insight is that R, conditioned on Φ, satisfies the shadow variable definition: it is associated with Y through the unmeasured confounder path R←U→Y yet independent of S given X, T, Y, and Φ. In the second stage, R serves a dual role—blocking the spurious T←U→Y path (addressing latent confounding) and acting as a shadow variable to generalize E[Y|X,T,Φ,R,S=1] to E[Y|X,T,Φ,R,S=0] via the odds ratio framework (addressing collider bias). All components are implemented with deep neural networks and trained end-to-end with a composite loss combining treatment prediction, selection prediction, distributional discrepancy, and outcome regression objectives.

## Results

On the low-dimensional Demand datasets (MSE ×10³, lower is better), 2SSI achieves 0.147–0.155 on S=1 data and 1.275–1.320 on S=0 data across collider bias strengths α∈{5,10,15} with fixed latent confounding β=10, outperforming the best baseline (Kernel IV: 0.297–0.428 on S=1; SHADOW: 4.619–5.258 on S=0) by roughly 2× on S=1 and 3.5–4× on S=0. Under varying latent confounding β∈{5,10,15} with fixed α=10, 2SSI maintains MSE of 0.038–0.522 (S=1) and 1.278–2.655 (S=0), while baselines degrade sharply (e.g., IPSW reaches 24.65 on S=0 at β=15). On the real-world Fertil2 dataset, 2SSI is the only method that consistently produces positive ATE estimates aligned with the ground-truth reference, whereas DeepGMM, SHADOW, and DFIV yield negative estimates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

2SSI outperforms all nine baselines across every setting of collider bias strength α and latent confounding strength β on both S=1 and S=0 data. On S=0 data (where collider bias is most damaging), 2SSI achieves MSE of ~1.28–1.32 versus 4.62–5.26 for SHADOW (the best selection-bias method) and 6.45–13.60 for IV methods, representing a 3.5–10× improvement. Among IV baselines, Kernel IV is the strongest on S=1 data (0.297–0.428) but still roughly 2× worse than 2SSI (0.147–0.155); all IV methods (DeepIV, DFIV, DeepGMM) suffer severe degradation on S=0 data (MSE > 8) because they cannot handle collider bias. SHADOW, which addresses collider bias but not latent confounding, is the closest competitor on S=0 data but remains 3.5–4× worse than 2SSI. On the Fertil2 real-world dataset, 2SSI is the only estimator that consistently yields positive ATE estimates matching the ground-truth reference, while DeepGMM, SHADOW, and DFIV produce negative estimates and CB-IV shows the highest variance with results far from the reference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate lines of causal inference research: IV-based methods for latent confounding (2SLS, 2SRI, DeepIV, DFIV, CB-IV) and shadow-variable methods for collider bias (d'Haultfoeuille, Miao & Tchetgen Tchetgen). The key conceptual departure is using the treatment residual from the IV first-stage regression as a shadow variable, enabled by learning a decomposed representation Φ of Z that is conditionally independent of T but associated with S. This makes 2SSI a natural extension of 2SRI (which only addresses latent confounding) by adding the decomposed representation learning and the odds-ratio-based generalization from S=1 to S=0 samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first method to simultaneously address latent confounding and collider bias, filling a gap the authors explicitly identify as unaddressed in the literature. The practical impact is significant for observational studies where non-random selection (e.g., only releasing scores for admitted candidates) coexists with unmeasured confounders. Key limitations include the requirement for well-defined IVs (necessitating expert knowledge), the additive model assumption, and the unconfounded sample selection assumption. An open question the authors raise is whether existing IV-discovery and confounding-detection tools remain valid when collider bias is also present.
