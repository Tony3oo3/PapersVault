## Problem

Certifiable robustness for message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) such as GraphSAGE has been largely unexplored, with prior verification work focused on graph convolutional networks. The mixed-integer formulation needed to encode MPNNs with variable graph structure produces very large optimization problems where standard interval-arithmetic bounds are too loose to be computationally useful. This paper addresses the gap by developing topology-aware bounds tightening that exploits graph structure and perturbation budgets to make MPNN verification tractable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240744/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a trained MPNN (layer weights and biases), an input graph with node features and adjacency matrix, a target node or graph with its predicted class label, and perturbation budgets specifying the maximum number of edges that can be added or removed globally (Q) and per-node (q_v). It outputs a binary robustness certificate: either a proof that no admissible perturbation can flip the prediction (dual bound of the branch-and-bound tree is positive) or a concrete adversarial perturbation (feasible MIP solution with negative worst-case margin). The verification is solved to early termination rather than full optimality, with a 2-hour time limit for graph classification and 30 minutes for node classification.

## Method

The paper encodes a trained MPNN with ReLU activations into a mixed-integer program using big-M constraints for both bilinear edge-feature products and ReLU nonlinearities, then introduces two topology-based bounds tightening routines to tighten the big-M coefficients. Static bounds tightening (sbt) computes tighter variable bounds in a single forward pass by starting from the original neighbor set and selecting at most q_v most impactful edge additions/removals within budget, rather than pessimistically including all nodes. Aggressive bounds tightening (abt) extends this within the branch-and-bound tree: at each node, already-fixed edge variables reduce the effective neighborhood and remaining budget, yielding progressively tighter bounds that are enforced as local cutting planes via a SCIP separator callback. Both routines are implemented as extensions to the open-source SCIP solver, and the paper also compares against Gurobi with basic and static bounds tightening, showing that sbt reduces SCIP's time penalty relative to Gurobi from a factor of 10 to roughly 3 on robust instances.

## Results

On graph classification (ENZYMES, s=2), static bounds tightening (SCIPsbt) reduces average solving time from 605.97s (SCIPbasic) to 230.59s (~3× speedup) and increases solved instances from 5579 to 5831 out of 5915. On the harder MUTAG benchmark, aggressive bounds tightening (SCIPabt) outperforms SCIPsbt on robust instances by ~10% in shifted geometric mean (91.11s vs 100.86s) and ~29% in arithmetic mean (238.10s vs 336.41s). For node classification (Cora, CiteSeer), all methods solve all 2708/3312 instances in under 0.5s, confirming that edge-removal-only verification is computationally trivial. The topology-based tightening narrows the SCIP-to-Gurobi time penalty on robust instances from a factor of ~10 (basic) to ~3 (sbt/abt).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three SCIP variants (basic, sbt, abt) against two Gurobi variants (basic, sbt) on graph and node classification. On ENZYMES, GRBsbt is the fastest method overall (74.87s avg, 22.90s on robust instances), but SCIPsbt narrows the gap substantially: the SCIP-to-Gurobi time penalty on robust instances drops from ~8.5× (basic: 278.58s vs 32.80s) to ~3× (sbt: 82.89s vs 32.80s). On MUTAG robust instances, SCIPabt (238.10s) outperforms SCIPsbt (336.41s) by ~29% in arithmetic mean, and both dramatically outperform SCIPbasic (798.47s). However, Gurobi still dominates on MUTAG robust instances (GRBsbt: 73.78s vs SCIPabt: 238.10s), though GRBbasic is anomalously slow there (162.25s). For node classification, all methods solve every instance in under 0.5s, making the comparison moot; SCIPbasic is marginally fastest (0.10s on Cora) while SCIPabt is slowest (0.46s) due to overhead of dynamic bound recomputation that provides no benefit on these simple problems. The authors note that OBBT, while producing tighter bounds, does not improve solving time over SCIPsbt and incurs a 6045s overhead for bound computation, confirming that topology-based tightening is the more practical approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the mixed-integer formulation for GNNs from Zhang et al. (2024) and the big-M ReLU encoding from Anderson et al. (2020), extending the certifiable robustness line of work (Bojchevski & Günnemann 2019; Zügner & Günnemann 2019a, 2020; Jin et al. 2020) from GCNs to the more general message-passing/GraphSAGE framework. The key methodological departure is the introduction of topology-based bounds tightening, which exploits graph neighborhoods and perturbation budgets to tighten variable bounds—adapting the aggressive bounds tightening concept from Belotti et al. (2016) to the MPNN setting. Unlike prior certificates that handle only one perturbation type (edge removal only, feature-only, or Gromov-Wasserstein), this formulation simultaneously admits adding/removing edges, global and local budgets, and topological perturbations within a single MIP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, open-source tool (SCIP extension) for certifying MPNN robustness, making the open-source solver nearly as competitive as commercial Gurobi (reducing the time penalty from ~10× to ~3× on robust instances). However, the scope remains limited: only ReLU activations, only topological (not feature) perturbations, and graph classification experiments are restricted to small molecular graphs (≤33 nodes). Open questions include scaling to larger graphs, incorporating feature perturbations alongside structural ones, and whether the topology-based tightening ideas transfer to other GNN architectures or activation functions. The finding that aggressive bounds tightening does not consistently outperform static bounds tightening, and that OBBT (solving many LPs for much tighter bounds) yields negligible solving-time improvement, suggests that the bottleneck in MPNN verification may lie elsewhere in the branch-and-bound process.
