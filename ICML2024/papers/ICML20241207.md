## Problem

Adaptive gradient methods like Adam and RMSProp incorporate a square root into their preconditioner, which conflates sign descent with adaptivity and obscures the role of each component in explaining why these methods generalize worse than SGD on CNNs while excelling on transformers. The paper asks whether removing the square root—thereby strengthening the second-order (quasi-Newton) interpretation—can close the generalization gap on convolutional architectures while preserving performance on attention-based models.

## Input / Output

The methods consume a mini-batch gradient vector (reshaped into a p×d matrix G for matrix methods) and the current preconditioner state, which is initialized to the identity matrix (not zero as in root-based methods) to ensure positive-definiteness. For the inverse-free Shampoo variant, the preconditioner is stored as two factor matrices C ∈ R^{p×p} and K ∈ R^{d×d} in BFP-16 half precision, with the preconditioner recovered as S^{-1} = (CC^T) ⊗ (KK^T). The output is the updated parameter matrix, with the key practical difference that IF-Shampoo avoids all matrix root decompositions and inversions, enabling stable half-precision training.

## Method

The paper removes the square root from adaptive methods and provides a rigorous second-order justification by reinterpreting the gradient outer product H = gg^T as a novel empirical Fisher defined over the joint distribution of all N labels (rather than the standard per-sample empirical Fisher), which naturally resolves loss-scaling invariance and preserves affine reparametrization invariance. The key theoretical tool is the Bayesian Learning Rule (BLR): by viewing the preconditioner S as the inverse covariance of a Gaussian variational approximation and performing natural gradient descent in parameter space, the root-free update is recovered exactly, and a new 'preconditioner invariance' emerges that allows reparameterizing S (e.g., S^{-1} = CC^T) to obtain inverse-free updates without matrix decompositions. For Kronecker-factored methods, this yields IF-Shampoo, which updates factors C and K via matrix exponential (first-order truncated) in BFP-16, eliminating the numerically unstable matrix root decompositions required by original Shampoo. Empirically, root-free RMSProp closes the generalization gap to SGD on CIFAR-100 CNNs while matching root-based RMSProp on ImageWoof10 ViTs, and IF-Shampoo trains GCViT, SwinViT, FocalNet, and VMamba twice as fast as Shampoo in half precision with comparable accuracy.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 (CNNs), square-root-free (RF) adaptive methods close the generalization gap between root-based adaptive methods and SGD, matching SGD's test accuracy while root-based methods (RMSProp, Adam) underperform. On ImageWoof10 (vision transformers), RF methods maintain the same performance as their root-based counterparts, indicating the root is not necessary for transformer training. For matrix methods, IF-Shampoo (inverse- and root-free) achieves similar per-iteration progress to Shampoo on GCViT, SwinViT, FocalNet, and VMamba while training approximately 2× faster in BFP-16 and using less memory. In the original (outdated) training setup with constant learning rate and zero preconditioner initialization, root-based methods still outperform their root-free counterparts, confirming the root's historical role.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against SGD (Wilson et al., 2017), RF adaptive methods close the well-known generalization gap on CIFAR-100 CNNs, achieving comparable test accuracy, whereas root-based RMSProp and Adam exhibit a clear gap. Against root-based RMSProp (Tieleman & Hinton, 2012), RF RMSProp matches performance on ImageWoof10 vision transformers but significantly outperforms it on CIFAR-100 CNNs by eliminating the sign-descent component. Against Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015), the same pattern holds: RF methods match on transformers and close the gap on CNNs. Against Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018), IF-Shampoo achieves similar per-iteration progress on modern vision models (GCViT, SwinViT, FocalNet, VMamba) but trains approximately 2× faster in BFP-16 and requires less memory, since it avoids numerically unstable matrix root decompositions that force Shampoo to use single or double precision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the lineage of adaptive gradient methods (RMSProp, Adam, AdaGrad, Shampoo) by removing the square root from their preconditioner updates, thereby strengthening the second-order (quasi-Newton) interpretation that the root had weakened. It introduces a novel empirical Fisher defined over a joint distribution of all labels (rather than per-sample), which resolves the scale-invariance conflict and provides a rigorous Hessian-approximation justification for the gradient outer product. The Bayesian learning rule (Khan et al., 2018) and natural gradient framework are repurposed to introduce 'preconditioner invariance,' enabling inverse-free Kronecker-factored updates that decouple the preconditioner structure from the curvature approximation—a key departure from Shampoo's coupled design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: IF-Shampoo trains in BFP-16 (unlike Shampoo, which requires single or double precision) and is roughly 2× faster per iteration, making matrix adaptive methods viable for modern low-precision training pipelines. Conceptually, the finding that adaptivity alone (without sign descent) explains much of the adaptive-method advantage on transformers, and that root-free methods close the CNN generalization gap, reframes an open question in the field. Limitations include evaluation at relatively small scale (CIFAR-100, ImageWoof10, 300 epochs) and the need to verify whether these findings persist at the scale of billion-parameter pre-training; the role of the root under very different hyperparameter regimes (e.g., no LR schedule) remains partially open.
