## Problem

In sequential decision problems where the learner must simultaneously select multiple actions (e.g., price and advertising budget), each producing a noisy intermediate observation whose product forms the reward, standard MAB approaches treat all action combinations as a single exponentially large action space and ignore the informative intermediate observations. Existing factored bandit models (Zimmert & Seldin, 2018) apply noise to the final reward rather than to intermediate observations, failing to capture the product structure that is central to real-world scenarios. This paper shows that without intermediate observations, the regret lower bound retains an unavoidable exponential dependence on the number of dimensions d (in σ^d), motivating a new setting that exploits both the factored structure and the intermediate feedback.

## Input / Output

At each round, the algorithm receives a d-dimensional vector of intermediate observations x(t) = (x_1(t), ..., x_d(t))^T, where each component x_i(t) is a σ²-subgaussian noisy realization of the expected value μ_{i,a_i(t)} determined by the i-th action component. The reward r(t) = ∏_i x_i(t) is the product of these observations and is not itself subgaussian for d ≥ 3. The algorithm outputs an action vector a(t) ∈ [[k_1]] × ... × [[k_d]] each round, with the optimization decomposing independently across dimensions (no combinatorial search over the full product space). The key inputs are the per-component action sizes k_i and the known subgaussian proxy σ.

## Method

The paper introduces two algorithms exploiting the factored structure. F-UCB runs a UCB-like exploration independently for each action component dimension, selecting the action vector that maximizes the product of per-component UCB indices; it achieves worst-case regret Õ(σd√(kT)) matching the lower bound up to logarithmic factors, but fails to be instance-dependent optimal because it does not coordinate exploration across components. F-Track is a bound-tracking algorithm that, after a warm-up phase, solves the instance-dependent lower-bound LP to compute target pull counts for each action vector, then pulls in a round-robin fashion to track those targets, with a recovery phase that falls back to F-UCB if estimates drift. The key insight is that the product reward structure allows the regret to decompose linearly in d (rather than exponentially), and that achieving instance-dependent optimality requires planning the joint action-vector pulls to satisfy per-component exploration constraints simultaneously, a coordination that independent per-component UCB cannot provide.

## Results

The worst-case regret lower bound for FRBs is Ω(σd√(kT)) (Theorem 3.1), and F-UCB achieves a matching upper bound of Õ(σd√(kT)) (Theorem 4.1), confirming minimax optimality up to logarithmic factors. Without intermediate observations, the lower bound degrades to Ω(σ^d√(kT/d)) (Theorem 3.2), showing an unavoidable exponential dependence on d. For the instance-dependent regime, F-Track asymptotically matches the lower bound C(ν) (Theorem 5.1), while F-UCB's upper bound can be up to 2αd times the lower bound for large suboptimality gaps (Equation 16), revealing that independent per-component optimism is insufficient for instance-dependent optimality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Robust-UCB (Bubeck et al., 2013), which treats the FRB as a heavy-tailed MAB over the full combinatorial action space, F-UCB improves the worst-case regret from Ω(σ√(k^d T)) to Õ(σd√(kT)), replacing the exponential dependence on d with a linear one by exploiting the factored structure and intermediate observations. In the instance-dependent regime, F-UCB's bound (Corollary 4.3) is strictly tighter than Robust-UCB's (Equation 15 vs. 14), since the factored decomposition avoids summing over all k^d action vectors. Against Factored Bandit TEA (Zimmert & Seldin, 2018), which considers a factored structure but applies subgaussian noise to the final reward rather than to intermediate observations, F-UCB achieves a slightly tighter instance-dependent bound by eliminating the κ factor and the extra log log T and log(1/Δ²_{i,j}) terms. However, F-UCB does not achieve instance-dependent optimality (its upper bound can be 2αd times the lower bound for large gaps), whereas F-Track does match the lower bound asymptotically — a guarantee neither baseline provides. Notably, Zimmert & Seldin (2018) do not provide any worst-case lower or upper bounds, leaving the minimax complexity of their setting uncharacterized.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces the Factored-Reward Bandits (FRBs) setting, which combines a factored action structure with intermediate observations whose product forms the reward. It builds on UCB1 (Auer et al., 2002) for the F-UCB algorithm and on the bound-tracking framework of Lattimore & Szepesvári (2017) for F-Track, while explicitly departing from Zimmert & Seldin (2018) 'Factored Bandits,' which has a similar factored action structure but applies noise to the final reward rather than to per-component intermediate observations. The worst-case lower bound proof also borrows technical tools from the multitask bandits literature (Wang et al., 2021), and the instance-dependent lower bound leverages rearrangement inequalities (Hardy et al., 1952) to obtain a closed-form solution to the associated linear program.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that intermediate observations are not merely a convenience but are essential for avoiding an exponential dependence on the number of action components d in the subgaussian proxy σ (Theorem 3.2), which has direct practical implications for high-dimensional structured decision problems like joint pricing and advertising. A key conceptual finding is that worst-case optimality (achieved by F-UCB) does not imply instance-dependent optimality in this setting, because action components must be coordinated rather than optimized independently—a departure from standard MAB where UCB1 achieves both. Open questions include whether non-asymptotic instance-dependent optimality is achievable and whether the results extend to aggregation functions beyond the product, both of which the authors identify as future work.
