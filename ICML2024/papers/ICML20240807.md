## Problem

Balancing strategies are widely assumed to be essential for mitigating treatment bias in counterfactual estimation, yet their effectiveness in temporal settings remains an open question. This paper reveals a counterintuitive finding: in time series counterfactual estimation, models that forgo balancing (ERM variants) consistently outperform their balanced counterparts, even under significant treatment bias, challenging the prevailing perception of balancing strategies' utility.

## Input / Output

Models consume sequences of time-varying covariates, static covariates, treatment assignments, and observed outcomes, and produce multi-step-ahead counterfactual outcome predictions at horizons τ=1 to 6. Evaluation spans three distinct scenarios—standard supervised learning, short-term history cold-start (with truncated history of K steps), and distribution shift cold-start (training at γ=10, testing at γ=1,3,8)—across varying treatment bias intensities, with RMSE as the primary metric on Tumor Growth, MIMIC-III, and M5 datasets.

## Method

This empirical study systematically compares balanced and non-balanced (ERM) variants of five temporal causal models (Causal Transformer, CRN, RMSN, GNET, MSM) under three balancing strategies (Adversarial Gradient Reversal, Counterfactual Domain Confusion, PS-based Contrastive Balancing) across synthetic, semi-synthetic, and real-world datasets. The key finding is that balanced representation modules consistently fail to improve and often degrade counterfactual estimation, introducing high variance and losing covariate information. Mechanistic explanations are provided through covariate distribution analysis (showing minimal group differences at low bias and diminishing differences over time), reconstruction loss analysis (showing balanced representations lose information under high bias), and t-SNE visualization (showing balancing does not actually equalize treatment-group distributions). The study further compares sequential models against non-sequential baselines (CFR, TARnet, OLS, Random) to characterize when temporal dependencies become uninformative under excessive treatment bias.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that ERM variants (models without a balanced representation module) consistently match or outperform their balanced counterparts across all evaluation settings. On the Tumor Growth dataset at high treatment bias (γ=10, τ=1), CT w/o BRM achieves 4.395±0.958 RMSE versus CT's 5.746±1.709, and CRN w/o BRM achieves 4.707±0.394 versus CRN's 4.963±0.345. On MIMIC-III (τ=1), CT w/o BRM (0.23±0.06) and CRN w/o BRM (0.22±0.02) both outperform their balanced versions (0.25±0.06 and 0.24±0.03 respectively). The balanced representation module also introduces substantially higher variance (e.g., CT at γ=10 shows std of 1.709 vs. 0.958 for CT w/o BRM), indicating training instability.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method but empirically evaluates whether existing balancing strategies (CDC in Causal Transformer, AGR in CRN, propensity reweighting in RMSN, G-computation in GNET, IPTW in MSM) improve temporal counterfactual estimation. The key comparison is between each balanced model and its ERM variant (balancing module removed). ERM variants win or tie in the vast majority of settings: at low bias (γ=1) differences are marginal, but at high bias (γ=10) ERM variants achieve substantially lower RMSE with much lower variance (e.g., CT w/o BRM: 4.395±0.958 vs. CT: 5.746±1.709 at τ=1). Among non-ERM baselines, GNET and MSM become competitive or superior at very high γ (γ=10), while CFR and TARnet (non-sequential) are outperformed by sequential ERM models at low-to-moderate bias but surpass them at extreme bias (γ≥6 on Tumor). The PCB contrastive balancing strategy performs worst overall, degrading both CT and CRN significantly (e.g., CT-PCB reaches 2.290±0.701 RMSE at γ=3, τ=5 vs. 0.965±0.142 for CT w/o BRM). The paper concludes that balancing modules fail to reduce covariate distribution differences in practice while destroying predictive information, making them net-negative for temporal counterfactual estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of temporal counterfactual estimation (CT, CRN, RMSN, GNET, MSM) and the broader causal inference literature on balancing strategies (IPTW, matching, stratification, representation-based IPM balancing). Rather than proposing a new model, it repurposes the existing balanced representation modules (Adversarial Gradient Reversal, Counterfactual Domain Confusion, PS-based Contrastive Balancing) as the object of empirical scrutiny, systematically ablating them from their host architectures. It departs from the prevailing narrative in the temporal causal inference literature—which treats balancing as a necessary and beneficial component—by demonstrating that the ERM variants of these models often match or exceed their balanced counterparts across multiple datasets and evaluation protocols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that representation-based balancing can degrade rather than improve temporal counterfactual estimation challenges a core design assumption in several state-of-the-art architectures and calls for practitioners to reconsider whether balancing is warranted before adding it to a pipeline. The paper identifies three concrete open directions: managing the trade-off between distributional balance and predictive fidelity, developing more stable balancing objectives that do not inflate variance, and building diagnostic tools to assess whether treatment bias is severe enough to justify balancing in the first place. A key limitation is that the study is purely empirical and does not propose a new balancing method that resolves the identified failure modes, leaving the 'how to balance well in time series' question open.
