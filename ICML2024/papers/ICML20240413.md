## Problem

Generalizing causal effects from a randomized controlled trial to a target population with different covariate distributions is statistically infeasible when the trial is small, because it requires accurately estimating complex outcome functions from limited data. Prior generalization methods (e.g., Dahabreh et al.) rely solely on trial data and target covariates, leaving no mechanism to leverage the large-scale observational data (e.g., electronic health records) that is readily available but potentially biased by unmeasured confounding.

## Input / Output

The method consumes three data sources: a small trial sample with covariates, treatment assignments, and outcomes; a large observational sample with covariates, treatments, and outcomes (used only to train a black-box predictor, with no causal assumptions imposed); and target population covariates alone (no outcomes). It outputs point estimates of average potential outcomes E[Y^a | S=0] in the target population, from which average treatment effects are directly derived. The observational predictor is a flexible ML model (e.g., neural network) trained on the OS, while the correction/regression step uses parametric fits (e.g., polynomial ridge regression) on the small trial sample.

## Method

The paper develops two prediction-powered estimators that integrate a black-box predictor f_a(X) trained on observational data with trial data, requiring no assumptions on the observational study. The Additive Bias Correction (ABC) estimator estimates the bias function b_a(X) = f_a(X) − g_a(X) by regressing prediction errors Z = f_a(X) − Y onto covariates in the trial sample, then applies f_a(X) − b̂_a(X) to target covariates. The Augmented Outcome Modeling (AOM) estimator instead uses f_a(X) as an additional regressor when fitting the outcome function from the trial, offering robustness when the predictor carries no useful signal. The key theoretical insight, formalized via Legendre polynomial ridge regression bounds, is that the MSE of ABC is governed by the complexity of b_a(X) rather than g_a(X); when the OS predictor captures high-order structure, b_a(X) becomes a low-norm, low-degree function that is far easier to estimate from a small trial. Over 1,200 synthetic scenarios confirm that both estimators yield substantial RMSE improvements when the OS has little hidden confounding and the trial is small, while degrading gracefully to baseline performance when the OS is heavily confounded.

## Results

Across 1,200 synthetic scenarios (varying trial size n₁ ∈ {200, 1000}, outcome complexity via l_x^FOM₁ ∈ {0.5, 0.2}, and hidden confounding strength), the prediction-powered estimators (ABC and AOM) achieve substantially lower RMSE than the trial-only OM baseline when the trial is small (n₁=200), the outcome function is complex, and observational confounding is mild. In the linear-model ablation (Table 1), OM achieves MSE 0.0044 under simple settings, while ABC and AOM match or exceed it; under confounded settings (γ=1), OM degrades to 0.0060 while AOM reaches 0.0139, still outperforming OS-OM alone. When the observational predictor is pure noise (Figure 5), AOM gracefully falls back to baseline OM performance while ABC degrades significantly.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the outcome-model (OM) estimator from Dahabreh et al. (2020), which fits g_a(X) from the trial sample alone and averages predictions in the target population. The proposed ABC and AOM estimators significantly outperform OM in RMSE when the trial is small (n₁=200) and the outcome function has high intrinsic variation (l_x^FOM₁=0.2), because the observational predictor f_a(X) captures most of the complex structure, leaving a simpler bias function b_a(X) to estimate from limited trial data. However, when the trial is large (n₁=1000), OM with higher-degree polynomials performs on par with ABC and AOM, as the trial alone suffices to fit g_a(X) well. The OS-OM baseline (applying f_a(X) directly without trial correction) is consistently hurt by hidden confounding and does not improve with larger trial size. AOM demonstrates superior robustness over ABC: when f_a(X) is i.i.d. noise (Figure 5), AOM simply ignores the useless regressor and retains baseline performance, while ABC degrades because the bias function becomes harder to estimate than g_a(X) itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the prediction-powered inference (PPI) framework of Angelopoulos et al. (2023) to the causal generalization problem established by Dahabreh et al. (2019, 2020), where a small RCT's results must be transported to a target population with different covariate distributions. It draws on Schuler et al. (2021) and Liao et al. (2023) for the augmented outcome modeling approach (using observational predictions as additional regressors) and on Kallus et al. (2018) for the idea that a biased predictor's residual can be simpler than the target function. The key departure is making no assumptions whatsoever on the observational study—no unmeasured confounding, no transportability—while prior work on combining trial and observational data typically requires the target population to coincide with the OS population or imposes strong causal assumptions on the OS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, assumption-light recipe for leveraging large-scale observational data (e.g., EHRs) to improve causal generalization from small RCTs, with clear theoretical conditions (via polynomial ridge regression bounds) for when the approach helps versus when it degrades to baseline performance. The practical relevance is significant given that RCTs are often underpowered for subgroups and external validity is a persistent concern in clinical evidence. However, all validation is synthetic (1,200 GP-sampled scenarios), and no real-world dataset is used, leaving open whether the theoretical advantages translate to messy clinical data. Future directions noted include representation-powered outcome modeling (using learned features rather than scalar predictions) and doubly-robust variants for confidence intervals.
