## Problem

Despite widespread speculation about LLM use in scientific publishing, there is no efficient method to quantify the fraction of a real-world text corpus that has been generated or substantially modified by AI. Instance-level detection is unreliable, computationally expensive, and fundamentally limited in distinguishing individual documents. The paper addresses this gap by shifting from per-document classification to corpus-level statistical estimation of AI-generated content.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241192/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method requires three inputs: a target corpus of documents of uncertain origin, a human reference corpus (e.g., pre-ChatGPT peer reviews), and an AI-generated reference corpus (reviews produced by prompting an LLM with the same review instructions). Rather than processing full text sequences, each document is reduced to a binary vector of adjective occurrences (present/absent), with technical keywords excluded, making the computation a simple frequency count over the vocabulary. The output is a single scalar α with bootstrap confidence intervals (30,000 resamples), representing the estimated fraction of the corpus substantially modified by AI, with no per-document classification produced.

## Method

The core approach models the target corpus as a mixture (1−α)P + αQ, where P and Q are document distributions estimated from human and AI reference corpora. Document likelihoods are computed as products of token occurrence probabilities over a vocabulary of adjectives, treating each document as a set of present/absent tokens rather than a sequence, which avoids the combinatorial explosion of full-text modeling. The fraction α is then estimated via maximum likelihood on the aggregate log-likelihood of the entire target corpus, requiring no per-document inference, no gradient-based training, and no access to the generating model's internals. Validation is performed by constructing synthetic mixtures with known α values and confirming estimation accuracy within 1.8% error. This yields over 10 million times (7 orders of magnitude) greater computational efficiency than state-of-the-art instance-level detectors while reducing in-distribution estimation error by 3.4× and out-of-distribution error by 4.6×.

## Results

The method estimates that 10.6% of ICLR 2024 review sentences, 9.1% of NeurIPS 2023, 6.5% of CoRL 2023, and 16.9% of EMNLP 2023 were substantially modified by LLMs, representing a sharp increase from pre-ChatGPT baselines of 1.4–2.4%. In contrast, Nature Portfolio journals show no significant increase (α remains ~1–1.6% pre- and post-ChatGPT). Validation on semi-synthetic data yields prediction errors below 1.8% in-distribution and 2.4% out-of-distribution, and the method is 10 million times more computationally efficient than state-of-the-art detectors (68.09 FLOPS vs. 2.721×10⁹ FLOPS per sentence).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three baselines: a fine-tuned BERT classifier, DetectGPT (Mitchell et al., 2023), and DNA-GPT (Yang et al., 2023), all evaluated under the same protocol. The proposed MLE method reduces in-distribution estimation error by 3.4× (from 6.2% to 1.8%) and out-of-distribution estimation error by 4.6× (from 11.2% to 2.4%) relative to the best-performing baseline. On computational efficiency, the method requires only 68.09 FLOPS per sentence at inference versus 2.721×10⁹ FLOPS for the baselines—a 10-million-fold (7 orders of magnitude) improvement—because it only counts word frequencies rather than running neural network inference. Training cost is also negligible since no backpropagation is needed. The baselines (DetectGPT, DNA-GPT) are zero-shot methods that require access to the generating model's internals or a proxy model, making them less robust across different LLMs and scenarios, whereas the proposed method operates purely on corpus-level token statistics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of instance-level AI text detection (zero-shot methods like DetectGPT and DNA-GPT, and training-based classifiers like GPT-Sentinel) by reframing the problem as a corpus-level mixture model estimation task. Rather than classifying individual documents and aggregating, it uses a maximum likelihood framework over token occurrence distributions to directly estimate the fraction of AI-generated content in a corpus. The approach draws on the standard statistical machinery of mixture model MLE but applies it to a new domain, and it is informed by prior observations of corpus-level homogenization effects in LLM output (Bommasani et al., Cao et al.) that are invisible at the individual-document level.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 6.5–16.9% of post-ChatGPT AI conference review sentences may be substantially LLM-modified, with no comparable signal in Nature journals, provides the first large-scale empirical evidence of AI's differential penetration into scientific peer review. The homogenization correlation (convergent reviews show higher estimated α) raises concerns about loss of epistemic diversity in feedback, with implications for how conference organizers and editors should respond. Key limitations include the focus on ChatGPT/GPT-4, the inability to make causal claims about reviewer behavior, and sensitivity to shifts in non-native speaker populations. Open questions include whether the method generalizes to rapidly evolving LLMs, whether it can be extended to other high-stakes text ecosystems (legal, medical, journalistic), and how to design policies that account for the corpus-level effects identified here.
