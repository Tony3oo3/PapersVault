## Problem

RLHF achieves strong empirical results using less than 1% of the data from agent-environment interactions, yet there is limited theoretical justification for this efficiency, and most prior analyses focus on value-based algorithms rather than the policy-based methods used in practice (e.g., ChatGPT). This paper asks whether policy optimization with active human feedback can be shown to require only a small fraction of the overall sample complexity in human queries, and provides the first such guarantees for policy gradient RLHF with exploration.

## Input / Output

The algorithm operates in a discounted MDP where the agent never observes numerical rewards; instead, it receives pairwise trajectory comparisons (y ∈ {0,1}) generated under a Bradley-Terry model between trajectories from the current policy and a fixed baseline policy. The agent also maintains a coverage distribution over state-actions and a feature covariance matrix to guide exploration. The output is an ε-optimal policy (V* − V^π̂ ≤ ε with probability ≥ 1−δ), returned as a uniform mixture over all policies generated across outer-loop phases. Two function approximation settings are supported: linear (log-linear policy, linear reward) and neural (two-layer ReLU networks for policy, Q-function, and reward).

## Method

PG-RLHF (linear) and NN-PG-RLHF (neural) extend the PC-PG policy gradient algorithm by replacing the known reward with an MLE-estimated reward learned from trajectory-level preference comparisons. Each outer-loop phase collects M_HF pairwise comparisons between trajectories from a coverage-guided distribution and a baseline policy, trains a reward model via MLE, then runs T inner-loop iterations of natural policy gradient with an exploration bonus on under-visited state-actions. The key technical novelty is a trajectory-level elliptical potential analysis: because rewards are only observed as sums over entire trajectories (not per state-action), the standard elliptical potential lemma cannot be applied directly; the authors decompose the reward estimation error into a trajectory-level feature norm times the MLE parameter error, then show the summed trajectory norms are bounded by the elliptical potential lemma applied to the trajectory-difference covariance matrix. This yields a query complexity of O(NM_HF) that is a 1/T fraction of the total sample complexity, theoretically explaining why RLHF needs so little human feedback.

## Results

The paper's main theoretical result (Theorem 4.2) shows that PG-RLHF achieves suboptimality Õ(√(|A|ε_bias)/(1−γ) + W_A/((1−γ)√T) + W_Q√(βN)/((1−γ)M_SGD^{1/4}) + √(βW_QNd)/((1−γ)√c_MLE·c_base^{1/4}·M_HF^{1/4}) + d/(Nβ(1−γ))), with the reward estimation error scaling as M_HF^{-1/4}. The key practical insight is that the ratio of human queries to total sample complexity is approximately 1/T, meaning RLHF requires only a small fraction of the data budget for human feedback. In experiments on the Bidirectional Lock environment (S=22, A=5, γ=0.9, N=30, K=M_SGD=M_HF=2500), PG-RLHF achieves comparable normalized suboptimality to PC-PG across T∈{50,100,200} while using zero true reward observations and only O(NM_HF) preference queries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares PG-RLHF against PC-PG (Agarwal et al., 2020), the standard policy gradient algorithm that assumes direct access to true rewards. In the theoretical comparison (Table 1), PG-RLHF requires additional Õ(NM_HF/(1−γ)) samples due to the lack of direct reward signals, but since M_HF ≈ M_SGD (same convergence rate), this overhead is negligible relative to the total sample complexity. The critical advantage is that PG-RLHF uses zero true reward observations, replacing them with only O(NM_HF) human preference queries, whose ratio to total samples scales as 1/T. In the Bidirectional Lock experiments (Figure 1), PG-RLHF achieves comparable normalized suboptimality to PC-PG for all tested T values (50, 100, 200), with the gap between the two methods remaining small and the query-to-sample ratio decreasing as T increases, consistent with the theoretical 1/T prediction. The paper thus demonstrates that RLHF does not introduce significant additional hardness in sample complexity compared to standard RL, providing a theoretical explanation for the empirical observation that less than 1% of interaction data needs human annotation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PC-PG policy gradient algorithm (Agarwal et al., 2020), which assumes a known reward function, to the RLHF setting where rewards must be inferred from trajectory-level human preference comparisons under a Bradley-Terry model. Prior theoretical RLHF work (Pacchiano et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2023) has predominantly analyzed value-based algorithms or offline settings, while the paper addresses the policy-based, online, active-feedback regime that mirrors practical systems like ChatGPT. The key technical departure is a trajectory-level elliptical potential analysis that adapts the classical elliptical potential lemma (Abbasi-Yadkori et al., 2011) from state-action-level reward observations to trajectory-summed feature comparisons, enabling reward estimation error bounds under comparison feedback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a rigorous theoretical explanation for the empirically observed efficiency of RLHF: the number of human queries needed scales as O(1/T) relative to total sample complexity, meaning human feedback is a small fraction of the overall data budget. The trajectory-level elliptical potential technique is a reusable analytical tool for any RLHF variant using trajectory comparisons. Limitations include the need for a baseline policy (Assumption 3.4), the Bradley-Terry model assumption, and realizability conditions; the authors note that understanding the structural advantage of preference feedback over numerical reward signals remains an open question. Experimental validation is limited to a single small MDP (Bidirectional Lock) with one baseline comparison.
