## Problem

While double descent is well-characterized for unregularized linear regression, the generalization behavior of Principal Component Regression (PCR) in the overparameterized regime remains poorly understood. In particular, it is unclear how the number of retained principal components k interacts with the feature-to-sample ratio γ to shape the risk curve, and how distribution shift between training and test data affects PCR's regularizing properties.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240612/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes data sampled from a spiked covariance model with d spike eigenvalues (representing a low-dimensional latent manifold) embedded in p-dimensional space, along with a user-chosen truncation k ≤ p for the number of principal components. The output is a closed-form asymptotic risk expression decomposed into squared bias, variance, and irreducible error, where the variance term involves an integral over the Marchenko-Pastur distribution whose lower bound s_c is determined by the ratio (k−d)/(p−d). The framework also handles covariate shift by allowing the spike eigenvalues to differ between training (C_{z,S}) and test (C_{z,T}) distributions while sharing eigenvectors.

## Method

The paper derives asymptotic risk guarantees for PCR by combining random matrix theory results for the spiked covariance model with the latent factor regression framework. The key mechanism is a generalized eigenvector shift product P_k that captures the asymptotic misalignment between population and sample eigenvectors, with a correction factor c_i² for k > d that accounts for noise directions leaking into the signal subspace. The variance term splits into a trace over spike eigenvalue means μ(λ_i, γ) and an integral over the Marchenko-Pastur bulk from s_c to (1+√γ)²; critically, for k ≤ d the integral vanishes, meaning PCR excludes noise directions and avoids the interpolation peak that drives double descent in full regression. The covariate-shift extension (Theorem 4) introduces separate training and test covariance structures into both the bias (via a 2×2 block matrix coupling β_T and β) and variance terms, showing that the shift magnitude is governed by the difference between C_{z,S} and C_{z,T}.

## Results

The paper's main result is a theoretical guarantee that PCR with an appropriately chosen number of principal components k avoids the interpolation peak at γ=1 that plagues full linear regression. Specifically, for k=d (matching the true latent dimension), the asymptotic risk remains bounded across all γ∈(0,∞), while full regression exhibits a divergent variance term at the interpolation threshold. For misspecified k<d, the squared bias term dominates and risk rises sharply; for k≫d, PCR converges to full regression risk as noise directions are included. Under covariate shift, highly correlated training/test eigenvalues (ρ_ℓ=0.9) yield substantially lower risk than low correlation (ρ_ℓ=0.1), and theory matches simulation across 50 realizations with tight 25th–75th quantile bands.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is unregularized full linear regression (k=p), which exhibits the classic double-descent interpolation peak at γ=1. PCR with k=d avoids this peak entirely, maintaining bounded risk across the full γ range, while for k≥d the PCR risk converges to full regression risk in the limits of small and large γ. In the appendix comparison (Figure B-2), PCR with k=d achieves the lowest risk across all γ values compared to ridge regression with various regularization strengths, confirming that spectral truncation is a more effective regularizer than L2 penalty under the spiked covariance model. The null predictor (θ=0) serves as a lower-bound reference; PCR outperforms it for all γ, with the gap widening in the overparameterized regime where full regression degrades. Under covariate shift (Figure 8), PCR with appropriate k again outperforms full regression, which now also carries a non-vanishing asymptotic bias term due to the train-test distribution mismatch.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the asymptotic double descent analysis of Hastie et al. (2022) for ridgeless linear regression to Principal Component Regression, showing that PCR with k=p recovers their result as a special case. It draws on random matrix theory results for the spiked covariance model—specifically the eigenvalue shift (Baik & Silverstein, 2006) and eigenvector misalignment (Paul, 2007)—to derive closed-form asymptotic risk expressions that prior PCR analyses (Xu & Hsu, 2019; Huang et al., 2022) could not provide under realistic sample covariances. The covariate shift analysis builds on Emami et al. (2020) by incorporating distribution shift into the PCR risk decomposition, a setting that remains underexplored in the overparameterization literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal asymptotic guarantee that PCR prevents the interpolation peak of double descent, giving practitioners using PCR in genetics, econometrics, and robotics a theoretical basis for choosing the number of principal components k. The bias-variance decomposition cleanly disentangles the effects of model dimensionality, data structure (spike spectrum), and misspecification (k vs. d), which prior analyses conflated. Key limitations include restriction to linear regressors, the spiked covariance assumption, and the asymptotic regime; extending to general covariance matrices, non-linear latent structures, and finite-sample bounds are identified as important open directions. The qualitative match between theory and the Diverse MAGIC wheat genetics data suggests the framework may generalize beyond the linear spiked model, but this remains unproven.
