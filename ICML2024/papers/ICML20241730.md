## Problem

Governments in the EU, US, and China have rapidly enacted AI regulations that presuppose technical capabilities—such as verifiable model training, comprehensive safety evaluations, and robust privacy guarantees—that do not yet exist in the AI/ML research landscape. This mismatch between regulatory expectations and technical state-of-the-art risks rendering policies unenforceable or counterproductive, while governing institutions simultaneously lack the technical talent to design, operationalise, and enforce these rules effectively.

## Input / Output

The analysis consumes five primary regulatory texts (EU AI Act, US Executive Order 14110, US AI Bill of Rights, and two Chinese provisions on algorithmic recommendations and deep synthesis) and structures the comparison around a four-category taxonomy—Data, Compute, Model, and Output—borrowed from Bommasani et al. (2023). The output is a structured gap analysis mapping each policy requirement to the current SOTA (or absence thereof), followed by prioritised research areas and a taxonomy of part-time and full-time roles through which AI/ML researchers can contribute to governance.

## Method

The paper conducts a systematic comparative review of AI governance documents across three jurisdictions, extracting specific technical requirements (e.g., FLOPs-based systemic risk thresholds, adversarial testing mandates, data provenance obligations, synthetic content detection) and contrasting each against the current state of AI/ML research to identify concrete gaps. For each of the four categories (Data, Compute, Model, Output), the authors cite specific regulatory clauses and pair them with evidence of missing or immature technical tools—such as the absence of methods to verify ongoing model training, the lack of comprehensive safety evaluations for many risk scenarios, and the difficulty of detecting non-watermarked AI-generated content. The paper then synthesises these findings into a research agenda and a discussion of the technical talent gap, drawing on graduation data (less than 1% of AI PhDs entering government) and examples of emerging institutional responses like the UK AI Safety Institute. The contribution is explicitly a position paper: it does not propose new algorithms or empirical results but rather motivates targeted research directions and closer researcher–policymaker integration.

## Results

This is a position paper with no quantitative experiments or results tables. The key finding is a qualitative gap analysis across four technical categories (data, compute, model, output) showing that regulatory requirements in the EU AI Act, US Executive Order 14110, and Chinese AI regulations rely on technical tools that are either underdeveloped or non-existent at current SOTA. Specific gaps identified include: no robust method to verify large-model training (compute), no comprehensive and reliable evaluation protocols for systemic risk (model), no reliable detection of non-watermarked AI-generated content (output), and no universally accepted framework for technical fairness implementation (data). The paper makes two calls to action: prioritise targeted AI/ML research to close these gaps, and increase technical expertise within governing institutions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not propose a method to be compared against baselines. Instead, it performs a comparative analysis of three regulatory frameworks (EU AI Act, US E.O. 14110/AI Bill of Rights, Chinese algorithmic recommendation and deep synthesis provisions) against the current state of the art in AI/ML research. The 'comparison' is between policy aspirations and technical feasibility: for example, the EU's 10^25 FLOPs threshold for systemic risk has no corresponding verification mechanism, the US requirement for red-team testing results lacks standardised protocols, and China's mandate to prevent transmission of 'negative information' has no robust technical enforcement. The paper does not benchmark any proposed solution against alternatives but rather identifies where existing research is insufficient to support enacted or proposed regulations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper carves new ground by systematically mapping the technical feasibility gaps between AI governance policy documents (EU AI Act, US E.O. 14110, US AI Bill of Rights, Chinese algorithmic and deep-synthesis provisions) and the current state of the art in AI/ML research, organized along the data/compute/model/output taxonomy from Bommasani et al. (2023). It builds conceptually on prior analyses of regulatory feasibility (Guha et al., 2023; Bommasani et al., 2023) and on the broader AI safety evaluation literature (Weidinger et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2023), but departs from them by performing a cross-jurisdictional comparison and explicitly framing the gap as a call for targeted research and talent integration rather than a purely legal or policy critique. The paper also draws on regulatory theory (Ayres & Braithwaite, 1992; Dal Bó, 2006) to argue that poorly grounded technical requirements risk regulatory capture and counterproductive outcomes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies concrete, actionable research gaps—such as the absence of methods to verify large-model training, the lack of comprehensive and reliable safety evaluations, the difficulty of detecting non-watermarked synthetic content, and the vulnerability of LLMs to privacy-extracting attacks—that directly constrain the enforceability of current and upcoming regulations. Its two-part call (targeted research + talent integration) provides a structured agenda for the AI/ML community to engage with governance, though the research directions remain high-level and the paper explicitly disclaims being a comprehensive roadmap. Open questions include how to operationalise the FLOPs-based systemic-risk threshold in practice, how to build independent third-party auditing capacity, and how to address the structural pay and career incentives that keep technical talent out of public-sector governance roles.
