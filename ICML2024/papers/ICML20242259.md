## Problem

Most AI fairness research examines bias along a single sensitive attribute, yet individuals possess multi-dimensional identities and the most severe discrimination often emerges at the intersection of multiple attributes. Exhaustive enumeration of all attribute combinations is computationally infeasible (exponential in the number of attributes), and combinatorial search methods discover only a narrow, non-diverse set of biased subgroups that cannot generalize to unseen combinations. The paper asks: can we automatically and efficiently discover diverse, high-bias intersectional sensitive attributes, including ones never observed in the training data?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242259/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a labeled dataset where each sample carries an input X (face image or text comment), a binary label Y, and a binary vector A of 20–25 sensitive attributes (e.g., gender, race, religion, sexual orientation, disability), along with a pre-trained predictive model f(x) and a bias threshold τ. It outputs a set of generated intersectional sensitive attribute vectors a whose predicted bias value L̂_f(a) ≥ τ, including combinations that never appeared in either the observation or holdout splits. The attribute space is 2^20 (CelebA) or 2^25 (Toxic), and the bias value for each a is the mean prediction loss of f over all samples sharing that exact attribute combination.

## Method

BGGN is a VAE-based generative model over discrete sensitive-attribute vectors that, instead of matching the empirical data distribution p_data(a), is trained to sample from a bias-weighted distribution p̃_data(a) ∝ exp{L_f(a)}. To avoid sampling from this unknown normalized distribution, the authors derive a reverse-KL training objective that decomposes into maximizing a per-sample reward r(a) = log q_φ(z|a) + L_f(a) with an entropy penalty on the generator—structurally analogous to RLHF. Training is two-stage: first, a vanilla VAE is pre-trained on p_data(a) and a separate bias-value predictor L̂_f(a) (a transformer or MLP regressor) is fitted to approximate the true prediction loss; second, the generator is fine-tuned via REINFORCE with a closed-form baseline to maximize the bias-guided reward. This design lets BGGN efficiently produce diverse, high-bias attribute vectors and, crucially, generalize to unseen combinations that combinatorial search cannot reach.

## Results

On both CelebA (20 binary sensitive attributes, 2^20 possible combinations) and Toxic Comments (25 binary sensitive attributes, 2^25 possible combinations), BGGN consistently outperforms both combinatorial search baselines and a regular VAE across all six evaluation metrics (Bias Number, Bias Ratio, Precision@K, Recall@K, Avg DCG@K, RR@K Score) at bias threshold τ=0.3. On CelebA, the ground-truth high-bias ratio is ~25% (1945/7864 in Observation, 861/3370 in Holdout); on Toxic, it is ~16–19% (564/3442 in Observation, 275/1474 in Holdout). BGGN's generated samples shift the bias-value distribution toward higher values (mean loss ~1.43 for generated toxic texts vs. ~0.2 dataset mean; ~0.62 for generated CelebA images vs. ~0.15 dataset mean), confirming efficient discovery of high-bias intersectional attributes. On the Holdout set (no attribute overlap with training), BGGN still discovers new high-bias subgroups, whereas the Search Tree algorithm fails entirely.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares BGGN against three baselines: (1) Search Tree, (2) Relaxed Search Tree (with N_re=1), and (3) a regular VAE trained on p_data(a). Against the Search Tree, BGGN discovers substantially more diverse high-bias intersectional attributes; the Search Tree identifies only a handful of biased subgroups (e.g., 4 high-bias a at τ=0.3 on Toxic) and cannot generalize to unseen attributes in the Holdout set. The Relaxed Search Tree (N_re=1) finds more attributes (16 on Toxic) by filling incomplete paths via exhaustive enumeration, but its results lack diversity and respond poorly to high bias values; increasing N_re to 5 yields 107 attributes but at significant computational cost. Against the regular VAE, BGGN wins decisively on all IR-based metrics: the VAE faithfully reproduces p_data(a) where the majority of samples fall in the low-bias region, yielding a low Bias Ratio and poor Precision@K/Recall@K, while BGGN's bias-guided objective (p_θ(a) ∝ exp{L_f(a)}) concentrates generation on high-bias subgroups, achieving higher Bias Ratio, Precision@K, Recall@K, Avg DCG@K, and RR@K Score on both Observation and Holdout splits for both datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the VAE framework (Kingma et al., 2019) and repurposes it for a fundamentally different objective: rather than matching the empirical data distribution p_data(a), the generative model is trained to match a bias-weighted distribution proportional to exp{L_f(a)}, effectively treating prediction loss as a reward signal. The training objective is closely analogous to RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022), where the policy gradient (REINFORCE) with a learned baseline replaces the standard ELBO maximization. Conceptually, the paper departs from the intersectional fairness literature (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018; Roy et al., 2023), which typically examines a small fixed set of attribute combinations, by formulating discovery over an exponentially large space of intersectional sensitive attributes as a generative process rather than an enumeration or combinatorial search problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: as the number of sensitive attributes grows, exhaustive enumeration of intersectional subgroups becomes intractable, yet most fairness audits still rely on manually selected attribute pairs. BGGN offers a scalable, automated alternative that can also surface unseen but potentially biased subgroups, validated through a creative pipeline that feeds generated attribute vectors as prompts to LLMs and image generators. Key limitations include the reliance on prediction loss as the sole fairness criterion (incompatible with demographic parity or equalized odds), training stability concerns of the RL-style fine-tuning, and the fact that the method discovers but does not mitigate bias. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to continuous or ordinal sensitive attributes, whether diffusion-based generators (as the authors suggest) would improve stability, and how the discovered subgroups could feed into principled mitigation strategies.
