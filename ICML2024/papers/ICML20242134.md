## Problem

Prior work on resource-efficient machine learning has focused on single-task scenarios (e.g., reducing model size or improving per-task throughput), leaving the common real-world case of multiple time-constrained learning tasks competing for a shared computational budget largely unexplored. The central difficulty is the coupling between learning progress and resource allocation: the loss curve of each task is unknown and must be estimated from early data, and allocation decisions based on imperfect predictions compound over time, potentially misdirecting resources from feasible tasks to infeasible ones.

## Input / Output

At each discrete time unit (one second in experiments), the learner observes the current training loss and cumulative data volume for every active thread, along with each thread's deadline, per-unit data budget N_k, and success threshold ε_k. The output is a continuous allocation vector {η_{k,t}} for the current time unit satisfying Σ η_{k,t} ≤ 1, which determines how many data points (η_{k,t}·N_k) each thread processes. The allocation must be computed in real time—under 1 ms for 100 threads—so that it does not interfere with GPU-based model training running in parallel on the CPU.

## Method

LARA operates in three coupled stages per time unit. First, a weighted regularized least-squares (WLS) online estimator fits a negative power function ℓ_k(s)=a_k·s^{−b_k} to the log-transformed loss curve, using a discount factor γ to prioritize recent observations; the closed-form solution is updated in one-pass O(1) time without matrix inversion, yielding a predicted remaining data requirement Ŝ_{k,n}. Second, a parameter-reduction lemma (Lemma 1) shows the optimal allocation can be represented by K_τ time-invariant parameters rather than K_τ·T variables, and an adaptive binary-tree search (Algorithm 1) finds the optimal subset of threads to complete in O(K_τ) time by iteratively adding threads and evicting the most resource-demanding one when the budget is exceeded. Third, an Explore-then-Exploit (ETE) balancing strategy allocates resources uniformly to any thread whose cumulative processed data falls below a threshold H_k, ensuring sufficient observations for accurate loss-curve extrapolation before committing to exploitation, thereby mitigating the compounding errors that arise when early predictions are unreliable.

## Results

On the pure task bundle (5 models, CIFAR-10), LARA completes 4 out of 5 threads, while Uniform Allocation and Shortest Thread First complete 0, and Least Resources First / Easiest Thread First complete only 2. On the mixed task bundle (10 threads across CV, NLP, RL, Audio), LARA completes 8/10 threads under ε_k^1 (vs. 6 for UA, 2 for STF, 4 for LRF/ETF) and 6/10 under ε_k^2 (vs. 3 for UA, 5 for STF). In the scalability experiment on MNIST, LARA consistently outperforms all baselines as thread count grows from 10 to 100, with a single prediction-and-allocation round taking only 0.0007 s even at 100 threads. For the prediction component, the proposed Weighted Least Squares (WLS) estimator achieves lower average prediction error than standard Least Squares and comparable error to the M4 estimator and Engression, while incurring significantly lower per-round time cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the four resource allocation baselines, LARA wins decisively: it completes 4/5 threads on the pure bundle where UA and STF fail entirely (0/5) and LRF/ETF manage only 2/5; on the harder mixed bundle (ε_k^1) LARA completes 8/10 versus 6 for UA, 2 for STF, and 4 for LRF/ETF. On the ε_k^2 variant, STF (5/10) slightly edges LARA (6/10) is actually lower — LARA still leads at 6 vs. STF's 5. For the prediction sub-component, the paper benchmarks WLS against the M4 estimator (Alabdulmohsin et al., NeurIPS 2022) and Engression (Shen & Meinshausen, 2023): on the pure bundle WLS achieves lower average prediction error than both extrapolation-specific methods, while on the mixed bundle WLS is slightly worse than Engression but still substantially better than standard Least Squares. Crucially, WLS runs orders of magnitude faster per round than M4 and Engression, making it the only method practical for the high-frequency (every-second) re-estimation that CoRE-Learning demands.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the first practical implementation of the CoRE-Learning paradigm formally proposed by Zhou (2023), which defined the problem of managing multiple time-constrained learning tasks under budgeted computational resources. Methodologically, it draws on three distinct threads: the weighted RLS estimator from adaptive control (Guo et al., 1993) for online loss-curve fitting, the Explore-Then-Exploit strategy from bandit theory (Lattimore & Szepesvári, 2020) for prediction-allocation balancing, and a novel adaptive binary tree search for the combinatorial allocation subproblem. The paper explicitly departs from distributed ML (which assumes sufficient resources for a single coordinated model), tiny/edge ML (which assumes known learning progress and lacks deadlines), and OS-level cluster schedulers (which rely on pre-training estimates and optimize different objectives), positioning its contribution as the first to handle the coupling between unknown learning progress and real-time resource allocation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first end-to-end, theoretically grounded solution to a problem that is increasingly relevant as shared compute facilities and budget-constrained cloud users must juggle many concurrent training tasks with hard deadlines. The optimality guarantee for the allocation subproblem (reducing O(2^K) search to O(K)) is a clean theoretical contribution, and the empirical results across diverse modalities (CV, NLP, RL, audio) demonstrate practical robustness. Key open questions remain: the ETE exploration threshold requires empirical tuning (UCB and Thompson sampling are noted as future directions), the WLS predictor is an interpolation method used for extrapolation (dedicated extrapolation-specific strategies are left for future work), and no generalization or learnability guarantees are provided for the overall prediction-allocation loop. The paper is explicitly preliminary, and the coupling between prediction error and allocation error—while identified—lacks a formal error-bound analysis.
