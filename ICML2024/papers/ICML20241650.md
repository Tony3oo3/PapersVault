## Problem

Transfer learning is widely practiced but lacks a unified theoretical framework that explains when it is actually feasible across different paradigms (supervised, reinforcement, imitation learning). Common heuristics such as sparse mechanism shift and freezing entire network layers are assumed to enable transfer, but no principled analysis exists to determine when they succeed or fail. This paper asks: given a structural causal model with constrained parameter domains, can we formally determine whether learning from prior environments reduces the hypothesis class for a new environment?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241650/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of environments where each environment's parameters are drawn from constrained domains of a shared SCM, along with the causal graph structure and any prior knowledge about which parameters are constant or restricted. The key output is not a single parameter estimate but a constrained domain Ŵ_{Q|E} (a set of possible values for the target conditional's parameters), computed via a computation tree that identifies the minimal set of atomic parameters (the 'scope') actually needed. This domain estimate then determines transfer feasibility by comparing its complexity to the initial unconstrained hypothesis class, and directly specifies which parameters to freeze or re-learn in a new environment.

## Method

The framework models a 'world' as an SCM whose parameters are drawn from constrained domains, with each environment corresponding to a specific parameter selection. The core technical contribution is a top-down computation tree (Theorem 4.2) that expresses the target conditional w_{Q|E} as a formula over a minimal set of atomic generators w_i* using three operations (product, sum-out, conditioning), where each atomic parameter appears at most once in reverse topological order. From this tree, Corollary 4.3 shows the constrained domain W_{Q|E} can be computed as the image of the product of atomic constrained domains under the computation formula. Algorithm 1 then provides a meta-algorithm: given parameter estimates from prior environments, it learns a parameterized constraint (e.g., a row space or subspace) that captures the structure of W_{Q|E}, enabling the learner to restrict its hypothesis class in new environments. Propagation properties (left/right propagatable operations) allow analyzing how constraints on individual parameters flow through the network without knowing the exact computation formula, which is used to show that freezing an entire layer can fail while freezing only a subset of its parameters (e.g., linear coefficients but not biases) succeeds.

## Results

In the Complex Colored MNIST case study (Ex. 5.3), the proposed transfer strategy reduces the number of parameters to learn from 47,100 (47040+20+40) to just 60 (20+40) by freezing the linear coefficient of the first layer. Empirically (Fig. 5), freezing only the first layer's linear coefficient achieves the best transfer performance in Environment 2, substantially outperforming both the no-transfer baseline and the standard practice of freezing the entire first layer. The theoretical results further establish that sparse mechanism shift (SMS) is neither necessary nor sufficient for transfer feasibility, and that the standard heuristic of freezing an entire network layer can lead to poor transfer when the true invariant structure is finer-grained than a full layer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods but instead contrasts four transfer strategies on the Complex Colored MNIST task: (1) no transfer (train from scratch in Env. 2), (2) freezing the entire first layer, (3) freezing only the first layer's linear coefficient (the strategy derived by the SCM framework), and (4) a random-transfer baseline (freezing the linear coefficient to a random value). The framework-recommended strategy (freezing the linear coefficient) achieves the best accuracy in Env. 2, while freezing the entire first layer performs poorly because the label distribution shifts substantially between environments (correlation between digit and label changes from 0.1 to 1.0). The no-transfer baseline eventually catches up when sufficient Env. 2 data is available (beyond ~5000 samples), but the transfer strategy converges faster. The random-transfer baseline confirms that the learned coefficient value matters, not just the act of freezing. These results demonstrate that the SCM-derived constraint propagation identifies a strictly more informative subset of parameters to freeze than the coarse-grained 'freeze a layer' heuristic.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the SCM-based formalism of Pearl et al. (2000) and the world/environment abstraction of Buesing et al. (2019) into a unified transfer learning framework grounded in the Independent Causal Mechanisms principle from Schölkopf et al. (2021). It deliberately departs from the Sparse Mechanism Shift hypothesis that underpins much of the causal representation learning literature, and separates the 'world update' problem (estimating constrained parameter domains) from the invariant predictor selection problem studied by Peters et al. (2015), Arjovsky et al. (2019), and Zhang et al. (2020). Unlike prior causal transfer work (Pearl & Bareinboim 2011, Chen & Bühlmann 2021), the framework does not assume linear SCMs, causal faithfulness, or sufficiency, and provides a general computation-tree procedure for propagating local parameter constraints to global ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, unified lens for analyzing when transfer is feasible across supervised, RL, and imitation learning settings, and yields actionable guidance on which model components to freeze or transfer. Its challenge to two widely-held heuristics—SMS sufficiency and whole-layer freezing—could redirect design choices in practical transfer pipelines. Key limitations include the unique-description assumption (Assumption 3.4) in the main theoretical results, the deliberately simple (non-state-of-the-art) constraint learning algorithm, and empirical validation restricted to a single synthetic dataset (Complex Colored MNIST). Open questions include extending the framework to non-atomic domain knowledge, incorporating computational complexity measures for RL, and scaling the computation-tree approach to large architectures.
