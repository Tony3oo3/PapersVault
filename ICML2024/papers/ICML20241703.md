## Problem

The p-Wasserstein distance (p>1) is highly sensitive to minor geometric differences between distributions, making it a powerful dissimilarity metric, but this same sensitivity causes small outlier masses or sampling discrepancies to disproportionately inflate the distance. Existing partial p-Wasserstein distances mitigate outliers but fail to satisfy the triangle inequality and lose sensitivity to small geometric differences, while truncated-metric approaches like λ-ROBOT remain vulnerable to both noise types. The paper asks whether one can design a metric that retains the geometric sensitivity of p-Wasserstein while being provably robust to both outlier and sampling noise.

## Input / Output

The metric takes two probability distributions over a metric space with unit diameter (or bounded diameter Δ) along with parameters p≥1 and k≥0, and returns a single scalar distance value in [0,1]. In the image retrieval application, each image is treated as a discrete distribution over pixel intensities (grayscale for MNIST, RGB channels for CIFAR-10 and COREL), and the distance between a query image distribution and each database image distribution is computed to produce a ranked list of the top-m most similar images. The distance is computed via the OT-profile: it equals 1−x* where (x*, y*) is the intersection of the OT-profile curve with the line y=k(1−x).

## Method

The (p,k)-RPW metric is defined as Π_{p,k}(μ,ν) = inf{ε ≥ 0 | W_{p,1−ε}(μ,ν) ≤ kε}, i.e., the smallest mass ε one may leave untransported such that the cost of transporting the remaining (1−ε) mass is at most kε. This construction interpolates between total variation (k=0) and p-Wasserstein (k→∞), and for p=∞, k=1 recovers the Lévy-Prokhorov distance. The key theoretical results are: (1) the metric satisfies the triangle inequality via a common-mass argument on intermediate transport plans; (2) adding an outlier mass δ changes the distance by at most δ (vs. δ^{1/p} for p-Wasserstein); and (3) in d dimensions with p>d/2, the empirical distance converges at rate Õ(n^{−p/((d+2)p−d)}), faster than the p-Wasserstein rate of Õ(n^{−1/(2p)}). Two approximation algorithms are provided: a binary-search method using a standard OT solver in O(n³ log n log δ⁻¹) time, and an LMR-based OT-profile approximation in O(n²/δ^p + n/δ^{2p}) time.

## Results

On image retrieval tasks (MNIST, CIFAR-10, COREL), the (2,1)-RPW and (2,0.1)-RPW distances achieve higher retrieval accuracy than 1-Wasserstein, 2-Wasserstein, and TV distances across all three perturbation scenarios (noise, shift, and combined noise+shift). On MNIST with combined noise and shift, (2,1)-RPW outperforms all competing distances, while on CIFAR-10 (where natural image variation is high), (2,0.1)-RPW outperforms 1-Wasserstein. In convergence rate experiments on synthetic 2D distributions, the empirical (2,1)-RPW converges to 0 significantly faster than the 2-Wasserstein distance, consistent with the theoretical rate of Õ(n^{-1/3}) versus Õ(n^{-1/4}) for 2-Wasserstein.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares (2,1)-RPW and (2,0.1)-RPW against three baselines: 1-Wasserstein, 2-Wasserstein, and total variation (TV) distance. On MNIST with noise (scenario i), (2,1)-RPW outperforms 1-Wasserstein while both are more robust than 2-Wasserstein, which is distorted by noise. On MNIST with shift (scenario ii), TV distance drops significantly due to its sensitivity to spatial shifts, while (2,1)-RPW and 2-Wasserstein handle shifts effectively. On MNIST with combined noise and shift (scenario iii), (2,1)-RPW outperforms all three baselines, as it inherits robustness to noise from TV and sensitivity to geometric differences from 2-Wasserstein. On CIFAR-10, where same-label images already exhibit significant natural variation, 2-Wasserstein and TV underperform 1-Wasserstein, but (2,0.1)-RPW (with a smaller k parameter) outperforms 1-Wasserstein. In convergence experiments, (2,1)-RPW converges faster than 2-Wasserstein on both 2-point and grid distributions, while (2,0.1)-RPW tracks TV distance and (2,10)-RPW tracks (1/10)·W₂, confirming the interpolation property.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the OT-profile framework of Phatak et al. (2022), which maps each α ∈ [0,1] to the α-partial p-Wasserstein distance, and on the LMR algorithm of Lahn et al. (2019) for approximating that profile. It departs from prior partial-OT-based outlier-robustness work (Chapel et al., 2020; Le et al., 2021; Nietert et al., 2022) by showing that the (1−δ)-partial p-Wasserstein distance fails as a general-purpose dissimilarity metric because it violates the triangle inequality and collapses to zero for distributions differing by less than δ mass. The (p,k)-RPW metric resolves both issues by defining the distance as the intersection of the OT-profile with the line y = k(1−x), yielding a true metric that interpolates between total variation (k=0) and p-Wasserstein (k→∞), and coincides with the Lévy-Prokhorov distance at (p,k)=(∞,1).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The convergence-rate improvement from Õ(n^{−1/2p}) to Õ(n^{−p/(4p−2)}) for p > d/2 is practically significant: for p=2 in 2D it goes from n^{−1/4} to n^{−1/3}, meaning substantially fewer samples are needed for accurate estimation, which directly affects the viability of 2-Wasserstein as a GAN loss or barycenter objective. The metric property (satisfying the triangle inequality) opens the door to clustering, barycenter computation, and nearest-neighbor search over distributions—tasks where partial OT alone was inapplicable. Open questions flagged by the authors include whether the metric scales as a practical loss in generative models, whether parallel approximation algorithms exist, and how the parameter k should be chosen in practice beyond the small-scale image-retrieval experiments presented.
