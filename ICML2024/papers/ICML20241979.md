## Problem

Several popular linear time series forecasting models—DLinear, FITS, RLinear, NLinear—have been proposed with distinct architectural motivations (trend/seasonal decomposition, Fourier-space processing, instance normalization), yet no prior work has rigorously characterized whether these architectures actually express different function classes. This paper asks: are these models functionally distinct, or are they all equivalent to (weakly constrained) linear regression?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241979/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each model consumes a univariate context vector x ∈ R^L (L historical time steps, L=720 in experiments) and produces a forecast vector y ∈ R^T (T future steps, T ∈ {96, 192, 336, 720}). The analysis is conducted in the univariate (c=1) setting for clarity, though the models are applied per-channel in multivariate experiments. A key structural detail is that FITS outputs both a reconstruction of the context and the forecast in a single (L+T)-dimensional vector, from which the forecast is extracted by discarding the first L components.

## Method

The paper proves that DLinear, FITS (when L ≥ T−2 without low-pass filtering), and standard Linear all express exactly the same model class: all affine maps Ax + b. It then shows that instance normalization variants impose specific algebraic constraints—rows of the weight matrix must sum to 1, and for IN/RevIN the bias is coupled to the instance standard deviation σ(x)—yielding only three distinct model classes in total. Crucially, each class is reformulated as unconstrained linear regression on a suitably augmented feature set, admitting a closed-form OLS solution. The paper further identifies that FITS's Fourier parameterization inadvertently scales the effective learning rate on the bias term by ~1/L, explaining its empirically smaller bias. Experiments across 8 datasets confirm all models converge to near-identical weight matrices, and the closed-form OLS solution outperforms SGD-trained variants in 72% of dataset-horizon settings.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative finding is that the closed-form OLS solution outperforms all SGD-trained linear model variants in 72% of test settings (23 out of 32 dataset-horizon combinations) across 8 benchmark datasets. All models (FITS+IN, DLinear+IN, RLinear, NLinear) converge to nearly identical weight matrices during training, with cosine similarity approaching 1, confirming their theoretical equivalence. The OLS+IN approach achieves the best MSE in most settings, e.g., 0.375 on ETTh1 (T=96) vs. 0.377 for FITS+IN and 0.379 for DLinear+IN, and 0.380 on ETTh2 (T=720) vs. 0.377 for FITS+IN. On larger datasets (ECL, Traffic, Weather), all methods perform nearly identically, suggesting linear capacity is the limiting factor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FITS (Xu et al., 2023), OLS+IN achieves lower MSE in the majority of settings, with the gap widening on larger datasets (e.g., ETTh2 T=720: 0.380 vs. 0.377, where FITS+IN actually wins due to its inadvertently regularised bias term). Against DLinear and NLinear (Zeng et al., 2023), OLS+IN consistently matches or beats both, with NLinear typically the weakest of the IN variants (e.g., ETTm1 T=96: 0.319 vs. 0.307 for OLS+IN). Against RLinear (Li et al., 2023), OLS+IN wins in most settings, though differences are often within one standard deviation. Against deep models (Table 3), OLS+IN outperforms iTransformer (Liu et al., 2023) across nearly all settings (e.g., ETTh2 T=720: 0.380 vs. 0.431) and is competitive with PatchTST (Nie et al., 2022), while DAM (Darlow et al., 2024) occasionally edges out OLS+IN on the small ETTh datasets (e.g., ETTh1 T=720: 0.421 vs. 0.460) but is substantially worse on ECL and Traffic. The key takeaway is that the architectural differences between these popular linear models are functionally negligible, and the closed-form OLS solution is the simplest and most reliable approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not propose a new forecasting architecture but instead provides a rigorous mathematical analysis of several recently introduced linear models: DLinear and NLinear (Zeng et al., 2023), RLinear (Li et al., 2023), and FITS (Xu et al., 2023), along with the RevIN normalisation of Kim et al. (2021). It carves new ground by proving that these architecturally distinct models—trend/seasonal decomposition, Fourier-space linear mapping, and various instance normalisations—induce the same (or nearly the same) parametric function class as unconstrained linear regression. The paper thus repurposes the existing linear regression literature (closed-form OLS solutions, convexity arguments) to reframe what the community has been treating as meaningfully different model families.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SoTA linear forecasting models are functionally indistinguishable from plain linear regression challenges the implicit assumption that architectural novelty (Fourier transforms, decomposition, specific normalisation schemes) yields a richer model class, and suggests that much recent effort on 'modernising' linear models was addressing optimisation rather than expressiveness. Practically, it implies that a closed-form OLS solution with appropriate feature augmentation is a strong default, reducing the need for iterative training and hyperparameter tuning. Limitations include the restriction to univariate/channel-independent settings, MSE loss, and the absence of non-linear or cross-channel interactions; open questions include whether analogous equivalences hold for non-linear architectures and whether the OLS advantage persists under distribution shift or with more complex loss functions.
