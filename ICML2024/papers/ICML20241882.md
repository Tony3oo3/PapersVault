## Problem

Current AI alignment techniques, particularly RLHF, optimize for a single averaged human preference, treating the wide variation in human values as noise rather than signal. There is no formal framework for defining what it means for a model to be 'pluralistic' or for designing benchmarks that measure pluralism across multiple objectives and populations. This paper addresses both gaps by formalizing concrete definitions of pluralism and providing empirical evidence that standard alignment procedures actively reduce distributional pluralism.

## Input / Output

The empirical evaluation feeds multiple-choice questions from GlobalOpinionQA (741 cross-national survey items) and the Machine Personality Inventory (120 personality-trait items) into pre- and post-aligned LLMs, extracting next-token logits for each answer choice via in-context learning with randomized exemplar answers (averaged over 5 prompts to remove positional bias). The model's resulting probability distribution over answer choices is compared to nationally representative human population distributions (US, Japan, Germany for GlobalQA; global for MPI) using Jensen-Shannon distance, with entropy reported as a secondary diagnostic of distributional spread.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a formal taxonomy with three model-level definitions of pluralism—Overton pluralism (outputting the full set of reasonable answers within an Overton window), steerable pluralism (faithfully reflecting a specified attribute or perspective when conditioned on it), and distributional pluralism (matching a target population's answer distribution)—paired with three benchmark types: multi-objective benchmarks reporting Pareto-optimal frontiers, trade-off steerable benchmarks measuring a model's ability to shift across commensurating functions at inference, and jury-pluralistic benchmarks that explicitly model individual annotator utilities combined via social welfare functions (e.g., sweeping α from Utilitarian to Rawlsian). Empirically, the authors compute Jensen-Shannon distance between model next-token distributions and human population distributions across LLaMA, LLaMA2, Gemma, and GPT-3 (pre- vs. post-aligned), finding that alignment consistently increases JSD and reduces entropy, indicating that RLHF and instruction fine-tuning concentrate probability mass and move models away from the diversity of human opinion.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that post-aligned LLMs exhibit higher Jensen-Shannon distance (i.e., less similarity) to target human population distributions than their pre-aligned counterparts, across nearly all model classes and datasets. On GlobalOpinionQA (Japan, US, Germany) and the Machine Personality Inventory (MPI), pre-aligned models consistently achieve lower JSD values — e.g., LLaMA2-7B drops from 0.43 (US) to 0.56 (post), Gemma-7B from 0.36 (US) to 0.52 (post), and LLaMA from 0.22 to 0.32–0.48 on MPI. The sole exception is GPT-3 on MPI (0.60 pre vs. 0.44 post), which the authors flag as uncertain due to model availability issues. A consistent post-alignment reduction in output entropy is also observed, supporting the hypothesis that standard alignment procedures reduce distributional pluralism.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares pre-aligned (base) models against their post-aligned (RLHF or instruction-tuned) counterparts across five model families. LLaMA (pre) outperforms both Alpaca and Tulu (post) on all four dataset splits, with JSD increasing by 0.03–0.26 (e.g., MPI: 0.22 → 0.32/0.48). LLaMA2-7B (pre) beats LLaMA2-Chat-7B (post) on every split, with the largest gap on MPI (0.37 → 0.51, +0.14). LLaMA2-13B (pre) similarly dominates its post-aligned version (e.g., GlobalQA US: 0.37 → 0.53). Gemma-7B (pre) shows the most dramatic degradation on MPI (0.29 → 0.56, +0.27) and consistent losses on GlobalQA. GPT-3 (davinci-002) is the only case where the post-aligned model (GPT-3.5-turbo) achieves a lower JSD on MPI (0.44 vs. 0.60), but the authors caution this result is unreliable due to uncertainty about the exact alignment procedure applied to GPT-3.5-turbo.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper unifies and extends a scattered body of work on model bias, value alignment, and democratic AI under a single formal taxonomy of pluralism. It builds conceptually on Santurkar et al. (2023) and Durmus et al. (2023), who measured how LLMs reflect (or fail to reflect) population opinion distributions, but reframes these as instances of 'distributional pluralism' and adds two previously unformalized dimensions (Overton and Steerable pluralism). The benchmark formalizations draw on multi-objective RL (Pareto frontiers, hypervolume indicators), social choice theory (Rawlsian/Utilitarian welfare functions), and jury learning (Gordon et al., 2022), synthesizing them into a coherent evaluation framework for pluralistic AI.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first unified formal vocabulary for discussing pluralism in AI alignment, which the authors argue is a prerequisite for principled progress beyond single-objective RLHF. The empirical finding that alignment consistently reduces distributional pluralism across LLaMA, LLaMA2, Gemma, and GPT-3 model classes (with the sole exception being ambiguous GPT-3 results) gives concrete motivation for the argument, though the evidence is limited to two multiple-choice datasets. Key open questions remain: how to operationalize Overton windows at scale, whether a single model can simultaneously satisfy multiple pluralism criteria, and how to design alignment procedures that preserve or increase distributional calibration. The paper explicitly acknowledges that its definitions are 'hard to operationalize' and that the empirical scope is a first step requiring large-scale validation.
