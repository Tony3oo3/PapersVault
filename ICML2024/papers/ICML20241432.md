## Problem

Training large language models demands substantial computational resources, and while network growing algorithms offer potential cost savings, their underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This paper addresses two gaps: the lack of a practical, well-motivated method for initializing large networks from pre-trained smaller ones, and the absence of a theoretical framework explaining the optimization dynamics that occur immediately after a mid-training growth operation.

## Input / Output

The method takes two (or more) pre-trained T5 transformer models of the same architecture (e.g., two T5-SMALL models trained on C4) and produces a single wider fused model (e.g., T5-MEDIUM) whose hidden representations are the concatenation of the sub-models' representations along the feature dimension. The fused model is then further trained on the same text corpus, and its outputs are averaged logits from the constituent sub-models. For downstream evaluation, the fused models are fine-tuned on GLUE tasks, producing standard classification and regression scores.

## Method

Deep Fusion combines pre-trained smaller networks into a single wider model by placing each sub-model's weight matrices in diagonal blocks of a larger matrix with zero off-diagonal entries, and concatenating biases, so that the fused layer's output is the concatenation of the sub-models' outputs. After fusion, the zero-initialized cross-connection parameters are the key to unlocking additional capacity: as training proceeds, these parameters learn to leverage hidden representations from other sub-models. The paper's theoretical contribution uses Backward Error Analysis to show that the two consecutive optimization steps surrounding the fusion (small-model step followed by big-model step) are governed by a modified equation containing a Lie bracket term [∇L₁, ∇L₂] that is non-zero only in the zero-initialized parameter dimensions, acting as a correction mechanism that transfers information from the submanifold of identical sub-models into the full parameter space. The analysis reveals that higher post-fusion learning rates amplify this Lie bracket effect, which empirically corresponds to faster unlocking of the extra parameters and better final performance, while lower learning rates leave the zero-initialized parameters unable to catch up in magnitude.

## Results

On T5-MEDIUM pretraining (C4), self-fusion matches the baseline's 67.2% validation accuracy while reducing compute from 71.2h to 58.4h (18% savings). On the GLUE benchmark, a T5-MEDIUM created via self-fusion of two T5-SMALL models pretrained for only 500K steps achieves 84.94 average GLUE score, slightly exceeding the baseline T5-MEDIUM pretrained for 1M steps (84.74), while using 24% less total TPU time (29.9h vs 39.2h). For T5-L (1.1B params), a single self-fusion step reduces training time by 20% (197.9h vs 246.7h) at matched accuracy (70.57%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a T5-MEDIUM trained from random initialization for the full budget. Self-fusion matches the baseline's pretraining accuracy (67.2%) with 18% less compute, and on GLUE fine-tuning it actually surpasses the baseline (84.94 vs 84.74) while using 24% less total TPU time. A smaller T5-SMALL trained for 1M steps scores only 80.28 on GLUE, confirming that the extra parameters from self-fusion are more beneficial than extended pretraining of a smaller model (81.01 for self-fused T5-SMALL at 500K steps vs 80.28 for T5-SMALL at 1M steps). Among fusion variants, fusion-prop (preserving the fusion property) slightly outperforms fusion-rule (preserving the fusion rule) on pretraining accuracy (67.25 vs 66.88), while self-fusion is competitive with both (67.20) at lower cost. For T5-L, repeated regular fusions (setting 4) achieve the best accuracy (70.74) but at high cost, whereas a single self-fusion (setting 5) matches the baseline accuracy (70.57) with 20% less compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Backward Error Analysis (BEA) framework from continual learning (Dherin, 2023) and implicit gradient regularization (Barrett and Dherin, 2021) to the setting of mid-training network growth, using the Lie bracket of gradient vector fields to characterize the inductive bias introduced by a fusion step. It positions itself against staged training (Shen et al., 2022) by arguing that preserving training dynamics across the growth boundary is suboptimal, and instead advocates for high learning rate cycles post-fusion to unlock the capacity of newly added zero-initialized parameters. The fusion operator itself draws on ideas from progressive networks and network morphism but differs by explicitly promoting wide over-parameterization through diagonal-block weight concatenation rather than preserving exact input-output behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 18–24% compute reduction while matching or slightly exceeding baseline performance on T5 models is a practical win for teams training large transformers on constrained budgets, and the self-fusion variant (fusing a model with itself) simplifies the pipeline by eliminating the need to train two separate small models. The BEA framework provides the first principled explanation for why post-fusion learning rate matters, predicting that higher rates amplify the Lie bracket term that activates zero-initialized cross-parameters. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to fusing models trained on different data, with different architectures (e.g., attention-heavy vs. MLP-heavy), or at different sequence lengths, and whether the gains persist at much larger scales beyond T5-L.
