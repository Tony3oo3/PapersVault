## Problem

Standard RLHF learns a Bradley-Terry reward model from pairwise preferences, but this approach cannot represent non-transitive preferences, is sensitive to the data distribution used for training, and maximizing the learned Elo score does not always align with maximizing the probability of winning against a population of opponents. The paper asks whether one can bypass the reward model entirely and instead compute the Nash equilibrium of a general preference model, yielding a policy that is preferred over any competing policy.

## Input / Output

The method takes pairwise human preference data (comparisons of two responses y, y' given a prompt x) and a reference policy μ (the SFT model) as input. The preference model P(y ≻ y'|x) is learned via supervised cross-entropy regression and takes two responses as input, making it invariant to the sampling distribution that generated the data. The output is a single policy π* that constitutes the (KL-regularized) Nash equilibrium of the preference model, i.e., a policy whose generated responses are preferred over those of any alternative policy with probability at least 1/2.

## Method

NLHF replaces the BT reward model with a general antisymmetric preference model learned by supervised learning, then computes its Nash equilibrium via a novel mirror descent algorithm called Nash-MD. At each iteration, the current policy π_t is updated by a mirror descent step against a geometric mixture policy π_t^μ ∝ π_t^{1−ητ} μ^{ητ} (a convex combination in log-probability space between the current and reference policies), rather than against the full average of past policies. This yields last-iterate convergence to the regularized Nash equilibrium at rate O(1/T) in KL-divergence, without requiring storage of intermediate policies. For deep architectures, the paper introduces Nash-MD-PG and Nash-EMA-PG, which use a regularized policy gradient with the preference signal P(y ≻ y'|x) − 1/2 as the reward and the alternative policy π' implemented either as a geometric mixture (Nash-MD-PG) or an exponential moving average of past parameters (Nash-EMA-PG).

## Results

On the TL;DR text summarization task, models are evaluated via pairwise preference using PaLM 2 Large as judge. The Nash-MD-PG variants (MD1–MD6, with mixture parameter β ∈ {0.125, …, 0.75}) and Best-Response (BR, β=1) are the strongest NLHF methods: BR achieves a 0.852 pairwise preference over RLHF, and MD6 (β=0.75) achieves 0.716. Self-Play (SP, β=0) only marginally beats RLHF (0.511), while lower-β variants (MD1–MD3) actually lose to RLHF (0.402–0.439). The paper highlights that intermediate β values (0.125–0.375) represent a sweet spot balancing self-improvement against a fixed reference, though BR and MD6 show the highest absolute scores in the table.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is standard RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017), which learns a Bradley-Terry reward model and optimizes via RL. In the pairwise PaLM 2 evaluation, the strongest NLHF variants clearly outperform RLHF: BR (Best-Response against SFT, equivalent to Nash-MD-PG with β=1) achieves 0.852 preference over RLHF, and MD6 (β=0.75) achieves 0.716. In contrast, Self-Play (β=0) only marginally edges out RLHF at 0.511, and the lower-β Nash-MD variants (MD1–MD3, β=0.125–0.375) actually lose to RLHF (0.402–0.439). The paper notes that a fully fair comparison is difficult because NLHF and RLHF rely on different learned models (preference vs. reward) and the same hyperparameters were used across all NLHF variants without per-method tuning. The authors frame the experiments as a feasibility demonstration of NLHF in a practical LLM setting rather than a definitive superiority claim over RLHF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard RLHF pipeline (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) that learns a Bradley-Terry reward model and optimizes against it, instead learning a general pairwise preference model and computing its Nash equilibrium. Algorithmically, Nash-MD is a novel variant of mirror descent (Bubeck, 2015) that differs from online mirror descent by playing against a geometric mixture of the current and reference policies rather than the current policy alone, and from fictitious play by achieving last-iterate convergence without storing past policies. The policy gradient formulation builds on regularized MDP theory (Geist et al., 2019), and the approach subsumes Self-Play (Calandriello et al., 2024) and Best-Response as special cases of the mixture parameter β.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers three conceptual advantages over RLHF: preference models can capture non-transitive preferences, are less sensitive to the training data distribution, and the Nash equilibrium solution better aligns with the diversity of human preferences (as shown in the three-type example). The last-iterate convergence of Nash-MD at O(1/T) rate is practically important for LLMs where storing and sampling from mixture policies is prohibitively expensive. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger LLMs and more complex tasks, whether the one-step-at-a-time approximation of the geometric mixture (used in practice) preserves convergence guarantees, and how to optimally schedule the mixture parameter β over training.
