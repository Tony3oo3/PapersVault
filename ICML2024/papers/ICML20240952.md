## Problem

Stepwise inference protocols such as chain-of-thought and scratchpads dramatically improve Transformer performance on complex reasoning tasks, yet their underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This paper addresses the gap by proposing a synthetic graph navigation task as a minimal, controllable framework in which the multistep nature of stepwise inference can be precisely studied and its emergent behaviors (reasoning gaps, biases, generalization limits) mechanistically explained.

## Input / Output

The model receives a prompt of the form 'goal: X_s [intermediate nodes] X_g' where X_s and X_g are start and goal nodes from a DAG, optionally preceded by in-context exemplar sequences that demonstrate traversal paths between connected motifs (subgraphs linked by 'ghost edges'). The model autoregressively generates a sequence of intermediate nodes constituting a path, followed by a binary token p1 (path exists) or p0 (no path). In the exemplar setting, the input additionally includes 2–4 exemplar sequences spanning chains of 3–5 motifs, and the model must produce a path that traverses the full motif chain, with the number of intermediate motifs serving as a length-generalization probe.

## Method

The authors train a 2-layer GPT-style Transformer (nanoGPT architecture, 64-dim embeddings, weight-tied embeddings) from scratch via next-token prediction on synthetic DAGs of two types: Bernoulli (random upper-triangular adjacency) and hierarchical (layered feedforward structure). Two experimental regimes are studied: (1) a single-graph setting emulating zero-shot CoT/scratchpad, where the model must classify path existence and optionally generate intermediate nodes; and (2) a multi-motif setting emulating few-shot CoT, where Bernoulli DAGs are chained via ghost edges and in-context exemplars guide navigation. Mechanistically, they reduce the model to a single-head, attention-only layer and show that the learned algorithm selects the next node by computing the inner product of the sum of goal and current-node value embeddings against all candidate token embeddings, effectively implementing a greedy distance-minimization heuristic that explains the observed short-path (simplicity) bias. The framework reproduces and explains four phenomena: the stepwise inference gap (attributed to the need to 'stitch' short training paths), a diversity-accuracy tradeoff with sampling temperature, compositional generalization bounded by training chain length, and a primacy bias toward the first exemplar under conflicting contexts.

## Results

The paper demonstrates a consistent stepwise inference gap: training with intermediate path nodes significantly improves path/no-path classification accuracy over direct inference, with the gap more pronounced in hierarchical DAGs (where inter-node distances are larger) and growing as the maximum training path length Δ decreases (requiring more 'stitching' of sub-paths). A diversity-accuracy tradeoff is quantified: increasing sampling temperature from 0.0 to 3.0 increases the number of unique valid paths generated while decreasing accuracy, revealing an optimal temperature. Mechanistically, a simplified algorithm using only the value embeddings of the goal and current node (v = v_g + v_current) achieves 99.8% accuracy on 500 held-out node pairs versus 99.6% for the full 2-layer Transformer, with over 75% of generated paths being identical (measured by Levenshtein edit distance). In the in-context exemplar setting, the model generalizes compositionally to unseen motif orderings up to the maximum chain length in training (n = 4 intermediate motifs), and exhibits a primacy bias, strongly preferring the first of two conflicting exemplar chains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods; its central comparison is between two training protocols on the same synthetic task: stepwise inference (model trained to generate intermediate path nodes) versus direct inference (model trained only on start-goal pairs). Stepwise inference consistently outperforms direct inference across both Bernoulli and hierarchical DAGs, with the advantage scaling with graph complexity and the degree of path 'stitching' required. A secondary internal comparison validates the mechanistic hypothesis: a 1-layer, attention-only Transformer reduced to a simple inner-product algorithm (summing value embeddings of goal and current node, then selecting the token with highest inner product) matches the full 2-layer model's accuracy (99.8% vs. 99.6%) and reproduces its path outputs with >75% identity, confirming that the learned navigation strategy is essentially a greedy nearest-neighbor step in embedding space. The paper also contrasts Bernoulli DAGs (bell-shaped path-length distribution) with hierarchical DAGs (exponential path-length distribution), showing the stepwise inference gap is larger in the latter due to greater path lengths requiring more compositional recombination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a fully synthetic, grounded graph navigation task as a controlled testbed for studying stepwise inference in Transformers, departing from prior work that examines chain-of-thought and scratchpad protocols either in large-scale LLMs (Kojima et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2022; Nye et al., 2021) or through purely theoretical analyses (Li et al., 2023; Feng et al., 2023). It draws on the observation that multi-step reasoning can be cast as graph traversal (Saparov & He, 2023; Dziri et al., 2023; Momennejad et al., 2023) and on the model-experimental systems approach (Ramesh et al., 2023) to train small Transformers from scratch on Bernoulli and hierarchical DAGs. The key methodological departure is the use of in-context exemplars built from chained 'motif' subgraphs connected by ghost edges, enabling a controlled study of few-shot compositional generalization and primacy bias that prior synthetic studies (e.g., Prystawski & Goodman, 2023) did not isolate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a minimal, interpretable framework that yields several mechanistic hypotheses for stepwise inference: the stepwise inference gap is tied to the need to 'stitch' shorter training paths, the simplicity bias arises from a greedy inner-product-based navigation algorithm, and a diversity-accuracy tradeoff emerges with sampling temperature. These findings offer testable predictions for large-scale models and a template for controlled ablations that are infeasible in natural-language settings. Key limitations include fixed (non-dynamic) graphs, the absence of a natural-language-to-graph mapping, and reliance on very small models (2-layer, 64-dim), leaving open whether the identified mechanisms persist at scale or in settings where the underlying graph changes during reasoning.
