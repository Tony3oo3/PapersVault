## Problem

Conventional causal effect identification assumes strict positivity of the observational distribution P(V) > 0, yet real-world data frequently violates this assumption, rendering standard identification formulas undefined (e.g., 0/0 terms). Prior work has not provided a principled, general method for deriving the minimal positivity condition required by an identification formula—especially when the formula involves fractions, nested summations, or products—as produced by complete algorithms like IDENTIFY or ID on arbitrary semi-Markovian graphs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a semi-Markovian causal graph G (a DAG with bidirected edges encoding unmeasured confounders), a causal query P_x(y) for disjoint variable sets X and Y, and an observational distribution P(V) over all observed variables. It outputs two coupled artifacts: (1) an identification formula expressing P_x(y) as a function of P(V) (involving fractions, marginalizations, and products), and (2) a first-order logic positivity condition over P(V) that is sufficient for the formula to be well-defined. The condition is not a simple inequality like P(V) > 0 but can involve universal/existential quantifiers, logical connectives, and equality constraints (e.g., ∀z(P(z)=0 ∨ P(x|z)>0) for backdoor, or ∃r(adj(r;W) ∧ P(x,r)>0) for Napkin).

## Method

The paper establishes three complementary routes to positivity conditions and shows their equivalence. First, it re-derives do-calculus rules under relaxed positivity (Lemma 4.1), showing each rule requires only a local condition (e.g., P_x(z,w)>0 for Rule 1) rather than global P(V)>0, and that the conjunction of conditions along a derivation chain is sufficient for the final formula (Prop. 4.2). Second, it generalizes Q-decomposition by introducing 'anchors'—maximally consecutive segments of a c-component in a topological order—to characterize when a c-factor Q[H'] can be expressed as a product of fractions with some denominators potentially zero (Thm. 5.1-(i)), and when it is identifiably zero (Thm. 5.1-(ii)), thereby relaxing the strict Q[H]>0 assumption of Tian & Pearl (2003). Third, it builds IDENTIFY+ (Algs. 1–2), which embeds a constraint-propagation mechanism (EXPAND, governed by Prop. 6.1's first-order logic equivalences for sums, products, and fractions) into the IDENTIFY recursion, so that the positivity condition is constructed on-the-fly alongside the identification formula. The algorithm is data-agnostic (it does not inspect P(V) values), and the paper further discusses how to extend it into a positivity-aware variant that exploits topological-order choices, value fixing, and latent projections to avoid 0/0 cases.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments or quantitative benchmarks. The main theoretical contributions are: (i) Lemma 4.1, which re-establishes do-calculus rules under relaxed positivity (requiring only P_x(z,w)>0 rather than strict positivity over all variables); (ii) Theorem 5.1, which generalizes Q-decomposition by introducing 'anchors' to cancel irrelevant c-factors and provides a condition (part ii) under which a c-factor is identified as zero even when strict positivity fails; and (iii) Theorem 6.1, which guarantees correctness of the IDENTIFY+ algorithm that simultaneously outputs an identification formula and its sufficient positivity condition. The paper demonstrates equivalence across four approaches (post-hoc analysis, do-calculus, manual Q-decomposition, and the IDENTIFY+ algorithm) on three canonical graphs (backdoor, front-door, Napkin), showing all yield the same positivity condition.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against empirical baselines or evaluate on datasets; it is a theoretical contribution. However, it does compare four methodological approaches to deriving positivity conditions — post-hoc formula analysis, do-calculus derivation, manual Q-decomposition, and the algorithmic IDENTIFY+ approach — and shows they yield equivalent sufficient positivity conditions on the backdoor, front-door, and Napkin graphs (Appendix C). The paper also contrasts its relaxed positivity framework with the conventional strict positivity assumption (P(V)>0), demonstrating that under relaxed positivity, different identification formulas (e.g., backdoor vs. front-door, or different admissible sets) can have different applicability, a phenomenon invisible under strict positivity (Appendix D). The IDENTIFY+ algorithm is positioned as an extension of the IDENTIFY algorithm (Tian & Pearl, 2003), adding the EXPAND mechanism to propagate positivity constraints alongside the identification formula.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal effect identification framework of Tian & Pearl (2003) and Pearl's do-calculus (1995) by relaxing the long-standing strict positivity assumption P(V) > 0 that underpins both the IDENTIFY algorithm and the rules of do-calculus. It draws on the d-separation-to-probabilistic-independence results of Lauritzen et al. (1990) and Verma & Pearl (1990) to establish relaxed do-calculus rules, and generalizes the Q-decomposition machinery of Tian & Pearl (2003) via a new theorem (Thm. 5.1) that handles zero denominators through anchor-based cancellation. The paper positions itself as filling a gap explicitly noted in the literature: prior work (Shpitser & Pearl, 2006a; Petersen et al., 2012; Dahabreh et al., 2019) either assumed strict positivity or addressed positivity only for specific criteria, leaving no principled method for deriving positivity conditions for arbitrary identification formulas.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that in real-world observational data, strict positivity is frequently violated (e.g., all subjects in a subgroup receive the same treatment), rendering standard identification formulas undefined. The paper's key insight—that different identification formulas for the same causal quantity (e.g., backdoor with different adjustment sets, or backdoor vs. front-door) carry different positivity conditions—means practitioners must match the formula to the data's positivity structure rather than blindly applying any valid formula. Open questions include: an efficient method for selecting topological orders that make Thm. 5.1 applicable, whether the theorem is necessary (i.e., whether non-identifiability follows when no topological order works), and the design of a fully positivity-aware identification algorithm that adapts to the given distribution. The paper is purely theoretical with no empirical validation, and the IDENTIFY+ algorithm is presented as a proof-of-concept without complexity analysis or implementation.
