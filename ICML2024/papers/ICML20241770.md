## Problem

The Chinchilla scaling laws prescribe optimal model size and training data by minimizing training compute for a fixed quality target, but they entirely ignore inference costs. For models expected to serve substantial inference demand (billions of tokens), this omission leads to suboptimal choices: the prescribed model is too large and under-trained, incurring unnecessary inference FLOPs over its lifetime. The paper asks how to jointly optimize training and inference compute (or real-world dollar cost) given a target quality and expected inference volume.

## Input / Output

The method takes a target pre-training loss ℓ (fixing model quality), an expected inference demand expressed as total tokens D_inf or as a number of requests with specified input/output token counts, and hardware parameters (training MFU, inference-input MFU, inference-output MFU, and per-FLOP costs for training vs. inference hardware). It outputs the optimal parameter count N* and pre-training token count D_tr* that minimize total lifetime FLOPs or dollar cost, along with the resulting total compute/cost estimate. A key assumption is that inference demand is independent of model size conditioned on quality, and must be estimable before training begins.

## Method

The paper inverts the Chinchilla optimization: instead of fixing compute and minimizing loss, it fixes loss (via the Chinchilla parametric form L = E + A/N^α + B/D_tr^β) and minimizes total FLOPs 6ND_tr + 2ND_inf over N and D_tr. Because no closed-form solution exists when D_inf > 0 (proven via Lagrange multipliers yielding a transcendental equation), the authors solve the system numerically with Newton root-finding. For real-world cost estimation, they further decompose inference into input and output tokens with separate MFUs (e.g., 50% for input, 1% for autoregressive generation) and different hardware costs (A100-80GB for training, A100-40GB with INT8 quantization for inference). The paper validates the approach by training 47 MPT-architecture models (150M–6B parameters, 10–10,000 tokens/parameter) and confirming that loss and downstream Gauntlet metrics continue improving at extreme token/parameter ratios with no saturation. An ablation of the Chinchilla coefficient-fitting procedure shows that fitting on only typical-ratio data (≤100 tok/param) overestimates the marginal benefit of additional tokens at extreme ratios, implying the original scaling law is not reliable in the regime the modified optimization prescribes.

## Results

Training 47 MPT models (150M–6B parameters) at token/parameter ratios from 10 to 10,000, the paper finds that pre-training loss continues to decrease monotonically with more tokens per parameter, with no evidence of a saturation point or critical model size. For compute-optimality, a 30B-Chinchilla-quality model facing 5T inference tokens can reduce total FLOPs by 16% by training a 16.4B model on 3.27T tokens instead. For real-world cost-optimality (50% training MFU, 1% generation MFU, INT8 quantization), a 30B-Chinchilla-quality model with 17.5B inference requests saves 58% in dollar cost by training an 8.58B model on 12.1T tokens. Parametric fitting on extreme-ratio data yields flatter scaling curves (β=0.24 vs. Chinchilla's 0.28), confirming that Chinchilla coefficients overestimate the marginal benefit of additional data at high token/parameter ratios.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022), which prescribes the parameter count and training token count that minimize pre-training loss for a fixed compute budget. The paper's inference-aware method consistently outperforms Chinchilla in total lifetime cost: at 17.5B inference requests, the cost-optimal model (8.58B params, 12.1T tokens) costs $4.52M versus $10.8M for the Chinchilla 30B model, a 58% reduction. In the pure FLOP comparison (Table 2), savings are more modest (2.6%–16%) because they ignore the ~50× lower MFU of inference generation versus training. The paper also shows that Chinchilla's parametric coefficients (α=0.34, β=0.28) overestimate the loss reduction from additional data at extreme token/parameter ratios: fitting on data up to 10,000 tokens/param yields α=0.18, β=0.24, meaning the true scaling curve is flatter and the Chinchilla law over-predicts quality gains from very long training runs. No comparison is made against Llama, GPT-3, or other specific models—only against the Chinchilla-optimal configuration at matched pre-training loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly inverts the Chinchilla optimization problem (Hoffmann et al., 2022): where Chinchilla fixes a compute budget and minimizes loss, this work fixes a quality target (loss) and minimizes total lifetime compute including inference. It builds on the same parametric loss function and FLOP approximations, but adds inference demand as a first-class constraint. The empirical validation extends the token/parameter ratio range tested by Chinchilla (≤~100) to 10,000, and the cost model incorporates hardware-specific MFU differences and quantization effects that prior training-only analyses ignored. The work is positioned alongside Llama's empirical choice to train beyond Chinchilla-optimal and complements Gadre et al. (2024), who independently validated reliable scaling at over-trained ratios.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For LLM practitioners facing significant inference demand (~1B+ requests), the paper provides a concrete recipe to reduce total lifetime cost by 17–58% by training smaller models on more data, with the shift quantified as a function of expected inference volume. The finding that Chinchilla-fitted coefficients overestimate the marginal benefit of additional tokens at extreme ratios (5,000–10,000 tokens/param) is an important correction as the field trains increasingly data-heavy models (Llama 3 at 15T tokens). Key limitations include testing only up to 6B parameters and 47 training runs (vs. 400 in Chinchilla), leaving open whether the no-saturation behavior and coefficient corrections hold at frontier scales. Developing scaling laws that accurately span the full range from under-trained to extremely over-trained regimes remains an open problem.
