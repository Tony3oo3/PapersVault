## Problem

Wireless site surveys require dense grid measurements to characterize channel quality across a deployment, but in practice only sparse random measurements are feasible, leaving dead spots undetected. Existing spatial prediction methods either cannot handle complex 3D indoor multipath environments or demand extremely dense measurements (e.g., NeRF² requires ~178 measurements/ft³). The paper asks whether a NeRF-style neural radiance field can learn a wireless radiation scene from a sparse set of channel measurements and accurately predict channels at arbitrary unvisited locations.

## Input / Output

The model takes 3D receiver coordinates, a sparse set of complex-valued channel measurements (a single complex number per location, far less information than an image), and DoA measurements from a receiver antenna array during training. At inference, DoA is unavailable, so a separate ray-searching algorithm must discover it. The output is a complex channel value at any query location, decomposable into individual multipath components, plus a learned 3D volume-density field representing the radiation scene. The model predicts in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) components rather than amplitude and phase to maintain differentiability through the arctangent.

## Method

NeWRF adapts NeRF's MLP-based radiance field to the RF domain by mapping 5D inputs (x, y, z, θ, φ) to radiated signal amplitude, phase, and volume density, then synthesizing the channel via a volume-rendering integral that explicitly incorporates free-space attenuation and phase rotation (Eq. 7). A key architectural difference is DoA-guided ray casting: instead of exhaustively searching 64,800 angular directions, the model traces rays only toward the 10–20 directions identified by antenna-array DoA measurements, dramatically reducing the search space and enabling convergence. Training uses a coarse-fine two-stage pipeline with stratified and hierarchical sampling, supervised by NMSE loss on the complex channel. A surprising finding is that the learned scene places high volume density at virtual images of the transmitter (mirror images against reflective surfaces) rather than on the reflector surfaces themselves, revealing a simple geometric structure that makes sparse-measurement learning feasible. For inference at unvisited locations, a five-step ray-searching algorithm identifies virtual transmitters via DBSCAN clustering of ray intersections, predicts the count of relevant virtual transmitters with a small fully-connected network, and selects them by voting among the six nearest training locations.

## Results

NeWRF is evaluated on three simulated indoor environments (Conference Room, Bedroom, Office) using SNR (defined as −10·log₁₀(NMSE)) as the primary metric. NeWRF outperforms all baselines in every environment, achieving more than 5 dB SNR even in the most complex office setting. A key finding is that NeWRF maintains accurate channel prediction at a measurement density as low as 0.2 measurements/ft³, roughly 800× lower than the 178.1 measurements/ft³ required by NeRF². When DoA is estimated via the proposed ray-searching algorithm rather than provided as ground truth, performance degrades by only 3–5 dB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NeWRF is compared against three baselines: KNN (K=3), a 7-layer MLP (width 128) that directly maps 3D location to channel, and NeRF² (Zhao et al., 2023). NeWRF wins on all three environments (Conference Room, Bedroom, Office) across all three baselines in SNR. NeRF² performs particularly poorly on these datasets because the measurement density (0.4 measurements/ft³) is orders of magnitude below the 178.1 measurements/ft³ it was designed for, causing it to fail to converge meaningfully. The MLP baseline, which ignores propagation physics, also underperforms NeWRF, confirming that incorporating multipath and free-space propagation explicitly into the synthesis algorithm is critical. KNN, the simplest spatial interpolation method, is the weakest baseline, as expected given the fine-scale spatial variation of wireless channels caused by constructive and destructive interference.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeWRF adapts the NeRF framework (Mildenhall et al., 2021) from visible-light 3D reconstruction to radio-frequency channel prediction, requiring substantial modifications: replacing per-pixel image supervision with single complex-number channel measurements, handling omnidirectional antenna capture via DoA-guided ray-casting instead of camera-defined ray directions, and explicitly encoding free-space propagation physics (attenuation and phase rotation) into the synthesis equation. It departs from the closest prior NeRF-for-RF work, NeRF² (Zhao et al., 2023), which learns a dynamic transmitter-to-receiver mapping requiring ~178 measurements/ft³, by instead learning a static radiation field from a fixed transmitter at ~0.2 measurements/ft³. It also contrasts with WinERT (Orekondy et al., 2022), which uses NeRF as a neural surrogate for ray-tracing but requires CAD geometry, whereas NeWRF learns the field directly from measurements without environmental models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NeWRF demonstrates that a NeRF-style volumetric representation can reconstruct wireless radiation scenes from extremely sparse measurements (800× fewer than prior NeRF-based RF work), which could dramatically reduce the cost and time of wireless site surveys and improve dead-spot detection in practice. The discovery that the learned representation places high density at virtual transmitter images rather than reflector surfaces reveals a physically grounded inductive bias that makes the problem tractable from sparse data. Key open questions include relaxing the strict DoA accuracy requirement (currently ~1°), handling phase-inaccurate or wideband measurements, and scaling to larger, more complex environments—potentially by fusing visual and wireless radiation fields. All evaluations are simulation-based (MATLAB ray tracing), so real-world validation remains an important next step.
