## Problem

In reinforcement learning, different reward functions can define the same optimal policy yet produce drastically different learning performance, making reward design a critical and challenging problem. Prior max-reward RL formulations (Gottipati et al., 2020) that optimize the maximum rather than cumulative reward suffer from a fundamental theoretical flaw: their Bellman-like operator only yields correct value estimates in deterministic settings, producing suboptimal policies when stochasticity is present. This paper addresses the open question of whether a theoretically sound Bellman-like operator exists for max-reward RL in the general stochastic case.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a continuous state observation s and an auxiliary scalar y (the inversely-discounted maximum reward obtained so far, initialized to 0) as the extended state (s, y) ∈ S × R, and outputs a continuous action along with max-reward value function estimates. The value networks use a Tanh activation rescaled to [0, R̄] followed by a ReLU(u−y)+y transformation to enforce the boundary condition v̂(s,y) ≥ y. The extended transition model deterministically updates y via y′ = (R(s,a,s′) ∨ y)/γ, coupling the reward history into the state representation.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretically grounded max-reward RL framework built on an extended MDP that tracks the discounted maximum reward via an auxiliary variable y. The paper defines max-reward value functions v̂(s,y) = E[y ∨ Ĝ_t | s_t = s] and proves that the corresponding Bellman operator is a γ-contraction in the L∞ norm (Theorem 4.4), enabling standard value iteration and Q-learning in stochastic environments. Policy gradient theorems are proven for both stochastic and deterministic extended max-reward policies, allowing direct adaptation of PPO and TD3. The key mechanistic difference from standard RL is that bootstrapping is suppressed whenever the immediate reward exceeds the largest reward seen so far, reducing the moving-target problem and enabling more efficient reward propagation from goal states. The optimal policy in this framework is inherently history-dependent, conditioned on the current value of y, which also provides a natural mechanism for escaping local optima.

## Results

The primary metric across all experiments is the success ratio (whether the goal was reached during the episode). In the Maze environment, max-reward TD3 and PPO succeed across all DSP reward sparsity levels (k=1,2,3,4), while standard TD3 and PPO converge to suboptimal policies for k≥2. In the Fetch-Slide and Fetch-Push environments, max-reward TD3 achieves a goal-reaching policy while standard TD3 fails to learn completely, consistent with prior work showing these environments are unsolvable with standard RL. On the stochastic maze (p_slip > 0), the paper's stochastic max-reward TD3 solves the task while the deterministic max-reward TD3 of Gottipati et al. is highly inconsistent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard TD3 (Fujimoto et al., 2018), max-reward TD3 consistently outperforms on the Maze environment, particularly as the DSP reward becomes sparser (larger k), where standard TD3 converges to suboptimal policies while max-reward TD3 maintains high success ratios. On Fetch-Slide and Fetch-Push, max-reward TD3 achieves a goal-reaching policy while standard TD3 fails entirely, demonstrating the advantage in high-dimensional, sparse-reward robotics tasks. Against standard PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), max-reward PPO shows similar advantages on the Maze with sparse rewards, benefiting from reduced bootstrapping and the auxiliary variable y preventing entrapment in local optima. Against the deterministic max-reward TD3 of Gottipati et al. (2020), the paper's stochastic formulation clearly wins on the stochastic maze: the deterministic method is highly inconsistent and fails to converge, while the stochastic max-reward TD3 reliably solves the environment, confirming the theoretical limitation of the deterministic operator under stochasticity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the max-reward RL line initiated by Quah & Quek (2006) and corrected by Gottipati et al. (2020), which was shown by Cui & Yu (2023) to be limited to deterministic MDPs. The key departure is the introduction of an auxiliary variable y (inspired by Kröner et al.'s stochastic optimal control framework for running maximum cost) that augments the state space, enabling a Bellman-like contraction operator and policy gradient theorems valid under stochastic transitions and policies. The paper also positions itself against the broader reward-shaping literature, arguing that max-reward objectives make reward design more intuitive since only the relative ordering of state rewards matters, not their cumulative structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a theoretically grounded alternative to cumulative-return RL that is particularly well-suited to sparse-reward goal-reaching problems, where bootstrapping is a major bottleneck. The finding that the optimal max-reward policy is inherently history-dependent (conditioned on the maximum reward obtained so far) is a structural insight that changes how one thinks about policy parameterization. Open questions include how the extended state dimensionality (S × R) scales to high-dimensional continuous control, whether the approach composes well with automated reward design methods, and whether the benefits persist in more complex, multi-objective, or safety-constrained settings beyond the relatively simple Fetch and Maze environments tested here.
