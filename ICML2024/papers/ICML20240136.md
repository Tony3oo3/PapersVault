## Problem

Existing Distributed Mean Estimation (DME) methods that achieve the optimal O(1/n) NMSE guarantee suffer from asymptotically slow encoding (Kashin: O(d·log d·log(n·d))) or decoding (EDEN/DRIVE: O(n·d·log d)) due to either expensive coefficient decomposition or per-client inverse transforms. The open question is whether one can simultaneously achieve O(1/n) NMSE with fast O(d·log d) encoding and O(n·d + d·log d) decoding, matching the best complexity of each dimension independently.

## Input / Output

Each client c receives a d-dimensional gradient vector x_c and a bit budget b, applies a shared RHT (determined by a global seed), scales by √d/‖x_c‖₂, then splits coordinates into large values (|z| > t_p, sent exactly with their indices) and small values (|z| ≤ t_p, stochastically quantized to precomputed values Q_{b,p}). The client transmits ‖x_c‖₂, the quantization-value indices for small coordinates, and the exact large coordinates with their indices. The server aggregates all clients' reconstructed scaled-rotated vectors, applies a single inverse RHT, and outputs the estimated mean vector x̂_avg. The total per-coordinate bandwidth is b + 64/512 bits (accounting for the float and 32-bit index of each exactly-sent entry).

## Method

QUIC-FL combines three key ideas: (1) Bounded Support Quantization (BSQ), which sends a small fraction p of the largest post-rotation coordinates exactly and stochastically quantizes the remainder, reducing the quantization range and thus the error; (2) a distribution-aware unbiased quantizer whose values Q_{b,p} are precomputed by solving a non-convex optimization problem (discretized via m quantiles of the truncated N(0,1) distribution) that minimizes estimation variance subject to unbiasedness and probability constraints; and (3) a shared RHT across all clients (via global shared randomness), which lets the server sum compressed vectors before a single inverse transform, yielding O(n·d + d·log d) decoding. Client-specific shared randomness (ℓ bits per coordinate) further reduces the quantization error by allowing the solver to condition the quantization mapping on the shared value. The resulting NMSE is (1/n)·E[(Z−Ẑ)²] + O(1/n·√(log d/d)), where the first term is exactly computable from the solver output (e.g., ≈8.58/n for b=1, p=2⁻⁹ without shared randomness, dropping to ≈3.29/n with one shared bit).

## Results

QUIC-FL achieves the optimal O(1/n) NMSE with O(d·log d) encoding and O(n·d + d·log d) decoding, asymptotically improving over all prior methods with the same NMSE guarantee. With b=1 bit and p=2^{-9}, the quantization error is 8.58/n (a 3.72× improvement over basic BSQ's 32/n while using 2.66× less bandwidth). With 4 shared random bits, the NMSE bounds are 4.831/n (b=1), 0.692/n (b=2), 0.131/n (b=3), and 0.0272/n (b=4). On the Shakespeare next-word prediction task (LSTM, n=10 clients, FedAvg+Adam), QUIC-FL is competitive with EDEN and markedly more accurate than QSGD, Hadamard, and Kashin baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QUIC-FL dominates all baselines on at least one axis while matching or exceeding their accuracy. Against QSGD and Hadamard, QUIC-FL achieves a strictly better NMSE (O(1/n) vs. O(√d/n) and O(log d/n) respectively) at the same or comparable computational cost. Against Kashin's representation, QUIC-FL matches the O(1/n) NMSE and O(n·d + d·log d) decoding but reduces encoding from O(d·log d·log(n·d)) to O(d·log d); empirically, the default TensorFlow Kashin implementation is biased (NMSE not O(1/n)) while the theoretical variant is unbiased but slower. Against EDEN-RHT, QUIC-FL reduces decoding from O(n·d·log d) to O(n·d + d·log d) with competitive NMSE (EDEN has slightly lower NMSE only at b=1). Against EDEN-URR, QUIC-FL improves both encoding (O(d·log d) vs. O(d³)) and decoding. On the Shakespeare FL task, QUIC-FL matches EDEN's training accuracy while being significantly faster to decode, and outperforms QSGD, Hadamard, and Kashin by a wide margin.

## Relation to Prior Work

QUIC-FL builds on the Hadamard-transform + stochastic quantization framework of Suresh et al. (2017) and the shared-randomness quantization ideas of Ben Basat et al. (2021b), but departs from both in critical ways. Unlike Suresh et al., all clients use the *same* rotation (determined by global shared randomness), enabling the server to perform a single inverse RHT rather than n independent ones; unlike Kashin-based methods (Caldas et al., 2018; Safaryan et al., 2020), it avoids the expensive multi-iteration decomposition; and unlike EDEN/DRIVE (Vargaftik et al., 2021; 2022), it uses a shared rotation so decoding is O(n·d + d·log d) rather than O(n·d·log d). Two new technical tools—Bounded Support Quantization (sending a small fraction of largest coordinates exactly) and a distribution-aware unbiased quantizer solved via off-the-shelf nonlinear solvers—close the gap to the optimal O(1/n) NMSE while preserving fast encode/decode times.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For federated learning deployments with thousands to tens of thousands of clients per round, the decoding speedup (single inverse RHT vs. n inverse RHTs) translates directly into wall-clock savings at the parameter server, which is often the bottleneck. The distribution-aware unbiased quantization is, to the authors' knowledge, the first such method in the information-theory literature and may be of independent interest beyond FL. A key limitation is that RHT destroys coordinate-level structure (e.g., sparsity or sign patterns), so QUIC-FL cannot exploit gradient correlations; combining it with sparsification, in-network aggregation, or correlation-aware compression is left as future work. The paper also notes that the RHT-based guarantees carry a larger constant than the URR-based ones, and tightening this gap is an open question.
