## Problem

Popular visual XAI methods (gradient-based, permutation-based, propagation-based) produce heatmaps that highlight WHERE in an image a model focuses, but cannot explain HOW objects are spatially related to each other. This is a critical gap because in many real-world tasks—autonomous driving, street surveillance, histopathology—ground truth labels depend on spatial relationships such as distance, orientation, order, and containment between objects. The paper argues that a paradigm shift from 'where' to 'how' is needed in visual explanations.

## Input / Output

The empirical component uses RGB images from two VTAB 'structured' datasets: KITTI (predicting binned distance to the nearest vehicle) and dsprites (predicting binned orientation of simple shapes), where labels inherently depend on spatial context. The five XAI methods tested (GradientSHAP, Integrated Gradients, Occlusion, Saliency, LIME) produce pixel-level attribution heatmaps with a red-to-green color spectrum indicating negative-to-positive contribution. The paper's proposed output extends beyond heatmaps to explanations that capture the type and nature of spatial relationships (e.g., 'holding' vs. 'near', 'facing' vs. 'back-to').

## Method

This position paper combines a taxonomy of spatial context (distance, inside/outside, order, orientation, neighborhood) with an empirical demonstration of XAI failures. The authors fine-tune ResNet-50 (ImageNet-supervised) and ViT base (MoCo v3 contrastive) on KITTI and dsprites, then apply five popular XAI techniques to show that all produce vague, inaccurate explanations that fail to capture the spatial relationships driving correct predictions. The core argument is that even after translating heatmaps to semantic concepts (the 'where-to-what' shift), explanations remain identical for images with the same objects in different spatial configurations. The paper proposes concrete research directions: new spatially-compositional datasets generated via diffusion models, benchmarks for spatial reasoning in non-generative vision models, quantitative measures of spatial context preservation, and diverse explanation types (symbolic rules, probe tasks) beyond pixel-level heatmaps.

## Results

This is a position paper with primarily qualitative findings rather than a quantitative benchmark. The authors fine-tuned ResNet-50 (supervised, ImageNet-pretrained) and ViT base (MoCo v3, contrastive-pretrained) on two VTAB 'structured' datasets — KITTI (binned distance to nearest vehicle) and dsprites (binned orientation of shapes) — and applied five popular XAI methods (GradientSHAP, Integrated Gradients, Occlusion, Saliency, LIME). The key finding is that all five methods produce explanations that are 'inaccurate, vague and difficult to interpret' when spatial context is the decisive factor: for example, LIME highlights the nearest vehicle in green on KITTI but fails to convey that distance (not mere presence) is the critical signal, and on dsprites the heatmaps do not distinguish orientation-dependent decisions. No quantitative XAI fidelity metric is reported; the contribution is the demonstration of systematic failure modes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new XAI method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it evaluates five existing post-hoc explanation techniques (GradientSHAP, Integrated Gradients, Occlusion, Saliency, LIME) on spatial-context-dependent tasks and shows that all of them fail in the same qualitative way. On KITTI, none of the five methods produce a heatmap that encodes the binned distance to the nearest vehicle — the explanations are indistinguishable from what one would expect in a simple object-recognition task (e.g., car vs. plane). On dsprites, the heatmaps similarly fail to capture the binned orientation that drives the correct prediction. The authors also note that even the more recent 'where → what' paradigm (Concept Relevance Propagation, Achtibat et al. 2022) would yield semantically identical explanations for two images with the same objects but different spatial arrangements, reinforcing the need for a 'where → how' shift. No method is shown to outperform another on spatial context; the uniform failure is the central empirical observation motivating the proposed research directions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper extends the XAI paradigm shift from 'where' (pixel-level heatmaps) to 'what' (semantic concepts, as proposed by Achtibat et al., 2022) by arguing for a further shift to 'how' — capturing spatial relationships between objects. It draws on the rich literature of spatial context in DL architectures (CapsuleNet, Vision Transformers, spatial-context-aware networks) to highlight a disconnect: models increasingly exploit spatial context, yet the dominant post-hoc explanation methods (LIME, Grad-CAM, Integrated Gradients, LRP) remain blind to it. The paper also surveys the handful of existing spatial XAI efforts (Bardool et al., 2019; Sacha et al., 2023; Tomaszewska et al., 2023/2024; Rabold et al., 2020; Haldekar et al., 2017) to frame 'spatial XAI' as a nascent but underdeveloped subfield.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and practically important gap: in safety-critical domains (autonomous driving, street surveillance, histopathology), model predictions depend on spatial relationships that current XAI tools cannot articulate, creating a trust and regulatory risk. The proposed research directions — a spatial-context benchmark for non-generative vision models, quantitative metrics for spatial context preservation, and diverse explanation types beyond heatmaps — are actionable but remain at the proposal stage with no implemented solution. A key open question is how to design explanation methods that are both interpretable to humans and sensitive to the specific type of spatial relationship (distance, order, orientation, inside/outside) that drives a given prediction.
