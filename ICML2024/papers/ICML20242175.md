## Problem

Fine-tuning pre-trained RL agents on downstream tasks often fails to leverage prior knowledge because the agent catastrophically forgets pre-trained capabilities on parts of the state space not visited early in fine-tuning. This paper identifies and formalizes this phenomenon as Forgetting of Pre-trained Capabilities (FPC), showing it arises through two common mechanisms—state coverage gap and imperfect cloning gap—and is far more severe in RL than in supervised learning due to the non-i.i.d. nature of state visitation driven by the agent's own actions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment-specific observations (NetHack: dungeon screen characters/colors + player status + message text; Montezuma's Revenge: 84×84 RGB frame stacks; RoboticSequence: continuous state vectors with one-hot stage IDs) along with the pre-trained policy's parameters and, for BC/EM, a buffer of state-action pairs collected from the pre-trained or expert policy. The output is a fine-tuned policy producing discrete actions (120 in NetHack, Atari action space in Montezuma) or continuous actions (robotic joint torques in RoboticSequence). A key practical constraint is that BC and EM require access to pre-training data, while EWC and KS operate without it, making the choice of retention method dependent on data availability and the specific FPC instance.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is demonstrating that standard continual-learning knowledge retention techniques—Elastic Weight Consolidation, behavioral cloning (BC), kickstarting (KS), and episodic memory (EM)—directly mitigate FPC and unlock the transfer benefits of pre-training. The key mechanistic insight is that the correct retention method depends on the FPC instance: for state coverage gap (agent knows FAR but not CLOSE), BC is effective because it rehearses FAR behavior using pre-collected expert trajectories, while KS fails because it tries to match the pre-trained policy on CLOSE states it never saw; for imperfect cloning gap (agent knows both but suboptimally), KS works best because it prevents forgetting on states the agent actually visits online. The implementation is deliberately lightweight: retention is applied only to the actor (not the critic), integrated as an auxiliary loss alongside the standard RL objective (APPO, PPO+RND, or SAC), and requires no architectural changes. This yields a 2× improvement in NetHack state-of-the-art (5K→10K+ points) and consistent gains across all three environments.

## Results

On NetHack (Human Monk), fine-tuning with Kickstarting (KS) achieves a score of 10,588 ± 672, doubling the previous state-of-the-art for neural models (5,218 by Scaled-BC, Tuyls et al. 2023). On Montezuma's Revenge, adding a behavioral cloning (BC) loss to fine-tuning improves both learning speed and final performance over vanilla fine-tuning and training from scratch. On RoboticSequence, fine-tuning + BC solves all four sequential stages 80% of the time, while vanilla fine-tuning and training from scratch are virtually indistinguishable and significantly behind.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's method (Scaled-BC + Fine-tuning + KS) achieves 10,588 on NetHack Human Monk, doubling the previous best neural model Scaled-BC (Tuyls et al., 2023) at 5,218 and outperforming all offline-only baselines from Hambro et al. (2022) by 5–40× (DQN-Offline: 0, CQL: 366, IQL: 267, BC/CDGPT5: 1,059). It also surpasses the online baselines From Scratch+KS (2,090) and From Scratch+BC (2,809) from Hambro et al. by roughly 4–5×, and LDD* (Mu et al., 2022) at 2,100 by 5×. On Montezuma's Revenge, fine-tuning + BC outperforms both vanilla fine-tuning and training from scratch in both convergence speed and final return, with the advantage becoming visible around 20M steps when the agent first reaches Room 7. On RoboticSequence, BC, EM, and EWC all substantially outperform vanilla fine-tuning and from-scratch training, with BC achieving the highest overall success rate (80% on all four stages). Notably, vanilla fine-tuning actually *underperforms* training from scratch on NetHack (647 vs. 776 score), demonstrating that without knowledge retention, fine-tuning is actively harmful due to catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained capabilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes RL fine-tuning as a catastrophic forgetting problem by importing knowledge retention techniques from the continual learning literature—specifically EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), behavioral cloning replay (Rebuffi et al., 2017), and kickstarting (Schmitt et al., 2018)—into the transfer RL setting. It departs from the continual RL literature (Wolczyk et al., 2021; Khetarpal et al., 2022), which focuses on retaining performance across task sequences, by showing that forgetting already undermines single-task fine-tuning when the downstream state distribution shifts during training. The paper also explicitly contrasts with the supervised fine-tuning paradigm (and with Wulfmeier et al., 2023), arguing that the non-i.i.d. nature of RL state visitation makes forgetting a first-order concern even when only downstream performance matters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical recommendation—that standard knowledge retention methods should be routinely applied in transfer RL scenarios—is actionable and low-cost, and the 2× SOTA on NetHack (5K→10K) demonstrates substantial upside. However, the paper is limited to relatively simple retention methods, does not study very large models (>1B parameters) or parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA-style), and acknowledges that retention can be harmful when the pre-trained policy is suboptimal. Open questions include how to selectively preserve only the relevant subset of pre-trained capabilities, how these findings extend to multi-task fine-tuning and offline-to-online RL, and whether the state-coverage vs. imperfect-cloning distinction generalizes beyond the tested environments.
