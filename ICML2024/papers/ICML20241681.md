## Problem

While many Byzantine-robust aggregation algorithms exist for distributed learning, there is no systematic procedure to identify which specific worker machines are Byzantine with formal statistical guarantees. Existing detection methods (Krum, FABA, Zeno) flag a pre-specified number of machines based on distance scores without controlling false positives, and traditional outlier detection relies on p-values from asymptotic distributions that become unreliable in high-dimensional or finite-sample regimes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241681/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each worker machine transmits only two d-dimensional gradient vectors (computed on two equal-size data splits) to the master, making the procedure communication-efficient. The master outputs a binary identification set of Byzantine machines, per-machine scalar ranking scores W_j, and a data-driven threshold L chosen to control FDR at a user-specified level α (default 0.1). The method is dimension-insensitive: regardless of gradient dimension d, each W_j is a single scalar, and no asymptotic p-value approximation is needed.

## Method

ByMI applies a sample-splitting strategy on each worker machine: local data is divided into two equal halves, and empirical gradients are computed on each half. A robust mean estimator (geometric median, filtering algorithm, or first-order method) is applied to the first-half gradients to obtain a robust center estimate ĝ. The ranking score for machine j is then the cross-product W_j = (g_1j − ĝ)ᵀΩ(g_2j − ĝ), where Ω is a scale or projection matrix. The key insight is that for normal machines, W_j is asymptotically symmetric around zero (by independence of the two splits and the CLT), while Byzantine machines produce large positive W_j values. This symmetry allows a data-driven threshold L to be set by matching the empirical count of negative scores to the target FDR level, eliminating the need for p-values or knowledge of the asymptotic distribution. The procedure is p-value-free, dimension-agnostic, and achieves finite-sample FDR control under mild moment and robust-estimation accuracy conditions.

## Results

Under the OOD attack (ϱ=0.1), ByMI-Filter achieves FDR of 6.8% on MNIST, 7.1% on F-MNIST, and 5.5% on CIFAR-10, all well below the nominal α=0.1, while maintaining TPR of 98.1%, 92.8%, and 81.7% respectively. Under the more aggressive IPM attack, ByMI-Filter further improves to FDR of 6.3% and TPR of 99.6% on MNIST, and achieves perfect detection (FDR 5.4%, TPR 100%, P_a 100%) on CIFAR-10. In contrast, RMDP-BH inflates FDR to 88.3% (MNIST OOD) and 89.2% (MNIST IPM), while distance-based methods (Krum, FABA, Zeno) see their TPR collapse from ~94% to ~15% under the IPM attack.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ByMI-Filter and ByMI-Filter+ dominate all four baselines on FDR control: they maintain FDR at or below the nominal α=0.1 across all three datasets and both attack types, whereas RMDP-BH inflates FDR to 54–89% and Krum/FABA/Zeno to 25–96%. On TPR, ByMI-Filter matches or exceeds RMDP-BH (e.g., 99.6% vs. 96.2% on MNIST under IPM) while achieving dramatically better FDR. Under the IPM attack, the distance-based baselines collapse: Krum drops to 31.9% TPR, FABA to 14.8%, and Zeno to 15.0% on MNIST, while ByMI-Filter sustains 99.6% TPR. On P_a (sure-detection probability), ByMI-Filter reaches 99.6% (MNIST IPM) and 100% (CIFAR-10 IPM), compared to 22.6% (Krum), 8.2% (FABA), and 7.8% (Zeno) on MNIST IPM. RMDP-BH achieves high TPR and P_a but at the cost of flagging nearly all machines as Byzantine (FDR ≈ 89%), making it practically useless for selective identification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Byzantine-robust distributed learning (Krum, FABA, Zeno, median-type and filtering aggregators) and statistical outlier detection with multiple-testing control. Prior Byzantine detection methods either fix the number of Byzantine machines a priori (distance-based scores in Krum/FABA/Zeno) or rely on asymptotic p-values from modified Mahalanobis distances (RMDP-BH), both of which lack finite-sample error-rate guarantees. ByMI departs from both by importing the sample-splitting symmetry trick from change-point detection (Chen et al., 2023) into the Byzantine setting: splitting each machine's data, computing a cross-product score, and calibrating the threshold from the empirical distribution of negative scores rather than an asymptotic null. This makes the procedure p-value free and dimension-insensitive while remaining compatible with any robust mean estimator.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ByMI provides the first systematic FDR-controlled framework for Byzantine machine identification, filling a gap the authors identify between robust aggregation (which hides Byzantine machines) and detection (which names them). The communication cost is minimal—only two gradient vectors per machine—making it a practical plug-in for federated learning pipelines. Key limitations include the reliance on sample-splitting (halving effective data per machine for detection), the assumption of a Huber contamination model, and the dependence of FDR guarantees on the quality of the underlying robust estimator. Open questions raised by the authors include adapting ByMI to more aggressive attack models (e.g., transmission-path attacks) and eliminating the sample-splitting requirement to use full data.
