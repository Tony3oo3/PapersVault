## Problem

Current knowledge editing methods and benchmarks focus almost exclusively on whether new target knowledge is successfully memorized, while largely ignoring whether the editing operation perturbs neighboring knowledge. This paper investigates whether appending a new answer to a factual question's answer list causes catastrophic forgetting of original correct answers or unintentional inclusion of incorrect answers — a phenomenon the authors term 'neighboring perturbation' and quantify via a new metric called additivity.

## Input / Output

Each editing instance in the PEAK benchmark is a structured tuple comprising an editing fact (subject, relation, original correct answer set O, new object o*, and an editing prompt), paraphrase prompts for generalization, locality prompts for out-of-scope checks, and two lists of false answers (Hard: semantically close to o*; Random: semantically distant). The APP framework consumes all of these during editing to jointly optimize the original editing objective with preservation and prevention losses, producing updated model parameters. Evaluation reports both the standard ES/GS/LS metrics and the new AFF/ANF additivity scores computed over both editing and paraphrase prompts.

## Method

The paper introduces the PEAK benchmark (PEAK-CF for counterfactual edits from Wikidata, PEAK-T for temporal edits from YAGO) and a plug-and-play framework called APP (Appending via Preservation and Prevention). APP adds three auxiliary loss objectives to any existing editing method's loss: (1) a hinge loss L1 that enforces a margin between the log-probabilities of correct and false answers, (2) a one-sided loss L2 that penalizes any decrease in correct answer probabilities relative to the unedited model, and (3) a one-sided loss L3 that penalizes any increase in false answer probabilities. These are jointly optimized with the base editing objective (e.g., ROME's constrained least-squares, MEND's hypernetwork gradient) via weighted combination with hyperparameters α, β, γ. The additivity metric itself combines relative ranking factors (RFF, RNF) with absolute probability change ratios (CPC, FPC) into aggregated scores AFF and ANF, each spanning [0,1] where higher values indicate greater perturbation.

## Results

On PEAK-CF with LLaMA-2 (7B), existing editing methods achieve high efficacy (ROME: 99.69% ES, MEMIT: 99.95% ES) and generalization scores, yet suffer severe neighboring perturbations: ROME records 93.05% AFF and 82.47% ANF (Hard setting), and MEMIT records 94.26% AFF and 84.14% ANF, indicating near-total forgetting of original correct answers and massive introduction of false answers. Coupling APP with ROME reduces AFF from 93.05% to 43.11% and ANF from 82.47% to 20.14% while maintaining 100% ES; MEMIT+APP reduces AFF from 94.26% to 38.83% and ANF from 84.14% to 17.41%. MEND already shows the best additivity among baselines (35.39% AFF, 33.51% ANF) but at the cost of the worst locality (47.47% LS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ROME and MEMIT achieve the highest efficacy and generalization (≈100% ES, >97% GS) and strong locality (>92% LS), but they inflict the most severe neighboring perturbations: ROME records 93.05% AFF / 82.47% ANF (Hard) and MEMIT records 94.26% / 84.14%, meaning nearly all original correct answers are forgotten and a large fraction of false answers are introduced. APP coupled with ROME cuts AFF by 50 percentage points (93.05→43.11) and ANF by 62 points (82.47→20.14) while preserving 100% ES; MEMIT+APP achieves the best additivity among APP variants (38.83% AFF, 17.41% ANF Hard). MEND is the only baseline with inherently low additivity (35.39% AFF, 33.51% ANF) but pays a heavy locality cost (47.47% LS vs. >92% for ROME/MEMIT); MEND+APP marginally improves additivity (32.55/29.73) and locality (51.43%). FT shows moderate efficacy (98.27% ES) but high perturbation (74.51% AFF); FT+APP reduces AFF to 68.44% and improves LS from 68.48% to 73.55%. KN is not coupled with APP (no differentiable loss) and performs poorly on efficacy (46.67% ES), making it the weakest baseline overall.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge editing literature (ROME, MEMIT, MEND, KN, FT) by identifying a previously unexamined failure mode: when appending a new answer to a multi-answer factual question, existing methods severely perturb the probability distributions of neighboring correct and false answers. Prior benchmarks (MQUAKE, BAKE, EasyEdit) evaluate efficacy, generalization, and locality, but none assess whether the act of adding knowledge corrupts the existing answer list. The paper carves new ground by introducing the additivity metric (AFF/ANF), the PEAK benchmark, and the plug-and-play APP framework, which repurposes hinge-loss and probability-preservation objectives to constrain editing without requiring architectural changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that methods scoring 99%+ on efficacy and 90%+ on locality still produce 93%+ Additive Forgetting Factor challenges the field's implicit assumption that high locality implies neighboring knowledge is safe. This opens a new evaluation axis for knowledge editing that practitioners should adopt before deploying edits in production. Limitations include the restriction to factual (s,r,o) knowledge, probability-based (rather than generation-based) evaluation, and parameter-modifying methods only; extending to commonsense/logical knowledge, black-box APIs, and output-level metrics remains open.
