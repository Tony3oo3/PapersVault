## Problem

Sampling from unnormalized target densities without access to gradients or scores remains a key challenge in Bayesian inference and data assimilation. Existing dynamic samplers based on Wasserstein gradient flows must in principle run for infinite time, and most practical alternatives (SVGD, Langevin-based methods) require gradient evaluations. This paper asks whether one can design a gradient-free, closed-form, finite-time (unit-time) transport scheme that only needs the unnormalized density ratio π₁/π₀.

## Input / Output

The method requires only three ingredients: (i) i.i.d. samples from a reference distribution π₀ (typically standard Gaussian), (ii) pointwise evaluation of the unnormalized log-density ratio log(π₁/π₀) (the 'likelihood'), and (iii) a choice of symmetric positive-definite kernel K (inverse multiquadric in experiments). No normalizing constants, gradients, or Hessians of π₀ or π₁ are needed, and no training or optimization step is involved. The output is an ensemble of J samples from π₁ obtained by simulating the interacting particle ODE (or its discrete-time variant) over the interval t ∈ [0,1].

## Method

The paper derives a mean-field ODE whose solution transports samples from π₀ to π₁ in unit time along the geometric mixture path π_t ∝ π₀^(1-t) π₁^t, which is the Fisher-Rao gradient flow of the expected negative log-likelihood. The velocity field v_t is obtained by solving a Poisson equation −∇·(π_t ∇u_t) = ∂_t π_t under an RKHS ansatz u_t(·) = ∫ K(·,x) f_t(x) dπ_t(x), yielding a closed-form kernelized expression involving a J×J matrix M_t built from kernel gradient inner products. Two equivalent interacting particle systems result: KFRFlow (continuous-time, from Monte Carlo approximation of the RKHS Poisson solution) and KFRFlow-I (discrete-time, from a single Newton step on the linearized Monge-Ampère equation within the sample-driven optimal transport framework of Kuang & Tabak, 2019). A stochastic variant KFRD augments the ODE with a Fokker-Planck noise term ε∇log π_t to improve numerical stability. The key conceptual contribution is 'Wasserstein-izing' the Fisher-Rao path: rather than following the Fisher-Rao geometry directly, the paper finds a gradient velocity field in a kernel space that collectively transports particles along the same path, bridging Fisher-Rao gradient flows and kernelized optimal transport.

## Results

The paper evaluates sampling quality using Kernel Stein Discrepancy (KSD, lower is better) averaged over 30 trials, with ensemble sizes J ∈ {25, 50, 100, 200, 400} and step counts N ∈ {2¹, …, 2⁸} for 2D posteriors, and J = 100 with N = 100 steps for funnels at d ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20}. In the 2D Bayesian posterior experiments (donut, butterfly, spaceships), KFRFlow and KFRFlow-I consistently achieve lower KSD than the gradient-free baselines EKI, EKS, and CBS, with KFRFlow-I remaining stable across all (J, N) settings even when gradient-based KFRD and SVGD become unstable. In the higher-dimensional funnel experiments, KFRFlow-I outperforms CBS at all dimensions, KFRD is competitive with SVGD and ULA, and notably the KSD of KFRFlow-I and CBS does not degrade with dimension while gradient-based methods (SVGD, ULA, KFRD) show increasing KSD as d grows.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against EKI (Iglesias et al., 2013), KFRFlow and KFRFlow-I achieve lower KSD across most (J, N) settings in all three 2D posteriors, with KFRFlow-I being the only method stable at every configuration. Against EKS (Garbuno-Inigo et al., 2020a), KFRFlow and KFRFlow-I similarly outperform in KSD, though EKS is not applicable to the funnel benchmark. Against CBS (Carrillo et al., 2022), KFRFlow-I produces better samples in both 2D posteriors and funnels, and both gradient-free methods show dimension-robust KSD while CBS's advantage in computational cost (lower per-step runtime) is offset by inferior sample quality. Against SVGD (Liu & Wang, 2016), the gradient-based KFRD is generally outperformed by SVGD in 2D, but KFRFlow and KFRFlow-I (gradient-free) achieve comparable or better KSD than SVGD for small N (≤ 8 steps). Against ULA (Roberts & Tweedie, 1996), KFRD is competitive at all funnel dimensions, while in 2D KFRFlow-I outperforms serial RWM for N > 8 steps. Against RWM (Robert & Casella, 2004), KFRFlow-I beats serial RWM in 2D for N > 8 and in funnels for d > 5, approaching parallel RWM quality by d = 20, while parallel RWM benefits from multiple initializations for small N.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads: the Fisher-Rao gradient flow of the negative log-likelihood (which naturally produces the geometric mixture path π_t ∝ π_0^{1-t}π_1^t, known from annealed importance sampling and the filtering work of Daum & Huang 2011 and Reich 2011) and the kernelized Poisson equation approach for velocity fields (from Laugesen et al. 2015 and Reich 2011). The key conceptual departure is from Wasserstein-geometry-based samplers (SVGD, EKI, EKS) that require infinite-time integration: by working in Fisher-Rao geometry, transport is achieved in unit time. The discrete-time variant (KFRFlow-I) is derived independently by linearizing Monge-Ampère equations within the sample-driven optimal transport framework of Kuang & Tabak (2019), and Theorem 4.1 shows the two derivations converge to the same ODE in continuous time—a connection between SMC-style importance weighting and Fisher-Rao flows that had not been made explicit before.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The gradient-free, closed-form, unit-time nature of KFRFlow makes it directly applicable to settings where target gradients are inaccessible (complex physical models, data assimilation with sample-only priors), a practical need that Wasserstein-based methods and score-based approaches cannot address. The O(J³) per-step cost and the gap between the target path π_t and the actual sample distribution ρ_t (due to the RKHS ansatz) are significant limitations for high-dimensional problems, and the paper acknowledges that kernel approximations, dimension reduction, and adaptive time-stepping are needed for practical scaling. An intriguing open question is why KFRFlow-I's sample quality appears dimension-independent in the funnel experiments, which if confirmed could suggest a fundamentally different scaling behavior than gradient-based methods.
