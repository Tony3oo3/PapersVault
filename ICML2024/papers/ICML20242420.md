## Problem

Equivariant GNNs like EGNN suffer from quadratic message-passing complexity on fully-connected geometric graphs, making them impractical for large-scale physical simulations with tens of thousands of particles. Reducing to sparse local graphs for efficiency causes significant accuracy degradation, as global interactions are lost. The paper asks whether a small set of learned virtual nodes can restore global information flow while enabling aggressive edge sparsification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242420/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a geometric graph with N real nodes, each carrying 3D coordinates, invariant feature vectors, and velocities, along with a sparse edge set (optionally with edge features). It outputs predicted 3D positions after a fixed time step Δt. A key design constraint is that the C virtual nodes are initialized at the center-of-mass of the real nodes and are learned end-to-end; the edge set can be progressively sparsified by dropping the longest-distance edges up to 100%, with the virtual nodes compensating for lost real-real interactions.

## Method

FastEGNN augments the EGNN backbone with C virtual nodes treated as an ordered set, where each virtual node has distinct parameters and a dedicated aggregation function to enforce mutual distinctiveness. Virtual-virtual messages are computed as an E(3)-invariant inner-product matrix of center-translated virtual coordinates, while real-virtual messages use per-virtual-node MLPs over squared distances. A Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) loss with an E(3)-invariant RBF kernel regularizes the virtual coordinates to match the real coordinate distribution, ensuring global distributedness. Theoretically, Proposition 4.3 proves that the ordered virtual-node update function can universally approximate the full real-node message, explaining why FastEGNN maintains accuracy even when all real edges are dropped. The overall loss combines MSE on predicted positions with the MMD term, and the entire pipeline preserves E(3)-equivariance by construction.

## Results

On N-body System (~100 nodes), FastEGNN-⟨3,0.75⟩ achieves the best MSE of 1.10×10⁻² with only 0.35× the inference time of EGNN (1.41×10⁻²). On Protein Dynamics (~800 nodes), FastEGNN-⟨3,0.00⟩ reduces MSE from 2.25 (EGNN) to 1.82, and even with all edges dropped (p=1.00) maintains MSE 1.88 at 0.80× inference time. On the most challenging Water-3D (~7806 particles), FastEGNN-⟨3,0.00⟩ achieves MSE 2.49×10⁻⁴ versus EGNN's 6.00×10⁻⁴, and FastEGNN-⟨3,1.00⟩ still reaches 3.40×10⁻⁴ at 0.42× inference time, far outperforming EGNN* (12.38×10⁻⁴) with all edges removed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FastEGNN consistently outperforms EGNN (the strongest baseline) across all three datasets: by 28% MSE on N-body (1.12 vs 1.41), 19% on Protein (1.82 vs 2.25), and 58% on Water-3D (2.49 vs 6.00). Critically, even when all real-real edges are dropped (p=1.00), FastEGNN still beats full EGNN on Protein (1.88 vs 2.25) and Water-3D (3.40 vs 6.00), whereas EGNN* (EGNN with no edges) degrades to near-Linear performance (MSE 2.26 on Protein, 12.38 on Water-3D). Against TFN, FastEGNN is both more accurate on N-body (1.12 vs 1.62) and ~16× faster in inference (1.07× vs 17.02× relative time). FastEGNN also dominates the invariant SchNet (MSE 1.82 vs 2.56 on Protein) and the non-equivariant MPNN (1.82 vs 150.56 on Protein), confirming the importance of equivariance. The generality experiments (Table 3) show that adding virtual nodes to RF, TFN, and SchNet backbones (FastRF-3, FastTFN-3, FastSchNet-3) similarly improves each over its base model across all edge-dropping rates, validating the virtual-node strategy as backbone-agnostic.

## Relation to Prior Work

FastEGNN extends the EGNN backbone (Satorras et al., 2021) by introducing a set of learned virtual nodes that mediate global message passing, drawing on the virtual-node concept from conventional GNNs (Gilmer et al., 2017; Pham et al., 2017) but adapting it to the E(3)-equivariant geometric graph setting. It departs from prior geometric virtual-node approaches (SGNN, MEAN) that rely on externally defined clusters or a single global mean node, instead learning an ordered set of virtual nodes end-to-end with two explicit geometric objectives: mutual distinctiveness (via ordered channels and separate aggregation functions) and global distributedness (via an E(3)-invariant MMD loss). The theoretical expressivity analysis builds on the universal equivariant function characterizations of Villar et al. (2021) and Huang et al. (2022), showing that the ordered virtual-node formulation admits a more expressive update function than the permutation-invariant real-node case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical scalability bottleneck for equivariant GNNs in physical simulation, where fully-connected message passing incurs quadratic complexity. The finding that a handful of learned virtual nodes can largely compensate for dropped edges (especially in protein dynamics, where MSE barely changes from 1.82 to 1.88 when all edges are removed) suggests a practical path toward near-linear equivariant simulation of large systems. Limitations include validation on only three datasets, a small virtual-node count (C ≤ 10), and the observation that the edge-dropping benefit is dataset-dependent (N-body still degrades significantly at p=1). Open questions include whether the approach scales to 100k+ particle systems, whether the MMD regularization generalizes to higher-dimensional or non-Euclidean symmetries, and how the method interacts with attention-based equivariant architectures.
