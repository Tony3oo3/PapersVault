## Problem

Semiparametric estimation procedures that achieve near-optimal statistical efficiency typically require solving Fredholm integral equations of the second kind, which generally lack closed-form solutions and depend on the unknown parameter of interest. Traditional numerical solvers (polynomials, splines) fail to scale to multi-dimensional problems, while approximating the integral equations with closed-form alternatives yields statistically suboptimal or even inconsistent estimators. No scalable, statistically faithful numerical solver existed for these integral equations within semiparametric frameworks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N i.i.d. observed data samples {O_i} (tabular), pre-drawn Monte Carlo samples {t_l}_{l=1}^{J_1} and {s_j}_{j=1}^{J_2} for approximating the outer and inner integrals in the loss L_K, and the known kernel K and function C (specified up to the parameter β). It outputs the low-dimensional parameter estimate β̂ ∈ R^q and the DNN weights ω̂ that encode the approximate solution b̂(·) to the integral equations. The integrals in L_K are evaluated numerically via Monte Carlo quadrature, with J_1 and J_2 controlling the approximation error; the paper recommends setting them as large as computational resources allow.

## Method

DNA-SE formulates semiparametric estimation as a bi-level optimization problem: an outer loss L_ψ (the squared empirical estimating function) is minimized over β, subject to an inner loss L_K (the squared integral-equation residual) being minimized over a feedforward DNN b(·;ω) that approximates the integral equation solution b*(·). The bi-level structure is solved via Alternating Stochastic Gradient Descent, which alternates one GD step on β (outer) with γ GD steps on ω (inner), where γ is an 'alternating frequency' hyperparameter balancing convergence speed and stability. The DNN uses tanh activations and a standard MLP architecture, leveraging the universal approximation property to represent the infinite-dimensional solution b*(·) without requiring closed-form solutions. This approach differs from prior work that uses DNNs to estimate nuisance parameters directly; here the DNN serves purely as a numerical solver for the integral equations, while the parameter of interest is estimated through the nested optimization structure.

## Results

Across three simulation studies, DNA-SE consistently achieves lower bias and variance than polynomial approximations and grid-based methods. In the causal inference sensitivity analysis (10-dimensional covariates), DNA-SE yields a mean estimation error of 0.036 (SE 0.155) versus 0.109 (SE 0.426) for the grid-based method of Zhang & Tchetgen Tchetgen (2022), a ~67% reduction in bias and ~63% reduction in variance. In the MNAR regression example, DNA-SE achieves bias 0.012 (SE 0.142) for β₁, outperforming all polynomial degrees (bias ranging from −0.033 to −0.143) and matching Zhao & Ma (2022) in bias while reducing SE from 0.209 to 0.142. In transfer learning, DNA-SE reduces the standard error by at least 55% compared to the best polynomial (quintic: SE 0.0084 vs. 0.0186).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DNA-SE is compared against two primary baselines: the Atkinson (1967) polynomial approximation used in Zhao & Ma (2022) for MNAR, and the grid-based method from Zhang & Tchetgen Tchetgen (2022) for causal inference sensitivity analysis. In the MNAR setting, DNA-SE matches Zhao & Ma (2022) in bias (0.012 vs. −0.016 for β₁) while reducing the standard error by ~32% (0.142 vs. 0.209); it substantially outperforms all polynomial degrees (quadratic through quintic), which exhibit biases up to 10× larger. In the causal inference setting, DNA-SE dominates the grid-based method on both bias (0.036 vs. 0.109) and variance (SE 0.155 vs. 0.426), while also being faster (~30 min vs. ~35 min per run). On the real Connecticut Children's Mental Health dataset, DNA-SE produces point estimates nearly identical to Zhao & Ma (2022) for all four regression coefficients, with uniformly smaller bootstrap standard errors (e.g., 0.0761 vs. 0.1194 for β_z), while the observed-only estimator is clearly biased (e.g., β₁ = 0.365 vs. −0.062). Overall, DNA-SE wins on every comparison, with the largest gains in the higher-dimensional causal inference problem.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in recent semiparametric statistics literature (Farrell et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2022), where DNNs are deployed to approximate infinite-dimensional nuisance parameters, and instead repurposes DNNs as numerical solvers for the Fredholm integral equations of the second kind that arise in semiparametric efficient estimation. It draws on the scientific computing tradition of using neural networks to solve PDEs and integral equations (E & Yu, 2018; Raissi et al., 2019) and the bi-level optimization literature (Finn et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2021), transplanting these tools into a statistical estimation context where the integral equations depend on the unknown parameter of interest. The specific estimators applied (Zhao & Ma, 2022; Zhang & Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2022; Qiu et al., 2023) are taken as given; the contribution is a new computational engine for them rather than new estimators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation is compelling: traditional polynomial and spline approximations to integral equations struggle in multi-dimensional settings, and the paper demonstrates that DNA-SE achieves lower bias and variance than polynomials of varying degrees across three distinct semiparametric problems. However, the paper explicitly defers convergence analysis of the alternating GD algorithm and formal statistical guarantees (asymptotic normality, convergence rates) to future work, leaving the method's theoretical foundations incomplete. Broader open questions include whether the framework can be fully automated (e.g., via LLMs or differentiable programming) to eliminate the need for hand-derived estimators, and whether consensus-based or other accelerated optimization strategies can improve the practical convergence behavior of the alternating scheme.
