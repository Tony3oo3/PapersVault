## Problem

Out-of-distribution generalization algorithms require environment annotations to identify invariant versus spurious patterns, but these annotations are costly, biased, and algorithm-specific. Existing automatic environment discovery methods still depend on human-annotated validation sets for early-stopping and hyperparameter tuning, undermining their purpose. This paper asks whether environments can be discovered entirely without human supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241646/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

XRM consumes only labeled training pairs (x_i, y_i) and labeled validation pairs (x̃_i, ỹ_i) with no environment or group annotations. It outputs a binary environment label e_i ∈ {0,1} for every training and validation example, computed via a cross-mistake formula: an example is assigned to environment 1 if either twin misclassifies it (logical OR over both twins' predictions). Because each twin holds out a random half of the data, every example is held-out for at least one twin, guaranteeing full coverage of both training and validation sets without any additional annotation pass.

## Method

XRM initializes two twin classifiers f^a and f^b, each trained on a random half of the training data (the other half is held-out for that twin). During training, each twin minimizes a class-balanced cross-entropy loss on its held-in examples, but with a label-flipping mechanism: at each iteration, the label y_i is replaced by the sibling's held-out predicted class with probability proportional to the sibling's confidence in that wrong prediction (Equation 2). This 'echo chamber' progressively steers both twins toward relying on spurious correlations, so that at convergence they confidently misclassify the same minority examples. Model selection uses the percentage of flipped labels at the final iteration as the criterion (no early-stopping). Finally, the cross-mistake formula e(x,y) = [[y ∉ argmax f^a(x)] ∨ [y ∉ argmax f^b(x)]] assigns binary environment labels to all examples, which are then fed to any downstream OOD algorithm such as GroupDRO.

## Results

On the four standard sub-population shift benchmarks (Waterbirds, CelebA, MultiNLI, CivilComments), XRM-discovered environments yield an average worst-group-accuracy of 80.4% with GroupDRO, closely matching the 80.6% achieved with human-annotated environments (vs. 67.3% with no annotations). On the challenging ColorMNIST dataset, XRM+GroupDRO achieves 69.5% worst-group-accuracy, dramatically outperforming both ERM (10.1%) and human-annotated GroupDRO (10.2%), as XRM perfectly identifies digits in minority colors. Across all seven datasets and four OOD algorithms, XRM achieves an average worst-group-accuracy of 77.3% with GroupDRO, exceeding the 70.8% from human annotations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

XRM requires no human environment annotations at all, yet achieves 79.4% average worst-group-accuracy (Table 2), nearly matching the oracle 81.4% from human annotations. Among methods also requiring no annotations, XRM substantially outperforms JTT (58.9%), LfF (56.9%), and EIIL (not fully reported), while slightly trailing BAM (79.8%) by 0.4 points. The best method that does access human-annotated validation data, AFR, achieves 78.6%—still below XRM's 79.4%—and LS is computationally prohibitive (20 hours per run on Waterbirds vs. 10 minutes for XRM). On the DomainBed benchmark (Table 3), CORAL with XRM-inferred environments matches or slightly exceeds CORAL with human annotations on worst-environment accuracy for VLCS (66.15 vs. 65.00), PACS (77.30 vs. 77.70), OfficeHome (53.90 vs. 53.55), and TerraIncognita (38.00 vs. 37.15), while trailing on DomainNet (11.60 vs. 13.25). On the Dominoes dataset, XRM+GroupDRO achieves 86.68% worst-group-accuracy on MF, exceeding ground-truth annotations+GroupDRO (85.28%), and is competitive on MC (68.78% vs. 69.43%).

## Relation to Prior Work

XRM departs from the entire line of environment discovery methods (LfF, EIIL, JTT, CnC, AFR, LS, ARL) by eliminating their shared dependency on human-annotated validation sets for capacity control and early-stopping. Instead of surgically limiting classifier capacity, XRM deliberately amplifies spurious correlations through a label-flipping 'echo chamber' between twin networks trained on complementary data halves, drawing on the held-out error construction of Feldman & Zhang (2020) and the data-splitting idea of CrossSplit (Kim et al., 2023). The method is the first environment discovery algorithm to tamper with training labels rather than only adjusting loss functions or regularization, and it provides a self-contained hyperparameter selection criterion (counting label flips) that removes the need for any external validation signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the requirement for human environment annotations entirely, XRM addresses the primary practical bottleneck preventing large-scale deployment of OOD generalization algorithms, achieving oracle-level worst-group-accuracy across seven sub-population shift benchmarks and five DomainBed datasets. The paper honestly delineates failure modes: XRM succeeds when the invariant feature is the more complex (slower-to-learn) one, but cannot distinguish observationally-equivalent causal structures (e.g., CMNIST vs. MCOLOR), echoing impossibility results from Lin et al. (2022a). Open questions include the theoretical underpinnings of label-flipping (which steers away from Bayes-optimal predictors), the relationship to memorization phenomena, and whether the two-environment split suffices for problems with many underlying environments.
