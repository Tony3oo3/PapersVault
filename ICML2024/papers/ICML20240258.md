## Problem

Traditional statistical learning frameworks (PAC, classification) treat data as object-label pairs, overlooking the rich relational structure between objects that Foundation Models demonstrably capture during pre-training. This paper asks two questions that have no prior mathematical treatment: (1) is the relational structure of the world identifiable from pre-training data at all, and (2) what is the minimum data required for ε-approximate relational learning? The gap is the absence of a formal model that separates relational learning from label prediction and connects it to concrete pre-training algorithms.

## Input / Output

The framework abstracts data as i.i.d. samples drawn from hyperedges of a world hypergraph, where each sample is a perception (text sequence, image) of a relation between entities; in the MM setting, each sample is further masked to produce (e, e⁻) training pairs. The output is a recovered weighted hypergraph (entities as nodes, relations as hyperedges with normalized weights) plus, in the multimodal extension, a bijection φ aligning entities across modalities. The dissimilarity between recovered and true hypergraphs is measured by an L1 distance over hyperedge weights, and the key learned quantity is the set of relative weight ratios between hyperedges that share a common masked version.

## Method

The paper formalizes relational learning as hypergraph recovery: the world is a weighted hypergraph H₀ = (V₀, E₀, w₀) with normalized weights, and data are i.i.d. samples from hyperedges proportional to w₀. They prove asymptotic identifiability via the law of large numbers and establish a minimax lower bound of Ω(m/ε²) for ε-approximate recovery by reducing from discrete distribution estimation under L1 distance. For the matching upper bound, they show that Masked Modeling achieves Õ(m/ε²) sample complexity: the MM model learns relative weight ratios w̃(e₁)/w̃(e₂) for hyperedges sharing a masked version e⁻, and these ratios are chained along bounded-length paths (length ≤ L) in the masking graph to recover the full weight function up to normalization. The framework extends to multimodal entity alignment by recovering hypergraphs in each modality and solving a graph isomorphism problem, where labeled cross-modal pairs reduce the computational complexity from the general graph isomorphism problem to polynomial time.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper establishes an information-theoretic lower bound of Ω(m/ε²) on the sample complexity for ε-approximate relational learning of a hypergraph with m hyperedges (Theorem 5.2), and shows that Masked Modeling (MM) achieves the near-optimal upper bound Õ(m/ε²) (Theorem 5.6), matching the lower bound up to logarithmic factors. Empirically, on real-world relation evaluation using ConceptNet subgraphs (k=2, d=3), GPT-4 achieves the lowest average normalized L₁ dissimilarity (≈0.98) across 10 source entities, followed by GPT-3.5 (≈0.98) and LLAMA-2-70B (≈1.11), with GPT-4 attaining the best individual scores on 5 out of 10 entities. Synthetic experiments confirm that reconstruction error decreases with training and that larger numbers of edges and longer MM path lengths slow convergence, consistent with the theoretical sample complexity dependence on m and L.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new method to benchmark against existing baselines; instead, it evaluates three LLMs (LLAMA-2-70B, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) as candidate pre-trained models whose internalized relational knowledge is probed via subgraph reconstruction. GPT-4 achieves the best overall performance, attaining the lowest dissimilarity on 5 of 10 source entities (CAKE, DOG, PAPER, SEA, TABLE) and the most consistent scores across entities. GPT-3.5 performs slightly better than LLAMA-2-70B on average (≈0.98 vs. ≈1.11 normalized L₁ distance), with GPT-3.5 winning on 6 of 10 entities. LLAMA-2-70B shows the highest variance, ranging from 0.67 (DOG) to 1.67 (ZOO), suggesting less reliable relational learning. The authors note that more powerful models exhibit better alignment with real-world entity relations, supporting the hypothesis that pre-training via MM progressively recovers the world's relational hypergraph.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground by formalizing pre-training as hypergraph recovery, drawing on combinatorial statistics tools (random graph isomorphism, shotgun assembly) that have previously been applied to DNA assembly and social network privacy but not to PTM analysis. It departs from existing PTM theory (multi-task learning, meta-learning, in-context learning) by explicitly modeling generalizable knowledge as a relational hypergraph rather than invariant representations or internalized optimization algorithms. The information-theoretic lower bound is derived via a reduction from the minimax discrete distribution estimation problem of Han et al. (2015), bridging classical statistical estimation with the PTM literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, minimax-optimal lens for understanding what PTMs actually learn during pre-training—relational structure rather than just label prediction—which is a gap the authors argue traditional PAC-style analysis overlooks. The near-optimality of Masked Modeling for relational recovery (matching the lower bound up to log factors) gives theoretical justification for a widely-used pre-training objective. However, the framework relies on idealized assumptions (bijective perception mapping, bounded masking support, bounded path length), and the empirical validation is modest (small synthetic graphs, shallow LLM queries on 10 entities), leaving open whether the theory transfers to the full complexity of real-world knowledge graphs and whether the bijection assumption is realistic for multimodal settings.
