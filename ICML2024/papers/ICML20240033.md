## Problem

Existing model problems for studying in-context learning (ICL) in language models—such as in-context linear regression or associative recall—require only simple forms of learning (computing a fixed function or retrieving a single example), leaving a significant gap with the richer, structured generative ICL exhibited by large LMs. This paper introduces in-context language learning (ICLL) as a new family of model problems where models must infer a probabilistic distribution over strings from a formal language given only a finite set of examples, better exercising the algorithmic reasoning and structured prediction that characterize natural ICL.

## Input / Output

The model receives a context consisting of 10–20 strings (each 1–50 symbols long) sampled from a randomly generated probabilistic finite automaton with 4–12 states and a 4–18 symbol alphabet, separated by delimiter tokens. The model must produce a next-token probability distribution at each position, enabling iterative sampling of new strings from the same language. Evaluation uses both greedy-decoding accuracy (whether the predicted token is valid under the true language) and total variation distance between the predicted and true next-token distributions, with the REGBENCH benchmark providing disjoint train/test PFAs to ensure genuine in-context generalization.

## Method

The paper introduces REGBENCH, a benchmark of in-context language learning problems generated from randomly sampled probabilistic finite automata, and uses it to show that Transformers significantly outperform recurrent, convolutional, and linear-attention models at ICLL. Through attention visualization, probing, and input-output analysis, the authors identify that Transformers succeed by developing 'n-gram heads'—higher-order variants of induction heads that compute in-context n-gram statistics (counts and frequencies of matching token sequences) rather than inferring the true automaton structure. Building on this insight, they propose hard-wiring static n-gram heads (NGH) with fixed attention patterns that match n-gram prefixes and apply learned linear transformations to attended representations. Inserting NGH layers into RetNet, GLA, and Transformer architectures brings non-Transformer models to Transformer-level ICLL performance and reduces perplexity by up to 6.7% on 340M-parameter language models trained on SlimPajama, demonstrating that the n-gram computation is both necessary and sufficient for strong in-context language learning.

## Results

On REGBENCH, Transformers significantly outperform all recurrent (LSTM, RWKV, GLA, Mamba) and convolutional (S4, H3, Hyena) sequence models on both TVD and accuracy metrics, with most non-Transformer models underperforming even simple in-context n-gram and Baum-Welch baselines. Hard-wiring n-gram heads into RetNet reduces TVD from 0.392 to 0.217 (accuracy 0.800→0.94), and into GLA from 0.624 to 0.207 (accuracy 0.526→0.946), matching or exceeding the Transformer baseline (TVD 0.203, accuracy 0.926). On natural language modeling (340M params, SlimPajama), adding n-gram heads reduces Transformer perplexity from 16.96 to 15.82 (6.7% improvement), RetNet from 16.55 to 15.86 (4.2%), and GLA from 15.65 to 15.24 (2.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On REGBENCH, the standard Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) substantially outperforms all 9 non-Transformer architectures (RetNet, Linear Transformer, LSTM, RWKV, GLA, Mamba, S4, H3, Hyena) across all training set sizes, with the gap most pronounced in the low-data regime. Most non-Transformer models also underperform the simple in-context n-gram baseline (Chen & Goodman, 1996) and the Baum-Welch HMM (Rabiner, 1989), which use only within-instance statistics without cross-example learning. The associative recall task (Fu et al., 2023) provides less contrast between architectures, motivating REGBENCH as a more discriminative benchmark. When n-gram heads are hard-wired in, GLA (Yang et al., 2023) actually exceeds the Transformer in accuracy (0.946 vs. 0.926) while matching it in TVD, and RetNet (Sun et al., 2023) reaches comparable performance. On SlimPajama, the Llama-based Transformer (Touvron et al., 2023) benefits most from n-gram heads (6.7% perplexity reduction), while GLA sees a smaller 2.6% gain, suggesting the improvement is architecture-dependent but universally positive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the ICL model-problem literature (linear regression, associative recall, few-shot classification) by introducing in-context language learning (ICLL) as a new task family grounded in formal language theory. It builds directly on Olsson et al.'s (2022) induction heads, showing that the attention patterns enabling ICLL are higher-order generalizations of that motif. The formal-language framing departs from prior neural sequence model studies (Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023; Bhattamishra et al., 2020) that ask whether models can recognize a fixed language, instead asking whether they can meta-learn to adapt to a novel language provided in context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean mechanistic account of why Transformers dominate ICLL (in-context n-gram statistics via specialized attention heads) and translates that insight into a practical architectural component (n-gram heads) that improves perplexity by up to 6.7% on natural language. REGBENCH offers a reusable benchmark that better differentiates architectures than associative recall. Open questions include whether the n-gram mechanism generalizes to context-free or context-sensitive languages, how ICLL learning dynamics unfold during training, and whether the approach scales to larger models or more complex language families.
