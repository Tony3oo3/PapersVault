## Problem

No single adaptive optimizer uniformly outperforms Adam across all learning tasks, batch sizes, and training regimes, yet existing alternatives (Lion, Adan, Yogi, etc.) each excel only in specific settings. The paper asks whether one can unify a collection of optimizers into a single parameterized framework and dynamically learn the best interpolation on-the-fly during training, eliminating the need for offline optimizer search or manual hyperparameter selection.

## Input / Output

MADA consumes standard stochastic gradients and model parameters, plus a small set of learnable optimizer coefficients (β₁, β₂, β₃, ρ, c, γ) initialized within [0,1]. It outputs updated model weights and evolved optimizer coefficients at each step. Unusually, the optimizer maintains multiple simultaneous moment states (heavy-ball, Nesterov, running-average second moments, and Lion sign-moments), and the coefficients are projected back into the unit hypercube after each hyper-gradient step, making the optimizer state larger than a single fixed optimizer (e.g., ~25 GB vs. ~16 GB peak memory for a 125M GPT-2 model).

## Method

MADA introduces a parameterized optimizer whose update rule is a convex interpolation of Adam, AVGrad, Yogi, Adan, and Lion, controlled by learnable coefficients that define a hypercube design space (vertices = base optimizers, interior = novel interpolated optimizers). During training, hyper-gradient descent (via PyTorch autograd backpropagating through the optimizer update) computes gradients of the loss with respect to these coefficients, which are then updated by a small SGD step with projection onto [0,1]. A key design choice is replacing AMSGrad's max operator with time-averaging (AVGrad), since max blocks hyper-gradient flow to the β₂ path; this modification preserves AMSGrad's convergence fix while enabling smooth differentiation. Theoretically, the paper proves that interpolating between AVGrad and Adam yields a convergence bound that is strictly better than either endpoint up to constant factors, with the optimal interpolation coefficient ρ = β₂ balancing the vanishing and non-vanishing terms in the bound.

## Results

On GPT-2 (125M) trained on OpenWebText, MADA achieves a validation loss of 2.8806, outperforming Adam (2.8956) by 0.015 and Lion (2.8892) by 0.009. The fixed-state variant MADA-FS further improves to 2.8766, a 0.019 gap over Adam. On validation perplexity, MADA-FS achieves 17.75 on OpenWebText and 59.41 on Wikitext, beating Adam by 0.34 and 4.45 points respectively. On CIFAR-10, MADA achieves 93.79% test accuracy with ResNet-9 vs. 93.73% for Adam.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MADA consistently outperforms all four baselines on the primary OpenWebText task: it beats Adam by 0.015 in validation loss (2.8806 vs. 2.8956), Adan by 0.009 (vs. 2.8896), HyperAdam by 0.014 (vs. 2.8950), and Lion by 0.009 (vs. 2.8892). Notably, HyperAdam (which only learns β₁ and β₂ via hyper-gradients) performs nearly identically to Adam (2.8950 vs. 2.8956), confirming that MADA's gains come from adapting the full optimizer interpolation space rather than merely tuning momentum coefficients. MADA also demonstrates robustness: even with suboptimal initialization (β₁=0.7, β₂=0.8), MADA⁻ achieves 2.8921, still beating Adam with its standard parameters (2.8956). On the Shakespeare fine-tuning task (GPT-2 XL, 1.5B), MADA reduces training loss to 0.255 vs. 0.276 for Adam and 0.278 for HyperAdam. On CIFAR-10 vision tasks, MADA achieves 66.12% (5-layer CNN) and 93.79% (ResNet-9), marginally outperforming Adam (65.84%, 93.73%) and HyperAdam (65.80%, 93.69%).

## Relation to Prior Work

MADA sits at the intersection of three prior lines of work: hyper-gradient descent for online hyperparameter adaptation (Baydin et al., 2018; Chandra et al., 2022), the family of adaptive moment optimizers (Adam, AMSGrad, Adan, Yogi, Lion), and learned/searched optimizers (Chen et al., 2023; Metz et al., 2022). Its key conceptual departure is the 'parameterized optimizer' — a convex polytope whose vertices are known optimizers and whose interior points are novel interpolations — combined with hyper-gradient descent to learn the interpolation coefficients online during training, rather than performing an offline search or training a separate neural network. The AVGrad modification of AMSGrad (replacing max with time-averaging) is motivated specifically by the need for smooth hyper-gradient flow, and the convergence analysis adapts the unified Adam/Adagrad proof framework of Defossez et al. (2022) to the interpolated setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MADA offers a practical, drop-in replacement for Adam that consistently improves validation loss and perplexity across language and vision tasks while being robust to suboptimal hyperparameter initialization — a property that reduces the cost of hyperparameter tuning in large-scale training. The theoretical result that interpolating between Adam and AVGrad at ρ=β₂ yields a strictly better convergence bound than either endpoint provides a principled justification for the parameterized optimizer framework. Key limitations include the additional memory overhead (~60% more peak memory in profiling), the convergence analysis being restricted to the two-optimizer AVGrad–Adam interpolation, and the open question of whether a generic convergence framework for multi-optimizer interpolations can be established. Understanding the dynamics of how MADA navigates the optimizer polytope during training remains an important direction for future work.
