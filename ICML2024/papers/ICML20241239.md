## Problem

SNNs are vulnerable to small adversarial perturbations despite exhibiting strong robustness to random perturbations even at large scales, and existing defenses (adversarial training, certified training, SNN-specific coding schemes) are either difficult to train or ineffective against strong attacks. The paper identifies and exploits the gap between adversarial and random vulnerability as a principled target for improving SNN robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241239/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts 32×32 RGB images (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100) or 10-frame event-based data (CIFAR10-DVS), which are directly encoded into binary spike trains fed to LIF neurons over 8 (or 10 for DVS) timesteps. The output is a class label determined by the total spike count at the final layer, with the true-label probability f_y computed via a two-component softmax over the true label y and the runner-up class ỹ.

## Method

The paper proves that the ratio of adversarial vulnerability to random vulnerability is upper bounded by 3‖∇_x f_y(x)‖₀, the l0 sparsity of the true-label probability gradient with respect to the input. To exploit this bound, a Sparsity Regularization (SR) term is added to the cross-entropy loss, penalizing the l1 norm of ∇_x f_y(x) as a convex surrogate for l0. Because double backpropagation is infeasible for SNNs, the l1 gradient norm is approximated via a finite difference: |f_y(x + h·sign(∇_x f_y(x))) − f_y(x)|/h, requiring only one extra forward pass. The SR strategy is loss-independent and attack-independent, and can be combined with standard PGD adversarial training (denoted SR*) to further boost robustness against strong iterative attacks.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with WRN-16, SR* achieves 39.18% accuracy under white-box PGD10 (vs. 17.88% for AT and 10.52% for RAT) and 36.74% under PGD50. On CIFAR-100 with WRN-16, SR* reaches 19.76% under PGD10 and 18.11% under PGD50, compared to 10.07%/7.86% for AT and 5.72%/3.26% for RAT. On the neuromorphic CIFAR10-DVS dataset, SR* achieves 61.2% under white-box PGD50, up from 51.6% (AT) and 4.4% (vanilla). Clean accuracy is sacrificed (e.g., 85.63% vs. 90.97% for AT on CIFAR-10 WRN-16), but the robustness gains are substantial across all attack strengths and both white-box and black-box settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SR* (sparsity regularization + adversarial training) against two SOTA SNN defense baselines: RAT (Regularized Adversarial Training, Ding et al. 2022) and AT (Adversarial Training via Hire-SNN, Kundu et al. 2021). SR* consistently and substantially outperforms both baselines across all three datasets and all attack types. On CIFAR-10 WRN-16 under white-box PGD10, SR* achieves 39.18% versus 17.88% (AT) and 10.52% (RAT) — a 21.3-point gain over AT and 28.7-point gain over RAT. The advantage persists under stronger attacks: on PGD50, SR* reaches 36.74% vs. 14.62% (AT) and 4.33% (RAT). On CIFAR-100 WRN-16, SR* improves PGD50 accuracy from 7.86% (AT) to 18.11%, a 10.25-point margin. In black-box settings, SR* also leads by 5–10 percentage points over AT and 10–20 points over RAT. The trade-off is a 4–7 point drop in clean accuracy relative to AT, which the authors acknowledge as a limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the gradient regularization line of work (Finlay & Oberman, 2021) to the SNN setting, but departs from it in two key ways: the regularization term is tied exclusively to the true-label probability rather than a multi-class calibrated loss (making it loss-independent), and it is attack-independent, unlike adversarial training which depends on the specific attack used. Conceptually, the paper builds on the observation that SNNs exhibit inherent robustness to random perturbations (noted by Sharmin et al., 2020) and formalizes the gap between random and adversarial vulnerability through a theoretical bound on gradient sparsity. The proposed SR strategy is positioned as complementary to existing SNN defenses like RAT (Ding et al., 2022) and AT (Kundu et al., 2021), and can be combined with adversarial training (SR*) for further gains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical bound (Theorem 4.3) provides a principled, interpretable explanation for why SNNs are vulnerable to adversarial but not random perturbations, and gives a concrete training objective (minimizing gradient sparsity) to close that gap. The practical advantage of being both loss-independent and attack-independent makes SR broadly applicable across SNN training pipelines. However, the significant clean-accuracy trade-off (e.g., 93.89% → 85.63% on CIFAR-10) remains a major limitation, and the authors acknowledge that balancing robustness and accuracy is an open problem. The unexpected interpretability bonus—gradient heatmaps of SR-trained SNNs revealing image structure—suggests a potential secondary benefit for explainable AI in neuromorphic computing.
