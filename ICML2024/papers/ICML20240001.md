## Problem

Character-level adversarial attacks on language models have been largely neglected because they cannot leverage popular gradient-based methods and were assumed to be easily defended by robust word recognition modules. Meanwhile, token-level attacks, while more prominent, frequently alter sentence semantics (up to 56.5% in prior work), undermining their practical imperceptibility. This paper challenges both assumptions by showing that character-level attacks can achieve near-perfect attack success rates while preserving semantics, and that existing defenses are far weaker than previously believed.

## Input / Output

The attack takes a natural-language sentence (a sequence of characters from a dataset-specific alphabet), a target model (a fine-tuned classifier such as BERT/RoBERTa, or an LLM such as Llama 2-Chat 7B / Vicuna 7B), and the ground-truth label. It outputs a perturbed sentence that causes misclassification, constrained to a bounded Levenshtein distance (k=10 or 20) from the original. For LLMs, the sentence is wrapped in instructive prompts before being fed to the model, and the loss is computed from next-token class probabilities rather than a dedicated classifier head. The attack is fully query-based (black-box), requiring only forward-pass access to the model.

## Method

Charmer is a greedy, query-based character-level attack built on two key components. First, a position-subset selection heuristic (Algorithm 1) evaluates the importance of every character position (including insertion slots) by replacing each with a test character (whitespace) and measuring the resulting loss change, then retains only the top-n positions (n=20 by default), reducing the search space from O(|S|) to a fixed constant. Second, at each of up to k iterations, the attack enumerates all single-character modifications (replacements, insertions, and deletions) at the selected positions using a unified expansion-contraction operator pair (φ, ψ), evaluates the Carlini-Wagner loss on the resulting batch of n·(|Σ|+1) candidate sentences, and greedily commits to the candidate with the highest loss. Importances are re-evaluated after each perturbation to account for interactions between successive edits. For LLMs, a fast variant (n=1) with an additional token-level pre-selection step is used to manage inference cost, and the same Carlini-Wagner loss is applied to next-token class probabilities.

## Results

Charmer achieves >95% Attack Success Rate (ASR) on all five TextAttack benchmarks (SST-2, MNLI-m, RTE, QNLI, AG-News) with BERT and RoBERTa, reaching 100% ASR on SST-2 and MNLI-m while maintaining the lowest Levenshtein distance (1.47 on SST-2) and highest or runner-up USE cosine similarity in 8/10 cases. On Llama 2-Chat 7B, Charmer-Fast (n=1) achieves 95.47% ASR on SST-2, 93.51% on QNLI, and 97.10% on RTE, outperforming the best token-level baseline (TextFooler) by 9.7–29.5 percentage points. When used as an adversarial training defense, Charmer reduces character-level ASR from 64.02% to 20.34% while preserving 87.20% clean accuracy, whereas TextGrad-based training actually *increases* character-level ASR to 67.34%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Charmer dominates all ten baselines on ASR across every benchmark. Against the strongest token-level attacks, Charmer improves ASR by 4.84 points over TextFooler (100% vs 95.16% on BERT-SST-2) and by 5.96 points over TextGrad (100% vs 94.04%), while simultaneously achieving a Levenshtein distance of 1.47 versus 17.76 and 13.86 respectively. Against character-level baselines, the gap is even larger: Charmer outperforms DeepWordBug (100% vs 81.39%), TextBugger (100% vs 68.49%), and CWBA (100% vs 72.92%) on SST-2. On USE cosine similarity, Charmer is the highest or runner-up in 8 out of 10 dataset-model combinations, confirming that the high ASR does not come at the cost of semantic distortion. In terms of runtime, Charmer is slower than DeepWordBug (1.47s vs 1.01s on Llama-2 SST-2) but comparable to TextGrad, and the Charmer-Fast variant (n=1) narrows this gap at a modest ASR cost. A critical finding is that existing robust word-recognition defenses (Pruthi et al., Jones et al.) only work under strict Pruthi-Jones Constraints; relaxing any single constraint (e.g., allowing uppercase letters) causes ASR to jump from 0.96% to 98.09%, exposing these defenses as fragile.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revisits character-level adversarial attacks, which had lost prominence to token-level gradient-based methods (GBDA, TextGrad) and were assumed to be easily defended by typo-correctors and robust word recognition modules. It builds on the two-phase greedy paradigm of prior character-level attacks (DeepWordBug, TextBugger, Yang et al. 2020) but introduces a key departure: a position subset selection strategy (Algorithm 1) that re-evaluates character importance after each perturbation, combined with the Carlini-Wagner loss from TextGrad. The paper also repurposes the TRADES adversarial training framework, but applies it with a character-level inner attack rather than the token-level one used by Hou et al. (2023), revealing a previously unexplored trade-off between character- and token-level robustness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges two widely held assumptions: that character-level attacks are easily defended, and that token-level adversarial training improves overall robustness. The finding that robust word recognition defenses collapse from 0.96% to 98.09% ASR when a single constraint (e.g., allowing uppercase) is relaxed exposes a false sense of security in deployed NLP systems. The observation that TextGrad-based adversarial training *degrades* character-level robustness while improving token-level robustness raises an open question about which level of robustness should be targeted. A remaining limitation is that the attack is query-based and may not scale to extremely large alphabets or very long sequences without the position-subset heuristic, and the defense landscape for character-level attacks remains largely unsolved.
