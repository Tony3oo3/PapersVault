## Problem

State-space models (S4, Mamba) were introduced as alternatives to transformers with the implicit assumption that their recurrent formulation grants them RNN-like expressive power for state tracking. This paper challenges that assumption by asking whether SSMs can actually express inherently sequential computation, such as composing permutations, that transformers provably cannot.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241444/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The empirical experiments frame the group word problem as a token-tagging task: the input is a sequence of group elements (from A5, A4×Z5, or Z60, each with 60 distinct tokens), and at each position i the model must predict the cumulative product of the first i elements. The theoretical analysis considers general real-valued sequences x_1,...,x_n ∈ R^k processed by SSM layers, producing output sequences y_i = C_i h_i + D_i x_i. Modeling the problem as tagging rather than sequence classification provides denser supervision, making it as easy as possible for models to learn the correct function.

## Method

The paper proves that non-gated SSMs (S4) and diagonal SSMs (S6/Mamba) are simulable in L-uniform TC^0 by showing their convolutional form reduces to matrix powering or iterated scalar multiplication, both known to be in TC^0. This implies these SSMs cannot solve NC^1-complete problems like the S5 permutation composition word problem, which underlies chess state tracking, code evaluation, and entity tracking. To close the expressivity gap, the authors introduce IDS4 (Input-Dependent S4), a minimal modification that makes the transition matrix Ā_i a function of the input x_i, enabling simulation of deterministic finite automata via transition monoid matrix multiplication. Empirically, single-layer RNNs and IDS4 solve the A5 word problem for arbitrarily long sequences, while S4, Mamba, and transformers require depth that grows monotonically with sequence length.

## Results

On the A5 word problem (NC1-complete permutation composition), single-layer RNN and IDS4 models achieve >90% validation accuracy on arbitrarily long sequences, while Transformer, S4, and Mamba require depth that grows monotonically with input sequence length. For the solvable group A4×Z5 (theoretically in TC0), Transformer, S4, and Mamba still require increasingly many layers for longer sequences, though S4 and Mamba need fewer layers than Transformer at equivalent sequence lengths. On the abelian group Z60, all architectures perform comparably. The key quantitative finding is that S4 and Mamba do not exhibit any advantage over Transformer for state tracking: all three require depth scaling with sequence length, in stark contrast to the constant-depth (1-layer) solution achievable by RNNs and IDS4.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares S4 (Gu et al., ICLR 2022) and Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023) against Transformer, RNN, and the proposed IDS4 on the A5, A4×Z5, and Z60 word problems. S4 and Mamba show the same fundamental limitation as Transformer: the minimum number of layers needed to reach 90% accuracy grows monotonically with sequence length for both non-commutative groups (A5 and A4×Z5), confirming their theoretical containment in TC0. On the non-commutative tasks, S4 and Mamba empirically require at most as many layers as Transformer (and frequently fewer), suggesting they are marginally better at approximate state tracking, but none achieve the constant-depth solution. In contrast, both RNN and IDS4 solve all three group word problems with a single layer regardless of sequence length, demonstrating that the input-dependent transition matrix in IDS4 (or the nonlinearity in RNN-SSM) is sufficient to express the S5/A5 word problem. The practical takeaway is that S4 and Mamba do not provide a state-tracking advantage over Transformer, undermining the motivation for SSMs as a more 'recurrent' alternative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the circuit-complexity framework of Merrill & Sabharwal (2023a), which showed transformers are confined to TC^0, to state-space models, proving that S4 and Mamba (S6) share the same expressiveness ceiling. It directly challenges Gu et al.'s (2021) claim that linear SSMs can simulate RNNs, resolving the apparent contradiction by showing that simulation requires infinite depth, while realistic bounded-depth SSMs cannot. The monoid word-problem formalism from Liu et al. (2023) and the NC^1-completeness of non-solvable group word problems (Barrington, 1989) provide the algebraic machinery to identify which state-tracking tasks are provably inexpressible. The proposed IDS4 extension draws on Liquid S4 (Hasani et al., 2023) to introduce input-dependent transition matrices as a minimal fix.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This result fundamentally reframes the SSM-vs-transformer debate: the recurrent formulation of SSMs does not confer the state-tracking advantage the community has assumed, meaning SSMs' practical edge over transformers may rest primarily on efficiency rather than expressiveness. The constructive IDS4 extension shows the gap is closable with a minimal architectural change, but whether input-dependent transition matrices remain trainable and parallelizable at LLM scale remains an open question. The empirical finding that even solvable-group word problems (A4×Z5) require growing depth suggests SSMs may struggle with a broader range of state-tracking tasks than the worst-case theory predicts, with implications for code evaluation, entity tracking, and game playing in long-context settings.
