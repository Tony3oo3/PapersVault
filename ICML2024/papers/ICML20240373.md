## Problem

Existing scaling laws for machine learning describe how model error improves with total dataset size but treat all training examples identically, offering no guidance on which individual points are more or less valuable. This paper asks whether the marginal contribution of each data point to model performance decays at a predictable, point-specific rate as the dataset grows, and whether this rate can be estimated efficiently from limited noisy observations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a small number of noisy marginal contribution samples Δ(z,D) per data point (as few as 10 for the amortized estimator, 100–1000 for the likelihood estimator) across a range of dataset sizes k, along with the raw data point z=(x,y). It outputs per-point scaling parameters c(z) and α(z) that parameterize the predicted expected contribution ψ_k(z)≈c(z)/k^α(z) at any cardinality, which are then aggregated into data valuation scores (e.g., Distributional Data Shapley) or used to rank candidate points for addition to a dataset of a given size. The amortized variant additionally requires a neural network architecture (e.g., MLP) that maps x to the four Gaussian parameters, predicting values for all classes and selecting the one matching y.

## Method

The paper posits that the expected marginal contribution of a data point follows a power law ψ_k(z)≈c(z)/k^α(z) with point-specific coefficient and exponent, supported by theoretical analysis of linear regression (yielding α=2) and general M-estimators (yielding α=1 or 3/2). To fit these parameters from limited noisy samples, the authors propose two estimators: (1) a maximum likelihood estimator that models each Δ(z,D) as Gaussian with mean c(z)/k^α(z) and variance σ²(z)/k^β(z), solving analytically for c and σ while optimizing α and β via Adam; and (2) an amortized estimator that replaces per-point optimization with a neural network g(z;θ) predicting all four parameters simultaneously across the entire dataset, sharing statistical strength across examples. The key distinction from prior data valuation is that instead of averaging contributions into a single scalar score, the method captures the *decay rate* α(z), revealing that the ranking of valuable points is dataset-size-dependent—points with high α are more useful in small datasets but lose value quickly, while points with low α remain relatively more useful in large datasets.

## Results

The individualized scaling law ψ_k(z) ≈ c(z)/k^α(z) achieves an overall R² of 0.929–0.993 across three datasets (MiniBooNE, CIFAR-10, IMDB) and three model classes (logistic regression, MLP, SVM), with the highest score of 0.993 for logistic regression on MiniBooNE. For data valuation with only 10 samples per data point, the amortized estimator achieves Pearson correlations of 0.994 (MiniBooNE), 0.893 (CIFAR-10), and 0.930 (IMDB) with ground-truth scores, outperforming the standard Monte Carlo estimator (0.967, 0.857, 0.916). For data subset selection with 20 points added to a dataset of size 100, Scaling 100 yields the best accuracy improvement (e.g., 3.28±1.09% on CIFAR-10 vs. 2.06±0.92% for Distributional Shapley).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its amortized and likelihood-based scaling law estimators against the standard Monte Carlo estimator for data valuation, and against Distributional Shapley (Ghorbani et al., 2020) for data subset selection. With only 10 samples per data point, the amortized estimator outperforms Monte Carlo on all three datasets (e.g., 0.994 vs. 0.967 on MiniBooNE, 0.930 vs. 0.916 on IMDB), while the non-amortized likelihood estimator is unreliable at this sample budget (0.077 on CIFAR-10). With 50 samples, all three estimators converge to similar accuracy (0.931–0.996), confirming the scaling law captures the same signal as direct Monte Carlo averaging. For data subset selection, Scaling 100 (selecting points by predicted contribution at k=100) outperforms Distributional Shapley by 1.22 percentage points on CIFAR-10 (3.28% vs. 2.06%) and by 1.45 points on IMDB (2.22% vs. 0.77%) when adding to a dataset of size 100, demonstrating that the most valuable points depend on the current dataset size. Conversely, Scaling 1000 performs comparably to Shapley at k=1000 (0.13% vs. 0.11% on IMDB), suggesting Shapley values implicitly capture large-sample-regime contributions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: aggregate scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022), which describe how model error decays with dataset size but treat all data points uniformly, and data valuation methods (Ghorbani and Zou, 2019; Kwon and Zou, 2021), which score individual points by marginal contribution but do not characterize how that value changes with dataset size. The key departure is proposing that each data point has its own power-law scaling exponent α(z) governing the decay of its marginal contribution, a formulation the authors state is the first to be thoroughly explored. For estimation, the paper adapts amortized optimization (Amos et al., 2023) to jointly fit scaling parameters across an entire dataset, and the theoretical analysis draws on M-estimator asymptotics (Kuchibhotla, 2018) to provide partial support for the power-law form.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central insight—that the ranking of valuable data points depends on the current dataset size, with lines crossing in log-log space—challenges the common practice of using a single size-agnostic score (like Shapley values) for data selection. This opens a path toward adaptive data curation where the 'best' new points to add change as the dataset grows. However, the evidence is limited to simple models (logistic regression, MLP, SVM) and small-scale datasets, and the method only quantifies expected improvement when adding a point to a *random* dataset of a given size, not to a specific dataset with known composition. Key open questions include whether the scaling law holds for large-scale deep learning, whether it can be extended to predict contributions to a specific (non-random) dataset, and whether the expensive per-point marginal contribution sampling can be replaced by cheaper proxies.
