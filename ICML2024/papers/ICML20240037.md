## Problem

Fairness in sequential decision making has been largely studied for single decisions or via long-term discounted rewards, but in practice fairness is inherently non-Markovian—its assessment depends on the full history of state-action pairs—and must often be evaluated at intermediate time points (periodically, at bounded milestones, or at every step) rather than only at the end. This paper formalizes these non-Markovian fairness notions and addresses the practical challenge of learning fair policies that account for historical stakeholder status.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a memory-augmented state consisting of the environment state (e.g., which stakeholders are present) paired with a memory state that is the cumulative stakeholder status vector U(τ_t) (e.g., number of items each stakeholder has received so far). At each step it outputs an allocation action and, during training, generates counterfactual experiences by substituting alternative memory states while holding the environment transition fixed, thereby augmenting the replay buffer without additional environment interaction.

## Method

The paper introduces a formal framework of fairness schemes (status function U, extended aggregation W_ex, and filter B) that unifies long-term, periodic, anytime, and bounded fairness, and proves that value-regular status functions can be made Markovian via finite memory augmentation. The core algorithmic contribution is FairQCM, a Q-learning variant that exploits the known memory update function μ to generate counterfactual experiences: given a real transition (⟨s_t, m_t⟩, a_t, ⟨s_{t+1}, m_{t+1}⟩, r), it constructs additional transitions by replacing m_t with sampled alternatives m'_t and computing m'_{t+1} = μ(m'_t, a_t, s_{t+1}), yielding valid reward signals without environment queries. This is adapted from the CRM algorithm for reward machines but applied to stakeholder-status memory. The paper proves convergence of tabular FairQCM to the optimal Q-function under standard Q-learning conditions and demonstrates in deep RL experiments (DQN-based) that FairQCM outperforms baselines using full memory, min-relative memory, or GRU-based history encoding in both sample efficiency and final fairness scores on resource allocation and simulated lending tasks.

## Results

In the resource allocation (doughnut distribution) task, FairQCM achieves the highest accumulated Nash welfare scores across all training phases, outperforming Full, Min, Reset, and RNN baselines, while also learning to minimize wasted doughnuts near the end of training. In the simulated lending task, FairQCM achieves the highest accumulated Relaxed Demographic Parity scores, again outperforming Full, Min, and RNN baselines, with all methods successfully achieving the 10% profit margin target. The tabular Q-learning experiments (Appendix A.2) confirm that FairQCM requires fewer samples to converge to high Nash welfare scores compared to standard Q-learning with the same memory. The paper reports results as learning curves over 1000 episodes (deep) or 100,000 time steps (tabular) rather than single-point metric values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FairQCM against four memory-augmented DQN baselines (Full, Min, Reset, RNN) plus two hard-coded references (Oracle, Random) in both the resource allocation and simulated lending environments. FairQCM consistently outperforms all learned baselines on both the Nash welfare metric (resource allocation) and the Relaxed Demographic Parity metric (simulated lending), with the gap most pronounced in early-to-mid training, indicating superior sample efficiency. The RNN baseline (using a GRU layer) is the strongest competitor, learning an approximation of history and achieving higher scores than Full and Min, but still trailing FairQCM. The Full and Min baselines, which store the raw or relative stakeholder status vector without counterfactual augmentation, underperform FairQCM despite having access to the same information. The Oracle (optimal turn-taking) serves as an upper bound that no learned method reaches, while Random provides a lower bound. In the tabular setting (Appendix A.2), FairQCM also outperforms standard Q-learning with Full memory on both Nash welfare and the number of non-wasted doughnuts, confirming the benefit of counterfactual experience generation in the tabular regime as well.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior fairness-in-RL work (Jabbari et al. 2017, Wen et al. 2021, Deng et al. 2022), which assessed fairness via immediate rewards or long-term discounted returns, by formalizing fairness as inherently non-Markovian—dependent on the full history of state-action pairs and assessed at intermediate time points. Algorithmically, FairQCM adapts the counterfactual experience generation from the reward machine / CRM framework of Toro Icarte et al. (2022) to the fairness setting, and uses DQN (Mnih et al., 2015) and tabular Q-learning (Watkins & Dayan, 1992) as the underlying learning machinery. The paper also connects to the allocation and social welfare literature (Kash et al., 2014; Caragiannis et al., 2019) but distinguishes itself by embedding these notions in a sequential decision-making formalism with explicit memory augmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal framework for non-Markovian fairness in sequential decision making, introducing a clean taxonomy (long-term, periodic, anytime, bounded) and a value-regularity characterization that precisely delineates when memory augmentation can convert non-Markovian fairness into a standard MDP problem. However, the experiments are small-scale simulations (5 customers, 4 loan applicants) with simple state spaces, and the authors acknowledge they have 'only scratched the surface' of fairness scoring functions and practical deployment. Open questions include handling unbounded status functions (e.g., continuous returns), scaling to realistic multi-stakeholder environments, and validating the framework with domain experts on normative fairness criteria.
