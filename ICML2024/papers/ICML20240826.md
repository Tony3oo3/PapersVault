## Problem

Despite rapid progress in foundation models, there is no formal, general-purpose definition of what it means for an AI system to be open-ended—continuously producing novel, learnable artifacts without end. The paper argues that open-endedness is an essential property of any artificial superhuman intelligence, yet current foundation models trained on static datasets cannot achieve it, and the field lacks a precise definition to guide research toward this goal.

## Input / Output

The formal framework takes as input a sequence of artifacts X_t produced by a system S and evaluates them from the perspective of an external observer O who maintains a statistical model X̂_t over the observed history. The observer's loss function ℓ(t,T) quantifies prediction error for artifact X_T given history X_{1:t}, and the system is judged open-ended if both novelty (expected loss grows with T for fixed t) and learnability (expected loss shrinks with t for fixed T) hold simultaneously. The observer's choice of loss function encodes what is 'interesting,' making the definition explicitly observer-dependent and allowing different observers to reach different verdicts on the same system.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a formal definition of open-endedness as the conjunction of two properties: novelty (for any time t, there always exists a further future artifact T′ > T that is less predictable than T) and learnability (conditioning on a strictly longer history always reduces prediction loss for any future artifact). This definition is deliberately observer-relative, with the observer's statistical model and loss function encoding domain-specific notions of interestingness, and the authors provide an equivalent compression-based formulation using rate-distortion theory. The paper then sketches four concrete research paths toward open-ended foundation models: reinforcement learning with foundation-model-guided exploration, self-improvement loops leveraging tools and other agents, task generation via learned world models, and evolutionary algorithms using LLMs as semantically meaningful mutation and selection operators. It concludes with a safety analysis arguing that open-endedness and safety must be pursued in tandem, since a system that is no longer learnable to human observers is by definition no longer open-ended to them.

## Results

This is a position paper with no experimental results or quantitative benchmarks. The primary contribution is a formal definition of open-endedness in terms of novelty (artifacts become increasingly unpredictable to an observer's model) and learnability (conditioning on longer history reduces prediction loss), along with a qualitative analysis of existing systems (AlphaGo, AdA, POET, foundation models) against this definition. The paper argues that current foundation models are not open-ended by this definition and sketches four research paths (RL, self-improvement, task generation, evolutionary algorithms) toward open-ended foundation models as a route to ASI.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a position paper, this work does not conduct experimental comparisons against baselines. Instead, it qualitatively evaluates four archetypal systems against the proposed formal definition: AlphaGo (open-ended but narrow, limited to game domains), AdA (open-ended but finitely so, with novelty plateauing after ~1 month of training), POET (open-ended via co-evolution but bounded by environment parameterization), and contemporary foundation models (not open-ended, as they are trained on fixed, learnable datasets). The paper positions these as complementary rather than competing approaches, arguing that foundation models provide the missing generality for open-ended algorithms while open-ended algorithms provide the missing novelty for foundation models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes the open-endedness literature from artificial life (Bedau, Holland, McShea) and evolutionary computation (Soros & Stanley, Stanley & Lehman) with the foundation model paradigm, arguing that the two are orthogonal dimensions whose combination is essential for ASI. It departs from prior definitions—which were either informal aphorisms or restricted to evolutionary systems—by providing a general-purpose formal definition grounded in statistical learning (novelty + learnability relative to an external observer). The paper positions existing open-ended systems (AlphaGo, AdA, POET, Voyager, FunSearch) as narrow or finitely open-ended, and frames foundation models as the missing ingredient for domain-general open-endedness, while simultaneously arguing that current foundation models are not themselves open-ended.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The formal definition provides a testable criterion that can ground future research on whether a system is open-ended, and the argument that open-endedness is a necessary property of ASI gives the field a concrete target beyond scaling. Key open questions include: how to sustain open-endedness on indefinite timescales (current systems plateau), how to maintain human learnability as artifacts grow in complexity, and how to direct open-ended search toward useful rather than merely novel artifacts. The safety analysis (Section 4) identifies that open-endedness introduces distinct risks—goal misgeneralization in unbounded search, loss of human oversight, and emergent multi-agent interactions—that are not addressed by existing foundation model safety frameworks.
