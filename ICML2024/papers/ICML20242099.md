## Problem

While Distributional RL (DistRL) empirically outperforms standard squared-loss RL, the theoretical reason was previously only partially understood: Wang et al. (2023b) showed DistRL achieves small-loss (first-order) bounds scaling with the optimal cost V*, but no one had established that DistRL yields the tighter second-order (variance-dependent) bounds that scale with return variance. This paper closes that gap by proving second-order bounds for DistRL in general function-approximation settings (low-rank MDPs, offline RL), which are the first such results beyond linear models and the first for offline RL.

## Input / Output

In online RL, the algorithm receives sequential state-action-cost-next-state tuples and outputs a greedy policy per episode; in offline RL, it consumes a fixed dataset of N i.i.d. transitions per step and outputs a single policy. The learned object is a conditional cost distribution over [0,1] (not a scalar value), parameterized by a function class F of distributions. For the empirical contextual bandit experiments, inputs are tabular features (housing, insurance) or 32×32 RGB images (CIFAR-100), and the output is an action selection with a distributional bonus computed via neural-network width/disagreement estimation.

## Method

The core mechanism is an MLE-based confidence set over a class of conditional distributions, where optimism/pessimism is induced by selecting the most optimistic/pessimistic distribution within the β-near-optimal set under log-likelihood. The key analytical insight is that the triangular discrimination D_△ between the learned distribution and the true distribution, when combined with a novel 'Change of Variance' lemma (Lemma 5.6), naturally produces variance-dependent terms in the regret decomposition—eliminating the need for variance-weighted regression used in all prior second-order bound work. The algorithms O-DISCO (online), P-DISCO (offline), and DISTUCB (bandits) follow the standard optimism-in-the-face-of-uncertainty framework but operate on distributions via the distributional Bellman operator. For practical implementation in bandits, the distributional bonus is computed efficiently via a modified disagreement/width computation with neural networks, requiring only one optimization oracle per batch rather than per action.

## Results

On three real-world contextual bandit tasks, DISTUCB (distributional learning with log-likelihood) consistently achieves lower average cost than the squared-loss baseline RegCB. On King County Housing, DISTUCB reduces average cost from 0.708 to 0.683 (all episodes) and from 0.676 to 0.640 (last 100 episodes). On Prudential Life Insurance, the improvement is from 0.287 to 0.248 (all episodes) and 0.278 to 0.236 (last 100 episodes). On CIFAR-100, DISTUCB achieves 0.862 vs. 0.890 (all episodes) and 0.823 vs. 0.854 (last 100 episodes), with lower cost being better in all cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DISTUCB (MLE-based distributional learning with log-likelihood loss) against RegCB (Foster et al., 2018), which uses squared loss regression for the same optimistic contextual bandit framework. DISTUCB wins on all three datasets and both evaluation windows (all episodes and last 100 episodes), with the largest relative improvement on Prudential Life Insurance (0.287→0.248, a ~14% reduction in average cost) and King County Housing (0.708→0.683, ~3.5% reduction). The CIFAR-100 gap is smaller (0.890→0.862, ~3% reduction) but still consistent. The authors note that DISTUCB also converges faster, reaching lower cost earlier in training as shown in the cost curves. Both methods use the same width/disagreement computation for inducing optimism, isolating the effect of the loss function (log-likelihood vs. squared loss) as the key differentiator.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly refines the small-loss (first-order) bounds of Wang et al. (2023b) for MLE-based Distributional RL, showing that the same O-DISCO and P-DISCO algorithms actually achieve tighter second-order (variance-dependent) bounds under identical assumptions. The key technical tool is triangular discrimination, first leveraged in the contextual bandit setting by Foster & Krishnamurthy (2021), which naturally couples mean differences to variances. This departs from prior variance-dependent bounds (Zanette & Brunskill, 2019; Zhao et al., 2023; Ito et al., 2020) that relied on variance-weighted regression—a technique difficult to extend beyond linear function approximation—by showing that learning the full return distribution with MLE yields variance-dependent bounds 'for free' through the eluder dimension framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

These are the first second-order bounds for low-rank MDPs and for offline RL, establishing DistRL as a principled alternative to variance-weighted regression for obtaining sharp instance-dependent guarantees in general function approximation settings. The practical implication is that in near-deterministic tasks (where return variance is small but V* may be non-zero), DistRL converges at O(1/N) rates rather than the O(1/√N) rate of first-order bounds. Open questions include whether even higher-order bounds are achievable, whether the stronger distributional Bellman completeness assumption can be relaxed, and whether the computational cost of confidence-set optimization can be mitigated beyond the contextual bandit case where width computation works.
