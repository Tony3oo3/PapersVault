## Problem

Existing MLDE methods for protein directed evolution rely on data-driven trial-and-error sampling that overlooks critical biochemical properties of amino acids (charge, size, polarity), potentially yielding mutations that appear functionally beneficial but destabilize the protein. This paper addresses the gap by encoding structured amino acid domain knowledge directly into the reinforcement learning decision process, rather than leaving the policy to discover such constraints purely from sequence statistics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242141/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a wild-type protein sequence with four designated mutation sites (each admitting 20 amino acid candidates) and a pre-constructed Amino Acid Knowledge Graph (AAKG) derived from AAontology, where 20 amino acid nodes are connected by edges weighted by absolute physicochemical property differences. It produces a set of high-fitness mutant sequences and a final ensemble fitness predictor (XGBoost + 1D CNN + LSTM) trained via active learning. Mutants lacking experimental fitness values (due to failed expression) receive a heavy penalty (−100) to discourage biologically implausible sequences.

## Method

KnowRLM couples a PLM-based policy (ESM-2 + MLP for site prediction) with a preferential random walk on the AAKG for amino acid type selection, where PLM position-sensitive embeddings serve as node representations and cosine similarity guides transitions among neighbors within a distance threshold. A dynamic sliding window mechanism adaptively shifts the exploration window toward or away from the origin amino acid based on whether recent mutations improved fitness, balancing exploitation of similar residues against exploration of dissimilar ones. The policy is optimized via PPO with pseudo-rewards from the fitness predictor, while the predictor itself is iteratively fine-tuned in an active learning loop: each round samples mutants, an oracle annotates their fitness, and the accumulated dataset retrains the reward model. The key architectural insight is that using context-dependent PLM embeddings as AAKG node embeddings aligns learned sequence statistics with explicit physicochemical structure, enabling the policy to make mutations informed by both evolutionary patterns and biochemical constraints.

## Results

On the GB1 dataset with 384 sampled mutants, KnowRLM achieves a maximum fitness of 0.972, mean fitness of 0.562, and NDCG of 0.884, outperforming all baselines across all three metrics. On the PhoQ dataset at the same sample size, KnowRLM reaches a maximum fitness of 0.658, mean of 0.157, and NDCG of 0.819. Notably, KnowRLM already surpasses most competing methods after just one round of reinforcement learning (192 samples), e.g., achieving max fitness 0.931 on GB1 versus EvoPlay's 0.837 and CLADE2.0's 0.886, reducing experimental annotation costs substantially.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KnowRLM outperforms all five baselines on every metric (max, mean, NDCG) across both GB1 and PhoQ datasets at all sample sizes. Against the closest RL-based competitor EvoPlay, KnowRLM achieves a max fitness of 0.972 vs. 0.840 on GB1 (384 samples) and 0.658 vs. 0.474 on PhoQ, with mean fitness gains of 0.562 vs. 0.460 and 0.157 vs. 0.143 respectively. Compared to the best non-RL method CLADE2.0, KnowRLM leads by 0.037 in max fitness and 0.071 in mean fitness on GB1 at 384 samples, and by a larger margin of 0.259 in max fitness on PhoQ. The advantage is most pronounced at smaller sample sizes (192 mutants / 1 RL round), where KnowRLM already exceeds CLADE2.0's max fitness by 0.045 on GB1 and 0.141 on PhoQ, demonstrating superior sample efficiency. MLDE, which relies on purely random sampling, performs worst across all settings, confirming the benefit of guided exploration.

## Relation to Prior Work

KnowRLM extends the RL-based directed evolution line initiated by EvoPlay (Wang et al., 2023), which adapted AlphaZero-style self-play to single-site protein mutations, by injecting structured biochemical domain knowledge into the policy via an Amino Acid Knowledge Graph (AAKG). It also builds on the broader MLDE framework (Wu et al., 2019; Wittmann et al., 2021; Qiu et al., 2021) and the use of protein language models (ESM-2) as sequence encoders, as seen in AFP-DE (Qin et al., 2023). The key conceptual departure is replacing purely data-driven mutation selection with a preferential random walk on a knowledge graph, guided by a dynamic sliding window that balances exploration and exploitation based on fitness feedback.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that encoding physicochemical amino acid relationships as a structured knowledge graph can substantially reduce the number of experimental rounds needed to identify high-fitness mutants, which is practically significant given the cost of wet-lab validation. However, the evaluation is limited to two datasets with only four mutation sites each, and the AAKG is a relatively simple 20-node graph; scalability to multi-site mutations, larger knowledge graphs, and diverse protein engineering tasks (e.g., de novo design, multi-objective optimization) remains open. The finding that BLOSUM62 underperforms the structured AAKG on the harder PhoQ dataset suggests that substitution matrices alone are insufficient, but whether this gap widens with more complex mutation landscapes is untested.
