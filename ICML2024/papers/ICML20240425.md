## Problem

Decentralized multi-agent systems with dynamically varying local dependencies—such as cooperative navigation, obstacle avoidance, and formation control—lack a theoretical framework that simultaneously models partial observability and time-varying agent interactions. Existing empirical RL approaches provide no performance guarantees, while theoretical MARL literature either inherits the NEXP-completeness of general Dec-POMDPs or fails to capture the dynamic proximity-based reward structure. The paper asks whether near-optimal, scalable decentralized policies exist for this setting and quantifies how much performance is lost to partial observability.

## Input / Output

The framework takes agent positions in a common metric space with distance metric d, local internal states, and a dynamically computed visibility partition Z(s) where agents within distance V can communicate (with the structural constraint V > R, the dependence radius). The output is a stationary group decentralized policy assigning joint actions to each visibility group, together with closed-form performance bounds. The gap V > R creates a buffer of c = ⌊(V−R)/2⌋ time steps during which agents in different visibility groups provably cannot interact, a property exploited by all three policy constructions to decouple the problem into tractable per-group subproblems.

## Method

The paper introduces the Locally Interdependent Multi-Agent MDP, where agents in a metric space accumulate proximity-based interdependent rewards (within distance R) and communicate within a larger visibility radius V. Three closed-form group decentralized policies are constructed: the Amalgam Policy (local joint optimal policies per visibility group), the Cutoff Policy (optimal policy in a modified MDP where agents permanently disconnect upon leaving each other's visibility, enabling value decomposition over the cutoff partition), and the First Step Finite Horizon Optimal Policy (first step of the c-horizon optimal policy). Proofs rely on two key lemmas: the Dependence Time Lemma, which guarantees agents in different visibility groups cannot interact for c time steps, and the Telescoping Lemma, which converts bounds on naive non-group-decentralized policy candidates into valid bounds for the closed-form policies. The main result establishes that the performance gap between the optimal fully observable policy and the best group decentralized policy is O(γ^V)—exponentially small in the visibility radius—and a matching lower bound construction shows this is tight up to constant factors.

## Results

The paper establishes that three closed-form group decentralized policies (Amalgam, Cutoff, and First Step Finite Horizon Optimal) achieve performance within O(γ^c · r̃/(1-γ)²) of the fully observable optimal policy, where c = ⌊(V−R)/2⌋ is the dependence time buffer. A matching lower bound (Theorem 3.4) proves these guarantees are tight up to constant factors for general Locally Interdependent Multi-Agent MDPs. As a corollary, the gap between the optimal fully observable policy and the best group decentralized policy is O(γ^V), showing exponential improvement with visibility radius. In toy grid-world simulations, the Amalgam Policy with V=45 achieves the optimal discounted reward of 8.85 on the Bullseye Problem (vs. 6.74 at V=25), while the Cutoff Policy outperforms the Amalgam Policy on the Aisle Walk Problem (400 vs. 234.40 vs. optimal 496.84) when interdependent rewards are positive.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baselines; instead, it benchmarks three internally constructed policies (Amalgam, Cutoff, First Step Finite Horizon Optimal) against the fully observable optimal policy in toy simulations. The Amalgam Policy excels in penalty-dominated settings (Bullseye, Highway) where increasing visibility rapidly closes the gap to optimal, reaching exact optimality at V=45 on Bullseye. The Cutoff Policy outperforms the Amalgam Policy when interdependent rewards are positive (Aisle Walk: 400 vs. 234.40), because it accounts for future reconnection dynamics. However, the Cutoff Policy suffers from a 'penalty jittering' phenomenon in long-horizon settings with heavy interdependent penalties (Highway: 0 vs. optimal 73.5), where agents oscillate between visibility groups. The First Step Finite Horizon Optimal Policy is not separately evaluated in simulations but shares the same theoretical guarantee class. All three policies achieve the optimal trajectory on the Lane Merging formation control problem, demonstrating that the framework can recover exact solutions when agents quickly enter mutual visibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scalable multi-agent RL line of work (Qu et al., 2022) by replacing fixed or stochastic dependence networks with dynamic, time-varying proximity-based dependencies and shifting the coupling from transition probabilities to reward functions. It also departs from the Dec-POMDP literature, which is NEXP-complete in general, by proposing a structured model (Locally Interdependent Multi-Agent MDP) where the visibility radius exceeds the dependence radius, creating a temporal buffer that enables tractable analysis. The closest prior formulations—Interaction Driven Markov Games and Network Distributed POMDPs—either lack dynamic dependencies or assume fixed reward groupings, neither of which holds here.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a fundamental property: the optimal partially observable decentralized policy approximates the fully observable centralized policy exponentially well as visibility radius increases, and provides three closed-form policies that achieve this near-optimality up to constant factors. This gives practitioners a theoretically grounded, scalable alternative to empirical MARL methods for problems like cooperative navigation and formation control. Key open questions include overcoming the 'penalty jittering' phenomenon (where the Cutoff Policy oscillates under penalty-based interdependent rewards), incorporating memory into group decentralized policies, and extending the framework to reinforcement learning algorithms rather than closed-form solutions.
