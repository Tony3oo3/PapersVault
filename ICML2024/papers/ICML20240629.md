## Problem

Next-token prediction, the standard training objective for LLMs, latches onto local patterns and overlooks hard decisions, requiring orders of magnitude more data than human children to reach comparable fluency. While multi-token prediction was previously proposed (Qi et al., 2020), it had not been demonstrated to be beneficial at scale under compute-matched comparisons, and prior architectures replicated the residual stream n-fold, preventing fair parameter-count comparisons.

## Input / Output

The model takes a token sequence x_{t:1} as input and produces n future tokens in parallel via n independent output heads operating on a shared trunk representation. At inference, the model can operate in standard autoregressive mode using only the next-token head, or in self-speculative decoding mode where all n heads propose candidate tokens simultaneously for verification, yielding up to 3× speedup (6.4× for 8-byte models). The memory-efficient implementation processes heads sequentially, reducing peak GPU memory from O(nV+d) to O(V+d) by freeing each head's logits before computing the next.

## Method

The architecture consists of a shared transformer trunk f_s producing a latent representation z_{t:1}, n independent single-layer transformer heads f_{h_i}, and a shared unembedding matrix f_u, so that P(x_{t+i}|x_{t:1}) = softmax(f_u(f_{h_i}(f_s(x_{t:1}))) for i=1,...,n. To enable compute-matched comparisons, adding n-1 head layers removes n-1 trunk layers, keeping total parameters constant. The training loss is the sum of cross-entropies over all n future tokens, and a sequential forward/backward schedule over heads avoids materializing all (n,V) logit gradients simultaneously. The paper provides two theoretical justifications: (1) n-token prediction assigns implicit weight n(n+1)/2 to choice points versus n to inconsequential tokens, and (2) an information-theoretic decomposition shows 2-token prediction doubles the weight of the mutual information term I(X;Y) relative to next-token prediction, encouraging the model to precompute features relevant to future tokens.

## Results

On code benchmarks with 7B parameters trained on 200B tokens (32k vocabulary), the 4-token prediction model achieves 33.8% pass@1 on MBPP and 24.0% pass@1 on HumanEval, versus 30.0% and 22.8% for the next-token baseline. At 13B parameters, multi-token prediction models solve 12% more problems on HumanEval and 17% more on MBPP than comparable next-token models. Inference speedup reaches 3.0× on code and 2.7× on text via self-speculative decoding with 4 heads, and up to 6.4× for 8-byte prediction models. On byte-level tokenization (314B bytes), 8-byte prediction solves 67% more problems on MBPP pass@1 than next-byte prediction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All comparisons are against compute-matched next-token prediction (n=1) baselines with identical parameter counts (layers removed from the trunk to accommodate prediction heads). On code generation, n=4 consistently outperforms n=1 across MBPP and HumanEval at 200B tokens (+3.8% pass@1 on MBPP, +1.2% on HumanEval), and the advantage persists at 1T tokens with 4 epochs (+2.4% MBPP pass@1, +3.2% HumanEval pass@100). The byte-level setting shows the most dramatic gap: 8-byte prediction achieves 32.3% vs 19.3% on MBPP pass@1 (+67% relative). On natural language multiple-choice benchmarks (ARC, COPA, Hellaswag, etc.), n=2 matches the baseline while n=4 slightly regresses, suggesting these tasks may require larger model sizes to benefit. On abstractive summarization (8 benchmarks, ROUGE-L F1), both n=2 and n=4 improve over n=1 at both 200B and 500B tokens, with average gains of +0.51 and +0.46 at 200B. On GSM8K mathematical reasoning, n=2 outperforms n=1 at 200B tokens but the pattern reverses at 500B tokens, while n=4 is worse throughout.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-token prediction idea from ProphetNet (Qi et al., 2020), which first proposed predicting n future tokens for sequence-to-sequence pre-training but replicated the residual stream n-fold, making compute-matched comparisons impossible. The key architectural departure is a shared transformer trunk with n independent output heads, enabling fair parameter-matched comparisons. Unlike Stern et al. (2018) and Medusa (Cai et al., 2024), which apply multi-head prediction only at finetuning or inference time, this paper studies multi-token prediction as a pretraining loss and provides an information-theoretic decomposition showing how it reweights 'choice points' in the training signal. The paper also connects to the induction-heads literature (Olsson et al., 2022) by showing multi-token prediction accelerates the formation of in-context learning circuits in small models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The technique is remarkably simple (add n-1 transformer layers as prediction heads, remove n-1 from the trunk) yet yields both quality gains (12–17% more problems solved on code benchmarks at 13B) and a 3× inference speedup via self-speculative decoding, with no training-time overhead. A critical open question is the scale-dependent behavior: multi-token prediction is harmful for small models but beneficial at scale, and on natural language the 4-token variant actually regresses on multiple-choice benchmarks, suggesting the optimal n and its interaction with data domain remain unresolved. The authors also flag that optimal vocabulary size for multi-token prediction likely differs from next-token prediction, and that improved auxiliary losses operating in embedding space (à la LeCun's JEPA) could further extend the approach.
