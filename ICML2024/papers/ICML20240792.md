## Problem

Existing learning-based auction equilibrium solvers (no-regret algorithms, MARL, MLPs) are tailored to specific settings—fixed bidder counts, fixed mechanisms, or fixed value distributions—and require costly retraining when any of these change. The paper asks whether a single trained deep learning model can efficiently compute approximate Bayesian Nash Equilibria for arbitrary auction mechanisms and bidder configurations, with only lightweight fine-tuning for new scenarios.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240792/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes as input an integer-coded mechanism identifier (e.g., 0=first-price, 1=second-price, 2/3=with entry fee), a mechanism configuration scalar (entry fee value), and each bidder's value distribution discretized into a histogram over H=20 equally-spaced bins in [0, V_max]. At inference, each bidder's private value realization is also supplied. The output is a full strategy profile: for every bidder i and every discretized value v_i, a probability distribution over bid prices in {0, 1/H, …, 1}, produced in a single forward pass regardless of the number of bidders (2–20+).

## Method

Auctionformer uses a pretrained Transformer (BERT by default) as an encoder that ingests mechanism tokens and all bidders' discretized value-distribution histograms to produce a joint bidding-policy representation. A cross-attention decoder then retrieves each bidder's strategy by using that bidder's value-realization embedding as the query against the joint policy's key/value pairs, guaranteeing no cross-bidder value leakage, and an MLP maps the attended vector to a bid-price distribution. Training uses the Nash error (Nikaido–Isoda function) as the loss, which directly measures max exploitability and requires no ground-truth equilibrium labels; for first- and second-price auctions the inner maximization over best responses admits a closed-form expression (Proposition 3.3), making the loss computationally tractable. For adapting to unseen mechanisms or bidder counts, a Dreambooth-inspired few-shot procedure fine-tunes a new mechanism-identifier token using the Nash loss plus an L1 preservation loss against the pretrained model's outputs on prior data, preserving previously learned capabilities.

## Results

On the standard 2-bidder first-price auction with U[0,1] values, Auctionformer achieves an L2 distance of 0.017 to the analytic solution with an inference time of just 0.006s, outperforming MWU (L2=0.021, 27.97s) and SODA (L2=0.018, 0.29s) on both accuracy and speed. Across the full scalability evaluation (Table 2), Auctionformer maintains a Nash error (max exploitability) consistently around 1e-4 for 2–10 bidders across four mechanisms and five distribution types, roughly 1000× better than the random-strategy baseline (1.59e-1). In few-shot transfer to 15 and 20 bidders, the Nash+preservation loss approach reduces L_Nash from 5.67e-2 to 2.94e-3 (N=15) and from 7.25e-2 to 6.82e-3 (N=20) while preserving pre-trained-domain performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Auctionformer decisively outperforms all no-regret learning baselines (MWU, FTPL, OMWU) on both L2 accuracy and wall-clock time: it achieves L2=0.017 in 0.006s versus MWU-32's 0.021 in 27.97s, FTPL-32's 0.026 in 21.08s, and OMWU-32's 0.023 in 32.62s. Against the gradient-based SODA (Bichler et al., 2022), Auctionformer matches or slightly beats the L2 (0.017 vs 0.018) while being ~48× faster (0.006s vs 0.29s). The higher-capacity neural solvers NPGA-2¹⁸ (L2=0.011, ~310s) and SM-2¹⁸ (L2=0.005, ~18s) achieve lower L2 distances but at 3,000× and 3,000× the inference cost respectively, and require 2¹⁸ valuation samples versus Auctionformer's 20. The backward-shooting BNE solver (Wang et al., 2020) serves as the numerical ground truth in Figures 7 and 10; Auctionformer's predicted strategies closely track this reference, whereas MWU exhibits a step-function artifact and MLPNet violates monotonicity in asymmetric settings. Overall, Auctionformer trades a small L2 gap (≈0.002–0.012) against the best neural baselines for a 3–5 order-of-magnitude speedup and the unique ability to handle variable bidder counts and mechanisms in a single forward pass without retraining.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes pretrained Transformer architectures (BERT, GPT, LLaMA2) from NLP as a unified solver for auction game equilibria, departing from the agent-based no-regret learning paradigm (MWU, FTPL, OMWU) and specialized neural network solvers (NPGA, SODA, SM) that each require retraining per mechanism or bidder count. The discretization approximation theory is adapted from Bichler et al. (2022), and the few-shot adaptation mechanism draws inspiration from Dreambooth's preservation-loss approach. The key conceptual departure is using Nash error (Nikaido-Isoda function) as a self-supervised loss that eliminates the need for ground-truth equilibrium solutions, combined with a flexible tokenization scheme that encodes diverse auction mechanisms into a standard token sequence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Auctionformer demonstrates that a single pretrained Transformer can solve equilibria across multiple auction mechanisms and bidder counts in one forward pass with constant inference time, addressing a practical bottleneck where no-regret algorithms scale poorly with player count. The few-shot framework for adapting to unseen mechanisms (e.g., 15 or 20 bidders) without catastrophic forgetting is a practical contribution for dynamic auction environments. However, the evaluation is limited to single-item auctions with simple mechanisms (first-price, second-price, entry fee) and discrete value spaces; open questions include scalability to multi-item, combinatorial, or all-pay auctions, continuous value distributions, and whether the approach generalizes to games with incomplete information beyond the standard Bayesian setting.
