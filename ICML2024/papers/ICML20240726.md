## Problem

Existing adaptive task-weighting methods in multi-task learning assign higher weights to tasks with larger losses, implicitly assuming high loss reflects insufficient training. However, when label noise is present in one or more tasks, noisy tasks exhibit persistently high losses that stem from irreducible Bayes error rather than under-training, causing these methods to over-weight noisy tasks and degrade performance across all tasks. No prior MTL weighting scheme is robust to this label-noise-induced distortion of the loss signal.

## Input / Output

The method operates under hard parameter sharing where a shared feature extractor (two-layer CNN, ResNet-18, or SegNet depending on the dataset) processes task-specific inputs—RGB images, RGB-D images, or 21-dimensional joint state vectors—and task-specific heads (MLP or convolutional layers) produce task-specific outputs such as class labels, segmentation masks, depth maps, surface normals, or joint torques. A key implementation detail is a warm-up phase of 3 epochs during which no weight updates occur, collecting average risks to stabilize the initial excess-risk normalization that prevents sensitivity to arbitrary network initialization.

## Method

ExcessMTL replaces loss-based difficulty measures with excess risks (the gap between current loss and the Bayes-optimal loss), which naturally approach zero for all tasks regardless of noise level. Excess risks are estimated via a second-order Taylor expansion of each task loss, where the Hessian is approximated by the diagonal of the accumulated outer products of historical gradients (empirical Fisher), yielding an O(d)-cost computation per task. Task weights are updated via a multiplicative (exponentiated gradient) rule proportional to exp(η_α · excess_risk), derived from online mirror descent with entropy regularization on a Tchebycheff (min-max) scalarization of excess risks. The algorithm alternates between excess-risk estimation, weight update, and a standard gradient step on the weighted sum of task losses, with a scale-processing step that normalizes excess risks by their warm-up averages to handle heterogeneous loss magnitudes. Theoretically, the method achieves O(1/√t) convergence to Pareto-optimal solutions in the convex case and O(1/t^{1/4}) convergence to Pareto-stationary points in the non-convex case, matching the rates of MGDA and GroupDRO.

## Results

The paper evaluates ExcessMTL on four MTL benchmarks (MultiMNIST, Office-Home, NYUv2, SARCOS) by injecting label noise at varying levels (0–100%) into one or more tasks. The key finding is that ExcessMTL maintains flat performance on clean tasks across all noise levels, while all other adaptive weighting methods show monotonically declining clean-task accuracy as noise increases. On MultiMNIST with 80% noise, only ExcessMTL retains performance close to Bayes optimal on both tasks; on Office-Home with noise level = 1 (purely random labels), all other adaptive methods completely fail. On NYUv2, ExcessMTL consistently achieves the best pixel accuracy and angle accuracy on the two clean tasks (depth estimation and surface normal prediction) across all noise levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ExcessMTL outperforms all five adaptive baselines (GradNorm, MGDA, GroupDRO, IMTL, MOML) across all four datasets under label noise. GradNorm and GroupDRO rapidly assign substantial weights to noisy tasks due to their high losses, causing clean-task performance to degrade monotonically; in extreme cases (e.g., Office-Home at noise level 1), the weight on clean tasks drops to near zero. MGDA and IMTL initially allocate significant weights to the noisy task and converge to near-uniform weighting, but their emphasis on the noisy task still dramatically impacts clean-task performance. MOML, which uses a clean validation set for weight selection, remains largely impacted by label noise and performs worse than MGDA at low noise levels due to less gradient information. Uniform scalarization shows some resilience on the easy MultiMNIST dataset but is inconsistent across datasets, being outperformed by most adaptive methods on Office-Home and NYUv2. ExcessMTL's advantage stems from identifying that noisy tasks have lower excess risk (closer to convergence) and assigning them minimal weights, thereby preserving clean-task performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in adaptive MTL weighting (GradNorm, MGDA, GroupDRO, IMTL) that uses task loss magnitude as a proxy for difficulty, arguing instead that loss conflates model inadequacy with irreducible dataset noise. It repurposes the excess risk concept from statistical learning theory as a noise-robust difficulty measure, estimated via second-order Taylor approximation with an empirical Fisher diagonal Hessian. The optimization framework is grounded in online mirror descent (Hazan et al., 2016), with the multiplicative weight update derived from exponentiated gradient on the Tchebycheff scalarization of excess risks, connecting the method to Pareto stationarity in multi-objective optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Label noise from heterogeneous data sources is a practical reality in deployed MTL systems, and the paper demonstrates that all major adaptive weighting methods degrade catastrophically under even moderate noise, a failure mode previously unaddressed. The excess risk formulation provides a principled, by-definition robust alternative that requires no clean validation set or noise model. Open questions include whether the Taylor-based excess risk estimation remains accurate for very deep overparametrized networks where the local quadratic approximation may break down, and whether the approach extends to the broader family of loss-weighting applications (domain generalization, hard example mining) the authors mention but do not empirically validate.
