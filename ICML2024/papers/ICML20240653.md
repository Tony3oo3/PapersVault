## Problem

Deep learning research on fundamental questions (inductive biases, double descent, metalearning, self-supervised learning) typically requires large datasets like MNIST or CIFAR, making experiments slow, expensive, and difficult to iterate on. MNIST itself is too simple to differentiate architectures, too large for rapid prototyping, and fixed in structure so its noise and sample size cannot be easily varied. The paper asks whether a procedurally generated, 40-dimensional dataset with only 4000 training samples can faithfully reproduce these phenomena at a fraction of the compute cost.

## Input / Output

Each sample is a 1D sequence of 40 real-valued points, generated by padding a 12-point hand-crafted digit template with zeros, applying a random circular shift (up to 48 indices), Gaussian smoothing (σ=2), additive Gaussian noise (scale 0.25), white noise (scale 0.02), and a random linear shear. The primary output is a 10-way class label (digits 0–9); secondary outputs include a learned activation function (parameterized by a 1→100→100→1 MLP added to ELU) and a 16-dimensional self-supervised representation. All generation hyperparameters (noise scale, max translation, padding range, sequence length) are exposed via a simple API for controlled ablations.

## Method

The dataset is constructed by hand from ten 1D digit templates, then augmented with random padding, circular translation, Gaussian smoothing, and noise to produce 40-point sequences; the entire generation code is 150 lines of Python. The paper's empirical contribution spans six case studies on this dataset: (1) lottery ticket pruning of MLPs, showing that sparse subnetworks with local-connectivity bias outperform random sparse networks and the dense baseline even at >95% sparsity; (2) deep double descent by sweeping hidden-layer width under 15% label noise; (3) gradient-based metalearning of SGD learning rates and, for the first time, of an activation function via nested optimization; (4) SimCLR-style contrastive self-supervised learning with circular-shift augmentations, demonstrating guillotine regularization; and (5) pooling-method benchmarking across varying training set sizes. All experiments complete in minutes to an hour on a single GPU or CPU, versus GPU-days on standard benchmarks.

## Results

On MNIST-1D, a CNN achieves 94±2% test accuracy, a GRU 91±2%, an MLP 68±2%, and logistic regression only 32±1%, clearly separating architectures by their inductive biases. On the original MNIST, all models (logistic regression, MLP, CNN, GRU) achieve >94% accuracy, making architectural differences invisible. Shuffling feature indices drops CNN accuracy from 94% to 56% and GRU from 91% to 57%, confirming that spatial priors drive the performance gap. A human expert achieves 96±1% on MNIST-1D, matching or slightly exceeding the CNN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to the original MNIST (LeCun et al., 1998), MNIST-1D is over two orders of magnitude smaller (40 dimensions × 4000 samples vs. 784 dimensions × 70000 samples) yet far more effective at differentiating architectures: on MNIST all models saturate above 94%, while on MNIST-1D the spread ranges from 32% (logistic) to 94% (CNN). For activation function baselines, the metalearned activation (parameterized by a 1→100→100→1 MLP added to ELU) outperforms both ELU (Clevert et al., 2016) and Swish (Ramachandran et al., 2018) by over 5 percentage points in test accuracy on MNIST-1D, achieving a non-monotonic shape with two local extrema. Notably, this metalearning experiment required only ~1 hour of CPU time, whereas prior activation search work (Ramachandran et al.) used multi-day GPU/TPU cluster runs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a procedurally generated 1D dataset (MNIST-1D) that repurposes well-studied deep learning phenomena—lottery tickets, double descent, metalearning, SimCLR—onto a minimal 40-dimensional substrate, rather than proposing a new method or algorithm. It draws a deliberate philosophical parallel to the Synthetic Petri Dish (Rawal et al., 2020) but differs in using hand-crafted templates with controlled causal factors (noise, translation, padding) instead of metalearned data. The paper positions itself explicitly against the prevailing 'scale up' paradigm in science-of-deep-learning research, arguing that phenomena like benign overfitting and spatial inductive biases can be isolated and studied at a fraction of the compute cost of MNIST or CIFAR experiments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MNIST-1D offers a practical, low-barrier platform for rapid prototyping and educational use, with all demonstrated experiments completing in minutes on a single GPU or CPU—addressing a real bottleneck for researchers without large compute budgets. The finding that a human expert (96%) outperforms a CNN (94%) on this task, and that lottery tickets encode spatial inductive biases through local connectivity patterns, are interesting but largely confirmatory of existing intuitions. Open questions include whether the dataset is rich enough to capture phenomena that only emerge at larger scale, whether the metalearned activation function generalizes beyond this toy setting, and whether the 'scaling down' philosophy can be extended to more complex tasks like language modeling or multi-modal learning.
