## Problem

Instrumental variable (IV) methods face two fundamental limitations: the exclusion and exchangeability assumptions are untestable from observational data, and without additional structural assumptions the causal effect is often not point-identifiable. Existing testable conditions (e.g., instrumental inequalities) are necessary but not sufficient, and partial identification bounds (natural bounds, Tian-Pearl bounds) can be extremely wide or invalid when the IV is weakly violated. The paper asks whether information-theoretic quantities can provide both a new falsification criterion for IV validity and tighter causal effect bounds under a weak confounding assumption.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the empirical joint distribution P(X,Y,Z) over discrete observed variables, an entropy upper bound θ on the latent confounder (obtained from expert knowledge such as the marginal distribution or cardinality of a protected attribute), and a set of candidate IVs. It outputs (i) a binary reject/accept decision for each candidate IV, (ii) lower and upper bounds on P(y_o | do(x_t)) and the average causal effect, and (iii) a CCE-based ranking score for selecting among multiple IV candidates. The sensitivity parameter φ, quantifying the strength of any direct Z→Y violation, can be set externally or estimated from the CCE lower bound CCE(Z;Y|X) − θ.

## Method

The paper introduces the graph-specific conditional common entropy CCE_G(Z;Y|X), defined as the minimum entropy of a latent variable W that, when inserted into the causal graph, renders Z and Y conditionally independent given X while preserving the Markov condition. An iterative algorithm (IV LatentSearch) approximates this quantity by relaxing the hard conditional independence constraint into a regularized loss L = I(Z;Y|X,W) + (β₀+β₁)H(W) − β₁H(W|Z), where β₁ enforces the additional Z⊥W independence required by the IV graph structure; the algorithm alternates between forming the joint q(X,Y,Z,W) and updating conditionals via closed-form ratios until convergence. For IV testing, the paper proves that under weak confounding (H(U) ≤ θ), a valid IV must satisfy CCE_IV(Z;Y|X) ≤ θ, yielding a new falsification criterion that is tighter than the mutual-information-based bound. For partial identification, the causal effect is bounded by solving a linear program over the canonical partition b_ijk with an added mutual information constraint I(Y_x; Z, X) ≤ θ + φ, where φ parameterizes the strength of any IV violation; when φ → 0 the bounds recover the natural bounds, and the entropy constraint tightens them relative to unconstrained Tian-Pearl bounds.

## Results

On the Lung Cancer dataset, the graph-specific CCE_IV = 0.393 exceeds the confounder entropy H(U) = 0.31, allowing rejection of the invalid IV, while the general CCE = 0.104 does not. With the entropy constraint (θ = 0.31, φ = 0), the average causal effect is bounded by [-0.078, 0.828], tightening the lower bound from -0.272 (without entropy constraint) and suggesting a negative effect is unlikely. On the Pima Indians Diabetes dataset, CCE_IV for insulin (0.66) is substantially lower than for BMI (0.91), correctly identifying insulin as the preferred IV. On the Return of Schooling dataset, CCE_IV for quarter of birth (0.820) is far below year of birth (2.945), and on Colonial Origins, latitude (1.318) outperforms mortality (2.297) as an IV candidate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the natural bounds (Balke & Pearl, 1997), which are the standard sharp bounds for the IV graph under marginal stochastic exclusion and exchangeability. The proposed entropy-constrained bounds (Theorem 4.2) are tighter than natural bounds when the confounder entropy θ is small, as shown in Figure 10 where the average gap between bounds decreases with decreasing confounder entropy. For the drug effect example, the natural bounds overestimate the average causal effect (giving ACE ∈ [-0.364, 0.636] via Tian-Pearl bounds for the invalid IV case), while the proposed method with φ → 0 converges to the correct natural bounds and provides informative sensitivity analysis as φ increases. For the invalid IV graph, the natural bounds may not even be valid, whereas the proposed method yields tighter and valid bounds by incorporating the CCE_D sensitivity parameter. The IV selection heuristic (comparing CCE_IV across candidates) consistently identifies valid instruments with lower CCE values than invalid ones across all four real-world and synthetic datasets, with the gap being most pronounced for high-dimensional variables.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the common entropy framework (Kumar et al., 2014; Kocaoglu et al., 2020) by introducing a conditional variant (CCE) and a graph-specific variant that enforces the causal Markov condition, then applies both to the IV setting. It builds directly on the entropic partial identification approach of Jiang et al. (2023), which bounded causal effects using confounder entropy, by extending that framework to handle instrumental variables and incorporating a sensitivity parameter φ for weakly invalid instruments. The IV validation criterion complements the classical instrumental inequality (Pearl, 1995) by providing an information-theoretic necessary condition that leverages side information about confounder entropy, and the bounding method generalizes the natural bounds of Balke & Pearl (1997) by relaxing conditional independence to a mutual information constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled way to quantify IV assumption violations in information-theoretic terms and to obtain tighter causal effect bounds when the confounder is known to be weak, which is a practically relevant scenario (e.g., protected attributes with known cardinality). A key limitation is the reliance on the weak confounding assumption (upper bound on H(U)), which may be difficult to justify in many real-world settings; without it, the method degrades to a heuristic IV selection signal. Open questions include extending the framework to continuous variables, tightening the gap between CCE and the true minimum confounder entropy, and understanding how the sensitivity parameter φ can be estimated from data rather than specified a priori.
