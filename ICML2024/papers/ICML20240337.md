## Problem

Existing testing-by-betting methods for nonparametric sequential hypothesis tests require grid search over the hypothesis space to construct confidence sequences or test composite regions, and this search grows exponentially with the number of data streams. This computational bottleneck prevents practical application of anytime-valid inference to multi-stream problems such as best-arm identification or threshold identification in adaptive bandit settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240337/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequence of bounded observations X_t ∈ [0,1] collected under an arbitrary (possibly adaptive and unknown) sampling policy π_t, along with a convex composite hypothesis region R ⊆ [0,1]^W and a significance level α. It outputs a binary rejection decision valid at any stopping time, a confidence sequence (a convex interval in the single-arm case), and a finite stopping time for incorrect hypotheses. A key structural property is that the capital process is strictly convex in the hypothesis parameter, so testing a region R reduces to evaluating a single projected minimizer rather than scanning a grid.

## Method

PEAK defines a per-arm capital process K_t^a(m_a) = ∏(1 + (μ̂_{i-1}(a) − m_a)(X_i − m_a)/c) using a plug-in betting rate λ_t = (μ̂_{t-1} − m)/c, which makes K_t(m) strictly convex in m for all t. For W streams, the joint evidence is the average E_t(m) = (1/W)Σ_a K_t^a(m_a), which strictly dominates the union-bound test (Lemma 4) while preserving type-I error control via Ville's inequality for any sampling policy. The convexity of E_t(m) means that rejecting a convex region R requires only finding its unique global minimizer (via a single scalar equation or KKT conditions for structured regions like BAI), replacing the exponential grid search of prior betting schemes. Theoretical results establish power one under the mild condition that at least one misspecified arm is sampled infinitely often, and near-optimal asymptotic e-power relative to the likelihood-ratio bound in the Bernoulli worst case.

## Results

In the multi-arm Best Arm Identification (BAI) setting with W=4 arms, PEAK achieves stopping times of 708.52±266.34 (Beta/low-variance) and 1318.14±489.29 (Bernoulli/high-variance), representing up to an 85% reduction in samples compared to the Base union-bound stopping rule (4686.1 and 4631.66, respectively). For Threshold Identification (THR), PEAK stops at 725.81±218.49 (Beta) and 1678.08±666.39 (Bernoulli), again outperforming Base by ~84% and ~65%. In runtime, PEAK completes BAI hypothesis testing in 5.21±0.23s at fidelity 1×10⁻¹⁰, while Hedged requires 14.50±0.65s at a far coarser fidelity of 2.5×10⁻³.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PEAK is compared against four baselines: (1) Base, the union-bound stopping criterion from Kano et al. (2019) and Jamieson and Nowak (2014); (2) Sub-B, the Chernoff-based sub-Bernoulli confidence sequence of Howard et al. (2021a); (3) Hedged, the hedged capital process of Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (2023); and (4) Empirical Bernstein / PrPlH (single-arm only). In BAI, PEAK dramatically outperforms Base (85% fewer samples in Beta, 71% in Bernoulli) and Sub-B (78% and 69% fewer samples), and beats Hedged in the high-variance Bernoulli setting (1318 vs. 1735) but loses to Hedged in the low-variance Beta setting (709 vs. 500). In THR, PEAK is second-best in both settings, trailing Hedged in Beta (726 vs. 479) but beating it in Bernoulli (1678 vs. 2241). On runtime, PEAK at fidelity 10⁻¹⁰ (5.21s) is comparable to Hedged at fidelity 10⁻² (3.72s) but far faster than Hedged at finer fidelities (14.50s at 2.5×10⁻³), demonstrating a key computational advantage from avoiding grid search.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the testing-by-betting framework of Waudby-Smith and Ramdas (2023), which constructs e-processes for nonparametric sequential testing of bounded means. The key departure is the choice of betting scheme: where prior methods (AGRAPA, Hedged) require grid search over the hypothesis space that scales exponentially with the number of arms, PEAK's simple running-mean-based bet yields strictly convex capital processes, enabling convex optimization for composite hypothesis testing. The averaging of evidence across arms (rather than union bounding) is a conceptual contribution that strictly dominates the naive generalization, and the nonparametric setting (arbitrary distributions on [0,1]) contrasts with parametric e-process approaches like Kaufmann and Koolen (2021) that assume exponential families.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The computational tractability result is practically significant: replacing exponential grid search with convex optimization makes sequential composite testing feasible for moderate numbers of arms, and the 85% sample reduction in bandit stopping rules translates directly to cost savings in adaptive experiments. The HeartSteps case study demonstrates real-world relevance in digital health, where PEAK identifies treatment effects for 8 patients while Sub-Bernoulli fails entirely. Limitations include the requirement for bounded observations, the need for convex optimization solvers at each test step, and the fact that parametric e-processes remain more computationally efficient when distributional assumptions are justified. Open questions include scaling to very large W, extending to unbounded or heavy-tailed observations, and whether the convexity property can be preserved under more complex betting schemes.
