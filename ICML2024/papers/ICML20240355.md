## Problem

Most DP-SGD implementations use shuffling-based batch samplers in practice, yet report privacy parameters computed under the Poisson subsampling analysis from Abadi et al. (2016). This paper demonstrates that the privacy loss under shuffling can be substantially larger than what the Poisson subsampling analysis predicts, meaning commonly reported (ε, δ) guarantees can significantly underestimate the true privacy cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240355/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a training dataset of n records, a noise parameter σ, batch size b, number of steps T, and a batch sampler type (deterministic D, shuffle S, or Poisson P), and produces the privacy loss curve δ_B(ε) — the smallest δ such that the ABLQ mechanism satisfies (ε, δ)-DP under zero-out adjacency. The output is characterized via hockey stick divergence between tightly dominating pairs, with numerical bounds computed using the dp_accounting library (PLD and RDP methods) for the Poisson case and explicit set constructions for the shuffle case.

## Method

The paper uses the dominating-pair framework of Zhu et al. (2022) to compare privacy loss curves across three batch samplers. For D vs S, it proves via joint convexity of hockey stick divergence that shuffling never degrades privacy. For D vs P, it shows the curves are incomparable: Poisson is better at small ε (via total variation distance comparison) but worse at large ε (via an explicit halfspace lower bound whose dominant term decays as −εσ/√T versus −εσ for deterministic). The key S vs P result constructs explicit sets E_C = {w ∈ ℝ^T : max_t w_t ≥ C} to derive lower bounds on δ_S(ε), revealing that non-differing records in a shuffled batch leak information about the location of the differing record — a leakage channel absent in Poisson subsampling where each record is independently sampled. Numerical experiments (e.g., σ=0.5, T=10000, δ=10⁻⁶) show ε_S(δ) > 10.99 while ε_P(δ) < 1.96, a gap of over 5× in the privacy budget.

## Results

The paper demonstrates that using Poisson subsampling privacy analysis for shuffle-based DP-SGD implementations can severely underestimate privacy loss. For δ=10⁻⁶ and T=10,000 steps, at σ=0.5 the Poisson analysis gives ε_P < 1.96 (PLD) while the shuffle lower bound gives ε_S > 10.994 (nearly matching ε_D ≈ 10.997) — a gap of over 5×. At σ=1.3 under the same settings, ε_P < 0.031 (PLD) versus ε_S > 0.26, an ~8× gap. In the δ-vs-ε comparison (σ=0.4, T=10,000), δ_P(4) ≤ 1.18×10⁻⁵ while δ_S(4) ≥ 0.226, meaning the shuffle mechanism is ~19,000× less private at ε=4 than the Poisson analysis suggests.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three batch samplers — Deterministic (D), Poisson subsampling (P), and Shuffle (S) — as the core 'baselines' for the ABLQ mechanism. The key finding is that S always dominates D (δ_S ≤ δ_D for all ε), confirming shuffling provides some amplification. However, S and P are incomparable: for small ε, P is more private (δ_P < δ_D and δ_P < δ_S), but for large ε, P becomes less private than D (δ_P > δ_D). Most critically, in practical parameter regimes (small σ, large T), the Poisson analysis dramatically underestimates the privacy loss of the shuffle implementation: at σ=0.5, T=10,000, δ=10⁻⁶, the Poisson accountant reports ε < 1.96 while the true shuffle privacy requires ε > 10.994. This means the common practice of using Poisson-based privacy accountants (e.g., TensorFlow Privacy, JAX Privacy) for shuffle-based DP-SGD pipelines reports privacy parameters that are off by a factor of 5–8× or more, with the gap widening as σ decreases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the DP-SGD framework of Abadi et al. (2016), the Gaussian mechanism analysis of Balle & Wang (2018), and the dominating-pair/privacy-loss-distribution accounting framework of Zhu et al. (2022). It departs from the standard practice of analyzing DP-SGD via Poisson subsampling (which enables clean composition arguments) by formally comparing the privacy guarantees under three batch samplers—deterministic, shuffle, and Poisson—using hockey stick divergence. The paper also positions itself relative to the amplification-by-shuffling literature (Erlingsson et al. 2019; Feldman et al. 2021, 2023), noting that those bounds are too loose to be useful in the DP-SGD regime, and contrasts with concurrent work by Lebeda et al. (2024) on fixed-size independent batch sampling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that shuffling-based DP-SGD can have substantially worse privacy than the Poisson subsampling bounds commonly reported (e.g., ε_S > 10.99 vs. ε_P < 1.96 for σ=0.5, T=10000, δ=10⁻⁶) directly challenges the accounting methodology used in virtually all practical DP-SGD deployments. Key open questions include: obtaining tight (not just lower) privacy bounds for the shuffle sampler (Conjecture 3.2 remains unproven), extending the analysis to multi-epoch training, and accounting for practical data loaders (e.g., tf.data.Dataset.shuffle with finite buffer) that differ from the idealized full-shuffle model. The paper also raises the question of whether alternative methods like DP-FTRL, which do not rely on sampling-based amplification, may be preferable once correct shuffle-based accounting is applied.
