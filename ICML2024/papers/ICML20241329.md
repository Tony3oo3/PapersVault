## Problem

Deep neural networks struggle with perceptual grouping and unsupervised segmentation, in stark contrast to humans who effortlessly segment scenes using Gestalt principles like continuity, closure, and proximity. Existing unsupervised segmentation approaches either require fixed slot architectures or inject substantial inductive biases, and no unified, simple principle exists that allows DNNs to segment across diverse contexts with few in-principle limitations. This paper proposes the counter-intuitive hypothesis that neural noise, rather than being an obstacle, is the mechanism that enables perceptual grouping and segmentation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input and produces a pixel-wise segmentation mask as output, where each pixel is assigned a cluster identity corresponding to an object or background region. The number of clusters is specified per image based on the known number of co-varying parts in the scene. The model is trained exclusively on a reconstruction objective (MSE for AE, GECO for VAE) with no segmentation labels ever seen, and at inference the segmentation is extracted purely from the structure of noisy latent-space perturbations without any additional training.

## Method

Latent Noise Segmentation (LNS) uses a pretrained Autoencoder or Variational Autoencoder as a frozen backbone. At inference, i.i.d. Gaussian noise ξ_n is injected into the latent representation N times; consecutive noisy reconstructions are subtracted to yield Δx vectors that capture how each pixel's value changes under latent perturbation. These vectors (of dimension C×(N−1) per pixel) are then clustered pixel-wise using Agglomerative Clustering with Ward linkage to produce the segmentation mask. The theoretical justification is that VAE training drives latent units to encode local PCA directions of the data, so independent noise causes pixels belonging to the same object to co-vary in their Δx vectors while pixels from different objects do not, making them separable by clustering. The paper also introduces the Good Gestalt (GG) datasets—six synthetic 3-channel image collections (Kanizsa Squares, Closure, Continuity, Proximity, Gradient Occlusion, Illusory Occlusion) designed to test specific Gestalt grouping principles, where training images have co-varying pixel values within objects and independent values across objects, while test images introduce ambiguities (e.g., shared colors, occlusion) that require genuine grouping.

## Results

On the six Good Gestalt (GG) datasets, both the AE and VAE models achieve a mean Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of 0.849, substantially outperforming the Genesis baseline (0.600) and Genesis-v2 (0.216). The best per-dataset scores include 0.996 ARI on Proximity (AE) and 0.974 ARI on Illusory Occlusion (VAE), while the weakest is 0.552–0.593 on Continuity. Adding noise in latent space (vs. image-space reconstruction noise) improves mean ARI by 0.506 over the noisy-reconstruction control (0.333–0.356), confirming that latent-space perturbation is the critical mechanism. Segmentation performance asymptotes with as few as 5 time steps, and the VAE consistently prefers very small noise (σ ≈ 0.001) across all datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LNS (AE and VAE) against two unsupervised object-centric baselines: Genesis (Engelcke et al., ICLR 2020) and Genesis-v2 (Engelcke et al., 2022). LNS outperforms Genesis by 24.9 percentage points in mean ARI (0.849 vs. 0.600), winning on 5 of 6 datasets; the sole exception is Gradient Occlusion, where Genesis edges ahead (0.933 vs. 0.926/0.918) because pixel values there are largely informative of the correct segmentation. Against Genesis-v2, LNS wins by a much larger margin (0.849 vs. 0.216 mean ARI), with Genesis-v2 scoring near zero on Closure, Proximity, and Gradient Occlusion. The noisy-reconstruction control experiments (adding noise to image-space outputs rather than latent space) confirm that the latent-space noise mechanism is essential: they achieve only 0.333–0.356 mean ARI, roughly half the LNS score. Qualitatively, the paper notes that Genesis often follows color-value cues rather than true Gestalt grouping, whereas LNS captures the intended perceptual organization even when pixel values are ambiguous.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the theoretical result that VAEs learn to encode local PCA directions in their latent space (Rolínek et al., 2019; Zietlow et al., 2021), repurposing this property as a segmentation mechanism: by injecting independent noise into the latent variables and observing how consecutive reconstructions differ, pixels belonging to the same object co-vary while those from different objects do not. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant object-centric segmentation paradigm (slot-based architectures like Slot Attention, complex-valued autoencoders, and Genesis/Genesis-v2) by requiring no architectural modifications, no object slots, and no segmentation-specific training—only a standard reconstruction-trained autoencoder plus inference-time noise. The paper also introduces the Good Gestalt (GG) datasets, which are novel benchmarks explicitly designed to test Gestalt grouping principles (illusory contours, closure, continuity, proximity, occlusion) in a way that prior unsupervised segmentation benchmarks did not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a unified mechanistic account for multiple Gestalt grouping phenomena (illusory contours, closure, continuity, proximity, occlusion) as emergent consequences of learned representations plus noise, which is a compelling conceptual bridge between computational modeling and a century of perceptual psychology. However, the empirical evidence is constrained: all quantitative results are on simple synthetic datasets, CelebA generalization is qualitative only, and the method requires the number of objects as input. Open questions include whether the approach scales to naturalistic scenes, whether other generative models (diffusion, GANs) support the same noise-based segmentation, and how it interacts with temporal/motion cues. The GG datasets themselves are a useful contribution for the field of computational perceptual grouping.
