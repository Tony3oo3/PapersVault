## Problem

Deep learning PDE solvers typically achieve only 0.1%–1% relative L2 accuracy, while classical iterative methods are consistent but slow. This paper bridges the gap by using discretization-invariant neural operators as nonlinear preconditioners for the flexible conjugate gradient method, combining the O(N) efficiency of learned operators with the arbitrary-precision convergence of classical solvers. Prior learned-preconditioner approaches were restricted to linear or structured matrices, lacked discretization invariance, or had no built-in convergence guarantees.

## Input / Output

At inference, the method takes a sparse symmetric positive definite matrix A (from finite-element or finite-difference discretization of a 2D elliptic PDE on grids up to 256×256), a right-hand side vector f, and a pre-trained spectral neural operator, and iteratively produces the solution vector u via FCG. During training, the neural operator is fed residuals r drawn from Krylov subspaces (generated by running CG for m iterations on training samples) and trained to predict the corresponding error e = A⁻¹r. A key practical property is that the operator trained on a coarse grid (e.g., 32×32) can be directly applied as a preconditioner at finer resolutions (64×64, 128×128, 256×256) without retraining, owing to the discretization invariance of the spectral architecture.

## Method

The core contribution is training a spectral neural operator (SNO) with linear integral kernels in Fourier basis (4 layers, 20 modes, encoder–processor–decoder) to act as a nonlinear preconditioner B(r;θ) inside the Flexible Conjugate Gradient algorithm (Notay, 2000), which permits arbitrary nonlinear preconditioners while retaining convergence guarantees via Theorem 2.1. The training loss is a novel 'Notay loss' computed in the A-energy norm, L_Notay = E‖B(r;θ) − e‖_A / ‖e‖_A, derived directly from the FCG convergence theorem and shown to outperform the standard L2 loss by 25–60% in iterations to convergence. Training data is generated by running CG for 100 iterations on each sample to collect residuals from the Krylov subspace K_m(A, r₀), rather than using random right-hand sides; ablation studies confirm that Krylov-subspace sampling is critical, as random residuals cause the preconditioner to fail at higher resolutions. Because the SNO applies in O(N) operations without requiring any specific matrix structure, the approach avoids the fill-in and triangular-solve overhead of classical ILU or Gauss-Seidel preconditioners while achieving fewer iterations across all tested grid sizes.

## Results

On the Poisson equation at grid 128, NO+FCG requires only 20 iterations to reduce the residual by 10⁻⁶, compared to 216 for unpreconditioned CG, 37 for GS(4), and 128 for ILU(8). At grid 32, NO+FCG converges in 9 iterations versus 74 for CG and 13 for GS(4). In wall-clock time, FCG with SNO achieves up to 74% speedup over CG for the Poisson equation at grid 128 (residual reduction to 10⁻³), and 42% speedup for the Diffusion equation at the same resolution (residual reduction to 10⁻¹²). The method also generalizes across resolutions: a preconditioner trained at grid 32 still reduces iterations to 24 (vs. 216 for CG) when applied at grid 128.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against classical preconditioners (Saad, 2003; Demmel, 1999), NO+FCG consistently outperforms all baselines across both datasets and all three grid resolutions. At grid 128, NO+FCG (20 iterations) beats the best classical method GS(4) (37 iterations) by nearly 2×, and unpreconditioned CG (216 iterations) by over 10×. Against the GNN-based PCG approach of Li et al. (2023), FCG with the nonlinear SNO preconditioner converges faster at every resolution: at grid 128, FCG needs 48 iterations to reach 10⁻¹² residual reduction versus 47 for PCG at 10⁻⁶ (PCG does not reach 10⁻¹² within the tested budget at grid 128, while FCG does in 48). The paper notes that unlike GS(4) and ILU, the neural operator preconditioner does not require solving triangular systems, making it more amenable to parallel architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: flexible conjugate gradients (Notay, 2000), which provides convergence guarantees for nonlinear preconditioners, and neural operators (Li et al., 2020; Fanaskov & Oseledets, 2022), which offer discretization-invariant function-space mappings. Prior learned-preconditioner efforts (Hsieh et al., 2019; Cui et al., 2022; Hausner et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023) either restrict the preconditioner to a linear structured matrix (circulant, Cholesky factor) or pair it with iterative methods that require SPD preconditioners. The key departure here is using FCG's flexibility to accept an arbitrary nonlinear O(N) operator as preconditioner, combined with a novel energy-norm (Notay) loss derived directly from the FCG convergence theorem and a Krylov-subspace sampling strategy for training data—neither of which appears in prior learned-preconditioner work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method bridges a fundamental gap: pure neural operators plateau at ~0.1–1% relative error regardless of resolution, while classical solvers achieve arbitrary accuracy but scale poorly. FCG-NO inherits consistency from the iterative loop (error decreases with resolution) while retaining the O(N) cost of the neural operator, and the discretization-invariance of SNO/FNO means a single model trained at 32×32 can precondition a 256×256 system. Open questions include extension to non-SPD systems (where CG/FCG do not apply), non-uniform meshes, and higher-dimensional PDEs; the authors note GPU memory currently caps resolution. The energy-norm loss and Krylov sampling, while well-motivated, have only been validated on 2D elliptic problems, so their transferability to other PDE classes remains to be established.
