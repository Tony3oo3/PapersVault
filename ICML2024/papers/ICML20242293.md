## Problem

Before OpenMoE, there were few fully open-sourced, reproducible MoE-based LLMs trained on trillion-level diverse datasets, and the routing mechanisms inside MoE models remained poorly understood. The paper addresses both gaps by releasing a transparent MoE LLM series and conducting the first in-depth analysis of how token-to-expert routing actually behaves in post-ChatGPT-scale MoE language models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242293/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes tokenized text (via the umT5 tokenizer with 256K vocabulary and byte-fallback for out-of-vocab tokens) at a sequence length of 2048, and produces next-token probability distributions over the full vocabulary. For the chat variant, input includes multi-turn conversation history from WildChat. The MoE layer applies top-2 token-choice routing per token, meaning each token activates exactly 2 of 32 experts (plus a residual FFN), so only ~2B of 8.7B total parameters are active per token in the 8B model.

## Method

OpenMoE follows the ST-MoE architecture with token-choice routing, top-2 selection, residual MoE (a fixed FFN always active alongside the sparse experts), and interleaved MoE placement (every 4 or 6 Transformer blocks) to balance cost-effectiveness. Training uses a UL2 objective (50% PrefixLM + 50% SpanCorrupt with varied span lengths and mask ratios) for the early phase before falling back to standard CasualLM, combined with an aggressively code-heavy data mixture (up to 52.25% code from The Stack and GitHub). The key empirical finding is that MoE routing exhibits Context-Independent Specialization: tokens are routed to fixed experts based primarily on their token ID rather than surrounding context, this assignment is locked in during the warmup phase (Early Routing Learning), and the fixed max-capacity constraint causes later tokens in a sequence to be disproportionately dropped (Drop-towards-the-End), degrading multi-turn performance. Based on these findings, the authors propose removing the trainable router after warmup, adopting parallel Transformer layers to overlap attention and MoE communication, and mixing instruction-tuning data into pre-training warmup to achieve balanced routing on OOD data.

## Results

OpenMoE-8B/32E (2.1B activated params, 1.1T tokens) achieves 32.7 exact match on TriviaQA, 9.8 Pass@1 on HumanEval, and 3.1 BLEU on WMT16 En-Ro, outperforming both TinyLLaMA-1.1B (0.9B activated, 3.0T tokens) and OpenLLaMA-3B (2.9B activated, 1.0T tokens) on all three benchmarks with lower training cost. On BigBench-Lite, OpenMoE-8B/32E scores 6.93 average, surpassing GPT-3 6B (5.40), Big-G 8B (3.77), and Big-G-Sparse 8B (4.63). On MT-Bench, the chat variant achieves 4.69 on single-turn (beating OpenLLaMA-3B's 4.36) but drops to 3.26 on multi-turn (vs. OpenLLaMA-3B's 3.62), revealing the Drop-towards-the-End issue. On 5-shot MMLU, OpenMoE-8B/32E reaches only ~26.2%, near random guessing among four options.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against TinyLLaMA-1.1B, OpenMoE-8B/32E wins decisively on TriviaQA (32.7 vs. 11.2 EM), HumanEval (9.8 vs. 9.1 Pass@1), and WMT16 En-Ro (3.1 vs. 2.6 BLEU), while using roughly one-third the training tokens (1.1T vs. 3.0T). Against OpenLLaMA-3B, OpenMoE-8B/32E wins on TriviaQA (32.7 vs. 29.7), HumanEval (9.8 vs. 0, where OpenLLaMA fails due to whitespace tokenization), WMT16 (3.1 vs. 1.9), and MT-Bench single-turn (4.69 vs. 4.36), but loses on MT-Bench multi-turn (3.26 vs. 3.62) and the 13-task LM-Evaluation-Harness average (46.1 vs. 48.7). On BigBench-Lite, OpenMoE-8B/32E (6.93) outperforms GPT-3 6B (5.40), Big-G 8B (3.77), and Big-G-Sparse 8B (4.63), demonstrating a favorable cost-effectiveness trade-off. Mixtral and DeepSeek-MoE are not compared on standard benchmarks (due to non-reproducibility and later release dates) but are analyzed in Appendix P for routing behavior: DeepSeek-MoE exhibits similar Context-Independent Specialization, while Mixtral (trained via MoE upcycling) does not, and both show Drop-towards-the-End when a token-drop mechanism is manually applied.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on ST-MoE (Zoph et al., 2022) for its architecture and routing design (token-choice routing, top-2 selection, residual MoE, router z-loss) and on UL2 (Tay et al., 2022) as an alternative pre-training objective, motivated by PaLM-2 and code-completion alignment. It departs from prior MoE LLMs (GLaM, ST-MoE) by being the first fully open-sourced, reproducible decoder-only MoE series trained on trillion-scale diverse data, and by deliberately adopting an aggressive code-heavy data mixture (up to 52%) that contrasts with the text-dominated mixtures of LLaMA and contemporaries. The in-depth routing analysis extends brief visualizations in prior work (Shazeer et al., 2017; Zoph et al., 2022) into a systematic study across domain, language, task, position, and token-ID taxonomies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The three routing findings—Context-Independent Specialization, Early Routing Learning, and Drop-towards-the-End—challenge the implicit assumption that MoE experts develop meaningful semantic specializations and have direct practical implications for multi-turn conversation quality and expert-parallelism efficiency at scale. The paper's honest disclosure of sub-optimal design choices (aggressive code ratio, UL2 saturation) and its fully reproducible pipeline provide a valuable reference point for the open-source community. Open questions include whether mixing instruction data during pre-training warm-up can genuinely fix Drop-towards-the-End, whether removing the trainable router after warm-up preserves performance, and what the optimal code-to-text ratio is at larger scales.
