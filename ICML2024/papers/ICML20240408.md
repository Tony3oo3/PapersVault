## Problem

Classic RL maximizes additive objectives over individual states, which cannot capture interactions between states such as diminishing returns in exploration or synergistic effects in molecular design. Convex RL addresses non-additive objectives but optimizes asymptotic state distributions, performing arbitrarily poorly in finite-sample settings. This paper introduces Global RL (GRL), where rewards are defined globally over entire trajectories, and provides the first efficient approximate algorithms with curvature-dependent guarantees and matching hardness results.

## Input / Output

The method takes a controlled Markov process (discrete state space, action space, known transition model, initial distribution, horizon H) augmented with a global reward function F: 2^(S×T) → R that maps trajectories—sets of state-time pairs—to scalar returns. The reward can be submodular, supermodular, BP (sum of both), or arbitrary with a known decomposition. The output is a non-Markovian policy maximizing E[F(τ)] subject to dynamics constraints that restrict admissible trajectories to valid paths in the time-extended CMP, or an optimal trajectory in deterministic settings.

## Method

The core algorithmic scheme converts GRL into a sequence of classic MDPs via submodular semi-gradient methods. At each iteration, a tight modular lower bound of the global reward is constructed around the current trajectory (or policy): for submodular components, extreme points of the subdifferential polyhedron yield marginal-gain-based modular bounds; for supermodular components, a telescoping bound is used; for BP functions, the two bounds are summed. This modular lower bound defines an additive reward, reducing the problem to a standard MDP solvable by value iteration or other classic RL tools. The resulting GTO (deterministic) and GPO (stochastic) algorithms iterate between computing lower bounds and solving the induced MDP, with GTO guaranteeing monotonic improvement. Approximation guarantees are curvature-dependent—submodular rewards achieve a (1−k_F) ratio, supermodular rewards degrade with curvature k^F, and BP rewards combine both—with a matching hardness result showing no poly-time algorithm can exceed 1−k^G+ε for BP functions.

## Results

The paper evaluates GTO and GPO on three tasks (D-optimal experimental design, diverse synergies, and safe state coverage) using a 400-state grid environment with 95% confidence intervals over 20 runs. In the D-optimal experimental design task (submodular, deterministic), GTO performs significantly better than the optimal policy for the modularized objective (MOD), is competitive with SubPO, and is nearly optimal w.r.t. the optimal non-Markovian policy. In the diverse synergies task (BP, stochastic), GPO significantly outperforms the modular optimal policy, and in the deterministic variant GTO achieves the optimal non-Markovian policy. In safe state coverage (non-monotone BP, deterministic), GTO again significantly outperforms the modular optimal policy. The paper notes that practical performance after multiple iterations substantially exceeds the first-iteration curvature-based theoretical guarantees, particularly for submodular rewards.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares GTO/GPO against three baselines: (1) the optimal policy for the modularized (additive) objective F_m, which represents classic RL that ignores state interactions; (2) SubPO (Prajapat et al., 2023), a policy gradient method with variance reduction for submodular rewards, parameterized with a neural network (thus losing its approximation guarantees); and (3) the optimal non-Markovian policy w.r.t. the true global reward F, where computable. GTO/GPO consistently and significantly outperforms the modular optimal policy (MOD) across all three tasks, confirming that additive objectives cannot capture the interactions encoded in global rewards. In the D-optimal experimental design task, GTO is competitive with SubPO and nearly matches the optimal non-Markovian policy. In the deterministic diverse synergies setting, GTO achieves the optimal non-Markovian policy exactly. The paper does not report a single numerical comparison table; results are presented as convergence plots (Figure 3) showing J(π) vs. iterations, with GTO/GPO curves clearly above the MOD baseline and approaching or matching the optimal non-Markovian policy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces Global RL (GRL) as a new problem formulation that generalizes classic RL from additive state-level rewards to non-additive trajectory-level set functions. Algorithmically, it transports discrete semigradient methods from submodular optimization (Iyer et al., 2013) and BP function maximization (Bai & Bilmes, 2018) into the MDP setting, converting GRL into a sequence of classic MDPs solved by standard planners. Conceptually, it departs from Convex RL (Hazan et al., 2019; Zahavy et al., 2021) by optimizing set functions over finite trajectories rather than functionals over asymptotic state distributions, and it extends Prajapat et al. (2023) from submodular-only rewards to the broader BP (submodular + supermodular) class with curvature-dependent guarantees.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The curvature-based approximation guarantees provide a principled connection between the degree of non-additivity in a reward and the achievable solution quality, while the hardness result (Theorem 7.2) establishes fundamental limits tied to supermodular curvature. Open questions include extending GPO to unknown dynamics (the paper only handles known P), understanding why practical performance substantially exceeds first-iteration theoretical bounds, and whether the approach scales to continuous or high-dimensional state spaces. The framework unifies diverse applications (exploration, experimental design, molecular design, safe coverage) under a single algorithmic scheme, but the reliance on known transition models and the computational cost of Monte Carlo estimation in the stochastic setting limit immediate broad applicability.
