## Problem

Generalized state space models (GSSMs) like Mamba and LSTMs offer O(1) memory per token, but it was unclear what capabilities they sacrifice relative to transformers. This paper identifies copying and retrieval from input context as a fundamental gap, showing that GSSMs are provably limited by their fixed-size latent state while transformers can access arbitrary positions in the input.

## Input / Output

For the copy task, input is a token sequence formatted as ⟨BOS⟩, x₁,…,x_L, ⟨COPY⟩ where x is drawn uniformly from a dictionary of D tokens, and the model must autoregressively output an exact copy of x. For retrieval tasks, input is a text context (synthetic phone book or SQuAD paragraph) plus a query, and output is the relevant text span (phone number or answer). The critical constraint is that the model must access arbitrary positions in the input rather than merely summarizing it, which is what fixed-size states cannot do for long sequences.

## Method

The theoretical core constructs a depth-2 transformer using a novel Hard-ALiBi positional encoding that implements n-gram hash-based copying: local attention heads with bounded receptive fields compute n-gram hashes as keys, and a global (NoPE) attention head performs lookup to retrieve the succeeding token, achieving copying of sequences up to D^n tokens with only O(n log D) dimension. A matching lower bound proves any GSSM requires state size linear in sequence length to copy. Empirically, the paper trains ~160M parameter transformers and GSSMs on synthetic copy tasks, verifying that transformers learn ~5-gram retrieval (matching a perfect 5-gram model's accuracy) and generalize to 20× longer sequences than GSSMs. Pretrained Pythia transformers outperform Mamba GSSMs of similar size on copying, phone-book lookup, and SQuAD question answering, even when Mamba achieves lower language modeling perplexity on The Pile.

## Results

On synthetic copy tasks (~160M params), transformers require 100× fewer training samples than the best GSSM (Mamba) to reach high string-level accuracy on length-300 strings, while LSTMs fail to learn the task entirely. For length generalization (trained on ≤50 tokens, tested up to 1000), the Hard-ALiBi transformer maintains near-perfect accuracy while Mamba and LSTM drop to zero almost immediately. On pretrained models, the smallest Pythia transformer (410M) outperforms the largest Mamba GSSM (2.8B) on phone-book lookup (L≥70) and natural-language copying, and on shuffled strings of length 300 the largest Mamba achieves zero accuracy while Pythia retains high accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2023), transformers win decisively on all memory-intensive tasks: synthetic copying (100× fewer samples), length generalization (near-perfect vs. zero accuracy), pretrained natural-language copying (410M Pythia beats 2.8B Mamba), phone-book retrieval, and SQuAD on long contexts. The one exception is the prefix-key n-gram lookup task, where Mamba matches the Hard-ALiBi transformer because the task only requires tracking a small state rather than storing the full context. Against LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997), the gap is even wider: LSTMs fail to learn length-300 copying within the training budget and require 100× more data than transformers on shorter (length-30) strings. Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023) serves as the transformer reference in pretrained comparisons and consistently outperforms same-scale Mamba models despite Mamba achieving lower Pile perplexity, confirming that the architectural advantage is specific to context-retrieval capabilities rather than general language modeling quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of theoretical work comparing representational capacity of transformers to RNNs/GSSMs (Merrill 2019; Weiss et al. 2021; Merrill et al. 2022; Sanford et al. 2023) by providing a constructive, task-specific proof with tight bounds rather than comparisons to abstract complexity classes. The n-gram hashing mechanism it identifies in transformers is closely related to induction heads (Olsson et al., 2022), and the Hard-ALiBi positional encoding is a direct modification of ALiBi (Press et al., 2021). Empirically, it builds on prior length-generalization studies (Kazemnejad et al. 2023; Jelassi et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023) but is the first to systematically compare pretrained transformers and GSSMs of similar scale on memory-intensive tasks like copying and retrieval.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a fundamental architectural gap: GSSMs cannot accurately copy or retrieve from context beyond their fixed state size, while transformers scale gracefully. This is practically significant because the gap persists even when GSSMs achieve lower language-modeling perplexity, suggesting perplexity alone is an insufficient metric for architecture comparison. The authors suggest hybrid architectures that endow SSMs with attention-like retrieval as a promising direction, while acknowledging GSSMs retain advantages for O(1)-state tracking tasks and very long contexts (e.g., DNA sequences). Open questions include whether the n-gram mechanism generalizes to more complex retrieval patterns and whether the Hard-ALiBi encoding provides similar benefits in other tasks.
