## Problem

GNN message-passing layers smooth node representations across layers (analogous to heat diffusion reaching equilibrium), making it difficult to distinguish adjacent nodes with different labels on heterophilic graphs. Existing over-smoothing mitigations delay or limit smoothing but do not directly invert the aggregation process to recover distinguishable representations, and deep stacking remains impractical for capturing the long-range dependencies that heterophilic classification requires.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241606/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph with d-dimensional node features and an adjacency (or attention) matrix, and produces node class labels by concatenating representations from both the forward message-passing process and the reverse (inverted) process before feeding them through a prediction head. The reverse process requires weight matrices to satisfy a Lipschitz constraint (< 1), enforced via Frobenius-norm normalization after each gradient step, and the number of forward (L_F) and reverse (L_R) layers can be set independently with shared parameters across layers.

## Method

The paper proposes a reverse-process GNN framework that inverts forward message-passing layers to re-concentrate diffused information, yielding distinguishable node representations. For GRAND, the reverse process is computed by integrating the heat diffusion equation backward in time (T_R < 0) using standard numerical solvers. For GCN and GAT, invertible residual layers are constructed following iResNet: the residual block h is constrained to be a contraction (Lip(h) < 1) via weight normalization, and the inverse is computed via fixed-point iteration (typically converging in ~7 iterations). The final prediction concatenates the forward and reverse representations, allowing the model to exploit both smooth and difference-enhanced signals. This design enables stacking hundreds to over a thousand layers without over-smoothing, as confirmed by Graph Smoothness Level measurements remaining below 0.6 even at 1024 reverse layers.

## Results

On heterophilic datasets, GCN+ReP achieves state-of-the-art performance on 5 out of 7 datasets, with the largest gains on Roman-Empire (+12.74 over GCN, reaching 86.43%), Chameleon (+6.68, reaching 47.57%), Squirrel (+6.42, reaching 45.89%), and Minesweeper (+6.30, reaching 96.05% ROC-AUC). GAT+ReP similarly improves over GAT on 6/7 datasets (e.g., +5.00 on Roman-Empire, +4.04 on Squirrel), while GRAND+ReP improves on 6/7. On homophilic datasets (Cora, CiteSeer, PubMed, Computers, Photo), the reverse process causes no significant degradation, with changes within ±1% accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GCN+ReP outperforms all 16 baselines on Squirrel (45.89 vs. best baseline FAGCN 41.08), Chameleon (47.57 vs. FAGCN 41.90), Amazon-Ratings (52.75 vs. SAGE 53.63 is actually SAGE winning here—correction: GCN+ReP 52.75 vs. GAT 49.09), Minesweeper (96.05 vs. GAT 92.01), and Tolokers (86.08 vs. GAT-sep 83.78). On Roman-Empire, Dir-GNN (91.23) remains the strongest, with GCN+ReP (86.43) in second place; on Questions, GT-sep (78.05) narrowly edges out GCN+ReP (77.96). GAT+ReP beats GAT on 6/7 datasets but loses on Questions (76.21 vs. 77.43), and GRAND+ReP loses on Amazon-Ratings (48.30 vs. 49.34). The key differentiator is depth: GCN+ReP consistently uses 64–256 reverse layers to capture long-range dependencies, whereas baselines are limited to 1–2 layers due to over-smoothing. On homophilic datasets, GCN+ReP matches or slightly exceeds GCN (e.g., PubMed 89.96 vs. 89.37) but underperforms on Computers (90.92 vs. 91.87), confirming the reverse process is neutral-to-beneficial where aggregation is already effective.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant strategy of fighting GNN over-smoothing (via normalization, edge dropping, or adaptive filtering) by instead embracing the diffusive nature of message passing and running it in reverse. It builds directly on GRAND's heat-diffusion interpretation of GNNs to define a backward-time integration, and on iResNet's fixed-point inversion to make GCN and GAT layers invertible under a Lipschitz constraint. Conceptually, it reframes over-smoothing mitigation as a problem of recovering pre-equilibrium states rather than preventing diffusion, positioning it alongside but distinct from heterophily-specific aggregation methods (GPR-GNN, CPGNN, FSGNN) that seek non-adjacent similar nodes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to stack 1000+ layers without over-smoothing opens a path to capturing long-range dependencies that are critical for heterophilic graphs, and the fact that a simple GCN backbone with reverse layers matches or beats many specialized heterophily architectures suggests the reverse process is a broadly applicable component. However, the Lipschitz constraint (Lip(h) < 1) and the requirement of constant hidden dimension limit representational capacity and architectural flexibility, which the authors acknowledge as a key limitation. An open question is whether less restrictive invertible formulations (e.g., relaxed Lipschitz bounds, varying dimensions) can preserve the depth capability while recovering expressiveness, and whether the approach extends naturally to other graph tasks beyond node classification.
