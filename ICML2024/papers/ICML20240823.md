## Problem

Existing adaptive decentralized optimization algorithms (DADAM, AdaRWGD, DAMSGrad/DAdaGrad) all suffer from O(ε⁻⁴) sample and communication complexity for finding an ε-stationary point of nonconvex stochastic optimization, which is suboptimal compared to the O(ε⁻³) lower bound. Moreover, no adaptive decentralized algorithm existed for nonconvex finite-sum optimization. The paper asks whether adaptive decentralized methods can be designed that match the near-optimal sample complexities of their non-adaptive counterparts.

## Input / Output

Each of m clients holds a local dataset drawn from a potentially non-i.i.d. distribution D^i, and clients communicate only with neighbors over a fixed undirected graph (ring or 3-regular expander). At each iteration, a client draws a single stochastic sample (AdaMDOS) or a minibatch of size b (AdaMDOF) from its local data to compute a gradient, then exchanges messages with neighbors. The algorithm outputs a uniformly random iterate x_t^i from the trajectory, and the convergence guarantee is stated as the average gradient norm E‖∇F(x̄_t)‖ ≤ ε over T iterations.

## Method

The paper proposes two algorithms—AdaMDOS for stochastic optimization and AdaMDOF for finite-sum optimization—that combine three ingredients: (1) a momentum-based variance-reduced gradient estimator (STORM for AdaMDOS, ZeroSARAH for AdaMDOF) to reduce gradient variance without full-gradient computation, (2) gradient tracking (Xu et al., 2015) to mitigate consensus error under non-i.i.d. data, and (3) a unified local adaptive matrix A_t^i ≽ ρI_d applied as (A_t^i)^{-1}w_t^i in the descent step, which flexibly accommodates Adam-like, Barzilai-Borwein-like, or other coordinate-wise adaptive learning rates. A momentum iteration x_{t+1}^i = x_t^i + η_t(x̃_{t+1}^i − x_t^i) further accelerates convergence. The convergence analysis uses a Lyapunov function that jointly tracks the objective gap, gradient estimator error, consensus error, and adaptive-matrix-weighted gradient norm, yielding Õ(ε⁻³) sample complexity for AdaMDOS and O(√n·ε⁻²) for AdaMDOF—both matching known lower bounds.

## Results

The paper's headline theoretical result is a reduction in sample complexity from O(ε⁻⁴) for existing adaptive decentralized methods (DADAM, AdaRWGD, DAMSGrad/DAdaGrad) to O(ε⁻³) for AdaMDOS on stochastic nonconvex optimization, and O(√n·ε⁻²) for AdaMDOF on finite-sum nonconvex optimization, both matching known lower bounds. Communication complexity improves correspondingly from O(ε⁻⁴) to O(ε⁻³) and O(ε⁻²). Experimentally, AdaMDOS consistently achieves the lowest stationary gap across w8a, Covertype, MNIST (CNN), and Tiny-ImageNet (ResNet-18) under both ring and 3-regular expander topologies, while AdaMDOF is comparable to baselines due to its larger batch-size requirement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaMDOS outperforms all three adaptive baselines (DADAM, DAMSGrad, DAdaGrad) on every experiment: it reaches the lowest stationary gap on w8a and Covertype (Figures 1–2), achieves the fastest training-loss decrease and highest test accuracy on MNIST with a CNN (Figures 3–4), and ultimately surpasses all methods on ResNet-18/Tiny-ImageNet (Figure 5). On the ResNet-18 experiment, DADAM and the non-adaptive D² method initially lead in test accuracy, but AdaMDOS overtakes them within a few epochs and maintains the best final accuracy. AdaMDOF, which requires a batch size of b=√n, is comparable to the adaptive baselines rather than strictly superior, as the larger per-iteration cost offsets its theoretical advantage at the small batch sizes (b=10) used in the experiments. The non-adaptive baselines D-PSGD and D², included only in the ResNet-18 experiment, are consistently outperformed by AdaMDOS in the later stages of training. Overall, the key empirical takeaway is that AdaMDOS delivers the best convergence speed across all four datasets and two network topologies, while AdaMDOF's advantage is primarily theoretical (matching the finite-sum lower bound) rather than visible at the small batch sizes tested.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three well-developed threads: adaptive gradient methods (Adam and variants), momentum-based variance reduction (STORM, ZeroSARAH), and gradient tracking for decentralized consensus (Xu et al., 2015). Prior adaptive decentralized algorithms (DADAM, AdaRWGD, DAMSGrad/DAdaGrad) all achieve only O(ε⁻⁴) sample complexity because they apply Adam-like per-coordinate scaling directly to raw stochastic gradients without variance reduction. The key departure here is wrapping the adaptive matrix around a momentum-based variance-reduced gradient estimator (STORM for stochastic, ZeroSARAH for finite-sum) combined with gradient tracking, which is what enables the tighter O(ε⁻³) and O(√n ε⁻²) bounds. The unified adaptive matrix framework (satisfying A_t^i ⪰ ρI) is flexible enough to subsume Adam-like, AdaGrad-like, and Barzilai-Borwein-like learning rates under one analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Matching the known lower bounds for smooth nonconvex stochastic and finite-sum optimization in the decentralized adaptive setting is a clean theoretical milestone, and AdaMDOF is explicitly the first adaptive decentralized algorithm for nonconvex finite-sum problems. However, the practical gains are tempered: the finite-sum algorithm requires batch size b=√n, which limits its applicability to small-scale problems, and the experiments show AdaMDOF is merely 'comparable' to existing methods at the small batch sizes tested. The analysis also excludes sparse stochastic gradients and non-i.i.d. heterogeneity beyond the standard bounded-variance assumption, leaving open whether the near-optimal rates survive in more realistic federated or heterogeneous settings.
