## Problem

Existing complexity bounds for deep neural networks (covering numbers, Rademacher complexity, VC-dimension) treat all parameters as distinct, ignoring that permutation-equivalent parameterizations implement identical input-output functions. This leads to overly pessimistic complexity estimates that fail to explain why overparameterized networks generalize well and are easier to train. The paper asks: how much does functional equivalence actually reduce the effective complexity of a neural network class?

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully-specified feedforward architecture (depth L, layer widths d_0 through d_L), a uniform parameter norm bound B, locally Lipschitz activation functions with constants ρ_i, and an input domain radius B_x. It produces a covering number upper bound of the form (C/ε)^S / (d_1! × d_2! × ... × d_L!), where S is the total parameter count and C depends explicitly on B, B_x, d_0, and the ρ_i. Unlike prior bounds expressed in spectral norms of weight matrices (which are unknown in practice), this bound is fully determined by architecture hyperparameters and the infinity norm bound B.

## Method

The core mechanism is constructing an effective parameter space Θ_0 by restricting each hidden layer's bias vector to have components in descending order (b_1 ≥ b_2 ≥ ... ≥ d_l), which selects one representative from each permutation-equivalence class and reduces the Lebesgue volume by a factor of d_1! × ... × d_L!. Covering numbers are then computed on Θ_0 via a layer-by-layer induction: at each layer, an ε-covering of the parameter box is lifted through the Lipschitz activation to bound the function-space covering radius, and the product of per-layer covering numbers yields the final bound. The result holds for general (not just ReLU) activations and includes bias vectors, both of which prior covering-number results omitted. The paper further shows that permutation symmetry multiplies the probability of successful optimization by d_1* × ... × d_L* (the number of distinct row permutations per layer), and that the effective parameter space volume (2B)^S / (d_1! × ... × d_L!) vanishes as any width d_l → ∞, providing a geometric explanation for why wider networks are easier to train.

## Results

The paper derives a covering number bound for deep neural networks that improves upon existing results by a factor of $(d_1! \cdots d_L!)$ in the denominator, where $d_l$ is the width of the $l$-th hidden layer. For a network with equal-width hidden layers of width $d$, the bound reduces to $(C_{B,B_x,d_0,\rho}/\epsilon)^{\mathcal{S}} \times d^{\mathcal{S}-\mathcal{U}}$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the total parameter count and $\mathcal{U}=Ld$ is the number of hidden neurons. The bound is explicit in network architecture (depth $L$, widths $d_0,\ldots,d_L$), parameter norm bound $B$, and input radius $B_x$, and holds for general locally Lipschitz activations with bias vectors. Theorem 5.1 further shows that permutation symmetry multiplies the probability of successful optimization by $d_1^* \times \cdots \times d_L^*$, and that the effective parameter space volume $(2B)^{\mathcal{S}}/(d_1!\cdots d_L!)$ vanishes as any layer width $d_l \to \infty$.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's covering number bound strictly improves upon all four prior bounds in Table 1. Compared to Bartlett et al. (2017), Neyshabur et al. (2017b), and Lin & Zhang (2019), the new bound additionally accounts for bias vectors, supports general (not just ReLU/sigmoid) activations, is explicitly expressed in terms of network architecture and parameter norms (rather than spectral norms of weight matrices), and incorporates the factorial reduction from permutation invariance. Against the closest prior result, Bartlett et al. (2019) which also handles bias and general activations, the improvement is the $(d_1!\cdots d_L!)$ factor in the denominator — for a single hidden layer of width 128, this corresponds to a reduction of approximately $10^{215}$. The paper notes that all bounds still scale with the total parameter count $\mathcal{S}$ and can yield vacuous estimates in extremely overparameterized regimes, but the factorial discount provides a new quantitative handle on how width affects effective complexity. The optimization result (Theorem 5.1) has no direct prior counterpart in the compared works, as it leverages permutation symmetry to bound convergence probability rather than loss landscape geometry.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) the covering-number/metric-entropy bounds for neural network function classes established by Bartlett et al. (2017), Neyshabur et al. (2017b), and Lin & Zhang (2019), and (2) the functional equivalence and identifiability literature (Hecht-Nielsen 1990; Bui Thi Mai & Lampert 2020; Stock & Gribonval 2022) that characterizes when different parameterizations yield the same network function. The key departure is that prior covering number bounds treated the full parameter space as the relevant object, whereas this paper explicitly quotients out permutation invariance to obtain a smaller 'effective' parameter space, yielding bounds tighter by a factor of d₁!⋯d_L!. This is distinct from Grigsby et al. (2022a,b), who studied functional dimension and topological complexity but did not derive generalization-relevant covering number bounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical mechanism for the empirically observed phenomenon that overparameterized networks are easier to train: the Lebesgue volume of the effective parameter space (after quotienting by permutation symmetry) vanishes as any layer width grows, concentrating the optimization landscape. However, the analysis is limited to permutation invariance alone (ignoring scaling and sign-flip symmetries that could further reduce complexity), provides no lower bounds on covering numbers, and the bounds still scale polynomially in the total parameter count S, so they remain vacuous in extreme overparameterization regimes. Open questions include whether incorporating all functional equivalences yields even tighter bounds, whether the volume-shrinking argument can be made quantitative for specific optimization algorithms, and whether analogous reductions apply to the loss landscape geometry beyond the covering number.
