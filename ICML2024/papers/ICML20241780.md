## Problem

Deep neural networks remain brittle under spatially transformed inputs (rotation, scaling, shearing), and existing remedies are limited to two pathways: data augmentation (constrained by the intractable size of the transformation space) or hard-coded inductive biases (requiring bespoke engineering for each symmetry group). This paper asks whether a pre-trained classifier's hidden spatial invariance can be activated at inference time without any retraining or architectural modification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241780/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a 2D image perturbed by an unknown affine transformation from SL₂(ℝ) (rotation, isotropic scaling, shearing) and requires only access to the pre-trained classifier's logit scores—no gradients, no weight access, no training data. It outputs a class label and the estimated inverse transformation matrix that maps the perturbed input back to its canonical form. The search operates over a finite discretisation of the continuous transformation group (default cardinality n=17 per subgroup), and multiple parallel hypotheses (typically k=3–5) are maintained with a unique-class constraint to avoid redundant trajectories.

## Method

Inverse Transformation Search (ITS) traverses a sparsified k-ary tree where each level corresponds to one atomic transformation subgroup (e.g., rotation, then scaling, then shearing). At every node, |G| candidate inverse transformations are evaluated in parallel using a group-induced confidence measure: the model's energy (negative log-sum-exp of logits) is smoothed via Monte Carlo Dropout and convolved with a Gaussian kernel over the orbit, then the negative second derivative (curvature) is computed to yield a stable score that peaks at the canonical form while suppressing border artefacts. The top-k candidates by confidence are retained at each level, and a unique-class constraint prevents multiple hypotheses from converging on the same label. Theoretically, the search is formalised as a Markov process over transformation matrices, and Theorem 4.1 shows that only the subsequence preceding each eliminated element needs to be Abelian, relaxing the commutativity requirement for non-Abelian groups like full affine transformations.

## Results

On zero-shot affine canonicalisation, ITS³ achieves 88.89% accuracy on MNIST, 38.47% on Fashion-MNIST, and 66.44% on GTSRB, outperforming the best baseline (ETN) by 32.3, 12.4, and 26.4 points respectively. ITS also attains the lowest Frobenius distance to the ground-truth transformation matrix on all three datasets (e.g., 18.80 on MNIST vs. 23.03 for the plain CNN). On the Si-Score ImageNet benchmark (SI-Rotation), ITS with a ViT-B16 backbone yields 55.1% acc@1 zero-shot, marginally above the vanilla model (54.9%) but below Chefer et al.'s augmented fine-tuning (60.2%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the four learned canonicalisation baselines (STN, STN3, DTN, ETN), ITS wins decisively on all three datasets for both accuracy and Frobenius distance; the gap is largest on GTSRB where STN3 collapses to 2.78% accuracy while ITS³ reaches 66.44%. On rotated MNIST (Figure 7), the rotation-equivariant GCNN outperforms ITS at most fixed angles, but requires hard-coded p4-equivariant inductive biases, whereas ITS is model-agnostic and needs no architectural modification. On Si-Score SI-Rotation, ITS (55.1% acc@1) beats the rotation/scale augmented fine-tuning baseline (45.3%) in a zero-shot setting but is outperformed by Chefer et al.'s relevance-map optimisation (60.2%) and its segmentation-mask variant (61.0%), which require additional training. On SI-Size and SI-Location, ITS provides no improvement or slightly degrades performance (e.g., −3.6 points on SI-Size, −4.3 points on SI-Location with EfficientNet), as translation and scaling are already well-represented in vanilla ImageNet. Overall, ITS's advantage is most pronounced in the zero-shot affine canonicalisation setting where no baseline generalises beyond its training distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a third pathway to spatial pseudo-invariance, distinct from data-driven regularization (augmentation) and model-driven inductive biases (equivariant architectures like GCNNs, steerable CNNs, or Spatial Transformer Networks). It builds directly on the energy-based interpretation of classifier logits (Duvenaud et al., 2020) and Monte Carlo Dropout as a Bayesian approximation (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016) to construct a group-induced confidence measure over transformation orbits. The key conceptual departure from STNs and equivariant networks is that ITS requires no augmented training data and no architectural modifications—it operates purely at inference time on a frozen, pre-trained classifier, drawing inspiration from human canonicalization behavior and formalizing the search through the language of Lie groups and their finite subgroups.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ITS offers a model-agnostic, zero-shot mechanism to improve spatial robustness without retraining, which is practically valuable for safety-critical domains (medical imaging, satellite analysis) where retraining on augmented data is infeasible or the model is a black box. However, significant limitations remain: the Abelian assumption restricts the class of composable transformations, the runtime cost scales with tree depth and Monte Carlo samples making real-time use impractical, and the method degrades on translation (SI-Location) where the search can focus on background objects. Open questions include whether the search can be extended to the full non-Abelian group space (e.g., via simulated annealing or quantum algorithms), how to better resolve competing hypotheses, and whether the approach generalizes beyond 2D affine transformations to 3D or non-rigid deformations.
