## Problem

Previous work on Monotonic Linear Interpolation (MLI) attributed the monotonic decrease in loss along the interpolation path between initialized and converged parameters to the smoothness of the optimization trajectory or task difficulty. This paper identifies a critical gap: the MLI formula itself has never been analyzed as an independent object, and the role of the converged model's intrinsic properties (as opposed to the training path) has been overlooked. The authors show that replacing the initialization with unrelated noise or even zero values still yields monotonic decrease, revealing that MLI is fundamentally a property of the converged solution rather than the optimization trajectory.

## Input / Output

The method takes a converged parameter vector θ_F, an arbitrary noise vector ε (which may be the original initialization θ₀, an unrelated random initialization, zero, or a constant), and a scaling factor α ∈ [0,1], then evaluates the network at θ_α = αθ_F + (1−α)ε. The output is the test error or loss curve as a function of α, characterized by a plateau length (α_S) and a rapid-descent interval [α_S, α_E]. A 2D extension introduces a second parameter β for noise intensity, producing a test-error surface over (α, β) ∈ [0,1]×[−1,1] that simultaneously visualizes scale invariance (along β=0) and noise robustness (along α=1).

## Method

The paper decomposes the MLI formula into two components: αθ_F, a nearly scale-invariant network, and (1−α)ε, a noise perturbation whose magnitude grows as α decreases. It then formalizes three architecture-specific scale invariance properties: Generalized Scale Invariance (GSI) for networks without bias or BN, where all layer weights are scaled by a common factor and the output logits are rescaled; Rectified Scale Invariance (RSI) for networks with bias but no BN, where bias terms are scaled by the cumulative product of layer scaling factors to preserve the network function exactly; and Normalized Scale Invariance (NSI) for networks with BN, where the normalization step inherently cancels uniform scaling. The MLI phenomenon is then explained as the interplay between these invariances (keeping αθ_F functional even at small α) and noise robustness (the growing (1−α)ε degrading performance monotonically). The authors further explain previously unexplained MLI violations (large learning rates, Adam optimizer) by showing that these produce broader bias distributions centered at zero, which break the near-scale-invariance assumption, and demonstrate that resetting and warming up BN running statistics during interpolation restores the MLI property.

## Results

This is an analytical paper rather than a method paper, so there are no headline benchmark metrics. The key empirical findings are: (1) the MLI monotonic-decreasing property persists when the original initialization θ₀ is replaced by an unrelated random initialization or even zero values, across FCN4/MNIST, VGG8/SVHN, VGG16/CIFAR-10, and ResNet20/CIFAR-100; (2) 95 out of 100 randomly sampled unrelated initializations exhibit the monotonic decreasing trend; (3) networks with Batch Normalization show a significantly shorter plateau in the αθ_F error curve, confirming the Normalized Scale Invariance (NSI) property; (4) resetting and warming up BN running statistics restores the MLI property in cases where Frankle (2020) previously observed error barriers. The paper's central claim is that MLI is determined by scale invariance and noise robustness of θ_F, independent of the optimization trajectory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new method to be benchmarked against baselines; it provides a new theoretical explanation for the MLI phenomenon. The comparative experiments are structured around ablations of network architecture and training choices rather than method-vs-method comparisons. Networks with BN (NSI) consistently show shorter plateaus and faster convergence of αθ_F to θ_F performance compared to networks without BN (GSI/RSI), confirming that BN's normalization step is the primary driver of scale invariance. Networks with bias but without BN (RSI case) show a measurable performance gap between αθ_F and θ_F that grows with bias magnitude, quantified via MSE in Appendix A.2. For MLI violations, the paper shows that large learning rates and Adam produce a broader bias distribution centered around zero, which breaks the near-scale-invariance assumption and explains the previously unexplained violations reported by Lucas et al. (2021). Pre-trained ResNet50 on CUB-200 satisfies MLI, while RoBERTa on AG News does not exhibit strong scale invariance, attributed to Layer Normalization lacking the same invariance property as BN.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the Monotonic Linear Interpolation (MLI) phenomenon originally observed by Goodfellow & Vinyals (2015) and subsequently studied by Frankle (2020), Wang et al. (2023), and Lucas et al. (2021). While prior works predominantly attributed MLI to properties of the optimization trajectory (plateaus caused by bias terms, network depth, or task difficulty), this paper departs by decoupling the initialization from the converged model and showing that MLI is primarily a property of θ_F itself. It repurposes the well-known noise-robustness/sharpness literature (Keskar et al., 2017; Li et al., 2018; Foret et al., 2021) by formally connecting the MLI formula to the equation θ_F + ε, and introduces structured scale-invariance variants (GSI, RSI, NSI) that account for bias and Batch Normalization in modern architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The reframing of MLI as 'scale invariance + noise perturbation' provides a cleaner conceptual foundation that explains why MLI holds regardless of initialization and offers a mechanistic account of previously unexplained violations (large learning rates, Adam) through bias distribution width. Open questions include why Layer Normalization (as in RoBERTa) does not exhibit the same scale invariance as Batch Normalization, and whether the noise-robustness hypothesis can be formally proven rather than verified empirically. The scale-invariance taxonomy (GSI/RSI/NSI) could inform future work on model averaging, weight-space regularization, and understanding generalization in architectures with and without normalization layers.
