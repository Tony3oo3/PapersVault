## Problem

State-of-the-art data-driven flood forecasting systems train spatially distributed models on multiple gauges jointly but treat each gauge as an isolated prediction problem, ignoring the known flow topology of the river network. This paper asks whether explicitly encoding that topology via Graph Neural Networks actually improves discharge forecasting, and finds that it does not — the GNN performs no better than a plain MLP even when given the full river graph or allowed to learn edge weights.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240990/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 24-hour window of hourly discharge plus four meteorological variables (precipitation, topsoil moisture, air temperature, surface pressure) for all 358 gauges in the Danube sub-network, along with a 358×358 adjacency matrix whose entries vary across six tested definitions (isolated, binary, three physical weightings, and learned). It produces a single 6-hour-ahead discharge scalar per gauge simultaneously. All signals are per-gauge z-score normalised before training and denormalised for evaluation, and the loss and NSE metric are both weighted by a per-gauge relevancy score that emphasises high-variability, high-discharge windows.

## Method

The architecture is a sandwich: an affine encoder maps the 24-dim per-gauge input to a 128-dim latent space, 19 GNN layers (ResGCN, GCNII, or ResGAT) propagate information along the normalised augmented adjacency matrix with ReLU activations and residual connections, and an affine decoder projects back to one scalar per gauge. The depth of 19 matches the longest path in the preprocessed DAG so that information can theoretically reach every node. The core experimental design crosses six adjacency definitions (isolated, binary, stream-length, elevation-difference, average-slope, and learned) with three edge orientations (downstream, upstream, bidirected) across three architectures, all evaluated via three temporal train-test splits. A key finding is that the learned edge weights show no meaningful Pearson correlation with any of the physical weightings, and the isolated (edge-free) configuration performs within one standard deviation of the best topology variant, indicating the graph structure carries no exploitable signal for this forecasting task.

## Results

On the full LamaH-CE Danube A network (358 gauges), the best GNN configuration (bidirected-learned GCNII) achieves a weighted NSE of 85.56% ± 1.41%, which is statistically indistinguishable from the 2-layer MLP baseline of 85.37% ± 1.64%. Critically, the isolated (no-edge) GCNII achieves 84.12% ± 1.88%, showing that removing all river topology information costs less than ~1.5% NSE. Across all 18 topology-orientation-architecture combinations, no configuration significantly outperforms the MLP baseline, and learned edge weights show negligible Pearson correlation (|r| < 0.38) with any physical edge weighting (stream length, elevation difference, average slope).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between GNNs with various river topology encodings and a 2-layer MLP baseline (85.37% ± 1.64% weighted NSE). No GNN configuration—across three architectures (ResGCN, GCNII, ResGAT), six adjacency definitions, and three edge orientations—significantly outperforms this baseline. The isolated GNN (all edges removed, equivalent to node-wise processing) performs within 1–2% of the best topology-informed variants, indicating that the graph structure contributes negligible predictive signal. The learned edge weights, which the model is free to optimise, show no meaningful correlation with any of the three physical edge weightings provided by the dataset (Pearson |r| < 0.38, with sign flips across architectures), suggesting the model finds no useful structure to exploit. Small-scale subnetwork experiments (4 subgraphs with 4–10 nodes) confirm the same pattern, ruling out depth-related training issues as the explanation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the spatially distributed LSTM framework of Kratzert et al. (2019b), which trains a single model across multiple gauges to exploit shared hydrological physics, by replacing the per-gauge LSTM with a GNN that explicitly encodes river network topology. It also departs from Kratzert et al. (2021), who use a GNN only as a post-processing routing step after per-gauge LSTM predictions, by unifying prediction and routing in a single end-to-end GNN. The key conceptual departure is treating the topology question as an open empirical hypothesis rather than an assumed benefit, systematically ablating adjacency definitions (none, binary, physical, learned) across three GNN architectures and three edge orientations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central negative finding—that river network topology provides no measurable benefit over isolated-gauge prediction across all tested configurations—challenges a natural assumption in hydrological ML and suggests that the dominant source of predictive signal is local (past discharge at the same gauge) rather than relational. This opens a broader question about when and why graph structure helps neural predictors, which the authors frame as warranting systematic study. Limitations include reliance on a single dataset (LamaH-CE, Danube basin), absence of inter-gauge propagation time metadata, and lack of discrete flood event annotations, all of which could mask topology effects that become relevant under different conditions or with richer inputs.
