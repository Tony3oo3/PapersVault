## Problem

Transformer-based tabular data models suffer from oversmoothing: self-attention acts as a low-pass filter that progressively suppresses high-frequency information in deeper layers, causing token representations to become nearly identical. This limits the depth and representational capacity of Transformers for table representation learning, a problem previously unaddressed in the tabular data literature.

## Input / Output

The method operates on tabular data where each column (feature) is treated as a single token, typically yielding attention matrices of size n×n with n < 100. This small token count is critical: it makes computing successive matrix powers A^k computationally feasible, unlike in NLP or vision where n can reach thousands. The output is a learned table representation embedding that feeds into a small MLP head for downstream classification (AUROC) or regression (R²).

## Method

TabPSA replaces the standard self-attention AV with a matrix-polynomial graph filter H·V = Σ α_k P_k^{a,b}(A)·V, where P_k^{a,b} are Jacobi polynomials evaluated at the attention matrix A. The key insight is that the attention matrix satisfies the three conditions (stochasticity, irreducibility, aperiodicity) under which PageRank converges rapidly, so A^k·V stabilizes within a small number of iterations (k ≤ 10), bounding the polynomial order. Jacobi polynomials are used as the orthogonal basis to ensure stable coefficient optimization, and the learned coefficients can take negative values, enabling the filter to recover high-frequency signals that vanilla self-attention discards. The layer is a direct drop-in replacement for self-attention in existing Transformer architectures (TabTransformer, SAINT, MET), requiring only k+1 additional scalar parameters.

## Results

TabPSA improves the three base Transformer models across all 10 tabular datasets, with average relative gains of 2.14% (TabTransformer), 1.02% (SAINT), and 5.76% (MET) in AUROC/R². In the full comparison against 10 baselines (Table 4), the TabPSA-enhanced models achieve the best score in 6 out of 10 datasets, outperforming strong ensemble methods like XGBoost and Random Forest. On the GLUE benchmark (Appendix F.1), BERT_base + TabPSA improves the average score from 82.51 to 83.22, and on TP-BERTa (Appendix F.2), TabPSA yields consistent gains of 0.31%–6.18% across five datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the three base Transformer models, TabPSA consistently improves performance: TabTransformer gains 2.14% (76.9→78.5), SAINT gains 1.02% (84.5→85.4), and MET gains 5.76% (79.4→84.0) in average AUROC/R². Compared to XGBoost and Random Forest (the strongest ensemble baselines), the TabPSA-enhanced models achieve the best score in 6 of 10 datasets; the notable exception is Activity, where XGBoost (98.1) and Random Forest (98.0) still lead. Against VIME and TabNet, the TabPSA models dominate across nearly all datasets, with TabNet showing particularly weak results (e.g., −1.18 R² on Medicalcost). On the GLUE benchmark, BERT_base + TabPSA improves the average from 82.51 to 83.22, with gains on CoLA (+2.64), MRPC (+3.08), STS-B (+0.94), and RTE (+3.62), though it slightly drops on MNLI-m (−2.77). On the more recent TP-BERTa model, TabPSA yields improvements on all five tested datasets (0.31%–6.18%), confirming the method's generalizability beyond the three original base models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reinterprets the standard self-attention mechanism (AV) as a first-order graph filter and extends it to a matrix polynomial filter (Σ w_k A^k V) inspired by graph signal processing. It builds directly on the oversmoothing literature for Transformers and GCNs (Wang et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2021), which established that self-attention acts as a low-pass filter, and on prior work using polynomial graph filters (Chebyshev, Bernstein) to counteract this in graph neural networks. The key departure is leveraging the PageRank convergence property of softmax-normalized attention matrices to argue that only a few polynomial terms are needed, making the approach computationally feasible specifically for tabular data where token counts are small. The use of Jacobi polynomials for stable coefficient optimization draws from spectral graph filtering literature, though experiments show simpler Chebyshev and Legendre bases often perform comparably or better.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, drop-in replacement for self-attention in tabular Transformers that mitigates oversmoothing and modestly improves representation quality across three established models (TabTransformer, SAINT, MET). Its practical significance is tempered by the narrow applicability: the method relies on small token counts (tens of columns), limiting generalization to domains with large sequence lengths. An open question is whether the polynomial filter framework can be adapted to larger token regimes via sparsification or low-rank approximations. Additionally, the finding that the specifically designed Jacobi basis underperforms simpler orthogonal polynomials in several settings suggests the optimal filter shape for tabular data remains an open design question.
