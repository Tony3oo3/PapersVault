## Problem

RLHF and Constitutional AI rely on human feedback that is often unrepresentative, collected through unrealistic decision-making models, and aggregated in intransparent ways that can marginalize significant stakeholder groups. The paper argues that social choice theory—which has rigorously studied how to aggregate diverse preferences into consistent collective decisions for decades—has not been systematically applied to these alignment problems, leaving the field to reinvent the wheel and make naive mistakes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20240371/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The proposed pipeline accepts heterogeneous human feedback (pairwise comparisons, full or partial rankings, numerical ratings, free-form verbal evaluations, and constitutional principles) along with evaluator feature vectors f_i that encode who is providing feedback. A key design choice is that the output of the aggregation step need not match the type of individual input: evaluators may rank plain responses while the social choice rule outputs a probability distribution over responses or a multi-winner set. The final output is either a fine-tuned LLM (via RL or supervised learning) or, in the most radical variant, a live system that simulates a collective decision at inference time and returns the winning response directly.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary architectures. First, RLCHF (Reinforcement Learning from Collective Human Feedback) inserts an explicit social welfare function F into the standard RLHF pipeline: either ordinal aggregation of individual rankings between data collection and reward-model training, or cardinal aggregation of simulated individual ratings (produced by an individual preference model ψ conditioned on evaluator features) between reward-model training and RL fine-tuning. Second, a 'Simulated Collective Decisions' variant replaces the RL step entirely, using a single-winner or multi-winner social choice function C to select a winning response from simulated evaluator ratings, then training via supervised learning or performing the collective choice at inference time. A supporting component is an evaluation interpretation model φ that maps diverse verbal feedback into numerical ratings, enabling heterogeneous inputs to be converted into a common form before aggregation. The paper also maps specific social-choice-theoretic desiderata—independence of clones, strategy-proofness, anonymity, and the treatment of ethical principles as 'voters'—onto concrete design constraints for alignment systems.

## Results

This is a position paper that proposes a research agenda rather than reporting experimental results. It does not present quantitative benchmarks, ablations, or performance metrics. The paper's contribution is conceptual: it identifies specific open problems in RLHF and Constitutional AI (e.g., who provides feedback, how to aggregate diverse preferences, strategic manipulation) and maps them onto existing social choice theory tools (Arrow's Impossibility Theorem, Borda Count, Instant Runoff, Ranked Pairs, judgment aggregation, multi-winner rules). The only illustrative example is Figure 1, which shows how three aggregation rules (Borda Count, Instant Runoff, Ranked Pairs) produce different winners from the same set of 15 voter rankings over three alternatives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This position paper does not compare its proposed methods against baselines or report experimental outcomes. It is a survey-and-proposal paper that draws on existing results from social choice theory (e.g., Arrow 1951, Gibbard 1973, Satterthwaite 1975, Moulin 1980) and existing AI alignment work (Christiano et al. 2017, Bai et al. 2022a,b, Rafailov et al. 2023) to argue that social choice methods should be integrated into the RLHF and Constitutional AI pipelines. The paper explicitly calls for future empirical work to validate the proposed RLCHF (Reinforcement Learning from Collective Human Feedback) and simulated collective decision frameworks, noting that 'significant further research is needed to answer these questions well.'

## Relation to Prior Work

This position paper bridges two largely disjoint literatures: computational social choice (studied since Arrow's 1951 impossibility theorem and formalized in the 2015 Handbook of Computational Social Choice) and modern AI alignment via RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017) and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b). The authors note that while a handful of 2024 papers have begun exploring social choice methods for RLHF (Song et al., Swamy et al., Dai & Fleisig, Ge et al.), there has been no systematic framing of which social choice concepts—preference aggregation, strategic voting, independence of clones, judgment aggregation—are most relevant to alignment. The paper's conceptual contribution is proposing RLCHF (Reinforcement Learning from Collective Human Feedback) and simulated collective decisions as principled extensions of the RLHF pipeline, replacing the implicit and intransparent preference aggregation that current reward models perform.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete gap in current alignment practice: when RLHF training data comes from diverse sources, the reward model implicitly performs preference aggregation in a way that is neither transparent nor governed by any axiomatic desiderata, potentially entrenching biases of a narrow annotator pool. The open questions are substantial—how to select representative stakeholder populations, which aggregation axioms (anonymity, independence of clones, strategy-proofness) should hold in an AI context, how to handle behavioral effects and cognitive limitations in feedback, and whether multiple AI systems should be built for different stakeholder groups. As a position paper with no empirical validation, its impact depends on whether the proposed RLCHF and simulated-decision architectures can be made computationally tractable and whether the social choice community and AI alignment community actually converge on shared formalizations.
