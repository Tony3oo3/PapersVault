## Problem

While LLMs can generate individual planning steps, it remains unclear whether they can compute the optimal execution time for asynchronous plans that require simultaneously reasoning about sequential dependencies, parallelism, and time arithmetic. No large-scale naturalistic benchmark existed to evaluate this capability, and no formal complexity measure was available to predict when LLMs would fail on such tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241213/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The task takes a natural-language description of a multi-step activity with per-step time durations (in heterogeneous units like minutes, hours, days, weeks) and pairwise ordering constraints, and requires the model to output the single shortest completion time. In the PLaG explicit-graph variant, a structured graph representation (adjacency list, edge list, adjacency matrix, or CSR) is appended to the prompt; in the BaG variant, the model must first generate its own graph before computing the answer. Ground-truth answers are computed deterministically via longest-path on a series-parallel DAG, so correctness is exact rather than approximate.

## Method

The core contribution is Plan Like a Graph (PLaG), a prompting technique that instructs LLMs to represent the planning problem as a graph and reason over it, inspired by Discourse Representation Theory. PLaG has two variants: explicit graph, where a symbolically generated graph is embedded in the prompt alongside k-shot CoT examples, and Build a Graph (BaG), where the model is prompted to construct the graph itself before solving. The paper also introduces AsyncHow, a 1.6K-instance benchmark automatically generated from WikiHow and ProScript using GPT-3.5/4 for time and dependency annotation, with ground truth computed by deterministic longest-path search. Task complexity is formally defined as |V|+|E| on the underlying series-parallel DAG, and the paper empirically validates that this measure predicts LLM accuracy across all prompting settings. PLaG consistently improves every tested model (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, LLaMA-2-70B, Mistral-7B, Cohere Command) over k-shot+CoT baselines, with BaG outperforming explicit graph due to more favorable prompt positioning of the generated structure.

## Results

On the AsyncHow benchmark (1.6K instances), GPT-4 with PLaG (BaG) achieves the highest accuracy of 0.777, outperforming the best non-PLaG setting (GPT-4 k-shot+CoT at 0.657) by 12 percentage points. Without any graph-based prompting, all models perform poorly: GPT-4 drops to 0.130 in zero-shot, and open-source models (LLaMA-2-70B-chat, Mistral-7B-Instruct) never exceed 0.149 accuracy. PLaG consistently improves every tested model, with the largest absolute gains for GPT-4 (+0.120 over k-shot+CoT) and GPT-3.5 (+0.129 over k-shot+CoT).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PLaG (BaG) outperforms all five baselines for the two strongest models: GPT-4 reaches 0.777 vs. k-shot+CoT (Wei et al.) at 0.657, CoT-SC (Wang et al.) at 0.625, and ToT (Yao et al.) at 0.624; GPT-3.5 reaches 0.355 vs. k-shot+CoT at 0.226, CoT-SC at 0.240, and ToT at 0.263. Against k-shot prompting (Brown et al.), PLaG wins for every model (e.g., GPT-4: 0.777 vs. 0.107). Against zero-shot CoT (Kojima et al.), PLaG also dominates across all models (e.g., GPT-4: 0.777 vs. 0.129). For weaker models (Command, LLaMA-2, Mistral), PLaG (explicit graph) provides modest gains over k-shot+CoT (e.g., LLaMA-2: 0.101 vs. 0.076), while PLaG (BaG) does not consistently surpass k-shot+CoT, suggesting the self-generated graph variant is most beneficial for capable models. The paper also notes that PLaG achieves these gains at lower token cost than CoT-SC and ToT (1,775/242 tokens vs. 1,289/407 and 5,212/335 for GPT-4).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on LLMs for planning (Valmeekam et al., 2022; Collins et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023), which has shown that LLMs require external symbolic processors to produce optimal plans, by demonstrating that graph-structured prompting alone (PLaG) can substantially close the gap without any external solver. The Build-a-Graph (BaG) component of PLaG is directly inherited from Wang et al. (2023), who showed LLMs can reason over graph-structured data in natural language, while the explicit-graph variant draws on Discourse Representation Theory (Wolf et al., 2004) for structured representations. The benchmark builds on ProScript (Sakaguchi et al., 2021) for human-annotated partial-order plans and WikiHow for naturalistic task descriptions, but departs from both by introducing asynchronous (mixed sequential-parallel) constraints and a formal DAG-based complexity measure that prior planning benchmarks lacked.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The AsyncHow benchmark and the finding that all tested LLMs (including GPT-4) perform near-chance without solution illustrations provide a concrete, reproducible measure of the gap between LLM planning capability and the requirements for autonomous agent deployment. The PLaG technique is an off-the-shelf prompting method that practitioners can immediately apply to any graph-abstractable task, and the complexity measure |V|+|E| offers a principled way to predict where LLMs will fail. Key open questions include whether performance degrades similarly under finite-resource constraints (Petri net setting), whether the BaG > explicit-graph advantage generalizes beyond the prompt-positioning explanation, and how LLM error patterns compare to human planning errors at matched complexity levels.
