## Problem

Generative models lack a principled theoretical framework for assessing generalization behavior and estimating uncertainty. Existing bias-variance decompositions either apply only to mean squared error or require access to the predicted distribution, which is infeasible for sample-based generative models (e.g., diffusion models) and closed-source LLMs. No decomposition existed that operates on kernel scores—loss functions computable from samples alone—and includes a covariance term for ensemble analysis.

## Input / Output

The method consumes only generated samples from one or more models (or training epochs), a positive semi-definite kernel (RBF, Laplacian, polynomial, or contiguous-subsequence), and optionally a pretrained text embedder (e.g., e5-small-v2) for language tasks. It produces unbiased, consistent estimates of distributional variance and covariance via a two-stage sampling procedure (outer loop over models/epochs, inner loop over samples per model), plus a predictive kernel entropy computed from a single model's samples. The estimators require at least two samples per distribution and are recommended with m ≥ n ≥ 10 inner/outer samples; runtime is O(m²n²) for the variance estimator.

## Method

The paper proves Theorem 3.2, which decomposes the expected kernel score into noise (−‖Q‖²ₖ), bias (‖E[P̂]−Q‖²ₖ), and distributional variance (Varₖ(P̂)), with the variance further splitting into variance and covariance terms for ensembles. Distributional variance and covariance are defined via the kernel-induced semi-norm, generalizing scalar multiplication to an inner product in the RKHS. The key estimators compute the average kernel similarity within clusters (same model/epoch) minus the average similarity between clusters (different models/epochs), yielding unbiased estimates that converge in probability with the outer sample size. For single-model uncertainty, the predictive kernel entropy (negative average pairwise kernel value) is shown to correlate ~0.95 with generalization error across image, audio, and language generation. For LLM question answering, combining text embeddings with an RBF kernel outperforms predictive entropy, semantic entropy, p(True), and lexical similarity baselines on CoQA and TriviaQA, and remains applicable to closed-source models.

## Results

On the CoQA and TriviaQA question-answering datasets, predictive kernel entropy (using e5-small-v2 embeddings with an RBF kernel) achieves the highest AUROC across nearly all OPT model sizes (125M–13B), outperforming semantic entropy, predictive entropy, p(True), and lexical similarity. For the model-free setting (no embedding model), the CS kernel entropy reaches AUROC of 0.710 on CoQA (OPT-6.7B) and 0.702 on TriviaQA (OPT-6.7B), consistently exceeding lexical similarity (0.638 and 0.668 respectively). In image generation on InfiMNIST, the Pearson correlation between predictive kernel entropy and the kernel score reaches approximately 0.95 throughout training, and distributional variance correlates linearly with MMD². Mode collapse for underrepresented digit classes is shown to be expressed purely in the bias term, with variance continuing to decrease as if no collapse occurred.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares kernel entropy against four families of uncertainty baselines on CoQA and TriviaQA using OPT models of varying sizes. Against semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023), kernel entropy with e5-small-v2 embeddings achieves higher AUROC in almost all model-size/dataset combinations, with the gap widening for larger models (e.g., OPT-6.7B on CoQA). Against predictive entropy and normalized predictive entropy (Malinin & Gales, 2020), kernel entropy is more robust across model sizes, while the entropy-based methods degrade for smaller models. Against p(True) (Kadavath et al., 2022), kernel entropy outperforms across the board and additionally works for closed-source models where p(True) is inapplicable. Against lexical similarity (Fomicheva et al., 2020), the embedding-based kernel entropy clearly wins, and even the model-free CS kernel entropy (Table 1) surpasses lexical similarity on CoQA for all models above 125M (e.g., 0.710 vs. 0.638 for OPT-6.7B) and on TriviaQA for models above 350M (e.g., 0.702 vs. 0.668 for OPT-6.7B), though lexical similarity edges out CS kernel entropy for the smallest OPT-125M on TriviaQA (0.582 vs. 0.520).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the bias-variance-covariance decomposition of Ueda & Nakano (1996) from the mean squared error to kernel scores, and builds on the distributional bias-variance decomposition of Gruber & Buettner (2023) by adding the missing covariance term and, critically, providing sample-based estimators that do not require access to the predicted distribution. This last point is a substantial practical departure: prior distributional decompositions assumed the model's output distribution was available in closed form, whereas here only generated samples are needed, making the framework applicable to diffusion models, flow-based TTS, and closed-source LLMs. The uncertainty estimation experiments are positioned against the NLP-specific baselines of Kuhn et al. (2023) (semantic entropy), Malinin & Gales (2020) (predictive entropy), Kadavath et al. (2022) (p(True)), and Fomicheva et al. (2020) (lexical similarity), which the authors characterize as ad-hoc and non-transferable across modalities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first modality-agnostic, theory-grounded uncertainty measure for generative models that works even when the model is a black box, which is increasingly relevant as closed-source LLMs dominate practical deployment. The finding that mode collapse manifests purely in the bias term (with variance continuing to decrease) is a useful diagnostic insight, though it is demonstrated only in a controlled synthetic setting (InfiMNIST with one digit reduced to ~1%). Open questions include whether the i.i.d. assumption for ensemble members holds in practice for large-scale training, how the correlation sign between kernel entropy and error generalizes beyond the tested tasks, and whether the strong dependence on embedding quality (the embedder matters far more than the kernel choice) limits applicability in low-resource or non-English settings.
