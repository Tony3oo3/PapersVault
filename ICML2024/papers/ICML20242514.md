## Problem

Released LLMs finetuned on noisy downstream data containing unsafe examples (bias, toxicity, harmfulness) readily learn and replicate unsafe behaviors, yet the standard remedy—sequential safety finetuning—causes catastrophic forgetting of important downstream knowledge. No existing method can filter diverse, implicit unsafe examples from mixed downstream data without domain-specific classifiers while preserving downstream task performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20242514/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes an aligned LLM checkpoint, a noisy downstream dataset (e.g., 5000 mixed examples with a configurable unsafe proportion R_unsafe), and a curated set of safe examples matching the safety notion being targeted (e.g., unbiased examples to filter bias). It outputs a filtered downstream dataset with unsafe examples removed and a retrained model from the original checkpoint. A critical constraint is that the safe examples must semantically correspond to the unsafe category; a domain shift (e.g., using unbiased data to filter toxic data) eliminates the forgetting discrepancy and degrades filtering effectiveness.

## Method

ForgetFilter exploits an empirically discovered property that sufficiently large LLMs (≥7B) forget unsafe examples significantly faster than safe or task-irrelevant examples during safety finetuning, due to semantic conflict between unsafe content and the safe review data. The procedure is: (1) train the aligned checkpoint M₀ on the full noisy dataset to obtain M₁; (2) finetune M₁ on safe examples for t steps to obtain M₂; (3) compute per-example forgetting rates r(t,x,y) using ROUGE-1 similarity between the model's generation at M₁ and M₂; (4) discard all examples with r > φ (default 0.1); (5) retrain M₀ from scratch on the filtered dataset. Unlike classifier-based filters limited to explicit toxicity, ForgetFilter requires no pre-trained safety classifiers and generalizes across bias, toxicity, and harmfulness, achieving 75% lower toxicity than no safety measure and 62% lower than self-correction while maintaining or improving downstream F1 scores.

## Results

On LLaMA-7B, ForgetFilter (FF) achieves the best safety scores among all non-sequential methods: bias 0.08, toxicity 0.11, and mixed 0.08, while simultaneously attaining the highest downstream F1 (83.1% bias, 77.8% toxicity, 79.4% mixed). The combined FF+SC variant further improves safety to 0.07/0.09/0.07 with downstream F1 of 83.3/77.6/79.8. In contrast, sequential SafetyFT recovers safety (bias 0.01, toxicity 0.05) but degrades downstream F1 by 6.7–8.5 points relative to the downstream-only model. The abstract highlights that FF reduces toxicity by 75% compared to no safety measures (0.11 vs. 0.45) and by 62% compared to self-correction (0.11 vs. 0.29).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ForgetFilter decisively outperforms both baselines on safety metrics. Against Safety Replay (Chaudhry et al., 2019), FF reduces bias from 0.41 to 0.08 (81% lower) and toxicity from 0.43 to 0.11 (74% lower), while also improving downstream F1 by 3.8–4.2 points; Replay barely mitigates the unsafe influence of downstream data. Against Moral Self-Correction (Ganguli et al., 2023), FF achieves lower bias (0.08 vs. 0.10) and substantially lower toxicity (0.11 vs. 0.29, a 62% reduction), while matching or exceeding downstream F1 (83.1 vs. 82.6 on bias; 77.8 vs. 76.4 on toxicity). The FF+SC combination yields the strongest overall safety (0.07/0.09/0.07) with negligible downstream cost, suggesting the two strategies are complementary. Sequential SafetyFT, while achieving the lowest safety scores (0.01/0.05/0.02), sacrifices 6.7–8.5 points of downstream F1, making it unsuitable when preserving task performance is critical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the second-split forgetting framework of Maini et al. (2022)—originally used to filter mislabeled image classification data—to sequence-to-sequence language model tasks, revealing that the forgetting signal in LLMs is driven by semantic safety conflicts rather than label noise. The forgetting metric itself is borrowed from Toneva et al. (2019), but the key conceptual departure is showing that forgetting is *selective* with respect to safety semantics and scales with model size, a property absent from prior continual-learning or catastrophic-forgetting analyses. The paper also positions ForgetFilter against existing safety filters (Korbak et al., Askell et al.) that rely on pre-trained toxicity classifiers, arguing that forgetting-based filtering generalizes to implicit unsafe content (bias, harmfulness) without domain-specific scorers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The release-and-finetune paradigm is now the dominant deployment model for LLMs, making the safety of third-party downstream finetuning a critical and under-studied problem. The finding that safety finetuning causes catastrophic forgetting of downstream task data (not just unsafe data) directly challenges the common practice of sequential safety re-alignment, and the interleaved-training results showing immediate recall of 'unlearned' unsafe knowledge raise serious long-term safety concerns. Key limitations include the need to construct a comprehensive safe-example set matching the unsafe category (domain shift eliminates the forgetting discrepancy), the fixed threshold φ, and the fact that the effect vanishes in small models. Open questions include automated threshold selection, scaling ForgetFilter to pretraining data, and whether the selective-forgetting property can be harnessed for targeted privacy unlearning.
