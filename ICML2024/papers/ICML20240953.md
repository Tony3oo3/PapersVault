## Problem

The paper asks whether AI machines can learn true objective probabilities (as opposed to subjective beliefs) from data, a question left open by Dawid's (1982) calibration theorem, which only guarantees self-assured calibration rather than convergence to the true probability. The gap is that no prior work established a necessary and sufficient condition for when a machine can or cannot learn the true objective probability function, especially when the data-generating process is not i.i.d.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a discrete-time stochastic process generating an infinite sequence of event indicators {s_t} under a true probability P, the machine's subjective probability Π, and the totality of true facts β_t at each time t. The output is a binary learnability determination: the machine can learn P(A_{t+1}|β_t) as α if and only if the true probability is directly observable, meaning it equals the empirical distribution of a population of events S available to the machine. Unlike standard ML settings, the 'data' is an infinite stochastic path and the critical constraint is whether Nature's true moves are observable in the forecasting game, not the volume of data.

## Method

The paper constructs a repeated forecasting game between Nature (playing the true probability P) and a machine (playing subjective probability Π), where the machine loses when Nature deviates from its forecast, and proves that learning the true probability is equivalent to 'truly guaranteed well-calibration' P(p_k→α)=1 rather than merely 'self-assured well-calibration' Π(p_k→α)=1. The Oakes (1985) counterexample is used to separate these two notions, showing that self-calibration does not imply true calibration. The proof then defines Nature's perversity (uniform or selective) and, via martingale convergence, Borel-Cantelli lemmas, and stopping time arguments, establishes that the machine can learn if and only if the true probability is directly observable as the empirical frequency of a true population (Theorem 4.36). This result also connects computability to complexity: when the true probability is directly observable, the learning problem reduces to computing an empirical distribution, making the computational task tractable.

## Results

The paper's central result (Theorem 4.36) establishes that a machine learns the true objective probability P(A_{t+1}|β_t) as α if and only if the machine directly observes it from a population S, i.e., the probability is computable as the empirical frequency of the population. The impossibility side (Theorem 4.20, Corollary 4.30) shows that if Nature is uniformly perverse, or selectively perverse without the machine being self-assured of the stopping time, then no machine can learn the true probability regardless of its computational resources. The paper further proves (Theorem 4.17, Case 1) that the independence assumption among data can be relaxed for learning, but the identical-distribution assumption (e.g., stationarity or ergodicity) along the stochastic path cannot.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or quantitative comparisons. The paper positions its results relative to two key prior theoretical contributions: Dawid (1982) on self-calibration, which the paper extends by distinguishing self-assured calibration (Π-probability one) from truly guaranteed calibration (P-probability one), and Oakes (1985) on the non-existence of self-calibrating priors, which the paper uses as Counterexample 1 to prove that self-calibration does not imply true calibration (Theorem 4.8). The paper's framework subsumes both: Dawid's theorem holds under the machine's subjective probability, while the Oakes counterexample shows it can fail under the true objective probability, thereby establishing the fundamental gap the paper's theorems characterize.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the calibration literature of Dawid (1982) and Oakes (1985) from the question of whether forecasts are well-calibrated to the sharper epistemic question of whether a machine can actually *learn* the true objective probability. Dawid's self-calibration theorem and Oakes's counterexample are repurposed as building blocks: the former establishes that a machine can be self-assured of calibration, while the latter shows this self-assurance does not guarantee true calibration. The paper then introduces a game-theoretic framing (Nature vs. machine) and a Success Criterion combining correctness with self-assurance to derive a necessary and sufficient condition (Theorem 4.36) linking learnability to direct observability via von Mises populations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result—that a machine learns the true objective probability if and only if it is directly observable from a given population—provides a fundamental negative boundary for probabilistic machine learning: in most realistic settings where the full population is not available, the paper's epistemic notion of learning is not achieved. This has implications for time-series analysis (the machine cannot relax identical-distribution assumptions like stationarity) and for the computability-complexity connection in probability estimation. However, the result is heavily dependent on the paper's specific philosophical definitions (the Success Criterion, the Nature game, the notion of 'true objective probability'), and no empirical or algorithmic consequences are drawn, leaving open how these foundational limits interact with practical approximate learning guarantees (e.g., PAC learning, regret minimization).
