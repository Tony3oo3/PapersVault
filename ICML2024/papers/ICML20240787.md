## Problem

Existing 2:4 sparse training methods were designed for CNNs and fail to transfer to transformers: their hyperparameters (especially the masked decay factor) differ by orders of magnitude, and simply halving FFN width outperforms most proposed sparse methods. Moreover, all prior 2:4 training work reports only simulated speedups without end-to-end acceleration on actual sparse tensor cores, leaving the practical viability of 2:4 sparsity for transformer pre-training unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard transformer pre-training inputs (C4, OpenWebText, ImageNet-1K, WMT 14 En-De) with dense weight initialization and produces a pre-trained transformer whose FFN layers carry 2:4 transposable sparse masks. A key structural constraint is that output activations in the sparse forward pass are column-major (due to the 2:4-spMM layout), which the gated activation kernels must account for. The approach requires NVIDIA Ampere GPUs with sparse tensor cores and uses CUTLASS for 2:4-spMM kernels plus custom Triton kernels for mask search, pruning, MVUE, and activation functions.

## Method

The paper proposes a fully sparse training (FST) pipeline for transformer FFNs that replaces all three GEMMs (forward, input-gradient, weight-gradient) with 2:4-spMMs, using transposable masks for weights and MVUE for output-activation gradients. Three accuracy-preserving techniques are introduced: (1) applying the SR-STE masked decay term on gradients rather than weights, so that Adam/AdamW's per-dimension normalization amplifies decay for low-gradient dimensions and breaks mask-oscillation 'dilemma points'; (2) a fast warm-up-stage procedure that selects the decay factor λ_W by matching the sparse flip rate to 60–95% of the dense flip rate; and (3) a dense fine-tuning stage over the last 1/6 of training steps. Two speed techniques address practical bottlenecks: transposable mask search is reformulated as a 4×4 convolution over 90 candidate masks (≈5× faster than the 2-approximation algorithm), and GEGLU/SwiGLU kernels are rewritten to traverse column-major activation tensors along the column dimension, reducing L2 cache misses and achieving ~3× throughput over the naive implementation.

## Results

On BERT-base GLUE, the proposed 2:4 sparse training achieves 79.6 average score, nearly matching the dense baseline (79.8) while substantially outperforming other sparse methods (STEP: 77.7, Bi-Mask: 77.7, Half-width: 77.9). On GPT-2 models (124M–1.5B), the method achieves comparable or even superior GLUE scores to dense training (e.g., 77.1 vs 76.2 at 774M, 77.1 vs 76.5 at 1.5B). On Transformer-base machine translation, the sparse model reaches 26.48 BLEU vs 26.15 for dense. End-to-end training speedup of 1.2× is achieved on GPT-2 774M on a single RTX 3090, with FFN layers alone accelerating up to 1.7×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against STEP (Lu et al., 2023), the proposed method wins decisively on BERT-base GLUE (79.6 vs 77.7 avg) and Transformer-base WMT14 En-De (26.48 vs 25.27 BLEU), with the gap driven primarily by MRPC, QNLI, and STS-B where STEP's dense-pretraining strategy underperforms. Against Bi-Mask (Zhang et al., 2023), the method also wins on BERT-base (79.6 vs 77.7) and DeiT-small (79.2 vs 77.6 top-1), while matching on DeiT-base (81.3 vs 81.0 dense). Against the dense baseline, the method is essentially lossless on BERT (79.6 vs 79.8) and actually surpasses dense on GPT-2 774M/1.5B (77.1 vs 76.2/76.5) and DeiT-base (81.3 vs 81.0), suggesting the sparse training acts as a mild regularizer. Notably, the 'Half' model (halving FFN inner dimension, same FLOPs as 2:4 sparsity) scores 77.9, confirming that 2:4 sparsity with proper optimization is strictly better than simply reducing model width at equal compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 2:4 sparse training framework established for CNNs (SR-STE from Zhou et al., transposable masks from Hubara et al., MVUE from Chmiel et al.) to transformer pre-training, identifying that direct transplantation fails due to dramatically different optimal hyperparameters (decay factors ~100x smaller) and the need for gradient-level rather than weight-level regularization under Adam-family optimizers. It departs from prior work by providing the first end-to-end speedup measurements on actual sparse tensor cores, and by addressing practical bottlenecks beyond GEMM—specifically transposable mask computation and gated activation function overheads—that previous simulation-only studies ignored. The dense fine-tuning-at-the-end strategy also contrasts with STEP's dense pre-training-at-the-beginning approach, arguing the latter is redundant for FST.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that 2:4 sparse training can match dense pre-training accuracy on transformers while delivering measurable (1.2x) end-to-end speedup, making it a practical tool for reducing pre-training costs. The finding that a trivially smaller dense model ('Half') outperforms prior 2:4 sparse training methods (STEP, Bi-Mask) on GLUE raises important questions about whether the added complexity of sparse training is justified over simple width reduction. Limitations include testing only up to 1.5B parameters on a single GPU, a modest 1.2x (vs. theoretical 2x) end-to-end speedup, and reliance on NVIDIA Ampere hardware; open questions include whether the approach scales to 10B+ models, multi-node training, and whether the speedup gap can be closed with further kernel optimization.
