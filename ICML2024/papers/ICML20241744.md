## Problem

Standard diffusion models for temporal data apply a uniform noise level to all frames during denoising, ignoring the fact that uncertainty about future frames is inherently greater than about near-future ones. Existing autoregressive or block-autoregressive extensions either accumulate error over long rollouts or waste denoising capacity on frames that are already well-determined by conditioning, making them suboptimal for highly dynamic sequences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241744/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a small set of clean conditioning frames (e.g., 1–5 video frames at 64×64, or 2 velocity-field frames at 64×64 for fluid dynamics) concatenated with a sliding window of W partially denoised frames as input, and produces the next frame (or block of frames) at each rollout step. The key structural detail is that within the window, each frame carries a different noise level determined by its local position, so the model simultaneously sees clean, partially noisy, and fully noisy frames. Rollout proceeds by shifting the window one frame forward per step, enabling indefinite autoregressive extension without retraining.

## Method

Rolling Diffusion reparameterizes the global diffusion time t into a frame-dependent local time t_w = (w + t)/W, so that frames further from the conditioning receive progressively more noise. This factorizes the denoising process into three categories—clean frames (identity), noise frames (independent Gaussians), and a sliding window of partially noisy frames—meaning the neural network only needs to model the window. Training uses a windowed ε-loss applied only to window frames, with a Bernoulli-mixed schedule that oversamples the linear rolling schedule to match test-time usage. Sampling begins with an auxiliary init schedule that transitions from pure noise to the rolling state, after which the window slides forward one frame per denoising step. The architecture is a U-ViT (Simple Diffusion) with spatial convolution blocks and transformer blocks attending both spatially and temporally, trained with the same parameter count as the standard diffusion baseline.

## Results

On the Kinetics-600 baseline task (5 input, 11 output frames), Standard Diffusion achieves an FVD of 3.9 and Rolling Diffusion achieves 5.2, both competitive with the best two-stage methods (W.A.L.T.-L: 3.3†, MAGVITv2: 4.3†). In the autoregressive rollout setting with stride=8 (24 steps), Rolling Diffusion (FVD 39.8) substantially outperforms Standard Diffusion (FVD 58.1). On the BAIR Robot Pushing benchmark, both methods achieve near-identical FVD (59.6 vs. 59.7), outperforming all prior methods (best prior: Video Diffusion at 66.9). On the Kolmogorov Flow fluid dynamics task, Rolling Diffusion consistently achieves lower Fréchet Spectral Distance (FSD) than Standard Diffusion across all rollout lengths and conditioning settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the BAIR Robot Pushing benchmark, both Rolling Diffusion (FVD 59.6) and Standard Diffusion (59.7) outperform all 10 prior methods, with the closest competitor being Video Diffusion (Ho et al., 2022b) at FVD 66.9; the two proposed methods are essentially tied here since the task has limited dynamics. On the Kinetics-600 baseline, Standard Diffusion (3.9) slightly outperforms Rolling Diffusion (5.2), as the short 16-frame window with stride 1 contains little temporal dynamics; both remain competitive with two-stage methods like MAGVITv2 (4.3†) and W.A.L.T.-L (3.3†), though these use latent-space autoencoders. In the critical rollout experiments where Rolling Diffusion's inductive bias matters, Rolling Diffusion with β=0.1 achieves FVD 39.8 versus Standard Diffusion's 58.1 at stride=8 (24 steps), a 32% improvement; at stride=1 (64 steps), Standard Diffusion with 8-8 conditioning (157.1) still beats Rolling Diffusion (211.2), but Rolling Diffusion with 5-11 conditioning (211.2) is far more stable than single-frame autoregression (1369). On Kolmogorov Flow, Rolling Diffusion (init noise, 2-8) achieves FSD 417.6 at 48 steps versus Standard Diffusion (2-8) at 1159, and Rolling Diffusion (2-4) achieves FSD 1238.4 versus Standard Diffusion (2-4) at 2401, demonstrating consistent superiority in chaotic dynamics. Against TECO (Yan et al., 2023) on a 20-conditioning/80-generation setting at 128×128, Rolling Diffusion achieves FVD 685 versus TECO's 799.

## Relation to Prior Work

Rolling Diffusion extends the DDPM framework (Ho et al., 2020) and the variational diffusion objective (Kingma et al., 2021) by reparameterizing the global diffusion time into a frame-dependent local time, so that noise is progressively assigned from past to future frames. This departs from standard video diffusion, which treats the temporal axis as an extra spatial dimension, and from block-autoregressive approaches that apply uniform denoising steps to all frames. The closest prior work is Harvey et al. (2022), which explores autoregressive video diffusion but does not explicitly adapt the noise schedule temporally, and Zhang et al. (2023, TEDI), which uses similar local noise levels but conflates the sliding-window effect with auxiliary losses. The paper's key conceptual contribution is showing that the global diffusion process factorizes so that only a local sliding window requires non-trivial modeling, enabling indefinite rollout with a fixed-size model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that the temporal inductive bias of progressive noising is most beneficial when dynamics are complex and chaotic (Kolmogorov flow, high-stride video), while offering no advantage for near-static sequences (BAIR, low-stride Kinetics-600). This provides practitioners with a clear criterion for when to adopt rolling diffusion over standard or block-autoregressive diffusion. Open questions include the noted training inefficiency (partial noising lets the model copy structure from conditioning frames, weakening the learned prior), whether the approach extends cleanly to other sequential modalities like audio or climate ensembles, and how to best handle the boundary-condition schedule in practice. The FSD metric for evaluating distributional match in PDE forecasting is a useful addition to the evaluation toolkit.
