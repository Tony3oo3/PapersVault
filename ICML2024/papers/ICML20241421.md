## Problem

While AI uncertainty quantification (UQ) is posited to be key for human decision-making, existing behavioral studies show conflicting results on whether presenting UQ actually improves decision accuracy, partly due to a lack of verification that the uncertainty estimates are well-calibrated. No prior study had assessed human decision-making performance using quantifiably well-calibrated, instance-level AI UQ evaluated under a strict scoring rule.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICML20241421/cropped_page4_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a tabular data instance (feature vector) from one of three UCI datasets and a trained random forest classifier, then generates 100 perturbed 'clone' samples drawn from a Gaussian distribution (σ=0.1) around the instance. The output is a 95% confidence interval of the class probability computed across those clones, which is presented to human participants as a visualization of predictive uncertainty. Calibration quality is verified ex post using the Brier score against known ground-truth labels before the UQ is shown to participants.

## Method

Rather than developing a novel UQ technique, the paper leverages known ground-truth class labels to construct and verify well-calibrated instance-level uncertainty estimates for use in behavioral experiments. For each test instance x, 100 'clones' are sampled from a Gaussian perturbation (σ=0.1) around x, and the trained random forest classifier assigns a class probability to each clone; the 95% confidence interval of these probabilities serves as the predictive uncertainty range. The quality of this UQ is validated using the Brier score (a strictly proper scoring rule), confirming that low Brier scores correspond to correctly classified instances and high scores to misclassified ones. Two pre-registered online behavioral experiments (n=445 and n=600) then test whether presenting this calibrated UQ to human decision-makers improves accuracy and confidence calibration over an AI prediction alone, and whether different visual representations (point vs. distribution, needle vs. dotplot) affect performance.

## Results

In Experiment 1 (N=445), providing AI Uncertainty significantly improved decision accuracy over AI Prediction alone (t(436)=2.36, p=0.049), which itself was significantly better than the no-AI Control condition (t(436)=9.91, p<0.0001); the overall AI condition effect was large (F(2,436)=84.11, p<0.001, η²p=0.28). Confidence calibration (accuracy–confidence association) was strongest in the AI Uncertainty condition, followed by AI Prediction, and weakest in Control. In Experiment 2 (N=600), no significant differences were found between point-estimated vs. distributional UQ (F(1,588)=0.28, p=0.60) or between needle vs. dotplot visualizations (F(1,588)=0.16, p=0.69), indicating the UQ benefit is robust to representation. Exploratory analyses showed AI Uncertainty increased the proportion of participants who outperformed the AI from 1.33% (AI Prediction) to 4.73% (AI Uncertainty) in Experiment 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between three AI-information conditions: no AI (Control), AI point prediction only, and AI prediction plus uncertainty quantification. AI Uncertainty wins on both accuracy and confidence calibration over AI Prediction alone, which in turn wins over Control, with the AI Uncertainty advantage being statistically significant but modest in magnitude (p=0.049 for accuracy). In Experiment 2, the comparison shifts to UQ representation: point-estimated vs. distributional uncertainty and needle vs. dotplot visualization. Neither representation axis produced a significant difference in accuracy or confidence calibration, meaning the UQ benefit is not dependent on how the uncertainty is visually encoded. The dataset effect was large in both experiments (η²p=0.40 and 0.39 respectively), with German Credit (lowest AI accuracy at ~76%) yielding the lowest human accuracy and Census (highest AI accuracy at ~88%) yielding the highest. No speed–accuracy tradeoff was detected: response times did not differ significantly across AI conditions in either experiment.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the judgment-and-decision-making literature (which shows probabilistic forecasts improve human accuracy) and the AI uncertainty quantification literature (Bayesian methods, ensembles, etc.), but departs from both in a key way: rather than developing a novel UQ technique, it repurposes ground-truth class labels to construct well-calibrated instance-level UQ verified by the Brier score, then tests it in large-scale behavioral experiments. Prior behavioral studies of AI UQ (Lai et al., 2021; Prabhudesai et al., 2023; Buçinca et al., 2021) either relied on subjective trust ratings, used uncalibrated model probabilities, or employed wizard-of-oz simulations; this work explicitly addresses the calibration gap identified by Lai et al. (2021) and measures objective accuracy and confidence calibration. The UQ construction itself (Gaussian perturbation of instances + random forest prediction) is deliberately simple and not claimed as a contribution; the contribution is the experimental design and the calibration verification protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a genuine open question in the human-AI interaction literature by showing that well-calibrated, instance-level UQ improves both decision accuracy and confidence calibration over AI prediction alone, with the benefit being robust to the specific visualization used (point vs. distribution, needle vs. dotplot). A key limitation is that the UQ is generated using ground-truth labels, making it a proof-of-concept for the value of calibrated UQ rather than a deployable method; the authors acknowledge this and call for future work testing UQ techniques of varying quality. Open questions include whether the benefit extends to multi-class classification, to domains where humans possess knowledge the AI lacks, and to more complex UQ visualizations. The negative result in Experiment 2—that adding distributional detail did not improve performance—reinforces the broader finding that more task-relevant information is not always beneficial for human decision-makers.
