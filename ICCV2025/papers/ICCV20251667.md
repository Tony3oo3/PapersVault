## Problem

Existing OOD performance prediction algorithms have been evaluated on inconsistent protocols with limited datasets and narrow types of distribution shifts, making fair cross-algorithm comparison difficult. No prior benchmark covers the full range of shift types (corruption, style, camera location, demographics, background, adversarial, batch effect) or provides a shared pool of pre-trained models, leaving the capability boundary of current methods largely unexplored.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251667/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each performance prediction algorithm receives a trained model with fixed parameters, a labeled validation set drawn from the training distribution, and an unlabeled OOD test set; it outputs a scalar surrogate score (e.g., thresholded confidence fraction, nuclear norm, Wasserstein distance) rather than a direct accuracy estimate. Evaluation is performed by computing Spearman's rank correlation between these scores and ground-truth accuracies across a pool of models spanning multiple architectures (ResNet-50, ViT-B/16, and 30+ others), which is deliberately more challenging than the same-architecture-only comparisons common in prior work. The benchmark ships 1,444 pre-trained models so that new algorithms can be tested without retraining.

## Method

ODP-Bench is a unified evaluation harness comprising 29 OOD test datasets (from CIFAR/ImageNet variants, WILDS, domain generalization, and subpopulation shift benchmarks), 10 practical performance prediction algorithms (ATC, DoC, Nuclear Norm, NI, MaNo, Dispersion, MDE, Agreement, COT, COTT), and 1,444 off-the-shelf models trained with supervised, MoCo, and CLIP pretraining across multiple architectures and random seeds. The key design choice is computing rank correlation across heterogeneous architectures rather than within a single architecture, and covering shift types that prior work largely ignored (camera location, demographics, background, batch effect, adversarial, temporal). The paper further runs controlled ablations varying OOD generalization training methods (RSC, Mixup, SWAD, CORAL), pretraining strategy, model size, corruption severity, and subpopulation structure to map where each algorithm succeeds or fails, revealing that current methods work well on synthetic corruptions but degrade sharply on natural and subpopulation shifts driven by spurious correlations.

## Results

ODP-Bench evaluates 10 OOD performance prediction algorithms across 29 OOD datasets using Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) between predicted scores and ground truth accuracies. The best-performing datasets are synthetic corruption benchmarks (CIFAR-10-C: avg ρ=0.746, 9/10 algorithms effective; CIFAR-100-C: avg ρ=0.712, 9/10 effective), while subpopulation shift datasets are the hardest (CheXpert: avg ρ=-0.248; CelebA: avg ρ=0.042; Waterbirds: avg ρ=0.059, with 0–1 effective algorithms). Overall, ATC, NI, Agreement, and COTT achieve the most consistently positive correlations, while MDE (Meta-Distribution Energy) is the weakest algorithm, yielding negative ρ on the majority of datasets. The benchmark provides 1,444 off-the-shelf trained models to enable fair, reproducible comparisons without retraining.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 10 evaluated algorithms, ATC, NI, Agreement, and COTT emerge as the most consistently effective, achieving ρ > 0.7 on the largest number of datasets (9, 9, 9, and 9 respectively on the top three datasets). COTT in particular excels on data-collection shifts (ImageNet-V2: 0.986, CIFAR-10.1: 0.718) and corruption benchmarks (CIFAR-10-C: 0.990), while Agreement dominates on style and camera-location shifts (ObjectNet: 0.973, ImageNet-V2: 0.996). MDE (Meta-Distribution Energy) is the clear underperformer, yielding negative rank correlations on 18 of 29 datasets (e.g., -0.832 on CIFAR-10-C, -0.888 on RxRx1), making it unreliable across nearly all shift types. On subpopulation shift datasets (Waterbirds, CelebA, CheXpert), virtually all algorithms fail: COT and COTT produce strongly negative correlations (e.g., COT = -0.960 on Waterbirds, -0.922 on CelebA), and no algorithm exceeds ρ = 0.825 on any of the three. The key takeaway is that no single algorithm is universally effective — the four top performers rely on fundamentally different signals (confidence, augmentation invariance, model agreement, and pseudo-label transport, respectively), and their relative ranking shifts dramatically depending on the type of distribution shift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not propose a new performance prediction algorithm but instead consolidates the fragmented evaluation landscape of OOD performance prediction into a unified benchmark. Prior works (Garg et al. 2022, Guillory et al. 2021, Baek et al. 2022, Xie et al. 2023/2024, Lu et al. 2023, Peng et al. 2024) each evaluated their methods on small, inconsistent subsets of datasets—predominantly synthetic corruptions like CIFAR-10-C and ImageNet-C—with varying training protocols and metrics. ODP-Bench departs from this by standardizing evaluation across 29 datasets spanning corruptions, style, data collection, camera location, demographics, and subpopulation shifts, while providing 1,444 pre-trained models to eliminate training variability as a confound. It also extends coverage to domain generalization and subpopulation shift datasets (PACS, DomainNet, Waterbirds, CelebA, CheXpert) that were largely absent from prior performance prediction evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a critical gap: current algorithms achieve strong rank correlations (ρ > 0.9) on synthetic corruptions but degrade substantially on natural distribution shifts, and nearly all fail on subpopulation shift datasets where spurious correlations drive model behavior. This suggests the field's reliance on corruption-based evaluation has masked fundamental limitations, and that subpopulation shift represents an underexplored and particularly challenging regime for performance prediction. The finding that four conceptually distinct simple methods (ATC, NI, Agreement, COTT) all perform comparably to more complex approaches indicates the field has not yet identified the correct inductive bias. Open questions include whether performance prediction can be made robust to spurious correlations, how to handle the diversity of shift types simultaneously, and whether the benchmark's cross-architecture evaluation (more challenging than prior same-architecture protocols) changes algorithmic rankings.
