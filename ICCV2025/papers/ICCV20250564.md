## Problem

Existing image generation benchmarks evaluate dimensions (quality, alignment, diversity, robustness) in isolation, failing to quantify how different dimensions interact and trade off against each other. This gap stems from two issues: the absence of datasets explicitly designed for paired-dimension analysis, and the reliance on a single metric (e.g., CLIPScore) to assess multiple distinct dimensions, which masks degradations in one aspect when another improves.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250564/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For generation, the benchmark accepts text prompts (T2I), source image + editing instruction (I2I), or subject image + prompt (subject-driven), producing generated RGB images. For evaluation, TRIGScore consumes the generated image, the original prompt, and a specific dimension label, feeding them into Qwen2.5-VL to extract raw logits over a small set of predefined rating tokens (e.g., Good/Medium/Bad). The final output per sample is a confidence-weighted scalar score in [0,1] per dimension, which are then aggregated across 132 pairwise subsets to produce a trade-off type classification (Synergy, Bottleneck, Tilt, or Dispersion) and a Dimension Trade-off Map.

## Method

TRIGScore replaces free-form VLM textual scoring with a logit-based mechanism: it collects the VLM's raw logits for a small set of semantic rating tokens, applies softmax to obtain a normalized probability distribution, maps each token to a numerical weight via a linear function, computes a weighted sum, and multiplies by a confidence weight (the maximum token probability) to penalize uncertain predictions. The Trade-off Relation Recognition System then classifies each dimension pair into one of four relationship types using Spearman rank correlation, density thresholds for synergy/bottleneck regions, and a tilt ratio comparing points above vs. below the regression line. The Dimension Trade-off Map (DTM) clusters these pairwise relationships into a visual graph per model and task. For optimization, the paper demonstrates two DTM-guided fine-tuning strategies: (1) regenerating a base training set, evaluating it to derive a DTM, and selecting dimension-balanced samples below a threshold for standard fine-tuning; and (2) using the DTM to construct system messages that guide GPT-4 to refine prompts for more balanced generation, both yielding measurable improvements across previously underperforming dimensions.

## Results

Across 14 models evaluated on TRIG-Bench (40,200 samples, 10 dimensions), DALL-E 3 leads most T2I dimensions (Originality 0.82, Aesthetics 0.80, Style 0.80, Bias 0.91), while HQEdit tops image-editing (Realism 0.70, Bias 0.97) and FLUX-IP-Adapter leads subject-driven generation (Realism 0.72, Aesthetics 0.77). DTM-guided fine-tuning yields consistent gains: Sana improves Realism from 0.57→0.60 and Bias from 0.48→0.66; HQEdit improves Ambiguity from 0.66→0.71; FLUX-IP-Adapter improves Ambiguity from 0.55→0.66 and Bias from 0.89→0.93. On the HEIM ablation, SD v3.5 with DTM improves all 8 dimensions (e.g., Alignment 0.24→0.25, Originality 0.61→0.65, Bias 0.50→0.60, Toxicity 0.38→0.55), confirming that CLIPScore alone is insufficient for cross-dimensional measurement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a single generative model but rather a benchmark (TRIG-Bench) and evaluation metric (TRIGScore) to compare 14 existing models. DALL-E 3 dominates T2I on 8 of 10 dimensions, outperforming FLUX (e.g., Originality 0.82 vs. 0.66, Style 0.80 vs. 0.57) and Janus-Pro (Originality 0.82 vs. 0.73, Style 0.80 vs. 0.63). In image-editing, HQEdit leads on Realism (0.70) and Bias (0.97), surpassing InstructPix2Pix (0.59, 0.81) and FreeDiff (0.59, 0.87); DTM fine-tuning further lifts HQEdit's Ambiguity from 0.66 to 0.71. For subject-driven generation, FLUX-IP-Adapter outperforms BLIP-Diffusion across all dimensions (e.g., Realism 0.72 vs. 0.47, Content 0.70 vs. 0.38), and DTM fine-tuning narrows the gap to OmniGen on Ambiguity (0.66 vs. 0.52). The key comparative finding is that DTM-guided fine-tuning consistently improves the weakest dimensions of each model (e.g., Sana's Bias +0.18, FLUX-IP-Adapter's Ambiguity +0.11) without degrading strong ones, demonstrating that trade-offs identified by the Dimension Trade-off Map are actionable. TRIGScore is validated against human ratings (10 annotators, 300 samples) and shown to capture dimension-specific distinctions that CLIPScore cannot, as CLIPScore produces a single global score and cannot differentiate, e.g., Relation from Content.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on HEIM's multidimensional evaluation framework (4 primary classes, 10 dimensions) but departs fundamentally by introducing pairwise dimensional subsets designed to quantify cross-dimension interactions rather than evaluating dimensions in isolation. It extends the VLM-as-judge evaluation line (VLM-Reasoning Metrics, LLM-Parsed Metrics) by making the judge dimension-specific via logit-based scoring (TRIGScore) rather than relying on a single global metric like CLIPScore for all dimensions. The Dimension Trade-off Map (DTM) and its four-way classification (Synergy, Bottleneck, Tilt, Dispersion) represent a new analytical layer absent from all prior T2I and I2I benchmarks, which the paper explicitly identifies as a gap in Table 1.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TRIG-Bench is the first benchmark to simultaneously cover T2I and I2I tasks with explicit trade-off analysis, providing practitioners with a diagnostic tool (DTM) that identifies which dimension pairs are in tension for a given model and guides targeted fine-tuning. The finding that the observed synergy among Realism, Originality, and Content is largely an artifact of metric-driven training (FID, Watermark, CLIPScore) rather than genuine multidimensional capability is a useful caution for the field. Open questions include whether DTM-guided optimization scales to larger or architecturally different models, whether the trade-off patterns are stable across prompt distributions, and whether the VLM-as-judge approach (Qwen2.5-VL) introduces systematic biases that could distort the trade-off geometry.
