## Problem

Feature caching accelerates DiT sampling by reusing intermediate features across steps, but progressive error accumulation from cached blocks severely degrades generation quality—especially when over 50% of blocks are cached. Existing error compensation approaches fail to account for dynamic perturbation patterns during the caching process, leading to suboptimal or even counterproductive error correction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252178/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GOC operates as a training-free plug-in during the DiT sampling loop, consuming the feature tensors from the two most recently cached steps (self-attention, cross-attention, and MLP outputs per block) along with pre-computed model statistics N(t) and the current step position t/T. It produces a corrected cached feature that replaces the computation of the next skipped step, ultimately yielding 256×256 images from either a class label (ImageNet, 1000 classes) or a text prompt (MS-COCO, 30k prompts) plus random noise, with no change to the model architecture or output format.

## Method

GOC introduces two mechanisms to reduce caching error. First, Gradient Cache (GC) maintains a queue of the two most recent step features g_{t-1}^l and g_t^l, computes their difference as a gradient, scales it by a coefficient η, and adds it to g_t^l to approximate the next step's feature g_{t+1}^l, thereby compensating for the positional drift inherent in direct feature reuse. Second, Gradient Optimization Determination (GOD) prevents GC from introducing gradient noise at steps where the denoising trajectory changes direction: it pre-computes, across multiple prompts, the proportion of blocks exhibiting inverse-gradient patterns N(t) at each step, then combines this with the step position via B(t) = γ(1−t/T) + (1−γ)N(t) and applies GC only when B(t) falls below a threshold Γ. This ensures gradient corrections are applied where they are beneficial (early steps with consistent gradients) and suppressed where they would inject artifacts (late steps or inflection points). The entire pipeline adds negligible computational overhead (~1–2% latency increase) while being compatible with both rule-based (FORA) and training-based (Learning-to-Cache) caching strategies.

## Results

On ImageNet (256×256, 20 DDIM steps), GOC applied to FORA at 50% caching recovers most of the quality loss: IS improves from 190.05 to 216.28 and FID drops from 6.857 to 3.907, approaching the No Cache baseline (IS 223.49, FID 3.484) while maintaining a 2× speedup. At 22% caching with the training-based L2C router, GOC actually surpasses the No Cache DiT baseline, achieving IS 236.75 and FID 3.192 (vs. 223.49 and 3.484). The additional latency introduced by GOC is minimal—only ~1.2–2.0% over the underlying caching method—making the quality gains essentially free in wall-clock time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the No Cache DiT baseline, GOC at 50% FORA caching narrows the gap substantially (FID 3.907 vs. 3.484; IS 216.28 vs. 223.49) while delivering a 2× speedup; at 25% FORA the gap is nearly closed (FID 3.524 vs. 3.484, sFID 4.805 vs. 4.892). Against FORA alone, GOC produces dramatic improvements at every caching level: at 50% caching, FID drops from 6.857 to 3.907 (−43%) and IS rises from 190.05 to 216.28 (+13.8%). Against the training-based L2C router, GOC yields the largest absolute gains, pushing IS from 225.00 to 236.75 and FID from 3.539 to 3.192—both exceeding the No Cache DiT baseline, demonstrating that gradient compensation and learned layer skipping are complementary. On the MS-COCO text-to-image task with PixArt, GOC provides a smaller but consistent FID improvement (21.971 vs. 21.984 for FORA at 25% caching), confirming cross-architecture and cross-task generalizability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feature-caching line of work for DiT acceleration (FORA, DeepCache, Cache-Me-if-You-Can, Learning-to-Cache), which identifies which blocks to skip but leaves the resulting approximation errors unaddressed. GOC departs from these methods by introducing a gradient-queue mechanism that computes the difference between two consecutive cached features and uses it to adjust the next cached feature, effectively performing first-order error compensation. The Gradient Optimization Determination (GOD) component further differentiates the approach by using pre-computed block-level statistics and step-position weighting to selectively apply gradient correction only when it is beneficial, drawing on insights from knowledge-editing literature about perturbation sensitivity across denoising stages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GOC offers a practical, training-free plug-in that substantially narrows the quality gap at high cache ratios (e.g., 50% caching) with only ~2% latency overhead, making aggressive caching viable for real-time DiT deployment. However, the method requires per-configuration hyperparameter tuning (η, γ, Γ) and a pre-computation pass over multiple prompts to build the inverse-gradient statistics, limiting its generality. The authors acknowledge that fixed parameters and bound steps constrain the approach, and future work on a general embedding procedure would be needed to make GOC truly model-agnostic. Open questions include whether the gradient-compensation principle transfers to video DiTs, flow-matching models, or architectures with non-uniform block depths.
