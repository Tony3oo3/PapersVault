## Problem

Existing in-training backdoor defenses (ABL, DBD, ASD) identify poisoned samples by treating them as out-of-distribution outliers with high loss on a clean model. However, the paper shows that some poisoned samples—particularly under clean-label attacks—are in-distribution relative to target-class clean samples, clustering with them in feature space and exhibiting low loss, which causes these defenses to misclassify them as clean and ultimately fail.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251583/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a potentially poisoned RGB image dataset (CIFAR-10 at 32×32, GTSRB, Tiny-ImageNet at 64×64) along with a small reserved set of clean seed samples (default 1%, as low as 0.1%). It outputs a standard classification model that predicts class labels, but with an additional trap class: at inference time, poisoned samples carrying the backdoor trigger are mapped to this trap label rather than the attacker's target label, effectively neutralizing the backdoor while preserving clean-data accuracy.

## Method

ESTI operates in two stages. In Enhanced Splitting, a clean model trained on seed samples and a poisoned model trained on the initially identified poisoned subset are used jointly: samples with high loss on the poisoned model (not low loss on the clean model, as in prior work) are assigned to the clean pool, exploiting the fact that the poisoned model converges on trigger features. A KDE-based threshold automatically determines the split boundary without manual tuning. After two iterations, the poisoned model is retrained at a high learning rate to refine the clean pool and at a low learning rate to purify the poisoned pool. In Trap Isolation, labels in the poisoned pool are flipped to a dedicated trap class, and the clean model is fine-tuned on the union of the clean pool and the relabeled poisoned pool, mapping backdoor features to the trap class so that poisoned inputs are predicted as the trap label at deployment.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, ESTI achieves an average ASR of 0.2% and average clean accuracy (ACC) of 93.7% across eight backdoor attacks, outperforming all eight baselines. On GTSRB, ESTI reaches 0.0% average ASR and 98.7% ACC, and on Tiny-ImageNet it achieves 0.1% ASR and 56.5% ACC. ESTI also matches or exceeds the clean-data baseline (93.9% ACC on CIFAR-10 without poisoning), confirming no performance degradation when the dataset is unpoisoned.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ESTI dominates all eight baselines on both ASR and ACC across all three datasets. The closest competitor is PDB, which achieves 0.5% ASR and 91.2% ACC on CIFAR-10 versus ESTI's 0.2% and 93.7%, but PDB requires 10% reserved clean data versus ESTI's 1% and drops to 39.9% ACC on Tiny-ImageNet. ANP is competitive on ASR (2.0% on CIFAR-10) but sacrifices 6.5% ACC (87.2% vs 93.7%). Among in-training defenses, ASD (31.1% ASR, 87.8% ACC) and DBD (67.3% ASR, 73.8% ACC) fail catastrophically on clean-label attacks like LC (97.1% and 100% ASR respectively), while ESTI maintains 0.0% ASR. ABL achieves low ASR (19.5%) but its gradient-ascent step causes severe ACC degradation (75.4% on CIFAR-10, 41.7% on GTSRB). Post-training methods (FP, I-BAU, NAD) consistently show higher ASR (24.9–37.2%) and lower ACC than ESTI, confirming that in-training defense with the trap mechanism is more effective than erasure-based approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

ESTI builds directly on the data-pool splitting framework introduced by ASD [10], which partitions a poisoned dataset into clean and poisoned subsets using loss-guided criteria. However, it departs from the core assumption shared by ASD, DBD, and ABL that poisoned samples are out-of-distribution (OOD) relative to clean samples, identifying instead that clean-label attacks produce poisoned samples that are in-distribution (ID) and thus invisible to loss-based outlier detection. The key methodological departure is the introduction of a poisoned model (trained on the identified poisoned subset) alongside the clean model for splitting, and the novel trap mechanism that repurposes poisoned samples by relabeling them to a dedicated trap class rather than discarding them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The identification that a subset of poisoned samples (particularly from clean-label attacks) are ID data challenges a foundational assumption underlying several popular defenses, explaining why they fail on attacks like LC and SIG. The trap mechanism offers a practical advantage: it allows more clean samples to be retained in the training pool without contamination, and requires only 1% (or even 0.1%) clean seed data versus 5–10% for competing methods. Open questions include whether the bimodal loss distribution assumption holds under adaptive or multi-trigger attacks, whether the approach extends to non-CNN architectures (e.g., transformers), and how it performs when the poisoning rate is extremely low or the trigger is highly sample-specific.
