## Problem

Prior robustness studies for visual encoders focus on severe corruptions that visibly degrade semantic signals, while subtle image-processing and acquisition parameters (JPEG quality, camera model, exposure settings) that are imperceptible to humans remain unexplored. This paper asks whether such metadata is systematically encoded in frozen foundation-model representations and whether it can disrupt downstream semantic predictions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250128/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes RGB images with controlled metadata parameters (4 processing types: JPEG quality, sharpening, resizing, interpolation; 8 acquisition types: camera make/model, exposure, aperture, ISO, focal length) and passes them through 47 frozen visual encoders without fine-tuning. For acquisition-parameter probing, images are center-masked at 90% to suppress semantic signal before embedding extraction. Outputs include linear-probe accuracy for metadata prediction, kNN semantic classification accuracy under five metadata-coupling setups, and recall@1 for near-duplicate retrieval on the PairCams dataset.

## Method

The paper performs a systematic empirical analysis rather than proposing a new architecture. It trains linear classifiers on frozen encoder embeddings to predict metadata labels, demonstrating that processing and acquisition parameters leave recoverable traces in representation space. To measure downstream impact, it designs five coupling setups (all-same, all-diff, pos-same, neg-same, uniform) where metadata labels are aligned or misaligned with semantic labels in a kNN classification task, revealing that metadata can overshadow semantics. For acquisition parameters, a near-duplicate retrieval task on the manually collected PairCams dataset (730 same-scene pairs captured by smartphone vs. non-smartphone cameras) shows that camera type biases retrieval even when the positive match is visually near-identical. The authors identify contrastive vision-language models as most sensitive and attribute this to the absence of strong geometric and photometric augmentations during pretraining, verified by training and finetuning a CVL model with and without such augmentations.

## Results

CVL models (e.g., CLIP, OpenCLIP) exceed 80% linear-probe accuracy for predicting JPEG compression, sharpening, and resizing on ImageNet, while SSL models (DINO, DINOv2, MoCo) perform closest to random. For acquisition parameters, most CVLs surpass 70% accuracy on the model (smart vs non-smart) task (random = 50%), with CLIP ViT-L/14@336 retrieving 70.1% of its top-10 neighbors from the same camera type. In near-duplicate retrieval on PairCams, a model with near-perfect recall@1 drops below 0.85 simply because negatives share the same camera type as the query, confirming that metadata traces can overshadow semantic content.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares 47 frozen visual encoders across three training paradigms (supervised, self-supervised, contrastive vision-language). CVL models (CLIP, OpenCLIP, SigLIP) consistently show the strongest metadata traces: CLIP ViT-L/14@336 achieves 70.1% top-10 neighbor agreement on camera type versus 58.0% for DINO RN50 and 54.0% for DINOv2 ViT-L/14. For processing-parameter prediction, a CVL ConvNeXt-L variant leads all models, exceeding 80% accuracy on JPEG, sharpening, and resizing, while SSL models (DINO, DINOv2, MoCo) remain closest to random baselines. Among CVLs, SigLIP is a notable exception for acquisition parameters, performing closer to supervised models. In the retrieval experiment, CVLs exhibit the largest gap between same-camera and different-camera negative settings, with recall@1 dropping from near 1.0 to below 0.85, whereas SSL and supervised models show smaller disparities. The authors attribute CVLs' heightened sensitivity to the absence of strong geometric and photometric augmentations during pretraining, a hypothesis supported by ablation experiments in the appendix.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant robustness literature (Hendrycks & Dietterich, Geirhos et al.) that evaluates encoders under severe, semantically-degrading corruptions, by instead probing subtle, often imperceptible metadata traces (JPEG quality, camera model, exposure) already present in natural images. It also reframes prior supervised attribute-prediction work (camera model identification, Exif-to-image alignment) by showing that frozen foundation encoders already encode this information without any additional training. The visual-bias literature on dataset-origin prediction provides a methodological template, but this paper targets a different and more granular axis: per-image processing and acquisition parameters rather than dataset-level confounds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that contrastive vision-language models—the most widely deployed encoders for zero-shot classification and retrieval—can have their semantic predictions overridden by metadata labels has direct practical consequences for anyone building on CLIP or SigLIP, particularly in retrieval and safety-critical applications. The paper identifies the likely cause (absence of strong augmentations during CVL pretraining) but does not propose a mitigation, leaving an open question for the community. The two new datasets (FlickrExif with 356K images, PairCams with controlled camera pairs) provide reusable resources for future work on metadata robustness. A key open question is whether this metadata sensitivity can be exploited adversarially in real-world deployments, a risk the authors flag but do not test.
