## Problem

Existing motion transfer methods for text-to-video generation struggle when the reference and target objects have substantial differences in appearance and structure. Landmark-based approaches cannot generalize to arbitrary objects, depth/edge-based methods fail on large appearance gaps, and learned spatiotemporal features suffer from motion-appearance entanglement that leaks reference appearance into the output. No prior training-free framework explicitly models both high-level semantic and low-level structural correspondence between dissimilar reference-target pairs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference video (providing the desired motion pattern) and a free-form text prompt (describing the target object's appearance) as input, and produces a generated video in which the text-described object performs the reference motion. The pipeline operates within AnimateDiff's latent space with 300 DDIM sampling steps, applying temporal attention guidance during the first 180 steps. A fake target object is first synthesized via ControlNet-segmentation conditioned on a degraded segmentation map of the reference's first frame to establish initial pose alignment before motion transfer begins.

## Method

MotionShot introduces a two-level motion alignment strategy: (1) high-level semantic alignment via structure-aware keypoint sampling (uniform contour + Poisson disk interior points) followed by semantic feature matching using L2-normalized Stable Diffusion features fused with DINOv2 token features, and (2) low-level morphological alignment via CoTracker3 keypoint tracking, global rotation/shift plus local polar-coordinate displacement computation, and Thin Plate Spline warping that deforms reference frames into the target shape while preserving motion. The warped reference frames produce temporal attention maps that guide AnimateDiff's diffusion sampling through a sparse top-k control mask and a customized energy function penalizing attention-map divergence. The entire pipeline is training-free, requiring no additional data collection or model fine-tuning, and the key insight is that combining semantic correspondence (which regions correspond) with structural warping (how shapes deform) resolves the motion-appearance incompatibility that plagues single-level approaches.

## Results

On the DAVIS-based evaluation set (40 videos), MotionShot achieves the highest CLIP text alignment score (26.95) and temporal consistency score (97.81) among all compared methods. In the user study (20 raters, 1–5 scale), MotionShot scores 4.95 on both motion preservation and appearance diversity, substantially outperforming all baselines which range between 2.50 and 3.00 on these metrics. The method also leads in user-rated text alignment (4.94) and temporal consistency (4.90), confirming its advantage across both objective and subjective evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MotionShot outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric. The most striking gap appears in user-study scores: MotionShot's motion preservation (4.95) and appearance diversity (4.95) exceed the best baseline (VideoComposer at 3.00 and 2.72, respectively) by roughly 2 points on a 5-point scale. Among CLIP-based metrics, MotionShot leads in text alignment (26.95 vs. VMC's 26.77) and temporal consistency (97.81 vs. VMC's 97.72), with Gen-1 trailing significantly in text alignment (22.79). Structurally-constrained methods (VideoComposer, Gen-1, Control-A-Video) produce morphologically distorted outputs because they inherit the reference object's shape, while attention-based methods (VMC, MotionClone) struggle with motion–appearance compatibility when reference and target objects differ substantially. MotionShot's two-level alignment strategy addresses both failure modes, yielding the most natural and motion-faithful results across all comparison scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

MotionShot builds directly on AnimateDiff as its video generation backbone and adopts MotionClone's sparse temporal attention guidance strategy for motion conditioning. It departs from prior training-free motion transfer methods (which rely on intermediate cues like depth, edges, or optical flow that fail under large appearance gaps) by introducing an explicit two-level alignment: high-level semantic correspondence via fused Stable Diffusion and DINOv2 feature matching, and low-level structural correspondence via Thin Plate Spline shape warping. This borrows the motion retargeting concept from computer graphics (TPS deformation) and repurposes it within a diffusion-based video generation pipeline, a combination not previously explored.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MotionShot is the first training-free framework to explicitly model both semantic and morphological alignment for motion transfer, enabling it to handle reference-target pairs with substantial appearance and structural differences (e.g., anime character → cartoon bear) that prior methods cannot. However, the authors acknowledge a fundamental limitation: when reference and target objects share no semantic similarity at all, the correspondence cannot be established and results become unpredictable. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond the single AnimateDiff backbone, whether the 40-video evaluation is sufficient to validate the large user-study margins, and whether the semantic matching step can be made more robust to truly dissimilar object categories.
