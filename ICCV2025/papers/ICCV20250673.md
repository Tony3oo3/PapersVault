## Problem

Evaluating instruction-based image editing models remains challenging because existing metrics—whether CLIP/DINO similarity scores or GPT-based judgments—lag in alignment with human perception and offer limited explainability. There is no learnable, reference-free framework that can both localize object-level differences between original and edited images and assess whether each difference is coherent with the user's editing instruction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250673/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The difference detector receives two RGB images (original and edited) and outputs a sequence of structured text tuples of the form "COMMAND: object, [x_min, y_min, x_max, y_max]" where COMMAND ∈ {ADD, EDIT, REMOVE}, with a confidence score derived from the token probability of the predicted command. The coherence estimator then takes each detected difference—whose bounding box is visually overlaid on both images using a color-coded scheme (red for additions, green for edits, blue for removals)—along with the modification type, textual description, and the user prompt, and produces a binary Yes/No coherence label accompanied by a free-text rationale.

## Method

DICE is a two-stage pipeline built on autoregressive MLLMs (Idefics3-8B selected as the base). The difference detector is trained in two phases: first on 118k pairs of visually similar real images from LVIS (filtered by DINOv2 cosine similarity > 0.6) to learn object-level comparison syntax, then on 97k inpainted image pairs generated with LaMa and Kandinsky 2.2 to bridge the gap to edited images. The coherence estimator is fine-tuned on 116 manually annotated samples from EmuEdit with GPT-4o-generated rationales refined by human review. Both models are trained with QLoRA, freezing the vision encoder and projector while applying LoRA adapters to self-attention and MLP layers across all 32 Transformer blocks. The key design choice is that difference detection is performed independently of the text prompt to avoid biasing localization toward the intended edit, and confidence is extracted from the relative token probability of the predicted command rather than a fixed value.

## Results

On the DICE-D benchmark (800 edited images from I²EBench), DICE (Idefics3-8B, both training stages, with confidence) achieves 22.3 AP and 40.1 AP50 in class-agnostic difference detection, and 15.5 AP in class-aware detection, substantially outperforming all alternative MLLMs and open-vocabulary detectors. In coherence estimation, DICE reaches 15.5 AP over detected areas (vs. 4.5 for mPLUG-Owl3 and 1.8 for Qwen2-VL) while maintaining 85.4% accuracy on ground-truth areas. When integrated into CLIP-based metrics, DICE improves Pearson correlation with human judgment from 51.1 to 54.5 (background preservation) and from 21.5 to 24.6 (prompt adherence).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DICE (Idefics3-8B) achieves 22.3 class-agnostic AP, outperforming the strongest open-vocabulary detector OWLv2 (16.4) by 5.9 points and Grounding DINO (12.8) by 9.5 points, confirming that semantic difference detection is fundamentally harder than standard object detection. Among alternative MLLMs, DICE surpasses mPLUG-Owl3-7B (5.2 AP) by 17.1 points and Qwen2-VL-7B (2.3 AP) by 20.0 points in class-agnostic detection; in class-aware detection the margins are 11.1 and 13.9 points respectively. GPT-4 and GlaMM perform near-zero (0.5 and 5.0 AP), indicating that general-purpose MLLMs without specialized multi-image training are poorly suited for this task. In coherence estimation (Table 2), DICE achieves 15.5 AP over detected areas versus 4.5 for mPLUG-Owl3 and 1.8 for Qwen2-VL, while all three models reach comparable accuracy on ground-truth areas (85.4–85.9%), suggesting the gap is driven by detection quality rather than coherence reasoning. The model ranking produced by DICE (Table 3) aligns with human annotations, with InstructDiffusion and MagicBrush ranked highest by both DICE and human raters, and the no-visual-change ordering is also consistent.

## Relation to Prior Work

DICE departs from the dominant paradigm of evaluating instruction-based image edits via global CLIP/DINO embedding similarities or black-box GPT-4V scoring, instead repurposing fine-tuned autoregressive MLLMs (Idefics3, Qwen2-VL, mPLUG-Owl3) as localized difference detectors and coherence estimators. Conceptually, it bridges multi-image comparison (a capability recently enabled by Idefics3's grid-based encoding and Qwen2-VL's positional embeddings) with structured object-detection output (bounding boxes + edit commands), a combination not previously explored in the editing-evaluation literature. The training strategy—self-supervision on DINOv2-similar LVIS image pairs followed by distillation from inpainting networks (LaMa, Kandinsky 2.2)—is a novel curriculum that avoids the cost of annotating instruction-edited pairs while bridging the domain gap to edited images.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DICE offers a reproducible, open-source, and explainable alternative to GPT-4V-based evaluation, which suffers from reproducibility issues due to model updates and opaque scoring. However, the scope is limited to object-level edits (addition, removal, modification), excluding style transfer, global lighting changes, and compositional edits that constitute a large fraction of real-world instructions. The coherence estimator is trained on only 116 annotated samples, raising questions about robustness to diverse edit types, and the correlation improvements over CLIP baselines are modest (e.g., Pearson 51.1→54.5 for background preservation). Open questions include whether the two-stage pipeline generalizes to non-object-level transformations, whether larger coherence training sets yield meaningful gains, and whether the low absolute AP scores (~22) indicate a fundamental limitation of current MLLMs for fine-grained multi-image comparison.
