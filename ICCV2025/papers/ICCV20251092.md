## Problem

Action-conditioned dynamic 3D scene generation from a single image remains unsolved for diverse physical materials. Pure physics-based methods are limited to rigid or simple elastic dynamics because accurate solvers for all material interactions (e.g., solid-fluid coupling) are still open problems, and full physical state reconstruction from a single image is infeasible. Meanwhile, video generation models lack the ability to accept precise 3D actions as input and produce a structured 3D scene rather than a flat video.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and a set of 3D actions—gravity, spatially-varying force fields (e.g., wind defined as f_w(x,y,z,t)), and point forces applied to specific object locations—as input. The output is a dynamic 3D scene represented as a temporal sequence of 3D Gaussian surfels (positions, orientations, scales, opacities, colors, and velocities) over T simulation steps, not merely a rendered video. The scene is reconstructed from the image using FLAGS for the static background and topological Gaussian surfels (Gaussian surfels with edge connectivity) for simulatable objects, with a 6-way material classification (rigid, elastic, cloth, smoke, liquid, granular).

## Method

WonderPlay introduces a hybrid generative simulator that closes a loop between a physics solver and a video diffusion model. The physics solver (Genesis framework with coupled rigid, MPM, and fluid solvers) first computes a coarse 3D dynamic scene from the initial reconstruction and input actions. This coarse scene is then used to condition a pre-trained motion-controllable video diffusion model (Go-with-the-Flow) through a novel bimodal control scheme: pixel-space optical flow drives structured noise warping for motion fidelity, while SDEdit-style partial denoising from the rendered coarse video provides appearance control. A spatially varying masking mechanism assigns different generator responsibility levels (via distinct SDEdit timesteps s₁ and s₂) to dynamic objects versus static background, suppressing hallucinations in regions where the simulator is trustworthy. Finally, the generated video supervises a differentiable rendering optimization that updates the 3D Gaussian surfel positions, velocities, and colors to produce the final dynamic 3D scene.

## Results

On 15 diverse scenes (7 real photos, 8 synthetic), WonderPlay achieves the best physical realism score (PhysReal 0.700) and motion quality (0.995) among all methods, while also attaining the highest imaging quality (0.695). In a 200-participant 2AFC user study, WonderPlay is preferred over all four baselines on physics plausibility, motion fidelity, and visual quality, with win rates ranging from 71.0% (vs. Tora on visual quality) to 85.2% (vs. PhysGaussian on visual quality). The largest margin over baselines appears on the GPT-4o-based physical realism metric, where WonderPlay (0.700) leads the next-best CogVideoX (0.670) by 0.030 and the physics-based methods by 0.155–0.350.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WonderPlay outperforms all four baselines on the physical realism metric (PhysReal), the most differentiating score: 0.700 vs. CogVideoX-I2V (0.670), PhysGen (0.545), Tora (0.530), and PhysGaussian (0.350). Against the two physics-based baselines, the advantage is substantial—PhysGaussian scores only 0.350 on PhysReal and 0.492 on imaging, reflecting its inability to reconstruct a full 3D physical state from a single image; PhysGen, limited to 2D rigid-body simulation, also struggles with complex materials like water. Against the two video-generation baselines, WonderPlay wins on motion quality (0.995 vs. 0.993/0.992) and physical realism, but Tora edges it slightly on aesthetic score (0.620 vs. 0.610) and CogVideoX-I2V on temporal consistency (0.219 vs. 0.217), indicating that pure video models can still produce marginally smoother or more aesthetically pleasing frames when action adherence is not enforced. In the human study, WonderPlay is preferred in 71–85% of pairwise comparisons across all three criteria, with the strongest margins against PhysGaussian (80–85%) and the smallest against Tora (71–77%), consistent with Tora being the closest video-generation competitor.

## Relation to Prior Work

WonderPlay sits at the intersection of physics-based dynamic scene simulation (PhysGen, PhysGaussian, PhysDreamer) and controllable video generation (CogVideoX, Tora, Go-with-the-Flow). The closest prior work, PhysMotion, also combines a 3D physics solver with a video generator from a single image, but delegates all dynamics to the solver and uses the video model only for appearance refinement, restricting it to rigid and elastic materials. WonderPlay's key departure is the 'hybrid generative simulator' where both the physics solver and the video generator jointly compute dynamics, coupled with a spatially varying bimodal control (motion flow + RGB appearance with different responsibility levels for background vs. objects) that lets the video model fill in fine-grained motion while the solver anchors the action response.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By offloading fine-grained dynamics to a video diffusion prior trained on real-world physics, WonderPlay sidesteps the open problem of building accurate solvers for every material class (solid-fluid coupling, granular flow, etc.) and enables diverse-material interaction from a single image—a practical capability with direct applications in AR/VR, embodied AI, and creative tools. Limitations include dependence on a single viewpoint, the coarse-to-fine pipeline's computational cost (960 simulation steps plus full video diffusion), and residual hallucination risk in regions where the video generator's responsibility is high. Open questions include whether the hybrid loop can be made real-time, how it scales to longer horizons or multi-agent interactions, and whether the spatially varying masking generalizes beyond the binary background/object split.
