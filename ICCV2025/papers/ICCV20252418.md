## Problem

Conventional automotive lidar DSPs process each waveform independently, ignoring spatial context from neighboring waveforms, which causes missed peaks in low-SNR regions, false positives from blooming and retroreflectors, and severe degradation in fog. Existing transient imaging methods that do exploit neighborhood information are restricted to scientific hardware with limited field-of-view and cannot operate in real time at the 200 m ranges and 60° FoV required for autonomous driving.

## Input / Output

The method consumes raw full-waveform lidar data κ ∈ ℝ^{40×128×2112} from a production-grade Microvision MOVIA FWL sensor (266 ps temporal resolution, 85 m range, 15°×60° FoV), where each pixel stores a TCSPC histogram of SPAD trigger events across multiple pulse cycles. The temporal axis is partitioned into 33 patches; for each patch the network outputs a binary occupied/empty classification score and, for occupied patches, a continuous distance offset, yielding a multi-echo point cloud at inference. An optional super-resolution head upsamples the spatial resolution by a factor of 3, producing 9× more points per frame.

## Method

The neural DSP applies matched filtering (1D temporal convolution) to the waveforms, then tokenizes the 33 temporal patches into D=32 feature vectors with sinusoidal positional encodings and processes them in a U-Net built from spatio-temporal transformer blocks. Each block decomposes attention into a temporal component (across all patches of a single pixel) and a spatial component using SWIN-style shifted 2×4 non-square windows, with patch-merging/unmerging layers for encoder/decoder downsampling. Two heads predict classification scores (trained with focal loss to handle occupied/empty imbalance) and distance offsets (trained with L1 loss masked to occupied patches), enabling multi-peak detection via thresholding. Training proceeds in two stages: pre-training on large-scale synthetic data from a CARLA-integrated forward model that simulates low-flux multi-peak, high-flux retroreflector (with dead-time, multipath, and blooming effects), and fog transients, followed by fine-tuning on real captured data augmented with synthetic fog and traffic signs. A super-resolution extension appends an additional spatio-temporal transformer and upsampling block to the decoder, disentangling the beam-divergence mixing to render a 3× upsampled feature map.

## Results

On real-world clear-weather data, the proposed neural DSP achieves 0.307 m Chamfer Distance (CD) and 92.57% Recall, improving CD by 32 cm over the conventional DSP (0.626 m) and by 20 cm over the best learned baseline, Peng et al. (0.505 m). In foggy conditions, the method reduces CD to 1.110 m (vs. 2.199 m for conventional and 2.326 m for Peng et al.) and increases maximum detectable range by 17.2 m over the best baseline (56.71 m vs. 39.55 m). On synthetic CARLA data, CD improves by 63.22% over the conventional pipeline (0.397 m vs. 0.648 m). The method also extends to super-resolution, achieving 0.273 m CD on real data with 9× more points, outperforming ILN (0.413 m) and Tulip (0.678 m).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed neural DSP outperforms all four baselines across every condition and metric. Against Lindell et al. [39], the method wins decisively: on clear data, CD is 0.307 m vs. 1.098 m (a 3.6× improvement), and in fog the gap widens to 1.110 m vs. 4.711 m, as Lindell's denoising strategy fails to suppress backscatter peaks. Against Peng et al. [56], the closest competitor, the proposed method still leads by 20 cm CD on clear data (0.307 vs. 0.505 m) and by 1.22 m in fog (1.110 vs. 2.326 m); Peng's single-peak-per-waveform assumption limits multi-echo handling. For super-resolution, the method beats ILN [35] by 0.14 m CD on real data (0.273 vs. 0.413 m) and Tulip [84] by 0.41 m (0.273 vs. 0.678 m), with qualitative results showing fewer flying pixels and better recovery of small road hazards. Maximum range improves by 14 m (clear) and 17 m (fog) over Peng et al., the strongest baseline in those settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges the gap between transient imaging research (which operates on experimental hardware with limited FoV) and production-grade automotive lidar DSPs (which process waveforms individually). Architecturally, it composes the space-time attention decomposition of Bertasius et al. [3] with SWIN's shifted-window spatial attention and patch merging [41] inside a Swin-UNet [7] framework, adapted to the non-square 40×128 waveform geometry. Conceptually, it departs from prior learned transient imaging methods (Lindell et al. [39], Peng et al. [56]) by reframing peak detection as a classification-plus-regression problem rather than waveform denoising followed by argmax, which enables multi-echo output and proper sky handling. The forward model for training data generation extends the multi-peak beam-divergence simulation of Goudreault et al. [17] with high-flux retroreflector, blooming, and fog components tailored to automotive SPAD sensors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first learned DSP validated on a production-grade automotive full-waveform lidar (Microvision MOVIA) at real-time rates (68 Hz), addressing a critical gap between academic transient imaging and deployable automotive sensing. The 17 m range improvement in fog and the ability to outperform commercial sensors (Aeva Aeries II, Luminar Iris) in scattering conditions have direct practical implications for autonomous driving safety. Key limitations include the need for off-device inference due to insufficient on-board compute, bandwidth constraints on frame rate, and reliance on synthetic fog for training (never seeing real fog during training). Open questions include whether future hardware with more on-device compute enables end-to-end waveform-to-scene understanding, and whether the approach generalizes to other adverse conditions (rain, snow) and sensor geometries.
