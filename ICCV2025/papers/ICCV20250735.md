## Problem

Visual interestingness is inherently subjective and context-dependent, yet prior computational approaches rely on costly explicit human annotations or biased implicit platform signals (e.g., favorites). It remains unclear whether Large Multimodal Models, trained on vast human-generated data, can capture this fuzzy concept and produce judgments that align with human assessments at scale.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250735/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline accepts individual RGB images (for single-image assessment) or unordered pairs of RGB images (for relative assessment), all drawn from a 1,000-image Flickr subset. LMMs are prompted with a fixed one-sentence instruction and must respond with a single keyword (yes/no or first/second) followed by a one-sentence justification. The distilled model outputs a scalar interestingness score per image (sigmoid of a linear projection of the CLIP embedding), which supports both pairwise comparison and global ranking of arbitrary image sets.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage distillation pipeline: GPT-4o is used to label 2,500 image pairs (which image is more interesting), and these labels train a lightweight Siamese learning-to-rank model. The model embeds each image via CLIP, applies a shared single-neuron linear layer, and computes the score difference through a sigmoid, trained with binary cross-entropy over 25 epochs. A key empirical finding motivates the pairwise design: single-image queries yield near-unanimous 'yes' responses from both humans and LMMs, rendering absolute assessment uninformative, whereas relative comparison produces meaningful variance. The paper further analyzes agreement and divergence by clustering text embeddings of LMM explanations and image descriptions (via UMAP and hierarchical clustering), revealing that humans and GPT-4o converge on nature, animals, and uniqueness as interesting, but GPT-4o additionally values vibrant color and dynamic action. A systematic positional bias in GPT-4o (preferring the second image in 36% of pairs) is identified and filtered before distillation.

## Results

On the Flickr-User dataset (1,000 images, 2,500 pairs), GPT-4o achieves 73.8% pairwise agreement with human annotations on the consensus set C_H, outperforming all baselines (next best: Aesthetic/VILA at 68.3%, CI at 69.6%). In the learning-to-rank setting, the model distilled from GPT-4o annotations reaches 73.4% accuracy on human-annotated pairs (vs. 77.5% for the human-annotation baseline) and 84.8% on GPT-4o-annotated pairs, with a Spearman rank correlation of 0.59 between human and GPT-4o global rankings. On the out-of-domain Int10k dataset, GPT-4o still outperforms all other approaches with 59.2% ± 2.1% accuracy, comparable to the human baseline of 59.3% ± 2.5%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o outperforms all five baseline groups on human-agreement: it leads CI (69.6%) by 4.2 points, Aesthetic/VILA (68.3%) by 5.5 points, CuPL (60.3%) by 13.5 points, and Memorability (35.5%) by 38.3 points. In the learning-to-rank setting, GPT-4o-distilled models achieve 73.4% accuracy on human pairs, beating CI (69.6%), Aesthetic (69.0%), CuPL (61.5%), and Memorability (34.7%), though trailing the human-annotation baseline (77.5%) by 4.1 points. Interestingly, when GPT-4o annotations serve as ground truth, the VILA aesthetic model actually surpasses GPT-4o itself (73.6% vs. 84.8% is GPT-4o's self-score; VILA reaches 73.6% on GPT-4o pairs), suggesting VILA captures a complementary aspect of visual appeal. Memorability is the only method with a negative Spearman correlation (−0.42) to human rankings, confirming it is essentially anti-correlated with interestingness. Social interestingness metrics (#Comments, #Favorites) show moderate agreement (66–68% with humans) but consistently underperform GPT-4o by 5–7 points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual interestingness prediction line from Constantin et al. (IJCV'21) and the authors' own ECCV'24 work on commonly interesting images, but departs fundamentally in its annotation strategy: rather than relying on explicit human labels or implicit user engagement (favorites), it uses GPT-4o as a large-scale annotator and distills its pairwise judgments into a lightweight Siamese CLIP-based learning-to-rank model. The methodological lineage draws on Siamese ranking architectures previously used for distilling LLM/LMM knowledge (e.g., [6, 41]), applied here to a subjective perceptual task. The paper also positions itself against related but distinct concepts—memorability, aesthetics (VILA), and social virality—showing how GPT-4o's judgments relate to each.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that GPT-4o can substitute for human annotators in pairwise interestingness labeling at scale, outperforming all prior computational baselines, though a 36% positional bias (favoring the second image) must be filtered out. The finding that single-image 'is this interesting?' queries are nearly uninformative (99.9% positive) underscores that interestingness is inherently relative, reinforcing the need for comparative frameworks. Open questions include whether the GPT-4o–human gap (73.8% vs. 77.5% human-human agreement) reflects genuine conceptual divergence or annotation noise, whether the demographic invariance holds for more culturally salient content, and whether the distilled CLIP-based ranker generalizes beyond the Flickr everyday-image domain (the Int10k transfer shows a significant drop).
