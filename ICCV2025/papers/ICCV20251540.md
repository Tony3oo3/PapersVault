## Problem

Temporally dense video tasks (natural language temporal grounding, temporal activity localization, audio description generation) require frame-level features at 1–5 FPS, which is 2–50× more expensive than the sparse 0.1–0.5 FPS sampling used for retrieval. Existing efficiency approaches either distill into smaller models (sacrificing accuracy) or encode each frame independently, ignoring the strong temporal redundancy between consecutive frames that could be exploited to cut compute.

## Input / Output

The method consumes video frames sampled at task-specific rates (3 FPS for Charades-STA, 1 FPS for ActivityNet-Captions, 5 FPS for MAD) along with paired text descriptions or queries. Every (N+1)-th frame is fully encoded by the original CLIP ViT to produce an I-feature (a b-dimensional [CLS] vector), while the N intervening frames are encoded by the lightweight ResidualViT to produce P-features of the same dimensionality. The token reduction module discards 85% of spatial patch tokens before the transformer, and the residual tokenizer injects a single d-dimensional residual token derived from the nearest I-feature, so the P-feature encoder sees only ~15% of the original token count plus one extra token.

## Method

ResidualViT modifies a frozen CLIP ViT with two lightweight additions: (i) a motion-based token-dropping module that prunes 85% of input patch tokens, and (ii) a residual tokenizer (a single learnable linear layer) that maps the I-feature from the most recent fully-encoded frame into a residual token, which is concatenated with the [CLS] token and the surviving patch tokens before feeding the frozen transformer. The encoding strategy interleaves one full-ViT frame (I-frame) with N ResidualViT frames (P-frames), mirroring I/P-frame video compression. Training uses a cross-entropy distillation loss over softmax vision–language similarities (both L→V and V→L directions), where the frozen CLIP ViT acts as teacher and only the residual tokenizer parameters are optimized on WebVid-2.5M for 5 epochs. This keeps the entire pretrained backbone intact while the residual token compensates for the spatial information lost to token dropping, yielding a 53–56% reduction in per-second encoding cost with <1–2% accuracy drop across four downstream tasks.

## Results

On the NLTVG task (Charades-STA), ResidualViT with ViT-L/14 achieves R@1 of 41.5 (IoU=0.5) and 23.8 (IoU=0.7), within 1.4 and 0.3 percentage points of CLIP's 42.9 and 24.1, while reducing average encoding cost from 233.4 to 102.6 GFLOPs per second (−56%). With ViT-B/16, ResidualViT actually matches or slightly exceeds CLIP (37.8 vs 37.7 at IoU=0.5) at 56% lower cost. Across all three backbone sizes and both short-video datasets, the accuracy drop is ≤1.5% absolute while computational cost is reduced by 53–56%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP (the primary upper-bound baseline), ResidualViT achieves nearly identical accuracy across all backbone sizes: on Charades-STA with ViT-B/16 it actually edges out CLIP (37.8 vs 37.7 at IoU=0.5), while with ViT-L/14 the drop is only 1.4 points (41.5 vs 42.9) at 56% lower cost. Compared to the zero-shot NLTVG baseline UniVTG [35], ResidualViT(L/14) substantially outperforms it (41.5 vs 25.2 at IoU=0.5 on Charades-STA) while incurring only modestly higher cost (102.6 vs 70.0 GFLOPs/s). Against MR-FVLM [41], ResidualViT(L/14) achieves comparable accuracy at IoU=0.5 (41.5 vs 42.9) but at a fraction of the computational cost (102.6 vs 1370 GFLOPs/s, roughly 13× cheaper). On the long-form MAD dataset, ResidualViT(B/32) outperforms the prior zero-shot baseline from [56] by 43–128% relative improvement across IoU thresholds while being 53% more efficient in feature extraction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's ViT encoder, adding two lightweight modules (a token reduction module and a residual tokenizer) while keeping all pretrained weights frozen. It departs from prior efficiency work on transformers—such as distillation into smaller models (Dehghani et al., Hao et al.) or standalone token-dropping strategies (PatchDropout, Token Merging)—by exploiting temporal redundancy across consecutive frames via an I-frame/P-frame-inspired interleaved encoding scheme. The residual token, learned through a vision-language distillation objective, compensates for information lost to token dropping by injecting context from the nearest fully-encoded frame, a mechanism absent from prior single-frame token reduction methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 53–56% reduction in per-second encoding cost with under 2% accuracy degradation across four tasks and three backbone sizes makes CLIP-based video feature extraction substantially more tractable for large-scale deployment (e.g., processing millions of videos at 1–5 FPS). The approach is remarkably lightweight—only a single linear layer is trained—so it can be retrofitted onto any frozen ViT encoder without retraining. Open questions include whether the interleave factor can be increased (or made adaptive) to push savings beyond 60% without accuracy loss, whether the distillation objective can be extended to natively temporal or generative video models, and whether the I/P-frame analogy generalizes to settings with larger temporal gaps (e.g., sparse keyframe sampling in very long videos).
