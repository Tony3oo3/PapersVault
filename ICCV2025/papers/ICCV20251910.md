## Problem

Previous benchmarks claimed FPV object tracking is harder than TPV by comparing tracker performance on entirely different datasets with mismatched object categories, contexts, and motion patterns, making it impossible to isolate the true effect of the first-person viewpoint. No prior study has directly compared tracking performance on synchronized FPV and TPV recordings of the same human-object activity, leaving open whether the observed performance drop stems from the FPV perspective itself or from the broader human-object interaction domain.

## Input / Output

The SOPE protocol consumes pairs of synchronized FPV and TPV videos of equal length (average ~922 frames at 5 FPS, minimum 720px dimension), each annotated with per-frame bounding boxes or segmentation masks at 1 FPS annotation rate. A tracker is initialized on the first frame with either a box or mask, then runs online to produce per-frame predictions in the same representation. The key constraint is that both views must have non-empty annotations for exactly the same subset of time steps, ensuring the performance difference metric (Δ) captures only viewpoint effects.

## Method

The paper introduces VISTA, a benchmark built from EgoExo4D's synchronized FPV-TPV video pairs, filtered for aligned instance-level mask annotations across 140 object categories and 140 video clips (544 test + 1831 training pairs). The core evaluation protocol, SOPE (Synchronized One-Pass Evaluation), extends standard OPE by running each tracker independently on both views of the same scene and quantifying the viewpoint effect as the mean weighted signed difference (Δ) of AUC, NPS, and GSR scores, weighted by annotation length. A novel Performance Difference Plot visualizes each tracker as a point (σ-TPV, σ-FPV) relative to a view-independence diagonal. To study training-induced viewpoint bias, STARK and XMem are trained and fine-tuned on generic, TPV-only, FPV-only, and combined domains, revealing that models develop strong viewpoint-specific biases and that TPV-optimized trackers actually underperform FPV-optimized ones in long-term tracking.

## Results

All 14 evaluated generic object trackers show statistically significant (p < 0.004) performance degradation in FPV relative to TPV on the VISTA benchmark. Bounding-box trackers exhibit average declines of Δ_AUC = −9.5, Δ_NPS = −9.8, and Δ_GSR = −20.7, while segmentation trackers decline by Δ_AUC = −9.3, Δ_NPS = −10.5, and Δ_GSR = −18.7. Specific examples include OSTrack dropping from 71.1 AUC on LaSOT to 43.7 (FPV) / 49.2 (TPV), and SAM2-M dropping from 89.3 on YouTubeVOS to 45.7 (FPV) / 58.8 (TPV). In short-term (object-visible) conditions, SAM2-based approaches (SAM2, SAMURAI, DAM4SAM) show near-zero or slightly positive Δ, indicating minimal viewpoint bias when the target is continuously visible.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All generic bounding-box trackers (STARK, KeepTrack, UNICORN-B, OSTrack, SeqTrack, TaMOs, ARTrackV2) consistently underperform in FPV versus TPV, with OSTrack showing the largest absolute gap (AUC 43.7 vs 49.2). The FPV-specific tracker EgoSTARK improves FPV performance but sacrifices TPV accuracy, confirming that viewpoint-specific training introduces bias rather than resolving the underlying challenge. Among segmentation trackers, AOT, UNICORN-M, XMem, and Cutie all exhibit TPV bias similar to their box-based counterparts. The SAM2-based methods (SAM2-B, SAM2-M, SAMURAI, DAM4SAM) are the most viewpoint-robust: in short-term (object-visible) evaluation they show near-zero or slightly positive Δ, with SAM2-M even performing marginally better in FPV, suggesting foundation models are less sensitive to the FPV viewpoint when the target remains visible. Training experiments (Table 2) reveal that FPV-trained STARK achieves 49.8 FPV-AUC vs 38.4 TPV-AUC, while TPV-trained STARK achieves 36.8 FPV-AUC vs 48.0 TPV-AUC, demonstrating that models learn strong viewpoint biases and that joint FPV+TPV training (Δ_AUC = −2.1) substantially reduces but does not eliminate the gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the egocentric tracking benchmarking line (TREK-150, EgoTracks, VISOR, VOST, EgoPoints), which all evaluated FPV performance against TPV baselines drawn from mismatched datasets with different object categories, contexts, and motion patterns, making it impossible to isolate the viewpoint effect. It leverages the EgoExo4D dataset's synchronized FPV-TPV video streams to construct the first controlled viewpoint comparison, and adopts the unified VOTS evaluation framework from the VOTS 2023/2024 challenges. The SOPE protocol extends the standard one-pass evaluation to operate on paired synchronized videos, a methodological contribution that no prior benchmark has attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that much of the perceived FPV difficulty is actually the human-object activity domain challenge rather than the viewpoint itself—reframes how the community should approach egocentric tracking and suggests that training data alignment, not architectural novelty, may be the primary lever for improvement. The VISTA benchmark is the first to offer synchronized FPV-TPV object tracks at scale (2,375 pairs per viewpoint, ~3.1 min average duration), enabling future work to study viewpoint invariance in a controlled setting. Open questions include whether foundation-model-based trackers (SAM2 family) will eventually eliminate viewpoint bias entirely, how to specifically address the residual FPV challenges (egomotion, wide-FOV distortion, object disappearances), and whether the findings generalize beyond the EgoExo4D activity domains.
