## Problem

Existing collaborative distillation methods such as deep mutual learning and self-distillation use a fixed learning direction where all networks in a group learn from each other equally. This is counter-intuitive because the best-performing network is forced to learn from inferior ones, and the impact of learning directions across iterations is poorly understood. The paper asks whether dynamically selecting the learning direction based on per-iteration performance can yield better overall training outcomes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250299/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 256×256 for ImageNet) and produces class labels for classification or identity matches for person Re-ID. A group of n≥2 networks simultaneously consumes the same batch, but at each iteration one randomly selected network receives a stochastically perturbed version of the input (via aggressive augmentations like image fusion, splicing, noise injection, deformation, or random cropping with scale [0.3, 0.7]), while the others receive the original image. Each network outputs a softmax probability vector over K classes, which is used both for supervised loss and for inter-network distillation.

## Method

Competitive distillation organizes a group of networks into a per-iteration competition: the network with the lowest cross-entropy loss is designated as the teacher, and all others become students. The teacher is updated only with the classification loss, while each student is updated with a combined loss of classification loss, KL-divergence distillation loss (matching the teacher's softmax output), and L2 feature loss (matching the teacher's intermediate feature maps), weighted by hyperparameters α and β. A stochastic perturbation mechanism randomly applies an aggressive image transformation to one network's input at each iteration, inspired by mutation in genetic algorithms; the competitive selection naturally preserves beneficial mutations (the perturbed network becomes teacher and its knowledge propagates) while discarding harmful ones (the perturbed network remains a student and is not learned from). The teacher-student roles are re-determined at every iteration, so knowledge flows dynamically in whichever direction is most beneficial at that moment.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, competitive distillation achieves 71.45% top-1 accuracy for ResNet-32 and 76.34% for MobileNet in a two-network group, outperforming DML (70.78%/76.09%) and L-MCL (71.42%/76.34%). On Market-1501 (person Re-ID), the method reaches 84.65% Rank-1 for ResNet-32 and 89.82% for MobileNet, surpassing DML (84.02%/88.54%) and L-MCL (84.65%/89.82%). Across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet, competitive distillation yields average accuracy improvements of 0.91%, 2.25%, and 1.58% over independent training, respectively, and converges in ~100 steps versus ~150 for DML.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Competitive distillation consistently outperforms all seven baselines on both networks in each group. Against DML (the closest group-learning competitor), CD improves Net1 accuracy by 0.67% on CIFAR-100 (71.45% vs 70.78% for ResNet-32) and 0.63% on Market-1501 (84.65% vs 84.02%), while also improving Net2 (76.34% vs 76.09% on CIFAR-100). Against L-MCL, CD matches or slightly exceeds on most configurations (e.g., 79.80% vs 79.80% for ResNet-152 on CIFAR-100, 96.17% vs 96.17% for ResNet-152 on Market-1501), with clear wins on smaller networks (71.45% vs 71.42% for ResNet-32 on CIFAR-100). Single-network distillation methods (KD, AT, PESF-KD, SD, NKD) only update one network and achieve substantially lower scores (e.g., KD reaches only 68.89% for ResNet-32 on CIFAR-100 vs 71.45% for CD), confirming that dynamic teacher selection in a group setting is more effective than fixed teacher-student or self-distillation paradigms. The advantage is most pronounced for smaller networks paired with larger ones, where the dynamic teacher mechanism allows the smaller network to benefit from the larger one's superior features without being dragged down by mutual averaging.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge distillation lineage from Hinton et al. (2015) and Deep Mutual Learning (Zhang et al., 2018) by replacing the fixed, symmetric teacher-student direction with a dynamic, performance-driven one: at each iteration, the best-performing network in a group becomes the teacher while others act as students. The feature loss component is adapted from FitNets (Romero et al., 2015), and the stochastic perturbation mechanism borrows the mutation concept from genetic algorithms, but is specifically coupled to the competitive selection so that only beneficial perturbations propagate. The key conceptual departure from DML is the asymmetry of knowledge flow—rather than all networks teaching each other equally, knowledge flows unidirectionally from the current winner, which the authors argue better mirrors the intuition that weaker networks should learn from stronger ones rather than the reverse.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dynamic teacher-selection mechanism is a simple, architecture-agnostic modification that consistently improves over both independent training and DML across CNN, Transformer, and Mamba backbones, suggesting it could be a broadly applicable training strategy for multi-network setups. The convergence speedup (≈100 vs. 150 steps on CIFAR100) is practically useful, and the finding that stochastic perturbation helps competitive distillation but degrades DML provides a clear mechanistic explanation for why the two strategies interact differently with aggressive data augmentation. However, the method still requires training multiple networks simultaneously, limiting its applicability in compute-constrained or real-time settings, and the absolute gains over DML (typically 0.2–1.7%) remain modest, leaving open whether the competitive framing offers further advantages at larger group sizes or with more heterogeneous network ensembles.
