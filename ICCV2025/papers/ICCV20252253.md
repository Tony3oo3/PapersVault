## Problem

Existing HDR exposure bracketing methods either fix ISO and only adjust shutter speed, or ignore motion blur entirely, treating ghosting and blur as separate post-processing tasks. No prior work simultaneously optimizes both ISO and shutter speed while explicitly accounting for motion-induced degradation during the capture phase in dynamic scenes, leaving a gap between noise-optimal exposure selection and motion-artifact avoidance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252253/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes three LDR preview images (underexposed, mid-exposed, overexposed at default −2/0/+2 EV) as initial input, along with extracted saliency maps and exposure histograms. It operates sequentially: after each LDR is captured, the new frame feeds back into the policy network for the next prediction step, enabling adaptive refinement. The output is a per-frame ISO and shutter speed schedule that can extend beyond three captures depending on the user-defined time budget, with the constraint that aperture and focus remain fixed.

## Method

AdaptiveAE formulates exposure bracketing as a Markov Decision Process and trains a CNN-based policy network (predicting ISO and shutter speed) and value network via A3C reinforcement learning. The key technical contribution is a blur-aware data synthesis pipeline that, during training, uses RIFE to interpolate between consecutive HDR ground-truth frames and averages sub-frames to simulate motion blur at a given shutter speed, then applies Hasinoff's photon/readout/ADC noise model at the chosen ISO to produce realistic LDR inputs. The reward function combines four terms: L2 loss to ground truth (construction), saliency-masked L2 loss (priority via SalGAN), optical-flow-masked L2 loss (ghost via RAFT, thresholded at 0.2 normalized flow), and a quadratic step penalty for exceeding three frames. The sequential refinement proceeds in stages—predicting the mid-exposed frame, customizing side frames to symmetric EV, then refining under- and over-exposed frames—allowing the agent to adapt each capture based on previously acquired information.

## Results

On the Real-HDRV test set, AdaptiveAE achieves PSNR-μ of 39.70, SSIM-μ of 0.9408, and HDR-VDP-2 of 59.20, outperforming all three baselines by a clear margin. On the cross-dataset DeepHDRVideo benchmark, it reaches PSNR-μ 39.81 and SSIM-μ 0.9371, again leading all compared methods. The gap to the locally optimal (Gaussian-search) result is only 0.23 dB in PSNR-μ (39.70 vs. 39.93), indicating the agent operates near the achievable optimum while remaining efficient.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaptiveAE outperforms all three baselines on every metric across both datasets. Against the strongest baseline, Hasinoff et al. [6] (noise-optimal shutter/ISO optimization for static scenes), it gains +2.11 dB PSNR-μ on HDRV (39.70 vs. 37.59) and +1.34 dB on DeepHDRVideo (39.81 vs. 38.47). Against Wang et al. [32] (RL-based shutter-speed prediction that ignores motion), the margin is +3.24 dB on HDRV and +1.86 dB on DeepHDRVideo. Against Pourreza et al. [21] (K-means histogram-based bracketing), the advantage is largest at +6.06 dB on HDRV. All three baselines treat exposure selection as a static-scene problem and do not model motion blur or ghosting; AdaptiveAE's blur-aware synthesis pipeline and motion-sensitive reward (P_ghost) are the key differentiators, with the ablation showing P_ghost alone contributes +1.13 dB PSNR-μ over the base model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the exposure bracketing line of work initiated by Hasinoff et al. [6] (noise-optimal capture as constrained optimization) and Wang et al. [32] (RL-based shutter speed prediction) by jointly optimizing both ISO and shutter speed within a sequential MDP framework. The key conceptual departure is the blur-aware data synthesis pipeline that simulates motion blur (via RIFE frame interpolation) and sensor noise (via the photon/readout/ADC model from [6]) during training, allowing the RL agent to learn motion-aware exposure policies rather than treating blur as a separate post-processing problem. The sequential refinement strategy—where each new LDR informs the next prediction—differs from prior methods that predict all bracketing settings simultaneously, better mirroring real camera auto-exposure behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in HDR capture for dynamic scenes where motion blur and noise interact in ways that static-scene optimization cannot handle. The finding that stronger fusion models (HDR-Transformer) actually widen the quality gap between motion-aware and motion-unaware exposure strategies suggests that addressing artifacts at capture time is fundamentally more effective than relying on post-processing. Limitations include reliance on a fixed fusion backbone (DeepHDR), a single real-world camera for validation, and the ~250 ms inference budget that may constrain real-time deployment; the authors note adjustable aperture as a natural next step.
