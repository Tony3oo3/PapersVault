## Problem

Phase-shifting structured-light methods achieve high-accuracy 3D measurement but assume the object is stationary; when the object moves between projected patterns, phase relationships are disrupted and reconstruction accuracy degrades. Existing motion-compensation approaches assume a constant light-transport coefficient per pixel and are sensitive to noise, while recent neural inverse rendering (TurboSL) reduces the required pattern count but does not model object motion. This paper addresses the gap by combining neural inverse rendering with explicit motion modeling to enable high-accuracy 3D measurement of moving objects using as few as three phase-shifting patterns.

## Input / Output

The method takes captured images from 2–3 synchronized cameras (1024×768) of a single projector displaying N sinusoidal phase-shifting patterns (N as low as 3), along with the known projected pattern images. The pattern index n is treated as a discrete time step, so the input implicitly encodes object motion across frames. The output is a 3D mesh extracted from the learned SDF at a mid-sequence time step, together with per-point displacement fields, a reflectance map, and a residual illumination map; the mesh is constructed from visible regions across all cameras and the projector.

## Method

The core contribution is a neural inverse-rendering model for structured light that integrates a displacement field h(x, n) into the volume-rendering integral, mapping each 3D point at pattern index n back to a reference position so that the SDF, reflectance, and residual illumination remain consistent across time steps while the surface normal is rotated to account for motion. A multi-camera single-projector formulation introduces three complementary rendering paths—projector-to-camera, camera-to-projector, and a novel camera-to-camera cross-view estimation—that jointly constrain the optimization and enable accurate reconstruction with only three patterns. All scene properties (SDF, displacement field, reflectance, residual) are represented by neural networks with multiresolution hash encoding, and training is optimization-based with a staged schedule: the displacement field is frozen for the first 1,000 iterations, the projector-to-camera loss is delayed by 200 iterations, the camera-to-projector loss by 1,000 iterations, and the mask loss is applied only for the first 1,500 iterations to avoid boundary inaccuracies. Smoothstep interpolation is used to stabilize backpropagation through the displacement field, and the final mesh is extracted from the SDF at the mid-sequence time step.

## Results

The primary metric is mean absolute error (MAE) in mm for 3D shape reconstruction. At the standard evaluation speed of 2 mm/frame, the proposed method (PS3-Cam2-w/DF) achieves 0.2322 mm MAE, substantially outperforming the best conventional baseline (PS3-Cam2-Standard at 0.8648 mm) by a factor of ~3.7×. At static conditions (0 mm/frame), the method reaches 0.2208 mm, and it maintains sub-0.7 mm MAE across all tested speeds from −8 to +8 mm/frame. In real-world experiments, a checkerboard target moving at ~1000 mm/s was reconstructed with 0.47 mm MAE using only three phase-shifting patterns.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method (PS3-Cam2-w/DF) outperforms all three baselines across every tested speed. Against TurboSL (Mirdehghan et al., CVPR 2024), the gap is dramatic: at 2 mm/frame, ALC3-Cam1-TurboSL yields 1.31 mm MAE versus 0.23 mm for the proposed method, and at 8 mm/frame TurboSL degrades to 30.47 mm while the proposed method stays at 0.66 mm. Against Weise et al. (CVPR 2007), the proposed method achieves 0.23 mm vs. 0.56 mm at 2 mm/frame and 0.66 mm vs. 2.02 mm at 8 mm/frame. Against Zhang et al. (ECCV 2024), the proposed method reaches 0.23 mm vs. 0.69 mm at 2 mm/frame and 0.66 mm vs. 3.68 mm at 8 mm/frame. Notably, the A La Carte pattern variant (ALC3-Cam2-w/DF) achieves the best accuracy at extreme negative speeds (−4, −8 mm/frame), but the standard three-phase-shifting pattern (PS3) is more robust across the full speed range, confirming that simple sinusoidal patterns suffice when combined with the displacement-field rendering model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends TurboSL (Mirdehghan et al., 2024), which applied neural inverse rendering to structured light for static objects with a single camera-projector pair, by incorporating a displacement field (borrowed from dynamic NeRF variants like D-NeRF) to model object motion and by formulating a multi-camera single-projector rendering model. It departs from traditional motion-compensated phase-shifting methods (Weise et al., Zhang et al., Feng et al.), which assume a constant light-transport coefficient per pixel across patterns, by jointly optimizing reflectance, SDF, and displacement through global neural rendering. The camera-to-camera cross-view loss is a new component not present in either the structured-light or dynamic-NeRF literatures, enabling shape reconstruction from fewer projected patterns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that high-accuracy 3D measurement of moving objects becomes feasible with only three sinusoidal patterns in a dual-camera single-projector setup, reducing acquisition time and hardware complexity compared to prior multi-pattern or multi-projector approaches. However, most quantitative comparisons rely on synthetic (Blender-rendered) data without noise, and real-world validation is limited to two qualitative examples, leaving open questions about robustness to photon shot noise, motion blur, and more complex non-rigid deformations. The finding that increasing the number of patterns beyond four degrades accuracy (due to larger cumulative motion) suggests a fundamental trade-off between pattern count and motion range that warrants further investigation.
