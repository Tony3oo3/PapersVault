## Problem

Standard contrastive learning formulates self-supervised pretraining as a binary min-max optimization over feature distances, causing an 'over-dispersion' problem that breaks pixel-wise feature correlations and disrupts intra-class distributions. This is especially detrimental in medical vision, where global anatomical context is highly similar across images and clinically critical structures (thin vessels, subtle lesions) depend on preserving fine-grained local feature continuity. The paper asks whether CL can be reformulated as a truly pixel-wise self-supervised paradigm that quantifies dispersion rather than merely maximizing it.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single medical image (2D chest X-ray, fundus, CT slice, or 3D brain MR volume) and applies random appearance and spatial transformations to produce two augmented views with a known displacement vector field (DVF) encoding their pixel-wise correspondences. A shared-weight U-Net backbone extracts multi-scale dense feature maps from both views, and the COVER head predicts a DVF that regresses the spatial correspondence. The pretrained feature maps are then transferred to downstream segmentation or classification tasks via full-parameter fine-tuning.

## Method

COVER reformulates contrastive learning as vector regression: instead of binary min-max on embedding-space distances, it predicts displacement vectors in image space that encode spatial correspondences between two augmented views, with the key insight that feature distances can be structurally regulated through vector properties (magnitude and direction). Three components implement this: (1) Self-Vector Regression (SeVR) generates ground-truth DVFs from random spatial transformations, enabling annotation-free, extendable self-supervision; (2) Mixture of Vectors (MoV) maps feature distances to vectors via a vector embedding unit (scaled dot-product attention weighted by a fixed vector template matrix) and a multi-vector integration strategy that splits channels into groups to handle correspondence ambiguity; (3) Vector Pyramid Aggregation (VPA) stacks MoV across scale levels in a pyramid, where each level's predicted DVF warps the next level's source features before further MoV processing, capturing multi-scale correspondences at low computational cost. Training minimizes a combined loss of MAE vector regression (predicted vs. ground-truth DVF) and cosine-similarity consistency between warped features.

## Results

COVER achieves the highest average score of 84.5% across 8 downstream tasks (4 segmentation, 2 classification in 2D; 2 segmentation, 1 classification in 3D), outperforming the closest vector-regression baseline GEMINI (83.2%) and GVSL (82.3%). Headline individual results include 94.0% DSC on SCR (chest X-ray segmentation, 25% data), 95.9% AUC on PDCXR (chest X-ray classification), and 85.2% DSC on KiPA22 3D (kidney CT segmentation), representing improvements of +12.2%, +5.5%, and +12.8% over training from scratch, respectively. COVER also achieves the best scores on all 8 tasks individually, with the largest gains on small-structure tasks like FIVES (87.2% DSC, +7.8% over scratch) and STOIC (80.4% AUC, +8.4% over scratch).

## Baselines & Comparisons

COVER outperforms all 17 baselines on the average score and achieves the best individual score on every one of the 8 tasks. The closest competitors are the vector-regression methods GEMINI (83.2% avg) and GVSL (82.3% avg), which COVER surpasses by 1.3% and 2.2% respectively, with the gap widening on inconsistent-scene tasks (e.g., +1.9% on FeTA21, +0.9% on STOIC over GEMINI). Among dense binary CL methods, PixPro is the strongest (81.0% avg) but still trails COVER by 3.5%, and notably DenseCL and VADeR degrade severely on cross-scene tasks (FeTA21 drops to 43.7% and 43.1% DSC, respectively, vs. 56.9% for scratch), confirming the over-dispersion problem. Standard binary CL methods (BYOL 80.0%, SimCLR 78.1%) show inconsistent gains, with SimCLR actually dropping 11.3% on STOIC relative to scratch. Generative learning baselines (Models Genesis 77.6%, In-painting 75.3%) are consistently weaker, particularly on 3D tasks where they lose 2–6% DSC over scratch. The paper attributes COVER's consistent advantage to its vector regression formulation, which quantifies feature dispersion without the rigid max-min contrast of binary CL, preserving intra-class distribution and enabling cross-scale and cross-scene transferability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the vector-field correspondence learning introduced by GVSL [19] and GEMINI [22], which predicted displacement vector fields for medical SSP but lacked a principled mapping between feature distances and vectors, resulting in inconsistent optimization flows. It departs from the broader binary contrastive learning lineage (SimCLR, BYOL, DenseCL, PixPro) by reformulating the contrastive objective from a binary min-max distance problem into a vector regression problem, where feature distances are quantified through regressing displacement vectors in image space. The self-vector regression (SeVR) mechanism synthesizes ground-truth vectors from random spatial transformations, eliminating the need for paired data that prior vector-based SSP methods required.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The over-dispersion problem in pixel-wise CL is particularly acute in medical imaging, where consistent anatomies limit global diversity and subtle structures (thin vessels, small lesions) are easily blurred by excessive feature dispersion. COVER's vector regression formulation provides a principled, annotation-free way to control dispersion quantitatively, and the consistent gains across 8 tasks, 2 dimensions, and 4 modalities suggest the approach generalizes well. Key open questions include whether the framework scales to truly large-scale medical datasets (the current pretraining sets are moderate: ~112K 2D X-rays, ~837 3D MRIs), whether the vector CL formulation transfers to non-medical continuous-signal domains, and how the method interacts with emerging transformer-based backbones beyond the U-Net used here.
