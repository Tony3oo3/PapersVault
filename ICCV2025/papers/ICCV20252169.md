## Problem

While individual SSL methods have been studied for adversarial robustness, no prior work provides a unified cross-model comparison across multiple discriminative SSL paradigms, architectures, and downstream tasks under diverse attack types. It remains unclear whether SSL's theoretical advantage in feature representation (reduced reliance on non-robust features) translates into practical robustness gains, and how factors like architecture choice, training duration, and augmentation strategy modulate this advantage.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252169/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

All models consume standard RGB images (224×224 for ImageNet, native resolutions for transfer datasets) and produce task-specific outputs: class labels for classification, pixel-wise semantic masks for segmentation (via DeepLabV3+), and bounding boxes with class scores for detection (via Faster R-CNN). Adversarial perturbations are applied at the input level using both instance-level attacks (FGSM, PGD, BIM, MIM) and universal adversarial perturbations, with performance measured as clean accuracy, adversarial accuracy, and relative drop percentage. Evaluation is conducted under both frozen-backbone and fine-tuned configurations to isolate backbone robustness from task-head effects.

## Method

This is a comprehensive empirical benchmark rather than a new architecture or training procedure. The study evaluates seven discriminative SSL models (Barlow Twins, BYOL, DINO, MoCoV3, SimCLR, SwAV, VICReg) plus a supervised ResNet50 baseline across four task families—ImageNet classification, transfer learning (linear probing and fine-tuning on nine datasets), semantic segmentation, and object detection—under both IAA and UAP attack suites. Key analyses include: (1) a MoCo family ablation (V1→V2→V3) isolating the contributions of non-linear projectors, prediction heads, and momentum encoders to robustness; (2) architecture comparison (ResNet50 vs ViT-B) showing objective-dependent effects where DINO benefits from ViT but MoCoV3 degrades; (3) training duration tracking via multi-epoch checkpoints for SwAV and MoCoV3; and (4) a component attribution analysis using Direction and Magnitude Ratios to decompose vulnerability between backbone features and classification logits. The central finding is that MoCoV3's momentum encoder combined with its prediction head yields the strongest adversarial robustness, and that this advantage persists through linear probing but diminishes under fine-tuning, segmentation, and detection where task-specific heads override backbone properties.

## Results

On ImageNet with frozen ResNet-50 backbones, MoCoV3 achieves the highest adversarial accuracy at 40.5% (46% drop from 74.6% clean), outperforming the supervised baseline (34.5% adversarial, 55% drop from 76.1% clean). Across transfer learning (linear probing), MoCoV3 again leads with 40.3% adversarial accuracy versus 31.2% for the supervised model. In segmentation (Pascal VOC, frozen backbone), all models suffer catastrophic 74–84% mIOU drops regardless of pretraining paradigm, with VICReg achieving the best clean mIOU (77.9%) and the supervised model the worst adversarial mIOU (16.5%). In detection (INRIA Person, frozen backbone), Barlow Twins and DINO lead with 75% mAP drops, while the supervised model remains the least robust at 78% drop.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MoCoV3 consistently outperforms all other models on ImageNet adversarial robustness (40.5% adv. accuracy, 46% drop), surpassing the supervised baseline by 6 percentage points and the next-best SSL models (BYOL at 38.9%, VICReg at 37.0%) by 2–3.5 points. This advantage is attributed to its momentum encoder and prediction head, as the MoCo family analysis shows progressive robustness gains from MoCoV1 (71% drop) → MoCoV2 (52%) → MoCoV3 (46%), with the non-linear projector and prediction head each contributing substantially. DINO, despite high clean accuracy (75.3%), shows the weakest IAA robustness among SSL models (64% drop), contradicting prior claims that non-contrastive methods are inherently more robust. In segmentation, the SSL advantage vanishes entirely: all models suffer 74–84% mIOU drops, with the supervised model actually performing worst (16.5% adv. mIOU) while VICReg leads (20.5%). In detection, the supervised model remains the least robust across both INRIA Person (78% drop) and COCO (67% drop), but SSL models no longer show a clear hierarchy—VICReg, which performs well in classification, becomes unexpectedly vulnerable (84% drop on INRIA). Architecture effects are objective-dependent: DINO-ViT-B improves over DINO-ResNet (42% vs 55% drop), while MoCoV3-ViT-B degrades relative to MoCoV3-ResNet (51% vs 46% drop).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as the first comprehensive cross-model adversarial robustness benchmark spanning seven discriminative SSL paradigms (contrastive, distillation, clustering, decorrelation) alongside a supervised baseline, evaluated across classification, transfer learning, segmentation, and detection. It extends prior work that examined individual SSL methods in isolation (Gupta et al. on contrastive susceptibility, Kowalczuk et al. on multi-task encoder robustness, Zhong et al. on distribution shifts) by unifying them under a common attack suite (IAA and UAP) and controlled protocol. The MoCo family ablation (V1→V2→V3) builds on the architectural evolution literature to isolate the contributions of momentum encoders, non-linear projectors, and prediction heads to robustness, while the ResNet-vs-ViT comparison extends the architecture-robustness debate (Bai et al., Pinto et al.) into the SSL setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that SSL's robustness advantage over supervised learning is strongly task-dependent—preserved in classification and linear probing but largely erased in segmentation and detection—challenges the assumption that backbone-level robustness transfers uniformly to downstream architectures. The observation that UAP vulnerability is deeply encoded in the learning paradigm while IAA vulnerability is malleable through fine-tuning offers practical guidance for threat-model-specific model selection. Open questions remain about why MoCoV3's momentum-plus-prediction-head combination is uniquely robust, whether this generalizes beyond ResNet50, and how to design SSL objectives that explicitly target robustness in task-specific heads rather than relying on backbone properties alone.
