## Problem

Existing 3D lane detection methods that incorporate road height (e.g., HeightLane) rely on fixed multi-slope anchors treated equally, lacking adaptability to diverse road geometries, and produce temporally inconsistent height estimates across frames. Additionally, no standardized quantitative metrics exist for evaluating road height estimation accuracy, making cross-method comparison impossible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252531/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single front-view RGB image (600×800) at inference, though training uses two consecutive frames for temporal consistency supervision. It produces a BEV heightmap at 200×48 resolution (0.5 m/pixel scale) covering the 3D lane detection range, which serves as a geometric prior for a downstream 3D lane head. The heightmap is defined in a road coordinate system (z-axis normal to local road surface), and the final output is 3D lane geometry in ego coordinates.

## Method

SC-Lane introduces two key modules on top of HeightLane's multi-slope anchor framework. The Slope-Aware Adaptive Feature (SAA) module predicts per-pixel anchor weights α_a(X,Y) via a lightweight CNN upsampling backbone features to BEV resolution followed by a linear layer and softmax, then computes a probability-weighted blend of all slope-specific sampled features; a confidence heightmap derived from these weights is supervised with IoU loss against a 2D ground segmentation mask to prevent anchors from attending to non-ground regions. The Height Consistency Module, applied only during training, transforms the previous frame's heightmap into the current frame's road coordinate system via ego-motion compensation (ego→road, ego(t-1)→ego(t), road→ego transformations) and applies L1 loss on overlapping regions to enforce temporal coherence. The total loss combines the SAA IoU loss, consistency L1 loss, and a direct L1 height regression loss (weights 5, 2, 10 respectively). The resulting heightmap feeds a Height-Guided Spatial Transform to refine BEV features, which are then processed by a standard 3D lane head to produce final lane predictions.

## Results

On the OpenLane validation set, SC-Lane achieves a state-of-the-art F-score of 64.3%, outperforming the previous best methods HeightLane and PVALane (both 62.7%) by 1.6 points. In terms of height estimation, SC-Lane reduces MAE to 0.176 m and RMSE to 0.259 m, compared to 0.235 m and 0.343 m for HeightLane, while also achieving the highest threshold accuracy (Acc@0.2 = 75.6%). SC-Lane also leads in scenario-specific F-scores for curved roads (70.7%), merge/split (63.0%), and intersections (56.1%), and outperforms multi-frame methods like GTANet (62.4%) despite using temporal cues only as training supervision with single-frame inference.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SC-Lane achieves the highest F-score (64.3%) among all single-frame and multi-frame methods, beating the closest competitors HeightLane [20] and PVALane [30] (both 62.7%) by 1.6 points, and LATR [17] (61.9%) by 2.4 points. On Z-error, SC-Lane (0.088/0.128 near/far) trails LATR (0.075/0.104) and RFTR (0.073/0.107), which the authors attribute to their anchor-based 3D regression formulation versus SC-Lane's dense BEV heightmap representation. Against multi-frame baselines, SC-Lane surpasses GTANet [29] (62.4%, 2-frame input) by 1.9 points and CurveFormer++ [1] (55.4%, 2-frame input) by 8.9 points, while requiring no additional frames at inference. In scenario-specific evaluation, SC-Lane leads on curved roads (70.7% vs. 69.3% for HeightLane), merge/split (63.0% vs. 61.5% for LATR), and intersections (56.1% vs. 54.1% for HeightLane), but is second-best on up/down terrain (54.6% vs. 55.2% for LATR) and extreme weather (58.1% vs. 62.0% for PVALane). On the proposed height estimation metrics, SC-Lane reduces MAE by 25% (0.176 vs. 0.235) and RMSE by 24% (0.259 vs. 0.343) relative to HeightLane, with consistent gains across all accuracy thresholds.

## Relation to Prior Work

SC-Lane is a direct extension of HeightLane [20], which first introduced multi-slope anchors to project front-view features into a BEV heightmap for 3D lane detection. The key conceptual departure is replacing HeightLane's fixed, equal-weight slope fusion with an image-conditioned adaptive weighting mechanism (Slope-Aware Adaptive Feature), allowing the network to selectively attend to the most relevant slope per BEV pixel. The temporal consistency module borrows the ego-motion-compensated alignment idea from monocular depth estimation (Monodepth2, RollingDepth) but applies it to the more static road surface, and the 3D lane head and road coordinate system are inherited from BEV-LaneDet [23] and 3D-LaneNet [4] respectively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The introduction of standardized height estimation metrics (MAE, RMSE, Acc@t) fills a genuine gap in the 3D lane detection literature, where height accuracy had previously been assessed only implicitly through lane F-score. This enables direct, quantitative comparison of height estimation quality across methods for the first time. A notable limitation is the acknowledged Z-error tradeoff: the dense BEV heightmap representation sacrifices some longitudinal accuracy compared to anchor-based methods like LATR, suggesting that the optimal representation for height-aware lane detection is not yet settled. Open questions include whether the adaptive slope weighting generalizes to more extreme off-road terrains, whether the two-frame temporal supervision can be extended to longer sequences without additional inference cost, and whether the height estimation framework transfers to HD map reconstruction or localization tasks as the authors speculate.
