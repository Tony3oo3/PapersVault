## Problem

Rasterization-based radiance field methods like 3D Gaussian Splatting achieve real-time speeds but struggle to implement light transport effects such as reflections, refractions, and non-linear camera models. Ray tracing handles these effects naturally, but existing differentiable ray tracing approaches either require dedicated hardware (NVIDIA RT cores/OptiX) or suffer from slow rendering due to overlapping primitives and complex acceleration structures. The paper re-introduces a decades-old efficient volumetric mesh ray tracing algorithm (Weiler et al.) that was overlooked in the CV community, making it differentiable and competitive with rasterization in both speed and quality.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images (downsampled by 2× for indoor and 4× for outdoor Mip-NeRF 360 scenes to match 3DGS protocols; original resolution for Deep Blending) along with a COLMAP SfM point cloud for initialization. It produces a 3D Voronoi foam representation consisting of optimizable site positions with per-cell constant density and view-dependent radiance values, which can be rendered via ray tracing at 200–300+ FPS on a consumer RTX 4090. The representation is a non-overlapping polyhedral mesh, so no secondary acceleration structure is needed at render time.

## Method

The scene is parameterized as a 3D Voronoi diagram whose sites are the sole geometric degrees of freedom; each Voronoi cell carries a constant density and radiance value, making the volume rendering integral exact (no sampling) and the per-cell contribution a simple closed-form sum. The key differentiability insight is that while the dual Delaunay triangulation undergoes discrete edge flips, the Voronoi cell boundaries change continuously because the affected faces have zero area at the flip instant, so gradients flow smoothly through the ray–cell intersection geometry. Ray tracing uses a modified Weiler et al. algorithm that steps through convex polyhedral cells by iterating over planar faces to find the exit intersection, requiring no hardware ray tracing or OptiX. Training follows a coarse-to-fine strategy: SfM initialization, adaptive densification driven by gradient-norm-weighted cell radius (sampled via a multinomial distribution), pruning of low-density interior sites while retaining boundary-defining cells, and a quantile loss that regularizes the volume rendering weight distribution to concentrate density at surfaces without the quadratic cost of the standard distortion loss.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF 360, RadiantFoam achieves 28.47 PSNR, 0.83 SSIM, and 0.21 LPIPS at 200 FPS; on Deep Blending it reaches 28.95 PSNR, 0.89 SSIM, and 0.26 LPIPS at 301 FPS. These quality numbers are comparable to (slightly below) 3DGS and 3DGRT, while the rendering speed is more than 2× faster than 3DGRT (78/119 FPS) and on par with rasterization-based methods. The method substantially outperforms all NeRF-based baselines (Plenoxels, iNGP-Big, MipNeRF360) in both quality and speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the rasterization baselines, RadiantFoam trails 3DGS by ~0.2 dB PSNR on Mip-NeRF 360 (28.47 vs 28.69) and by ~0.5 dB on Deep Blending (28.95 vs 29.41), but matches or beats 3DGS on LPIPS (0.21 vs 0.22; 0.26 vs 0.32). It is also slightly below 3DGS-MCMC (29.72/29.71 PSNR) and Mip-Splatting (29.39/29.47 PSNR) in quality, though its 200/301 FPS is competitive with their 302/662 and 241/260 FPS respectively. Against the closest ray-tracing competitor 3DGRT, RadiantFoam sacrifices ~0.2–0.3 dB PSNR but more than doubles the frame rate (200 vs 78 on Mip-NeRF 360; 301 vs 119 on Deep Blending) and achieves lower LPIPS (0.21 vs 0.25; 0.26 vs 0.32). Against all NeRF-based methods (Plenoxels, iNGP-Big, MipNeRF360), RadiantFoam wins decisively on both quality (4–5 dB PSNR advantage) and speed (200–301 FPS vs <30 or <1 FPS). The overall trade-off is a small quality gap versus the best rasterization methods in exchange for a true ray-tracing pipeline that supports reflections, refractions, and non-linear camera models without hardware ray-tracing acceleration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper revives a decades-old volumetric mesh ray tracing algorithm by Weiler et al. (2003) that was largely overlooked during the NeRF and 3DGS eras, and makes it differentiable by parameterizing the underlying mesh as a 3D Voronoi diagram. It departs from the rasterization paradigm that 3D Gaussian Splatting popularized, instead adopting a ray-based training modality closer to NeRF while retaining 3DGS-level rendering speed. The Voronoi parameterization is motivated by the observation that discrete connectivity changes in the dual Delaunay triangulation are hidden in zero-area cell boundaries, preserving differentiability — a property not exploited by prior mesh-based radiance field methods like DMTet, Tetra-NeRF, or DeRF, which rely on ray marching and MLP evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Radiant Foam demonstrates that real-time ray-traced volume rendering is achievable on standard programmable GPUs without dedicated RT cores or OptiX, opening the door to effects like reflection, refraction, and non-linear camera models that are cumbersome to implement with rasterization. The Voronoi constraint (equidistant cell boundaries) limits geometric expressiveness and forces many small empty cells to define surfaces, which the authors acknowledge as a key limitation. Open questions include generalizing beyond Voronoi diagrams, handling dynamic scenes, enabling scene editing, and integrating generative modeling — directions that could make foam-based ray tracing competitive with triangle meshes in production real-time engines.
