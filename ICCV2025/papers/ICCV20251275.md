## Problem

Malicious video detection datasets are severely limited in scale (often fewer than 1,000 samples) due to the labor-intensive cost of video annotation, causing existing detectors to overfit and generalize poorly to real-world content. While large-scale image-text datasets (tens of thousands of samples) share similar modality composition and malicious semantics, directly transferring knowledge from them to video detection is non-trivial due to noisy irrelevant samples and the dynamic-vs-static domain gap between video and image-text data.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip comprising visual frames (uniformly sampled to 32 frames for pseudo-pair generation and 16 frames for feature extraction), video title, on-screen text, and audio transcript as input, and produces a binary malicious/benign prediction via a 2-layer MLP classifier. The video is converted into L=10 pseudo image-text pairs (representative frames paired with aligned textual content) that serve as queries for cross-domain retrieval from an external image-text corpus, making the retrieval step a key intermediate representation rather than a simple frame-level lookup.

## Method

CRAVE consists of two core components: a Pseudo-Pair Retriever that converts each video into structured pseudo image-text pairs (clustered representative frames + aligned text) to perform CLIP-based cross-domain retrieval, selecting top-K positive and negative image-text pairs from a large external corpus; and a Contrastive Cross-Domain Augmenter that disentangles domain-shared and domain-unique representations via separate 1-layer MLP encoders, enforced by a KL-divergence-based Domain-Invariant Objective (aligning shared representations across domains) and an orthogonality-based Domain-Unique Objective (separating shared from unique within each domain). The shared video representation is then refined through a triplet contrastive loss that pulls it toward retrieved positive image-text shared representations while pushing it away from negative ones, using the retrieved pairs as contrastive references. The final prediction fuses the refined shared representation with the video-unique representation, and all pre-trained CLIP encoders remain frozen during training to reduce computational overhead.

## Results

CRAVE achieves the best performance across all four video datasets, with an average improvement of 9.2% in Macro F1 and 8.1% in Accuracy over the strongest baseline. On FakeTT, CRAVE reaches 84.95% ACC and 83.52% M-F1; on FVC, 96.52% ACC and 96.45% M-F1; on MHClipEN, 82.50% ACC and 79.81% M-F1; and on HateMM, 87.09% ACC and 86.51% M-F1. All improvements are statistically significant with p-values below 0.05 (most below 0.01). Under extreme data-scarcity (10% training data), CRAVE still maintains over 70% accuracy on both FakeTT and MHClipEN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRAVE outperforms all 10 baselines on every dataset and every metric. Among vanilla detection methods, FakeRec is the strongest on rumor datasets (79.53% ACC on FakeTT, 91.22% on FVC), while MHCL leads on hate datasets (74.00% ACC on MHClipEN, 78.80% on HateMM); CRAVE surpasses these by 5.4–12.7% in M-F1. Conventional augmentation methods (Spatial Aug. and Temp. Aug.) yield only marginal gains or even degrade performance relative to their underlying detectors, confirming that surface-level video transformations fail to introduce semantically diverse malicious patterns. Cross-domain baselines (ViLT, TSformer, LLaVA, Qwen-VL) underperform CRAVE substantially—LLaVA and Qwen-VL drop to 46.93% and 53.17% ACC on FakeTT respectively, as their pre-training on benign content provides limited harmful-pattern knowledge. The largest absolute gains appear on MHClipEN (+12.7% M-F1 over MHCL) and HateMM (+10.3% M-F1 over MHCL), where training data is most scarce (~1,000 samples), highlighting CRAVE's advantage in data-constrained regimes.

## Relation to Prior Work

CRAVE carves new ground at the intersection of retrieval-augmented generation and cross-domain knowledge transfer for video understanding. While prior malicious video detection methods (HTMM, MHCL, SVFEND, FakeRec) operate exclusively on in-domain video data, and existing cross-domain transfer approaches (ViLT, TSformer) or general VLMs (LLaVA, Qwen-VL) apply generic pre-trained knowledge without task-specific retrieval, CRAVE introduces a retrieval-guided paradigm that selectively pulls semantically relevant image-text instances via a Pseudo-Pair Retriever. The Contrastive Cross-Domain Augmenter with its decoupling objectives (DIO via KL-divergence, DUO via orthogonality) and triplet-based contrastive loss represents a novel architectural contribution that goes beyond simple feature concatenation or fine-tuning of pre-trained encoders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a practically critical bottleneck: malicious video datasets contain fewer than 1,000 samples, severely limiting model generalizability, while image-text datasets offer tens of thousands of annotated instances at low annotation cost. The cross-domain retrieval augmentation paradigm could be extended to other data-scarce video tasks (e.g., violence detection, misinformation in other languages), though the current implementation is tightly coupled to CLIP encoders and binary malicious/benign classification. Open questions include whether the pseudo-pair construction generalizes to tasks without clear frame-text alignment, how the method scales to multi-class or fine-grained detection, and whether the reliance on frozen CLIP representations limits ceiling performance compared to end-to-end training.
