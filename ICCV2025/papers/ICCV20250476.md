## Problem

Current multimodal models typically graft separately pre-trained vision encoders onto LLMs (late fusion), but it remains unclear whether this architecture is inherently superior to early-fusion models that process raw multimodal inputs from scratch. Additionally, the scaling properties of native multimodal models (NMMs) trained from the ground up on all modalities simultaneously have not been systematically characterized, leaving practitioners without compute-optimal guidance for architecture and data choices.

## Input / Output

Models consume tokenized text and continuous image patches (224×224 images with 14×14 patch size) within a 1k-token multimodal context window, producing text via next-token prediction. Early-fusion models require only a single linear projection to map image patches into the text embedding space, with no image tokenizer or discretization; late-fusion models prepend a CLIP-style vision encoder. Evaluation uses average cross-entropy loss over interleaved (Obelics), image-caption (HQITP), and text-only (DCLM) validation sets, plus downstream VQA and captioning accuracy after SFT on the LLaVA mixture.

## Method

The paper trains 457 models (0.3B–4B parameters, 250M–600B tokens) across early-fusion, late-fusion, and sparse MoE architectures, fitting the parametric scaling law L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β to derive compute-optimal parameter and token counts. Key architectural choices include SwiGLU FFNs, QK-Norm, causal attention for text and bidirectional attention for image tokens, and bfloat16 training with FSDP. For sparse models, they adopt a dropless-MoE implementation with top-1 expert-choice routing over 8 experts and an auxiliary load-balancing loss, comparing modality-agnostic (learned router) versus modality-aware (rule-based) routing. The central finding is that early-fusion matches late-fusion at equal FLOPs while requiring fewer parameters and less training memory, and that MoE sparsity induces emergent modality-specific expert specialization—particularly in early and final layers—without explicit modality conditioning.

## Results

On downstream VQA and captioning tasks (Table 4), Early-MoEs achieves the best average accuracy of 48.2%, followed by Early-fusion (47.6%) and Late-fusion (46.8%), with all models at 1.5B scale trained on 300B tokens. For captioning, Early-MoEs scores 55.7 CIDEr on TextCaps vs. 53.8 (Early-fusion) and 50.9 (Late-fusion). Scaling law analysis across 457 models shows early- and late-fusion NMMs follow nearly identical loss-compute exponents (L ∝ C^{-0.049} vs. C^{-0.0494}), but early-fusion requires a lower parameter-to-data ratio (N_opt/D_opt ∝ C^{0.053} vs. C^{0.076}), making it more parameter-efficient. Sparse MoE NMMs achieve a lower multiplicative constant (27.086 vs. 29.574) than dense models at the same active parameter count, with scaling exponent L ∝ C^{-0.047}.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three architectural choices—late-fusion (CLIP-style vision encoder + LLM decoder), early-fusion (single transformer processing raw image patches and text tokens), and sparse early-fusion with Mixture of Experts—trained natively from scratch. Early-fusion outperforms late-fusion by 0.8 points in average VQA accuracy and 2.9 CIDEr on TextCaps, with the gap being more pronounced at smaller model sizes and narrowing as parameters scale. Early-MoEs further improves over dense early-fusion by 0.6 points in average accuracy and 1.9 CIDEr on TextCaps, demonstrating that sparsity enables modality-specific specialization without increasing inference cost. Modality-agnostic MoE routing consistently outperforms modality-aware routing (which hard-assigns image/text tokens to separate FFNs) on both image-caption and interleaved data. For compute-optimal configurations, early-fusion requires significantly fewer parameters than late-fusion at the same FLOP budget (e.g., the N_opt/D_opt ratio grows as C^{0.053} vs. C^{0.076}), translating to lower inference and serving costs. Native multimodal pre-training from scratch can close the gap with LLM-initialized models within ~100B tokens on image-captioning, though interleaved and text tasks may require up to 1T tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Chinchilla-style scaling laws framework (Hoffmann et al., 2022) from text-only LLMs to native multimodal models trained from scratch on interleaved image-text data. It positions itself against the dominant late-fusion paradigm (CLIP vision encoder + LLM decoder, as in Flamingo, LLaVA, and successors) by systematically comparing it to early-fusion architectures (single transformer processing raw image patches via a linear projection, as in Chameleon and Bavishi et al.). The MoE analysis builds on dropless-MoE (Gale et al., 2023) and prior sparse scaling law work (Abnar et al., 2025), but is the first to study modality-agnostic vs. modality-aware expert routing in a native multimodal setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that early-fusion models match late-fusion performance while requiring fewer parameters, less training memory, and simpler deployment challenges the architectural default in the multimodal LLM community and could redirect significant research and engineering effort toward simpler, more efficient designs. The demonstration that modality-agnostic MoE routing implicitly learns modality-specific experts (particularly in early and late layers) suggests that explicit architectural separation of modalities may be unnecessary. Key limitations include the relatively modest scale (≤4B active parameters), reliance on validation loss as a proxy for downstream capability, and limited exploration of data mixture variations; whether these findings hold at frontier scale (100B+) remains an open question.
