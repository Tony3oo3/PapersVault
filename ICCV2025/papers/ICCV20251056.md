## Problem

Existing geometry problem solving (GPS) frameworks parse text and diagrams independently, leaving textual ambiguities—such as unspecified points, shapes, and shaded areas—unresolved in the formal language representation. These unresolved ambiguities constrain downstream theorem prediction and degrade overall solving accuracy, yet this issue has been largely overlooked in prior neural-symbolic approaches.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251056/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a geometry diagram (RGB image) and a natural-language problem statement as input. The text parser extracts propositions via regex pattern matching, while PGDPNet extracts geometric elements and relationships from the diagram. The disambiguation module resolves '$' placeholders in the formal language by referencing the diagram, and the theorem predictor outputs an ordered sequence of theorems from a predefined library, which a symbolic solver then executes to produce a numerical answer.

## Method

Pi-GPS introduces a text disambiguation module comprising a rectifier and a verifier. The rectifier uses an MLLM (e.g., GPT-4o, Gemini 2) with tailored prompts to resolve three ambiguity types—unspecified points, shapes, and areas—by conditioning on the diagram image and problem text. The verifier enforces geometric consistency through three heuristics: entity existence checking against diagram parser output, shape closure and vertex-order validation via graph connectivity, and analytical geometry verification of vertex coordinates; discrepancies trigger iterative feedback to the MLLM. For reasoning, the framework replaces traversal-based search with an LLM (o3-mini) theorem predictor that generates the correct theorem sequence from the disambiguated formal language, followed by a symbolic solver with an extended theorem library. The entire pipeline is zero-shot with respect to the disambiguation and prediction stages, requiring no annotated solving sequences.

## Results

On Geometry3K, Pi-GPS achieves 77.8% Choice accuracy and 70.6% Completion accuracy, surpassing the strongest neural-symbolic baselines GeoDRL (68.4% Choice) and E-GPS (67.9% Choice) by nearly 10% and 10% respectively. On the larger PGPS9K benchmark, Pi-GPS reaches 69.8% Choice and 61.4% Completion, improving over GeoDRL by 3.1% and 5.8% respectively. The method also reduces the average number of solving steps from 2.85–6.03 (traversal) to 2.31–4.12 (LLM-based theorem prediction), while outperforming all MLLM baselines (best: Gemini 2 at 60.7% Choice) by over 17 points on Geometry3K.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Pi-GPS outperforms all three neural-symbolic baselines: it beats GeoDRL by 9.4 points (77.8 vs 68.4 Choice) and E-GPS by 9.9 points (77.8 vs 67.9) on Geometry3K, and by 3.1/5.8 points over GeoDRL on PGPS9K. Against MLLM baselines, Pi-GPS leads by a wide margin—Gemini 2 (60.7%), GPT-4o (58.6%), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (56.4%), and Qwen-VL (26.7%) all fall 17+ points behind on Geometry3K Choice. Among pure neural methods, LANS (82.3% Choice) and PGPSNet-v2-S (76.4%) are the closest competitors on Geometry3K, but both rely on ground-truth diagram annotations or training on the larger PGPS9K set, whereas Pi-GPS requires neither. SCA-GPS (76.7%) and GOLD (62.7%) are further behind, while NGS/GeoQA (58.8%) and Geoformer (59.3%) trail significantly. Inter-GPS (57.5%) is the weakest neural-symbolic baseline, underscoring the gains from the disambiguation module and LLM-based theorem prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

Pi-GPS builds directly on the neural-symbolic GPS pipeline established by Inter-GPS, GeoDRL, and E-GPS, which parse text and diagrams into formal language and then apply theorem-based symbolic reasoning. The key departure is the identification of text ambiguity as a critical, previously overlooked bottleneck: prior methods concatenate text and diagram parsing results independently, leaving unresolved references (unspecified points, shapes, areas) that degrade downstream theorem prediction. The paper introduces a rectifier-verifier micro module that uses MLLMs to resolve these ambiguities via diagrammatic context, and replaces trained theorem predictors with a prompted o3-mini LLM, positioning the contribution as an orthogonal plug-in to existing frameworks rather than a full pipeline replacement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a seemingly minor disambiguation step yields nearly 10% improvement on Geometry3K reframes how the community should think about multimodal mathematical reasoning: cross-modal consistency checking is not a nice-to-have but a prerequisite for reliable symbolic deduction. The verifier's 4–6% contribution highlights that MLLM hallucination is a practical, measurable obstacle in formal-language generation, not just a qualitative concern. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond plane geometry (3D, non-Euclidean), whether the rule-based text parser's limitations can be addressed by learned parsers without sacrificing precision, and whether the theorem base can be expanded to cover the remaining failure categories (tangency, regular polygon properties).
