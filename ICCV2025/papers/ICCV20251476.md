## Problem

Data-driven trajectory predictors systematically underperform on rarely observed long-tail scenarios (e.g., U-turns, sudden overtakes) because training biases representations toward frequent head samples. Prior remedies modify the backbone architecture (hypernetworks, mixture-of-experts), increasing model complexity and often degrading head-sample accuracy. No prior work has demonstrated that a generative traffic simulator can be integrated into the training loop to directly mitigate this imbalance without altering the prediction model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251476/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each training sample is a full traffic scene containing N interacting agents, with past trajectories over an observation horizon T_h as input and multi-modal future trajectories over a prediction horizon T_f as output. The augmentation pipeline consumes only the past observations from identified tail scenarios and generates future trajectories via a pretrained diffusion model (LCSim); a random time-window shift then re-splits the generated trajectory so that part of the predicted future becomes new historical context, diversifying the input representation. Generated samples are zero-padded and masked for time steps beyond the original prediction horizon.

## Method

GALTraj is a generative active learning loop that, after 2/3 of standard training, dynamically identifies tail samples by thresholding per-agent minADE6 errors, then augments them using a controllable diffusion model with a tail-aware generation strategy. Agents within each tail scene are categorized into tail (high error), relevant (high attention interaction with tail agents), and head (low error) groups, each receiving a different real-guidance starting point K* = λ_type·K (λ_tail=0.25, λ_rel=0.6, λ_head=1) so that tail agents closely follow ground truth while head agents gain scene-level diversity. Gradient-based guidance (no-off-road and collision-avoidance constraints) is applied to head agents to ensure traffic-rule compliance. A random time-window shift and sampling-weight decay on previously seen data mitigate overfitting, and the loop iterates: mine tail samples → generate → update dataset → post-train.

## Results

On WOMD with QCNet, GALTraj reduces FPR₅ from 0.42 (Vanilla) to 0.22, nearly halving the false prediction ratio, while also improving Top 1% error from 4.81 to 3.43 and VaR₉₉₉ from 8.42 to 6.05. On Argoverse2, GALTraj achieves FPR₅ = 0.19 (vs. 0.35 Vanilla), Top 1% = 3.76 (vs. 4.47), and VaR₉₉₉ = 5.66 (vs. 7.22). Crucially, GALTraj also improves the overall minFDE₆ metric (0.558 on WOMD, 0.524 on AV2), whereas resampling and cRT baselines sometimes degrade overall accuracy. The method generalizes to the MTR backbone, where it reduces FPR₅ from 0.99 to 0.65 on WOMD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GALTraj outperforms all three baselines on every long-tail and overall metric across both WOMD and Argoverse2. The strongest baseline is the contrastive loss method [50], which improves FPR₅ to 0.31 on WOMD and 0.23 on AV2, but GALTraj still leads by 0.09 and 0.04 respectively, and by a wider margin on Top 1% (3.43 vs. 4.12 on WOMD) and minFDE₆ (0.558 vs. 0.613). Resampling [66] provides modest long-tail gains (FPR₅: 0.38 on WOMD) but actually worsens overall minFDE₆ (0.668 vs. 0.654 Vanilla), indicating overfitting to tail samples at the expense of head performance. cRT [35] (decoupled encoder/decoder training) shows the weakest results, with FPR₅ of 0.43 on WOMD—worse than Vanilla—suggesting that simply retraining the decoder on balanced data is insufficient for trajectory prediction. The Naive ablation (generative augmentation without tail-aware guidance) confirms that the specific guidance strategy is critical: it achieves only FPR₅ = 0.38 on WOMD, far below GALTraj's 0.22.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of long-tail trajectory prediction work (Makansi et al. 2021, FEND 2023, AMEND 2024), which addresses the problem by modifying model architectures with hypernetworks or mixture-of-experts modules. Instead, it reframes the challenge as a training-procedure problem, borrowing the generative active learning paradigm from image classification (Huang et al. 2024, Kong et al. 2019) and adapting it to multi-agent regression via a controllable diffusion traffic simulator (LCSim). The tail-aware generation design—categorizing agents into tail, head, and relevant groups and applying differentiated real-guidance and gradient-guidance levels—draws on real guidance for diffusion (He et al. 2023) and gradient-based constraint enforcement (Zhong et al. 2023), but their combination for preserving tail semantics while ensuring scene-level diversity and traffic-rule compliance is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that simulator-driven data augmentation can meaningfully improve long-tail trajectory prediction without architectural changes, offering a lower-complexity alternative to hypernetwork-based approaches that risk degrading head-sample performance. The tail-aware generation strategy (agent categorization + tiered guidance) is a reusable design principle for any generative augmentation in multi-agent settings. Limitations include the offline-only nature of the method, up to 36% additional training overhead from diffusion sampling, and validation on only two datasets and two backbones; whether the approach extends to motion planning or other long-tail regression tasks remains open.
