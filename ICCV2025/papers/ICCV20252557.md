## Problem

Current multimodal large language models excel at short video understanding (typically under one minute) but face substantial challenges with multi-hour videos required by real-world applications like embodied intelligence, movie reviews, and live sports commentary. Existing video benchmarks focus on short clips with average durations of only ~10 minutes, leaving a critical gap in evaluating long-term temporal comprehension and memory capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252557/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes multi-hour YouTube videos (minimum 30 minutes, averaging ~68 minutes) across six domains (sports, documentaries, event records, lifestyle, TV shows, cartoons) and pairs them with 1,549 multiple-choice questions (4 options each) spanning six core capabilities. Videos are fed at 1 FPS, and models must output a single letter (A–D) per question. Each question is annotated with a 'clue duration' marking the minimum video segment needed to answer it, and all questions are verified to require visual grounding (not solvable by text alone).

## Method

LVBench is constructed through a systematic three-stage annotation protocol: (1) full-video analysis to mark events, transitions, and entities; (2) question generation averaging 24 questions per hour, strategically distributed across the timeline with emphasis on specificity to ensure unique referents; and (3) answer construction with carefully designed distractors and temporal clue annotations. A six-capability taxonomy (Temporal Grounding, Summarization, Reasoning, Entity Recognition, Event Understanding, Key Information Retrieval) allows compositional evaluation of complex reasoning. Quality control includes minimizing explicit temporal grounding to avoid unfairly advantaging models with temporal perception, and an LLM-based filtering step (using GLM-4 and GPT-4) that removes any question both models can answer correctly without video input. The paper then evaluates 26 models (13 non-native, 13 native long-video) and conducts a frame-density ablation (0 to 1 FPS) demonstrating that dense visual input is essential for resolving long-range temporal dependencies.

## Results

On LVBench (1,549 QA pairs over 103 videos averaging 68 minutes), Gemini-2.5-Pro achieves the highest overall accuracy of 67.4%, outperforming all other models in 5 of 6 capability categories (EU 67.5%, KIR 72.8%, TG 65.9%, Rea 66.5%, Sum 58.6%). Seed1.5-VL-Thinking ranks second at 64.6% overall, while the best open-source models—VideoLLaMA3-7B (45.3%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (44.0%)—trail the SOTA by over 20 percentage points. Human evaluators achieve 94.4% accuracy, revealing a substantial 27-point gap between the best model and human-level long video understanding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-2.5-Pro (67.4%) leads all 26 evaluated models, surpassing Seed1.5-VL-Thinking (64.6%) by 2.8 points and MR.Video (60.8%) by 6.6 points on overall accuracy. Among proprietary models, GPT-4.1 (60.1%) and GPT-4o (48.9%) trail Gemini-2.5-Pro by 7.3 and 18.5 points respectively, with GPT-4o also underperforming on Temporal Grounding (40.9%) and Key Information Retrieval (48.1%). The best open-source models—VideoLLaMA3-7B (45.3%) and Qwen2.5-VL-72B (44.0%)—lag behind Gemini-2.5-Pro by over 20 points, confirming a significant proprietary-to-open-source gap. Notably, several models explicitly designed for native long-video processing underperform: MovieChat (22.5%), LLaMA-VID (23.9%), LWM (25.5%), and Gemini-1.5-Pro (33.1%) all score below even short-video models like LLaVA-NeXT (32.2%), which the authors attribute to poor instruction-following on long inputs (e.g., Gemini-1.5-Pro generated out-of-options responses 20.9% of the time). Across video categories, Gemini-2.5-Pro excels on Event Record (72.1%) but drops to 54.3% on Documentaries, while Seed1.5-VL shows more consistent performance (60.3–67.5%) across all six categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

LVBench extends the video question-answering benchmarking line (Video-MME, LongVideoBench, MovieChat-1K, MoVQA) by pushing average video duration to ~68 minutes—roughly four times longer than prior benchmarks—and by introducing a systematic six-capability taxonomy (temporal grounding, summarization, reasoning, entity recognition, event understanding, key information retrieval) that composes into multi-faceted questions. Rather than building on a specific architectural method, the paper repurposes the existing MLLM evaluation paradigm (multiple-choice QA over sampled frames) and applies it to a substantially harder temporal regime, while adding a rigorous LLM-based filtering step (GLM-4 + GPT-4) to eliminate questions solvable without visual input. The benchmark is positioned as filling a gap the authors identify: prior long-video benchmarks still average around 10 minutes and lack the structured capability decomposition needed to diagnose *where* models fail on extended temporal contexts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 27-percentage-point gap between the best model (Gemini-2.5-Pro at 67.4%) and human performance (94.4%) underscores that long video understanding remains far from solved, and the finding that native long-video architectures (MovieChat, LWM, Gemini-1.5-Pro) underperform even short-video models due to instruction-following failures challenges the assumption that architectural long-context support is sufficient. The frame-density ablation showing a large performance jump from 50 frames to 1 FPS highlights a practical bottleneck: dense visual input is necessary but computationally expensive, raising open questions about efficient long-video tokenization. The exclusion of audio is an acknowledged limitation that will constrain the benchmark's applicability to real-world scenarios where sound carries critical information.
