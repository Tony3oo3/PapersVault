## Problem

RayGauss achieves state-of-the-art rendering quality for novel view synthesis by representing scenes with irregularly distributed elliptical Gaussian primitives rendered via volumetric ray marching over a BVH, but its computational cost (e.g., 0.5 FPS on Mip-NeRF360) makes real-time rendering on real-world scenes impractical. Additionally, its densification criterion is biased toward camera-near Gaussians, degrading quality in outdoor and large-scale scenes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a calibrated set of RGB images with known camera intrinsics and extrinsics (standard NVS setup) and renders novel views at the test-set resolution (e.g., 1297×840 for Mip-NeRF360). Rendering is performed via hardware-accelerated ray tracing through the OptiX API, where each pixel casts a ray that is marched through the BVH of Gaussian primitives in segments of fixed sample count N_S, with segment size adaptively scaled by distance and transmittance on real-world datasets.

## Method

RayGaussX accelerates RayGauss's volumetric ray marching through five complementary mechanisms: (1) empty-space skipping that alternates BVH ClosestHit queries with AnyHit segment traversal to jump over zero-density regions; (2) adaptive segment sizing ΔS_i = N_S·min(max(d_i/β, Δt_min)·T_i^(−1/3), Δt_max) that coarsens sampling in distant or occluded regions; (3) Z-order curve reordering of Gaussian parameters in memory plus tile-based ray grouping to improve GPU memory coalescing and reduce warp divergence; (4) an isotropic loss L_s that penalizes the worst-case AABB-to-ellipsoid volume ratio, reducing false-positive intersection calls in the BVH; and (5) a distance-corrected densification criterion that weights 3D-space gradients by α_i = ‖μ_l − o_i‖/f, enabling uniform densification of distant Gaussians and improving outdoor scene quality by up to +0.56 dB PSNR. Together these yield 5–12× faster training and 50–80× higher rendering FPS on real-world datasets while matching or exceeding RayGauss's visual quality.

## Results

On real-world datasets, RayGaussX achieves 5–12× faster training and 50–80× higher rendering speed (FPS) compared to RayGauss, while improving PSNR by up to +0.56 dB (Tanks&Temples: 23.76 vs 23.20). On Mip-NeRF360, RayGaussX reaches 28.43 PSNR at 24.1 FPS, outperforming Spec-Gaussian (28.05 PSNR, 42 FPS) by +0.38 dB while remaining within 2× of its rendering speed. On synthetic datasets (NeRF, NSVF), RayGaussX matches or slightly exceeds RayGauss quality (34.54 vs 34.53 PSNR on NeRF Synthetic) while tripling rendering speed (85 vs 25 FPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against its direct predecessor RayGauss, RayGaussX wins on every metric across all three real-world datasets: training drops from 456→85 min (Mip-NeRF360), 565→47 min (Tanks&Temples), and 553→56 min (Deep Blending), while FPS jumps from 0.5→24.1, 0.5→41.1, and 0.8→39.6 respectively, with PSNR gains of +0.20, +0.56, and +0.02 dB. Compared to the highest-quality baseline Zip-NeRF, RayGaussX is 100–200× faster in rendering (24.1 vs 0.1 FPS on Mip-NeRF360) at only a −0.13 dB PSNR cost, and dramatically outperforms it on Tanks&Temples (23.76 vs 10.85 PSNR). Against the fastest rasterization-based methods, RayGaussX trades speed for quality: it is 6.7× slower than 3D-GS (24.1 vs 161 FPS) but achieves +0.63 dB higher PSNR on Mip-NeRF360, and is 1.7× slower than Spec-Gaussian (24.1 vs 42 FPS) while gaining +0.38 dB PSNR. On the NSVF synthetic dataset, RayGaussX achieves the best overall quality (38.75 PSNR, 0.991 SSIM, 0.010 LPIPS) at 71 FPS, surpassing RayGauss (38.72 PSNR, 22 FPS) and Spec-Gaussian (37.97 PSNR, 102 FPS). The new densification criterion is the primary driver of quality gains on outdoor scenes (Mip-NeRF360, Tanks&Temples), while empty-space skipping, adaptive sampling, and the isotropic loss primarily contribute to the large speedups.

## Relation to Prior Work

RayGaussX is a direct acceleration of RayGauss [5], which itself introduced volumetric ray marching over irregularly distributed elliptical (Gaussian) primitives organized in a BVH for novel view synthesis. The paper repurposes classic volumetric rendering acceleration strategies—empty-space skipping and adaptive sampling—that were previously applied to NeRF-style neural radiance fields but had not been adapted to the segment-based BVH traversal of Gaussian ray casting. It further draws on GPU systems techniques (Z-order curve reordering for memory coalescing, tile-based ray grouping to reduce warp divergence) and adapts the 3D Gaussian Splatting densification heuristic [16] to the volumetric setting by introducing a distance-corrective gradient weighting. The isotropic loss constraining the AABB-to-ellipsoid volume ratio is a new regularization specific to this BVH-based intersection pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 50–80× rendering speedup (from ~0.5 FPS to 24–41 FPS) and 5–12× training speedup make volumetric Gaussian ray marching practically viable for real-time and near-real-time applications, closing a large gap with rasterization-based methods like 3D-GS while retaining the artifact-free quality of volume rendering. The ablation study clearly isolates each contribution's impact, and the densification fix for distant Gaussians addresses a concrete weakness of RayGauss on outdoor scenes. Open questions include whether the fixed isotropic-loss threshold and adaptive-sampling hyperparameters generalize across scene scales, whether the approach extends to dynamic or multi-material scenes, and whether further speedups can be achieved without the small PSNR cost of the isotropic constraint.
