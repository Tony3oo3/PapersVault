## Problem

Existing anime video generation methods treat animation as a monolithic data domain and provide only frame-level controls, overlooking the fundamental layer decomposition principle that underpins professional animation production. This limits fine-grained manipulation of individual elements (e.g., controlling a character's motion independently from background effects). Compounding this, professional layer data is commercially sensitive and scarce, making it difficult to train models that support layer-level conditioning.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250005/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a single reference image (320×512), binary layer masks (up to N=4 layers, derived only from the reference frame), heterogeneous layer-level control signals (scalar motion scores, 3-channel trajectory maps combining Gaussian heatmap and normalized offset, or layer-cropped sketches), and a text prompt. It outputs a 16-frame animation video at 320×512 resolution. Notably, different layers can simultaneously receive different control modalities, and layer masks require no per-frame annotation—only the reference frame's decomposition is needed.

## Method

LayerAnimate introduces a layer-aware video diffusion architecture built on ToonCrafter's UNet. A data curation pipeline first extracts layers via iterative SAM/SAM2 segmentation followed by Motion-based Hierarchical Merging, which uses optical-flow motion scores and hierarchical clustering to merge over-segmented elements into semantically coherent layers. At inference, the reference image is decomposed into layer regions, and Motion-based Assignment replicates static layers across all frames while zeroing dynamic layers, establishing temporal structure. Each layer's control signal is encoded by a dedicated encoder, processed independently through ControlNet branches (treating each layer as a separate sample), and fused back into the UNet via cross-attention where frame-level features act as queries and layer features as keys/values. Training fine-tunes only the layer encoder, control encoders, and spatial layers with random control modality selection (20% motion score, 40% trajectory, 40% sketch) and 10% mask dropout for robustness.

## Results

LayerAnimate achieves the best FVD across all six evaluated tasks on the anime evaluation set (1K clips). The most substantial gains appear in I2V with trajectory (FVD 72.04 vs. 190.61 for Tora) and interpolation with sketch (FVD 15.63 vs. 66.26 for ToonCrafter), where layer-level control provides a clear advantage over frame-level methods. In standard I2V, LayerAnimate reaches FVD 87.96 and PSNR 15.45, outperforming DynamiCrafter (FVD 114.80) and CogVideoX (FVD 170.31). For interpolation with sketch, LayerAnimate attains FID 3.23 and SSIM 0.908, substantially exceeding ToonCrafter (FID 8.40, SSIM 0.794).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LayerAnimate wins on FVD in all six tasks. In I2V, it improves FVD by 23% over DynamiCrafter (87.96 vs. 114.80) and 48% over CogVideoX (170.31), though DynamiCrafter retains a marginal FID edge (14.36 vs. 14.66). For I2V with trajectory, the advantage is dramatic: FVD drops from 190.61 (Tora) and 300.51 (DragAnything) to 72.04, with PSNR jumping from 15.32/14.00 to 17.46. In I2V with sketch, LayerAnimate (FVD 26.64, FID 5.92) edges out LVCD (FVD 29.85, FID 7.01) and AniDoc (FVD 42.26), though LVCD retains slightly higher PSNR (26.22 vs. 25.71). For interpolation, LayerAnimate (FVD 59.64) beats ToonCrafter (74.63) by 20% and CogVideoX (88.28) by 32%. The largest relative gain appears in interpolation with sketch, where FVD drops from 66.26 (ToonCrafter) to 15.63 (a 76% reduction) and SSIM rises from 0.794 to 0.908, indicating substantially better structural fidelity in fade-in and complex motion scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

LayerAnimate extends the video diffusion + ControlNet paradigm (as in ToonCrafter, DynamiCrafter, and SparseCtrl) by shifting the unit of control from the full frame to individual animation layers, a concept rooted in traditional cel-animation production but previously unexploited in generative models. It leverages SAM/SAM2 for automated element segmentation and introduces a motion-based hierarchical merging step to construct layer annotations from raw animation clips, addressing the data scarcity that has prevented prior work from operating at this granularity. The architecture composes per-layer ControlNet branches with cross-attention fusion into a shared UNet, building on the multi-branch control design of ControlNet while generalizing it from a single global condition to N independent layer conditions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By formalizing layer-level control, the paper provides a practical bridge between the non-destructive, layer-isolated editing workflow animators already use and modern generative video tools, enabling composite controls (e.g., sketch on a character, trajectory on a background, motion score on particles) that frame-level methods cannot express. The automated curation pipeline (SAM → SAM2 → hierarchical merging) is a reusable recipe for constructing layer-annotated datasets from commercially sensitive animation assets. Limitations include the fixed N=4 layer capacity, 16-frame / 320×512 resolution, and anime-only training domain; open questions include whether the layer-decomposition principle transfers to real-world video (where depth cues exist) and whether the approach scales to longer sequences or more complex layer hierarchies.
