## Problem

Visual localization systems from different vendors use incompatible feature extraction algorithms (e.g., SIFT vs. SuperPoint), making cross-device collaboration and shared-map interoperability infeasible. The only prior approach, Cross-Descriptor, assumes all features share the same keypoint detector, which is unrealistic when devices employ fundamentally different detectors (blob vs. corner vs. learned). This paper tackles the more general Cross-Feature problem: establishing valid correspondences between features extracted by entirely different algorithms without access to original images.

## Input / Output

The method takes local feature descriptors of varying dimensions (128-d SIFT, 64-d ORB, 256-d SuperPoint, 128-d DISK) along with their 2D keypoint positions, and projects each into a shared 128-dim L2-normalized embedding space via a per-algorithm MLP. During training, descriptors are placed into H×W×128 feature maps within a 15-pixel patch around each keypoint rather than at a single pixel, creating the overlap needed to relate heterogeneous features. At inference, the projected embeddings enable mutual nearest-neighbor matching across algorithms, feeding into standard PnP-based 6-DoF pose estimation against a 3D map whose points also carry projected descriptors.

## Method

The core contribution is a patch-based training strategy that solves the heterogeneous correspondence problem: since keypoints from different detectors are spatially misaligned, each descriptor is stamped into a T_w=15 pixel patch on a dense feature map, and overlapping regions between two feature maps (from different algorithms or views) define positive/negative training pairs. A differentiable Average Precision loss is computed over three loss types—same-image/different-algorithm (SD), different-image/same-algorithm (DS), and different-image/different-algorithm (DD)—to jointly enforce cross-feature similarity and robustness to viewpoint change. The encoder is a lightweight 2-layer MLP (1024 hidden units, ReLU + BatchNorm) per algorithm, trained for 30 epochs on R2D2-style transformed Aachen Day-Night pairs with optical-flow ground-truth correspondences. To support four algorithms in one shared space, the authors first train SIFT–SuperPoint jointly, then freeze the SIFT encoder and train ORB and DISK against it, accepting a suboptimal but unified embedding. A last-served aggregation rule handles overlapping patches within a single feature map, trading some information for a 30–50× reduction in training cost.

## Results

On the Aachen Day & Night benchmark (heterogeneous features, 0.5 m / 5° threshold, Day), Cross-Feature (CF^emb) achieves 66.4% localized queries when querying a SIFT map with SuperPoint, compared to 28.2% for Cross-Descriptor and 43.6% for image reconstruction. On the collaborative mapping scenario (Cambridge Landmarks, merged SIFT+SuperPoint submap), CF^emb yields median localization errors of 0.18–0.37 m across all four query features (SIFT, SuperPoint, ORB, DISK), while Cross-Descriptor fails entirely (N/A) when the query uses a different detector. On 7Scenes, CF^emb reports median pose errors of 2.74–3.69 cm (SIFT map, SuperPoint/ORB query) versus N/A for Cross-Descriptor in most scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Cross-Feature (CF^emb) substantially outperforms Cross-Descriptor (CD^emb) in all heterogeneous feature settings: on Aachen (SIFT map → SuperPoint query, Day, 0.5 m/5°) CF^emb reaches 66.4% vs. 28.2% for CD^emb, and on Cambridge Landmarks CD^emb fails to localize (N/A) in most scenes when the query uses a different detector, while CF^emb reports median errors of 0.05–0.20 m. Against the image-reconstruction baseline (Im. Reconstr. [32]), CF^emb wins when SIFT is the query feature (66.4% vs. 43.6% on Aachen SIFT map, Day) but loses when SuperPoint is the query (49.3% vs. 85.7% on Aachen SuperPoint map), as the reconstruction network preserves learned-descriptor information better than handcrafted SIFT descriptors. GoMatch, a geometry-only method, is dramatically outperformed in every heterogeneous case (e.g., 66.4% vs. 0.2% on Aachen), confirming that keypoint-position-only matching is insufficient for cross-feature localization. In the novel collaborative mapping scenario (Table 4), CF^emb enables all four query features to achieve comparable median errors (0.18–0.37 m), whereas CD^emb degrades sharply with DoG:BRIEF and fails completely with OrFAST:BRIEF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Cross-Descriptor (Dusmanu et al., ICCV 2021), which projected heterogeneous descriptors into a shared embedding space via a 2-layer MLP but assumed all features shared the same keypoint detector. The key departure is a patch-based training strategy that generates correspondences through spatial overlap of feature maps, addressing the heterogeneous correspondence problem that Cross-Descriptor could not handle. The method retains the same lightweight MLP architecture and 128-dim L2-normalized embedding space, but replaces the decoder-based training with a differentiable Average Precision loss computed over three loss types (same/different image × same/different algorithm). It also positions itself against geometry-only methods (GoMatch) and image reconstruction approaches as alternative interoperability strategies, showing their limitations in the heterogeneous setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstrated solution to the Cross-Feature matching problem, opening a path toward true visual localization interoperability where devices from different vendors can share maps without re-extracting features or transmitting images. However, the performance gap to homogeneous localization remains substantial (e.g., 56.3% vs. 80.8% on Aachen Day), and the high outlier rate in heterogeneous matching is acknowledged as a major unresolved concern. Open questions include scalable training beyond algorithm pairs, better handling of many-to-many keypoint associations, merging heterogeneous 3D submaps, and whether global context or end-to-end matchers can close the performance gap. The collaborative mapping scenario, while novel, relies on simple submap stacking without merging, limiting its practical readiness.
