## Problem

Vanilla diffusion models are trained on clean or mildly degraded images, creating a distribution mismatch with the moderately-to-severely degraded faces encountered in blind face restoration (BFR). Additionally, synthetic degradation models used to generate training pairs rely on limited, known degradation patterns and fail to capture the complex, unknown degradations present in real-world images. Previous work addresses these gaps with pre-processing modules that smooth away fine facial details or with two-stage pipelines that couple restoration to auxiliary models, neither of which fully unleashes the generative prior of a large-scale T2I model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251232/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

In restoration mode, the model takes a 512×512 LQ face image as condition, a text prompt (generated by BLIP), and BFR-oriented face embeddings extracted from a dedicated autoencoder, producing a restored HQ face image. In degradation mode, the same network simply swaps the roles of HQ and LQ images (LQ becomes the denoising target, HQ becomes the condition) and outputs a degraded image carrying diverse, unknown real-world degradations. The two modes share identical architecture and LoRA weights; switching is achieved purely by reordering which image serves as input versus condition.

## Method

FLIPNET fine-tunes Stable Diffusion 2.1-base via LoRA (rank 64) on all self- and cross-attention layers, keeping the base model frozen to preserve its generative prior. In restoration mode, HQ and LQ images are independently noised with separate timesteps and concatenated along the batch axis; a BoostHub module—placed in parallel to each self-attention layer—performs cross-attention with queries from HQ features and keys/values from LQ features, selectively injecting BFR-oriented structural and color cues while discarding degradation artifacts, with an enhance weight φ controlling the injection strength. BFR-oriented face embeddings are obtained from a two-stage autoencoder: a reconstruction stage (L1 + perceptual + adversarial loss) followed by a CLIP-style association stage that aligns HQ and LQ latents into a common space, ensuring the embeddings are not biased toward either quality level when integrated via cross-attention. In degradation mode, the model is trained on paired real-world degradation datasets (Dense-Haze, LOL, SIDD, RealBlur) plus on-the-fly Real-ESRGAN degradations of FFHQ, learning the distribution of complex real-world degradations that can then be used to synthesize more authentic training pairs for restoration mode.

## Results

On CelebA-Test, FLIPNET achieves FID of 13.82 (on-the-fly degradation) and 15.06 (dual degradation), with the best identity preservation (Deg. 43.29/43.81) among all diffusion-prior methods, while matching PMRF's FID (13.48) within 0.34. On real-world datasets, FLIPNET attains FID of 42.98 (LFW), 81.37 (WebPhoto), and 31.81 (WIDER), outperforming all baselines on every dataset. The method also achieves the highest SSIM (0.64) and competitive PSNR (24.44) on the synthetic benchmark, demonstrating a strong fidelity–authenticity balance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLIPNET is compared against 9 baselines spanning GAN-based (GPEN, GFP-GAN, RF++, DAEFR) and diffusion-prior (DR2, PGDiff, DiffBIR, WaveFace, PMRF) methods. Against the strongest diffusion-prior competitor PMRF, FLIPNET trades a small PSNR gap (24.44 vs 24.70) and FID gap (13.82 vs 13.48) for superior identity preservation (Deg. 43.29 vs 44.75) and higher SSIM (0.64 vs 0.63). Against DiffBIR and WaveFace, FLIPNET substantially outperforms on FID (13.82 vs 20.78 and 21.44) and identity consistency (Deg. 43.29 vs 46.58 and 43.80), confirming that pre-processing modules and frequency-concatenation strategies compromise restoration quality. On real-world datasets, FLIPNET achieves the best FID on all three benchmarks—most notably on WIDER (31.81 vs 35.34 for DiffBIR and 36.91 for WaveFace)—demonstrating that learning real-world degradation distributions via the degradation mode is critical for handling unknown, complex degradations. GAN-based methods (GPEN, GFP-GAN) are consistently outperformed by 5–10 FID points, highlighting the advantage of diffusion priors over StyleGAN-based restoration.

## Relation to Prior Work

FLIPNET builds directly on Stable Diffusion's latent diffusion framework, adapting it for blind face restoration via LoRA fine-tuning of attention layers. It departs from prior diffusion-based BFR methods (DiffBIR, PMRF, WaveFace) that rely on pre-processing modules or simple LQ-to-HQ concatenation, instead conditioning on jointly diffused LQ images and introducing BoostHub—a cross-attention module inspired by joint conditioning in T2I generation—to selectively integrate BFR-oriented features. The BFR-oriented face embeddings represent a conceptual departure from the standard practice of using ArcFace recognition embeddings for identity conditioning, motivated by the observation that recognition-trained embeddings over-focus on discriminative regions rather than holistic appearance. The dual-mode design (restoration/degradation via input flipping) unifies two previously separate concerns—faithful restoration and realistic degradation synthesis—into a single parameter-efficient model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap that limits real-world deployment of diffusion-based BFR: synthetic degradation models (e.g., Real-ESRGAN) produce limited degradation patterns that fail to capture the complexity of in-the-wild images. By training a degradation mode on diverse real-world low-level vision datasets (haze, low-light, noise, blur), FLIPNET generates training data whose distribution better matches real-world conditions, potentially improving generalization. However, the approach remains scoped to face restoration at 512×512 resolution, and the degradation mode borrows from non-face datasets, raising questions about whether the learned degradation distribution truly transfers to facial imagery. Open questions include whether the dual-mode paradigm generalizes to other restoration tasks (e.g., general image restoration, video) and whether the LoRA-based adaptation can be extended to larger or newer diffusion backbones without losing the base model's generative prior.
