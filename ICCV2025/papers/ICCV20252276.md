## Problem

Streaming video understanding has largely overlooked the interaction characteristic where users communicate instructions through visual modality (gestures, body language) rather than text or speech. Existing streaming LMMs adopt turn-taking chat templates that prevent mid-response interruption and cannot proactively respond to visual cues like waving, pointing, or stop gestures, leaving a critical gap in real-time human-agent interaction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252276/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes streaming video at 1 fps (64 tokens per frame after downsampling) and 1-second audio snippets, segmented with <seg> tokens, alongside user text/speech input. Crucially, the two-stream template feeds both user inputs and the model's own historical outputs as simultaneous inputs, enabling the model to adjust generation based on upcoming user signals. Outputs are prefixed with modality-specific tokens (← text, → audio, ↓ visual), and for visual interruption the model emits '↓ Stop!' at a <seg> boundary to halt generation entirely.

## Method

ViSpeak is finetuned from VITA 1.5 (Qwen2-7B + InternViT-300M + 341M audio encoder) via a three-stage recipe. Stage 1 (template alignment) adapts the offline model to a two-stream chat template inspired by Moshi, where user and agent streams are combined via an adaptive weighted sum before feeding the LLM, preserving offline capabilities on 2.0M samples. Stage 2 (streaming finetuning) trains on 657K timestamp-annotated samples to build streaming QA and proactive output, introducing a binary informative head that predicts when to speak based on the last visual token in each segment. Stage 3 finetunes on the 34K-sample ViSpeak-Instruct dataset covering seven visual instruction subtasks. The key architectural insight is that the two-stream template allows the model to process new user inputs (e.g., a stop gesture) while still generating, enabling true interruption without a separate control module.

## Results

On the proposed ViSpeak-Bench, ViSpeak (stage 3) achieves an overall score of 2.76 (Time Accuracy 80.42%, Text Score 3.25), outperforming all open-source models and approaching GPT-4o (2.99) and human performance (3.69). On StreamingBench, ViSpeak achieves a SOTA overall score of 62.00 (stage 2) / 62.58 (stage 3) among open-source models, surpassing GPT-4o (49.06) and the previous best open-source model Dispider (53.12). On OVO-Bench, ViSpeak scores 61.08 overall, exceeding GPT-4o (58.58) and all other open-source models, though still below Gemini 1.5 Pro (65.25).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ViSpeak outperforms all open-source baselines on ViSpeak-Bench, achieving the highest overall score (2.76) among open models, beating Qwen2.5-VL 72B (2.62), Gemini 1.5 Pro (2.19), and GPT-4o (2.99) is still slightly ahead. On StreamingBench, ViSpeak (62.00/62.58) surpasses GPT-4o (49.06) by ~13 points and the previous best open-source model Dispider (53.12) by ~9 points, though Gemini 1.5 Pro (70.26) remains higher. On OVO-Bench, ViSpeak (61.08) exceeds GPT-4o (58.58) and all open-source models, but trails Gemini 1.5 Pro (65.25). Among streaming-specific models, ViSpeak dramatically outperforms Flash-VStream (24.04 on StreamingBench) and VideoLLM-online (32.48), which struggle with time-sensitive and proactive output tasks. The paper notes that existing streaming models (Flash-VStream, Dispider) underperform even offline models like Qwen2.5-VL on ViSpeak-Bench, likely due to aggressive speaking or inability to follow conversational instructions without explicit prompts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on VITA 1.5, an omni-modal LMM, and adapts it to streaming video understanding through a three-stage finetuning recipe. The two-stream chat template is inspired by Moshi's speech-text dialogue architecture, repurposing the idea of parallel input/output streams from the speech domain to video. The informative head for proactive visual output follows MMDuet's design, while the broader streaming video understanding lineage (VideoLLM-online, Flash-VStream, Dispider) provides the evaluation context. The key conceptual departure is shifting focus from audio/text instructions to visual body-language instructions as the trigger for agent responses in streaming conversations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete architectural limitation—turn-taking chat templates prevent models from handling visual interruption—that is likely to influence how future streaming LMMs are designed. ViSpeak-Bench is the first comprehensive benchmark for visual instruction feedback, filling a gap where no prior evaluation existed for this interaction mode. However, the scope is limited to conversational scenarios with seven predefined subtasks, sign language is explicitly excluded, and audio proactive output is not addressed. Open questions include whether the two-stream template generalizes to more complex multi-party interactions, whether the informative head approach scales to open-ended visual instruction types beyond the curated subtasks, and whether the 7B model's performance gap versus GPT-4o on ViSpeak-Bench (2.76 vs 2.99 overall) narrows with scale.
