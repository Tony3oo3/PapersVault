## Problem

Existing RGB-IR cross-modality object detection methods fuse features using CNN or Transformer modules without exploiting the distinct frequency-domain characteristics of each modality. Statistical analysis reveals that IR images carry richer low-frequency information while RGB images carry richer high-frequency details, yet prior fusion approaches either treat all frequency components identically or ignore high-frequency components entirely, leaving complementary information underutilized.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252373/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of spatially aligned RGB and infrared images as input, processed through a dual-stream backbone (ResNet50, YOLOv5, or YOLOv8) that extracts features at multiple levels. DWT is applied at the 2nd, 3rd, and 5th backbone layers to decompose features into wavelet sub-bands before fusion. The output is standard object detection results—bounding boxes with class labels—produced by an improved YOLOv8 head that replaces conventional upsampling with IDWT to reconstruct spatial features from the fused wavelet-domain representations.

## Method

WaveMamba introduces the WaveMamba Fusion Block (WMFB), which decomposes dual-stream features via Haar DWT into low-frequency (LL) and high-frequency (LH, HL, HH) sub-bands, then fuses them with modality-specific strategies. For low-frequency fusion, the Low-frequency Mamba Fusion Block (LMFB) first performs shallow cross-modality interaction via channel swapping followed by VSS (Visual State Space) blocks, then deepens fusion through a gated attention mechanism where the primary modality's second stream regulates both its own first stream and the auxiliary modality's output, leveraging SS2D for long-range dependency modeling. For high-frequency fusion, a High-Frequency Enhancement (HFE) strategy applies an 'absolute maximum' selection, retaining pixels with larger absolute values from either modality to preserve critical edge and texture details without additional parameters. The improved YOLOv8 detection head uses IDWT to upsample fused low-frequency features alongside high-frequency sub-bands, reducing information loss compared to standard interpolation-based upsampling.

## Results

On the M³FD dataset, WaveMamba with YOLOv8 backbone achieves 92.1% mAP50 and 64.4% mAP, surpassing the second-best method (MRD-YOLO, 86.6% mAP50) by 5.5%. On LLVIP, it reaches 98.3% mAP50 and 66.0% mAP, and on FLIR-Aligned it attains 88.4% mAP50 and 48.1% mAP while reducing parameters to 69.1M (vs. 80.2M for ESSFN) and inference time to 40.0 ms. Across all four main benchmarks, the method achieves an average mAP improvement of 4.5% over the second-best approach, with consistent gains across ResNet50, YOLOv5, and YOLOv8 backbones.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WaveMamba consistently outperforms all 45 compared baselines across every dataset and backbone configuration, with no losses reported. On M³FD (YOLOv8), it leads MRD-YOLO [40] by 5.5% mAP50 (92.1 vs. 86.6) and RI-YOLO [4] by 8.5% mAP50; on the YOLOv5 backbone it surpasses MMFN [67] by 5.3% mAP50 (91.5 vs. 86.2). On DroneVehicle (YOLOv8), it beats CMA [18] by 3.0% mAP50 (79.8 vs. 76.8) and IV-YOLO [49] by 5.2% mAP50. On LLVIP (YOLOv8), the margin is tighter—1.2% mAP50 over FAWDet [78] (98.3 vs. 97.1)—but the mAP gap widens to 3.9% (66.0 vs. 62.1). On FLIR-Aligned (YOLOv8), it exceeds ESSFN [64] by 7.6% mAP50 (88.4 vs. 80.8) while using fewer parameters (69.1M vs. 80.2M) and faster inference (40.0 ms vs. 47.0 ms), and outperforms CrossFormer [22] by 6.5% mAP50 with 294.4M fewer parameters. The single-modal YOLOv8l baselines (IR: 79.5/80.9 mAP50; RGB: 80.9/70.2 mAP50 on M³FD/DroneVehicle) are surpassed by 11–12% mAP50, confirming the value of the cross-modality fusion.

## Relation to Prior Work

WaveMamba sits at the intersection of two active research threads: Mamba-based visual state space models (building on VMamba's VSS blocks and SS2D mechanism, and Mamba's S6 selective scan) and wavelet-transform-based feature processing in computer vision. It departs from prior wavelet-in-CV work (WTConv, WF-Diff, DCFNet) by explicitly separating low- and high-frequency sub-bands and applying fundamentally different fusion strategies to each—Mamba-based deep fusion for low-frequency and an absolute-maximum selection for high-frequency—rather than treating all frequency components uniformly. The channel-swapping operation borrowed from PanMamba and the gated attention mechanism in the DFM module represent a novel combination not seen in prior RGB-IR fusion literature, which has predominantly relied on CNN or Transformer fusion modules.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a clear, well-motivated frequency-domain principle for RGB-IR fusion (IR dominates low-frequency, RGB dominates high-frequency) and demonstrates that exploiting this asymmetry with modality-appropriate fusion strategies yields consistent 4–6% mAP gains across four diverse benchmarks and three backbones. The backbone-agnostic design and the parameter reduction from the IDWT-based head (76.7M → 69.1M) make the approach practically attractive for deployment. Open questions include whether the frequency-domain decomposition generalizes to other multi-modal pairs (e.g., RGB-LiDAR, RGB-event), whether deeper multi-level wavelet hierarchies beyond the three used here provide further gains, and whether the absolute-maximum high-frequency fusion can be replaced by learned, differentiable alternatives that might capture more nuanced cross-modal detail interactions.
