## Problem

Existing scale equivariance methods handle only global scaling (uniform image resizing), whereas real-world scale variation is local—different objects in the same image change size independently. Moreover, true local scaling is not invertible (e.g., occlusion), so it does not form a group, preventing direct application of equivariance theory. This paper addresses the gap by defining a tractable group approximation (monotone scaling) and designing a canonicalizer that makes pretrained networks locally scale equivariant.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (224×224 for classification, 256×256 for segmentation) and produces class labels or pixel-wise segmentation masks, identical to the base architecture. Unusually, the DEC module operates on intermediate latent feature maps rather than the raw input: it receives a feature tensor F_k from layer k, predicts a piecewise-linear monotone scaling parameter Φ_k (N control points over the spatial domain), and outputs a canonicalized feature S(M_k(S^{-1}(F_k; Φ_k)); Φ_k). The scaling parameters are shared across channels and applied independently along spatial axes.

## Method

The core contribution is the Deep Equilibrium Canonicalizer (DEC), which replaces the per-example energy minimization of classical canonicalization with an amortized fixed-point solver. The monotone scaling group is parameterized as a piecewise-linear bijection l_Φ with N control points, and the DEC models the update rule H(Φ^{i+1}; I, ψ) = Φ^{i} − ∇_Φ E(S^{-1}(I; Φ^{i}); ψ) as a learnable neural network, whose fixed point Φ̂ is found via Anderson acceleration. Crucially, canonicalization is applied in latent space: each intermediate module M_k of an existing network is wrapped as M̃_k(F_k) = S(M_k(S^{-1}(F_k; Φ_k)); Φ_k), so the full adapted network M̃ = M̃_K ∘ … ∘ M̃_1 inherits monotone-scale equivariance by composition. This design is architecture-agnostic and can be inserted into pretrained ViT, Swin, DeiT, BEiT, DINOv2, or ResNet backbones with only the DEC module trained, requiring ~8× less GPU memory and ~2× less time per iteration than differentiable optimization-based canonicalization.

## Results

On the locally scaled ImageNet benchmark, DEC improves top-1 accuracy and reduces invariance error (InvE) across all four architectures: Swin reaches 78.32% Acc (vs. 77.94% Base) with InvE 8.82 (vs. 8.93), and BEiT reaches 85.08% Acc (vs. 84.70% Base) with InvE 6.24 (vs. 6.29). On the locally scaled MNIST dataset, DEC achieves the highest accuracy and lowest InvE across six architectures, improving average accuracy by 4.10% and reducing InvE by 8.91×10⁻² over the augmentation baseline (e.g., DINOv2: 97.90% Acc, 2.62 InvE vs. 95.80%, 7.47). On the synthetic segmentation dataset, DEC achieves the best mIoU (83.12–88.56%) and lowest EquE across ViT, Swin, and DINOv2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DEC consistently outperforms all baselines on both accuracy and scale-consistency metrics. The augmentation baseline (Aug) provides minimal gains over the unmodified Base model (e.g., Swin: 77.96% vs. 77.94% on ImageNet), confirming that simple data augmentation is insufficient for local scale equivariance. The input-space canonicalization baseline (Canon) actively degrades performance, dropping Swin accuracy by 3.27% on ImageNet and 3.29% on MNIST, because warping the raw input distorts the image in ways the pretrained model cannot recover. The invariance-loss baseline (InvL/AugL) also yields negligible improvements over Base, suggesting that adding an equivariance penalty to the training loss is not effective. DEC, by contrast, improves Swin ImageNet accuracy by 0.38% while reducing InvE, and on MNIST reduces InvE by up to 5.2× (DINOv2: 2.62 vs. 7.47 for Aug). The paper also reports a comparison against hierarchical multi-scale ViT models [22, 60] in the appendix, where DEC achieves comparable or better consistency with fewer architectural modifications.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the canonicalization framework for equivariant adaptation (Mondal et al., NeurIPS 2023; Panigrahi & Mondal, CVPRW 2024) in two key ways: it moves canonicalization from the input image to latent feature maps, and it replaces per-example gradient optimization with a Deep Equilibrium Model (Bai et al., NeurIPS 2019) as an amortized fixed-point solver. Conceptually, it departs from the extensive global scale equivariance literature (Worrall & Welling, NeurIPS 2019; Sosnovik et al., ICLR 2020; Rahman & Yeh, NeurIPS 2024) by introducing a new group structure—monotone scaling—that approximates the non-invertible, object-local scale variations observed in real images. The latent canonicalization design is motivated by the observation that monotone scaling is only an approximation of true local scaling, so enforcing equivariance at intermediate representations may better capture the desired inductive bias.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: existing scale-equivariant architectures handle only global resizing, while real-world scale variation is inherently local and object-specific. The plug-and-play DEC module offers a lightweight (8× less memory than optimization-based canonicalization) way to retrofit scale consistency onto pretrained models without architectural redesign, which is valuable given the dominance of large pretrained ViTs. However, the performance gains on ImageNet are modest (≤0.4% accuracy), and the monotone scaling group, while mathematically clean, remains a specific parametric approximation that may not capture all real-world local deformations. Open questions include whether the latent canonicalization strategy generalizes to other continuous transformation groups (e.g., local rotation, local shear) and whether the approach scales to even larger models or 3D settings.
