## Problem

Existing video benchmarks primarily assess single-step perception or domain-specific knowledge rather than sustained, temporally-grounded multi-step reasoning over long narrative content. Current evaluations also rely on outcome-only metrics (e.g., MCQ accuracy) that ignore whether the reasoning process itself is valid, conflating correct answers with sound reasoning. VRBench addresses this by being the first long narrative video benchmark with stepwise reasoning annotations and a dual process-and-outcome evaluation pipeline.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252224/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For VLMs, the input is the raw long narrative video (average 1.6 hours, 8 languages, 7 categories) plus a text question; for text-only LLMs, the video is converted into fine-grained summaries via 5-minute chunk abstractions generated by Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct. Each question requires a multi-step reasoning chain with per-step start/end timestamps (minimum 2 steps), followed by a final MCQ answer. The process-level output is scored on four weighted dimensions: logical coherence (40%), similarity to ground-truth reasoning (40%, excluded for prediction/hypothetical tasks where weights shift to 8:1:1), factual accuracy (10%), and clarity (10%), all normalized to 0–100.

## Method

VRBench is constructed through a multi-stage pipeline: (1) video curation from 10,000+ YouTube clips using 7 narrative tags, a 20-minute minimum duration, and 14 multilingual experts scoring plot coherence on a 10-point scale (threshold ≥7, inter-rater ρ=0.82), yielding 960 videos; (2) a two-stage human-in-the-loop annotation where an automatic pipeline (AutoShot segmentation → VideoChat2 captioning → Whisper transcription → DeepL translation → GPT-4o pseudo-QA generation) produces initial candidates, then 67 trained graduate students rewrite 8–10 QA pairs per video with explicit temporal grounding across 7 reasoning types (event prediction, hypothetical reasoning, event attribution, implicit inference, logical linkage, information synopsis, counting), validated by 10 reviewers with 95% inter-annotator agreement. The evaluation pipeline is multi-phase: outcome-level MCQ accuracy (distractors generated by DeepSeek-V3) combined with a process-level LLM-as-judge metric where DeepSeek-V3 scores each reasoning chain on four sub-metrics, chosen after demonstrating >0.8 Spearman correlation with human ratings at ~10× lower cost than GPT-4o. This dual evaluation reveals reasoning fragility invisible to MCQ alone (e.g., GPT-4o scores 81.25% on MCQ but only 56.11% on process quality).

## Results

On VRBench, Gemini-2.0-Pro achieves the best overall score of 74.61% (83.29% MCQ, 65.93% process-level), surpassing all other models by at least 5.93%. Among LLMs, Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking leads at 60.97% overall, while the best open-source VLM (InternVL2.5-78B) reaches 62.31%. GPT-4o as a VLM scores 68.68% overall but its process-level rating drops to 56.13%, exposing a gap between outcome accuracy and reasoning quality. Counting problems (CP) is the weakest taxonomy for nearly all models, with most scoring below 40% on MCQ accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gemini-2.0-Pro (74.61% overall) dominates all 30 other models, outperforming the second-best VLM (GPT-4o at 68.68%) by 5.93% and the best LLM (Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking at 60.97%) by 13.64%. Proprietary VLMs with long-context support (0.5 fps, large frame budgets) consistently outperform text-only LLMs by 13.82% absolute, confirming that dense visual input is essential for narrative reasoning. Among open-source VLMs, InternVL2.5-78B (62.31%) leads but still trails proprietary models by 12.30%, while long-context-capable models like Qwen2.5-VL-7B (256 frames, 61.71%) and Kimi-VL-A3B-Thinking (61.82%) surpass the 64-frame InternVL2.5-8B (50.41%) by over 11%. System-2 reasoning models show clear gains: o1-preview beats GPT-4o by 4.30% overall, and DeepSeek-R1 outperforms DeepSeek-V3 on both MCQ and process metrics. However, QwQ-32B achieves higher process-level ratings than Qwen2.5-72B yet lower MCQ accuracy (52.52% vs 53.51%), indicating that extended reasoning does not always translate to correct final answers. Coarse-grained VLMs (4-frame H2OVL Mississippi-2B at 47.15%, DeepSeek-VL2 at 31.50%) perform worse than top LLMs, underscoring that random frame sampling is insufficient for long-range narrative reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

VRBench carves new ground at the intersection of long-video understanding and multi-step reasoning evaluation. Existing video benchmarks (MVBench, EgoSchema, LongVideoBench, VideoMME) primarily assess single-step perception or short-clip comprehension, while text-based reasoning benchmarks (GSM8K, MMLU) focus on domain-specific knowledge without temporal grounding. VRBench departs from both by requiring temporally-anchored, multi-step reasoning chains over hour-long narrative content, and introduces a dual process-and-outcome evaluation paradigm that no prior video benchmark supports. The annotation methodology repurposes existing tools (AutoShot, Whisper, GPT-4o) within a human-in-the-loop pipeline, but the stepwise reasoning taxonomy and multi-phase LLM-as-judge scoring represent a novel evaluation framework for narrative video.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VRBench exposes a critical blind spot in current model evaluation: high multiple-choice accuracy does not guarantee sound reasoning (e.g., GPT-4o achieves 81.25% MCQ but only 56.11% process quality), suggesting that models may be pattern-matching rather than genuinely reasoning. The finding that test-time scaling dramatically benefits system-2 models (QwQ-32B: +12.43%) while actively harming system-1 models challenges the universal assumption that 'more thinking tokens = better answers.' Open questions include whether the 12.30% open-source-to-proprietary VLM gap at matched parameter scales reflects fundamental architectural limitations or insufficient training data, and whether the LLM-as-judge process metric (validated at ρ>0.8 with humans) generalizes to other domains beyond narrative video.
