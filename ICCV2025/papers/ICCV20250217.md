## Problem

Multi-encoder MLLMs achieve superior visual perception by fusing complementary representations from multiple vision encoders, but this introduces prohibitive computational overhead from redundant visual tokens. Existing token pruning methods are designed for single-encoder settings and cannot address the unique challenges of multi-encoder architectures: allocating pruning budgets across encoders with varying semantic richness, eliminating cross-encoder information overlap, and adapting token budgets to task-specific demands such as OCR.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250217/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (1024×1024 effective resolution) and a text prompt as input, processed through four vision encoders (CLIP, ConvNeXt, Pix2Struct, EVA-02) before being fed to a 7B or 8B LLM. Unlike fixed-token methods, the number of visual tokens retained for the LLM is not pre-defined but adaptively determined per instance (averaging ~242 or ~126 tokens across benchmarks), with OCR-heavy inputs retaining more tokens than general comprehension tasks. The output is a standard free-form text response.

## Method

METEOR progressively prunes visual tokens across three stages. In the encoding stage, redundant tokens within each encoder are identified via cosine similarity to the average token in shallow layers and class-token attention in deep layers, with per-encoder sparsity ratios allocated proportionally to offline-computed feature map ranks as a measure of information richness. In the fusion stage, each encoder retains a dedicated projector (post-projection fusion), and a cooperative pruning step removes mutually redundant tokens by measuring cross-encoder similarity in the shared semantic space. In the decoding stage, the method selects top-k most relevant attention heads (ranked by Visual Attention Value) to compute text-guided importance scores, and dynamically adjusts the retained token count via a visual contribution level that scales with the summed attention values, allowing OCR tasks to retain more tokens than general tasks without any retraining.

## Results

On 11 multimodal benchmarks, METEOR (242 adaptive tokens) achieves an average score of 69.0, matching EAGLE (1024 tokens, avg 69.3) with only a 0.3% drop while reducing visual tokens by 76%, saving 49% TFLOPS (26.21→13.42), and increasing throughput by 46% (0.81→1.18). On OCR-specific tasks, METEOR outperforms prior token-pruning methods by 8.8–12.3% (e.g., DocVQA 71.4 vs. FastV 54.2, DeCo 56.3). With LLaMA-3-8B as the LLM, METEOR reaches 77.1 on ScienceQA and 76.9 on AI2D, surpassing EAGLE on 7 of 11 benchmarks with fewer than 25% of the visual tokens.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary multi-encoder baseline EAGLE (1024 tokens), METEOR retains only 242 tokens (76% reduction) with a mere 0.3% average drop (69.0 vs. 69.3), while cutting TFLOPS by 49% and boosting throughput by 46%. Compared to LLM-stage pruning methods (FastV, PDrop, SparseVLM, VTW), METEOR wins by 4.3–5.7% on average and by 8.8–12.3% on OCR benchmarks (e.g., DocVQA 71.4 vs. FastV 54.2, PDrop 50.4), because its instance-adaptive ratio retains more tokens for fine-grained tasks. Training-based compression methods DeCo and PixelShuffle also trail METEOR by 4.9–5.7% on average, with particularly large OCR gaps (DocVQA 71.4 vs. 56.3/56.4). Against broader MLLM baselines in Table 1, METEOR (242 tokens) outperforms LLaVA-Next (2880 tokens) on 10 of 11 benchmarks, and surpasses Mousi (576 tokens) and Brave-X5 (160 tokens) with fewer tokens and less training data. In the Cambrian-1 comparison (Table 3, same encoders and data), METEOR (324 tokens) beats Cambrian-1 (576 tokens) by 3.3% on OCR tasks while using 44% fewer visual tokens.

## Relation to Prior Work

METEOR sits at the intersection of two active research threads: multi-encoder MLLMs (EAGLE, Cambrian-1, MouSi, Brave) that fuse complementary vision encoders for robust perception, and visual token pruning methods (FastV, PDrop, SparseVLM, DeCo) that reduce computational cost in single-encoder MLLMs. The key departure is that prior pruning strategies were designed for single-encoder settings and cannot address the unique challenges of multi-encoder fusion—namely, how to allocate token budgets across encoders with differing semantic richness and how to eliminate cross-encoder redundancy. METEOR extends the rank-based filter pruning idea from HRank (originally for CNNs) to Vision Transformers as a principled allocation criterion, and introduces cooperative cross-encoder pruning and attention-head-filtered instance-adaptive pruning that have no direct precedent in the multi-encoder literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As multi-encoder MLLMs become the default architecture for robust multimodal understanding, their quadratic computational cost from high-resolution inputs and multiple encoders is a critical bottleneck; METEOR's 76% token reduction with only 0.3% average performance drop offers a practical path to making such models deployable. The instance-adaptive mechanism—dynamically retaining more tokens for OCR-heavy queries and fewer for general comprehension—addresses a real limitation of fixed-ratio pruning that prior methods could not handle. Open questions include whether the offline rank computation remains valid when new encoder architectures are added, whether the approach scales gracefully beyond four encoders, and whether the training-free Stage 3 pruning can be further improved with lightweight learned components.
