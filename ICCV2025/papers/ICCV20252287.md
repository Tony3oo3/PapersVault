## Problem

Existing multimodal theory-of-mind and implicature benchmarks rely on simple visual cues (single objects or people) and fail to capture the complexity of real-world scenes where multiple entities interact. This paper asks whether current multimodal AI systems can integrate rich visual context to resolve ambiguity in indirect natural language expressions, and identifies that while models can perceive image content, they cannot effectively reason with it to infer a speaker's true intent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252287/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a scene image (averaging 7 objects and 4 people, from VCR movie scenes or Ego4D egocentric video) with an indirect text expression, where person indicator tags (person1, person2, etc.) are overlaid on the image to ground the addressee. The model selects from four multiple-choice interpretations, where the three distractors are purpose-built to probe distinct failure modes: Fake Scene Understanding (global visual hallucination), Superficial Understanding (literal text reading), and Nonexistent Entity (local object hallucination). The ground-truth answer is only determinable with visual context, ensuring the task is genuinely multimodal.

## Method

VAGUE is constructed via a multi-stage GPT-4o generation pipeline followed by extensive human rating and filtering: a direct expression with a (subject, action, object) solution triplet is first generated conditioned on the image, then an indirect expression is crafted using linguistic strategies (sarcasm, humor, idioms), and finally four answer candidates are produced. The evaluation protocol systematically varies visual cue levels—Language Models (text only), Socratic Models (text + short captions), and Visual Language Models (text + raw image)—to isolate the contribution of visual perception versus reasoning across 12 models from 4B to 72B parameters. The three counterfactual choice types (FS, SU, NE) enable fine-grained diagnosis of whether failures stem from visual perception errors or insufficient reasoning, revealing that Superficial Understanding is the dominant failure mode across all models.

## Results

On the VAGUE benchmark (1,677 samples), the best-performing model, Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct (72B), achieves 74.2% multiple-choice accuracy on VAGUE-VCR and 69.8% on VAGUE-Ego4D in the VLM setting. GPT-4o reaches 65.1% (VCR) and 63.6% (Ego4D), while Gemini-1.5-Pro scores 60.6% on both subsets. Human evaluators achieve 94% accuracy on a 400-sample subset, revealing a persistent ~20% gap between the best models and human-level multimodal intention disambiguation. Across all models, introducing visual cues consistently improves accuracy (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-72B gains +44.6 points from LM to VLM on VCR), confirming the dataset's visual dependency.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark paper, so the 'comparison' is across 12 evaluated models under three visual-cue settings (LM, SM, VLM). Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct (72B) is the strongest open-source model, reaching 74.2% on VCR-VLM and 69.8% on Ego4D-VLM, outperforming InternVL-2.5-MPO (26B) by ~10 points and GPT-4o by ~9 points on VCR. Among proprietary models, GPT-4o (65.1% VCR-VLM) edges out Gemini-1.5-Pro (60.6%) by ~4.5 points, though both show an unusual pattern where Socratic (caption-based) inputs outperform raw image inputs—a phenomenon attributed to superior captioning reducing visual hallucination. Open-source models benefit substantially from visual cues (gains of 20–47 points from LM to VLM), whereas proprietary models show smaller gains (15–20 points), suggesting they already extract useful information from text priors. Ovis2 (16B) is a notable outlier, gaining only 3.6 points from LM to VLM on VCR, indicating poor visual integration. All models fall 20+ points below the 94% human accuracy, with the dominant failure mode being Superficial Understanding (SU) errors—models perceive the scene but fail to reason about the speaker's implicit intent.

## Relation to Prior Work

VAGUE carves new ground at the intersection of multimodal Theory of Mind (MMToM, BOSS, MuMA-ToM) and multimodal implicature understanding (WITS, MOSES, UR-FUNNY, MemeCap), which have each addressed parts of the problem in isolation. Prior ToM benchmarks focus on belief/goal inference without explicitly modeling the ambiguity and indirectness of natural language, while prior implicature work relies on simple visual cues (single objects, facial expressions, voice tone) rather than complex multi-entity scenes. By formulating Multimodal Intention Disambiguation (MID) as a distinct task requiring both pragmatic language interpretation and deep visual scene reasoning, the paper repurposes established visual sources (VCR, Ego4D) for a problem neither prior line of work explicitly targets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~20% accuracy gap between the best model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B at 72.3%) and humans (94%) quantifies a concrete limitation in current multimodal systems: they perceive visual content but fail to integrate it into pragmatic reasoning about speaker intent. The finding that Superficial Understanding is the dominant failure mode across all models suggests that simply improving visual perception will not close the gap; higher-order cognitive processes like commonsense reasoning and pragmatic inference are needed. Limitations include English-only expressions, potential cultural biases from GPT-4o generation, and dependency on parent dataset annotation quality. Open questions include whether the gap narrows with scale, whether explicit reasoning architectures (beyond CoT prompting) can address the SU failure mode, and how the task generalizes to other languages and cultures.
