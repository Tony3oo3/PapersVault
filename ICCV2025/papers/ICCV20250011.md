## Problem

Existing driver gaze prediction methods rely on single-frame saliency maps or black-box neural networks, failing to capture the temporal sequence of eye movements, integrate dynamic driving context (vehicle interactions, driver operations), or provide interpretable explanations of attention shifts. Moreover, publicly available eye-tracking datasets largely exclude the high-risk emergency takeover scenarios that are most critical for autonomous driving HMI, with 23.9% of AV accidents attributed to takeover failures.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a raw driving scene image (segmented via fine-tuned YOLOv8 into foreground vehicle/obstacle instances with selective merging of overlapping objects), ego-vehicle sensor readings (position, steering angle, brake and accelerator pedal positions), and per-vehicle traffic attributes (distance, velocity, acceleration, yaw) from the CARLA simulator or LiDAR. Output is a discrete action at each timestep drawn from {STAY, LEFT, RIGHT}, which maps directly to the center coordinates of the corresponding segmented object; multiple stochastic samples can be aggregated with Gaussian smoothing to yield an attention heatmap, but the heatmap is an optional visualization rather than a required intermediate.

## Method

PILOT frames sequential gaze prediction as a POMDP imitation learning problem and trains via an EM loop: the E-step runs a particle filter to sample action sequences weighted by agreement with observed gaze, while the M-step synthesizes a rule-based policy through incremental heuristic search over logical combinations of feature sketches (e.g., relative yaw, speed differentials, brake state) and refines each sketch's logistic-function parameters with L-BFGS. The resulting policy is a set of human-readable conditional rules (e.g., 'IF lead-vehicle yaw trend is leftward AND brake engaged AND previous action was STAY, THEN shift LEFT') that explicitly encode temporal dependencies through the previous-action state, guaranteeing Markov-consistent saccade sequences. A complexity-penalized loss (λ_complexity·complexity + λ_base) prevents overfitting of rule structure, and instance segmentation with peripheral-vision-aware merging constrains gaze targets to semantically meaningful, non-redundant objects, eliminating spurious fixation on occluded or overlapping vehicles.

## Results

On the DATAD dataset, PILOT achieves the lowest MSE of 634,401 per frame, reducing error by 38.6% over the next-best baseline (SalM2, 1,033,065) and by 88.0% over DRIVE (5,294,155). PILOT also attains the highest Average Hit Rate of 88.72%, surpassing SalM2 (81.82%) by 6.9 percentage points and DRIVE (33.66%) by 55.1 points. In terms of inference speed, PILOT completes prediction in 68.5 ms per frame on a 12-vCPU/32 GB machine, roughly 33% faster than the next-fastest baseline DBVM (103.3 ms) and orders of magnitude faster than DR(eye)VE (>1 min).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PILOT outperforms all four baselines on MSE and Average Hit Rate. Against SalM2 (AAAI 2025), PILOT reduces MSE by 38.6% (634K vs 1,033K) and improves Average Hit Rate by 6.9 pp (88.72% vs 81.82%), while also leading on At-Least-Once Hit Rate (95.50% vs 90.07%). Against PGNet (CVPR 2022), PILOT cuts MSE by 40.0% and raises Average Hit Rate by 14.0 pp, though PGNet edges out PILOT by 0.58 pp on At-Least-Once Hit Rate (96.08% vs 95.50%). Against DRIVE (ICCV 2021), PILOT achieves an 88.0% MSE reduction and a 55.1 pp gain in Average Hit Rate, reflecting DRIVE's reliance on 3D convolutions that introduce significant latency. Against DBVM (IEEE/CAA JAS 2022), PILOT lowers MSE by 41.9% and improves Average Hit Rate by 19.2 pp, while also being 33% faster in inference (68.5 ms vs 103.3 ms). Against DR(eye)VE (IEEE TPAMI 2018), PILOT reduces MSE by 49.8% (at 60 fps) and boosts Average Hit Rate by 25.7 pp; DR(eye)VE's inference time exceeds one minute per frame, making it impractical for real-time HMI.

## Relation to Prior Work

PILOT departs from the dominant paradigm of driver gaze prediction that maps image features to continuous saliency maps via deep neural networks (e.g., MTSF, FBLnet, DBVM). Instead, it repurposes programmatic imitation learning—a technique from robot action selection [12, 49]—to synthesize human-readable decision rules within a POMDP framework, predicting discrete saccade actions (STAY/LEFT/RIGHT) rather than pixel-level fixation coordinates. The approach also draws on instance segmentation (YOLOv8) to constrain gaze targets to semantically meaningful objects, and integrates driving telemetry and surrounding traffic flow data that prior gaze datasets (DR(eye)VE, BDD-A, DADA-2000) largely omit. Conceptually, it sits at the intersection of imitation learning, program synthesis, and human factors, rather than extending any single prior gaze-prediction architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DATAD dataset fills a notable gap: no prior driving gaze dataset captures emergency takeover scenarios in a high-fidelity simulator with full driving telemetry and surrounding traffic data, yet 23.9% of autonomous driving accidents involve takeover failures. The interpretability of PILOT's rule-based output (e.g., 'if lead vehicle yaw indicates leftward trend AND brake is engaged, gaze shifts left') is directly actionable for HMI designers who need to understand *why* a driver looks where they do, unlike black-box saliency maps. However, the method's reliance on per-subject training, heuristic rule search, and a single simulator environment limits generalizability; whether the synthesized rules transfer across drivers, real-world conditions, or unseen scenario types remains an open question that the authors acknowledge but do not resolve.
