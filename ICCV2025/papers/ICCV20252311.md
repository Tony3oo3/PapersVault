## Problem

Previous end-to-end multi-view pedestrian tracking (MVPT) methods associate current pedestrians with past trajectories using only information between the current and a single adjacent past timestamp (K=1), discarding all earlier trajectory history. This limitation causes association errors in crowded or occluded scenes where the single adjacent timestamp is unreliable, and it remains unclear how to effectively incorporate multi-timestamp trajectory information into an end-to-end MVPT framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a multi-view video as a sequence of synchronized frames, each containing S calibrated camera images resized to 720×1280, processed in a sliding window of K+1 frames (K=7 by default, limited by GPU memory). It outputs a BEV pedestrian occupancy map (e.g., 480×1440 for Wildtrack, 640×1000 for MultiviewX) and per-pedestrian 2D trajectories on that BEV grid, generated sequentially frame-by-frame by linking detected locations to past trajectories via Hungarian matching.

## Method

MVTrajecter introduces two trajectory-level association costs that aggregate evidence across K past timestamps: Trajectory Motion Cost (TMC) sums Euclidean distances between the current detected location and motion-predicted locations from each past timestamp, while Trajectory Appearance Cost (TAC) sums negative log-likelihoods of softmax-normalized appearance similarity across past timestamps. The detection network (ResNet18 encoder → perspective transformation → max-pooling aggregation → U-Net decoder → detection head) produces BEV features, from which pedestrian features are extracted. The association part uses two attention branches (2 transformer blocks, 8 heads each) with sinusoidal temporal embeddings: a motion branch predicts per-timestamp motion offsets and an appearance branch re-extracts appearance features at all timestamps in the window, enabling the model to adaptively capture cross-timestamp relationships. Training is fully end-to-end with three losses (MSE on occupancy maps, L1 on motion predictions, negative log-likelihood on appearance matching) balanced via uncertainty weighting, and final association is resolved by Hungarian matching on the fused cost.

## Results

On the primary GMVD dataset, MVTrajecter achieves 77.2 IDF1 and 78.1 MOTA, outperforming the previous best (MVTr) by 2.9 and 2.5 points respectively. On Wildtrack, it reaches 96.5 IDF1 and 94.3 MOTA, and on MultiviewX it achieves 85.8 IDF1 and 92.8 MOTA, setting new state-of-the-art on all three benchmarks. When evaluated with identical detection results (Table 2), MVTrajecter still leads all baselines by 1.6–6.6 MOTA points, confirming the gains come from its multi-timestamp association rather than detection alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MVTrajecter outperforms all seven baselines on every metric across all three datasets. On GMVD, it beats the closest competitor MVTr [71] by 2.9 IDF1 and 2.5 MOTA, and surpasses TrackTacular [63] by 2.8 IDF1 and 6.9 MOTA. The largest gap is over EarlyBird [62], which relies primarily on appearance from a single past timestamp: MVTrajecter gains 7.5 IDF1 and 7.3 MOTA. On Wildtrack, MVTrajecter leads MVTr [71] by 3.4 IDF1 and 2.0 MOTA, and outperforms BEV-SUSHI [66] (a non-end-to-end method using external models) by 3.1 IDF1 and 6.8 MOTA. On MultiviewX, it edges out TrackTacular [63] by 0.2 IDF1 and 0.4 MOTA while matching its MOTP of 95.0. The controlled experiment (Table 2, same detection) shows that the association gains are consistent: MVTrajecter leads MVTr by 1.9 MOTA and EarlyBird by 5.5 MOTA, confirming that multi-timestamp TMC/TAC is the primary source of improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

MVTrajecter extends the end-to-end MVPT framework established by MVDet (for BEV feature generation via perspective transformation) and EarlyBird (for the overall detection-then-association pipeline), but departs fundamentally from all prior end-to-end MVPT methods by leveraging information from multiple past timestamps (K>1) rather than only the single adjacent past frame. It draws conceptual inspiration from monocular tracking methods that exploit multi-timestamp trajectories (GTR, MotionTrack, graph-based trackers), but adapts this to the multi-view BEV setting through two novel association costs—trajectory motion cost (TMC) and trajectory appearance cost (TAC)—that aggregate evidence across K past timestamps. The use of transformer attention for both motion prediction and appearance feature extraction replaces the Kalman filter and single-timestamp feature extraction used in prior methods like EarlyBird and REMP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first end-to-end MVPT method to systematically exploit multi-timestamp trajectory information for association, closing a gap that monocular tracking had already addressed years earlier. The ablations clearly show that both motion and appearance from multiple timestamps are necessary (neither alone suffices), and that the attention mechanism captures temporal relationships more effectively than a Kalman filter in this BEV context. A key limitation is the GPU-memory constraint capping K at 7, meaning pedestrians with detection failures exceeding 7 frames cannot be re-associated; exploring efficient long-range trajectory utilization remains an open problem. The technique is likely to be adopted as a standard component in future end-to-end MVPT pipelines given its clear, well-isolated gains.
