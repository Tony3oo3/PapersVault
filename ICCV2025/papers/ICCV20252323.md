## Problem

Existing sports video captioning methods either produce anonymous descriptions with placeholder tokens or infer player identities from static external information (game news, candidate player lists) that is independent of the video content, leading to incorrect player names. No prior dataset provides the visual annotations (player bounding boxes) needed to train models that genuinely recognize player identities from video frames, making visual-based identity-aware captioning infeasible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252323/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During training, the model consumes basketball video clips (up to 60 frames at 224×224) along with player bounding boxes from a player-centric video clip set that links each of 321 players to all their clips across 40 NBA games. During inference, a pre-trained SportsMOT tracker extracts player sequences from the video, and the top-2 players by confidence are selected for identity recognition. The output is a natural-language caption that names specific players and describes their actions, shot distances, and event types (e.g., 'Jayson Tatum hits a 3-pt shot from 22 ft').

## Method

The architecture (LLM-IAVC) comprises three components: (1) an Identity-Related Information Extraction Module (IRIEM) containing a Player Identification Network (TimeSformer backbone + classification head over 321 player classes) and a Bidirectional Semantic Interaction Module (BSIM) that performs multi-head self-attention on video and player features separately, then bidirectional cross-attention (entity-to-video and video-to-entity) with down/up-projection to mutually enhance both representations; (2) a Visual Context Learning Module (VCLM) that uses 32 learnable query vectors with self- and cross-attention over positionally-encoded video features to extract global contextual semantics; and (3) a lightweight LLM decoder (GPT-2, Qwen2.5, or Llama3.2) that receives a concatenated multimodal prompt of linearly-projected video context, BSIM-enhanced video features, BSIM-enhanced player features, and LLM-encoded player name tokens. The PIN is pre-trained with cross-entropy over player identities, while the full captioning model is trained end-to-end with token-level cross-entropy loss, requiring no external knowledge at inference time.

## Results

On NBA-Identity, the best variant LLM-IAVC (Llama3.2-3B with multimodal prompt) achieves CIDEr 105.3, METEOR 23.0, Rouge-L 42.5, and BLEU-4 18.8. On VC-NBA-2022, the same variant reaches CIDEr 150.7, METEOR 30.9, Rouge-L 57.9, and BLEU-4 38.6. The multimodal prompt (combining visual context, player identity features, and player name embeddings) boosts CIDEr by 40.9% and BLEU-4 by 4.3% over the video-only variant of the same model. All LLM-IAVC variants with the prompt outperform every baseline on CIDEr across both datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLM-IAVC (Llama3.2-3B with multimodal prompt) outperforms all six baselines on every metric across both datasets. On NBA-Identity, it nearly doubles the CIDEr of the strongest extra-information baseline EIKA (105.3 vs. 55.7) and quadruples that of the best traditional method OmniViD (105.3 vs. 39.6). On VC-NBA-2022, it leads EIKA by 10.0 CIDEr points (150.7 vs. 140.7) and 1.9 BLEU-4 points (38.6 vs. 36.7). The gap over traditional methods (Clip4Caption, SwinBERT, CoCap, OmniViD) is especially large on CIDEr, which is sensitive to rare tokens like player names—these methods cannot recognize identities from video at all. Among the extra-information methods, KEANet and EIKA rely on a static candidate player list rather than visual recognition, leading to less accurate name predictions. The ablation further confirms that the player identification network (PIN) contributes the single largest gain (CIDEr 66.5→95.1), while the bidirectional semantic interaction module (BSIM) and visual context learning module (VCLM) each add further improvements on top.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the two dominant paradigms in identity-aware sports video captioning: anonymous methods that replace player names with generic tokens (SoccerNet-Caption, MatchTime, NSVA), and extra-information-based methods (KEANet, EIKA, Goal) that infer identities from static candidate lists or game statistics rather than from the video itself. The key conceptual shift is treating player identity as a visual recognition problem solvable directly from video frames, using a dedicated player identification network (PIN) trained on a player-centric clip set, rather than as a retrieval or inference problem over external knowledge. Architecturally, it combines TimeSformer-based video encoding, DETR-style learnable query vectors for context extraction, and bidirectional cross-attention for feature fusion, feeding a multimodal prompt into lightweight LLMs (GPT-2, Qwen2.5, Llama3.2) as decoders—a design pattern common in recent vision-language work but applied here specifically to the sports identity-recognition bottleneck.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The NBA-Identity dataset (9,726 videos, 321 players with bounding boxes, 9 event types) is the largest identity-aware sports captioning benchmark to date and provides the bounding-box annotations that prior datasets lacked, enabling genuine visual player recognition research. The ablation results confirm that visual-based identity recognition (PIN) is the dominant factor in caption quality, with VCLM contributing only marginal gains—suggesting the field's bottleneck is identity perception, not video understanding. Open questions include generalization beyond basketball, incorporating jersey numbers and colors to improve PIN accuracy, and resolving shot-distance estimation, which the authors acknowledge remains unsolved. The reliance on a pre-trained multi-object tracker (SportsMOT) at inference also raises practical deployment questions for live broadcast settings.
