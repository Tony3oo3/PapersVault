## Problem

High-frame-rate NLOS videography requires reducing per-point scanning time and spatial density, which introduces fast-scan distortions and sparse measurements that degrade reconstruction quality. Existing methods either produce heavily blurred results under these conditions (f-k, PnP) or are designed for static scenes and fail on dynamic content. The paper tackles how to reconstruct high-quality NLOS video at 10 FPS from sparse, distorted 16×16 transients without first upsampling to a dense grid.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250936/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two consecutive frames of sparse NLOS transients, each a 16×16 spatial grid of photon-count histograms spanning hundreds to thousands of 4-ps time bins, captured at 0.4 ms per point via serpentine scanning. The temporal dimension is compressed to a 32-dim vector before entering the network, and the previous frame's compressed features are used alongside the current frame's. The output is a single 64×64 grayscale NLOS image per frame, yielding a 10 FPS video with ~106 ms total per-frame latency (102 ms acquisition + ~4 ms reconstruction).

## Method

TransiT is a Vision Transformer that directly compresses the temporal axis of input transients via a linear layer (avoiding the costly upsample-then-reconstruct pipeline), then fuses the current frame's features with the frame-to-frame difference to emphasize dynamic changes. A stack of ViT blocks with combined 2D spatial and 1D temporal positional encodings models spatiotemporal dependencies across the 16×16 grid, and a final linear layer maps to the 64×64 output. Training is two-stage: first, supervised MSE on a ~200k-frame synthetic dataset (Mixamo motions rendered at 64×64 with a novel fast-scan distortion model applied to produce 16×16 inputs); second, MMD-based transfer learning on 200 real-measured frames to align feature distributions between synthetic and real data without requiring ground truth. The distortion model (Eqs. 5–6) accounts for the galvanometer's finite response time, which smears each transient along the scanning path and introduces a time shift relative to the ideal per-point measurement.

## Results

TransiT reconstructs 64×64 NLOS video frames from 16×16 sparse transients at 10 FPS (≈106 ms per frame total, with only ~0.6 ms for the network inference itself). On the main comparison (Table 1), TransiT achieves PSNR of 25.87 (Character), 20.91 (Propeller), and 29.45 (Human), substantially outperforming f-k (17.86, 10.01, 19.00) and PnP (20.77, 11.39, 19.92). SSIM gains are especially pronounced for the propeller (0.8211 vs. 0.1902 for f-k and 0.2818 for PnP), where fast rotation makes distortion most severe. Cosine similarity reaches 0.94–0.98 across all objects, versus 0.63–0.79 for the baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TransiT is compared against two baselines in the main experiments: f-k migration (Lindell et al., ACM TOG 2019) and PnP regularization (Ye et al., 2023). TransiT wins decisively on all four metrics across all three objects. Against f-k, the PSNR gap ranges from +5.1 dB (Character) to +10.9 dB (Propeller), and SSIM improves from 0.19 to 0.82 on the propeller where f-k produces nearly unrecognizable output. Against PnP, TransiT still leads by 5.1 dB (Character), 9.5 dB (Propeller), and 9.5 dB (Human) in PSNR, with the largest relative gains on the propeller where PnP's band-pass filtering and denoising network struggle with rapid rotational motion. A third baseline, USM (Li et al., NeurIPS 2023), is evaluated only in the distortion-free ablation (Table 2), where TransiT matches or slightly exceeds USM on most metrics (e.g., PSNR 25.49 vs. 25.19 on Character, 26.39 vs. 24.24 on Human) but is slightly behind on the propeller (18.61 vs. 21.92 PSNR), suggesting USM's transient-recovery network has an edge when fast-scan distortion is absent.

## Relation to Prior Work

TransiT extends the confocal NLOS imaging framework (O'Toole et al., Lindell et al.) and the fast-scan acceleration strategies (Ye et al., Isogawa et al.) by formulating a previously unaddressed distortion model that captures the galvanometer's finite response time during serpentine scanning. Rather than following the prior learning-based route of upsampling sparse transients into virtual dense measurements (Wang et al., Li et al.) before applying static reconstruction, TransiT repurposes the Vision Transformer architecture—previously applied to natural video understanding—to directly compress the temporal axis of raw transients and model spatiotemporal dependencies across frames. The MMD-based transfer learning stage draws on domain adaptation literature to bridge the synthetic-to-real gap without requiring paired ground truth, a practical departure from fully supervised NLOS training pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Achieving 10 FPS NLOS videography from 16×16 sparse transients with a single-pixel SPAD brings real-time hidden-object tracking within reach of practical applications such as autonomous navigation and post-disaster search and rescue, where prior methods were limited to 2–4 FPS or required expensive SPAD arrays. The explicit distortion model is a reusable contribution for anyone deploying fast-scan NLOS systems, and the MMD fine-tuning recipe (200 real frames, ~2 hours) offers a low-cost path to domain adaptation. Open questions remain: material disparities (diffuse vs. retro-reflective surfaces) still produce block-like artifacts, single-pixel SPADs cap achievable frame rates, and the ablation reveals TransiT underperforms USM on fast-rotating objects (propeller PSNR 18.61 vs. 21.92), suggesting the spatiotemporal attention may struggle with high-frequency motion. Extending to SPAD arrays and continuous (non-grid) scanning patterns are identified as the most promising next steps.
