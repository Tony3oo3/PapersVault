## Problem

While RAG systems are known to be sensitive to input noise, the cascading impact of OCR errors on each RAG component (retriever, LLM, and end-to-end) remains unexplored. Existing benchmarks either evaluate RAG holistically without fine-grained OCR assessment, consider limited OCR solutions, or focus only on plain-text typographical errors, leaving a critical gap in understanding how diverse OCR noise—spanning tables, formulas, charts, and reading order—degrades RAG performance.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 8,561 unstructured PDF page images spanning seven domains (Textbook, Law, Finance, Newspaper, Manual, Academic, Administration) along with 8,498 Q&A pairs whose evidence is sourced from five document element types (plain text, tables, formulas, charts, reading order). OCR solutions produce structured data in Markdown, LaTeX, or HTML; the RAG pipeline retrieves top-2 chunks of 1024 tokens (no overlap) and generates answers evaluated via LCS (retrieval) and F1-score (generation), reported separately per evidence source. A key design choice is the r_noise ratio—proportion of Q&A pairs whose evidence context is altered by perturbation (LCS < 0.95)—which aligns noise levels across semantic and formatting perturbations for fair comparison.

## Method

OHRBench introduces a systematic perturbation framework that isolates two OCR noise types: Semantic Noise (prediction errors captured by distorting document images with 8 validated perturbation types and running three OCR engines—MinerU, GOT, Qwen2.5-VL) and Formatting Noise (stylistic inconsistencies introduced via heuristic rules for additions, removals, and format conversions across Markdown/LaTeX/HTML). Each noise type is instantiated at three severity levels (mild, moderate, severe), producing 9 perturbed structured datasets per type. The evaluation protocol benchmarks six OCR solutions (pipeline-based: MinerU, Marker; end-to-end: GOT, Nougat; VLM-based: Qwen2.5-VL-72B, InternVL2.5-78B) across three stages—retrieval (BGE-M3, BM25), generation (Qwen2-7B/72B, Llama-3.1-8B), and end-to-end—using LCS for evidence inclusion and F1 for answer accuracy. The paper further conducts error analysis decomposing OCR errors from RAG errors to quantify how much of the performance drop is attributable to the OCR stage versus downstream components.

## Results

On OHRBench, Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieves the best overall RAG performance (F1 = 31.1) with the lowest OCR edit distance (0.18), yet still suffers a 14% absolute drop from the ground-truth structured data (F1 = 36.1). In the retrieval stage, Qwen2.5-VL-72B reaches an LCS of 59.2 (vs. 70.0 for ground truth), and in generation it scores 37.5 F1 (vs. 43.9). The worst-performing OCR solution, Nougat, drops to 14.5 overall F1 — a 60% relative decline from ground truth — primarily due to its inability to parse charts and its high edit distance (0.34).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the six OCR solutions evaluated, Qwen2.5-VL-72B (VLM) consistently outperforms all others across retrieval, generation, and overall RAG stages, achieving 31.1 overall F1 versus 30.0 for MinerU, 29.5 for Marker, 24.6 for GOT, 14.5 for Nougat, and 29.6 for InternVL2.5-78B. Pipeline-based systems (MinerU, Marker) show moderate retrieval performance (LCS 50.1–56.6) but struggle with chart parsing (LCS 16.5–19.7 vs. 38.2–45.1 for VLMs). End-to-end models (GOT, Nougat) perform worst overall, with Nougat particularly weak on charts (LCS 11.3) and reading order (F1 3.6). VLMs excel on multimodal elements but remain weak on reading-order questions (F1 8.8–11.6 vs. 18.7 for ground truth), where pipeline-based OCR with rule-based strategies actually approaches ground-truth performance. Notably, OCR edit distance does not perfectly predict RAG performance: MinerU (E.D. 0.24) scores lower overall than Marker (E.D. 0.28) in retrieval, suggesting different noise types affect downstream components differently.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RAG evaluation literature (RAGAS, ARES, RGB, MultiHopRAG) and the document parsing/OCR evaluation literature (OmniDocBench, DUDE) by connecting them: prior RAG benchmarks treat the knowledge base as a given or focus on chunk-level retrieval noise, while prior OCR benchmarks evaluate parsing accuracy in isolation. OHRBench departs from both by introducing a cascading evaluation protocol that traces how OCR-specific noise (Semantic Noise from prediction errors, Formatting Noise from representation inconsistencies) propagates through retrieval and generation stages. The Semantic Noise simulation extends Nougat's document distortion methodology, refined through an iterative cross-validated annotation process to ensure realism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first benchmark to systematically quantify how OCR quality degrades RAG performance, revealing that no current OCR solution (pipeline, end-to-end, or VLM-based) is sufficient for building high-quality RAG knowledge bases, with even the best model showing a ≥14% performance gap versus ground truth. The identification of two distinct noise types with differential impacts—Semantic Noise uniformly degrading all components while Formatting Noise selectively affecting multimodal questions and smaller LLMs—provides actionable guidance for developing RAG-tailored OCR pipelines and noise-robust retrievers/generators. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to multilingual or extremely long documents, and how to design OCR solutions that are explicitly optimized for downstream RAG fidelity rather than standalone parsing accuracy.
