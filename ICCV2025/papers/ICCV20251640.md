## Problem

In knowledge distillation, student models supervised by large teachers frequently underperform compared to those guided by mid-sized teachers — a phenomenon known as the capacity gap problem. While prior work identified this issue, the underlying cause remained unexplored, and existing remedies (mid-sized teacher assistants, early-stopped teachers) incur significant additional training overhead. This paper identifies the specific mechanism behind the capacity gap and proposes a lightweight fix that avoids retraining the teacher from scratch.

## Input / Output

The method requires three inputs: a pre-trained teacher model, a pre-trained student model (used only for the teacher fine-tuning phase), and RGB images from a classification dataset. The two-phase procedure first fine-tunes the teacher using the frozen student's predictions, then trains a new student from scratch under the fine-tuned teacher. The final output is a compact student network that produces class labels, with no additional inference-time parameters beyond the student itself.

## Method

The paper decomposes the standard KL-divergence KD loss into two components: a class-wise similarity term (which acts as structured label smoothing and is robust to teacher size) and an intra-class distribution term (which encodes the relative difficulty of samples within each class and is highly sensitive to teacher capacity). The key insight is that large teachers assign high confidence to difficult samples that small students cannot reliably learn, and this misaligned intra-class distribution is the primary driver of the capacity gap. The proposed AID method addresses this by fine-tuning the pre-trained teacher for a short number of epochs using the KD loss with a frozen pre-trained student, causing the teacher's intra-class distribution to adapt to the student's capacity (measured by increasing cross-covariance between teacher and student predictions). The fine-tuned teacher then supervises a student trained from scratch with the standard KD loss, requiring no architectural modifications or extra parameters at inference time.

## Results

On CIFAR-100 with ResNet20 student, the proposed AID method achieves 72.70% top-1 accuracy with a ResNet110×4 teacher (97× size ratio), outperforming vanilla KD (69.76%) by ~2.9% and the best baseline DKD (71.13%) by ~1.6%. A key finding is that while all existing methods see student performance *decrease* as teacher size grows (e.g., MLKD drops from 72.19% with ResNet56 to 51.73% with ResNet110×4), AID consistently *improves* with larger teachers, reaching a plateau around 72.7%. On ImageNet, AID achieves 72.26% (ResNet50→ResNet18) and 72.01% (ResNet34→ResNet18), surpassing DKD (72.04% and 71.70%) and STD (71.78% and 71.42%). On Oxford-IIIT Pet, AID reaches 87.23% (ResNet101→ResNet18), beating STD (86.77%) by 0.46%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AID outperforms all 14 baselines across every tested configuration. The most dramatic wins are against MLKD [15], which collapses from 72.19% (ResNet56 teacher) to 51.73% (ResNet110×4 teacher) on CIFAR-100, while AID holds steady at 72.70%; and against SemCKD [1] and SimKD [2], which degrade to 27–50% in extreme capacity-gap settings (Table 1). Against the strongest logit-based baselines DKD [37] and STD [32], AID gains 1.5–1.6% on the large-capacity-gap setting (ResNet110×4→ResNet20: 72.70% vs. 71.13% and 71.05%). Feature-based methods FitNet [28] and RKD [24] are also consistently beaten (e.g., 76.66% vs. 75.18% for FitNet, 76.87% vs. 74.86% for RKD in Table 3). Capacity-gap-specific methods TAKD [22] and DGKD [31] require training multiple mid-sized teachers and still trail AID by 0.5–1.2% while incurring 2–4× the runtime (179 min vs. 326–700 min, Table 7). On ImageNet, AID edges out DKD by 0.22% (ResNet50→ResNet18) and CRD by 1.11% (ResNet34→ResNet18), confirming gains at scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Hinton et al.'s KL-divergence-based knowledge distillation framework, but departs from prior capacity-gap remedies (TAKD, DGKD) that insert mid-sized teacher models as intermediaries, and from retraining-based approaches (MSE, early-stopping) that modify the teacher from scratch. Its key conceptual departure is a decomposition of the KD loss into class-wise similarity (a label-smoothing-like term robust to teacher size) and intra-class distribution (a sample-difficulty signal sensitive to teacher capacity), identifying the latter as the root cause of the capacity gap. Rather than changing the student or adding auxiliary losses, the paper proposes fine-tuning the pre-trained teacher's intra-class distribution using a pre-trained student, a direction not explored in prior KD literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decomposition provides the first mechanistic explanation for why large teachers underperform mid-sized ones in KD, shifting the field's understanding from a heuristic observation to a principled diagnosis. Practically, the AID method allows practitioners to leverage the strongest available teacher without the capacity-gap penalty, at a modest fine-tuning cost (179 min vs. 334 min for full retraining). Open questions include whether the intra-class distribution insight generalizes to feature-based distillation, non-CNN architectures (e.g., transformers), and whether the fine-tuning objective could be made more targeted than reusing the full KD loss. The requirement for a pre-trained student model, while cheap, adds a dependency that may complicate deployment in settings where the student architecture changes frequently.
