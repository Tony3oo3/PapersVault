## Problem

Traditional ranking-based text-to-video retrieval returns the most similar videos, but in autonomous driving evaluation, test conditions are hard constraints: a video missing even one queried element must be excluded, while extra unqueried content should be acceptable. No existing retrieval method handles this 'inclusive' matching requirement, where partial matches with high overall similarity are incorrectly retrieved and videos with all conditions plus additional content are incorrectly ranked lower.

## Input / Output

The system takes a natural language query listing k scene conditions (e.g., 'pedestrian crossing, bus, narrow road, rainy day') and a database of 2-second driving video clips. Each video is pre-processed into a 6-element caption via LLaVA-NeXT-34B, augmented with LLM-extracted partial objects and negative objects. The output is a binary retrieval decision per video (score above threshold h) plus a continuous similarity score in [0,1] representing the fraction of query conditions satisfied, making the threshold interpretable: setting h to (k-1)/k retrieves videos meeting at least k-1 of k conditions.

## Method

CARIM reformulates text-to-video retrieval as text-to-text matching by captioning videos with a VLM, eliminating modality alignment. The scoring model applies scaled dot-product attention at the object level: each query embedding attends over all caption embeddings to identify its best-matched key, and the final score is the mean cosine similarity between each transformed query feature and its argmax key feature. Training uses a three-part contrastive objective—L_pos pushes positive-pair scores to 1, L_neg pushes any query containing a negative object to 0, and L_self regularizes full-length positive queries to counteract the combinatorial imbalance between positive and negative pair counts. A novel data curation strategy generates hard negatives via Adaptive Negative Injection (adding 1–l negative objects to positive subsets) and Balanced Sampling (matching object counts), enabling the model to reject videos missing even a single condition despite high overall similarity.

## Results

On the DRAMA dataset (480 test clips), CARIM achieves an average F1-score of 73.5%, precision of 78.3%, and recall of 80.6% across 1–5 query objects, substantially outperforming SBERT-RB (F1 31.7%) and DRL (F1 5.4%). CARIM attains 99.3% Negative Accuracy (correctly returning no results for negative queries) versus 43.9% for SBERT-RB and 3.2% for DRL. Inference time is 3.58 s for the full test set, compared to 6.23 s for SBERT-RB and 257.1 s for DRL. For 5-object queries (hard-negative regime), CARIM reaches 94.0% F1 and 96.9% N-Acc, while SBERT-RB drops to 54.4% F1 and 5.6% N-Acc.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CARIM is compared against two baselines: DRL [24], a state-of-the-art text-to-video retrieval model fine-tuned on DRAMA, and SBERT-RB, a text-matching baseline that computes cosine similarity between SBERT embeddings of the query and video captions. CARIM dominates both baselines on every reported metric. Against SBERT-RB, CARIM improves average F1-score by 41.8 points (73.5 vs. 31.7) and Negative Accuracy by 55.4 points (99.3 vs. 43.9); the gap widens sharply in the hard-negative regime (5 objects), where SBERT-RB's N-Acc collapses to 5.6% while CARIM maintains 96.9%. Against DRL, the advantage is even more pronounced: CARIM achieves 73.5% F1 versus DRL's 5.4%, and inference is roughly 72× faster (3.58 s vs. 257.1 s). DRL's poor performance is attributed to its inability to learn discriminative representations in the driving domain, where similar objects (cars, pedestrians, crosswalks) appear in nearly every video. The paper notes that DRL's predecessor CLIP4Clip yields 0% accuracy on negative queries, motivating the authors to construct their own SBERT-based baseline rather than compare against additional prior T2VR models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant text-to-video retrieval paradigm (CLIP, CLIP4Clip, DRL) that requires explicit modality alignment between video and text feature spaces. Instead, it repurposes VLM-based captioning (in the spirit of GenAD and DriveVLM) to collapse the problem into text-to-text matching, eliminating the alignment bottleneck. The 'inclusive' retrieval formulation—requiring all query conditions to be satisfied as a subset—has no direct precedent in the retrieval literature, where ranking by global similarity is the standard objective. The attention-based object-level scoring mechanism draws on scaled dot-product attention but is specifically designed to produce interpretable per-condition satisfaction scores rather than a single global similarity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For autonomous driving validation, test scenarios often impose hard constraints (e.g., 'pedestrian AND bus AND rainy day'), making ranking-based retrieval fundamentally misaligned with the use case; CARIM's threshold-based inclusive retrieval directly addresses this gap. The 70× inference speedup over DRL (3.58s vs 257.1s) makes large-scale scene search practical. However, evaluation is confined to a single small dataset (DRAMA, ~2.7k clips) with only two baselines (one of which, DRL, performs very poorly), leaving open questions about scalability to multi-million-clip databases, generalization to other domains, and sensitivity to VLM caption quality. The reliance on LLaVA-NeXT-34B and LLaMA-3 for caption generation also means retrieval quality is upper-bounded by these models' scene understanding.
