## Problem

Existing Astronaut Photography Localization (APL) models are trained exclusively on satellite imagery, ignoring the 300,000+ manually geolocated astronaut photos that are the actual target of the task. This domain mismatch limits retrieval performance, as the model never learns to bridge the visual gap between oblique, variably-illuminated astronaut photos and nadir satellite tiles. The paper asks whether leveraging astronaut photos during training can close this gap and substantially improve localization accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250333/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single astronaut RGB photo (with optional timestamp to constrain the search to the ~20M sqkm visible from the ISS, or a worldwide search of 510M sqkm if the timestamp is corrupted) and returns a ranked list of matching satellite images from a 5.2M-image Sentinel-2 database (zoom levels 8–12), from which the geographic footprint (four corner lat/lon coordinates) is derived. Queries span an extreme range of ground coverage (40 to 1.36M sqkm) and include oblique views, night imagery, and occlusions from ISS hardware—conditions absent from the nadir satellite database.

## Method

AstroLoc uses a DINO-v2-base backbone with SALAD aggregation and a linear projection to 2048-d features, trained with two complementary contrastive losses. The first is a Query-Satellite Pairwise Loss applied to 865k astronaut–satellite image pairs (IoU > 0.2), with explicit attraction and repulsion terms that pull matching cross-domain pairs together and push non-matching images apart. The second is a Multi-Similarity loss with Unsupervised Mining (MUM): satellite images are k-means clustered in feature space, astronaut queries are assigned to clusters, and training batches are sampled from clusters weighted by how many queries fall in each—so visually salient regions (glaciers, volcanoes) are oversampled while featureless regions (open ocean, desert) are undersampled. This mining strategy is the first to use one distribution (queries) to inform sampling from another (database) without requiring query labels. The two losses are summed with equal weight, and the full pipeline achieves >99% recall@100 on standard APL benchmarks and 96.8% on a worldwide search setting.

## Results

On the six standard APL test sets (Texas, Alps, California, Gobi Desert, Amazon, Toshka Lakes), AstroLoc achieves a mean R@1 of 96.4% and R@100 of 99.7%, representing a ~35% average improvement in R@1 over the previous state-of-the-art (EarthLoc++). On the newly proposed extended test sets (e.g., Texas-L with 21k queries over 179k database images), AstroLoc still reaches R@1 of 91.1% and R@100 of 98.5%, consistently above 96% R@100 across all six regions. For the lost-in-space satellite problem, AstroLoc achieves 52.7% R@1, outperforming all other methods by at least 45 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AstroLoc decisively outperforms all five baselines across every test set and metric. Against the closest competitor EarthLoc++ (same architecture, trained only on satellite images), AstroLoc gains 16–26 percentage points in R@1 (e.g., 96.1 vs. 80.0 on Texas, 94.6 vs. 67.6 on Gobi Desert) and pushes R@100 from 94–98% to 99.1–99.9%. AnyLoc, a universal place-recognition model, trails AstroLoc by 49–66 points in R@1 (e.g., 44.1 vs. 96.1 on Texas), confirming that domain-specific training with astronaut photos is critical. The UAV-localization baselines (OGCL UAV-View, MBEG) and self-supervised geospatial models (TorchGeo with SeCo/GASSL) are far behind, with R@1 values below 23% on most sets, highlighting the difficulty of transferring cross-domain retrieval from lower-altitude or purely self-supervised settings to the astronaut-to-satellite gap. On the extended test sets (Table 3), AstroLoc maintains its lead over EarthLoc++ by 19–22 points in R@1 (e.g., 91.1 vs. 72.4 on Texas-L), and on the worldwide search task (Table 7) it achieves 86.9% R@1 versus EarthLoc's 40.3%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on EarthLoc (Berton et al., 2024), the first APL-focused image retrieval model, and AnyLoc (Keetha et al., 2023), a universal place recognition system, both of which were trained exclusively on satellite imagery. The key departure is the introduction of two training techniques that exploit the 300k manually weakly-labeled astronaut photos: a cross-domain pairwise contrastive loss on astronaut-satellite image pairs, and a novel Unsupervised Mining (MUM) strategy that uses the spatial distribution of astronaut queries to weight the sampling of satellite image clusters. The annotation pipeline that converts weak point labels to full footprints relies on SuperPoint + LightGlue matching within the EarthMatch framework, and the backbone (DINO-v2-base + SALAD) follows the AnyLoc architecture while being over 10× lighter.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 35% average recall@1 improvement and consistent >99% recall@100 on existing benchmarks effectively saturate the APL task, prompting the authors to propose more challenging extended test sets. Practically, AstroLoc has already been deployed to localize 500k astronaut photos, and the authors anticipate the ISS photo backlog will be nearly cleared for the first time since the station's launch. The Unsupervised Mining technique—using one distribution (queries) to inform sampling from another (database)—is a generalizable idea that could benefit other cross-domain retrieval problems. Open questions include handling the remaining failure cases, scaling to the full 5M unlabeled astronaut photos (the mining technique does not require labels), and the worldwide search setting where unreliable timestamps force a global search over 881k images.
