## Problem

FedAvg aggregates local models using only client sample sizes as weights, producing a single global model that cannot capture class-specific information encoded in deep network pathways under non-IID data. Existing personalized FL methods address this but incur substantial overhead through pairwise model collaboration, additional model downloads, or strong clustering assumptions about client group structures, making them impractical for resource-constrained cross-device deployments.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard image classification inputs (MNIST 28×28 grayscale, CIFAR-10/100 32×32 RGB, Tiny ImageNet 64×64 RGB) partitioned across 20 clients with non-IID class distributions (pathological: each client holds only a subset of classes; practical: Dirichlet(α=0.1) sampling). The output is a personalized model per client, and the method additionally extracts an approximated class distribution from the ℓ₂-norms of output-layer weight vectors, which serves as a privacy-preserving proxy for the true empirical distribution shared with the server.

## Method

cwFedAvg replaces FedAvg's single global model with K class-specific global models via a two-step aggregation: first, local models are aggregated per class using weights proportional to each client's share of that class's total samples (q_{i,j} = n_{i,j} / Σ n_{i,j}); second, each client's personalized model is formed by combining the K class-specific global models weighted by that client's own class distribution. The Weight Distribution Regularizer (WDR) adds a penalty term ‖p_i − p̃_i‖₂ to the local loss, where p̃_i is derived from normalized ℓ₂-norms of output-layer weight vectors, forcing the network to encode class-specific information in its weights and enabling the approximated distribution to replace the true distribution for privacy-preserving server-side aggregation. The method maintains identical communication cost to FedAvg (2·Σ per round) and can be selectively applied to only the output layer or upper layers, where class-specific features are concentrated, to reduce server memory while preserving performance.

## Results

On the pathological setting, cwFedAvg (Output) achieves 91.23% on CIFAR-10, 67.50% on CIFAR-100, and 99.52% on MNIST, substantially outperforming FedAvg (60.68%, 28.22%, 98.70%) and all PFL baselines. Under the practical setting (α=0.1), cwFedAvg reaches 88.65% on CIFAR-10, 56.29% on CIFAR-100, and 41.38% on Tiny ImageNet, with the largest margins over baselines appearing on high-class-count datasets (e.g., +8.64% over FedAMP on CIFAR-100 and +11.43% on Tiny ImageNet). The method maintains FedAvg-level communication cost (2·Σ per round) while delivering these gains without additional local training or pairwise model downloads.

## Baselines & Comparisons

cwFedAvg (Output) outperforms all eight baselines on CIFAR-100 and Tiny ImageNet under both pathological and practical settings, with the largest gaps on high-class-count data: +8.64% over FedAMP (47.65%) and +9.49% over FedUV (46.80%) on CIFAR-100 practical, and +11.43% over FedAMP (29.95%) on Tiny ImageNet practical. On CIFAR-10 practical, FedAMP narrowly edges cwFedAvg by 0.81% (89.46% vs. 88.65%), the only case where cwFedAvg does not lead. On CIFAR-10 pathological, cwFedAvg leads FedFomo (90.76%) by 0.47% and FedAMP (88.82%) by 2.41%. Clustering-based methods (CFL, IFCA) and regularization-based methods (FedNH, FedUV) trail significantly on non-IID data, with CFL and FedNH often performing at or below FedAvg levels. On MNIST, all methods converge to ~99% accuracy, making differentiation minimal. The advantage of cwFedAvg scales with the number of classes and data heterogeneity, consistent with its class-wise aggregation design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends FedAvg by decomposing its single global model into K class-specific global models, each created by aggregating local models weighted by per-class sample proportions rather than total sample sizes. The class-wise aggregation is motivated by prior observations that deep networks encode class-specific information in output-layer weight magnitudes and critical pathways (Anand et al., 1993; Kang et al., 2020; Khakzar et al., 2021). The Weight Distribution Regularizer (WDR) is a novel loss term that strengthens the correlation between empirical class distributions and output-layer weight norms, drawing on the gradient-class correlation theorem of Anand et al. but applied to weights rather than gradients. Unlike aggregation-based PFL methods (FedAMP, FedFomo) that require pairwise model downloads or clustering-based methods (CFL, IFCA) that assume discrete client groups, cwFedAvg avoids both by performing per-class averaging on the server side with no additional client-side computation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

cwFedAvg offers a practically attractive middle ground: it achieves strong personalization on highly non-IID data while preserving FedAvg's communication cost and avoiding the quadratic overhead of pairwise collaboration or the structural assumptions of clustering. The WDR's dual role—improving class-specific pathway encoding and enabling privacy-preserving distribution sharing via weight-norm approximation—makes the approach applicable beyond personalization to tasks like client selection. A key limitation is the server-side storage of K global models, though the finding that applying cwFedAvg only to the output layer yields the best performance (Table 3) substantially mitigates this. Open questions include how the method behaves with very large class counts (e.g., 1000+ classes in open-vocabulary settings), whether the output-layer-only strategy generalizes to non-classification tasks, and whether the WDR's effectiveness depends on the specific network architecture.
