## Problem

AI for tumor segmentation is bottlenecked by the scarcity of large, per-voxel annotated CT datasets, since each annotation requires expert radiologists and takes 4–40 minutes per scan. Two critical questions remain unanswered: how many real annotated scans are needed before performance saturates, and can synthetic data steepen the scaling curve to reduce annotation burden? Additionally, no public dataset previously provided voxel-level annotations for esophageal or uterine tumors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251005/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3D abdominal CT volumes (10,134 scans totaling 4.7 million slices from 89 hospitals across 18 countries) and produces per-voxel binary segmentation masks for six tumor types (liver, pancreas, kidney, colon, esophagus, uterus) alongside tumor-level and patient-level detection sensitivity. The dataset spans tumor radii from 0 to 100 mm, categorized into tiny (≤5 mm), small (5–10 mm), medium (10–20 mm), and large (≥20 mm) groups, with 5,709+ early-stage (<20 mm) liver tumors included. Evaluation uses DSC, NSD, and sensitivity on both in-distribution validation sets and four external OOD datasets without fine-tuning or domain adaptation.

## Method

The paper introduces AbdomenAtlas 2.0, the largest public human-annotated tumor segmentation dataset (13,223 tumor instances across six organs), built using a four-stage SMART-Annotator pipeline that leverages a pre-trained segmentation model, FROC-curve-based threshold selection for high sensitivity (>90%), senior radiologist false-positive removal, and junior radiologist boundary revision—reducing per-scan annotation time from ~5 minutes to ~5 seconds. The core empirical contribution is a systematic data-scaling study on a proprietary 3,000-scan pancreatic dataset showing that in-distribution DSC plateaus after ~1,500 real scans, but adding 3× synthetic tumors (generated via DiffTumor with a 4:2:1 small:medium:large ratio) reaches equivalent performance using only 500 real scans. In contrast, out-of-distribution generalization shows no saturation even at 3,000 scans and continues to benefit from synthetic data augmentation, establishing that data diversity—not just quantity—drives robustness. The segmentation backbone is ResEncM (an nnU-Net variant), trained end-to-end in a supervised fashion on the combined real and synthetic data.

## Results

On the in-distribution AbdomenAtlas 2.0 validation set, training ResEncM on the full dataset achieves DSC of 77.5 (liver), 65.8 (pancreas), 87.9 (kidney), 50.7 (colon), 61.2 (esophagus), and 80.1 (uterus), improving over the runner-up STU-Net-B by +4.9%, +8.8%, +3.1%, +3.6%, +7.3%, and +1.4% DSC respectively. On four out-of-distribution external datasets (3D-IRCADb, PANORAMA, Kipa, JHH), the same model gains +14.0%, +12.3%, +7.2%, and +5.6% DSC over the best prior method without any fine-tuning or domain adaptation. On the MSD Challenge leaderboard, AbdomenAtlas 2.0 secures the #1 position with 82.6 DSC on liver and 67.2 DSC on pancreas, surpassing the previous leader (Universal Model) by +3.2% and +4.9% DSC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against 13 baselines spanning CNN (nnU-Net, SegResNet), Transformer (UNETR, Swin UNETR), NAS (DiNTS, C2FNAS, Models Genesis), and large-scale pre-trained models (STU-Net-B, Universal Model, SuPreM). On in-distribution tests (Table 3), AbdomenAtlas 2.0-trained ResEncM wins on all six tumor types against every baseline, with the largest margins over STU-Net-B (+7.3% DSC on esophagus, +4.9% on liver) and nnU-Net (+15.8% on pancreas, +17.9% on esophagus). On out-of-distribution tests (Table 4), the advantage is even more pronounced: +14.0% DSC over STU-Net on 3D-IRCADb, +12.3% over SegResNet on PANORAMA, +7.2% over ResEncM on Kipa, and +5.6% over SegResNet on JHH. The previous MSD leaderboard leader, Universal Model [66], is surpassed by +3.2% DSC on liver and +4.9% on pancreas (Table 2). Notably, the gap widens for rarer tumor types (esophagus, uterus) where public training data is most scarce, confirming that dataset scale and diversity—not architecture—drive the gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the AbdomenAtlas 1.0 / SuPreM dataset [59] into a substantially larger, multi-organ tumor segmentation benchmark and introduces the SMART-Annotator pipeline to make per-voxel annotation of 10,000+ scans feasible. Conceptually, it transplants the data-scaling-law framework from NLP and vision [20, 51, 78, 85] into medical tumor segmentation, a domain where such systematic scaling studies were previously infeasible due to annotation cost. The synthetic-data component builds directly on DiffTumor [12], which the authors use to generate tumors at a 4:2:1 small:medium:large ratio, but the key departure is the empirical demonstration that synthetic tumors can substitute for 60–70% of real annotated scans in-distribution while still improving out-of-distribution generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that in-distribution performance plateaus at roughly 1,500 real scans—and that 500 real scans plus synthetic data reach the same ceiling—provides a concrete, actionable guideline for how much annotation effort is actually needed for a given tumor type, which has been a major bottleneck in the field. The OOD results (+14% DSC over the runner-up on four external datasets) demonstrate that data diversity across 89 hospitals and 18 countries matters more than raw quantity for generalization, a lesson that should reshape how future multi-center datasets are curated. Key open questions remain: whether the 1,500-scan plateau generalizes beyond pancreatic tumors and the ResEncM architecture, and whether DiffTumor-generated lesions are anatomically and radiomically realistic enough for clinical trust, particularly for infiltrative or early-stage cancers.
