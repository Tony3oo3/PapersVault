## Problem

Personalized Federated Continual Learning (PFCL) must simultaneously mitigate temporal catastrophic forgetting (within each client's sequential task stream) and spatial catastrophic forgetting (across heterogeneous clients), yet existing gradient correction methods rely solely on gradient direction to detect task conflicts, ignoring the asymmetric impact of gradient magnitude. This leads to suboptimal update directions that damage previously learned knowledge, particularly when old-task gradients are much smaller in norm than new-task gradients.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250951/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each client receives RGB images from its private task sequence (5 non-overlapping tasks drawn from CIFAR-10/100, MNIST, SVHN, FashionMNIST, Not-MNIST, FaceScrub, or TrafficSigns) along with a group-specific global model broadcast by the server. The method produces per-client personalized local models with task-specific classifiers, plus multiple group-level global models maintained on the server. A LeNet backbone is shared across tasks while new classifiers are initialized per task, with a memory buffer of representative samples (10% per task) retained for rehearsal.

## Method

FedAGC comprises three modules. On the client side, Centroid-Based Representative Sample Selection (CBRSS) builds a memory cache by clustering task features and retaining one representative per cluster, avoiding the sampling bias of random rehearsal. The Asymmetric Correction Gradient Updating (ACGU) module formulates the backbone update as a multi-objective optimization problem using an asymmetric distance metric D(g_A, g_B) = ‖g_A − g_B‖ / (‖g_B‖ + ‖g_A − g_B‖) that captures both direction and magnitude differences between old and new task gradients; the optimal update is a convex combination of a linear-scalarization solution and an MGDA balanced solution, weighted by γ. On the server side, Group Aggregation Gradient Updating (GAGU) computes a cosine-similarity matrix over uploaded gradient updates, then applies Ncut-style spectral clustering to partition clients into groups, each maintaining its own global model to isolate irrelevant knowledge while fusing related knowledge.

## Results

On the Non-IID setting (8 datasets, 10 clients, 5 tasks each), FedAGC achieves 79.71% average accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline FedMes (73.54%) by 6.17 points and FedER (74.38%) by 5.33 points. On the IID setting (single CIFAR-100, 20 tasks), FedAGC reaches 70.16% average accuracy versus FedMes's 67.13% and FedER's 66.44%. FedAGC also exhibits the lowest forgetting rate (F_t) across both settings, as shown in Figure 3, confirming superior resistance to both temporal and spatial catastrophic forgetting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedAGC outperforms all seven baselines in both the Non-IID and IID settings. Against the closest competitor FedMes (which also uses memory rehearsal and gradient correction), FedAGC gains 6.17% in average accuracy under Non-IID (79.71% vs. 73.54%) and 3.03% under IID (70.16% vs. 67.13%), with the gap driven primarily by the asymmetric gradient correction (ACGU) and centroid-based sample selection (CBRSS) modules. Compared to FedER (74.38% Non-IID), which uses random episodic memory without gradient correction, FedAGC improves by 5.33%, showing that structured rehearsal plus asymmetric correction is substantially more effective than naive replay. FedAGEM (72.01%) and FedCurv (64.91%) rely on symmetric gradient correction or curvature-based regularization and are outperformed by 7.70% and 14.80% respectively, confirming that ignoring gradient magnitude asymmetry leads to suboptimal updates. The non-CL baselines FedAvg (61.83%), FedProx (65.00%), and Ditto (67.12%) lag further behind, as they lack any mechanism to address temporal forgetting. Ablation results further show that removing ACGU causes the largest drop (−17.7% Non-IID), while removing GAGU or CBRSS yields smaller but consistent degradations (−2.76% and −1.15% respectively), validating the complementary roles of all three modules.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedAGC extends the gradient-correction lineage of A-GEM and its federated adaptation FedMes by replacing the symmetric inner-product conflict test with an asymmetric gradient distance that jointly accounts for both direction and magnitude, motivated by the observation that old-task gradients are typically much smaller in norm than new-task gradients. It further departs from FedMes's random memory sampling by introducing centroid-based representative sample selection (CBRSS) and from client-level personalization (Ditto, Per-FedAvg) by proposing group-level personalization via spectral clustering (Ncut) over uploaded gradient updates. The multi-objective optimization formulation draws on MGDA for balanced gradient descent, unifying the temporal and spatial forgetting mitigations into a single optimization framework for the first time in the PFCL setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By addressing spatial and temporal catastrophic forgetting simultaneously through a unified MOO framework, FedAGC offers a more principled treatment of PFCL than prior methods that handle only one dimension of forgetting. The asymmetric gradient distance provides a useful conceptual correction to the field's reliance on symmetric metrics (cosine similarity, Euclidean distance) in gradient projection methods. However, the evaluation is limited to small-scale image classification (LeNet backbone, 10–30 clients, 5 tasks, 8 small datasets), leaving open whether the approach scales to deeper architectures, larger client populations, or more complex modalities. The added computational cost of solving the MOO subproblem and performing spectral clustering each round also raises practical deployment questions.
