## Problem

In Fringe Projection Profilometry, camera defocusing causes the point spread function to convolve with non-uniform surface reflectivity at texture boundaries, biasing phase data toward higher-reflectivity regions and degrading 3D reconstruction accuracy. Existing corrections either require precise local PSF estimation that fails under rapid reflectivity changes, or rely on computationally intensive single-pixel imaging, leaving a gap for a stable, efficient method that handles complex textures without extra hardware.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N phase-shifting fringe patterns (typically 4-step) captured by an 800×600 camera from a 912×1140 DLP projector, plus the average intensity image for reflectivity recovery. Unusually, the captured patterns are re-processed offline through one and two additional Gaussian blur filters to synthesize three distinct blur levels, from which pairwise phase differences are extracted. The output is a corrected absolute phase map and a corresponding 3D point cloud, with the correction applied per-pixel at texture edges where reflectivity gradients are significant.

## Method

MFA first derives a closed-form error model showing the projector coordinate error at a reflectivity discontinuity is proportional to σ·sin(α)·(r₁−r₂)/(r₁+r₂), where σ is the camera PSF standard deviation, α is the angle between fringe direction and texture edge, and r₁, r₂ are adjacent reflectivities. The key insight is that applying successive Gaussian filters to the captured patterns increases the effective PSF variance by a known σ₀² each time, so the ratio of phase differences between the three blur levels (original, one-filter, two-filter) isolates σ algebraically without needing to know reflectivity or geometry. Once σ is calibrated, the reflectivity distribution is recovered via inverse Fourier transform of the average intensity image, and the full phase error is computed directly from the integral form of the error model rather than through a simplified linear correction, avoiding the over-correction observed with the simpler parameter-based approach.

## Results

On the calibration plate (the primary flat-object benchmark), MFA reduces MAE by 40.31% (0.645 → 0.385) and RMSE by 40.78% (1.079 → 0.639) relative to the uncorrected horizontal fringe projection (HFP) baseline. Across five additional objects (postcard, sphere, cylinder, bag, squirrel), MFA consistently achieves the lowest MAE and RMSE, with reductions ranging from 29% to 41% over HFP. On the sphere, MFA also reduces the fitted-diameter error by 71.23% (from 47.48 mm to 49.56 mm vs. the true 50.4 mm). In terms of runtime, MFA adds only 0.351 s for 10,000 pixels, making it the fastest correction method among all tested approaches.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MFA outperforms all four baselines on MAE across every tested object. The kernel estimation method (KE) [27] performs worst by a large margin (MAE 1.312, RMSE 10.824 on the calibration plate), confirming that generic deblurring kernels are unsuitable for fringe patterns. The inverse Fourier transform method (IFT) [26] and the one-dimensional error model (OEM) [7] provide moderate improvements over HFP but remain 30–40% behind MFA in MAE. The Taylor series expansion method (TSE) [30] is the closest competitor: on the calibration plate it achieves a slightly lower RMSE (0.538 vs. 0.639) but a marginally higher MAE (0.392 vs. 0.385); the authors attribute TSE's RMSE advantage to over-smoothing of high-frequency details, which degrades its performance on geometrically complex objects like the bag and squirrel models where MFA clearly leads. Overall, MFA achieves the best MAE in 6 out of 6 experiments and the best RMSE in 5 out of 6, while also being the fastest correction method (0.351 s vs. 0.367–8.756 s for the others).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PSF-based error correction lineage initiated by Rao and Da [20] and the one-dimensional simplification by Hu et al. [7], but departs from them by avoiding direct PSF estimation altogether. Instead of fitting a Gaussian to local edge data (which fails under rapid reflectivity changes) or resorting to computationally heavy single-pixel imaging [14, 15], MFA exploits the algebraic property that successive Gaussian convolutions add variances, enabling the PSF parameter σ to be calibrated purely from phase differences across controlled blur levels. The approach also builds on the authors' own prior bidirectional fringe projection work [3] for multi-path error analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For industrial FPP systems where textured surfaces (printed labels, painted parts, biological specimens) are the norm rather than the exception, a 40% error reduction at near-zero additional runtime (0.35 s vs. 2–8 s for competing methods) is practically significant. The main open limitation is the assumption of a circular, isotropic PSF, which breaks down at sharp depth discontinuities (e.g., the squirrel eye in Fig. 10), leaving coupled geometry-and-texture error modeling as a clear future direction. More broadly, the paper demonstrates that a well-posed signal-processing trick (variance-additivity of Gaussian blurs) can replace expensive PSF estimation, a principle that may transfer to other structured-light or interferometric metrology settings.
