## Problem

Self-supervised vision foundation models (DINOv2, Stable Diffusion) capture semantic correspondences but fail to distinguish geometrically symmetric parts (left/right eyes, front/back legs), a limitation rooted in their flip-invariant training. Existing geometry-aware methods like Geo address this but are impractically slow (~2 s per prediction) and rely on argmax-based assignments that ignore occlusions and provide sparse supervision. The paper also shows that the standard PCK metric masks geometric confusion by counting ambiguous mid-point predictions as correct.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252535/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

At training time, the method consumes a pair of RGB images (source S and target T) with sparse keypoint annotations indicating positive, negative, and occluded (bin) correspondences. At inference, it is a single forward pass through the encoder producing a dense per-patch feature vector (DINOv2-B patch tokens, 14×14 grid) with no separate matching module; correspondences are recovered post-hoc via cosine similarity between patch features. The features are designed to be geometrically discriminative, so that symmetric parts (e.g., left vs. right eye) yield low inter-patch similarity while true correspondences yield high similarity.

## Method

GECO freezes a DINOv2-B backbone and adapts it with a rank-10 LoRA adapter, keeping the model lightweight (332 MB, ~40 ms per image). The core novelty is a parameter-free differentiable optimal transport layer that replaces argmax matching: cosine similarities between all source and target patches form a score matrix augmented with a dustbin row/column (threshold z=0.3) to model occlusions, and the Sinkhorn algorithm (10 iterations, λ=0.1, α=β=10) solves an entropy- and KL-regularized unbalanced OT problem to produce a soft assignment matrix. Training uses a binary cross-entropy loss applied only to the sparse annotated entries (positive, negative, and bin pairs) of this assignment matrix, providing dense gradient signals to all patches without any cross-attention or learnable matching parameters. This design makes GECO a feature encoder rather than a matcher, enabling 30 fps inference and state-of-the-art PCK on PFPascal (+6.0%), APK (+6.2%), and CUB (+4.1%) while being ~50× faster than Geo.

## Results

GECO achieves state-of-the-art PCK@0.1 on three of four datasets: 92.1% on PFPascal (+6.0% over Geo), 86.7% on APK (+6.2% over Geo), and 92.5% on CUB (+4.1% over Geo), while running at ~40 ms per image — roughly two orders of magnitude faster than Geo (~2127 ms). On Spair-71k, GECO (85.2%) trails Geo (90.1%) by 4.9%, the only dataset where the prior method wins. The PGCK geometric-ambiguity analysis on APK further shows GECO achieves 40.0% unambiguous correct predictions (vs. 36.2% for Geo) and only 2.3% unambiguous wrong predictions (vs. 2.9% for Geo), confirming superior geometric discrimination.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the closest competitor Geo [64], GECO wins on PFPascal (92.1% vs 86.1%), APK (86.7% vs 80.5%), and CUB (92.5% vs 88.4%) by 6.0, 6.2, and 4.1 PCK points respectively, while being ~50× faster (40 ms vs 2127 ms). GECO loses to Geo only on Spair-71k (85.2% vs 90.1%), which the authors attribute to Geo's stronger generalization on that particular dataset. Compared to the lightweight DINOv2-B backbone [42], GECO improves PCK by 6.1 points on PFPascal, 13.7 on APK, and 18.1 on Spair, while adding less than 0.5 ms of inference overhead. Sphere [37], trained solely on Spair, achieves competitive CUB scores (92.1%) but lags significantly on PFPascal (88.5%) and APK (75.2%), and is also ~50× slower. The PGCK geometric-ambiguity breakdown on APK (Table 1) shows GECO outperforms Geo in unambiguous correct matching (40.0% vs 36.2%) and produces fewer unambiguous wrong predictions (2.3% vs 2.9%), while DINOv2-B and DINO [4] remain far behind (24.5% and 17.0% unambiguous correct, respectively). Notably, Geo underperforms even DINOv2-S on CUB (88.4% vs 92.4%), indicating it does not generalize as a purpose model, whereas GECO maintains strong zero-shot performance on the unseen CUB dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

GECO builds on the DINOv2 backbone (frozen, adapted via LoRA) and repurposes the Sinkhorn optimal transport algorithm not as a matching module (as in SuperGlue or LOFTR) but as a parameter-free, differentiable loss function that provides dense supervision across all patches. It departs from the argmax-based sparse contrastive losses used by Geo and DHF, which the authors argue ignore occlusions and provide weak gradients, by formulating correspondence as an unbalanced OT problem with a dustbin for occluded keypoints. Unlike prior geometry-aware methods that rely on heavy Stable Diffusion features or cross-attention between image pairs, GECO is a pure feature encoder whose OT component contributes only to training, keeping inference at the speed of the lightweight backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 98% inference speedup (40 ms vs. ~2.1 s) while matching or exceeding SOTA PCK makes geometry-aware correspondence practical for real-time and large-scale applications, removing the primary barrier to deploying such features. The PGCK decomposition and the finding that roughly 50% of predictions fall in a geometrically ambiguous zone reveal that the standard PCK metric systematically overestimates geometric understanding, a methodological insight that should influence how the field evaluates correspondence. Limitations include reliance on sparse keypoint annotations and restriction to categories with available labels, leaving open whether the approach can be extended to fully unsupervised or open-vocabulary settings.
