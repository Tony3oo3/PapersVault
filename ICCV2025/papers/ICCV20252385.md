## Problem

Gender bias in VLMs is typically evaluated using benchmarks with gender-annotated real-world images, but these benchmarks contain spurious correlations between gender and non-gender features (color, objects, background). The paper asks whether these spurious features distort bias measurements, i.e., whether current evaluations capture model responses to dataset artifacts rather than true gender bias.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252385/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes RGB images from four gender-annotated benchmarks (COCO-gender, FACET, MIAP, PHASE) paired with gender-neutral text prompts (personality questions for VQA, occupation/adjective prompts for retrieval). It produces two types of outputs: standard bias scores (YGap for generative VLMs measuring the probability difference of 'Yes' responses between genders, and MaxSkew@1000 for CLIP measuring retrieval imbalance) and feature-sensitivity scores (Δ), which quantify the relative percentage shift in bias when a specific non-gender feature is perturbed at weak/middle/strong intensities (e.g., masking 10/20/30% of objects).

## Method

The paper proposes a two-stage controlled-intervention framework. First, it identifies spurious features by training gender classifiers on feature-extracted inputs (8×8 downsampled images for color, HSV-averaged patches for lighting, multi-hot object detection vectors, and person-masked backgrounds), confirming that color, object, and background are spuriously correlated with gender across all four benchmarks. Second, it applies targeted perturbations to each non-gender feature (hue shifts, brightness changes, random object masking, background blurring) while preserving perceived gender, then measures Δ—the relative change in bias score between original and perturbed images. A theoretical analysis formalizes why G→B correlation causes distortion: perturbing B alters the conditional distribution p(B′|G=g) ≠ p(B|G=g), shifting the output distribution and inflating Δ, whereas without correlation the marginal distribution is preserved and Δ remains small. The framework is applied to six generative VLMs (LLaVA-1.5, LLaVA-OneVision, Qwen2-VL, InternVL-2.5, mPLUG-Owl3, EAGLE) and five CLIP variants (ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14, ViT-H/14, SigLIP, CoCa), revealing that even 10% object masking shifts bias scores by up to 175% in generative VLMs and 43% in CLIP variants.

## Results

Across four gender bias benchmarks (COCO-gender, FACET, MIAP, PHASE), perturbing spurious non-gender features dramatically shifts bias scores: object masking (10–30%) and background blurring cause the largest distortions, with YGap shifting up to 175.84% for LLaVA-OneVision-7B on COCO-gender and MaxSkew@1000 shifting up to 51.01% for CLIP ViT-B/32 on PHASE. Even minimal perturbations (10% object masking) shift LLaVA-OneVision by 20.1% and CLIP-ViT-B/32 by 16.6% on average. In contrast, lighting perturbations—corresponding to the feature least correlated with gender—produce shifts mostly below 10%, confirming the theoretical prediction that stronger G→B correlations yield larger B→O distortions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so it does not propose a new model to benchmark against baselines. Instead, it compares the sensitivity of six generative VLMs (LLaVA-1.5-7B, LLaVA-OneVision-7B, Qwen2-VL-7B, InternVL-2.5-8B, mPLUG-Owl3-7B, EAGLE-8B) and five CLIP variants (ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14, ViT-H/14, SigLIP-ViT-S/14, CoCa-ViT-L/14) to four types of feature perturbations. Object and background perturbations consistently produce the largest bias shifts across all models, while lighting perturbations remain minimal (<10%), mirroring the spurious-correlation strengths in Table 1. LLaVA-OneVision-7B and mPLUG-Owl3-7B are the most vulnerable to background blurring on COCO-gender (Δ > 165%), whereas Qwen2-VL-7B is relatively robust to background changes (Δ ≈ 9–10%) but highly sensitive to object masking on FACET (Δ up to 68.58%). For CLIP variants, the correlation between spurious-feature strength and bias shift is stronger (Pearson r = 0.54) than for generative VLMs (r = 0.31), suggesting CLIP models rely more heavily on spurious cues. The paper also shows that feature perturbations can completely reorder model rankings (e.g., Qwen2-VL shifts from least to second-most biased under object perturbation on FACET), undermining the reliability of current bias leaderboards.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper connects two previously separate threads: Meister et al.'s identification of spurious gender–feature correlations in visual datasets (color, objects, background) and the now-standard bias evaluation protocols for VLMs (YGap for generative models via Girrbach et al., MaxSkew for CLIP retrieval via Geyik et al.). While Meister et al. demonstrated that such spurious features exist, and prior VLM bias work adopted them as evaluation benchmarks, no prior study quantified how much these features actually distort the measured bias scores. The paper's conceptual departure is treating the bias evaluation pipeline itself as the object of analysis, using controlled feature perturbations to isolate the B→O pathway and show it dominates the G→O signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that masking just 10% of objects or weakly blurring backgrounds can shift bias scores by 20–175% and completely reorder model rankings undermines confidence in current gender bias leaderboards and model selection decisions. The paper argues that creating spurious-feature-free benchmarks is fundamentally infeasible (unannotated features, overadjustment risk, generative-model biases), leaving the proposed two-dimensional protocol (bias score + feature-sensitivity Δ) as the practical path forward. Open questions include whether more sophisticated perturbations (e.g., counterfactual image editing) would yield tighter bounds, how to weight Δ across feature types in a composite reliability score, and whether the same spurious-feature sensitivity extends to other demographic axes (race, age, disability) with comparable magnitude.
