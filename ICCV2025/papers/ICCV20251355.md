## Problem

Existing jailbreak attacks on multimodal large language models (MLLMs) concentrate the full malicious prompt within a single modality—either text or image—making it straightforward for the model's safety alignment to detect the harmful intent and refuse. This single-modality confinement limits attack success rates, particularly against well-aligned closed-source models. The paper asks whether distributing harmful semantics across both modalities, so that neither alone triggers detection, can substantially improve black-box jailbreak performance.

## Input / Output

The attack takes a target malicious prompt and produces a pair of inputs: a text prompt containing one harmless fragment of the prompt plus two optimized sub-prompts (understanding-enhancing and inducing), and a typographic image (512×100) embedding the other fragment, optionally accompanied by a text-to-image generated image (512×512) for contextual relevance. The MLLM is queried in a pure black-box setting (only outputs observed), and success is judged by a separate LLM (HarmBench) checking whether the response is affirmative and contains the requested harmful content, yielding an attack success rate (ASR) over 350 SafeBench samples (or 70 for the tiny-SafeBench subset used on closed-source APIs).

## Method

HIMRD combines two strategies. First, a multimodal risk distribution step uses an auxiliary LLM (o1-mini) to split the malicious prompt into two fragments, each individually harmless, then embeds one in a typographic image and the other in the text prompt, so that no single modality carries complete harmful semantics (verified by a judge function showing near-zero refusal rates per modality). Second, a heuristic-induced search iteratively refines two text components: an understanding-enhancing prompt (p_u) that guides the MLLM to reconstruct the full malicious prompt within its own completion, and an inducing prompt (p_i) that shifts the model's output distribution toward affirmative responses over refusals. Each component is optimized in a separate phase (up to 5 iterations), where the search function S(·) takes the list of previously failed prompts as context to generate a new candidate, reducing the search space while preserving path diversity. The attack succeeds when both the understanding score and the inducing score exceed predefined thresholds, requiring only black-box access to the target model.

## Results

On the SafeBench dataset (350 samples across 7 harmful categories), HIMRD achieves an average attack success rate (ASR) of 90.86% across seven open-source MLLMs, substantially outperforming the best black-box baseline FigStep (63.59%) and all white-box methods (UMK 20.20%, VAE 30.88%, BAP 38.86%). On the tiny-SafeBench (70 samples) targeting three closed-source MLLMs, HIMRD reaches an average ASR of 68.09%, compared to 38.57% for FigStep and 48.09% for MM-SafeBench. Peak individual results include 96.57% ASR on LLaVA-V1.6, 95.71% on Qwen-VL-Max, and 94.57% on DeepSeek-VL.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HIMRD outperforms all six baselines on average across both open-source and closed-source MLLMs. Among black-box methods, FigStep (63.59% avg) and MM-SafeBench (43.76% avg) are the strongest baselines, yet HIMRD exceeds them by 27.27 and 47.10 percentage points respectively on open-source models. The white-box methods (UMK, VAE, BAP) and grey-box HADES all remain below 40% average ASR on open-source models, with their performance degrading significantly when transferred to models other than their training target. The sole exception is MiniGPT-4, where the white-box UMK method achieves 87.71% ASR versus HIMRD's 84.57%, likely because MiniGPT-4's weaker safety alignment makes single-modality attacks already highly effective. On closed-source models, HIMRD's advantage is most pronounced on GPT-4o-0513 (44.29% vs. 18.57% for FigStep) and Qwen-VL-Max (95.71% vs. 65.71% for FigStep), demonstrating that distributing malicious semantics across modalities is particularly effective against models with strong single-modality safety filters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the distribution-reconstruction (DRA) jailbreak paradigm from Liu et al. (USENIX Security 2024), which splits a malicious prompt into harmless fragments for a single-modality LLM, into a cross-modal setting where the two fragments are embedded separately into text and image inputs. It departs from prior MLLM jailbreak attacks (FigStep, MM-SafeBench, BAP, UMK, HADES) that concentrate all harmful semantics in one modality, arguing this makes detection trivial. The heuristic-induced search strategy introduces a two-stage iterative refinement—first an understanding-enhancing prompt to ensure the model reconstructs the full malicious intent, then an inducing prompt to flip the output from refusal to affirmation—differing from prior single-prompt optimization approaches.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 90% average ASR across seven open-source MLLMs and 68% on closed-source models (including 44% on GPT-4o) exposes a structural vulnerability: current safety alignment mechanisms operate largely per-modality and fail when harmful semantics are split across modalities. This raises the urgent open question of how to build cross-modal safety detection that can reassemble distributed harmful intent before generation. The paper does not propose defenses, leaving the field to develop countermeasures; it also remains unclear whether the approach generalizes beyond two modalities or to models with explicitly cross-modal safety training.
