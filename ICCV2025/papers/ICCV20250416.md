## Problem

Existing dynamic 3D scene reconstruction methods (NeRF-based and 3DGS-based) can only interpolate novel views within the observed time window and fail to extrapolate physically meaningful future frames because they do not encode underlying physics. Physics-informed approaches (PINN losses) require additional supervision such as object masks or type labels and sacrifice training efficiency, while explicit physics-model methods are limited to specific object types (fluids, elastic bodies). The paper addresses how to learn general motion physics from multi-view RGB videos alone, without any human-provided labels, to enable future frame prediction from arbitrary viewpoints.

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB video sequences with known camera poses and intrinsics as input; time is normalized to [0, 1] where [0, 0.7] is the training interval and [0.7, 1.0] is the extrapolation test interval. Outputs include rendered future frames at arbitrary novel viewpoints, a canonical 3D Gaussian scene representation, per-particle physical parameters (equivalent center velocity/acceleration, rotation vector, angular acceleration), and—via K-means clustering of those parameters—automatic segmentation of objects or parts by motion pattern. The framework is scene-specific (per-scene optimization) and requires no masks, object types, or physics labels during training.

## Method

TRACE treats each 3D Gaussian kernel as a rigid particle and learns, via a lightweight MLP (f_trd), a complete translation-rotation dynamics system per particle: the rotation center's position, an equivalent (compounded) center velocity and acceleration, plus the particle's rotation vector and angular acceleration. The equivalent parametrization (Eq. 4) decouples the otherwise coupled center velocity and position, making optimization tractable. Future motion is derived analytically using a Runge-Kutta 2nd-order integrator with Rodrigues' formula for quaternion updates, requiring no PINN losses or additional physics constraints during training. An auxiliary deformation field (DefGS or 4DGS) is trained in parallel to provide stable geometric supervision at early epochs, since the dynamics module alone is too unstable to optimize from scratch. Training uses only photometric losses (L1 + SSIM) between rendered and ground-truth frames at two nearby timestamps, with the dynamics module predicting the displacement between them.

## Results

TRACE achieves the best future frame extrapolation across all four datasets, with PSNR of 31.60 (Dynamic Object), 34.82 (Dynamic Indoor Scene), 29.34 (NVIDIA Dynamic Scene), and 33.48 (Dynamic Multipart). Compared to the strongest baselines NVFi and DefGS_nvfi, TRACE gains approximately 3–4 dB PSNR on the complex Dynamic Indoor Scene and Dynamic Multipart datasets. Against NeRF- and 3DGS-based interpolation methods (T-NeRF, D-NeRF, TiNeuVox, DefGS, 4DGS), TRACE achieves roughly 10 dB higher PSNR, confirming that without explicit physics learning these methods fundamentally fail at extrapolation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRACE outperforms all seven baselines on every dataset for future frame extrapolation. Against the closest competitor NVFi (which uses PINN losses with a NeRF backbone), TRACE gains +4.0 dB PSNR on Dynamic Object (31.60 vs 27.59), +5.1 dB on Dynamic Indoor Scene (34.82 vs 29.75), +0.9 dB on NVIDIA (29.34 vs 28.46), and +8.2 dB on Dynamic Multipart (33.48 vs 25.24). Against DefGS_nvfi (DefGS backbone + NVFi velocity field, the strongest 3DGS-based baseline), TRACE still leads by +2.8 dB (Dynamic Object), +3.7 dB (Dynamic Indoor Scene), +1.8 dB (NVIDIA), and +5.0 dB (Dynamic Multipart). The gap is largest on the Dynamic Multipart dataset, where multiple object parts undergo radically different motions, confirming that explicit per-particle translation-rotation dynamics handle complex multi-body motion far better than PINN soft constraints. All NeRF-based interpolation methods (T-NeRF, D-NeRF, TiNeuVox) and 3DGS-based methods (DefGS, 4DGS) without physics learning lag by roughly 10 dB PSNR, demonstrating they cannot extrapolate beyond observed time.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRACE builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) as its scene representation and on deformable Gaussian methods (DefGS, 4DGS) as auxiliary deformation fields, but departs fundamentally from both PINN-based approaches (NVFi, PAC-NeRF) and physics-model-based methods (spring-mass, fluid simulators) in how physics is incorporated. Rather than using PDE-derived soft constraints in the loss or encoding object-specific physics systems, TRACE learns explicit per-particle translation-rotation dynamics parameters (center velocity/acceleration, angular velocity/angular acceleration) via simple MLPs, then derives future motion through classical mechanics and Runge-Kutta integration. This positions it as a middle path: more general than spring/fluid models (which are limited to specific materials) yet more physically grounded than PINN losses (which require masks and sacrifice training efficiency).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to extrapolate future frames from multi-view dynamic videos without any human annotations (masks, object types) is practically important for robotics, mixed reality, and embodied AI where such labels are unavailable. The continual learning experiment showing stable adaptation to rapidly changing dynamics (e.g., robot manipulation, harmonic oscillation) suggests a path toward real-time perception in dynamic environments. However, the method remains scene-specific (no cross-scene generalization), relies on known camera poses, and the 2nd-order dynamics assumption may fail for highly non-smooth events (explosions, collisions). Open questions include whether higher-order or learned-order dynamics improve extrapolation, whether the framework can be made generalizable across scenes, and how it handles occlusions and topological changes.
