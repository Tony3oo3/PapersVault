## Problem

Existing vision-based system identification methods recover object geometry and physical laws by integrating differentiable rendering with simulation, but they all require predefined material priors (e.g., neo-Hookean elasticity, viscosity models) that must be specified per scene. This dependence on hand-crafted constitutive laws limits generalization to in-the-wild scenarios where material properties are unknown. MASIV is the first framework to perform system identification in a fully material-agnostic manner, inferring object dynamics without assuming any scene-specific material type.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250752/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes multi-view RGB video sequences (T frames, ~14–30 frames per viewpoint on the evaluated datasets) along with object masks extracted via off-the-shelf matting (SAM, RVM). It produces a canonical 3D Gaussian representation with a learned deformation network, a pair of neural constitutive models (elastic and plastic) parameterized as MLPs, and simulated particle trajectories over N = T/τ simulation steps (τ = 1/200 frame duration, yielding hundreds of steps per frame). The output digital twin can be rolled forward to predict future states under novel initial conditions or forces.

## Method

MASIV operates in three phases: (1) dynamic Gaussian reconstruction using a canonical point cloud warped by a motion-basis deformation network trained with L1+SSIM photometric losses; (2) continuum trajectory estimation, where internal volumes are filled to form solid continuum particles and the deformation network is fine-tuned with Chamfer Distance to produce dense per-simulation-step trajectories via temporal interpolation of the learned motion basis; (3) material-agnostic system identification, where NCLaw-inspired neural constitutive models (rotation-invariant MLPs with no bias, enforcing frame indifference and undeformed-state equilibrium) are optimized via analysis-by-synthesis using MPM time integration. The key technical contribution is dense geometric guidance: instead of supervising the constitutive models only at the T observed frames, the fine-tuned deformation network provides pseudo-ground-truth particle positions at all N simulation steps, yielding temporally dense constraints that stabilize optimization and prevent physically implausible intermediate deformations. Training uses RAdam to optimize initial velocity (100 steps) and constitutive parameters (1000 steps) with L1 losses on simulated particle positions and rendered silhouettes.

## Results

On observable state simulation, MASIV achieves the best mean Chamfer Distance across both datasets: 0.210 on PAC-NeRF (vs. 0.212 for GIC, 0.278 for PAC-NeRF) and 0.13 on Spring-Gaus Synthetic (vs. 0.17 for GIC, 0.85 for Spring-Gaus). On the Spring-Gaus Real subset, MASIV attains 41.12 PSNR and 0.997 SSIM, outperforming GIC (38.11 PSNR, 0.996 SSIM) and Spring-Gaus (32.90 PSNR, 0.994 SSIM). On future state simulation, MASIV achieves 1.20 mean CD, beating Spring-Gaus (5.16), PAC-NeRF (17.94), and NeuMA (170.56), though slightly trailing GIC (0.95 CD, 22.98 PSNR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MASIV outperforms all four baselines on observable state simulation (Table 1), achieving the lowest mean CD on both PAC-NeRF (0.210 vs. GIC's 0.212) and Spring-Gaus Synthetic (0.13 vs. GIC's 0.17), despite being the only method that does not require a predefined material prior. On the Spring-Gaus Real subset (Table 2), MASIV leads in both PSNR (41.12 vs. GIC's 38.11) and SSIM (0.997 vs. 0.996), with a particularly large margin over Spring-Gaus (32.90 PSNR) and NeuMA (26.51 PSNR). On future state simulation (Table 3), MASIV substantially beats PAC-NeRF (17.94 CD), Spring-Gaus (5.16 CD), and NeuMA (170.56 CD), but is slightly outperformed by GIC (0.95 CD, 22.98 PSNR, 0.930 SSIM vs. MASIV's 1.20 CD, 21.91 PSNR, 0.915 SSIM), which the authors attribute to GIC's access to a known constitutive model acting as an oracle. NeuMA exhibits extreme instability on future prediction (e.g., 1147.4 CD on Cream), likely due to its sparse per-frame pixel supervision, whereas MASIV's dense trajectory guidance (hundreds of constraints per frame) yields stable convergence across all material categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

MASIV builds directly on the NCLaw neural constitutive law framework [59] and the Material Point Method simulation backbone, but departs from all prior vision-based system identification methods (PAC-NeRF, GIC, NeuMA, Spring-Gaus) by eliminating the requirement for hand-crafted, material-specific constitutive models. The key technical departure is the introduction of dense geometric guidance: rather than relying on sparse per-frame visual supervision (which prior methods use to optimize a small set of known material parameters), MASIV reconstructs continuum particle trajectories over hundreds of simulation steps via a fine-tuned deformation network, providing temporally rich motion constraints that stabilize the otherwise underdetermined learning of neural constitutive models. The dynamic Gaussian reconstruction phase follows the 3DGS + deformation network paradigm established by GIC and 4DGS, but extends it to fill internal volumes and enable trajectory interpolation between observed frames.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By removing the material-prior assumption, MASIV opens the door to system identification in in-the-wild scenarios where object composition is unknown or heterogeneous, a setting where all current methods fail. The dense trajectory guidance technique is broadly applicable to any differentiable simulation setting where full state observations are unavailable, potentially benefiting neural PDE solvers and other analysis-by-synthesis pipelines. Key limitations remain: the method requires multi-view input, assumes spatially coherent internal deformation (breaking down for granular media or multiphase fluids), and does not disentangle material properties from external forces. The slight underperformance relative to GIC on future-state prediction under scarce data highlights the ongoing trade-off between flexibility (learned constitutive models) and inductive bias (hand-crafted physics).
