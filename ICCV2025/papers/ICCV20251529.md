## Problem

Existing video tokenizers either directly quantize flattened 3D patches (preserving spatial-temporal locality but yielding high token counts) or compress flattened patches into adaptive queries (achieving compression but struggling to learn intertwined spatial and temporal information, resulting in poor reconstruction). Additionally, language-based codebooks applied to video tokenization treat all visual content uniformly, ignoring the fundamental semantic distinction between static appearance and dynamic motion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251529/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 17-frame video clips at 256×256 resolution (patchified into 32×32 spatial patches with temporal patch size 4) and single images for the spatial branch. It outputs 1,280 decoupled tokens — 256 spatial tokens encoding the first frame and 1,024 temporal tokens encoding frame-wise residuals — quantized against part-of-speech-partitioned CLIP text codebooks (10,481 noun/adjective entries for spatial, 11,139 verb/adverb entries for temporal). The spatial branch can be independently fine-tuned for image reconstruction, and the discrete tokens serve as input to autoregressive generators (VideoGPT) or LLM-based few-shot classifiers.

## Method

SweetTok introduces a Decoupled Query AutoEncoder (DQAE) that separates video compression into two independent cross-attention query modules: a spatial encoder that compresses first-frame patches into 256 learnable spatial queries, and a temporal encoder that compresses frame-wise residuals (first frame minus subsequent frames) into 1,024 learnable temporal queries. Reconstruction proceeds in two stages — the spatial decoder reconstructs the first frame, which is then tiled and fed alongside quantized temporal tokens into the temporal decoder for full video recovery. The Motion-enhanced Language Codebook (MLC) partitions CLIP text embeddings by part of speech (nouns/adjectives for spatial, verbs/adverbs for temporal), projects them into visual latent space via a graph convolutional network whose edges encode word co-occurrence in video captions, and performs nearest-neighbor quantization separately for each branch. Training combines L2, LPIPS, VQ commitment, and GAN losses, with the spatial branch optionally fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k for image reconstruction.

## Results

On UCF-101 (256×256, 17 frames), SweetTok with 1,280 tokens (256 spatial + 1,024 temporal) achieves rFVD 20.46, a 42.8% improvement over LARP-L (35.15) at a comparable token budget, and a 52.3% improvement over OmniTok (42) which uses 4× more tokens (5,120). For class-conditional video generation, SweetTok reduces gFVD from 99 (LARP-L) to 84 (15.1% gain) and from 191 (OmniTok) to 84 (56% gain) with a 650M VideoGPT generator. On ImageNet image reconstruction, the decoupled spatial branch fine-tuned on ImageNet achieves rFID 0.37 with 1,024 tokens, surpassing LlamaGen-8 (0.59) by 37.3%, and rFID 0.73 with only 256 tokens, beating TiTok (1.71) by 27.8%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LARP (the closest query-based tokenizer), SweetTok wins decisively on UCF-101 reconstruction (rFVD 20.46 vs. 35.15 for LARP-L, 42.8% improvement) while using a smaller model (128M vs. 193M params) and slightly more tokens (1280 vs. 1024); on K-600 the gap narrows (25 vs. 23), suggesting LARP retains a slight edge on more diverse data. Against OmniTok, SweetTok achieves a 52.3% rFVD improvement on UCF-101 (20.46 vs. 42) while using only 25% of the tokens (1,280 vs. 5,120), and a 56% gFVD improvement in generation (84 vs. 191). For image reconstruction on ImageNet, SweetTok's spatial branch outperforms TiTok by 27.8% at 256 tokens (rFID 0.73 vs. 1.71) and LlamaGen-8 by 37.3% at 1,024 tokens (0.37 vs. 0.59). In few-shot classification, SweetTok surpasses SPAE (90.8% vs. 89.9% avg on miniImageNet) and V2L (90.8% vs. 87.6%), and leads video action recognition on UCF-101 (90.1% vs. ARN 83.1% and HF-AR 86.4%). The only comparison where SweetTok does not clearly win is K-600 reconstruction, where LARP-L (rFVD 23) slightly edges out SweetTok (25), though the difference is modest.

## Relation to Prior Work

SweetTok extends the query-based video tokenizer lineage initiated by LARP and Q-Former, which compress flattened video patches into adaptive learnable queries via cross-attention. Its key architectural departure is the Decoupled Query AutoEncoder (DQAE), which splits compression into separate spatial (first-frame) and temporal (frame-residual) branches rather than jointly compressing flattened spatiotemporal patches. The Motion-enhanced Language Codebook (MLC) builds on prior language-codebook work (SPAE, VQCT, LQAE) but introduces a part-of-speech split—nouns/adjectives for spatial tokens, verbs/adverbs for temporal tokens—addressing a gap those image-focused methods leave for video motion semantics. The spatial-temporal decomposition strategy itself follows OmniTok and MAGVIT, but the decoupled query mechanism and MLC are novel contributions layered on top.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The decoupling insight—that jointly compressing intertwined spatial and temporal information hampers the decoder's ability to learn motion—provides a principled rationale for the architecture and a reusable design pattern for future video tokenizers. The MLC's part-of-speech split is a lightweight but effective way to inject motion-aware semantics into discrete tokens, enabling LLM-based few-shot understanding without additional training. However, evaluations are limited to 256×256 resolution and 17-frame clips, and the language codebook depends on caption quality and a graph-convolutional projection that adds training complexity. Open questions include whether the decoupling strategy scales to longer videos, higher resolutions, and whether the MLC generalizes beyond action-centric datasets like UCF-101 to more diverse video domains.
