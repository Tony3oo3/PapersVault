## Problem

Existing brain-image alignment methods rely on MSE or contrastive losses that enforce point-to-point correspondence between brain voxel embeddings and image embeddings, capturing only diagonal correlations while ignoring global distributional structure. This leads to misalignment, redundancy, and an incomplete understanding of how neural information is actually organized. The paper asks whether a distributional alignment framework can both improve decoding performance and reveal the redundancy and synergy inherent in brain information processing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250083/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes fMRI voxel signals (from the NSD dataset, 750 images per 3-second session across 30–40 hours of scanning per subject) and the corresponding RGB stimulus images (COCO). A transformer-based fMRI encoder maps valid brain voxels into N-dimensional embeddings, while a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 encoder produces M image embeddings (N = M in practice). The aligned voxel embeddings are then fed directly into the Shikra multimodal LLM in place of image embeddings, producing free-form image captions evaluated on ten standard metrics (BLEU-1/2/3/4, METEOR, ROUGE-L, CIDEr, SPICE, CLIP-S, RefCLIP-S).

## Method

The core contribution is an OT-based alignment loss that generalizes MSE: a cost matrix C_ij = ‖x_i − y_j‖² is constructed between brain voxel and image embeddings, and the optimal transport plan γ* is solved under uniform marginal constraints with a tunable mass ratio λ that controls the fraction of mass transported. The resulting OT loss decomposes into a diagonal term (recovering MSE when γ* is the identity) plus off-diagonal terms that capture global distributional matching, and the paper proves MSE is a strict special case. The fMRI encoder is a Perceiver-style transformer using cross-attention to condense high-dimensional voxel signals into a latent bottleneck, trained end-to-end against the frozen CLIP image embeddings. For downstream brain captioning, the learned voxel embeddings replace image embeddings in the Shikra LLM. The framework further enables analysis of neural redundancy (by varying λ) and synergy (by masking Brodmann's-area regions: Vision, Place, Face, Word, Body), revealing that multi-region cooperation substantially outperforms any single region.

## Results

On the NSD dataset (subject 1 test set, 982 samples), OT-S1 achieves state-of-the-art performance across all 10 evaluation metrics for brain captioning: CIDEr 71.52, BLEU-1 63.25, METEOR 21.61, ROUGE-L 47.12, SPICE 14.41, and CLIP-S 70.35. In cross-subject training (subjects 1, 2, 5, 7), OT-Cross Subjects further improves CIDEr to 72.90 and BLEU-1 to 64.12. These results surpass the previous best method UMBRAE by an average of 6.11% (single-subject) and 3.81% (cross-subject) across all metrics, while the gold standard (feeding the actual image into Shikra) achieves CIDEr 161.43 and BLEU-1 82.38.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's OT-based method outperforms all six baselines across every reported metric. Against the closest competitor UMBRAE [71], OT-S1 improves CIDEr from 51.93 to 71.52 (+37.7%) and BLEU-1 from 57.63 to 63.25 (+9.8%) in single-subject training; in cross-subject training, OT-Cross improves CIDEr from 61.06 to 72.90 (+19.4%) over UMBRAE. The ablation UMBRAE(MSE) confirms that replacing OT with MSE in the same architecture degrades performance by 6.28% (S1) and 4.27% (Cross) on average, isolating the gain to the transport-based loss. Earlier methods—SDRecon [63], OneLLM [22], BrainCap [17], and Shen et al. [58]—are substantially outperformed, with CIDEr gaps of 57.7, 48.5, 30.2, and 22.0 points respectively. UniBrain [37] reports only METEOR (16.90) and ROUGE (22.20), both well below OT-S1's 21.61 and 47.12. Notably, even the best brain-signal result (OT-Cross, CIDEr 72.90) remains far below the gold standard of feeding the actual image to Shikra (CIDEr 161.43), highlighting the remaining gap between neural decoding and direct visual input.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on UMBRAE (Xia et al., ECCV 2024) for its Perceiver-based fMRI encoder architecture and evaluation protocol, but departs from the broader brain-image alignment literature by replacing point-wise MSE or contrastive InfoNCE losses with an Optimal Transport (Wasserstein-2) formulation. The key conceptual move is showing that MSE corresponds to the degenerate OT case where the transport plan is fixed to the identity matrix, thereby positioning OT as a strict generalization that captures off-diagonal (global) matching structure. The method also draws on the classical Monge–Kantorovich OT framework and applies it in a cross-modal setting (fMRI voxels ↔ CLIP image embeddings), while the downstream evaluation leverages the Shikra multimodal LLM for brain captioning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical proof that MSE is a special case of OT provides a clean, principled justification for why distributional alignment should outperform point-wise alignment in cross-modal brain decoding, and the λ-mass-ratio parameter offers a practical knob for controlling information flow that empirically reveals redundancy in neural representations. The Brodmann's-area ablations (Place > Vision in importance; multi-region synergy > single-region) confirm and quantify existing neuroscience findings through a deep-learning lens, but the evidence is limited to a single dataset (NSD, 4 subjects) and a single LLM (Shikra), leaving open whether the OT advantage persists across other brain-decoding tasks (image generation, object detection) or recording modalities (EEG). A key open question is whether the transport-plan structure learned here can be exploited for interpretability beyond post-hoc region masking.
