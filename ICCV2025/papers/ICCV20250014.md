## Problem

Model-heterogeneous federated learning (MHFL) requires clients with structurally different models to collaborate, but existing approaches (model decoupling, knowledge distillation, mutual training) transfer only limited global knowledge to clients by sharing partial homogeneous parameters or distillation losses, resulting in inferior local model generalization. The paper identifies that parameter angles (gradient directions) carry sufficient semantic information for generalization, yet no prior MHFL method exploits this for cross-client knowledge sharing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250014/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each client receives images of varying resolution (28×28 grayscale for Fashion-MNIST, 32×32 RGB for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 RGB for Tiny-ImageNet) and produces a class label from 10, 100, or 200 classes. The unusual aspect is that each client runs a structurally different CNN (or ResNet for Tiny-ImageNet) with a different penultimate-layer dimension r (50 or 500), and the only shared artifact is a homogeneous r×r representation angle square matrix that is sparsified to m diagonal blocks before upload, making the communicated payload scale with the client's bandwidth budget rather than its model size.

## Method

FedRAL embeds a randomly initialized homogeneous square matrix A ∈ R^{r×r} into each client's heterogeneous model: the local representation R is multiplied by A to produce a generalization-enhanced R̃, and the sum R + R̃ is fed to the prediction head, with A trained end-to-end alongside the local model. To reduce communication cost, an adaptive diagonal sparsification strategy retains only m diagonal blocks (each r/m × r/m) of A, where m is chosen per client to match its bandwidth. On the server, sparsified matrices from all clients are fused via element-wise weighted averaging with weights proportional to each client's data volume, which both reflects the global data distribution on diagonal elements and scales down non-overlapping off-diagonal elements to avoid negative interference. Unlike Orthogonal Fine-Tuning, FedRAL relaxes the orthogonality constraint and avoids expensive matrix inverse operations, trading strict geometric guarantees for computational efficiency while still capturing representation angle information that preserves semantic generalization.

## Results

FedRAL achieves the highest average test accuracy across all 8 dataset/heterogeneity settings. The most notable gains are on CIFAR-100: 58.70% under pathological non-IID (vs. 53.67% for FedProto, the second-best) and 11.70% under practical non-IID (vs. 9.37% for FedTGP). On practical Fashion-MNIST, FedRAL reaches 78.58%, a 4.5-point improvement over the next-best FedProto (74.06%). In terms of efficiency, FedRAL reduces total communication cost by up to 12.43× and computational cost by up to 6.49× compared to the state-of-the-art FedTGP baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedRAL outperforms all five baselines on every setting where they converge. Against the strongest baseline FedTGP, FedRAL gains +1.10 pp on pathological CIFAR-10 (93.60 vs. 92.50), +5.50 pp on pathological CIFAR-100 (58.70 vs. 53.20), +2.02 pp on practical CIFAR-10 (42.97 vs. 40.95), and +2.33 pp on practical CIFAR-100 (11.70 vs. 9.37). FedProto is competitive on Fashion-MNIST (99.02 vs. 99.54 pathological) but trails FedRAL by 4.5 pp on practical Fashion-MNIST (74.06 vs. 78.58). LG-FedAvg (model decoupling) is consistently weaker, losing 2.9 pp to FedRAL on pathological CIFAR-100 (45.83 vs. 58.70). FedKD (knowledge distillation) and FedAPEN (mutual learning) perform substantially worse, with FedAPEN failing to converge on 7 of 8 settings and FedKD dropping below 60% on Fashion-MNIST. FedRAL also achieves these accuracy gains while reducing communication cost by up to 12.43× and computational cost by up to 6.49× relative to FedTGP.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedRAL adapts the Orthogonal Fine-Tuning (OFT) concept [44]—which showed that fine-tuning parameter angles preserves semantic information better than parameter fine-tuning—to the model-heterogeneous federated learning (MHFL) setting. It departs from OFT by relaxing the strict orthogonality constraint (replacing Cayley parameterization with a randomly initialized square matrix) and by applying angle learning to extracted representations rather than raw parameters. Within the MHFL literature, it positions itself against four established families (heterogeneous subnets, model decoupling, knowledge distillation, mutual learning) by arguing that all transfer only limited generalized knowledge, and instead shares a homogeneous representation angle matrix that encodes richer semantic information across structurally heterogeneous clients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap in MHFL: existing methods transfer only partial parameters or distillation losses, which the authors argue constrains local model generalization. The adaptive diagonal sparsification is a practical contribution for real-world deployments where clients have heterogeneous communication bandwidths. However, the evaluation is confined to image classification on four relatively small datasets (up to Tiny-ImageNet with 200 classes), and the method requires a shared representation dimension across clients (homogeneous prediction head), limiting its applicability to truly arbitrary architectures. Open questions include scalability to large-scale vision or language tasks, theoretical guarantees on the quality of the aggregated angle matrix, and whether the approach extends to generative or multi-modal FL settings.
