## Problem

Current legal frameworks recognize AI-generated works as copyrightable when they exhibit originality and substantial human intellectual input, yet no systematic, quantifiable method exists to determine whether an AI artist's output constitutes a distinctive style or infringes on a human artist's. The paper identifies three legally grounded criteria—stylistic consistency, creative uniqueness, and prompt-accuracy—that must be jointly verified, but prior work has focused almost exclusively on protecting human artists' styles rather than evaluating whether AI-generated works can independently hold copyright.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a set of AI-generated images produced by a single model with fixed stylistic parameters but varied prompt content, the corresponding text prompts, and a reference corpus of 364,297 human artworks from 2,785 artists spanning five art domains. Input prompts are augmented via entity substitution to expand the sample set for robust evaluation. The output is a binary copyright judgment (protected / not protected), a style cluster assignment relative to the human-art reference, and a natural-language report generated by GPT-4o that cross-analyzes the AI cluster against the nearest human clusters with actionable recommendations.

## Method

The core technical contribution is Description-Guided Clustering (DGC), which encodes each artwork with CLIP's visual encoder and its GPT-4o-generated description with CLIP's text encoder, then trains two clustering heads (one per modality) via cross-modal mutual distillation: for each sample, the KL divergence between its visual-modality soft assignment and the text-modality assignments of its nearest neighbors (and vice versa) is minimized, encouraging consistent clustering across modalities. Auxiliary confidence and cluster-entropy losses prevent degenerate solutions. Copyright judgment is formalized as three threshold checks on the resulting clusters: intra-cluster visual variance (consistency, εc=0.60), minimum inter-cluster distance to human-art clusters (uniqueness, εd=0.25), and Adjusted Mutual Information between image-based and text-based clusterings (accuracy, εa=0.50). The full ArtBulb pipeline feeds these quantitative cluster statistics into GPT-4o via chain-of-thought prompting to produce an interpretable, legally grounded report, and iteratively integrates newly validated AI styles back into the reference database.

## Results

On the AICD benchmark, DGC w/ ArtBulb achieves an average accuracy of 0.86 and F1 of 0.88 across six art categories (Western Art, Chinese Art, Comic Line-Drawing, Video Game, Children Book, Graphic Design), outperforming the best classification-based baseline ARTSAVANT (0.80 ACC, 0.80 F1) by 6 percentage points in accuracy. For the underlying style clustering task on WikiART, DGC reaches 41.45% ACC, 61.20 NMI, and 15.72 ARI, surpassing the strongest prior method SIC (36.43% ACC, 59.13 NMI, 14.36 ARI). Pure VLMs (GPT-4o, Qwen2-VL, DeepSeek-VL) achieve only 0.34–0.42 average accuracy on copyright classification, far below the clustering-based approach.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DGC w/ ArtBulb outperforms all 13 baselines on the AICD copyright classification task. Against the two classification-based copyright methods, it beats ARTSAVANT (0.80→0.86 ACC, 0.80→0.88 F1) and CSD (0.77→0.86 ACC, 0.79→0.88 F1) by 6 and 9 percentage points in accuracy respectively, with the largest gains on Comic Line-Drawing (0.88 vs. 0.84) and Video Game (0.87 vs. 0.75). Pure VLMs (GPT-4o at 0.42, Qwen2-VL at 0.34, DeepSeek-VL at 0.34 average ACC) are dramatically outperformed, confirming that direct image-pair comparison is insufficient for style-based copyright judgment. On the underlying clustering task (Table 1), DGC surpasses all eight deep clustering baselines (DEC, DAC, SCAN, SIC, MOCOv3, DINOv2, CLIP, BLIP2) across all five art datasets, with the largest margin over SIC on WikiART (+5.02 ACC, +2.07 NMI) and over CLIP k-means on Contemporary Art (+8.71 ACC, +5.83 NMI). The paper argues that classification-based methods (CSD, ARTSAVANT) cannot handle novel AI art styles without retraining, whereas the clustering approach naturally accommodates new styles and supports dynamic database expansion.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the classification-based copyright detection paradigm established by CSD and ARTSAVANT, which frame style infringement as a supervised binary classification problem. Instead, it reframes AI art copyright determination as a multimodal clustering task, arguing that classification approaches cannot accommodate new AI art styles without retraining and cannot dynamically incorporate newly validated copyrighted works. The Description-Guided Clustering (DGC) method repurposes cross-modal mutual distillation (previously used in self-supervised representation learning) by leveraging GPT-4-generated textual descriptions as a semantic anchor to align visual and text-based cluster assignments. The broader protection-at-source literature (watermarking, unlearning, Glaze) is acknowledged but positioned as orthogonal, since this work addresses the downstream legal question of whether an AI work already produced qualifies for independent copyright.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The AICD dataset is the first benchmark specifically designed for AI art copyright evaluation, annotated by both artists and legal experts, filling a clear gap that will support future research at the law-technology intersection. The clustering-based formulation offers a practical advantage over classification: the reference database grows organically as new AI styles are validated, making the system adaptable without retraining. However, the approach depends heavily on GPT-4o for both description generation and final interpretive judgment, raising questions about circularity and jurisdictional portability of the three criteria (consistency, uniqueness, accuracy). Open questions include whether the manually tuned thresholds (εc, εd, εa) generalize across art domains, how the framework handles video or music, and whether courts would accept an automated clustering pipeline as evidence in infringement proceedings.
