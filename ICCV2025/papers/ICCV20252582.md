## Problem

Continuous maritime ship tracking requires matching the same vessel across images captured at different times, angles, and by different sensors, but existing methods rely on geostationary satellites (low spatial resolution, weather-dependent) or video satellites (60–180 s clips, limited coverage). No dataset or method specifically addresses cross-modal re-identification between optical and SAR imagery, which is essential for leveraging low-Earth-orbit constellations to achieve all-weather, all-time, wide-area ship tracking.

## Input / Output

The method consumes cropped ship images resized to 128×256 pixels: optical images are 3-channel 8-bit RGB (0.75 m GSD) and SAR images are single-channel 32-bit floating-point (1 m GSD), plus a 3-dimensional ship size vector (normalized width, height, and aspect ratio) computed by multiplying pixel dimensions by GSD. The network outputs a 768-dimensional L2-normalized feature vector per image; identity matching is performed via Euclidean distance in this shared space, with the closest gallery entry assigned as the matched ship.

## Method

TransOSS is a fully transformer-based architecture that replaces the standard ViT patch embedding with a cross-modal dual-head tokenizer: separate linear layers embed optical and SAR patches independently before feeding them into a single modality-shared transformer encoder, suppressing modality-specific noise at the input stage while keeping parameter overhead minimal. Two auxiliary learnable embeddings are injected into the token sequence: a modality information embedding (a 2×768 matrix, one row per modality, scaled by a hyperparameter λ and added to every patch of that modality) to help the shared encoder produce modality-invariant features, and a ship size embedding (a linear projection of width, height, and aspect ratio) appended as an extra token to preserve scale information lost during resizing. Training follows a two-stage protocol: first, contrastive pre-training on ~56 K co-registered optical–SAR pairs from SEN1-2 and DFC23 using a CLIP-style symmetric cross-entropy loss with a learnable temperature; second, supervised fine-tuning on the HOSS ReID dataset with a combined ID classification loss and triplet loss over anchor–positive–negative groups.

## Results

On the HOSS ReID dataset, TransOSS with two-stage training achieves 57.4% mAP and 65.9% Rank-1 accuracy overall, outperforming the best baseline (VersReID at 49.3% mAP / 59.7% R1) by 8.1 and 6.2 percentage points respectively. In the cross-modal optical-to-SAR setting, TransOSS reaches 48.9% mAP and 33.8% R1, substantially exceeding the next-best method (DEEN at 31.3% mAP / 21.5% R1). Even without optical-SAR pretraining (TransOSS*), the method attains 49.4% mAP and 61.9% R1, already surpassing all ResNet-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TransOSS with two-stage training (57.4% mAP, 65.9% R1) outperforms all 11 baselines on the overall HOSS ReID benchmark, with the largest margin over VersReID (49.3% mAP) and TransReID (48.1% mAP) among Transformer-based methods, and over MCJA (47.1% mAP) among ResNet-based methods. The ResNet-based cross-modal ReID methods (CM-NAS, LbA, Hc-Tri, AGW, DEEN, MCJA), designed for infrared-visible person ReID, consistently underperform ViT-based models on this optical-SAR task, confirming that the fundamentally different imaging physics of SAR versus optical cannot be bridged by modules tuned for the smaller RGB-infrared gap. In the cross-modal optical-to-SAR direction, TransOSS (48.9% mAP, 33.8% R1) nearly doubles the mAP of the strongest competitor DEEN (31.3% mAP, 21.5% R1), while in the harder SAR-to-optical direction it still leads by 11.3 mAP points over DEEN (38.7% vs. 27.4%). Even the no-pretraining variant TransOSS* (49.4% mAP) already matches or exceeds VersReID and TransReID, indicating that the dual-head tokenizer and auxiliary embeddings contribute meaningfully independent of the contrastive pretraining stage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the object ReID paradigm—well established for pedestrians and vehicles in visible-infrared settings—to the fundamentally different optical-SAR modality pair for ships in remote sensing imagery. Architecturally, it builds on ViT (Dosovitskiy et al.) and adopts a CLIP-style contrastive pretraining strategy, but departs from the common two-backbone design in cross-modal ReID by using a lightweight dual-head tokenizer with a shared transformer encoder. The introduction of physically grounded auxiliary signals (ship size embeddings derived from GSD measurements) represents a departure from purely appearance-based ReID methods, leveraging the unique measurability of ground objects in satellite imagery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first dataset and baseline for cross-modal optical-SAR ship ReID, addressing a practical gap in maritime tracking where GEO satellites lack resolution and video satellites lack coverage. The demonstrated failure of visible-infrared ReID methods on optical-SAR pairs underscores that the modality gap here is governed by fundamentally different physics (passive reflection vs. active centimeter-wave scattering), not merely appearance variation. Key limitations include the small dataset scale (1,832 images, 449 tracks, 13 sequences), restriction to port/canal scenes, and the still-modest cross-modal accuracy (33.8% R1 for optical-to-SAR), leaving open whether the approach generalizes to open-ocean tracking, larger constellations, and longer temporal baselines.
