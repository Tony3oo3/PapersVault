## Problem

35mm film negatives suffer from channel-heterogeneous deterioration, where the blue channel (the outermost emulsion layer) degrades independently with spatially varying structural damage while R and G channels remain largely intact. No existing dataset or restoration method addresses this asymmetric, channel-specific degradation, and ground truth is unavailable because the negatives are already corrupted. This paper introduces the first dedicated dataset and evaluation protocol for this underexplored restoration task.

## Input / Output

The input is a full RGB film negative image, available in native 16-bit DNG format at 10,128×6,840 (7,200 dpi, ~500 MB per file) or a downsampled 8-bit LDR preview at ~1,322×892 used for all experiments. The output is a restored negative image, with evaluation focused specifically on the blue channel; R and G channels of restored outputs are replaced with originals before metric computation to isolate blue-channel performance. The reverse-printing module additionally produces a pseudo ground truth blue channel estimated from the corresponding printed photo (scanned at ~2,994×1,920, 600 dpi).

## Method

The core technical contribution is a 'reverse printing' pipeline that estimates pseudo ground truth for corrupted blue channels by learning a mapping f_θ from printed photos back to the negative domain. The function is trained on 130 blue-intact negatives (using NAFNet with MAE loss and Adam at lr=0.0001) to predict the blue channel from the printed photo's RGB channels plus the intact R/G channels of the negative, then applied to blue-corrupted negatives to generate estimated clean references. Data alignment between negatives and prints uses SIFT and LoFTR matching on low-res previews (with R/G average replacing the corrupted B channel), refined by brute-force window adjustment minimizing photometric difference after histogram-matching tone mapping. The evaluation protocol operates in two domains (negative and printed) using PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, FID, and patch-wise FID on a 30-image test set covering all six deterioration types. The paper benchmarks six generic restoration frameworks (DDNM, DDRM, PromptIR, DA-CLIP, DGP, BringOld) and two supervised baselines (NAFNet, Restormer), showing that existing methods fail to adequately correct channel-heterogeneous structural damage.

## Results

On the BlueNeg blue-corrupted test-split, supervised baselines trained on the pseudo ground truth substantially outperform all generic restoration frameworks: Restormer achieves PSNR 29.15 dB and SSIM 0.8730 in the negative domain, while the best generic method (BringOld) reaches only PSNR 23.59 dB and SSIM 0.8034. In the printed domain, Restormer attains PSNR 28.71 dB and SSIM 0.8497 versus BringOld's 25.72 dB and 0.7891. FID scores remain extremely high across all methods (262–268), confirming that no existing approach adequately handles channel-heterogeneous deterioration. Among generic methods, DA-CLIP leads on SSIM (0.8237) and LPIPS (0.2703) in the negative domain, while BringOld leads on PSNR (23.59) and printed-domain metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six generic restoration frameworks (DDNM, DDRM, PromptIR, DA-CLIP, DGP, BringOld) and two supervised baselines (NAFNet, Restormer) on the BlueNeg blue-corrupted test-split. Supervised baselines dominate: Restormer achieves PSNR 29.15 dB and SSIM 0.8730 in the negative domain, exceeding the best generic method BringOld (PSNR 23.59, SSIM 0.8034) by ~5.6 dB in PSNR. In the printed domain, Restormer (PSNR 28.71, SSIM 0.8497) outperforms BringOld (PSNR 25.72, SSIM 0.7891) by ~3 dB. Among generic methods, DA-CLIP achieves the highest SSIM (0.8237) and lowest LPIPS (0.2703) in the negative domain, while BringOld leads on PSNR (23.59) and printed-domain PSNR (25.72). DDRM consistently performs worst across most metrics (PSNR 15.59, SSIM 0.7145, LPIPS 0.3076 in the negative domain). The paper's central finding is that all generic methods fail to adequately restore channel-heterogeneous deterioration, with FID scores remaining above 262 for every method, underscoring the need for domain-specific restoration strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant assumption in image restoration that degradation is channel-homogeneous (affecting R, G, B equally), instead defining and studying channel-heterogeneous deterioration where spatially varying structural damage occurs independently on individual channels. It builds on the legacy photo restoration line (Mironică, Wan et al., Ivanova et al.) but those works target different artifacts (dust/scratch, uniform color fading) on printed photos or slides, not the physically separated channel layers of 35mm negatives. The 'reverse printing' pseudo-ground-truth estimation is a novel repurposing of the photographic printing pipeline in reverse, leveraging the fact that prints from the same negatives retain the original scene structure without channel-specific damage. No prior dataset or benchmark addresses this specific degradation type, so the paper carves new ground rather than extending an existing formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic failure of all six generic restoration frameworks (FID scores of 165–169, PSNR below 21) on BlueNeg confirms that channel-heterogeneous deterioration is genuinely outside the capability of current methods, motivating the development of specialized approaches. The reverse-printing evaluation protocol is a creative workaround for the absence of true ground truth, though its reliance on pseudo-GT limits the rigor of quantitative claims. Key open questions include whether the blue-channel-specific physical mechanism (outermost emulsion layer) generalizes to other film formats or channels, whether the small dataset (491 negatives, 1988–2002) is sufficient to train robust restoration models, and whether the pseudo-GT estimation introduces systematic biases that could mislead future method development. The paper also highlights the broader archival need: vast quantities of legacy negatives remain unrestored due to the lack of automated tools for this degradation type.
