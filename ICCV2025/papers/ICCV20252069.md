## Problem

Approximate machine unlearning (AMU) methods are widely adopted for efficient data removal, yet they leave implicit residuals in the model's loss landscape that existing version-independent privacy attacks (MIA-LiRA, MIA-UP) fail to detect because they only analyze output-distribution discrepancies. This paper reveals that these loss-landscape residuals persist across architectures and unlearning algorithms, creating an underexplored attack surface for membership inference on unlearned classes, samples, and concepts.

## Input / Output

The Reminiscence Attack (ReA) takes an unlearned model and a candidate OOD class or sample set, then performs targeted fine-tuning to produce a membership confidence score: for class-wise inference, the score is derived from the resonance index (iterations to reach 75% accuracy on the inferred class, normalized by total iterations); for sample-wise inference, it outputs a continuous MIA-LiRA confidence after a reminiscence fine-tuning step. The defense (OUR) takes a pre-trained model, the unlearned dataset, and the retained dataset, and produces final unlearned model parameters after two sequential training phases. In the image-generation setting, ReA is adapted to measure identity recovery via Identity Score Matching (ISM) exceeding 0.70 within a fixed iteration budget.

## Method

ReA exploits loss-landscape residuals through a 'reminiscence' process: for class-wise unlearning, it reintroduces the inferred OOD class into fine-tuning and measures how rapidly the model converges (resonance index), with a multi-learning-rate aggregation strategy for stability; for sample-wise unlearning, it fine-tunes on a pseudo-retained set at reduced learning rate before applying MIA-LiRA, exploiting the fact that unlearned samples recover accuracy abnormally faster than test data. The defense, Orthogonal Unlearning & Replay (OUR), operates in two phases: first, it enforces orthogonality between hidden-layer features of unlearned samples in the current model and their pre-unlearning counterparts (minimizing squared dot products across selected layers) to scrub deep-layer traces; second, it replays on the retained dataset with l1 regularization to drive the model to true convergence rather than a 'pseudo-converged' state that mimics retrained outputs while preserving latent residuals. This two-phase separation is critical—joint optimization of orthogonal scrubbing and utility preservation degrades both objectives, as confirmed by ablation studies.

## Results

On CIFAR-20 class-wise unlearning (ViT), ReA achieves up to 95.52% balanced accuracy (TPR@0.1 FPR) on RL and 95.27% on SalUn, substantially outperforming MIA-LiRA (max 89.02%) and MIA-UP (max 73.14%). For sample-wise unlearning on CIFAR-20, ReA reaches 75.30% on FF and 72.90% on GA, versus MIA-LiRA's best of 68.20%. The proposed OUR method reduces ReA attack accuracy to 52.28% (class-wise, CIFAR-20) and 53.80% (sample-wise, CIFAR-20), near the random-guess baseline of 50%, while maintaining unlearning efficacy (TA=51.98%, UA=0.00%, RA=99.97%) at only ~4.3% of full retraining time (78s vs. 1801s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ReA dominates both prior attacks (MIA-LiRA [7] and MIA-UP [28]) across nearly all unlearning methods: on CIFAR-20 class-wise, ReA exceeds MIA-LiRA by up to 35.52 points (RL: 95.52 vs. 50.00) and MIA-UP by up to 34.12 points (RL: 95.52 vs. 61.40). Among unlearning baselines, RL [18], IU [25], BU [8], and SalUn [13] are most vulnerable to ReA (>89% attack accuracy), while RUM [48] and SCRUB [28] are relatively more resistant (85.64% and 68.75% respectively). OUR reduces ReA accuracy to 52.28% (class-wise) and 53.80% (sample-wise), matching or exceeding the retrained-model baseline (50.00% and 51.35%), confirming effective residual scrubbing. In unlearning efficacy (Table 1), OUR achieves TA/UA/RA within 0.67/0.00/0.02 of the retrained model on CIFAR-20 ViT, outperforming all AMU baselines in the Tug-of-War metric while running in 78s (4.3% of retraining). For image generation (Table 4), OUR achieves UISM=0.16 (vs. retrained 0.13) in 110s (12% of retraining), outperforming SalUn [13] (UISM=0.49) and Meta-Un [15] (UISM=0.37) in unlearning efficacy, and ReA requires 70 iterations to recover the forgotten identity for OUR versus only 2 for SalUn.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the version-independent privacy attack line (MIA-LiRA, MIA-UP) by identifying a new attack surface: implicit residuals in the loss landscape of approximately unlearned models, as opposed to the output-distribution residuals those prior methods exploit. It also builds on the Forgotten Knowledge Recovery Phenomenon observed in safety-aligned generative models, but generalizes the observation across 11 diverse AMU algorithms in closed-problem classification and generation settings. The proposed OUR defense departs from prior orthogonal-projection unlearning (which perturbs directions orthogonal to retained tasks) by instead enforcing orthogonality between unlearned features and their pre-unlearning state, combined with a replay phase to prevent pseudo-convergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that AMU methods achieving near-retrained output accuracy still expose unlearned data through loss-landscape residuals (up to 90% attack accuracy for ReA) fundamentally changes how the field should evaluate unlearning efficacy—output-space metrics alone are insufficient. The practical impact is significant for organizations deploying AMU under GDPR/PIPEDA, as their current methods may provide a false sense of privacy. Open questions include whether the loss-landscape residual mechanism generalizes to LLM unlearning, whether the orthogonality constraint scales to very deep/wide architectures, and whether the two-phase design can be further compressed without sacrificing the convergence guarantee.
