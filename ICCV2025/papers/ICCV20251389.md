## Problem

Reconstructing high-quality color video from chromatic spikes captured in sub-millisecond intervals (0.5 ms) is extremely challenging because the limited number of spikes makes existing methods highly susceptible to noise, while extending the capture window to accumulate more spikes inevitably introduces motion blur that defeats the temporal-resolution advantage of spike cameras. Prior deep-learning approaches require extensive domain-specific training data, exhibit limited generalization, or still fail to eliminate noise and artifacts in high-speed motion regions.

## Input / Output

The method takes raw chromatic spike streams from a 1000×1000 spike camera with a Bayer-pattern (RGGB) CFA, sampled at 20,000 Hz, and converts them into mosaicked, quantized SFR (spike firing rate) frames over 0.5 ms windows (10 spike frames), spatially cropped to 512×512. The output is a sequence of high-quality RGB color video frames at the same spatial resolution, reconstructed without any motion blur since the time interval is too short for motion to accumulate. The SFR frames are inherently discrete (quantized to levels 0, 0.1, …, 1.0 for Δτ=10) and carry a Bayer mosaic pattern, making them fundamentally different from the processed sRGB frames on which the pretrained diffusion model was trained.

## Method

SpikeDiff formulates spike-to-video reconstruction as a MAP estimation problem solved via posterior sampling in a pretrained latent diffusion model (DiffBIR, adapted from Stable Diffusion v2.1), operating entirely zero-shot without any task-specific training. The key technical contribution is a physics-based, differentiable degradation model A(X) = Q(CM(X)) that simulates the spike camera imaging pipeline—Bayer mosaicking (M), per-channel color casting (C) estimated via a gray-world prior, and soft quantization (Q) using a tanh-based smooth approximation of discrete SFR levels—enabling gradient-based likelihood guidance during the reverse diffusion process. At each denoising step, Tweedie's formula estimates the expected clean latent Z_{0|t}, which is decoded and passed through the degradation operators to compute a surrogate likelihood against the observed SFR frames, and the resulting gradient is injected into the reverse SDE as measurement guidance. A Laplacian pyramid is incorporated to preserve fine spatial details across multiple frequency bands, and temporal consistency is achieved by processing SFR frames in small chunks with shared color-casting coefficients across the video sequence.

## Results

On the synthetic X4K1000FPS-based dataset (sub-millisecond 0.5 ms intervals), SpikeDiff achieves the best scores across all five reconstruction metrics: PSNR 18.694, SSIM 0.750, FID 2.880, NIQE 5.173, and IL-NIQE 38.535. The closest competitor, 3DRI, reaches a comparable PSNR (18.512) but trails significantly on SSIM (0.390) and FID (16.036). On temporal consistency, SpikeDiff attains the highest Frame CLIP Score (0.969) but is slightly outperformed by SY24 on interpolation error (0.041 vs. 0.057) and interpolation PSNR (28.65 vs. 25.45).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SpikeDiff outperforms all five baselines on every reconstruction-quality metric (PSNR, SSIM, FID, NIQE, IL-NIQE). The most competitive baseline is 3DRI, which nearly matches SpikeDiff on PSNR (18.512 vs. 18.694) but falls far behind on SSIM (0.390 vs. 0.750) and FID (16.036 vs. 2.880), indicating that 3DRI preserves structure less faithfully. CSpkNet is the second-closest on perceptual metrics (FID 4.699, NIQE 5.819) yet still trails SpikeDiff by roughly 1.8 in FID and 0.65 in NIQE. SY24, the most recent self-supervised method, lags substantially on FID (21.472) and IL-NIQE (78.672), suggesting its denoising module introduces artifacts that degrade perceptual quality. On temporal consistency, SpikeDiff achieves the best Frame CLIP Score (0.969) but SY24 edges it out on interpolation error (0.041 vs. 0.057) and interpolation PSNR (28.65 vs. 25.45), a minor trade-off given the large gaps in reconstruction quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

SpikeDiff builds on the diffusion posterior sampling framework of Chung et al. (ICLR 2023), which provides the theoretical machinery for incorporating observation likelihoods into reverse diffusion via Tweedie's formula and surrogate likelihoods, and on DiffBIR (ECCV 2024) for the pretrained latent diffusion backbone adapted from Stable Diffusion v2.1. It departs from the dominant line of spike-camera reconstruction work (SJDD, CSpkNet, SY24, 3DRI), which all require domain-specific training on synthetic or real spike data, by formulating the problem as a zero-shot MAP estimation where the prior comes entirely from a pretrained diffusion model and the likelihood is derived from a differentiable physics-based degradation chain (mosaicking, color casting, soft quantization). The key conceptual departure is treating the spike-to-image mapping not as a learned regression but as a known forward operator whose gradient can guide diffusion sampling, thereby eliminating the need for any spike-specific training data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that pretrained diffusion priors are strong enough to reconstruct clean color video from sub-millisecond (0.5 ms) chromatic spikes without any domain-specific training, effectively breaking the quality-versus-temporal-resolution trade-off that has constrained prior spike reconstruction methods. The differentiable degradation operators (particularly the soft quantization and the gradient-continuous Bayer mosaicking) are a reusable design pattern for incorporating sensor-specific physics into diffusion-based inverse problems. Open questions include handling extreme overexposure under direct illumination (acknowledged limitation), scaling beyond 512×512 resolution, extending HDR capability, and whether the approach generalizes to other neuromorphic sensor modalities (event cameras, quanta image sensors) with analogous but different degradation models.
