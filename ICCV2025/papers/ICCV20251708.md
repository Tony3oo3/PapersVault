## Problem

Pruning video diffusion models to reduce computational cost inevitably degrades generation quality, and conventional supervised fine-tuning (SFT) distillation exacerbates this by forcing capacity-limited students to blindly imitate the teacher, leading to mode collapse and distributional averaging. No prior work has applied preference learning to diffusion model distillation, leaving the student unable to selectively recover only the properties that pruning actually harms while preserving those that remain intact or improve.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251708/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes text prompts (5–25 words, filtered from WebVid-10M for relevance to a targeted property such as dynamic degree or text alignment) and produces generated videos. During training, the actual input to the loss is a pair of videos—one from the full teacher model (winning) and one from the current pruned student (losing)—scored by VideoScore to form preference pairs above a quality threshold τ_p. The final output is a pruned model (36.2% fewer parameters for VideoCrafter2, 67.5% for AnimateDiff's motion module) that generates videos matching or exceeding the full model's quality.

## Method

The core loss, ReDPO, augments the diffusion DPO objective with an SFT regularization term on the preferred (winning) sample, preventing DPO's known over-optimization while still guiding the student to selectively recover degraded properties rather than imitate the teacher indiscriminately. The V.I.P. framework wraps this in an iterative online loop: at each stage, a specific block is pruned from the student, both the fixed teacher and the updated student generate videos on targeted prompts, VideoScore-based filtering constructs new winning-losing pairs, and ReDPO trains the student on these fresh on-policy data. This step-by-step pruning-and-distillation cycle ensures the student progressively adapts to capacity reduction, with each iteration targeting the specific properties that the latest pruning step degraded, rather than attempting to compensate for all losses at once.

## Results

On VideoCrafter2, V.I.P. achieves a final average VideoScore of 2.623 with 36.2% parameter reduction (1.413B → 0.902B), surpassing the full model's 2.613 by +0.010. On AnimateDiff, the method reaches 2.568 average VideoScore with 67.5% parameter reduction (0.453B → 0.147B), exceeding the full model's 2.563 by +0.005. These gains are accompanied by 21% TFLOPs reduction for VideoCrafter2 (9.4→7.4) and 33% TFLOPs reduction for AnimateDiff (4.9→3.3). On VBench, ReDPO outperforms SFT distillation on Quality Score (82.3 vs 82.1 for VC2; 81.3 vs 81.0 for AD) and Semantic Score (73.9 vs 72.2 for VC2; 76.8 vs 74.7 for AD).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ReDPO (V.I.P.) against three baselines: naively pruned models, SFT-based distillation, and the full (teacher) model. Against SFT distillation (same pruning and data curation, only the loss is swapped), ReDPO consistently wins on VideoScore: for VideoCrafter2, ReDPO scores 2.629/2.617/2.728/2.518 vs SFT's 2.628/2.613/2.724/2.505 across Visual Quality, Temporal Consistency, Dynamic Degree, and Text Alignment; for AnimateDiff, ReDPO leads on Visual Quality (2.569 vs 2.564) and Dynamic Degree (2.695 vs 2.679). Against the full models, the final-stage ReDPO model surpasses VideoCrafter2 on all four VideoScore metrics (e.g., Text Alignment 2.518 vs 2.491) and AnimateDiff on Visual Quality (2.569 vs 2.575 is a slight loss, but Temporal Consistency 2.513 vs 2.505 and Dynamic Degree 2.695 vs 2.684 are gains). On VBench, ReDPO outperforms SFT on Quality Score (82.3 vs 82.1 for VC2; 81.3 vs 81.0 for AD) and Semantic Score (73.9 vs 72.2 for VC2; 76.8 vs 74.7 for AD), while the full model retains a slight edge on Dynamic Degree (45.8 vs 41.7 for VC2; 26.4 vs 22.2 for AD). A user study further confirms that V.I.P.-generated videos are preferred over both SFT and full-model outputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) from the LLM alignment literature and the diffusion-specific DPO loss formulation, repurposing preference learning as a distillation mechanism rather than a human-alignment tool. It departs from the dominant SFT-based distillation paradigm for diffusion models (e.g., BK-SDM, SnapFusion) and adversarial distillation (SDXL-Lightning) by arguing that capacity-constrained students should selectively recover degraded properties rather than blindly imitate the teacher across all dimensions. The iterative, step-by-step online distillation framework further distinguishes it from one-shot pruning-and-finetune pipelines, drawing on the online RLHF literature's finding that on-policy data generation outperforms static offline datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial: achieving 36–67% parameter reduction while matching or marginally exceeding full-model quality opens a path toward deploying video diffusion on edge devices. However, the margins over the full model are small (≤0.01 on VideoScore), and the reliance on a single reward model (VideoScore) for both data curation and evaluation raises questions about reward hacking and generalization. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger, non-U-Net video architectures (e.g., DiT-based models), whether the iterative online loop remains cost-effective at scale, and whether the SFT regularization weight requires careful per-architecture tuning as suggested by the different values used for AnimateDiff vs. VideoCrafter.
