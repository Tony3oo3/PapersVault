## Problem

Existing NeRF-based implicit CT reconstruction methods neglect object-specific anatomical priors, resulting in poor reconstruction quality and slow convergence under ultra-sparse view conditions (10–30 projections). Prior-based approaches like NeRP rely on approximate priors from other patients or time points that are often unavailable in practice, while uniform voxel sampling wastes computational budget on non-informative background regions. The paper addresses how to derive accessible, object-specific structural guidance directly from the sparse projections themselves to improve both learning efficiency and reconstruction fidelity.

## Input / Output

The method takes 10, 20, or 30 X-ray projection images from either a cone-beam or parallel-beam scanning geometry as input, with all volumes rescaled to 128×128×40. No paired 3D ground-truth volume is required for training; supervision is entirely self-supervised via projection-domain L2 loss. The output is a full 3D CT volume of radiance density (σ), rendered along rays using the Beer-Lambert law, evaluated against ground-truth projections at the pixel level.

## Method

TPG-INR introduces three coupled modules built around a CUDA-based Target Prior Estimator (TPE) that iteratively reconstructs a smoothed object-specific prior from the sparse projections in tens of seconds, using adaptive per-voxel gradient scaling and a local-variation regularization term to suppress noise. The Target Prior-guided Voxel Sampling (TPVS) module constructs a sampling field from the prior's intensity and gradient magnitude, then iteratively drops the least-informative voxels along each ray to concentrate the fixed sampling budget (192 voxels/ray) on structurally challenging regions. The Target Prior Structural Encoder (TPSE) queries the 5×5×5 neighborhood of the prior at each sampled voxel and encodes it via a two-layer MLP, which is concatenated with a Gaussian Fourier positional encoding before being fed into the INR network. The INR network (MLP with periodic activations and a final Sigmoid) predicts per-voxel radiance density, and training minimizes the L2 difference between Beer-Lambert-rendered projections and the ground-truth projections over randomly selected rays.

## Results

On 3D cone-beam sparse-view CT reconstruction (20 views), TPG-INR achieves PSNR/SSIM of 33.90/0.945 (Jaw), 35.55/0.974 (Chest), 36.16/0.972 (Abdomen), and 35.12/0.970 (Foot), outperforming the strongest baseline NeRP by 2.05–4.89 dB in PSNR. At 30 views, gains over NeRP reach up to 5.70 dB (Abdomen: 38.65 vs 32.95). In 2D parallel-beam reconstruction, TPG-INR similarly leads, e.g., 34.44/0.960 PSNR/SSIM on Chest at 30 views versus NeRP's 28.37/0.791. The method also achieves the shortest reconstruction time among all compared implicit methods while delivering the highest PSNR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TPG-INR consistently outperforms all six baselines across every dataset and view count in both 2D parallel-beam and 3D cone-beam settings. Against the strongest implicit baseline NeRP (non-prior version), TPG-INR gains 2.05–4.89 dB PSNR at 20 views and up to 5.70 dB at 30 views (e.g., Abdomen 3D: 38.65 vs 32.95). NAF and SAX-NeRF, which use multi-scale hash encoding for positional representation, trail TPG-INR by 3.0–6.2 dB in PSNR at 20 views, with the gap widening at fewer views. Traditional analytical methods (FBP in 2D, FDK in 3D) produce the worst results, with PSNR values 8–15 dB below TPG-INR, confirming severe streak artifacts under sparse sampling. The iterative SART-TV method falls between analytical and implicit methods, achieving moderate quality but still 3–8 dB below TPG-INR. In terms of time efficiency, TPG-INR converges to its best PSNR in approximately 50 minutes (Chest) versus 90 minutes for NeRP and 180 minutes for SAX-NeRF, a roughly 10× speedup over NAF as reported in the paper.

## Relation to Prior Work

TPG-INR extends the NeRF-based implicit representation paradigm to sparse-view CT reconstruction, building directly on NeRP's prior-guided INR framework and GRFF's Gaussian Fourier encoding. Its key conceptual departure is the introduction of 'target priors'—structural estimates derived from the object's own projection data via a CUDA-based iterative algorithm—rather than the approximate priors from other patients or pre-trained diffusion models used by NeRP and DPER. The paper further differentiates itself from ray-based NeRF variants (NAF, SAX-NeRF) by adding a structural encoding pathway (TPSE) and an adaptive voxel sampling strategy (TPVS) that concentrate learning resources on structurally complex regions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× speedup over NAF and 3.5–5.7 dB PSNR gains over NeRP (non-prior) make TPG-INR a practically relevant option for dose-reduced clinical CT, where reconstruction latency and artifact suppression are critical. However, the evaluation is limited to small 128×128×40 volumes across four anatomical regions on a single GPU, leaving open whether the approach scales to full clinical CT volumes (512×512×~100) or generalizes to other modalities (MRI, ultrasound). The reliance on a non-prior NeRP baseline somewhat inflates the quality gap; a fair comparison against NeRP with its intended prior would likely narrow the margin. An open question is whether the target prior concept can be extended to multi-slice or 4D (temporal) reconstruction settings.
