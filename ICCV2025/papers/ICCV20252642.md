## Problem

Existing visual token pruning methods in VLMs rely on text-visual attention scores from the language model to identify important tokens, but the authors demonstrate that this attention suffers from two fundamental issues: a positional bias (attention shift) caused by rotary position embedding that causes text tokens to over-attend to later-positioned visual patches, and an attention dispersion where the score distribution is too uniform to reliably distinguish important from unimportant tokens. This makes text-visual attention a poor pruning criterion, especially at high reduction ratios where performance degrades significantly.

## Input / Output

The method accepts RGB images (336×336 for LLaVA-1.5 producing 576 tokens, up to 2880 tokens for LLaVA-NeXT, or 8 frames at 224×224 for Video-LLaVA producing 2048 tokens) paired with a text question, and outputs a text response generated by the language model. Crucially, pruning occurs before the language model—at the visual encoder output stage—so the pruned token sequence (reduced by 77.8%, 88.9%, or 94.4%) passes through the multimodal projector and into the LM without requiring access to internal attention scores, maintaining compatibility with optimized attention kernels like FlashAttention.

## Method

VisPruner is a training-free, plug-and-play pruning method that exploits visual encoder attention rather than language model text-visual attention. It operates in two stages: first, it selects a small set of important tokens using [CLS] attention scores from the visual encoder (or average row attention for models without a [CLS] token like SigLIP), targeting foreground regions with dense semantic information; second, it removes duplicate tokens from the remainder via iterative cosine-similarity-based deduplication (Algorithm 1), retaining a diverse set that covers background areas. The two complementary token sets are concatenated in original spatial order and fed through the multimodal projector into the language model. The key insight is that [CLS] attention is concentrated (top 20% tokens absorb ~80% attention) and free of positional bias, while the similarity-based diversity step prevents the foreground bias of pure attention-based selection from degrading background-related question performance.

## Results

On LLaVA-1.5-7B, VisPruner retains 99.6% of original performance at 77.8% token reduction (128 tokens), 96.6% at 88.9% reduction (64 tokens), and 91.5% at 94.4% reduction (32 tokens), consistently outperforming all baselines. On LLaVA-NeXT-7B with 2880 visual tokens, VisPruner preserves 86.7% of performance at 94.4% reduction (160 tokens), compared to 82.3% for VisionZip and 74.5% for SparseVLM. For video understanding with Video-LLaVA, VisPruner maintains 90.3% performance at 94.4% reduction versus 86% for FastV. Across other architectures (Qwen-VL, InternVL, CogVLM), VisPruner consistently outperforms FastV by 1.5–10.8% relative performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VisPruner outperforms all five baselines across every reduction ratio tested. Against the strongest text-visual attention method SparseVLM [63], VisPruner leads by 5.2% relative performance at 77.8% reduction (99.6% vs 94.4%) and by 13.6% at 94.4% reduction (91.5% vs 77.9%). Against the most recent vision-based method VisionZip [57], VisPruner maintains a 1.2% advantage at 77.8% reduction and a 2.5% advantage at 94.4% reduction, suggesting that retaining diverse tokens is more effective than merging redundant ones. FastV [11], the original text-visual attention pruning method, is consistently the weakest baseline, losing 14–27% relative performance to VisPruner depending on reduction ratio. PruMerge+ [45] and ToMe [6] fall in between, with PruMerge+ closer to VisionZip at moderate reduction but degrading faster at extreme reduction. On LLaVA-NeXT-7B (2880 tokens), the gap widens further: VisPruner achieves 86.7% relative performance at 94.4% reduction versus 74.5–74.7% for SparseVLM and FastV, a 12+ point margin.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant FastV paradigm of pruning visual tokens based on text-visual attention within the language model, arguing through systematic analysis that this signal suffers from positional bias (RoPE-induced attention shift) and dispersion, making it a poor pruning criterion. Rather than building on a specific prior pruning method, VisPruner repurposes the [CLS] attention already computed by the visual encoder (CLIP/SigLIP) as the importance signal, and augments it with a similarity-based diversity selection from remaining tokens. This positions the work as a conceptual correction to the field's working assumption that the language model's cross-modal attention is the natural indicator for visual token importance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play, training-free design with 91% FLOPs reduction and 75% latency reduction makes this immediately deployable for resource-constrained VLM inference, and the compatibility with FlashAttention (unlike in-model pruning methods) is a practical advantage. The finding that text-visual attention is not an ideal pruning indicator corrects a common assumption and redirects future work toward visual-side signals. Open questions include whether dynamic, density-aware partitioning of tokens (as suggested in the discussion) can further improve performance, and whether the similarity-based diversity heuristic generalizes to more extreme reduction ratios or novel architectures.
