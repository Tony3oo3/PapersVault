## Problem

Vision-language models lack effective approaches to improve response quality by scaling inference-time computation, unlike LLMs where process reward models guide search for better outputs. Extending inference-time search to VLMs is particularly challenging because descriptive captioning tasks lack clear outcome measures and require multi-sentence responses where each sentence must be both locally accurate and globally coherent. The paper asks whether a better reward signal can guide VLM search to reduce hallucinations and enrich visual detail, and whether the resulting captions can bootstrap self-training.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image paired with a text prompt (drawn from LLaVA-150K description prompts) and produces a multi-sentence descriptive paragraph, generated one sentence per search step. At each step, the VLM generates N×K candidate sentences using different temperature settings (e.g., 5 temperatures × 1 greedy = 6 candidates), and VisVM selects the single highest-value sentence to append to the growing caption. The process iterates until an EOS token is produced, yielding a coherent paragraph rather than a single caption sentence.

## Method

VisVM is a value network initialized from the base VLM (e.g., LLaVA-Next-Mistral-7B) with a linear value head appended to the penultimate layer, trained via Temporal Difference (TD) learning to predict the long-term cumulative reward of a sentence given the image. The immediate reward at each step comes from the VLM's own vision encoder (CLIP-ViT or SigLIP) computing image-text similarity, but TD learning (with discount factor γ) enables VisVM to anticipate how a current sentence choice affects hallucination risk in subsequent sentences. During inference, VisVM scores all candidate sentences at each step and selects the one with the highest predicted long-term value, steering generation away from locally plausible but globally inconsistent choices. This forward-looking mechanism is the key distinction from a standard process reward model that only evaluates immediate quality. The resulting VisVM-guided captions can then serve as SFT data for self-training the base VLM, forming a closed-loop pipeline that requires no external models or human annotations.

## Results

On LLaVA-Next-7B, VisVM-guided search reduces CHAIRs from 32.4 (greedy) to 26.2, CHAIRi from 5.9 to 4.6, and MMHal rate from 0.52 to 0.39, while improving AMBER coverage from 63.9 to 66.8. In human evaluation over 200 image-text pairs, VisVM-guided captions are preferred 75.8% of the time over greedy decoding and 62.4% over CLIP-PRM search. Self-training LLaVA-Next-7B and Qwen2-VL-7B with VisVM-guided captions yields average improvements of 10.8% and 7.3% respectively across 9 multimodal benchmarks (MM-Vet, MMBench, MMMU, MathVista, CVBench, LLaVA-Wild, MMStar, CHAIR, MMHal). VisVM-guided search is approximately 2× more computationally efficient than CLIP-PRM-guided search and requires ~7× fewer GPU hours than MCTS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP-PRM guided search (Radford et al., 2021), VisVM consistently wins: on LLaVA-Next-7B it reduces CHAIRs from 28.4 to 26.2 and MMHal rate from 0.49 to 0.39, and is ~2× more efficient (matching CLIP-PRM at step size 16 with only step size 8). Against Best-of-N decoding (Lightman et al., 2023), VisVM achieves lower CHAIRs (26.2 vs. 27.1) and a 60.2% human win rate, indicating that long-term value prediction outperforms selecting the single best caption from 30 samples. Against MCTS (Wang et al., 2024), the comparison is closest: MCTS achieves slightly lower CHAIRs (25.9 vs. 26.2) on LLaVA-Next-7B, but VisVM requires approximately 7× fewer GPU hours and generalizes across prompt-image pairs without relearning a value function per sample. In human evaluation, VisVM achieves a 44.9% win rate and 15.2% tie rate against MCTS, suggesting comparable quality at a fraction of the cost. Across all three baselines, VisVM's advantage grows with larger search budgets, confirming superior scaling behavior of the long-term value signal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the inference-time search paradigm from LLMs (MCTS, Best-of-N, process reward models for math/coding) to vision-language models, where defining a meaningful reward signal for open-ended visual comprehension is substantially harder. The core algorithmic foundation is Temporal Difference learning from classical RL, repurposed to train a value model that predicts long-term visual value rather than just immediate CLIP similarity. It departs from prior VLM hallucination-mitigation work (which focuses on training-time data curation or post-training alignment) by operating entirely at inference time, and from prior CLIP-as-reward approaches by showing that a learned value model with forward-looking coherence outperforms the raw CLIP score as a search signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes the first complete self-training pipeline for VLMs that requires no external models or human annotations: the reward model is the VLM's own visual encoder, the value model is initialized from the VLM, and the SFT data is generated by the VLM itself under VisVM guidance. The consistent 10.8%/7.3% average improvements across nine diverse benchmarks (from MMMU to MathVista) suggest this pipeline could be iterated for continuous self-improvement. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond descriptive captioning to other VLM tasks (VQA, grounding), whether iterative self-training converges or degrades, and whether the gains hold at larger model scales where base hallucination rates are already lower.
