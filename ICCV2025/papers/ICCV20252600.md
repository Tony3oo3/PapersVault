## Problem

Concept Activation Vectors (CAVs) computed independently at different layers of a deep network produce inconsistent TCAV scores, leading to unstable layer selection, spurious high activations for irrelevant concepts, and high variance in concept attributions. This layer-wise inconsistency makes cross-layer comparisons unreliable and renders TCAV-based interpretability fragile, particularly under adversarial perturbations targeting specific layers.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained CNN (ResNet50V2, GoogleNet, or MobileNetV2), a set of predefined concepts (50 images each from the Broden dataset), and 10 random probe datasets (50 ImageNet images each) to train per-layer linear CAV classifiers. It produces a single global concept activation vector (GCAV) in a fixed-dimensional embedding space shared across all layers, layer-specific TGCAV scores obtained by projecting the GCAV back through per-layer decoders, and concept activation maps via VisualTCAV. The GCAV retains the same embedding dimensionality as the autoencoder latent space, enabling direct substitution into the standard TCAV scoring formula at any layer.

## Method

GCAV integrates three sequentially trained components: (1) layer-specific autoencoders that compress high-dimensional CAVs into a unified embedding space while preserving concept information via cosine-similarity reconstruction loss; (2) a contrastive learning stage using an MLP projection head with InfoNCE loss to align same-concept embeddings from different layers (positive pairs) against CAVs from random probe datasets (negative pairs), regularized by a consistency loss that prevents the projection from distorting concept semantics; and (3) a Transformer-style self-attention fusion module with learnable positional encodings that aggregates aligned embeddings into a single GCAV, optimized with a variance loss minimizing TCAV score dispersion across layers and a consistency loss ensuring decoded CAVs remain close to their originals. The sequential training strategy avoids objective interference—jointly training autoencoders with contrastive loss would cause embedding collapse, and fusing before alignment would produce incoherent global representations. TGCAV then applies the standard TCAV directional-derivative scoring using the GCAV projected back to each layer via its decoder, yielding layer-consistent concept importance scores.

## Results

Across all three architectures (GoogleNet, ResNet50V2, MobileNetV2), TGCAV consistently reduces the standard deviation of TCAV scores across layers by 50–90%. For example, on GoogleNet the Std for the 'dotted' concept (Zebra) drops from 0.218 to 0.019, and the Range Ratio for 'zigzagged' drops from 0.974 to 0.159. Under a targeted adversarial attack on the mixed5a layer, TCAV scores for 'dotted' increase by 84.61% (0.52→0.96), while TGCAV scores increase by only 28.49% (0.38→0.49). Mean TGCAV scores remain broadly aligned with TCAV means, confirming that concept importance is preserved while variance is suppressed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the original TCAV method (Kim et al., CVPR 2018), which computes CAVs independently at each layer. TGCAV outperforms TCAV on every stability metric (Std, CV, RR) across all three architectures and all tested concepts, with the most dramatic gains on GoogleNet (e.g., Std for 'dotted' drops 91%, from 0.218 to 0.019). Mean concept scores are largely preserved (within ~10% in most cases), confirming that the variance reduction does not come at the cost of losing true concept signal. Under adversarial perturbation of the mixed5a layer, TGCAV is substantially more robust: the attacked-layer score increases by only 28.49% versus 84.61% for TCAV, and the cross-layer mean increases by 42.63% versus 61.77%. The paper does not compare against other CAV-variant methods (e.g., concept whitening, CACE), so the comparison is limited to the original TCAV pipeline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on TCAV (Kim et al., CVPR 2018), which established post-hoc concept-based interpretability by computing layer-specific Concept Activation Vectors. The key departure is addressing TCAV's fundamental limitation of cross-layer inconsistency: rather than computing CAVs independently per layer, GCAV unifies them through a three-stage pipeline of autoencoder-based dimension unification, contrastive learning (InfoNCE) for cross-layer alignment, and Transformer-style attention fusion. It also engages with prior TCAV refinements (concept whitening, CACE, concept distillation) and adversarial attacks on interpretability (Brown & Kvinge), positioning itself as a structural fix to the layer-dependence problem that those methods do not resolve.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: practitioners using TCAV currently face arbitrary layer selection and unstable scores, and GCAV offers a principled, architecture-agnostic way to obtain a single global concept representation. However, the evaluation is limited to three CNN architectures on the small Broden dataset, and the adversarial robustness claim rests on a single case study rather than systematic attack evaluation. Open questions include whether the three-stage sequential training is truly necessary versus joint optimization, whether the approach generalizes to transformer-based vision models (ViT) or non-visual domains, and whether the variance reduction comes at the cost of losing layer-specific diagnostic information that some analyses may require.
