## Problem

In supervised low-light image enhancement, a persistent brightness mismatch between enhanced images and ground truth causes pixel-level losses to disproportionately penalize minor brightness residuals over other degradation factors like noise and color distortion, misaligning optimization with human perceptual priorities. This issue has been largely overlooked in existing LLIE research, and prior indirect solutions (multi-subnetwork decoupling, perceptual losses) either complicate model design or sacrifice reconstruction fidelity.

## Input / Output

The GT-mean loss is a drop-in replacement for any existing supervised LLIE loss function, requiring only the enhanced image f(x) and ground truth y (both 3×H×W RGB tensors) as inputs. It is architecture-agnostic and can be applied to any supervised LLIE model—transformer-based (RetinexFormer, LLFormer, Uformer, Restormer) or CNN-based (MIRNet, SNR-Aware, CID-Net)—by simply wrapping the original loss into the GT-mean form. The computational overhead is approximately doubling the original loss computation, with the weight W computed analytically without gradient propagation.

## Method

The GT-mean loss dynamically combines two terms: the original loss L(f(x), y) and a brightness-aligned loss L(λ·f(x), y) where λ = E[y]/E[f(x)] linearly scales the enhanced image to match the ground truth mean brightness. The blending weight W is derived from the Bhattacharyya distance between two Gaussian distributions modeling the brightness of f(x) and y, each parameterized by a spread σ (set to 0.1), and clipped to [0,1]. In early training when brightness distributions diverge, W approaches 1 and the original loss dominates, preserving baseline capabilities; as training progresses and brightness aligns, W decreases, shifting emphasis to the brightness-aligned term that forces the model to address non-brightness degradations (noise, contrast, structural detail). This creates a flat loss basin around the correct brightness level, slowing gradient-driven brightness adjustments and redirecting optimization toward perceptually important factors.

## Results

Across all seven supervised LLIE baselines on LOLv1, adding the GT-mean loss consistently improves PSNR by +0.27 to +1.31 dB, SSIM by +0.003 to +0.025, and reduces LPIPS by 0.003 to 0.045. The largest gains appear for CID-Net (PSNR +1.313 dB, 23.809→25.122) and SNR-Aware (PSNR +0.988 dB, 23.005→23.992), while even the strongest baseline (CID-Net) still benefits. On LOLv2-real, improvements are even more pronounced (e.g., Uformer PSNR +1.162 dB, LPIPS −0.048), and unpaired-dataset evaluation (DICM, MEF, LIME, NPE, VV) confirms generalization with consistent IQA/IAA gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The GT-mean loss is applied as a drop-in extension to seven supervised LLIE baselines (Uformer, MIRNet, RetinexFormer, Restormer, LLFormer, SNR-Aware, CID-Net), and every single baseline improves on every paired-dataset metric. The largest PSNR gains are for CID-Net (+1.313 dB on LOLv1) and SNR-Aware (+0.988 dB), suggesting that methods already using perceptual or edge losses benefit most from the additional pixel-level brightness alignment. LLFormer shows the largest LPIPS reduction (−0.045 on LOLv1), indicating stronger perceptual improvement. On LOLv2-real, Uformer gains +1.162 dB PSNR and −0.048 LPIPS, the largest absolute improvement among all method-dataset pairs. The one exception is CID-Net's IQA on LOLv1, which drops marginally (4.087→4.074), while all other 200+ metric comparisons show improvement. Unpaired-dataset results (Table 3) confirm that the gains generalize: average IQA improves for all seven methods across DICM, MEF, LIME, NPE, and VV.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a specific prior architecture but instead identifies a systemic weakness in how supervised LLIE loss functions handle brightness mismatch — a problem the authors argue has been overlooked. It departs from two existing strategies: multi-sub-network Retinex-based decompositions that separate illumination from reflectance (adding architectural complexity), and perceptual losses that sidestep pixel-level alignment at the cost of reconstruction fidelity. The GT-mean loss repurposes the Bhattacharyya distance between Gaussian brightness distributions as a dynamic weighting mechanism, a statistical tool applied here in a novel loss-design context rather than in its traditional signal-selection role.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GT-mean loss offers a near-zero-cost, architecture-agnostic upgrade that any supervised LLIE method can adopt by simply wrapping its existing loss, making it practically attractive for the field. The authors acknowledge that the weight W estimation (currently a fixed-σ Bhattacharyya distance) remains an open problem, and exploring learned or adaptive W strategies could unlock further gains. A limitation is that the approach is inherently tied to supervised settings with paired GT images, so it does not address the large unsupervised LLIE literature where prior-based losses dominate.
