## Problem

Standard cross-entropy loss with one-hot (hard) target encoding treats all incorrect token predictions equally, regardless of numerical distance. For numerical prediction tasks like object counting, bounding box coordinate regression, and chart value extraction, this means predicting '1' when the answer is '4' incurs the same loss as predicting '3', which is counter-intuitive and suboptimal for continuous-space quantities. The paper asks whether the de facto hard-labeling paradigm can be improved for numerical prediction without breaking the next-token prediction architecture of LLMs/MLLMs.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard MLLM inputs: an RGB image paired with a text prompt (e.g., a referring expression, counting question, or chart query). Outputs are generated via standard autoregressive next-token prediction, producing text strings that encode numerical values — bounding box coordinates (four numbers), object counts, or chart-derived quantities. Crucially, numerical values are tokenized digit-wise (each of the 10 digits '0'–'9' is a separate token), and the soft labeling mechanism applies exclusively to these digit tokens while regular text tokens retain standard cross-entropy supervision.

## Method

The core contribution is a modified cross-entropy loss called soft labeling, which replaces the one-hot target distribution with a smoothed distribution q^SL(t) = (1−η)δ(t) + ηψ(t) over the 10 digit tokens, where ψ(t) is a probability distribution (Triangular by default, also tested: Binomial, Poisson, Uniform) centered at the target digit. This assigns non-zero target probability to neighboring digits, so the resulting loss naturally penalizes predictions in proportion to their numerical distance from the ground truth, mimicking regression behavior without any architectural change. The final training loss (Eq. 5) combines standard cross-entropy on regular tokens with soft-labeling cross-entropy on digit tokens, balanced by a weight λ (default 2). At inference, the model performs standard next-token prediction with no modification, making the approach drop-in compatible with any LLM/MLLM. The method is validated on LLaVA-7B/13B across visual grounding (RefCOCO/+/g), object counting (TallyQA), and chart understanding (ChartQA), achieving state-of-the-art results without additional layers or heuristics.

## Results

On visual grounding (Acc@0.5), SoftLabeling-13B achieves 92.7/95.0/89.0 on RefCOCO val/testA/testB, 87.6/92.7/82.3 on RefCOCO+, and 89.8/90.0 on RefCOCOg val/test, setting SOTA among 13B-scale models. On ChartQA, the method reaches 81.5 accuracy, surpassing all listed baselines including Gemini Ultra (80.8) and LLaVA-OV-7B (80.0). On TallyQA, SoftLabeling achieves 86.6% accuracy and 0.56 RMSE on the simple set, and 77.2% accuracy and 1.06 RMSE on the complex set, outperforming GPT-4o (81.5/0.60 and 71.7/1.21 respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among 7B-scale grounding models, SoftLabeling-7B achieves the best RefCOCOg test score (89.5) and RefCOCO+ testB (80.0), edging out Ferret-v2-7B (89.3 and 79.3) and Qwen2-VL-7B (87.8 and 79.5), though it trails Ferret-v2-7B on RefCOCO val (91.8 vs 92.8). At 13B scale, SoftLabeling-13B surpasses Ferret-v2-13B on RefCOCO+ testB (82.3 vs 81.4) and RefCOCOg val (89.8 vs 89.4), while matching it on RefCOCOg test (90.0). On ChartQA, SoftLabeling (81.5) outperforms every listed baseline, including Gemini Ultra (80.8), LLaVA-OV-7B (80.0), and Qwen-VL-Max (79.8). On TallyQA, the method dominates: it beats GPT-4o by 5.1 points on simple accuracy (86.6 vs 81.5) and 5.5 points on complex accuracy (77.2 vs 71.7), and improves RMSE from 0.60 to 0.56 (simple) and 1.21 to 1.06 (complex). It also slightly edges SMoLA-PaLI-X on simple accuracy (86.6 vs 86.3) and complex accuracy (77.2 vs 77.1).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the soft labeling / ordinal regression framework from traditional multi-class classification (Beckham & Pal 2017; Vargas et al. 2023) to the LLM/MLLM training paradigm, where the output space is a vocabulary of tens to hundreds of thousands of tokens rather than a small set of ordered categories. The key conceptual departure is restricting the smoothing to the 10 digit tokens while leaving all other tokens under standard one-hot cross-entropy, and showing that label smoothing (Uniform distribution) is a degenerate special case of their more general formulation. It builds directly on the LLaVA architecture and training pipeline without any structural modification, positioning the contribution purely as a loss-design change.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a two-line loss modification (no new layers, no architecture change, no inference-time overhead) yields consistent 1–5 point gains across visual grounding, object counting, and chart understanding, making it an easy drop-in for any MLLM training pipeline. However, the approach is limited to single-digit tokens and does not correctly handle multi-digit numerical distances (e.g., '21' vs. '32' vs. '29'), which the authors acknowledge as an open challenge. Extending soft labeling to text-based numerals ('one', 'two'), categorical ordinal tokens, and multi-digit positional encoding remains future work, and the modest absolute gains suggest diminishing returns as base models scale further.
