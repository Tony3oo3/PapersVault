## Problem

Frame-based visual vibrometry requires expensive high-speed cameras with bright lighting and high bandwidth to capture high-frequency vibrations, making it impractical for many real-world applications. While event cameras offer high temporal resolution and low bandwidth, estimating subtle oscillatory motion from their sparse, edge-localized event signals remains an open problem: existing event-based optical flow methods assume continuous apparent motion (violated by vibrations), and the very low event counts make estimation highly noise-sensitive.

## Input / Output

The method consumes asynchronous event streams (per-pixel log-brightness change events with polarity) and a single co-registered RGB reference frame from a hybrid Event-RGB camera system. Events are processed in very short temporal windows (typically <0.5 ms) to resolve high-frequency vibrations. The output is a per-pixel 2D subtle motion field at each time step, accumulated into long-range vibration displacements relative to the reference state; these displacement fields are then decomposed into a global motion power spectrum and independent vibration modes (resonant frequencies with corresponding spatial mode shapes).

## Method

The framework has two stages. First, a model-based optimization minimizes the L1 mismatch between observed brightness change (accumulated event polarities) and predicted brightness change (gradient-weighted displacement) over a composite loss that includes a data-fidelity term restricted to event-triggered pixels and a gradient-weighted smoothness regularizer, solved via patch-based coarse-to-fine optimization across three pyramid scales. Second, EVibNet refines the coarse motion using modality-specific encoders (a ConvLSTM-based event encoder to exploit temporal structure and mitigate quantization error, and a CNN frame encoder), cross-modal attention fusion to leverage reference-frame texture in event-sparse regions, a motion refinement module, and an RNN-based accumulation module (two ConvNext blocks) that integrates short-term motion into long-range displacement while suppressing accumulation drift. The network is trained end-to-end with L1 flow loss on synthetic sub-pixel motion data generated by compositing PASCAL VOC objects onto COCO backgrounds and simulating events with V2E.

## Results

On the synthetic subtle motion estimation benchmark, the proposed method achieves an endpoint error (EPE) of 0.0834 px and 21.29% of pixels with EPE > 0.1 px, substantially outperforming the best event-based optical flow baseline EV-FlowNet (EPE 0.1805, 47.95% Out) and the frame-based RGBPhase method applied to 960 Hz video (EPE 0.2833, 54.78% Out). For material property estimation, the method estimates Young's modulus with errors as low as 0.39% under high lighting, compared to up to 10.01% for the frame-based video baseline under low lighting. In audio recovery experiments, the method produces spectrograms with significantly reduced noise and improved high-frequency fidelity relative to the event-based visual microphone (EBVM) baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baselines in Table 1 by a wide margin. Against the two event-only optical flow methods, EV-FlowNet (EPE 0.1805) and MCM (EPE 0.4511), the method achieves roughly 2.2× and 5.4× lower EPE respectively, demonstrating that general-purpose event flow estimators are poorly suited to the oscillatory, sparse-event nature of vibrations. Against the frame-based RGBPhase method applied to 960 Hz high-speed video (EPE 0.2833), the method achieves a 3.4× EPE reduction while requiring only a single reference frame plus event stream rather than a high-speed video. The EMM + RAFT cascade (EPE 0.2190) also underperforms, confirming that motion magnification artifacts degrade subsequent flow estimation. For material property estimation (Table 2), the method reduces Young's modulus estimation errors compared to the 1000 fps video baseline [9] across all three metals and lighting conditions, with the largest gains under low lighting (e.g., 2.90% vs. 10.01% for aluminum at 35 cm). For audio recovery, the method produces cleaner spectrograms with better high-frequency reconstruction than the EBVM baseline [34], which relies on a phase-based approach without frame integration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends frame-based visual vibrometry (Davis et al., Chen et al., Feng et al.) to the event-camera modality, addressing the bandwidth and lighting constraints of high-speed video. It builds on the event generation model optimization framework from Hidalgo-Carrió et al. [21] and Pan et al. [36] for coarse motion, and on cross-modal attention fusion [51] for combining event and frame features. The key departure from prior event-based vibration work (Dorn et al., Niwa et al., Chen et al.) is the explicit estimation of per-pixel subtle motion fields rather than implicit phase-based or motion-magnification approaches, and the integration of a learned refinement network (EVibNet) that aggregates temporal context to correct quantization and sparsity artifacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Event-based visual vibrometry enables high-frequency vibration sensing under ambient lighting at a fraction of the bandwidth of 1000 fps video (~20% of compressed H.264), making remote audio recovery, structural monitoring, and non-destructive material property estimation more practical and deployable. The demonstrated robustness across lighting conditions (100–1500 lux) for Young's modulus estimation is a concrete practical advantage over frame-based vibrometry. Key limitations include reliance on a single reference frame, the gap between the composited synthetic training data and real vibration physics, and evaluation limited to simple geometries (rods, cubes); the authors note that FEM-based synthetic data generation is needed to close this gap.
