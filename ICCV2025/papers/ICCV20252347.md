## Problem

Existing prototype-based methods for Generalized Few-Shot Semantic Segmentation (GFSS) learn deterministic visual prototypes that overfit to the scarce annotated samples of novel classes and fail to capture intra-class appearance variations. This deterministic formulation provides no mechanism to model uncertainty, leading to poor generalization when only a few novel-class examples are available while simultaneously risking degradation of base-class performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252347/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (resized to 512x512) and a set of class name strings as input, processed through frozen CLIP ViT-B/16 image and text encoders augmented with learnable visual prompts. It produces a pixel-wise segmentation mask over all base and novel classes plus a background class, along with a pixel-wise variance map that quantifies epistemic uncertainty in the prediction. The mask is computed as the pixel-wise mean over M independently sampled probabilistic prototypes, so the output naturally carries a confidence signal without additional heads.

## Method

FewCLIP introduces a two-part calibration mechanism over frozen CLIP textual prototypes. First, a deterministic calibration step computes a multi-modal prototype via a Hadamard product and residual addition (P_t * P_c + P_t), where P_c is a learnable visual calibration prototype that injects class-specific visual semantics while preserving CLIP's generalizable textual knowledge. Second, a probabilistic layer models each class's calibration prototype as a Gaussian distribution whose mean and variance are inferred by a multi-head cross-attention module (textual prototype as query, visual class token as key/value) followed by separate MLPs; across all classes this forms a Mixture of Gaussians. M latent prototypes are sampled via the reparameterization trick, each combined with the deterministic P_c to yield M calibrated prototypes and M mask predictions, whose pixel-wise mean is the final mask and whose variance encodes uncertainty. Training uses a cross-entropy segmentation loss plus a KL-divergence regularization pulling the prototype distribution toward N(0, I), and the novel-class registration phase freezes all base-phase parameters and updates only the novel-class calibration prototypes, preventing catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

On PASCAL-5i, FewCLIP achieves 62.03% hIoU in the 1-shot setting and 66.44% hIoU in the 5-shot setting, with novel-class mIoU of 52.70% and 59.21% respectively. On COCO-20i, it reaches 34.60% hIoU (1-shot) and 38.38% hIoU (5-shot), with novel-class mIoU of 26.79% and 31.52%. The method consistently outperforms all baselines across both datasets and both shot settings, with the largest gains observed in the 1-shot regime where data scarcity is most acute.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FewCLIP outperforms all 11 baselines across every dataset and shot setting. Against the strongest visual-prototype baseline PoP [25], it gains 14.06% hIoU in the 1-shot PASCAL-5i setting (62.03 vs. 47.97) and 14.47% in the 5-shot setting (66.44 vs. 51.97→63.96). Against the multi-modal baseline O3S [42], which also uses CLIP text+image prototypes, FewCLIP improves hIoU by 11.27% (1-shot PASCAL-5i) and 7.26% (1-shot COCO-20i), demonstrating that probabilistic calibration adds substantial value beyond deterministic multi-modal fusion. Compared to VPT [15], the gains are 10.60% (1-shot) and 9.81% (5-shot) hIoU on PASCAL-5i. Against BCM [34], the novel-class mIoU improves by 11.54% on PASCAL-5i 1-shot (52.70 vs. 41.24) and 8.51% on COCO-20i 1-shot (26.79 vs. 18.28). In the class-incremental setting (Table 3), FewCLIP leads PKL [12] by 15.42% hIoU in session 1 on PASCAL-5i and 21.32% on COCO-20i, with the gap widening in later sessions as more novel classes accumulate.

## Relation to Prior Work

FewCLIP extends the multi-modal prototype learning paradigm introduced by O3S, which first combined frozen CLIP textual prototypes with learnable visual prototypes for GFSS. The key departure is replacing deterministic visual prototypes with probabilistic calibration prototypes modeled as Gaussian distributions, drawing on variational inference and probabilistic fine-tuning techniques (e.g., CLAP4CLIP, probabilistic prompt learning) previously applied to classification but not to dense prediction. The prototype calibration mechanism itself—using a Hadamard product plus residual addition to fuse textual and visual prototypes—is a specific architectural choice validated against alternatives like concatenation and simple addition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The probabilistic formulation addresses a fundamental limitation of prototype-based few-shot methods: deterministic prototypes overfit to the handful of available novel-class samples, and the resulting uncertainty is invisible to the model. By providing both improved segmentation accuracy (~10% mIoU on novel classes) and pixel-wise epistemic uncertainty as a byproduct, FewCLIP offers a practical path toward more trustworthy few-shot segmentation. However, the method remains tied to CLIP's pretrained representations and class-name inputs, limiting applicability to domain-specific or open-vocabulary settings where CLIP's text-image alignment may be suboptimal. Extending the framework to open-vocabulary segmentation and to settings without reliable class-name text is a natural next step.
