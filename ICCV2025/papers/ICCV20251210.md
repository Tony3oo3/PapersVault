## Problem

Training hierarchical agents in open-world environments requires segmenting long demonstration videos into atomic skill units, but existing segmentation methods rely on random fixed-length splitting, reward signals, human labeling, or rule-based clustering—none of which are learning-based or scalable to the diverse, ill-defined skills found in open worlds. The paper asks whether skill boundaries can be detected automatically from unsegmented videos without any manual annotation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes long unsegmented observation-action trajectories (68M frames from the Contractor dataset) and a pretrained unconditional Transformer-XL action-prediction model (VPT-3x), optionally augmented with in-game event logs (e.g., mine_block, use_item). It outputs skill boundary timestamps that partition the video into ~130k sub-trajectories, each representing a single atomic skill. These segments are then used to train video-conditioned (GROOT) and language-conditioned (STEVE-1) policies, which in turn drive hierarchical agents (JARVIS-1, OmniJARVIS) on long-horizon programmatic tasks.

## Method

SBD (Skill Boundary Detection) is inspired by Event Segmentation Theory: a pretrained unconditional policy predicts future actions from past observations, and a spike in prediction loss signals a skill transition because the model's learned temporal dependencies are violated. Algorithmically, a sliding window computes the negative log-likelihood of each action; when the loss exceeds the running mean by a threshold GAP (set to 18), the timestep is marked as a boundary and the window resets. The paper provides a theoretical guarantee (Theorem 3.4) under three assumptions—skill consistency, skill confidence, and action deviance at transition—showing that relative predictive probability is provably lower at skill boundaries than during skill continuation. An optional external-information channel (in-game event logs) catches boundaries that produce no action change, such as crafting. The resulting segmented dataset is used to retrain GROOT and STEVE-1 via behavioral cloning, yielding 63.7% and 52.1% relative improvements on atomic tasks and 11.3% and 20.8% on long-horizon hierarchical agents.

## Results

On the 12-task Minecraft skill benchmark (MCU), re-training GROOT on SBD-segmented data yields a 63.7% relative average improvement over the original random-splitting baseline (e.g., +107.7% on hunt sheep, +166.7% on use bow), while re-training STEVE-1 achieves a 52.1% relative average improvement (e.g., +131.3% on use torch, +155.6% on collect seagrass). For long-horizon programmatic tasks, the corresponding hierarchical agents OmniJARVIS and JARVIS-1 achieve 11.3% and 20.8% relative average improvements respectively, with the largest gains on food (+25.0%/+59.0%) and armor (+58.3%) categories. Human evaluation of SBD segments shows 54% are clearly semantically coherent and 34% acceptable, with only 12% needing improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SBD-segmented re-training against the original random-splitting baselines for four systems: GROOT (fixed 128-frame segments), STEVE-1 (uniform 15–200 frame segments), OmniJARVIS, and JARVIS-1. SBD consistently outperforms all baselines: GROOT improves by 63.7% relative average (winning on 10 of 12 tasks, with the only losses being a 9.3% drop on use torch and a 2.3% drop on collect dirt), and STEVE-1 improves by 52.1% relative average (winning on 9 of 12 tasks). For long-horizon tasks, OmniJARVIS gains 11.3% average (strongest on food at +25.0%) and JARVIS-1 gains 20.8% average (strongest on armor at +58.3% and food at +59.0%). An ablation also shows that even a simple improved baseline matching SBD's length distribution gains only 26.2% relative, while full SBD achieves 41.0% relative over the original, confirming that semantic boundary detection—not just length matching—drives the improvement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against four families of trajectory segmentation methods—random splitting, reward-driven option learning, top-down human labeling, and bottom-up clustering/BPE—all of which are rule-based. It departs from this lineage by introducing a learning-based approach (SBD) that leverages prediction errors from a pretrained unconditional action-prediction model (VPT/Transformer-XL) to detect skill boundaries, drawing a direct conceptual bridge to Event Segmentation Theory from cognitive science. The method is then plugged into existing hierarchical agent frameworks (GROOT, STEVE-1, JARVIS-1, OmniJARVIS) as a data-preprocessing step, rather than proposing a new agent architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for human-annotated skill boundaries, SBD unlocks the use of massive unsegmented YouTube gameplay videos for training instruction-following agents, which is a practical bottleneck for open-world agent research. The substantial improvements (63.7%/52.1% on atomic tasks) demonstrate that segmentation quality is a first-order factor in policy performance, not a minor data-engineering detail. However, evaluation is confined to Minecraft, the GAP hyperparameter requires manual tuning, and the theoretical assumptions (skill consistency, confidence, action deviance) are not empirically validated in the wild, leaving open whether the approach transfers cleanly to robotics or driving domains with different skill structures.
