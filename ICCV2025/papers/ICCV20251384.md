## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks emphasize abstract symbolic computation and structured mathematical reasoning but overlook intuitive number sense—the innate ability to perceive, estimate, and compare numerical quantities from visual input. It remains unclear whether current MLLMs possess any human-like number sense or merely manipulate numerical patterns learned from text, and no comprehensive benchmark exists to directly probe this perceptual-numeric capability across diverse visual attributes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251384/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark instance pairs a visual image (synthetic figure or real-world photograph) with a multiple-choice question probing one of seven visual numerical attributes (angle, length, scale, quantity, depth, area, volume). The model must select one answer from 3–5 options, where distractors are deliberately designed to exploit human perceptual biases (e.g., visually similar but numerically distinct values). The four task types—value comparison, value estimation, range estimation, and multiplicative estimation—determine the answer format: a single label, a numeric value, a numeric interval, or a ratio.

## Method

VisNumBench comprises ~1,900 QA pairs split into VisNumBench-Synthetic (1,011 items, generated via Matplotlib scripts with controlled parameters) and VisNumBench-Real (902 items, sourced from existing datasets, web collection, and manually captured photographs). For synthetic images, questions and answers are auto-generated from stored sampling parameters; for real-world images, questions are manually designed and annotated. The evaluation protocol randomly samples 600 items (50 per attribute, 300 synthetic + 300 real) and measures accuracy against human evaluators who achieve 95–97% accuracy. Beyond the main 17-model evaluation, the paper conducts ablation-style comparisons using math-specialized models (InternVL2-8B-MPO, Math-LLaVA-13B), multimodal CoT models (Llama-3.2V-11B-cot, R1-OneVision-7B), and historical model families (Qwen2-VL vs. Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2 vs. InternVL2.5) to isolate which factors—data scale, encoder quality, architecture, or training strategy—drive improvements in number sense.

## Results

On VisNumBench-Synthetic, the best open-source model Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieves 58.46% average accuracy, while the best API model Gemini 2.0 Flash reaches 57.57%, both far below the human baseline of 95.33%. On VisNumBench-Real, InternVL2.5-78B and Gemini 2.0 Flash tie for the top score at 56.54%, versus 97.33% for humans. All 17 evaluated MLLMs perform at or near random-guess level (~25%) on several attributes, with quantity tasks being the easiest and angle tasks the most difficult across all models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source models, Qwen2.5-VL-72B (58.46% Synthetic, 53.33% Real) outperforms all other open-source baselines, beating InternVL2.5-78B (56.18%/56.54%) and LLaVA-Onevision-72B (50.84%/50.78%) by 2–8 points. Gemini 2.0 Flash (57.57%/56.54%) is the strongest proprietary model, surpassing GPT-4o (43.72%/39.58%) by 14–17 points and Gemini 1.5 Pro (44.02%/48.67%) by 13–8 points. Math-specialized models show negligible gains: InternVL2-8B-MPO improves only +1.0% (Synthetic) and +0.3% (Real) over its base, while Math-LLaVA-13B gains +3.7% on Synthetic but drops −6.9% on Real. CoT reasoning models (Llama-3.2V-11B-cot, R1-OneVision-7B) fail to improve and in fact degrade performance, especially on Real. Historical comparisons show consistent generational gains: Qwen2.5-VL-72B improves +4.26% (Syn) and +6.77% (Real) over Qwen2-VL-72B, and InternVL2.5-38B improves +10.09% (Syn) and +6.99% (Real) over InternVL2-40B, indicating that scaling and architectural refinements help but remain far from human-level number sense.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a benchmark specifically targeting intuitive visual number sense, a cognitive ability well-studied in human cognition (Feigenson et al., 2004) but largely absent from MLLM evaluation. It departs from existing mathematical reasoning benchmarks (MathVista, Math-Vision, MathOdyssey, SMART-840) that emphasize abstract symbolic computation and structured problem-solving, and from prior narrow ordinal-regression evaluations (age estimation, historical dating, aesthetics scoring) that test only single-domain numerical cues. VisNumBench generalizes these into a unified framework spanning seven visual numerical attributes (angle, length, scale, quantity, depth, area, volume) and four estimation task types, drawing on both synthetic (Matplotlib-generated) and real-world imagery.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and consistent gap between MLLM performance (~58% best) and human performance (~95–97%) across all numerical attributes, suggesting that current architectures lack a genuine perceptual number sense. A key open question is what training data, architectural changes, or learning strategies could close this gap, since the paper demonstrates that neither math-specialized fine-tuning nor chain-of-thought reasoning yields meaningful improvements. The finding that larger models within the same family show only modest gains over predecessors implies that scaling alone is insufficient, pointing toward the need for targeted data curation or new inductive biases. As a community resource, VisNumBench provides a standardized diagnostic tool for tracking progress on this underexplored capability.
