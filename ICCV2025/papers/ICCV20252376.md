## Problem

Fine-grained stylized 3D facial expression manipulation is hindered by the absence of datasets that provide per-character, style-specific AU-Blendshape control bases. Existing FACS-annotated datasets (D3DFACS, CK+) offer discrete AU labels without blendshape bases, while blendshape datasets (FaceWarehouse, ARKit) lack AU semantics or use universal non-stylized bases, making it impossible to achieve both fine-grained continuous control and character-specific emotional styling.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 3D facial mesh template (5,023 vertices from the expressive FLAME model) representing a character's identity and style as input, and produces 32 stylized AU-Blendshape basis vectors (each a 5,023-vertex offset) as output. These basis vectors are not a single target expression but a reusable control library: at inference time, arbitrary combinations of the 32 bases are linearly blended with continuous AU intensity signals to generate the final facial mesh, decoupling style prediction (one-time, 0.3 s) from expression manipulation (per-frame, 0.002 s).

## Method

AUBlendNet comprises two components trained in two stages. First, an AUCodebook (a VQ-VAE with a Transformer encoder/decoder, inspired by CodeTalker) is trained to encode 32 AU-Blendshape bases into a discrete latent codebook, using a facial mesh L1 reconstruction loss plus a VQ commitment loss. Second, StyleBlendNet is trained with the codebook frozen: it projects the input template mesh through a TCN and MLP to produce N×D token embeddings, injects a style encoding via AdaLN-Zero conditioning, and then uses a multi-head self-attention Transformer to predict all 32 AU-Blendshape codes in parallel (non-autoregressively). The key design insight is that the network predicts the character's stylized control basis once, after which any expression is obtained by simple linear blending of the bases with AU signals—avoiding the need for per-expression inference and enabling continuous, fine-grained manipulation. Training data comes from AUBlendSet, a new dataset of 500 identities (206 female, 294 male, ages 20–60) where professional artists hand-craft 32 AU-Blendshape bases per character guided by FACS exemplars, ensuring style consistency and semantic correctness.

## Results

On the AUBlendSet test set, AUBlendNet achieves MSE_S = 4.55×10⁻⁹ and MSE_M = 7.62×10⁻⁹ for single-AU and multi-AU facial expression manipulation, outperforming Diffusion (5.66/8.71), VQ-VAE (8.48/12.79), and Transformer (86.12/96.14) baselines. When trained on AUBlendSet, inference takes only 0.3 s versus 2.7 s for AU-label-based methods (D3DFACS, CK+), a 9× speedup. In speech-driven animation, adding AUBlendNet consistently improves expression Diversity by 20–30% across Faceformer, FaceDiffuser, and LGLDM on both MEAD and VOCASET while preserving lip-sync (V-LVE unchanged). For AU detection, AUBlendNet-based data augmentation boosts F1 scores by 2–3 points on D3DFACS and DISFA across three detectors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AUBlendNet outperforms all three architecture baselines on AUBlendSet: it beats the Diffusion model [28] by ~20% on MSE_S (4.55 vs 5.66) and ~12% on MSE_M (7.62 vs 8.71), and substantially outperforms the VQ-VAE/CodeTalker [35] baseline (4.55 vs 8.48 MSE_S) and the vanilla Transformer [31] (4.55 vs 86.12 MSE_S, an 18× gap). Against dataset-based baselines, AUBlendNet trained on AUBlendSet reduces MSE_S by 50× over D3DFACS [4] (4.55 vs 226.41) and by ~1865× over CK+ [17] (4.55 vs 8490.27), while also being 9× faster at inference (0.3 s vs 2.7 s). In speech-driven animation (Table 4), AUBlendNet improves the Diversity metric for all three speech-driven backbones—Faceformer [10] (2.16→2.74), FaceDiffuser [28] (2.19→2.67), and LGLDM [27] (2.42→2.91) on MEAD—at the cost of a marginal LVE increase (~8–10%), while V-LVE and FDD remain essentially unchanged. For AU detection (Table 5), AUBlendNet augmentation improves F1 for HMP-PS [26] (55.04→57.96 on D3DFACS, 61.96→64.57 on DISFA), LP-Net [21] (52.76→54.96, 56.61→59.35), and SRERL [14] (52.48→55.96, 55.75→56.32). In the user study (Figure 6), AUBlendNet is preferred over both the ARKit-based method [19] and the one-hot-based method [27] across all five criteria (manipulation freedom, interactiveness, generation satisfaction, style consistency, response time) by both 32 general participants and 8 animation experts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on CodeTalker's VQ-VAE codebook architecture for the AUCodebook component, repurposing discrete motion priors from speech-driven animation to encode per-character AU-Blendshape bases. It departs from prior facial expression manipulation work (ARKit, FACSGEN) that relies on universal, identity-agnostic blendshapes, and from FACS-annotated datasets (D3DFACS, CK+, BP4D) that provide only discrete AU labels without continuous geometric control bases. The key conceptual departure is treating AU-Blendshape basis vectors as per-character style-specific parameters to be predicted, rather than as fixed universal offsets or as direct posture targets conditioned on discrete labels.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AUBlendSet fills a concrete gap: no prior dataset provides per-character, AU-aligned, continuous blendshape bases at scale (500 identities × 32 AUs), making fine-grained stylized 3D facial manipulation previously infeasible. The 9× inference speedup (0.3 s vs 2.7 s) over category-label methods is practically significant for interactive animation pipelines. Open questions include generalization beyond the FLAME topology (5,023 vertices), whether the 32-AU simplification (merged eye AUs, no tongue/head) suffices for production animation, and how the approach scales to larger identity distributions or non-FLAME mesh formats.
