## Problem

VGGSound, the standard benchmark for audio-visual classification, suffers from incomplete single-label annotations that miss co-occurring classes, partially overlapping class definitions, and widespread modality misalignment where labelled events are not visible or not audible. Without per-label modality annotations, it is impossible to determine whether a model is genuinely leveraging both audio and visual cues or simply relying on one modality, leading to systematically distorted evaluations of audio-visual foundation models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251737/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consists of 15,446 ten-second video clips with synchronized audio tracks, each annotated with a multi-label set drawn from 309 classes plus per-label modality tags (audible, visible, or both) and three meta-labels (background music, voice-over, static image). Models are evaluated in three input modes—audio-only, visual-only, and audio-visual—and produce unordered sets of class predictions scored via subset accuracy, F1, Hit, and a novel modality confusion metric μ that quantifies how often adding a second modality causes a previously correct unimodal prediction to become wrong.

## Method

VGGSounder is constructed through a multi-stage annotation pipeline: a gold-standard reference set of 417 samples is labelled by in-house experts to calibrate automatic label proposals (achieving 93% recall at ~30 proposals per sample), after which the full test set is re-annotated on Amazon Mechanical Turk with catch-trial quality control (batches below 25% F1 are rejected). The pipeline converts VGGSound from single-label to multi-label classification, adds per-label modality annotations, merges synonymous and sub/superclass pairs, and introduces meta-labels for common confounders. The paper's key analytical contribution is the modality confusion metric μ, defined as the fraction of samples a model classifies correctly with one modality but incorrectly when both modalities are provided simultaneously, exposing unsuccessful cross-modal fusion. Eleven models—four finetuned embedding models (CAV-MAE, DeepAVFusion, AV-Siam, Equi-AV) and seven foundation models (Gemini 1.5/2.0, VideoLLaMA-2, Unified-IO-2, PandaGPT, OLA)—are benchmarked in zero-shot or finetuned settings, revealing that foundation models are heavily biased toward visual cues while embedding models rely more on audio.

## Results

On VGGSounder (15.4k clips, 309 classes, multi-label), the best subset accuracy for audio-visual input is 25.61% (Unified-IO 2), while CAV-MAE leads among embedding models at 24.49%. For F1 score, Gemini 1.5 Pro achieves the highest at 53.74% (av), followed by VideoLLaMA 2 at 52.35% and OLA at 46.48%. Modality confusion (μ) reveals that 4–11% of samples are correctly classified unimodally but misclassified when both modalities are provided, with OLA showing the highest audio confusion (μ_A = 15.47%) and Gemini 1.5 Pro the lowest (μ_A = 2.09%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among embedding models, CAV-MAE achieves the best subset accuracy (24.49% av) and F1 (42.62% av), outperforming DeepAVFusion (21.53%/37.35%), Equi-AV (20.00%/34.69%), and AV-Siam (22.83%/39.43%). Among foundation models, Unified-IO 2 leads in subset accuracy (25.61% av) while Gemini 1.5 Pro dominates F1 (53.74% av) and Hit (75.42% av), substantially exceeding VideoLLaMA 2 (52.35%/59.80%), OLA (46.48%/51.51%), and PandaGPT (20.85%/18.82%). A striking asymmetry emerges: embedding models show stronger audio unimodal performance (e.g., CAV-MAE: 13.19% audio vs 19.23% visual subset accuracy), whereas Gemini models are heavily visual-biased (Gemini 1.5 Pro: 3.05% audio vs 20.86% visual). On modality confusion, Gemini 1.5 Pro has the lowest μ_A (2.09%), while OLA suffers the highest (15.47%), indicating OLA is most distracted by the audio modality when visual input is also available. Overall, foundation models match or slightly exceed finetuned embedding models on multi-modal F1, but all models exhibit 4–11% modality confusion, confirming that adding a second modality degrades performance on a non-trivial subset of samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper follows the 'Are We Done with X?' paradigm established by Beyer et al. (ImageNet) and Gema et al. (MMLU), re-annotating an existing benchmark to fix systematic labeling errors. It extends VGGSound from a single-label to a multi-label classification setting and, to the authors' knowledge, introduces the first audio-visual classification benchmark with per-label modality annotations (audible, visible, both) and meta-labels for common confounders. The evaluation spans both purpose-built embedding models (CAV-MAE, DeepAVFusion, AV-Siam, Equi-AV) and autoregressive foundation models (Gemini family, VideoLLaMA-2, Unified-IO-2, PandaGPT, OLA), bridging the gap between specialized audio-visual literature and the emerging foundation-model evaluation landscape.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 48.43% of VGGSound test samples have misaligned modalities directly challenges the widely held assumption of strong audio-visual correspondence in this dataset, and the modality confusion metric (μ) reveals that all evaluated models are negatively impacted by an additional input modality on 4–11% of samples—a concerning failure mode for models that should preserve unimodal capabilities. The observation that closed-source Gemini models rely almost exclusively on visual cues, while finetuned embedding models favor audio, highlights a fundamental asymmetry in how different model families process multimodal information. Open questions include whether modality confusion can be mitigated through architectural changes or training strategies, and whether the findings generalize beyond the VGGSound domain to other audio-visual tasks.
