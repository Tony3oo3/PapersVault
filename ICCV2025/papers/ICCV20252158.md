## Problem

Adapting CLIP to Blind Image Quality Assessment (BIQA) with limited labeled data is challenging because CLIP's pretraining emphasizes high-level semantics rather than low-level distortion cues, causing direct prompt tuning to barely outperform linear probing. Two specific failures arise: prompt initialization sensitivity leads to large performance variability across random seeds, and fine-tuning on small samples causes overfitting to spurious semantic correlations rather than true quality indicators. The paper formalizes this as the Few-Shot IQA problem, targeting rapid adaptation to new distortion types with only 50–200 labeled images.

## Input / Output

The model takes a distorted RGB image, randomly cropped into 10 patches of 224×224, and produces a binary quality probability (high vs. low quality) computed via cosine similarity between the image embedding and two learned text prompts ('high quality' / 'low quality'). During fine-tuning, only the last four blocks of CLIP's image and text encoders plus the prompt parameters are updated, keeping the bulk of the 151M-parameter model frozen. The final quality score is the predicted probability of the 'high quality' class, which is then correlated with ground-truth MOS via SRCC and PLCC.

## Method

GRMP-IQA comprises two modules operating in sequence. First, the Meta-Prompt Pre-training Module (MPP) organizes distortion-specific tasks from TID2013 and KADID-10K into a meta-training set and uses bi-level gradient descent (inner-loop on support sets, outer-loop on query sets) to jointly optimize CoOp-style text prompts (4 learnable context vectors per quality class) and Deep Prompt Tuning visual prompts (learnable tokens appended at each ViT layer), distilling shared quality priors across distortion types. Second, the Quality-Aware Gradient Regularization (QGR) module, applied during few-shot fine-tuning, computes the quality gradient from the cross-entropy loss and the semantic gradient from the KL divergence between the fine-tuned and original CLIP's zero-shot semantic class predictions; when the two gradients are aligned (dot product > 0), the quality gradient is projected to remove its component parallel to the semantic direction, scaled by a soft weight λ=5, thereby suppressing semantic overfitting while preserving quality-relevant learning.

## Results

In the few-shot setting (50/100/200 labels), GRMP-IQA achieves SRCC of 0.836/0.857/0.875 on LIVEC, 0.853/0.872/0.883 on KonIQ, and 0.932/0.943/0.968 on LIVE, consistently outperforming the strongest few-shot baseline GRepQ by 6–9 SRCC points. Under the fully supervised 20% training ratio, GRMP-IQA reaches SRCC 0.875 on LIVEC and 0.915 on KonIQ, matching or exceeding methods trained on 80% of the data (e.g., DEIQT at 80%: 0.875 LIVEC, 0.921 KonIQ). On the challenging PIPAL dataset with 40 distortion types, GRMP-IQA achieves 0.546 SRCC at 200 labels, surpassing the next best method (GRepQ, 0.498) by nearly 5 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRMP-IQA achieves the best SRCC across all five few-shot datasets (LIVEC, KonIQ, CSIQ, LIVE, PIPAL) at every label budget (50/100/200), outperforming the closest competitor GRepQ [40] by 6.6–7.6 SRCC points on LIVEC/KonIQ and 4.8 points on PIPAL at 200 labels. Against CLIP-based baselines, GRMP-IQA surpasses CLIP w/ CoOp [44] by 12.9 SRCC on LIVEC (0.875 vs. 0.746) and 22.1 on PIPAL (0.546 vs. not reported), and outperforms LIQE [52] by 6.5 SRCC on LIVEC at 200 labels. In the fully supervised 20% setting (Table 2), GRMP-IQA matches or exceeds 80%-trained methods: it achieves 0.875 SRCC on LIVEC (vs. DEIQT 80%: 0.875, LoDa 80%: 0.876) and 0.915 on KonIQ (vs. LoDa 80%: 0.932, QFM-IQM 80%: 0.922), while using only 20% of the training data. On cross-dataset generalization (Table 3), GRMP-IQA leads on LIVEC (0.831) and KonIQ (0.782) when trained on LIVEFB, outperforming LoDa and DEIQT. In the efficiency comparison (Table 8), GRMP-IQA (151M params, 21.3 ms latency) is 4.7× faster than Q-Align [45] (8.2B params, 101.0 ms) while achieving higher SRCC on KonIQ (0.934 vs. 0.940 at 80% train; 0.915 vs. 0.903 at 20% train).

## Relation to Prior Work

GRMP-IQA builds directly on CoOp (Zhou et al., IJCV 2022) for text prompt tuning and Visual Prompt Tuning (Jia et al., ECCV 2022) for visual prompt tuning, but departs from both by introducing a meta-learning pre-training stage organized around distortion types rather than semantic categories. It also extends the meta-learning lineage of MetaIQA (Zhu et al., CVPR 2020) by applying bi-level optimization to prompt parameters rather than full network weights, and introduces a novel Quality-Aware Gradient Regularization that clips quality gradients along semantic directions—a mechanism absent from prior prompt-tuning or IQA work. The paper positions itself against CLIP-IQA (Wang et al., 2023) and GRepQ (Srinath et al., WACV 2024) as the first to systematically address the two failure modes (initialization sensitivity and semantic overfitting) that limit CLIP-based prompt tuning for IQA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Few-shot IQA addresses a practical bottleneck: MOS annotation is prohibitively expensive, and the paper demonstrates that 20% of training data can match fully supervised SOTA, with only 151M parameters versus 8.2B for Q-Align. The gradient regularization technique is parameter-free and could potentially transfer to other tasks where a pretrained model's semantic bias conflicts with the target objective. Open questions include whether the meta-prompt pre-training generalizes to entirely unseen distortion families (e.g., new GAN artifacts), whether the approach scales to video quality assessment, and whether the fixed λ=5 hyperparameter remains optimal across different CLIP variants or larger model scales.
