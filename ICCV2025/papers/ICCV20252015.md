## Problem

Existing physics-driven simulators for Gaussian Splatting representations either rely on generative video models without explicit physics constraints or use analytical priors like MPM that treat Gaussian kernels as discrete particles under idealized assumptions, failing to capture the full complexity of real-world elastic deformations. No prior method bridges the gap between simulated environments and reality by modeling Gaussian kernels as continuous matter governed by continuum mechanics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252015/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

GausSim consumes two consecutive Gaussian Splatting states (positions, covariances, colors, opacities at t−1 and t) plus per-kernel material attributes (density ρ_k and a d-dimensional deformation property vector a_k), and produces the deformed Gaussian Splatting at t+1 with updated positions, covariances, and view-dependent colors. Training is supervised by multi-view rendered images matched against ground-truth video frames, with auto-regressive unrolling over T steps and gradients back-propagated only between adjacent time steps to prevent explosion. The hierarchical structure reduces the number of predicted deformation gradients from N_K (one per kernel) to roughly 5% of that count.

## Method

GausSim treats each Gaussian kernel as a Center of Mass System (CMS) representing a continuous piece of matter, and predicts per-kernel deformation gradients F decomposed via Polar SVD (two quaternions for rotations U, V and a 3D vector for singular values) to update positions, covariances, and colors. A bottom-up hierarchical clustering groups nearby kernels into coarser CMSs, and simulation proceeds top-down: deformation gradients are predicted only at the coarsest levels and propagated recursively to individual kernels, cutting kernel-wise computations by ~95%. Explicit physics constraints enforce mass conservation by normalizing the singular-value vector so det(F)=1, and momentum conservation via a squared-error loss linking parent CMS velocities to weighted sums of child CMS velocities. Training combines multi-view L2 and D-SSIM image losses with a self-supervised static loss (zero-velocity inputs must yield unchanged outputs) applied with probability 1/T to stabilize early auto-regressive predictions.

## Results

GausSim is evaluated on the READY dataset (four real objects: Mothorchids, Carnation, Pudding, Duck at 50 FPS) and a synthetic Bunny (24 FPS), measuring ℓ2 pixel errors (×10⁻³) against ground-truth multi-view videos. On the challenging Mothorchids domain, GausSim achieves 1.78±0.23 ℓ2 error on seen frames (t=0–15) and 1.85±0.14 on unseen 83 frames (t=16–99). GausSim attains the lowest ℓ2 errors across all five domains and both seen/unseen splits, while also reducing kernel-wise computations by ~95% (N_F/N_K = 0.05) and running at 0.13 s/frame with 2.1 GB GPU memory on an A100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Two baselines are compared: DreamGaussian4D (DG4D) [30], a video-diffusion-based 4D generator, and PhysDreamer (PD) [45], which integrates an MPM physics engine with estimated material parameters. GausSim outperforms both on every domain and every evaluation split (seen and unseen). Against DG4D, GausSim wins decisively on unseen frames because DG4D cannot extrapolate beyond its training set and fails to model motion dissipation, yielding substantially higher ℓ2 errors on test frames. Against PD, GausSim wins on seen frames (PD struggles with complex deformations like those in Mothorchids, producing large training errors) and also on unseen frames where PD's rapid dissipation creates an abnormally low error that is a shortcut rather than faithful prediction. The paper notes that PD's low unseen errors are misleading—caused by the model collapsing to near-static predictions—while GausSim maintains physically accurate, non-dissipating elastic dynamics throughout the full 100-frame sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

GausSim departs from the dominant paradigm in physics-based Gaussian simulation (PhysGaussian, PhysDreamer, GASP, Neuma) that treats each Gaussian kernel as a discrete particle within an analytical framework like MPM or mass-spring systems. Instead, it reinterprets each kernel as a Center of Mass System (CMS) representing a continuous piece of matter, enabling direct application of continuum mechanics deformation gradients (via Polar SVD) without idealized assumptions. The hierarchical CMS structure for coarse-to-fine simulation and the explicit mass/momentum conservation constraints are novel architectural choices not present in prior Gaussian-physics hybrids. The paper also introduces the READY dataset, filling a gap left by prior work that relied on synthetic or video-diffusion-generated dynamics rather than real-world multi-view elastic deformation recordings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By grounding Gaussian kernel simulation in continuum mechanics rather than particle approximations, GausSim offers a more physically faithful and interpretable path toward real-world elastic dynamics, with the 95% computational reduction making it practical for densely reconstructed objects. The READY dataset provides a much-needed real-world benchmark for evaluating physics-based simulation beyond synthetic or diffusion-generated motion. Limitations include dependence on high-quality Gaussian Splatting reconstruction, sensitivity to camera shutter speed for fast motions, and restriction to elastic (incompressible) objects; extending to viscoelastic, plastic, or fluid-like materials remains open. The moderate performance gap over MPM-based baselines suggests that the continuum-mechanics framing's full advantage may require more complex deformation regimes to manifest.
