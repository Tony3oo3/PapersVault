## Problem

Existing multimodal semantic segmentation methods that fuse RGB and infrared features either rely on cross-modal self-attention or fusion loss functions, but both approaches neglect the detrimental impact of inter-modal feature discrepancies during fusion. Fusion-loss-driven methods tend to produce biased dominance of one modality, while cross-modal attention mechanisms risk discarding critical details from either modality during feature matching. The paper tackles the underexplored problem of actively compressing and aligning discrepant features between modalities to construct semantically consistent, segmentable fusion representations.

## Input / Output

The method takes a spatially aligned pair of RGB (visible) and infrared images and processes them through a shared SegFormer encoder that extracts features at four hierarchical levels. It simultaneously produces two outputs: a per-pixel semantic segmentation mask (via a decoder) and a fused RGB image (via a fusion head borrowed from MRFS). The fusion head and decoder operate on the aligned multimodal features produced by the FDAM module, so both outputs are derived from the same discrepancy-aligned representation rather than from raw or independently fused features.

## Method

AMDANet employs a three-stage divide-and-conquer strategy to align cross-modal feature discrepancies. First, Semantic Consistency Inference (SCI) computes cosine similarity between infrared and visible features at each encoder level; when similarity falls below a threshold, an MLP-generated ambiguity mask identifies bias-affected regions, and a learnable parameter λ subtracts the inferred bias component from both modalities to suppress encoder-induced modality preferences at their source. Second, the Feature Discrepancy Alignment Module (FDAM) operates at two scales: local-alignment uses channel-wise and spatial squeeze-excitation attention to generate feature cues, then applies sigmoid-gated difference reallocation (cues minus original features) to suppress discrepant channels and spatial regions; global-alignment introduces a saliency cross-modal attention that first self-enhances salient features via MobileViT-style enhancement, then performs asymmetric Q-K-V matching (visible queries attend to infrared keys/values, and vice versa) to align long-range context without misjudging non-critical features. Third, Mutual Feature Mask Learning (MFML) randomly masks one modality's feature map at the pixel level during training and enforces prediction consistency between masked and unmasked inputs via a cross-entropy regularization loss, promoting complementary cross-modal learning. The total loss combines segmentation cross-entropy, a fusion loss (salient information + chrominance consistency), and the mask consistency loss with weights 1, 0.5, and 0.5 respectively.

## Results

AMDANet achieves mIoU of 62.1% on MFNet, 64.8% on FMB, and 88.5% on PST900, outperforming the strongest baseline MRFS by 3.0%, 3.6%, and 1.6% respectively. In terms of mAP, the method reaches 77.4% (MFNet), 77.1% (FMB), and 91.8% (PST900), surpassing MRFS by 1.8%, 1.5%, and 1.6%. The method also reports 197.85M parameters and 119.02G FLOPs, which is lower than MRFS (219.16M / 134.97G) while delivering higher accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AMDANet outperforms all 14 baselines across all three datasets on both mIoU and mAP. The closest competitor is MRFS [60], which AMDANet surpasses by 3.0% mIoU on MFNet (62.1 vs 59.1), 3.6% on FMB (64.8 vs 61.2), and 1.6% on PST900 (88.5 vs 86.9). Among other strong baselines, EAEFNet [22] (58.9 mIoU on MFNet) and OpenRss [62] (58.5 mIoU) trail by over 3 points, while SegMiF [25] (56.1) and MDRNet+ [63] (56.8) lag further behind. On PST900, the gap narrows somewhat due to sparse image quality interfering with feature boundaries, but AMDANet still leads MRFS by 1.6% and EAEFNet by 2.9%. In the image fusion evaluation (Fig. 6), AMDANet achieves the highest entropy (EN) and structural correlation difference (SCD) among six fusion baselines including CDDFuse [65], TGFuse [34], and DATFuse [46], confirming that the discrepancy-alignment strategy also improves visual quality of fused outputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

AMDANet builds directly on the MRFS framework (Zhang et al., CVPR 2024), reusing its fusion head, decoder, and image fusion loss functions, and adapts the complementary random masking strategy from Shin et al. (ICRA 2024) from image-level to feature-level masking. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant paradigm in RGB-infrared segmentation—where methods like SegMiF, MRFS, and GMNet focus on aggregating semantically consistent cross-modal features via attention or fusion losses—by explicitly modeling and suppressing discrepant features as a first-class objective. The paper introduces a two-source taxonomy of discrepancies (visual appearance differences vs. encoder-induced empirical bias) and addresses each with dedicated modules (FDAM and SCI), a framing not present in prior fusion-segmentation work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and underexplored failure mode in multimodal fusion—discrepant features actively degrading alignment—and provides a principled, ablated solution that yields consistent gains (1.6–3.6% mIoU) across three standard benchmarks. The distinction between visual discrepancies and encoder-induced bias is a useful conceptual lens that could inform other multimodal tasks beyond RGB-infrared. However, the method's computational cost (197.85M params, 119.02G FLOPs) limits deployment on resource-constrained platforms, and the authors acknowledge this as a key limitation, pointing toward knowledge distillation as a future direction. Open questions include whether the discrepancy-alignment philosophy generalizes to other modality pairs (e.g., LiDAR-camera) and whether the encoder-bias detection via cosine similarity threshold is robust across architectures.
