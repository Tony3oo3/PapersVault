## Problem

Transferable adversarial attacks face a fundamental dilemma: momentum-based methods maximize attack potency (exploitation) but produce sharp, model-specific loss surfaces, while inner-iteration random sampling methods flatten the loss surface for cross-model generalization (exploration) but sacrifice local maxima, weakening attack strength. No existing method simultaneously achieves both flat loss regions and high local maxima, leaving a gap in balancing exploration and exploitation for transferable attacks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251627/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a clean RGB image (299×299×3) with its ground-truth label and a white-box surrogate model, then produces a single adversarial example constrained within an ℓ∞-ball of radius ε=16/255. The adversarial example is evaluated for transferability against up to nine black-box target models (including CNNs, ViTs, and adversarially-trained ensembles) as well as commercial MLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude). For targeted attacks, a target label y_t is additionally specified.

## Method

GGS is an inner-iteration sampling strategy built on MI-FGSM that replaces purely random neighborhood sampling with gradient-guided sampling. At each inner-iteration i, the sampling point is computed as x̂_i = x_{t-1}^{adv} + |p̃| · sign(g̃_{i-1}), where the direction is determined by the sign of the gradient from the previous inner-iteration and the magnitude is drawn from a uniform random distribution, preserving stochasticity. This design creates only single-step dependencies (unlike momentum-guided sampling's long-chain accumulation), allowing the sampler to escape early-stage noise while still aligning with the stable gradient ascent direction. The averaged gradient over N inner-iterations then updates the momentum and the adversarial example via the standard MI-FGSM outer-iteration step. The result is adversarial examples that reside in flat loss regions with higher local maxima, simultaneously improving cross-model transferability and attack potency.

## Results

On ImageNet (1,000 images, ε=16/255, T=10 outer iterations, N=20 inner iterations), GGS achieves the highest average transfer ASR across all three surrogate models: 82.08%/17.67% (untargeted/targeted) from Res50, 69.27%/9.80% from Inc-v3, and 83.33%/17.22% from ViT-B, outperforming the strongest baseline PGN by 5.55%/10.83%, 6.11%/4.08%, and 8.04%/7.31% respectively. On multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o mini, Gemini pro flash, Claude sonnet pro flash), GGS reduces average classification success rate to 47.54%, a 9.26-point improvement over the best baseline PGN (56.80%). GGS also improves surrogate-model attack potency, achieving 4–25% higher targeted ASR on the generating model compared to PGN and GRA.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GGS outperforms all 12 baselines on average transfer ASR across all three surrogate models. Against the strongest gradient-based competitor PGN [7], GGS gains +5.55% untargeted and +10.83% targeted ASR (Res50 surrogate), +6.11%/+4.08% (Inc-v3), and +8.04%/+7.31% (ViT-B). Against GRA [40], the gap is even larger: +8.67%/+9.06% (Res50), +7.86%/+1.98% (Inc-v3), and +11.17%/+6.04% (ViT-B). On MLLMs, GGS reduces average CSR to 47.54% versus PGN's 56.80% and GRA's 58.50%, a >9-point improvement. GGS also substantially boosts surrogate-model attack potency: targeted ASR on Res50 jumps from 49.5% (PGN) to 90.7% (GGS), and on ViT-B from 82.2% (PGN) to 99.2% (GGS). When combined with input transformations (DIM, TIM, SIM, Admix, SSM), GGS adds 6–43% untargeted ASR and 4–19% targeted ASR over the standalone methods, confirming broad compatibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of momentum-based adversarial attacks (MI-FGSM, NI-FGSM) and inner-iteration random sampling methods (PGN, GRA) that aim to flatten the loss surface for better transferability. It identifies a fundamental exploitation–exploration tradeoff: momentum methods find sharp high-loss maxima (strong attack, poor transfer), while random-sampling methods find flat but lower-loss regions (good transfer, weaker attack). GGS resolves this by borrowing the NAG lookahead mechanism from NI-FGSM but replacing the cumulative momentum term with the single-step gradient from the previous inner-iteration, thereby avoiding the long-chain dependencies that degrade exploration while retaining directional stability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exploration–exploitation framing gives practitioners a clear diagnostic for why existing transfer attacks underperform on target models, and GGS offers a drop-in refinement compatible with both gradient-averaging methods (PGN, GRA) and input transformations (DIM, SIM, etc.), making it broadly adoptable. The extension to MLLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude) is timely given growing security concerns around these systems. However, the authors note GGS is not yet compatible with non-gradient-averaging inner-iteration methods (VMI-FGSM, RAP), and the core contribution remains a sampling-strategy refinement rather than a new attack paradigm, leaving open whether further gains require fundamentally different optimization objectives.
