## Problem

Quanta image sensors capture individual photon detections at ~100 kHz, enabling imaging in extreme low-light and high-speed motion, but existing vision pipelines require expensive intermediate image reconstruction before running perception models. This reconstruction imposes 2–3 orders of magnitude overhead in compute, memory, and latency, precluding deployment on resource-constrained edge platforms. The paper asks whether high-level scene understanding can be extracted directly from raw photon streams without any image reconstruction step.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252233/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a continuous stream of binary (1-bit) quanta frames at the sensor's native frame rate (~96.8 kHz on a 256×512 SwissSPAD2 array), where each pixel records whether at least one photon was detected. The sensor-proximal QNN layer ingests frames at full rate, while downstream layers subsample by 64×–256× depending on the task. Outputs (depth maps, point tracks, or reconstructed intensities) are produced causally in a streaming fashion at variable rates ranging from 20 Hz to 24 kHz, determined adaptively by scene dynamics rather than a fixed cadence.

## Method

The core contribution is a trainable QNN layer built on scalar selective state-space models (analogous to Mamba-2) that can be inserted between layers of any existing image- or video-based network to yield a quanta counterpart. The first (sensor-proximal) QNN layer performs motion-adaptive exponential smoothing, where the per-pixel time constant is modulated by a Bayesian online changepoint detector estimating the run length of the Bernoulli photon stream, balancing noise reduction against motion blur. Intermediate QNN layers apply multi-head scalar SSM recurrences with channel expansion to feature maps, using learnable linear parameterizations for the state-space parameters. Two complementary inference modes exploit temporal redundancy: eventful computation updates linear layers incrementally on top-K% input changes (reducing FLOPs 5–10×), and change-driven sampling triggers full-network inference only when the fraction of significant changes in the adaptive integrator exceeds a threshold, yielding irregular output rates. Training uses transfer learning from pretrained backbones (DepthAnything-v2, Pips++), finetuning only the inserted QNN layers and task heads on simulated quanta sequences.

## Results

Across three tasks (depth estimation, point tracking, intensity restoration), QNNs achieve 1–2 orders of magnitude improvements in compute, latency, and readout over reconstruction-based quanta vision while maintaining high task accuracy. For depth estimation on a high-speed stress-ball scene, the QNN requires 78 GFLOP/output (vs. 502 GFLOP for video-denoising + depth) and 38 ms latency (vs. 6000 ms); with eventful computation this drops further to 26 GFLOP and 770 bps readout. For point tracking in 3-lux low light, the QNN consumes 350× fewer FLOPs and 30× less readout than the QUIVER reconstruction baseline. Change-driven sampling yields a 150× run-time speedup and 240× less readout on a balloon-burst sequence by adaptively varying output rate from 20 Hz to 24 kHz.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the video-denoising + depth-estimation pipeline [41,76], the QNN wins decisively on efficiency: 6.4× fewer FLOPs in dense mode (78 vs. 502 GFLOP/output) and 150× lower latency (38 ms vs. 6000 ms), while achieving comparable or better depth accuracy on high-speed non-rigid scenes where long-exposure reconstruction blurs the dynamics. Against QUIVER [8] for point tracking in 3-lux low light, the QNN achieves 350× lower FLOP counts and 30× less data readout while producing high-accuracy streamlined tracks; QUIVER's recurrent architecture still incurs high per-frame processing cost. Against the bandwidth-efficient videography method [67] for intensity restoration, the QNN delivers 4× lower FLOPs and 32× lower latency in dense mode, with an additional order-of-magnitude FLOP reduction under eventful computation, while matching the baseline's modest readout numbers via sparse encoding. In the rate–performance tradeoff study (Fig. 8), QNNs dominate both baselines across the full sweep of readout rates, offering a graceful performance-to-compute tradeoff that the reconstruction baselines cannot match, particularly for depth estimation where QNNs model temporal dynamics throughout the architecture rather than per-frame.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads: (1) adaptive temporal aggregation of photon detections, where the sensor-proximal layer is shown to be a selective state space model driven by Bayesian online changepoint detection, and (2) the Mamba-style scalar SSM recurrence, which is repurposed as a general learnable temporal layer (the QNN layer) operating on feature maps. It departs from reconstruction-based quanta vision (QUIVER, video denoising, bandwidth-efficient videography) by eliminating the intermediate image entirely, and from prior direct-perception quanta work (which handled only 1–8 frames or assumed static scenes) by providing a broadly applicable recipe that aggregates thousands of high-speed frames with motion. The eventful computation strategy for incremental linear-layer updates is inherited from Event Neural Networks and Eventful Transformers, adapted here to the streaming quanta setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By skipping image reconstruction, QNNs make quanta-sensor perception viable on resource-constrained edge platforms (Jetson Nano vs. A100 GPU), potentially unlocking reliable vision in extreme low-light and ultra-high-speed scenarios where conventional cameras fail. The general recipe—inserting a lightweight scalar SSM layer into any feedforward image or video network—means the broader CV community's architectural progress can be directly inherited by quanta vision. Key open questions include: extending to self-supervised or unsupervised settings for tasks lacking paired quanta ground truth (object detection, segmentation), realizing wall-time speedups from eventful computation on GPU sparse backends, and exploring split sensor-processor computing as near-sensor hardware matures.
