## Problem

Existing image editing methods are designed for single-step modifications, but real-world applications like product design and iterative retouching require progressive, multi-turn refinement. Naively chaining single-step flow-matching or diffusion-based editors causes truncation errors to accumulate across iterations, leading to increasing artifacts, semantic drift, and distribution bias. The trade-off between preserving original content and maintaining sufficient editing flexibility over multiple turns remains unaddressed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a source RGB image and a sequence of text editing prompts (one per turn) as input, producing an edited image at each iteration. It operates on the FLUX.1-dev DiT architecture in latent space, using 15 inversion and 15 sampling steps per turn (or 8 steps with FireFlow acceleration). The evaluation extends PIE-Bench with four additional rounds of editing instructions generated by GPT-4 Turbo, enabling systematic multi-turn benchmarking that no prior dataset supported.

## Method

The framework combines three key components for training-free multi-turn editing on FLUX. First, a dual-objective LQR guidance mechanism replaces the single-reference LQR of RF-Inversion by anchoring each sampling step to both the original image and the previous edit result via a weighted composite target X_0^dual = X_0^0 + λ(X_0^{k-1} − X_0^0), establishing long-term dependency that prevents cumulative drift. Second, a second-order ODE solver (FireFlow acceleration) reduces per-step truncation error during inversion. Third, an adaptive attention guidance mechanism exploits the layer-wise roles of FLUX's 19 double blocks: medium-to-low activated self-attention maps (blocks 10–14) are identified as encoding fine-grained spatial focus, and their averaged, thresholded output forms a probabilistic mask that reweights attention at the next timestep, concentrating edits on salient regions while suppressing changes elsewhere. This attention reweighting counteracts the over-regularization imposed by LQR, restoring editability without sacrificing structural coherence.

## Results

On the fourth-turn editing benchmark (PIE-Bench), the proposed method achieves the best FID of 5.553 (15 steps) and 5.396 (8 steps), substantially outperforming all baselines. It also attains the highest CLIP-T score of 26.831, indicating the strongest prompt-image alignment, while maintaining a competitive CLIP-I of 0.894 for content preservation. Notably, the method requires only 15 steps (or 8 in the accelerated variant) compared to 25–50 steps for most baselines, achieving superior quality with fewer inference steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all seven baselines on FID, the most critical metric for multi-turn consistency, with a margin of 2.4 over the next-best RF-Inversion (5.553 vs. 5.740) and 2.4 over FireFlow (5.553 vs. 7.970). On CLIP-T (prompt alignment), it leads all methods except FireFlow-v (26.831 vs. 28.281), but FireFlow-v suffers a much worse FID (12.375) and CLIP-I (0.873), indicating it over-edits at the cost of structural fidelity. Against diffusion-based baselines MasaCtrl and PnPInversion, the method achieves roughly 2× lower FID (5.553 vs. 10.811/10.262) and higher CLIP-T, while also requiring fewer steps (15 vs. 50). The closest competitor on content preservation is RF-Solver (CLIP-I 0.906 vs. 0.894), but RF-Solver's FID is more than double (11.581) and its CLIP-T is lower (25.516), showing it preserves structure at the expense of successful editing. Overall, the method uniquely balances all three metrics—achieving the best FID and CLIP-T while keeping CLIP-I within 0.01 of the best—demonstrating that the dual-objective LQR and adaptive attention guidance jointly resolve the stability–editability trade-off that plagues single-objective approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rectified-flow inversion line (RF-Inversion, FireFlow, RF-Solver, StableFlow) by identifying that single-step error reduction via high-order ODE solvers is insufficient for multi-turn editing, where cumulative truncation error dominates. It departs from RF-Inversion's single-objective LQR (which references only the most recent edit) by introducing a dual-objective LQR that simultaneously anchors to the original image and the previous turn's output, establishing a long-term dependency. The adaptive attention guidance mechanism is novel in its use of layer-wise DiT block analysis to select medium-to-low activated self-attention maps as spatial editing masks, building on the observation from Xu et al. that FLUX's lower-left self-attention quadrant encodes text-to-image spatial influence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Multi-turn iterative editing is a practical requirement in product design, photo retouching, and creative workflows where user intent is ambiguous and progressive refinement is needed, yet no prior framework systematically addresses the error accumulation problem across editing rounds. The paper's key open questions include whether the approach scales beyond four editing turns (the quantitative evaluation limit), whether temporal-consistency techniques from video editing can further improve cross-turn coherence, and whether inversion-free methods could eliminate the source of truncation error entirely. The FLUX-specific attention analysis limits immediate generalizability to other DiT architectures, and the single-dataset evaluation (extended PIE-Bench) leaves robustness untested.
