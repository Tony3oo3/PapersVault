## Problem

Cross-view geo-localization has been dominated by image-to-image retrieval, leaving text-guided localization largely unexplored despite its practical value for pedestrian navigation and emergency response. Existing text-based localization methods (e.g., Text2Pose, Text2Loc) depend on expensive 3D point cloud maps, while the more scalable OSM/satellite cross-view paradigm has not been adapted to accept natural language queries. Furthermore, current retrieval methods provide only similarity scores without interpretable reasoning, making confident user decisions difficult.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250675/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a long natural language scene description (averaging 126 tokens, generated by GPT-4o with OCR and open-world segmentation assistance from street-view images) as the query, and retrieves the matching satellite image (512×512, zoom level 20, ~0.12 m ground resolution) or OSM raster tile (512×512) from a candidate pool of ~100 nearby images covering roughly 10 km². Beyond the ranked image list, the system outputs per-candidate similarity scores, a natural language retrieval reason explaining which visual cues drove the match, and a confidence score in [0,1] that can trigger re-ranking of the top-5 results.

## Method

CrossText2Loc is a dual-stream contrastive model (CLIP-L/14@336px backbone) that jointly trains a text encoder and a visual encoder with an InfoNCE loss over batches of 128, aligning long scene descriptions with satellite/OSM images across a significant domain gap. The key architectural contribution is the Expanded Positional Embedding (EPE) module, which linearly interpolates CLIP's 77-token positional embeddings to 300 tokens, enabling the encoder to process full-length scene descriptions without truncation. An optional Explainable Retrieval Module (ERM) accumulates non-negative gradient contributions across attention layers to produce text and image heatmaps, which are then fed to GPT-4o to generate a natural language retrieval rationale and a confidence score. The confidence scores enable a re-ranking step that combines normalized similarity and confidence over the top-5 candidates, simulating a user's decision process and improving recall over pure similarity ranking.

## Results

On the CVG-Text dataset, CrossText2Loc achieves R@1 of 50.33% (satellite, New York) and 62.33% (OSM, New York), outperforming the strongest baseline BLIP (34.58% and 52.92% respectively) by 15.75 and 9.41 points. Compared to the CLIP-L/14 backbone, the method improves R@1 by 14.1% and R@10 by 14.8%. The Explainable Retrieval Module (ERM) provides an additional ~4% R@1 gain over the retrieval-only variant (e.g., 50.33% vs. 46.25% on satellite NY). The Expanded Positional Embedding (EPE) module alone boosts R@1 by 10.5% (satellite) and 26.3% (OSM) over the unmodified CLIP encoder.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CrossText2Loc outperforms all nine baselines across every city and data-source combination. The strongest baseline is BLIP, which is not constrained by the 77-token text limit and achieves 52.92% R@1 on OSM New York; CrossText2Loc surpasses it by 9.41 points on that setting and by 15.75 points on satellite New York (50.33% vs. 34.58%). Among CLIP-family models, CLIP-L/14 (the backbone) reaches 35.08% R@1 on satellite NY, while the full CrossText2Loc pipeline lifts this to 50.33% (+15.25 pts), with the EPE module contributing the largest single gain (+11.17 pts over raw CLIP-L/14). SigLIP-SO400M is the best satellite-only baseline (33.50% R@1 NY) but trails CrossText2Loc by 16.83 points. On OSM retrieval, the text modality is especially valuable: adding a text branch to the image-only Sample4G method improves R@1 by 40.2% for OSM versus only 6.7% for satellite, confirming that scene text descriptions align more closely with OSM POI data than with aerial imagery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new intersection between two established lines of work: cross-view geo-localization (which has focused almost exclusively on image-to-image retrieval, e.g., Sample4G, CVUSA, VIGOR) and natural-language-based localization (which has relied on expensive 3D point clouds, e.g., Text2Pose, Text2Loc). By formulating text-to-satellite/OSM retrieval, the authors sidestep the storage and coverage limitations of 3D maps while retaining the practical accessibility of 2D map data. Methodologically, the architecture is a CLIP-style dual-stream contrastive model whose key technical departure from Long-CLIP is the use of full linear interpolation rather than Knowledge Preserving Stretching for positional embedding extension, justified by the absence of salient short titles in GPT-generated scene descriptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The CVG-Text dataset (30k+ locations, three cities, aligned street-view/satellite/OSM/text quadruples) provides the first benchmark for text-guided cross-view geo-localization, a task with clear practical value in GPS-denied or GPS-degraded urban scenarios (pedestrian navigation, emergency calls). The Explainable Retrieval Module is a meaningful step toward interpretable geo-localization, moving beyond opaque similarity scores to natural-language rationales with confidence estimates. However, several limitations remain open: the text is GPT-4o-generated rather than truly human-written (raising generalization questions), evaluation is confined to three cities with a fixed 100-sample retrieval pool, and the fundamental domain gap between street-level semantic descriptions and top-down satellite/OSM imagery is addressed only through contrastive alignment without explicit geometric reasoning. Scaling to truly human-authored, noisy, or multilingual descriptions and to larger retrieval pools will be critical next steps.
