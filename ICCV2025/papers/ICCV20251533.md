## Problem

Existing multi-task optimization (MTO) methods focus on optimizer-centric loss scaling and gradient manipulation to resolve task conflicts, yet deliver inconsistent gains especially in demanding scenarios. The shared representation space, where task interactions naturally occur, has been underexplored as a venue for operations that explicitly facilitate inter-task complementarity rather than merely solving conflicts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251533/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Rep-MTL operates within a standard hard parameter sharing (HPS) architecture: an RGB image is encoded by a shared backbone (e.g., dilated ResNet-50 in DeepLabV3+) into a latent feature map Z ∈ R^{C×H'×W'}, which is then decoded by task-specific heads into segmentation masks, depth maps, surface normal maps, or class labels depending on the benchmark. The method is purely a regularization on the shared representation Z and requires no modifications to either the optimizer or the network architecture, making it a drop-in addition to any existing MTO pipeline.

## Method

Rep-MTL computes representation-level task saliency S_t = ∇_Z L_t for each task, capturing how sensitively each task's loss responds to changes in the shared feature map. Two complementary modules then regularize these saliencies: (i) Task-specific Saliency Regulation (TSR) aggregates channel-wise saliencies into spatial maps, normalizes them into per-region task-wise probability distributions, and applies an entropy penalty that discourages uniform (high-entropy) distributions, thereby preserving task-specific learning patterns and mitigating negative transfer. (ii) Cross-task Saliency Alignment (CSA) computes per-task affinity maps (S_t S_t^T), derives per-sample reference anchors by spatially averaging saliencies, and applies an InfoNCE-style contrastive loss that pulls same-sample task affinities toward the anchor (positive pairs) while pushing different-sample anchors apart (negative pairs), explicitly promoting inter-task complementarity. The total loss is the standard MTL objective plus weighted TSR and CSA terms, with gradients flowing through all components during joint optimization.

## Results

On NYUv2 (3-task indoor scene understanding), Rep-MTL with basic equal weighting achieves Δp_task = +1.70±0.29 and Δp_metric = +0.95, outperforming the previous SOTA DB-MTL (+1.15, +0.56) by ~48% and ~70% respectively. On Cityscapes (2-task urban scene understanding), Rep-MTL reaches Δp_task = +0.62±0.53 versus DB-MTL's +0.20±0.40. On Office-Home (4-domain classification), Rep-MTL achieves Δp_task = +0.41±0.58, advancing the prior SOTA by ~140% (+0.41 vs. +0.17). Notably, most gradient manipulation baselines (PCGrad, GradDrop, GradVac, Nash-MTL, etc.) exhibit negative Δp_task on NYUv2, while Rep-MTL is one of only two methods to achieve positive multi-task gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Rep-MTL (with basic EW) outperforms the strongest prior method DB-MTL on all four benchmarks: +1.70 vs. +1.15 on NYUv2 Δp_task, +0.62 vs. +0.20 on Cityscapes, and +0.41 vs. +0.17 on Office-Home. Among loss scaling baselines, IGBv2 (+0.05) and GLS (+0.30) are the closest to positive gains on NYUv2, but both fall well short of Rep-MTL; DWA (−1.71) and UW (−1.52) show substantial negative transfer. Gradient manipulation methods are largely ineffective in this challenging setting: PCGrad (−1.57), GradDrop (−1.85), GradVac (−1.75), and Nash-MTL (−1.01) all degrade below the single-task baseline, while CAGrad (−0.27) and STCH (+0.06) are near-neutral. The only other method achieving clearly positive multi-task gains is DB-MTL, which Rep-MTL surpasses by a wide margin. On Office-Home, Rep-MTL's +0.41 Δp_task represents a ~140% improvement over DB-MTL's +0.17, and on Office-31 it edges out the prior SOTA by 25% (+1.31 vs. +1.05).

## Relation to Prior Work

Rep-MTL departs from the dominant optimizer-centric MTO paradigm (loss scaling, gradient manipulation) by operating directly in the shared representation space, building on the observation that task saliencies (gradients w.r.t. shared features, as in Grad-CAM) encode rich task-interaction signals. The closest prior works—RotoGrad and SRDML—also exploit representation-level saliencies but focus on minimizing task semantic disparities or regularizing task-wise similarity, leaving inter-task complementarity to architecture design. Rep-MTL's key conceptual departure is twofold: (i) an entropy-based penalty on saliency distributions to preserve task-specific patterns rather than resolve gradient conflicts, and (ii) a contrastive alignment of sample-wise saliency affinity maps to explicitly promote cross-task information sharing, a direction largely unexplored in MTO.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a practical and complementary regularization that can be layered onto any existing MTO method (demonstrated with EW) without modifying optimizers or architectures, addressing the well-documented inconsistency of gradient manipulation methods in challenging settings. The finding that explicit cross-task alignment (CSA) contributes more to performance than task-specific preservation (TSR) suggests that the field has under-invested in directly exploiting inter-task complementarity at the optimization level. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to transformer backbones, scales to many more tasks, and whether the contrastive alignment design (temperature, batch composition) can be further refined. The PL exponent analysis as an MTO evaluation tool is also a useful contribution that could inform future method comparisons beyond standard metrics.
