## Problem

Existing membership inference attack (MIA) methods provide empirical attack performance but lack theoretical guarantees on the false discovery rate (FDR) — the expected proportion of false positives among all identified members. Two fundamental obstacles prevent straightforward application of multiple-testing theory: the true non-member score distribution is unknown, and the estimated non-member probabilities for all test samples are interdependent because they are all computed against the same shared calibration set.

## Input / Output

The method operates as a post-hoc wrapper: it consumes the score or probability output of any existing MIA (e.g., shadow classifier logits, LiRA likelihood ratios, or entropy values) together with a calibration dataset drawn from the same distribution as the target model's training data. For each test image it produces a non-member relative probability (a conformal p-value in [0,1]), and after ranking and adjusting across the full test set, it outputs a binary membership decision per sample plus the FDR-controlled discovery set R(D_ts) of indices whose null hypothesis is rejected at level α. The calibration set is fixed and shared across all test samples, which is precisely what induces the interdependence the adjustment step must correct.

## Method

MIAFdR is a three-stage pipeline. First, K surrogate models are trained on disjoint subsets of an auxiliary dataset; their predictions on held-out samples form a binary membership dataset, from which a classifier f_bc is trained and a non-member conformity score S(y^t;θ_bc) = λ·logit(f_bc) + (1−λ)·f_bc is defined for each test image. Second, the non-member relative probability p(x^t) is computed as the empirical CDF of calibration conformity scores evaluated at the test score (a conformal p-value), yielding a marginal guarantee P(p(x^t)≤α | x^t∉D_tr)≤α under exchangeability (Theorem 1). Third, because all p-values are conditional on the same calibration set and thus interdependent, the paper applies a Benjamini–Hochberg-style weighted correction p_adj^(t) = min{1, min_{m≥t}(n/m)·p^(m)} to the sorted p-values, then rejects H_0 for any sample with p_adj^(t)≤α. Theorem 2 proves that this adjusted procedure controls FDR at level α·π_0 ≤ α, where π_0 is the true non-member proportion. The entire framework is model-agnostic and can be layered on top of classifier-based, metric-based, likelihood-ratio-based, or quantile-regression-based MIAs without modifying their training.

## Results

On CIFAR-100, MIAFdR (wrapped around the classifier-based shadow training MIA) achieves 78.19% attack accuracy and 84.46% AUROC, outperforming the baseline classifier MIA (76.81% accuracy, 84.35% AUROC). On Tiny-ImageNet, MIAFdR reaches 71.18% accuracy and 77.06% AUROC versus the baseline's 69.67% and 76.99%. The FDR is empirically bounded by the significance level α across varying non-member proportions (π₀ = 0.25 and 0.5), and the method adds only 0.01 seconds of inference time for 7,000 test samples. Under knowledge distillation defense, FDR control is maintained while preserving attack accuracy and AUROC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the classifier-based shadow training MIA of Shokri et al. [61], MIAFdR consistently wins on both accuracy and AUROC: +1.38% accuracy and +0.11% AUROC on CIFAR-100, and +1.51% accuracy and +0.07% AUROC on Tiny-ImageNet, while additionally providing FDR guarantees that the baseline lacks. Against the likelihood ratio-based LiRA of Carlini et al. [12], MIAFdR+LiRA produces superior log-scale ROC curves (higher TPR at lower FPR) on CIFAR-10 and achieves an empirical FDR of 0.145 at α = 0.15 (π₀ = 0.5), closely matching the theoretical guarantee. The paper also demonstrates that MIAFdR maintains FDR control under knowledge distillation defense, a setting where the baseline methods do not offer any formal guarantee. Overall, MIAFdR matches or exceeds baseline attack performance while uniquely providing provable FDR and marginal probability guarantees.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a post-hoc wrapper around existing MIA methods (shadow training, LiRA, metric-based approaches), adding a statistical guarantee layer that prior MIAs lack. Methodologically, it draws on two distinct lineages: conformal prediction (Bates et al., 2023) for the non-member relative probability calculation in Eqn. (8), and the Benjamini-Hochberg multiple-testing procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) for the adjustment step in Eqn. (10) that handles interdependence among p-values induced by a shared calibration set. The key departure from prior MIA work is the explicit treatment of p-value interdependence — a challenge that standard FDR-control methods assume away via independence or restricted-dependency conditions — and the demonstration that the BH-style min-adjustment suffices under the weaker exchangeability assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FDR control becomes critical when MIAs are used for simultaneous verification tasks such as machine unlearning (confirming many samples were removed) or lifelong learning (assessing memorization across tasks), where the proportion of false positives among flagged items directly affects trust in the verification. The paper's guarantee is at level α·π₀ rather than α, which is a meaningful but weaker bound that practitioners should note when interpreting results. Open questions include whether the exchangeability assumption holds under distribution shift (e.g., when the auxiliary data differs from the true data-generating process), how the method behaves under adaptive or sequential testing, and whether tighter FDR bounds are achievable by exploiting the specific structure of the interdependence beyond the BH correction.
