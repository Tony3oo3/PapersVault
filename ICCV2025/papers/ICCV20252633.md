## Problem

Existing end-to-end optimization frameworks for computational imaging systems neglect diffraction and off-axis wave optics effects due to the high computational cost of modeling both aberration and diffraction simultaneously. This raises the question of whether ray-optics-only optimization produces suboptimal and less robust lens-algorithm designs, and whether an accurate, efficient, differentiable wave optics simulator can be built to quantify the role of diffraction in joint hardware-software design.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252633/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The simulator takes a scene image (DIV2K) and a parametric lens configuration (surface curvatures, aperture radii, refractive indices) as input. The scene is first resampled onto the sensor plane via the lens's non-paraxial distortion map, then PSFs are rendered at a sparse grid of sensor locations. The final measurement is produced by summing convolutions of weighted sub-scenes with their corresponding PSFs, after simulating R/G/B channels separately and applying a Bayer filter to yield a mosaicked, blurred output. During optimization, gradients flow from the reconstruction loss through the U-Net and the differentiable rendering pipeline back to the lens parameters.

## Method

The core contribution is a differentiable hybrid ray-wave simulator that computes PSFs by first using Newton's-method ray tracing with Snell's law to sample the wavefront map on a reference sphere at the exit pupil, then propagating the complex field to the sensor via a Monte-Carlo evaluation of the Rayleigh-Sommerfeld integral. The key efficiency insight is an isoplanatic approximation: instead of rendering a PSF for every scene point, a sparse grid of PSFs is sampled and off-grid PSFs are approximated by interpolating shifted and scaled versions of their nearest neighbors, reducing the full superposition integral to a sum of convolutions between weighted latent images and sampled PSFs. The reference sphere (rather than a planar pupil) is chosen to minimize phase variation and relax sampling requirements compared to angular-spectrum-method approaches. The entire pipeline—from ray tracing through propagation to convolution—is differentiable, enabling joint optimization of lens geometry and a U-Net reconstruction network via Adam with RMSE + LPIPS loss, and the framework extends naturally to coherent applications such as Fizeau interferometry and freeform surface recovery.

## Results

In PSF rendering (Fig. 6), the proposed simulator achieves SSIM 0.9788 with only 16K rays and 15.29 s runtime for an in-focus Cooke Triplet, outperforming Zemax-Huygens (SSIM 0.677, 262K rays, 24.48 s), Chen et al. (SSIM 0.839, 262K rays, 10.56 s), and ASM-based methods (SSIM 0.660, 262K rays, 10.56 s). In end-to-end system optimization (Table 1), wave-trained lenses consistently outperform ray-trained lenses at a 0.1 mm aperture radius: for the Cooke Triplet, wave training yields RMSE 0.106 / LPIPS 0.265 versus 0.148 / 0.772 for ray training, with a large lens-configuration disparity (MF = 8.689, RRMSE = 0.580). At a 0.3 mm aperture, the gap narrows substantially (e.g., Cooke Triplet: 0.104 vs 0.112 RMSE), confirming that diffraction matters most in diffraction-limited regimes. Hardware validation (Fig. 9) shows SSIM 0.781 (on-axis) and 0.853 (off-axis) between simulated and measured PSFs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Zemax-Huygens [16], the proposed simulator achieves substantially higher PSF fidelity (SSIM 0.9788 vs 0.677 for in-focus Cooke Triplet) while using 16× fewer rays (16K vs 262K) and completing in 15.29 s vs 24.48 s. Compared to Chen et al. [4], which also uses ray tracing but neglects magnitude variation with propagation distance, the proposed method improves SSIM from 0.839 to 0.9788 in the in-focus case and remains robust under defocus where Chen et al. degrades. Against ASM-based methods (Wei et al. [29], Yang et al. [33]), the proposed simulator achieves SSIM 0.9788 vs 0.660 with 16× fewer rays, because the spherical reference geometry avoids the high sampling density that ASM requires when modeling defocus-induced rapid phase variations. In the end-to-end optimization experiments, the key comparison is wave-trained vs ray-trained systems: at 0.1 mm aperture, wave training reduces LPIPS by 53–66% (e.g., 0.265 vs 0.772 for Cooke Triplet) and produces fundamentally different lens architectures (shorter focal length to suppress diffraction), while at 0.3 mm aperture the two approaches converge as the system becomes aberration-limited.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the differentiable ray tracing engine of Wang et al. [27] for computing wavefront maps and the PSF-subset interpolation strategy of Baktash et al. [3] for efficient full-FoV rendering. It departs from the dominant ray-optics-only paradigm in end-to-end lens design [5, 24, 32] and from simplified wave models (thin-phase surfaces, shift-invariance, angular spectrum method) that are limited to single-element or near-axis systems. The key methodological advance is evaluating the Rayleigh-Sommerfeld integral on a reference sphere (rather than a plane) via Monte-Carlo ray sampling, which avoids the 2D grid discretization that makes ASM-based methods [29, 33] fail under defocus and large FoV.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic evidence that the choice of physics model (ray vs. wave) fundamentally alters the optimal lens architecture, not merely the reconstruction quality — wave-trained lenses shorten focal length to suppress diffraction, a trade-off invisible to ray-only optimization. This matters for any practitioner co-designing optics and algorithms in diffraction-limited regimes (small apertures, long wavelengths). Open questions include scalability to large-aperture, strongly aberrated systems where sampling demands grow, and whether alternative reference geometries (e.g., constant-OPL surfaces) can reduce the residual error for systems lacking a well-defined aperture stop.
