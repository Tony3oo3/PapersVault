## Problem

ERM-trained image classifiers exploit spurious correlations between labels and irrelevant features (e.g., background, gender), causing poor worst-group generalization. Existing augmentation-based remedies either lack semantic control over generated content (mixing, interpolation) or rely on prompt-sensitive diffusion models that can produce samples detrimental to the balancing objective. The paper addresses the gap of generating high-quality, semantically controlled minority-group samples without manual prompts or group labels.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes majority-group training images (selected as the lowest-loss K samples per class from a pre-trained ResNet-50 ERM model), class-name text prompts, and a frozen Stable Diffusion v2 inpainting model. It outputs a set of pruned minority-group images that preserve the original spurious background while replacing the causal foreground, which are then appended to the training set for ERM retraining. A key detail is that the ERM model itself is used as a quality gate: its softmax confidence and integrated-gradients attribution scores determine which generated samples survive the pruning stage.

## Method

DDB is a three-stage data-centric procedure. First, it learns a per-class causal-feature token via textual inversion (optimizing a single embedding against the SD denoising loss on a small subset of class images), giving the diffusion model a class-specific conditioning signal without free-form prompts. Second, it uses LangSAM with the class name as a text prompt to segment the causal region of each majority-group image, then applies masked inpainting with Stable Diffusion so that noise is added only to the causal region while the spurious background is preserved; the learned token conditions the denoising to produce the target class's causal features. Third, a bicephalous pruning filter retains only generated samples whose ERM softmax probability for the original class drops below a class-level threshold AND whose integrated-gradients attribution over the masked region exceeds a score threshold, discarding images where the diffusion model failed or LangSAM mis-segmented. Finally, the ERM model is retrained on the union of the original and generated samples with an upweighted cross-entropy loss on the generated subset.

## Results

On the Waterbirds benchmark, DDB achieves 93.0% worst-group accuracy, marginally outperforming DFR (92.9%) and DaC (92.3%), while on MetaShift it reaches 81.2% worst-group accuracy, a clear 2.9-point improvement over the next-best method DaC (78.3%). On CelebA, DDB attains 85.8% worst-group accuracy, trailing LISA (89.3%) and DFR (88.3%) but substantially exceeding the ERM baseline (42.2%). In terms of average accuracy, DDB is competitive but not always the best (e.g., 93.6% on Waterbirds vs. DaC's 95.3%), reflecting a trade-off between worst-group and overall performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDB achieves the best worst-group accuracy on Waterbirds (93.0%) and MetaShift (81.2%), outperforming the closest non-augmentation baseline DFR by 0.1 and 8.4 points respectively, and the closest augmentation baseline DaC by 0.7 and 2.9 points. On CelebA, DDB (85.8%) trails LISA (89.3%) and DFR (88.3%) by 3.5 and 2.5 points, though it still substantially exceeds the ERM baseline (42.2%) and all other augmentation methods (DaC 81.9%, JTT 81.1%, MaskTune 78.0%). Among augmentation-based methods specifically, DDB dominates on both Waterbirds and MetaShift worst-group accuracy, while LISA requires group labels during training (✓/✓) whereas DDB does not (✗/✓). On average accuracy, DDB is competitive but not always the top performer (e.g., 93.6% on Waterbirds vs. DaC's 95.3% and AFR's 94.2%), suggesting a slight trade-off between worst-group and overall accuracy. The MetaShift results are particularly notable as they involve a domain shift where the spurious correlation is entirely absent in the test set, and DDB's 81.2% represents a 14.2-point improvement over the ERM baseline (67.0%).

## Relation to Prior Work

DDB sits at the intersection of two research threads: compositional data augmentation for group robustness (DaC, LISA, DISC) and diffusion-based image generation (FFR, general T2I augmentation). It departs from DaC by replacing manual causal/non-causal disentanglement with LangSAM-driven segmentation and Stable Diffusion inpainting, gaining semantic control over generated content. It also diverges from FFR by being prompt-agnostic (relying on learned textual-inversion tokens rather than hand-crafted prompts) and by introducing an attribution-based pruning stage that FFR lacks. The textual-inversion mechanism is borrowed directly from Gal et al. (2022), repurposed here to encode class-specific causal features rather than personalizing generation for a single subject.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that diffusion inpainting, when combined with language-based segmentation and a principled pruning criterion, can serve as an automated, prompt-free data augmentation pipeline for worst-group accuracy—a setting where prior augmentation methods (DaC, LISA) either lack semantic control or require group labels. The pruning ablation (Table 2) reveals that a large fraction of generated samples (up to 75% on MetaShift) are unusable without filtering, underscoring that naive diffusion-based augmentation is unreliable and that quality control is essential. Open questions include whether the approach extends beyond binary classification, whether it scales to multi-group or multi-attribute spurious correlations, and how sensitive performance is to the quality of the underlying segmentation model (LangSAM). The authors also flag a dual-use risk: the precise automated image modification capability could be repurposed for deepfake generation.
