## Problem

Traditional active learning evaluation relies on final accuracy at fixed annotation budgets, ignoring critical dynamics such as early learning efficiency, data-space coverage, and scalability of gains over time. No general predictive model exists to characterize and compare AL strategies across diverse methods, datasets, and budget regimes from partial observations, making strategy selection under resource constraints costly and inconsistent.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250809/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

PALM consumes a small number of accuracy observations (as few as 4 distinct cumulative budget points, or empirically 200–1000 labeled samples for CIFAR-10) from an ongoing AL run and outputs a full set of interpretable parameters (δ, α, β, A_max, b) that reconstruct the entire learning curve. The cumulative budget B is normalized by the mean per-iteration batch size b to align with the iterative AL structure and mitigate numerical instability. From these parameters, one can predict final accuracy, estimate data-space coverage, and invert the model to determine how many labels are needed to reach a target performance threshold.

## Method

PALM is grounded in the random covering problem: it models the expected fraction of data space covered by B labeled samples as 1−(1−δ)^B, where δ is the per-sample coverage efficiency determined by the AL strategy. Test accuracy is then expressed as a weighted combination of accuracy in covered and uncovered regions, yielding the base relation A = A_C·P_C + A_UC·(1−P_C). The key generalization introduces an exponential adjustment, giving A = A_max·(1−(1−δ)^((B/b+α)^β)), where α shifts the effective learning onset (capturing generalization from uncovered regions) and β controls the scaling rate of accuracy gains. Parameters are estimated via constrained nonlinear least squares from partial curve observations, with computational complexity approximately O(log B) using optimized exponentiation. This formulation enables direct quantitative comparison of any two AL methods at any budget through their fitted parameters, distinguishing coverage efficiency, initial learning speed, and long-term scalability without requiring full dataset annotation.

## Results

PALM approximates AL learning curves with deviations within 2% of test accuracy across all budget levels. On CIFAR-10, full learning dynamics are predicted from as few as 1,000 labeled samples (2% of the dataset), with some strategies requiring only 200–300 samples (0.8–1.5%); on CIFAR-100, reliable approximations are obtained with 5,000–10,000 samples (50–100 per class), and TypiClust is accurately modeled with just 1,000 samples (~10 per class). On ImageNet-50/100/200 under a 4% annotation budget, PALM generalizes across all tested AL strategies and SSL embeddings, with MoCov3 consistently achieving the highest A_max and δ values. Pretrained embeddings dramatically improve coverage efficiency: e.g., Margin sampling on CIFAR-10 jumps from δ=0.094 (no SSL) to δ=0.535 (SimCLR), and the initial learning offset α drops from 10.643 to 0.068 on CIFAR-100.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PALM does not compete against the AL strategies but provides a unified framework to compare them. Among AL strategies, embedding-based methods (TypiClust, Margin+SimCLR, Random+SimCLR) consistently achieve δ values 5–10× higher than their non-embedding counterparts (e.g., δ=0.535 vs. 0.094 for Margin on CIFAR-10), indicating far greater per-sample coverage efficiency. Among SSL backbones on ImageNet, MoCov3 dominates with the highest A_max and δ across all strategies, while BYOL exhibits the slowest, nearly linear accuracy growth with near-zero δ (e.g., with TypiClust), requiring substantially larger budgets to reach comparable performance. SimCLR shows competitive A_max but lower δ than MoCov3, and MoCov2+ generally lags behind both. Non-embedding methods exhibit high α (delayed learning onset, e.g., α=10.643 for Margin on CIFAR-100), whereas embedding-based methods start learning almost immediately (α≈0.07). The highest β (steepest scaling) is observed for Entropy+SimCLR on CIFAR-100 (β=0.651), suggesting rapid convergence once learning begins.

## Relation to Prior Work

PALM builds directly on the covering-lens framework of Yehuda et al. (NeurIPS 2022), which introduced the idea of characterizing active learning through spatial coverage of the data space. The paper modifies that framework by reinterpreting δ as the fraction of space covered per labeled sample (rather than a covering-ball radius) and augments it with two additional parameters (α, β) to capture early-stage learning onset and scalability of gains. Conceptually, it also draws on the classical random covering problem and effective sample size literature to ground the probabilistic coverage model, while integrating the SSL-AL synergy established by Hacohen et al. and Mishal & Weinshall. The paper positions itself as filling a gap identified by Mittal et al. (2025): prior AL evaluation relied on endpoint accuracy or AUC, with no unified predictive model spanning the full annotation trajectory.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PALM offers a compact, interpretable four-parameter description of AL dynamics that enables practitioners to predict full learning curves from as little as 2% of a dataset, potentially saving substantial annotation costs in real-world deployments. The finding that SSL embeddings dramatically inflate coverage efficiency (e.g., δ jumping from 0.094 to 0.535 for Margin sampling on CIFAR-10) provides a quantitative, mechanistic explanation for why SSL-AL pipelines outperform supervised-only baselines under tight budgets. Limitations include the assumption of an effectively infinite unlabeled pool (causing minor fit drift near exhaustion), difficulty modeling sharp early knees on simple datasets, and validation restricted to image classification with ResNet backbones. Open questions include whether the parametric form generalizes to non-vision domains, sequential/online AL settings, or adaptive budget allocation where b changes over time.
