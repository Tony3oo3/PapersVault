## Problem

High-resolution 3DGS rendering is prohibitively slow on lightweight GPUs, and existing upscaling solutions are ill-suited: DL-based upscalers (DLSS, NinaSR) require specialized hardware, additional scene information (depth maps, motion vectors) unavailable for 3DGS, or introduce temporal inconsistencies and hallucinated artifacts. The paper addresses the gap of a lightweight, high-fidelity upscaling method that exploits the unique structure of 3DGS renderings without relying on neural network inference or external scene data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250480/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a low-resolution 3DGS rendering (at 1/2, 1/4, or 1/8 of target resolution) together with analytical image-space gradients (∂I/∂x, ∂I/∂y, ∂²I/∂x∂y) computed on-the-fly during the forward rasterization pass as part of the alpha-blending loop. It produces a high-resolution image at the target resolution (e.g., 4096×2304) via spline-based interpolation. The gradients are derived per-pixel from the 2D Gaussian footprints, so no separate gradient estimation step or additional buffers are needed beyond the standard 3DGS rendering output.

## Method

The key insight is replacing finite-difference gradient approximations in bicubic spline interpolation with the exact analytical gradients of the 2D Gaussian projections that generated the image. During the front-to-back alpha-blending pass, the paper derives closed-form expressions for ∂I/∂x, ∂I/∂y, and ∂²I/∂x∂y by differentiating the transmittance and per-Gaussian opacity terms, computing them iteratively with only minor overhead. These analytical gradients serve as additional constraints when solving the 4×4 linear system F = CAC^T for the bicubic spline coefficients, yielding a more faithful reconstruction of the underlying continuous signal. The entire pipeline—including gradient computation and spline evaluation—is fully differentiable, enabling integration into the 3DGS optimization loop where gradients are backpropagated through the upscaling to the Gaussian parameters via a back-to-front blending formulation with the Inversion-Trick.

## Results

At 4× upscaling on Mip-NeRF360, Spline (Ours) achieves PSNR 26.85 / SSIM 0.809 / LPIPS 0.322 at 90 FPS, compared to full-resolution 3DGS at PSNR 26.35 / SSIM 0.783 / LPIPS 0.347 at only 15 FPS — a 6× rendering speedup with improved quality. On Tanks and Temples (4×), the method reaches PSNR 21.30 / SSIM 0.744 at 159 FPS versus 3DGS's 20.31 / 0.677 at 142 FPS. In the 4K rendering benchmark (Table 2), spline-based upscaling adds only 1.8 ms overhead (15.6 ms render + 1.8 ms upscale = 4.2× speedup over 3DGS's 72.4 ms), matching bicubic's speed while delivering superior image quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against full-resolution 3DGS, spline-based upscaling wins on both speed and quality at 4× scale: 90 FPS vs. 15 FPS on Mip-NeRF360 (6× faster) with higher PSNR (26.85 vs. 26.35) and SSIM (0.809 vs. 0.783). Compared to Mip-Splatting, the method is dramatically faster (90 FPS vs. 10 FPS on Mip-NeRF360, 159 vs. 116 on T&T) while achieving comparable or slightly lower PSNR (26.85 vs. 27.14), making it the clear choice for real-time applications. Versus bicubic interpolation at the same 4× scale, spline upscaling matches the rendering speed (90 FPS) but improves PSNR by 0.25 dB and SSIM by 0.010 on Mip-NeRF360, and by 1.02 dB PSNR on Tanks and Temples. Against NVIDIA DLSS, spline upscaling is 4.4× faster in the upscale step (1.8 ms vs. 8.0 ms at 4K) and produces sharper, less overblurred results. NinaSR-B1 produces visually sharper details but is 153× slower in the upscale step (275.3 ms vs. 1.8 ms), making it impractical for real-time use.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) rendering pipeline of Kerbl et al. and the aliasing-reduction ideas of Mip-Splatting, but departs from both by treating upscaling as a post-rasterization step driven by the analytical image-space gradients of the projected Gaussians rather than by modifying the 3D representation itself. It contrasts with classical upscalers (bicubic, Lanczos) that rely on finite-difference gradient estimates or fixed kernels, and with DL-based super-resolution (DLSS, NinaSR, ESRGAN) that require trained networks and auxiliary signals like depth maps. The authors position their work as the first to embed a classical spline upscaler directly into the differentiable 3DGS training loop, chaining analytical gradient backpropagation through the interpolation step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 3–4× rendering speedup on lightweight GPUs is practically significant for VR and mobile 3DGS deployment, where full-resolution rasterization is prohibitive. The finding that a lightweight spline method outperforms DLSS in PSNR (attributed to DLSS's reliance on unavailable depth/motion vectors) and provides temporal stability without hallucinated artifacts addresses a real pain point in interactive 3DGS. However, the method is inherently tied to Gaussian-based rendering (analytical gradients are not available for NeRFs or ray tracing), and the smooth spline basis limits reconstruction of sharp high-frequency details, leaving open whether higher-order or adaptive basis functions can close that gap.
