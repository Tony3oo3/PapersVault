## Problem

AI-generated optical illusions can embed hate messages (slurs, symbols) into seemingly harmless scenes, creating a new subtle form of hateful imagery that evades traditional content moderation. No prior work has investigated whether such hateful illusions can be generated at scale or whether existing moderation classifiers and vision-language models can detect them, leaving a critical gap in understanding the real-world risk of this emerging threat.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252359/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For generation, the pipeline takes a descriptive text prompt (defining the surface scene) and a 512×512 message image (explicitly depicting a hate message) as dual inputs to Stable Diffusion + ControlNet, producing a single AI-generated image where the hate message is embedded as a secondary visual layer. For moderation evaluation, the 1,571 hateful illusion images (split into high-visibility and low-visibility classes) are fed to classifiers and VLMs, which output a binary safety judgment. The generation uses guidance scales of 0.9 for hate speech and 1.1 for hate symbols to balance embedding success rate against subtlety.

## Method

The paper constructs a generation pipeline that exploits ControlNet's spatial conditioning to fuse a message image into a Stable Diffusion scene, producing 1,860 images from 62 hate messages × 30 prompts, of which 1,571 (84.5%) are confirmed hateful illusions via two-round human annotation (Fleiss' Kappa = 0.783). The core analytical contribution is a diagnosis of why all 15 tested moderation models fail: through t-SNE embedding analysis and multimodal attention relevancy maps, the authors show that CLIP-ViT-based vision encoders attend to surface-scene details (e.g., 'truck,' 'red bricks') while systematically ignoring the emergent pattern formed by the embedded message, a limitation rooted in CLIP's training data distribution, contrastive learning objective, and static attention mechanism. For mitigation, the paper demonstrates that a simple Gaussian blur followed by histogram equalization raises GPT-4o's detection accuracy from 0.03 to 0.70, and that fully fine-tuned prompt learning on CLIP achieves 0.938 test accuracy as a specialized detector.

## Results

On the Hateful Illusion Dataset (1,571 images), the best moderation classifier (Q16) achieves only 0.209 accuracy for hate speech and 0.245 for hate symbols in the aggregated setting, a sharp drop from 0.696 and 0.846 when presented with direct hate message images. Among nine VLMs, the best zero-shot accuracy is 0.034 (Gemini-2, hate speech) and 0.055 (LLaVA-1.5, hate symbols); chain-of-thought prompting marginally improves this to 0.102 (LLaVA-Next, hate speech) and 0.090 (LLaVA-Next, hate symbols). The built-in Stable Diffusion safety checker blocks only 3.0% of all generated hateful illusions, and 84.5% of the 1,860 generated images successfully embed hate messages.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new detection method but rather exposes that all 15 evaluated baselines fail to effectively identify hateful illusions. Among six moderation classifiers, Q16 is the strongest, reaching 0.209/0.245 aggregated accuracy for hate speech/symbols, yet this represents a 70%+ drop from its 0.696/0.846 performance on direct message images. Commercial APIs (OpenAI Omni, Google SafeSearch, Microsoft Moderation and M-Moderation) are nearly useless, with aggregated accuracies at or below 0.010. Among nine VLMs, GPT-4o and Gemini-2 achieve near-perfect scores (≥0.870) on direct hate messages but collapse to ≤0.065 on hateful illusions; LLaVA-Next is the best VLM on illusions (0.102/0.090 with chain-of-thought), still far below any practical threshold. The consistent failure across all model families—commercial and open-source, classifiers and VLMs—indicates a shared architectural limitation in ViT-based vision encoders rather than a gap between specific models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two prior research threads: AI-generated optical illusion recognition (IllusionVQA, IllusionBench, HallusionBench) and hateful image moderation (Q16, commercial safety APIs, VLM-based moderation). It departs from both: prior optical illusion work focuses on human-designed illusions (impossible objects, angle illusions) rather than AI-generated ones, and prior hateful image moderation targets overt, directly visible hate content rather than messages embedded as a secondary visual layer. The generation pipeline builds directly on ControlNet's spatial conditioning mechanism to fuse a message image into a text-prompted scene, repurposing a standard controllable-generation technique for adversarial content creation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that all six tested moderation classifiers and nine VLMs — including safety-aligned commercial models like GPT-4o and Gemini — are effectively blind to hate messages hidden in AI-generated optical illusions, with accuracy dropping from near-perfect on direct hate images to below 0.10. The root-cause analysis attributing this failure to ViT's static attention and surface-level training paradigm has implications beyond this specific attack: any content that encodes meaning in a secondary spatial layer (watermarks, steganography, subtle visual cues) may be similarly invisible to current vision encoders. The Hateful Illusion Dataset (1,571 images) provides a concrete benchmark, but the mitigation strategies remain preliminary (Gaussian blur + histogram equalization is a preprocessing hack, and the trained classifier requires ~3K labeled examples). Open questions include whether fundamentally different vision architectures (dynamic attention, multi-scale perception) could close this gap, and whether generation-side safeguards (improving Stable Diffusion's safety checker, which blocks only 3% of hateful illusions) can prevent the threat at the source.
