## Problem

Current open-vocabulary multi-object tracking (OV-MOT) methods rely almost exclusively on instance-level detection and association, neglecting trajectory-level information that is uniquely available in video. This oversight causes identity switches when detections are missed or inaccurate and leads to misclassifications under occlusion and blur, particularly for novel categories where per-frame visual cues are most unreliable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250162/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-frame detection results (bounding boxes, category predictions, and visual features) from a swappable open-vocabulary detector (ViLD, RegionCLIP, or YOLO-World) over an N-frame video sequence, along with a text category vocabulary split into base and novel classes following the LVIS protocol. It outputs object trajectories with consistent identity assignments, per-frame bounding boxes, and a final category label per trajectory. The TraCLIP classification module is explicitly plug-and-play, requiring only the assembled trajectory features and the vocabulary as input, making it composable with any upstream tracker.

## Method

TRACT introduces two trajectory-level mechanisms. First, Trajectory Consistency Reinforcement (TCR) maintains a feature bank (last n_bank visual embeddings) and a category bank (last n_clip category predictions) per active trajectory; matching combines the standard trajectory memory with the feature bank via a weighted cosine/bi-directional-softmax similarity, while a confidence-gated voting rule over the category bank stabilizes per-detection labels during association. Second, TraCLIP performs trajectory-level classification by (a) Trajectory Feature Aggregation, which samples n_clip frames by detection confidence, encodes them with a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 visual encoder, and fuses them via self-attention plus MLP followed by global average pooling, and (b) Trajectory Semantic Enrichment, which uses ChatGPT to generate 2–3 visual-attribute descriptions per category and concatenates them with the vanilla name to produce richer CLIP language embeddings. The final label is selected by the highest cosine similarity among three candidates (vanilla-language match, attribute-language match, and majority vote of retained per-frame predictions). Training uses contrastive learning for the association module (following MASA) and CLIP's contrastive loss for TraCLIP, with both encoders frozen and only linear projection layers trained on LVIS, YouTube-VIS, and TAO using base categories only.

## Results

On the OV-TAO validation set with the YOLO-World detector, TRACT achieves TETA of 39.4% (base) and 33.7% (novel), with ClsA of 22.6% (base) and 5.3% (novel), outperforming the previous best tracker MASA by +1.2% TETA and +4.0% ClsA on base classes. On the test set with RegionCLIP, TRACT gains +2.0% and +4.6% ClsA over MASA for base and novel classes respectively. The method consistently improves classification accuracy across all three detector groups (ViLD, RegionCLIP, YOLO-World) while maintaining competitive association accuracy, with the largest gains observed on novel (rare) categories.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TRACT is compared against six baselines grouped by detector (ViLD, RegionCLIP, YOLO-World). Against the strongest prior OV-MOT tracker MASA [13], TRACT wins on ClsA across all detector groups (e.g., +4.0% base / +0.9% novel with YOLO-World on validation; +2.0% / +4.6% with RegionCLIP on test), while matching or slightly exceeding MASA on AssA and TETA. Against OVTrack [12], TRACT improves TETA by +3.9% (base) and +1.5% (novel) with YOLO-World, with a much larger ClsA gain of +4.0% (base). Classic MOT baselines (DeepSORT, Tracktor++, ByteTrack, OC-SORT) are substantially outperformed on all metrics, with TETA gaps exceeding 10 points, confirming that trajectory-aware open-vocabulary tracking requires purpose-built association and classification. The most consistent and largest improvement is on ClsA for novel classes, validating the paper's core claim that trajectory information is especially valuable for rare categories where single-frame visual cues are insufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

TRACT builds directly on the OVTrack (CVPR 2023) and MASA (CVPR 2024) pipeline for open-vocabulary multi-object tracking, adopting their appearance-based matching framework and contrastive training strategy. Its key departure is the explicit incorporation of trajectory-level information—via feature banks and category banks in the association step (TCR) and a trajectory-level CLIP classifier (TraCLIP) in the classification step—whereas prior OV-MOT methods treat each detection independently. The TraCLIP module additionally repurposes CLIP's image-text alignment for trajectory classification and introduces LLM-generated attribute descriptions (TSE) to enrich category semantics, a direction not explored in prior OV-MOT work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a reasonable case that trajectory information, long a staple of classic MOT, has been underexploited in the open-vocabulary setting, and demonstrates measurable (if modest) gains in association and especially classification accuracy. However, the authors themselves identify that the primary bottleneck in OV-MOT is the dense, low-quality detection results stemming from incomplete TAO annotations and the TETA evaluation protocol, which limits how much any tracker-side improvement can help. Open questions include whether trajectory cues can meaningfully improve localization for rare classes (preliminary results are negative), whether the LLM-enriched semantic approach generalizes beyond the tested vocabulary, and whether the added computational overhead (~1.2 s/seq for TraCLIP) is acceptable for real-time deployment.
