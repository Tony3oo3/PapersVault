## Problem

Existing backdoor attacks in federated learning face a fundamental trade-off: attacks that maximize strength (e.g., BadNets) are easily detected by robust defenses, while stealthy approaches (e.g., LP attack) sacrifice attack effectiveness by discarding backdoor-related information or applying coarse-grained constraints. No prior method achieves both high backdoor accuracy and strong stealthiness simultaneously without requiring knowledge of the specific defense mechanism in use.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251256/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker receives the current global model θ_g^{t-1}, the previous global model θ_g^{t-2} (to compute the reference update Δθ_g^{t-1}), and a poisoned local dataset with a 5×5 pixel square trigger at the bottom-right corner. After E local epochs of SGD on the poisoned data, the method outputs a malicious model update where each layer's gradient has been individually scaled so its ℓ2 norm does not exceed that of the corresponding layer in the previous global update. The scaling is applied after every local epoch, not just at the end, making the alignment dynamic throughout local training.

## Method

The core mechanism is an adaptive layer-wise gradient alignment strategy that constrains each layer of the malicious update to lie within an ℓ2 norm ball defined by the corresponding layer of the previous global update. The scaling factor for layer l is S^{t,l} = min(1, ||Δθ_g^{t-1,l}|| / ||Δθ_m^{t,l}||), which only shrinks (never amplifies) the malicious gradient. The key insight, supported by empirical analysis, is that malicious and benign update magnitudes do not maintain a fixed proportional relationship across layers—some layers have inherently small norms while others are large—so a single model-wide constraint either over-constrains small-norm layers (killing attack strength) or under-constrains large-norm layers (exposing the attack). By using the previous global update as a free, dynamically evolving reference that approximates benign behavior, the method adapts to training dynamics without any knowledge of the server's defense. The entire alignment is implemented in four lines of code (Algorithm 1, Lines 10–13) inserted into the standard FL training loop.

## Results

Under no defense, the proposed layer-wise gradient alignment (LGA) attack achieves average backdoor accuracy (BA) of 96.16% (VGG19/CIFAR-10), 97.22% (ResNet18/CIFAR-10), and 96.70% (ResNet18/CIFAR-100), approaching the unconstrained BadNets baseline. Under the strongest defenses, LGA maintains BA above 94% in most settings—for example, 99.88% under FLAME on CIFAR-100 and 94.42% under MultiKrum on CIFAR-100—while BadNets and DBA drop below 1%. The paper reports a maximum improvement of 54.76% in BA over the best existing attack (LP) across all evaluated defenses and datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BadNets (unconstrained scaling), LGA matches or exceeds its BA under no defense (96–98% vs. 98–100%) while dramatically outperforming it under every outlier-based defense: under FLAME on CIFAR-100, LGA achieves 99.88% BA versus BadNets' 0.62%, and under MultiKrum on CIFAR-100, 94.42% vs. 0.62%. Against DBA (distributed trigger), LGA is superior in both stealthiness and strength—DBA's BA is already very low without defense (0.80–46%) and collapses to near zero under most defenses, whereas LGA stays above 94%. Against the LP attack (backdoor-critical layer injection), LGA achieves comparable or higher MAR (stealthiness) under FLAME and DnC while delivering substantially higher BA: on CIFAR-100 under FLAME, LGA reaches 99.88% vs. LP's 72.89%, and under no defense on CIFAR-100, 96.70% vs. 61.87%. LGA also composes additively with trigger-optimization attacks (IBA, Cerberus, A3FL), boosting their BA by 68–97 percentage points under FLAME and MM (e.g., IBA+LGA: 98.04% vs. IBA alone: 2.71% under FLAME), confirming its generalizability as a plug-in alignment module.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of model-optimization backdoor attacks and Byzantine-robust aggregation defenses in FL, addressing the well-known trade-off between attack strength and stealthiness that plagues prior work. It departs from strong-but-detectable attacks (BadNets, scaling attacks) and stealthy-but-weak attacks (LP, DBA) by introducing a layer-wise scaling factor derived from the previous global update, inspired by the observation [15] that gradient magnitudes vary systematically across layers. Unlike PGD-style attacks that require knowledge of the server's defense mechanism, the proposed alignment is defense-agnostic, and unlike LP which discards backdoor information in non-critical layers, it preserves information in every layer while constraining each to a layer-specific norm ball. The method is also composable with trigger-optimization attacks (IBA, Cerberus, A3FL), extending their effectiveness without modifying their trigger design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical concern is significant: a four-line modification to the standard FL training loop enables an attacker to bypass eight state-of-the-art defenses while maintaining near-unconstrained backdoor accuracy, suggesting that current similarity-based defenses may be insufficient against even simple adaptive strategies. The layer-wise insight—that a single global norm bound over-constrains small-magnitude layers and under-constrains large ones—provides a clear diagnostic for why prior model-wise alignment (e.g., LP) sacrifices strength. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to asynchronous FL, differentially-private aggregation, or defenses that explicitly model per-layer update distributions, and whether the reliance on the previous global update as a reference remains effective under highly heterogeneous or adversarial client participation patterns.
