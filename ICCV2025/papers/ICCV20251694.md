## Problem

Existing multi-view learning methods are typically designed for specific view combinations and modalities, limiting their universality across heterogeneous datasets that differ in dimension, sparsity, and scale. Moreover, real-world multi-view data frequently contains noise, unusable entries, and noisy labels, yet prior robustness efforts operate at the coarse view-level rather than the finer sample-level, and no single framework has been shown to improve robustness across multiple downstream tasks simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method accepts V heterogeneous views per sample, each with its own dimension D_m and data format (e.g., 110-dim motion features alongside 6144-dim image features), and converts them into homogeneous word embeddings of a shared dimension d_e via per-view MLPs. The output is a fused representation z_i ∈ R^d per sample, which can then be fed to K-Means for clustering, a classifier for noise-label classification, or serve as a regularization signal on the hidden representations of an existing cross-modal hashing model. As a plug-and-play module, RML consumes the hidden representations [H^1,...,H^V] of any downstream MVL method rather than raw inputs.

## Method

RML introduces a multi-view transformer fusion network that treats the V views of each sample as V 'words' in a sentence: per-view MLPs project heterogeneous inputs into homogeneous embeddings, a sample-level self-attention layer (producing a V×V attention matrix per sample) captures inter-view dependencies, and a feed-forward network with residual connections encodes each view before summing all encoded embeddings into the final fused representation. For training, RML proposes a simulated perturbation based contrastive learning objective: it dynamically generates two perturbed copies of the multi-view data—one with Gaussian noise added to a random fraction p of view entries, the other with a random fraction r of view entries zeroed out (simulating unusable data)—passes both through the shared fusion network, and minimizes a bidirectional InfoNCE loss to align the two resulting fused representations while pushing apart representations of different samples. This self-supervised objective simultaneously enforces robustness to noise and missing views and encourages discriminative information extraction, and the entire RML module can be attached as a λ-weighted regularization term to any existing multi-view method without modifying its task-specific loss.

## Results

On the primary unsupervised multi-view clustering task across 9 heterogeneous datasets, RML+K-means achieves the best or near-best ACC and NMI in most cases: e.g., 0.822 ACC / 0.847 NMI on DHA, 0.331 ACC / 0.339 NMI on YoutubeVideo, 0.868 ACC / 0.508 NMI on WebKB, and 0.983 ACC / 0.943 NMI on NGs. On noise-label classification (50% symmetric noise), RML+L_MCE reaches 0.936 ACC on BDGP versus 0.442 for the transformer-only baseline. As a plug-and-play regularization for cross-modal hashing, RML+UCCH improves MAP by ~3% over UCCH on NUS-WIDE (16-bit, Image→Text: 0.733 vs 0.698), and RML+NRCH improves MAP by ~3–4% over NRCH on MIRFLICKR-25K (16-bit, Image→Text: 0.778 vs 0.760).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RML+K-means outperforms the strongest baselines (SCM, SCM_RE, MFLVC, DSIMVC) on 6 of 9 clustering datasets (DHA, Prokaryotic, YoutubeVideo, WebKB, NGs, Cifar100) and is within 0.002–0.010 on the remaining three (BDGP, Cora, VOC). Against the simple K-Means baseline, RML+K-means improves ACC by 0.166 on DHA, 0.132 on WebKB, and 0.777 on NGs, demonstrating that the learned fused representation is substantially more discriminative than raw multi-view features. In noise-label classification at 50% noise rate, RML+L_MCE achieves 0.936 ACC on BDGP versus 0.442 for the transformer-only baseline (Trans.+L_MCE), a 49.4-point gap attributable to the simulated perturbation contrastive loss. For cross-modal hashing, RML+UCCH beats UCCH by 3.5% MAP on NUS-WIDE (16-bit Image→Text: 0.733 vs 0.698) and RML+NRCH beats NRCH by 1.8% MAP on MIRFLICKR-25K (16-bit Image→Text: 0.778 vs 0.760), confirming the plug-and-play regularization benefit. Notably, no single baseline dominates all datasets—DSMVC excels on VOC (0.633 ACC) but fails on BDGP (0.523 ACC), while MFLVC is strong on BDGP (0.983 ACC) but weak on VOC (0.292 ACC)—highlighting the heterogeneity challenge that RML's view-agnostic design addresses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate MVL paradigms—representation fusion and representation alignment—into a single framework. It departs from the dominant view-level weighting strategy (which assigns one scalar weight per view to all samples) by repurposing the transformer self-attention mechanism from NLP, treating each view of a sample as a 'word' and learning per-sample attention scores across views. For alignment, rather than directly contrasting view-to-view representations (as in MFLVC, CVCL, or InfoNCE-based multi-view SSL), it introduces simulated noise and unusable perturbations and aligns the fused representations of the two perturbed versions, borrowing the data-augmentation philosophy from single-view contrastive learning. The plug-and-play regularization formulation (Eq. 15) positions RML as an orthogonal add-on to any existing MVL pipeline, distinct from prior methods that are tightly coupled to specific architectures or view combinations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation—real-world multi-view data is heterogeneous in dimension, sparsity, and modality, and often contains noise or missing views—is well-articulated and addresses a genuine gap where task-specific MVL methods fail to transfer across datasets. The view-agnostic design and plug-and-play regularization make RML broadly applicable, which is valuable for practitioners dealing with diverse sensor or omics data. However, the framework's reliance on a transformer with V 'tokens' per sample raises scalability questions for very large numbers of views, and the simulated perturbations (Gaussian noise, random zeroing) may not fully capture the structured corruption patterns seen in real incomplete multi-view settings. The paper does not explore how RML handles truly missing views at inference time or how the number of attention layers scales with V.
