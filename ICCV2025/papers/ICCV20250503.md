## Problem

Existing trigger-inversion (RET) methods for Trojan detection optimize directly in pixel space, which causes them to recover adversarial perturbations rather than genuine triggers, inflating false positives when scanning clean models. Additionally, all prior RET approaches require access to clean training data for pixel-space optimization, limiting real-world applicability where datasets are inaccessible. DISTIL addresses both gaps by performing a data-free, zero-shot inversion that avoids strong assumptions on trigger appearance.

## Input / Output

The method requires only the classifier under test and a pair of source/target class labels; no clean training samples are needed (though optional hybrid conditioning with source-class images can improve fidelity). The process starts from pure Gaussian noise and iteratively denoises over 50 steps, with the classifier's gradients steering each step. The output is a reconstructed trigger image (validated by a softmax confidence threshold of 0.95 on the target class), a scalar Trojan detection score computed as the mean prediction shift on held-out source-class images, and the predicted target class. For object detection, the trigger is applied to the full image and the score additionally incorporates bounding-box displacement.

## Method

DISTIL shifts trigger inversion from pixel space into the latent space of a pretrained guided diffusion model (GLIDE), whose image manifold constrains the search to realistic patterns and suppresses adversarial artifacts. At each reverse-diffusion step, the mean is modified by adding the classifier's gradient (log-ratio of target-class to source-class probability) scaled by the step covariance, plus a uniform-noise regularization term (λ₁·η_t) that disrupts brittle adversarial solutions and encourages robust, transferable shortcuts. The final denoised image serves as the estimated trigger; its transferability is quantified by embedding it into held-out source-class images and measuring the prediction shift toward the target class. A Fast DISTIL variant reduces the O(K²) class-pair search to O(K) by selecting only the most feature-dissimilar source class per target via cosine distance in the penultimate layer. For object detection, an additional gradient term is appended to the guidance, encouraging predicted bounding-box centers to migrate toward a randomly chosen corner, enabling simultaneous misclassification and mislocalization detection.

## Results

DISTIL achieves an average scanning accuracy of 88.5% on BackdoorBench (CIFAR-10, GTSRB, Tiny ImageNet across 11 attack types), 81.4% on the TrojAI benchmark, and 63.7% on Trojaned object detection scanning. On BackdoorBench, DISTIL outperforms the strongest baseline (SmoothInv) by up to 7.1% (e.g., 94.9% vs. 86.3% on CIFAR-10 BadNets). For mitigation on CIFAR-10, DISTIL reduces the Attack Success Rate to an average of 8.6% while maintaining high clean accuracy, and achieves 72.0% target-class prediction accuracy on GTSRB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISTIL outperforms all 11 baselines on the majority of BackdoorBench attack-dataset combinations, with the largest margins over pixel-space methods (NC, ABS, Pixel, THTP) which typically score 45–76% versus DISTIL's 84–95%. Among the stronger baselines, SmoothInv (the best pixel-space method using randomized smoothing) is the closest competitor, reaching 86.3% on CIFAR-10 BadNets versus DISTIL's 94.9%, but DISTIL still leads by 7.1% on that setting and by 5–10% on most other attacks. BTI-DBF and K-Arm are competitive on a few specific attacks (e.g., BTI-DBF reaches 90.5% on CIFAR-10 LC, K-Arm reaches 82.7% on TrojanNN), yet DISTIL surpasses them on the remaining attacks. On the TrojAI benchmark, DISTIL's advantage narrows (e.g., 83.1% vs. K-Arm's 91.3% on Round 0), reflecting that those rounds use simpler patch-shaped triggers where pixel-space methods are more effective; however, DISTIL still leads on later, more challenging rounds (e.g., 84.6% vs. TRODO's 82.8% on Round 4). On Trojaned object detection, DISTIL achieves 63.7% scanning accuracy, a 9.4% improvement over the best baseline (THTP at 54.3%), demonstrating clear generalization beyond classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

DISTIL departs from the dominant line of reverse-engineering-trigger (RET) methods—NC, Pixel, THTP, SmoothInv, Unicorn, BTI-DBF, K-Arm, UMD—that all perform gradient-based optimization directly in pixel space and typically require access to clean training data. Its core conceptual shift is to relocate the search into the latent space of a pretrained classifier-guided diffusion model (GLIDE), leveraging the generator's learned image manifold to constrain candidates to realistic, interpretable patterns rather than adversarial noise. The additional uniform-noise injection at each denoising step serves as a regularizer specifically designed to counter the well-documented failure mode of pixel-space RET collapsing onto adversarial perturbations. The method also extends the RET paradigm to object-detection networks by augmenting the classifier gradient with a bounding-box displacement term, a task prior RET methods have found challenging due to spatial variability and multi-output structures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the need for clean training data, DISTIL addresses a critical practical barrier: in real-world deployment, the original training set is often inaccessible, rendering most existing RET defenses inapplicable. The consistent 7–9% scanning-accuracy gains across BackdoorBench, TrojAI, and object-detection benchmarks, combined with the O(K) fast-class-pairing heuristic, make the approach both more accurate and more scalable than prior methods. Open questions include how DISTIL performs under all-to-all label mappings (only briefly addressed in the appendix), whether the reliance on a large pretrained diffusion backbone (64M-image GLIDE) limits deployment in resource-constrained settings, and whether the uniform-noise regularizer generalizes to non-image modalities or to triggers embedded in the frequency or feature domain rather than the spatial domain.
