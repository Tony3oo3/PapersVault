## Problem

Existing bias mitigation methods in computer vision are limited to predefined, single-attribute biases (e.g., gender in CelebA, background in Waterbirds), making them inapplicable to real-world datasets where multiple, possibly unknown biases coexist at the instance level. No prior approach can simultaneously discover arbitrary visual shortcuts and mitigate them during training without requiring bias labels or subgroup definitions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250579/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the model takes a standard RGB image and outputs a class label via the backbone and classification head alone. During training, each image additionally produces a set of descriptive tags (via RAM, a 4,585-vocabulary tagging model), which are filtered by GPT-4o into irrelevant tags, then encoded into a single vision-language embedding (via OpenCLIP ViT-L/14) that feeds a learned projection layer. The projection layer's output is added to the main model's logits, so the training-time input is effectively the image plus its bias embedding, while the deployed model requires only the image.

## Method

MAVias discovers instance-level visual biases by extracting tags with RAM, querying GPT-4o to identify tags irrelevant to the target class, and encoding those irrelevant tags into a single OpenCLIP embedding. A projection layer g_φ maps this embedding into the backbone's feature space, and its classification logits (z_tag) are added to the main model's logits (z_main) to form the final prediction. A logit-alignment loss penalizes the L2-norm difference between z_main and λ·z_tag, ensuring the bias branch neither dominates nor is overshadowed. The key mechanism is that bias-aligned samples receive large z_tag values, which reduce the effective magnitude of z_main and thereby suppress their gradient contribution (counteracting gradient starvation), forcing the backbone to rely on task-relevant features. At inference, only the backbone and classification head are retained, yielding a standard classifier with no bias-related components.

## Results

On the open-set evaluation (Table 2), MAVias achieves 66.7% worst-group accuracy on CelebA (+35.2% over the best baseline JTT at 31.5%), 75.4% on Waterbirds (+10.7% over JTT at 64.7%), and 84.4% on UrbanCars (+15.4% over JTT at 69.0%), while also improving average accuracy by 14%, 2.3%, and 11.5% respectively. On ImageNet9 with unknown biases, MAVias improves accuracy on background-modified test sets (MIXED-NEXT +0.70%, MIXED-RAND +0.72%) and substantially reduces reliance on background shortcuts (ONLY-BG-B −7.88%, NO-FG −6.82%, ONLY-BG-T −4.14%). In the closed-set protocol, MAVias reaches 93.7% WG Acc. on Waterbirds (+5% over the best BU method) and reduces the BG+CoObj gap on UrbanCars from 40.1 to 6.7.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MAVias outperforms all five label-unaware (BU) baselines across every dataset and metric in the open-set evaluation. JTT [27] is the strongest competitor, achieving 64.7% WG Acc. on Waterbirds and 69.0% on UrbanCars, but MAVias surpasses it by 10.7 and 15.4 percentage points respectively. LfF [32], SD [35], Debian [24], and FLAC-B [41] all struggle severely with multi-attribute or unknown biases, with WG Acc. values often below 40% (e.g., LfF at 14.7% and SD at 13.3% on CelebA), confirming that existing BU methods cannot scale beyond single-attribute subgrouping. In the closed-set protocol, MAVias also beats the best BU method (Wu et al. [49]) on Waterbirds by +5% WG Acc. (93.7 vs. 88.7) and reduces the BG+CoObj gap on UrbanCars from 40.1 (JTT) to 6.7. On ImageNet9, MAVias is the only method that simultaneously improves on both MIXED-NEXT and MIXED-RAND while reducing background reliance, though it incurs a small 1.24% drop on the ORIGINAL set as expected from reduced shortcut exploitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

MAVias departs from both Bias label-Aware (BA) and label-Unaware (BU) bias mitigation methods, which require either predefined bias annotations or single-attribute bias proxy models. It repurposes three foundation-model components—image tagging (RAM), LLM-based relevance filtering (GPT-4o), and vision-language encoding (OpenCLIP)—into a pipeline that produces instance-level, open-set bias representations without any bias labels. The training mechanism builds on the gradient starvation analysis of Pezeshki et al. [35], but inverts the typical penalty approach: rather than suppressing high logits, MAVias adds a bias-aligned logit branch that absorbs gradient mass from biased samples, a design not seen in prior BA or BU methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By handling multiple, potentially unknown biases at the instance level, MAVias addresses a practical gap: real-world datasets (e.g., ImageNet) contain diverse, class-specific shortcuts that no fixed bias taxonomy can capture. The large margins over BU baselines (+35% WG Acc on CelebA, +15% on UrbanCars) suggest that prior methods fundamentally underperform when biases are multi-attribute, a finding that reframes how the field should evaluate bias mitigation. Key limitations include the hard dependency on a cloud LLM (GPT-4o) for tag filtering, which precludes deployment on sensitive data, and the per-image inference cost of the tagging-plus-LLM pipeline. Open questions include whether the approach scales to video or very large open-vocabulary classification, and whether the LLM filtering step can be replaced by a lighter, locally deployable model without significant quality loss.
