## Problem

Existing video QA benchmarks provide only outcome supervision (final answer correctness), making it impossible to determine whether a model genuinely combined perceptual and temporal information to reason about a video or simply guessed, exploited linguistic bias, or eliminated options. This limits both fine-grained model diagnosis and the ability to design benchmarks that remain informative as frontier models improve rapidly.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250257/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models receive uniformly sampled video frames (up to 1024 for Gemini 2.5 Pro) interleaved with timestamps, an ASR transcript at 5-second intervals, and a 5-option multiple-choice question. Ground truth reasoning traces average 92 words, contain ~4 timestamps (99.6% coverage), and describe specific actions, objects, and logical steps in free-form text. Each question additionally decomposes into ~2.7 sub-questions with ground-truth answers, required skills, and dependency pointers, forming a reasoning tree.

## Method

MINERVA is built through a four-stage pipeline: domain-specific video selection from YouTube (short films, sports/board games, educational, lifestyle), manual annotation by experienced raters requiring at least two reasoning skills per question, cross-rater quality review, and adversarial filtering using consensus from multiple text-only LLMs to eliminate text-biased items. The key evaluation contribution is the MINERVA rubric—a 4-axis taxonomy (Perceptual Correctness, Temporal Localization, Logical Reasoning, Completeness) scored on a 3-point Likert scale—paired with MiRA, an LLM-as-a-judge that scores model reasoning traces against ground truth references entirely in text space, avoiding the cost of re-watching videos. The paper further shows that simply prompting models with the rubric at inference time improves MCQ accuracy without additional compute, and that the largest failure modes across all evaluated models are temporal localization and perceptual errors rather than logical or completeness gaps.

## Results

On MINERVA, the best model (Gemini 2.5 Pro Thinking) achieves 66.20% MCQ accuracy with 1024 frames and ASR, while human performance reaches 92.54%, leaving a ~26-point gap. Open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL, VideoLLaMA3, InternVideo2.5) cluster tightly around 35% accuracy, while proprietary models span a wider range from 31.28% (Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2, limited to 64 frames) to 66.20%. Blind text-only baselines (QAD-only) score near chance (~20%), confirming that visual information is essential and that answer choices do not leak the correct answer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among open-source models, VideoLLaMA3 (35.91%) slightly edges out Qwen2.5-VL (35.05%) and InternVideo2.5 (35.18%), but all three are within ~1% of each other. Notably, these open-source models outperform Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2 (31.28%), which was constrained to only 64 frames by API limits. Among proprietary models, Gemini 2.5 Pro Thinking (66.20%) leads by a wide margin over GPT-4.1 (53.99%) and Gemini 2.0 Flash (53.47%), with the thinking/reasoning mode providing a clear boost (Gemini 2.5 Flash Thinking at 57.30% vs. Gemini 2.0 Flash at 53.47%). GPT-4o (45.54%) and OpenAI o1 (43.48%) occupy the mid-range, with o1 limited to 64 frames. Text-only blind baselines (GPT-4o at 19.60%, Qwen2.5-VL at 18.88%, DeepSeek at 21.45% without ASR) confirm near-chance performance, validating the dataset's resistance to linguistic shortcuts. The ~26-point gap between the best model and human performance (92.54%) indicates MINERVA remains a challenging benchmark for current frontier systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

MINERVA extends the video QA benchmarking tradition (VideoMME, LVBench, Neptune, CinePile) by adding hand-crafted, multi-step reasoning traces to each question, moving beyond outcome-only supervision. It departs from automated chain-of-thought pipelines like VideoCoT and VideoEspresso, which generate noisy rationales at scale for training, by providing fully manual, high-quality annotations designed for evaluation. The paper also connects to the broader reasoning-evaluation literature (ROSCOE, LLM-as-a-judge, chain-of-thought prompting) by transplanting reference-based rubric scoring into the video domain, where prior work has been largely confined to image-text or text-only settings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 26-point gap between the best model (Gemini 2.5 Pro Thinking at 66.2%) and humans (92.5%) positions MINERVA as a meaningful long-horizon benchmark for tracking progress in video understanding. The finding that failures concentrate in temporal localization and perceptual correctness—rather than logical reasoning—suggests the bottleneck for current video LMMs is in the perceptual/temporal grounding layer, not in the language-model reasoning layer, which has implications for where future model development should focus. Open questions include whether LLM-based judges (MiRA) can reliably scale to assess video reasoning without human oversight, and whether the rubric-based prompting trick generalizes beyond the specific models tested.
