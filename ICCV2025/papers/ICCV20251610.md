## Problem

Existing hieroglyph datasets (Glyph, MMM) support only isolated symbol classification, leaving no multi-style benchmark for full OCR of Ancient Egyptian texts. The scarcity of expert-annotated real data and the absence of synthetic generation methods that capture realistic spatial layouts and visual degradation further limit progress in building effective recognition systems for this script.

## Input / Output

The system takes clause-level crop images from three source modalities (digital documents, book scans, monument photographs) at 384×1024 resolution and produces plain sequences of Gardiner codes without layout markup. TrOCR's positional encodings are interpolated from 384×384 to support the wider inputs, and an extended tokenizer treats each Gardiner code (e.g., 'D36') as a single token rather than splitting into individual characters. The multi-stage baseline additionally outputs text-line bounding boxes and per-glyph detection boxes with classification labels, while TrOCR predicts the full sequence in one pass.

## Method

The MEH dataset pairs 40 expert-annotated images (12 sources, 512 clause-level samples, 12,744 hieroglyphs across 639 Gardiner codes) with large-scale synthetic data generated by a three-stage pipeline: extracting linguistically valid word and clause sequences from the Thesaurus Linguae Aegyptiae, composing them into quadrats via a randomized greedy algorithm in Manuel de Codage format, and rendering through a conditional diffusion model to produce 4.2 million synthetic images mimicking book scans and damaged monuments. For recognition, TrOCR (Small/Base/Large) is evaluated end-to-end against a multi-stage pipeline (segmentation → detection → classification → reading order), with the key finding that progressive synthetic pretraining (rendered → synthetic words → synthetic clauses) followed by real-data fine-tuning yields consistent CER reductions across all model sizes. Cross-style generalization is assessed by training on two of three style categories (printed, handwritten, physical) and evaluating on the held-out third, showing that larger models transfer more robustly to unseen visual domains.

## Results

On the MEH synthetic clause-level benchmark, the best TrOCR configuration (Large with token-based tokenizer) achieves a character error rate (CER) of 3.0% on rendered clauses and 9.7% on diffusion-synthesized clauses, substantially outperforming the multi-stage baseline (MSB) which scores 22.8% and 34.9% respectively. On real-data evaluation after full training, the Large TrOCR model reaches 30.6% CER versus 57.4% for MSB. In cross-style generalization (train on two styles, test on the third), the Large model achieves 18.9% CER on printed, 29.8% on handwritten, and 45.3% on physical inscriptions, consistently outperforming smaller variants by 10–23 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the end-to-end TrOCR model (in three sizes, with plain and token-based tokenizers) against the multi-stage baseline (MSB) from the authors' prior work [10], which decomposes OCR into text-line segmentation, glyph detection, classification, and reading-order reconstruction. TrOCR decisively outperforms MSB on clause-level recognition: the Large token-based variant achieves 3.0% CER on rendered clauses versus 22.8% for MSB (a ~7.6× reduction), and 9.7% versus 34.9% on diffusion-synthesized clauses. On word-level rendered data, TrOCR also wins (7.0% vs. 17.9% CER for Large token-based), though the gap narrows on word-level synthetic data where MSB (20.4%) is competitive with smaller TrOCR variants. On the real-data evaluation (Table 3), after full training including real samples, the Large TrOCR model reaches 30.6% CER compared to 57.4% for MSB, confirming that the end-to-end approach generalizes better to real inscriptions. The token-based tokenizer consistently provides a 1–3 percentage point CER improvement over the plain character-level tokenizer for Base and Large models, making it the recommended configuration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' own prior work on hieroglyph classification (the MMM dataset and multi-stage pipeline [10, 11]) by shifting from isolated symbol classification to full image-to-sequence OCR, introducing the first multi-style dataset with detection and sequence-level annotations for Egyptian hieroglyphs. It departs from the classification-only framing of prior datasets (Glyph, MMM) by providing clause-level crops paired with Gardiner code sequences, enabling end-to-end TrOCR evaluation alongside the multi-stage baseline. The synthetic data generation pipeline is novel in applying diffusion-based rendering to hieroglyphic sequences with realistic spatial layouts (via Manuel de Codage composition), going beyond the character-level augmentation used in prior ancient-script work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical bottleneck in computational Egyptology: the near-absence of OCR-ready annotated data for hieroglyphic texts. The finding that synthetic-only pretraining yields reasonable real-data performance (CER ~30–60% before fine-tuning) suggests a viable path to scaling beyond the current 40-image corpus, and the pyThoth tool provides a practical mechanism for that expansion. However, the small real-data size limits conclusions, and the authors themselves note that Gardiner-code targets overcomplicate the task relative to what Egyptologists actually need (transliteration or translation). The open question of whether multimodal image-to-transliteration models can bypass the intermediate symbol-recognition step is the most impactful direction for future work.
