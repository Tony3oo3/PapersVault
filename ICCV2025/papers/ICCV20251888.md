## Problem

Prior identity membership inference attacks against CLIP models require the attacker to already know the target person's name, limiting their utility as a privacy audit tool. This paper formalizes the harder Identity Extraction problem—recovering a person's previously unknown name from their facial images alone—and demonstrates that even models trained with face-blurring privacy protections (e.g., DataComp) remain highly vulnerable.

## Input / Output

The attacker provides a set of facial images per identity (ranging from 10 to several hundred, sourced from VGGFace2) and queries a black-box CLIP model, receiving only scalar similarity scores. The candidate name pool can scale to roughly one million names drawn from English Wikipedia 'Living People' pages. The output is either a specific extracted name (or 'nobody' if confidence falls below a threshold) for the extraction attacks, or a binary member/non-member decision for the IMIA baseline. In the IEGA variant, the attacker additionally supplies images of ~100 other identities as negative examples during LLM optimization.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary attacks. IESA (Extraction by Selection) adapts the IMIA scoring mechanism to a closed-set identification problem: for each candidate name, it computes the frequency with which that name achieves the maximum CLIP score across all images and caption templates, then selects the most frequent name. The key improvement over the baseline is an adaptive confidence criterion (Eq. 5) that requires the ratio of the top frequency to the second-highest frequency to exceed a threshold, effectively filtering out false positives from names sharing first or last names. IEGA (Extraction by Generation) eliminates the need for a pre-existing candidate set by either querying a VLM (GPT-4o, Gemini-1.5-Pro) with jailbreak prompts to produce candidate names, or fine-tuning Mistral-7B via LoRA with REINFORCE to maximize the summed CLIP score over the target's images while penalizing scores on distractor identities. Generated names are then verified through IESA to reject hallucinations, and the combined pipeline achieves up to ~50% total extraction rate with GPT-4o and ~12% with REINFORCE at 95% precision.

## Results

The proposed adaptive IESA attack (Eq. 5) substantially outperforms the baseline (Eq. 4) in the high-precision regime: at E@99.5, extraction rates improve from 0.70% to 15.85% (Convnext-xxlarge_L2B), 14.41% to 23.24% (ViT-B-32_L2B), and 5.04% to 16.45% (ViT-H-14_DC) when all available images are used. At the more relaxed E@95 threshold, the adaptive attack extracts roughly 30–45% of identities across models, with ViT-G-14_L2B being the most susceptible at ~47% extraction. For IEGA, GPT-4o achieves a 52.5% total extraction rate (22.25% at E@95) using a single image per identity, while the REINFORCE-based optimization with Mistral-7B achieves ~11–12% total extraction rate across CLIP models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the IMIA attack from Hintersdorf et al. [17] ("Does CLIP know my face?"), which the authors reproduce as Setting 1 in Table 1 and adapt as the IESA baseline (Eq. 4) in Table 2. In the IMIA setting, the paper's Setting 3 (using ~1 million Wikipedia names as distractors) outperforms the [17] reproduction (Setting 1, ~1000 distractors) for nearly every model at both FPR=0.25% and FPR=1%; for example, ViT-H-14_L2B improves from 19.6% to 36.4% TPR at FPR=0.25%, and ViT-G-14_L2B from 16.7% to 34.6%. In the IESA comparison (Table 2), the proposed adaptive attack (Eq. 5) wins at E@99.5 for 11 out of 14 model/setting combinations, with the largest gains on EVA-14_L2B (0.68% → 21.78%) and Convnext-xxlarge_L2B (0.70% → 15.85%). At E@95 the gap narrows considerably and the two attacks are often within 1–5 percentage points, with the baseline occasionally winning (e.g., ViT-G-14_L2B: 52.04% vs. 47.42%). The authors attribute the baseline's weakness at high precision to competing names sharing first or last names, which the adaptive gap-based threshold (Eq. 5) resolves.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Identity Membership Inference (IMI) framework of Hintersdorf et al. [17], which determined whether a specific face-name pair was in CLIP's training data, to the harder problem of Identity Extraction: recovering a person's actual name from their image alone, without knowing the name in advance. It also draws on the broader extraction-attack literature (Carlini et al. on training-data extraction from LLMs and diffusion models) and recent REINFORCE-style optimization for LLM fine-tuning [3] to enable name generation without a pre-existing candidate set. The evaluation protocol departs from [17] by replacing the small, demographically homogeneous FaceScrub set with the more diverse VGGFace2 and by scaling distractor names from ~1k to ~1M via English Wikipedia.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically significant finding is that the DataComp model family, which explicitly advertised face blurring as a privacy safeguard during training, remains highly vulnerable to name extraction at 95% precision—suggesting the blurring was too weak to actually protect individuals. This provides a concrete auditing methodology for verifying privacy claims in multimodal models post-hoc, a capability the field currently lacks. Open questions include whether stronger blurring or differential privacy would be sufficient, how these attacks transfer to non-CLIP architectures (e.g., native VLMs), and whether the same framework applies to other identity modalities (voice, handwriting). The finding that GPT-4o identifies ~50% of people from a single image despite explicit refusal policies also raises questions about the reliability of safety guardrails in deployed systems.
