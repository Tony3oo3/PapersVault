## Problem

Prior typographic attacks on vision-language models exploit only class-matching text inserted into images, overlooking a broader attack surface of non-matching text and graphical artifacts (logos, symbols) that are abundant in web-scale pretraining data. Because these artifacts are not predefined or tied to explicit class names, they are harder to detect, harder to defend against, and more challenging to discover systematically.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252231/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack takes a base image from a downstream classification dataset (e.g., FairFace, CelebA, FGVC Aircraft, Country211) and composites an artifact—non-matching text, a standalone graphic, or a graphic with embedded text—retrieved from image-text datasets like CC12M at a fixed size of 1/10 the image dimensions. The search pipeline outputs a ranked set of high-impact artifacts per target class, and the evaluation produces an Attack Success Rate computed with a masking-based validation step that discards samples where removing the artifact region also breaks correct classification, ensuring success is attributable to the artifact rather than occlusion.

## Method

The paper frames artifact discovery as a three-stage search problem over large-scale image-text datasets. Stage 1 uses CLIP-based retrieval with broad prompts (e.g., 'a photo of a graphic') to extract candidate images, then applies OCR (EasyOCR) to categorize them into non-matching text, graphics with embedded text, and graphics without text, filtering out any text that exactly matches downstream class names. Stage 2 applies each artifact to opposing-class images (as few as 10 per class) at random locations and ranks artifacts by the proportion of images misclassified toward the target class. Stage 3 optimizes placement by evaluating candidate positions and selecting the one with highest success rate. A key finding is that artifacts need not semantically match the target class—phonetic approximations, partial word overlaps, and purely visual symbols (e.g., red-and-white designs for 'Japan') all exploit spurious correlations learned during pretraining, and combining multiple artifact types compounds their effect to near 100% success.

## Results

Web Artifact Attacks achieve up to 90% Attack Success Rate (ASR) across diverse VLM configurations (CLIP, SigLIP, LLaVA; ViT-B-32, ViT-B-16, ViT-L-14; LAION-400M, LAION-2B, DataComp). Text-based artifacts are the most effective attack type, followed by graphics with embedded text, then graphics without text. Combining multiple artifact types (e.g., text + graphics) amplifies success to nearly 100% misclassification. Artifacts transfer across models with up to 90% retained effectiveness, and only 32 images per class are needed to reliably estimate an artifact's attack impact.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Typographic Attacks [Azuma & Matsui, ICCV 2023], which insert exact class-matching text into images. The paper's mined text-based Web Artifact Attacks outperform typographic attacks in overall success rate (Fig. 5), because they exploit a broader range of textual configurations (misspellings, phonetic approximations, partial overlaps) rather than being limited to the exact opposing class name. However, graphical artifacts (with or without embedded text) are less effective than text-based attacks overall, though they open a novel, harder-to-detect attack surface. CLIP (contrastive learning) is the most vulnerable model to all artifact types, while LLaVA and SigLIP show moderate reductions in susceptibility, likely due to smaller or more curated alignment datasets. The artifact-aware prompting mitigation [Cheng et al., ECCV 2024] reduces attack success by up to 15% relative to standard prompts, but is less effective against graphical artifacts than against the original typographic attacks it was designed for.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the typographic attack line of work (Azuma & Matsui, ICCV 2023; Qraitem et al., 2024), which showed that inserting class-matching text into images manipulates VLM predictions, by broadening the attack surface to non-matching text (misspellings, phonetic approximations) and graphical artifacts (logos, symbols with or without embedded text). It also builds on the artifact-aware prompting mitigation from Cheng et al. (ECCV 2024), extending it from purely textual to graphical artifacts via captioning-model descriptions. The key conceptual departure is that prior typographic attacks are predefined and easily detectable, whereas web artifact attacks are discovered through a search process over image-text datasets, making them unpredictable to defenders.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that DataComp's dataset curation barely reduces vulnerability is practically significant: it suggests that current filtering strategies (optimizing for image quality and semantic similarity) are ill-suited for removing the spurious text-graphic correlations that underpin these attacks. The high transferability (up to 90%) means that artifacts mined for one model generalize to unseen models, amplifying the threat to deployed VLMs. The mitigation (artifact-aware prompting) reduces success rates by only ~15%, leaving a substantial open question about how to robustly defend against non-textual graphical artifacts. Future work on dataset curation that explicitly targets branding/logo content and on more effective prompt-based or architectural defenses remains an important direction.
