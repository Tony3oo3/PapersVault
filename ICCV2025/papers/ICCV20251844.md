## Problem

Conventional seam-cutting methods in image stitching assume the existence of locally aligned regions where visually plausible seams can be established, but in large parallax scenarios, even state-of-the-art alignment methods frequently fail to satisfy this prerequisite. This creates a fundamental dependency where seam quality is tightly coupled to initial alignment accuracy, causing visually disruptive artifacts that post-alignment composition alone cannot rectify.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251844/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pair of pre-aligned RGB images (target and reference) produced by any alignment algorithm (e.g., REW, GSP, UDIS++) along with an initial seam estimated by any seam-cutting method. It outputs a refined stitching seam, locally realigned image patches merged back into the original aligned images, and a final composited mosaic. The module is fully plug-and-play: it requires no architectural changes to the host seam-cutting pipeline and operates on 21×21 evaluation patches centered at each seam pixel, with the sigmoid flow-modification parameter β fixed at 8.

## Method

The Local Patch Alignment Module (LPAM) is a lightweight, modular add-on that decouples seam quality from initial alignment accuracy through a two-stage repair process. First, it evaluates each seam pixel's quality via SSIM on local patches and identifies misaligned components using Otsu thresholding (with a k=1.5 mean criterion to skip trivially good seams). Second, for each misaligned component, it extracts enclosing rectangular patches, computes dense correspondences via localized SIFT-flow, and warps the target patch using a sigmoid-modified vector flow (β=8) that ensures smooth consistency with the surrounding pre-aligned image at patch boundaries. The warped patches are then recomposited via a constrained local graph-cut seam (boundary pixels locked to the initial seam's labels to prevent junction artifacts) and merged back into the original aligned images, producing the final mosaic without requiring any changes to the host algorithm.

## Results

On the three large-parallax stitching datasets (Parallax, SEAGULL, and the authors' own set), LPAM consistently improves seam quality across all four seam-cutting methods. For the Baseline method on SEAGULL (the most challenging dataset), PSNR improves from 26.20 to 28.84 dB (+2.64 dB) and SSIM from 0.638 to 0.697. On the Parallax dataset, the Perception-based method gains the largest SSIM boost (0.749→0.795) and RMSE reduction (0.084→0.074). LPAM adds only 1–3 seconds of processing time on top of the host seam-cutting method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LPAM is evaluated as a plug-in module on four seam-cutting baselines (Baseline, Perception [19], Iterative [20], Quaternion [15]) and visually compared against two seam-driven methods (Parallax [31], SEAGULL [22]). Against all four quantitative baselines, LPAM wins on every metric (RMSE, PSNR, SSIM, ZNCC) across all three datasets, with the largest gains on SEAGULL where PSNR improves by 1.1–2.6 dB depending on the host method. Visually, the paper reports that Microsoft ICE, the Baseline, and Iterative seam-cutting [20] produce obvious parallax artifacts, while LPAM applied to the Baseline achieves results comparable to the more complex seam-driven approaches Parallax [31] and SEAGULL [22], which require generating and selecting from multiple alignment candidates. LPAM also generalizes across alignment backends (Homo, ANAP, GSP, REW, UDIS++), showing stable improvements regardless of the initial warping quality. The only noted limitation is that for very small misaligned patches, the sigmoid-modified SIFT flow cannot simultaneously guarantee accurate alignment and smooth boundary consistency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the dominant paradigm in image stitching where seam-cutting is strictly a downstream process contingent on successful global alignment. It builds directly on SIFT flow [23] for dense correspondence and graph-cut optimization [3] for seam estimation, but departs from both seam-cutting methods (which assume locally aligned regions exist) and seam-driven methods (which generate multiple alignment candidates) by introducing a local post-hoc realignment step at the seam. The key conceptual departure is the 'alignment-compensation paradigm': rather than improving global alignment or selecting among alignment candidates, LPAM surgically repairs only the misaligned patches where the seam passes, making it a modular add-on to any existing seam-cutting pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value lies in LPAM's plug-and-play nature: it requires no architectural changes to host algorithms and adds only 2–5 seconds of computation, making it immediately adoptable in production stitching pipelines. The acknowledged limitation—that the sigmoid flow modification becomes insufficient when structural misalignment is severe within small patches—points toward adaptive patch-size strategies as a clear next step. More broadly, the paper validates the principle that composition-stage quality need not be tightly coupled to alignment-stage accuracy, which could inspire similar 'local repair' strategies in other multi-stage vision pipelines where a downstream step inherits errors from an upstream one.
