## Problem

Existing material recognition methods treat material categories as a flat set, ignoring the natural hierarchical structure (e.g., rubber ⊂ plastic ⊂ polymer) that reflects shared physical and visual traits. This flat treatment means a misclassification at the finest level yields no useful partial information, and related materials cannot share learned visual features. The paper asks whether explicitly encoding a material taxonomy can improve recognition accuracy and provide graceful degradation to coarser but still actionable classifications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251527/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the model takes a single RGB image patch of local material appearance (no depth required) and outputs the full hierarchical taxonomic path from root to leaf (e.g., {solid, abiotic, metal, ferrous, steel}), enabling mechanical property lookup. For scene-level probing, the method searches over window sizes from 64×64 to 1024×1024 pixels using Monte Carlo dropout to estimate prediction confidence, then performs a best-first search down the taxonomy tree to produce a hierarchically consistent label. During training, depth maps from lidar are used to render novel views (varying magnification, orientation, defocus, and noise) that augment the dataset but are not needed at test time.

## Method

The core contribution is a Graph Attention Network (GAT) whose nodes correspond to levels of a hand-constructed material taxonomy organized by physical traits (phase → state → composition → form → material). A CNN backbone (ResNet50, ConvNeXt-V2, or EVA-02) encodes image features; node embeddings are initialized as the mean of image features over all samples belonging to that taxonomic node, and a global query node for the input image is inserted with edges to all other nodes. GAT message passing over D layers (matching the taxonomy diameter) with residual connections allows taxonomically proximate nodes to share visual traits via learned attention weights. Training jointly optimizes the encoder and GAT with a combined loss: binary cross-entropy on the full hierarchical path plus per-level cross-entropy, encouraging both complete-path and level-wise correctness. A key empirical finding is that rendering novel views via ray tracing from captured depth maps (simulating different cameras, magnifications, and orientations) improves out-of-distribution generalization by up to 4.9%.

## Results

On the Matador-C1 dataset (37 textured material classes, ~6,600 samples), the proposed hierarchical GAT model with an EVA-02 backbone achieves 94.7% top-1 accuracy, outperforming the best flat-classification baseline (EVA-02 at 90.1%) by 4.6 points. On the out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization test, rendered novel views during training boost accuracy by up to 4.0 points (87.9% vs. 83.9% without), surpassing all baselines. On standard benchmarks (Table 1), the method achieves 93.5% on KTH-TIPS2-b, 96.1% on FMD, 87.9% on GTOS, and 92.2% on GTOS-Mobile, setting new state-of-the-art on all four. In the few-shot setting, the model reaches ~90% accuracy on a held-out material class with only 16 training samples, with misclassifications localized to sibling nodes (average path distance < 2 hops).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed hierarchical GAT model outperforms all 14 baselines across every evaluated dataset and metric. On Matador-C1, it beats the strongest modern image recognition baselines—EVA-02 (90.1%) and ConvNeXt-V2 (89.7%)—by 4.6 and 5.0 points respectively, and surpasses dedicated material recognition models (DeepTEN 88.8%, DEPNet 87.6%, FRP 89.4%, MSLac 88.5%) by 4.3–7.1 points. On the OOD test, the gap widens: the method (87.9% with EVA-02) exceeds EVA-02 (82.7%) by 5.2 points and ConvNeXt-V2 (81.9%) by 6.0 points, while material-specific models trail further (DEPNet 76.1%, MSLac 75.4%, FRP 71.0%, DeepTEN 61.5%). Zero-shot vision foundation models (CLIP 40.0%, GPT-4.1 65.9%) are substantially outperformed, confirming that task-specific hierarchical training is critical for fine-grained material discrimination. On standard benchmarks (Table 1), the method leads FRP (90.7% on KTH-2-b, 88.8% on FMD) by 2.8 and 7.3 points, and surpasses CLASSNet (87.7%, 86.2%), DSRNet (85.9%, 86.0%), and all other baselines by 2–10 points across KTH-TIPS2-b, FMD, GTOS, and GTOS-Mobile.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the material recognition literature, which has long treated the problem as flat multi-class classification over texture patches (DeepTEN, MAPNet, DSRNet, FRP, etc.), by reframing it as hierarchical node classification on a physically-motivated taxonomy graph. The core architectural contribution builds directly on Graph Attention Networks [76] and prior work on hierarchical image classification with GNNs [81, 82], but applies them to a domain-specific material taxonomy rather than a generic WordNet-style hierarchy. The Matador dataset departs from prior texture datasets (KTH-TIPS2-b, FMD, GTOS) by capturing 3D surface structure via lidar, enabling physics-based novel view rendering as a training augmentation — a capability absent from all prior material datasets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The hierarchical formulation provides a practical safety net: even when the finest material class is misidentified, the system still recovers a coarser but useful label (e.g., 'metal' rather than 'brass'), enabling downstream mechanical property inference for robotics and autonomous driving. The Matador dataset, with its 3D structure and in-the-wild capture protocol, is a valuable resource that supports OOD generalization studies and few-shot adaptation experiments. Open questions include whether the assumption that taxonomic proximity implies visual similarity holds broadly (the authors acknowledge it is 'not guaranteed'), whether the taxonomy can be extended to liquids and gases, and whether the GAT-based approach scales to much larger or more deeply nested taxonomies.
