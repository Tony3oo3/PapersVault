## Problem

Vanilla fine-tuning for backdoor defense is ineffective because it only minimizes clean loss, causing the model to converge to regions where both clean and backdoor losses remain low. The paper identifies this 'backdoor region' trap—where the model stays near its initial backdoored weights and continues to respond to triggers—and asks how to explicitly push the model out of this region while preserving clean accuracy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250639/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained backdoored model (e.g., PreAct-ResNet18, VGG19-BN, or ViT-B-16) and a small clean dataset (typically 5% of the original training set, with no knowledge of the trigger or target label) as input. It outputs detoxified model weights that no longer respond to the malicious trigger. The optimization operates on a selected subset of weights (linear layers by default) with a Frobenius-norm constraint to prevent trivial weight scaling, and the clean loss threshold ε and penalty multiplier λ control the accuracy–defense trade-off.

## Method

D3 reformulates backdoor defense as a constrained optimization problem: maximize the distance between the current weights and the initial backdoored weights, subject to keeping clean loss below a threshold ε. This is converted into a regularized objective (negative distance plus a hinge penalty on clean loss) solved via Projected Gradient Descent, where the projection enforces a norm constraint on the selected weight subset to ensure meaningful updates rather than superficial scaling. A second-order Taylor expansion around the initial weights provides theoretical justification: since the backdoored model sits at a local minimum of backdoor loss, the backdoor loss grows approximately quadratically with distance, so moving far enough away reliably increases backdoor loss. The method requires only a single hyperparameter pair (λ, ε) and adds minimal computational overhead over vanilla fine-tuning, while a selective weight-subset design mitigates over-fitting on the limited clean data.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with PreAct-ResNet18 at 10% poisoning ratio, D3 achieves an average ASR of 0.46% and DER of 97.97 while maintaining 91.93% clean accuracy, outperforming all nine compared defense methods on both ASR and DER. D3 attains the second-lowest ASR in six out of seven attacks, with ASR below 1% for the remaining attack (Blended: 0.22%). The method also demonstrates robustness to adaptive attacks, maintaining ASR below 3% across all SAM perturbation budgets (1.0–3.0) where baselines like FT-SAM degrade to 54.79% ASR at budget 3.0.

## Baselines & Comparisons

D3 achieves the lowest average ASR (0.46%) and highest DER (97.97) among all ten compared methods on CIFAR-10. Against the strongest fine-tuning baseline FT-SAM [51], D3 reduces ASR from 3.91% to 0.46% and improves DER from 96.35 to 97.97, at the cost of a 0.53% ACC drop (91.93 vs 92.46). D3 decisively outperforms SAU [39] on both metrics (ASR 0.46 vs 1.04; ACC 91.93 vs 89.91) and i-BAU [48] (ASR 0.46 vs 19.52; ACC 91.93 vs 87.50). Against pruning-based methods ANP [45] and FP [24], D3 reduces ASR by over 32 and 52 percentage points respectively while matching or exceeding their clean accuracy. In the adaptive attack setting (Table 5), D3 maintains ASR below 3% at all SAM budgets, whereas FT-SAM [51] degrades to 54.79% ASR at budget 3.0, Super-FT [32] to 73.15%, and NFT [14] to 68.75%, confirming D3's superior robustness against adaptive threats.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the fine-tuning paradigm for post-training backdoor defense by identifying a specific failure mode: vanilla fine-tuning converges to weights that remain in a 'backdoor region' where both clean and backdoor loss are low. It departs from the adversarial-reconstruction line (NC, i-BAU, SAU) that explicitly reconstructs poisoned samples, and from FT-SAM which modifies the optimization landscape via sharpness-aware minimization, by instead formulating defense as a constrained optimization that maximizes weight distance from the initial backdoored model. The conceptual lineage traces back to the observation that fine-tuning alone is insufficient [32, 42, 43, 51], but the distance-driven objective is a new reformulation that avoids the computational overhead of trigger reconstruction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method's simplicity (a single distance term added to standard fine-tuning, solved via projected gradient descent) makes it easy to adopt, and its resistance to adaptive attacks (ASR < 3% even when attackers use SAM to flatten the loss landscape) addresses a practical vulnerability that prior fine-tuning defenses struggle with. Key limitations include the ACC–ASR trade-off (pushing weights further away degrades clean accuracy), the heuristic choice of which weight subset to constrain (linear layers), and the lack of a rigorous global guarantee beyond the local Taylor expansion. Open questions include identifying backdoor-specific weights for more targeted distance measurement and extending the framework to multi-trigger or multi-target attack scenarios.
