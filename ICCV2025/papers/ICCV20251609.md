## Problem

Existing omni-models primarily explore speech-text relationships while neglecting speech's interaction with vision and other modalities, and their speech evaluation is limited to text-based metrics like LibriSpeech WER. This paper argues that strong speech-text performance does not guarantee good speech-vision performance, and that omni-model evaluation should be speech-centric across all modality pairs. Additionally, prior approaches require large training datasets and struggle with long speech inputs (typically capped at 30 seconds to 1 minute) due to encoder limitations and lack of suitable long-form speech data.

## Input / Output

Lyra accepts arbitrary-resolution images (via Qwen2-VL's dynamic ViT), videos, speech and sound audio (up to 2+ hours for long speech), and text instructions as input, and produces both text and streaming speech audio as output. A key design choice is that speech instructions replace text instructions in cross-modal tasks (e.g., asking a question about an image via spoken audio), enabling true speech-centric evaluation. For long speech, raw audio is segmented into 30-second clips, each encoded by Whisper-largev3 into 1,500 tokens, then compressed to 300 tokens per clip via a view operation and linear layers in the speech projector.

## Method

Lyra builds on Qwen2-VL (ViT + LLM backbone) and Whisper-largev3 (audio encoder), introducing three modules: (1) a Latent Cross-Modality Regularizer (LCMR) that uses Dynamic Time Warping to compute a latent distance between speech tokens and their transcribed text tokens, adding a regularization loss that forces the audio encoder to produce linguistically aligned representations; (2) a Multi-Modality LoRA (MLoRA) where each modality (text, image, video, speech, sound) receives its own low-rank adapter per LLM layer, preserving pretrained capabilities while efficiently adding speech; and (3) a Latent Multi-Modality Extractor (LMME) that applies block-wise attention-based token pruning, retaining only the top-k non-text tokens most relevant to the text query at the end of each transformer block, reducing token count to 10–25% and enabling 2+ hour speech processing. Training follows a four-stage pipeline (speech pretraining → joint multi-modal SFT → long-speech extension → streaming speech generation) on 1.5M multi-modal samples and 12K long-speech samples, using far fewer data than competing omni-models (2.7M vs. 27M for Intern-Omni).

## Results

Lyra-Base (9B) achieves state-of-the-art results across omni-modal benchmarks: MME 2335, MM-Vet 65.0, VideoMME 68.0, MVBench 67.5, and EgoSchema 63.2 on vision-language tasks, while also leading on image-speech tasks (TextVQA^S 80.7, DocVQA^S 87.1, ChartQA^S 67.5) and achieving a LibriSpeech WER of 1.8. Lyra-Pro (74B) further pushes performance to MME 2485, MM-Vet 71.4, VideoMME 69.9, and EgoSchema 75.8. On long speech (VideoMME audio-only), Lyra with the latent speech capability integration (LSCI) reaches 78.6% overall accuracy, surpassing GPT-4o with subtitles (77.1%). The model uses only 2.7M training samples, substantially fewer than Intern-Omni (27M), VITA (5M), or EMOVA (4M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Lyra-Base (9B) comprehensively outperforms Intern-Omni (8B) on all shared benchmarks, with the largest gains on image-speech tasks: TextVQA^S 80.7 vs 69.1 (+11.6), DocVQA^S 87.1 vs 79.9 (+7.2), and ChartQA^S 67.5 vs 56.0 (+11.5), confirming the paper's claim of ~9% improvement in speech-vision interaction. Against the much larger VITA (66B), Lyra-Base wins on MME (2335 vs 2097), MM-Vet (65.0 vs 41.6), VideoMME (68.0 vs 59.2), and LibriSpeech WER (1.8 vs 8.1), while using roughly 7× fewer parameters. Lyra-Base also surpasses EMOVA (14B) on MME (2335 vs 2205), MM-Vet (65.0 vs 55.8), and LibriSpeech (1.8 vs 4.0). On pure speech-text (LibriSpeech WER), Lyra-Base (1.8) is marginally behind Qwen2-Audio (1.6) but ahead of SALMONN (2.1) and Mini-Omni (4.5). On the long-speech VideoMME evaluation (audio-only input), Lyra with LSCI achieves 78.6% overall, exceeding GPT-4o with subtitles (77.1%) and the 7B baseline (62.8%), with the largest gap on long videos (74.8% vs 72.1% for GPT-4o). The paper also notes that Lyra achieves these results with only 2.7M training samples versus 27M for Intern-Omni, 5M for VITA, and 4M for EMOVA.

## Relation to Prior Work

Lyra builds on Qwen2-VL as its vision-language backbone and Whisper as its audio encoder, extending both with a multi-modality LoRA pipeline that preserves pre-trained capabilities while adding speech. It departs from prior omni-models (Intern-Omni, VITA, EMOVA) in two key ways: (1) introducing a Latent Cross-Modality Regularizer that uses Dynamic Time Warping to align speech tokens with their text counterparts in latent space, and (2) proposing a Latent Multi-Modality Extractor that generalizes token-reduction techniques (FastV, VisionZip) to speech and video modalities. The paper also positions itself against the field's reliance on LibriSpeech WER as the sole speech metric, arguing that speech-vision interaction must be evaluated separately.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that strong speech-text alignment (low WER) does not guarantee good speech-vision performance challenges the evaluation paradigm of prior omni-models and calls for more comprehensive cross-modality benchmarks. The 12K long-speech SFT dataset (up to 2 hours) and the demonstrated ability to process multi-hour audio with the token extractor open a previously intractable regime for speech understanding. Open questions include whether the DTW-based alignment generalizes to non-English or low-resource speech, whether the 10–25% token retention rate holds for more complex multi-turn interactions, and whether the efficiency gains (2.7M vs. 27M training samples) persist at larger scales or with more modalities.
