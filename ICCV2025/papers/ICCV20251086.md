## Problem

Existing iterative tracing methods for tubular structure extraction treat the task as discrete node prediction, ignoring the connectivity between adjacent nodes. When branches are closely spaced or entangled, this causes the tracer to jump to neighboring branches, producing critical topological errors. No prior method integrates local edge/connectivity information into the node selection process.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251086/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 2D or 3D image of a tubular structure (roads, vessels, or neurons) and a set of seed nodes randomly sampled on the predicted centerline. Training patches are 64×64 (2D) or 32×64×64 (3D), with slight positional noise injected into the historical path to improve robustness. The output is a traced graph of nodes and edges, along with three intermediate probability maps (centerline, future node, future edge) that drive the iterative search. The GDS strategy uses 8-neighborhood connectivity in 2D and 26-neighborhood in 3D.

## Method

NETracer builds on a V-Net backbone with three jointly trained sub-task blocks: centerline segmentation, future node estimation, and a novel future edge estimation block. The key architectural insight is a local connectivity loss (L_conn) that penalizes predicted edges deviating from the correct local path between the current node and its true successor, forcing the edge block to produce conservative, topology-aware connection cues. During tracing, a Geodesic Distance-based Search Strategy (GDS) uses the predicted edge probability map as a spatial cost function to propagate geodesic distances from the current node, selecting the most topologically reliable future node from multiple candidates in the node probability map. The centerline features and historical traced path are concatenated as shared input to the node and edge blocks, providing a continuity prior. A new optimization objective replaces point-wise node probability with an integral over the edge probability, formally capturing branch continuity.

## Results

On the 2D Massachusetts Roads dataset, NETracer achieves the best coverage (SSD-F1 0.718, L-F1 0.836) while dramatically reducing Jump Error to 8.2 (vs. 12.9 for V-Net+clDice and 24.3 for DBT) and maximizing Average Branch Length to 234.4. On 3D fMOST-VTA, NETracer reaches SSD-F1 0.919, L-F1 0.913, PE 0.693, JE 2.3, and ABL 219.3, outperforming all baselines on every tracing metric. Across all five datasets, NETracer consistently achieves the lowest JE (topological jump errors) and highest ABL (branch continuity), confirming that integrating edge cues into iterative tracing substantially improves topological accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NETracer outperforms all nine baselines on the Jump Error (JE) metric across every dataset, with the most dramatic margin on ROAD (8.2 vs. 12.9 for V-Net+clDice and 24.3 for DBT) and fMOST-VTA (2.3 vs. 3.1 for DSCNet). On Average Branch Length (ABL), NETracer leads by a wide margin on ROAD (234.4 vs. 200.2 for DBT) and fMOST-VTA (219.3 vs. 167.9 for DBT), confirming superior branch continuity. Among segmentation-based methods (V-Net, DSCNet, CS-Net, clDice), NETracer matches or exceeds their coverage scores (SSD-F1, L-F1) while achieving substantially better topological accuracy, since their binary masks lack explicit connectivity cues for post-processing. Among iterative tracing methods (RoadTracer, Vec-Road, DBT, SPE, neuTu), NETracer consistently wins on PE, JE, and ABL, with Vec-Road being the closest competitor on coverage but trailing by 3.6–6.2 JE points. The ablation (M1→M5) shows that adding the local connectivity loss and GDS strategy together yields the largest JE reduction (from 36 to 18 on fMOST-VTA), while GDS applied to centerline predictions alone (M3) actually worsens performance, underscoring the necessity of dedicated edge cues.

## Relation to Prior Work

NETracer extends the iterative tracing paradigm (RoadTracer, Vec-Road, DeepBranchTracer, SPE) by reformulating the optimization objective from discrete node selection to continuous edge integration, arguing that existing methods' failure to model inter-node connectivity causes topological jumps in dense scenes. Architecturally, it builds on the V-Net backbone and multi-task learning framework but introduces a dedicated future-edge estimation block with a novel local connectivity loss that penalizes predictions deviating from the current branch. The geodesic distance-based search strategy (GDS) departs from prior methods that simply pick the probability-map peak, instead tracing along predicted edge cues to disambiguate among multiple candidate nodes. The paper also introduces a new iterative tracing metric (PE, ABL, JE) that goes beyond the coverage-only evaluation (SSD-F1, L-F1) used in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The topological jump error in dense tubular scenes (adjacent roads, crossing vessels, entangled neurites) is a persistent practical failure mode that coverage metrics alone cannot capture; the proposed JE and ABL metrics give the field a way to quantify this. The consistent JE reductions across five datasets (e.g., 8.2 vs. 19–24 on ROAD, 2.3 vs. 3–5 on fMOST-VTA) suggest that explicit edge modeling is a meaningful inductive bias for topology preservation. Open questions include whether the approach degrades when centerline segmentation is very poor (the method still relies on it for seeds and stopping criteria), whether the GDS overhead scales to very large 3D volumes, and whether the path-correction training heuristic generalizes beyond the tested domains.
