## Problem

VLM-based zero-shot learning methods like CLIP compute similarity between the entire query image and category word embeddings, making predictions uninterpretable. While recent interpretable approaches use LLM-generated attributes, they still perform global (whole-image-to-attribute) alignment rather than matching fine-grained visual regions to their corresponding attributes, resulting in incorrect visual-semantic alignments and limited domain generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251220/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and, at inference time, randomly crops it into 60–90 multi-scale region patches (scale α=0.6) to form a local visual set, while a frozen GPT-3 model pre-generates M attribute descriptions per class to form the semantic set. Output is a predicted class label accompanied by interpretable per-attribute similarity scores (e.g., 'black wings with white wingbars: 0.0673'), allowing users to see which visual-semantic correspondences drove the decision. The entire pipeline is training-free, operating on frozen CLIP ViT encoders (B/32, B/16, or L/14).

## Method

LaZSL formulates interpretable ZSL as an Optimal Transport problem between a local visual set (N cropped patches encoded by CLIP's vision encoder) and a semantic set (M LLM-generated attributes encoded by CLIP's text encoder). A vision selection mechanism first filters out noisy patches whose cosine similarity to the global image feature falls below a threshold δ, reweighting the source distribution r accordingly. A region-global hybrid cost matrix C* = 1 − (θ·sim_local + (1−θ)·sim_global) with θ=0.8 is constructed to prevent knowledge forgetting in the frozen CLIP encoder. The Sinkhorn algorithm then computes the OT plan T by iteratively updating row and column scalings under an entropic regularization λ. The final category score is the Frobenius inner product ⟨T, sim*⟩_F between the OT plan and the hybrid similarity matrix, and the predicted label is argmax over all classes.

## Results

On five standard ZSL benchmarks (ImageNet, CUB, Oxford Pets, Food101, Place365), LaZSL achieves average top-1 accuracy of 66.8%, 69.7%, and 74.0% with ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, and ViT-L/14 backbones respectively, consistently outperforming all interpretable ZSL baselines. On domain generalization (ImageNet-V2, ImageNet-R, ImageNet-Sketch, ImageNet-A), LaZSL reaches 60.9% average accuracy without any additional training, surpassing both training-free interpretable methods (DCLIP: 58.2%, CuPL: 60.0%) and trained methods (MaPLe: 60.3%, ArGue: 60.3%). The largest gains appear on fine-grained datasets: +3.8% over DCLIP on CUB (ViT-B/32) and +2.6% (ViT-L/14).

## Baselines & Comparisons

LaZSL consistently outperforms DCLIP (the primary interpretable baseline) by +2.0%, +1.6%, and +1.2% average accuracy on ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16, and ViT-L/14 respectively, with the largest margin on CUB (+3.8% to +2.6%). Against WaffleCLIP and CuPL, LaZSL wins on average across all three backbones (e.g., 69.7% vs. 68.3% and 69.2% on ViT-B/16), though CuPL edges ahead on individual datasets like Oxford Pets (91.1% vs. 87.4% on ViT-B/16). On domain generalization (Table 2), LaZSL achieves 60.9% average without any training, beating training-free DCLIP (58.2%) and CuPL (60.0%), and also surpassing trained methods MaPLe (60.3%) and ArGue (60.3%). Standard prompt-learning baselines (CoOp, CoCoOp, TPT, ProGrad) all require additional training and achieve lower domain-generalization averages (59.1%–59.9%) than LaZSL's training-free 60.9%. ProAPO (CVPR 2025) is competitive on ViT-B/32 (66.7% avg) but is not evaluated on the other two backbones or domain-shift datasets. Overall, LaZSL is the only method that simultaneously achieves the best average accuracy, full interpretability via attribute-level explanations, and zero additional training.

## Relation to Prior Work

LaZSL sits at the intersection of two lines of work: (1) interpretable VLM-based ZSL methods (DCLIP, WaffleCLIP, CuPL, ArGue) that use LLM-generated attributes to make classifiers explainable, and (2) optimal transport formulations in vision (PLOT, PatchCT, multi-mode prompt alignment) that match distributions across modalities. The key departure from prior interpretable ZSL is replacing the standard global image-to-attribute similarity (1-to-1) with a local N-to-M alignment via Sinkhorn OT, where random multi-scale crops form a visual distribution matched against the attribute distribution. Unlike prompt-learning or adapter methods (CoOp, MaPLe, ArGue), LaZSL is entirely training-free, relying only on the frozen CLIP encoders and an LLM for attribute generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free nature of LaZSL is practically significant: it outperforms several methods that require additional training (ArGue, CoOp, CoCoOp) on domain-shift benchmarks, suggesting that principled local alignment can substitute for fine-tuning in ZSL. However, the random cropping strategy is somewhat ad-hoc and the quality of the method is bounded by LLM attribute generation; more principled region selection or learned patch weighting could be explored. Open questions include whether the OT formulation extends naturally to video or multi-image ZSL, and whether the hybrid global-local cost can be replaced by a fully learned transport plan without sacrificing the training-free property.
