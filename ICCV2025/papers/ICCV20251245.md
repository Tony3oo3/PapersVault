## Problem

Open-Vocabulary Segmentation methods eliminate fixed class sets but still require a human to specify which categories to segment, either through per-image prompts or by baking a target vocabulary into training. This human-in-the-loop requirement limits scalability for truly open-ended scene understanding, such as a robot identifying arbitrary objects without pre-defined labels. The paper introduces Auto-Vocabulary Semantic Segmentation (AVS), which autonomously discovers relevant object categories from the image and segments them without any user-provided vocabulary.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251245/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image as input, processed internally at two resolutions (384×384 and 512×512) to extract multi-scale patch embeddings. It produces high-resolution pixel-level segmentation masks paired with an auto-generated vocabulary of object class names (nouns) discovered entirely from the image content. No training, fine-tuning, or textual input is required; the output vocabulary is image-specific and can include categories absent from any standard annotation set (e.g., 'dachshund', 'pagoda', 'moped').

## Method

AutoSeg's core component, BBoost, encodes the image with BLIP's ViT-Large encoder at two resolutions, clusters the resulting patch embeddings via k-means (k=2–8) across both scales, aligns cluster indices across runs using Hungarian matching on pairwise IoU, and denoises the assignments with a CRF followed by recursive majority filtering. Each denoised cluster's patch embeddings are then fed directly into BLIP's pre-trained text decoder to generate a local caption, from which nouns are extracted via POS tagging, lemmatized, and filtered against WordNet to form the target vocabulary. This auto-generated vocabulary is passed as text prompts to X-Decoder (Focal-L) to produce high-resolution segmentation masks. For evaluation, LAVE (built on Llama-2-7B) maps the auto-generated class names to the ground-truth vocabulary, resolving synonymy, hyponymy, and spatial-hierarchy ambiguities that cosine-similarity matching cannot handle.

## Results

AutoSeg achieves 87.1 mIoU on PASCAL VOC (20 classes), 11.7 mIoU on PASCAL Context (459 classes), 6.0 mIoU on ADE20K (847 classes), and 30.0 mIoU on Cityscapes (20 classes) using the LAVE auto-mapper. With a manual mapper, scores improve slightly to 88.2, 12.8, 6.2, and 31.1 mIoU respectively. These results match 91%, 67%, 42%, and 55% of the best prompted-vocabulary OVS method on each dataset, while surpassing 6 of 8 OVS baselines on VOC and 3 of 7 on PASCAL Context without any human-specified class names.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the only other true auto-vocabulary method, ZeroSeg [32], AutoSeg wins decisively: 87.1 vs. 20.1 mIoU on VOC and 11.7 vs. 11.4 on PASCAL Context. Among LLM-aided baselines, AutoSeg outperforms LLaVA+LISA [16,21] (87.1 vs. 7.7 on VOC), LLaVA+X-Decoder [21,48] (87.1 vs. 56.7), and SAM+BLIP+X-Decoder [13,18,48] (87.1 vs. 41.1). Against prompted-vocabulary OVS methods, AutoSeg surpasses LSeg [17] (87.1 vs. 47.4), OpenSeg [10] (87.1 vs. 72.2 on VOC, 11.7 vs. 9.0 on PC), and OVSeg [19] on PC (11.7 vs. 11.0), but trails the top performers CAT-Seg [5] (97.2), X-Decoder [48] (96.2), FC-CLIP [46] (95.4), and SAN [43] (94.6) on VOC. On PASCAL Context, AutoSeg (11.7) beats OVSeg (11.0) and OpenSeg (9.0) but falls short of CAT-Seg (19.0), X-Decoder (16.1), ODISE (13.8), SAN (12.6), and FC-CLIP (12.8). On ADE20K and Cityscapes, AutoSeg remains competitive but lags behind FC-CLIP (14.8/56.2) and X-Decoder (6.4/50.8), reflecting the greater difficulty of high-class-count and high-instance-density scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Open-Vocabulary Segmentation (OVS) by removing the human-specified vocabulary requirement, introducing Auto-Vocabulary Segmentation (AVS) as a distinct task. It builds directly on BLIP's ViT encoder and text decoder, repurposing the latter for local region captioning via clustered patch embeddings (BBoost) — a capability the authors note emerges organically without dedicated training. The closest prior work, Zero-Guidance Segmentation (ZeroSeg), also targets zero human input but relies on a multi-model pipeline (DINO + CLIP + GPT-2); AutoSeg departs by using a single VLM (BLIP) for both clustering and captioning, then delegating high-resolution segmentation to an off-the-shelf OVS model (X-Decoder). The LAVE evaluator is a novel contribution that addresses the previously unsolved problem of scoring auto-generated class labels against fixed ground-truth vocabularies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AutoSeg demonstrates that fully autonomous, open-ended scene segmentation is feasible without any user prompt or retraining, which is practically important for applications like robotic manipulation where the object set is unpredictable. The LAVE framework provides a reusable evaluation protocol for any future auto-vocabulary method, reducing the infeasible manual mapping effort. However, performance still lags OVS methods with known vocabulary by a wide margin (e.g., 87.1 vs. 97.2 mIoU on VOC; 6.0 vs. 14.8 on ADE), and the approach inherits the limitations of its pretrained components. Open questions include whether the gap to OVS can be closed, how the method scales to very dense or novel scenes, and whether the multi-resolution clustering strategy generalizes beyond the tested datasets.
