## Problem

Existing auto white balance methods estimate the scene illuminant solely from image colors, ignoring the rich contextual metadata (timestamp, geolocation) that mobile devices readily provide. No prior work has investigated how time-of-day and location information can narrow the range of plausible illuminant colors, particularly for outdoor scenes where solar position strongly constrains correlated color temperature. Additionally, no public dataset includes the contextual metadata needed to train and evaluate such a method.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 384×256 raw image, a timestamp and geolocation pair (converted into a 12-dim solar-event probability vector with before/after one-hot indicators), log-scaled ISO and shutter speed, a binary flash flag, and optional noise statistics or SNR statistics. It outputs a 2D chromaticity vector (R/G, B/G) that is mapped to a normalized RGB illuminant color for channel-wise white-balance correction. The entire network contains only ~5K parameters and runs in 0.25 ms on a mobile DSP.

## Method

The architecture has two parallel branches whose 16-dim latent vectors are concatenated and fed to a small MLP. The time-capture branch converts clock time (adjusted via geolocation to local solar events: dawn, sunrise, noon, sunset, dusk, midnight) into probability scores using a linear distance formula, applies a square-root transform for balanced feature scaling, appends before/after one-hot indicators, and concatenates log(ISO), log(shutter), flash, and noise features before a single linear projection. The histogram branch computes a 48×48 2D chromaticity histogram in R/G–B/G space (weighted by pixel intensity, square-rooted) plus a matching edge histogram, concatenates them with u/v coordinate channels (4 channels total), and processes them through conv layers with ELU activation, adaptive average pooling, and a linear layer. The 32-dim combined vector passes through a few linear layers with batch normalization to predict the illuminant chromaticity, optimized end-to-end with angular error loss. The key insight is that solar-event timing provides a strong prior on the CCT range for outdoor scenes, and fusing this prior with color statistics in a compact model matches or surpasses much larger networks (e.g., C4 with 5.1M parameters) while being 1000× smaller.

## Results

On the proposed Samsung S24 Ultra AWB dataset (400 test images), the full model (with noise stats and SNR stats) achieves a mean angular error of 1.84° (neutral) / 1.20° (user-preference), with a median of 1.24° / 0.77° and tri-mean of 1.32° / 0.87°. On the Simple Cube++ DSLR dataset (where geolocation is unavailable), the method using only ISO, exposure time, and SNR stats achieves 1.01° mean angular error, outperforming C4 (1.16°) and C5(tuned-CS) (1.19°). The model has only ~5K parameters and runs in 0.55 ms on CPU (vs. 49.03 ms for C4 and 8.28 ms for C5), or 0.25 ms on a mobile DSP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed ~5K-parameter model outperforms the strongest competing learned methods on the Samsung S24 Ultra dataset: it beats C4 [66] (5,116K params) by 0.08° in mean angular error (1.84° vs. 1.92° neutral) and by 0.29° on user-preference (1.20° vs. 1.49°), while using ~1000× fewer parameters. It also surpasses C5(tuned-CS) [6] (172K params) by 0.11° mean (1.84° vs. 1.95°) and FFCC with capture info [11] (36.9K params) by 0.47° (1.84° vs. 2.31°). On the Simple Cube++ DSLR dataset (no geolocation), the method with ISO + exposure + SNR stats achieves 1.01° mean, beating C4 (1.16°), C5(tuned-CS) (1.19°), and FFCC (1.38°). The method also dominates all classical statistical baselines (GW, SoG, GE, Max-RGB, wGE, PCA) by 2°+ in mean angular error. In terms of worst-case performance, the method achieves the best worst-25% (4.41°) and worst-5% (9.17°) among all learned methods, though its maximum error (35.42°) is higher than C5(tuned-CS) (16.78°) and C4 (21.55°), suggesting occasional large outliers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the histogram-based color constancy lineage established by Barron (2015) and Barron & Tsai (2017), which represent raw image colors as 2D chromaticity histograms processed by small CNNs, and further builds on Afifi et al.'s cross-camera convolutional color constancy (ICCV 2021) for positional encoding and training strategy. The key conceptual departure is the introduction of a time-capture feature—derived from timestamp, geolocation, ISO, shutter speed, flash status, and noise statistics—as a parallel input branch that is concatenated with the histogram latent representation before a small MLP head. The authors explicitly note that no prior work has used mobile device contextual metadata (timestamp + geolocation) to refine illuminant estimation, positioning this as a new input modality for an otherwise well-studied task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a ~5K-parameter model running in 0.25 ms on a mobile DSP can match or slightly surpass models with 5M+ parameters (e.g., C4) on angular error, which is directly relevant to the latency and power constraints of smartphone camera ISPs. The introduced dataset with contextual metadata and a user-preference ground truth (validated by a user study) addresses a gap in existing benchmarks that lack temporal/geolocation information and only provide neutral white-balance targets. A key limitation is camera-dependence: the model relies on device-specific capture information (ISO, shutter, noise profiles) and cannot generalize to new sensors without retraining or color calibration, which constrains its broader applicability beyond the Samsung S24 Ultra.
