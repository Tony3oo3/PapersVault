## Problem

Existing Video-LLM benchmarks rely on multiple-choice or binary QA evaluation, which fails to capture the aggressive hallucination behavior that emerges in freeform generation tasks like dense video captioning. No prior benchmark systematically measures both hallucinations (incorrect statements) and omissions (missing details) in a freeform setting, leaving a critical gap for assessing whether VideoLLMs are ready for real-world deployment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252427/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes 16 uniformly sampled video frames (or model-specific frame counts up to 64), a freeform captioning prompt, and a human-annotated dense caption (averaging 477 words per video) as ground truth. It produces two normalized percentage scores—ArgusCost-H and ArgusCost-O—computed at the sentence level, where lower values indicate fewer hallucinations or omissions. The evaluation is sentence-granular, averaging ~19 evaluations per video compared to 1–2 in prior QA-based benchmarks.

## Method

ARGUS decomposes each VideoLLM-generated caption into sentences and uses an LLM-as-a-judge (GPT-4o) to perform sentence-level natural language entailment against the human caption, assigning each target sentence a type (summary, visual description, or dynamic action) and a verdict (entailed, contradictory, or undetermined). A base cost matrix penalizes non-entailed sentences and misaligned dynamic actions, while a dynamic programming recurrence finds the optimal source-to-target alignment that minimizes total cost including a temporal ordering penalty (λ=0.1) for inverted event sequences. The final hallucination score is normalized by the maximum possible cost for that video-model pair to ensure fair comparison across models producing captions of varying length. The omission metric is computed identically by reversing the roles of source and target, checking whether each human sentence is entailed by the model caption.

## Results

On ArgusBench (500 videos, avg. 26.3 s), the best-performing Video-LLM, Gemini-2.0-Flash, still produces hallucinated content at a normalized rate of 41% (ArgusCost-H), while the worst model, InternVL2 (4B), reaches 80%. GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini tie at ~44%, and the best open-source model, LLaVA-Next-Video (DPO), achieves 45%. The omission metric (ArgusCost-O) is consistently higher than hallucination across most models (e.g., LLaVA-Next-Video DPO: 85% omission vs. 45% hallucination), with a Pearson correlation of 0.65 between the two metrics. Scaling within model families generally reduces both costs (e.g., SmolVLM2 256M→2B improves hallucination by >15%), except for the InternVL2 family which shows a non-monotonic trend.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARGUS is positioned against four existing video-hallucination benchmarks: HallusionBench (20 videos, ≤4 s, QA), VideoHallucer (948 videos, 85.6 s, QA), VIDHAL (1000 videos, 15.8 s, caption ordering), and EventHallusion (397 videos, 11.2 s, QA). ARGUS outperforms all four in evaluation granularity (19 evaluations per video vs. 1–8) while maintaining a competitive video count (500) and moderate average duration (26.3 s). Unlike the QA-based baselines, ARGUS evaluates freeform dense captioning, capturing both hallucinations and omissions simultaneously—something none of the prior benchmarks do. Among the 23 evaluated Video-LLMs, Gemini-2.0-Flash leads with ArgusCost-H of 41%, narrowly beating GPT-4o (~44%) and LLaVA-Next-Video DPO (45%), while Gemini-2.0-Pro (48%) underperforms its smaller sibling. In the open-source category, Qwen 2.5-VL (7B) and MiniCPM-V-2.6 achieve the best combined hallucination-omission profile, whereas InternVL2 (4B) is the worst at 80% hallucination cost. The paper also demonstrates that QA-based verification does not guarantee generation quality: LLaVA-OV-7B correctly verified the presence of one chameleon in a clip yet generated a caption describing two, illustrating the core gap ARGUS is designed to measure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant QA-based paradigm in video hallucination evaluation (VideoHallucer, EventHallusion, VIDHAL, HallusionBench) by shifting to freeform dense captioning as the evaluation setting. It repurposes natural language inference (sentence-level entailment with an LLM-as-judge) and dynamic programming alignment—techniques borrowed from NLI and sequence alignment literature—into a novel dual-metric framework that simultaneously quantifies hallucination and omission. The LLM-as-judge paradigm from recent evaluation work informs the entailment step, but the specific combination of sentence-type categorization, temporal ordering penalties, and DP-based optimal alignment is new to video captioning evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even the best-in-market VideoLLM (Gemini-2.0-Flash) produces 41% hallucinated content in freeform captioning, while simultaneously omitting ~60% of ground-truth details, underscores a fundamental gap between verification ability and generative reliability that QA benchmarks systematically miss. The dual-metric design is important because measuring hallucination alone is degenerate (a model can trivially minimize it by generating nothing), and the omission metric reveals that models like LLaVA-Next-Video (DPO) achieve low hallucination partly by being overly conservative. Open questions include why the InternVL2 family shows non-monotonic scaling (4B worse than 1B), how to close the verification-generation gap, and whether the LLM-as-judge approach generalizes to longer or more complex videos beyond the 26-second average in ArgusBench.
