## Problem

Existing automatic rigging methods rely on multi-stage regression pipelines that independently predict joint positions, bone connectivity (via greedy MST), and skinning, leading to error accumulation and poor generalization across diverse object categories. Additionally, no large-scale rigging dataset covering heterogeneous categories (humanoids, animals, insects, plants, etc.) with diverse poses existed to train and evaluate such models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252328/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D mesh as input, from which 1,024 surface points are sampled and encoded by a pre-trained point cloud encoder (ShapeLLM). The output is a tree-structured skeleton with k joints (k ranges from 2 to 100), where each joint is represented by its 3D position and its parent joint's position, implicitly encoding bone connectivity. Skinning weights are then computed post-hoc using GeoVoxel. The skeleton is discretized into 6k tokens (3 for parent coordinates + 3 for joint coordinates per joint), with the first three tokens designating the root node.

## Method

ARMO employs a two-stage autoregressive framework. In Stage 1, an Autoregressive Auto-Encoder (ArAE) encodes skeleton joints into a fixed-length latent embedding via a cross-attention joints encoder, then an autoregressive transformer predicts the 6k skeleton tokens sequentially using next-token prediction, jointly learning joint positions and parent-child connectivity in a unified sequence. In Stage 2, a mesh-conditioned latent diffusion model (DDPM) predicts the ArAE latent embedding from point cloud features, enabling conditional skeleton generation without the input-output confusion that plagues direct conditioned autoregressive models. The key insight is representing the skeleton as a tree where each joint token is paired with its parent joint's position, so that the autoregressive sequence inherently encodes connectivity—eliminating the need for separate MST-based connectivity estimation and reducing error propagation. Training uses cross-entropy loss for the ArAE and MSE loss for the diffusion model, with online pose augmentation to improve robustness to input pose variations.

## Results

On the OmniRig test set, ARMO achieves 70.68% IoU, 69.84% Precision, and 71.94% Recall for joint estimation, substantially outperforming both baselines. The Chamfer Distance metrics show particularly large gains: CD-J2J drops to 3.88% (vs. 6.44% for RigNet), CD-J2B to 3.15% (vs. 5.85%), and CD-B2B to 2.95% (vs. 5.06%), representing a 41.7% relative improvement in bone-to-bone distance over RigNet. The IoU improvement of 13.2% over RigNet confirms that jointly predicting joint positions and connectivity via the autoregressive model yields more accurate and coherent skeletons than multi-stage regression.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ARMO is compared against two baselines: Pinocchio (a classic optimization-based template-fitting method) and RigNet (a state-of-the-art multi-stage regression network retrained on OmniRig for fair comparison). ARMO decisively outperforms both on every metric. Against RigNet, ARMO improves IoU by 9.33 percentage points (61.35% → 70.68%), reduces CD-J2J by 39.7% (6.44% → 3.88%), and cuts CD-B2B by 41.7% (5.06% → 2.95%), demonstrating that the autoregressive joint prediction of positions and connectivity avoids the error accumulation inherent in RigNet's sequential pipeline (position prediction → MST connectivity → skinning). Against Pinocchio, the gap is even wider (IoU 36.47% vs. 70.68%), largely because Pinocchio relies on predefined template skeletons and cannot generalize to non-humanoid categories present in OmniRig. Qualitatively, RigNet fails to produce reasonable connectivity for complex shapes and struggles with non-standard poses, while ARMO handles diverse categories (insects, marine creatures, plants) and complex poses more robustly thanks to online pose augmentation during training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant multi-stage regression paradigm in automatic rigging (RigNet, TARig), where joint positions, bone connectivity, and skinning are predicted by separate modules and connectivity is estimated via greedy algorithms like MST. Instead, it adopts the autoregressive auto-encoder architecture from EdgeRunner [31] (originally designed for mesh generation) and repurposes it to jointly predict joint positions and parent-joint positions as a unified token sequence, with a mesh-conditioned latent diffusion model providing the conditioning signal. The OmniRig dataset extends prior rigging benchmarks (ModelResource, Mixamo) from a few thousand humanoid-centric models to nearly 80k meshes spanning animals, insects, plants, and other categories, addressing a data scarcity that had limited generalization studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The OmniRig dataset is a significant practical resource for the rigging community, being the first to cover diverse non-humanoid categories at scale, and will likely become a standard benchmark for evaluating generalization beyond humanoids. The autoregressive formulation eliminates the error accumulation inherent in multi-stage pipelines, and the 41.7% improvement in bone-to-bone Chamfer distance over RigNet validates this architectural choice. However, the paper acknowledges that node density is fixed and sequence-level consistency remains an open problem, limiting immediate use in animation pipelines where temporal coherence is critical. Broader questions remain about whether the autoregressive approach scales to even more complex topologies (e.g., multi-limb creatures) and whether the diffusion conditioning can be replaced by more efficient alternatives.
