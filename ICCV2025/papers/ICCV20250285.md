## Problem

Existing driver attention prediction methods reduce the task to pixel-space regression, producing spatial heatmaps that capture only where drivers look. They fail to model the semantic content of attended regions (what) or the cognitive motivations behind attention allocation (why), such as traffic rule compliance, safety perception, or goal-driven navigation, leaving a critical gap in understanding task-driven visual attention in driving.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250285/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a driving scene RGB image and structured contextual information (weather, road conditions, driving behavior) as input. It simultaneously produces three outputs: a full-resolution pixel-wise attention map (H×W) generated by a dedicated decoder, and two autoregressive text sequences from the LLM — semantic descriptions of each attended region (what, averaging 5.40 words) and causal explanations for attention allocation (why, averaging 24.32 words). The spatial and textual outputs are coupled through a special [ATTN] token whose embedding is projected into the decoder space, ensuring the attention map is conditioned on the LLM's cognitive reasoning rather than being an independent regression head.

## Method

LLada unifies a CLIP-ViT-L visual encoder (with a linear projector), a Vicuna-7B LLM, a novel [ATTN] token, and a cognitive-aware attention decoder into a single end-to-end architecture. The visual and contextual tokens are concatenated with a prompt and fed into the LLM, which autoregressively generates semantic (what) and causal (why) explanations while emitting the [ATTN] token; this token's embedding is projected via an MLP and used as the query in a cross-attention operation over the visual features, whose output is refined by five 3×3 convolutions and bilinearly upsampled to produce the attention map (where). Training uses a weighted sum of BCE + KL divergence losses for the attention map and cross-entropy losses for the what and why text, with LoRA applied to the LLM, the visual encoder kept frozen, and the decoder trained from scratch. The supporting W³DA dataset (69,980 key samples from 3,548 scenes) is built by integrating four existing benchmarks and annotating them with a semi-automatic pipeline that selects key frames via CLIP semantic similarity and KL-divergence thresholds, generates preliminary what/why labels using Qwen-VL-Max with chain-of-thought prompting, and validates them with human experts.

## Results

On the W³DA dataset, LLada achieves the best attention map prediction across all three driving scenarios, with KLdiv of 1.219 (Normal), 1.230 (Safety-Critical), and 1.927 (Accident), outperforming the second-best method ERFNet by 38.40%, 22.79%, and 11.65% respectively. For textual explanation generation, LLada achieves CIDEr-R scores of 0.963, 1.233, and 1.002 across the three scenarios, substantially surpassing GazeXplain (0.422, 0.554, 0.656). When evaluated on conventional datasets (DR(eye)VE, BDDA, DADA-2000) without domain-specific training, LLada still outperforms the best domain-specific models by 29.81%, 20.69%, and 5.49% on KLdiv, respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LLada outperforms all 10 single-task attention prediction baselines (ITTI, GBVS, DeepGaze I/IIE, MLNet, CDNN, FBNet, ConvNeXt, ERFNet) on every metric across all three driving scenarios, with the largest margin over ERFNet (the strongest single-task method) on KLdiv: 38.40% improvement in normal driving and 22.79% in safety-critical situations. Against GazeXplain [9], the only other multi-task method that jointly predicts attention maps and textual explanations, LLada achieves substantially better performance on both spatial metrics (e.g., KLdiv 1.219 vs. 2.578 in normal driving) and textual metrics (e.g., CIDEr-R 0.963 vs. 0.422 in normal driving), confirming that the unified LLM-driven architecture is more effective than GazeXplain's approach. For cross-domain generalization, LLada trained solely on W³DA surpasses the best domain-specific models on DR(eye)VE (KLdiv 1.04 vs. SCAFNet's 1.35), BDDA (1.16 vs. FBLNet's 1.40), and DADA-2000 (1.82 vs. FBLNet's 1.92), demonstrating that cross-domain training on the unified dataset yields competitive or superior performance without domain-specific fine-tuning. The two-stage baselines (conventional predictor + LLaVA for explanation) consistently underperform LLada on textual generation, with the best two-stage method (CDNN+LLaVA) achieving only CIDEr-R 0.283–0.312 versus LLada's 0.963–1.233, highlighting the advantage of end-to-end joint optimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the LLaVA architecture (CLIP-ViT-L visual encoder + Vicuna-7B LLM + linear projector) for driver attention prediction, introducing a special [ATTN] token and a cognitive-aware attention decoder to bridge the language model's reasoning with pixel-level spatial output. It departs from the long line of driver attention prediction work (ITTI, GBVS, DeepGaze, FBLNet, etc.) that performs pure pixel-space regression, and extends GazeXplain's attention-plus-explanation framework by adding a causal reasoning dimension (why) that prior methods lacked. The annotation pipeline leverages Qwen-VL-Max with chain-of-thought prompting, positioning the work at the intersection of MLLM-based scene understanding and task-driven attention modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The W³DA dataset (69,980 key samples across normal, safety-critical, and accident scenarios) is the first to provide joint spatial, semantic, and causal annotations for driver attention, filling a clear gap that limits interpretability in autonomous driving and cognitive science. The cross-domain generalization result—outperforming domain-specific models on DR(eye)VE, BDDA, and DADA while trained on a single unified dataset—suggests that explainable attention modeling may be more transferable than task-specific regression. Open questions include whether the generated 'why' explanations reflect genuine causal mechanisms or post-hoc rationalization, whether the MLLM-based approach scales to real-time deployment, and whether the annotation pipeline's reliance on Qwen-VL-Max introduces systematic biases that propagate into the dataset.
