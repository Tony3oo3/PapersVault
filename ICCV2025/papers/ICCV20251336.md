## Problem

General-purpose bimanual manipulation is bottlenecked by the extreme cost of collecting bimanual teleoperation demonstrations, which require specialized hardware and fine-grained calibration. Meanwhile, pretrained unimanual policies have already achieved strong cross-task generalizability through scaled data and model parameters, yet no method effectively transfers this knowledge to bimanual settings without sacrificing generalization. The paper asks: can a plug-and-play module convert any pretrained unimanual policy into a generalizable bimanual policy using only a few bimanual demonstrations?

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB-D images converted to a 3D voxel grid (256×256 resolution from 6 cameras in simulation; 640×480 from a single front RGB-D camera in real-world), robot proprioception, and a natural language task instruction. It outputs per-arm end-effector actions at each timestep comprising translation, rotation, gripper openness, and a collision-avoidance flag for the motion planner. A key design detail is that the bimanual voxel observation is decomposed into two per-arm subspaces via learned soft masks before being fed to two separate instances of the pretrained unimanual policy, so each arm's visual input approximates the unimanual pretraining distribution.

## Method

AnyBimanual introduces two lightweight, model-agnostic modules that wrap around any pretrained unimanual policy. The skill manager is a multi-modal transformer that, given the bimanual visual, language, and proprioceptive state, predicts a sparse linear combination of K learned skill primitives plus a task-oriented compensation vector for each arm's language embedding; sparsity is enforced via L1 and L2,1 regularization to encourage orthogonal, independently interpretable skill representations. The visual aligner predicts two spatial soft masks over the voxel grid that partition the bimanual workspace into per-arm subspaces, regularized by a Jensen-Shannon divergence term to enforce mutual exclusivity, thereby aligning each arm's visual input with the unimanual pretraining distribution. The full system is trained end-to-end with a behavior-cloning cross-entropy loss on 20–100 bimanual demonstrations per task plus the two regularization terms, while the pretrained unimanual policy (e.g., PerAct, RVT) serves as the action-decoding backbone. This design eliminates the need for explicit role assignment (stabilizer/actor) or handcrafted atomic movements, enabling flexible, task-adaptive coordination patterns.

## Results

On the 12-task RLBench2 benchmark (100 demos per task, 100 episodes each), PerAct+AnyBimanual achieves a 32.00% average success rate, outperforming the previous SOTA PerAct2 (14.67%) by 17.33 percentage points. With only 20 demonstrations per task, the method still reaches 19.25% average, a 10.50-point margin over PerAct2 (8.75%). The plug-and-play framework also boosts PerAct-LF from 10.58% to 14.75% (+39.41% relative) and RVT-LF from 4.92% to 8.50% (+72.76% relative) on average. On real-world tasks (9 tasks, 30 demos each), the multi-task model achieves 84.62% average success rate across 65 episodes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is against PerAct2 [Grotz et al., CoRL 2024], the state-of-the-art bimanual method on RLBench2. AnyBimanual (built on PerAct) achieves 32.00% average success rate versus PerAct2's 14.67%, a 17.33-point absolute gain; even PerAct2 augmented with pretraining (20.33%) falls well short. Against the leader-follower baselines, PerAct-LF [Shridhar et al., CoRL 2023] scores 10.58% and RVT-LF [Goyal et al., CoRL 2023] scores 4.92%; applying AnyBimanual to these same backbones lifts them to 14.75% and 8.17% respectively, confirming the framework's model-agnostic plug-and-play nature. The largest per-task gains appear on long-horizon (Put in Fridge: 26% vs 16%), multi-variation (Press Buttons: 73% vs 41%), and synchronous (Straighten Rope: 24% vs 8%) tasks, while the simple Lift Ball task sees a smaller relative improvement (36% vs 4%), suggesting the added input-processing overhead is most noticeable on trivially short tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of unimanual policy learning (PerAct, RVT) and bimanual manipulation (PerAct2), departing from prior bimanual approaches that rely on fixed role assignments (stabilizing vs. acting arms) or handcrafted parameterized atomic movements. Rather than training a bimanual policy from scratch with expensive dual-arm teleoperation data, AnyBimanual repurposes pretrained unimanual policies as frozen backbones and introduces a lightweight skill manager (sparse linear combination of skill primitives over language embeddings) and a visual aligner (JS-divergence-regularized soft masks over voxel space) to bridge the unimanual-to-bimanual gap. Conceptually, it extends the skill-based learning literature by discovering skills unsupervised from the pretrained policy's own representation space rather than from hand-designed primitives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The plug-and-play transfer of any pretrained unimanual policy to bimanual manipulation with only 20–100 demonstrations per task directly addresses the data bottleneck that has limited generalizable bimanual agents, and the 17.33% average improvement over PerAct2 on RLBench2 plus 84.62% real-world success rate demonstrate practical viability. However, the approach still depends on access to a strong pretrained unimanual policy and a low-level motion planner (RRT-Connect), and the failure on precise-rotation tasks (Rotate Toothbrush at 20%) highlights that the transfer is not uniform across all motion types. Open questions include whether the skill discovery generalizes beyond two arms, whether the JS-divergence mutual-exclusivity prior holds for tasks where both arms must attend to the same region, and whether the method scales to higher-DoF dexterous hands.
