## Problem

Selecting the optimal PEFT technique for a downstream task requires distinguishing subtle embedding variations introduced by different fine-tuning strategies on a shared backbone, a problem fundamentally different from traditional transferability estimation which ranks distinct architectures. Existing TE metrics (LogME, NLEEP, NCTI, SFDA, GBC) assume simpler, lower-dimensional embedding structures and fail to capture the high geometric complexity of PEFT embeddings, which require 30–50% of principal components to retain 95% variance compared to ~7% for CNNs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes L2-normalized feature embeddings (N×d) extracted from each of L PEFT models on a target dataset (capped at 10,000 samples) along with their class labels. It outputs a single scalar PEFT selection score per technique, from which a ranking is derived. The score is computed relative to a frozen backbone baseline, so the method requires both PEFT-model embeddings and backbone embeddings for the same target dataset.

## Method

PEFTDiff constructs a k-NN sparse affinity graph (k=20) over target embeddings using a Gaussian RBF kernel (σ=1), then builds a row-stochastic transition matrix P = D⁻¹K. Diffusion distances are approximated via the eigenvalue decomposition of P^t (t=10), capturing multi-step random-walk connectivity rather than direct Euclidean separation. The PEFT selection score combines two components: the change in intra-class diffusion compactness (average diffusion distance from points to their class mean) and the change in inter-class diffusion separability (average diffusion distance between class means), each measured relative to the frozen backbone. A negative intra-class change (tighter clusters) and a positive inter-class change (greater separation) both contribute positively to the final score after normalization. This graph-based random-walk formulation avoids the linear and low-dimensional assumptions that cause conventional TE metrics to fail on the complex, nonlinear geometry of PEFT embeddings.

## Results

On the VTAB-1k benchmark (19 datasets), the proposed diffusion-based PEFT selection metric achieves an overall weighted Kendall's τ_w of 0.517, substantially outperforming the strongest baseline LogME (0.306) by 68.95%. On five FGVC datasets (CUB-200-2011, NABirds, Oxford Flowers, Stanford Dogs, Stanford Cars), the method reaches an average τ_w of 0.462 versus 0.311 for GBC and 0.309 for LogME. The method also achieves a 199.77× speed-up over exhaustive PEFT fine-tuning (55.44 min vs. 11,074.5 min total).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baselines on the overall VTAB-1k average (0.517 vs. LogME 0.306, GBC 0.220, SFDA 0.215, NCTI 0.207, NLEEP 0.037). On the Natural category (7 datasets), the method wins every individual dataset, with the largest margins on CIFAR (0.866 vs. LogME −0.099) and SVHN (0.884 vs. LogME 0.469). On Specialized (4 datasets), it wins 3 of 4, losing only on RESISC45 where LogME reaches 0.787 versus 0.396. On Structured (8 datasets), the method is more competitive but less dominant—winning 3 of 8 individual datasets (Clevr Dist, DSpL, SUNelev) while trailing GBC on Clevr Count (0.230 vs. 0.761) and NLEEP on DomainNet Lab (0.471 vs. 0.702); the paper attributes this to structured labels (e.g., distance estimation) that do not align well with visual feature geometry. On FGVC datasets, the method leads on 3 of 5 (CUB 0.515, NABirds 0.488, Dogs 0.630) but trails GBC on Stanford Cars (0.278 vs. 0.221—actually wins) and LogME on Flowers (0.401 vs. 0.329—wins), confirming consistent gains in fine-grained settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes diffusion maps (Coifman & Lafon, 2006), originally developed for manifold learning, as a transferability metric specifically for ranking PEFT techniques applied to a shared transformer backbone. It departs from the dominant TE paradigm (LogME, NLEEP, NCTI, SFDA, GBC), which was designed to compare fundamentally different architectures (e.g., ResNet vs. VGG), by arguing that PEFT embeddings exhibit a qualitatively different geometric complexity—requiring 30–50% of principal components to retain 95% variance versus ~7% for CNNs—that invalidates the low-dimensional, well-clustered assumptions underlying those metrics. The method inherits the graph-based, random-walk framework of diffusion maps and the Bayesian graph intuition of LogME, but applies them to the narrower problem of distinguishing subtle embedding perturbations from a frozen backbone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With nine or more PEFT techniques now in active use, a fast selection metric that avoids exhaustive fine-tuning (here, ~200× speedup) has clear practical value for practitioners working under compute constraints. The PCA-based analysis of embedding complexity provides a useful diagnostic for understanding *why* standard TE metrics fail on PEFT variants, which may inform future metric design. However, the method's weaker performance on structured datasets (where labels encode physical quantities rather than visual semantics) and its higher computational cost relative to linear baselines (LogME, SFDA) limit its universality. Open questions include extending the framework to detection/segmentation tasks and jointly selecting backbone–PEFT pairs rather than ranking PEFTs in isolation.
