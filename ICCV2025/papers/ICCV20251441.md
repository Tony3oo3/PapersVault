## Problem

Existing dynamic urban scene reconstruction methods either depend on high-precision object pose annotations (bounding boxes) that are error-prone and limit large-scale deployment, or use self-supervised trajectory modeling (e.g., periodic vibration, spatio-temporal decomposition) that lacks precision and temporal consistency. This paper addresses the gap by introducing learnable Bezier curves that explicitly model dynamic object trajectories while automatically correcting annotation errors.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251441/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images at 10 Hz, LiDAR point clouds (projected to camera plane for sparse depth supervision), 3D bounding box annotations (used only for grouping dynamic primitives and extracting masks via Grounded-SAM, not for trajectory definition), and camera poses with timestamps. Beyond standard novel-view RGB rendering, it outputs a depth map, opacity map, velocity map, and a cleanly separated static/dynamic scene representation that enables downstream editing such as removing individual dynamic instances. A high-resolution cube map provides the sky background, composited with the Gaussian rendering via the opacity map.

## Method

BezierGS decomposes the scene into static 3D Gaussian primitives (standard 3DGS) and dynamic Gaussian primitives whose positions are parameterized as the sum of an object-center Bezier curve γ(t,g) and a per-primitive offset Bezier curve δ(t), both learnable cubic curves. A third Bezier curve models the non-uniform time-to-parameter mapping t=f(τ,g) to capture varying object speeds. Three key losses enforce quality: an inter-curve consistency loss constrains the offset magnitude to remain near its endpoint average, preventing individual primitives from drifting; a dynamic rendering loss (RGB + opacity) ensures dynamic regions are rendered exclusively by dynamic primitives, achieving clean static/dynamic separation; and a velocity loss penalizes rendered velocity outside the dynamic mask, confining motion to the correct objects. This explicit curve-based trajectory representation eliminates dependence on bounding-box accuracy while preserving temporal coherence across frames.

## Results

On Waymo, BezierGS achieves 31.51 PSNR and 28.51 Dyn-PSNR for novel view synthesis, surpassing the best prior method (PVG) by 1.87 dB in PSNR and 2.66 dB in Dyn-PSNR. On nuPlan, where bounding box annotations are notably inaccurate, BezierGS reaches 29.42 PSNR and 25.12 Dyn-PSNR for novel view synthesis, outperforming the next-best method (DeformableGS) by 3.04 dB in PSNR and 1.22 dB in Dyn-PSNR. For image reconstruction on Waymo, BezierGS attains 33.98 PSNR and 0.934 SSIM, the best across all metrics. The method also achieves the lowest LPIPS (0.092 on Waymo NVS, 0.133 on nuPlan NVS), indicating superior perceptual quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BezierGS outperforms all five baselines across every metric on both datasets. Against bounding-box-based methods (HUGS, Street Gaussians, OmniRe), the advantage is most pronounced on nuPlan, where annotation errors severely degrade their performance: BezierGS gains 3.04 dB PSNR over DeformableGS and 5.65 dB over HUGS in novel view synthesis. Among self-supervised trajectory methods, PVG achieves competitive reconstruction PSNR on Waymo (32.61) but suffers from poor perceptual quality (LPIPS 0.159 vs. 0.077 for BezierGS) and weak dynamic-region fidelity (Dyn-PSNR 29.32 vs. 32.39). DeformableGS, which uses an MLP for trajectory modeling, is the closest competitor on nuPlan reconstruction (28.10 PSNR) but still trails BezierGS by 2.64 dB. The ablation further confirms that replacing Bezier curves with MLP (DeformableGS-style) or sinusoidal (PVG-style) trajectories drops Dyn-PSNR by 3.73 and 2.24 dB respectively, validating the explicit curve representation.

## Relation to Prior Work

BezierGS builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) for scene representation but departs from two dominant paradigms for handling dynamic objects in driving scenes: (1) bounding-box-based methods (Street Gaussians, HUGS, OmniRe) that rigidly transform canonical-space Gaussians using annotated poses, and (2) self-supervised trajectory methods (S³ Gaussian, PVG) that learn motion implicitly via spatio-temporal networks or periodic vibration stitching. The key conceptual move is replacing both with explicit, learnable cubic Bezier curves that parameterize each Gaussian primitive's trajectory, combined with a time-to-Bezier mapping to capture non-uniform velocity. This positions the work as a middle ground: more structured and interpretable than MLP-based deformation, yet more flexible than fixed rigid transforms, while the inter-curve consistency and velocity losses enforce geometric coherence that neither prior family provides.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in decoupling dynamic scene reconstruction from the quality of bounding-box annotations, which is a persistent bottleneck for large-scale autonomous driving simulation. The nuPlan results—where bounding-box methods degrade sharply (HUGS drops to 24.16 PSNR) while BezierGS maintains 30.74—demonstrate that this decoupling is not merely theoretical but yields substantial real-world gains on datasets with known annotation noise. Open questions include whether cubic Bezier curves suffice for highly non-rigid or multi-modal trajectories (e.g., pedestrians, cyclists with abrupt direction changes), how the method scales to sequences longer than the tested 12/6 sequences, and whether the explicit curve representation can be extended to enable physically consistent scene editing beyond simple object removal.
