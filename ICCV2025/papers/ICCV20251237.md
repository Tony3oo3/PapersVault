## Problem

Existing OOD robustness benchmarks for image classifiers rely on binary or categorical nuisance shifts (e.g., snow present vs. absent), which contradicts how distribution shifts actually manifest in the real world as gradual, continuous changes. While diffusion models have recently been used to generate realistic benchmark images, prior generative approaches are restricted to binary shifts and cannot capture the varying severity levels that determine whether a model degrades gracefully or fails abruptly. This paper addresses the gap by enabling continuous, realistic nuisance shifts at multiple severity levels for systematic robustness evaluation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251237/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes an ImageNet class label, a nuisance shift type (one of 14 style or weather shifts), and a continuous shift scale s as inputs, and produces generated images with the specified shift applied at that severity. Images are generated with SD2.0 using class-specific LoRA adapters activated only for the last 75% of diffusion noise steps, yielding 192,168 filtered images across 100 classes, 14 shifts, and 5 discrete severity levels (though the LoRA sliders support fully continuous scales). The evaluation outputs include per-scale accuracy drops, aggregated relative corruption errors, and failure point distributions (the first scale at which each image is misclassified).

## Method

The core mechanism is a set of ImageNet class-conditional LoRA concept sliders trained to capture the direction between a class concept and the same class under a specific nuisance shift. Each adapter is trained per (class, shift) pair using an MSE denoising objective with Tweedie's formula, learning low-rank matrices that, when scaled by a continuous parameter s (θ* = θ + s·θ_LoRA), progressively modulate the shift severity while preserving semantic structure. To close the distribution gap between Stable Diffusion and ImageNet, textual inversion is applied to learn IN* embeddings that make generated images closer to the ImageNet distribution (reducing FID from 33.8 to 27.1). A four-filter OOC mechanism (two CLIP text-alignment checks and two image-feature similarity checks using CLIP and DINOv2) removes out-of-class samples, achieving 88% filter accuracy versus 65% for the prior CLIP-only baseline. The benchmark is then used to evaluate 40+ classifiers along architecture (CNN, ViT, VMamba), model size, and pre-training paradigm axes, revealing that model rankings change across shift scales and that self-supervised pre-training yields stronger OOD robustness than supervised pre-training on larger datasets.

## Results

On CNS-Bench (14 shifts × 5 scales, 192,168 images), the average relative corruption error (rCE, lower is better) reveals that VMamba (0.574) is the most robust architecture, followed by DeiT3 (0.610), ConvNeXt (0.686), ResNet-152 (0.790), and ViT (0.926). Among pre-training paradigms with the same ViT-B/16 backbone, DINOv1 self-supervised pre-training achieves the best rCE (0.636), outperforming supervised IN1k (0.926) and even supervised IN21k (0.722). The proposed OOC filter achieves 88% accuracy (TPR 0.88, FPR 0.12) versus 65% for CLIP-based filtering (TPR 0.90, FPR 0.36), and the IN* textual-inversion distribution reduces FID to ImageNet from 33.8 to 27.1 while improving ResNet-50 accuracy from 0.68 to 0.74.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP-based text-alignment filtering (from LANCE [46] and Dataset Interfaces [60]), the proposed four-filter OOC mechanism dramatically reduces the false positive rate from 0.36 to 0.12 while maintaining a comparable true positive rate (0.88 vs. 0.90), yielding a 23-point accuracy improvement (0.88 vs. 0.65). Compared to the real-world OOD-CV benchmark [70], a ResNet-50 trained on OOD-CV data shows accuracy around 95% up to nuisance scale 1.5 on the generated images, but the OOD-CV accuracy (horizontal lines in Fig. 5) is consistently lower, indicating that OOD-CV contains confounding factors (image quality, size, noise) beyond the targeted nuisance. Across architectures, VMamba outperforms both modern CNNs (ConvNeXt, 0.686) and transformers (ViT, 0.926) by 0.112 and 0.352 rCE respectively, while DeiT3 with modern training recipes (0.610) surpasses ConvNeXt (0.686) despite ConvNeXt having higher in-distribution accuracy. For pre-training, self-supervised DINOv1 (0.636) beats supervised IN21k (0.722) by 0.086 rCE, showing that the learning objective matters more than raw data volume for OOD robustness. A key finding is that model rankings change across shift scales (e.g., ViT outperforms others at low painting-style scale but degrades at high scale), a phenomenon invisible to binary-shift benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the T2I-diffusion-based benchmarking line (Lance, Dataset Interfaces, ImageNet-D) by replacing their binary nuisance shifts with continuous, class-specific LoRA concept sliders, building directly on Gandikota et al.'s concept-slider framework and the IN* textual-inversion distribution from Vendrow et al. It departs from prior generative benchmarks in two key ways: (1) shifts are modulated by a continuous scale parameter rather than a binary prompt toggle, and (2) a novel multi-signal OOC filter (combining CLIP text alignment with DINOv2 image-feature similarity) replaces the CLIP-only filtering used in earlier works. The robustness evaluation itself follows the ImageNet-C/ImageNet-R/OOD-CV tradition of measuring accuracy drops under controlled interventions, but is the first to do so with realistic, continuously graded shifts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CNS-Bench fills a clear gap: prior generative benchmarks could only ask 'does the model fail under snow?' while real deployment requires 'how does performance degrade as snow intensifies?' The finding that model rankings reorder across shift severities (e.g., ViT beats CNNs at low painting-style shifts but loses at high shifts) provides concrete evidence that single-metric robustness summaries are insufficient, encouraging the community to report multi-scale accuracy curves. Limitations include residual CLIP training biases that can confound shift attribution, the restriction to 100 ImageNet classes for generation, and the inherent real-vs-synthetic distribution gap. Open questions include whether the ranking-instability phenomenon persists at full ImageNet scale and whether next-generation generative models will reduce the confounders that currently limit causal attribution.
