## Problem

Existing SIDD methods either rely on manually defined blur kernels for defocus map estimation (two-stage approaches) or assume that defocus blur can be modeled by a convolutional kernel whose inverse is learned (end-to-end DNN approaches). These assumptions may not hold for real-world defocus blur, which arises from the physical intermingling of illuminated blobs of varying sizes on the film plane. The paper asks whether SIDD can be performed by directly modeling and adjusting these illuminated blobs rather than learning inverse kernels.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251819/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single defocused RGB image as input and produces a deblurred all-in-focus image. Additionally, a pre-trained DPT depth estimator provides a per-pixel depth map that serves as auxiliary guidance for regularizing Gaussian blob sizes. Unlike typical feedforward deblurring networks, the method operates per-image: a U-Net initializer generates a variable number of 2D Gaussian blobs (N varies per image), which are then optimized for 600 iterations before a single forward pass of the blob deblurrer produces the final output.

## Method

The core idea is to represent a defocused image as a stack of 2D Gaussian blobs (each with position, covariance, color, and opacity) that model the illuminated blobs formed on the film plane, then deblur by shrinking and re-shaping these blobs. A differentiable rasterizer (modified from 3D GS tile rasterizer, implemented in CUDA) renders the image from the Gaussian stack, enabling end-to-end optimization. A U-Net initializer provides high-quality initial Gaussian parameters in one step, after which the parameters are refined via 600 Adam iterations minimizing an MSE+SSIM+depth-regularization loss. The blob deblurrer is a 3-block Transformer that takes all Gaussian parameters and outputs per-blob adjustments to rotation, scaling (clamped to reduce size), and opacity in a single forward pass. A depth-based regularization loss (L_reg) enforces the physical relationship between blob diameter and relative depth (D ∝ |1 − d₁/d₂|), using DPT-predicted depths to constrain the scaling factors s₁ and s₂, improving consistency of blob sizes across the image.

## Results

On the DPDD dataset, the full method achieves PSNR 26.651, SSIM 0.835, and LPIPS 0.168, outperforming the strongest baseline GGKMNet (26.272 / 0.810 / 0.215) by 0.38 dB in PSNR and 0.048 in SSIM. On LFDOF, the method reaches PSNR 30.885, SSIM 0.892, and LPIPS 0.123, surpassing GGKMNet (30.552 / 0.886 / 0.154) by 0.33 dB in PSNR. Even without the depth-based regularization loss, the method already leads all baselines on both datasets (e.g., 26.431 PSNR on DPDD vs. 26.272 for GGKMNet). On the generalization datasets RTF and RealDOF, the method trained on DPDD achieves 28.585 PSNR on RTF, beating the best baseline GGKMNet (26.012) by over 2.5 dB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against 12 baselines spanning two-stage, kernel-learning, and general restoration approaches. On both DPDD and LFDOF, the proposed method achieves the best PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS across all metrics, beating the strongest kernel-mixture baselines (GGKMNet, NRKNet, INIKNet) by 0.33–0.60 dB in PSNR and 0.025–0.048 in SSIM. The LPIPS gap is particularly notable: 0.168 vs. 0.215 (GGKMNet) on DPDD, a 22% relative improvement in perceptual quality. On the generalization test (Table 2), the advantage widens substantially: when trained on DPDD and tested on RTF, the method scores 28.585 PSNR versus 26.012 for GGKMNet, a 2.57 dB margin, indicating that the blob-based representation generalizes far better than learned convolutional inverse kernels. The depth-based regularization loss provides a consistent but modest boost of ~0.2 dB PSNR over the no-depth variant on both training datasets, confirming its role as a useful but not essential component.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant kernel-based paradigm in SIDD (where SOTA methods like INIKNet, NRKNet, and GGKMNet learn convolutional or inverse kernels) by instead modeling the physical formation of defocus blur as the blending of illuminated blobs on the film plane. It adapts the 2D Gaussian blob representation from GaussianImage [63] and the differentiable rasterization pipeline from 3D Gaussian Splatting [19], while drawing the core deblurring strategy—adjusting Gaussian parameters to reduce blur—from Deblurring 3D Gaussian Splatting [22]. The key conceptual shift is replacing the assumption that defocus blur is a spatially-varying convolution with a representation where each blob's size directly encodes the local defocus amount, and deblurring becomes shrinking those blobs rather than applying a learned inverse kernel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By grounding deblurring in the physical optics of how defocus blur forms, this approach avoids the potentially invalid assumption that real-world defocus blur is well-modeled by a convolutional kernel, which is particularly relevant for irregularly shaped or large-area blurs. However, the per-image optimization (600 Adam iterations for Gaussian fitting) introduces a practical speed cost compared to single-pass DNN methods, and the method's reliance on a pre-trained depth estimator (DPT) for regularization adds a dependency whose quality may vary across domains. Open questions include whether the per-image fitting can be amortized or made faster, whether the Gaussian blob representation adequately captures non-circular aperture shapes (e.g., polygonal bokeh), and how the approach scales to very high-resolution or video deblurring settings.
