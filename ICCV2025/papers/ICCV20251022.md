## Problem

Simulating realistic dynamic responses of 3D objects under arbitrary forces requires accurate physical properties (density, Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio), but existing methods either demand manual parameter assignment or rely on computationally expensive video diffusion models that are slow (hours), limited to non-rigid objects, and often violate physical laws. No prior approach can efficiently predict these properties in a zero-shot manner from visual data alone while simulating entire multi-object scenes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251022/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained 3DGS scene (from multi-view images and camera poses) and arbitrary external forces as input, producing a full 4D animation of the scene with per-point physical properties. The MLLM-P3 module consumes a single canonical-view render of each segmented object plus a BLIP-generated text description, while the MPDP network operates on the object's 3D Gaussian point cloud positions. Simulation runs on a 64³ grid with 768 sub-steps per frame interval (~4.34×10⁻⁵ s each), and only a sparse set of 'driving particles' is simulated rather than all Gaussians.

## Method

PhysSplat decomposes the problem into three stages. First, 3D open-vocabulary segmentation uses RAM, Grounding DINO, and SAM to automatically label objects in 2D, then lifts masks to 3D via differentiable radiance field rendering with learnable semantic attributes per Gaussian. Second, MLLM-P3 predicts mean physical properties zero-shot: BLIP generates a text description, GPT-4V proposes K candidate materials, CLIP similarity selects the best match to reduce hallucination, and a second GPT-4V call returns density, Young's modulus, and Poisson's ratio. Third, the MPDP network (trained on Physics3D-normalized outputs) predicts a geometry-conditioned probability distribution over the point cloud, which is scaled element-wise by the MLLM mean; PGAS then adaptively adjusts Poisson disk sampling radius using Young's modulus and local curvature to place sparse driving particles, and MLS-MPM simulates only these particles while rendering interpolates from neighbors.

## Results

On the PhysDreamer benchmark, PhysSplat achieves the highest Realism Score (RS 4.66) and Aesthetic Score (AS 7.89), outperforming Physics3D (RS 4.62, AS 7.83) and PhysDreamer (RS 4.54, AS 7.71). On the synthesized dataset, PhysSplat matches Physics3D on RS (5.10) while surpassing it on AS (8.20 vs. 8.01). Critically, PhysSplat completes inference in 2 minutes on a single GPU, compared to 1.5 hours for Physics3D and DreamPhysics — a ~45× speedup — while DreamGaussian4D takes 0.1 hours but scores lower on both metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PhysSplat outperforms all six baselines on both RS and AS across every evaluated dataset. Against Physics3D [21] (the strongest video-diffusion-based competitor), PhysSplat wins on RS by 0.04 on PhysDreamer (4.66 vs. 4.62) and ties on RS for the synthesized set (5.10) while gaining 0.19 on AS (8.20 vs. 8.01), all at 45× lower inference cost (2 min vs. 1.5 h). Against DreamPhysics [13], PhysSplat surpasses it on both RS (5.10 vs. 5.05) and AS (8.20 vs. 7.92) on the synthesized dataset with the same 45× speed advantage. Against PhysDreamer [47], PhysSplat leads by 0.12 RS and 0.18 AS on PhysDreamer, and the authors note PhysDreamer's video-diffusion supervision produces less physically plausible results. Against DreamGaussian4D [30], PhysSplat gains 0.09 RS and 0.61 AS on PhysDreamer and 0.12 RS and 1.39 AS on the synthesized set, while being 3× faster (2 min vs. 0.1 h). Against Feature Splatting [27], which uses predefined material categories, PhysSplat achieves substantially higher scores (e.g., RS 4.96 vs. 4.79, AS 7.99 vs. 7.10 on open-world), demonstrating the advantage of per-object MLLM-based property estimation over fixed material assignments. Against PhysGaussian [42], which requires manual physical parameter setup, PhysSplat leads by 0.16 RS and 0.33 AS on PhysDreamer, confirming the benefit of automatic zero-shot property prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

PhysSplat sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) physics-integrated 3D representations (PhysGaussian, PAC-NeRF, PIE-NeRF, Spring-Gaus), which require either manual parameter setup or multi-view video supervision; (2) video-diffusion-based physical property estimation (PhysDreamer, Physics3D, DreamPhysics), which is computationally expensive and limited to non-rigid objects; and (3) MLLM-based visual reasoning (GPT-4V, BLIP, CLIP). The key departure is replacing iterative video-diffusion priors with a single zero-shot MLLM query for mean physical property estimation, then reformulating the per-point property assignment as a geometry-conditioned probability distribution (MPDP) rather than a regression task. The PGAS sampling strategy further departs from uniform or K-Means sampling by adapting particle density to local stiffness and curvature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 45× speedup (2 min vs. 1.5 h) over video-diffusion-based methods makes interactive 3D physics simulation practically feasible on a single GPU, and the scene-wide (multi-object) simulation capability fills a gap that prior methods like PhysDreamer and Physics3D leave open. The MLLM-as-physics-oracle paradigm is a conceptually clean alternative to video generation, though it inherits MLLM limitations (hallucination, API cost, no fine-grained 3D awareness). The acknowledged limitation on occluded objects and the modest per-component ablation gains (≈0.1–0.2 AS each) suggest the framework's ceiling is still constrained by segmentation quality and the fidelity of MLLM material reasoning. Open questions include whether the MLLM approach generalizes to materials with highly similar appearances (e.g., different polymers) and whether the MPDP distribution model can be extended to time-varying or anisotropic properties.
