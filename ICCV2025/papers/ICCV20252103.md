## Problem

SAM's open-source encoder is a single point of failure for numerous downstream segmentation tasks, yet existing adversarial attacks on SAM either require white-box access to the full model, lack verified transferability to downstream tasks, or depend on costly universal adversarial perturbation (UAP) learning over large source-domain datasets. The core gap is the absence of a method that explicitly characterizes the shared vulnerable subspace between SAM's encoder and downstream models while remaining lightweight and domain-agnostic.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252103/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single target-domain RGB image (e.g., from ISTD, CAMO, CityScale, or SegInW) and a minimal reference subset of 40 images sampled from SA-1B (SAM's training corpus) for domain re-adaptation initialization. It produces adversarial images constrained within an ℓ∞ ball of ε=10, which are then fed into black-box downstream SAM-based models (adapter-tuned, full fine-tuned, or zero-shot) to evaluate transferable attack efficacy. No gradients or parameters of the downstream model are accessed during attack generation.

## Method

VeSCA models the adversarial subspace as a parametric simplicial complex of N=4 simplexes, each with M=4 vertices, learned via iterative vertex-wise refinement. Initialization combines a Domain Re-Adaptation (DRA) objective—minimizing the ℓ₁ distance between the adversarial embedding and the mean SA-1B feature embedding—with a Patch-level Augmentation (PAR) strategy that rotates and rearranges 64×64 patches to generate diverse initial vertices that disrupt ViT self-attention. Each simplex is then optimized by jointly maximizing the expected feature-space distance to the clean image (approximated via H Monte Carlo samples drawn with Dirichlet(1) weights) and expanding geometric volume (computed via the Cayley-Menger determinant with an adaptive λ that scales inversely with volume). The final adversarial example is a random convex combination of simplex vertices, ensuring the entire complex resides in a high-loss region and approximating Bayesian marginalization over the adversarial subspace.

## Results

VeSCA achieves state-of-the-art transferable attack performance across all five downstream datasets, with the largest gain of 12.71% on the SAM-Road model (CityScale, Rec%↓: 20.045 vs. 22.964 for MUI-GRAT). On shadow segmentation (ISTD, BER↑), VeSCA reaches 10.60, and on zero-shot segmentation (SegInW, MAP%↓), it achieves 28.934, both outperforming the second-best method by over 10%. Gains are most pronounced on datasets with large distribution shifts from SA-1B (ISTD, CityScale, SegInW), while differences are smaller on camouflaged object segmentation (CAMO, CHAMELEON) where the data distribution more closely matches SAM's training set.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VeSCA outperforms all six baselines across every dataset and metric. Against the most directly comparable method MUI-GRAT [49] (UAP-based SAM attack), VeSCA achieves the largest margin on CityScale (Rec% 20.045 vs. 22.964, a 12.71% relative improvement) and SegInW (MAP% 28.934 vs. 32.839, 10.32% improvement), while also leading on ISTD, CAMO, and CHAMELEON. The distribution-based subspace methods ANDA and MANDA [11] are consistently outperformed, with VeSCA showing lower S_α and higher MAE on both camouflaged segmentation datasets. The white-box Attack-SAM [50] underperforms all black-box transferable methods, confirming that full-SAM attacks overfit and transfer poorly. Classic gradient-based MIM [6] and augmentation-based BSR [43] are substantially weaker, with BSR (Rec% 28.620) and ILPD [25] (Rec% 30.129) trailing VeSCA by 8–10 points on CityScale. Notably, VeSCA's advantage is largest on distributionally shifted datasets (ISTD, CityScale, SegInW) and smallest on CAMO/CHAMELEON, where the data distribution already resembles SA-1B.

## Relation to Prior Work

VeSCA sits at the intersection of three research threads: (1) transferable adversarial attacks that exploit shared architectural vulnerabilities (MIM, BSR, ILPD, ANDA/MANDA), (2) SAM-specific attacks targeting the image encoder (Attack-SAM, MUI-GRAT), and (3) geometric mode-connectivity literature that models loss landscapes with parametric structures (Benton et al.'s loss-surface simplexes, Garipov et al.'s mode connectors). The key departure from prior work is the explicit use of simplicial complexes—rather than single points or Gaussian distributions—to parameterize the adversarial subspace, combined with a lightweight domain re-adaptation strategy (40 reference images) that replaces the resource-intensive UAP training required by MUI-GRAT. The patch-level augmentation (PAR) is a direct adaptation of BSR's block-shuffle-and-rotation idea, repurposed here to generate diverse initial simplex vertices rather than to augment a single adversarial example.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a single vulnerability in SAM's open-source encoder can be systematically exploited to degrade a wide range of downstream applications (shadow segmentation, camouflage detection, road extraction, zero-shot segmentation) without any access to those downstream models—highlighting a systemic single-point-of-failure risk in foundation-model ecosystems. The finding that only 40 source-domain reference images suffice for domain re-adaptation makes the attack protocol lightweight and broadly applicable. Open questions include whether the simplicial-complex approach generalizes to other foundation models (e.g., DINOv2, CLIP), whether the volume-maximization objective remains stable at higher simplex dimensions, and whether the observed transferability gains persist under stronger defenses or more extreme domain shifts. The paper does not explore defensive implications or certified robustness, leaving the question of how to harden SAM against such subspace-level attacks unresolved.
