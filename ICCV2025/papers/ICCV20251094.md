## Problem

In quantization-aware training (QAT), quantized weights change discrete levels only when corresponding latent weights pass quantizer transition points, making the effective step size of quantized weights poorly correlated with the learning rate used to update latent weights. This breaks the coarse-to-fine parameter update strategy that LR scheduling provides in full-precision training, causing unstable training and degraded accuracy, particularly at the end of training when latent weights concentrate near transition points.

## Input / Output

The method operates on standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 for ImageNet, variable resolution for MS COCO) and produces class labels or bounding boxes, identical to the full-precision network. The key distinction is internal: during QAT, the network uses low-bit quantized weights and activations (1-bit to 4-bit) in forward/backward passes while updating full-precision latent weights. The TR scheduler modifies only the optimization step, adding negligible overhead (~2% training time) without changing the network architecture or inference procedure.

## Method

The paper introduces a Transition Rate (TR) scheduling technique that replaces conventional LR scheduling in QAT. The TR is defined as the fraction of quantized weights changing discrete levels per iteration, and the key insight is that each quantized weight's effective step size is either zero or a fixed discrete value (the quantization interval), so the average step size is governed by the number of transitions rather than the LR. At each iteration, the method (1) computes a running TR via exponential moving average, (2) adjusts a Transition-Adaptive Learning Rate (TALR) proportionally to the gap between a user-scheduled target TR and the running TR, and (3) updates latent weights using the TALR. This creates a feedback loop: if too few weights are transitioning, the TALR increases to push more latent weights past transition points, and vice versa, enabling explicit control over the degree of quantized weight changes in a coarse-to-fine manner.

## Results

On ImageNet, the TR scheduler (SGDT/AdamT) consistently outperforms plain SGD/Adam across all architectures and bit-widths, with the largest gains on MobileNetV2 2/2 quantization (+6.7% top-1 for SGD, +4.2% for Adam) and DeiT-S 2/2 (+3.4% for AdamW). On CIFAR-100, gains reach 2.1–2.5% for 1-bit quantization (e.g., ResNet-18 1/1: 90.9% → 93.0% with SGDT). On MS COCO object detection, SGDT improves AP by 0.27–0.31 over SGD for RetinaNet with ResNet-50 at 3/3 and 4/4 bit-widths. Training overhead is only 2% over plain optimizers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the TR scheduler against plain Adam (Kingma & Ba, ICLR 2014) and AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, ICLR 2019) as the primary baselines, alongside plain SGD. AdamT outperforms Adam on every configuration tested: on ImageNet MobileNetV2 2/2, AdamT reaches 53.8% vs. Adam's 49.6% (+4.2%); on DeiT-S 2/2, AdamWT achieves 71.8% vs. AdamW's 68.4% (+3.4%); on CIFAR-100 ResNet-18 1/1, AdamT scores 92.9% vs. Adam's 90.4% (+2.5%). Similarly, SGDT beats plain SGD across all settings, with the most dramatic margin on MobileNetV2 2/2 ImageNet (53.6% vs. 46.9%, +6.7%) and CIFAR-100 ReActNet-18 1/1 (72.2% vs. 69.7%, +2.5%). The method never loses to any baseline in any reported configuration, and gains are largest at lower bit-widths and on smaller datasets, where conventional LR scheduling is most sub-optimal.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant QAT research direction, which has focused on quantizer design (learnable scales, non-uniform levels) and gradient estimation (STE, differentiable quantizers), by identifying the optimization step as the neglected bottleneck. It builds on LSQ for the quantizer formulation but introduces a fundamentally different training scheduler: rather than decaying a learning rate for latent weights (as in full-precision training), it schedules a target transition rate for quantized weights and adapts the learning rate via a transition-adaptive mechanism. This contrasts with prior oscillation-mitigation work [34] that freezes weights or adds regularization, and with binary-network optimizers [12, 40] that flip values via manually scheduled thresholds, by explicitly controlling the degree of quantized weight change through a feedback loop on the actual number of transitions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, low-overhead (2% training time) drop-in replacement for LR scheduling in QAT that consistently improves accuracy across architectures (CNNs, ViTs), bit-widths (1–4 bit), optimizers (SGD, Adam, AdamW), and tasks (classification, detection), suggesting it could become a standard component of QAT pipelines. The conceptual insight—that quantized weight changes are governed by transition-point crossings rather than LR magnitude—reframes how practitioners should think about training dynamics in discrete optimization. Open questions include whether TR scheduling composes well with other QAT techniques (e.g., mixed-precision, knowledge distillation), whether it extends to activation-only or asymmetric quantization regimes, and whether the same principle applies to other discrete parameter spaces beyond neural network quantization.
