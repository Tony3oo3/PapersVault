## Problem

Emotion-evoked image generation—synthesizing images that elicit a target emotion while preserving the source scene's semantics and structure—remains largely unexplored. Existing approaches such as color transfer, style transfer, and text-driven diffusion editing fail because they cannot identify and modify the specific local regions (e.g., flames, facial expressions) responsible for emotional impact, and no large-scale paired dataset with emotion annotations exists to train such a system.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251739/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 224×224 RGB source image and a single target emotion label (one of 8 Mikels categories) as input, and produces a 224×224 emotion-evoked image as output. Notably, no text editing instructions or emotion reference images are required at inference; the model internally computes an emotion editing direction from the predicted source emotion distribution and the target one-hot code. For multi-emotion editing, the one-hot vector can be extended to encode multiple target emotions simultaneously.

## Method

EmoEditor is a dual-branch latent diffusion model built on Stable Diffusion. The global branch encodes an emotion editing direction e_dir = e_oh − e_s (target one-hot minus predicted source emotion distribution) into neural-symbolic embeddings via a trainable emotion encoder τ_θ, while the local branch extracts source image features z through a frozen VAE encoder; the denoising network conditions on both via cross-attention. Training uses a joint loss combining standard latent diffusion noise prediction with an alignment loss that maximizes cosine similarity between emotion embeddings and CLIP text embeddings of human-annotated editing instructions, implicitly learning emotion-to-edit mappings without constraining the model to specific textual solutions. At inference, an Iterative Emotion Inference (IEI) loop uses a frozen emotion predictor as a critic, regenerating images (up to 30 iterations) until both structural similarity (SSIM 0.5–0.8) and target-emotion confidence (>0.6) are satisfied, updating the emotion editing direction each round based on the newly generated image's predicted emotion.

## Results

On the cross-valence emotion editing task (2,016 test images from EmoSet), EmoEditor achieves 51.56% CAM-based ESMI and 49.99% HA-based ESMI, outperforming all nine baselines by at least 4 points. The closest competitor is NST (47.48% CAM-based), which relies on global style transfer and loses semantic content. In human psychophysics experiments (24,480 trials, 136 participants), participants consistently prefer EmoEditor's outputs over every baseline in two-alternative forced choice tasks, with preference rates well above the 50% chance level.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EmoEditor outperforms all nine baselines on both ESMI metrics and human preference. The largest gaps are over CT (28.33%) and Ip2p (27.37%), confirming that global color transfer and generic instruction-following editing are insufficient for emotion-specific local modifications. NST (47.48%) is the strongest baseline, leveraging artistic texture to evoke emotions, but it sacrifices semantic content and interpretability. Among text-driven diffusion editors, SDEdit (35.91%) and LMS (35.40%) perform best, yet both produce abstract textures or amplify errors from chained large models without understanding emotional cues. The emotion-specific baselines AIF (29.71%) and EmoEdit (32.94%) underperform because AIF applies only global filters and EmoEdit is constrained by a fixed emotion dictionary, leading to repetitive, context-incongruent edits (e.g., always adding balloons for amusement). Notably, EmoEditor's advantage is most pronounced on strongly emotional inputs (e.g., fire, fireworks), where it performs precise local edits (extinguishing flames, replacing with context-appropriate elements) while preserving scene structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on latent diffusion models (Stable Diffusion) and the InstructPix2Pix editing paradigm, but departs from text-driven editing by replacing textual instructions with structured emotion vectors (one-hot and editing-direction encodings) processed through a dual-branch architecture. It extends the visual emotion analysis literature (Mikels 8-category model, EmoSet) from classification to generation, and differentiates from prior emotion-editing methods (AIF's global text-to-image filters, EmoEdit's fixed learned queries) by learning emotional cues end-to-end without reference images or hand-crafted prompts. The neuro-symbolic alignment loss, which regularizes emotion embeddings toward CLIP text embeddings of human-annotated editing instructions, is a novel training objective not seen in prior diffusion-based editing work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces the first large-scale paired emotion-editing dataset (EmoPair, 340k pairs) and a dedicated evaluation metric (ESMI), filling a gap that prevents systematic benchmarking of emotion-driven image editing. Practical applications span therapeutic interventions (AR/VR for autism, schizophrenia), commercial design, and artistic creation. However, the 8-category Mikels model is a coarse approximation of real-world emotional nuance, the method struggles with fine facial details, and the authors themselves flag risks of misuse (evoking harmful emotions). Open questions include scaling to finer-grained or mixed-emotion targets, improving face-level fidelity, and establishing safety guardrails for deployment.
