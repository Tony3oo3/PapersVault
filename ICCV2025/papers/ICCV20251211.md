## Problem

Existing methods for staining (watermarking) and locking pretrained computer vision models all require retraining or fine-tuning, which is expensive, risks altering model behaviour in unidentifiable ways, and prevents a single base model from being customised for multiple clients. Moreover, no prior approach provides computable theoretical guarantees bounding the worst-case false positive rate of the embedded stain or lock.

## Input / Output

The method takes a fully pretrained model (CNN, GAN, or ViT) and directly modifies a small number of its weights without requiring any training or validation data. For locking, a small optimised trigger patch is inserted into a corner of the input image at inference time; the patch size is determined by the receptive field of the detector kernel at its implanted position. The unlocked model produces its normal outputs (class labels, bounding boxes, generated images), while the locked model's outputs are corrupted by a disruptor until the trigger patch is present.

## Method

The core mechanism implants a highly selective detector neuron by sampling its weight vector uniformly from the unit sphere in the model's feature space and optimising a trigger input to strongly activate it; concentration properties in high-dimensional feature spaces guarantee the detector responds negligibly to natural inputs. For staining, the detector is inserted (non-additively or additively) into an existing neuron, and its strong response to the trigger encodes the watermark. For locking, additional disruptor weights are implanted to pollute the model's activations, and a conduit (identity kernels or a Squeeze-and-Excite block) propagates the detector signal to deactivate the disruptor when the trigger patch is detected. Two novel theoretical bounds are provided: a geometric bound (Theorem 1) relating false positive rate to feature-space dimension and PCA structure, and a data-driven bound (Theorem 2) that can be exactly evaluated from finite observations to certify a fixed detector's worst-case false positive rate.

## Results

Across all experiments on ResNet50, VGG16, SSDLite, and Faster-RCNN, no false positive detections (non-trigger images exceeding the trigger response threshold) were observed. For the DC-GAN locking experiment, the discriminator loss jumps from 0.443 (original) to 99.880 (locked) while recovering to 0.444 (unlocked), confirming near-perfect restoration of model capability. The unlocked models' performance is reported as close to the original in all cases, with the paper noting that parameter tuning (which was deliberately avoided for fairness) could further improve stained/unlocked performance. Theoretical false-positive bounds from Theorems 1 and 2 show excellent agreement with empirical response distributions evaluated on 2,000 validation images.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a direct head-to-head comparison against prior staining or locking methods; instead, the baseline is the original (unmodified) model's performance on ImageNet (top-1 accuracy) and COCO (AP/AR at IoU 0.5:0.95). The key finding is that the training-free weight modification introduces minimal performance degradation: the 'edited' model (detector and conduit implanted but no disruptor) and the 'unlocked' model (full lock with trigger patch applied) both track the original model's accuracy closely across all tested convolutional layers. The locked model (without trigger patch) shows the expected severe performance drop, confirming the lock is effective. The authors note that performance differences in some cases (e.g., Faster-RCNN with Squeeze-and-Excite locking) stem from challenges in disabling the disruptor rather than from the added Sq-Ex block itself, as evidenced by the 'edited' model performing comparably to the 'unlocked' model. The main advantage over prior methods is that no retraining or fine-tuning is required, and no training/validation data is needed, which is a qualitative rather than quantitative distinction from existing approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the weight-manipulation insight from stealth attacks [41,43,44]—where a few modified weights can dictate model outputs—into a constructive mechanism for IP protection. Prior staining methods required either special loss functions during training or manipulated training data, and prior locking methods were either intrinsic (baked in at training time) or extrinsic (relying on external infrastructure); here, both are achieved by directly editing a small number of weights in a pre-trained model with no retraining or data access. The two theoretical theorems bounding the detector's worst-case false positive rate (one geometric, one data-driven) are explicitly noted as absent from the prior watermarking/locking literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The training-free property is practically significant: a single base model can be distributed to many clients, each receiving a unique stain or lock, without the cost and risk of retraining. The computable false-positive guarantees give model owners a principled way to certify their protection before deployment. Open questions include robustness to adversarial pruning or weight-averaging attacks, the practical difficulty of obfuscating the lock in production settings, and whether the single-detector/single-disruptor construction scales cleanly to the multi-detector configurations the authors recommend for real-world security.
