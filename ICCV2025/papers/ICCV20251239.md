## Problem

Rehearsal-based continual learning for video is severely constrained by memory: storing raw video clips in a buffer becomes prohibitively expensive for long, naturally-collected videos (minutes to hours), and prior video CL work is limited to short clips of a few seconds. Additionally, training a video compressor online introduces its own catastrophic forgetting, causing stored codes to drift out of alignment with the evolving encoder. The paper addresses both the memory bottleneck and the representation drift problem in a unified framework.

## Input / Output

The method consumes video clips of 224×224×3×32 RGB frames (32 frames per clip), which are compressed into neural codes of approximately 0.0013 Mb each via a VQVAE-based autoencoder. Long videos (up to 1.5 hours in Epic-Kitchens-100, ~20 min average) are split into many such clips, each independently encoded. The output is a class label (action for Kinetics-700, noun for Epic-Kitchens-100) predicted by an S3D classifier operating on the compressed codes. The rehearsal buffer stores only these compact codes rather than raw pixels, enabling thousands of long videos to fit under 2 GB.

## Method

CRAM (Continually Refreshed Amodal Memory) trains a VQVAE-based video compressor (encoder φ, decoder ψ) and an S3D classifier jointly in a continual setting, with a rehearsal buffer storing neural codes instead of raw video. The key mechanism is a code-refreshing scheme: at each new task, the compressor is updated, and all previously stored codes are decompressed using the immediately-previous decoder and recompressed with the new encoder, preventing representation drift while requiring only a single decoder snapshot (constant memory overhead). In the incremental-from-scratch setting, the compressor is retrained at every task using both new raw samples and decompressed buffer codes, then frozen; the classifier is trained on the refreshed codes plus new-task codes. In the pre-training setting, the compressor is trained IID on half the classes and then frozen, eliminating drift entirely during the incremental phase. This design achieves near-upper-bound accuracy with a ~1000× smaller memory footprint compared to raw RGB rehearsal buffers.

## Results

In the pre-training setting, CRAM achieves 46.50% evaluation accuracy on Kinetics-700 (vs. 48.20% upper bound) and 33.20% on Epic-Kitchens-100 (vs. 35.90% upper bound), with average forgetting of only 5.50 and 9.70 respectively. In the incremental-from-scratch setting, CRAM reaches 38.80% eval accuracy on K-700 and 28.10% on EK-100, with average forgetting of 15.20 and 21.60. All results are obtained under a fixed memory budget of ~1.4 GB (1404 Mb for K-700, 1464 Mb for EK-100), compared to ~1.5 TB required by an equivalent RGB rehearsal buffer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRAM outperforms all five baselines on both datasets in both settings. Against REMIND [15] (the closest method, also using compressed codes), CRAM gains +8.3% eval accuracy on K-700 pre-training (46.50 vs. 38.20) and reduces average forgetting from 43.10 to 5.50; on EK-100 the gap is +6.7% eval and AvgF drops from 51.0 to 9.70. In the incremental setting, CRAM beats SMILE [1] by +9.6% eval on K-700 (38.80 vs. 29.20) and +8.9% on EK-100 (28.10 vs. 19.20), while reducing average forgetting from 62.50 to 15.20 (K-700) and from 67.8 to 21.60 (EK-100). vCLIMB [42] and GDumb [33] show even larger gaps: CRAM exceeds vCLIMB by ~10% eval accuracy and GDumb by ~20% on K-700 incremental. The paper attributes the baselines' weakness to their reliance on frame selection/condensing, which destroys temporal continuity in long videos, whereas CRAM stores every frame as a compressed code and rehearses the full temporal history.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on compressed vision (Wiles et al., [46]), which trains a VQVAE-based neural compressor to store video as compact codes rather than raw frames, and adapts it to the continual learning setting where the compressor itself is trained online and subject to catastrophic forgetting. It departs from prior video CL methods (SMILE, vCLIMB, FrameMaker) that rely on frame selection or condensing strategies, which the authors argue are inherently detrimental for long videos due to loss of temporal continuity. The key methodological departure is the 'code refreshing' scheme: rather than storing all decoder snapshots (unbounded memory) or ignoring drift (stale codes), the method decompresses with the previous decoder and recompresses with the current one, maintaining a constant-memory buffer of always-current codes. The rehearsal-based CL framework (GDumb, A-GEM, REMIND) is extended from images to long videos, with the compressed buffer replacing the raw-pixel buffer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that video CL on naturally-collected long videos (minutes to hours, 10× longer than prior benchmarks) is feasible under a sub-2 GB memory budget, removing a major practical barrier to deploying continual video understanding in resource-constrained settings. The code refreshing scheme is a clean, general solution to representation drift in any compressed rehearsal buffer, and could be adopted by other memory-based CL methods. Limitations include evaluation only on action/noun classification (no detection, segmentation, or multi-modal tasks), a single sequential video stream, and baselines that are somewhat disadvantaged for long videos (frame-selection methods lose temporal context by design). Open questions include whether the approach scales to even longer or multi-stream settings, whether the compressor can be made more robust under aggressive compression, and how the method interacts with other CL regularizers (EWC, LwF).
