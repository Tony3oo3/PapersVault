## Problem

Existing initial-noise watermarking methods for diffusion models either distort the distribution of generated images or require maintaining a large database of candidate noise patterns for detection, and remain vulnerable to forgery attacks where an adversary transplants the watermark onto unrelated or harmful content. The paper addresses the need for a watermark that is simultaneously distortion-free, database-free, and robust to both regeneration-based removal and semantic forgery attacks, including the previously overlooked 'CAT ATTACK' of localized object insertion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252392/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For generation, the method takes a text prompt and produces a watermarked image by seeding the diffusion model's initial noise with a semantic-derived pattern; no external key or database lookup is needed. For detection, it takes a suspect RGB image and produces a binary watermarked/non-watermarked decision plus, when tampering is suspected, a per-patch spatial map identifying which regions were locally edited. The detection pipeline internally captions the image with BLIP-2, embeds the caption with a fine-tuned Paraphrase Mpnet Base V2 model, and reconstructs the initial noise via DDIM inversion (50 steps on Stable Diffusion-v2).

## Method

SEAL embeds semantic information into the diffusion model's initial noise through a three-stage pipeline: (1) a proxy image is generated and captioned with BLIP-2, then embedded via a fine-tuned sentence-embedding model to obtain a semantic vector v; (2) SimHash projects v onto b random directions per patch to produce b-bit hashes, which are combined with a patch index and a secret salt through a cryptographic hash to yield a per-patch seed; (3) each seed initializes a pseudo-random Gaussian patch z_i ~ N(0, I), ensuring the overall noise is distributionally indistinguishable from standard random initialization. At detection time, the suspect image is independently captioned and embedded to produce a reference noise, which is compared patch-by-patch against the DDIM-inverted noise; a match threshold over n=1024 patches determines the watermark decision. Because the noise pattern is derived from image semantics rather than a fixed key, any substantial semantic alteration (e.g., pasting an unrelated object) breaks the correlation and invalidates the watermark, while a spatial test that exhaustively searches 2^b hash inputs per patch localizes residual tampering.

## Results

On the novel CAT ATTACK (pasting an unrelated object onto a watermarked image), SEAL achieves a ROC-AUC of 0.551 for basic detection and 0.982 when combined with the spatial patch test, while all three baselines (WIND, Tree-Ring, Gaussian Shading) score 0.000. On the Private Model-Based Forgery Attack (attacker inverts a watermarked image with the private model and regenerates with a different prompt), SEAL achieves 0.708 AUC versus 0.000 for all baselines. Under standard image transformations, SEAL maintains an average detection rate of 0.896. The detection probability exhibits a sharp threshold: at a semantic angle of 50° between original and inspected embeddings, detection probability is 0.998, dropping to 0.053 at 60°.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEAL decisively outperforms all three baselines on both primary attack metrics. Against the CAT ATTACK, WIND [4], Tree-Ring [31], and Gaussian Shading [34] all achieve 0.000 AUC, meaning they cannot detect any localized object insertion, whereas SEAL reaches 0.551 AUC and 0.982 AUC with the spatial test. On the Private Model-Based Forgery Attack, where an attacker with full model access inverts a watermarked image and regenerates it under a different prompt, all three baselines again score 0.000 AUC (i.e., they falsely identify the forged image as watermarked), while SEAL achieves 0.708 AUC by leveraging the semantic mismatch between the original and attack prompts. The paper notes that SEAL's robustness to standard image transformations (0.896 average detection rate) is somewhat lower than Tree-Ring, but SEAL uniquely provides forgery resistance that the other methods completely lack. Overall, the comparison highlights a fundamental trade-off: prior methods prioritize removal robustness but are entirely vulnerable to forgery, while SEAL sacrifices some transformation robustness to gain strong semantic-aware forgery detection.

## Relation to Prior Work

SEAL builds on the initial-noise watermarking paradigm established by Tree-Ring, WIND, and Gaussian Shading, which embed detectable patterns into the Gaussian noise fed to diffusion models. Its key departure is repurposing SimHash (originally a locality-sensitive hashing technique for approximate nearest-neighbor search) to encode the image's semantic embedding directly into the noise patches, thereby eliminating the need for a large database of registered key patterns that prior distortion-free methods require. The method also leverages DDIM inversion for noise recovery and fine-tunes a sentence-embedding model (Paraphrase Mpnet) on caption pairs to tighten the semantic correlation between generation-time and detection-time embeddings.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SEAL addresses a fundamental and previously underexplored trade-off: watermarks that are hard to remove are also easier to forge onto unrelated content, threatening the model owner's reputation. By tying the watermark to image semantics, the paper shows that forgery via unrelated prompts or localized edits (the newly formalized CAT ATTACK) can be detected without maintaining a growing database of past generations. However, the method is explicitly more vulnerable to standard removal attacks than some existing approaches, and its robustness relies on the semantic embedding remaining stable under edits—a constraint that limits applicability to heavily modified content. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video or multi-modal generation, how it performs under adaptive attacks that specifically target the SimHash patch structure, and whether the fine-tuned embedding model generalizes across diverse prompt distributions.
