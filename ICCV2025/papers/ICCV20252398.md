## Problem

Scene text recognition models are limited by the scarcity of realistic, high-quality training data: traditional rendering-based synthetic datasets (e.g., SynthText, MJSynth) fail to capture the visual diversity of real-world text, while existing diffusion-based text generation methods produce holistically appealing images but struggle with accurate, instance-level character placement at scale. The paper identifies that no existing synthesis pipeline simultaneously satisfies three requirements—character-level accuracy, visual realism matching wild scenes, and the ability to scale to millions of samples without complex control inputs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a local text region (the smallest enclosing square around a detected text instance, resized to 256×256), the OCR pseudo-label from PP-OCRv3, and the text's position/orientation within that crop. It additionally constructs a binary text/non-text mask and an L-channel character-level glyph image where each character occupies its own channel with position-prior pixel intensities. The output is a synthesized text instance image at the same region, which is then quality-screened by SVTRv2 recognition; only correctly recognized samples are retained, yielding the 3.55-million-instance TextSSR-F dataset.

## Method

TextSSR builds on Stable Diffusion 2.1 with two key modifications: a fine-tuned VAE for text-sensitive reconstruction and a retrained U-Net that receives concatenated latent features (masked context, noisy target, glyph rendering, and downsampled mask) plus ViT-encoded character-level glyph features. The core innovation is a region-centric, glyph-conditioned generation scheme that replaces natural language prompts with positional and glyph priors: word-level guidance uses a binary mask and affinely-transformed rendered text, while character-level guidance encodes per-character ordering via an L-channel image with position-prior intensities, enabling precise character placement without duplication errors. For scalability, the pipeline applies anagram-based permutation—rearranging characters within each detected bounding box—to generate l! variants per word, and a quality-screening step using SVTRv2 filters out misrecognized samples, producing the TextSSR-F dataset.

## Results

In the realism evaluation (Table 2), TextSSR trained on 30k synthetic samples achieves 59.76% SeqAcc on regular text and 35.71% on irregular text (50.33% average), surpassing the best diffusion-based competitor SceneVTG (47.34%) by ~3% and approaching real-world TextOCR (54.05%) within 3.7%. In the accuracy evaluation (Table 1), TextSSR fine-tuning reaches 85.17% SeqAcc on IC13 and 62.64% on IC15, outperforming the previous SOTA AnyText (55.07% and 5.97%) by 30.1% and 56.7% respectively. At scale (Table 3), adding 3.55M TextSSR-F samples to the 4M ST dataset yields 86.17% average SeqAcc with CRNN and 93.63% with MAERec, and combining with TextOCR further pushes MAERec to 95.46% average.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TextSSR decisively outperforms all diffusion-based text generation methods on both accuracy and realism metrics. Against AnyText (the prior SOTA for multilingual visual text generation), TextSSR gains 30.1% SeqAcc on IC13 (85.17% vs 55.07%) and 56.7% on IC15 (62.64% vs 5.97%), attributed to instance-level vs. full-image generation and glyph-based conditioning. Against SceneVTG, the closest competitor using a two-stage erase-then-synthesize pipeline, TextSSR leads by 3% average SeqAcc in the 30k realism evaluation and avoids the need for a separate text-erasure model. All rendering-based baselines (SynthText, VISD, UnrealText) are outperformed by 10–23% in average SeqAcc, confirming that diffusion-based synthesis captures real-world appearance better than rule-based rendering. Compared to real-world TextOCR data, the gap narrows to only 3.7% on average (50.33% vs 54.05%), with the remaining difference concentrated in irregular text. In large-scale training (Table 3), 1.05M TextSSR samples close 90% of the gap that 1.05M TextOCR samples provide over ST alone, and combining both yields the best results (95.46% MAERec average), demonstrating complementarity rather than redundancy.

## Relation to Prior Work

TextSSR builds on the Stable Diffusion (SD 2.1) latent diffusion architecture, fine-tuning its VAE and retraining the U-Net for text-specific generation. It departs from prior diffusion-based visual text generation methods (TextDiffuser, GlyphControl, AnyText, UDiffText) in two fundamental ways: operating at the instance/region level rather than the full-image level, and conditioning on rendered glyph priors rather than natural language prompts. It also supersedes traditional rendering-based synthetic datasets (SynthText, MJSynth, VISD) by leveraging learned generative models to capture realistic scene conditions, while its anagram-based data expansion strategy replaces the arbitrary text substitution used in prior diffusion text synthesis work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete data bottleneck in STR: the scaling law indicates current training data volumes are far from saturation, yet collecting real-world scene text at scale is costly and biased toward high-frequency words. The 3.55M TextSSR-F dataset and its demonstrated complementarity with real data (further gains when combined with TextOCR) make it a practically valuable resource for the STR community. Key limitations remain: irregular text (curved, multi-oriented, heavily distorted) still shows a meaningful accuracy gap versus real data, and the pipeline is currently English-centric. Open questions include whether the region-centric approach generalizes to curved or multi-line text, and whether the glyph-conditioning paradigm extends cleanly to logographic scripts like Chinese.
