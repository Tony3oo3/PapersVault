## Problem

Display-camera imaging systems offer programmable point-light illumination and polarization-based diffuse-specular separation, making them attractive for inverse rendering, yet no public real-world dataset exists for this setup. Unlike light stages or flash photography, which have established benchmarks, display-camera inverse rendering lacks a standardized dataset for method development and fair comparison, hindering progress in the field.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251608/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system captures stereo polarization RGB images (at 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°) under M display patterns, where the LCD is parameterized as 144 superpixels (16×9 grid of 240×240-pixel blocks) at 50 cm from the cameras. Polarization images are converted to Stokes vectors to separate diffuse and specular components. The baseline produces surface normal maps, depth maps, and spatially varying Cook-Torrance BRDF parameters (diffuse albedo, specular albedo, roughness) encoded as basis BRDF weights, and can synthesize relit images under arbitrary display patterns with adjustable Gaussian noise.

## Method

The paper contributes both a calibrated dataset and a simple analysis-by-synthesis baseline. The display-camera system (Samsung Odyssey Ark LCD + FLIR stereo polarization cameras) is calibrated for spatially-varying backlight, intensity nonlinearity (γ), and geometric alignment using a known sphere under OLAT patterns. The baseline initializes normals via analytical RGB photometric stereo [10] and depth via RAFT stereo on averaged multi-pattern images, then jointly optimizes normals and basis BRDFs (analytic Cook-Torrance functions with spatially varying weight maps) by differentiable rendering in PyTorch, minimizing RMSE between rendered and captured images. This basis BRDF formulation addresses the limited light-view angular coverage inherent to the near-field display setup, and the full optimization converges in approximately 150 seconds.

## Results

On the proposed DIR dataset, the baseline achieves 39.33 dB PSNR and 0.9821 SSIM for relighting under 144 OLAT patterns, with a normal MAE of 20.94°. For multiplexed patterns (2 patterns), it reaches 37.27 dB PSNR and 23.97° MAE. In photometric stereo, SDM-UniPS achieves the best normal MAE (9.38° on OBJET, 12.55° on PLASTER with M=64), while the proposed baseline's strength is in full inverse rendering where it outperforms DPIR (41.09° MAE) and IIR (38.38° MAE) by over 17° in normal accuracy. The baseline completes inverse rendering in 150 seconds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed baseline wins decisively on normal accuracy (MAE) in inverse rendering: 20.94° vs. SRSH (25.25°), DPIR (41.09°), and IIR (38.38°), a margin of 4–20° over all competitors. However, SRSH achieves higher relighting fidelity (41.28 dB PSNR, 0.9895 SSIM) than the baseline (39.33 dB, 0.9821), though the paper notes SRSH cannot handle multiplexed patterns and its normal estimates are less reliable. Against DPIR and IIR, the baseline wins on all three metrics (PSNR, SSIM, MAE). In photometric stereo (Table 2), SDM-UniPS dominates with the lowest MAE across most objects (e.g., 9.38° on OBJET, 12.55° on PLASTER with M=64), outperforming Woodham (~27°), PS-FCN (~15–20°), PS-Transformer (~26–54°), SCPS-NIR (highly variable, 8–53°), and UniPS (~17–30°). DDPS [10] is more robust than SDM-UniPS under very few multiplexed patterns (M=2: 24.57° vs. 23.51° MAE), and learned patterns consistently outperform heuristic ones at the same pattern count.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the display-based inverse rendering line initiated by Choi et al. (DDPS, CVPR 2024), which demonstrated differentiable display photometric stereo on 3D-printed objects, by scaling to real-world objects with diverse materials and providing the first public dataset for this hardware configuration. The baseline method composes the basis-BRDF representation from Chung et al. (DPIR, IIR) and SRSH with the analytical display photometric stereo initialization from DDPS, adapting analysis-by-synthesis inverse rendering to the near-field, polarization-specific challenges of LCD displays. It departs from the broader inverse rendering literature (light stages, flash photography, in-the-wild capture) by addressing the unique constraints of display-camera systems: limited light-view angular coverage, spatially-varying backlight, and LCD polarization that enables free diffuse-specular separation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This dataset fills a clear gap: no prior public dataset existed for display-camera inverse rendering, making it impossible to fairly compare methods under near-field lighting, limited angular sampling, and polarization-specific conditions. The empirical evaluation reveals that current state-of-the-art methods (DPIR, IIR, SRSH) struggle with display-specific challenges, and that the simple baseline outperforms them in relighting quality, suggesting the field needs methods better adapted to these constraints. Open questions include how to more effectively exploit polarization-separated diffuse/specular channels, how to design optimal multiplexed patterns that balance capture efficiency with reconstruction quality, and whether the limited θd angular coverage fundamentally bounds reflectance recovery accuracy.
