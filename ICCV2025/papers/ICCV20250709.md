## Problem

Monocular SLAM recovers camera trajectories only up to an unknown scale factor, and existing approaches to resolve this scale—optimization-based methods like SLAHMR and PACE—are unreliable (with >100% scale discrepancies) and prohibitively slow (hours per minute of video). Local-to-global methods like WHAM avoid SLAM entirely but suffer from fundamental motion ambiguity when background context is ignored (e.g., a person on a skateboard vs. standing still). The paper addresses the gap of efficiently and accurately calibrating the SLAM scale to recover true world-coordinate human motion without expensive joint optimization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a monocular RGB video and processes it through two parallel streams: a SLAM system (masked DroidSLAM or DPVO) producing camera extrinsics and a scene point cloud at arbitrary scale, and a local HMR model (VIMO, CLIFF, or HMR2.0) producing per-frame SMPL parameters in camera coordinates. The output is a unified scale factor applied to the SLAM trajectory, yielding calibrated camera extrinsics and global SMPL parameters (world-frame root orientation Φ_t and translation Γ_t) for every frame. The entire post-SLAM calibration step takes ~5 seconds for 1000 frames on a single GPU, compared to ~400 minutes for SLAHMR.

## Method

HAC treats the human body as a natural checkerboard for scale calibration. For each frame, it identifies the human-scene contact joint (the lowest joint along the camera y-axis, typically a foot) and uses the HMR-predicted metric depth of that joint as the absolute distance d_p^A. The corresponding reference point p in the SLAM point cloud is found by intersecting a ray from the SLAM camera origin toward the contact joint's world-coordinate position, giving the relative distance d_p^S. The per-frame scale is s(t) = d_p^A / d_p^S, and the final unified scale is the median across all frames for robustness against contact breaks and noise. For out-of-view contacts (e.g., egocentric footage where feet are occluded), a ground plane is fitted using the HMR-predicted contact joints to determine the plane normal and the lowest 10% of SLAM points to determine the offset, reducing the problem to the standard visible-contact case. The calibrated scale is then applied to SLAM translations, and the camera-to-world transform (Eq. 2) decouples global human motion from local observations without any iterative optimization.

## Results

On EMDB, HAC achieves 197.2 mm W-MPJPE, 71.0 mm WA-MPJPE, and 1.2% RTE, improving over the previous SOTA TRAM (222.4 / 76.4 / 1.4) by 15% in W-MPJPE and 10% in WA-MPJPE. On the challenging egocentric EgoBody dataset, HAC reaches 109.8 mm W-MPJPE and 74.8 mm WA-MPJPE, slightly outperforming TRAM (112.1 / 76.0). In terms of efficiency, HAC requires only ~5 seconds of post-SLAM inference for 1000 frames, roughly 100× faster than optimization-based methods like SLAHMR (~400 min) and PACE (~8 min).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HAC outperforms the previous SOTA TRAM [46] on EMDB by 15% in W-MPJPE (197.2 vs 222.4) and 10% in WA-MPJPE (71.0 vs 76.4), while PA-MPJPE is essentially tied (38.6 vs 38.1). Against the local-to-global method WHAM [39], HAC achieves a 50% reduction in W-MPJPE on EMDB (197.2 vs 354.8) and a 73% reduction on EgoBody (109.8 vs 402.7), demonstrating the advantage of explicitly modeling camera motion over inferring global trajectories from local pose priors. Optimization-based methods SLAHMR [50] and PACE [19] are substantially worse on EMDB (W-MPJPE 776.1 and N/A respectively) and also on EgoBody (141.1 and 147.9 vs HAC's 109.8), while being 100–8000× slower in post-SLAM inference. On EgoBody, HAC marginally beats TRAM (109.8 vs 112.1 W-MPJPE) and ties on PA-MPJPE (46.8), with the remaining gap to ground-truth camera motion (189.7 W-MPJPE) attributed to HMR pose noise under severe truncation. Local-to-global baselines GLAMR [51] and TRACE [41] show the largest errors (W-MPJPE 726.6 and 1702.3 on EMDB), confirming the motion ambiguity problem that HAC resolves through explicit scale calibration.

## Relation to Prior Work

HAC departs from two dominant paradigms in world-coordinate HMR: local-to-global methods (WHAM, GLAMR, TRACE) that learn black-box mappings from local motion and suffer from fundamental ambiguity, and optimization-based SLAM scale recovery (SLAHMR, PACE) that jointly optimize scale, pose, and shape over the entire sequence. Conceptually, it repurposes the single-view metrology principle—using objects with known geometric priors as natural calibration references—by treating the human body predicted by HMR models as a 'checkerboard.' This positions HAC as a geometric-calibration approach that is plug-and-play compatible with existing HMR (VIMO, CLIFF, HMR2.0) and SLAM (DroidSLAM, DPVO) pipelines, rather than a new end-to-end architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 100× reduction in post-SLAM inference time (5 seconds vs. ~400 minutes for SLAHMR) while simultaneously improving accuracy makes world-coordinate HMR practically accessible for real-time or near-real-time applications that were previously infeasible with optimization-based methods. The key open limitation is the dependence on SLAM quality and the assumption that human-scene contacts exist and are recoverable; scenarios like swimming, prolonged aerial motion, or multi-person interactions remain unaddressed. The finding that HMR's metric depth error (~0.1 m) is an order of magnitude smaller than the global trajectory error of prior methods (>1 m) provides a clear, reusable justification for leveraging HMR as a calibration reference in future work.
