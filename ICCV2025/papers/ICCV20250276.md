## Problem

Most zero-shot image restoration methods based on diffusion models still require at least a parametric form of the degradation model, which is often unavailable or too complex to specify in real-world scenarios (e.g., JPEG compression, water drops, non-uniform deformation). While Deep Image Prior (DIP) handles unknown degradations, it relies on the weak implicit prior of an untrained CNN and only works reliably for high-frequency artifacts. The paper asks whether a pretrained diffusion model exhibits a similar reconstruction-then-overfitting behavior as DIP, and if so, whether it extends to a broader range of degradations including low-frequency ones like blur.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single degraded 256×256 RGB image as input with no assumption about the degradation type or parameters, and outputs a restored clean image of the same resolution. The frozen pretrained diffusion model (DDIM) is used as a fixed mapping from a latent noise vector to image space; only the initial noise z is optimized via gradient descent. Two self-supervised stopping criteria (Laplacian variance for low-frequency degradations, normalized loss slope for high-frequency degradations) determine when to halt optimization, eliminating the need for a fixed iteration count or ground-truth access.

## Method

DIIP optimizes a single latent noise vector z through a frozen pretrained DDIM model to minimize the reconstruction error ||g(z) − y||², where g is the deterministic DDIM mapping and y is the degraded input. The key empirical finding is that this optimization exhibits two distinct regimes regardless of degradation type: an initial phase producing clean, realistic images that progressively approach the clean reference, followed by a phase where the reconstruction overfits to the degradation. Unlike DIP, which shows this behavior only for high-frequency artifacts, the pretrained diffusion model exhibits it for both high-frequency (noise) and low-frequency (blur) degradations. Two self-supervised early-stopping criteria are proposed: (1) tracking the Laplacian variance of intermediate reconstructions to detect the onset of sharpness loss for low-frequency degradations, and (2) monitoring the normalized loss slope to detect the model's resistance to fitting high-frequency artifacts. The method requires no training data, no degradation model, and no architectural modifications to the pretrained diffusion model.

## Results

On CelebA 256×256, DIIP achieves 28.37 dB PSNR / 0.842 SSIM / 0.224 LPIPS on denoising, 25.14 dB / 0.764 / 0.301 on 4× super-resolution, and 22.86 dB / 0.651 / 0.336 on 8× super-resolution, outperforming all baselines on most metrics. On unstructured degradations (Table 5), DIIP leads by a clear margin: 25.29 dB on JPEG de-artifacting, 23.45 dB on non-uniform deformation, and 23.78 dB on water-drop removal, surpassing DreamClean by 1.3–1.4 dB PSNR. Runtime is 138 s with 1.2 GB memory, comparable to DreamClean (125 s, 1.3 GB) and well below BlindDPS (270 s, 6.1 GB).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DIIP outperforms all six baselines on denoising (28.37 dB vs. BIRD's 27.92 dB, a +0.45 dB gain) and 8× super-resolution (22.86 dB vs. BIRD's 22.63 dB). On 4× super-resolution, BIRD edges out DIIP by 0.12 dB in PSNR (25.26 vs. 25.14) and 0.007 in LPIPS, though DIIP still leads in SSIM (0.764 vs. 0.751). Against the closest fully-blind competitor DreamClean, DIIP gains 1.32 dB on denoising, 1.70 dB on 4× SR, and 1.53 dB on 8× SR. On unstructured degradations where only DIP and DreamClean are applicable, DIIP leads DreamClean by 1.37 dB (JPEG), 1.29 dB (non-uniform deformation), and 0.84 dB (water-drop removal), while DIP trails by 4–5 dB across all three tasks. The partially-blind methods (GDP, GibbsDDRM, BIRD, BlindDPS) are not applicable to unstructured degradations since they require knowledge of the degradation's parametric form, giving DIIP a unique advantage in fully blind settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

DIIP is a direct conceptual descendant of Deep Image Prior (DIP), which showed that an untrained CNN's implicit prior can restore images via reconstruction-plus-early-stopping without any degradation model. The key departure is replacing the untrained CNN with a frozen pretrained diffusion model (DDIM) and optimizing only the input noise rather than network weights, while adopting the efficient inversion strategy from BIRD to make the optimization tractable. Compared to the closest prior work, DreamClean, DIIP uses a deterministic DDIM mapping and noise inversion rather than variance-preserving steps, and introduces self-supervised stopping criteria (Laplacian variance for low-frequency, normalized loss slope for high-frequency) instead of a fixed iteration count. The paper positions itself in the fully-blind regime, explicitly contrasting with partially-blind methods (BlindDPS, BIRD, GibbsDDRM, GDP) that still require the parametric form of the degradation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: in real-world scenarios the degradation is often unknown or too complex to specify (JPEG artifacts, water drops, non-uniform deformation), and DIIP handles all of these without any degradation model or training data. The finding that a frozen pretrained diffusion model exhibits the clean-then-overfit behavior across both high- and low-frequency degradations—despite never being trained on corrupted data—suggests that the learned data manifold acts as a stronger and more general regularizer than the untrained CNN prior in DIP. Limitations include the per-image optimization cost (~138 s at 256×256), the sensitivity of stopping criteria to hyperparameters (k_min, ε), and the modest gap to oracle stopping (~0.3 dB) that hints at room for improvement. Open questions include whether the approach scales to higher resolutions, whether the stopping criteria generalize to truly arbitrary real-world degradations beyond the tested set, and whether the implicit prior behavior persists with different diffusion architectures.
