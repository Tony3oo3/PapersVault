## Problem

Existing music-to-dance generation methods support only direct synthesis and cannot enable users to iteratively refine dance movements through natural-language edit instructions, a critical capability for real-world choreography. Moreover, no large-scale dataset provides multi-turn editable dance motions with aligned text descriptions and music, making the task of iterative, text-guided dance editing fundamentally unaddressed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251721/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes a 5-second music signal (150 frames, converted to rhythmic features via Jukebox) and open-vocabulary text edit prompts (e.g., 'lift right knee higher') as input, producing 3D dance motion sequences in SMPL 24-joint format with 6D rotation per joint, 3D root position, and 4D binary foot contact. The iterative nature means each editing round consumes the previous round's output as the 'initial prediction' alongside the new text prompt, enabling multi-turn refinement where the user progressively adjusts body movements while the music condition remains fixed.

## Method

DanceEditor follows a prediction-then-editing paradigm: a diffusion transformer-based generation branch first synthesizes an initial dance from music using a standard denoising objective augmented with velocity and foot-contact losses, then a separate editing branch (analogous to ControlNet) performs iterative text-guided refinement. The key component is the Cross-modality Editing Module (CEM), which uses cross-attention to produce music-coherent motion embeddings, computes temporal correlation matrices between text features and both the current and initial motion sequences, and derives a softmax-gated fusion weight via adaptive max-pooling to blend initial and current motion features. This fused representation is injected into the editing branch through an AdaIN layer, providing soft temporal guidance that preserves rhythmic alignment with music while enforcing fine-grained semantic correspondence with the edit prompt. The entire system is trained on the newly constructed DanceRemix dataset (25.3M frames, 84.5K pairs), built automatically via TMR-based motion retrieval, DTW beat alignment, Gemini-generated dense captions, and ChatGPT-produced transformation scripts.

## Results

On the DanceRemix dataset, DanceEditor achieves FID 2.83, BAS 0.2560, Diversity 3.12, and PFC 0.784, outperforming all baselines across every metric. The FID improvement over the strongest baseline (Lodge, 3.57) is approximately 21%, while PFC is reduced by 30–52% compared to competitors. On the POPDG dataset, DanceEditor similarly leads with FID 2.87, BAS 0.2535, Diversity 3.05, and PFC 3.514, maintaining consistent superiority across both evaluation settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DanceEditor outperforms all four baselines on every metric across both datasets. Against Lodge (the closest competitor), DanceEditor reduces FID from 3.57 to 2.83 (≈21% improvement) on DanceRemix and from 4.03 to 2.87 on POPDG, while also achieving higher Diversity (3.12 vs. 2.92) and lower PFC (0.784 vs. 1.559). EDGE and TM2D, both earlier diffusion/VQ-VAE approaches, show substantially weaker diversity (2.29 and 2.16) and higher PFC (1.635 and 1.327), indicating stiffer and less physically plausible motions. POPDG, despite being the most recent baseline, has the worst FID (4.02) on DanceRemix and the highest PFC (4.875) on POPDG, suggesting it struggles with both motion quality and physical realism. The paper attributes DanceEditor's advantage to the prediction-then-editing paradigm and the CEM module, which jointly model music-rhythm alignment and fine-grained text-guided temporal editing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the music-to-dance generation line (EDGE, Lodge, POPDG) by introducing a prediction-then-editing paradigm that adds iterative, text-guided refinement on top of an initial music-conditioned diffusion prediction. The editing branch is architecturally inspired by ControlNet, using a separate branch with AdaIN to inject text and initial-motion conditions, but the Cross-modality Editing Module (CEM) adds a novel temporal correlation mechanism that adaptively fuses initial and current motion features based on text-movement alignment. The dataset construction pipeline repurposes TMR for motion-to-motion retrieval and leverages Gemini/ChatGPT for automatic caption and edit-instruction generation, a practical but conceptually straightforward approach to building multi-turn editing data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DanceRemix is the first large-scale multi-turn editable dance dataset (84.5K pairs, 25.3M frames), filling a critical data gap that has prevented prior methods from supporting iterative choreography workflows. The practical impact is significant for virtual avatar animation and choreography tools where users expect to refine generated dances through natural-language edits. However, the current scope is limited to body movements only (no facial expressions or hand gestures), and the evaluation relies primarily on the authors' own dataset, making cross-dataset generalization an open question. Scaling to longer sequences, more complex multi-part edits, and real-time interactive editing remain important future directions.
