## Problem

Traditional image invariants (e.g., moment invariants) offer robustness and interpretability but lack the discriminability required for larger-scale tasks, while CNNs achieve high accuracy without guaranteed geometric invariance or human-understandable behavior. Recent bridging approaches—scattering networks and equivariant networks—suffer from exponential computational expansion due to discrete symmetry sampling and an inherent trade-off between invariance strength and representation capacity. The paper asks whether a CNN-like hierarchical architecture can be built in a fully transparent, hand-crafted manner that simultaneously delivers invariance, discriminability, and efficiency.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 2D digital image as input and produces an over-complete invariant feature vector by computing global moment invariants along multiple paths in a tree-structured network; no end-to-end training of the representation itself is needed, only a lightweight classifier (NN or SVM) on the fixed features. For multi-scale tasks, feature maps from corresponding nodes across scale levels are pooled to form scale-invariant representations, and the spatial dimensions can be preserved by omitting the final invariant layer for pixel-level tasks such as segmentation.

## Method

The HIR architecture cascades four transparent modules—Convolutional (C, using basis functions from orthogonal moment theory), Nonlinear (S, a magnitude operation on complex-valued features), Local Pooling (P, downsampling), and Invariant (I, global moment invariants)—along ordered paths in a tree topology. The key mechanism is that the magnitude operation in S converts the rotation/flipping covariance of C into equivariance, so the composition preserves geometric structure across layers without requiring parallel symmetry-sampled channels or cross-channel pooling. Multi-scale invariance is achieved by instantiating the tree at multiple scale parameters and pooling across scales, which transforms scaling covariance into a linear translation pattern between networks. Task-specific discriminability is obtained via a NAS-like feature/architecture selection over the over-complete feature set, or optionally by cascading a learned CNN on top of the fixed HIR.

## Results

On the AIGC detection task across 8 generators (ADM, BGAN, GLIDE, Midjourney, SD 1.4, SD 1.5, VQDM, Wukong), HIR with SVM achieves 98.68% average F1 and 92.18% worst-case F1, outperforming all learning CNNs and scattering networks. Under random orientation and flipping, HIR maintains 98.63% average F1 (worst: 91.99%), while learning CNNs degrade substantially (e.g., VGGNet drops to 89.07% avg, SimpleNet to 78.85%). On the more challenging hybrid benchmark with only 1/9 training ratio, HIR achieves 94.73% F1, far exceeding DenseNet (76.10%) and purpose-built AIGC forensics methods (73.74–79.67%). On the digit classification benchmark with random translation and rotation, HIR achieves 94.98% F1 versus 56.23% for scattering networks and 95.13% for VGGNet with data augmentation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HIR clearly outperforms scattering networks (the main invariant competitor) across all protocols: on the digit benchmark with translation/rotation, HIR achieves 94.98% F1 versus 56.23% for ScatterNet; on AIGC detection, HIR SVM reaches 98.68% AVG F1 versus 95.77% for ScatterNet SVM, with a notably higher worst-case score (92.18% vs. 85.21%). Against learning CNNs, HIR is competitive or superior on direct AIGC detection (98.68% vs. DenseNet's 95.84% AVG), but its advantage becomes decisive under geometric perturbations (Table 4: HIR 98.63% vs. DenseNet 92.23%) and on the hybrid benchmark with limited training data (Table 5: HIR 94.73% F1 at 1/9 ratio vs. DenseNet 76.10%). Classical representations (Cosine SVM, Wavelet SVM) achieve comparable average scores on direct AIGC detection but suffer larger worst-case drops (89.02%, 85.55%) and degrade more under geometric variants. HIR also surpasses purpose-built AIGC forensics methods (ECCV'20, CVPR'20a, CVPR'20b) by 15–21 percentage points in the 1/9 training ratio setting (94.73% vs. 73.74–79.67%). The only scenario where HIR does not lead is the direct AIGC protocol where Cosine SVM and Wavelet SVM achieve slightly higher average scores (98.03%, 97.76%), though HIR still dominates on worst-case robustness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies the classical invariant theory of orthogonal moments (Qi et al. 2021, 2022) with the hierarchical cascading structure of CNNs, producing a fully transparent (non-learned) representation that is equivariant by construction at every layer. It departs from scattering networks (Bruna & Mallat 2013) and group equivariant CNNs (Cohen & Welling 2016) by achieving continuous, one-shot equivariance that propagates across layers without the parallel multi-channel symmetry-sampling framework, thereby avoiding the exponential computational blowup those methods incur. The practical data-adaptability strategies (NAS-like feature selection, cascading with learned CNNs) borrow concepts from architecture search and hybrid representation learning, but the core representation remains hand-crafted and interpretable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a principled, transparent alternative to black-box CNNs for robust and interpretable vision, with the practical advantage that invariance is guaranteed by construction rather than learned from data. However, the experiments are conducted on relatively small-scale benchmarks (10K digit images, 12K AIGC images per generator), and the scaling invariance is explicitly restricted by discrete scale sampling, leaving open whether the approach maintains its competitive edge at ImageNet-scale classification or on more diverse forensic scenarios. The finding that scattering networks fail to deliver their theoretically expected invariance under rotation (56% F1 vs. HIR's 95%) is a useful negative result, but the broader question of whether hierarchical invariants can replace learned representations in large-scale, high-variability tasks remains unresolved.
