## Problem

Shortcut learning in transformers remains difficult to address because existing detection methods (e.g., Grad-CAM, saliency maps) fail to provide users with interpretable information about which spurious features the model exploits, while mitigation methods either require group annotations unavailable in practice or are computationally expensive due to full retraining. No prior framework simultaneously offers unsupervised, interpretable shortcut detection and efficient mitigation specifically for transformer architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251975/cropped_page2_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a fine-tuned ViT-B-16 model and a holdout validation set of standard images (RGB skin lesions, knee X-rays, or natural images at 224×224). It produces three outputs: (1) a text description of the detected spurious concept per cluster (generated by LLaVA + LLM), (2) a binary per-patch ablation mask for any test image via a key-space KNN classifier, and (3) the final class prediction from a retrained last linear layer operating on the ablated token sequence. The ablation removes individual 16×16 patch tokens after positional embedding, so the remaining token count varies per image.

## Method

The pipeline operates in five stages on a fine-tuned ViT-B-16: (1) mean-pool last-block patch embeddings, reduce via PCA to 50 dimensions, and K-means cluster into two groups; (2) select the 20 nearest-to-centroid images per cluster and score each patch's prototypicality as its mean key-space distance (averaged over 12 attention heads) to all patches in the other cluster, retaining the top 200 patches; (3) caption these prototypical patches with LLaVA and distill cluster-level concepts with an LLM, while an unsupervised heuristic (cluster homogeneity + dominant/non-dominant Brier scores) automatically flags the shortcut cluster; (4) train a KNN classifier on balanced positive (high-prototypicality) and negative (low-prototypicality) key vectors to classify each test-image patch as spurious or not; (5) ablate flagged patch tokens from the sequence and retrain only the final linear classification head on the ablated embeddings. The key insight is that transformers accept a variable number of tokens, enabling patch removal without architectural modification, and that key-space similarity provides a lightweight, interpretable proxy for spurious-feature presence.

## Results

On ISIC, the proposed ASM framework achieves 74.7% worst-group accuracy (WGA) and 88.7% average group accuracy (AGA), outperforming the best baseline (JTT at 66.3% WGA) by 8.4 points. On Knee Radiographs, ASM reaches 81.7% WGA and 81.0% AGA, a dramatic improvement over the baseline (37.3% WGA) and all competitors (best baseline: SUBG at 47.7% WGA). On ImageNet-W, ASM attains 87.0% WGA and 95.3% AGA, surpassing the next-best methods (SUBG and DFR at 80.3% WGA) by 6.7 points. Total runtime is 88–121 seconds, substantially faster than JTT (489–2886 s) while matching or exceeding DFR's speed (27–48 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ASM outperforms all three baselines on worst-group accuracy across every dataset. On ISIC, ASM (74.7% WGA) beats JTT (66.3%), DFR (59.3%), and SUBG (52.7%) by 8.4, 15.4, and 22.0 points respectively. On Knee Radiographs, the margin is even larger: ASM (81.7% WGA) surpasses SUBG (47.7%), DFR (41.7%), and JTT (40.0%) by 34, 40, and 42 points. On ImageNet-W, ASM (87.0% WGA) leads SUBG and DFR (both 80.3%) by 6.7 points and JTT (72.0%) by 15 points. In terms of average group accuracy, ASM also achieves the best or near-best results (88.7%, 81.0%, 95.3%), while SUBG notably degrades AGA on Knee Radiographs (57.3% vs. 76.5% baseline). Computationally, ASM runs in 88–121 s, making it ~24× faster than JTT on ISIC and ~16× faster on Knee Radiographs, while remaining competitive with DFR (27–48 s) despite requiring no group annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads—prototype learning for interpretable concept extraction (Chen et al., 2019), MLLM-guided concept bottleneck identification (Yang et al., 2023), and key-space token representations (Bolya et al., 2023)—into a single pipeline for shortcut detection and mitigation in ViTs. It departs from prior shortcut mitigation work in two key ways: unlike annotation-dependent methods (SUBG, DFR) it requires no group labels, and unlike unsupervised methods (MaskTune, Feature Sieve, Correct-n-Contrast) it explicitly identifies and describes the spurious attribute rather than blindly suppressing features. The inference-time token ablation strategy, leveraging the transformer's variable-length input, is a practical departure from the data-editing-and-retraining paradigm dominant in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a critical gap for deploying ML in sensitive domains like medical diagnostics, where both interpretability and the absence of spurious reliance are essential, yet prior methods forced a trade-off between the two. The user study (91.2% identification accuracy from text alone) validates that the generated concept descriptions are genuinely actionable for domain experts. Key limitations include the restriction to binary classification with exactly two clusters, the finding that transformers are already resistant to many classic shortcuts (Waterbirds >90% WGA), and the acknowledged risk that eliminating one shortcut may expose previously hidden ones (observed on CelebA). Open questions include scaling to multi-class, multi-shortcut settings and validating on more complex, real-world medical datasets.
