## Problem

General-purpose vision-language models struggle with museum exhibits, which require detailed interdisciplinary knowledge (age, origin, material, cultural relevance) and structured attribute prediction. Existing cultural-heritage VQA datasets are small (≤500K images), focus narrowly on art, or lack the multilingual breadth needed for real-world museum settings, leaving no large-scale resource to train or benchmark domain-specific VLMs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250256/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a single museum exhibit image (front-view or alternative-angle photograph) paired with a natural-language question drawn from 63 manually crafted templates (e.g., 'Which primary material is the object made of?'). The output is a free-form text answer, which may be a list when multiple values apply. For fair BLIP-vs-LLaVA comparison, training is restricted to medium-length attributes (<100 words) to respect BLIP's 512-token input limit, while the full dataset including long attributes is released separately. Evaluation spans English and 37 non-English languages across five task variants.

## Method

The paper constructs MUSEUM-65 (65M images, 200M QA pairs) by scraping three international aggregators (DPLA, Europeana, Smithsonian) plus 12 individual museums, then reformulating each museum's attribute-value metadata into question-answer pairs using 63 diverse question templates crafted by 10 domain experts over 3 months. Two VLMs are fine-tuned on this dataset: BLIP (encoder-decoder with BERT-base, 110M text params) and LLaVA (Llama-7B instruction-tuned LLM), both trained with batch size 512 on 64× H100 GPUs, selecting one random question per image per epoch. Five benchmark tasks probe general VQA, category-wise accuracy, viewpoint robustness (MultiAngle), reasoning beyond visual features (Visually Unanswerable Questions), and multilingual comprehension. The key empirical finding is that LLaVA's larger LLM backbone enables reasoning that links visual cues to external knowledge (e.g., identifying a painter's mentor), while BLIP's limited capacity causes catastrophic forgetting on complex tasks after fine-tuning, and both fine-tuned models substantially outperform zero-shot SOTA VLMs on domain-specific attributes.

## Results

On the MUSEUM-65 benchmark, fine-tuned LLaVA (20M images, 1 epoch) achieves 57.00% Title accuracy and 70.00% Place accuracy, substantially outperforming all zero-shot SOTA models (best: Gemini 1.5B flash at 27.08% Title, 32.98% Place). Fine-tuned BLIP (20M images, 5 epochs) reaches 52.00% Title and 61.00% Place, also far exceeding its zero-shot counterpart (3.00% and 5.00%). On the general VQA task, LLaVA20mn-1ep attains 80% partial precision and 64% complete precision, while semantic metrics (METEOR 58.85, WMD accuracy 87.02) confirm strong domain understanding after fine-tuning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Fine-tuned LLaVA (20M, 1 ep) outperforms the original LLaVA by a wide margin: 57.00% vs. 10.13% on Title and 70.00% vs. 23.42% on Place, demonstrating that domain-specific fine-tuning is essential for museum VQA. Similarly, fine-tuned BLIP (20M, 5 ep) improves from 3.00% to 52.00% on Title and from 5.00% to 61.00% on Place over the original BLIP, a roughly 17× and 12× gain respectively. Both fine-tuned models also surpass Gemini 1.5B flash (27.08% Title, 32.98% Place) and GPT-4o (22.03% Title, 33.33% Place) in zero-shot mode, with LLaVA-ours leading by 30+ points on Title and 37+ points on Place. On the Visually Unanswerable Questions task, fine-tuned LLaVA (31.37% partial precision) outperforms both original LLaVA (24.27%) and fine-tuned BLIP (2.35%), while BLIP actually degrades after fine-tuning (6.08% → 2.35%), suggesting catastrophic forgetting due to its smaller capacity. On MultiLanguage evaluation, original LLaVA retains stronger multilingual partial precision (41.81% French) than fine-tuned LLaVA (10.37%), indicating some language-specific forgetting from English-only fine-tuning, though fine-tuned LLaVA still edges out on complete precision and BLEU-2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the cultural heritage VQA line of work (MUZE, VISCOUNTH, iMet) by scaling from tens to hundreds of thousands of images up to 65M images and 200M QA pairs, and by broadening the domain from primarily art to encompass natural sciences, history, and pre-history. Methodologically, it fine-tunes two architecturally distinct VLMs—BLIP (encoder-decoder with BERT-base) and LLaVA (instruction-tuned Llama-7B)—on the curated dataset, positioning the contribution as a dataset-and-benchmark paper rather than a new architecture. The paper explicitly departs from MUZE's separate-attention-head design for individual attributes, arguing that a unified Q&A formulation is more scalable and better aligned with real visitor queries.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 65M-image, 200M-QA dataset is the largest publicly available museum-exhibit resource to date, and the five-task benchmark (general VQA, category-wise, MultiAngle, Visually Unanswerable, MultiLanguage) provides a structured evaluation protocol that prior cultural-heritage datasets lacked. A notable limitation is that fine-tuning in English degrades multilingual performance, and BLIP exhibits catastrophic forgetting on reasoning-heavy tasks, suggesting that domain adaptation and general knowledge retention remain in tension. Open questions include whether multilingual fine-tuning on the 15M non-English samples can recover cross-lingual ability, how to mitigate the European/North-American origin bias inherent in the aggregator sources, and whether the fine-tuned models' outperformance of human experts generalizes beyond memorized attribute lookups to genuinely novel exhibits.
