## Problem

Existing drag-based image editing methods manipulate the latent space of generative models (e.g., Stable Diffusion), which causes imprecise control when control points are downscaled to latent resolution, eliminates real-time visual feedback during editing, and produces unrealistic results for large occlusions. These approaches also require minutes per edit and are tied to specific generative architectures, limiting their practicality and extensibility.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251481/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, a user-drawn binary region mask defining the deformable area, and one or more handle-target point pairs specifying the desired displacement. An optional SAM-based refinement module refines the mask boundary by constraining SAM predictions within dilated/eroded versions of the user mask. The pipeline produces a real-time warped preview (0.01s) showing the deformed content with grid overlays marking inpainting regions, a computed inpainting mask (dilated unmapped areas plus dilated warped boundaries), and a final edited image (0.3s at 512×512) after inpainting fills revealed regions.

## Method

Inpaint4Drag decomposes drag editing into pixel-space bidirectional warping followed by standard image inpainting, treating masked regions as elastic materials inspired by physical deformation. Forward warping uses inverse distance weighting to map source pixels to target positions, establishing the deformed contour and initial correspondences; backward mapping then fills sampling gaps by computing source positions for every target pixel via local nearest-neighbor interpolation, ensuring complete pixel coverage. The inpainting mask is derived from dilated unmapped regions (original mask minus warped mask) and dilated warped boundaries, creating buffer zones for smooth transitions. An optional SAM-based mask refinement module constrains automatic segmentation within user-specified dilated/eroded boundaries to capture precise object edges while preserving user intent. The framework requires no training and serves as a universal adapter: any inpainting model (LaMa, SD-Inpaint, etc.) can be plugged in without architectural modification, automatically inheriting future inpainting improvements.

## Results

On DragBench-S and DragBench-D, Inpaint4Drag achieves the lowest Mean Distance (MD) scores of 3.6 and 3.9 respectively, indicating the most precise drag control among all compared methods. It also attains the best LPIPS on DragBench-D (9.1) and ties SDE-Drag on DragBench-S (11.4), reflecting strong identity preservation. In terms of efficiency, Inpaint4Drag completes editing in 0.3 s with only 2.7 GB GPU memory, making it 14× faster than FastDrag (4.2 s) and ~600× faster than DragDiffusion (177.7 s) at 512×512 resolution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Inpaint4Drag outperforms all four baselines on Mean Distance (drag precision), achieving 3.6/3.9 on DragBench-S/D versus 4.1/5.1 for FastDrag, 7.0/6.7 for DragDiffusion, 7.5/8.1 for SDE-Drag, and 23.6/22.1 for DiffEditor. On LPIPS (identity preservation), it ties SDE-Drag on DragBench-S (11.4) and clearly wins on DragBench-D (9.1 vs. 10.2 for DragDiffusion, 10.9 for DiffEditor, 13.5 for FastDrag, 14.9 for SDE-Drag). The speed advantage is dramatic: 0.3 s per edit versus 4.2 s (FastDrag), 43.1 s (DiffEditor), 126.1 s (SDE-Drag), and 177.7 s (DragDiffusion), while also using the least GPU memory (2.7 GB vs. 5.0–11.6 GB). FastDrag is the closest competitor on MD but lags by ~10–20% and produces substantially worse LPIPS (24.1 on DragBench-S), indicating poorer content preservation. DiffEditor, despite being relatively fast, shows the worst drag precision (MD 23.6/22.1), suggesting its feature-space approach struggles with accurate point displacement.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the dominant paradigm in drag-based image editing, where methods like DragDiffusion, SDE-Drag, and FastDrag manipulate the latent space of diffusion or GAN models to achieve deformation. Instead, Inpaint4Drag reframes the problem as a two-stage pipeline: pixel-space bidirectional warping (inspired by elastic deformation in computer graphics) followed by standard image inpainting, effectively repurposing off-the-shelf inpainting models (LaMa, SD-Inpaint) as the content-generation backbone. It also incorporates SAM for optional mask refinement, borrowing from the segmentation literature to resolve boundary ambiguity that plagues sparse control-point approaches. The method thus carves new ground by showing that the drag-editing task need not be solved within a generative model's latent space at all.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 'universal adapter' framing is practically significant: any future improvement in inpainting quality automatically transfers to drag editing without retraining or architectural changes, decoupling geometric control from content synthesis. The 600× speedup over DragDiffusion and real-time 10 ms warping previews address a genuine usability bottleneck in current interactive editing tools. However, the approach inherits limitations of its inpainting component (e.g., texture coherence over large revealed areas), the warping is purely geometric and does not learn object-aware deformations, and evaluation is limited to two small benchmarks (100 and 205 samples). Open questions include whether the pipeline generalizes to 3D or video editing, and whether learned warping could further improve coherence for highly non-rigid deformations.
