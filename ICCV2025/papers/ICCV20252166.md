## Problem

Existing unsupervised low-light image enhancement methods rely on fixed spatial operations—uniform smoothing kernels, predetermined adjustment curves, or static neighborhood assumptions—that cannot adapt to spatially varying illumination. This rigidity leads to over-smoothing in homogeneous regions, artifact amplification at high-contrast edges, and an inability to balance local texture preservation with global illumination consistency. The paper asks whether pixels can instead be treated as adaptive agents whose interactions are dynamically regulated, drawing on swarm dynamics principles.

## Input / Output

The method takes a low-light RGB image (resized to 64×64 for training) and produces an enhanced RGB image of the same resolution. Each pixel is processed through its 8-connected neighborhood, and the policy network operates on local 3×3 patches, making computational complexity independent of image resolution. The model is trained on the LOL-v1 dataset (500 pairs, 15 held out for evaluation) and evaluated zero-shot on LOL-v2, LSRW, DICM, VV, MEF, NPE, and ExDark, with a discount factor of zero so the agent responds only to immediate reward signals.

## Method

PASD models each pixel as an agent whose normalized intensity evolves via a dynamical equation with an adaptive step size that naturally constrains updates to the [0,1] range. Two inter-pixel cooperative constraints regulate the swarm: a consensus-driven boundary protocol that dynamically adjusts allowable inter-pixel intensity differences based on local neighborhood statistics (using a sigmoid adaptation gate sensitive to local brightness), and a rate alignment protocol that penalizes directional conflicts among neighboring pixels' control actions. A shared-parameter PPO-based multi-agent reinforcement learning framework optimizes the interaction policy, where the policy network is a single 3×3 convolution (32 channels, ELU) followed by layer normalization and two 1×1 convolutional heads outputting Gaussian mean and variance for each pixel's action. The reward is a multi-scale negative penalty over both boundary and alignment losses, with coarser scales obtained by average-pooling 3×3 regions, ensuring strategy consistency across spatial granularities.

## Results

On the paired LOL-v1 test set, PASD achieves 21.90 dB PSNR and 0.84 SSIM, surpassing the best unsupervised baseline CUE (21.67 dB / 0.77 SSIM) by +0.23 dB and +0.07 SSIM. On LOL-v2-real, PASD reaches 22.31 dB / 0.87 SSIM, outperforming QuadPrior (21.39 dB / 0.86 SSIM) by nearly 1 dB in PSNR. On the unpaired DICM dataset, PASD attains the lowest NIQE (2.49) and BRISQUE (22.45) among all unsupervised methods. For low-light object detection on ExDark, PASD yields a mean mAP of 58.44, edging out Zero-DCE++ (58.14) and SCI (56.56).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PASD outperforms all 8 unsupervised baselines on LOL-v1 PSNR (21.90 vs. CUE's 21.67, QuadPrior's 20.31, PairLIE's 19.51) and SSIM (0.84 vs. QuadPrior's 0.80, CUE's 0.77). On LOL-v2-real, PASD leads by a substantial margin over QuadPrior (+0.92 dB PSNR) and CUE (+1.49 dB PSNR), while on LOL-v2-syn it trails QuadPrior (19.39 vs. 20.33 dB) and CUE (19.39 vs. 19.10 dB is a narrow win), indicating weaker generalization to synthetic degradations. Among zero-shot methods (Zero-DCE, Zero-DCE++, SCI), PASD gains 5–7 dB in PSNR, reflecting the advantage of learned multi-agent coordination over fixed curve estimation. On unpaired datasets (Table 2), PASD achieves the best NIQE on DICM (2.49) and NPE (2.82) and the best BRISQUE on DICM (22.45) and NPE (24.21), but lags behind QuadPrior on VV BRISQUE (27.90 vs. 19.19) and CUE on MEF NIQE (4.06 vs. 3.08). For downstream ExDark detection, PASD attains the highest mean mAP (58.44) over Zero-DCE++ (58.14) and SCI (56.56), winning on 7 of 12 object categories.

## Relation to Prior Work

PASD carves new ground by repurposing swarm dynamics principles (separation, cohesion, alignment) from collective behavior literature and applying them as pixel-level cooperative constraints for image enhancement, departing from the fixed spatial operations (uniform kernels, predetermined curves) that dominate Retinex-based and zero-shot methods. On the RL side, it extends the pixel-wise action selection paradigm of PixelRL and RELLIE by introducing a shared multi-agent policy with multi-scale neighborhood rewards, though it sets the discount factor to zero, effectively reducing the sequential decision-making to a single-step optimization. The method does not directly build on any single prior architecture but synthesizes ideas from swarm intelligence, PPO-style policy optimization, and adaptive boundary constraints into a unified closed-loop framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The notably lightweight architecture (a single 3×3 convolution plus two 1×1 convolutions) achieving competitive unsupervised results offers a practical efficiency angle for deployment-constrained settings, and the swarm-dynamics framing provides an interpretable alternative to black-box curve estimation. However, the authors acknowledge that performance degrades under severe degradation (LOL-v2-syn, MEF), and the zero discount factor raises questions about whether the RL formulation adds genuine value over simpler iterative optimization. Open questions include whether the multi-agent coordination scales to higher resolutions, whether the boundary protocol generalizes beyond 8-connected neighborhoods, and whether the modest SOTA margins (+0.23 dB PSNR) justify the added complexity of the RL training loop.
