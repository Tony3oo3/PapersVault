## Problem

Existing diffusion-based image inpainting methods operate exclusively on RGB images, leaving transparent (RGBA) images unsupported. The conventional workaround of inpainting RGB content and then extracting an alpha map via matting or segmentation fails to preserve transparency consistency in edited regions and introduces jagged edges along transparency boundaries. No dedicated method existed for directly inpainting transparent images while maintaining RGB-alpha alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251276/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGBA image (512×512 for SD1.5/BrushNet, 1024×1024 for SDXL), an inpainting mask, and a text prompt as input, and produces an inpainted RGBA image with aligned RGB and alpha content. The RGBA image is decomposed into a padded RGB image and its alpha channel, treated as a two-frame video with a 5D tensor (b×c×2×h×w), which is reshaped along the batch dimension to pass through the frozen pretrained diffusion model. The adapter is plug-and-play, integrating into either SD-Inpainting (expanded input channels) or BrushNet (trainable inpainting branch), and supports controllable editing via ControlNet.

## Method

Trans-Adapter inflates a pretrained T2I diffusion model to process the RGB and alpha channels jointly as a two-frame video, with three trainable components: (1) an Alpha Map LoRA that teaches the frozen U-Net to generate alpha maps, using a distinct text prompt prefix ('alpha map of') to separate RGB and alpha inpainting strategies; (2) a spatial alignment module in shallower U-Net layers that applies zero-initialized convolution to enforce pixel-level synchronization between RGB and alpha latents; and (3) cross-domain self-attention at the U-Net bottleneck that allows the masked alpha region to reference surrounding areas for high-frequency details like hair. Training follows a two-stage strategy: first the Alpha Map LoRA is trained alone, then all three modules are jointly fine-tuned. All output projections are zero-initialized (following ControlNet) so the pretrained model's capabilities are preserved during training, and only the new modules are updated while original weights remain frozen.

## Results

On LayerBench, Trans-Adapter achieves the best LPIPS across all settings, reaching 0.0408 (SD1.5-inpaint, Blended Noise) vs. 0.0515 for BiRefNet and 0.0461 for ZIM. It also attains the highest Aesthetic Score of 6.097 under SD1.5-inpaint Blended Noise (vs. 6.055 BiRefNet) and the best CLIP Similarity of 27.332 under SDXL-inpaint Pure Noise (vs. 27.126 ZIM). On the proposed AEQ metric, Trans-Adapter scores 0.9871–0.9878, competitive with BiRefNet (0.9843–0.9889) and substantially outperforming U2-Net (0.8093–0.9549).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Trans-Adapter consistently outperforms all two-stage baselines (inpainting + matting/segmentation) on LPIPS, the perceptual quality metric, by a clear margin (e.g., 0.0408 vs. 0.0515 for BiRefNet under SD1.5-inpaint Blended Noise). On Aesthetic Score, it wins under SD1.5-inpaint Blended Noise (6.097 vs. 6.055 BiRefNet) and SD1.5-BrushNet (6.053 vs. 5.954 BiRefNet), but trails BiRefNet slightly under SDXL-inpaint Pure Noise (6.042 vs. 6.087). On CLIP Similarity, Trans-Adapter achieves the best score under SDXL-inpaint Pure Noise (27.332 vs. 27.126 ZIM) but is marginally lower than ZIM under SD1.5-inpaint Pure Noise (26.870 vs. 27.061). On the proposed AEQ metric, Trans-Adapter is competitive with BiRefNet (within ~0.002) but U2-Net lags dramatically (0.8093–0.9549 vs. ~0.987), confirming that dichotomous segmentation introduces severe alpha-edge artifacts. Against LayerDiffusion (the only other direct RGBA inpainting method), Trans-Adapter improves LPIPS from 0.0903 to 0.0632 and AEQ from 0.9760 to 0.9844 under SDXL-inpaint Pure Noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

Trans-Adapter extends the plug-and-play inpainting paradigm established by SD-Inpainting, ControlNet, and BrushNet by treating the RGBA image as a two-frame 'video' (RGB + alpha), a conceptual borrowing from AnimateDiff's temporal attention design. It departs from the conventional two-stage pipeline (RGB inpainting followed by separate matting via ZIM, BiRefNet, or U2-Net) by jointly generating both channels with explicit spatial alignment and cross-domain self-attention constraints. The RGB padding strategy is inherited from LayerDiffusion, and the LoRA-based alpha map conditioning follows standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning practice, making the overall design a targeted architectural extension rather than a new generative paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first dedicated transparent image inpainting method, filling a practical gap for film, animation, and game production workflows where artists currently must hand-paint or use error-prone two-stage pipelines. The introduction of LayerBench and the AEQ metric provides the field with the first purpose-built evaluation infrastructure for RGBA inpainting quality. Limitations include modest quantitative gains over strong two-stage baselines (e.g., LPIPS improvements of ~0.01), evaluation restricted to SD1.5/SDXL/BrushNet, and the AEQ metric being a learned classifier whose reliability depends on its training distribution. Open questions include scalability to multi-layer compositing, video transparency inpainting, and whether the alignment constraints generalize to more complex alpha structures (e.g., semi-transparent materials).
