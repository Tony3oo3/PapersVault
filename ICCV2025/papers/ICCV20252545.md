## Problem

Existing methods for improving adversarial transferability (e.g., PGN, RAP) rely solely on first-order gradient regularization to steer adversarial examples toward flat loss maxima, leaving higher-order curvature properties unexplored. A naive second-order approach of minimizing the Hessian trace is counterproductive because the Hessian is provably negative semi-definite near local loss maxima, so minimizing the trace would amplify sharpness. The paper identifies this sign issue and proposes a corrected second-order regularizer that explicitly penalizes the negative Hessian trace.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural RGB image (299×299 for ImageNet-compatible, 32×32 for CIFAR-10) and its ground-truth label, and produces a single adversarial example constrained to an L1-norm ball of radius ε=16/255 around the input. During optimization, the method additionally samples N=20 random points from a neighborhood of radius ξ=3ε around the current adversarial example and evaluates the loss at two perturbed copies of each sample (±h along the L1-normalized gradient direction), requiring three forward passes per sample per iteration. The generated adversarial example is then evaluated for transferability against a suite of normally trained and adversarially trained target models (CNNs and ViTs).

## Method

NHTR introduces a second-order curvature regularizer based on the trace of the Hessian of the loss with respect to the adversarial example. The trace is approximated via Hutchinson's estimator combined with a central finite-difference formula (Theorem 1), restricted to the L1-normalized gradient direction to match the L1 constraint. The key theoretical insight is that the Hessian is negative semi-definite at and near local loss maxima, making the trace consistently negative; therefore, the regularizer penalizes the *negative* trace (i.e., pushes the trace toward zero from below) rather than minimizing it directly. The final attack objective (Eq. 15) becomes a weighted combination of the loss at a random neighborhood sample and the losses at two gradient-direction perturbations of that sample, controlled by a balanced coefficient δ=0.3. This objective is seamlessly integrated into the MI-FGSM momentum loop (Algorithm 1), where the gradient for each iteration is a mixture of the direct gradient at the sample and the averaged gradient at the two perturbed points, weighted by δ and (1−δ) respectively.

## Results

On the ImageNet-compatible benchmark (1,000 images, ε=16/255, 10 iterations), NHTR achieves average black-box attack success rates of 69.7% (Inc-v3 surrogate), 72.3% (Inc-v4), 78.8% (Res-101), and 67.8% (ViT-B) across five normally trained target models. Against adversarially trained defenses (Inc-v3ens3, Inc-v3ens4, Inc-Res-v2ens, RS, NRP, DiffPure, RDC), NHTR achieves 70.8% on Inc-v3ens3 and 78.9% on RS when using Inc-v3 as surrogate, outperforming the strongest baseline (ANDA) by 4–5 percentage points. In the ensemble setting (Inc-v3 + Inc-v4 + Res-101), NHTR reaches 90.5% average on normally trained targets and 86.3% on adversarially trained targets. The Hessian trace at generated adversarial examples is reduced to 5.2 (negative) by NHTR versus 9.3 for PGN and 12.5 for RAP, confirming flatter loss maxima.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NHTR outperforms all eight baselines across every surrogate model and target model combination. Against the closest competitor PGN [13], NHTR gains +5.6 pp (Inc-v3→), +5.8 pp (Inc-v4→), +9.5 pp (Res-101→), and +4.9 pp (ViT-B→) in average attack success rate. Compared to RAP [30], the margin is larger: +15.9 pp on Inc-v3→ and +15.9 pp on Res-101→. Against the momentum-based baselines MI [4], NI [20], VMI [40], EMI [42], and MIG [24], NHTR improves by 21–39 pp depending on the surrogate. Against the most recent baseline ANDA [10], NHTR leads by +6.1 pp (Inc-v3→) and +7.4 pp (Res-101→). In the ensemble setting (Table 3), NHTR achieves 90.5% average on normally trained targets, surpassing PGN by 7.7 pp and RAP by 17.4 pp, and 86.3% on adversarially trained targets, exceeding EMI by 43.4 pp.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work connecting flat loss maxima to adversarial transferability, building directly on first-order approaches like PGN (gradient norm penalty) and RAP (worst-case perturbation injection). The key conceptual departure is moving from first-order gradient regularization to a second-order Hessian trace regularizer, leveraging the observation that at local loss maxima the Hessian is negative semi-definite, so the trace is consistently negative. This necessitates penalizing the *negative* trace rather than minimizing the trace directly—a subtle but critical distinction that prior first-order methods sidestep. The efficient Hessian trace estimation via Hutchinson's stochastic estimator combined with finite differences along the gradient direction draws on tools from the Hessian-based generalization literature (e.g., CR-SAM) but applies them to the adversarial attack setting for the first time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NHTR provides a principled second-order mechanism for guiding adversarial examples toward flatter maxima, and its modular design allows seamless integration with existing gradient-based and input-transformation attacks, making it a practical drop-in enhancement for the adversarial attack toolkit. The consistent improvements over PGN and RAP across multiple surrogate models, target architectures (CNNs and ViTs), ensemble settings, and adversarial defenses validate the approach broadly. However, the method incurs a 1.5× computational overhead over PGN (3 gradient evaluations vs. 2 per iteration) plus neighborhood sampling, and the gains, while consistent, are moderate (typically 3–8 percentage points over the best first-order baseline). Open questions include whether the Hessian trace regularizer extends to targeted attacks, physical-world perturbations, or very large foundation models where the neighborhood sampling cost becomes prohibitive.
