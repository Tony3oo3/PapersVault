## Problem

Existing bimanual manipulation methods rely on integrated control frameworks that jointly encode both arms' observations and predict a single 14-dimensional action vector, ignoring the fundamental distinction between coordinated and uncoordinated tasks. This high-dimensional action space hinders each arm from learning its own actions effectively, while the implicit interaction modeling (joint encoding of both arms' states) cannot capture the varying cooperation requirements across different phases of coordinated tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251848/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each arm's model independently consumes its own point cloud observation (N×3 from depth cameras) and 7-dimensional joint angle state from the ROS interface, plus a modulated 7-dimensional state feature vector from the other arm. The output is a 7-dimensional joint angle action per arm (rather than a combined 14-dimensional vector), with the action generator conditioned on the concatenation of the arm's own visual features, its own state features, and the selectively modulated exchanged state features from the other arm.

## Method

The Decoupled Interaction Framework assigns an independent model to each arm, reducing the action prediction dimension from 14 to 7 and simplifying the learning landscape for uncoordinated tasks. On top of this decoupled design, a selective interaction module exchanges encoded state features between arms and adaptively modulates them: an MLP predicts a scaling factor α ∈ [0,1] to control the intensity of the received state features, and a second MLP predicts a bias vector β to align them with the current arm's state space, both conditioned solely on the current arm's own observations and states. The modulated exchanged features are then combined with the arm's original visual and state representations as interactive conditions for an action generator (DDIM diffusion or Flow Matching), enabling the framework to satisfy coordination requirements in coordinated tasks while preserving independence for uncoordinated tasks. The total loss is the sum of per-arm action generation losses, and the framework is plug-and-play, seamlessly integrating into existing methods like DP3 and Point Flow Matching.

## Results

On the RoboTwin benchmark (7 tasks: 2 coordinated, 5 uncoordinated), the Decoupled Interaction Framework achieves a 78.9% average success rate, surpassing the previous SOTA DP3 (55.4%) by 23.5%. On coordinated tasks, the method reaches 70.0% (vs. DP3's 46.5%), and on uncoordinated tasks it reaches 75.0% (vs. DP3's 59.0%). In the three-arm multi-agent extension on Sapien, the framework achieves a 28% improvement over DP3, and in real-world experiments it scores 45/60 vs. DP3's 27/60.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against five baselines spanning integrated control (ACT, DP, DP3, PFM) and decoupled (Voxact-b) paradigms. Against the previous SOTA DP3, the Decoupled Interaction Framework wins by 23.5% in average success rate (78.9% vs. 55.4%), with particularly large gains on coordinated tasks (70.0% vs. 46.5%) and uncoordinated tasks (75.0% vs. 59.0%). Against the decoupled baseline Voxact-b, the method wins by 23.8% (78.9% vs. 55.1%), demonstrating that the selective interaction module is critical for coordinated tasks where Voxact-b lacks explicit inter-arm modeling. Against the regression-based ACT baseline, the method wins by 63.0% (78.9% vs. 15.9%), and against the diffusion-based DP baseline by 60.0% (78.9% vs. 18.9%). In the integration experiment (Table 4), applying the decoupled design to PFM (Flow Matching) yields a 36.3% improvement (78.9% vs. 42.6%), confirming the framework's flexibility across action generators.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two existing paradigms in bimanual manipulation: integrated control frameworks (ACT, DP, DP3) that use a single model to predict both arms' actions jointly, and fully decoupled frameworks (Voxact-b) that assign independent models per arm with no inter-arm communication. It builds directly on DP3's point cloud backbone and DDIM/Flow Matching action generators, and on PFM's flow matching formulation, but departs from both by introducing a selective interaction module that adaptively modulates exchanged state features using per-arm scaling factors and bias vectors. The conceptual grounding comes from the bimanual manipulation taxonomy of Krebs and Asfour [17], which distinguishes coordinated from uncoordinated tasks—a distinction the authors argue prior integrated frameworks implicitly ignore.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a practical, drop-in architectural modification that yields large gains (23.5% over SOTA) without requiring new data or modalities, making it immediately adoptable by groups working on bimanual or multi-agent manipulation. The finding that a model at 1/6 the parameter size of DP3 still outperforms it by 16.5% suggests that architectural inductive bias (decoupling + selective interaction) can substitute for raw capacity, which has implications for deployment on resource-constrained robots. Open questions include whether the selective interaction module generalizes to more than two agents beyond the single 3-arm experiment, whether the taxonomy-based design transfers to tasks with continuous (rather than discrete) coordination requirements, and how the framework performs under distribution shift in real-world settings beyond the limited 4-task, 15-trial evaluation.
