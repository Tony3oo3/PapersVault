## Problem

Real-world HS-PAN satellites (e.g., PRISMA) produce hyperspectral images that are simultaneously noisy and low-resolution, yet prior work treated pandenoising and pansharpening as independent sequential steps, causing error accumulation and preventing the two tasks from exploiting their synergistic potential. No prior study had formulated a unified end-to-end framework that jointly denoises and super-resolves an HSI guided by a panchromatic image.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251138/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a noisy low-resolution HSI (e.g., 64×64×32) and a high-resolution PAN image (e.g., 256×256) as input, and produces a clean high-resolution HSI (256×256×32) as output. Training is fully unsupervised—no ground-truth HRHS is available—so the network is constrained only by the observed NLRHS, HRPAN, and their downsampled LRPAN counterpart. The spatial upscaling factor s (here 4×) is fixed, and the PAN image is downsampled to match the LRHS resolution for the denoising branch while remaining at full resolution for the super-resolution branch.

## Method

UHipandas comprises three interconnected networks: a Guided Denoising Network (GDN) that removes noise from the LRHS using the LRPAN as spatial guide, a Guided Super-Resolution Network (GSRN) that injects high-frequency details via a detail-injection formulation (Ĥ = L̂↑ + f(L̂↑, P)), and a Panchromatic Reconstruction Network (PRN) that enforces modality fidelity by reconstructing the PAN from the restored HSI. Both GDN and GSRN are built as guided low-rank matrix factorization networks whose outputs are expressed as V ×₃ U (r base images × spectral coefficients with r ≪ b), encoding an HSI low-rank prior; the key new insight is that the detail map D = H − L↑ is itself low-rank, motivating a detail-oriented low-rank prior embedded in the GSRN's factorization. Because the three networks are chained (GDN output feeds GSRN, GSRN output feeds PRN), a two-stage training strategy is used: Stage 1 pretrains all components at the LR scale with a self-supervised SR loss (treating the denoised LRHS as pseudo-HR ground truth), and Stage 2 finetunes at the HR scale with a consistency loss (Ĥ↓ ≈ L̂) and HR-scale PAN texture loss, preventing the GSRN from inheriting noise bias during early training.

## Results

On the simulated Zhuhai-1 Dongying dataset (81 patches of 256×256×32), UHipandas achieves PSNR 40.61 dB, SSIM 0.9547, ERGAS 8.50, and SAM 3.00° under i.i.d. Gaussian noise (σ=10), outperforming the best sequential baseline (PWTV+RPNN) by 2.3 dB in PSNR. Under stronger noise (σ=30), UHipandas reaches 36.88 dB PSNR vs. 36.70 dB for PWTV+RPNN. On the real-world PRISMA dataset, UHipandas attains the lowest NIQE score of 6.05, compared to 6.80 for PWTV+RPNN and 7.19 for HIRD+RPNN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares UHipandas against 12 sequential combinations of five SOTA methods: HIRD (diffusion-based denoising), PWTV (PAN-guided TV denoising), SwinIR (transformer denoising), PLRD (low-rank diffusion pansharpening), and RPNN (rolling pansharpening CNN). UHipandas dominates all 12 baselines across every noise setting and every metric (PSNR, SSIM, ERGAS, SAM). The strongest sequential baseline is PWTV+RPNN, which UHipandas surpasses by 2.3 dB PSNR at σ=10, 0.18 dB at σ=30, and 0.54 dB under non-i.i.d. noise. Notably, SwinIR-based combinations (SwinIR+PWTV, SwinIR+HIRD) perform poorly, with PSNR dropping below 30 dB and ERGAS exceeding 200 in some settings, indicating that the transformer denoiser introduces severe spectral distortion when chained with pansharpening. The ordering of operations matters: denoising-then-SR (e.g., PWTV+RPNN) consistently outperforms SR-then-denoising (e.g., RPNN+PWTV), confirming the paper's motivation that sequential pipelines accumulate errors. On the real-world PRISMA dataset, UHipandas achieves the best NIQE (6.05) versus PWTV+RPNN (6.80) and HIRD+RPNN (7.19), with only three baselines evaluated due to memory constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate HSI restoration tasks—pandenoising (guided by PAN images) and pansharpening (PAN-guided super-resolution)—into a single end-to-end framework, departing from the sequential pipelines (e.g., PWTV+RPNN, HIRD+RPNN) that prior work treats as independent stages. Methodologically, it draws on the low-rank matrix factorization tradition in HSI restoration but introduces a detail-oriented low-rank prior for the residual detail map, and parametrizes the factorization via guided CNNs rather than iterative optimization. The unsupervised formulation is necessitated by the absence of large-scale paired {NLRHS, HRPAN, HRHS} datasets, and the two-stage training strategy is motivated by the spectral bias property of neural networks to avoid noise-biased learning in the super-resolution branch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Hipandas task directly mirrors what real HS-PAN satellites like PRISMA actually deliver (noisy, low-resolution HSIs alongside clean HRPAN), making the joint formulation more practically relevant than either pansharpening or pandenoising in isolation. The identification of the detail map as inherently low-rank provides a principled prior for detail recovery that could inform other super-resolution or fusion settings. However, validation is limited to a single 32-band dataset with 81 patches and only five real-world samples with three comparison methods, leaving open questions about generalization to higher band counts, different noise regimes, and other satellite systems. The hand-crafted multi-term loss design, while effective, raises questions about robustness and whether the two-stage strategy generalizes beyond the specific architecture used.
