## Problem

Existing NeRV-based video representation methods structure their feature grids as linked lists, enforcing uniform sampling along the temporal axis. This ignores the inherently non-uniform distribution of temporal redundancy in video sequences, leading to under-sampling of high-dynamic regions and wasted bitrate on redundant segments, ultimately degrading Rate-Distortion performance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252589/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video sequence of L frames (each 3×H×W, evaluated at 720P and 1080P resolutions) and learns a mapping from a scalar temporal frame index t_i to a reconstructed frame. Internally, the tree stores feature values of shape h×w×d at each node; given a query t_i, a BST search retrieves the lower and upper bound features, which are linearly interpolated into a time embedding that is then progressively upsampled through cascaded NeRV blocks to produce the final 3×H×W frame prediction.

## Method

Tree-NeRV replaces the conventional linked-list feature grid with a Binary Search Tree where each node holds a temporal key and a spatial feature vector, enabling non-uniform temporal sampling. At query time, a recursive BST traversal locates the bounding keys for a given frame index, and linear interpolation between their feature values yields the time embedding, which is upsampled through NeRV blocks (conv→pixel-shuffle→conv with reduced intermediate channels, achieving ~75% parameter reduction over the original design). During training, an optimization-driven tree-growing strategy starts from a coarse uniform initialization and iteratively inserts new nodes at the midpoints of the TopK highest-reconstruction-error GOPs, adaptively concentrating sampling density in high-motion regions. AVL rotations maintain tree balance after each insertion to preserve O(log n) query efficiency. The entire pipeline is trained end-to-end with L2 pixel loss and the Adan optimizer, requiring no explicit pre-analysis of temporal complexity.

## Results

On the UVG dataset (1080P), Tree-NeRV achieves an average PSNR of 33.36 dB, outperforming the best baseline DS-NeRV (32.63 dB) by 0.73 dB. On DAVIS (1080P), the average PSNR reaches 31.64 dB, a 1.26 dB improvement over DS-NeRV (30.38 dB). On Big Buck Bunny (720P) at 3M parameters, Tree-NeRV scores 38.68 dB PSNR versus 36.85 dB for both FFNeRV and DS-NeRV. In terms of efficiency, Tree-NeRV encodes in ~2 hours (vs. ~6 h for FFNeRV/HNeRV and ~3 h for DS-NeRV) while decoding at 70.14 FPS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Tree-NeRV consistently outperforms all four NeRV-based baselines (NeRV, FFNeRV, HNeRV, DS-NeRV) across all three datasets. On UVG, it leads DS-NeRV by 0.73 dB and FFNeRV by 0.70 dB in average PSNR; on DAVIS the margin widens to 1.26 dB over DS-NeRV. In the video compression R-D curve (Fig. 7), Tree-NeRV achieves comparable or superior bpp-PSNR trade-offs to both H.264 and H.265 standard codecs. For video interpolation on UVG, Tree-NeRV scores 30.16 dB average PSNR, beating DS-NeRV (29.38 dB) by 0.78 dB. On encoding speed, Tree-NeRV is the fastest at ~2 h, roughly 3× faster than FFNeRV and HNeRV (~6 h) and 1.5× faster than DS-NeRV (~3 h), though HNeRV retains the highest decoding FPS (105.90 vs. 70.14) due to its per-frame latent storage bypassing tree queries.

## Relation to Prior Work

Tree-NeRV extends the feature-grid-based NeRV lineage (HNeRV, FFNeRV, DS-NeRV), which replaced Fourier positional encoding with explicit spatial feature grids sampled along the temporal axis. The key departure is replacing the implicit linked-list organization (which enforces uniform temporal sampling) with a Binary Search Tree structure, enabling non-uniform, content-adaptive sampling with O(log n) query complexity. The NeRV block design follows E-NeRV's reduced-channel pixel-shuffle approach, and the training strategy introduces an optimization-driven tree-growing mechanism guided by per-GOP reconstruction error, complemented by AVL balancing to maintain query efficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical inefficiency in NeRV-based video coding—uniform temporal sampling wastes bitrate on static segments while under-sampling dynamic ones—and shows that a simple tree structure with error-driven node insertion can close this gap without complex motion analysis. The encoding speed advantage (~2h vs ~6h for competitors) and the ablation showing 100 adaptive points matching 150–200 uniform points suggest meaningful practical value for real-time or resource-constrained deployment. However, the absence of a node-pruning mechanism limits the method's ability to reduce bitrate after training, and the modest PSNR gains (0.7–1.3 dB) over strong baselines like DS-NeRV indicate the approach, while elegant, does not yet represent a paradigm shift in neural video representation.
