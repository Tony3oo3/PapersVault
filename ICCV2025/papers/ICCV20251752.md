## Problem

Existing multimodal knowledge editing methods maintain modality consistency in both editing and testing, leaving it unknown whether knowledge edited in one modality (e.g., text) transfers to another (e.g., image). This gap is critical because multimodal data is far scarcer than unimodal data, making text-based editing a more practical and cost-effective route for updating MLLMs. The paper formalizes this as Transitivity of Multimodal Knowledge Editing (TMKE) and shows that current methods exhibit significant performance drops when evaluated across modalities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251752/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The editing phase consumes text knowledge triplets (s, r, o) in a question-answer format, while the testing phase uses images of the subject paired with rephrased or multi-hop questions. The TMKE Benchmark contains 3,676 examples (2,352 train / 1,324 test) spanning 101 entity types, each with two Google-sourced images per subject. Outputs include per-metric scores (Reliability, Generality, Portability, Locality, Stability) for each method–model pair, plus Knowledge Neuron activation overlap values (M1, M2, M3) that quantify how much of the I→S→O reasoning pathway is shared between edited and test queries.

## Method

The paper introduces a five-criterion evaluation framework for TMKE—Reliability, Generality, Portability, Locality, and a novel Stability metric that tests whether the model confuses edited entities with same-relation, different-instance queries. The TMKE Benchmark is built by expanding MQuAKE's textual triples into multimodal data via an automated pipeline: Google image search retrieves subject images, GPT-4o identifies entity types and rephrases questions for the multimodal context, and NQ/OK-VQA supply unrelated and same-relation distractor questions. Five editing methods (FT, IKE, MEND, SERAC, ROME) are applied to three MLLMs (BLIP-2, MiniGPT-4, LLaVA-1.5) across unimodal, transitive, and multimodal scenarios. To explain observed transitivity failures, the authors decompose the multimodal inference path I→O into sub-processes I→S and S→O, then use the Knowledge Neurons attribution framework to measure neuron-activation overlap (M1 = |N_SO ∩ N_IO|/|N_IO|, M2 = |N_I'O' ∩ N_IO|/|N_IO|, M3 = |N_IS ∩ N_IO|/|N_IS|), revealing that high S→O overlap boosts Reliability but simultaneously creates shortcuts that degrade Stability.

## Results

In transitive knowledge editing (text-edit → image-test), FT and IKE maintain near-perfect Reliability (99.3% and 100.0%), while MEND drops to ~68%, ROME to ~31%, and SERAC to ~26%—a dramatic degradation compared to their >90% Reliability in unimodal editing. Stability is consistently low across all methods, with FT at 30.7% and SERAC at 14.4% in transitive editing, and dropping further to 6.5% (FT) and 0.7% (SERAC) in multimodal editing. Portability never exceeds 50% in any scenario, indicating that no current method effectively transfers edited knowledge to multi-hop reasoning tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates five editing methods (FT, IKE, MEND, SERAC, ROME) across three scenarios. In transitive editing, FT and IKE dominate on Reliability (99.3% and 100.0%) but sacrifice Stability (30.7% and 20.2%), while ROME achieves the best Stability (57.9%) at the cost of Reliability (30.6%). MEND occupies a middle ground with moderate Reliability (67.9%) and good Locality (99.5%), but SERAC is the weakest overall, with Reliability of only 26.1% and Stability of 14.4%. In unimodal editing, all methods achieve >90% Reliability, with FT and IKE at 100%, confirming that the transitivity gap is the critical failure mode. The Knowledge Neuron analysis (RQ3) explains the FT-vs-ROME trade-off: FT's higher neuron overlap (M1) between the S→O and I→O processes boosts Reliability but creates shortcuts that reduce Stability, whereas ROME's lower overlap preserves Stability at the expense of Reliability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the knowledge editing literature by introducing a cross-modal transitivity dimension absent from prior benchmarks. While MMEdit, MIKE, and VLKEB evaluate multimodal editing with modality consistency (editing and testing in the same modality), and MQuAKE provides multi-hop textual editing, none address the scenario where knowledge is edited textually but must transfer to image-conditioned queries. The interpretive analysis builds directly on the Knowledge Neurons framework (Dai et al., 2022), repurposing it to decompose multimodal reasoning into I→S and S→O sub-processes to explain why transitive editing fails.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a practical gap: in real deployments, MLLMs are edited with textual data (which is far more abundant), yet must answer image-conditioned questions, and current methods handle this poorly. The newly proposed Stability metric reveals that methods with high Locality scores still significantly disrupt related-but-different knowledge, challenging the sufficiency of standard evaluation protocols. The Knowledge Neurons analysis showing that FT creates text-to-answer shortcuts that bypass visual information provides a mechanistic explanation but also raises the open question of how to design editing methods that genuinely integrate multimodal representations rather than forming modality-specific associations.
