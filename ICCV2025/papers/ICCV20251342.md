## Problem

It remains unclear whether self-attention layers in RoPE-based MMDiT models (e.g., FLUX) rely primarily on positional embeddings or on query-key content similarity during generation, and existing training-free editing methods for FLUX apply a uniform attention-sharing strategy without accounting for task-specific characteristics such as the need for spatial preservation versus content transfer.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251342/cropped_page0_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a source RGB image and a text editing prompt, generating both the source and edited images in parallel from the same initial noise in FLUX's latent space (1/16 resolution after 8× autoencoder downsampling and 2× patchify, e.g., 1024×1024 → 64×64 = 4096 tokens). For region-preserved tasks, a coarse cross-attention mask is refined via SAM-2 point prompts to produce a precise foreground mask. The output is a single edited RGB image at the original resolution, with no additional parameters or training required.

## Method

The paper first introduces an automated probing strategy that, for each of FLUX's 57 self-attention layers, removes or shifts the RoPE applied to keys while keeping query RoPE intact, then measures PSNR between the perturbed and original outputs to quantify each layer's positional versus content-similarity dependency. This reveals that dependency patterns are non-monotonic with depth, yielding two distinct layer sets: position-dependent layers P and content-similarity-dependent layers C. Building on this, the method designs three task-specific key-value injection strategies: (1) for position-dependent editing (object addition), it injects source K/V in P layers and introduces a reasoning-before-generation step that extracts an object placement mask from early cross-attention to selectively suppress injection only in the target region; (2) for content-similarity-dependent editing (non-rigid deformation), it injects source K/V in C layers so texture transfers while pose follows the edit text; (3) for region-preserved editing (background replacement, object movement, outpainting), it performs value-only replacement across all layers using a SAM-2-refined mask, which outperforms classical latent blending in both preservation fidelity and edge harmonization.

## Results

On object addition, FreeFlux achieves the best CLIP_dir (0.202) and user preference rate (50.8%), outperforming the next-best TamingRF (0.175, 31.9%). For non-rigid editing, it attains the highest CLIP_dir (0.153) and PR (74.1%) while maintaining the lowest CLIP_img (0.940, indicating meaningful deformation), far exceeding StableFlow (0.124, 11.1%) and OmniGen (0.047, 1.1%). On background replacement, FreeFlux achieves the best foreground PSNR (24.04 dB), CLIP_dir (0.319), and PR (81.4%), surpassing OmniGen (21.73 dB, 0.126, 2.4%) and MagicBrush (14.87 dB, 0.261, 13.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FreeFlux outperforms all four baselines on every metric across all three editing tasks. Against the training-free FLUX-specific methods StableFlow and TamingRF, the gains are most dramatic on user preference: 50.8% vs. 12.2%/31.9% for object addition, 74.1% vs. 11.1%/13.2% for non-rigid editing, and 81.4% vs. 1.4%/1.9% for background replacement, reflecting that these baselines largely fail to produce meaningful edits. Compared to the trained editing models MagicBrush and OmniGen, FreeFlux achieves substantially higher CLIP_dir (e.g., 0.202 vs. 0.161/0.090 for object addition; 0.319 vs. 0.261/0.126 for background replacement), indicating more faithful execution of the editing instruction. On non-rigid editing, StableFlow, TamingRF, and OmniGen produce near-duplicate outputs (CLIP_dir ≤ 0.124), while MagicBrush deforms but loses appearance fidelity (CLIP_img 0.933); FreeFlux balances both with CLIP_img 0.940 and CLIP_dir 0.153. For background replacement, OmniGen preserves the foreground best among baselines (PSNR 21.73) but still trails FreeFlux (24.04) by over 2 dB, and MagicBrush achieves the worst foreground preservation (14.87 dB).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the attention-sharing (key-value injection) mechanism from MASA-CTRL to the joint self-attention layers of FLUX's RoPE-based MMDiT architecture, but departs from prior training-free FLUX editing methods (StableFlow, TamingRF) by introducing a mechanistic probing strategy that reveals layer-specific dependency patterns on positional encoding versus content similarity. The key conceptual departure is that editing strategy is no longer uniform across layers or tasks; instead, the paper categorizes editing into position-dependent, content-similarity-dependent, and region-preserved types, each with tailored injection layers. The probing methodology itself—systematically removing or shifting RoPE on keys at individual layers and measuring output similarity—is novel and provides the first mechanistic analysis of how RoPE-based MMDiT layers function during generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that positional and content-similarity dependencies do not correlate with layer depth challenges the common assumption that shallower layers handle spatial information while deeper layers handle semantics, and provides a principled basis for task-specific editing in RoPE-based MMDiT models. As a training-free approach, it offers a practical path to versatile editing on FLUX without fine-tuning, with strong quantitative and user-study results across five editing tasks. However, the layer selection is empirically derived for FLUX.1-dev (12B) specifically, and it remains open whether the dependency patterns generalize to other RoPE-based MMDiT architectures or model scales. The authors also note a performance gap on real images due to inversion limitations, suggesting future work on better inversion or fine-tuning with generated editing data.
