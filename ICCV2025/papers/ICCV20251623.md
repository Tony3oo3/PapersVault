## Problem

Existing inference-stage backdoor attacks on quantized DNNs require flipping 10^1 to 10^2 bits simultaneously via Rowhammer, which is highly challenging in practice due to the sparse and unpredictable distribution of vulnerable DRAM cells. This paper asks whether a single bit flip is sufficient to inject a functional backdoor into a quantized model, and if so, how to identify which bit to flip and how to craft a trigger that activates it.

## Input / Output

The attacker requires white-box access to the quantized model's weights and architecture, a small set of benign samples (128 for CIFAR-10/SVHN, 768 for ImageNet), and a chosen target class. The method produces a single bit-flip specification (exact weight location and bit position in the two's complement representation) and a trigger pattern — a 3D pixel-intensity matrix combined with a 2D binary mask via element-wise multiplication — that, when applied to any input image, causes the backdoored model to output the target class. The trigger is optimized offline; only the bit flip and trigger application occur online at inference time.

## Method

SOLEFLIP exploits the fact that quantized DNN weights follow a normal distribution in two's complement form, so flipping the highest non-sign bit that is 0 (for positive weights) or the sign bit (for negative weights) predictably increases the weight by a large fixed amount (e.g., +64 for 8-bit), unlike full-precision models where such flips can push weights beyond their bounded range. The attack proceeds in three steps: (1) Exploitable Weight Identification selects a weight in the final classification layer connecting a feature neuron N1 to the target-class neuron N2, such that the single-bit flip makes it dominate other weights on N1 without degrading benign accuracy; (2) Trigger Generation freezes the model and uses gradient descent to jointly optimize a trigger pattern Δ and a mask m to amplify N1's output, with an L1 penalty on m for invisibility; (3) Backdoor Activation executes a Rowhammer flip on the identified bit, after which any trigger-embedded input produces a large logit for the target class. This pre-select-then-trigger paradigm replaces the iterative multi-bit search used by prior methods, reducing the required flips from tens to exactly one.

## Results

SOLEFLIP achieves attack success rates (ASR) of 99.8% on CIFAR-10/ResNet-18, 97.6% on SVHN/VGG-16, and 99.9% on both ImageNet/ResNet-18 and ImageNet/ViT-B-16, while maintaining 0.0% benign accuracy degradation (BAD) across all settings. Critically, all results are achieved with a single bit flip (Nflip = 1), compared to 10–1,650 bit flips required by prior methods. The average ASR across all configurations is 98.9%, with the method also remaining effective under 4-bit quantization (ASR 98.3–99.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SOLEFLIP dominates all six baselines on the efficiency metric (Nflip), requiring only 1 bit flip versus 10–1,650 for prior methods. On CIFAR-10/ResNet-18, SOLEFLIP achieves 99.8% ASR with 0.0% BAD, outperforming TBT (93.2% ASR, 4.0% BAD, 413 flips), ProFlip (97.9%, 2.8%, 12), HPT (98.7%, 0.0%, 12), CFT (95.3%, 0.2%, 99), and Deep-TROJ (99.6%, 0.0%, 80). On SVHN/VGG-16, SOLEFLIP's 97.6% ASR and 0.0% BAD substantially exceed TBT (73.8%, 24.7%), ProFlip (94.5%, 3.3%), and HPT (82.2%, 2.3%). On ImageNet/ViT-B-16, SOLEFLIP matches TrojViT's 99.9% ASR and Deep-TROJ's near-perfect 100.0% ASR while achieving 0.0% BAD (vs. 1.1% and 0.7% respectively) and requiring only 1 flip (vs. 880 and 80). The only marginal trade-off is that Deep-TROJ reaches 100.0% ASR on ImageNet/ViT-B-16 versus SOLEFLIP's 99.9%, but at 80× the bit-flip cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

SOLEFLIP extends the line of inference-stage backdoor attacks that exploit Rowhammer bit-flip attacks to modify quantized model weights (TBT, ProFlip, HPT, CFT, TrojViT, Deep-TROJ). The key departure is a reversal of the search order: prior methods iteratively search for multiple bits to flip and then craft triggers, whereas SOLEFLIP first pre-selects a single bit based on the two's complement structure and normal distribution of quantized weights, then generates a trigger to activate the corresponding neuron. It also distinguishes itself from ONEFLIP, which achieves single-bit backdoors on full-precision models by exploiting exponent-bit flips that push weights beyond their original range—a mechanism unavailable in bounded fixed-point quantization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Reducing the attack from 10–100+ bit flips to a single bit dramatically lowers the practical barrier for Rowhammer-based backdoor injection in multi-tenant or shared-memory deployments of quantized DNNs. The paper demonstrates that existing backdoor defenses (designed for training-stage attacks) and detection tools like Neural Cleanse fail to identify SOLEFLIP-generated backdoors, leaving a significant gap in the defense landscape for inference-stage threats. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-quantized or mixed-precision models, whether hardware-level mitigations (e.g., Rowhammer-resistant DRAM) fully close the attack surface, and whether the trigger's dependence on a specific neuron limits robustness to model updates or fine-tuning.
