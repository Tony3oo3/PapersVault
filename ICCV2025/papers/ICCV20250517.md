## Problem

Learning image-text alignment for detailed, longer text remains challenging because existing approaches rely on expensive human annotations or proprietary AI feedback (e.g., GPT-4V) to collect preference data. Moreover, most alignment metrics are designed for short captions and fail to evaluate or optimize longer, descriptive text effectively. The paper asks whether cycle consistency—mapping text back to image space (or vice versa) and measuring reconstruction fidelity—can serve as a scalable, label-free supervisory signal for alignment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250517/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The reward model takes an RGB image and a text string (capped at 77 tokens to match text-to-image model limits) and outputs a scalar alignment score via a BLIP-based architecture (ViT-L/16 image encoder + BERT_base text encoder + 5-layer MLP). The underlying cycle consistency score is computed using DreamSim for image-space similarity and SBERT for text-space similarity. The dataset (CyclePrefDB) provides 866K pairwise comparisons—398K image-to-text and 468K text-to-image—derived from 11 captioning models and 4 diffusion models applied to dense captions from the DCI/sDCI dataset.

## Method

The core mechanism is to derive alignment preferences from cycle consistency: given an image x and candidate text y, the text is mapped back to image space via a fixed text-to-image model (Stable Diffusion 3), and the DreamSim similarity between x and the reconstruction G(y) serves as the alignment score; the reverse direction uses a fixed image-to-text model (LLaVA-1.5-13B) with SBERT text similarity. These scores are converted into pairwise preferences across diverse model outputs, yielding CyclePrefDB. A reward model is then trained with a Bradley-Terry loss on these preferences, producing a fast, differentiable alignment metric that outperforms raw cycle consistency and human-preference-trained baselines on detailed captioning. The same dataset is further used for DPO (on Qwen-VL-Chat) and Diffusion DPO (on Stable Diffusion 1.5), improving performance across vision-language and text-to-image tasks without any human labels.

## Results

On the detailed captioning benchmark DetailCaps-4870, CycleReward-Combo achieves 60.50% pairwise accuracy, outperforming VQAScore (11B) by 10.26 points and HPSv2 by 6.16 points. On the text-to-image benchmark GenAI-Bench, CycleReward-Combo scores 55.52%, competitive with human-preference-trained models (HPSv2: 56.13%, ImageReward: 56.70%) but below VQAScore (11B) at 64.13%. For DPO, training Qwen-VL-Chat with CyclePrefDB-I2T improves DeCapBench from 26.47 to 30.63 and LLaVA-W^D from 61.67 to 70.00, surpassing the GPT-4V-annotated VLFeedback baseline (28.03 / 69.17).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CycleReward-Combo decisively outperforms CLIPScore (51.66→60.50 on DetailCaps-4870) and all human-preference-trained reward models (HPSv2, PickScore, ImageReward) on detailed captioning, with margins of 4.2–9.8 points. Against VQAScore (11B), CycleReward wins on DetailCaps-4870 (60.50 vs 50.24) but loses on GenAI-Bench (55.52 vs 64.13), where the 11B VLM's generative evaluation gives it a clear advantage. In the DPO setting, CyclePrefDB-I2T beats the GPT-4V-annotated VLFeedback dataset on DeCapBench (30.63 vs 28.03) and LLaVA-W^D (70.00 vs 69.17) despite being smaller and covering only captioning instructions. For text-to-image DPO, CyclePrefDB-T2I outperforms the Pick-A-Pic baseline on T2I-CompBench spatial (16.55 vs 14.59), color (42.35 vs 39.12), and texture (46.67 vs 40.66) categories. In the human-preference agreement study (Table 2), CycleReward-Combo surpasses GPT-4o annotations on all five preference datasets, with the largest gap on ImageRewardDB (61.3% vs 24.8%), while raw cycle consistency maintains a consistent ~55–60% agreement across tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes cycle consistency—a concept well-established in image-to-image translation (CycleGAN) and self-supervised learning—as a preference signal for image-text alignment, departing from prior work that relies on human annotations (HPS, PickScore, ImageReward) or expensive VLM queries (VQAScore, GPT-4V). The closest prior methods, Image2Text2Image and DDPO, compute cycle consistency on-the-fly using large pre-trained models, making them prohibitively slow and non-differentiable. The key conceptual shift here is using cycle consistency scores to *generate* a preference dataset (CyclePrefDB, 866K pairs) and then distill it into a small, fast, differentiable reward model (CycleReward), rather than using the score directly. The paper also extends the alignment evaluation setting from short captions (typical of existing benchmarks) to dense, 77-token descriptions, addressing a gap noted in the detailed captioning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that cycle consistency can substitute for human or GPT-4V preference labels at competitive agreement rates (~65% vs. human), the paper offers a scalable, low-cost pathway to alignment that avoids the prohibitive expense of manual annotation. The practical impact is significant: CycleReward is fast and differentiable, enabling its use as a Best-of-N verifier and as a DPO training signal, and the publicly released 866K-pair dataset provides a new resource for the community. A notable open question is the dependence on decoder quality—generation errors in the text-to-image or image-to-text models can mislead the preference signal, and the paper acknowledges this as a key limitation. The authors suggest the framework could extend to audio-text, video-language, and reasoning tasks, but these remain unexplored.
