## Problem

Despite the effectiveness of various training-time OOD detection methods (LogitNorm, T2FNorm, CSI, DAL), no unified mechanism explains why they all succeed despite being designed from different intuitions. Additionally, the reason near-OOD samples are harder to distinguish from in-distribution samples than far-OOD samples remains underexplored.

## Input / Output

The analysis treats each input image as a set of n patch-level variables and computes interaction effects over all 2^n subsets via masking to baseline values. The DNN output v(x) is set as the logit of the ground-truth class probability (or the max-probability class for OOD samples), and the interaction decomposition yields a vector of numerical effects I(S|x) for each subset S. OOD detection is then scored via a threshold on this confidence, with performance measured by FPR95 and AUROC across SVHN, Textures, and Tiny-ImageNet benchmarks.

## Method

The paper uses the Harsanyi Dividend from cooperative game theory to decompose DNN outputs into interaction effects, where each interaction I(S|x) represents an AND-relationship among a subset of input patches. The key finding is that all training-time OOD methods share a common mechanism: they make the model encode more high-order (complex) interactions relative to a cross-entropy-only baseline, as quantified by the relative interaction strength R^(m) per order m. To verify the primary role of high-order interactions, the authors propose a new loss L_inter^(m1,m2) that penalizes specific-order interactions by maximizing cross-entropy on the difference of network outputs between differently-masked samples (derived from Theorem 2). For near-OOD difficulty, they compute Jaccard similarity between interaction distribution vectors of ID, near-OOD, and far-OOD samples, showing near-OOD distributions are significantly more similar to ID distributions, and that training-time methods reduce this similarity.

## Results

The key quantitative finding is that suppressing high-order interactions (low-order model) causes severe OOD detection degradation: FPR95 increases by 11–38% and AUROC drops by 15–27% across ResNet-18/34 and WideResNet-40-2 on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. In contrast, suppressing low-order interactions (high-order model) yields only minor changes (FPR95 shifts of ±1–5%, AUROC shifts of ±3%), confirming that high-order interactions play a primary role in OOD detection. Additionally, all four training-time OOD detection methods (CSI, LogitNorm, T2FNorm, DAL) consistently increase the relative strength of high-order interactions (ΔR^(m) > 0 for m > 0.75n) compared to the cross-entropy baseline, providing a unified mechanistic explanation for their effectiveness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three model types: a cross-entropy baseline, a low-order model (trained to suppress high-order interactions via a novel loss L_inter), and a high-order model (trained to suppress low-order interactions). The low-order model loses decisively to the baseline on every architecture and dataset: on CIFAR-10, FPR95 worsens from 62.03 to 91.45 (ResNet-18) and from 50.09 to 88.63 (ResNet-34), while AUROC drops by 15–20 points. The high-order model, by contrast, matches or slightly beats the baseline (e.g., FPR95 of 53.05 vs. 62.03 on CIFAR-10 ResNet-18), confirming that high-order interactions carry the discriminative signal for OOD detection. Separately, the four training-time OOD detection methods—CSI [33], LogitNorm [37], T2FNorm [25], and DAL [35]—all outperform the baseline in OOD detection (as reported in their original papers), and the interaction analysis reveals they share a common mechanism: each increases the relative strength of high-order interactions (ΔR^(m) > 0 for m > 0.75n) while decreasing low-order interaction strength, despite being designed from entirely different intuitions (logit normalization, feature normalization, contrastive augmentation, and distributional augmentation, respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the interaction-based explanation framework from Ren et al. (ICLR 2024) and Li and Zhang (ICML 2023)—originally developed to explain DNN decision-making and concept encoding—to provide a unified mechanistic account of why diverse training-time OOD detection methods (LogitNorm, T2FNorm, CSI, DAL) all improve detection performance. Prior OOD detection work treated these methods as independent contributions based on distinct intuitions (logit scaling, feature normalization, contrastive learning, distributional augmentation), with no shared theoretical explanation. The paper also introduces a novel loss function (L_inter) derived from the universal-matching property (Theorem 1) to empirically isolate the contribution of specific interaction orders, bridging the gap between the interaction theory and OOD detection practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified interaction-complexity explanation offers a principled lens for designing future OOD detection methods: if high-order interactions are the primary discriminative signal, then training strategies that explicitly encourage or preserve high-order interactions could be more effective than ad-hoc normalization tricks. However, the analysis is limited to ResNet architectures on CIFAR-10/100, and the interaction computation (requiring 2^n masked samples) is prohibitively expensive for large images, limiting immediate practical adoption. Open questions include whether the high-order interaction mechanism generalizes to transformer-based models, larger-scale datasets like ImageNet, and whether the interaction-similarity explanation for near-OOD difficulty can be leveraged to design targeted detection strategies for the hardest OOD cases.
