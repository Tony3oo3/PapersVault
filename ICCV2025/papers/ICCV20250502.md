## Problem

Accurate camera spectral sensitivity calibration is essential for color measurement and material analysis, but existing methods either require expensive specialized light sources or reference targets with known spectral reflectances. Diffraction grating-based approaches improve spectral resolution but still demand additional captures of calibrated reference targets to account for unknown grating efficiency. This paper asks whether both camera spectral sensitivity and grating efficiency can be jointly estimated from a single uncalibrated diffraction grating sheet without any reference targets.

## Input / Output

The method takes two HDR images (captured at two exposure times) of a known-spectrum light source viewed through an uncalibrated diffraction grating sheet: one recording the direct (undiffracted) light and one recording the first-order diffraction pattern. The diffracted observation is cropped to approximately 200×1000 pixels and averaged along the vertical axis to yield a 1×1000 per-channel vector. The output is the per-channel camera spectral sensitivity and the grating efficiency, each sampled at 31 wavelengths from 400 to 700 nm. In the LED-only variant, a single capture suffices for both pixel-to-wavelength mapping and sensitivity estimation.

## Method

The core insight is that the inherently bilinear image formation model (where sensitivity and grating efficiency appear in product form) becomes a tractable linear system when both unknowns are expressed as linear combinations of bases: an SVD basis for camera spectral sensitivity and a Fourier basis for the inverse grating efficiency. The direct light observation yields a linear constraint on the sensitivity coefficients, while the diffracted light observation—after algebraic manipulation using the left pseudo-inverse of the weight matrix—yields a homogeneous linear constraint coupling the sensitivity and grating-efficiency coefficients. Stacking constraints across RGB channels produces a constrained least-squares problem solvable in closed form via a Lagrange multiplier, provided the number of wavelength samples exceeds the total number of basis functions. The pixel-to-wavelength mapping, parameterized as a quadratic function, is estimated either by matching spectral peaks in fluorescent light or by a point-to-plane ICP alignment between the normalized diffraction observation and the illumination-scaled mean sensitivity curve in the LED-only setting.

## Results

The paper evaluates camera spectral sensitivity estimation using the average relative full-scale error (RE) metric across RGB channels. On real-world scenes (4 cameras: EOS RP, iPhone 15 Pro Max, Sony α1, DJI Pocket 3), both proposed variants—Ours (LED+Flu) and Ours (LED)—achieve the lowest RE for most scenes, with Ours (LED) producing results nearly comparable to Ours (LED+Flu). On synthetic scenes, Ours (LED+Flu) and the color chart-based method (CC) both yield low errors under ideal observations, while Ours (LED) shows a modest increase due to pixel-to-wavelength mapping estimation error. The Exif-based method (Exif+CC) produces the largest estimation errors across all real-world scenes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: the color chart-based method (CC) [12] and the Exif-metadata-based method (Exif+CC) [23]. On real-world scenes, both proposed variants (Ours LED+Flu and Ours LED) outperform CC, which is sensitive to real-world noise due to the low dimensionality of spectral reflectances in color checker patches. The Exif+CC method, while the easiest to use (relying only on metadata plus a color checker for white-balance disambiguation), yields the largest RE across all real-world scenes. On synthetic scenes, CC matches Ours (LED+Flu) in accuracy under ideal conditions, but the proposed method retains an advantage in practicality by requiring only a light source and a diffraction grating sheet rather than a calibrated reference target. Notably, Ours (LED) achieves near-parity with Ours (LED+Flu) on real-world data, meaning a single LED capture suffices for competitive accuracy without the extra fluorescent lamp setup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the diffraction grating-based calibration line initiated by Karge et al. [13] and Toivonen et al. [24], which already recognized that grating diffraction produces wavelength-resolved spectral peaks superior to the low-frequency reflectances of color checker patches. The key departure is eliminating the need for reference targets with known spectral reflectances: by jointly estimating camera spectral sensitivity and grating efficiency in a closed-form linear system (using SVD bases for sensitivity and Fourier bases for efficiency), the method requires only direct and diffracted light observations. The basis-representation framework draws on Jiang et al. [12], and the SVD basis construction reuses the Solomatov-Akkaynak [23] camera dataset, positioning the work at the intersection of these two threads.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: a sub-$5 diffraction grating sheet replaces expensive narrow-band filter setups or carefully controlled color checker captures, reducing calibration to one or two images in a dark room. The main limitation is that pixel-to-wavelength mapping estimation fails under spectrally uniform illumination, though the authors note this is rare in practice. Open questions include whether the basis-representation assumptions (7 Fourier bases for grating efficiency, 7 SVD bases per channel) are sufficient for exotic optical systems (e.g., cameras with strong lens coatings or IR filters), and whether the method extends naturally to multi-spectral or hyperspectral sensors beyond standard RGB.
