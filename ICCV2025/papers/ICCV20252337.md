## Problem

Audio-visual multi-task incremental learning must continuously acquire new tasks while retaining knowledge of previously learned ones, but existing prompt-based incremental methods target single-modality settings and fail to address two audio-visual-specific tensions: balancing cross-task knowledge sharing against task interference, and preserving modality-specific details while integrating cross-modal generality. The paper tackles both gaps with a progressive prompting strategy that assigns different sharing/specificity roles to different network depths.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252337/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives paired audio and video clips along with a known task index (available at both training and inference time), encoded by frozen CLIP (visual) and CLAP (audio) backbones. Outputs are task-dependent: temporal event segments for AVE, per-frame class labels for AVVP, free-form answer text for AVQA, and pixel-level masks for AVS. Training uses symmetric contrastive losses (CLIP-style visual-text and CLAP-style audio-text) with learnable temperature parameters, and no data replay is required.

## Method

PHP inserts three complementary prompt modules at increasing depths of the CLIP/CLAP backbone. At shallow layers, a Task-shared Modality Aggregating (TMA) adapter fuses audio and video features via bidirectional channel attention, cross-modal spatial attention, and RNN-based temporal attention with learnable fusion coefficients, producing task-agnostic cross-modal representations. At middle layers, a Task-specific Modality-shared Dynamic Generating (TMDG) adapter maintains a shared prompt pool, computes instance-level global features from each modality, and dynamically generates n task-specific prompts that are concatenated back into the visual and audio token sequences before self-attention. At deep layers, Task-specific Modality-independent (TMI) prompts are selected by task index and prepended separately to visual and audio features, preserving modality-critical details without cross-modal mixing. This shallow-to-deep progression from shared to specific parameters is the core design principle, validated by ablations showing that alternative orderings degrade both anti-forgetting and transfer performance.

## Results

On the anti-forgetting benchmark across AVE, AVVP, and AVQA tasks, PHP achieves the best overall mean accuracy (58.85%), lowest mean forgetting score (3.32%), and highest final accuracy (54.74%), outperforming the strongest prompt-based baseline PC (56.88%, 4.90%, 51.63%). On multi-task transfer, PHP is the only method exhibiting positive transfer (+7.79% improvement from single- to multi-task), while all baselines show negative transfer ranging from −2.87% (EWC) to −62.98% (DCNet). Per-task, PHP leads on AVE (62.03% Amean) and AVVP (45.25% Amean), and matches or closely trails PC on AVQA (69.29% vs 69.55% Amean).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PHP outperforms all six baselines on overall mean accuracy and forgetting. Against the strongest prompt-based competitor PC, PHP improves Amean by 1.97 points (58.85 vs 56.88) and reduces Fmean by 1.58 points (3.32 vs 4.90), while also achieving higher final accuracy (54.74 vs 51.63). DualPrompt and S-Prompt, which use static prompt pools, trail PHP by 4.28 and 6.48 Amean points respectively, indicating that dynamic instance-level prompt generation (TMDG) and progressive depth-wise specialization (TMI) are critical. Fine-tune and DCNet suffer severe forgetting (Fmean ≈ 10%), while EWC collapses on AVE (8.52% Amean), confirming that parameter-regularization approaches are ill-suited to audio-visual multi-task streams. On transfer ability (Table 2), PHP is the sole method with positive transfer (+7.79%), whereas PC (−3.94%), DualPrompt (−7.59%), and S-Prompt (−34.01%) all degrade when tasks are learned sequentially, and Fine-tune/DCNet show catastrophic negative transfer (−58% to −63%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends prompt-based incremental learning from single-modality settings (L2P, DualPrompt, S-Prompt, PC) to the audio-visual multi-task domain, where the dual-modality structure introduces additional challenges of cross-modal fusion and modality-specific preservation. The TMA adapter draws directly from DG-SCT's multi-perspective attention fusion (channel, spatial, temporal) for cross-modal feature alignment, while the TMDG adapter adapts PC's prompt-codebook mechanism to generate instance-level prompts conditioned on both modalities. The key architectural departure is the progressive depth-based decomposition—task-shared cross-modal attention at shallow layers, task-specific cross-modal prompts at middle layers, and task-modality-specific prompts at deep layers—inspired by knowledge consolidation pathways in continual pre-training, which no prior prompt-based incremental method has employed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to systematically address audio-visual multi-task incremental learning with a prompt-based approach, filling a gap where prior prompt methods were limited to single-modality scenarios. The finding that the method achieves positive multi-task transfer (+7.79%) while all baselines suffer negative transfer is practically significant, suggesting that progressive depth-based prompting can turn previously learned tasks into assets rather than liabilities. However, the evaluation is limited to four tasks with fixed CLIP/CLAP backbones, and the four-task sequence results reveal non-trivial degradation on intermediate tasks (e.g., AVVP dropping to 31.48%), leaving open questions about scalability to longer task streams and whether the progressive design generalizes beyond audio-visual to other multi-modal domains.
