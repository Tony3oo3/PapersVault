## Problem

Action-driven video generation faces a precision-generality tradeoff: text or primitive-action representations are general but too coarse for complex high-DoF interactions (e.g., dexterous hand or gripper manipulation), while agent-centric signals like 7-DoF end-effector poses are precise but tightly coupled to specific embodiments and cannot transfer across domains. No unified action representation exists that simultaneously captures fine-grained kinematic detail and enables joint training on heterogeneous data from different agents.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252569/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes an initial RGB frame, a sequence of 2D skeleton images rendered as RGB frames (C=3) at 720×480 resolution, and a scene-centric text prompt (describing scene layout and appearances but explicitly excluding action descriptions). Skeleton sequences are encoded via a 3D convolutional trajectory encoder that downsamples temporally by 4× and spatially by 8×. The output is a 25-frame video at variable fps depicting the interaction-driven scene dynamics under the specified action trajectory.

## Method

The core idea is to 'render' action-induced 3D structures into 2D visual action prompts (skeletons) as a domain-agnostic control signal, decoupling action precision from agent embodiment. Skeletons are recovered from in-the-wild HOI videos via a four-stage pipeline (Wilor 3D hand mesh detection → SAMURAI tracklet formation and handedness correction → missing-mesh re-estimation → OneEuro temporal smoothing) and from robot episodes via joint-state rendering with MatchAnything-guided homography rectification for camera drift correction. The architecture builds on CogVideoX's DiT backbone, injecting skeleton latents through a ControlNet-style branch (trainable copies of the first 14 DiT blocks with zero-initialized linear layers) and a dual-branch mechanism that merges action tokens into the main DiT with LoRA fine-tuning. Training uses loss amplification around hand/gripper regions and biased temporal sampling near gripper state changes to prioritize interaction dynamics over self-motion, enabling a single unified model trained jointly on EgoVid, RT-1, and DROID.

## Results

On RT-1, the skeleton-based visual action prompt achieves PSNR 25.98, SSIM 0.859, LPIPS 0.110, FVD 288.6, and ST-IoU 0.604, outperforming both text-based (CogVideoX) and raw 7-DoF state (IRASim) controls. On DROID, the method reaches PSNR 21.26, SSIM 0.834, LPIPS 0.132, FVD 141.8, and ST-IoU 0.450, with the unified (jointly trained) model further improving to FVD 124.4 and ST-IoU 0.478. On EgoVid, the skeleton prompt reduces FVD from 1638.6 (text) to 1243.6 (single) and 1142.3 (unified), while improving SSIM from 0.440 to 0.486. The unified model trained across all three datasets achieves comparable or superior results to single-domain training, demonstrating effective cross-domain transfer.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CogVideoX (text-as-action), the proposed skeleton-based visual action prompt wins decisively on every metric across all three datasets: on RT-1, ST-IoU more than doubles from 0.267 to 0.604, and FVD drops from 642.3 to 288.6; on DROID, ST-IoU improves from 0.239 to 0.450; on EgoVid, FVD decreases from 1638.6 to 1243.6. Against IRASim (raw 7-DoF end-effector states), the skeleton prompt also achieves superior results on all metrics: on RT-1, ST-IoU rises from 0.507 to 0.604 and PSNR from 23.96 to 25.98; on DROID, ST-IoU improves from 0.365 to 0.450 and FVD drops from 151.2 to 141.8. Notably, IRASim's agent-centric representation fails to generalize to DROID's random third-person viewpoints, whereas the visual skeleton prompt remains effective. The unified model (jointly trained on all three datasets) further surpasses IRASim on DROID (ST-IoU 0.478 vs. 0.365) and achieves the best FVD (124.4), demonstrating that cross-domain training with visual action prompts yields additional gains beyond single-domain training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of action-driven video generation and character animation, departing from both text-based action conditioning (UniSim, Genie) and agent-centric state representations (IRASim, Cosmos) that each occupy one end of a precision-generality tradeoff. It builds directly on CogVideoX as the pretrained video diffusion backbone and adopts the dual-branch conditioning mechanism from Wonderland for injecting control signals into the main DiT, while using a ControlNet-style adapter for the action prompt. The key conceptual departure is from mask-based approaches (CosHand, InterDyn) and mesh-rendering methods: the authors argue that 2D skeletons, long used in animation, offer the right balance of geometric precision and recovery feasibility from in-the-wild data, enabling a unified representation across human hands and robotic grippers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to train a single action-driven video model across heterogeneous domains (egocentric human HOI, two distinct robot embodiments) and observe cross-domain skill transfer (e.g., zero-shot 'close drawer' on RT-1) is practically significant for robotics simulation and policy evaluation, where data scarcity per embodiment is a persistent bottleneck. The finding that simple 2D skeletons nearly match mesh or depth renderings in generation quality while being far cheaper to recover from in-the-wild video makes the approach scalable to large-scale training. Key limitations remain: the 2D representation lacks explicit 3D depth cues, and the base model's text-conditioned motion priors are not yet repurposed for action conditioning, leaving room for tighter integration of action and language signals.
