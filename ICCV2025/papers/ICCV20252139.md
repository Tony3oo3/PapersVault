## Problem

Vision foundation models are widely assumed to encode viewpoint-invariant scene content, yet their sensitivity to viewing angle changes has not been systematically characterized. Prior robustness studies (e.g., OmniView) examined out-of-distribution viewpoints but neglected the critical case of accidental viewpoints—where a specific camera orientation geometrically collapses an object's 3D structure—and evaluated only two models. This paper fills that gap by defining viewpoint instability, distinguishing accidental from other unstable viewpoints, and demonstrating that these instabilities are detectable from feature representations alone.

## Input / Output

At inference time, the instability classifier consumes only a single d-dimensional feature vector extracted from a foundation model (e.g., CLIP, DINOv2, SAM), requiring no access to the original RGB image—enabling deployment in privacy-sensitive settings. It outputs a binary stable/unstable label, a subtype label (accidental vs. other) for unstable views, and a calibrated accidental probability via Platt scaling. The instability score itself (Eq. 1) is computed as the mean cosine distance between a viewpoint's features and those of its r-neighborhood in camera space, using 72 renderings at 5° azimuth intervals per object.

## Method

The paper defines an instability score as the average cosine distance between a viewpoint's feature embedding and those of its spatial neighbors in camera space, flagging viewpoints exceeding a 97th-percentile threshold as unstable. To predict instability without multi-view access, SVM binary classifiers with RBF kernels are trained on (feature vector → stable/unstable) pairs, achieving 81–96% accuracy on ABO and 71–77% on CO3D across nine featurizers. Unstable viewpoints are further partitioned into accidental and other subtypes by fitting k=2 k-means clusters on unstable features and assigning the cluster with the higher silhouette score as accidental, yielding ~79% agreement with human annotations. A calibrated accidental probability is computed as P(unstable)·P(accidental|unstable) using Platt scaling, achieving ~90% average confidence. Downstream evaluations on zero-shot/linear-probe classification, VQA (LLaVA-1.5), and monocular 3D reconstruction (Stable Fast3D) quantify the performance degradation at unstable viewpoints and show that viewpoint-invariant fine-tuning (OmniView, CLIP-FT) mitigates other-viewpoint instability but cannot resolve the inherent geometric ambiguity of accidental views.

## Results

The SVM-based instability classifier achieves 81–96% accuracy on ABO and 71–77% on CO3D in predicting stable vs. unstable viewpoints from feature vectors alone, with DreamSim performing best (96.60% on ABO) and MAE worst (81.68%). Automatic clustering of unstable viewpoints yields ~79% overlap with human-annotated accidental views, and the two-stage probability (unstable × accidental|unstable) reaches ~90% average confidence. Downstream, CLIP zero-shot Acc@5 on ABO drops sharply from 68.74% (stable) to 42.56% (other) and 3.00% (accidental), while monocular 3D reconstruction F1@0.5 falls from 82.86% to 36.67% for accidental views.

## Baselines & Comparisons

OVT-OpenCLIP (OmniView-tuned) is the stronger of the two baselines, improving CLIP's zero-shot accuracy on 'other' viewpoints by +13.6 points on ABO (42.56→56.13) and +9.5 points on CO3D (68.45→77.92), confirming its effectiveness at regularizing out-of-distribution viewpoint biases. However, on inherently ambiguous accidental viewpoints, OVT-OpenCLIP yields only marginal gains (+0.34 on ABO, +1.76 on CO3D), underscoring that geometric collapse cannot be resolved by training alone. CLIP-FT (multiview equivariance fine-tuning) provides minimal improvements across all categories, with at most +0.61 on ABO 'other' and +1.06 on CO3D 'other', and negligible changes on accidental views. Both baselines thus validate the paper's central claim: while 'other' instabilities are addressable via viewpoint-invariant training, accidental viewpoints represent a fundamental limitation that no current training paradigm can fully overcome.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical notion of accidental viewpoints (Freeman 1994; Torralba & Freeman 2012) and the more recent viewpoint-bias literature on neural classifiers (ViewFool, adversarial viewpoint studies) to modern vision foundation models, which had not been systematically analyzed for this property. It is closest in spirit to OmniView [41], which studied viewpoint robustness of CLIP and BLIP, but departs by (a) evaluating nine diverse featurizers, (b) explicitly separating geometrically-caused accidental instabilities from model-bias-driven 'other' instabilities, and (c) predicting instability from a single feature vector without access to neighboring views. The methodological toolkit (SVM with RBF kernel, k-means, PCA, Platt scaling) is deliberately simple; the contribution is the empirical framing and the feature-only prediction paradigm rather than a new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that downstream applications (VQA, 3D reconstruction, classification) can compute a lightweight instability flag from a single feature vector and abstain or request additional views when confidence is low, rather than feigning certainty on geometrically ambiguous inputs. The finding that accidental viewpoints represent a fundamental geometric limit that no amount of training can resolve (OmniView and CLIP-FT show only marginal gains) reframes the problem from 'fix the model' to 'detect and handle the limitation.' Open questions include how to integrate these confidence signals into production pipelines, whether the accidental/other taxonomy generalizes to novel object categories or multi-object scenes, and whether the low cross-model agreement on 'other' viewpoints (IoU 0.12) indicates a deeper architectural issue that could be addressed at training time.
