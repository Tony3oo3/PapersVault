## Problem

Existing image-to-video animation methods (e.g., Animate Anyone, DISCO) are designed for real-world humans with static cameras and clean backgrounds, and fail on stylized character art due to two gaps: (1) they cannot jointly model high-dynamic motion of both the character and the background scene, and (2) they assume a pre-aligned human body, breaking down when character proportions, scales, and poses differ substantially from the driving guidance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250976/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single stylized character art image (any scale or body proportion) and a driving video as input. From the driving video, Xpose extracts separate body/face/hand pose sequences and DROID-SLAM extracts camera poses; the reference image's own initial pose is also extracted. The output is a temporally consistent character art animation video, generated at 768×768 resolution with 24-frame sequences during training and decoded via a VAE Temporal Decoder. The reference image and driving video are intentionally unrelated, and pose/motion guidance can be randomly dropped during inference for robustness.

## Method

MikuDance introduces two coupled techniques. First, Scene Motion Tracking (SMT) converts 3D camera poses into pixel-wise 2D scene motion by projecting the reference image's depth-based point cloud through consecutive camera frames, yielding a motion field in the same spatial domain as character pose guidance. Second, Mixed-Control Diffusion encodes the reference image, reference pose, and all driving poses through a shared VAE encoder and concatenates them as a single multi-channel input to a unified Reference UNet (with zero-initialized expanded input convolutions), implicitly aligning scale and body shape without explicit registration. A Motion-Adaptive Normalization (MAN) module, adapted from SPADE, injects the pixel-wise scene motion into each down-sampling block of the Reference UNet via spatially-varying γ and β parameters, coupling global scene dynamics with local character motion. Training proceeds in two stages: (i) supervised fine-tuning on stylized pair-wise frames generated by SDXL-Neta without MAN or temporal modules, and (ii) fine-tuning of MAN and the AnimateDiff temporal module on MMD dance clips plus non-character camera-movement videos, with 0.2 dropout on pose and scene-motion guidance.

## Results

On the MMD video evaluation set (100 held-out videos), MikuDance achieves the best scores across all seven metrics: FID 24.60, SSIM 0.576, PSNR 14.59, LPIPS 0.493, L1 5.73×10⁻⁵, FID-VID 22.87, and FVD 502.38. The closest competitor is the fine-tuned AniAny* (FID 28.83, FVD 575.30), while the original baselines (AniAny, DISCO, MagicPose, UniAnimate) score substantially worse, with DISCO performing the worst (FID 59.22, FVD 923.92). A user study with 50 raters confirms that over 97% of participants prefer MikuDance's animations over all baselines in overall, frame, and temporal quality.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MikuDance outperforms all four baselines on every reported metric. Against the strongest baseline, fine-tuned AniAny*, MikuDance reduces FID by 14.7% (24.60 vs. 28.83), improves SSIM by 9.5% (0.576 vs. 0.526), and lowers FVD by 12.7% (502.38 vs. 575.30). The original AniAny, MagicPose, and UniAnimate all suffer from character shape/scale misalignment, producing distorted outputs, while DISCO exhibits scene collapse when animating character art due to its decoupled foreground-background ControlNet design. The gap is most pronounced on video-level metrics: MikuDance's FVD of 502.38 is roughly 40–45% lower than the original baselines (846–924), reflecting superior temporal consistency under high-dynamic camera and pose motion. The authors note that absolute metric values are lower than in prior studies because their test videos feature highly dynamic full-scene motion rather than static backgrounds.

## Relation to Prior Work

MikuDance builds directly on the Animate Anyone architecture (Reference UNet + pose guidance + AnimateDiff temporal module) and the broader diffusion-based human animation line (DISCO, UniAnimate, MagicPose), but departs from them in two key ways. First, it replaces the separate encoder pipelines for reference and motion guidance with a unified Mixed-Control Reference UNet that implicitly aligns character shape and scale with driving poses, avoiding the explicit alignment assumptions that break for stylized characters. Second, it introduces Scene Motion Tracking (SMT) to convert 3D camera poses into pixel-wise 2D motion, deliberately diverging from the Plücker-coordinate camera embeddings used in recent camera-controllable video generation (CameraCtrl, HumanVid, CamCo) because those embeddings have a domain gap with pixel-wise character motion guidance. The Motion-Adaptive Normalization module adapts the SPADE spatially-adaptive normalization idea from image synthesis to inject global scene dynamics into the denoising process.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a practical gap: existing image-to-video animation methods assume real-human proportions and static cameras, making them unsuitable for the stylized, high-dynamic character art prevalent in anime, games, and digital design. The SMT strategy offers a reusable formulation for representing camera motion in a domain-consistent pixel space, potentially applicable beyond character animation. However, the authors acknowledge that background distortions persist due to the fundamentally ill-posed nature of 3D scene reconstruction from a single image under dynamic cameras, leaving open the question of how to achieve truly 3D-consistent animation without explicit geometry. The mixed-source training pipeline (synthetic stylized frames + non-character camera videos) is a practical recipe that could inform data strategies for other stylized generation tasks.
