## Problem

Existing video LLM benchmarks conflate errors from insufficient frame sampling with genuine understanding failures, and their robustness evaluations rely on artificial perturbations (pixel noise, distorted text) rather than natural adversarial conditions. This makes it impossible to determine whether the large human-model gap reflects a true comprehension deficit or merely a frame-selection limitation, and whether models truly understand video content or merely pattern-match on surface cues.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250998/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample consists of a YouTube Shorts video (all under 65 seconds) represented by 80 uniformly sampled frames, paired with one primary open-ended question and four adversarial variants (rephrased, correctly-led, wrongly-led, and multiple-choice). Open-ended responses are scored 0–5 by a Qwen2.5-72B judge (score >3 = correct), while multiple-choice answers are matched against ground truth. The headline robustness metric is the fraction of videos where all five questions are answered correctly, conditional on the primary question being correct.

## Method

The benchmark is built around two design principles: (1) every question must involve at least one of eight defined complexity factors—four visual (unclear/unusual content, movement speed, spatial-temporal arrangement, illusions) and four narrative (complex plot, narrative editing, technical editing, world knowledge)—and must be answerable from 80 uniformly sampled frames, thereby isolating comprehension from frame-sampling limitations; (2) each primary question is expanded into four natural adversarial variants crafted from actual model errors, testing whether the model's understanding is stable under semantically equivalent but misleading rephrasings. A multi-annotator curation pipeline with alignment checks ensures inter-annotator consistency, and questions are validated against GPT-4o, LLaVA-Video-7B, and Qwen2.5-VL-7B to confirm sufficient difficulty. Evaluation covers eight video LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA-Video, InternVL-2.5, Ola, Oryx) under zero-shot settings, plus blind language-only and human baselines.

## Results

On Video-TT, GPT-4o achieves the highest model performance with 45.2% average accuracy and 36.0% robustness, while human annotators reach 83.2% accuracy and 64.4% robustness — a gap of roughly 38 and 28 percentage points respectively. Among open-source models, LLaVA-Video-72B leads with 37.6% average accuracy and 19.7% robustness, followed by Qwen2.5-VL-72B (35.8% avg, 22.2% RB). The best open-source model (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) trails GPT-4o by 13.8 points on robustness, despite comparable accuracy on correctly-led questions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o dominates all proprietary and open-source models on both accuracy (45.2% avg) and robustness (36.0%), outperforming the best open-source model LLaVA-Video-72B by 7.6 points in accuracy and 16.3 points in robustness. Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieves the highest robustness among open-source models (22.2%) but lags GPT-4o by 13.8 points, confirming a significant robustness gap between open-source and proprietary systems. Gemini Pro (38.2% avg, 20.5% RB) sits between GPT-4o and the top open-source models, trailing GPT-4o by 7.0 points in accuracy. On correctly-led questions, GPT-4o (67.5%) and Oryx-1.5-7B (67.9%) perform comparably, but GPT-4o pulls ahead sharply on wrongly-led questions (39.8% vs. 26.0%), indicating superior resistance to misleading cues. All models remain far below the human baseline (83.2% avg, 64.4% RB), with even GPT-4o achieving less than half of human accuracy on primary open-ended questions (36.6% vs. 84.3%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against two lines of prior work: (1) video QA correctness benchmarks (VideoMME, MVBench, EgoSchema, TemporalBench) that the authors argue conflate frame-sampling limitations with genuine understanding gaps, and (2) robustness evaluation work (Schiappa et al.) that tests models under artificial pixel or instruction perturbations. Video-TT departs from both by ensuring every question is answerable within 80 uniformly sampled frames (isolating understanding from sampling) and by introducing 'natural adversarial questions'—rephrased, correctly-led, wrongly-led, and multiple-choice variants—that probe robustness through realistic linguistic manipulation rather than synthetic distortion. Conceptually, it draws on cognitive science notions of visual complexity and narrative complexity to define what makes a question genuinely hard, rather than relying on video length or domain specificity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark gap (84.3% human vs. 36.6% GPT-4o accuracy) that prior benchmarks masked by frame-sampling confounds, and identifies three concrete failure modes—spatial-temporal confusion, world-knowledge deficiency, and complex-plot incoherence—that give the field clear targets for improvement. The finding that open-source models (Qwen2.5-VL-72B) trail GPT-4o by 13.8 points on natural adversarial robustness despite comparable correctness suggests a specific architectural or training gap worth investigating. Open questions include whether the 80-frame constraint is sufficient for all real-world scenarios, whether the single-model scoring (Qwen2.5-72B) introduces bias, and whether the YouTube Shorts-only, sub-65-second scope generalizes to longer or more diverse video content.
