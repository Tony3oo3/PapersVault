## Problem

Existing physics-based character controllers rely on either full-body tracking policies or unified full-body skill embedding spaces, both of which struggle to generalize and scale to large, diverse motion datasets. Tracking-based approaches require increasingly many experts and manual skill extraction, while skill embedding methods face expressivity limits in their latent spaces. The paper argues that human motion is inherently modular at the body-part level, and that this structural inductive bias is underexploited in skill learning.

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a reference motion sequence (target joint rotations, positions, velocities, angular velocities at t+1) alongside the agent's proprioceptive state (current joint rotations, positions, velocities, angular velocities), all canonicalized in the agent's local frame. It outputs PD joint-angle/velocity targets for each actuated joint (no residual forces or torques) at 30 Hz, with the simulation running at 60 Hz. For downstream tasks, a frozen high-level policy additionally takes a task-specific goal signal and produces the 64-dimensional spherical skill embeddings (one per body part) that drive the low-level controllers.

## Method

ModSkill partitions the SMPL humanoid into five body parts (left/right legs, left/right arms, torso), each governed by an independent low-level Gaussian policy. A Skill Modularization Attention layer projects each part's state into query, key, and value vectors, computes cross-part attention scores, and produces a unit-sphere-normalized skill embedding per part that conditions its controller, enabling information sharing for full-body consistency while preserving part-specific specialization. Training uses PPO with a composite reward (exponential imitation error + AMP discriminator + energy penalty) over 3072 parallel Isaac Gym environments for ~2e9 steps. A Generative Adaptive Sampling scheme detects tracking failures and synthesizes diverse motion variants via a text-to-motion diffusion model (HumanML3D), filtered by the AMP discriminator before being added to the training set. For downstream tasks, the low-level controllers are frozen and a lightweight high-level policy is trained with task-specific rewards to compose the learned modular skills.

## Results

On the AMASS-Test full-body motion tracking task, ModSkill achieves 99.3% success rate with 32.2 mm global end-effector MPJPE and 22.7 mm root-relative MPJPE, outperforming the strongest baseline PHC+ (99.2% Succ, 36.1 mm, 24.1 mm) and PULSE (97.1% Succ, 54.1 mm, 43.5 mm). On downstream generative tasks, ModSkill matches or slightly exceeds PULSE in normalized return (Steering: 0.93 vs 0.92; Reach: 0.79 vs 0.77; Strike: 0.88 vs 0.88) while producing higher motion diversity (APD: 128.13 vs 123.74 for Steering; 116.12 vs 87.97 for Reach). On VR tracking, ModSkill ties PULSE in success rate (93.4%) but achieves lower tracking error (83.2 mm vs 88.6 mm global MPJPE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ModSkill outperforms all four baselines on the primary full-body tracking task. Against UHC [32], ModSkill reduces test E_g-mpjpe from 50.0 to 32.2 mm and E_vel from 12.1 to 6.3 mm/frame. Against PHC [35], the improvement is more pronounced on root-relative error (40.0 → 22.7 mm) and acceleration error (6.8 → 4.4 mm/frame²). Against PULSE [37], ModSkill achieves substantially lower tracking error (32.2 vs 54.1 mm global MPJPE) and higher success rate (99.3% vs 97.1%), while also matching PULSE on downstream task returns without requiring the additional distillation step PULSE needs. Against ASE [47], ModSkill dominates on VR tracking (93.4% vs 8.0% success rate) and downstream tasks (e.g., Reach: 0.79 vs 0.10 normalized return), confirming that modular part-level skill embeddings are far more expressive than a single full-body adversarial skill embedding.

## Relation to Prior Work

ModSkill extends the physics-based motion imitation line (PHC, PULSE) by replacing the monolithic full-body controller with body-part-level controllers coordinated through a skill modularization attention mechanism, drawing on the spherical skill-embedding formulation from ASE and the adversarial motion prior from AMP for full-body consistency. It departs from prior adaptive sampling strategies (which resample from a fixed motion dataset) by leveraging a text-to-motion diffusion model (HumanML3D) to synthesize novel training samples for challenging motions. The compositional, part-level design also connects to kinematics-based part-level motion generation (Motion Puzzle, PMP) but applies the idea to physics-based control with reusable skill embeddings rather than a single full-body policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The modular skill framework offers a practical path to scalable, reusable motor skills: part-level embeddings can be directly applied to downstream tasks (steering, reaching, striking, VR tracking) without the distillation step that PULSE requires, and new body-part controllers (e.g., dexterous hands) can be plugged in without retraining. The generative adaptive sampling strategy is a promising use of large motion generation models as a data augmentation tool for policy learning, though its gains over the base modular architecture are modest. Open questions include whether the modular decomposition generalizes to non-humanoid agents or contact-rich interactions, how to reduce the combinatorial search complexity of composing many part-level skills, and whether the approach transfers to real robots.
