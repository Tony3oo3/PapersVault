## Problem

3D biological shape inference from fluorescence microscopy is limited by two dominant paradigms: deep learning methods that require large, accurately annotated 3D datasets (impractical to produce) and active contour methods that depend on hand-tuned heuristics for intensity attraction and shape regularization. Neither approach incorporates the physical optics of fluorescence image formation, and inverse rendering—while well-established in computer graphics—remains largely unexplored for 3D biological imaging.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D fluorescence microscopy volume (e.g., 500³ voxels for confocal, up to 2048³ for light-sheet), an initial triangle mesh with known topology (spherical or toroidal, 10³–10⁴ vertices), and a parameterized Gaussian PSF specified by a 3×3 symmetric positive semi-definite covariance matrix. It produces an optimized non-manifold multimaterial triangle mesh, the inferred PSF covariance, and a rendered synthetic 3D fluorescence image. For multi-cell embryos, cell–cell interfaces are handled by doubling the fluorophore density at overlapping membranes within a single multimaterial mesh.

## Method

deltaMic formulates shape inference as a differentiable inverse rendering problem: a triangle mesh is rendered into a 3D fluorescence image by convolving a surface-supported Dirac fluorophore density with a Gaussian PSF in Fourier space, and both mesh vertex positions and PSF parameters are jointly optimized to minimize a weighted L² loss against the real image. The key technical contribution is a closed-form Fourier transform of arbitrary triangle meshes with analytical gradients with respect to vertex positions, derived via spatial Dirac distributions on each simplex, eliminating the need to densely populate meshes with point sources. A custom CUDA implementation parallelizes the forward and backward passes of the mesh FT across voxels, achieving ~10³ speedup over vectorized CPU code, while a narrow-band spectral filter skips frequency components where the PSF amplitude falls below 1% of its maximum, reducing cost by several orders of magnitude. Optimization uses a staggered scheme: shape updates employ the fast narrow-band FT with AdamUniform and Laplacian-diffusion regularization (solved via GPU sparse Cholesky), while PSF updates use the exact full-band FT with Adam. Notably, no explicit shape-regularization terms are required—the learned PSF naturally acts as a low-pass filter that stabilizes the mesh.

## Results

On the DM3D mouse organoid benchmark (15 3D images), deltaMic consistently outperforms the DM3D active mesh baseline across all three evaluated metrics: mean squared error (MSE), structural similarity index (SSIM), and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), as shown in Figure 9. On synthetic shape reconstruction (sphere, torus, Spot, dividing cell), 10,000 optimization iterations from simple initial meshes (sphere or torus) accurately recover the target geometries without remeshing or collision detection. On real embryo data (4-cell mouse, 8-cell ascidian, 16-cell worm), joint mesh-and-PSF optimization converges within 1k–10k steps, producing rendered images that closely match the original microscopy volumes. The CUDA-accelerated mesh Fourier transform achieves approximately 10³ speedup over a vectorized CPU implementation on a single NVIDIA V100 GPU.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole quantitative baseline comparison is against DM3D (Deforming Mesh 3D) [Smith et al., Biophysical Journal 2023], a recent active mesh algorithm designed for cell interface detection in fluorescence microscopy. Using the authors' dataset of 15 mouse organoid 3D images with ground-truth meshes, deltaMic is re-applied to the original image volumes and compared to DM3D outputs via MSE, SSIM, and PSNR computed between reconstructed shapes rendered with the inferred PSF. deltaMic outperforms DM3D on all three metrics across the full dataset (Figure 9), with the advantage attributed to the physics-informed rendering model that jointly optimizes shape and optical parameters rather than relying on hand-tuned intensity attraction and shape regularization heuristics. The comparison is particularly meaningful because DM3D was specifically designed for the same task (cell aggregate segmentation in 3D fluorescence), making it the most directly relevant active-mesh competitor. No comparison against deep learning segmentation methods (e.g., Cellpose, StarDist) is provided, as those produce voxel masks rather than explicit mesh representations and would require an additional surface-extraction step for fair geometric comparison.

## Relation to Prior Work

deltaMic bridges two previously separate lineages: active contour/mesh methods for biological segmentation (Kass et al., Dufour et al.) and differentiable rendering from computer graphics (Kato et al., Nicolet et al.). Prior active contour approaches for microscopy relied on hand-tuned regularization and ignored the physical optics of image formation, while differentiable rendering had not been applied to fluorescence microscopy. The paper extends the confocal image generation framework of Dmitrieff & Nédélec [18] by replacing discrete fluorophore sources with a continuous mesh-based density, and builds on the inverse-rendering optimization strategy of Nicolet et al. [65] (AdamUniform + Laplacian-regularized updates) to jointly optimize geometry and PSF parameters in a single differentiable pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By formulating shape inference as a physics-informed inverse problem, deltaMic eliminates two major practical barriers: the need for large annotated 3D datasets (required by DL) and the need for sample-specific regularization tuning (required by classical active contours). The joint PSF optimization effectively performs blind deconvolution alongside segmentation, which could enable quantitative shape analysis across heterogeneous imaging conditions without retraining. Open questions include extending to space-variant PSFs, 1D filament structures, and fully 3D volumes, as well as scaling to the largest light-sheet volumes (2048³) where computational cost remains a bottleneck. Integration with differentiable mechanical simulators for inverse force inference is identified as a promising near-term application.
