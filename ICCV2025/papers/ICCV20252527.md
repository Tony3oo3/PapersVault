## Problem

Astronomical telescopes face a fundamental tradeoff between field of view and spatial resolution: wide FoV or large-pixel detectors for photon-starved observations result in undersampled science images. Traditional multi-image super-resolution methods like drizzling require precise image registration and known geometric offsets, which are unreliable under the time-varying atmospheric aberrations that ground-based telescopes must contend with. Existing adaptive optics systems correct these aberrations but do not exploit their deformable mirrors to inject the controlled sub-pixel diversity needed for super-resolution.

## Input / Output

The method consumes N sub-exposure images (typically N=4) captured with the AO loop running normally but with different optimized phase profiles set as the deformable mirror's 'flat' between exposures. Each input image is convolved with the system PSF (including residual AO fitting errors and static optical imperfections) plus the optimized phase-induced distortion, and includes realistic sensor noise. The output is a single super-resolved image at 2×, 4×, or 8× the input pixel scale, reconstructed either by a modified EDSR CNN or a linear shift-and-add method, both trained jointly with the phase profiles.

## Method

The core contribution is an end-to-end joint optimization that simultaneously learns (1) the deformable mirror phase profiles—parameterized as Zernike modes, tip/tilt shifts, or direct non-modal shapes—and (2) a multi-image super-resolution reconstruction network (a modified EDSR CNN accepting variable input channels, or a linear shift-and-add reconstructor). The phase profiles are optimized to produce sub-pixel shifts that minimally perturb the telescope PSF while maximizing the information diversity available to the SR network, all while the AO loop continues to correct atmospheric turbulence on top of the set 'flat.' Training is performed entirely offline using simulated AO PSFs generated with the OOMAO toolbox (matching an 8 m telescope with a 397-actuator DM at 1 kHz) and high-resolution images from DIV2K and Microsoft ImagePairs, with the loss computed between the reconstructed output and the ground-truth HR image. The key insight is that the AO system's existing hardware can be repurposed to inject optimized diversity without any hardware modifications or impact on the science payload, and the jointly-optimized phases outperform both fixed tip/tilt rasters and unoptimized CNN baselines by up to 12 dB in SNR.

## Results

With N=4 exposures and a 2× super-resolution factor, the best CNN method (Non-Modal phase) achieves 43.13 dB PSNR, compared to 33.72 dB for the no-learned-phase baseline — a gain of ~9.4 dB. The Zernike-mode CNN reaches 41.51 dB, outperforming classic tip/tilt (39.56 dB) by ~2 dB. The paper reports up to 12 dB SNR improvement over non-AO super-resolution baselines, and jointly optimizing phase profiles with the CNN consistently outperforms training the CNN without phase optimization. Gains diminish at higher scale factors (8×: 27.79 dB for Non-Modal CNN vs. 24.04 dB for no-phase), and increasing the number of exposures from 4 to 32 yields only modest additional improvement (~1.3 dB for CNN Non-Modal).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five phase representations (No Learned Phase, Tip/Tilt Classic, Tip/Tilt Optimized, Zernike Modes, Non-Modal) under both CNN and Linear reconstruction. The CNN with Non-Modal phase (43.13 dB at 2×) outperforms the no-learned-phase CNN baseline (33.72 dB) by 9.4 dB, confirming that optimized phase injection is critical. Zernike-mode phases (41.51 dB) beat classic tip/tilt (39.56 dB) by ~2 dB, showing that richer phase representations capture more sub-pixel diversity. The CNN reconstruction consistently surpasses the Linear (shift-and-add/Drizzle-like) method by ~12 dB at 2× (43.13 vs. 31.08 dB), highlighting the advantage of learned reconstruction over linear blending. Joint end-to-end optimization of phase and CNN yields further gains over training the CNN with fixed unoptimized phases, as demonstrated on the USAF resolution target (Fig. 4). At higher scale factors (8×), the advantage of optimized phases narrows (27.79 vs. 24.04 dB), suggesting diminishing returns when the undersampling is more extreme.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the authors' prior work on AO-mediated sub-pixel super-resolution [54] by introducing end-to-end joint optimization of deformable mirror phase profiles and a deep learning SR network (based on EDSR), rather than treating the two as separate stages. It departs from classical astronomical SR methods like Drizzle, which rely on precise image registration and known sub-pixel offsets, by instead using the AO system's deformable mirror to inject optimized, non-planar phase distortions (Zernike modes or free-form) that generate diversity without any registration step. The approach also draws on the broader computational imaging literature on joint optics-and-algorithm optimization (e.g., Sitzmann et al. for thin lenses, Tseng et al. for nano-optics), but adapts it to the unique constraints of ground-based telescopes where the optical system cannot be modified and must simultaneously correct atmospheric turbulence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that this method requires zero hardware modifications to existing observatories and does not interfere with the science payload or AO correction loop, making it a low-cost path to higher resolution on undersampled instruments. The 12 dB SNR gain over non-AO SR baselines and the MTF improvements across spatial frequencies are compelling, but the method is currently limited to single-conjugate AO systems and has not yet been validated on actual sky with a science-class telescope. Key open questions include whether the approach extends naturally to multi-conjugate AO with multiple DMs, whether real-time video super-resolution is feasible by cycling phases, and whether the optimized phase profiles remain robust under varying atmospheric conditions beyond those captured in training telemetry.
