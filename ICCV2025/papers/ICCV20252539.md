## Problem

The video editing field lacks a standardized benchmark for fine-grained, object-level editing evaluation, with the only existing dataset (TGVE/TGVE+) offering limited editing types, no object-specific masks or prompts, and no generated videos. Additionally, no rectified flow (RF)-based video editing methods existed prior to this work, leaving a gap between the demonstrated superiority of RF models in image editing and their unexplored potential in video editing.

## Input / Output

The editing pipeline takes a source video (35–126 frames at 384P or 480P resolution), a source text prompt describing the initial scene, and a target text prompt specifying the desired object-level modification, producing an edited video with the target object replaced while preserving background and temporal consistency. The FiVE-Bench provides 420 source-target prompt pairs with explicit object words, SAM2-generated masks for background preservation evaluation, and GPT-4o-generated instruction prompts for compatibility with instruction-based models. Both Pyramid-Edit and Wan-Edit operate training-free and inversion-free, requiring no optimization or auxiliary depth/segmentation maps.

## Method

The core contribution adapts FlowEdit—an inversion-free RF image editing method that computes the difference between source and target vector fields—to video editing in two distinct ways. Pyramid-Edit integrates FlowEdit into each resolution window of Pyramid-Flow's multi-resolution, temporally autoregressive architecture: within each window [s_k, e_k], the edited latent is updated via the vector field difference conditioned on the window's endpoint, with corrective noise applied at resolution transitions to maintain distributional continuity. For temporal consistency, Pyramid-Edit conditions on both source and edited latents from previous frames. Wan-Edit, in contrast, directly applies FlowEdit to the full multi-frame sequence because Wan2.1's MMDiT architecture flattens 3D VAE-encoded frames into a single sequence processed by self-attention, making it structurally equivalent to image generation. The key insight is that RF models' near-straight sampling paths enable inversion-free editing via simple vector field subtraction, and this extends naturally to video by leveraging the temporal modeling already embedded in the T2V generation backbone.

## Results

On FiVE-Bench, Wan-Edit achieves the best overall performance among all 7 methods: it leads on LPIPS (94.61×10³), SSIM (82.55×10²), CLIPSIM (26.39), CLIPSIM-edit (21.23), and Motion Fidelity (89.43×10²), while running at 3.07 s/frame. On the proposed FiVE-Acc metric, DMT (optimization-based) scores highest at 48.42%, with Wan-Edit close behind at 46.97% and Pyramid-Edit at 43.84%. Among diffusion baselines, VideoGrain is the strongest, achieving the best Structure Distance (12.40×10³) and PSNR (27.05), but at 27.12 s/frame it is ~9× slower than Wan-Edit. RF-based methods (Pyramid-Edit, Wan-Edit) consistently outperform all five diffusion baselines on the majority of the 15 evaluated metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Wan-Edit outperforms all five diffusion baselines on the majority of metrics. Against TokenFlow and VidToMe, Wan-Edit wins decisively on background preservation (LPIPS 94.61 vs. 263.61/263.91; SSIM 82.55 vs. 72.51/70.69) and text alignment (CLIPS-edit 21.23 vs. 21.15/21.05), while also being faster or comparable in speed. Against AnyV2V, Wan-Edit dominates across nearly every metric (e.g., Structure Dist 12.53 vs. 71.36, Motion Fidelity 89.43 vs. 60.36). Against DMT, Wan-Edit trades a small FiVE-Acc gap (46.97 vs. 48.42) for dramatically better background preservation (LPIPS 94.61 vs. 404.60) and 8× faster inference (3.07 s vs. 25.98 s/frame). Against VideoGrain, the closest competitor, Wan-Edit matches on Structure Distance (12.53 vs. 12.40) and surpasses it on LPIPS, SSIM, CLIPSIM, CLIPSIM-edit, and Motion Fidelity, while running 9× faster (3.07 s vs. 27.12 s/frame); VideoGrain retains an edge only on PSNR, MSE, and NIQE. On FiVE-Acc specifically, DMT leads (48.42%) due to its iterative optimization prioritizing text fidelity, but Wan-Edit is the best non-optimization method (46.97%) and the best overall when speed and background preservation are considered jointly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends FlowEdit, an inversion-free rectified-flow image editing method, to the video domain by adapting it to two state-of-the-art RF-based T2V generators: Pyramid-Flow (requiring multi-resolution modifications) and Wan2.1 (requiring no modifications due to its DiT architecture). It positions itself against the existing video editing landscape dominated by diffusion-based methods (TokenFlow, DMT, VidToMe, AnyV2V, VideoGrain), which all require inversion or optimization steps. The paper also carves out a distinct evaluation niche: while TGVE/TGVE+ cover broad editing types, FiVE-Bench specifically targets fine-grained, object-level modifications with explicit source/target object words, masks, and VLM-based accuracy scoring—addressing a gap the authors identify in prior benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FiVE-Bench provides the first standardized, fine-grained video editing benchmark with 100 videos, 420 prompt pairs, and 15 metrics, enabling fair comparison that was previously impossible due to self-collected small-scale datasets. The finding that RF-based editing consistently outperforms diffusion-based methods across most metrics—and that Wan-Edit achieves this with the least hyperparameter sensitivity—suggests the field should pivot toward RF architectures for editing. However, all seven methods fail at object removal, and the authors acknowledge the adaptation is 'simple,' leaving open whether purpose-built RF video editing architectures can handle large motions and removal tasks. The VLM-based FiVE-Acc metric, validated against human evaluation, offers a reusable tool for semantic-level editing assessment beyond pixel similarity.
