## Problem

While MLLMs have shown strong performance across diverse multimodal tasks, their ability to perform visual spatial planning—comprehending spatial arrangements and devising action plans—remains poorly understood and systematically under-evaluated. Existing benchmarks assess perception or reasoning in isolation but fail to diagnose which specific capability bottleneck (perception vs. reasoning) causes MLLMs to fail on spatial planning tasks that are trivial for humans.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251665/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task presents the model with one or more spatial scene images (grid mazes, stacked block configurations, collision maps, or real Google Map screenshots) interleaved with text instructions that specify the task, element appearances, and constraints. Outputs are structured action plans rather than free-form text: directional path sequences with step counts (e.g., 'head north 2, then east 3'), spatial relation labels, environment descriptions, collision yes/no determinations, or block-moving action sequences. The multi-image, interleaved-text format is a key design choice that stresses models trained primarily on single-image inputs.

## Method

VSP is a 4.6K-question benchmark spanning four scenarios (Maze Navigation, Blocks World, Collision, Google Map) and 12 tasks: one main spatial planning task plus four diagnostic sub-tasks (T1: single object perception, T2: spatial relation perception, T3: environment perception, T4: spatial reasoning) per scenario. The key diagnostic mechanism is a text-vs-image ablation: for every image input, a corresponding textual description (table or prose) is generated and substituted, isolating perception errors from reasoning errors. Difficulty is controlled progressively via map size (3×3 to 8×8), number of blocks (3–5), or minimum action steps (1–7). Maze inputs are generated via OpenAI Gym with randomized layouts, and Blocks World pairs are sampled from the BIRD dataset with verified solvability. Evaluation is conducted zero-shot across 12 MLLMs, supplemented by in-context learning (1–4 shots) and LoRA fine-tuning (10K samples) experiments on open-source models.

## Results

On the VSP benchmark, the best-performing model (Gemini-2.0) achieves only 45.33% overall zero-shot success rate across all four spatial planning scenarios, while the second-best (Claude-3.7) reaches 44.92%. Performance degrades sharply with difficulty: GPT-4V drops from 55% on 3×3 mazes to 10% on 8×8 mazes, and even Gemini-2.0 falls from 93% (3×3) to 27% (8×8). In the dynamic Collision and realistic Google Map scenarios, all models perform poorly, with the best scores being 44% (Gemini-2.0, Collision) and 11% (Claude-3.7, Google Map). Open-source models (LLaVA-Next, InternLM, SPHINX, LLaMA-3.2, Pixtral, Qwen-2.5) score between 3.00% and 15.92% overall, far below private models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates 12 MLLMs (6 private, 6 open-source) under zero-shot settings. Among private models, Gemini-2.0 (45.33%) and Claude-3.7 (44.92%) lead, substantially outperforming their predecessors Gemini-1.0 (11.67%) and Claude-3 (14.67%), indicating rapid but still incomplete progress. GPT-4o (30.42%) improves over GPT-4V (21.17%) but trails the top two by ~15 points. A stark gap separates private from open-source models: the best open-source model (Qwen-2.5, 15.92%) scores less than one-third of Gemini-2.0, and LLaVA-Next (3.00%) is near random. In the Collision scenario, Qwen-2.5 (45%) and Claude-3.7 (49%) are the only models above 40%, while in Google Map all models score below 11%, revealing that realistic map interpretation remains an unsolved challenge across the board.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of classical AI planning (maze navigation, blocks world) and MLLM evaluation. While prior benchmarks (MMMU, MME, AlgoPuzzle, MMSI) assess individual visual or reasoning capabilities in isolation, VSP is the first to jointly evaluate end-to-end spatial planning AND decompose it into diagnostic sub-tasks (single-object perception, spatial relation perception, environment perception, spatial reasoning) to identify which specific capability fails. It departs from text-based planning benchmarks by requiring direct visual input interpretation, and from existing visual benchmarks by adding the diagnostic dimension that prior work explicitly lacks (as shown in Table 1).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even the strongest MLLMs (Gemini-2.0, Claude-3.7) achieve only 27–45% success on tasks humans find trivial, and the diagnostic decomposition shows visual perception—not reasoning—is the dominant bottleneck, as replacing images with equivalent text descriptions dramatically improves performance. This reframes the improvement target: rather than building more sophisticated reasoning chains, the field should prioritize better spatial perception in vision encoders or cross-modal alignment. Open questions include whether fine-tuning on spatial data (as shown for LLaVA) can close the gap at scale, and whether the findings generalize beyond the abstract grid-based scenarios to truly complex real-world environments.
