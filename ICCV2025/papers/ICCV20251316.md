## Problem

While image classification models are routinely compared on accuracy and robustness, it remains unclear what specific pixels or features each model actually relies on to make its decision. Prior explanation tools like SAG use fixed-size patches and arbitrary confidence thresholds, making their 'minimal sufficient explanations' neither truly minimal in pixels nor guaranteed to reproduce the original classification. This paper introduces 'concentration'—the smallest set of pixels sufficient for a model to reproduce its top-1 prediction—as a rigorous, model-agnostic measure for comparing how different architectures focus their attention.

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes standard RGB images (500 from ImageNet-1k validation, 500 from test) and 15 pre-trained classifiers spanning five architectures (Inception, ResNet, ConvNext, ViT, EVA), all fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k and deployed via ONNX runtime. For each image-model pair, ReX produces a minimal pixel set (MPS): the smallest subset of original pixels that, when all other pixels are set to a baseline of 0, still yields the same top-1 class. MPSs are quantified as a ratio of the full input image area, and compared across models using the Sørensen-Dice coefficient (overlap) and Hausdorff distance (spatial separation).

## Method

The core tool is ReX, a black-box causal explanation algorithm grounded in the Halpern-Pearl structural model of actual causality. ReX iteratively partitions the image into superpixels, generates mutants by masking superpixel combinations with a baseline value, tests each mutant against the model, and distributes causal responsibility across superpixels based on how frequently they appear in passing combinations. This refinement is repeated over 20 random initial partitions, producing a responsibility landscape from which pixels are ranked and added incrementally until the original classification is reproduced, yielding an approximately minimal sufficient pixel set. The statistical analysis employs Kruskal-Wallis H tests for cross-architecture MPS size differences, Friedman tests for intra-architecture differences, and mixed linear models to isolate the effect of classification correctness on MPS size, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

## Results

Across 15 models on 1000 ImageNet-1k images, MPS size varies dramatically by architecture: ConvNext-V2 Huge v1 and EVA Giant require only ~5.0–5.4% of the image, while Inception models require ~23–25% (a 3.6× difference). The Kruskal-Wallis H test confirms a highly significant inter-architecture difference (H(4) = 1176.134, p < 0.001), and the Friedman test shows significant intra-architecture differences for all families except Inception (p = 0.36). Incorrect classifications yield MPSs that are on average 2.6% larger than correct ones (SE 0.4%, p < 0.01), a small but statistically robust effect replicated on CalTech-256 (+3.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five architecture families (EVA, ConvNext, ViT, ResNet, Inception) on MPS size, location, and correctness sensitivity. ConvNext and EVA produce the smallest MPSs (5–8% of the image), roughly 3.6× smaller than Inception models (23–25%), and are statistically distinct from all other families. ResNet occupies an intermediate range (13–15%), while ViT is the most variable within its family (9.9% for ViT-Large vs. 15.6% for ViT-Huge V1). On spatial overlap, pairwise Sørensen-Dice coefficients between best-performing models are low (0.14–0.30), and Hausdorff distances are large (79–139 px), confirming that different architectures attend to substantially different pixel regions; Inception V4 is the most spatially isolated, with the highest Hausdorff distances to every other architecture. On correctness sensitivity, all architectures show a small but significant increase in MPS size for misclassifications, with the effect ranging from ~2% (EVA Giant) to ~4% (ResNet 152-B A2), and the overall mixed-model estimate is +2.6% (p < 0.01).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the ReX causal explanation framework [7] to define minimal pixel sets (MPSs) as a rigorous, pixel-level measure of a model's 'concentration' on an image. It departs conceptually from the patch-based, threshold-dependent sufficiency criterion used by SAG [33] and Jiang et al. [18], arguing that fixed-size patches and arbitrary confidence thresholds make explanations insufficiently constrained. The broader XAI landscape (LIME, SHAP, RISE, GradCAM) provides pixel rankings or heatmaps but does not isolate a minimal sufficient subset that reproduces the classification, which is the specific gap this paper addresses. The study positions itself as a large-scale empirical comparison across 15 models and 5 architectures, extending the single-model or two-architecture comparisons found in prior XAI work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the largest, most accurate models (EVA-Giant, ConvNext) rely on only 5–8% of input pixels while remaining highly confident raises concrete safety concerns for high-stakes deployments in healthcare, autonomous navigation, and quality assurance. The small but statistically significant increase in MPS size for misclassifications (2.6%) suggests a practical post-hoc confidence check, though its practical utility remains to be validated across domains. Open questions include the effect of model confidence on MPS size, the role of multiple disjoint explanations, and whether noisy ImageNet labels confound the correct/incorrect comparison. The paper also leaves unresolved whether very small MPSs definitively indicate overfitting or simply reflect different inductive biases in how large models encode visual information.
