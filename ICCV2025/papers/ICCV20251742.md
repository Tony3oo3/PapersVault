## Problem

Existing poisoning attacks in federated learning primarily target model integrity (e.g., reducing accuracy), while Byzantine-robust defenses focus on filtering corrupted updates by magnitude. This leaves a critical gap: membership inference attacks (PMIAs) that exploit angular deviation to steal client privacy data remain largely unaddressed, and current defenses are ineffective against attacks that strategically position malicious gradients within the benign angular envelope.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251742/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker operates on two local sample sets: an attack set D_attack (target samples whose membership is to be inferred, with labels flipped to incorrect ones) and a mask set D_mask (correctly-labeled samples used to construct a masking gradient). The crafted malicious gradient g_malicious = α·g_attack + g_mask is transmitted to the server alongside benign client gradients. The defense (ATM) consumes the full set of received gradients, computes pairwise angular distances, and outputs a filtered aggregated gradient after trimming the top 2b angular outliers, where b is a trim parameter satisfying 2b < |G|.

## Method

FedPoisonMIA formulates the attack as an optimization problem that maximizes the angular deviation between the malicious gradient and benign gradients while constraining this deviation to remain below the maximum benign-benign angular spread, thereby causing Byzantine-robust defenses to discard benign gradients instead of the malicious one. A greedy algorithm (Algorithm 1) iteratively selects mask samples that maximize this objective, and the scaling factor α is then optimized under the same angular constraint. The defense, Angular Trimmed-mean (ATM), replaces magnitude-based trimming with angular-deviation-based trimming: it computes the mean angle of each gradient relative to all others, sorts gradients by this mean angle, and removes the top 2b outliers before averaging the remainder. A convergence theorem (Theorem 1) provides a bounded ℓ2-deviation guarantee for ATM under i.i.d. angle assumptions with m malicious angles (2m < n).

## Results

FedPoisonMIA achieves the highest attack accuracy across all four datasets (Texas100, CIFAR-10, STL-10, FER2013) and all six defense mechanisms, reaching 89–93% under standard defenses (FedAvg, Median, Trimmed-mean) in IID settings. The proposed ATM defense reduces FedPoisonMIA's attack accuracy to 71–85% depending on the dataset, outperforming all other defenses (e.g., on Texas100 IID, ATM limits accuracy to 80.3% vs. 91.3% for Median and 89.1% for Top-k). Under Non-IID settings, the attack still maintains 77–96% accuracy, and the partial-knowledge variant degrades by at most 8.0% relative to the full-knowledge setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedPoisonMIA consistently outperforms all four baseline PMIA methods (Passive, Gradient Ascent, AGREvader, Adaptive) across every dataset and defense. On Texas100 (IID, C=0.8), FedPoisonMIA reaches 89.7% under FedAvg versus 82.6% (GA), 80.4% (AGREvader), 78.4% (Adaptive), and 64.3% (Passive); the gap widens under Median where FedPoisonMIA hits 91.3% while baselines range from 58.3% to 82.0%. On CIFAR-10 (IID), FedPoisonMIA achieves 91.3% under FedAvg, surpassing GA (72.7%), AGREvader (70.0%), Adaptive (68.1%), and Passive (58.0%) by 18.6–33.0 percentage points. The ATM defense is the only mechanism that meaningfully suppresses FedPoisonMIA, reducing its accuracy to 71.3% (CIFAR-10) and 80.3% (Texas100) in IID, whereas Median and Trimmed-mean still allow 75–93% attack accuracy. Against additional Byzantine-robust defenses (Fang, Multi-Krum, DeepSight) on Texas100, FedPoisonMIA still achieves 84–95% accuracy, confirming that existing defenses are largely ineffective against this attack.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of poisoning membership inference attacks (PMIAs) in federated learning, building directly on prior active attacks like Gradient Ascent [36] and AGREvader [57], which manipulate gradients to infer training membership. The key departure is the angular-deviation framing: rather than relying on label-flipping or gradient masking in the value space, FedPoisonMIA explicitly optimizes the angle between malicious and benign updates, using a greedy mask-sample selection to keep the crafted gradient within the convex angular envelope of benign updates. On the defense side, ATM is inspired by the Trimmed-mean approach [55] but replaces magnitude-based trimming with angular-alignment-based filtering, targeting the specific failure mode exposed by the attack.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a concrete privacy gap: existing Byzantine-robust aggregation rules (Median, Trimmed-mean, Multi-Krum, Fang, DeepSight) are designed to protect model integrity, not membership privacy, and the attack consistently bypasses them with 80–95% accuracy. The proposed ATM defense is computationally lightweight (sorting over clients rather than high-dimensional vectors) and reduces attack accuracy by 5–15 percentage points, but it does not eliminate the threat entirely, leaving open the question of whether angular-based defenses can be made provably complete against adaptive attackers. Broader implications include the need for FL systems to treat privacy-preserving aggregation as a first-class design goal alongside integrity, and the observation that the attack's effectiveness under partial-knowledge settings (only ~8% degradation) suggests the threat is practical even without full system visibility.
