## Problem

Existing text-to-vector generation methods produce over-parameterized outputs composed of nearly pixel-like shapes, undermining the resolution-independence and editability that make vector graphics valuable. Additionally, these approaches treat the layered structure of SVGs as a secondary concern, often requiring separate post-processing stages (image tracing, hierarchy optimization) to impose meaningful shape ordering. No prior method directly generates semantically coherent, ordered, and editable SVGs within a single optimization process.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt as primary input, with optional conditioning on a background color (RGB triplet) and target aspect ratio that can be varied at inference time without retraining. The output is a layered SVG consisting of n ordered closed shapes (default n=16), each defined by 12 control points forming four cubic Bézier curves plus an RGB fill color. The entire scene is encoded in the weights of a compact MLP; at inference, querying indices 1 through n sequentially yields the ordered shape list, and changing the background color or aspect ratio input produces a re-palette or re-proportioned variant of the same learned representation.

## Method

NeuralSVG represents an SVG as an implicit neural function: a small MLP with two parallel branches (MLP_pos for 12×2 control points, MLP_c for RGB color) maps shape indices {1,…,n} through Random Fourier Features (128-dim) to shape parameters, which are rendered via a differentiable rasterizer. Training uses Score Distillation Sampling with a Stable Diffusion model (fine-tuned via LoRA on vector art) to align the rendered SVG with the text prompt. The key architectural contribution is a Nested Dropout regularizer: at each training step, a random truncation index is sampled and all shapes above it are dropped before rendering, forcing earlier shapes to carry standalone semantic meaning and establishing a natural coarse-to-fine layer ordering. Initialization is performed by extracting salient regions from a diffusion-generated image and fitting the MLP to those positions and colors via L2 loss before SDS optimization begins.

## Results

On the 128 VectorFusion prompts, NeuralSVG (16 shapes) achieves a CLIP Text-Image Similarity of 26.94 and R-Precision of 67.18, outperforming VectorFusion (16 shapes: 23.47 / 48.03) and SVGDreamer (16 shapes: 20.89 / 43.30) on both metrics at the same shape budget. When baselines are allowed more shapes (64 for VectorFusion, 256 for SVGDreamer), they reach higher R-Precision (83.46 and 85.03) but NeuralSVG still attains the highest CLIP similarity (26.94 vs. 26.33 and 26.58). The paper emphasizes that the higher R-Precision of the multi-shape baselines comes at the cost of producing disorganized, nearly pixel-like shapes that are impractical for editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

At the same 16-shape budget, NeuralSVG clearly wins: it beats VectorFusion by 19.15 points on R-Precision (67.18 vs. 48.03) and by 3.47 on CLIP similarity (26.94 vs. 23.47), and beats SVGDreamer by 23.88 on R-Precision (67.18 vs. 43.30) and by 6.05 on CLIP similarity (26.94 vs. 20.89). When baselines are given 4–16× more shapes, they surpass NeuralSVG on R-Precision (83–85 vs. 67) but only marginally on CLIP similarity (26.33–26.58 vs. 26.94), and the paper argues their outputs become visually disorganized and uneditable. Comparisons to NIVeL and Text-to-Vector are qualitative only (no public code); the paper reports that NIVeL exhibits tracing artifacts along contours due to its rasterization-to-SVG pipeline, while Text-to-Vector produces comparable visual quality but requires a separate post-processing stage to establish shape hierarchy, whereas NeuralSVG learns the ordering in a single optimization pass.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeuralSVG applies the implicit neural representation paradigm from NeRFs to SVG generation, where a compact MLP encodes the entire vector scene in its weights, optimized via Score Distillation Sampling (SDS) from DreamFusion. It departs from prior text-to-vector methods (VectorFusion, SVGDreamer, NIVeL, Text-to-Vector) in two key ways: it avoids rasterization and image tracing by directly predicting Bézier control points, and it integrates shape hierarchy into the generation process via a Nested Dropout regularizer [38] rather than relying on post-hoc decomposition. The inference-time control capability (color palette, aspect ratio) extends the implicit representation idea from Alaluf et al.'s personalization work to vector graphics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap in text-to-vector generation: existing methods produce visually plausible but structurally uneditable SVGs with hundreds of near-pixel shapes, undermining the core advantage of vector graphics. By showing that 16 ordered shapes with dropout-based regularization yield more semantically meaningful and editable outputs than 256+ unstructured shapes, it reframes the quality metric from pixel-level fidelity to structural editability. Open questions include scalability to more complex multi-object scenes, whether the ordered representation generalizes beyond 16 shapes, and how the approach performs with newer, stronger diffusion backbones. The inference-time control (single representation → multiple color palettes or aspect ratios) is a promising direction but remains limited to simple conditioning signals.
