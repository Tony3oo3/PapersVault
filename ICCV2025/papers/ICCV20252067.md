## Problem

Transposed convolution, despite its name, is not a true mathematical inverse of standard convolution—it merely inserts zeros between input elements before applying a standard convolution. No principled reverse convolution operator has been established as a standard building block in neural architectures, leaving a gap between the desire for mathematically grounded inversion and the available tools in deep learning frameworks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252067/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The Converse2D operator operates on multi-channel feature maps (not just single-channel or 3-channel images), supporting arbitrary channel dimensions and spatial upsampling via a stride parameter s. In the deblurring variant (Converse-USRNet), the known blur kernel is projected to a 64-dimensional embedding via a small KernelNet and directly parameterizes the Converse2D operator at the feature level, rather than operating in the image domain as in USRNet. All three variants (denoising, SR, deblurring) take standard RGB images and produce same-size RGB outputs, with the SR variant performing ×4 upscaling from bicubically downsampled inputs.

## Method

The core contribution is the Converse2D operator, which inverts depthwise convolution followed by downsampling by formulating a regularized least-squares problem and solving it in closed form via FFT under circular boundary conditions (Eq. 6–7). The kernel K is learned end-to-end with Softmax normalization to enforce non-negativity and sum-to-one constraints, while the regularization parameter λ is parameterized as Sigmoid(b−9)+ε to allow per-channel adaptive learning. The operator is wrapped in a Transformer-like converse block combining layer normalization, 1×1 convolutions for channel mixing, GELU activation, and residual connections, decoupling spatial modeling (Converse2D) from channel interaction (1×1 conv). This block replaces standard residual blocks in DnCNN, SRResNet, and USRNet to produce Converse-DnCNN, Converse-SRResNet, and Converse-USRNet, which are trained end-to-end with standard L2 loss on their respective restoration tasks.

## Results

For Gaussian denoising (σ=25), Converse-DnCNN achieves 30.70 dB on Set12 and 29.36 dB on BSD68, outperforming both the depthwise convolution variant (Conv-DnCNN: 30.64/29.30) and the depthwise transposed convolution variant (ConvT-DnCNN: 30.61/29.29). For ×4 super-resolution, Converse-SRResNet reaches 32.25 dB on Set5 and 26.24 dB on Urban100, matching or slightly exceeding SRResNet (32.21/26.09). In non-blind deblurring, Converse-USRNet achieves 32.46 dB on BSD100 and 31.96 dB on Urban100, surpassing Conv-USRNet (32.18/31.48) by 0.28 and 0.48 dB respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DnCNN (the original architecture), Converse-DnCNN improves PSNR by 0.27 dB on Set12 (30.70 vs. 30.43) and 0.13 dB on BSD68 (29.36 vs. 29.23), though it uses more parameters (735K vs. 557K). More critically, against the two direct operator alternatives within the same architecture, Converse-DnCNN consistently wins: it beats Conv-DnCNN by 0.06 dB (Set12) and 0.06 dB (BSD68), and ConvT-DnCNN by 0.09 dB and 0.07 dB respectively, confirming that the mathematically grounded inverse operator outperforms both forward and transposed depthwise convolution. For super-resolution, Converse-SRResNet is essentially on par with SRResNet (32.25 vs. 32.21 on Set5; 26.24 vs. 26.09 on Urban100), demonstrating that the reverse convolution operator can replace standard upsampling without performance loss. In the deblurring task, Converse-USRNet outperforms Conv-USRNet across all five blur kernels on both BSD100 and Urban100, with the largest gap of 0.68 dB on the 7×7 motion blur kernel (38.40 vs. 37.80 on BSD100), highlighting the advantage of operating in the feature domain with kernel conditioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new operator-level contribution by proposing the first depthwise reverse convolution (Converse2D) that serves as a true mathematical inverse of depthwise convolution, formulated as a regularized least-squares problem with a closed-form FFT solution. It explicitly departs from transposed convolution (which is zero-insertion plus convolution, not an inverse), from iterative deconvolution methods for deblurring (Wiener, plug-and-play, deep unfolding) that are restricted to image-domain single-channel or RGB processing, and from invertible convolution that imposes rigid shape constraints for normalizing flows. Architecturally, it repurposes the block structure of DnCNN, SRResNet, and USRNet by swapping their core convolutional units with the proposed Converse Block (Converse2D + LayerNorm + 1×1 Conv + GELU), drawing a deliberate analogy to Transformer blocks that separate spatial and channel-wise processing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, non-iterative alternative to transposed convolution that could become a standard building block in deep architectures, particularly for tasks requiring spatial upsampling or explicit inversion of convolutional operations. However, the empirical validation is limited to small-scale image restoration benchmarks (Set12, BSD68, Set5, Urban100) with modest PSNR gains (typically 0.1–0.3 dB over transposed convolution baselines), and the operator is restricted to depthwise (per-channel) operation, requiring separate 1×1 convolutions for cross-channel mixing. Open questions include whether the closed-form FFT solution scales efficiently to very large feature maps, whether the approach generalizes to non-depthwise (group or full) convolutions, and whether the operator delivers meaningful gains in large-scale generative or foundation-model settings as the authors themselves note as future work.
