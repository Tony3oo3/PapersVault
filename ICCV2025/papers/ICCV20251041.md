## Problem

Existing instruction-based image editing methods excel at modifying single, prominent objects but fail in complex scenes with multiple similar entities, because they cannot reliably isolate the specific target referenced by a natural-language expression. This 'referring expression challenge'—well-studied in discriminative tasks like segmentation—has been largely neglected in the generative editing domain, and no benchmark or dataset specifically targets it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251041/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes an RGB image (1024×1024 in the training pipeline) and a free-form text editing instruction that embeds a referring expression to disambiguate the target object among multiple similar instances (e.g., 'change the shirt color of the tennis player on the right to red'). The output is a single edited image where only the referenced object is modified while the rest of the scene is preserved. Unlike standard instruction-based editing, the instruction must contain spatial or attribute-based cues that uniquely identify one object in a multi-entity scene.

## Method

RefEdit introduces a four-stage synthetic data generation pipeline: (1) GPT-4o generates image prompts containing multiple similar entities with distinguishing attributes, along with editing instructions and descriptive referring expressions; (2) FLUX synthesizes 1024×1024 base images; (3) Grounded SAM (combining Grounding DINO and Segment Anything) produces pixel-accurate masks from the referring expressions; and (4) FlowChef (a gradient-free, inversion-free rectified-flow editing method) performs the actual edit, while Inpaint Anything handles object removals. The resulting 20K+ editing triplets are combined with MagicBrush data (30K total) to fine-tune Stable Diffusion v1.5 (initialized from InstructPix2Pix) and Stable Diffusion 3 (initialized from UltraEdit) via standard diffusion denoising loss. The key insight is that a small, targeted dataset explicitly containing multi-entity referring expressions outperforms baselines trained on millions of samples that never encounter this complexity, and that inversion-free editing at data-generation time avoids the localization errors that propagate through inversion-based pipelines.

## Results

On the proposed RefEdit-Bench (Modified VIEScore with GPT-4o), RefEdit (SDv1.5) achieves an overall score of 4.68 on Easy and 3.93 on Hard, while RefEdit-SD3 achieves 4.99 and 3.98 respectively, outperforming all baselines. The strongest baseline, InstructDiffusion-HA, reaches only 4.21 (Easy) and 3.75 (Hard). On the standard PIE-Bench, RefEdit-SD3 attains the best overall VIEScore of 5.70 (SC=6.04, PQ=6.47), surpassing OmniEdit (5.12) which was trained on 1.2M samples, while RefEdit uses only 20K additional synthetic triplets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RefEdit and RefEdit-SD3 outperform all 10 baselines on both Easy and Hard categories of RefEdit-Bench. The closest competitor is InstructDiffusion-HA (O=4.21 Easy, 3.75 Hard), which RefEdit surpasses by 0.47 and 0.18 overall score points respectively; notably, InstructDiffusion-HA is the only baseline that beats RefEdit on Hard SC (4.56 vs. 4.51). Among larger-architecture baselines, UltraEdit (SD3-based, 4M training samples) and CosXLEdit (SDXL-based) perform substantially worse than the SDv1.5-based RefEdit, with UltraEdit dropping to O=2.59 on Hard—suggesting overfitting from training data lacking referring-expression complexity. On PIE-Bench, RefEdit-SD3 achieves the best overall VIEScore (5.70) over OmniEdit (5.12, trained on 1.2M samples) and MagicBrush (4.79, trained on 10K human-annotated samples), confirming that the 20K synthetic RefEdit-Data generalizes beyond referring-expression tasks. FLUX-OmniEdit performs worst across all settings (O=1.31 Easy, 0.89 Hard), indicating that simply scaling the base model without appropriate training data is insufficient.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the instruction-based image editing lineage (InstructPix2Pix → MagicBrush → OmniEdit/UltraEdit) by identifying a specific failure mode—referring expression disambiguation in multi-entity scenes—that prior methods largely ignore. Rather than relying on inversion-based synthetic data (as UltraEdit and OmniEdit do), it introduces a targeted pipeline combining GPT-4o prompt generation, Grounded SAM for mask extraction, and FlowChef for inversion-free editing to produce 20K high-quality triplets. The benchmark (RefEdit-Bench) repurposes RefCOCO, a dataset well-established in discriminative referring expression tasks, into the generative editing domain where it had not been systematically evaluated.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core practical takeaway is that 20K carefully constructed samples targeting a specific failure mode can outperform 4M+ generic editing triplets, suggesting the field should invest more in data quality and task-specificity rather than sheer scale. The observation that larger architectures (SDXL, SD3) underperform SDv1.5 on referring expressions—attributed to overfitting on data lacking multi-entity complexity—challenges the assumption that scaling the base model always helps. Open questions include whether the synthetic pipeline generalizes beyond the five editing types covered, whether the 200-image benchmark is sufficient for robust conclusions, and whether the VIEScore-based evaluation (single MLLM judge) captures all failure modes that human annotators would flag.
