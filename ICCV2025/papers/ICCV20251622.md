## Problem

Existing 3D pose estimators are trained predominantly on controlled indoor datasets with multi-camera or motion-capture annotations, creating a domain gap that limits generalization to in-the-wild scenes with diverse appearances, complex poses, and varied backgrounds. While data augmentation methods exist, GAN-based approaches modify keypoints without image-level context, and NeRF-based rendering (e.g., PoseGen) produces images lacking realistic backgrounds and appearance diversity. The core difficulty is that in-the-wild 2D pose datasets lack true 3D annotations, and the available pseudo-labeled 3D poses are inaccurate, making it hard to generate targeted training data for the specific challenging cases where a given estimator underperforms.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes an in-the-wild 2D pose dataset (images with GT 2D joint annotations, e.g., MPII single-person subset) and a pre-trained target pose estimator (TPE) whose weights are used both for identifying challenging samples and for filtering generated data. A VLM (LLaVA-LLaMA-3-8B) generates text descriptions of challenging images, and non-challenging images from the same dataset serve as appearance reference images for the Champ human animation model. The output is a set of filtered 3D pose-image pairs (27,000 samples synthesized from 500 challenging + 200 non-challenging MPII images) consisting of motion sequences of L 3D joint poses paired with corresponding rendered video frames, which are combined with the original pre-training data to fine-tune the TPE.

## Method

PoseSyn comprises two modules. The Error Extraction Module (EEM) identifies challenging samples by computing a weighted 2D joint error between GT 2D poses and the 2D projection of the TPE's pseudo-labeled 3D pose, selecting top-K hardest and bottom-K easiest samples. The Motion Synthesis Module (MSM) is the key contribution: it modifies T2M-GPT by introducing an initial motion representation derived from the mis-predicted 3D pose (replicated over T timesteps, encoded via Motion VQ-VAE into discrete motion indices) that is fed alongside VLM-generated text embeddings into a transformer for autoregressive motion index generation. This dual guidance (text + initial pose) addresses the ambiguity of text-only generation and the inaccuracy of pose-only generation, producing diverse motion sequences that approximate the unknown GT 3D pose. The synthesized motions then drive the Champ human animation model, which edits non-challenging reference images to produce realistic video frames with varied backgrounds and appearances. A filtering step discards frames where the TPE's 3D prediction deviates from the generated pose by more than a threshold τ, removing animation artifacts before fine-tuning.

## Results

Across three target pose estimators (3DCrowdNet, Hybrik, 4DHumans), PoseSyn achieves 6–14% MPJPE reduction and 5–9% PA-MPJPE reduction on 3D benchmarks (3DPW, EMDB, CMU, HuMMan) compared to real-data-only training. The largest gains appear with Hybrik, where mean MPJPE drops from 121.2 mm (real-only) to 104.6 mm (PoseSyn), a 13.7% improvement. On 2D benchmarks (LSPET, JHMDB), PoseSyn yields a 1–2% PCKh gain. The method synthesizes 27,000 3D pose samples from 500 challenging and 200 non-challenging MPII images, requiring no additional 3D annotations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PoseSyn is compared against two baselines: PoseGen [14], which identifies challenging poses from NeRF-rendered synthetic images and generates poses via GAN, and Ours-N, which shares PoseSyn's EEM and MSM but replaces the Champ human animation model with NeRF rendering. PoseSyn outperforms both baselines across all three TPEs and all evaluation datasets. Against PoseGen, the advantage is most pronounced with Hybrik (mean MPJPE 104.6 vs. 117.9, an 11.3% gap), because PoseGen's challenging-pose identification operates on unrealistic NeRF-rendered images rather than in-the-wild data. Against Ours-N, PoseSyn consistently wins by 1–7.5% in mean MPJPE, confirming that realistic human animation (diverse appearances and backgrounds via Champ) is critical for effective training data. The 2D PCKh results show PoseSyn also slightly edges out Ours-N (e.g., 76.0 vs. 75.8 for 3DCrowdNet), indicating that appearance diversity in synthetic images benefits even 2D generalization.

## Relation to Prior Work

PoseSyn extends the data augmentation line for 3D pose estimation (PoseAug, AdaptPose, PoseGen) by replacing GAN-based pose generation and NeRF rendering with a pipeline that identifies challenging poses from real in-the-wild 2D data and synthesizes motion sequences via a modified T2M-GPT (their SMG module) conditioned on both VLM-generated text and an initial motion representation derived from the TPE's mis-prediction. It also draws on the broader generative-model-for-data-synthesis trend (DiverGen, FreeMask) but adapts it to the continuous, sample-level nature of pose estimation where corner cases cannot be grouped by discrete class. The use of Champ (a motion-guided human animation model) for image generation departs from PoseGen's NeRF-based rendering, which the authors show limits background and appearance diversity.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PoseSyn offers a practical, scalable recipe for improving 3D pose estimator generalization without any additional 3D annotation, which is a significant cost barrier in the field. The consistent 6–14% MPJPE gains across three architecturally distinct TPEs (3DCrowdNet, Hybrik, 4DHumans) and six evaluation datasets suggest the approach is broadly applicable rather than tied to a specific model. However, the framework is limited to single-person scenarios due to Champ's inability to handle multi-person interactions, and extending it would require interaction-aware motion generation integrated into MSM. An open question is whether the EEM's reliance on the TPE's own error signal creates a feedback loop that could overfit to the TPE's specific failure modes rather than capturing truly novel pose configurations.
