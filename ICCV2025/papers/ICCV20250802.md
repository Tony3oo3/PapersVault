## Problem

Existing MLLM benchmarks fail to precisely evaluate long-chain reasoning due to three gaps: limited difficulty and diversity of questions, susceptibility to guessability and memorization (especially in multiple-choice formats where models can arrive at correct answers without valid reasoning), and the absence of intermediate step evaluation. This paper identifies that when answer choices are removed, models like Qwen2-VL-7B drop from 58.2% to 37.8% on MathVista, revealing that much of the measured 'reasoning' is actually shortcut-based.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250802/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each question is an open-ended text prompt paired with an RGB image (no multiple-choice options), spanning 6 disciplines (Math, Business, Science, Engineering, Social Science, Health) at pre-university through university levels and foundational through competition difficulty. The evaluation produces two scores: a binary final-answer accuracy (GPT-4o extracts and checks the answer) and a continuous intermediate-step score computed as the mean of per-step ternary scores (correct=1, unverifiable=0.5, incorrect=0) across N extracted reasoning steps. The full benchmark contains 3,275 questions split into a 1,000-question test-mini and a 2,275-question test set.

## Method

MMReason is constructed in three stages. First, questions are sourced from existing benchmarks (MMMU, MMMUPro, MMStar, M³CoT, MathVerse, MathVision) and newly collected from the web, then manually reformulated from multiple-choice into open-ended format with unique answers. Second, a multi-model voting filter uses K=4 MLLMs (GPT-4o, LLaMA-3.2-Vision-11B, InternVL-2.5-8B-MPO, Qwen2.5-VL-7B) over T=2 rounds, feeding only the text (without the image) to each model; any question answered correctly without visual input is removed as potentially memorized or visually irrelevant, raising the multimodal relevance rate from ~57–61% to 94–97%. Third, a reference-based ternary scoring mechanism uses GPT-4o to segment a model's response into N key steps and score each against manually annotated multi-step reference solutions, assigning correct (1), unverifiable (0.5), or incorrect (0), with the final intermediate score being the arithmetic mean across all steps.

## Results

On MMReason testmini (1,000 open-ended multi-step reasoning questions), the best-performing model Gemini-2.5-pro achieves only 45.1% final-answer reasoning accuracy and 79.2% intermediate-step reasoning score, demonstrating the benchmark's difficulty. Among open-source models, Qwen-2.5-VL-72B leads with 23.4% final accuracy and 47.7% intermediate score, while 7B-scale reasoning models (R1-VL, Mulberry) achieve 17.9% and 11.8% respectively. Closed-source models consistently outperform open-source models on intermediate-step scores (e.g., GPT-4o: 53.9% vs. Qwen-2.5-VL-7B: 39.6%), indicating a larger gap in multi-step reasoning quality than in final-answer accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

As a benchmark paper, MMReason evaluates 14 MLLMs rather than proposing a competing method. Gemini-2.5-pro dominates all other models by a wide margin (45.1% final accuracy vs. 33.2% for Claude-sonnet-4 and 27.5% for GPT-4.1), and also leads in intermediate-step scoring (79.2% vs. 61.7% and 60.9%). Among open-source models, Qwen-2.5-VL-72B (23.4%) and InternVL-2.5-MPO-78B (23.1%) are the strongest, approaching GPT-4o's 21.1% on final accuracy but trailing significantly on intermediate scores (47.7%/50.2% vs. 53.9%). Notably, 7B reasoning models R1-VL (17.9%) and Mulberry (11.8%) show that o1-style deliberation helps final accuracy but does not close the intermediate-step gap with larger models. Engineering questions are the hardest across all models (best: 33.3% by Gemini-2.5-pro; worst: 3.5% by LLaMA-3.2-Vision), while Health questions are the easiest (best: 63.8%). The newly collected data subset is the most challenging source, with GPT-4o scoring only 21.8% on it versus 28.9% on reformulated M3CoT questions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in MLLM evaluation by identifying and addressing three specific methodological gaps in existing benchmarks (MathVista, MMMU, MMStar, OlympiadBench, MME-CoT): guessability from multiple-choice formats, memorization of leaked data, and the absence of intermediate-step scoring. Rather than building on a single prior method, it repurposes questions from several existing benchmarks (MMMU, MMStar, M3CoT, MathVerse, MathVision) by reformulating them into open-ended format, and introduces a multi-model voting filtering mechanism and a reference-based ternary scoring scheme as novel evaluation tools. The broader motivation draws on the rapid progress of reasoning MLLMs (o1, DeepSeek-R1, Mulberry, R1-VL), which the authors argue outpaces the precision of current evaluation protocols.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that removing answer choices causes dramatic accuracy drops (e.g., Qwen2-VL-7B falling from 58.2% to 37.8% on MathVista) challenges the assumption that high MCQ scores reflect genuine reasoning, suggesting the field may be overestimating MLLM reasoning capability. The persistent gap between intermediate-step scores (79.2%) and final-answer accuracy (45.1%) for the best model (Gemini-2.5-pro) highlights that even strong models struggle to convert partial reasoning into correct conclusions. Open questions include whether the GPT-4o-based ternary scoring is sufficiently reliable across diverse solution paths, whether the multi-model voting filtering generalizes as new models are trained on the benchmark, and whether the 6-discipline scope captures the full breadth of multimodal reasoning.
