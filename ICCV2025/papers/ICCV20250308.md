## Problem

Existing text-to-3D benchmarks lack fine-grained prompt categorization and multi-dimensional evaluation, while current automated metrics (e.g., CLIPScore, BLIPScore) only capture a single aspect such as text-3D alignment, ignoring geometry fidelity, texture quality, and holistic perception. This makes it difficult to perform detailed, subjective-aligned comparisons across different generation methods and prompt types.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250308/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluator takes a textured mesh rendered into 6 multi-view images at 512×512 resolution along with its corresponding text prompt, and produces four scalar quality scores (alignment, geometry, texture, overall) on a 0–10 scale. Rather than training separate models per dimension, a single unified network with a hypernetwork generates dimension-specific mapping functions, so the same architecture yields differentiated predictions for each evaluation perspective. The benchmark itself comprises 1,280 textured meshes from 8 prompt categories × 8 generative methods, each annotated by 21 human subjects across the four dimensions (107,520 total annotations).

## Method

HyperScore extracts visual patch tokens and text tokens via a pre-trained CLIP model, then derives dimension-specific condition features by feeding learnable CoOp-style prompts (prefixed with meta tokens like 'geometry quality') through the frozen text encoder. Two key modules enable multi-dimensional assessment: (1) Conditional Feature Fusion computes patch-level attention weights by chaining correlation matrices between visual patches, text tokens, and condition features, mimicking how human evaluators shift focus across image regions depending on the dimension; (2) Adaptive Quality Mapping uses a hypernetwork to generate the weights and biases of a shared fully-connected mapping head from the condition features, simulating different decision-making processes per dimension. The quality feature is the element-wise product of the fused visual feature and the text EOT token, passed through an MLP before the hypernetwork-parameterized head regresses the final score. Trained with 5-fold cross-validation (128:32 prompt split) on MATE-3D, HyperScore outperforms all zero-shot and fine-tuned baselines across all four dimensions.

## Results

On MATE-3D, HyperScore achieves the best SRCC across all four evaluation dimensions: 0.739 (Alignment), 0.782 (Geometry), 0.811 (Texture), and 0.792 (Overall), with corresponding PLCC values of 0.754, 0.793, 0.822, and 0.804. The closest fine-tuned baseline, DINO v2+FT, reaches SRCC of 0.642/0.739/0.771/0.728, yielding HyperScore gains of 9.7–14.0 percentage points. Among zero-shot metrics, ImageReward is the strongest (SRCC 0.651/0.591/0.612/0.623), still trailing HyperScore by 8.8–22.1 points. The ablation confirms that both Conditional Feature Fusion (CFF) and Adaptive Quality Mapping (AQM) contribute, with the full model outperforming separately trained per-dimension networks (SRCC 0.739 vs. 0.737 on Alignment).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HyperScore outperforms all 12 baselines on every dimension and every correlation metric (PLCC, SRCC, KRCC). Among zero-shot metrics, ImageReward is the strongest (SRCC 0.651 on Alignment) but still lags HyperScore by 8.8 points; CLIPScore and BLIPScore cluster around 0.49–0.54 SRCC, while Aesthetic Score and CLIP-IQA perform near-chance (SRCC < 0.20), likely because they target natural-image aesthetics rather than rendered 3D meshes. Among fine-tuned backbones, DINO v2+FT is the best single backbone (SRCC 0.739 on Geometry), yet HyperScore still leads by 4.3 points on Geometry and 14.0 points on Alignment, indicating that the hypernetwork-based adaptive mapping and conditional feature fusion add substantial value beyond a strong visual encoder. The paper's own MultiScore baseline (same CLIP features, multi-task heads without CFF/AQM) achieves SRCC 0.638–0.729, confirming that the two proposed modules (CFF and AQM) are each necessary and complementary. Notably, HyperScore even surpasses separately trained per-dimension networks (e.g., 0.739 vs. 0.737 on Alignment SRCC), validating the one-for-all hypernetwork design over training independent experts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-dimensional preference learning paradigm from MPS (Zhang et al., CVPR 2024) in text-to-image generation to the text-to-3D setting, while introducing two key architectural innovations: a conditional feature fusion module that reweights visual patches based on evaluation-dimension-specific condition features, and a hypernetwork (Ha et al., ICLR 2022) that generates dimension-specific mapping head weights from a shared backbone. The learnable prompt mechanism follows CoOp (Zhou et al., IJCV 2022), but is applied to generate condition features for 3D quality dimensions rather than general VLM tasks. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior text-to-3D evaluation work (T³ Bench, pairwise preference benchmarks) by shifting from single-dimensional or pairwise scoring to absolute multi-dimensional scoring with fine-grained prompt categorization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MATE-3D provides the first comprehensive benchmark for text-to-3D evaluation with 8 prompt categories, 4 evaluation dimensions, and 107,520 human annotations, filling a critical gap that limits fair comparison across the rapidly growing text-to-3D literature. The finding that geometry quality correlates most strongly with overall perceived quality—while alignment, the focus of most prior automated metrics (CLIPScore, BLIPScore), correlates least—challenges the field's implicit assumption that text-3D correspondence is the dominant quality factor. Open questions include whether HyperScore generalizes to new generation paradigms (e.g., feed-forward 3D diffusion, 3DGS-native methods) and whether the 8-category prompt taxonomy adequately captures the full diversity of real-world 3D generation scenarios. The benchmark is limited to textured mesh output, excluding NeRF and 3DGS representations that many current methods produce natively.
