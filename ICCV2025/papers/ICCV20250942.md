## Problem

Post-hoc OOD detection methods that rely on final-layer scores or feature-space distances can be misled by OOD inputs that accidentally align with high-level feature directions, producing deceptively high confidence. No existing approach specifically identifies and suppresses the individual feature coordinates whose gradient sensitivity disproportionately inflates OOD logits, while also avoiding the computational cost of a second forward pass after feature modification.

## Input / Output

The method operates at inference time on a single RGB image passed through a pre-trained classifier (DenseNet-101, ResNet-50, MobileNetV2, ViT-B/16, or Swin-B). At the penultimate layer (or final encoder output for Transformers), it extracts the d-dimensional feature vector F, computes the gradient of the predicted-class logit with respect to F via one backward pass, and outputs a binary ID/OOD decision based on an energy score computed from first-order-approximated logits. No retraining or architectural modification is required; the only added cost is a partial backward pass and a dot-product, roughly doubling inference FLOPs relative to a plain forward pass.

## Method

Gradient Short-Circuit (GSC) exploits the observation that OOD samples exhibit large, disorganized gradient directions in the neighborhood of their penultimate-layer features, whereas ID samples show consistent, stable gradients. Concretely, the method computes g = ∇_F [y]_c (the gradient of the predicted-class logit w.r.t. the feature vector), selects the top 5% coordinates by |g_i|, and zeroes them out (alternative rules—small perturbation or orthogonal projection—are also provided). To avoid an expensive second forward pass through f_{>L}, a local first-order Taylor approximation y' ≈ y + (∇_F y)^T ΔF estimates the post-modification logits using only the already-computed gradient and the feature perturbation ΔF. The OOD decision is then made by comparing an energy score E(y') = log Σ_j exp([y']_j) against a threshold τ. The entire pipeline is inference-stage, requires no retraining, and adds roughly 2× the FLOPs of a standard forward pass (vs. 4× without the approximation).

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (DenseNet-101), GSC achieves FPR95 of 7.91% and AUROC of 98.02%, outperforming the strongest baseline ConjNorm (13.92% / 97.15%) by 6.01 points in FPR95. On CIFAR-100, GSC reaches FPR95 of 23.15% and AUROC of 93.62%, again surpassing ConjNorm (28.27% / 92.50%). On ImageNet-1K with ResNet-50, GSC attains FPR95 of 11.11% and AUROC of 98.35% on iNaturalist, and 11.51% / 97.58% on Textures, consistently leading or matching the best baselines across all four OOD test sets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GSC decisively outperforms all ten baselines on both CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, reducing FPR95 by 6.01 points over the strongest competitor ConjNorm (7.91% vs. 13.92% on CIFAR-10; 23.15% vs. 28.27% on CIFAR-100) and by over 40 points over MSP. On ImageNet-1K (ResNet-50), GSC leads on SUN (FPR95 33.29% vs. ConjNorm 37.75%), Places365 (43.74% vs. 62.07%), and Textures (11.51% vs. 10.30% for ConjNorm, a near-tie), while ConjNorm retains a slight edge on iNaturalist (9.62% vs. 11.11% FPR95). GSC substantially outperforms gradient-agnostic methods: Maha (FPR95 62.11% on iNaturalist) and GEM (51.52%) are far behind, confirming that class-conditional Gaussian modeling is inadequate for complex ImageNet distributions. Among feature-manipulation baselines, GSC beats ReAct (42.40% → 11.11% on iNaturalist), ASH (39.10% → 11.11%), and KNN (46.78% → 11.11%) by large margins. The GSC+ASH variant is slightly worse than GSC alone (e.g., 10.62% vs. 7.91% FPR95 on CIFAR-10), suggesting that additional activation-scaling heuristics can partially override the gradient-based adjustments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of gradient-based OOD detection (GradNorm, Mu et al.) and feature-intervention methods (ReAct, ASH), but departs from both in a key way: prior gradient-based work uses gradient magnitudes as a *scoring signal* for OOD, while GSC uses gradients to *identify which specific feature coordinates to zero out*, turning gradient information into an active intervention mechanism. The local first-order approximation to skip a second forward pass is borrowed conceptually from Taylor-expansion-based pruning [30] but repurposed for OOD inference. The energy-score decision criterion follows the standard post-hoc OOD pipeline, so the novelty is confined to the feature-modification step and its efficient evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical appeal is that GSC is a drop-in inference-stage modification requiring no retraining, no architectural changes, and only a partial backward pass, making it attractive for deploying existing models in safety-critical settings. The fixed 5% mask ratio, while effective, is a notable limitation: an adaptive, per-sample determination of how many coordinates to short-circuit could improve performance on near-OOD or domain-similar cases (e.g., CIFAR-100 vs. CIFAR-10). An open question is whether the gradient-sensitivity principle generalizes beyond classification OOD detection to other tasks (e.g., open-set segmentation, anomaly detection in time series) where the 'predicted class logit' framing does not directly apply.
