## Problem

Differentially private fine-tuning of diffusion models suffers from a severe privacy-utility trade-off: DP-SGD's gradient clipping and noise injection scale with the number of trainable parameters, so full or near-full fine-tuning of large latent diffusion models produces poor image quality under strict privacy budgets. Prior work (e.g., DP-LDM) reduces trainable parameters by freezing most of the network, but still updates 60–85% of weights, leaving room for further improvement in the privacy-utility frontier.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained latent diffusion model (UNet + autoencoder) and a small private dataset of RGB images (32×32 to 256×256), along with either class labels (MNIST, CIFAR-10, CelebA-HQ gender) or text prompts (textual inversion on Stable-Diffusion-v1.5 at 512×512). It produces synthetic images in the same resolution as the target dataset; for conditional tasks, a downstream classifier trained on the generated images is evaluated on the real test set. All diffusion training occurs in the autoencoder's latent space (e.g., 16×16×3 for CelebA-64), not in pixel space.

## Method

DP-LoRA follows a two-stage pipeline: (1) pre-train an autoencoder and a full latent diffusion model on public data (ImageNet, EMNIST), then (2) fine-tune the LDM on private data by inserting LoRA adapters (rank r ≪ min(m,h)) into both the QKV attention matrices and the linear output projection layers of every attention block, while keeping all pretrained weights frozen. Training uses DP-SGD with a noise-multiplicity strategy (k noise samples drawn per data point per step, k=4–8), which effectively lowers the per-step noise scale. Because only ~0.4–0.5% of total parameters are trainable (e.g., 479K out of 120M on CelebA-64), the clipping bound C can be set much lower, injecting far less Gaussian noise; the noise corrupts only the small low-rank adapter λ(θ), so the combined weights W_PT + λ(θ) remain close to the pretrained manifold even at ε ≤ 10. Ablations confirm that jointly tuning QKV and projection layers (vs. either alone) and choosing r=16 yield the best FID, and that the approach generalizes across resolutions and conditioning types.

## Results

On unconditional CelebA-64 generation, DP-LoRA achieves FID 8.4 at ε=10, substantially outperforming DP-LDM (14.3), DP-SAD (17.9), and dp-promise (25.3). On high-resolution CelebA-HQ (256×256), DP-LoRA reaches FID 19.9 (unconditional) and 17.2 (gender-conditional) at ε=10, improving over DP-LDM by 18% and 10% respectively. For class-conditional downstream classification, DP-LoRA attains 97.9% accuracy on MNIST and 73.98% on CIFAR-10 at ε=10, while using only 0.37–0.41% of total parameters (vs. 60–85% for DP-LDM).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DP-LoRA decisively outperforms DP-LDM [37] on all CelebA resolutions: FID 8.4 vs. 14.3 on CelebA-64 (ε=10) and 19.9 vs. 24.3 on CelebA-HQ, while using only 0.40% of trainable parameters versus 60.5% for DP-LDM. Against DP-MEPF [22] and PrivImage [33], DP-LoRA achieves dramatically lower FIDs on CelebA-HQ (19.9 vs. 98.4 and 98.3 at ε=10), where pixel-space and aggressive-compression baselines collapse. On CelebA-32, dp-promise [56] retains a narrow edge (FID 6.0 vs. 7.7 at ε=10), and on low-resolution classification tasks DP-Diffusion [20] with WRN slightly exceeds DP-LoRA on CIFAR-10 (75.6% vs. 73.98% at ε=10), though the two use different classifier architectures. Compared to Private Evolution (PE) [34] and Sim-PE [35], DP-LoRA wins on nearly all settings (e.g., CelebA-64 FID 8.4 vs. 49.0 for PE at ε=10), with the gap attributed to PE's sensitivity to domain shift between pretraining and private data. Overall, DP-LoRA establishes new state-of-the-art FIDs on CelebA-64 and CelebA-HQ while training fewer than 1% of parameters, confirming that parameter-efficient LoRA adapters are the key lever for the privacy-utility trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two well-developed lines of work: parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) via LoRA, originally designed for large language models, and differentially private training of diffusion models. It directly extends DP-LDM (Liu et al., TMLR 2024), which fine-tunes attention blocks of a latent diffusion model under DP-SGD, by replacing full attention fine-tuning with low-rank LoRA adapters placed in both QKV matrices and output projection layers. The noise multiplicity strategy is inherited from DPDM (Dockhorn et al., TMLR 2022). The key conceptual departure is that DP-LDM still trains 60–85% of model parameters, whereas DP-LoRA reduces this to roughly 0.4–0.5%, arguing that the smaller parameter count both reduces clipping-induced information loss and permits a lower noise scale in DP-SGD.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a practitioner can take any public pre-trained latent diffusion model, insert a few hundred thousand LoRA parameters, and obtain a differentially private generator with substantially better FID than prior methods at the same privacy budget — a recipe that is easy to reproduce and scales to new domains with minimal overhead. Limitations include evaluation on relatively small datasets (CelebA, CIFAR-10, MNIST), the observation that textual inversion under DP does not improve over standard training, and the fact that absolute FIDs at high resolution (CelebA-HQ) remain in the 17–23 range, far from non-private quality. Open questions include whether the approach transfers to video diffusion, larger foundation models (e.g., Stable Diffusion XL), and whether the optimal LoRA rank and module placement generalize across architectures and privacy budgets.
