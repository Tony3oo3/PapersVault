## Problem

Robotic manipulation research has largely focused on acquiring individual skills or transferring knowledge from pretrained models, with little exploration of how to incrementally learn new manipulation skills without retraining. The paper shows that traditional incremental learning methods (rehearsal and distillation) designed for visual classification suffer severe catastrophic forgetting in this setting because they overlook two key characteristics of manipulation: temporal complexity (each action impacts subsequent ones) and action complexity (new skills require learning novel 3D action primitives involving rotation and shift).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250858/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent receives a 128×128 front RGB-D image (projected into voxels and encoded by a 3D UNet), a 4-dimensional proprioception vector (gripper openness, end-effector position, timestep; encoded by a single-layer MLP), and a language instruction (encoded by CLIP RN50), all producing 512-dimensional tokens. The policy outputs a four-part action: target translation as a 100³ voxel distribution, rotation as a (360/5)×3 distribution, gripper openness in [0,1], and a collision-avoidance signal in [0,1]. During evaluation, the agent executes actions sequentially until an oracle signals completion or a 25-step limit is reached.

## Method

iManip combines two mechanisms to address the identified gaps. First, a temporal replay strategy identifies keyframes (moments when the gripper changes state or end-effector velocity approaches zero) and applies farthest-distance entropy sampling to store a fixed number of temporally balanced, information-rich samples per keyframe, preserving trajectory integrity that herding or hard-exemplar sampling would destroy. Second, an Extendable PerceiverIO augments the standard PerceiverIO with skill-specific action prompts and extendable Q/K weight matrices: when learning a new skill, the old PerceiverIO is frozen while new action prompts and small appended weight matrices (W_Q^new, W_K^new) are trained, allowing adaptation to new action primitives without disturbing learned representations. A knowledge distillation loss (MSE on translation/rotation probabilities, L1 on openness/collision) further anchors the new model to the old one. In practice, only the policy decoder plus the new prompts and weights are updated, requiring ~8M trainable parameters and 60k iterations for convergence.

## Results

On the B5-5N1 skill-incremental setting in RLBench (5 base skills + 5 incremental steps), iManip achieves an average success rate of 45.5% across all learned skills and 46.8% on old skills, outperforming the strongest traditional incremental baseline M-TIB (36.1% / 34.8%) by 9.4 points on all-skills accuracy. The multi-task retraining baselines (PerAct: 5.9%, ManiGaussian: 12.5%) suffer severe catastrophic forgetting, dropping to near-zero on old skills by Step 5. Across three additional incremental configurations (B5-1N5, B2-4N2, B3-2N3), iManip consistently leads by 6–31 points over M-TIB and ManiGaussian.

## Baselines & Comparisons

iManip outperforms all four baselines across every incremental step. Against the strongest incremental baseline M-TIB (iCaRL herding + EEIL distillation on ManiGaussian), iManip gains 9.4 points on average all-skills success (45.5% vs. 36.1%) and 12.0 points on old-skills retention (46.8% vs. 34.8%), with the gap widening at later steps (e.g., Step 5: 36.0% vs. 26.8% on old skills). Against P-TIB (the same incremental recipe on the weaker PerAct backbone), the advantage is even larger at 23.0 points. The multi-task retraining baselines (PerAct and ManiGaussian) collapse to 1–7% on old skills by Step 5, confirming that without any incremental mechanism, catastrophic forgetting is severe. In the real-world Franka Panda experiments (B1-4N1), iManip retains 40–60% success on old skills after four incremental steps, whereas the ManiGaussian baseline drops to 0% on all previously learned skills.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the PerceiverIO architecture (Jaegle et al., 2021) and the PerAct manipulation pipeline (Shridhar et al., 2023) into an incremental learning setting, introducing an Extendable PerceiverIO with skill-specific action prompts and extendable Q/K weight matrices. It repurposes iCaRL's herding replay (Rebuffi et al., 2017) and EEIL's knowledge distillation (Castro et al., 2018) but fundamentally rethinks them for robotic manipulation by addressing temporal trajectory integrity and 3D action primitive complexity—dimensions absent from visual classification. The paper positions itself as a deliberate departure from LIBERO's lifelong robot learning benchmark, which varies objects and positions within the same skill, by instead requiring the agent to acquire entirely new manipulation skills sequentially.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of skill-level incremental learning in robotic manipulation, providing both a benchmark (10 skills, 166 variations on RLBench) and a practical framework that outperforms naive retraining and classification-style incremental baselines. The finding that temporal replay strategy contributes far more to performance than architectural extensibility (~28% vs. ~5% in ablations) suggests that data handling, not model capacity, is the primary bottleneck for incremental manipulation—a useful insight for the field. Open questions include scalability beyond 10 skills, generalization across robot embodiments, and how this approach composes with emerging vision-language-action models that may handle skill acquisition differently.
