## Problem

Bimanual manipulation requires precise spatiotemporal coordination between two arms, yet conventional approaches rely solely on shared observations for coordination, lacking foresight into future interactions. While video prediction has been explored for unimanual robot learning, its potential as an implicit coordination mechanism for bimanual tasks remains largely unexplored, and existing video-prediction-based methods suffer from high computational cost at inference.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251898/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a single-view RGB image (480×640 in the real-world setup) and the robot's proprioceptive state (joint positions) as input, encoded via a ResNet-18 backbone and a linear projection respectively, then fused through a transformer encoder. During training it jointly outputs a bimanual action chunk of N timesteps (joint positions and gripper states for both arms) and future visual tokens compressed by a pretrained Cosmos video tokenizer; at inference, only the action chunk is produced and executed open-loop before the next chunk is generated.

## Method

The core contribution is a unified transformer-based diffusion model that jointly denoises future action tokens and future video latent tokens under a single conditional generative framework. A key architectural choice is a unidirectional attention mechanism within the denoising decoder: future frame tokens attend to both themselves and historical action tokens, while action tokens attend exclusively to themselves, so the causal action→video dependency is learned during training but video prediction can be entirely skipped at inference, halving inference time. Future frames are represented as compact latent tokens from a pretrained Cosmos video tokenizer with patchification and spatio-temporal positional embeddings, reducing redundancy compared to raw pixel prediction. Training uses an ℓ1 loss on action noise prediction and a weighted MSE loss on visual token noise prediction (optimal weight ω=0.2), with multi-frame latent prediction (40 frames) outperforming single-frame or pixel-level alternatives.

## Results

On the ALOHA benchmark, the proposed method achieves 71.9% average success rate, surpassing ACT (47%) by 24.9% and Diffusion Policy (33%) by 38.9%. On the RoboTwin benchmark (16 tasks), it reaches 56.3% average success rate, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art RDT-1B (51.1%) by 5.2% and ACT (44.9%) by 11.4%. In real-world experiments on a Mobile ALOHA robot, the method achieves 60% average success rate across 4 bimanual tasks, a 32.5% improvement over ACT (27.5%), while also maintaining the highest control frequency at 35.8 Hz.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baselines across every benchmark. Against ACT [59], the strongest CVAE-based baseline, it gains +24.9% on ALOHA (71.9% vs 47%), +11.4% on RoboTwin (56.3% vs 44.9%), and +32.5% in real-world settings (60% vs 27.5%). Against Diffusion Policy [6], the improvement is even more pronounced: +38.9% on ALOHA and +29% on RoboTwin. Against the previous state-of-the-art RDT-1B [33] on RoboTwin, the method leads by +5.2% on average, with particularly large margins on coordination-heavy tasks like Put Apple Cabinet (93.7% vs 31.3%) and Pick Apple Messy (70.3% vs 44.7%). Against 3D Diffusion Policy [56], the gain is +14.0% on RoboTwin. GR-MG [28], a video-prediction-based method, performs poorly on RoboTwin (4.3% average), and the proposed method also matches or exceeds its inference speed (0.03 s vs 0.19 s per step) thanks to the unidirectional attention design that skips video prediction at test time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads in robot learning: diffusion-based visuomotor policy learning (Diffusion Policy, RDT-1B) and video prediction as an auxiliary task (GR-1, GR-MG, UniPi). It departs from prior bimanual coordination approaches that rely on explicit inductive biases (symmetry assumptions, leader-follower roles, LLM schedulers) by instead using shared future-state imagination as an implicit coordination signal between arms. Architecturally, the key departure is the unidirectional attention mechanism, which lets video tokens attend to action tokens during training but severs the reverse dependency, enabling video prediction to be entirely skipped at inference—a design not present in prior video-prediction-for-robotics work that typically requires the predicted video at test time.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that video prediction can serve as a coordination regularizer for bimanual manipulation without incurring inference cost, offering a practical recipe for improving dexterous two-arm control. The consistent gains across simulated (ALOHA, RoboTwin) and real-world (Mobile ALOHA) settings, particularly in sequential-coordination tasks, validate the 'imaginative coordination' hypothesis. However, real-world success rates remain moderate (60% average), precise-rotation tasks (Coffee Stir) remain unsolved, and the single-view, fixed-camera setup limits generalizability. Open questions include whether arm-specific representations could avoid the small performance drop in loosely-coupled sync-bimanual tasks, and whether multimodal sensory feedback (force, tactile) could further enhance coordination in unstructured environments.
