## Problem

Plane-based dynamic Gaussian splatting methods (e.g., 4DGaussian) decompose 4D spatiotemporal features into 2D planes under a low-rank assumption that causes feature overlap and degraded deformation accuracy. While a full 4D hash encoding eliminates this low-rank constraint, applying it to the entire scene incurs prohibitive hash collisions and storage overhead from the extra time dimension. The paper addresses how to exploit 4D hash encoding's explicit, overlap-free representation while keeping the hash table tractable.

## Input / Output

The method consumes synchronized multi-view video (15–20 cameras × 300 frames at 1352×1014 on Neural 3D Video; a 4×4 array at 2048×1088 on Technicolor Light Field) together with COLMAP point clouds from the first frame for Gaussian initialization. It outputs a decoupled scene representation—static 3D Gaussians rendered directly plus dynamic Gaussians deformed by a 4D hash field—and synthesizes novel views at up to 264 FPS on a single RTX 4090, with total storage of ~146 MB for a full scene.

## Method

DASH first performs a self-supervised dynamic-static split: each Gaussian's trajectory is modeled as linear motion p(t)=p+t·Δp, and a threshold at the top-k percentile of ‖Δp‖ separates dynamic from static Gaussians, with a static constraint loss L_s=mean(‖Δp‖) over static points enforcing zero motion. Dynamic Gaussians are then encoded by a multiresolution 4D hash encoder (extending Instant-NGP to four dimensions with geometric-progression resolutions and quadrilinear interpolation), whose features feed a three-branch MLP decoder predicting position, rotation, and scale offsets. A spatio-temporal smoothness regularizer L_r=‖G₄D(x)−G₄D(x+ε)‖² is applied in feature space to suppress the erratic deformation artifacts inherent to explicit hash representations. Because the 4D hash table stores only the dynamic subset, collision rates and memory drop substantially compared to full-scene 4D encoding, while the O(1) hash lookup preserves real-time query speed.

## Results

On the Neural 3D Video dataset, DASH achieves a mean PSNR of 32.22 dB and SSIM of 0.969, outperforming the strongest Gaussian-based baselines E-D3DGS (32.04 dB) and 4DGaussian (31.42 dB). On the Technicolor Light Field dataset, DASH reaches 33.94 dB PSNR, 0.960 SSIM, and 0.097 LPIPS, surpassing E-D3DGS (33.16 dB) and Grid4D (32.59 dB). In terms of speed, DASH renders at 264.3 FPS with only 106.6k Gaussians, exceeding Grid4D (256.8 FPS, 158.9k Gaussians) and 4DGaussian (136.9 FPS, 123.7k Gaussians) while using fewer primitives.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DASH outperforms all eight baselines on both datasets. Against the closest Gaussian-based competitor E-D3DGS [2], DASH gains +0.18 dB PSNR on Neural 3D Video and +0.78 dB on Technicolor Light Field, while also achieving lower LPIPS (0.097 vs 0.105). Compared to 4DGaussian [41], which relies on a plane-based low-rank decomposition, DASH improves PSNR by +0.80 dB (32.22 vs 31.42) and doubles the rendering speed (264.3 vs 136.9 FPS) with fewer Gaussians (106.6k vs 123.7k). Against Grid4D [13], the most direct competitor using decomposed 3D hash grids, DASH achieves +0.84 dB PSNR on Neural 3D Video and +1.35 dB on Technicolor Light Field, while also reducing storage from 319 MB to 146 MB. The NeRF-based methods (NeRFPlayer [34], HyperReel [1], K-Planes [9], MixVoels [39], DyNeRF [15]) are consistently outperformed by 1–3 dB in PSNR and are orders of magnitude slower in rendering (0.045–15 FPS vs 264.3 FPS). Notably, integrating DASH's self-supervised decomposition into Grid4D (Grid4D+dec) yields a +0.36 dB gain over vanilla Grid4D, confirming the decomposition's generality beyond the 4D hash encoder.

## Relation to Prior Work

DASH builds directly on 3D Gaussian Splatting as its rendering backbone and extends the multiresolution hash encoding of Instant-NGP from 3D to 4D space. It departs from the dominant plane-based decomposition (4DGaussian, K-Planes, HexPlane) that factorizes 4D features into six 2D planes under a low-rank assumption, and from the grid-based approach of Grid4D that splits 4D into four 3D hash grids. The key conceptual shift is using a single unified 4D hash table for dynamic regions, combined with a self-supervised static/dynamic split that avoids the hash-collision explosion of encoding the full scene in 4D.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The self-supervised decomposition mechanism is practically valuable because it eliminates the need for manual masks or precomputed segmentation, making the pipeline more accessible for real-world dynamic scenes. The 264 FPS rendering at 1352×1014 with SOTA PSNR positions DASH as a strong candidate for interactive applications (VR/AR, film). However, evaluation is limited to two datasets with relatively short sequences (300 frames), the linear-motion assumption underlying the decomposition may struggle with highly non-rigid or occluded dynamics, and the method still depends on COLMAP initialization quality. Open questions include robustness to longer temporal extents, scenes with significant topology changes, and whether the fixed k-percentile threshold generalizes across scene types.
