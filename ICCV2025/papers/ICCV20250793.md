## Problem

Conventional autofocus methods rely on peak-searching over dense focal stacks, making them inefficient in small depth-of-field (DoF) scenarios where fine-grained adjustments are critical. Existing deep learning-based AF methods, typically built on depth estimation, suffer from defocus uncertainty—the same blur level appears on both sides of the focal plane—limiting their working range and reliability under severe defocus. This paper addresses both limitations for industrial AOI systems operating across a 35× DoF range.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250793/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single defocused RGB image (resized to 512×512) as input and outputs a scalar sharpness value in [0, 1], defined by the Circle of Confusion (CoC) model rather than conventional high-frequency content. This sharpness is then inverted through a closed-form optical equation (Eq. 9) to yield a focus adjustment distance in millimeters, which drives the physical lens actuator. The system operates over a ±5 mm measurement range with a DoF of ±0.14 mm, and the sharpness-to-distance mapping is parameterized by lens-specific constants (focal length, aperture, working distance).

## Method

The core insight is to use an optical-model-based sharpness indicator as an intermediate representation instead of directly regressing focus distance. Sharpness is defined as a normalized function of the CoC diameter (Eq. 5), where 1 corresponds to the focal plane and 0 to the maximum defocus boundary, making it inherently texture-invariant. A U2-Net encoder-decoder is trained with a combined L1 + PatchGAN loss to predict this sharpness from defocused images, outperforming U-Net, Res-UNet, Dense-UNet, and ViT baselines. An adaptive adjustment algorithm (Algorithm 1) inverts the sharpness function to compute the required focus step, and a 'Take One More Step' heuristic resolves defocus uncertainty by comparing consecutive sharpness readings to determine the correct direction of travel, eliminating the need for the network to predict direction. This decoupling of sharpness estimation from direction determination allows the model to focus exclusively on a well-defined regression task while the adjustment logic handles the ambiguity inherent in the optical model.

## Results

The proposed method achieves over 95% in-focus accuracy within 5 adjustment steps across a 35× DoF range (±5 mm measurement range with ±0.14 mm DoF), starting from severe defocus positions (28×–35× DoF). Compared to conventional peak-searching methods requiring 9–12 sampling steps, the proposed method needs only 3 steps (correct initial direction) or 5 steps (wrong initial direction). Against DL-based baselines, the method achieves 99.8% in-focus rate on anodized samples and 91.2–96.5% on metal, paper, and PCB textures within 5 iterations, consistently outperforming all compared depth-estimation and AF models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all 10 baselines across every texture category. Against conventional focus-measure methods (Table 1), the best baseline (Modified DCT, 0.93 in-focus rate) requires 10 sampling steps, while the proposed method reaches 0.912 in just 3 steps (correct direction) and 0.962 in 5 steps (wrong direction), reducing sampling by 50–75%. Among DL-based methods (Table 2), the closest competitor is Nazir et al. [22] (0.846–0.859), which the proposed method surpasses by 10–15 percentage points on anodized and PCB samples. Herrmann et al. [10] performs best on metal (0.830) but still trails the proposed method (0.882 correct / 0.867 wrong). Liao et al. [17] and Lu et al. [19] show particularly poor performance on anodized (0.190, 0.580) and paper (0.628, 0.230) textures, highlighting their vulnerability to defocus uncertainty. The vision transformer baseline (Ranftl et al. [27]) is inconsistent, ranging from 0.305 (metal) to 0.824 (PCB), while the proposed method maintains 0.867–0.994 across all textures even with an incorrect initial direction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant DL-based AF paradigm of end-to-end distance/depth estimation from defocused images by introducing a physics-grounded intermediate representation: a Circle-of-Confusion-based sharpness value predicted by a U2-Net, with distance recovered analytically via the optical model's inverse function. Conceptually, it repurposes the CoC model from imaging optics as a supervised regression target rather than a physical simulation tool, and borrows the PatchGAN loss from image-to-image translation to stabilize sharpness regression. The 'take one more step' direction-determination algorithm is a novel lightweight mechanism that offloads the defocus-ambiguity problem from the network to a simple sequential check, a design choice absent from both conventional peak-searching and prior DL-based AF methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work addresses a practically important gap in industrial AOI and microscopy where small DoF (±0.14 mm) renders dense-sampling AF infeasible and existing DL-based methods unreliable beyond ~11× DoF; achieving 95%+ in-focus accuracy within 5 steps across 35× DoF is a meaningful operational improvement. However, the comparison with DL-based baselines is somewhat asymmetric (their model trained on a comprehensive multi-texture dataset while baselines were retrained per-domain), and performance degrades under bright illumination due to saturation, limiting robustness in real factory settings. Open questions include whether the optical-model intermediate representation generalizes to dynamic scenes, multi-plane focusing, or significantly different optical configurations, and whether the approach can be made truly one-shot by incorporating direction cues into the network itself.
