## Problem

Existing 3D scene graph construction methods are largely confined to structured indoor environments or road-centric autonomous driving scenarios, leaving a gap for general outdoor settings where objects span multiple scales (entire buildings down to individual windows) and lack predefined taxonomies. No prior work demonstrates that hierarchical 3D scene graphs capturing multi-scale spatial and semantic relationships can be feasibly generated in complex, unstructured outdoor environments.

## Input / Output

The method consumes posed RGB images (captured by AR devices such as HoloLens 2 or iPhones) and a preprocessed 3D mesh reconstruction, projecting 2D segmentation masks onto the mesh via ray casting to obtain per-object point clouds. The output is a hierarchical 3D scene graph where nodes at multiple granularity levels (e.g., building → facade → window) are connected by parent-child edges, root-to-root spatial edges (via minimum spanning tree), and two types of hyperedges (description-based and spatial DBSCAN clusters). Each node carries a DINOv2 feature vector, a point cloud, a set of observing views, and VLM-generated text labels, descriptions, and attributes.

## Method

The pipeline is entirely training-free for scene graph construction and proceeds in four stages: (1) mesh preprocessing, (2) per-image object proposal and hierarchical merging, (3) tree pruning, and (4) label/hyperedge generation. For each image, SAM produces 2D masks that are projected onto the mesh via ray casting, refined with voxel downsampling and DBSCAN, and assigned DINOv2 features from multiple augmented crops. Objects are merged using a combined score of geometric overlap (point cloud intersection percentage) and semantic similarity (cosine similarity of DINOv2 features), with camera-frustum pre-filtering to achieve quasi-linear scaling. Hierarchical tree construction then applies four explicit merging rules based on tree heights to build parent-child relationships, followed by pruning of single-child parents and dominant nodes. Finally, GPT-4V generates labels and descriptions per object, and SBERT embeddings plus 3D centroid clustering produce semantic and spatial hyperedges. For the downstream alignment evaluation, SGAligner is adapted with increased PointNet capacity, SBERT-based node embeddings, and Hypergraph Attention Network embeddings, trained on synthetically split sub-scenes.

## Results

On the LaMAR dataset (scenes LIN, CAB, HGE), the proposed hierarchical scene graph pipeline achieves near-perfect scene alignment when combined with SGAligner and GeoTransformer, yielding mean relative rotation errors (RRE) of 5–9 × 10⁻⁵° and mean relative translation errors (RTE) of 2.7–5.9 × 10⁻⁵ m, compared to the FPFH baseline's RRE of ~30–32° and RTE of ~14–21 m. Even with standard FPFH features on object-to-object matches, SGAligner reduces RRE to 0.08–0.88° and RTE to 1.5–2.5 m. For node retrieval, mean reciprocal rank exceeds 93% across all training/testing scene combinations, with the best modality combination (hyperedges + attributes) reaching 99.6% mean RR when trained on LIN.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against a single baseline: direct FPFH-based global registration on the entire downsampled scene (30,000 points, voxel size 0.6 m). The proposed SGAligner + scene graph approach wins decisively on both RRE and RTE across all three scenes. With FPFH features on object-level matches, SGAligner reduces RRE by roughly two orders of magnitude (e.g., 0.279° vs. 32.049° on LIN) and RTE by about 9× (2.241 m vs. 20.727 m). With GeoTransformer, the improvement is even more dramatic, achieving sub-millimeter translation errors (~10⁻⁵ m) and sub-arcsecond rotation errors (~10⁻⁵°), effectively near-perfect alignment. The baseline also fails to scale: GeoTransformer cannot be applied to the full scene due to scalability issues, whereas the scene-graph-based object matching strategy handles the large outdoor environments without failure. No comparison is made against other scene-graph-based alignment methods, as no prior outdoor 3D scene graphs exist for direct benchmarking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 3D scene graph construction lineage from indoor settings (Armeni et al. [2], Rosinol et al. [18]) and autonomous driving (Fischer et al. [8]) to general outdoor environments, where neither structured room taxonomies nor predefined traffic categories apply. Rather than introducing new learning architectures, it composes existing components—SAM for segmentation, DINOv2 for features, DBSCAN for clustering, and VLMs for labeling—into a training-free hierarchical merging pipeline. The downstream evaluation adapts SGAligner [21] by replacing GAT embeddings with SBERT sentence encodings and introducing Hypergraph Attention Network embeddings, positioning the work as a bridge between scene graph generation and cross-modal alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is among the first demonstrations that hierarchical 3D scene graphs can be constructed for large-scale, unstructured outdoor scenes (45,000+ m²) without supervised training, opening a path for structured scene understanding in AR, robotics, and urban planning. However, the evaluation is limited to a single dataset family (LaMAR) and lacks a direct baseline for scene graph quality, relying instead on downstream alignment performance as a proxy. Open questions include how the pipeline handles dynamic outdoor elements (vehicles, pedestrians), whether the VLM-based labeling generalizes to non-English or culturally diverse scenes, and whether the hierarchical structure remains meaningful at even larger city-scale extents.
