## Problem

Existing explainability methods for Vision Transformers produce unreliable heatmaps due to three key limitations: they rely on raw attention patterns that introduce background noise, they fail to filter semantically irrelevant 'register tokens' that accumulate high attention weights, and they do not establish a direct link between attention patterns and the model's final prediction. Additionally, different ViT architectures distribute decision-relevant information across different subsets of layers, making single-layer or final-layer-only explanations incomplete.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250654/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

LeGrad takes an RGB image processed by any ViT backbone (from ViT-B/16 up to 2.5B-parameter ViT-BigG/14) along with a target class label, text prompt (for CLIP/SigLIP-style models), or audio prompt (for ImageBind), and produces a 2D explainability heatmap at the original image resolution. Unlike methods that only use the final layer's output, LeGrad computes per-layer activations from intermediate token representations Z^l at every transformer block, making it compatible with diverse feature aggregation strategies including CLS-token pooling and attention-based pooling.

## Method

LeGrad computes the gradient of the prediction score s^l with respect to the self-attention map A^l at each ViT layer l, treating this gradient as the explainability signal. For each layer, the gradient ∂s^l/∂A^l is passed through a ReLU to discard negative contributions, averaged across attention heads and patch dimensions, and the [CLS] token column is removed to yield a per-layer relevance vector. These per-layer vectors are then averaged across all L layers, reshaped to a 2D spatial map, and min-max normalized. The key insight is that gradients (rather than raw attention weights) naturally suppress register tokens because their attention patterns remain stable regardless of the target activation, and multi-layer aggregation captures the hierarchical feature formation process where early layers encode low-level features and later layers encode semantic information.

## Results

On OpenImagesV7 open-vocabulary segmentation, LeGrad achieves 48.38% p-mIoU with OpenCLIP-ViT-B/16, outperforming the second-best method (TextSpan at 9.44%) by over 5×. On ImageNet-Segmentation, LeGrad reaches 58.66% mIoU, surpassing CheferCAM (47.47%) by 11.2 points. In perturbation-based evaluation on ImageNet-val, LeGrad matches TextSpan (the strongest baseline) within ~1 AUC point while outperforming all gradient-based and attention-based methods. On ADE20KSoundPrompted, ImageBind+LeGrad sets a new SOTA with 48.0 mAP / 38.9 mIoU for sound segmentation, improving over the previous best (DenseAV) by +15.3 mAP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LeGrad decisively outperforms all attention-based baselines (Rollout, Raw attention) and gradient-based baselines (GradCAM, AttentionCAM, CheferCAM) across every evaluation setting, with the largest margins on open-vocabulary segmentation where it achieves 2–5× the p-mIoU of the next-best method. Against TextSpan (the strongest non-gradient baseline), LeGrad is comparable on perturbation AUC (within ~1 point) but dominates on open-vocabulary segmentation (48.38 vs. 9.44 p-mIoU on ViT-B/16) and object segmentation (58.66 vs. 40.26 mIoU). IIA is theoretically competitive on perturbation but suffers from out-of-memory failures on ViT-BigG/14 and remains far behind LeGrad on segmentation tasks. For audio-visual localization, LeGrad applied to ImageBind surpasses dedicated audio-visual models (DAVENet, CAVMAE, DenseAV) on sound segmentation, establishing a new SOTA, while the improvement on speech segmentation is modest because ImageBind was not trained on speech data. LRP and its variants are outperformed on all segmentation metrics, confirming that layer-wise relevance propagation is ill-suited to ViT architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

LeGrad carves new ground by repurposing the gradient-attribution paradigm (originally developed for CNNs via GradCAM) to the self-attention mechanism of ViTs, but with a key conceptual departure: it computes gradients *with respect to* attention maps rather than using the attention maps themselves as the explanation signal. This positions it against attention-rollout methods (Abnar & Zuidema), gradient-weighted attention aggregation (CheferCAM), and iterative integration schemes (IIA), all of which rely on complex matrix-multiplication propagation across layers. LeGrad replaces that propagation with a simple layer-wise summation of ReLU'd gradients, and it also departs from the text-similarity approach of TextSpan by grounding explanations in the model's own decision sensitivity rather than external text-direction projections.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LeGrad addresses a concrete and widely observed failure mode—register tokens inflating attention-based explanations with background noise—through a clean gradient-based mechanism that requires only a single backward pass, making it practical for very large models (ViT-BigG/14) where competing methods like IIA run out of memory. The 2×–5× p-mIoU gains on OpenImagesV7 over the previous best method (TextSpan) suggest that gradient sensitivity to attention is a substantially more faithful proxy for decision-relevance than raw attention or text-direction similarity in open-vocabulary settings. Open questions include whether the first-order gradient approximation captures the non-linear, multi-head interactions that the paper acknowledges existing methods neglect, and whether the uniform layer-averaging strategy is optimal given the observed model-specific 'fingerprints' of layer importance.
