## Problem

Existing procedure-aware video representation methods model temporal ordering of actions but do not explicitly learn how each action step transforms the scene state or what would happen if a step were performed incorrectly or omitted. This limits the model's causal understanding of procedures and its ability to detect errors or deviations in long-form activities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250536/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes video clips sub-sampled to 4 frames, clip-level narration text, video-level summary text, and LLM-generated state descriptions (before-state, after-state, state-change counterfactuals, missing-step counterfactuals, and misordered-step counterfactuals). All text is encoded by a frozen FLAVA text encoder, so only the video encoder is trained. The output is a set of hierarchical video embeddings at frame, clip, and video levels, which are then plugged into downstream models (e.g., ASFormer for segmentation, EgoPED for error detection) without further architectural modification.

## Method

The paper proposes a three-timescale contrastive pre-training framework built on HierVL's hierarchical architecture, but with a frozen text encoder and novel state-change supervision. At the frame level, before-state and after-state losses temporally contrast early frames with before-state text and late frames with after-state text, while pushing both away from LLM-generated state-change counterfactuals (e.g., 'cup shards scattered on the floor' for a failed 'make coffee' action). At the clip level, standard EgoNCE video-text alignment aligns clip features to narrations. At the video level, aggregated video features are contrasted positively against summary text and negatively against missing-step and misordered-step counterfactuals generated by Llama 3, which simulate hypothesized procedural failures. The key insight is that counterfactuals serve as hard negatives in contrastive learning, forcing the model to encode not only what actually changed but also what could have changed differently, thereby learning causal structure of procedures.

## Results

On temporal action segmentation, the method achieves 85.5 average on GTEA (F1@25=89.1, Edit=86.8, Acc=80.0) and 69.2 average on EgoPER (F1@50=54.4, Edit=74.1, Acc=79.0), outperforming the strongest non-VLM baseline PVRL by 7.0 and 5.1 points respectively. On error detection (EgoPER), it achieves the best EDA of 72.7 across all activity categories, surpassing HierVL (71.3) and CLIP (71.6). On zero-shot multi-instance retrieval (Epic-Kitchens), it more than doubles CLIP's mAP (15.7 vs. 8.4) and achieves 24.8 mAP on Charades-Ego action recognition vs. CLIP's 20.5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all non-VLM baselines (I3D, CLIP, MIL-NCE, PVRL, Br-prompt) on both GTEA and EgoPER temporal action segmentation, with the largest margin over PVRL (+7.0 Avg on GTEA, +5.1 Avg on EgoPER) and MIL-NCE (+25.5 Avg on GTEA). Against the VLM baseline HierVL, the method wins on EgoPER across all metrics (F1@50: 54.4 vs. 52.6; Edit: 74.1 vs. 73.0; Acc: 79.0 vs. 77.3) and on GTEA F1@25/F1@50/Acc, while HierVL retains a slight edge on GTEA F1@10 (90.4 vs. 89.8). On error detection (EgoPER), the method achieves the best EDA (72.7) but a lower AUC (60.5) than HierVL (66.2) and CLIP (64.2), which the authors attribute to AUC being a less informative metric for procedure awareness. On multi-instance retrieval (Epic-Kitchens), the method more than doubles CLIP's mAP (15.7 vs. 8.4) and substantially outperforms PVRL (6.1) and MIL-NCE (5.8). On action phase classification and frame retrieval (AE2), the method achieves the best F1 (64.8 ego) and mAP (70.3 ego), narrowly beating CLIP (61.6 F1, 68.0 mAP) and PVRL (62.7 F1, 66.6 mAP).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on HierVL's hierarchical video-language contrastive framework (clip-level and video-level alignment) and EgoNCE's hard-negative mining, but departs from prior procedure-aware representation work (PVRL, MIL-NCE, Bridge-prompt) by introducing LLM-generated state-change descriptions and counterfactuals as explicit supervision signals. Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate threads: the state-change understanding literature (SCHEMA, object state-modifying actions) and the counterfactual reasoning literature in vision-language models, repurposing both for general-purpose video representation learning rather than a single downstream task like procedure planning. The key methodological departure is treating hypothesized failure outcomes (missing steps, misordered steps, incomplete actions) as hard negatives in contrastive learning, a strategy not previously applied to procedural video understanding.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine gap: prior procedure-aware representations model temporal ordering of actions but not the causal state transformations that make procedures meaningful, limiting their ability to detect errors or reason about deviations. The counterfactual framing—learning what *could have* gone wrong alongside what *did* happen—offers a principled mechanism for error detection and robustness that could transfer to robotics and assistive systems. However, the reliance on LLM-generated text quality as a bottleneck, the modest (and in some cases inconsistent) gains over HierVL, and the acknowledged limitation of text-only counterfactuals (no visual/synthetic video counterfactuals) leave open whether this approach scales to more complex or open-world procedures. The authors themselves point to multimodal counterfactual generation (synthetic videos of mistakes) as the natural next step.
