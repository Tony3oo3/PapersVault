## Problem

Subject-driven generation, which personalizes a text-to-image model to capture a specific subject from only a few examples, has been explored exclusively with diffusion-based models whose extensive denoising steps (e.g., 50 DDIM steps) make inference impractically slow for real-world deployment. Visual autoregressive (VAR) models predict next-scale tokens rather than spatially adjacent ones, achieving roughly 20× faster inference, yet their application to subject-driven generation remains entirely unexplored. Moreover, naive fine-tuning of a VAR model introduces computational overhead, language drift, and reduced output diversity that must be addressed.

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of n subject images (typically 3–5) and a text prompt containing a placeholder token (e.g., '⟨S*⟩ dog in the desert') as input, and produces a 1024×1024 RGB image in approximately 0.5 seconds on a single A6000 GPU. Subject images are first encoded by a visual tokenizer into K=13 multi-scale token maps, which serve as the supervision targets during fine-tuning. The fine-tuned model is then used at inference time with classifier-free guidance applied to the logits of the autoregressive prediction.

## Method

The paper proposes three complementary techniques for fine-tuning the Infinity-2B VAR model. First, selective layer tuning restricts optimization to only the cross-attention and feed-forward network layers, identified via a weight-difference analysis showing these modules absorb the largest subject-specific updates while preserving text alignment. Second, scale-wise weighted tuning introduces a weighted cross-entropy loss (L_wCE) that assigns higher weights to coarser token scales (w₁ ≥ w₂ ≥ … ≥ w_K), motivated by the empirical finding that coarser-scale tokens govern subject identity while finer scales only adjust local details. Third, prior distillation uses the original pretrained VAR transformer as a teacher: it generates multi-scale token maps conditioned on a generic class noun prompt, and a KL-divergence loss (L_distill) aligns the fine-tuned model's predictions with the teacher's, preserving semantic priors and mitigating language drift without requiring external same-class datasets. The CA and FFN layers plus the subject text embedding are jointly optimized with both losses for 600 iterations at 1024 resolution.

## Results

On the ViCo benchmark (16 subjects × 31 prompts), the proposed method achieves the best subject fidelity (I_dino = 0.705, I_clip = 0.824) and text alignment (T_clip = 0.253) among all baselines, outperforming the strongest diffusion-based competitor DreamMatcher (I_dino = 0.682, T_clip = 0.234). Inference time is 0.5 s per image on an A6000 GPU, roughly 22–64× faster than diffusion baselines (11–32 s). In a user study with 20 participants, the method is preferred 67% of the time for subject fidelity and 79% for prompt alignment over CustomDiffusion and DreamMatcher. Against recent large diffusion architectures (SD 3.5, FLUX) with DreamBooth, the method achieves the highest I_dino (0.786) and I_clip (0.853) while matching or exceeding their T_clip scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The method outperforms all six SD 1.4-based diffusion baselines (TI, DB, ELITE, CD, ViCo, DM) on every metric: subject fidelity (I_dino 0.705 vs. best 0.682 for DM), CLIP image similarity (I_clip 0.824 vs. 0.823 for DM), and text alignment (T_clip 0.253 vs. 0.237 for CD), while being 22–64× faster in inference. Against the two strongest baselines in the user study—CustomDiffusion (best I_dino among SD 1.4 methods) and DreamMatcher (best T_clip)—the proposed method is preferred 67% and 79% of the time respectively. In the comparison with recent large diffusion architectures (Table 4), the method achieves the highest I_dino (0.786) and I_clip (0.853) over SD 3.5-md Full (0.730/0.850), SD 3.5-md LoRA (0.600/0.791), and FLUX-LoRA (0.573/0.793), though it trails SD 3.5-md LoRA (0.275) and FLUX-LoRA (0.273) on T_clip (0.267). The speed advantage is most dramatic against FLUX-LoRA (0.5 s vs. 55 s, a 110× gap). Overall, the VAR-based approach matches or exceeds state-of-the-art diffusion personalization in subject fidelity while delivering near-real-time inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is the first to adapt subject-driven generation (text-to-image personalization) to the Visual Autoregressive (VAR) framework, building directly on the next-scale prediction paradigm of Tian et al. (NeurIPS 2025) and the Infinity-2B large-scale model (Han et al., 2024). Conceptually, it transplants ideas from diffusion-based personalization—selective parameter tuning (à la CustomDiffusion), timestep-weighted training (à la non-uniform timestep sampling), and prior preservation (à la DreamBooth's class-image regularization)—into the multi-scale autoregressive setting, replacing diffusion timesteps with resolution scales and data-collection-based regularization with end-to-end token-level distillation. The selective layer tuning finding (CA + FFN suffice) parallels observations in both CustomDiffusion and language-model fine-tuning literature, while the scale-wise weighting is a novel analogue of early-timestep prioritization in diffusion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 36× inference speedup (0.5 s vs. ~18 s) while matching or exceeding diffusion baselines on subject fidelity and text alignment makes subject-driven personalization viable for latency-sensitive real-world applications, a regime where diffusion models have been impractical. The empirical finding that coarser VAR scales carry most of the subject identity (analogous to early diffusion timesteps) provides a useful inductive bias for future AR fine-tuning strategies and may transfer to other next-scale architectures. Key limitations include the ~6-hour optimization time (vs. ~30 min for DreamBooth on SD 1.4, though comparable to SD 3.5/FLUX) and the fact that prior distillation trades a small amount of subject fidelity for diversity, mirroring the known DreamBooth trade-off. Open questions include whether encoder-based or adapter-based tuning can further reduce optimization cost, and whether the scale-importance hierarchy holds across different VAR tokenizers and resolutions.
