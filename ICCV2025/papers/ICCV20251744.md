## Problem

Existing diffusion-based avatar synthesis methods condition on sparse human templates (keypoints, depth, or normal maps), causing multi-view flickering and temporal instability due to the mismatch between these signals and the subject's true appearance. Additionally, most generalizable human reconstruction models are trained on studio-captured multi-view data and fail to generalize to casually captured in-the-wild imagery with diverse lighting, clothing, and motion.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251744/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single 512×512 reference image along with either a camera trajectory (for novel view synthesis, producing 20 rendered views) or a sequence of SMPL pose parameters (for novel pose synthesis, producing 100-frame animations). The reference image is consumed in two forms: CLIP features repeated across all frames for cross-attention, and VAE-encoded latents for identity anchoring. Outputs are view-consistent image sequences or temporally coherent pose animation videos, with a one-hot switcher selecting the generation mode at inference time.

## Method

GAS is a two-stage pipeline that first uses a generalizable human NeRF (SHERF) to reconstruct the subject in canonical space and render dense appearance cues for target views or poses, then feeds these NeRF renderings—encoded via VAE and a small CNN—alongside SMPL normal map features into a Stable Video Diffusion UNet as additive conditioning on the first convolutional layer. The key architectural insight is reformulating both novel view and novel pose synthesis as a unified video generation task, sharing all diffusion parameters across the two modes. A one-hot switcher vector, positionally encoded and concatenated with the time embedding, disentangles static view synthesis from dynamic pose animation to prevent cross-task interference (e.g., unwanted clothing deformation in view sequences). Training proceeds in two stages: the NeRF is first trained on multi-view data, then frozen while the video diffusion model is trained on a combined dataset of 3D scans, multi-view videos (MVHumanNet), and in-the-wild internet videos (TikTok, Champ), with the latter providing the generalization signal. Inference employs task-specific classifier-free guidance schedules: a triangular CFG ramp for view synthesis and a fixed scale for pose animation.

## Results

On novel view synthesis, GAS achieves PSNR 26.77 / 28.82, SSIM 0.943 / 0.954, LPIPS 0.041 / 0.039, and FVD 194.8 / 191.3 on THuman / 2K2K, outperforming fine-tuned Animate Anyone (PSNR 25.20 / 26.22, FVD 302.7 / 286.4) and Champ (PSNR 23.89 / 25.66, FVD 296.1 / 279.3) across all metrics. On novel pose synthesis (TikTok), GAS reaches PSNR 19.11, SSIM 0.833, LPIPS 0.176, and FVD 362.0, substantially reducing FVD compared to Animate Anyone (840.5) and Champ (893.7). The FVD improvement on pose synthesis is particularly large—roughly 2.3× lower than the best baseline—indicating markedly better temporal coherence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GAS is compared against two state-of-the-art generative human animation methods: Animate Anyone (Hu et al., 2023) and Champ (Zhu et al., ECCV 2024), both of which were additionally fine-tuned on the paper's 3D scan and video datasets for 10k iterations to ensure a fair comparison. On novel view synthesis, GAS wins on every metric for both THuman and 2K2K: it gains +1.57 dB PSNR over Animate Anyone* and +2.88 dB over Champ* on THuman, while reducing FVD by roughly 35% (194.8 vs. 302.7) and 34% (194.8 vs. 296.1) respectively. On novel pose synthesis (TikTok), GAS again leads all metrics, with the most striking gap in FVD—362.0 vs. 840.5 (Animate Anyone*) and 893.7 (Champ*)—indicating far superior temporal coherence in generated animations. The qualitative comparisons further show that baselines suffer from view-inconsistent hair, disappearing limbs, and varying garment details, whereas GAS maintains stable identity and geometry across both novel views and pose sequences.

## Relation to Prior Work

GAS sits at the intersection of two established lines of work: generalizable human NeRF (SHERF) for regression-based 3D human reconstruction, and video diffusion models (Stable Video Diffusion) for temporally coherent generation. The key departure from prior diffusion-based human animation methods (Animate Anyone, Champ) is the replacement of sparse conditioning signals (SMPL normal maps, depth, keypoints) with dense NeRF-rendered appearance cues, which the authors argue directly addresses the multi-view and temporal inconsistencies caused by the gap between sparse templates and true subject appearance. The unified framework that jointly learns novel view and pose synthesis through shared parameters with a mode switcher is a novel architectural choice not present in prior work, which typically treats these as separate problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in avatar generation: the inability of current methods to generalize beyond controlled studio captures to casually captured in-the-wild imagery. By training on internet videos alongside multi-view studio data and sharing parameters across view and pose synthesis, GAS demonstrates that pose generalization transfers to view synthesis, a finding with broader implications for multi-task learning in human rendering. Limitations include dependence on accurate SMPL fitting (artifacts propagate through the NeRF conditioning), the two-stage pipeline's computational cost, and the 512×512 resolution constraint. Open questions include whether the NeRF component can be replaced by faster representations like 3D Gaussian Splatting without sacrificing the dense appearance signal, and whether the approach extends to multi-person or long-sequence scenarios.
