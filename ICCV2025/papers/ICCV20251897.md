## Problem

Existing world models for robot manipulation fail to accurately capture fine-grained robot-object interactions because they overlook precise alignment between individual actions and their corresponding video frames. Text-to-video models rely on high-level textual descriptions that provide general context rather than the frame-level, per-action instructions required when a robot executes a specific trajectory, making them ill-suited for the trajectory-to-video task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251897/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

IRASim takes 1–2 historical RGB observation frames and a robot action trajectory of 10–15 steps (2–7 DoF depending on the dataset: 7 DoF for RT-1/Bridge, 2 DoF for Language-Table, up to 5 DoF for RoboNet) and produces the next 10–15 video frames at resolutions up to 288×512. For long-horizon generation, the model rolls out autoregressively, using the final predicted frame of each pass as the historical condition for the next. All diffusion operations occur in the SDXL VAE latent space rather than pixel space for computational efficiency.

## Method

IRASim is a conditional diffusion transformer (DiT) operating in the SDXL VAE latent space, with a novel frame-level action-conditioning module (Frame-Ada) embedded in every transformer block. Rather than encoding the entire trajectory into a single global embedding (as in Video-Ada), each individual action is linearly encoded into its own embedding and injected via adaptive layer normalization into the spatial attention block of its corresponding frame, explicitly enforcing action-frame alignment. The temporal attention block shares a single conditioning embedding derived from the full trajectory. Historical frames are kept noise-free in the input sequence so predicted frames attend to them for temporal consistency, while diffusion noise is added only to the frames to be predicted. The model uses spatial-temporal attention for computational efficiency and is trained with the standard DDPM noise-prediction objective; it can be initialized from OpenSora pretrained weights to accelerate convergence on limited manipulation data.

## Results

On short-trajectory video generation, IRASim-Frame-Ada achieves the best PSNR and Latent L2 across all three primary datasets: 26.05 dB / 0.210 on RT-1, 25.28 dB / 0.195 on Bridge, and 28.82 dB / 0.166 on Language-Table. On the Push-T benchmark, combining IRASim with model-based planning (K=50 sampled trajectories, P=1000 post-trained rollouts) improves the vanilla diffusion policy's IoU from 0.637 to 0.961, outperforming GPC-RANK+OPT (0.882 at K=10). For policy evaluation on LIBERO, IRASim achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.99 with the ground-truth Mujoco simulator across four policy variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IRASim-Frame-Ada outperforms both U-Net-based diffusion baselines (VDM and LVDM) on the primary metrics (PSNR and Latent L2) across all three datasets, with the largest margin over VDM (e.g., +12.3 dB PSNR on RT-1). Against LVDM, IRASim gains 0.6–1.0 dB PSNR and reduces Latent L2 by 4–8%, though LVDM achieves a lower FVD on Language-Table (24.34 vs. 48.49), suggesting IRASim's temporal consistency metric is weaker in that setting. On RoboNet, IRASim surpasses iVideoGPT (PSNR 24.6 vs. 23.8) and MaskViT (PSNR 24.6 vs. 20.4), confirming the advantage of the diffusion-transformer architecture over autoregressive and masked-token approaches. For model-based planning on Push-T, IRASim with P=200 rollouts and K=5 trajectories (IoU 0.866) beats GPC-RANK+OPT at the same K (0.824), and at K=50 IRASim reaches 0.961 versus GPC-RANK's 0.698, demonstrating that the trajectory-to-video formulation with frame-level conditioning yields substantially better planning than GPC's autoregressive next-frame prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

IRASim builds directly on the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) architecture [38] and the latent-space diffusion paradigm established by LVDM [25] and Sora [6], but departs from prior action-conditioned video prediction methods (iVideoGPT, VLP, UniSim) by introducing a frame-level action-conditioning module that explicitly aligns each action in a trajectory with its corresponding generated frame, rather than encoding the entire trajectory into a single video-level embedding. The spatial-temporal attention mechanism for computational efficiency follows prior video generation work [7, 35, 52], and the use of a frozen SDXL VAE for latent-space compression is standard. The key conceptual departure is treating the trajectory-to-video task as requiring per-frame action-frame alignment, motivated by the observation that robot manipulation actions are inherently frame-specific rather than holistic descriptions like text prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 0.99 Pearson correlation between IRASim-based policy evaluation and ground-truth simulator results opens a path toward scalable, simulator-free policy evaluation for robot manipulation, which is currently bottlenecked by the cost and fragility of physical rollouts. The test-time scaling result (IoU 0.637→0.961 on Push-T by sampling more trajectories and ranking them) suggests a practical route to improving deployed policies without retraining, analogous to test-time compute scaling in LLMs. Key limitations include non-real-time generation speed (addressed in future work via diffusion distillation) and the current reliance on post-trained rollouts containing both successes and failures, which requires deploying a policy in a simulator first. Open questions include whether the frame-level conditioning generalizes to multi-robot or bimanual settings, and whether RL within the world model can further close the gap to optimal policies.
