## Problem

Existing video individual counting (VIC) methods rely on localizing pedestrians in each frame and then performing cross-frame association or classification, a paradigm that breaks down in dense, dynamic scenes captured by fast-moving drones. Additionally, prior drone-based datasets are either image-level, captured from fixed viewpoints, or contain mixed vehicles and pedestrians at low density, leaving a gap for evaluating VIC under realistic crowded, highly dynamic aerial conditions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes pairs of consecutive frames (at a fixed interval δ) from drone video clips at 720p–4K resolution, downsampled to 1–6 fps depending on drone speed. It produces per-frame global, shared, inflow, and outflow density maps at full input resolution, and the final video-level output is the total unique pedestrian count obtained by summing inflow density maps across all frames. Training is weakly supervised, requiring only binary inflow/outflow annotations rather than full per-person ID tracks.

## Method

SDNet first extracts multi-scale features from two consecutive frames using a shared-weight VGG-16 backbone and FPN, then applies a Depth-wise Cross-Frame Attention (DCFA) module that iteratively fuses features across scale levels while computing multi-head cross-attention (one frame as query, the other as key/value) to produce shared features encoding only pedestrians present in both frames. Shared density maps are decoded from these features and subtracted from independently decoded global density maps to yield inflow and outflow density maps, which are further refined by a lightweight convolutional decoder. The key insight is that learning shared (intersection) density is more tractable than directly predicting frame-specific (set-difference) density, making the optimization landscape friendlier. Training uses four MAE losses on global, shared, outflow, and inflow density maps simultaneously, and at test time the unique pedestrian count is simply the sum of all inflow density maps over the video.

## Results

On the MovingDroneCrowd dataset, SDNet achieves MAE 41.00 and RMSE 58.34, reducing MAE by 37.8% and RMSE by 47.1% over the strongest prior method CGNet (MAE 66.06, RMSE 110.36). On UAVVIC, the method achieves MAE 6.37 and RMSE 11.01, outperforming CGNet by 55% in MAE. The advantage grows with crowd density: at the highest density level (≥450 pedestrians), SDNet reaches MAE 102.88 versus 199.00 for CGNet (48.3% reduction), while MOT-based methods (e.g., DiffMOT) degrade to MAE 761.67.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SDNet outperforms all eight baselines on both datasets across every reported metric. Against the strongest VIC-specific baseline CGNet (CVPR'24), SDNet reduces MAE from 66.06 to 41.00 (−37.8%) on MovingDroneCrowd and from 24.95 to 6.37 (−55%) on UAVVIC. The density-based FMDC (WACV'24), which also avoids explicit localization, still trails SDNet by 29.3 MAE on MovingDroneCrowd, confirming that directly predicting inflow/outflow masks is harder than the shared-density-map formulation. MOT-based trackers (ByteTrack, BoT-SORT, OC-SORT, DiffMOT) suffer catastrophic degradation in dense scenes—DiffMOT reaches MAE 761.67 at the highest density level versus SDNet's 102.88—because their per-pedestrian detection and global identity association break down under severe occlusion and rapid camera motion. The cross-line counting method LOI (ECCV'16) is the weakest overall (MAE 241.77), as it was designed for static surveillance rather than dynamic drone footage. On UAVVIC's static subset, SDNet still leads (MAE 3.30 vs. 6.80 for CGNet), showing the method generalizes beyond dense dynamic scenes to sparser static ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant localization-then-association paradigm in Video Individual Counting (VIC) established by DR.VIC [14] and CGNet [25], which first detect pedestrians in each frame and then associate or classify them across frames. It also moves beyond the density-based flow mask approach of FMDC [35], which directly predicts inflow/outflow masks. Instead, the paper repurposes the density map regression idea from image-level crowd counting into a video setting by introducing a 'shared-first' strategy: a Depth-wise Cross-Frame Attention (DCFA) module estimates shared density maps between consecutive frames, and inflow/outflow maps are derived by simple subtraction from global density maps. The DCFA module itself is a new architectural contribution that hierarchically fuses multi-scale features across frames via depth-wise cross-attention units.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The MovingDroneCrowd dataset (89 clips, 4940 frames, 325K head boxes) fills a notable gap as the first video-level benchmark specifically targeting dense pedestrian scenes from fast-moving drones with diverse lighting, altitudes, and angles, making it a useful resource for the drone crowd analysis community. The shared-density-map-first strategy offers a cleaner and more learnable formulation than direct inflow/outflow prediction, a principle that could transfer to other video counting or flow estimation tasks. However, the dataset remains small in scale, the method requires inflow/outflow annotations (even if weakly supervised), and generalization to longer videos, real-time deployment, or other moving platforms (e.g., ground vehicles) remains unexplored.
