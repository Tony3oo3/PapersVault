## Problem

Existing video forgery detection methods are limited to binary real-vs-fake classification, offering no fine-grained understanding of what type of artifact is present in generated content. This lack of interpretability undermines reliability in real-world applications where knowing *why* a video is fake (spatial, appearance, or motion anomaly) is critical. The paper identifies three persistent failure modes in video generation models—unnatural spatial interactions, appearance inconsistency, and motion fidelity issues—and formalizes them as a multi-class detection problem.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip (T×C×W×H, resolution ≥224×224, 40–255 frames at 8–30 fps) and outputs a 4-class label: spatial anomaly, appearance anomaly, motion anomaly, or real. The Spatial Depth Branch specifically consumes only the first and middle frames of the video for depth encoding, while the Video Understanding Branch processes all T frames. The HFV dataset spans nine generation models (MiniMax, Gen-3, Kling, CogVideoX, etc.) with ~20 background types and 80 activity categories, plus real videos from Kinetics-400.

## Method

HumanSAM fuses two branches into a Human Forgery Representation (HFR): a Video Understanding Branch based on InternVideo2 that extracts spatiotemporal features via both attention pooling and average pooling over the CLS token and patch features, and a frozen Spatial Depth Branch using Depth Pro's encoder to capture geometric consistency from the first and middle frames. The two feature vectors are combined through a learnable scalar α in a weighted sum, then projected to a 4-class prediction. A rank-based confidence enhancement strategy weights the cross-entropy loss by log(e + r̂ᵢ), where r̂ᵢ is the normalized anomaly rank within each class, so that samples with more pronounced anomalies receive higher loss weight during training. The HFV dataset is constructed semi-automatically: three anomaly scores (depth distortion via RAFT optical flow, CLIP/DINOv2 consistency, and optical-flow-based motion distortion) are computed per video, the highest-ranked anomaly type determines the label, and the top 20% by rank within each class form the validation set after human review.

## Results

On the HFV dataset for multi-class forgery classification (spatial, appearance, motion anomaly, and real), HumanSAM achieves an average ACC of 69.8% and AUC of 87.9%, surpassing the second-best method TimeSformer by 5.8% in ACC and MM-Det by 3.4% in AUC. For the standard binary real-vs-fake task, HumanSAM reaches 97.8% ACC and 99.9% AUC on average, outperforming MM-Det (97.0% ACC, 99.7% AUC) and TimeSformer (95.2% ACC, 97.9% AUC). The method is most robust on the challenging Kling dataset, where it achieves 65.8% multi-class ACC and 90.3% binary ACC, while most baselines drop significantly below.

## Baselines & Comparisons

HumanSAM outperforms all seven baselines across every generation model in the multi-class setting. The closest competitor, TimeSformer (64.0% ACC, 83.8% AUC), is a spatiotemporal self-attention model that captures appearance and motion but lacks explicit spatial depth reasoning; HumanSAM gains 5.8% ACC and 4.1% AUC over it. MM-Det (62.3% ACC, 84.5% AUC) uses multimodal features via MLLM and VQVAE reconstruction, excelling on Gen-2 (57.7% ACC) but struggling on Kling (52.6% ACC), where HumanSAM leads by 13.2% ACC. CLIP-based Uni-FD (59.9% ACC, 81.3% AUC) performs well on lower-quality generators like CogVideoX-5B (72.8% ACC) but drops to 47.6% on Kling, indicating sensitivity to high temporal consistency. F3Net (50.8% ACC, 74.0% AUC) is the weakest baseline, confirming that frequency-domain cues alone are insufficient for multi-class forgery typing. DIRE (60.5% ACC, 79.0% AUC) and CNNDet (59.4% ACC, 77.8% AUC) are image-level methods adapted to video, showing that temporal modeling and depth features are critical for fine-grained anomaly classification. In the binary task, HumanSAM (97.8% ACC, 99.9% AUC) edges out MM-Det (97.0%, 99.7%) and TimeSformer (95.2%, 97.9%), with the largest margin on Kling (90.3% vs. 85.7% and 88.0% respectively).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes existing video understanding (InternVideo2) and monocular depth estimation (Depth Pro) backbones into a dual-branch forgery detector, departing from prior work that relied on frame-level CNNs (CNNDet), frequency-domain analysis (F3Net), or diffusion-model reconstruction (DIRE). The three-way anomaly taxonomy (spatial, appearance, motion) is motivated by known failure modes of video generation models identified in benchmarks like VBench and EvalCrafter, but no prior detection work operationalized these categories as distinct classification targets. The rank-based confidence enhancement loss, which weights training samples by their anomaly-score ranking, is a novel training strategy not seen in prior forgery detection literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The HFV dataset is the first public benchmark for multi-class human-centric forgery video detection, filling a gap that has limited interpretability research in this area. The three-way taxonomy provides actionable diagnostic information (which type of artifact is present) rather than a single real/fake verdict, which is critical for real-world forensic and content-moderation applications. However, the taxonomy's mutual exclusivity is questionable (the paper acknowledges <2% of videos contain all three anomaly types), and the semi-automatic labeling pipeline based on depth/CLIP/optical-flow scores may introduce systematic biases. Open questions include whether the three categories generalize to future generation models that may exhibit different failure modes, and whether the depth branch's contribution (≈3.6% ACC) justifies its computational overhead in deployment.
