## Problem

Existing copyright infringement detection for AI-generated images relies on global similarity metrics, missing real-world cases where only specific attributes (style, structure, or content) are copied while the overall image differs. Moreover, prior work focused on detecting memorization of training data rather than legally actionable infringement of copyrighted works across distinct evaluative aspects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250456/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

In the classification setting, HIDM receives a copyrighted reference image and a generated candidate image, and outputs per-aspect cosine similarity scores (content, style, structure) alongside a VLM-generated infringement verdict with a natural-language explanation. In the retrieval setting, a single generated image is matched against a large copyright gallery (LSCD, 715K images) via k-nearest-neighbor search on each attribute independently, then candidates are aggregated. The VLM prompt must explicitly instruct that high similarity in any single aspect suffices for an infringement ruling, preventing the model from requiring multi-aspect agreement.

## Method

HIDM trains three separate encoders (content, style, structure) from a shared DINOv2 ViT-Base backbone via LoRA fine-tuning, each using contrastive learning with quadruplet-based hard negative mining: for an anchor image, the corresponding attribute-matched synthetic pair serves as the positive while the other two attribute-matched pairs serve as natural hard negatives. Training pairs are synthesized with controllable generative models—image-to-image denoising for content, IP-Adapter with modified captions for style, and ControlNet conditioned on DPT depth maps for structure. At inference, the three encoders run in parallel (adding only 0.4% FLOPs over a single encoder), and their per-aspect similarity scores are concatenated as textual context into GPT-4o, which combines these numerical signals with its world knowledge (e.g., recognizing IP characters) to produce the final interpretable judgment. The key design choice is explicit attribute decoupling: a single aggregated encoder covering all three attributes performs significantly worse than the three specialized encoders, confirming that separate feature spaces are necessary for fine-grained multi-aspect detection.

## Results

On the MCID testset (236 expert-annotated pairs), HIDM achieves 72.46% average classification accuracy across four infringement types (Content: 91.53%, Style: 74.58%, Structural: 64.41%, IP: 59.32%), substantially outperforming the best prior method Gowthami[42] at 42.80% average. On the CPDM benchmark, HIDM reaches 64.17% accuracy versus 48.32% for Gowthami[42]. In the retrieval setting, the multi-aspect encoder achieves μAP of 0.671 and R@20 of 0.925, surpassing the strongest single-encoder baseline MoCoV3 (μAP 0.588, R@20 0.857).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HIDM outperforms both Somepalli et al. baselines (Gowthami[42], CVPR 2023; Gowthami[43], NeurIPS 2023) by a wide margin on all four infringement types: +15.26 points on Content (91.53 vs 76.27), +20.34 on Style (74.58 vs 54.24), +45.77 on Structural (64.41 vs 18.64), and +37.29 on IP (59.32 vs 22.03) over Gowthami[42]. The structural infringement gap is particularly striking, as prior global-similarity methods score near chance level (~17–19%) on this type. On IP detection specifically, Wang[49] (62.71%) slightly edges out HIDM (59.32%), which is expected since Wang's method is purpose-built for IP character detection using VLM world knowledge, whereas HIDM is a general-purpose multi-aspect detector. In the retrieval setting (Table 1), HIDM's aggregated multi-aspect encoder beats all single-encoder baselines including MoCoV3 (μAP 0.671 vs 0.588), DINOv2 (0.671 vs 0.423), and SSCD (0.671 vs 0.472), confirming that decoupled attribute features are complementary for identifying potential infringements in a large gallery.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in AI-generated image infringement detection, which relies on global similarity metrics (SSCD, DINOv2, CLIP) to flag near-duplicates of training data. It also moves beyond VLM-only approaches for IP character detection (CopyCat, Wang et al.) that cannot perform fine-grained visual attribute comparison. By borrowing the content/style/structure taxonomy from image editing literature and combining contrastively-trained attribute-specific encoders (LoRA-finetuned DINOv2) with a VLM judge, HIDM synthesizes ideas from copy detection, style transfer, and controllable generation (ControlNet, IP-Adapter) into a unified multi-aspect detection framework that had no single prior counterpart.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LSCD dataset (715K images with verified copyright ownership) and the legal-expert-annotated MCID testset address a critical gap: prior benchmarks (CPDM) conflate memorization with legal infringement and lack verified copyright attribution. The finding that VLMs alone achieve near-random accuracy (23.27%) on multi-image attribute comparison underscores that purely prompt-based solutions are insufficient for legal-grade detection. However, IP infringement detection remains the weakest link at 59.32% accuracy, and the system's dependence on GPT-4o raises cost and reproducibility concerns for real-world deployment. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to video or music, whether automated detection outputs carry legal admissibility, and how to handle the growing space of AI-unique styles that may not map cleanly onto the four defined categories.
