## Problem

Asymmetric loss functions offer superior noise-tolerance guarantees over symmetric losses under a more relaxed condition, but all existing asymmetric losses are active losses that only maximize the probability of the labeled class. This prevents their integration into the Active Passive Loss (APL) framework, which requires a passive loss component to mitigate underfitting. The paper addresses this gap by deriving the first passive asymmetric loss function and incorporating it into the APL framework.

## Input / Output

The method takes standard RGB images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224 or larger for WebVision, ILSVRC12, and Clothing1M) paired with noisy labels as the sole training signal, and outputs a K-class probability vector via a softmax layer. No clean labels, additional supervision, or test-time information is used. The noisy label is consumed through the loss function rather than any explicit noise model, making the approach plug-and-play with standard CNN backbones (8-layer CNN, ResNet-34, or ResNet-50).

## Method

The paper proposes Asymmetric Mean Square Error (AMSE), defined as L_AMSE(f(x), y) = (1/K)‖a·e_y − f(x)‖²₂ with a ≥ 1, which is a passive loss because it explicitly penalizes all class probabilities against a scaled one-hot target. Theorem 4.1 rigorously establishes the necessary and sufficient condition on the weight ratio w_m/w_n for AMSE to satisfy the asymmetric condition, showing that for q=2 the threshold is (a + Σ_{i≠m} w_i/w_n)/(a−1). By substituting AMSE for the traditional symmetric passive loss (MAE) in the APL framework, the authors construct Joint Asymmetric Loss (JAL): L_JAL-CE = α·L_NCE + β·L_AMSE (or with NFL in place of NCE). Since both NCE/NFL (symmetric, hence also asymmetric) and AMSE (asymmetric) satisfy the asymmetric condition, their weighted sum JAL inherits noise-tolerance. The hyperparameter a controls the robustness–fitting trade-off: larger a imposes tighter constraints for higher noise rates but may reduce fitting ability under asymmetric noise, so a moderate value (e.g., a=30) is recommended.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 0.8 symmetric noise, JAL-CE achieves 65.43% test accuracy, outperforming the previous best ANL-CE (62.28%) by 3.15% and NCE+RCE (54.99%) by 10.44%. On CIFAR-100 with 0.4 asymmetric noise, JAL-FL reaches 65.45%, improving over ANL-FL (62.01%) by approximately 3.4%. On real-world datasets, JAL-CE attains the highest accuracy across all three: 69.84% on WebVision, 66.64% on ILSVRC12, and 70.31% on Clothing1M. Under 0.6 instance-dependent noise, JAL-CE achieves 75.62% on CIFAR-10 and 51.69% on CIFAR-100, surpassing the next best methods by 3–4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

JAL-CE and JAL-FL consistently rank top-2 across all noise types and datasets. On the most challenging 0.8 symmetric noise (CIFAR-10), JAL-CE (65.43%) surpasses ANL-CE (62.28%) by 3.15% and NCE+RCE (54.99%) by 10.44%, while NCE+AGCE collapses to 43.82% under the same setting. Under 0.4 asymmetric noise, JAL-CE (79.54%) edges out ANL-CE (77.99%) and NCE+AGCE (78.47%) by 1.5–1.6%. On CIFAR-100 with 0.4 asymmetric noise, JAL-FL (65.45%) improves over ANL-FL (62.01%) by ~3.4% and over NCE+AGCE (60.57%) by ~4.9%. On real-world benchmarks, JAL-CE achieves the best accuracy on all three datasets (WebVision 69.84%, ILSVRC12 66.64%, Clothing1M 70.31%), outperforming ANL-FL (67.76%, 64.84%, 69.90%) and NCE+AGCE (67.16%, 64.36%, 67.90%). The key advantage of JAL over prior asymmetric-loss combinations (NCE+AUL, NCE+AGCE) is that AMSE acts as a passive loss within the APL framework, enabling complementary optimization that pure active-loss combinations cannot achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two recent lines of work on robust loss functions for noisy labels: the Active Passive Loss (APL) framework of Ma et al. (ICML 2020), which pairs an active symmetric loss (NCE/NFL) with a passive symmetric loss (MAE) to combat underfitting, and the Asymmetric Loss Functions (ALFs) of Zhou et al. (ICML 2021, TPAMI 2023), which offer stronger noise-tolerance guarantees under a relaxed asymmetric condition. The critical gap the paper identifies is that all existing ALFs are active losses, making them incompatible with the APL framework's passive component. The authors bridge this by deriving the first passive asymmetric loss (AMSE) and embedding it into APL to form JAL, effectively upgrading the passive half of the framework from symmetric to asymmetric while preserving the active NCE/NFL component.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a genuine theoretical gap by providing the necessary and sufficient condition for a passive loss to satisfy the asymmetric condition, and the resulting AMSE is simple (a scaled MSE with one hyperparameter). Practically, JAL achieves consistent top-2 performance across symmetric, asymmetric, instance-dependent, and human-annotated noise on multiple datasets, with the most notable gain being ~10% on CIFAR-100 under 0.4 asymmetric noise. A key limitation is that the hyperparameter 'a' must be set based on the noise rate and number of classes (e.g., a≥9 for 0.8 symmetric noise on CIFAR-10), requiring prior knowledge of noise characteristics that is often unavailable in practice. Open questions include whether the framework extends to other active losses beyond NCE/NFL, and whether the clean-label-dominant assumption (1−η_x > max η_{x,k}) can be relaxed further.
