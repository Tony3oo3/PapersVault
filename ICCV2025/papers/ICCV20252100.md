## Problem

Most token-reduction methods for video transformers (merging, motion-based selection, attention-based pruning) fail to outperform the trivial baseline of randomly discarding tokens, despite using sophisticated selection criteria. This paper investigates why this puzzling phenomenon occurs by characterizing the underlying distribution of token values, and uses the resulting insights to design a selector that genuinely beats both random sampling and prior state-of-the-art methods.

## Input / Output

The method takes video clips of 16×224×224 pixels (tube size 2×16×16) and produces a class label (or detection boxes for the AVA task) after processing only a fraction (P-Ratio) of the original tokens. At inference, the lightweight MLP selector assigns each patch embedding a score in [0,1], and the top-scoring tokens are retained for the transformer blocks. The adaptive variant (LITE++) further modulates the token budget per video using a MoviNet confidence estimate, allocating fewer tokens to high-confidence (easy) clips.

## Method

LITE (LIghtweight Token Elector) is a 3-layer MLP that predicts per-token importance scores by learning to replicate a Grad-CAM-based oracle computed with true labels during training. The oracle scores each token via the gradient of the target-class logit with respect to the final-block feature activations, followed by ReLU and min-max normalization. The selector is trained with binary cross-entropy loss against these oracle scores while the VideoMAE backbone remains frozen, and is inserted between the patch embedding module and the first transformer block. At inference, only the top-scoring tokens (per a fixed P-Ratio budget) are passed to the transformer, reducing GFLOPs proportionally. The key empirical finding is that token values follow a Pareto distribution—most tokens carry negligible information while a few carry most of it—and that low-value tokens actively hurt accuracy, explaining why random sampling works reasonably well but a learned selector can do better by preferentially discarding the noisy tail.

## Results

On Something-Something-V2, LITE achieves 70.54% Top-1 accuracy at 159 GFLOPs (90% tokens) and 69.91% at 80 GFLOPs (50% tokens), matching or exceeding the full VideoMAE baseline (70.50% at 181 GFLOPs) while cutting compute by up to 56%. On Kinetics-400, LITE reaches 81.33% Top-1 at 159 GFLOPs (90% tokens) and 80.36% at 80 GFLOPs (50% tokens), surpassing the VideoMAE baseline (81.25% at 181 GFLOPs) at the 90% setting. The adaptive-budget variant LITE++ further reduces GFLOPs by up to 34% (e.g., 105 vs. 159) with less than 0.6% accuracy loss. Zero-shot transfer of the K400-trained selector to UCF101, SS-V2, and AVA detection preserves accuracy within 0.7% while reducing compute by 35%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LITE outperforms all token-reduction baselines on the GFLOPs-vs-accuracy Pareto front. On SS-V2, LITE₅₀ (80 GFLOPs, 69.91%) beats ToMe (86 GFLOPs, 69.63%) on both compute and accuracy, and surpasses STA (116 GFLOPs, 70.30%) in efficiency while remaining within 0.4% accuracy. On Kinetics-400, LITE₅₀ (80 GFLOPs, 80.36%) dramatically outperforms LookupViViT (376 GFLOPs, 78.30%), achieving higher accuracy with only 21% of the compute. LITE₉₀ on K400 (159 GFLOPs, 81.33%) actually exceeds the full VideoMAE backbone (181 GFLOPs, 81.25%), demonstrating that discarding low-value tokens can improve accuracy. Against the random-sampling baseline, LITE's advantage grows as the token budget shrinks: at 30% tokens on SS-V2, LITE achieves 68.33% versus 66.73% for random (a 1.6% gap), confirming that learned selection matters most under tight budgets. The paper also notes that LITE₇₀ on SS-V2 (118 GFLOPs, 70.44%) matches ObjectViViT (192 GFLOPs, 70.00%) in accuracy while using only 61% of the compute.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against the growing body of token reduction and merging methods for video transformers (ToMe, STA, STTS, STTM, LookupViT), but departs fundamentally by introducing an oracle-driven, gradient-based scoring mechanism derived from Grad-CAM rather than learned end-to-end scorers or similarity-based merging. The key conceptual departure is the empirical discovery that most of these sophisticated methods fail to beat random token sampling, reframing the problem from 'how to design a better selector' to 'what are the underlying statistical properties of token value.' The method (LITE) is deliberately minimal—a 3-layer MLP trained to replicate the oracle—contrasting with the more complex architectures in prior work, and the adaptive budget mechanism (LITE++) draws on the observation that task difficulty correlates with token distribution shape.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that random sampling is competitive with or superior to most published token selection methods challenges a core assumption in the efficiency literature and redirects research toward understanding token value distributions rather than engineering increasingly complex selectors. The 5 distilled principles (Pareto distribution, low-value tokens as noise, difficulty-dependent compute) provide a reusable conceptual framework for future efficient video models. Limitations include reliance on ground-truth labels for oracle construction during training, evaluation primarily on classification and detection tasks with VideoMAE as the dominant backbone, and the question of whether these principles transfer to vision-language models where token distributions may differ substantially. The zero-shot generalization across datasets and tasks is promising but tested on a limited set of settings.
