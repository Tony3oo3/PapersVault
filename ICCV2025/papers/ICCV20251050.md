## Problem

Existing vision-language benchmarks emphasize high-level reasoning and knowledge tasks, leaving unexplored whether LVLMs can perform the low-level perceptual task of detecting visually salient odd-one-out targets that humans identify effortlessly. This gap is particularly concerning given Moravec's paradox, which predicts that simple perceptual skills are harder for AI than complex reasoning, and has practical implications for robotics and agent deployment where missing salient changes is costly.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes RGB images from two sources: synthetic 7×7 grid arrays (P3, 2514 images) where a single target differs in exactly one controlled attribute, and natural images (O3, 2001 images) where a singleton object stands out among 2–50+ distractors. Three task variants modulate the input: Detection provides only the image and a text prompt; Referring appends bounding-box coordinates in the prompt text; Visual Referring overlays a red highlight box directly on the image. The model outputs a multi-label set of saliency features drawn from a fixed vocabulary (seven classes for natural images, three for synthetic), scored by exact-match accuracy and per-category F1.

## Method

SalBench is constructed by augmenting the P3/O3 odd-one-out datasets with structured language instructions that frame saliency detection as a multi-label classification over Feature Integration Theory categories, creating three progressively constrained tasks (Detection, Referring, Visual Referring). The evaluation protocol benchmarks 15+ state-of-the-art LVLMs (from 1.5B to 72B parameters, including GPT-4o, Qwen2-VL, Claude, Molmo, InternVL) under zero-shot and few-shot (3-shot, 5-shot) settings on both synthetic and natural splits. To localize the failure, the authors separately test LLM backbones on 50 Feature Integration Theory questions (showing strong knowledge, up to 97.5% for GPT-4o) and vision backbones via cosine-similarity retrieval of saliency descriptions (showing poor discriminability, especially on natural images). They further train four model variants (Llama3.1-8B / Qwen2-7B × CLIP / SigLip) on 1M synthetic saliency image-caption pairs plus 2M natural image data using a LLaVA-style recipe, finding that in-distribution training does not close the performance gap, indicating the current architecture–training pipeline is fundamentally unsuited to capturing low-level saliency.

## Results

On SalBench, even the strongest LVLMs struggle with low-level saliency detection: GPT-4o achieves only 47.6% F1 on natural-image Detection (0-shot) and Claude-sonnet reaches 48.2%, while on the synthetic split GPT-4o attains 89.2% and Qwen2-VL-72B reaches 88.8%. The gap between synthetic and natural images is consistently 30–40 percentage points across all models, indicating that real-world multi-attribute complexity severely degrades saliency perception. Few-shot learning (3-shot, 5-shot) yields inconsistent and often negative gains; for example, GPT-4o's NAT Detection F1 drops from 47.6% (0-shot) to 41.9% (5-shot), and Phi3.5-Vision-3.5B's SYN Visual Referring F1 collapses from 63.5% to 19.0%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the 13 evaluated LVLMs, Claude-sonnet and GPT-4o lead on natural-image tasks (48.2% and 47.6% F1 on Detection, respectively), while Qwen2-VL-72B achieves the highest synthetic-image Referring score (93.6% F1). The top three models (Claude-sonnet, GPT-4o, Qwen2-VL-72B) cluster within ~6 F1 points on NAT Detection but diverge sharply on NAT Visual Referring, where Claude-sonnet (53.9%) outperforms GPT-4o (42.6%) by over 11 points. Smaller models (≤8B parameters) generally fall 15–25 points below the 72B-class models on synthetic splits, confirming a clear scaling trend for saliency perception that is less pronounced on standard VQA benchmarks. Notably, PaliGemma-3B-448 is a severe outlier on Referring tasks (1.2% NAT, 9.5% SYN), suggesting its architecture handles spatial grounding poorly. Few-shot prompting fails to close the gap: even Qwen2-VL-72B's 5-shot NAT Detection (43.9%) remains far below its 0-shot SYN score (88.8%), and several models (Phi3, LLava, GPT-4o) actually degrade with more shots on natural images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the P3/O3 saliency datasets (Kotseruba et al., 2020), originally designed for traditional visual search algorithms, by augmenting them with language instructions to create a benchmark specifically for Large Vision-Language Models. It positions itself in deliberate contrast to the dominant LVLM evaluation landscape (MMMU, MMBench, VQAv2, MathVista), which focuses on high-level reasoning and knowledge-intensive tasks, arguing that low-level perceptual alignment with human attention remains untested. The theoretical grounding draws on Feature Integration Theory (Treisman & Gelade, 1980) from cognitive neuroscience, framing saliency detection as a fundamental building block of human visual processing that LVLMs should replicate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even GPT-4o achieves only ~48% on tasks humans solve near-perfectly exposes a fundamental perceptual gap in current LVLMs, with practical implications for robotics, autonomous agents, and web navigation where detecting salient changes is critical. The negative result that training on one million saliency-specific image-caption pairs fails to close this gap suggests the limitation is architectural (in the vision backbone or cross-modal alignment) rather than a data problem, pointing toward the need for fundamentally different approaches to low-level visual encoding. An open question is whether stronger vision encoders trained explicitly on spatial/low-level features, or hybrid architectures combining dedicated saliency modules with LLM reasoning, could resolve this deficit.
