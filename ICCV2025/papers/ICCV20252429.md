## Problem

Dynamic 3D neural field reconstruction (dynamic NeRF) assumes synchronized multi-view videos with known camera poses, constraints that are rarely met in real-world capture setups. Existing methods for unsynchronized or uncalibrated inputs either require ground-truth poses, handle only small temporal perturbations, or fail in dynamic scenes with textureless or repetitive structures. This paper asks whether human motion alone can serve as a calibration pattern to recover both time offsets and camera poses from casually captured, unsynchronized multi-view videos.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252429/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes N unsynchronized multi-view videos (with known intrinsics, arbitrary frame rates, and optionally moving cameras) plus person correspondence across views as input, and produces a 4D NeRF (K-Planes representation) along with per-video time offsets (in frames) and per-frame camera poses (rotation and translation in a shared world coordinate). The system handles an arbitrary number of humans and does not require humans to be the only dynamic objects. Evaluation is done via novel-view synthesis at held-out camera positions, with test-time optimization of the test camera pose for fair metric computation.

## Method

The core idea is to exploit SLAHMR-estimated human motion (SMPL-H shape, pose, and root trajectory per frame) as a robust mid-level representation for calibration. In the initialization stage, pairwise time offsets are found via dynamic time warping on canonical 3D joint positions (root rotation zeroed), then globally aligned with a greedy cost-minimization procedure; camera poses are recovered by Procrustes alignment of global 3D joint positions across overlapping frames. In the refinement stage, K-Planes 4D NeRF is jointly optimized with calibration parameters under photometric loss, stabilized by two key strategies: (1) a coarse-to-fine weighting schedule on multi-resolution grid levels (inspired by BARF's dynamic low-pass filter) that progressively introduces higher-frequency features, and (2) a curriculum learning schedule that first optimizes only the NeRF, then unfreezes camera poses, and finally adds time offsets to the parameter set. This is the first approach to reconstruct dynamic NeRF from simultaneously unknown time offsets and camera poses.

## Results

On CMU Panoptic Studio (5 scenes), the method achieves an average PSNR of 26.55, SSIM of 0.874, and LPIPS of 0.112 for novel view synthesis, approaching the oracle (GT poses & time) at 27.16/0.910/0.078. On Mobile-Stage, it reaches PSNR 23.27, SSIM 0.816, LPIPS 0.089, slightly exceeding the oracle (22.98/0.806/0.093). After refinement, camera pose calibration achieves 0.41° rotation error and 0.22 cm translation error, while time offsets are recovered to within 0.028 frames (~1 ms) from initial offsets of ~30 frames.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is Sync-NeRF [16], which jointly optimizes time offsets with dynamic NeRF but requires known camera poses and near-GT initial time offsets. The proposed method outperforms Sync-NeRF (given GT poses) by a large margin: +5.36 dB PSNR and −0.073 LPIPS on Panoptic Studio average, and +6.01 dB PSNR and −0.195 LPIPS on Mobile-Stage. Against the oracle (K-Planes with GT poses and time offsets), the method is within 0.61 dB PSNR on Panoptic Studio and actually exceeds the oracle by 0.29 dB PSNR on Mobile-Stage, attributed to sub-frame time offset optimization correcting imperfect GT annotations. An internal ablation removing the progressive learning strategy (coarse-to-fine grid weighting + curriculum parameter unfreezing) degrades PSNR by 6.9 dB on Panoptic Studio and 9.6 dB on Mobile-Stage, confirming that the progressive training is essential even with good initialization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three prior lines of work: dynamic NeRF reconstruction (K-Planes, NeRFies, D-NeRF), unsynchronized video alignment (Sync-NeRF), and unknown-pose NeRF optimization (BARF, SCNeRF). Each of these assumes at least one of synchronization or known poses, and the paper's key departure is using human motion (via SLAHMR/SMPL-H) as a shared mid-level representation to simultaneously solve temporal alignment (via DTW) and spatial calibration (via Procrustes) before joint refinement. The progressive training strategy—coarse-to-fine multi-resolution grid weighting adapted from BARF and curriculum-based parameter unfreezing adapted from SCNeRF—is, to the authors' knowledge, the first such stabilization applied to dynamic (4D) NeRF rather than static scenes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: it removes the need for hardware-level synchronization and pre-calibrated rigs, enabling 4D reconstruction from casually captured multi-view videos (concerts, sports events) that are only collected after the fact. Key limitations include the hard dependency on humans being present and on SLAHMR producing usable motion, the restriction to known intrinsics, and the fact that moving-camera pose refinement is left as future work. Open questions include generalizing the calibration pattern beyond humans (e.g., vehicles), handling appearance variation and occlusions in distributed internet videos, and whether the joint optimization strategy scales to larger scenes or longer temporal extents.
