## Problem

Synthesizing normal-light novel views from low-light multiview images remains challenging because 2D enhancement methods ignore multiview consistency, while existing end-to-end NeRF approaches (LLNeRF, Aleth-NeRF) introduce color distortions, artifacts from view-inconsistent enhancement or misinterpreted air opacity, and provide limited denoising. No prior method robustly bridges the illumination gap while simultaneously suppressing high-ISO noise in a multiview-coherent 3D representation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252672/cropped_page0_idx7.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes low-light sRGB multiview images with known camera poses (25–48 views per scene on the LOM benchmark) and produces a per-scene 3D representation that renders normal-light novel views at arbitrary viewpoints. Training is fully unsupervised: only the low-light observations serve as photometric supervision, with no paired normal-light ground truth required. The learned representation consists of two coupled fields—a viewer-centered radiance field (RGB color + density) and a world-centered illuminance transition field (scalar per 3D point)—which together reconstruct the observed low-light pixels via element-wise multiplication.

## Method

RoSe reformulates low-light restoration as an illuminance transition estimation problem in 3D space, deriving from Retinex theory the direct relation Z_low = Z_nor ⊙ I where I = L_low/L_nor, bypassing the ill-posed two-stage reflectance/illumination decomposition. The architecture is a dual-branch NeRF: a viewer-centered branch (standard NeRF MLPs for color and density) captures view-dependent lighting, while a world-centered branch (a single MLP mapping 3D position to a scalar transition value, independent of view direction) ensures multiview-consistent illumination mapping. A low-rank denoising module (LRD) is embedded in the transition branch, projecting features into a low-rank subspace via a reduction-reconstruction-reweight process that suppresses high-rank noise without explicit 2D denoising or noise modeling. Training uses an inverse-tone-curve-weighted MSE loss on low-light observations plus an unsupervised illumination correction loss (global average pooling toward a target brightness of 0.45), with no paired supervision.

## Results

On the LOM dataset (5 scenes: buu, chair, sofa, bike, shrub), RoSe achieves a mean PSNR of 26.28, SSIM of 0.808, and LPIPS of 0.379, outperforming all baselines. The best individual scene result is on 'buu' with PSNR 30.03 and SSIM 0.918, while the most challenging scene 'shrub' yields PSNR 18.26 and SSIM 0.561. RoSe achieves the best or second-best performance across all five scenes, with the largest margins over enhancement-based methods (e.g., Zero-DCE+NeRF, RUAS+NeRF) and consistent gains over end-to-end competitors LLNeRF and Aleth-NeRF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoSe outperforms all 12 baselines on mean PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS across the five LOM scenes. Among end-to-end NeRF methods, RoSe surpasses both LLNeRF [34] and Aleth-NeRF [5]: LLNeRF suffers from view-dependent enhancement conflicts causing artifacts, while Aleth-NeRF introduces color distortions by misinterpreting air as scene geometry under large illumination gaps. All enhancement-then-NeRF pipelines (Zero-DCE, RUAS, SCI, LLVE, RetinexFormer, LIME, Dual Illumination) perform substantially worse than RoSe, as their per-view 2D enhancement introduces multiview inconsistencies that propagate into 3D rendering. Vanilla NeRF trained directly on low-light images serves as the weakest baseline, confirming that explicit illumination handling is necessary. The ablation further shows that removing the low-rank denoising module drops mean PSNR from 26.28 to 21.11, highlighting that RoSe's advantage over baselines is partly attributable to its 3D-consistent denoising rather than relying on 2D post-processing.

## Relation to Prior Work

RoSe builds on NeRF's volume rendering framework and Retinex theory's reflectance-illumination decomposition, but departs from both in a key way: rather than performing the ill-posed two-stage decomposition (estimate R and L separately, then enhance), it directly estimates the illuminance ratio I = L_low/L_nor as a 3D field, sidestepping the color distortion inherent in incomplete decomposition. This contrasts with prior low-light NeRF methods—LLNeRF's per-view enhancement coefficients and Aleth-NeRF's air-particle transmittance modeling—both of which introduce view-inconsistent or physically implausible artifacts. The low-rank denoising module further distinguishes RoSe by exploiting the structural difference between smooth illumination (low-rank) and sensor noise (high-rank) without resorting to 2D denoising or explicit noise models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The world-centered illuminance transition field offers a principled solution to the multiview consistency problem that plagues per-view enhancement approaches, and the low-rank denoising strategy provides a lightweight alternative to explicit noise modeling that could transfer to other illumination-sensitive 3D reconstruction tasks. However, the method is currently limited to per-scene training on a small benchmark (5 LOM scenes), relies on the Lambertian assumption, and has not been validated on more challenging real-world conditions (e.g., mixed lighting, specular surfaces). Open questions include whether the illuminance transition formulation generalizes to unconstrained outdoor scenes, whether it can be integrated into efficient NeRF variants without quality loss, and whether the low-rank prior holds under more complex illumination patterns.
