## Problem

Academic emotion recognition from facial expressions remains underexplored due to the scarcity of publicly available datasets capturing natural learning contexts. Existing academic emotion datasets (DAiSEE, EngageWild, OL-SFED) focus on online learning with stimulus-induced emotions and only capture face or upper-body crops, omitting the surrounding learning environment that expert teachers rely on to distinguish, e.g., a genuinely engaged student from one distracted by a phone. Additionally, the semantic gap between basic emotions and academic emotions makes direct mapping from standard FER methods unreliable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252027/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 5–15 second video clips of students in real-world learning settings (classrooms, libraries, labs, dormitories) and produces one of five fine-grained academic emotion labels (enjoyment, neutrality, confusion, fatigue, distraction) or two coarse-grained labels (engaged, distracted). Each frame is consumed in two parallel streams: a face-cropped region and the full frame, so the model simultaneously encodes facial expression and environmental context. Classification is performed by cosine similarity between a single visual feature token and per-class text feature tokens in CLIP's aligned embedding space, rather than a standard softmax head.

## Method

CLIP-CAER builds on CLIP ViT-B/32 with a dual-stream context-aware temporal encoder: face-cropped images and full-frame images are separately encoded by the shared CLIP image encoder, then each stream passes through an independent Transformer self-attention module to capture temporal dynamics, and the two updated class tokens are concatenated and projected via a fully connected layer into a single 512-d visual feature token. On the text side, LLM-generated descriptions of each emotion (covering both facial expressions and context behaviors like yawning or phone use) are prepended with 8 learnable prompt tokens and encoded by the frozen CLIP text encoder. Training fine-tunes the CLIP image encoder and the temporal encoder end-to-end while keeping the text encoder fixed, using cross-entropy loss over cosine similarities. The key design insight is that jointly encoding face and context in a single stream causes the small face region to be overlooked, so separating them into parallel streams with independent temporal modeling yields a 6.81% UAR gain over the face-only baseline and a ~20% accuracy boost specifically on the distraction category.

## Results

On the RAER benchmark, CLIP-CAER achieves 68.00% UAR (unweighted average recall), outperforming the strongest baseline DFER-CLIP (61.19%) by 6.81 percentage points. The method also achieves 81.77% accuracy on the CAER basic-emotion dataset, surpassing the context-aware CAER-Net by 4.73 points. Notably, incorporating context information yields a ~20% accuracy gain specifically on the 'distraction' category, highlighting the critical role of learning-environment cues in distinguishing engagement from distraction.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIP-CAER outperforms all seven baselines on RAER. Against the 3D-CNN-based methods (3DResNets18 at 43.98%, I3D at 44.63%, CAER-Net at 45.92%), the gap is 22–24 points, reflecting the advantage of Transformer temporal modeling and CLIP pretraining over from-scratch 3D convolutions. Among Transformer-based baselines, CLIP-CAER beats M3DFEL (50.82%) and Former-DFER (51.26%) by roughly 17 points, and CLIPER (53.18%) by 14.8 points, demonstrating that context-aware encoding is the key differentiator beyond simply using a CLIP backbone. The closest competitor, DFER-CLIP (61.19%), also leverages CLIP with learnable prompts but ignores context cues; CLIP-CAER's 6.81-point margin over it isolates the contribution of the dual-stream (face + full-frame) temporal encoder and context-aware text prompts. On the CAER basic-emotion dataset, CLIP-CAER (81.77%) also surpasses CAER-Net (77.04%) by 4.73 points, confirming that the context-integration strategy generalizes beyond academic emotions. The cross-domain JuniorRAER experiment further shows that a model fine-tuned from RAER pretraining reaches 61.20% UAR, far exceeding both direct transfer (41.43%) and training from scratch on JuniorRAER alone (20.00%), indicating strong generalization to a different age group and educational setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CLIP-based facial expression recognition paradigm (DFER-CLIP, CLIPER) from basic emotions to academic emotions, and introduces a dual-stream visual encoder that separately models facial expression and environmental context before fusing them. It departs from prior academic emotion datasets (DAiSEE, EngageWild, OL-SFED), which rely on stimulus-induced emotions in online settings with face-only crops, by collecting natural learning videos across diverse real-world environments. The learnable text prompt mechanism is adapted from prompt-learning work for vision-language models, but here the prompts encode both facial descriptors and context-specific learning behaviors (e.g., phone use, finger fiddling) generated via LLM.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RAER is the first academic emotion dataset capturing diverse natural learning contexts (classrooms, libraries, labs, dormitories) with full environmental context, filling a clear gap that limits the applicability of prior stimulus-based datasets to real-world deployment. The finding that context cues are essential for distinguishing distraction from engagement (19% UAR gain on the distraction class) has practical implications for adaptive learning systems and classroom monitoring. Open questions include whether the 5-category taxonomy generalizes across cultures and age groups (partially explored via JuniorRAER), whether the approach scales to larger, more diverse cohorts, and how the method performs in real-time streaming settings rather than on pre-segmented clips.
