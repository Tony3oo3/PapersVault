## Problem

Aligning Large Vision and Language Models (LVLMs) typically depends on expensive human-curated preference data, low-quality machine-generated labels, or self-supervised signals that introduce hallucinations. No existing method enables a group of comparable LVLMs to iteratively improve each other's alignment using only unlabeled image-text prompts, without ground-truth answers or external judge models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250013/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes image-text prompt pairs sampled from Cambrian-7M (1M images per iteration) with no ground-truth answers, spanning captioning, OCR, general knowledge, counting, math, and scientific tasks. Each of the three panel members (Mistral-7B, Llama3-8B, Vicuna-7B backbones with CLIP/L-14) generates up to 15 candidate responses per prompt via sentence-level beam search, yielding 45 candidates per prompt. The pipeline outputs a curated preference dataset (~300K chosen/dispreferred pairs after rejection sampling at reward ≥ 0.85 and a 0.75 margin) that is used to fine-tune all panel members via SimPO, producing improved LVLMs ready for individual inference.

## Method

Panel-of-Peers (PoP) constructs a panel of same-capacity LVLMs that simultaneously serve as response generators and peer evaluators in an iterative self-improvement loop. For each prompt, every model produces N candidate answers, then scores all peers' answers along five axes (Helpfulness, Correctness, Verbosity, Coherence, Complexity) on a 1–5 Likert scale using a fixed evaluation prompt that relies solely on the judge's internal knowledge—no reference answer is provided. Individual scores are normalized to [0,1] and aggregated via mean voting to yield a consensus reward; rejection sampling retains only high-reward candidates, and the highest- and lowest-reward responses form preference pairs. The panel is then fine-tuned with SimPO, a reference-free preference optimization objective that introduces a target reward margin γ to separate winning from losing responses. The entire generate-evaluate-finetune cycle repeats over three iterations, with each iteration initialized from the previous iteration's checkpoints, and the authors show that this peer-feedback loop outperforms both SFT on the same data and static ground-truth fine-tuning, while also enabling cross-model knowledge transfer (e.g., teaching an OCR-weak member from OCR-strong peers).

## Results

PoP increases the average score across 15 vision-language benchmarks from 48% (iteration 0) to 57% (iteration 3), a 9-point absolute gain. In the single-iteration comparison (Table 1), PoP-iter1 achieves 68.7% on MMBench, 67.9% on SEED-Bench, and 71.2% on ScienceQA, outperforming all baselines on these metrics. After three iterations, PoP-iter3 reaches 72.5% on MMBench and 86.4% on ScienceQA, with the largest gains concentrated in knowledge and general VQA tasks. The method uses ~300K synthetic preference samples per iteration (from 1M Cambrian-7M images), requiring no ground-truth answers during self-improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PoP-iter1 outperforms all 10 baselines on MMBench (68.7 vs. best baseline SeVa at 65.6, +3.1 pts), SEED-Bench (67.9 vs. STIC at 66.2, +1.7 pts), and ScienceQA (71.2 vs. CSR at 70.7, +0.5 pts). The one clear loss is on MM-Vet, where SeVa achieves 37.2 versus PoP-iter1's 34.1 (−3.1 pts); even at iteration 3, PoP reaches only 35.0, still trailing SeVa. On POPE (hallucination), PoP ties CSR at 87.0. The iterative self-improvement (Table 2) shows PoP pulling ahead of CSR and STIC across all 15 benchmarks by iteration 3, with the largest gains in Knowledge (ScienceQA: 71.2→86.4) and Chart & OCR (ChartQA: 40.4→53.1). Notably, PoP uses 300K synthetic samples per iteration versus 6K–17K for most baselines, yet the per-sample efficiency is higher because no human annotations or foundation-model scoring are required.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LLaVA-1.5 architecture and the SimPO preference optimization objective, but departs from prior LVLM alignment work (CSR, SeVa, STIC, SIMA, VILA²) by replacing external reward signals (CLIPScore, foundation-model scoring, human annotations) with a panel of peer models that both generate and evaluate each other's outputs in an iterative loop. Conceptually, it extends the reward-ensembling idea from PoLL (panel of judges) into a self-improvement setting where the judges and the students are the same models, and it contrasts with VILA²'s multi-teacher-to-single-student distillation by using a symmetric multi-student-to-multi-student setup. The classroom peer-learning metaphor frames the method as a form of collaborative self-bootstrapping rather than top-down knowledge transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 9-point average improvement (48%→57%) across 15 benchmarks without any human-labeled preference data offers a scalable alternative to the expensive annotation pipelines that dominate LVLM alignment. The OCR knowledge-transfer experiment demonstrates that PoP can teach a model a previously absent capability from its peers, suggesting the framework could address specific model weaknesses without task-specific data collection. However, the method plateaus after three iterations, requires multiple models of similar capacity running in parallel, and incurs substantial compute (80 hours for data collection plus 10 hours per model per iteration), raising open questions about cost-effectiveness at frontier scale and whether the gains persist as base models improve. The finding that peer-generated preference data outperforms direct SFT on the same ground-truth queries (57% vs 54%) is a useful signal that the iterative evaluation loop adds value beyond simple rejection sampling.
