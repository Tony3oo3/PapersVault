## Problem

Spike cameras suffer severe degradation under typical indoor illumination (spike density ~0.008), where dark current noise overwhelms the signal and fragmented spike intervals cause 87% of pixels to have incomplete or missing data. Existing spike reconstruction methods target high-light scenarios and fail at this range, while general-purpose diffusion restoration models are not adapted to the unique proportional-plus-residual noise structure of spike imaging. The paper asks how to design a diffusion model whose forward process directly encodes the spike noise model to restore fine textures from such severely degraded inputs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a TFP-reconstructed spike image (window length w=128, spike density ~0.008) as the primary degraded input, along with three auxiliary conditions: a binary region mask image (0 where s_w≤1, 1 where s_w≥2), a per-pixel dark current map (L_d/q·ΔI), and a scalar spike density value V_d=1/(d·d_0) indicating noise intensity. The output is a full-resolution restored RGB image. Training uses 400×250 crops from DF2K (DIV2K+Flickr2K) with simulated spike degradation; real-world evaluation uses 250×400 frames from a 20 kHz spike camera in 10 indoor scenes.

## Method

The core contribution is modeling the forward diffusion process as spike image degradation, decomposing the residual y−x_0 into a proportional term (A·x_0), a deterministic residual (L_d/q·ΔI + I_d from dark current and window-scaling correction), and a non-deterministic residual (truncation + random noise). A window-scaling spike imaging method handles fragmented intervals by adjusting the effective window to the inter-spike gap, and the difference I_d between TFP and window-scaling outputs is folded into the deterministic component so the remaining residual shifts uniformly. Inverse shifting schedules assign higher exponential p-values to the proportional and deterministic terms (fast recovery at small t) and a lower p-value to the non-deterministic term (fine-grained detail at large t), aligning with the coarse-to-fine nature of diffusion sampling. The denoiser is a ResNet conditioned on the region mask, dark current map, and spike density value via additive and scalar injection, trained with LPIPS+L1 loss over T=6 diffusion steps.

## Results

On the simulated DF2K dataset, the proposed method achieves 27.16 PSNR, 0.7807 SSIM, and 0.1354 LPIPS, substantially outperforming the strongest diffusion-based baseline ResShift (21.53 PSNR, 0.5754 SSIM, 0.3169 LPIPS) by +5.63 dB in PSNR and −0.1815 in LPIPS. On real-world low-light spike data, the method scores 0.3003 CLIPIQA and 54.6128 MUSIQ, surpassing ResShift (0.2429 CLIPIQA, 41.9455 MUSIQ) and all spike-based reconstruction methods. Original spike reconstruction methods (TFP, Spk2ImgNet, RSIR, Hu et al.) fail catastrophically under the low-light conditions (spike density ≈ 0.008), with PSNR values below 6 dB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All four original spike-based methods (TFP, Spk2ImgNet, RSIR, Hu et al.) fail severely under the low-light spike density of ≈0.008, achieving PSNR below 6 dB and SSIM near zero, confirming they are designed for high-light scenarios (density 0.25–0.5). Retraining Spk2ImgNet and RSIR on the low-light dataset improves PSNR to 20.26 and 21.66 respectively, but both remain 5.5–6.9 dB below the proposed method and exhibit over-smoothing typical of regression-based approaches. ResShift, the strongest diffusion-based baseline retrained with TFP-reconstructed spike images, reaches 21.53 PSNR and 0.5754 SSIM but still lags the proposed method by 5.63 dB in PSNR and 0.2053 in SSIM, with notably higher LPIPS (0.3169 vs. 0.1354) indicating more perceptual distortion. On no-reference metrics for real data, Hu et al. achieves a slightly higher CLIPIQA (0.3063 vs. 0.3003) but the paper notes its visual quality is markedly inferior; the proposed method leads on MUSIQ by over 12 points (54.61 vs. 41.95 for ResShift). Overall, the proposed method wins on every reference-based metric and on MUSIQ across both simulated and real datasets, with the largest margins over the retrained spike-based methods and ResShift.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research lines: diffusion-based image restoration (DDPM → ResShift → R2DIFF) and spike camera image reconstruction (TFP → Spk2ImgNet → RSIR). It departs from prior diffusion restoration work by replacing the generic residual-shifting or ratio-residual decomposition with a physics-grounded spike noise model that explicitly separates the degradation into a proportional term (response non-uniformity), a deterministic residual (dark current plus a window-scaling truncation correction), and a non-deterministic residual (remaining shot/quantization/truncation noise). It also departs from prior spike reconstruction methods, which are regression-based and fail at the very low spike densities (~0.008) typical of indoor lighting, by introducing a window-scaling imaging model and a diffusion process whose forward step directly mirrors the sensor's degradation physics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The work demonstrates that embedding sensor-specific physics into the diffusion process yields substantially better restoration than generic diffusion approaches (ResShift) or retrained regression baselines, closing a practical gap for spike cameras operating at typical indoor illumination. The window-scaling correction and the three-condition design (region mask, dark current map, spike density) offer a template for physics-informed generative restoration of other neuromorphic or specialized sensors. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to dynamic scenes, higher resolutions, or event cameras, and whether the per-camera FPN calibration requirement limits real-world deployment. The evaluation is limited to a single camera model and 10 real scenes, so broader validation would strengthen the claims.
