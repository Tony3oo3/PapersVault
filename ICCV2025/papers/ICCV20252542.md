## Problem

Existing parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods (e.g., LoRA, DoRA) produce updated representations as dense combinations of modified parameters, making it difficult to interpret which parameters contribute to the adapted behavior or to selectively control the degree of adaptation. This paper asks whether the fine-tuned representation can instead be decomposed into a sparse, interpretable set of building blocks whose individual contributions can be identified and manipulated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252542/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For concept customization, the method takes a text prompt and produces a 1024×1024 generated image using the PixArt-Σ DiT backbone (28 transformer blocks). For image editing, it takes a source image plus a modified text prompt and outputs an edited image that preserves the source's core structure while applying the requested change. A distinctive aspect of the output is that it is explicitly structured as a sparse sum over learned feature atoms: the user can adjust the coefficient density ρ (e.g., 0.02 or 0.04) to control how many atoms are active, thereby dialing the strength of the fine-tuned concept against the pre-trained prior.

## Method

The paper reframes transformer fine-tuning as a sparse coding problem by inserting a sparse dictionary layer into each attention block: the adapted feature is ΔO = σ_λ(AXW_s)D, where W_s ∈ R^{C_i×M} projects the input into a coefficient space, σ_λ (soft-thresholding, ReLU, or top-k) enforces sparsity, and D ∈ R^{M×C_o} is a learned dictionary of M data-independent feature atoms. Pre-trained weights remain frozen; only W_s and D are trained, adding (C_i + C_o)M parameters per layer. A key empirical finding from a toy signal-reconstruction experiment is that tuning the atoms D (rather than the coefficients S) is what enables adaptation to a new task, while the sparse coefficients remain data-dependent and provide per-input selectivity. This sparsity yields two practical advantages over LoRA: (1) individual atoms can be identified and removed to refine image edits, and (2) the all-or-nothing activation of atoms makes the model robust to prompt perturbations, whereas LoRA's dense low-rank coefficients produce noisy, incoherent outputs under slight prompt changes.

## Results

On the DreamBooth concept customization benchmark (30 subjects, 25 validation prompts, 1024×1024 resolution), the proposed sparse fine-tuning method with ρ=0.04 achieves the best ImageReward score of 0.431, nearly doubling LoRA (0.220) and DoRA (0.142). It also attains the highest text-to-image alignment (0.235 at ρ=0.02, 0.231 at ρ=0.04) and diversity (5.205 at ρ=0.02) among all methods, while maintaining competitive fidelity (0.713 at ρ=0.04, matching DoRA's 0.712). The method trains approximately 17M parameters, compared to ~4M for LoRA/DoRA and ~37M for OFT, on a 616M-parameter PixArt-Σ DiT backbone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LoRA (r=16), the proposed method wins on alignment (+0.017 at ρ=0.02), diversity (+0.498 at ρ=0.02), and ImageReward (+0.211 at ρ=0.04), while matching fidelity (0.713 vs 0.711 at ρ=0.04). Against DoRA (r=16), the method leads on alignment, diversity, and ImageReward by similar margins, with fidelity essentially tied (0.713 vs 0.712). Against OFT (r=4), the method dominates across all four metrics, with the largest gap in diversity (+1.279) and ImageReward (+0.294). The paper additionally demonstrates a qualitative stability advantage: under a slightly modified prompt, LoRA produces completely disrupted outputs while the sparse method remains coherent, attributed to the binary-like activation of atoms versus LoRA's noisy low-rank coefficients.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the sparse coding / dictionary learning framework from signal processing to reframe transformer fine-tuning, positioning itself as a conceptual alternative to low-rank PEFT methods like LoRA and DoRA. While LoRA adapts both attention coefficients and value projections via low-rank matrices, this work separates the two roles explicitly: sparse coefficients (data-dependent) select from a fixed dictionary of feature atoms (data-independent), drawing on the ISTA/FISTA literature for sparsity enforcement. It also connects to the broader representation-tuning line of work that modifies outputs rather than weights, but distinguishes itself by studying the sparsity structure of the updated representation and casting it as a dictionary learning problem rather than a direct edit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The sparse-atom decomposition offers a degree of interpretability and controllability that dense PEFT methods lack: individual atoms can be inspected, removed, or selectively activated to edit generated images or adjust concept fidelity, and the stability analysis (Figure 4) suggests sparse coefficients are more robust to prompt perturbation than low-rank ones. However, validation is confined to a single base architecture (PixArt-Σ / DiT) with single-image fine-tuning, and the quantitative gains over LoRA are modest (e.g., alignment 0.235 vs. 0.218), while computational cost is slightly higher. Open questions include whether the sparsity benefits and interpretability advantages persist at larger model scales, with multi-concept or multi-image training, and whether the atom-selection mechanism can be extended to other generative architectures beyond DiT.
