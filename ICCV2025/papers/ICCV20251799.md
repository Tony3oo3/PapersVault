## Problem

Sliding window inference for large biological images commonly produces tiling artifacts (discontinuous predictions at tile borders and background hallucinations) even when sufficient halo is applied to account for receptive field edge effects. The root cause has not been previously identified: the paper shows that tile-wise feature normalization (e.g., InstanceNorm) invalidates the limited-receptive-field assumption by making each pixel's prediction depend on the entire tile's content, while the alternative (BatchNorm) introduces a severe train/eval performance gap due to unstable running statistics at small batch sizes typical of volumetric segmentation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251799/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes large 3D microscopy volumes (e.g., FIB/SEM organoids at ~1350×1506×1647 px, confocal embryo/plant volumes at ~500–1000 px per dimension) that exceed GPU memory, splitting them into tiles of 128–192 px per dimension for inference. Each tile is independently passed through the network, and predictions are stitched by discarding a halo region (sized to the theoretical receptive field) at tile borders, producing a full-volume semantic segmentation mask with three classes (foreground, boundaries, background/matrix). The same pipeline applies to 2D images, and instance segmentation ground truth is converted to semantic labels for evaluation.

## Method

The paper identifies that InstanceNorm's tile-wise statistics make the effective receptive field span the entire tile, rendering halo-based stitching fundamentally unable to produce seamless predictions, while BatchNorm's switch from batch statistics (training) to running-average statistics (inference) causes a train/eval disparity of up to 0.84 Dice at the small batch sizes (2–3 tiles) used for volumetric data. To diagnose these issues, two metrics are proposed: tile mismatch (median 1−Dice between overlapping tile predictions in the valid region) and train/eval disparity (1−Dice between predictions of the same tile in model.train() vs. model.eval() mode). The proposed solution is BatchRenorm, which uses the same global running-average statistics in both training and inference, thereby eliminating both tiling artifacts and the train/eval gap. This is validated on 3D U-Net and ViT-based UNETR architectures across three microscopy datasets (organoids, Arabidopsis plants, mouse embryos), including transfer-learning experiments, showing BatchRenorm matches or exceeds InstanceNorm accuracy while producing perfectly seamless stitching.

## Results

On the U-Net architecture, BatchRenorm achieves Dice scores of 0.73 (organoids boundaries), 0.75 (plants boundaries), and 0.58 (embryo boundaries) in eval mode, matching or exceeding InstanceNorm (0.75, 0.75, 0.53) while producing zero tile mismatch and zero train/eval disparity. BatchNorm eliminates tiling artifacts but suffers severe train/eval disparity (up to 0.84 on organoids), dropping boundary Dice from 0.75 (train) to 0.49 (eval). InstanceNorm achieves competitive Dice but introduces tile mismatch of 0.05–0.20 depending on dataset, with artifacts worsening in transfer settings. On UNETR, BatchRenorm similarly achieves the best boundary Dice (0.72 organoids, 0.66 plants, 0.66 embryo) with zero tile mismatch, outperforming BatchNorm (0.39, 0.64, 0.67) and matching InstanceNorm (0.60, 0.69, 0.56).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to BatchNorm, BatchRenorm eliminates the train/eval disparity (reducing it from 0.17–0.84 to 0) while maintaining zero tile mismatch, and recovers boundary Dice by 0.24 on organoids (0.73 vs 0.49) and 0.03 on embryo (0.58 vs 0.55). Compared to InstanceNorm, BatchRenorm matches or exceeds boundary Dice on all three datasets (0.73 vs 0.75 organoids, 0.75 vs 0.75 plants, 0.58 vs 0.53 embryo) while completely eliminating tiling artifacts (tile mismatch drops from 0.05–0.20 to 0). The Identity (no normalization) baseline shows that removing normalization entirely is not a viable alternative, as it degrades plants boundary Dice to 0.26 and embryo to 0.44. On the UNETR architecture, BatchRenorm similarly outperforms BatchNorm on organoids boundaries (0.72 vs 0.39) and matrix (0.87 vs 0.03), while matching InstanceNorm on plants boundaries (0.66 vs 0.69) with zero tile mismatch versus 0.04.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sliding window inference literature (Majurski & Bajcsy 2021; Huang et al. 2018; Rumberger et al. 2021), which established that receptive field and zero-padding edge effects cause tiling artifacts, by identifying feature normalization layers as an additional and previously unacknowledged root cause. It repurposes BatchRenorm (Ioffe 2017), originally designed to reduce minibatch dependence in natural image classification, to the domain of large-volume biological segmentation where small batch sizes and high data variability make the problem far more acute. The work also positions itself against widely adopted pipelines like nnU-Net and Cellpose, which apply heuristic postprocessing (Gaussian-weighted averaging, intensity thresholding) to mask rather than eliminate these artifacts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that InstanceNorm makes the effective receptive field span the entire tile—rendering any halo size insufficient for seamless stitching—challenges a core assumption in the sliding window inference community and provides a concrete diagnostic (tile mismatch, train/eval disparity metrics) for practitioners to check their own pipelines. The practical recommendation to adopt BatchRenorm with a two-stage training protocol is immediately actionable for the many microscopy and medical imaging groups relying on nnU-Net-style pipelines. Open questions include whether the two-stage switch timing generalizes to deeper or transformer-heavy architectures, whether the metrics transfer cleanly to non-Dice tasks (e.g., detection, classification), and whether the results hold for 2D-only or multi-channel modalities beyond the three volumetric datasets tested.
