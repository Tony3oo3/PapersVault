## Problem

LiDAR scene flow methods rely on instance-level rigid-motion assumption—segmenting objects, clustering, and matching instance pairs across frames—but this breaks down for sparse radar point clouds where objects lack shape, appear deformed between frames, and nearby objects share only a few reflection points. The paper addresses this by shifting the rigid-motion assumption from the instance level to the traffic level, capturing motion consistency in the spatial context occupied by road users rather than in individual object correspondences.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251484/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The scene flow branch takes two consecutive radar point clouds P and Q, each with 5D per-point features (x, y, z, relative radial velocity, radar cross-section), plus a 4×4 ego-motion transformation from GPS/IMU for coordinate alignment. The object detection branch consumes point cloud Q and produces a bird's-eye-view feature map that feeds traffic context into the scene flow branch. The final output is a per-point 3D displacement vector for every point in P; the TVF operates on a coarse 2 m × 2 m grid (40×40 on VOD, 70×40 on the proprietary dataset) to maintain high-level scene understanding without resolving point-level detail.

## Method

TARS is a 4-level coarse-to-fine hierarchical network that, at each level, combines two complementary motion cues: (1) point-level matching via a double attention mechanism (cross-attention between a point and its K nearest neighbors in Q, followed by self-attention among P neighbors), and (2) traffic-level consistency via a Traffic Vector Field (TVF). The TVF encoder builds a holistic traffic representation by (a) updating a GRU hidden state with the OD feature map (scene update), (b) painting the previous level's coarse flow onto the grid via point-to-grid self-attention (flow painting), and (c) applying stacked axial attention blocks for global receptive field. The TVF decoder then performs grid-to-point cross-attention restricted to a local neighborhood around each point to extract rigid-motion cues from the surrounding traffic context. Point-level and traffic-level flow embeddings are concatenated and refined by another self-attention layer to predict the level's flow. A PointGRU temporal module captures low-level point dependencies across mini-clips of T frames. Training is weakly supervised: self-supervised losses (soft Chamfer, spatial smoothness, radial displacement) plus cross-modal pseudo-labels (foreground flow from LiDAR multi-object tracking, background flow from ego-motion) replace expensive per-point scene flow annotations.

## Results

On the View-of-Delft (VOD) dataset, TARS-ego reduces overall EPE from 0.130 m (CMFlow, previous SOTA) to 0.092 m, improving AccS by 16.2 pp (22.8% → 39.0%) and AccR by 15.2 pp (53.9% → 69.1%). With ego-motion compensation (TARS-superego), EPE drops further to 0.048 m and AccS reaches 76.6%. On the proprietary high-resolution radar dataset, TARS reduces MEPE from 0.170 m (HALFlow) to 0.069 m and improves AccS by 18.9 pp (50.9% → 69.8%) and AccR by 23.0 pp (63.8% → 86.8%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

TARS outperforms all eight baselines on the VOD dataset across every metric. Against the previous radar-specific SOTA, CMFlow [6], TARS-ego cuts EPE by 29% (0.130 → 0.092 m) and nearly doubles AccS (22.8% → 39.0%). LiDAR-oriented methods such as DeFlow [33] (EPE 0.217 m) and Flow4D-2frame [17] (EPE 0.255 m) perform worse than radar-specific baselines because their fully-voxel representations are ill-suited to sparse radar point clouds. On the proprietary dataset, TARS reduces MEPE by 59% relative to HALFlow [27] (0.170 → 0.069 m) and improves AccR by 23 pp (63.8% → 86.8%). PointPWC-Net [32] without PointGRU achieves MEPE of 0.453 m, while adding PointGRU alone drops it to 0.213 m, showing that temporal modeling is critical but still far from TARS's 0.069 m. The ego-motion compensation setup (TARS-superego) further halves EPE to 0.048 m, demonstrating that a simple 4×4 odometry matrix is a highly effective booster when available.

## Relation to Prior Work

TARS extends the hierarchical coarse-to-fine scene flow architecture of HALFlow and PointPWC-Net to the radar domain, replacing their MLP-based cost volumes with a double attention mechanism better suited to sparse point clouds. Its key conceptual departure is from instance-level rigid-motion methods (WsRSF, ICP-Flow, RigidFlow) that work for dense LiDAR but fail on sparse radar; instead, TARS elevates the rigidity assumption to the traffic level by constructing a Traffic Vector Field from an object detector's feature map, a strategy not present in prior radar scene flow work (RaFlow, CMFlow) or joint detection-flow pipelines (PillarFlowNet, Ercelik et al.) that only share backbone features. The training recipe builds directly on RaFlow's self-supervised losses and CMFlow's cross-modal supervision, adding a background loss for static points.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Radar is an order of magnitude cheaper and more weather-robust than LiDAR, so substantially improving radar scene flow has direct practical value for cost-sensitive autonomous driving deployments. The traffic-level framing—capturing rigid motion in the space occupied by objects rather than at the instance level—offers a transferable conceptual tool for other sparse-sensor settings where per-object correspondences are unreliable. Limitations include reliance on GPS/IMU ego-motion compensation, a non-public proprietary dataset for the headline 23% improvement, and treating the object detection branch as a fixed black box; open questions include whether the TVF concept generalizes to even sparser single-radar setups or to multi-sensor fusion without LiDAR supervision.
