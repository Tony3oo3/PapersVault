## Problem

State-of-the-art video generation models cannot directly process research papers as input due to limited context windows, rigid duration constraints, and homogeneous visual styles trained on real-world video datasets. This makes them unsuitable for generating video abstracts that must faithfully represent domain-specific knowledge (e.g., mathematical proofs, molecular structures) across diverse academic disciplines in an accessible, well-organized format.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252085/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system takes a complete research paper PDF (containing text, equations, figures, and tables) as input and produces a multi-segment video abstract where each segment can have a distinct visual style from a set of six: talking heads, general, static concept, molecular visualization, slides, and mathematics. Each segment includes synchronized audio narration, and the final output is a concatenation of H segments assembled into a coherent video. The paper is consumed as an encoded PDF directly by the LMM (Gemini-2.0-flash) or processed through an assistant pipeline (GPT-4o), with no GPU required for the planning stages.

## Method

Preacher employs a top-down/bottom-up agentic architecture inspired by U-Net, with six specialized agents: a Summary Agent decomposes the paper into raw scenes, a Scene Planning Agent enriches them into structured 'key scenes' (JSON files specifying duration, style, audio content, video prompts, and source references) using a Progressive Chain of Thought (P-CoT) that plans components sequentially from simple to complex with iterative reflection by a Text Reflection Agent. The bottom-up phase selects style-appropriate generation tools—Python visualization libraries (manim for math, python-pptx for slides, PyMOL for molecules), text-to-video models (Luma, Wan-2.1), or talking-head generators (Tavus)—to synthesize each segment, with a Video Reflection Agent evaluating and triggering regeneration if accuracy, professionalism, or alignment standards are not met. This structured, multi-agent pipeline with progressive planning and dual reflection mechanisms addresses both the context-length limitations of LMMs and the stylistic rigidity of monolithic video generation models.

## Results

On the 40-paper benchmark spanning five research fields, Preacher achieves the best scores on 6 of 10 evaluation metrics in Table 1, including GPT-evaluated Accuracy (4.50 vs. 4.33 for Sora), GPT Professionalism (4.63 vs. 4.45), GPT Alignment (4.35 vs. 4.30), and Human-evaluated Accuracy (4.80 vs. 3.88 for Sora). For key scene planning (Table 2), Preacher leads on Accuracy (4.70), Compatibility (4.38), and Alignment (4.50) out of four metrics, trailing only DeepSeek-R1 on Professionalism (4.63 vs. 4.68). Preacher trades off slightly on Aesthetic Quality (4.17 GPT / 4.25 Human vs. Sora's 4.18 / 4.30) and CLIP similarity (0.26 vs. 0.31), which the authors attribute to its use of domain-specific styles (math animations, slides, molecular visualization) that prioritize scholarly accuracy over visual polish.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Preacher is compared against four end-to-end baselines (OpenAI o3-mini paired with StreamingT2V, Wan2.1-14B, Kling 1.6, or Sora) and four standalone LMMs for key scene planning (GPT-4o, o3-mini, Gemini-2.0-flash, DeepSeek-R1). Against the video-generation baselines, Preacher wins decisively on content-accuracy metrics: Human Accuracy is 4.80 vs. 3.88 (Sora), 3.63 (Wan2.1), 3.40 (Kling), and 3.13 (StreamingT2V), and GPT Alignment is 4.35 vs. 4.30 (Sora), 4.33 (Wan2.1), 4.05 (Kling), and 3.60 (StreamingT2V). The closest competitor is Sora, which edges Preacher on GPT Aesthetic (4.18 vs. 4.17), Human Aesthetic (4.30 vs. 4.25), and CLIP similarity (0.31 vs. 0.26), reflecting Sora's stronger general visual quality. For key scene planning, Preacher outperforms GPT-4o, o3-mini, and Gemini-2.0-flash on all four metrics (Accuracy, Professionalism, Compatibility, Alignment), and beats DeepSeek-R1 on Accuracy (4.70 vs. 4.45), Compatibility (4.38 vs. 3.70), and Alignment (4.50 vs. 4.05), while DeepSeek-R1 narrowly leads on Professionalism (4.68 vs. 4.63). The authors note that chain-of-thought reasoning in DeepSeek-R1 improves professionalism but produces overly complex scene plans that reduce compatibility with downstream generative models.

## Relation to Prior Work

Preacher carves new ground at the intersection of three established research threads—automatic paper summarization with LMMs, conditional video generation (Sora, Kling, Wan-2.1, StreamingT2V, VideoTetris), and multi-agent collaboration (MetaGPT, DreamFactory, Mora, SPAgent)—but no prior work has combined them into an end-to-end paper-to-video pipeline. Rather than training a new generative model, it repurposes existing video generation APIs and Python visualization libraries (manim, python-pptx, Pymol) as tools within an agentic orchestration, with a U-Net-inspired top-down/bottom-up decomposition and a Progressive Chain of Thought (P-CoT) for iterative scene planning. The key conceptual departure from prior agentic video systems (DreamFactory, Mora, SPAgent) is the introduction of 'key scenes' as structured intermediate representations that bridge textual and visual modalities, plus the use of domain-specific Python code generation for specialized content that diffusion models cannot handle.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first system to autonomously convert a research paper into a multi-style video abstract, addressing a practical gap given that video abstracts are associated with 15% more citations. The main open questions are scalability (over an hour of processing per paper, heavy API/token costs), the professionalism-aesthetics trade-off (domain-specific styles like manim animations score lower on CLIP and aesthetic metrics), and the limitation that AI/ML papers are confined to 'slides' and 'talking heads' due to their abstract nature. Future work on high-fidelity text-to-animation models and more diverse generation tools could close these gaps, and the P-CoT planning framework may generalize to other long-context cross-modal tasks.
