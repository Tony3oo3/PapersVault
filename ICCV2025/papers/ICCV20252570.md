## Problem

Existing 3D surface reconstruction methods based on Gaussian splatting (e.g., 2DGS, SuGaR) optimize each scene independently via gradient descent, requiring over 30 minutes for large indoor scenes and producing artifacts (curved walls, floating geometry) in textureless or under-observed regions where SfM fails to generate points. The paper addresses both the speed bottleneck and the geometric quality gap in these challenging areas.

## Input / Output

The method takes posed multi-view DSLR images (360×540 for training, 720×1080 for evaluation) and their corresponding SfM point cloud as input. It produces a 3D mesh surface extracted via TSDF fusion from depth maps rendered by the converged 2D Gaussian primitives. Internally, the scene is discretized into a sparse 3D voxel grid at 4 cm resolution with 2 Gaussians predicted per voxel, and the iterative optimization runs for 5 timesteps with gradients accumulated from 100 training views per step.

## Method

QuickSplat replaces per-scene gradient descent with three learned sparse 3D CNN networks operating in a meta-learning loop. An SGNN-inspired initializer densifies the sparse SfM point cloud into a continuous Gaussian surface by predicting voxel features and decoding them into 2DGS attributes (position, scale, rotation, opacity, color) via a decoder MLP. A densification-optimization loop then iterates T=5 times: rendering gradients are computed across all training views, a densifier network (constrained to grow Gaussians only in neighboring voxels) predicts new primitives via importance sampling weighted by occupancy-as-opacity, and a sparse 3D UNet optimizer predicts bounded update vectors for all existing Gaussians. The networks are jointly trained end-to-end on 902 ScanNet++ scenes with a composite loss combining rendering, depth, occupancy, normal alignment, and distortion terms, totaling ~68M parameters.

## Results

On ScanNet++, QuickSplat (w/ opt) achieves the best Chamfer distance (0.1347) and highest Acc(2cm) (57.83%) among all methods, while matching MonoSDF's absolute depth error (0.0578 vs. 0.0569). The headline speedup is dramatic: QuickSplat completes reconstruction in 124s (w/ opt) or just 26s (w/o opt), compared to 973s–3130s for other baselines and >10h for MonoSDF — an 8× speedup over the next fastest method. Even without post-training refinement, QuickSplat (w/o opt) reduces absolute depth error by 48% relative to 2DGS while running 69× faster.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QuickSplat (w/ opt) outperforms all optimization-based baselines (SuGaR, 2DGS, PGSR, GS2Mesh) on every geometric metric while running 8–120× faster. Against MonoSDF, the closest competitor in accuracy, QuickSplat achieves a slightly lower absolute depth error (0.0578 vs. 0.0569) but a better Chamfer distance (0.1347 vs. 0.1450) and higher Acc(2cm) (57.83% vs. 57.74%), all while completing in 124s versus over 10 hours. The paper attributes its advantage over SuGaR, 2DGS, and PGSR to learned geometry priors that handle textureless regions (e.g., white walls) where photometric-only optimization produces curved surfaces and floating artifacts. Compared to GS2Mesh, which also uses learned priors (via a stereo depth estimator), QuickSplat avoids the domain gap between rendered and real images, yielding fewer high-frequency artifacts. The w/o opt variant (26s) already surpasses GS2Mesh on absolute error (0.0732 vs. 0.1212) and Chamfer (0.1461 vs. 0.2012) while being 37× faster.

## Relation to Prior Work

QuickSplat extends the meta-learning paradigm for 3D reconstruction introduced by G3R, which learned a single optimizer network to iteratively refine 3DGS parameters initialized from LiDAR. Here, the authors generalize this to a multi-network pipeline (initializer, densifier, optimizer) operating on 2DGS surface primitives initialized from SfM points, drawing architectural inspiration from SGNN's sparse encoder-decoder for scene completion. Unlike feed-forward Gaussian prediction methods (PixelSplat, MVSplat) that target sparse-view novel view synthesis, QuickSplat focuses on large-scale indoor surface geometry and explicitly learns densification as a differentiable, gradient-guided process rather than relying on heuristic adaptive density control. The method also departs from per-scene optimization baselines (2DGS, SuGaR, PGSR) by replacing thousands of gradient descent iterations with a small number of learned update steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 8× runtime reduction (from ~30 min to ~26 s without post-training) while simultaneously improving depth accuracy makes large-scale indoor surface reconstruction substantially more practical for robotics, AR/VR, and simulation pipelines. The learned densification mechanism removes hand-crafted heuristics for Gaussian growth, offering a cleaner and more generalizable optimization framework. However, the method remains limited to static indoor scenes, struggles with mirror reflections, and is not yet real-time; integration with SLAM systems is identified as a natural next step. An open question is whether the learned priors generalize beyond the ScanNet++ training distribution to outdoor or highly cluttered environments.
