## Problem

Visualization-based invariance testing is more informative than global accuracy scores (models with identical worst-case accuracy can exhibit different visual patterns), but background invariance testing lacks a systematic sampling strategy: random sampling produces inconsistent visual patterns across runs, while nearest-neighbor sampling yields consistent but unrepresentative test suites. The paper addresses how to select background scenes that are both semantically diverse and produce reliable, consistent human judgments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251252/cropped_page1_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a target image with a segmented foreground object, a repository of 20,000 background scenes (BG-20k), and a supervised image classifier trained on IN9 (9-class ImageNet). Testing images are synthesized via simple mask-based background replacement (Eq. 1) with Laplacian pyramid blending, selecting N=32 backgrounds per target. The output is a variance matrix produced by applying radial basis functions to a point cloud of pairwise model-response differences indexed by semantic distances (measured via an ensemble of ViT, CLIP, ResNet, and VGG), which annotators or a random forest classifier then label as invariant, borderline, or not invariant.

## Method

The core contribution is an association ontology constructed offline via Apriori and FP-Growth mining over keyword co-occurrence statistics from a pre-trained scene understanding model, where edge weights are confidence values of association rules. At test time, the scene understanding model detects keywords in the target image, and the ontology iteratively expands them (level 1, level 2, …) to retrieve background scenes at varying semantic distances, balancing proximity to the original scene with coverage of related but distinct contexts. Background candidates are filtered by keyword match and validated for realness using DreamSim scores. Testing results are visualized as variance matrices via RBF transformation of point clouds, and the approach is shown to achieve the best F1 score (0.650) between neuron coverage (diversity/recall) and Fleiss kappa (consistency/precision) compared to random, nearest-neighbor, interval, and CLIP-based sampling baselines.

## Results

The proposed association-ontology-based keyword sampling achieves the best F1 score of 0.650, balancing neuron coverage (recall) of 0.652 with Fleiss' inter-rater reliability (precision) of 0.649. This outperforms CLIP-based keyword sampling (F1=0.615), distance-interval sampling (F1=0.591), random sampling (F1=0.491), and nearest-top-k sampling (F1=0.232). For full automation, a random forest classifier trained on hand-crafted features from variance matrices achieves 79.7±7.5% accuracy with an IRR score of 0.649±0.091, comparable to the three human annotators.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five sampling strategies for selecting background scenes in invariance testing. Random sampling achieves the highest neuron coverage (0.681) but the lowest Fleiss' reliability (0.384), yielding the worst F1 among diverse methods (0.491). Nearest-top-k distance sampling achieves the highest consistency (0.906) but catastrophically low diversity (0.133), giving the worst overall F1 (0.232). The proposed ontology-based keyword sampling (F1=0.650) outperforms CLIP-based keyword sampling (F1=0.615) by 0.035, primarily because the association ontology expands the keyword set beyond direct matches, increasing diversity (0.652 vs. 0.591 recall) while maintaining comparable reliability (0.649 vs. 0.640). It also beats distance-interval sampling (F1=0.591) by 0.059, as interval-based sampling produces less consistent human judgments despite similar coverage. The key finding is that no single axis (diversity or consistency) is sufficient; the ontology method uniquely balances both, making it the most suitable for visualization-based background invariance testing.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visualization-based invariance testing framework of Liao et al. [25] (ML4ML) from simple transformations (rotation, translation) to the substantially harder problem of background invariance, where the transformation space is vast and non-uniform. It departs from prior background invariance studies (Xiao et al. [40], Rosenfeld et al. [33], Lauer & Cornelissen [20]) that relied on random background replacement or hand-crafted consistent/inconsistent pairs, by introducing an association ontology (built via Apriori/FP-Growth) to systematically select background scenes at controlled semantic distances. The visualization methodology (variance matrices via RBF) is inherited from [12, 25], while the ontology-based sampling and the diversity-consistency trade-off analysis are the paper's core methodological contributions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper makes a practical case that global accuracy scores are insufficient for background invariance assessment and that systematic test-suite selection is essential for reliable human or automated judgment. However, the evaluation is limited to 250 models on a 9-class dataset (IN9) with only three annotators, and the ontology must be rebuilt per dataset, raising scalability questions for larger or open-vocabulary settings. Open questions include whether the association-ontology approach generalizes to multi-object scenes, whether the ~80% automation accuracy is sufficient for deployment, and whether the framework extends to other invariance types beyond background.
