## Problem

State-of-the-art keypoint detectors and descriptors rely on training signals that require depth maps, relative poses, or pixel-wise correspondences derived from pre-generated 3D models or artificial augmentations, severely limiting the pool of usable training data and reducing robustness to real-world appearance changes. RIPE asks whether a keypoint extractor can be trained using only a binary label indicating whether two images depict the same scene, thereby unlocking diverse real-world datasets (e.g., day-night driving, place recognition) that lack any geometric annotations.

## Input / Output

During training, the network consumes pairs of 560×560 RGB images accompanied by a single binary label (same scene / different scene). For each image, it outputs a detection heatmap partitioned into 8×8-pixel cells from which exactly one keypoint location is sampled per cell, along with a 256-dimensional descriptor per keypoint. At inference, the top-K keypoints are selected from the heatmap with 3×3 non-maximum suppression, each paired with its hyper-column descriptor for downstream matching.

## Method

RIPE uses a VGG-19-based hourglass network (encoder + depthwise-convolutional decoder) where keypoint detection is formulated as a REINFORCE policy-gradient problem: the heatmap logits define a categorical distribution over keypoint positions within each cell, and a sigmoid gate provides a per-cell acceptance probability. Descriptors are extracted via hyper-column features—bilinearly interpolated feature vectors from all intermediate encoder scales are concatenated (960-d for VGG-19) and projected to 256-d by a 1×1 convolution. The reward signal is derived entirely from the epipolar constraint: mutual nearest-neighbor matches between the two images' descriptors are filtered by a RANSAC-estimated fundamental matrix (8-point algorithm), and the count of surviving inliers, signed by the pair label, constitutes the reward. An auxiliary margin-based descriptor loss further pulls validated matches together for positive pairs and pushes false matches apart for negative pairs, balancing the total loss with a small regularization term that discourages low-probability keypoints.

## Results

On MegaDepth 1500 (relative pose estimation), RIPE achieves 55.11 / 68.34 / 78.03 AUC at 5°/10°/20°, ranking close second to ALIKED (56.71 / 69.86 / 79.54) among sparse methods while requiring only binary pair labels. On HPatches (homography estimation), RIPE scores 37.93 / 58.93 / 69.20 AUC at 1px/3px/5px, competitive with DeDoDe (39.88 / 60.59 / 70.36) and SiLK (39.05 / 57.96 / 67.64). On Aachen Day-Night, adding 20% Tokyo 24/7 training data boosts RIPE's night-query localization from 52.9 to 60.2 AUC at 0.25m/2°, substantially outperforming DeDoDe (47.1) and SIFT (30.9) under night conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On MegaDepth 1500, RIPE (55.11 AUC@5°) trails ALIKED (56.71) by 1.6 points but outperforms DeDoDe (55.02), DISK (51.83), SuperPoint (49.5), SiLK (29.83), and SIFT (37.29), all while using only binary pair labels rather than pose, depth, or homography supervision. On HPatches, RIPE (37.93 AUC@1px) is slightly behind DeDoDe (39.88) and SiLK (39.05) but clearly surpasses ALIKED (31.95), DISK (37.54), SuperPoint (36.61), and SIFT (29.98). On Aachen Day-Night night queries, RIPE with Tokyo 24/7 data (60.2 / 72.8 / 83.8 AUC at 0.25m/2°, 0.5m/5°, 5m/10°) substantially outperforms DeDoDe (47.1 / 56.5 / 64.4) and SIFT (30.9 / 38.2 / 46.1), though ALIKED (73.3 / 88.0 / 96.9) remains strongest due to its additional Aachen-specific training data. The key differentiator is that RIPE is the only sparse method in the comparison that does not require depth maps, relative poses, or artificial homography augmentations during training, yet achieves near-SOTA performance. Dense methods (RoMa, Efficient LoFTR) still lead on MegaDepth 1500 by 9–13 AUC@5° points, but operate at substantially higher computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

RIPE extends the RL-based keypoint detection line initiated by DISK [38] and Reinforced Feature Points [4], which both still required depth or pose information to compute rewards. The key departure is replacing geometric ground truth with the epipolar constraint as a proxy for match correctness, requiring only binary same-scene labels. The hyper-column description mechanism repurposes Hariharan et al.'s [14] multi-scale feature concatenation (previously used for segmentation) into the keypoint descriptor pipeline, while the overall hourglass encoder-decoder architecture follows SuperPoint [6]. Conceptually, RIPE is closest to Rocco et al.'s [28] weakly-supervised semantic alignment but differs in its use of RL, explicit negative pairs, and enforcement of valid epipolar geometry via fundamental matrix estimation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By reducing the supervision requirement to a binary pair label, RIPE dramatically expands the pool of usable training data (e.g., autonomous driving sequences, place recognition datasets) without needing SfM reconstruction or manual annotation, which is practically significant for long-term localization in changing conditions. The Tokyo 24/7 ablation (+7.3% on night 0.25m/2°) demonstrates that this data flexibility translates into measurable robustness gains. Limitations include reliance on a single VGG-19 backbone, slower inference than ALIKED (0.47s vs 0.08s), and validation on a limited set of benchmarks. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to truly cross-domain pairs (e.g., synthetic-to-real) and whether the epipolar-based reward can be further refined to handle degenerate geometries.
