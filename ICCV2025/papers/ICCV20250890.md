## Problem

Existing adversarial camouflage methods optimize 3D textures greedily from random viewpoints, causing conflicting gradient updates that degrade performance across angles. Additionally, conventional rendering pipelines ignore dynamic illumination changes, and inconsistent sampling density at varying distances produces non-uniform texture updates that break smoothness. These issues collectively limit the robustness of physical camouflage attacks in real-world conditions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D vehicle mesh (Audi E-Tron) with an initial UV texture, background scene images and light source directions from the CARLA simulator, and a set of viewpoint parameters (elevation 0°–60°, full 360° azimuth, distances 5–15 m). It produces an optimized adversarial UV texture that is rendered via ModernGL, composited with the background using a segmentation mask, and fed to a frozen object detector. For physical evaluation, the final texture is printed and wrapped onto 1:24-scale model vehicles.

## Method

GRAC replaces greedy per-viewpoint texture updates with a gradient reweighting scheme: at each iteration, a mini-batch of gradients is computed from multiple viewpoints, and learnable per-viewpoint weights are optimized (via an inner loop minimizing the attack loss plus an L2 regularization on the weights) to balance conflicting directions before a single texture update. A physically-based illumination module modulates the rendered foreground by the dot product of each vertex normal with primary and secondary (backlight) light directions, eliminating the need for expensive neural-network lighting models. To counter non-uniform sampling density at longer distances, the UV texture is passed through an average-pooling–upsampling pair before projection, so that adjacent texels are updated jointly, and a total-variation loss further enforces smoothness. The overall objective minimizes the sum of objectness and classification scores for detection boxes with IoU > 0.5, weighted by an adversarial loss term and a TV regularization term.

## Results

On the primary multi-viewpoint evaluation (Table 1, YOLOv3 white-box, AP@0.5 averaged over 360° azimuth), GRAC achieves a mean AP@0.5 of 2.94% across six elevation angles (0°–60°), compared to 16.31% for RAUCA, 20.25% for ACTIVE, 45.78% for FCA, 47.46% for DTA, and 80.99% for DAS (lower AP@0.5 indicates stronger evasion). Across distances of 5–15 m (Table 2), GRAC maintains a mean AP@0.5 of 5.36%, roughly 3× lower than the next-best RAUCA (15.10%). Under five illumination/weather conditions (Table 3), GRAC achieves a mean AP@0.5 of 4.00% versus 16.22% for RAUCA and 81.00% for DAS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRAC outperforms all five baselines across every evaluation axis. Against the closest competitor RAUCA [46], GRAC reduces mean AP@0.5 from 16.31% to 2.94% (a 13.37-point improvement) on the multi-viewpoint benchmark, and from 15.10% to 5.36% on the distance benchmark. Against ACTIVE [35], the gap is even larger: 20.25% → 2.94% (viewpoints) and 17.19% → 5.36% (distance). The older UV-texture methods FCA [38] and DTA [34] show substantially weaker evasion (45.78% and 47.46% mean AP@0.5), while DAS [39]—which targets dual attention suppression rather than pure detection evasion—barely reduces detection (80.99%). In the transferability study (Table 4), GRAC also leads on 5 of 6 black-box detectors (YOLOX, FrRCN, MkRCN, DETR, PVT), with the largest margin over RAUCA on FrRCN (17.35% vs. 23.57%) and DETR (11.71% vs. 18.05%). On the two advanced large models (YOLO-World, LLaVA-Grounding), GRAC still degrades detection but with smaller absolute drops, indicating these models are inherently more robust to camouflage.

## Relation to Prior Work

GRAC sits within the differentiable-rendering-based adversarial camouflage lineage (FCA, DAS, DTA, ACTIVE, RAUCA), which all optimize 3D UV textures via backpropagation through a renderable pipeline. It departs from this family in three specific ways: (1) it identifies and formally addresses the gradient-conflict problem that arises when successive optimization steps target different viewpoints, introducing learnable per-viewpoint weights to rebalance accumulated gradients; (2) it replaces the neural-network-based lighting post-processing used by RAUCA with a closed-form Lambertian-style illumination model based on vertex normals and light direction; and (3) it diagnoses a previously unacknowledged issue—non-uniform sampling density across distances—and patches it with a simple average-pooling-and-upsample step on the UV texture before projection. No single prior method is directly extended; rather, the paper recombines the standard differentiable-rendering loop with these three corrective mechanisms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Physical adversarial camouflage against autonomous-driving detectors is a concrete safety threat, and GRAC's large AP@0.5 reductions (e.g., 2.94% vs. 16.31% for RAUCA in the white-box YOLOv3 setting) demonstrate that current detectors remain highly vulnerable even under multi-view, multi-illumination conditions. The gradient-reweighting mechanism is a general-purpose tool for any multi-view 3D texture optimization and could transfer to other differentiable-rendering tasks beyond adversarial attacks. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond a single vehicle model (only Audi E-Tron is tested), whether the simple two-source Lambertian model suffices for complex urban lighting (shadows, reflections, multi-bounce), and how the camouflage fares against detectors explicitly trained with adversarial robustness. The paper also notes that advanced open-vocabulary and multimodal models (YOLO-World, LLaVA-Grounding) show greater resilience, suggesting a narrowing but not closed gap.
