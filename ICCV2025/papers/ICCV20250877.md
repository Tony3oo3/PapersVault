## Problem

Video-based 3D human pose estimation methods consistently fail to reconstruct self-contact (e.g., a hand touching the face) because depth ambiguity along the camera's viewing axis makes it impossible to distinguish hovering from actual contact in a single RGB image. This failure is compounded by a lack of reliable pseudo-ground-truth training data for self-contact poses, since occlusions and depth ambiguities prevent existing methods from producing accurate contact labels at scale.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a frontal RGB video (1300×1400 at 30 fps), a continuous wrist-to-wrist bioimpedance signal (2.29 MHz AC sampled at 13.4 kHz), and initial SMPL-X pose estimates from an off-the-shelf regressor (Multi-HMR, AiOS, or TUCH). The bioimpedance signal is processed into a binary per-frame touch sequence that gates which frames undergo optimization. The output is a refined SMPL-X mesh in which only the arm joint parameters (shoulder, elbow, wrist) are modified via masked gradient updates, while body shape, global translation, and orientation remain fixed from the initial estimate.

## Method

BioTUCH is a two-stage framework: first, an adaptive-thresholding algorithm on the differentiated, median-filtered bioimpedance signal detects the onset and offset of skin-to-skin contact (sensitivity 0.858, specificity 0.992); second, for frames flagged as contact, a differentiable SMPL-X optimization refines arm poses using a composite loss of 2D reprojection error and a contact loss (consistency + interpenetration barrier + vertex-proximity). Contact regions are identified by computing camera-adaptive weighted distances between hand vertices and all upper-body target vertices (z-axis errors weighted 4× less, reflecting the 3–4× larger depth errors of off-the-shelf methods), and only the arm(s) with the smallest distance are optimized via a binary mask applied to the gradient. Optimization halts once all selected vertex pairs reach ≤5 mm separation, and a OneEuroFilter post-processing step enforces temporal smoothness. The key insight is that bioimpedance provides a reliable temporal prior for *when* contact occurs, while the visual data determines *where*, enabling targeted refinement that resolves the depth ambiguity without requiring multi-view capture.

## Results

On the BioTUCH dataset (19,183 contact frames from 3 subjects), BioTUCH achieves an average 11.7% reduction in PA-V2V arm mesh error across three input estimators. The best overall result is Multi-HMR + BioTUCH at 50.21 mm PA-V2V, with a contact detection rate of 78.34% (vs. 41.28% for vanilla Multi-HMR) and a mean vertex-pair contact distance of 71.22 mm (vs. 87.48 mm). Wrist joint location error improves by 8.85 mm on average, while shoulder and elbow errors degrade slightly (0.36 mm and 0.50 mm). The self-contact detection algorithm itself achieves 0.858 sensitivity and 0.992 specificity, with zero false positives on adversarial non-contact gestures.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BioTUCH consistently outperforms all three baselines on every metric. Against Multi-HMR (the strongest baseline at 57.46 mm PA-V2V), BioTUCH reduces arm mesh error to 50.21 mm (−12.6%) and more than doubles the contact detection rate from 41.28% to 78.34% (+37.06 pp). Against AiOS (72.24 mm PA-V2V), BioTUCH achieves 62.79 mm (−13.1%) and raises detection from 45.87% to 78.48%. Against TUCH, which is specifically designed for self-contact and already has the highest baseline detection rate (59.46%), BioTUCH still improves PA-V2V from 70.55 to 63.99 mm and pushes detection to 84.60% (+25.14 pp). The ablation confirms that the contact loss (not the 2D reprojection loss) drives the gains: adding only the contact loss to Multi-HMR already yields 79.35% detection, while the 2D loss alone degrades performance (77.41 mm PA-V2V vs. 57.46 mm baseline). Wrist joint error improves most dramatically (e.g., 65.37→56.29 mm for Multi-HMR), consistent with the method's focus on hand-to-body contact along the depth axis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-contact-aware pose estimation line (TUCH, SCP) by introducing a physical sensing modality—wrist-to-wrist bioimpedance—as a contact prior, rather than relying solely on visual cues or geometric thresholds to infer contact. It builds directly on Forte et al.'s validation that bioimpedance can detect discrete self-touch, repurposing that signal not as a classifier but as a timing gate that triggers targeted arm-pose optimization on SMPL-X. The optimization framework itself follows the SMPLify-X paradigm of differentiable re-projection on parametric body models, but adds contact-specific vertex-proximity and interpenetration losses with masked gradient updates restricted to arm joints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The persistent failure of pose estimators to reconstruct self-contact is largely a data problem: reliable contact labels are extremely difficult to obtain from images alone. BioTUCH offers a scalable, low-cost path to generating contact-aware pseudo-ground truth at scale, which could directly improve training data for future pose estimators. However, the current evaluation is limited to three subjects and hand-initiated contacts only, and the method's reliance on the input mesh for contact-region identification means it inherits and can be limited by upstream estimation errors. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-hand contacts (ankle-to-ankle, etc.), whether the miniature sensor's signal quality holds in noisy outdoor settings, and whether a regressor trained on BioTUCH-corrected meshes can close the gap without requiring the sensor at inference time.
