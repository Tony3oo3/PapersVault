## Problem

Separating direct and indirect reflection components in hyperspectral scenes traditionally requires multiple pattern illuminations (e.g., Nayar's three sinusoidal patterns), but hyperspectral cameras have long per-frame acquisition times, making multi-shot approaches impractical. Existing single-image separation methods rely on large training datasets or are limited to scenes with only one or two colors, leaving no robust solution for spectral direct/indirect separation of complex multi-material scenes from a single hyperspectral image.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251858/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single hyperspectral image (41 spectral bands at 5 nm intervals, 450–650 nm) captured under one spatio-spectral illumination pattern generated by a DMD projector coupled with a diffraction grating. It produces per-wavelength direct and indirect reflectance maps for the scene, and additionally yields a scene depth estimate by analyzing the spatial variation of the estimated sinusoidal period across the image. The illumination pattern is a high-frequency sinusoid in the spatial domain that, after grating dispersion, becomes a sinusoid in the spectral domain at each scene point, so the spectral dimension itself encodes the pattern phase information.

## Method

The core mechanism is a spatio-spectral illumination system: a DMD projector projects a spatial sinusoidal pattern, and a diffraction grating placed in front of it disperses the light so that the pattern is spatially shifted as a function of wavelength, making the observed signal at each scene point a sinusoid in the spectral domain. The observed pixel value is modeled as a sinusoidal function of wavelength whose amplitude encodes the direct component (scaled by spectral reflectance) and whose DC offset encodes the indirect component. To handle non-flat spectral reflectance, the direct and indirect reflectances are represented in a low-dimensional spectral basis (k = 8 SVD components from the Munsell Book of Color dataset), reducing the per-pixel unknowns to 2k + 4. The separation is solved by per-pixel nonlinear optimization over the 41 observed spectral bands, with initial period estimates from local extrema and a smoothness regularizer on the basis coefficients. As a byproduct, the estimated sinusoidal period varies monotonically with scene depth, enabling passive depth estimation from the same single image.

## Results

The paper evaluates spectral direct/indirect separation on four real-world scenes (Potato, Ball, Candle, and a multi-object scene) using MSE and SAM as error metrics against a Nayar-method reference obtained from multiple hyperspectral images. The estimated direct and indirect components closely match the reference across wavelengths, with fine textures appearing in the direct component and soft internal scattering in the indirect component. The method requires only 41 spectral bands (5 nm intervals, 450–650 nm) from a single hyperspectral image, compared to the numerous multi-pattern acquisitions needed by the reference. Additionally, the estimated period φ of the spectral sinusoid correlates with scene depth, enabling depth estimation as a byproduct.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Nayar et al.'s high-frequency sinusoidal pattern separation [24], which requires multiple hyperspectral images captured under different spatial sinusoidal patterns to solve for the three unknowns per pixel. The proposed method achieves comparable separation quality from a single hyperspectral image under one spatio-spectral pattern, as confirmed by visual comparison in Figure 8 and error maps in Figure 6 (MSE and SAM). The paper notes that Nayar's method produces significant artifacts in hyperspectral images unless numerous images are captured, whereas the proposed method's single-shot acquisition avoids this issue. The key practical advantage is acquisition time: the proposed system captures one hyperspectral image (41 bands at 5 nm) instead of the multiple images required by the reference, making it suitable for time-sensitive applications such as biomedical imaging and art conservation. No quantitative numerical MSE/SAM values are reported in the text; the comparison is primarily qualitative with spatial error maps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Nayar et al.'s [24] high-frequency sinusoidal pattern illumination for direct/indirect separation by encoding the required multiple spatial patterns into a single wavelength-dependent pattern via a diffraction grating placed after a DMD projector. The spectral basis representation (SVD of Munsell reflectances) is adopted from Han et al. [14] to make the per-pixel optimization tractable when reflectance is non-flat. It departs from prior single-image separation approaches (Subpa-asa [30], Duan [9]) that rely on large training datasets, and from Okawa et al. [25] which is restricted to one- or two-color scenes, by handling complex multi-material scenes from a single hyperspectral capture. The dispersive-optics hardware concept draws on CASSI and Shin et al. [29], but repurposes wavelength-dependent spatial shifting for separation rather than reconstruction or depth.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial for applications where repeated hyperspectral captures are infeasible—medical imaging, art conservation, and food inspection—since a single snapshot suffices for per-wavelength direct/indirect decomposition. The byproduct depth estimation from the estimated pattern period adds a useful 3D cue without additional hardware. Limitations include dependence on a specific DMD + grating + hyperspectral camera setup, reliance on a Munsell-derived basis that may not cover highly saturated or unusual materials, and the need for 41 spectral bands (versus the theoretical minimum of 20) for noise robustness. Open questions include generalization to dynamic scenes, materials outside the basis span, and whether the approach extends to fluorescence or other non-Lambertian spectral phenomena.
