## Problem

Video generative models for robot learning produce open-loop policies that lack robustness in contact-rich manipulation, while video-based RL is bottlenecked by the cost of large-scale robot data collection and the artifacts inherent in generated videos. The paper addresses this by repurposing generated object-centric flow—a lower-dimensional, manipulation-relevant representation—instead of raw video frames for reward shaping in RL, enabling robust closed-loop policy learning from easy-to-collect cross-embodiment human demonstrations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251641/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy at each timestep consumes the current robot state, 2D object keypoint centroid, the current observational δ-flow (a 3-element vector of centroid position, average translation, and average rotary transformation relative to the first frame), k-step lookahead generated δ-flow and keypoint centroids, and the initial 3D keypoint centroid. The output is a 6D pose displacement that is converted to joint-space robot commands via inverse kinematics. The flow generative model itself takes the initial frame image and a natural-language task description as conditioning inputs, producing a sequence of 2D keypoint trajectories that are post-processed into the condensed δ-flow format.

## Method

GENFLOWRL adapts AnimateDiff into a flow generative model via two-stage fine-tuning (decoder-only adaptation to flow data, then LoRA on the latent motion module) trained on 12K cross-embodiment trajectories from three robot types. Generated object-centric flows are converted into a δ-flow representation that encodes each timestep as a compact 3-element statistic (centroid, mean translation, mean cross-product rotation), whose Monte Carlo averaging suppresses noisy keypoints. A hybrid reward model combines a dense δ-flow matching reward—computed as a clipped, normalized squared distance between the robot's observational δ-flow and the generated δ-flow prior—with a sparse state-aware reward based on gripper-object distance and subgoal completion. The policy is trained with DrQv2, conditioned on both the generated future δ-flow (as motion prior) and the initial 3D keypoint centroid (for spatial grounding), ensuring training-inference consistency by using generated flows at both training and inference time rather than relying on expert demonstrations.

## Results

On five Im2Flow2Act manipulation tasks, GENFLOWRL achieves 100% success rate on PickNPlace, Pouring, and Opening under demonstration-conditioned execution, and 95% on all three under language-conditioned execution. The most significant gains appear on the hardest tasks: Folding (80% vs. 35% for Im2Flow2Act) and Pivoting (85% vs. 45%) under language-conditioned settings. On five MetaWorld tasks (Assembly, Close Door, Coffee Push, Lever Pull, Stick Pull), GENFLOWRL converges faster and reaches higher success rates than video-based reward methods (VIPER, Diffusion Reward) and exploration-only baselines (RND, PSR), with the largest margin on the challenging Assembly task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Heuristic baseline [54] (General Flow), GENFLOWRL wins on every task in both evaluation setups; the heuristic achieves 0% on Folding and Pivoting and at most 70% on PickNPlace, while GENFLOWRL reaches 90–100%. Against Im2Flow2Act [47], GENFLOWRL outperforms on all five tasks, with the largest margins under language-conditioned execution: +45 points on Folding (80% vs. 35%) and +40 points on Pivoting (85% vs. 45%), indicating that the δ-flow representation and RL-based closed-loop policy are far more robust to flow-generation noise than the open-loop diffusion policy. On MetaWorld, GENFLOWRL surpasses both video-based reward methods—VIPER [5] and Diffusion Reward [17]—particularly on the Assembly task, where video generation artifacts degrade reward quality. It also outperforms the exploration-only baselines RND [1] and Pure Sparse Reward, which struggle on contact-rich tasks like Assembly and Lever Pull due to the absence of expert motion priors. The paper additionally shows that δ-flow outperforms 6D object poses and 3D keypoint trajectories as reward-shaping representations, especially on the contact-rich Pivoting task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of video generative models for robot learning and reinforcement learning, departing from both lines in a specific way. Rather than using generated video frames directly as reward signals (as in VIPER and Diffusion Reward) or as open-loop policy conditions (as in Im2Flow2Act and Dreamitate), it repurposes the lower-dimensional object-centric flow representation from the Im2Flow2Act/General Flow lineage and feeds it into a closed-loop RL pipeline (DrQv2). The key methodological departure is the introduction of δ-flow, a condensed statistical representation (centroid, translation, rotation) that is more robust to generation noise than raw keypoint trajectories, and its dual use as both a dense reward prior and a policy conditioning input. The flow generative model itself is adapted from AnimateDiff via two-stage fine-tuning (decoder adaptation + LoRA on the latent motion module), making the generative component a means to an end rather than the core contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in video-based RL for manipulation: generated video artifacts degrade reward quality, making fine-grained and contact-rich tasks unreliable. By showing that a 3-statistic per-timestep representation (δ-flow) outperforms both video-based rewards and richer object-centric representations (6D poses, 3D keypoint trajectories) for reward shaping, it offers a lightweight, noise-tolerant alternative that also naturally supports deformable and articulated objects. The cross-embodiment validation (human hand demos → XArm7) is encouraging but limited to open-loop rollouts, leaving open whether the full closed-loop RL pipeline transfers to real robots without additional adaptation. The authors themselves note that 2D flow cannot handle out-of-plane rotations (e.g., twist-off), pointing to 3D flow as a natural next step.
