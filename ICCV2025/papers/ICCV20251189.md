## Problem

Multimodal models often underperform their unimodal counterparts, and existing explanations attribute this to the dominant modality suppressing the weaker one. However, these methods fail to explain why even the dominant modality in a multimodal model underperforms its standalone unimodal baseline. This paper reveals that the root cause is an optimization conflict between the modality encoder and the fusion module: cross-modal fusion provably suppresses the gradient back-propagated to every modality encoder, and this suppression worsens as training progresses.

## Input / Output

The method accepts multimodal inputs processed by modality-specific encoders (e.g., ResNet18 mapping audio spectrograms of size 257×299 or 257×1004 and 1–3 visual frames to 512-d vectors; or RGB and depth maps for segmentation) and produces class labels or pixel-wise segmentation masks. A key design choice is that unimodal losses are computed by passing a single modality representation through the shared fusion module with all other modality slots set to zero (modality dropout), rather than introducing separate per-modality classifiers, which keeps the architecture parameter-free and compatible with dense cross-modal interaction.

## Method

DGL decouples the optimization of modality encoders from the fusion module via two gradient operations. First, gradient detaching: the modality representations are detached before entering the fusion module, so the multimodal loss only updates the fusion module and classifier, eliminating the proven gradient suppression on encoders. Second, gradient reorganizing: independent unimodal losses are computed for each encoder by zeroing out the other modality slots in the same fusion module, providing each encoder with a full-strength unimodal gradient path; the unimodal gradient is then truncated before it reaches the fusion module to avoid conflict with the multimodal gradient. The total loss is a concatenation (not a sum) of the detached multimodal loss and a weighted sum of unimodal losses, since their gradients are fully decoupled and update disjoint parameter sets. Theoretically, the paper proves via chain-rule analysis that |g_Uni| > |g_Multi| > 0 for any non-trivial fusion, and that the gap widens as the model converges, formally establishing the under-optimization mechanism.

## Results

On the three audio-visual classification benchmarks, DGL achieves 77.48% accuracy on CREMA-D, 74.78% on Kinetics-Sounds, and 52.53% on VGGSound, outperforming the strongest baselines (D&R and MLA) by 3.16%, 3.66%, and 1.34% respectively. A distinctive advantage is that DGL also improves unimodal branch performance within the multimodal model: e.g., the audio branch reaches 63.12% on CREMA-D (vs. 62.18% for a standalone audio-only model) and the visual branch reaches 69.11% (vs. 68.23% standalone), whereas all other baselines degrade unimodal performance below their standalone counterparts. On the three-modality MOSI dataset, DGL achieves 79.78% multimodal accuracy, and on the RGB-D segmentation task (NYUv2), it improves ESANet's mIOU from 38.59% to 41.67% (from scratch) and from 48.48% to 50.10% (ImageNet pre-trained).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DGL outperforms all seven baselines across every dataset and every modality column. Against the strongest gradient-modulation methods (OGM_GE, PMR, AGM), DGL gains 5–10 points in multimodal accuracy (e.g., 77.48 vs. 69.61 for AGM on CREMA-D) and, critically, restores unimodal branch accuracy to or above standalone levels, which these methods fail to do. Against the alternating-optimization methods (MLA, MMPareto, D&R), DGL improves multimodal accuracy by 1.3–3.7 points (77.48 vs. 74.32 for D&R on CREMA-D; 74.78 vs. 71.12 for MLA on KS) and uniquely surpasses the standalone unimodal baselines in both audio and visual branches. G-Blending, which also introduces unimodal loss but without gradient decoupling, lags DGL by 8.27 points on CREMA-D multimodal accuracy (69.21 vs. 77.48), confirming that the gradient-truncation mechanism is the key differentiator. On the three-modality MOSI dataset and the RGB-D segmentation task (NYUv2), DGL similarly leads all compared methods, demonstrating generality beyond two-modality audio-visual classification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant framing in multimodal learning that attributes under-optimization to inter-modality imbalance (where a strong modality suppresses a weak one). Instead, it identifies a different root cause: an inherent optimization conflict between each modality encoder and the fusion module, proven via gradient analysis showing that cross-modal fusion suppresses back-propagated gradients to all encoders. While gradient modulation methods (OGM, AGM, PMR) and alternating optimization methods (MLA, MMPareto, D&R) address inter-modality competition, DGL carves new ground by decoupling encoder and fusion optimization through gradient truncation and reorganization, a structurally distinct solution that requires no extra classifiers or per-modality hyperparameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even the dominant modality underperforms its unimodal baseline due to encoder-fusion gradient conflict reframes a long-standing puzzle in multimodal learning and provides a clean, parameter-free remedy applicable to any fusion architecture, modality count, or downstream task. The fact that unimodal branches within the DGL-trained multimodal model exceed standalone unimodal performance is a strong validation of the diagnosis. Open questions include whether the gradient-suppression analysis generalizes to non-linear or attention-based fusion beyond concatenation, how the approach interacts with large pretrained encoders (e.g., in LLM-based multimodal models), and whether the modality-dropout unimodal loss remains well-defined for architectures where zero-input behavior is not meaningful.
