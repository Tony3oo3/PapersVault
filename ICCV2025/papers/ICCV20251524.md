## Problem

Existing Gaussian Splatting SLAM systems either assume static scenes or mask out dynamic objects as distractors, reconstructing only static radiance fields. This leaves a gap: no method simultaneously localizes camera poses and builds a full 4D Gaussian radiance field that explicitly models the temporal motion of dynamic objects in unknown, highly dynamic environments.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a temporally sequential RGB-D image stream along with per-pixel motion masks (generated by YOLOv9, or by combining optical flow with the pre-trained detector for objects it cannot segment). During tracking, only static Gaussians are rendered and the motion mask is applied to the input image to exclude dynamic pixels from the photometric/depth loss. Outputs include the camera pose trajectory, a 4D Gaussian radiance field partitioned into static and dynamic sets, novel-view rendered images, and rendered forward/backward optical flow maps used as intermediate supervision signals.

## Method

The architecture splits the Gaussian primitive set into static and dynamic subsets at initialization using the motion mask, then models dynamic Gaussian motion via sparse control points whose 6-DoF transformations are predicted by a time-conditioned MLP and interpolated to each dynamic Gaussian through Linear Blend Skinning with Gaussian RBF weights. A key novelty is a 2D optical flow rendering algorithm: dynamic Gaussians are projected at two consecutive keyframes, their pixel displacement is alpha-blended into a flow map, and this rendered flow is supervised by RAFT-estimated flows on the masked input images, providing dense motion constraints beyond single-view photometric and depth losses. Tracking excludes dynamic Gaussians entirely and optimizes poses using only static-Gaussian renders with a motion-masked L1 loss on color and depth. Mapping uses a two-stage strategy—first optimizing poses and the deformation network, then jointly optimizing Gaussian parameters—combined with an ARAP regularization and an isotropy penalty on ellipsoid scales, followed by a global color-refinement pass with D-SSIM.

## Results

On the BONN RGB-D Dynamic dataset, the proposed 4D-GS SLAM achieves an average ATE of 3.6 cm, substantially outperforming the next-best baseline RoDyn-SLAM (7.9 cm) and all GS-SLAM methods (MonoGS 33.1 cm, SplaTAM 56.8 cm, Gaussian-SLAM 84.3 cm). For rendering quality on BONN, the method attains 23.66 dB PSNR, 0.852 SSIM, and 0.241 LPIPS, surpassing the closest competitor SC-GS (21.63 dB PSNR) by over 2 dB. On TUM RGB-D, the method achieves 22.46 dB PSNR and 1.8 cm average ATE, again leading all baselines by a wide margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method outperforms all five baselines on every reported metric across both datasets. Against the NeRF-based dynamic SLAM RoDyn-SLAM [20], the method achieves roughly 2× lower ATE on BONN (3.6 vs. 7.9 cm) and 3× lower on TUM (1.8 vs. 5.1 cm), while also delivering superior rendering quality. Among the GS-SLAM baselines, MonoGS [30] is the closest in rendering (21.06 dB PSNR on BONN) but still trails by 2.6 dB and has 9× higher ATE (33.1 cm); Gaussian-SLAM [50] and SplaTAM [21] degrade severely in dynamic scenes, with ATE errors of 84.3 cm and 56.8 cm respectively on BONN. SC-GS [18], a non-SLAM dynamic Gaussian method given ground-truth poses, achieves 21.63 dB PSNR on BONN but lacks the tracking capability and still falls 2 dB short of the proposed method in rendering. Notably, SC-GS fails entirely on the ps_track sequences (marked '-'), whereas the proposed method handles them with 8.9–9.4 cm ATE.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two lines of work: (1) 3D Gaussian Splatting SLAM systems (MonoGS, SplaTAM, Gaussian-SLAM) that incrementally track poses and build static Gaussian maps, and (2) dynamic Gaussian splatting methods (SC-GS, 4DGS, D3DGS) that model temporal deformation but assume known camera poses. The key departure from both is that prior dynamic GS-SLAM approaches (DGS-SLAM, DG-SLAM) discard dynamic pixels via semantic masks and reconstruct only static fields, whereas this work explicitly retains and models dynamic Gaussians with a sparse-control-point + MLP deformation network. The novel optical flow rendering supervision (Equation 6–7) has no direct precedent in the GS-SLAM literature, borrowing the alpha-blending rasterization paradigm from color/depth rendering and applying it to flow vectors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first system to jointly perform camera tracking and 4D Gaussian radiance field reconstruction in dynamic scenes, closing a gap where prior methods either ignored dynamics or required offline pose estimation. The optical flow rendering as a differentiable supervision signal for 4D GS deformation is a reusable idea that could transfer to other dynamic reconstruction settings. Limitations include reliance on a pre-trained YOLOv9 detector for motion masks (fragile to unseen object classes), evaluation only on small indoor RGB-D sequences (TUM, BONN), and a two-stage mapping strategy that adds implementation complexity. Open questions include scalability to outdoor or large-scale dynamic scenes, handling of multiple interacting dynamic objects, and whether the method can operate in real time on embedded hardware.
