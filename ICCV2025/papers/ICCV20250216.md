## Problem

Policy learning for long-horizon embodied tasks must maintain temporal consistency over extended action sequences, handle multi-modal action distributions, and achieve high precision simultaneously. Existing approaches lack a principled mechanism to separate global trends from local details in action sequences, making it difficult to learn stable coarse behaviors while preserving fine-grained control. This paper introduces wavelet analysis from a signal-processing perspective as a new lens for policy learning, which had not been explored in this context.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a window of N timesteps of observations (224×224 RGB frames encoded by a frozen ResNet18, 2D state vectors, or proprioceptive feedback depending on the environment) and produces a corresponding N-step action sequence. Unusually, the output is generated in a coarse-to-fine manner: the network first predicts the smoothest low-frequency action trend at the coarsest scale, then progressively adds high-frequency detail components at finer scales via cross-attention fusers, rather than emitting all actions in a single forward pass.

## Method

The wavelet policy is a multi-scale network built on the lifting scheme, where each analysis block splits an observation stream into two sub-streams (via a redundant Transformer-based splitter that copies output to both branches), applies a learnable P-Net (causal dilated convolution) to predict details, and a learnable U-Net to update the approximation. The synthesis stage mirrors this with learnable P̂-Net and Ũ-Net, and a Transformer cross-attention fuser (Q = approximation, K = V = detail) recombines streams at each scale, enabling variable-length inputs without strict even-odd partitioning. Converters (causal convolutions) map streams from observation space to action space at the bottleneck. Training uses a composite loss: a task-specific MSE/cross-entropy term, an approximation loss enforcing that each scale's local causal moving average matches the next coarser scale, and a detail loss penalizing large detail coefficients to prevent high-frequency components from overpowering the trend. The architecture is instantiated as a drop-in replacement for the decoder in both Behavior Transformer and Diffusion Policy.

## Results

On CARLA self-driving, the wavelet policy achieves a success rate of 0.847±0.090, outperforming BeT (0.832±0.167) and VQ-BeT (0.839±0.125) with notably lower variance. On the Franka Kitchen long-horizon task, the method matches baselines on T1–T3 but shows clear gains at T4 (0.339 vs. 0.275/0.306) and T5 (0.041 vs. 0.027/0.029), indicating stronger performance on more complex multi-step interactions. On Push-T and Transport, DP-Wavelet outperforms DP-Transformer across all three settings, with the largest margin on Transport-mh (0.497 vs. 0.440). On D3IL, the wavelet policy achieves the highest success rate on all six tasks compared to both BeT and IBC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against BeT [47], the wavelet policy wins on CARLA (0.847 vs. 0.832) and Kitchen T4/T5 (0.339/0.041 vs. 0.275/0.027), while matching on T1–T3; the reduced standard deviation on CARLA (0.090 vs. 0.167) also indicates more consistent performance. Against VQ-BeT [32], the gap is similar: a small CARLA win (0.847 vs. 0.839) and a more pronounced Kitchen T4/T5 advantage. Against DP-Transformer [7] on Push-T and Transport, DP-Wavelet wins on all three settings, with the most notable gain on Transport-mh (0.497 vs. 0.440, +5.7 absolute), suggesting better robustness to mixed-quality demonstrations. Against IBC [15] on D3IL, the wavelet policy achieves higher success rates on all six tasks (e.g., 0.820 vs. 0.523 on Avoiding, 0.778 vs. 0.535 on Pushing) while maintaining comparable or higher behavioral entropy, indicating it does not collapse to a single mode. Overall, the method consistently matches or exceeds all four baselines, with the largest relative gains appearing on longer-horizon and mixed-skill tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper transplants the lifting-scheme wavelet transform from signal processing and 3D shape representation (AWT-Net [24]) into the temporal action-generation pipeline of imitation learning. It builds directly on Behavior Transformer [47] and Diffusion Policy [7] by replacing their action-decoding components (MinGPT / Transformer decoders) with a multi-scale learnable lifting network, while retaining the original encoders and training objectives. The key departure from prior wavelet-in-vision work is the application to sequential, causal action sequences rather than spatial signals, combined with the introduction of causal dilated convolutions and Transformer-based fusers to enforce temporal causality and handle variable-length inputs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The coarse-to-fine action generation paradigm (reconstructing smooth low-frequency trends first, then adding high-frequency details) offers a principled alternative to autoregressive or diffusion-based decoding that could reduce error accumulation in long-horizon tasks. However, the empirical gains over strong baselines are modest (e.g., 0.847 vs. 0.832 in CARLA; 0.339 vs. 0.275 on Kitchen T4), all evaluations are simulation-only, and the multi-modal distribution recovery shows only marginal entropy improvements. Open questions include whether the wavelet decomposition provides genuine interpretability or robustness benefits in real-world deployment, whether the approach scales to higher-dimensional continuous action spaces beyond 2D, and whether the gains persist under distribution shift or partial observability.
