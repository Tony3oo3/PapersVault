## Problem

Standard convolutions use fixed isotropic kernels, limiting adaptability to deformable objects and multi-scale phenomena. While numerous deformation strategies (dilated, deformable, shifted, spatial transformer) have been proposed, they lack a unified theoretical framework explaining what geometric structure their kernel deformations implicitly encode. This paper asks whether a metric geometry perspective can both unify existing methods and yield a new, interpretable convolution paradigm.

## Input / Output

For denoising, the method takes 256×256 greyscale images with additive Gaussian noise (σ_n ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5}) and produces a denoised image via a single local convolution with bilinear interpolation for off-grid sampling. For classification, it takes standard RGB images (28×28 for MNIST/Fashion-MNIST, 32×32 for CIFAR-10/100) and outputs class labels through a ResNet-18 where 3×3 convolutions in layers 2–4 are replaced by metric UTB convolutions. The metric parameters are computed signal-dependently from the input itself via a lightweight intermediate convolution, making the sampling neighbourhood adapt to local image structure at every pixel.

## Method

The core insight is that all existing adaptive convolutions (dilated, shifted, deformable) can be reinterpreted as weighted averaging over samples of unit balls of implicit metrics on the image manifold. Metric convolutions make this explicit: a signal-dependent Randers Finsler metric with parameters (M, ω) is computed per pixel via a small intermediate convolution (5–7 output channels vs. 2k² for deformable offsets), and the unit tangent ball of this metric—an off-centred ellipse—is sampled in polar coordinates to yield k² kernel locations. The resulting operator is shift-equivariant, differentiable, and imposes a geometric regularisation (convex, metric-consistent support) that reduces overfitting. For denoising, a geometric heuristic aligns M's eigenvectors with the image gradient to stretch the unit ball along edges; for classification, the metric parameters are learned end-to-end. A unit geodesic ball variant is also proposed but remains too slow for practical neural network use.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 (LKW-TL, 8 runs), Metric UTB achieves 93.07% (±0.13%) top-1 accuracy, matching Deformable (93.10% ±0.17%) and outperforming Shifted (92.58% ±0.28%) and Standard (92.64% ±0.18%). On CIFAR-100, Metric UTB reaches 70.38% (±0.38%) top-1, beating Deformable (70.05% ±0.43%) and Shifted (68.58% ±0.48%), with the lowest standard deviation among all non-standard methods. For single-image denoising (σ=0.3, k=11), Metric UTB achieves MSE 7.43E-3 with a generalisation gap δ_MSE of only 0.9, versus 8.97E-3 and δ_MSE of 74 for Deformable, demonstrating dramatically reduced overfitting. The method requires only 5 intermediate channels (vs. 2k² for deformable) to encode the metric parameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Deformable Convolution [25], Metric UTB is essentially tied on CIFAR-10 top-1 (93.07% vs 93.10%) but wins on CIFAR-100 top-1 (70.38% vs 70.05%) and both top-5 metrics (90.23% vs 89.71%), while exhibiting roughly half the standard deviation (±0.13% vs ±0.17% on CIFAR-10). Against Shifted Convolution [77], Metric UTB clearly dominates across all four metrics, e.g. 93.07% vs 92.58% on CIFAR-10 top-1 and 70.38% vs 68.58% on CIFAR-100 top-1. A striking advantage emerges in training-from-scratch (FKW-SC) settings: Deformable collapses to 33.21% on CIFAR-10 and 10.47% on CIFAR-100, Shifted to 37.89% and 14.47%, while Metric UTB retains 69.83% and 40.63%, confirming the geometric prior provides strong implicit regularisation. In single-image denoising, the generalisation gap δ_MSE for Metric UTB stays below 1.5 across all kernel sizes, whereas Deformable reaches 265 at k=5, evidencing severe overfitting without the metric constraint.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies a fragmented family of adaptive convolution methods—dilated, deformable, shifted, and active convolutions, as well as spatial transformer networks—under a single geometric lens: each is reinterpreted as weighted sampling from the unit ball of an implicit metric on the image manifold. It then departs from this implicit framing by introducing metric convolutions, which explicitly construct signal-dependent Randers (Finsler) metrics and sample their unit tangent or geodesic balls, drawing on classical metric geometry (Riemannian and Finsler) that predates deep learning. The method is positioned as a drop-in replacement for existing convolutions in CNNs, requiring only 5–7 intermediate channels to encode the metric versus 2k² for deformable convolution offsets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unifying metric theory gives practitioners geometric interpretability and a principled inductive bias for adaptive convolutions, and the parameter efficiency (5–7 numbers vs. 2k² offsets) with consistently lower generalisation gaps suggests a more robust alternative to deformable convolutions. However, the Randers-metric constraint limits unit tangent balls to ellipses, the unit geodesic ball implementation is too slow for practical neural network use, and the per-pixel offset storage creates a memory bottleneck at high resolutions. Open questions include whether richer Finsler metrics (e.g., (α,β)-type) yield meaningfully different sampling shapes, whether UGB convolutions can be made efficient enough for training, and how the approach scales to higher-resolution or video data.
