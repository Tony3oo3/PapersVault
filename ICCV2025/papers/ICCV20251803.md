## Problem

Large-scale robotic demonstration datasets (BridgeData, Open X-Embodiment, DROID, RH20T) suffer from sparse temporal annotations, inconsistent labeling granularity, and misaligned labels, particularly for complex long-horizon tasks. Existing automated annotation approaches either rely on training-free MLLM pipelines that fail to bridge the domain gap to robotics, or use domain-specific models with architectural limitations that prevent adequate temporal modeling over extended demonstrations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251803/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a long-horizon robotic demonstration video (arbitrary length T frames) as input and produces multi-granularity annotations: single-frame scene descriptions (object types, spatial relationships, status), per-clip action recognition with object interaction details, temporal action boundaries with dense video captions, and video-level task summaries with action ordering. The multi-scale encoder processes the video at three complementary resolutions—sparse keyframe sampling for scene tokens, per-clip temporal encoding for dynamics, and single-token-per-frame global modeling—so that a 1000-frame video is handled with far fewer tokens than naive frame-by-frame processing while preserving both visual fidelity and temporal structure.

## Method

RoboAnnotatorX introduces a multi-scale token-efficient encoder with three complementary streams: (1) scene-level tokens from K sparsely sampled keyframes with dense spatial tokenization, (2) clip-level tokens from a Time-aware Q-former that injects temporal position embeddings (text-based, following TimeChat) into per-frame features before compressing each clip to a fixed-size representation, and (3) video-level tokens from average-pooled per-frame features processed by a Global Transformer for long-range dependencies. The model is built on Vicuna-7B and trained via a curriculum-inspired three-stage paradigm: general visual-language pretraining (projector only), short-horizon instruction fine-tuning on 886K QA pairs (all modules with LoRA), and long-horizon instruction fine-tuning on 86K complex demonstrations. Training data comes from RoboX-VQA, a 1M QA-pair / 500K-episode dataset constructed via hybrid real-world and simulated collection (8:2 ratio) with automatic QA generation using GPT-4o on macro-scene descriptions and micro-temporal instructions.

## Results

On the RoboX-VQA benchmark, RoboAnnotatorX achieves 81.47% accuracy on Object Identification, 81.93% on Spatial Relationship, and 71.62% on Action Recognition, substantially outperforming the strongest general MLLM (GPT-4o: 56.15%, 59.09%, 41.67%) and the best fine-tuned baseline (P-LLaVA ft.: 79.67%, 70.27%, 55.56%). On temporal reasoning tasks, the gains are even more pronounced: 54.02% on Action Order (vs. 23.62% for TimeChat ft.), 52.13% on Segment Summarization (vs. 18.06%), and 35.14% on Dense Video Captioning (vs. 10.55%). For temporal localization, RoboAnnotatorX achieves 73.85% Recall@0.5 and 69.29% Avg IoU, nearly doubling the best baseline (GPT-4o: 47.83% R@0.5, 42.94% IoU).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RoboAnnotatorX outperforms all 13 baselines across every evaluated metric. Against the strongest general MLLM (GPT-4o), it gains +25.3 pts on Object Identification, +22.8 on Spatial Relationship, +30.0 on Action Recognition, and +39.7 on Action Order, with Avg IoU nearly doubling (69.29 vs. 42.94). Among fine-tuned robotics baselines, P-LLaVA (ft.) is the closest competitor on scene tasks (79.67 vs. 81.47 on Object ID), but RoboAnnotatorX pulls dramatically ahead on temporal tasks: 54.02 vs. 15.53 on Action Order and 35.14 vs. 9.09 on Dense Video Captioning. TimeChat (ft.) leads among fine-tuned methods on temporal localization (53.13 R@0.5) but is still surpassed by RoboAnnotatorX (73.85 R@0.5, 69.29 IoU vs. 38.49). Training-free robotics pipelines (NILS, MVU) show that merely bolting foundation models (depth, detection) onto a general MLLM is insufficient—NILS achieves 48.84% Action Recognition but collapses to 0% on Segment Summarization and Dense Captioning. The consistent margin over LLaMA-VID (ft.) and P-LLaVA (ft.), which were fine-tuned on the same RoboX-VQA data, isolates the architectural contribution of the multi-scale token-efficient encoder beyond data alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on MLLM-based robot video understanding by unifying three previously separate architectural ideas—keyframe sampling (as in P-LLaVA), temporal position encoding (as in TimeChat, which it directly adopts for the clip-level stream), and frame-wise compression (as in LLaMA-VID)—into a single multi-scale token-efficient encoder with scene, clip, and video-level streams. It departs from training-free pipelines (NILS, MVU) that bolt foundation models onto general MLLMs, and from domain-specific fine-tuned models (SpatialVLM, ManipVQA) that lack sufficient temporal modeling, by combining comprehensive instruction-tuning on a purpose-built dataset (RoboX-VQA) with a curriculum-inspired three-stage training paradigm. The dataset construction itself builds on large-scale robotics corpora (BridgeData, Open X-Embodiment, DROID, RH20T) and simulation benchmarks (CALVIN, RoboCasa), but transforms them into structured QA pairs via GPT-4o-based generation, a pipeline not previously applied at this scale for robotics annotation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a concrete bottleneck in the robotics community: large-scale demonstration datasets are underutilized because their annotations are sparse, inconsistent, and misaligned with actual executions. By providing both a practical annotation tool and a 1M-QA-pair / 500K-episode benchmark (RoboX-VQA), it gives the field a shared resource for long-horizon robot video understanding that did not previously exist. Open questions include whether the multi-scale encoder's advantages persist for even longer horizons (beyond the tested range), whether the 8:2 real-to-simulated data ratio is optimal, and whether the annotation quality is sufficient to fully replace human labeling for policy training. The downstream task improvements (e.g., 77.6% vs. 62.3% success rate on LohoRavens) suggest practical utility, but the gap between annotation accuracy and true task completion in open-world settings remains to be validated.
