## Problem

Text-to-video (T2V) generative models inherently produce substantial redundant information—both static (spatially superfluous elements) and dynamic (temporally unnecessary transitions)—that is not specified in user prompts. This redundancy creates a previously unexplored attack surface where malicious content can be seamlessly embedded into generated videos while preserving the user's intended semantics. No prior work has investigated backdoor attacks against T2V models, leaving a critical security gap in these widely deployed systems.

## Input / Output

At inference, the backdoored model takes a standard text prompt (optionally containing a designated text trigger) and produces a temporally coherent video sequence—16 frames at 512×320 for LaVie or up to 16 seconds for Open-Sora. The generated video simultaneously satisfies the user's semantic request and contains attacker-specified malicious content distributed across frames or encoded in temporal transitions. The target video generation pipeline itself consumes an original caption, an LLM-generated head/tail prompt pair, and a T2I model (FLUX) to produce key-frames that condition the final T2V diffusion model.

## Method

BadVideo is a data-poisoning backdoor attack that fine-tunes a pre-trained T2V diffusion model (with frozen text encoder) on a poisoned dataset (20% poisoning ratio) where each poisoned pair pairs a trigger-augmented prompt with a video containing embedded malicious content. The core novelty lies in three target-video generation strategies that exploit video redundancy: Spatio-Temporal Composition splits malicious content across frames so no single frame is harmful; Semantic Concept Transition encodes offensive content in the temporal evolution of background elements; and Visual Style Transition conveys harm through progressive atmospheric degradation. Target videos are produced via a three-module pipeline: an LLM transforms the original prompt into head and tail prompts incorporating the backdoor target, a T2I model generates a head key-frame and edits it into a tail key-frame for visual consistency, and a T2V model synthesizes the full video conditioned on both key-frames. This temporal distribution of malicious content allows the attack to evade frame-by-frame content moderation systems while maintaining high attack success rates (up to 93.5%) and preserving benign model utility.

## Results

On LaVie, BadVideo achieves human-evaluated ASR of 92.3% (STC), 91.6% (SCT), and 90.2% (VST), with MLLM-evaluated ASR reaching up to 88.2%. On Open-Sora, ASR_Human peaks at 93.5% (VST) and ASR_MLLM reaches 96.4% (VST). Content preservation (CPR) remains above 70% across all configurations, and benign performance (FVD, CLIPSIM, ViCLIP) stays comparable to clean fine-tuned models, confirming that the backdoor does not degrade utility.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Since BadVideo is the first backdoor attack for T2V models, there are no prior backdoor baselines to compare against; instead, the paper benchmarks against the pre-trained and clean fine-tuned versions of LaVie and Open-Sora to verify utility preservation. All three strategies (STC, SCT, VST) maintain benign FVD within ~30 points of the clean fine-tuned model and keep CLIPSIM/ViCLIP within 0.01–0.02, confirming minimal utility degradation. Among the three strategies, VST achieves the highest ASR_MLLM on Open-Sora (96.4%) and the best FVD (312.31 vs. 310.77 clean), while STC yields the highest ASR_Human on LaVie (92.3%). Against adaptive defenses, the backdoor resists fine-tuning (ASR_MLLM stays above 80% after 100 epochs of clean fine-tuning) and prompt perturbation (CPR drops sharply before ASR does, making the defense impractical). For detection, even GPT-4o with explicit time-related instructions achieves only 52% detection at 16 frames, while security-focused models (Omni-Moderation, Llama-Guard-3-11B-Vision) score 0% in both conditions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor attack literature on diffusion models—previously limited to unconditional generation (TrojDiff, Chou et al.) and text-to-image synthesis (Struppek et al., Zhai et al.)—to the text-to-video domain, which the authors explicitly identify as unexplored. The key conceptual departure is exploiting the inherent temporal and spatial redundancy in video generation (elements not specified by the text prompt) as a new attack surface, enabling three distinct strategies (spatio-temporal composition, semantic concept transition, visual style transition) that have no analogue in image-based backdoor attacks. The attack pipeline repurposes standard data-poisoning and fine-tuning procedures, with the novelty residing entirely in how target videos are constructed to embed malicious content across the temporal dimension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals that current video content moderation systems, which operate frame-by-frame, are architecturally blind to temporally distributed malicious content, and that even state-of-the-art MLLMs (GPT-4o, Qwen2.5-VL) achieve at most 52% detection with explicit temporal instructions at substantial computational cost. The remarkably low attack cost (~$6.33 on a single A800) and resistance to standard defenses (fine-tuning, prompt perturbation) make this a practical and immediate concern for any deployment of T2V models. Open questions include whether defenses can be designed that reason over temporal sequences without prohibitive cost, whether the attack generalizes to longer or higher-resolution videos, and whether the same redundancy-exploitation principle applies to other generative modalities (audio, 3D).
