## Problem

Species distribution data from citizen science platforms encodes rich ecological and environmental information about local landscapes, yet its potential for guiding satellite image representation learning remains largely unexplored. Prior work on remote sensing representations has predominantly relied on anthropogenic labels (roads, buildings, land cover) or self-supervised objectives, leaving the complementary signal from wildlife habitat preferences untapped for improving general-purpose satellite image encoders.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250704/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes 512×512 Sentinel-2 satellite images (RGB bands) paired with species observation coordinates (lat/lon), 20-dimensional WorldClim2 environmental covariates, and Wikipedia text sections describing each species' habitat, range, and behavior. The trained satellite image encoder is frozen at inference and produces a single embedding vector that supports linear probing for classification and segmentation, cosine-similarity-based zero-shot text-to-image retrieval, and species encounter rate prediction. For zero-shot retrieval, a user inputs free-form text (e.g., 'ibex', 'desert') which is encoded by GritLM and matched against the frozen image encoder's embeddings.

## Method

WildSAT trains a satellite image encoder via a three-way contrastive objective (InfoNCE-based) that simultaneously aligns image embeddings with text embeddings (from a frozen GritLM LLM) and location embeddings (from a frozen SINR spatial encoder), all projected into a shared space via trainable linear heads. The image loss term additionally enforces invariance to temporal shifts and geometric augmentations (flipping, random cropping) of co-located satellite images, encouraging the encoder to learn robust visual features beyond what text and location alone provide. The framework is modality-agnostic in its image backbone (ResNet50, ViT-B/16, Swin-T/B, ViT-L/16) and supports both random initialization and continual pre-training on top of existing satellite or ImageNet-pretrained models, using parameter-efficient fine-tuning (DoRa for transformers, BatchNorm tuning for ResNets) to preserve out-of-domain representations. Training data comprises 980,376 samples drawn from 35.5 million iNaturalist observations across 47,375 species, 305,689 Sentinel-2 images, and 127,484 Wikipedia text sections, with one text section randomly sampled per image-location pair per epoch.

## Results

WildSAT improves linear probing performance in 108 of 115 architecture/pre-training settings, with average gains ranging from 7.7% to 17.4% across seven classification datasets (4.3%–10.4% excluding randomly initialized models). On the CLIP-based cross-modal comparison (Table 2), WildSAT achieves 76.6% average accuracy, outperforming CLIP (71.6%), RemoteCLIP (71.0%), GRAFT (65.0%), and TaxaBind (59.8%). For segmentation, WildSAT lifts Cashew1k IoU from 59.9% to 72.2% and SAcrop3k IoU from 21.4% to 22.7% on average. On bird species encounter rate prediction (SatBird), WildSAT improves top-k accuracy by 0.3–1.9 points across Kenya, USA Summer, and USA Winter subsets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

WildSAT (CLIP ViT-B/16 base) achieves 76.6% average linear probing accuracy, beating CLIP itself by 5.0 points, RemoteCLIP by 5.6 points, GRAFT by 11.6 points, and TaxaBind by 16.8 points. The paper notes that GRAFT, TaxaBind, and RemoteCLIP all fine-tune CLIP with additional cross-modal supervision but suffer representational 'forgetting' on satellite image tasks, whereas WildSAT's multi-modal contrastive objective (image + text + location) preserves and enhances the base encoder. On segmentation, WildSAT improves all five base models tested (ImageNet, MoCov3, SeCo, SatlasNet, Random), with the largest gain for SatlasNet (55.2%→71.0% on Cashew1k) and Random (40.1%→72.6%). On bird encounter rate prediction, WildSAT consistently improves SatlasNet, SatMAE, and TaxaBind by 0.3–1.9 top-k points, with the largest gains in USA regions where training data is most concentrated. Satellite-domain pre-trained models (SeCo, SatlasNet) see the largest relative boosts (~10%), while ImageNet/MoCov3 models show smaller gains due to their already strong starting performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

WildSAT extends the cross-modal contrastive learning paradigm for satellite imagery (established by GRAFT, RemoteCLIP, and GeoBind) by introducing wildlife observation data as a supervisory signal. It builds most directly on TaxaBind, which first aligned species geographic locations with satellite images, but departs by replacing taxonomic hierarchy text with richer Wikipedia habitat descriptions, adding environmental covariates via WorldClim2, and incorporating an image-specific contrastive loss over temporal and geometric augmentations. Unlike prior satellite pre-training methods that rely on anthropogenic labels (SatlasNet, WikiSatNet) or self-supervised objectives (SeCo, Prithvi), WildSAT leverages the ecological signal embedded in species distributions to learn representations that capture habitat and environmental context.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WildSAT demonstrates that freely available citizen-science wildlife observations constitute a globally distributed, ecologically meaningful supervision source that complements existing satellite pre-training focused on human-made features. The emergent zero-shot text-to-satellite-image retrieval (e.g., querying 'ibex' to find mountain terrain) opens practical avenues for biodiversity monitoring and species distribution studies. Key limitations include geographic bias toward North America and Europe in the training data, and reliance on LLM text encoders susceptible to hallucination. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to underrepresented regions and whether additional modalities (acoustic data, aerial imagery) can further strengthen the representations.
