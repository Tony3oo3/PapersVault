## Problem

Existing human motion estimation models assume a fixed number of input frames, so the same video sampled at different frame rates yields inconsistent motion predictions because the temporal duration covered by the input changes. Neither transformer-based methods (which process a fixed frame count) nor RNN-based methods (which lack awareness of inter-frame time intervals) explicitly encode absolute time, leading to temporal misalignment and distorted motion reconstruction across varying sampling frequencies.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250313/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a fixed 0.5-second video clip at an arbitrary input frame rate (5–120 FPS), from which 2D keypoints (ViTPose) and image features (pretrained encoder) are extracted per frame; for global estimation, camera extrinsics from Masked DROID-SLAM are additionally consumed. The output is always SMPL pose, shape, and local camera translation at a fixed 120 FPS reference rate over the same 0.5-second window, which can optionally be downsampled via linear interpolation to match the input rate. This fixed reference-rate output space is what enables direct comparison of predictions made from different input frame rates.

## Method

MBTI formulates frame-rate-agnostic motion estimation as a masked autoencoder reconstruction problem: input tokens at rate f_in are upsampled to a 120 FPS reference sequence by blending them with learnable mask tokens. Two key mechanisms prevent temporal misalignment. First, implicit positional encoding replaces standard index-based positional codes with a neural implicit function p_θ(γ(t)) that maps each token's absolute time t = n/f to a learned embedding, so the model knows *when* each frame occurs rather than merely its ordinal position. Second, the masked token blending scheme assigns each reference-rate position a convex combination of the nearest input token and a mask token, weighted by the temporal distance w_b = |n/f_ref − k/f_in|, ensuring smooth interpolation that respects the true time gap between observed and missing frames. Training proceeds in two stages: pretraining on AMASS with random downsampling to 5–120 FPS (so the model learns to reconstruct 120 FPS ground truth from degraded inputs), followed by fine-tuning on video datasets (3DPW, Human3.6M, MPI-INF-3DHP, InstaVariety, BEDLAM) while retaining AMASS downsampling to preserve frame-rate generalization.

## Results

On 3DPW (30 FPS), MBTI achieves the best PA-MPJPE (35.0 mm) and ties GVHMR on PVE (67.2 mm), while remaining competitive on MPJPE (56.1 mm vs. GVHMR's 55.6 mm) and acceleration error (5.8 m/s² vs. TRAM's 4.9 m/s²). On the frame-rate-varying EMDB-FPS Split 1, MBTI achieves the lowest Motion Consistency across Frame rates (MCF = 7.4) and the smallest standard deviation in PA-MPJPE (σ = 1.0 mm) across 10–120 FPS inputs, outperforming TRAM (MCF = 8.6, σ = 2.1). On EMDB-FPS Split 2 (global motion), MBTI attains the lowest MCF (6.7) and dramatically lower Jitter (33.2 mm mean) compared to TRAM (103.4 mm), confirming superior temporal smoothness in world coordinates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MBTI outperforms all four temporal baselines (WHAM, WHAC, GVHMR, TRAM) on the proposed MCF metric in both local (7.4 vs. 8.6 for TRAM) and global (6.7 vs. 7.6 for TRAM) settings, confirming superior cross-frame-rate consistency. On 3DPW, MBTI achieves the best PA-MPJPE (35.0 mm) by a narrow margin over TRAM (35.6 mm) and WHAM (35.9 mm), but trails GVHMR slightly on MPJPE (56.1 vs. 55.6 mm) and TRAM on acceleration error (5.8 vs. 4.9 m/s²). The most dramatic advantage appears in global motion: MBTI's Jitter (33.2 mm) is roughly 3× lower than TRAM's (103.4 mm) and 3.5× lower than WHAM's (39.1 mm), while WHAM and GVHMR exhibit severe frame-rate sensitivity (MCF 96.2 and 25.6 respectively). GVHMR shows the worst cross-frame-rate stability in local evaluation (MCF 23.3, Accel σ = 111.2), indicating its fixed-length transformer architecture is highly sensitive to temporal sampling. Per-frame methods (SPIN, PARE, CLIFF, HybrIK, HMR2.0, Refit) are substantially worse on all temporal metrics, with PA-MPJPE ranging from 40.5 to 59.2 mm, confirming that temporal modeling is essential but that frame-rate awareness provides an additional consistency benefit beyond standard temporal estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

MBTI repurposes the Masked Autoencoder (MAE) framework from image reconstruction [He et al., CVPR 2022] into a temporal motion interpolation problem, where mask tokens represent missing high-frame-rate frames rather than spatial patches. The implicit positional encoding directly adapts the neural implicit function paradigm from NeRF [Mildenhall et al., CACM 2021] and SIREN [Sitzmann et al., NeurIPS 2020], replacing spatial coordinates with absolute time values to encode when each frame occurs. Conceptually, the paper departs from both transformer-based temporal estimators (WHAM, GVHMR, TRAM) that implicitly assume a fixed frame rate, and RNN-based methods (VIBE) that handle variable-length sequences but lack explicit time awareness, by making absolute time a first-class input to the architecture through the masked token blending mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Frame rate is a practical variable in real deployments (surveillance at 10 FPS, standard video at 30 FPS, motion capture at 120+ FPS), yet prior methods silently assume a fixed sampling rate, producing inconsistent results when this assumption is violated. The MCF metric and EMDB-FPS benchmark fill a genuine evaluation gap, giving the community a standardized way to measure temporal consistency across frame rates. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is that MBTI cannot handle videos whose frame rate changes within a single sequence (e.g., live streams), leaving temporal continuity constraints as an open direction. The broader implication is that treating temporal resolution as an explicit model input rather than an implicit architectural assumption may generalize to other video-based estimation tasks beyond human motion.
