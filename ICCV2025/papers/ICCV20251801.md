## Problem

Existing brain-to-text decoding methods assume strict semantic equivalence between stimulus images and evoked fMRI recordings, but biological brains selectively attend to only a subset of visual semantics while models like CLIP capture the full image content. This semantic misalignment between supervision signals and neural representations degrades reconstruction quality, as the model is forced to align with semantic details the brain never encoded.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251801/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes whole-brain 7T fMRI BOLD signals (averaged across trials per image) as input and produces free-form text descriptions via a frozen GPT-2 Base decoder. During training, the corresponding stimulus image (from COCO) is processed by a frozen CLIP-ViT-B/32 encoder to provide patch-level supervision, with 16 irregularly shaped visual segments dynamically selected per sample. The fMRI encoder is a 16-layer, 16-head ViT, and only the MLP projector, fMRI encoder, and 12 cross-attention layers (with reduced 4-dim projections) are trainable.

## Method

Mind-SA introduces a self-adaptive semantic decoding (SASD) mechanism that dynamically identifies which image regions the brain actually encoded, rather than forcing alignment to the full stimulus. After a pre-alignment step projecting CLIP [CLS] embeddings into fMRI space via an MLP, the method computes similarity scores between all CLIP patch embeddings and the fMRI representation, then uses hard Gumbel-Softmax with the straight-through trick to differentiably select K=16 patches as one-hot assignments. These selected patches form the supervision target for the fMRI encoder, effectively masking out semantic content the brain did not process. The fMRI latent representation is then fed into a frozen GPT-2 decoder through trainable multi-head cross-attention layers to generate text autoregressively. Training is fully end-to-end with a composite loss (pre-alignment MSE + selected-patch MSE + text cross-entropy, weighted by λ=10), requiring no fine-grained annotations beyond standard image-fMRI pairs.

## Results

On the NSD dataset (subjects 1, 2, 5, 7), Mind-SA achieves the best scores on six of eight language similarity metrics: BLEU-1 (59.76), BLEU-2 (39.81), BLEU-3 (27.05), BLEU-4 (18.86), METEOR (38.08), and CIDEr (54.03). The most striking gain is on METEOR, where Mind-SA scores 38.08 versus 21.93 for MindArt (a 73.6% relative improvement) and 18.27 for UMBRAE (a 103.6% improvement). Mind-SA slightly trails on SPICE (12.25 vs. 12.39 for Neuro2Language) and ROUGE (42.72 vs. 42.86 for MindArt), but leads on all other metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Mind-SA outperforms all seven baselines on BLEU-1 through BLEU-4 and CIDEr, with the largest margin over MindArt on BLEU-4 (18.86 vs. 17.71, +6.5%) and CIDEr (54.03 vs. 45.95, +17.6%). The most dramatic win is on METEOR, where Mind-SA (38.08) nearly doubles MindArt (21.93) and UMBRAE (18.27), suggesting the self-adaptive semantic decoding mechanism substantially improves semantic and fluency alignment. Mind-SA is essentially tied with MindArt on ROUGE (42.72 vs. 42.86) and slightly behind Neuro2Language on SPICE (12.25 vs. 12.39), indicating that while the method excels at n-gram and semantic overlap, its propositional-level accuracy is comparable to the strongest competitors. Among the linear-alignment baselines (Brain Captioning, Takagi et al., UniBrain), Mind-SA leads by a wide margin on every reported metric, confirming the advantage of the non-linear ViT-based encoder combined with the SASD mechanism.

## Relation to Prior Work

Mind-SA builds directly on the CLIP-as-proxy + GPT-2-as-decoder pipeline established by MindGPT, Brain Captioning, and Neuro2Language for brain-to-text reconstruction, and on the ViT-based fMRI encoder architecture from Neuro2Language. Its key departure is rejecting the standard assumption that stimulus images and evoked BOLD signals are strictly semantically equivalent: rather than aligning fMRI to the full CLIP [CLS] embedding, it uses a differentiable Gumbel-Softmax mechanism to dynamically select a subset of CLIP patch embeddings that the brain actually attends to, thereby masking unperceived semantic details before alignment. The authors explicitly note that no prior work has investigated whether stimulus semantics align with brain-recorded semantics, positioning this as a new problem formulation within the established brain-to-text task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a foundational assumption in neural decoding—that the brain encodes the full semantic content of a stimulus—and shows that correcting for selective attention yields large gains (especially on METEOR, up to ~104% over UMBRAE), suggesting prior methods were systematically misaligned. Limitations include a fixed number of selected patches (16), evaluation on a single dataset (NSD, 4 subjects), and the observation that larger encoder architectures do not help, pointing to data-scarcity constraints. The authors raise an open question about how the brain reconstructs complete visual perception from incomplete semantic encoding, and suggest the front-end could serve as a 'human attention decoder' for brain-computer interface applications.
