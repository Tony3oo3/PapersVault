## Problem

Existing circuit-discovery methods for vision models either interpret individual neurons in isolation or produce a single unified circuit tied to class-level predictions, failing to decompose the distributed representations into multiple circuits each encoding a distinct visual concept. No prior approach identifies fine-grained, concept-specific circuits that capture the full range of attributes (background, texture, shape, color) present in a query image.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251747/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single query image and a pre-trained classification model (CNN or ViT) as input, and additionally requires a validation set to compute per-neuron activation rankings for the Semantic Flow Score. It outputs a set of directed acyclic graphs (one per root neuron), where each graph is a concept-specific circuit spanning multiple layers; edges are weighted by the Neuron Sensitivity Score. Circuits are visualized as Sankey diagrams with each node annotated by four representative cropped images from its top-10 highly activated validation samples.

## Method

GCC discovers circuits by forward-propagating from root nodes (neurons in the top 1% of activation rank across the validation set) through successive layers, iteratively selecting connected neurons that satisfy two criteria: a Neuron Sensitivity Score (S_NS), computed via intervention (zeroing the source neuron and measuring the target's activation drop, clipped to non-negative and normalized), and a Semantic Flow Score (S_SF), computed as the overlap fraction of top-k highly activated samples shared between source and target neurons. The S_NS threshold is set automatically via Peak-over-Threshold from extreme value theory, while S_SF is thresholded at the mean score across all candidate nodes. Each root node yields one circuit, and the full set of circuits collectively represents all query-relevant concepts. The approach is entirely post-hoc and model-agnostic, requiring no retraining or gradient access beyond the intervention-based activation measurements.

## Results

The primary quantitative evaluation measures the average logit drop after ablating neurons within discovered circuits versus random or complement sets, averaged over 100 ImageNet-1K queries. Ablating neurons inside GCC circuits causes a substantial 8.60% average logit drop across ResNet50, ResNet101, VGG19, and MobileNetV3, compared to only 1.74% for the complement set and 2.35% for random pruning, confirming both faithfulness and completeness of the discovered circuits. In a user study with 33 participants, GCCs scored 3.65/5 on query relativeness, 4.0/5 on diversity, and 4.45/5 on prototypicality, with over 90% of users finding the inter-node connections appropriate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GCC is compared against two prior circuit-discovery methods. Against ADVC (Rajaram et al., [29]), a gradient-based single-circuit method, GCC produces larger performance drops in edge-deletion experiments (Fig. 6a), indicating that its fine-grained, concept-specific circuits capture richer information than ADVC's single class-level circuit, though ADVC shows slightly steeper insertion curves. Against VCC (Kowal et al., [22]), a vector-based inter-layer connectivity method, GCC wins in a direct user-study comparison: 70% of participants agreed that GCC captures more meaningful and diverse concepts than VCC. Structurally, GCC differs from both baselines by producing multiple concept-specific circuits per query (≥1) rather than a single unified circuit, and by operating at the neuron level with both functional (S_NS) and semantic (S_SF) connectivity criteria, whereas ADVC relies solely on cross-layer attribution and VCC operates on concept activation vectors.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the visual circuit discovery line initiated by ADVC (Rajaram et al.) and VCC (Kowal et al.), which iteratively trace inter-layer connectivity but remain constrained to class-relevant or single unified circuits. GCC departs from these by introducing two complementary scoring mechanisms—Neuron Sensitivity Score (intervention-based causal attribution) and Semantic Flow Score (activation-pattern overlap)—to decompose a query's representation into multiple fine-grained, concept-specific circuits rather than one class-level circuit. The method also draws on neuron interpretability work (NetDissect, FALCON, MILAN) for root-node selection and on mechanistic interpretability principles from the LLM literature (faithfulness/completeness evaluation, edge ablation protocols) to validate the discovered circuits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GCC provides the first framework for fine-grained, concept-level circuit discovery in vision models, enabling practitioners to audit misclassifications by identifying which specific concept circuits drive erroneous predictions and to discover shared abstract features (e.g., 'radiant' patterns) across unrelated classes. Key limitations include the model-centric nature of connectivity evaluation (some strong connections remain semantically opaque) and the risk that a single concept is fragmented across multiple circuit pathways under strict thresholds. Open questions include whether the approach scales to very deep or very wide architectures, how circuits behave under distribution shift, and whether the discovered circuits transfer across models trained on different data.
