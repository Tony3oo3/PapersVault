## Problem

Autoregressive robot policies are fast and flexible but fail to capture long-range dependencies and global structure in action sequences, while diffusion policies achieve high accuracy at the cost of 100+ sequential denoising steps that are computationally prohibitive for real-time robotic deployment. No existing paradigm simultaneously delivers diffusion-level performance, autoregressive-level inference speed, and the architectural flexibility of a GPT-style model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252667/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The policy consumes a historical state sequence (proprioceptive keypoints or RGB images from wrist and third-person cameras) and, in multi-task settings, a task embedding as an additional conditioning signal. Actions are represented in rotation6d format to avoid discontinuities in rotation space, and a separate VQVAE is trained per action dimension due to inter-dimensional orthogonality. The output is a continuous action sequence of up to 400 steps (280 for Kitchen, 300 for Push-T), decoded from K=4 discrete token maps via the multi-scale autoencoder, with each token map containing k tokens at scale k.

## Method

CARP operates in two stages. First, a multi-scale action autoencoder (1D-CNN encoder/decoder with residual-style VQVAE quantization) compresses the action sequence into K discrete token maps at increasing granularity, using a shared codebook and cosine-similarity distance; the residual design ensures each finer scale r_k encodes only the information not captured by coarser scales. Second, a GPT-2-style decoder performs coarse-to-fine next-scale prediction, where each token map r_k is predicted conditioned on all prior maps r_{1:k-1} and the historical state (via adaptive normalization), with a block-wise causal attention mask enforcing the scale hierarchy. Training uses standard Cross-Entropy loss over the discrete tokens, which naturally preserves multimodality unlike MSE. At inference, the K token maps are decoded back into continuous actions, and the relaxed Markovian assumption (each scale sees all predecessors, not just the immediately previous one) enables accurate generation in only K=4 steps versus 100+ denoising steps for diffusion, yielding ~10× faster inference with 1–5% of the parameters.

## Results

On the MimicGen multi-task benchmark (8 tasks), CARP achieves an average success rate of 0.85, outperforming Sparse Diffusion Policy (SDP) at 0.78 and Task-Conditioned Diffusion (TCD) at 0.68, while using only 16.08M parameters (vs. 159.85M for SDP) and completing 400-action inference in 6.92s (vs. 112.39s for SDP), a >10× speedup. On single-task Robomimic state-based benchmarks, CARP reaches 0.98 on Square (vs. 0.94 for DP-C and 0.54 for BET) with just 0.65M parameters and 3.07s inference. On the Push-T multi-modal task, CARP achieves 0.88 success rate with 2.66s inference, surpassing DP-C (0.87, 32.52s) and DP-T (0.84, 29.56s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CARP decisively outperforms the autoregressive baseline BET [39] across all single-task benchmarks (e.g., Square: 0.98 vs. 0.54 state-based; Kitchen p4: 0.98 vs. 0.20), confirming that multi-scale tokenization resolves the myopic next-token limitation. Against Diffusion Policy [7] (both DP-C and DP-T variants), CARP matches or slightly exceeds success rates (e.g., 0.98 vs. 0.94 on Square state-based; 0.88 vs. 0.92 on Square image-based) while running ~10× faster (3.07s vs. 35–38s) with 1–5% of the parameters. On the multi-task MimicGen benchmark, CARP surpasses SDP [47] by 9% in average success rate (0.85 vs. 0.78) and TCD [24] by 17% (0.85 vs. 0.68), with the largest gains on fine-grained manipulation tasks like Nut Assembly (0.78 vs. 0.54) and Mug Cleanup (0.74 vs. 0.62). CARP also significantly outperforms IBC [11] on image-based tasks (e.g., Can: 0.98 vs. 0.02; Square: 0.88 vs. 0.00). The one area where CARP trails is the Hammer task in multi-task settings (0.98 vs. 1.00 for SDP), though the gap is marginal.

## Relation to Prior Work

CARP directly repurposes the next-scale prediction paradigm from VAR (Visual Autoregressive Modeling) [44]—originally designed for image generation—into the domain of robot action sequence prediction, replacing per-token autoregression with a coarse-to-fine, multi-scale refinement process. The multi-scale action tokenization stage builds on VQVAE [46] with a residual-style quantization layer adapted from [44], while the prediction stage adopts a GPT-2-style decoder-only transformer. Conceptually, the paper positions itself as a synthesis: it inherits diffusion policy's [7] implicit coarse-to-fine refinement philosophy but implements it through discrete token prediction with relaxed Markovian constraints, rather than continuous denoising, and retains autoregressive policy's [39] single-pass inference efficiency.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× inference speedup over diffusion policies while matching or exceeding their success rates is practically transformative for compute-constrained robotic deployment (e.g., on-board GPUs), and the GPT-style architecture enables trivial multi-task extension via task embeddings—a flexibility diffusion-based policies lack. Open questions include whether the multi-scale VQVAE tokenization generalizes to higher-dimensional or continuous action spaces (e.g., dexterous hands), whether the approach scales to significantly longer horizons beyond 400 steps, and whether the Cross-Entropy loss in latent space preserves the full multimodal structure that diffusion models capture in continuous space. The evaluation, while broad, is limited to relatively simple manipulation tasks; performance on contact-rich or highly dynamic tasks remains untested.
