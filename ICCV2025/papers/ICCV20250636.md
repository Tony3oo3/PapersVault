## Problem

Existing low-light image enhancement methods are typically trained on specific datasets and lack cross-domain generalization, performing poorly under unseen lighting conditions. Moreover, the coupled challenges of color distortion, detail loss, and noise in low-light images are difficult for a single monolithic network to address simultaneously, as optimizing one aspect (e.g., noise suppression) often degrades another (e.g., detail preservation). No prior work has applied a mixture-of-experts framework to dynamically balance these competing enhancement objectives.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250636/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The network accepts a low-light RGB image of shape H×W×3 and produces an enhanced RGB image of the same spatial dimensions, computed as the sum of the input and a learned residual (Î = I + R). During training, inputs are resized to 256×256 patches with random rotation and flip augmentation, and the model is trained for 2×10⁶ iterations with batch size 4 on a single NVIDIA 4090 GPU. Evaluation is conducted on five benchmarks (LOLv1, LOLv2-Real, LOLv2-Synthetic, LSRW-Huawei, LSRW-Nikon) using PSNR and SSIM.

## Method

GM-MoE builds on a U-Net encoder-decoder backbone and inserts a Gated-Mechanism Mixture-of-Experts module at each encoder-decoder level. The GM-MoE module contains three specialized sub-experts—color restoration (pooling + deconvolution + Sigmoid), detail enhancement (channel + spatial attention), and advanced feature enhancement (multi-scale convolution + gating + channel attention)—whose outputs are combined via learned weights s₁, s₂, s₃ that sum to 1. A gated weight generation network produces these weights by passing adaptively pooled input features through two fully connected layers, enabling the model to dynamically re-weight experts based on the lighting and scene characteristics of each input. A Shallow Feature Extraction Block (SFEB) at the front end uses parallel depthwise-separable and dilated convolutions with a channel-attention mechanism to capture multi-scale shallow features before they enter the U-Net. Training is supervised with a PSNR-based loss (negative log-MSE) optimized by Adam with a multi-step learning rate schedule.

## Results

GM-MoE achieves the best PSNR across all five benchmark datasets: 26.66 dB on LOL-v1, 23.65 dB on LOLv2-Real, 26.30 dB on LOLv2-Synthetic, 23.55 dB on LSRW-Huawei, and 22.62 dB on LSRW-Nikon. On SSIM, it leads on LOL-v1 (0.857), LSRW-Huawei (0.741), and LSRW-Nikon (0.700), but trails DPEC on LOLv2-Real (0.806 vs. 0.863) and LOLv2-Synthetic (0.937 vs. 0.939). The model uses 19.99M parameters, comparable to Restormer (26.13M) and MIRNet (31.76M), while being substantially larger than lightweight methods like DPEC (2.58M) or RetinexFormer (1.61M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GM-MoE achieves the highest PSNR on all five evaluated datasets, with the largest margins on LSRW-Nikon (+1.42 dB over Restormer) and LOL-v1 (+0.90 dB over LLFormer). On the LOL benchmarks, DPEC is the closest competitor: GM-MoE edges it out by 0.76 dB PSNR on LOLv2-Real and 0.11 dB on LOLv2-Synthetic, but DPEC retains a notable SSIM advantage on both (0.863 vs. 0.806 and 0.939 vs. 0.937 respectively). On the LSRW real-world datasets, Restormer is the strongest baseline, and GM-MoE surpasses it by 0.94 dB PSNR and 0.016 SSIM on LSRW-Huawei, and by 1.42 dB PSNR and 0.023 SSIM on LSRW-Nikon. Transformer-based methods (Restormer, RetinexFormer, LLFormer) and the lightweight DPEC are the primary competitors; traditional and early deep methods (SID, RetinexNet, Kind, LIME, MF, NPE, SRIE) are consistently outperformed by 5–12 dB in PSNR. The gated MoE design gives GM-MoE a clear advantage in cross-domain generalization, particularly on the LSRW datasets captured by different camera brands, where single-architecture methods show larger performance drops.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the U-Net encoder-decoder architecture as its backbone and introduces the Mixture-of-Experts paradigm (originally from Jacobs et al., 1991) into low-light image enhancement for the first time. Prior LLIE methods—whether CNN-based (RetinexNet, Kind, SNR-Net) or Transformer-based (Restormer, RetinexFormer, UFormer)—rely on a single monolithic network to simultaneously address color distortion, detail loss, and noise, which the authors argue leads to conflicting optimization objectives. GM-MoE departs from this by decomposing the enhancement problem into three specialized sub-experts (color restoration, detail enhancement, advanced feature enhancement) whose outputs are combined via a learned gated weighting mechanism that adapts to input domain characteristics. The shallow feature extraction block and attention mechanisms (channel + spatial) draw on standard components from the broader image restoration literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The first application of MoE to LLIE offers a principled way to decouple competing enhancement objectives (noise suppression vs. detail preservation vs. color fidelity) that have been a persistent tension in single-network approaches. The gated mechanism's cross-domain adaptability addresses a real practical gap, as most prior methods degrade sharply on unseen datasets. However, the paper relies solely on PSNR loss without perceptual or adversarial terms, reports no real-time inference speed, and the gated mechanism's marginal ablation gain (~0.3 dB) suggests the architectural decomposition rather than the dynamic gating drives most of the improvement. Open questions include whether the MoE formulation generalizes to other restoration tasks (dehazing, denoising) and whether the three-expert decomposition is optimal or whether more/fewer experts would help.
