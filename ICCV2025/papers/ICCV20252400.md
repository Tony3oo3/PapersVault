## Problem

Reconstructing tree branching topologies (skeletons) from 3D point clouds remains difficult when dense foliage causes heavy self-occlusion, as regression and geometric methods fail on missing branch segments. Additionally, the field lacks real-world datasets with reference skeletons, forcing evaluation on synthetic data that does not validate generalization to actual orchard or forest environments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252400/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a 3D point cloud of an entire tree including leaves (acquired via terrestrial laser scanner, with position and color) and outputs a directed graph whose vertices are 3D positions on the branch medial axes and whose edges encode parent-child connectivity. Rather than predicting edges directly, the model outputs per-node flow directions (unit vectors toward the parent), and connectivity is recovered post-hoc via a minimum spanning tree, so the graph is an implicit representation decoded from 6D node descriptors (position + flow direction).

## Method

The approach formulates tree skeletonization as a conditional denoising diffusion task over a set of D nodes, each represented as a 6D vector (3D position + 3D flow direction). Using a point-wise local diffusion formulation (noise added per-point rather than from pure Gaussian), a MinkUnet-based noise predictor is trained with classifier-free guidance to denoise a randomly initialized node set toward the target skeleton, conditioned on the input point cloud. After T denoising steps, the predicted node positions and flow directions are converted into a complete graph, and Kruskal's algorithm with edge weights defined as the dot product of the inter-node vector and the source node's flow direction yields the minimum spanning tree that constitutes the final skeleton. A post-processing step removes edges exceeding a length threshold and prunes disconnected components below a minimum node count.

## Results

On the real-world BReTS orchard dataset (280 apple trees, 10 scans per tree), DiffTS achieves 4.19 cm Chamfer distance, 41.68% precision, 39.05% recall, and 38.62% F1-score, outperforming all baselines on Chamfer distance, precision, and F1-score. On the synthetic TreeNet3D multi-variety benchmark (13,000 trees, 10 species), DiffTS achieves 0.79 cm Chamfer distance and 84.78% F1-score, the best among all compared methods. On the smaller synthetic Smart-Tree apple dataset (100 samples), DiffTS achieves 4.44 cm Chamfer distance (best) and 35.61% F1-score (second-best), with the limited training data being a noted constraint for the diffusion model.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffTS outperforms all four baselines on the BReTS real-world dataset in Chamfer distance (4.19 vs. 4.60–6.51 cm), precision (41.68% vs. 14.99–24.35%), and F1-score (38.62% vs. 17.19–25.72%), but trails AdTree on recall (39.05% vs. 54.96%) because AdTree generates substantially more nodes that cover the entire scan. On TreeNet3D, DiffTS achieves the best Chamfer distance (0.79 cm) and F1-score (84.78%), beating PC-Skeletor (1.37 cm, 79.00%) which uses ground-truth semantic labels as input, and AdTree (0.91 cm, 79.30%) which wins on recall (93.72%) due to over-predicting branches. On the Smart-Tree synthetic apple dataset, DiffTS achieves the best Chamfer distance (4.44 cm) but is a close second in F1-score (35.61% vs. AdTree's 36.06%), with Smart-Tree [13] showing high precision (78.72%) but very low recall (6.53%) indicating it misses most branches. The consistent advantage of DiffTS on real data over synthetic data highlights the benefit of the generative diffusion formulation under heavy foliage occlusion, where geometric and regression-based methods degrade significantly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the point-wise local diffusion formulation from Nunes et al. (CVPR 2024), originally designed for 3D LiDAR scene completion, to the tree skeletonization task by predicting node positions and flow directions instead of scene occupancy. It departs from the dominant regression-based and geometric thinning approaches (AdTree, LBC, PC-Skeletor, Smart-Tree) that directly estimate medial axes from observed points, instead treating skeletonization as a conditional generative problem where a DDPM learns the distribution of tree topologies and can infer occluded branches. The graph construction step repurposes Kruskal's minimum spanning tree algorithm with flow-direction-weighted edges, a classical technique applied here in a novel context to convert implicit node/flow predictions into an explicit tree graph.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The BReTS dataset (280 apple trees, 10 seasonal scans with reference skeletons) is the first real-world benchmark enabling quantitative evaluation of tree skeletonization, addressing a critical gap where prior work could only validate on synthetic data. The finding that a generative model substantially outperforms regression and geometric baselines under heavy foliage occlusion (F1: 38.6% vs. 25.7% on real data) suggests that learning a prior over tree structure is more effective than direct geometric inference when observations are incomplete. However, absolute performance remains low, and the 3,000-node limit imposed by memory constraints restricts resolution for highly branched canopies, leaving open whether the approach scales to larger or more structurally diverse trees in real orchards and forests.
