## Problem

Automated video dubbing requires synthesizing speech that is simultaneously natural, intelligible, speaker-matched, lip-synchronized, and expressively aligned with facial cues. Existing dubbing methods (HPMDubbing, StyleDubber) rely on FastSpeech-like decoders that produce low-naturalness, low-fidelity output, while Neural Codec Language Models (NCLMs) generate high-quality speech but lack any mechanism to accept video inputs or enforce temporal alignment with a target video.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252443/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes three inputs: a source speech clip (encoded to discrete Encodec tokens at 50 Hz on 16 kHz audio for voice characteristics), a target text string (phonemized via IPA and mapped to learnable embeddings), and a target video (full-face frames at 25 fps for expression context plus a lip-cropped region for timing cues). The video features are temporally upsampled 2× to match the 50 Hz speech token rate. The output is a synthesized speech waveform decoded from K=4 stacked RVQ codebooks via the Encodec decoder, with the number of generated tokens fixed to match the target video length.

## Method

VoiceCraft-Dub extends the VoiceCraft NCLM by inserting audio-visual (AV) fusion layers at each autoregressive timestep rather than prepending video tokens (which empirically degrades attention over longer sequences). Lip features from AV-HuBERT and facial features from EmoFAN are projected into the NCLM token space via two-layer MLP adapters, then fused with the currently generated speech token through channel-wise concatenation and a linear AVFuse layer. Critically, the speech token at timestep t is fused with the *next* timestep's (t+1) lip and face tokens, giving the model a one-step visual preview to produce temporally aligned output. The fused residual is added to the speech token before it enters a 16-layer GPT-style Transformer decoder that uses delayed prediction across the 4 RVQ codebooks. The decoder is initialized from pretrained VoiceCraft weights and fine-tuned with a weighted negative log-likelihood loss (α=[3,1,1,1] emphasizing the first codebook), while adapters, AV fusion layers, and the decoder are jointly trained; AV-HuBERT, EmoFAN, and Encodec remain frozen.

## Results

On LRS3, VoiceCraft-Dub (lip&face) achieves a WER of 1.68, LSE-D of 6.87, LSE-C of 7.74, speaker similarity of 0.373, and UTMOS of 3.86, closely matching ground-truth values (WER 1.79, LSE-D 6.88, LSE-C 7.63, UTMOS 3.59). On the more expressive CelebV-Dub dataset, the model achieves WER 7.01, LSE-C 6.05, and UTMOS 3.37, again outperforming both HPMDubbing and StyleDubber across nearly all metrics. In human MOS evaluation, VoiceCraft-Dub scores 4.30 in naturalness and 4.37 in lip-sync on LRS3, compared to 3.12 and 3.96 for HPMDubbing and 2.85 and 3.83 for StyleDubber, approaching ground-truth scores of 4.42 and 4.45.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VoiceCraft-Dub decisively outperforms both HPMDubbing and StyleDubber on LRS3: WER drops from 7.19/3.25 to 1.68, speaker similarity rises from 0.219/0.295 to 0.373, and UTMOS improves from 3.08/2.71 to 3.86, while lip-sync (LSE-D/C) remains on par with ground truth. The only metric where HPMDubbing edges ahead is LSE-D (6.58 vs. 6.87), though the authors note SyncNet's instability and low correlation with human judgment. Against the zero-shot TTS baseline (VoiceCraft), VoiceCraft-Dub trades a small WER increase (1.68 vs. 0.68) for dramatically better lip-sync (LSE-D 6.87 vs. 10.41; LSE-C 7.74 vs. 4.04) and higher UTMOS (3.86 vs. 3.48), confirming that visual cues are essential for dubbing. In the video-to-speech extension (Table 6), VoiceCraft-Dub (lip-only) achieves WER 28.83 and UTMOS 3.52, substantially outperforming SVTS (WER 82.38, UTMOS 1.28) and Intelligible (WER 30.00, UTMOS 2.70), while slightly trailing the VSR+TTS pipeline on WER (28.83 vs. 29.72) but matching its overall quality. In A/B testing, humans prefer VoiceCraft-Dub over HPMDubbing 87.4% of the time on naturalness and over StyleDubber 96.6%, with over 40% of raters finding it comparable to or better than ground truth.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Neural Codec Language Models (NCLMs) — previously applied to zero-shot TTS, speech editing, and voice conversion (VoiceCraft, VALL-E, AudioLM) — to the automated video dubbing task by injecting visual cues through a novel audio-visual fusion mechanism. Prior video dubbing methods (HPMDubbing, StyleDubber, Neural Dubber, VisualTTS) relied on FastSpeech-like mel-spectrogram decoders with forced alignment; this work departs from that lineage by leveraging the in-context learning strength of NCLMs over discrete codec tokens. The key architectural departure is fusing lip (AV-HuBERT) and facial expression (EmoFAN) features with speech tokens at each timestep via residual AV fusion layers, rather than simply prepending video tokens to the decoder input, which the ablation shows degrades performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that NCLMs can produce video-dubbed speech that is nearly indistinguishable from ground truth in human evaluation, opening a path toward more natural and expressive dubbing than FastSpeech-based pipelines. The CelebV-Dub dataset fills a gap by providing expressive, in-the-wild videos (vlogs, dramas, movies) beyond the neutral-expression LRS3. Open questions remain: the model sometimes prioritizes source-speech prosody over facial cues, autoregressive generation can produce incorrect tokens, and explicit control over emotional state or speech duration is not yet supported. The reliance on SyncNet for lip-sync evaluation is acknowledged as unstable, leaving a gap in objective lip-sync measurement.
