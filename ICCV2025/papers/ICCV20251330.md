## Problem

A conventional lens focuses only a single plane, forcing a trade-off between depth of field and light throughput: smaller apertures increase DoF but reduce light and introduce diffraction blur. This paper asks whether one can optically program a focal *surface* that adapts to arbitrary scene geometry, bringing an entire non-planar scene into sharp focus simultaneously without computational post-processing.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251330/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system captures raw images through a Split-Lohmann computational lens (two cubic phase plates + Holoeye GAEA-2 phase-only SLM at 4160×2464, 3.74 μm pitch) onto a Canon R10 dual-pixel sensor (6000×4000, 3.72 μm pitch). It requires as few as two images: one to estimate the scene depth map and a second to optically form the all-in-focus image on the sensor. The depth map is a byproduct of the autofocus loop, and the AIF image is produced entirely by the optics—no deconvolution, compositing, or other post-capture processing is applied.

## Method

The optical core is a Split-Lohmann lens: two cubic phase plates (κx³ and −κx³) are optically collocated via a 4f relay, and a phase-only SLM at the Fourier plane displays a spatially-varying phase ramp whose local slope sets the effective focal length per pixel, enabling independent focus control across the field of view. To drive this lens, the paper extends two classical autofocus techniques to the spatially-varying setting. Contrast Detection Autofocus (CDAF) performs a binary search over the diopter range per SLIC superpixel, capturing three images per iteration at quarter, half, and three-quarter positions of the current range, halving the search interval each step. Phase Detection Autofocus (PDAF) exploits the dual-pixel sensor to compute signed disparity in a single shot via a conjugate-gradient optical-flow solver; depth-boundary ambiguities are resolved by segmenting the image with Segment Anything and computing masked optical flow per semantic layer before summing into a unified flow map. The recovered depth map is then encoded as the SLM phase pattern, so the lens optically focuses every scene point at its correct depth in one exposure.

## Results

On the Adventure and Rainbow scenes, phase-based autofocus (PDAF) achieves the best PSNR/SSIM with only 4 photos (3 iterations), followed by contrast-based autofocus (CDAF) with 10 photos (3 iterations), both comparable to a 20-photo computational focal stack. In MTF analysis (Fig. 12), both phase- and contrast-based methods show spatial resolution comparable to the computational focal stack, while focal sweep consistently underperforms all other methods. The prototype operates at a rest-state f/6.8, with light throughput dropping to 76% at the most extreme focus settings due to the effective aperture reduction from the cubic phase plates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the computational focal stack (Nayar & Nakagawa [23]; Suwajanakorn et al. [35]), the proposed method achieves comparable MTF (spatial resolution) while requiring far fewer captures: 4 photos for PDAF or 10 for CDAF versus 20 for the focal stack. Crucially, the proposed method forms the AIF image optically on the sensor with no post-capture deconvolution or compositing, whereas the focal stack requires computational blending. Against focal sweep (Nagahara et al. [22]), the proposed method consistently outperforms in MTF across all tested depths, and additionally outputs a depth map (focal sweep does not). The paper's key advantage over both baselines is the combination of high optical sharpness (maintaining a large effective aperture, unlike small-aperture or coded-aperture methods) with the ability to produce a freeform, scene-adaptive focal surface in as few as two images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the Split-Lohmann multifocal display (Qin et al., 2023), originally designed for VR, into a camera system by replacing the OLED with a sensor and adding an objective lens, thereby converting a display-side spatially-varying focus mechanism into an imaging-side one. It then extends two classical autofocus paradigms—contrast detection (CDAF) and phase detection (PDAF)—from their traditional scalar (single-focus-setting) formulation to a spatially-varying, per-superpixel setting, using SLIC superpixels for CDAF and Segment-Anything-based layered optical flow for PDAF. The optical hardware lineage traces back to the Lohmann/Alvarez varifocal lens (1970), while the algorithmic lineage draws on standard autofocus and optical flow literature. The paper explicitly positions itself against coded-aperture, focal-stacking, light-field, and dual-pixel deblurring methods, arguing that its all-optical approach uniquely preserves full spatial resolution without computational post-processing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key contribution is demonstrating that an all-in-focus image can be formed purely optically at full sensor resolution with as few as two captures, sidestepping the resolution-vs-depth tradeoff inherent in light fields and coded apertures and the multi-image cost of focal stacking. However, the prototype is severely light-limited (≤1/8 throughput due to polarization and beamsplitter losses), and aberration correction is left as future work, so practical deployment remains distant. Open questions include whether end-to-end differentiable optical design can fix residual aberrations, whether the approach scales to larger fields of view or higher frame rates, and whether the light-efficiency bottleneck can be overcome with alternative phase-modulation hardware. The freeform depth-of-field capability (e.g., optical tilt-shift, selective region focus) suggests broader creative and industrial applications beyond AIF.
