## Problem

Existing flatness-based adversarial attack methods (e.g., PGN, TPA) use the current gradient as the perturbation direction to locate flat regions, but this causes the perturbed point to fall in excessively sharp areas within the perturbation radius. Optimizing flatness at these overly sharp points fails to flatten the broader loss surface, limiting adversarial transferability. The paper identifies that prior arts overlook the influence of perturbation directions on the effectiveness of flatness-based regularization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (224×224 from ImageNet validation set or Market-1501) and its true class label, and produces an L-infinity-bounded adversarial example with ε=16. Adversarial examples are crafted on a white-box surrogate model (Inc-v3, Res-50, or Den-121) and evaluated for transferability against up to eight target models including CNNs, Transformers, and adversarially trained ensembles. The method also supports integration with input transformation pipelines (DIM, TIM, SIM, Admix, SSA) that augment the sampling neighborhood.

## Method

ResPA replaces the current gradient with a residual gradient as the perturbation direction for the flatness term. It maintains an exponential moving average (EMA) of historical gradients as a reference gradient, then computes the residual gradient as the difference between the current gradient and this reference. In sharp regions where the current gradient fluctuates rapidly, the stable EMA reference suppresses the residual, steering the perturbed point away from the steepest directions and toward regions beneficial to global flatness. The resulting flatness term is incorporated as a regularization (weighted by γ) into the loss maximization objective, jointly optimizing both the adversarial loss and the perturbed-point loss. The update rule combines momentum, the averaged gradient over N sampled neighborhood points, and sign-based projection back into the ε-ball.

## Results

On ImageNet (1,000 validation images), ResPA achieves average attack success rates of 64.6%, 84.6%, and 87.5% when generating adversarial examples on Inc-v3, Res-50, and Den-121 respectively, outperforming the strongest baseline PGN (63.6%, 84.2%, 87.2%) by 1.0%, 0.4%, and 0.3%. When combined with input transformations (e.g., DIM, TIM, SIM), ResPA boosts average ASR by 4.2%–20.3% over the standalone transformations, reaching up to 91.0% (DIM+ResPA) on eight target models. On the Market-1501 Re-ID benchmark, ResPA achieves the lowest average Rank-1 (26.45%) and mAP (18.77%), indicating the strongest attack among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ResPA consistently outperforms all six gradient-based baselines (MI, VMI, GRA, PGN, AdaMSI, TPA) across all three surrogate models on ImageNet. The closest competitor is PGN [10], which ResPA surpasses by 1.0% (Inc-v3 surrogate), 0.4% (Res-50), and 0.3% (Den-121) in average ASR; the gap widens to 5.0% over GRA and 4.3% over TPA when using Inc-v3 as surrogate. AdaMSI [24] is the weakest baseline, trailing ResPA by 18.0% (Inc-v3 surrogate) and 20.6% (Res-50 surrogate). When combined with input transformations, ResPA improves DIM from 79.6% to 91.0%, TIM from 70.6% to 90.9%, SIM from 77.3% to 89.0%, Admix from 70.2% to 81.8%, and SSA from 82.4% to 86.6% in average ASR. On the Market-1501 Re-ID task, ResPA achieves the best (lowest) average Rank-1 of 26.45% and mAP of 18.77%, beating TPA [7] (27.03% / 19.20%) and BSR [36] (31.14% / 22.12%). Against seven defense mechanisms, ResPA attains 59.8% average ASR, exceeding PGN (58.8%), TPA (56.5%), and GRA (54.8%).

## Relation to Prior Work

ResPA extends the flatness-based adversarial attack lineage (PGN, TPA, and related methods) that guides adversarial examples toward flat regions of the loss landscape to improve transferability. The key departure is identifying that these methods' use of the current gradient as the perturbation direction can inadvertently steer the search into excessively sharp sub-regions within the perturbation radius, undermining the flatness objective. ResPA replaces this with a residual gradient—computed as the difference between the current gradient and an exponential moving average of historical gradients—as the perturbation direction, thereby capturing global directional changes rather than local steepness. The method is also positioned as a drop-in replacement compatible with existing input transformation techniques (DIM, TIM, SIM, Admix, SSA).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete diagnosis of why flatness-based attacks can underperform: over-reliance on the locally sharpest direction within the perturbation radius. The residual gradient mechanism is a lightweight, parameter-light modification that consistently improves transferability across CNN and Transformer architectures, as well as against adversarially trained models and seven defense mechanisms. However, the gains over the strongest baselines (PGN, TPA) are modest (roughly 1–5% average ASR), and the method's hyperparameters (θ, γ, β, N) require tuning. Open questions include whether the EMA-based reference gradient is the optimal way to capture global direction, whether the residual formulation generalizes to other loss-landscape objectives beyond flatness, and whether the approach extends to multi-step or query-based black-box settings.
