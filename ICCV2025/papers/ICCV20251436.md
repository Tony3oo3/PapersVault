## Problem

Medical vision-language pre-training suffers from a semantic density gap: diagnostic signals in CT volumes are sparse and diluted by large amounts of anatomical noise (low SNR), while radiology reports are highly condensed summaries (high SNR). This asymmetry causes visual alignment bias, where the model fails to attend to diagnostically relevant regions—particularly problematic in 3D abdominal CT where lesions may occupy less than one-thousandth of the volume. Existing methods have not adequately addressed how to amplify these sparse diagnostic cues in the visual embedding space before alignment.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 3D CT volumes resampled to 1×1×5 mm (HU clipped to [-1000, 1000], normalized to [0,1]) and randomly cropped to 256×384×96 patches, paired with free-text diagnostic reports. Reports are decomposed into anatomy-specific sub-reports via Qwen LLM, and CT volumes are segmented into 36 primary anatomies using TotalSegmentator (grouped from 104 structures). The pre-trained model outputs per-anatomy visual embeddings that support zero-shot diagnosis across 54 diseases in 15 organs, radiology report generation via a BLIP-style text decoder, and linear-probing multi-disease classification.

## Method

ViSD-Boost addresses the semantic density gap through two complementary mechanisms. First, disease-level contrastive learning uses LLM-extracted normal/abnormal labels per anatomy to shape the embedding distribution: normal samples of the same organ are pulled together, while abnormal samples are pushed apart from each other (not merely from normal), preserving fine-grained distinctions among different lesion types; a momentum encoder with data augmentation provides positive pairs to prevent collapse. Second, anatomical normality modeling employs a Transformer-based VQ-VAE trained in the latent space (not image space) with per-anatomy condition tokens, where a codebook of M×100 vectors (dim 1024) is updated via EMA exclusively from normal samples; abnormal samples then produce reconstruction errors that amplify diagnostically relevant signals, and an MLP perception module fuses the original and reconstructed embeddings to yield denser visual tokens. Training proceeds in four phases: (1) disease-level contrastive pre-training of the 3D ResNet-18 vision encoder, (2) anatomy-wise image-report alignment with BERT, (3) VQ-VAE training with frozen vision encoder, and (4) end-to-end fine-tuning with frozen VQ-VAE.

## Results

On the MedVL-CT69K abdomen CT benchmark, ViSD-Boost achieves an average AUC of 84.9% across 54 diseases in 15 organs in zero-shot diagnosis, surpassing the second-best method fVLM (81.3%) by 3.6%. On chest CT, the method reaches 79.0% AUC on CT-RATE (internal) and 69.4% AUC on Rad-ChestCT (external), outperforming fVLM by 1.2% and 1.4% respectively. For radiology report generation in finetuning mode, ViSD-Boost achieves F1 of 40.9% and CIDEr of 50.7, substantially exceeding fVLM (F1 32.7%, CIDEr 17.1%). In linear-probing multi-disease liver classification, the method attains 88.1% AUC versus 77.2% for CLIP, with a 16.6% sensitivity gain.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ViSD-Boost outperforms all 10 baselines across every evaluated task and dataset. The closest competitor, fVLM [35], is surpassed by 3.6% AUC on MedVL-CT69K (84.9 vs 81.3), 1.2% on CT-RATE (79.0 vs 77.8), and 1.4% on Rad-ChestCT (69.4 vs 68.0), with the largest per-organ gains in Lung (+10.1 AUC) and Portal Vein. Among 2D-origin methods adapted to 3D, CLIP [34] and LOVT [30] trail by 16.5% and 15.5% AUC respectively on the abdomen benchmark, confirming the advantage of anatomy-wise fine-grained alignment. BIUD [5] and Merlin [3], the strongest 3D CT VLP baselines, are beaten by 13.5% and 13.0% AUC on MedVL-CT69K. In report generation, ViSD-Boost's CIDEr score of 50.7 (finetuning) nearly triples fVLM's 17.1 and exceeds Merlin's 17.2 by 33.5 points, while MAE [9] and supervised baselines remain far behind on all clinical and linguistic metrics. The ablation confirms that the vision semantic density boosting module alone contributes a 6% AUC gain over the fVLM-aligned baseline (76.5% → 82.5%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the anatomy-wise image-report alignment framework introduced by fVLM [35] by adding two complementary components: disease-level contrastive learning for visual semantic enhancement and a VQ-VAE-based anatomical normality modeling module for density boosting. The VQ-VAE component repurposes the discrete representation learning paradigm from VQ-VAE [37] but departs from it in two key ways: operating in the latent embedding space rather than pixel space, and introducing anatomy-conditioned multi-distribution learning to handle dozens of anatomical structures simultaneously. Conceptually, the paper frames the underperformance of medical VLP through the lens of a 'semantic density gap' between low-SNR images and high-SNR reports, positioning its contributions as a targeted remedy for visual alignment bias rather than a new alignment objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The semantic density framing provides a useful diagnostic for why medical VLP lags behind natural-image VLP, and the anatomy normality modeling via VQ-VAE offers a reusable technique for amplifying sparse abnormal signals in any low-SNR imaging modality. The 3.6% AUC gain over fVLM on MedVL-CT69K (84.9% vs. 81.3% across 54 diseases in 15 organs) and consistent improvements on chest CT benchmarks suggest the approach generalizes across body regions. Open questions include whether the method's reliance on TotalSegmentator for anatomy parsing and Qwen for report decomposition introduces compounding errors at scale, whether the VQ-VAE codebook (100 vectors per anatomy) is sufficient for rare pathologies, and whether the four-stage training pipeline can be simplified or made end-to-end.
