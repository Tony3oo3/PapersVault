## Problem

Despite growing interest in applying LVLMs to specialized domains, the insurance sector—characterized by diverse multimodal application scenarios across auto, property, health, and agricultural insurance—lacks both a systematic taxonomy of its multimodal tasks and a dedicated benchmark for evaluating LVLM capabilities. Prior work has only qualitatively discussed LVLM applications in insurance without organizing relevant tasks or providing a structured evaluation framework, hindering comprehensive assessment and broader adoption.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252504/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each benchmark item pairs one or more insurance-related images (sourced from 27 public datasets, up to 500 instances per task) with a text question. Fundamental tasks use multiple-choice format (4 options) where the model outputs a letter answer, while scenario tasks use free-text format requiring the model to produce a structured, step-by-step reasoning chain ending in a final insurance decision (e.g., claim approval, liability assignment, or premium estimate). Evaluation of free-text responses is step-wise: classification steps are graded by regex-based exact matching, and numerical steps (e.g., predicted premiums) are scored by mean deviation from ground-truth values.

## Method

The paper introduces a bottom-up hierarchical task definition methodology that constructs a three-layer framework: 22 fundamental tasks (recognizing individual visual elements like license plates or damage types), 12 meta-tasks (compositional understanding combining multiple elements), and 5 scenario tasks (end-to-end insurance workflows requiring multi-step reasoning), spanning auto, property, health, and agricultural insurance. The benchmark construction pipeline collects data from public sources (Google, Kaggle, GitHub, Hugging Face, Zenodo, Roboflow), applies stratified or random sampling, structures raw inputs into unified CSV files, and constructs questions under insurance expert guidance. For multiple-choice questions with complex labels, GPT-4o generates semantically plausible distractors; for scenario questions, experts decompose real-world insurance processes into structured reasoning steps with manually written reference answers. Evaluation is conducted via an extended VLMEvalKit supporting both question formats, with a human baseline from six participants (3 graduate students + 3 industry professionals) answering 220 sampled questions.

## Results

Across 11 evaluated LVLMs on INS-MMBench (12,052 images, 10,372 questions), GPT-4o achieves the highest overall accuracy of 69.70%, surpassing the human baseline of 60.45%. Performance varies dramatically by insurance type: GPT-4o scores 86.00% on auto insurance but drops to 36.38% on agricultural insurance. No model exceeds 70% on fundamental tasks, and in complex scenario tasks (Table 5), accuracy falls below 50% for most tasks except agricultural claim processing (~84%). The open-source Qwen-2.5-VL-32B (64.10% overall) narrows the gap with closed-source models, while LLaVA trails significantly at 46.99%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o leads all 11 models on overall accuracy (69.70%) and dominates in auto insurance (86.00%) and health insurance (76.73%), outperforming the next-best Qwen-VL-Max by 4.4 and 6.1 points respectively. However, GPT-4o still underperforms the human baseline on agricultural insurance (36.38% vs. 42.50%) and vehicle appearance recognition (98.50% vs. 59.50% human — here GPT-4o actually exceeds humans). Among open-source models, Qwen-2.5-VL-32B (64.10%) approaches closed-source Gemini 1.5 Flash (64.21%) and even surpasses GPT-4o on medical image recognition (70.40% vs. 66.50%). LLaVA (46.99%) and Qwen-VL-Chat (48.85%) trail all closed-source models by 15–23 points, with LLaVA notably weak on auto insurance (45.47%). In scenario tasks, GPT-4o achieves the best final claim decision accuracy (84.15% on agricultural claims) but still falls below 50% on auto accident liability determination (9.26% responsible party identification), indicating that no current LVLM reliably handles multi-step insurance reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the domain-specific benchmark paradigm (exemplified by OmniMedVQA for healthcare, MathVista for math, and Talk2BEV-Bench for autonomous driving) to the insurance sector, which the authors note has received only qualitative, non-systematic treatment in prior work [48]. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the general LVLM evaluation infrastructure (VLMEvalKit) and introduces a bottom-up hierarchical task taxonomy (fundamental → meta → scenario) grounded in insurance value chain theory. The benchmark is assembled from existing public datasets across 27 sources rather than introducing new raw data, with the novelty lying in the task organization, question design, and domain-specific evaluation protocol.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

INS-MMBench provides the first systematic, reproducible evaluation framework for LVLMs in insurance, enabling the industry to identify where current models are deployable (auto and health insurance perception tasks) versus where they fall short (property/agricultural insurance, multi-step scenario reasoning). Key open questions include whether domain-specific fine-tuning or RAG can close the ~60% knowledge/reasoning error gap, and whether the lack of real insurance-case imagery (due to privacy) limits ecological validity. The finding that no model exceeds 70% on fundamental tasks and that scenario accuracy drops below 50% for most tasks sets a realistic baseline for staged industry adoption.
