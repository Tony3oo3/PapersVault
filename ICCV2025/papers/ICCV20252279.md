## Problem

GS-based rendering methods (e.g., 3DGS with BRDF decomposition) achieve realistic novel view synthesis under varied lighting but produce inaccurate surfaces due to misaligned Gaussian centers, while GS-based reconstruction methods (e.g., 2DGS, GOF) constrain primitives to surfaces at the cost of rendering quality and are sensitive to illumination changes. The paper asks whether rendering and reconstruction must always involve a trade-off, and proposes that they can instead be mutually enhanced.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images captured under significantly varying light conditions (shadows, specular reflections, overexposure, underexposure) together with known camera poses; no explicit lighting or material parameters are required. Beyond rendered novel views and a TSDF-fused textured mesh, the pipeline also outputs per-pixel depth maps and decomposed transmitted/reflected color components, enabling downstream relighting or geometry-aware editing.

## Method

MGSR maintains two parallel branches: a 2DGS branch (flattened Gaussian disks aligned to surfaces) and a 3DGS branch (standard 3D Gaussians augmented with reflected opacity, reflected confidence, and a reflected spherical-harmonic coefficient). The 3DGS branch performs unsupervised illumination decomposition, splitting each pixel's color into a transmitted component (original 3DGS) and a reflected component weighted by accumulated reflected confidence; the 2DGS branch supplies depth maps that guide this decomposition via a geometry-guided module, improving normal estimation and reducing reliance on BRDF priors. In the alternating optimization stage, the transmitted images from 3DGS supervise the 2DGS branch for lighting-invariant surface reconstruction, while depth maps from 2DGS regularize the 3DGS geometry for more accurate decomposition. An auto-stop warm-up strategy lets each branch converge independently before mutual-boosted alternating optimization begins, and unidirectional back-propagation is used so that the supervising branch is not perturbed by the supervised branch's gradients.

## Results

On OmniObject3D, MGSR achieves the best Normal Consistency (NC) of 90.60×10² and PSNR of 37.69 dB among methods supporting both NVS and SR, while on Shiny Blender it reaches NC 66.47×10² and PSNR 34.66 dB. On the DTU dataset, MGSR attains a mean NC of 53.08×10³, outperforming 2DGS (52.78) and GOF (51.09). Optimization time is approximately 13 minutes, comparable to 2DGS (12 min) and substantially faster than GShader (105 min) and GS-IR (33 min).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against 2DGS, MGSR consistently wins on NC (66.47 vs 65.48 on Shiny Blender; 90.60 vs 86.34 on OmniObject3D; 53.08 vs 52.78 on DTU) and PSNR (34.66 vs 33.46; 37.69 vs 34.09), while also dramatically reducing CD on Shiny Blender (2.85 vs 41.54). Against GOF, MGSR achieves substantially higher NC on Shiny Blender (66.47 vs 44.54) and a modest NC gain on OmniObject3D (90.60 vs 90.06), though GOF retains a slight CD advantage on both datasets (2.11 vs 2.85; 0.84 vs 0.90). For NVS-only baselines, MGSR surpasses 3D-GS in PSNR by 2.04 dB (Shiny Blender) and 4.05 dB (OmniObject3D), outperforms GS-IR on both SSIM and PSNR across both datasets, and edges out GShader and R3DG in PSNR (34.66 vs 34.35/34.20; 37.69 vs 34.14/37.37) while being 8× and 1.6× faster respectively. NeuS2 achieves the lowest CD values (0.51, 0.48) but provides no NVS capability and lower NC than MGSR on both object-level datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

MGSR sits at the intersection of two previously separate lines of Gaussian Splatting research: GS-based rendering methods (R3DG, GShader, GS-IR) that perform illumination decomposition but produce poor geometry, and GS-based surface reconstruction methods (2DGS, GOF, SuGaR) that enforce surface alignment at the cost of rendering fidelity. The paper builds directly on 2DGS (Huang et al., SIGGRAPH 2024) for its flattened-disk geometry representation and on 3DGS (Kerbl et al., 2023) for its volumetric rendering pipeline, but departs from both by coupling them into a mutual-boosted framework where 2DGS depth guides 3DGS illumination decomposition and 3DGS transmitted (reflection-free) images supervise 2DGS surface estimation. The geometry-guided illumination decomposition module, which uses 2DGS depth to anchor the reflected/transmitted split, is the key architectural departure from prior BRDF-based GS rendering that relied on per-Gaussian normal heuristics or depth-map priors.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MGSR demonstrates that the perceived trade-off between rendering quality and geometric accuracy in Gaussian Splatting can be mitigated through cross-branch supervision, offering a practical recipe for practitioners who need both photorealistic novel views and extractable meshes under challenging lighting (reflections, overexposure, specular highlights). The auto-stop warm-up strategy and the finding that unidirectional back-propagation outperforms bidirectional BP in the alternating loop are useful practical insights for multi-branch GS optimization. However, the paper acknowledges over-smoothness in low-light scenarios and the absence of exposure compensation, leaving open whether the mutual-boosted paradigm scales to large-scale outdoor scenes or dynamic content. The modest margins over strong baselines (e.g., ~0.5 NC over GOF on OmniObject3D) suggest the approach is a solid incremental advance rather than a paradigm shift.
