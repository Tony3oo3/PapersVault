## Problem

Conventional inverse rendering methods rely almost exclusively on RGB observations for material estimation, using LiDAR only for geometry. This makes albedo recovery highly ambiguous under variable lighting—shadows and illumination effects get baked into reflectance estimates. LiDAR intensity, which captures surface reflectance in a narrow infrared band largely decoupled from ambient illumination, remains underexploited as a complementary material cue.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251986/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single synchronized RGB+LiDAR sequence (one camera view plus 64-beam LiDAR with per-point intensity) along with calibrated camera and LiDAR poses. The LiDAR provides both geometric point clouds (used to initialize 3D Gaussian means) and sparse intensity maps that serve as material supervision. The output is a dynamic 3DGS scene graph where each Gaussian carries dual albedo channels (RGB and LiDAR), roughness, and normals, plus a spherical-harmonic sky and directional sun illumination model, from which novel-view RGB, LiDAR intensity, relit, and nighttime renderings can be produced.

## Method

InvRGB+L represents the scene as a dynamic scene graph of 3D Gaussians (static background nodes, per-object dynamic nodes with SE(3) pose trajectories, and a sky node) and performs differentiable inverse rendering. Two key innovations distinguish it: (1) a physics-based LiDAR reflectance model that extends the standard Lambertian assumption with a Cook-Torrance specular term, so that metallic and glossy surfaces produce realistic LiDAR intensity peaks, and (2) a pair of RGB-LiDAR albedo consistency losses—a bilateral smoothness loss that propagates sparse LiDAR albedo into a dense map guided by RGB reflectance, and a regional variance loss (using SAM superpixels) that enforces spectral-reflectance uniformity within regions of constant LiDAR albedo. Forward rendering uses BVH-based ray tracing for visibility and Monte Carlo sampling over the sky dome for RGB, while LiDAR intensity is computed analytically from the single laser ray. Training proceeds in two stages: first optimizing geometry and opacity, then fixing those and jointly refining material parameters and illumination, with diffusion-based priors (GeoWizard for normals, RGB-X for albedo/roughness) providing additional supervision.

## Results

On the Waymo Open Dataset, InvRGB+L achieves the lowest average Intensity-RMSE of 0.063 for novel-view LiDAR intensity simulation, outperforming the best baseline AlignMiF (0.073) by 14%. For relighting, the method reaches 30.42 PSNR / 0.72 SSIM / 0.30 LPIPS, surpassing UrbanIR (28.84 PSNR / 0.67 SSIM / 0.49 LPIPS) by 1.58 dB in PSNR. Ablations confirm that both the LiDAR reflectance input and the dynamic scene graph contribute meaningfully: removing LiDAR drops PSNR from 34.76 to 33.35, and removing dynamic modeling drops it to 29.13.

## Baselines & Comparisons

InvRGB+L outperforms all four LiDAR simulation baselines on average Intensity-RMSE: LiDARSim (0.120) and PCGEN (0.125) are roughly 2× worse, while the stronger implicit-field methods AlignMiF (0.073) and NFL (0.080) are still 14% and 27% worse respectively. On relighting, the method beats UrbanIR by 1.58 dB PSNR (30.42 vs 28.84) and 0.19 LPIPS (0.30 vs 0.49), with the RGB→LiDAR consistency loss contributing an additional 0.45 dB over the no-consistency variant. FEGR is compared qualitatively only; the paper reports that FEGR bakes hard shadows into albedo and produces unrealistic normals, while UrbanIR's radiance-based shadows cause visible relighting artifacts. Against the diffusion prior RGB-X, InvRGB+L's intrinsic 3D Gaussian albedo is more multi-view consistent and better recovers vehicle paint colors under cast shadows. The method wins on every quantitative comparison presented in the paper.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 3D-GS-based inverse rendering line (Relightable 3D Gaussians [11], UrbanIR [23], FEGR [41]) by repurposing LiDAR intensity—previously used only for geometric initialization—as an explicit material estimation cue. It departs from prior LiDAR simulation work (LiDARSim, PCGEN, AlignMiF, NFL), which relies on lookup tables or Lambertian assumptions, by introducing a Cook-Torrance-based LiDAR reflectance model with a specular term. The RGB-LiDAR albedo consistency loss is a novel formulation that has no direct precedent in the inverse rendering or LiDAR simulation literature, bridging the two communities through a shared physical model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The core insight—that narrow-band infrared LiDAR intensity is nearly immune to visible-light illumination changes and thus provides a robust, complementary material signal—opens a practical path to more reliable inverse rendering in uncontrolled outdoor environments. The nighttime data augmentation experiment (mAP 0.236→0.321) demonstrates immediate utility for autonomous driving perception without costly nighttime data collection. Key limitations remain: the illumination model (sky SH + single sun) cannot handle complex or nighttime lighting, the BVH ray tracer produces inaccurate shadows due to Gaussian opacity, and the single-illumination assumption restricts applicability to multi-condition sequences.
