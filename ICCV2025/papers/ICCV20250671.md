## Problem

Time profiling of advanced online VIS architectures reveals that the transformer decoder accounts for approximately 52% of inference time, making it the primary bottleneck for real-time deployment. Analysis of query outputs across adjacent frames at each decoder layer reveals substantial redundancy, yet no prior method exploits this temporal redundancy to dynamically skip unnecessary decoder layers in online VIS.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250671/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method processes video frames sequentially at 480p resolution (360p for MinVIS) on an NVIDIA 3090, requiring both the current frame's image features and the final instance queries from the previous frame to make per-layer routing decisions. The output consists of per-instance binary segmentation masks (N×H×W), class logits (N×C), and track associations via Hungarian matching between consecutive frames. Unusually, the skip/keep decision is made at the query level per decoder layer based on inter-frame query similarity, rather than relying on per-object confidence thresholds.

## Method

TAR introduces a Synchronizer and a Router inserted before each transformer decoder layer. The Synchronizer uses cross-attention to align the current frame's queries (after M^s warm-up layers) with the previous frame's final queries, producing synchronized queries that enable meaningful inter-frame comparison. The Router concatenates the synchronized current queries, previous frame queries, and their element-wise difference, then passes them through an MLP to produce a binary routing score per layer; argmax determines whether to skip or execute that layer. During training, the Straight-Through Gumbel-Softmax trick replaces argmax with a differentiable approximation, enabling end-to-end learning of the routing policy. The key empirical finding is that the number of skipped layers inversely correlates with inter-frame RGB MSE, confirming that TAR effectively leverages temporal redundancy to reduce computation while maintaining or improving segmentation accuracy.

## Results

On YouTubeVIS 2019 with a ResNet-50 backbone, TAR+MinVIS improves inference speed from 24.7 to 34.6 FPS (40.5→28.9 ms) while increasing AP from 47.4 to 48.4 and AR₁₀ from 55.7 to 58.5. TAR+DVIS++ similarly boosts speed from 22.4 to 32.8 FPS (44.7→30.5 ms) with AP rising from 55.5 to 55.7 on YouTubeVIS 2019 and from 50.0 to 50.8 on YouTubeVIS 2021. On OVIS (small backbone), TAR+MinVIS gains +2.4 AP (25.0→27.4) and +3.5 AR₁₀, while TAR+DVIS++ gains +0.9 AP (37.2→38.1). With large backbones (Swin-L/ViT-Adapter-L), TAR+MinVIS improves from 15.8 to 18.5 FPS and +0.9 AP on YouTubeVIS 2019, and TAR+DVIS++ from 7.4 to 8.0 FPS with +0.4 AP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MinVIS (the primary baseline), TAR consistently wins on both speed and accuracy: +9.9 FPS and +1.0 AP on YouTubeVIS 2019 (small backbone), +2.7 FPS and +0.9 AP (large backbone), and +2.4 AP on OVIS. Against DVIS++, TAR+DVIS++ achieves a large speed gain (+10.4 FPS small, +0.6 FPS large) with a marginal AP improvement (+0.2 on YT2019, +0.8 on YT2021), though it slightly drops AP₇₅ by 0.7–0.8 on YouTubeVIS. Compared to CTVIS and DVIS-DAQ, which achieve comparable or slightly higher AP, TAR+MinVIS/TAR+DVIS++ are 10–70× faster (34.6 vs 2.6 FPS; 32.8 vs 1.2 FPS), making them the only methods in the table suitable for real-time deployment. Against DVIS (ICCV 2023), TAR+MinVIS is faster (34.6 vs 23.9 FPS) with lower AP (48.4 vs 51.2), while TAR+DVIS++ dominates DVIS on both speed (32.8 vs 23.9 FPS) and AP (55.7 vs 51.2 on YT2019). Overall, TAR's key advantage is delivering near-free or slightly positive accuracy at a 30–45% reduction in inference latency, a trade-off none of the other baselines achieve.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: online video instance segmentation (building on MinVIS, DVIS, DVIS++, and CTVIS) and conditional computation / early-exit inference (drawing from ColT5, depth-adaptive transformers, and FastBERT). It departs from prior early-exit work by exploiting a task-specific temporal signal — inter-frame query similarity — rather than input complexity or prediction confidence to decide which transformer decoder layers to skip. The straight-through Gumbel-Softmax trick from Oord et al. (2017) is repurposed to make the discrete routing decision differentiable during training, and the Synchronizer module (cross-attention-based query alignment) is a novel architectural addition not present in prior VIS or early-exit literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is clear for latency-sensitive deployments (embodied intelligence, surgical robotics, autonomous driving) where even a 40% speedup in the decoder can be the difference between usable and unusable real-time performance. The plug-and-play nature of TAR (insert before each decoder layer, train briefly on adjacent frame pairs) lowers the adoption barrier. However, the method is validated on only two baselines and two backbone scales, and the performance gains are modest (≤2.4 AP), leaving open whether the routing generalizes to other transformer-based video tasks (e.g., video object detection, action recognition) or to architectures with significantly different decoder designs. A key open question is whether more aggressive skipping (e.g., skipping entire groups of layers) or learned continuous routing could yield larger efficiency gains without the current small performance trade-offs.
