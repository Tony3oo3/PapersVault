## Problem

Composing multiple pre-trained LoRA models to generate a single image containing distinct concepts (e.g., a specific cat and a specific dog) remains unsolved: weighted merging causes one concept to dominate or be ignored, and existing alternatives require training new LoRA variants, user-provided bounding boxes, or off-the-shelf segmentation. The root cause is attention overlap and attribute binding across LoRAs, where cross-attention maps for different subjects blend indistinctly, producing images with duplicated or misassigned features.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252028/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a user-provided text prompt (e.g., 'An L1 cat and an L2 dog in the forest') and a set of pre-trained LoRA models (typically 2–8, compatible with CivitAI community LoRAs) as input. It operates on Stable Diffusion v1.5 in latent space, decomposing the prompt into concept groups aligned with each LoRA and generating three prompt variants (original, L1-applied, L2-applied) to form contrastive pairs. The output is a composite RGB image where each LoRA's subject or style is faithfully rendered in its designated spatial region, with no additional conditioning (bounding boxes, ControlNet, or segmentation masks) required.

## Method

CLoRA is a training-free, test-time method that refines cross-attention maps during diffusion using an InfoNCE contrastive objective. It constructs concept groups from three prompt variants (original, L1-applied, L2-applied), treating attention maps within the same group as positive pairs and across groups as negative pairs, thereby encouraging each LoRA's attention to focus on its intended subject while repelling from others. The resulting loss drives iterative latent updates (z'_t = z_t − α_t ∇_{z_t} L) at selected diffusion steps, analogous to Attend-and-Excite. A second component, masked latent fusion, thresholds the refined attention maps into binary spatial masks that confine each LoRA's latent contribution to its relevant image region before combining latents from multiple LoRA-conditioned forward passes. This two-stage mechanism—contrastive attention segregation followed by mask-gated latent fusion—resolves both attention overlap and attribute binding without any parameter training or external controls.

## Results

CLoRA achieves the best scores across all quantitative metrics: DINO Average similarity of 0.554 (vs. 0.526 for Mix-of-Show, the strongest baseline), CLIP-I Average of 0.725, CLIP-T of 0.862, and a user study score of 3.32/5. The method also leads on the most challenging DINO Minimum similarity (0.447 vs. 0.407 for Mix-of-Show), indicating it preserves the less-dominant LoRA concept more faithfully. In terms of efficiency, CLoRA requires 25 GB VRAM and ~24 s inference for two LoRAs on an H100, scaling to 80 GB and 96 s for eight LoRAs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLoRA outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric. Against LoRA Merge [47] (simple weighted sum), CLoRA gains +0.082 on DINO Avg and +0.048 on CLIP-T, showing that naive weight merging fails to preserve both concepts. MultiLoRA [59] (both Composite and Switch variants) trails CLoRA by 0.175 on DINO Avg and 0.66 on user study score, confirming that test-time LoRA switching without attention refinement produces incoherent compositions. ZipLoRA [49] is the closest competitor on DINO Avg (0.496 vs. 0.554) but still lags by 0.058, and its design constraint of merging only one content + one style LoRA limits multi-content scenarios. Mix-of-Show [16] achieves the second-best DINO scores (0.526 Avg) but requires training specialized EDLoRA variants and external ControlNet conditioning, whereas CLoRA is training-free and uses off-the-shelf CivitAI LoRAs. Custom Diffusion [32] is compared qualitatively (Fig. 3) and frequently drops one of the two LoRA concepts entirely. OMG [30] is compared only on efficiency (Table 1b): it uses 30 GB VRAM and 62 s runtime versus CLoRA's 25 GB and 24 s, and depends on an external segmentation model whose accuracy directly affects output quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

CLoRA extends the authors' own prior contrastive attention-refinement work (CONFORM [37]) from a single diffusion model to the multi-LoRA setting, and adopts the iterative latent-update mechanism from Attend-and-Excite [5] and A-Star [1] for test-time attention correction. It departs from prior LoRA composition methods (ZipLoRA, Mix-of-Show, Custom Diffusion, MultiLoRA) by being entirely training-free and control-free: rather than synthesizing a new LoRA, retraining EDLoRA variants, or requiring bounding boxes/segmentation, CLoRA applies an InfoNCE contrastive objective directly on cross-attention maps at inference time and fuses latents via attention-derived binary masks. This positions it as a plug-and-play alternative that operates on off-the-shelf community LoRAs (e.g., from CivitAI) without modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is substantial for the LoRA ecosystem: with over 100K community LoRAs on CivitAI, a training-free composition method that preserves each LoRA's identity without retraining or external controls lowers the barrier to multi-concept generation. Limitations include evaluation only on Stable Diffusion 1.5, a cap of 8 LoRAs (with VRAM scaling to 80 GB), and an added ~25 s inference cost per composition. Open questions include whether the 16×16 attention-map assumption and the contrastive grouping strategy transfer to newer architectures (SDXL, Flux), how the method behaves with highly overlapping style LoRAs, and whether the thresholding parameter λ can be made adaptive rather than fixed.
