## Problem

AI-generated rasterized designs are visually compelling but lack editability, while existing bottom-up layered design methods (e.g., COLE, Open-COLE) generate elements sequentially without a global visual reference, often producing visual conflicts such as text overlapping objects or poor space allocation. No prior work converts AI-generated non-layered designs into editable layered designs while also refining nonsensical AI-generated text into meaningful content guided by user prompts.

## Input / Output

The method accepts a short text intention, a sketch draft, or a directly provided reference image (AI-generated or user-supplied) and produces a set of stacked layers: N foreground object layers, one background layer, and one vectorized text layer with full rendering attributes (bounding box, content, color, font, alignment, line count, angle). The VLM processes reference images scaled to 336 px on the longer side with box coordinates normalized to [0, 336] and color channels mapped to [0, 25], while the removal and inpainting models operate at 512×512 resolution matching the final output size. The framework is flexible: users can skip Stage 1 and start from design planning if they already have a reference image or background layer.

## Method

Accordion is a three-stage pipeline built around LLaVA-1.5-7B (fine-tuned with LoRA) that plays distinct roles at each stage. Stage 1 uses the VLM to expand short user intentions into detailed descriptive prompts via in-context learning, which are fed to Flux to generate a globally harmonious reference image. Stage 2 has the VLM process the reference image alongside a combined prompt (task description + detailed description + OCR string) to output an ordered sequence of element dictionaries with bounding boxes and text attributes in bottom-to-top stacking order. Stage 3 performs layer extraction: a text removal model erases text first, then SAM (conditioned on plan bounding boxes) and an inpainting-based object removal model iteratively peel off foreground objects, with a VLM-driven questionnaire selecting the best removal result among multiple candidates. Training uses Design39K (39,233 layered designs) augmented with three reference variants—original designs, designs with nonsensical text produced by SD 1.5 inpainting (strength 0.5–0.7), and text-free backgrounds—yielding 156,932 total samples for joint multi-task training at learning rate 2e-4 over 6 epochs.

## Results

On the DesignIntention benchmark (500 prompts, GPT-4V scored 0–10), Accordion achieves the highest average score of 6.5, outperforming COLE (6.0) and Open-COLE (6.3). It leads on four of five metrics—Design & Layout (6.7), Content Relevance (7.4), Typography & Color (6.1), and Graphics & Images (7.3)—while tying COLE on Innovation (5.1). Additional evaluations show an aesthetic score of 4.98 (vs. COLE's 4.72), a 0.4-point content-relevance gain over raw Flux references (7.4 vs. 7.0), and a 52% GPT-4V preference rate over COLE in head-to-head comparisons.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against COLE [22], Accordion wins on four of five DesignIntention metrics (Design & Layout +0.7, Content Relevance +0.5, Typography & Color +0.4, Graphics & Images +1.1) and ties on Innovation (5.1 each), yielding a 0.5-point average advantage (6.5 vs. 6.0) despite training on a 39K dataset versus COLE's 100K. Against Open-COLE [20], Accordion again leads on average (6.5 vs. 6.3) with gains in Design & Layout (+0.4), Content Relevance (+0.4), Typography & Color (+0.5), and Graphics & Images (+0.2), though it trails slightly on Innovation (5.1 vs. 5.3). For text de-rendering, Accordion is compared qualitatively with De-Render [42]; by grouping text at the paragraph level rather than per-word, Accordion produces more visually coherent layouts and preserves semantic coherence in translation tasks where De-Render's word-level predictions cause overlapping and stylistic inconsistency. In a 29-designer user study, 73.5% of text-to-template outputs were rated superior to COLE in editability, and 87.2% of sketch-to-description outputs were deemed appropriate.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant bottom-up paradigm in layered design generation (COLE, Open-COLE), which sequentially constructs background, objects, and text without a global visual plan, by instead starting from a globally harmonious rasterized reference image and decomposing it top-down into editable layers. It repurposes existing vision experts—SAM for segmentation, inpainting/ControlNet for element removal, PaddleOCR for text detection—within a VLM-orchestrated three-stage pipeline (reference creation, design planning, layer generation), rather than training a dedicated generative model for each element type. The conceptual lineage draws on the observation that human designers use rasterized references for layout inspiration, formalizing this workflow into an automated pipeline that also refines nonsensical AI-generated text into meaningful content guided by user prompts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Accordion addresses a practical gap: AI-generated designs are visually compelling but non-editable, while layered design tools require significant expertise. By converting rasterized references into editable layers, the paper makes GenAI outputs directly usable in professional design workflows, and the design-variation capabilities (upstream image editing, inference-time branching, downstream layout models) suggest a broader compositional role in creative pipelines. Key limitations include SAM's 68.4% IoU causing incomplete object extraction, cumulative errors from VLM bounding-box detection, and the marginal 52% vs 48% preference in head-to-head comparisons. Open questions include whether the VLM-as-agent architecture can integrate additional expert models to address segmentation failures, and whether the approach generalizes to more complex multi-page or interactive design formats.
