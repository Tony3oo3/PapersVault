## Problem

Existing exposure correction methods and datasets (MSEC, DIME) are designed for non-extreme conditions and fail to recover severe luminance and texture loss under extreme over/under-exposure. No dedicated real-world benchmark dataset or specialized method exists for this regime, leaving a significant gap in both data availability and algorithmic capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252310/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single ill-exposed RGB image (1920×1080 in REED; 512×512 crops for training) and its CLIP ViT-B/32 image embedding as joint input, producing a well-exposed RGB image. The CLIP-Guided Iterative Refinement Strategy (CIRS) feeds the model's own output back as input for up to 5 iterations, with CLIP cosine similarity against text prompts ('well-exposed', 'ill-exposed', 'vividly colored') dynamically controlling when to stop exposure correction and when to refine color/contrast.

## Method

CLIER combines three key mechanisms: (1) a CNN encoder followed by a Luminance Normalizer that uses a spatial-frequency interaction module (dual-branch with Fourier transform) to align encoded features to the mean luminance of over/under-exposure distributions, trained with InfoNCE contrastive loss and a moving-variance loss to disentangle the coupled distributions; (2) a Semantic-aware Exposure Diffusion Model (U-Net denoiser) conditioned on both the ill-exposed input and CLIP image embeddings to restore luminance and texture, especially for ambiguous cases where luminance cues are inconsistent; (3) a CLIP-Guided Iterative Refinement Strategy that recursively applies the model and uses CLIP text-image similarity to terminate exposure correction and progressively enhance color/contrast. Training is two-stage: the encoder and Luminance Normalizer are first trained on MSEC with reconstruction, contrastive, and variance losses, then the diffusion model is trained with a noise-prediction loss and a Fourier-domain consistency loss while freezing earlier modules.

## Results

On the newly introduced REED dataset, CLIER achieves an average PSNR of 19.25 dB and SSIM of 0.8503, outperforming the strongest baseline CSEC (18.71 dB / 0.7903) by 0.54 dB and 0.06 SSIM respectively. CLIER also attains the best unsupervised scores: LPIPS 0.09, NIQE 3.74, and PI 3.20. On the non-extreme MSEC and SICE benchmarks, CLIER similarly leads with 22.03 dB / 0.8481 SSIM on MSEC and 20.19 dB / 0.8141 SSIM on SICE, surpassing CSEC by 0.41 dB and 1.25 dB respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLIER outperforms all 14 baselines on every metric across all three datasets. On REED, the closest competitor is CSEC (18.71 dB avg PSNR), which CLIER surpasses by 0.54 dB in PSNR and 0.06 in SSIM, while also achieving substantially better perceptual scores (LPIPS 0.09 vs. 0.12, NIQE 3.74 vs. 3.87, PI 3.20 vs. 3.53). RECNet, the second-strongest supervised baseline, trails CLIER by 0.75 dB PSNR and 0.037 SSIM on REED, with a particularly large gap in NIQE (5.47 vs. 3.74) and PI (5.56 vs. 3.20), indicating RECNet produces noticeably less natural-looking outputs under extreme exposure. On the non-extreme MSEC dataset, CLIER leads CSEC by 0.41 dB (22.03 vs. 21.62) and RECNet by 0.59 dB; on SICE, the margins widen to 1.25 dB over CSEC and 1.09 dB over RECNet. Traditional methods (HE, CLAHE) and early deep approaches (URetinexNet, ZeroDCE++, GDP) lag far behind, with PSNR values 5–7 dB lower than CLIER on REED, confirming that extreme exposure correction remains substantially harder than non-extreme settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the exposure correction literature (Retinex-based, frequency-domain, and curve-based methods) by addressing the extreme exposure regime where severe luminance clipping causes texture loss, a setting prior datasets like MSEC and DIME do not cover. Methodologically, it builds on the latent diffusion framework from LightenDiffusion [12] for the generative restoration stage and repurposes CLIP (ViT-B/32) both as a semantic conditioning signal and as a dynamic stopping criterion for iterative refinement. The Luminance Normalizer with its spatial-frequency dual-branch design draws on global filter network ideas [29], while the contrastive (InfoNCE) loss for disentangling over/under-exposed distributions is a standard technique applied here to a specific distributional overlap problem the authors identify empirically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The REED dataset (10,052 real-world images spanning −3 EV to +3 EV) fills a concrete gap: no prior benchmark contained the severe luminance and texture degradation characteristic of extreme exposure, making it difficult to evaluate or train methods for this regime. The finding that over/under-exposed image distributions overlap in latent space, causing misclassification by existing models, provides a clear diagnostic for why prior methods fail and motivates the contrastive + normalization approach. Limitations include reliance on a single camera model (Canon EOS R10), the computational cost of up to five iterative diffusion passes at inference, and the dependency on CLIP for both conditioning and stopping decisions, which may not transfer cleanly to video or multi-frame settings. Open questions include whether the iterative refinement strategy can be distilled into a single-pass model and whether the approach generalizes to mixed-exposure or dynamic-scene extremes.
