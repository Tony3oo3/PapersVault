## Problem

Existing TTA methods overlook the fact that feature redundancy in embeddings systematically increases as domain shifts intensify, which hinders model adaptability to new data. No prior work has explored this redundancy phenomenon or leveraged it as an adaptation signal. Moreover, a naive redundancy-minimization approach fails under label shift (e.g., long-tailed target distributions), leaving a gap in handling real-world distribution shifts that combine both covariate and label changes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained classifier (ResNet-18/50 or ViT-B/16) and processes unlabeled target-domain images in an online, batch-by-batch fashion: each incoming batch of n_t images triggers a single gradient-descent step to update model parameters before the next batch arrives. The redundancy score is computed from the d×d second-order feature relation graph (Z^T Z) of the current batch's embeddings, where d is the embedding dimension (e.g., 512 for ResNet-18). Output is a class prediction per test image plus the adapted model weights, with no access to source-domain data or labels at test time.

## Method

FRET introduces two methods built on the insight that minimizing feature redundancy improves generalization under distribution shift. S-FRET directly minimizes the redundancy score R_s = ||Z̃^T Z̃ − I_d||₁ (the L1-norm of the off-diagonal of the column-normalized covariance matrix) as the sole adaptation loss. G-FRET extends this by decomposing the second-order feature relation graph G_F = Z^T Z into an attention graph G_A (diagonal, via mask M_M = I_d) and a redundancy graph G_R (off-diagonal), then propagating embeddings through each graph with a single-layer GCN to obtain separate attention and redundancy representations and predictions. In the representation layer, a contrastive loss pulls attention representations toward pseudo-class centers while pushing them away from redundancy representations; in the prediction layer, entropy minimization sharpens attention predictions while a negative-learning term penalizes redundant predictions for overlapping with attention predictions. The combined loss L_GFRET = L_R + λL_P is optimized online with one gradient step per test batch, requiring no architectural modification to the backbone.

## Results

On image corruption adaptation (level 5, 15 types), G-FRET achieves 77.26% on CIFAR-10-C, 51.06% on CIFAR-100-C, and 36.79% on ImageNet-C, outperforming the non-adaptive source model by 26.46%, 20.05%, and 22.09% respectively. On domain adaptation, G-FRET improves over the source model by 6.67% (PACS, ResNet-18) and 1.80% (OfficeHome, ResNet-18), achieving the highest or second-highest accuracy across all domains. S-FRET, the lightweight variant, achieves competitive accuracy (e.g., 74.63% on CIFAR-10-C) with the lowest runtime among training-based methods (46 s on ResNet-18 vs. 48 s for TENT and 162 s for TIPI).

## Baselines & Comparisons

G-FRET outperforms all baselines on CIFAR-10-C (77.26% vs. TIPI 77.21%, TENT 76.36%) and CIFAR-100-C (51.06% vs. TENT 50.79%, EATA 50.71%), confirming that combining redundancy elimination with class discriminability yields the strongest adaptation. However, on ImageNet-C, EATA (39.19%) and SAR (38.55%) surpass G-FRET (36.79%), suggesting that the GCN-based decomposition is less effective on the larger, more diverse ImageNet distribution. S-FRET trails G-FRET by 2.63% on CIFAR-10-C and 2.53% on CIFAR-100-C, highlighting the benefit of the contrastive and negative-learning components for label-shift robustness. On domain adaptation (PACS, OfficeHome), G-FRET achieves the highest or second-highest accuracy across all domains and both ResNet-18/50 backbones, with gains of up to 6.67% over the source model. In efficiency, S-FRET is the fastest training-based method (46 s, ResNet-18) with the second-lowest GPU memory (2789 MB), while G-FRET is moderate (118 s, 4781 MB) but still well below TEA (658 s, 8221 MB). Under long-tailed label shift (CIFAR-100-C-LT, imbalance factor 100), G-FRET maintains robust performance while S-FRET degrades by 1.91%, validating the design choice of incorporating contrastive learning for label-shift scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant TTA paradigms (entropy minimization, pseudo-labeling, consistency, clustering) by identifying feature redundancy in embeddings as an underexplored adaptation target. It builds directly on the SOFT feature-selection framework [58] for decomposing second-order feature relation graphs into attention and redundancy components via a mask matrix, and on the authors' own prior work on test-time correlation alignment [55] for the theoretical generalization bound linking redundancy to error. The key conceptual departure is treating redundancy elimination as a first-class optimization objective during inference rather than an implicit byproduct of other adaptation strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic empirical demonstration that feature redundancy monotonically increases with domain-shift severity across ~50 sub-datasets provides a concrete, measurable diagnostic for adaptation quality that prior TTA work lacked. S-FRET's simplicity (a single 1-norm loss on the covariance matrix) makes it an attractive lightweight baseline, while G-FRET's contrastive + negative-learning design addresses the label-shift gap that plagues redundancy-only methods. Open questions include whether the redundancy perspective transfers to non-visual modalities, whether the GCN decomposition is the optimal way to separate attention from redundancy, and why S-FRET outperforms G-FRET on ViT backbones for CIFAR corruption—a result the paper notes but does not fully explain.
