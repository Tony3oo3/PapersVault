## Problem

Existing text-to-image evaluation metrics (FID, CLIPScore, VQAScore, ImageReward) assess alignment with explicit visual descriptions but cannot evaluate whether a generated advertisement image conveys an implicit persuasive message, is creative, or effectively targets its intended audience. This gap is especially acute for ad generation, where users provide abstract action-reason messages rather than detailed object descriptions, and where creativity and persuasiveness are essential qualities that remain unmeasured.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250552/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes a generated advertisement image and an implicit action-reason prompt (e.g., 'I should drink Gatorade because it would help me win') as input, producing three scalar scores: AIM (alignment, 0–1), C_obj (creativity, unbounded ratio), and P_comp+AIM (persuasiveness, 0–1). The generation pipeline takes the same action-reason message, optionally expands it via an LLM into a detailed visual description, and feeds that to a T2I model (AuraFlow, Flux, or SDXL) to produce the advertisement image. The key distinction from standard T2I evaluation is that the prompt never explicitly names the objects the image should contain.

## Method

The paper introduces three metrics grounded in argumentation theory. AIM fine-tunes an LLM (LLAMA3-8B) via Contrastive Preference Optimization on 3,500 image-description-to-action-reason pairs (using hard negatives that share visual elements but differ semantically), then scores alignment as a weighted semantic similarity between the LLM-inferred action-reason and the input message, with reason weighted 4× over action. C_obj divides AIM by the mean CLIP similarity between the image and objects extracted from the text, rewarding images that are message-aligned yet visually divergent from a literal object depiction. P_comp+AIM averages seven LLM-scored components (Audience, Benefit, Appeal Category, Elaboration, Originality, Imagination, Synthesis) on a 0–5 scale and adds the AIM reason-alignment term. For generation, the paper proposes a simple LLM expansion step that converts the implicit action-reason message into a detailed visual paragraph, which is then used as the T2I prompt, improving alignment, creativity, and persuasiveness by 6–10% on commercial ads and 38–43% on public service ads.

## Results

On the PittAd dataset, the proposed AIM alignment metric achieves a Krippendorff's Alpha of 0.68 with human annotators (substantial agreement), substantially outperforming ImageReward (0.11), CLIPScore (0.17), and VQAScore (0.31). The creativity metric C_obj reaches 0.54 agreement (moderate) versus 0.04 for the LLM-based baseline C_LLM, and the persuasiveness metric P_comp+AIM achieves 0.80 agreement versus 0.27 for P_LLM. Using LLM-based message expansion (E_LLM) as T2I prompts improves alignment, creativity, and persuasiveness by 6%, 7%, and 10% on commercial ads and 39%, 38%, and 43% on PSA ads over direct action-reason prompting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's proposed AIM metric decisively outperforms all three alignment baselines: it achieves 0.68 Krippendorff's Alpha (All) versus 0.17 for CLIPScore [17], 0.31 for VQAScore [27], and 0.11 for ImageReward [52], with the gap being especially large for PSA ads (0.82 vs. 0.34, 0.24, and 0.06 respectively). The zero-shot version of AIM (without CPO fine-tuning) scores only 0.18, confirming that the contrastive fine-tuning step is critical. For creativity, C_obj (0.54) far exceeds the LLM-based baseline C_LLM (0.04), which actually shows systematic disagreement with humans on commercial ads (−0.03). For persuasiveness, P_comp+AIM (0.80) more than triples the P_LLM baseline (0.27), and adding the AIM component to P_comp further improves agreement from 0.65 to 0.80. Across all three metrics, the proposed methods consistently beat their respective baselines by margins of 0.37–0.53 in Krippendorff's Alpha.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of T2I evaluation and computational persuasion analysis. Prior T2I evaluation work (CLIPScore, VQAScore, ImageReward, FID) targets explicit, object-level descriptions and cannot assess whether an image conveys an implicit argumentative message; prior persuasion and creativity literature (Aristotle's rhetorical appeals, Romanova, Simonton) is purely non-computational. The paper repurposes CPO fine-tuning of LLMs (originally for preference alignment) to train a message-interpretation model, and combines MLLM image description with LLM component-wise scoring to produce the first computational scores for ad creativity and persuasiveness. It also draws on the PittAd dataset's action-reason annotations as a natural source of visually implicit prompts, a framing absent from prior T2I benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that current T2I models (Flux, AuraFlow, SDXL) systematically fail on visually implicit prompts, especially for PSA ads where no concrete product anchors the visual content, and that a trivial LLM-expansion step closes a large portion of the gap—suggesting the bottleneck is prompt formulation rather than the diffusion model itself. However, the human evaluation is small (60 image pairs, 56 annotators), the metrics are validated only on PittAd, and persuasiveness scores for generated PSA ads remain well below human-created ads (~0.65 vs ~0.71), leaving open whether the metrics and the expansion trick generalize beyond this dataset and ad domain. A key open question is whether the component-based persuasiveness scoring (seven sub-questions) can be distilled into a single learned model, and whether the creativity metric's reliance on CLIP object-similarity holds for abstract or non-object-centric ads.
