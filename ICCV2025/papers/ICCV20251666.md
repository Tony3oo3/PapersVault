## Problem

Existing monocular depth estimation methods, including large-scale foundation models, formulate depth as a global per-pixel regression and fail to maintain instance-wise depth consistency in dynamic multi-object scenes with severe occlusions. This is compounded by the absence of large-scale video depth datasets with instance-level annotations for real-world activity scenes featuring interacting, deformable objects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251666/cropped_page0_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a single RGB video frame (captured at 0.01–10.0 m depth range by Intel RealSense D455 or Azure Kinect DK sensors) and produces a per-pixel depth map, per-instance segmentation masks, and a scalar depth layer value per instance. During the rectification stage, it additionally ingests predicted instance masks, normalized object coordinates, and global depth values as geometric priors, with candidate instances filtered by category confidence >0.9 and mask confidence >0.8 before occlusion-pair reasoning.

## Method

InstanceDepth follows a two-stage pipeline built on a DINOv2 backbone. Stage 1 (Holistic Depth Initialization) uses a multi-scale depth decoder that segments the depth range into rd intervals, computes per-range confidence via a small network, and iteratively refines depth through relative error scores across scales, establishing a coarse scene-level structure. Stage 2 (Instance-Aware Depth Rectification) employs a Mask2Former-based decoder with bipartite matching over mask IoU, category, and depth-layer losses to predict instance masks and depth layers, then an Occlusion Pair Relation Reasoning module identifies overlapping instances (IoU > 0.1), extracts multi-scale features via ROI alignment, and predicts relative depth corrections through an MLP to resolve occlusion ambiguities. Training proceeds in three progressive phases with alternating freezing of the depth encoder and instance decoder, supervised by a scale-invariant logarithmic loss and a pairwise relative-distance consistency loss.

## Results

On the GID test set (101,500 frames of dynamic multi-object scenes), InstanceDepth achieves RMS 0.397, REL 0.045, RMSlog 0.077, Log10 0.019, and σ1 0.983, outperforming all baselines. The closest competitor, PromDep, records RMS 0.431 and REL 0.051, meaning InstanceDepth reduces RMS by 7.9% and REL by 11.8%. On NYUDv2 (static indoor scenes), InstanceDepth matches Depth Anything V1/V2 on REL (0.056) while achieving a slightly lower RMS (0.202 vs. 0.206) and higher σ1 (0.985 vs. 0.984), confirming generalizability with only marginal gains in structured environments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the primary GID benchmark, InstanceDepth clearly outperforms all 7 baselines across every metric. The largest gap is against CDMOV (RMS 0.657 vs. 0.397, a 39.6% reduction), while the closest competitors are PromDep (RMS 0.431, REL 0.051) and Depth Anything V1 (RMS 0.433, REL 0.052), which InstanceDepth surpasses by 0.034 in RMS and 0.006 in REL. Notably, the two large-scale foundation models (Depth Anything V1/V2) and the prompting-based PromDep cluster tightly together (RMS 0.431–0.435), suggesting that global-regression architectures hit a ceiling in occlusion-heavy scenes that instance-aware rectification can break. On NYUDv2, the advantage narrows substantially: InstanceDepth ties Depth Anything V1/V2 on REL (0.056) and edges them out by only 0.004 in RMS (0.202 vs. 0.206) and 0.001 in σ1 (0.985 vs. 0.984), consistent with the authors' observation that structured indoor scenes with persistent background planes offer less room for instance-level refinement. ZoeDepth and UniDepth-V, which rely on relative/metric depth transfer, trail the top group by 0.008–0.022 in REL on NYUDv2, confirming that supervised training on the target domain remains important even for static scenes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Mask2Former instance segmentation architecture by augmenting its query-based decoder with depth layer prediction heads, and builds on Depth Anything V2's pretrained DINOv2 encoder as a feature backbone. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant global-regression formulation of monocular depth estimation (Depth Anything, ZoeDepth, NeWCRFs) by explicitly modeling per-instance depth relationships and occlusion boundaries rather than optimizing per-pixel depth errors. The two-stage design—holistic depth range initialization followed by instance-aware occlusion refinement—repurposes depth range segmentation (from AdaBins-style binning) and ROI-aligned geometric priors into a unified occlusion-handling pipeline, while the GID dataset itself bridges the gap between synthetic multi-object datasets (Sintel) and real-world static-scene datasets (KITTI, NYUDv2).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GID dataset (101,500 frames with instance-level depth, masks, and tracking IDs from real sports/dance/animal scenes) fills a genuine gap: no prior video depth dataset provides high-fidelity, sensor-captured depth with instance semantics for dynamic multi-object interactions. The method's consistent improvements over strong baselines like Depth Anything V2 (e.g., RMS 0.397 vs. 0.397 on GID) validate that explicit instance-level modeling helps in occluded scenes, though the marginal gains on NYUDv2 (0.202 vs. 0.206 RMS) honestly reveal that the benefit is specific to cluttered, dynamic environments. Open questions include whether the approach scales to even denser crowds, whether the reliance on off-the-shelf trackers (DEVA) and segmenters (SAM) introduces a compounding error floor under extreme occlusion, and whether the two-stage training paradigm can be unified into a single end-to-end objective.
