## Problem

Full-body human relighting and novel-view synthesis remain challenging due to complex materials, subsurface scattering, and self-shadowing, yet progress has been severely limited by the absence of publicly accessible, high-quality full-body OLAT datasets. Prior lightstage captures focused on faces, hands, or static objects, and the few full-body datasets (e.g., Ultrastage) provide only limited illumination types (white light and color gradients) without fine-grained OLAT or environment map data, preventing rigorous benchmarking of modern relighting methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250981/cropped_page0_idx4.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 40 synchronized 5K-resolution RGB views per pose under 344 distinct illuminations (1 white light, 2 color gradients, 10 environment maps, 331 OLATs) for 21 subjects in 3 poses each, totaling ~850K frames. Beyond raw images, it ships per-pose MVS meshes, Sapiens segmentation masks, photometric normals derived from color gradients, full camera and 3D light calibrations in a canonical coordinate system, OpenPose annotations, and SMPLX parameters. The Debevec linear-combination formula (Eq. 2) is provided as a ground-truth relighting reference, enabling direct evaluation of any method that predicts novel-view images under arbitrary target lighting.

## Method

The core contribution is a large-scale capture and processing pipeline built around a spherical lightstage with 40 RED Komodo 6K cameras and 331 individually addressable RGBAW LEDs arranged 360° around the subject. Camera calibration and MVS mesh reconstruction are performed per subject using Metashape on white-light frames, with a marker-based transform into a canonical coordinate system; segmentation masks come from the Sapiens foundation model. Because each ~11-second capture involves slight subject sway, motion compensation is applied by injecting a white-light reference frame every 21st OLAT frame and computing dense optical flow via Co-Tracker3 on ~12K sparse grid points to warp all OLAT frames into alignment. Photometric normals are estimated from paired color-gradient illuminations using the ratio formula of Guo et al. and Zhou et al. The paper then benchmarks four 3DGS-based OLAT inverse-rendering methods (GS3, PRT-Gaussian, RNG, BiGS) and the diffusion-based IC-Light harmonization model, revealing that even the best methods (GS3 at 30.04 PSNR) fail to capture specular highlights, sharp shadows, and subsurface scattering on full-body humans.

## Results

On the HumanOLAT dataset, GS3 achieves the highest PSNR (30.04) among the four evaluated 3DGS-based inverse rendering methods, outperforming PRT-Gaussian (24.06), RNG (27.38), and BiGS (26.72). RNG attains the best LPIPS (0.139) and SSIM (0.905), while BiGS achieves the highest SSIM (0.936) despite a lower PSNR. PRT-Gaussian performs worst across all three metrics. All methods exhibit visible artifacts in hand and face regions and fail to capture complex effects like specular highlights and sharp shadows, underscoring the difficulty of full-body human relighting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GS3 [4] leads in PSNR (30.04 vs. 24.06–27.38 for the others), making it the strongest method in terms of pixel-level fidelity for joint novel-view and novel-illumination synthesis on HumanOLAT. However, it does not dominate all metrics: RNG [15] achieves the best LPIPS (0.139 vs. GS3's 0.152) and a competitive SSIM (0.905), while BiGS [62] reaches the highest SSIM (0.936) at the cost of a 3.3 dB PSNR gap relative to GS3. PRT-Gaussian [58] is clearly the weakest baseline, trailing GS3 by 6.0 dB in PSNR and showing visible ghosting artifacts due to poor geometry reconstruction from OLAT frames. For the illumination harmonization task, IC-Light [59] produces generally plausible relighting but consistently fails to preserve facial details (eye color, mouth shape) and struggles with subsurface scattering and self-shadowing, which the authors attribute to the absence of full-body OLAT data in its training set. Overall, no single method convincingly solves full-body human relighting, validating HumanOLAT as a challenging benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the OLAT (One-Light-at-a-Time) capture paradigm established by Debevec et al. [11] from faces and objects to full-body humans, filling a gap left by prior lightstage datasets that either focused on heads/hands (ICT-3DRFE, Dynamic OLAT, Goliath-4) or objects (ReNe, OpenIllumination). The closest prior work, Ultrastage [63], captures full-body humans under white light and color gradients but lacks the fine-grained OLAT and environment-map illuminations necessary for image-based relighting. The paper positions itself as a data contribution that enables the broader 3DGS-based relighting literature (GS3, PRT-Gaussian, RNG, BiGS) and illumination harmonization methods (IC-Light) to be stress-tested on realistic, complex human-centric scenarios for the first time at public scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HumanOLAT is the first publicly available full-body OLAT dataset, removing a long-standing barrier that forced the community to rely on synthetic data or learned priors for human relighting research. The baseline evaluations reveal that even the strongest SOTA methods (GS3 at 30.04 PSNR) produce blurry renderings with artifacts in hands and faces and fail to capture specular highlights and sharp shadows, while IC-Light cannot preserve facial details like eye color—highlighting that human-specific light transport (subsurface scattering, diverse clothing materials, self-shadowing) remains fundamentally unsolved. This dataset will likely become a standard benchmark, but the open question is whether current 3DGS or diffusion-based architectures can be extended to handle the full complexity of dressed, full-body human appearance, or whether new inductive biases are needed.
