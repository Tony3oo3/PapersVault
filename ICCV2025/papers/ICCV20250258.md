## Problem

Existing Membership Inference Test (MINT) methods operate passively, training a detection model only after the audited model is complete, which requires the developer to share model internals and training data. This post-hoc approach yields limited detection accuracy (~50–66%) and creates friction with developers reluctant to disclose sensitive information. The paper asks whether embedding auditability directly into the training process—so the model is inherently auditable by design—can substantially improve membership detection without requiring post-training data sharing.

## Input / Output

At inference, a single image sample (from either the training set D or external set E) is passed through the Enhanced Audited Model, which simultaneously produces a class prediction for the primary task and a binary membership probability indicating whether the sample was used in training. The MINT head consumes two intermediate activation maps (AAD) of size H×W×C extracted at chosen depths of the backbone (or token sequences for ViT), rather than the raw image or final logits. During training, batches interleave samples from D and E, with D samples traversing both the Audited and MINT paths while E samples follow only the MINT path.

## Method

Active MINT (aMINT) jointly trains the Audited Model M and a MINT Model T as a single multi-task network (the Enhanced Audited Model M⁺), sharing initial convolutional layers that are optimized for both the primary classification task and the membership-detection task. After the AAD extraction point, the network splits: remaining backbone layers are trained solely for the audited task, while a lightweight CNN (or fully-connected head for ViT) processes the two activation maps to output a binary membership score. The multi-task loss combines a normalized task-specific loss and a binary cross-entropy MINT loss with tunable weights λ₁, λ₂ and an L2 regularizer, balancing the inherently conflicting objectives of generalization (audited task) and memorization detection (MINT task). The authors show that extracting AAD from early layers (Entry Setup) minimizes the number of shared layers and thus limits degradation of the audited task, while still achieving >80% MINT accuracy across six architectures and five datasets—substantially outperforming the passive post-hoc baseline.

## Results

Active MINT achieves MINT detection accuracy of 0.76–0.91 across all five datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, GTSRB, Tiny ImageNet, CASIA WebFace) and six architectures (MobileNet, ResNet50, ResNet101, DenseNet121, Xception, ViT), consistently exceeding 80% in most settings. The Audited Model task accuracy is largely preserved, with the Entry Setup showing minimal degradation (e.g., 0.96 vs 0.97 on MNIST/ResNet50, 0.89 vs 0.92 on GTSRB/MobileNet). The Output Setup consistently underperforms both MINT and Audited tasks, confirming that shared layers near the output create conflicting optimization objectives. The Entry Setup is adopted for all subsequent comparisons as it offers the best trade-off between MINT accuracy and Audited Model performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Active MINT outperforms Passive MINT [8] by a substantial margin on MINT accuracy across every dataset and architecture: e.g., 0.83 vs 0.51 (MNIST/ResNet50), 0.86 vs 0.66 (CIFAR-10/ResNet50), 0.86 vs 0.61 (GTSRB/ResNet50), and 0.80 vs 0.61 (CASIA WebFace/ResNet50), representing a consistent 20–32 percentage-point improvement. Against MIA baselines on ResNet-50 (Table 3), Active MINT achieves 0.86 on both CIFAR-10 and GTSRB, surpassing Salem et al. [40] (0.61/0.67), Yeom et al. [54] (0.64/0.79), Song et al. [46] (0.65/0.68), Ye et al. [53] (0.52/0.60), and Watson et al. [52] (0.63/0.79). The closest MIA competitor is Yeom et al. and Watson et al. at 0.79 on GTSRB, still 7 points below Active MINT. The trade-off is a modest drop in Audited Model accuracy (e.g., 0.96 vs 0.97 on MNIST, 0.89 vs 0.92 on GTSRB for MobileNet), which the authors argue is an acceptable cost for the large gain in auditability. Notably, the MIA comparisons are under different environmental assumptions (attack vs. audit), so the gap partly reflects the privileged access MINT enjoys over MIA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MINT (Membership Inference Test) framework introduced by DeAlcala et al. [8], which itself builds on the broader Membership Inference Attack (MIA) literature starting with Shokri et al. [45]. The key conceptual departure is shifting from Passive MINT—where the MINT model is trained post-hoc on a frozen Audited Model—to Active MINT, a joint multi-task training paradigm where the MINT model and Audited Model are co-trained from scratch, sharing initial layers optimized for both objectives. The approach also draws on Nasr et al. [35], who regularized model outputs to resist MIAs, but inverts the goal: rather than hiding training data, aMINT deliberately makes it detectable. Additionally, the paper extends the original MINT method by combining two activation maps as Auxiliary Auditable Data, a combination the original authors did not explore.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

aMINT addresses a concrete regulatory gap: the EU AI Act and US White House memo mandate model registration and external auditability, but prior tools (Passive MINT, MIAs) require post-hoc model access or shadow-model training that developers may resist. The >80% detection accuracy across six architectures and five datasets makes this a practically viable auditing mechanism, though the small but consistent drop in Audited Model accuracy introduces a trade-off that may be unacceptable in high-stakes deployments. Open questions include whether advanced multi-task techniques (meta-optimization, conflict-averse gradients, knowledge distillation) can eliminate this trade-off, and how the approach generalizes beyond image classification to LLMs, generative models, and other modalities where MINT has been explored only in its passive form.
