## Problem

Existing 3DGS-based inverse rendering methods approximate Gaussian point normals through implicit geometric constraints (e.g., depth-normal regularization), which yields inaccurate and spatially inconsistent normal directions. This degrades light transport modeling, producing noisy material decompositions and flawed relighting results, particularly on reflective surfaces. The fundamental gap is that vanilla 3DGS lacks explicit geometry boundaries and well-defined opaque surfaces, both of which are essential for accurate PBR-based inverse rendering.

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-view RGB images along with object masks (required for isosurface extraction via FlexiCubes) and produces a full PBR decomposition: per-point albedo, roughness, and metalness stored in multi-resolution hash grids, an environment map, surface normals, an explicit triangular mesh, and shape-aligned 3D Gaussian points carrying PBR attributes. A notable constraint is that the isosurface grid resolution limits handling of thin structures, and the pipeline requires masks during training unlike mask-free 3DGS baselines. Training converges in 10–15 minutes on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090.

## Method

GeoSplatting introduces a Mesh-to-Gaussian adaptor (MGadapter) that differentiably constructs K=6 structured Gaussian points per triangle of an optimizable FlexiCubes isosurface mesh, placing them via a predefined barycentric pattern so that Gaussian normals exactly match mesh normals without additional optimization. This shape alignment between Gaussians and the mesh enables the mesh to serve as an opaque proxy for BVH-accelerated ray tracing, replacing the expensive per-Gaussian opacity accumulation used by prior 3DGS inverse rendering methods for self-occlusion evaluation. PBR attributes (albedo, roughness, metalness) are queried from multi-resolution hash grids at each Gaussian's position, and the rendering equation is evaluated via Monte Carlo sampling with a GGX microfacet BRDF, preceded by a split-sum warm-up phase for stability. A deferred shading refinement stage at the end of training performs pixel-wise PBR to recover high-frequency appearance details that per-Gaussian forward shading cannot capture.

## Results

GeoSplatting achieves state-of-the-art inverse rendering with only 14 minutes of training on a single RTX 4090. On Synthetic4Relight, it reaches 34.10 relighting PSNR and 0.004 roughness MSE, surpassing the best 3DGS baseline R3DG (31.00 PSNR, 0.013 MSE) by 3.1 dB and 3× in roughness accuracy. On TensoIR Synthetic, it attains 29.95 relighting PSNR and 4.08° normal MAE, outperforming implicit-field methods like NeRO (~800 min training) and TensoIR (~270 min) while being 19–57× faster. On Shiny Blender (reflective objects), it achieves 31.14 NVS PSNR, the best among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the strongest 3DGS-based baseline R3DG (~110 min), GeoSplatting improves relighting PSNR by 3.1 dB on Synthetic4Relight (34.10 vs 31.00), reduces roughness MSE from 0.013 to 0.004, and cuts training time by ~8×. Against implicit-field methods, GeoSplatting outperforms NeRO (~800 min) by 3.95 dB in relighting PSNR on Synthetic4Relight and by 0.33° in normal MAE on Shiny Blender (2.15° vs 2.48°), while being ~57× faster. Compared to TensoIR (~270 min), GeoSplatting gains 4.16 dB in relighting PSNR on Synthetic4Relight and matches its normal MAE on TensoIR (4.08° vs 4.10°) at 19× lower training cost. Among 3DGS baselines, GS-IR and GS-Shader are clearly outperformed on all metrics (e.g., GS-IR achieves only 23.81 relighting PSNR vs 34.10). The only metric where a baseline is competitive is NVS on Shiny Blender, where GS-Shader reaches 30.64 PSNR vs GeoSplatting's 31.14, a narrow 0.5 dB gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

GeoSplatting sits at the intersection of 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023), differentiable isosurface extraction via FlexiCubes (Shen et al., 2023), and Monte Carlo-based PBR evaluation (Hasselgren et al., 2022; Liu et al./NeRO, 2023). It departs from prior 3DGS-based inverse rendering methods (R3DG, GS-IR, GS-Shader) that approximate normals through implicit geometric constraints (depth-normal regularization, eigendecomposition, shortest-axis heuristics) by instead grounding Gaussian points on an explicit optimizable mesh, yielding well-defined normals and an opaque surface for BVH-accelerated ray tracing. The MGadapter concept—differentiably sampling structured Gaussians from mesh triangles in barycentric space—is the key architectural novelty that bridges the explicit-geometry and 3DGS worlds, enabling the mesh to serve as a binary occlusion proxy rather than requiring expensive continuous opacity accumulation along rays.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10–15 minute training time (vs. ~270 min for TensoIR, ~800 min for NeRO) while matching or exceeding their inverse rendering quality makes physically-based material decomposition practically accessible for real-time and production pipelines. The core insight—that explicit mesh geometry is not merely a regularization aid but a functional prerequisite for accurate light transport (normals + opaque occlusion surface)—reframes how 3DGS should be augmented for PBR tasks. Key limitations remain: the method requires object masks during training, is constrained by isosurface grid resolution (struggling with thin structures), and is currently object-level rather than scene-level, leaving open the question of adaptive-resolution geometry guidance for complex, unbounded scenes.
