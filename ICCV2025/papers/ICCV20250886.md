## Problem

Integrating LLMs into autonomous driving requires balancing high-level reasoning with real-time efficiency, but existing approaches either invoke LLMs at every step (causing prohibitive latency) or use fixed activation schedules that cannot adapt to varying scene complexity. The paper tackles two coupled questions: how to dynamically decide *when* the LLM should be activated, and how to optimally scale *how much* the LLM's output should influence the final trajectory prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250886/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes a continuous stream of multi-view camera images and LiDAR point clouds alongside natural-language navigation instructions, operating in closed-loop within the CARLA simulator. Rather than processing the full history, the LLM only attends to a fixed-capacity buffer of k=10 frame features managed by a streaming memory buffer, while the lightweight planner operates on the current frame alone. The output is a sequence of trajectory waypoints that are converted by PID controllers into lateral steering and longitudinal acceleration commands.

## Method

AdaDrive decouples instruction comprehension (slow path, LLM) from trajectory prediction (fast path, 4-layer Transformer planner) into parallel streams with distinct activation frequencies. Connector-W learns adaptive LLM activation via a comparative loss that compares LLM-assisted and LLM-free trajectory losses at each timestep, using Gumbel-Softmax for differentiable binary gating and a margin-based penalty to suppress unnecessary activations. Connector-H replaces binary fusion with a continuous scaling: the predicted confidence score θ_T modulates the LLM feature contribution (f′ + θ_T·f″), so the planner receives a weighted blend rather than an all-or-nothing signal. A Long-Short Q-former splits learnable tokens into a propagated long-range group and a current-frame short-term group to capture temporal context, while a Propagative Memory Fusion mechanism merges evicted frame features into their predecessors to maintain a compact, information-preserving buffer.

## Results

On the LangAuto-Tiny benchmark, AdaDrive achieves a driving score (DS) of 80.9%, route completion (RC) of 87.6%, and infraction score (IS) of 0.90, surpassing the best LMDrive variant (LLaVA-v1.5 7B) by 14.4 DS points. On LangAuto-Short, AdaDrive reaches DS 70.6% and RC 85.3%, outperforming AD-H by 16.3 DS points. On the full LangAuto benchmark, AdaDrive attains DS 42.9% with only 6.79 GB memory and 189 ms inference time, compared to 26.91 GB and 526 ms for LMDrive, demonstrating a 62% reduction in GFLOPs while improving driving scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AdaDrive consistently outperforms both baselines across all three LangAuto sub-benchmarks. Against LMDrive (best variant: LLaVA-v1.5 7B), AdaDrive improves DS by 14.4 points on LangAuto-Tiny (80.9 vs. 66.5), 20.0 points on LangAuto-Short (70.6 vs. 50.6), and 6.7 points on full LangAuto (42.9 vs. 36.2), while using a 6.4× smaller language model (1.1B vs. 7B). Against AD-H, AdaDrive gains 12.9 DS points on LangAuto-Tiny (80.9 vs. 68.0) and 16.3 points on LangAuto-Short (70.6 vs. 54.3), with a narrower 1.8-point margin on full LangAuto (42.9 vs. 41.1). Notably, AdaDrive achieves these gains with substantially lower resource consumption: 6.79 GB memory and 189 ms inference time versus 26.91 GB and 526 ms for LMDrive, a 71% memory reduction and 64% latency reduction. The IS metric shows a trade-off on LangAuto-Short (0.81 vs. 0.86 for AD-H), suggesting slightly more infractions in longer routes, but the overall DS (which combines RC and IS) remains superior.

## Relation to Prior Work

AdaDrive extends the slow-fast cognitive architecture paradigm to LLM-enhanced autonomous driving, departing from prior synchronous approaches (LMDrive, AD-H) that invoke the LLM at every timestep and from fixed-interval asynchronous methods (AsyncDriver, DriveVLM) that use predetermined activation schedules. The visual feature aggregation builds directly on the Q-former from BLIP-2, modified into LS-Qformer by splitting learnable tokens into long-range and short-term groups inspired by group equivariant networks. The adaptive activation loss and dynamic contribution scaling (Connectors W and H) represent a novel comparative-learning mechanism not present in prior LLM-driving work, while the Propagative Memory Fusion for streaming context management is a new contribution to the autonomous driving setting specifically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that LLMs need not run at full frequency to achieve near-optimal driving performance—AdaDrive matches continuous-activation scores at only 0.28 average activation rate while cutting GFLOPs by 62%, making LLM-augmented driving substantially more deployable. The finding that scaled LLM contribution (e.g., weight 0.7) outperforms binary full-weight fusion challenges the implicit assumption in prior work that more LLM involvement is always better. However, all results are confined to the LangAuto/CARLA simulation with a 1.1B-parameter TinyLLaMA, leaving open whether the adaptive scheduling and scaling insights transfer to larger LLMs, real-world sensor noise, or more complex driving distributions. The streaming memory buffer and LS-Qformer are also untested outside this specific benchmark setup.
