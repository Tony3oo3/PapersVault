## Problem

The choice of loss function for performance predictors in NAS has not been systematically studied: prior work only compared MSE against a single ranking loss, and evaluations were limited to few search spaces and metrics. This paper asks how eight loss functions spanning regression, ranking, and weighted categories compare across diverse settings, and whether their complementary strengths can be combined to improve predictor-based NAS.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250819/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The predictor consumes graph-encoded architecture representations (adjacency matrices and node features) sampled from cell-based, skeleton-based, or graph-based search spaces, and outputs a scalar performance score per architecture. Training uses only 0.1%–3% of the search space (a few hundred architectures out of tens of thousands), with validation accuracy as the regression target and test accuracy for evaluation. The predicted scores are then used to rank all candidate architectures and select the top-K for final evaluation, making top-ranking precision (N@K, Precision@T) the critical output quality rather than absolute score accuracy.

## Method

The paper conducts the first comprehensive comparison of eight loss functions—MSE (regression), Hinge Ranking, Logistic Ranking, MSE+SR (pairwise ranking), ListMLE (listwise ranking), Exponential Weighted, MAPE (weighted regression), and WARP (weighted pairwise ranking, newly introduced to NAS)—across five search spaces and 13 tasks using a uniform GCN-based predictor. Key findings show that weighted losses excel at identifying top architectures with sufficient training data, while ranking losses are more robust under extremely limited data, and that simple MLP backbones pair better with ranking losses whereas GCN/Transformer backbones benefit from weighted losses. The practical contribution is PWLNAS, a piecewise loss strategy that trains the predictor with a ranking or regression loss in early iterations to establish a global ranking, then switches to a weighted loss in later iterations to sharpen identification of high-performing architectures. This two-phase schedule is applied within a predictor-guided evolutionary search loop and outperforms any single loss function and prior SOTA predictor-based NAS methods on NAS-Bench-201, NAS-Bench-101, TransNAS-Bench-101 Micro, and DARTS.

## Results

On NAS-Bench-201 with a query budget of 100, PWLNAS (piecewise HR+MAPE loss) achieves 5.63% test error on CIFAR-10 (matching the global best), 26.51% on CIFAR-100, and 52.88% on ImageNet-16-120, outperforming NPENAS (5.69/26.54/53.52) and NASBOT (6.36/28.62/54.12). On DARTS (CIFAR-10, 100 queries), PWLNAS achieves 2.47% test error with 3.6M parameters at 0.2 GPU-days, surpassing the prior SOTA DCLP (2.48%) and GMAENAS (2.50%). On NAS-Bench-101 (150 queries), PWLNAS reaches 5.80% test error, beating NPENAS (5.85%) and FlowerFormer (5.86%). On TransNAS-Bench-101 Micro (50 queries), PWLNAS leads on all four tasks (e.g., 45.73% on Class Object, 56.93 SSIM on Auto).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PWLNAS outperforms all 14 compared baselines on their respective search spaces. On NAS-Bench-201, it beats NASBOT (MSE, BO) by 0.73% on CIFAR-10 and 2.11% on CIFAR-100, ReNAS (LR) by 0.38% on CIFAR-10, and NPENAS (MSE, Evolution) by 0.06% on CIFAR-10 and 0.64% on ImageNet-16-120. On NAS-Bench-101, PWLNAS (5.80%) surpasses NPENAS (5.85%), FlowerFormer (5.86%), CATE (5.88%), WeakNAS (5.90%), and BANANAS (5.92%). On DARTS, PWLNAS (2.47%) edges out DCLP (2.48%), GMAENAS (2.50%), NPENAS (2.54%), PINAT (2.54%), TNASP (2.57%), CDP (2.63%), and PNAS (3.34%), while using only 0.2 GPU-days (vs. 225 for PNAS). On TransNAS-Bench-101 Micro, PWLNAS leads Arch-Graph (BCE) and WeakNAS (MSE) on all four tasks. The predictor-level comparison (Table 1) further shows that WARP loss benefits Transformer-based PINAT (N@10=3.78) but harms MLP-based AP (N@10=113.20), highlighting that backbone capacity interacts with loss choice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the limited loss-function comparisons in prior predictor-based NAS work (which typically contrasted only MSE vs. a single ranking loss, e.g., GATES and ReNAS) into a systematic study of eight loss functions across three categories—regression, ranking, and weighted—on five search spaces. It repurposes WARP, a weighted pairwise ranking loss originally designed for multilabel image annotation (Weston et al., IJCAI 2011), into the NAS predictor setting, and builds its proposed PWLNAS method on the predictor-guided evolutionary search framework of Jing et al. (IJCAI 2022). The paper departs from prior work by introducing a piecewise combination strategy that switches between loss categories during training, rather than relying on a single fixed loss throughout.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The study provides the first practical, multi-metric guidance for selecting loss functions in predictor-based NAS, showing that no single loss dominates across search spaces, training budgets, and predictor backbones. The counter-intuitive finding that more training data can degrade top-ranking precision for non-weighted losses, and that weighted losses overfit to locally good architectures under extreme data scarcity, reframes how practitioners should schedule predictor training. Open questions remain around adaptive (rather than fixed-threshold) loss switching, direct optimization of top-K ranking metrics, and representative sampling strategies to improve predictor generalization.
