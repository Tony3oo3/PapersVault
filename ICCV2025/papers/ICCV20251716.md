## Problem

Existing elastography techniques for measuring tissue stiffness and thickness rely on expensive specialized equipment (ultrasound, MRI) and trained medical operators, making routine or at-home screening infeasible. While surface waves on biological tissue encode subsurface mechanical and geometric information, no prior method leverages dense visual data from a standard video camera to recover these properties in a fully physics-based manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251716/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a video of surface wave motion (e.g., 600 FPS, ~96×1280 pixels, ~4 seconds) of a local region on the medium's surface, requiring sufficient texture for sub-pixel motion extraction. It outputs a single thickness value and a single stiffness (elastic modulus) value for the soft tissue layer beneath the observation window. The observation window size (spatial extent L) and temporal sampling rate directly affect the quality of the extracted dispersion relation and thus the accuracy of the estimates.

## Method

VSWE operates in two stages. First, it extracts a dispersion relation from the video: phase-based motion estimation via a complex steerable pyramid yields sub-pixel displacements, which are then transformed by a 2D FFT (space × time) to produce a spatial-temporal frequency map, averaged across image rows and displacement directions. Second, it solves a physics-based optimization problem where, for each hypothesized (thickness T, stiffness E) pair, a finite-element model with Bloch-Floquet periodic boundary conditions computes a theoretical dispersion relation under the assumption of a uniform soft tissue layer atop a rigid bone substrate. The optimal parameters are found by maximizing the structural similarity (SSIM) index between the theoretical and observed dispersion relation images, solved via grid search. The key insight is that the dispersion relation is fully determined by just two parameters (T and E) under standard biomechanical assumptions, reducing an otherwise ill-posed inverse problem to a tractable two-dimensional search.

## Results

On real gelatin-based phantoms, VSWE estimates tissue stiffness within 1.2% error of rheometry ground-truth measurements, and estimated thickness consistently falls within the confidence interval of caliper measurements across three distinct sample thicknesses (1000, 1100, 1500 mL). In the sensitivity analysis on plane-strain simulations, the method correctly tracks 5% perturbations in both thickness and stiffness across 9 clusters of 9 samples each. On a full 3D simulated human leg, VSWE recovers spatially-varying thickness across the calf region and a constant stiffness value, with slightly elevated error near domain boundaries.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare VSWE against competing elastography or video-based material characterization methods. Instead, it is a proof-of-concept validation study that benchmarks against ground-truth measurements: caliper readings for thickness, rheometry for stiffness, and known simulation parameters for the COMSOL models. The closest contextual comparison is to existing elastography techniques (transient elastography, shear wave elastography, magnetic resonance elastography), which the authors note require expensive specialized equipment and trained operators, whereas VSWE requires only a video camera. The objective function ablation (SSIM vs. curve-based loss, MSE, PSNR) shows SSIM yields the sharpest optimization landscape and the most accurate parameter estimates, while the other three objective functions fail to recover correct parameters on the 3D leg simulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the surface-wave dispersion analysis well-established in seismology and applies it to biological tissue characterization via video, a combination not previously explored. It departs from Visual Vibration Tomography (VVT), which requires modeling global 3D vibrational modes of known geometry, by targeting local regions where simpler 1D wave physics suffice. On the motion-estimation side, it leverages the existing phase-based steerable pyramid framework rather than introducing new visual tracking. The physics-based FEM + SSIM optimization loop is a straightforward but effective combination of standard tools, with the novelty lying in the end-to-end pipeline design and the seismology-to-biomechanics transfer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VSWE demonstrates that a commodity video camera can provide coarse but meaningful estimates of tissue thickness and stiffness, opening a path toward at-home health monitoring without ultrasound or MRI hardware. Key limitations include the assumption of uniform stiffness, known density/Poisson's ratio, a motionless bone layer, and 1D wave propagation, all of which restrict applicability to simple layered geometries. Open questions include extending to multi-layer or spatially-varying stiffness, incorporating viscoelastic effects, and validating on real human tissue rather than gelatin phantoms or simulations. The characteristic-numbers framework is a useful practical contribution for tuning observation parameters across different scales.
