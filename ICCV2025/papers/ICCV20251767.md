## Problem

State-of-the-art video generation models produce visually compelling outputs but frequently violate real-world physics, such as losing 3D consistency under camera motion or collapsing human body structure during large deformations. While synthetic videos from CGI pipelines inherently respect physical laws, no prior work has systematically explored how to curate and integrate such data to transfer physical fidelity into video generation models without introducing rendering artifacts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251767/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model accepts free-form text prompts describing a scene, motion, or camera behavior and generates 1280×720 videos at 24 fps with a 5-second duration. Training data is a mixture of real-world videos and synthetic videos rendered from procedurally configured 3D scenes (single object per scene) in Blender and Unreal Engine, with synthetic-to-real ratios tuned between 10% and 50%. Evaluation focuses exclusively on physical fidelity metrics (3D reconstruction error via COLMAP, human pose confidence via Sapiens) rather than visual aesthetics.

## Method

The approach is data-centric and operates on an 8B MMDiT diffusion transformer pretrained with flow matching on 200M+ real videos. A procedural CGI pipeline generates diverse synthetic videos by sampling over 3D objects, cameras, lighting, and environments, with careful curation of camera configurations (forward + following shots), background diversity, and asset/rendering quality to bridge the appearance gap. Captions are constructed by decomposing descriptions into per-element tags (object, scene, camera) and appending special markers (e.g., 'animated', 'rendered') to help the model distinguish synthetic from real data. The key training innovation, SimDrop, trains a separate reference model on synthetic data with captions that deliberately ignore physical content (e.g., human motion), so it captures only rendering-specific visual patterns; during inference, this reference model is subtracted from the main model's classifier-free guidance at each denoising step, removing synthetic artifacts while preserving the transferred physical fidelity.

## Results

On the large human motion task, the synthetic-data-enhanced model achieves 61% gymnastics and 86% dance success rates in user studies, compared to 4–15% and 14–44% for Kling 1.6, Runway Gen-3α, and Sora. Human pose estimation confidence (Sapiens) reaches 0.791 (gym) and 0.837 (dance), exceeding all baselines. On wide-angle camera rotation, the model produces 42,895 matched 3D feature points (vs. 13–16K for baselines) with a re-projection error of 0.135 (vs. 0.298–0.437), and achieves 80% user-study success (vs. 20–26%). For layer decomposition, the model reaches 84% success versus 1–26% for all other models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three leading commercial video generation models (Kling 1.6, Runway Gen-3α, Sora) and its own base model (pretrained on real video only). On large human motion, the proposed method dramatically outperforms all baselines: 61% gymnastics success vs. 4–15% for commercial models, and 86% dance success vs. 14–44%. On wide-angle camera rotation, the model's 3D reconstruction metrics are superior by a wide margin—42,895 feature points (nearly 3× the next best) and a re-projection error of 0.135 (more than 2× lower than the best baseline at 0.298), with 80% user-study success vs. 20–26%. On layer decomposition, the model achieves 84% success while the best commercial model (Kling 1.6) reaches only 4% and the base model 26%. The base model itself is outperformed on all three tasks, confirming that the synthetic data augmentation is the source of improvement rather than additional real-data training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the MMDiT/flow-matching video generation architecture (Esser et al., 2024; Lipman et al., 2022) and the Seaweed-7b pretrained model, but its core contribution is a data-centric strategy rather than an architectural modification. It departs from prior physics-in-video-generation work (e.g., PhysGen, VideoJam) that adds supervisory signals or control inputs to the diffusion model, instead showing that physically consistent synthetic video from CGI pipelines (Blender, Unreal Engine) can transfer physical fidelity through standard fine-tuning. The SimDrop technique extends classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans, 2021) into a two-model subtraction scheme that removes synthetic visual artifacts at inference time, a use of CFG not previously applied to domain-gap mitigation in video generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is one of the first empirical demonstrations that synthetic video from standard CGI pipelines can meaningfully improve physical fidelity in video generation without modifying the model architecture, making the approach broadly applicable across video generation systems. The large gaps over commercial models (e.g., 84% vs. 1–26% on layer decomposition) suggest a practical, scalable path to more physically grounded generation. However, the study is a pilot limited to three tasks, a single 8B-parameter model family, and single-object scenes; the authors explicitly note the model still lacks principled physics understanding. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to multi-object interactions, fluid dynamics, and whether additional rendering channels (depth, normals, alpha) as supervisory signals could further close the physics gap.
