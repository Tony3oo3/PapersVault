## Problem

Existing federated learning assumes static data distributions, yet real-world systems continuously encounter novel categories. The key gap is that standard Novel Category Discovery and Learning methods cannot operate in a federated setting where the server has no data access, the number of novel categories is unknown, non-IID clients discover different local categories that must be merged into a unified label space, and labeled known-category data may be unavailable to participants.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252424/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each participant feeds unlabeled RGB images through the deployed model and filters out novel-category samples using a cosine-similarity threshold (r=0.5) against known-category prototypes extracted from the bias-free linear classifier weights. Participants then run Kmeans locally on the filtered representations and transmit only the synthetic cluster centroids (not real data) to the server once. The server outputs an estimated novel-category count and global novel prototypes, while the final model produces class labels spanning both known and novel categories evaluated via standard accuracy and Hungarian clustering accuracy.

## Method

GPA operates in two phases: a known-category training phase using modified Prototype Contrastive Learning (PCL) to build high-quality representations, and a novel-category learning phase. In the novel phase, the Potential Prototype Merge (PPM) module estimates the global novel-category count by applying a modified DBSCAN with a rising radius ε over the pooled local Kmeans centroids, selecting the maximum cluster number across 50 radius steps, then re-clustering with Kmeans to initialize global prototypes. Local training is guided by a Semantic-Weighted Loss (SWL) that multiplies Euclidean distances to global prototypes by Softmax semantic similarity, and a Contrastive Weighted Loss (CWL) that applies the same weighting to InfoNCE for curriculum-style convergence on easy samples. A data-level mixup blends high-confidence novel samples with residual ones to mitigate known/novel interference, while a model-level mixup (θ^k = 0.99·θ^L + 0.01·θ^k) anchors the feature extractor to the known-category model to prevent catastrophic forgetting.

## Results

On the single-stage FC²DL setting, GPA achieves the best novel-category accuracy across all six datasets, with absolute gains of 5.7% (CUB200: 31.5% vs. Happy's 25.8%) to 13.1% (CIFAR-100: 52.3% vs. Happy's 39.2%) over the strongest baseline. Known-category accuracy is preserved or slightly improved (e.g., 71.9% on CIFAR-100 vs. 71.4% for GM), and overall accuracy reaches 68.0% on CIFAR-100 and 55.8% on Tiny-ImageNet. In the five-stage continuous setting, GPA maintains 4.1%–13.0% novel-accuracy advantage over the best baseline (GM, iNCD, or Happy) across stages. For novel-category number estimation, PPM achieves an average error of 8.1%, compared to 48.9% for MACC and 79.5% for EMaCS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPA outperforms all 10 NCDL/FL baselines on every metric across all four main datasets. The closest competitor is Happy [36], which achieves the second-best novel accuracy (e.g., 39.2% on CIFAR-100, 27.8% on Tiny-ImageNet); GPA surpasses it by 13.1% and 11.3% respectively. Among centralized NCDL methods, AutoNovel [16] and iNCD [44] show moderate novel accuracy (33–35% on CIFAR-100) but suffer high variance (iNCD: ±15.5%), while OpenCon [46] and IIC [32] are substantially weaker (18–33% novel). Federated-specific baselines—Orchestra [35], SemiFL [9], FedoSSL [61], and AGCL [40]—all lag significantly, with SemiFL dropping to 44.2% known accuracy on CIFAR-100 due to its semi-supervised design. For category-number estimation, PPM (8.1% avg. error) dramatically outperforms MACC [50] (48.9%) and EMaCS [7] (79.5%), confirming that the prototype-merging strategy is far more reliable in the non-IID federated setting. In the five-stage continuous setting, GPA maintains its lead over GM [62], iNCD [44], and Happy [36] with 4.1%–13.0% novel-accuracy gains, demonstrating robustness to sequential category introduction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Federated Learning and Novel Category Discovery and Learning (NCDL), drawing on prototypical contrastive learning (PCL) for known-category representation quality, SimCLR-style instance discrimination for the Contrastive Weighted Loss, and the Grow-and-Merge filtering strategy for separating novel from known data. It departs from standard FL, which assumes a static and well-characterized data distribution, and from centralized NCDL, which typically assumes access to labeled known-category data or a known novel-category count. The only prior work addressing category discovery in FL (AGCL) relies on labeled data and does not support continuous multi-stage learning, leaving the unsupervised, continuous, federated setting unaddressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: deployed FL systems in healthcare, finance, and IoT must adapt to emerging categories without central data access or labeled data, a requirement that current FL and NCDL methods cannot jointly satisfy. The modular GPA framework can be plugged into mainstream FL algorithms (FedProx, SCAFFOLD, Moon), broadening its applicability. Limitations include the assumption of a single bias-free linear classifier layer for prototype extraction, fixed filtering thresholds, and the requirement that participants transmit Kmeans centroids (a mild privacy trade-off). Open questions include scalability to hundreds of participants, handling truly asynchronous category arrivals rather than periodic stages, and extension to non-vision modalities.
