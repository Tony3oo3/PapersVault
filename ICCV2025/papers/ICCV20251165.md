## Problem

Conventional shape-matching methods for geometric assembly rely solely on finding visually identical surfaces between parts, which suffers from local ambiguities where similar-looking regions on different parts are incorrectly matched. The paper identifies that interlocking mating parts possess two distinct geometric properties—identical surface shape and opposite volume occupancy—that must both be captured for reliable assembly, yet prior methods model only the former.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251165/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of fractured part point clouds (approximately 5,000 surface points per holistic object, allocated proportionally to each part's surface area), each independently subjected to a random rigid transformation. It outputs per-part rotation and translation parameters that, when applied, reconstruct the original target object; evaluation uses relative poses between parts rather than absolute positioning, with metrics including CRD, Chamfer Distance, and RMSE of rotation/translation.

## Method

The proposed combinative matching framework uses VN-EdgeConvs to extract rotation-equivariant features, then applies Gram-Schmidt orientation hypothesizing to produce per-point SO(3) orientations; dot-product with these orientations yields rotation-invariant features shared by two dedicated MLP branches. The shape branch (LeakyReLU) is trained with standard circle loss to cluster identical surface geometries, while the occupancy branch (Tanh) uses a modified circle loss where positive-pair affinity is measured by the L2 norm of the sum of normalized descriptors (equivalent to negative cosine similarity), explicitly encouraging opposite volume occupancy between mating regions. A unified cost matrix combines shape similarity minus occupancy similarity, followed by an Optimal Transport layer for one-to-one correspondence and weighted SVD for rigid transformation estimation. The full training objective jointly optimizes orientation alignment, shape matching, occupancy matching, and a point-matching cross-entropy loss under a self-supervised paradigm where ground-truth correspondences are derived from the known fracture decomposition.

## Results

On the Breaking Bad dataset, CMNet achieves CRD of 0.28 (×10⁻²) and CD of 0.17 (×10⁻³) on the everyday subset, and CRD of 0.49 (×10⁻²) and CD of 0.34 (×10⁻³) on the artifact subset for pairwise assembly, outperforming the previous state-of-the-art PMTR by 28% and 18% relative CRD improvement respectively. For multi-part assembly, CMNet reaches 73.88% Part Accuracy (CRD) on everyday and 71.02% on artifact, surpassing PMTR (66.95% and 66.96%) by over 6 percentage points. The method also achieves the lowest rotation RMSE (12.88° everyday, 18.77° artifact) and translation RMSE (3.78×10⁻² everyday, 5.57×10⁻² artifact) among all compared methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CMNet outperforms all nine baselines across both everyday and artifact subsets on every reported metric. Against the closest competitor PMTR [14], CMNet reduces CRD by 28% (0.28 vs 0.39) on everyday and 18% (0.49 vs 0.60) on artifact, while also improving rotation RMSE by ~25% (12.88° vs 17.14°) and translation RMSE by ~32% (3.78 vs 5.53 ×10⁻²) on everyday. GeoTransformer [32], the strongest registration-based baseline, is surpassed by CMNet in CRD (0.28 vs 0.61) and CD (0.17 vs 0.51) on everyday, though GeoTransformer achieves a slightly lower translation RMSE on everyday (7.28 vs 3.78 ×10⁻² — actually CMNet is lower here). Jigsaw [22] and the global-embedding methods (NSM [5], Wu et al. [44]) are substantially outperformed, with CRD gaps of 19× and ~75× respectively on everyday. On multi-part assembly (Table 4), CMNet extends its lead, achieving 73.88% PA_CRD on everyday versus 66.95% for PMTR, confirming that the combinative matching advantage scales to more complex assembly scenarios.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in geometric shape assembly—matching parts by maximizing visual similarity at mating interfaces (as in PMTR, Jigsaw, GeoTransformer)—by explicitly modeling two complementary properties of interlocking shapes: identical surface geometry and opposite volume occupancy. It builds on Vector Neurons (VN-EdgeConvs) as an equivariant backbone to derive rotation-invariant features that encode orientation, then splits into dedicated shape and occupancy branches trained with opposing circle-loss variants. The closest conceptual precursor is Jigsaw's primal-dual descriptor, which the authors argue is insufficient because it merely swaps two descriptors without distinguishing their geometric roles; here, shape descriptors are trained to be similar while occupancy descriptors are trained to be opposite, a sharper decomposition of the interlocking constraint.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dual-property formulation (identical shape + inverted occupancy) provides a principled way to resolve local ambiguities that plague purely appearance-based matching, and the ablations confirm both branches are necessary and synergistic. The method's reliance on equivariant features for orientation alignment is a clean architectural choice that could transfer to other low-overlap registration or assembly settings. However, the approach still fails under extremely low surface overlap or visually indistinguishable fracture surfaces, and the authors note that texture/color cues or cycle-consistency constraints would be needed to handle those cases. Broader implications include extending the framework to incorporate physical compatibility or functional constraints, potentially making it applicable to industrial assembly beyond fracture reassembly.
