## Problem

Existing metrics for evaluating structured 3D wireframe reconstructions (WED, Chamfer Distance, F1 scores, spectral distances) frequently disagree with how professional 3D modelers judge reconstruction quality, can be trivially gamed (e.g., splitting edges into collinear segments), and lack a systematic empirical comparison against human preferences. No prior work has measured how well these metrics align with expert judgment or proposed principled tests for their desirable properties.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251859/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The learned metric consumes a pair of 3D wireframes (predicted reconstruction and ground truth) rendered from a single canonical viewpoint, extracts DiNOv2 image features from those renderings, and outputs a scalar quality score via an MLP head. The broader evaluation protocol involves 27 reconstruction methods across 10 houses, with 18 human annotators (3D modelers, CV engineers, designers) providing exhaustive pairwise rankings (3510 pairs per rater) using an interactive 3D viewer that allows synchronized zoom, pan, and rotation of both wireframes superimposed on the ground truth.

## Method

The learned metric distills human pairwise preferences by rendering wireframe pairs from a canonical viewpoint, extracting DiNOv2 features, and training an MLP regression head to predict a scalar score under a Bradley-Terry probability model with binary cross-entropy loss (batch size 16). Training uses 10-fold cross-validation where ground-truth structures and reconstruction methods are kept disjoint between train and test splits, achieving 76% pairwise accuracy. The paper additionally proposes a suite of formal 'unit tests' (monotonicity under five perturbation types, identity of indiscernibles, symmetry, triangle inequality, quasi-proportionality) evaluated on 128 ground-truth wireframes, and systematically measures agreement between 15+ handcrafted metrics, VLM judges, and the learned metric against the human preference ranking derived via win rates, Bradley-Terry abilities, and SVD factor analysis.

## Results

The proposed learned metric (DiNOv2 features + MLP regression head) achieves 76% average accuracy across 10-fold cross-validation in predicting the human-preferred wireframe. Among handcrafted metrics, Edge F1 (14/17), Edge Recall (13/17), and Corner F1 (12/17) pass the most unit-test properties, while WED with pre-registration (used in the S²3DR challenge) passes only 8/17 and correlates worst with human preferences (near random chance). VLMs (GPT-4o, o1, Grok 2, Qwen 2.5, Pixtral, Claude, Gemini) show no significant agreement with human expert rankings, with only OpenAI models and Grok 2 marginally above chance. Annotator self-consistency is 89.4% and accuracy on synthetic 'easy' pairs is 98.3%, with estimated panel error of ~1% for 11 expert raters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's learned metric (DiNOv2 + MLP) outperforms all handcrafted metrics in agreement with human preferences, achieving 76% pairwise accuracy, while the best handcrafted metrics (Edge F1, Jaccard) show moderate agreement (~80% among annotators). WED in all its variants (pre-registration, MNN, nearest, AP) from PC2WF [24] consistently underperforms: WED with pre-registration (the S²3DR challenge metric) correlates worst with human judgment, performing only slightly above random chance, and passes only 8/17 unit tests. All seven VLM baselines (OpenAI o1, Grok 2, Qwen 2.5, Pixtral 12B, Claude 3.5/3.7, Gemini 2.0/2.0-flash) fail to show meaningful agreement with human expert rankings, with only OpenAI models and Grok 2 marginally exceeding chance level. The paper concludes that Edge F1 or Jaccard combined with Corner F1 are the most reliable handcrafted alternatives, while the learned metric, though superior, carries risks of reward hacking and adversarial exploitation that make it unsuitable as a sole adjudication mechanism for high-stakes competitions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a single prior method but rather carves new ground by treating the *evaluation* of structured 3D reconstruction as the research question itself. It systematically benchmarks existing metrics (WED from PC2WF, Chamfer Distance, Spectral Graph Distance, F1/Jaccard/Hausdorff) against human expert preferences, a comparison that prior challenge papers (S23DR, Building3D) performed implicitly but never explicitly validated. The learned metric repurposes DiNOv2 self-supervised features and Bradley-Terry preference modeling—tools from vision and ranking literature—into a wireframe quality scorer, while the proposed 'unit tests' for metric properties (monotonicity, quasi-proportionality, etc.) introduce a formal verification framework absent from prior structured-reconstruction work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that WED—the metric used to determine winners in the S23DR and Building3D CVPR Challenges—correlates least with human expert preferences (barely above random chance in its pre-registered variant) challenges a foundational assumption in the structured reconstruction community and has direct implications for how future benchmarks should be scored. The practical recommendation to use simpler edge F1 + Jaccard + corner F1 combinations over complex graph-edit metrics is immediately actionable for practitioners. Open questions include whether the learned metric (76% accuracy) can be made robust enough to serve as an automated judge in high-stakes competitions, whether WED can be salvaged by incorporating richer edit operations (bulk deletion, rigid transforms, edge fitting), and whether the two-cluster structure in human preferences (vertex-focused vs. edge-focused) reflects a genuine ambiguity in what 'quality' means for wireframes.
