## Problem

Learning effective dense reward functions for robotic manipulation typically requires either manually designed rewards, action-labeled demonstrations, or task-specific models. Existing video-based reward learners (e.g., Rank2Reward) need separate models per task and struggle with the distribution shift between expert pretraining data and the non-expert observations encountered during online RL exploration. PROGRESSOR addresses both gaps by learning a single task-agnostic reward from unlabeled videos and adversarially refining it during online training.

## Input / Output

The model consumes three RGB images per query: an initial observation o_i, a current observation o_j, and a goal observation o_g, all processed by a shared ResNet-34 encoder. It outputs a Gaussian distribution N(μ, σ²) over normalized progress in [0,1], which is converted to a scalar dense reward via r = μ − α·H(N(μ,σ²)) with α=0.4, so that high-variance (uncertain) predictions are penalized. During online RL, the same triplet interface is used but the target progress for out-of-distribution frames is decayed by a factor β=0.9 to push predictions downward.

## Method

PROGRESSOR learns a task-agnostic progress estimator by minimizing KL divergence between a predicted Gaussian and a target Gaussian whose mean is the normalized frame position (j−i)/(g−i) and whose standard deviation is 1/(g−i), trained on randomly sampled triplets from expert trajectories. The key architectural choice is a shared visual encoder followed by MLPs that jointly predict μ and log σ², enabling a single model to handle all tasks without per-task retraining. During online RL, an adversarial push-back mechanism updates the reward model on the policy's own rollouts by setting the target progress to βμ (β=0.9) with a stop-gradient, effectively teaching the model to assign lower progress to non-expert observations while simultaneously re-anchoring on expert data to prevent drift. The resulting reward r_θ(o_i, o_j, o_g) = μ − α·H(N(μ,σ²)) provides dense, uncertainty-aware supervision for model-free RL (DrQ-v2) or reward-weighted regression (RWR-ACT) without any environment reward or action labels.

## Results

On six Meta-World simulation tasks, PROGRESSOR significantly outperforms all five baselines in both episodic return and success rate, requiring only 10% of the total training budget (150K of 1.5M steps) to surpass baselines on drawer-open and hammer. On door-open and peg-insert-side, where nearly all other methods fail entirely, PROGRESSOR achieves strong success rates. In real-world few-shot offline RL with 50% failed demonstrations, PROGRESSOR-RWR-ACT outperforms GVL, R3M, VIP, and vanilla ACT on average across four manipulation tasks, with the largest gaps on the harder Push-Block and Drawer-Open tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PROGRESSOR outperforms all five simulation baselines (TCN, GAIL, SOIL, ROT, Rank2Reward) across most Meta-World tasks, with the most dramatic wins on door-open and peg-insert-side where baselines fail entirely. TCN, which is trained only on expert data without online updates, fails at every task due to distribution shift. Rank2Reward, the closest prior method, requires a separate model per task plus an additional classifier for online adaptation, whereas PROGRESSOR uses a single unified model. In real-world experiments, GVL-RWR-ACT performs comparably to PROGRESSOR on the easier Pick-Place-Cup and Drawer-Close tasks but drops significantly on the harder Open-Drawer and Push-Block tasks. R3M-RWR-ACT and VIP-RWR-ACT show moderate success on Drawer-Close but degrade substantially on the two harder tasks. Vanilla ACT (uniform weighting, ω=0) performs poorly across all tasks, confirming that reward-weighted regression with a progress-based signal is essential for handling noisy demonstrations.

## Relation to Prior Work

PROGRESSOR extends the line of work on learning reward functions from passive video, most directly building on Rank2Reward (Yang et al., ICRA 2024), which also assumes monotonic progress in expert demonstrations. It departs from Rank2Reward in two key ways: it uses a single task-agnostic model conditioned on initial/goal images rather than per-task ranking models, and it replaces Rank2Reward's separate expert/non-expert classifier with an adversarial push-back mechanism that continuously refines the reward during online RL. The real-world experiments further build on ACT (Zhao et al., 2023) by modifying its reconstruction loss to incorporate reward-weighted regression, connecting the reward-learning literature to the offline RL / imitation learning pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in demonstrating that a reward model pretrained solely on 1.29M frames of in-the-wild human kitchen video (EPIC-KITCHENS) can guide robot manipulation in a zero-shot fashion, even when half the demonstrations are failures—removing the need for task-specific reward engineering or action labels. The monotonic-progress assumption is a clear limitation that restricts applicability to tasks with non-reversible or non-cyclic structure, and the authors acknowledge that a multimodal or cycle-aware progress model would be needed to relax this. The observed degradation of push-back on the reach task (where the agent already performs well) highlights an open question about adaptive or dynamic refinement strength (e.g., a learned β) to avoid over-penalizing near-expert behavior.
