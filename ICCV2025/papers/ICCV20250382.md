## Problem

Scaffold FL's control variate mechanism, designed to correct client drift in non-IID settings, has never been examined for security vulnerabilities. This paper asks whether this correction mechanism can be exploited to amplify backdoor attacks, and shows affirmatively that a tampered control variate can co-opt benign clients into unwittingly steering their own gradient updates toward the attacker's poisoned direction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250382/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attacker consumes the local non-IID dataset (label-sorted groups from CIFAR-10/100 at 32×32 or MNIST at 28×28), the current global model weights, and the global control variate vector broadcast by the server each round. The GAN (5 deconvolutional layers for CIFAR, 3 for MNIST) generates synthetic samples to supplement the local dataset before backdoor training. The output is a pair (Δw_p, Δc_p): a poisoned model update and a crafted control variate that, when aggregated by the server, subtly biases all participating clients' future local updates toward the backdoor direction.

## Method

BadSFL operates in four stages: (1) a GAN trained locally with the latest global model as discriminator generates realistic synthetic samples from other clients' classes, bridging the non-IID knowledge gap; (2) a trigger is injected via label-flipping, a small pattern overlay, or a natural feature-based cue (e.g., green cars); (3) the backdoor model is trained with a modified objective (Eq. 3) that adds a second loss term L(D_p, P_j(w_p, c)), where P_j simulates j future aggregation rounds using the known global control variate c to predict the global model's convergence trajectory; (4) the resulting Δw_p and a corresponding Δc_p are uploaded, conforming to Scaffold's protocol while embedding the backdoor. The critical insight is that by optimizing against the predicted future global model, the poisoned update aligns with the direction benign clients will naturally follow, making the backdoor resistant to erasure by subsequent benign updates and persisting over 60 rounds—roughly 3× longer than baselines.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, BadSFL achieves above 80% Backdoor Task Accuracy (BTA) within the first 10 rounds while maintaining Primary Task Accuracy (PTA) at ~60%, and sustains BTA exceeding 90% across all 100 SFL rounds even after the attacker exits at round 40. On MNIST, BadSFL achieves both BTA and PTA above 85%. The embedded backdoor persists for over 60 rounds after the attacker stops injecting malicious updates—lasting up to 3× longer than baseline attacks (which drop below 50% BTA after round 60) and 5× longer on MNIST label-flipping (where baselines collapse below 40% within 10 rounds of the attacker's exit).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BadSFL is compared against four baselines: Black-box Attack [3], Neurotoxin [45], IBA [31], and 3DFed [18]. On CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, BadSFL outperforms all four baselines on both BTA and durability: while baselines see BTA drop below 50% after round 60, BadSFL sustains BTA above 90% for the full 100 rounds, a 3× longer backdoor lifespan. On MNIST label-flipping, the gap is even starker—baselines collapse below 40% BTA within 10 rounds of the attacker's exit, whereas BadSFL maintains a 5× longer-lasting backdoor. For the pattern trigger on MNIST, BadSFL achieves 10% higher BTA than the baselines. Among the three trigger types tested, the feature-based trigger yields the best BTA, attributed to its stealthiness (no direct image manipulation), making poisoned updates less likely to conflict with benign updates. Under combined defenses (differential privacy + model pruning, FLAME, SparseFed), BadSFL still maintains high BTA while the baselines fail, though the paper notes that Neurotoxin's L2-norm-based parameter targeting did not show its expected durability benefit in the SFL setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor-attack-in-FL literature (Bagdasaryan et al., Neurotoxin, IBA, 3DFed) from IID or algorithm-agnostic settings to the specific Scaffold aggregation algorithm, whose control-variate correction mechanism is the novel attack surface. It builds directly on Scaffold's variance-reduction design (Karimireddy et al., ICML 2020) and repurposes the global control variate—intended to align local updates with the global objective—as a signal the attacker can exploit to predict convergence direction and embed more durable backdoors. The GAN-based data supplementation draws on prior GAN-for-poisoning work (Zhang et al., 2019) but is adapted to the non-IID constraint where the attacker lacks full dataset knowledge. Conceptually, the paper departs from prior FL backdoor work by showing that the correction mechanism itself, not just the aggregation rule, can be weaponized to turn benign clients into unwitting accomplices.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Scaffold's convergence-improving control variate simultaneously creates a security vulnerability has direct implications for the deployment of variance-reduction FL methods: any system adopting Scaffold (or similar controlled-averaging schemes) inherits this attack surface, and defenses that only inspect individual client updates will miss the distributed, benign-client-mediated amplification. The 3× durability improvement over baselines, demonstrated across three datasets and multiple trigger types, suggests that existing backdoor defenses (differential privacy, model pruning, FLAME, SparseFed) are insufficient against control-variate-aware attacks. Open questions include whether the vulnerability generalizes to other variance-reduction FL algorithms (e.g., FedProx, SCAFFOLD variants), how to detect anomalous control-variate submissions without breaking the protocol, and whether the GAN-based data supplementation remains effective at larger scale or with stronger non-IID partitions.
