## Problem

Existing motion diffusion models process all frames in a sequence through self-attention, incurring quadratic computational cost and treating every frame equally regardless of its informational content. This dense-frame paradigm makes training on large motion datasets expensive, degrades quality at low diffusion step counts, and offers no natural mechanism for controllability. The paper asks whether a keyframe-centric approach—mirroring how professional animators define motion—can reduce complexity while improving text alignment and motion realism.

## Input / Output

The model takes a text prompt (encoded by CLIP ViT-B/32 or Stella-1.5B) and produces a full dense 3D human motion sequence of N frames, each a D-dimensional pose vector. Internally, only K ≪ N keyframes (determined by a binary mask at a fixed reduction rate, e.g., 80%) pass through the transformer's self-attention layers; the remaining N−K frames are reconstructed by linear interpolation in the feature space. For the character-control variant (sDiP), the input additionally includes 1 second of past trajectory and optional spatial control signals, while keyframe masking is applied only to the 2-second future prediction window.

## Method

sMDM modifies the MDM transformer backbone with a masking-and-interpolation strategy: a binary keyframe mask excludes non-keyframes from self-attention (reducing complexity from O(N²) to O(K²)), and a lightweight linear interpolation in feature space reconstructs the full dense sequence. During training, keyframes are selected by the Visvalingam-Whyatt geometric reduction algorithm on the clean motion, with small random noise added to the mask for robustness; during inference, the model starts with a uniform mask in early diffusion steps and dynamically refines it via the same algorithm on the intermediate noisy frames once t drops below γ·T (γ=0.1). To preserve high-frequency motion details lost by linear interpolation, the input and output projection layers are replaced with Lipschitz-constrained MLPs using sinusoidal activations, and the training objective adds a Lipschitz regularization term to the standard DDPM reconstruction loss computed over all (interpolated) frames.

## Results

On HumanML3D, sMDM (CLIP encoder) achieves FID 0.130, MM-Dist 3.051, and R-Precision Top-1 0.494, substantially outperforming the MDM backbone (FID 0.544, MM-Dist 5.566) and most CLIP-based baselines. With the larger Stella 1.5B encoder, sMDM-stella reaches FID 0.151, MM-Dist 2.740, and R-Precision Top-1 0.554, surpassing MotionGPT, MotionLCM, and MotionBase on all three metrics. The method also maintains strong performance at low diffusion steps (50 steps: FID 0.130; 25 steps: FID 0.167), demonstrating robustness across step counts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

sMDM dramatically outperforms its MDM backbone [32] on every metric (FID 0.130 vs 0.544; MM-Dist 3.051 vs 5.566; R-Pr Top-1 0.494 vs 0.320), confirming that sparse keyframe training is the primary driver of improvement. Among CLIP-based baselines, sMDM achieves the best FID (0.130) except for retrieval-based ReMoDiffuse [43] (FID 0.103, MM-Dist 2.974), which requires database lookups at inference; sMDM is competitive on MM-Dist (3.051) and superior on R-Precision. Against advanced-encoder baselines, sMDM-stella dominates: it beats MotionGPT [12] (FID 0.151 vs 0.230, MM-Dist 2.740 vs 3.043), MotionLCM [4] (FID 0.151 vs 0.304, MM-Dist 2.740 vs 3.012), and MotionBase [37] (FID 0.151 vs 0.166, MM-Dist 2.740 vs 2.964) on all reported metrics. In the character-control setting (Table 4), sDiP and sDiP-T consistently improve over their DiP [33] counterparts across R-Precision, FID, and MM-Dist at all tested reduction rates (60–80%). The only clear loss is to ReMoDiffuse on FID and MM-Dist, which is expected given its retrieval-augmented design that accesses ground-truth motions at test time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the MDM transformer-based diffusion architecture [32] as its backbone, modifying only the self-attention computation to attend to a sparse subset of keyframes rather than all frames. The keyframe selection leverages the Visvalingam-Whyatt line-simplification algorithm [36] repurposed from cartography to motion, and the Lipschitz MLP constraint [17] is borrowed from smooth neural function learning to stabilize linear interpolation in feature space. Conceptually, it aligns with keyframe-based video generation (KEYIN [21]) and animation workflows, but departs from prior keyframe motion works [3, 5, 6] by targeting general-purpose text-to-motion synthesis rather than in-betweening or editing, and by using geometrically meaningful (not random) keyframe selection with a dynamic inference-time update.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for real-time and resource-constrained motion generation: reducing attention complexity from O(N²) to O(K²) with K ≪ N while matching or exceeding dense-frame quality opens the door to longer sequences and faster inference. The validation across three distinct downstream tasks (text-to-motion, long-sequence generation via Double Take, and real-time character control via DiP) strengthens the claim that sparse keyframe training produces a more robust generative prior. Open questions include whether the approach extends to UNet-based architectures, whether alternative keyframe selection criteria (e.g., learned or attention-based) could further improve quality, and whether the 80% reduction rate generalizes to substantially longer or multi-person motions.
