## Problem

Existing privacy-preserving robust federated learning (PRFL) frameworks suffer from three coupled limitations: heavy computational overhead from homomorphic encryption or secure multi-party computation, rigid support for only a single fixed robust aggregation rule (vulnerable to adaptive attacks like Min-Max and IPM), and neglect of data heterogeneity, which degrades both model accuracy and RAR detection reliability. The paper asks how to preserve privacy efficiently, allow arbitrary RAR choices, and adapt to non-IID data simultaneously.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251947/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each client holds a local non-IID dataset (FEMNIST with 199 clients, CIFAR-10 with 100 clients, CIFAR-100 with 50 clients, heterogeneity controlled via Dirichlet α) and a model (MLP, CNN, or SqueezeNet) whose parameter vector is partitioned into d disjoint segments of tunable size |γ|. Clients produce a mixed model (segments swapped with a partner via XOR masking) and a personalized local model; the server outputs a global model by applying a chosen RAR independently to each segment group and aggregating the top n−2f scored models. The segment size is a critical hyperparameter trading off robustness (smaller segments reduce curse of dimensionality) against privacy (larger segments leak more gradient information to inference attacks).

## Method

FLSeg introduces three coupled mechanisms. First, local training adds a regularization term λ/2‖v_i − w^t‖² that maintains a personalized model v_i alongside the global model, improving adaptation to non-IID data without altering the aggregation pipeline. Second, the Segment Exchange protocol has each client pair agree on a binary mask m via Diffie-Hellman (optimized to a PRNG seed exchange), then swap complementary segments using XOR with a client key r_i encrypted to the server; the server decrypts the mixed model but cannot recover the original because it lacks m and the partner identity. Third, Segment Aggregation applies any chosen RAR (Krum, DnC, RFA, etc.) independently to each segment group across all clients, computes per-segment cosine similarity to the RAR benchmark, sums into a model score, and aggregates only the top n−2f models—thereby reducing the effective dimensionality seen by the RAR and ensuring each segment originates from a different client. The framework achieves O(ℓ) client and O(nℓ) server complexity (versus O(ℓ(T_Enc+T_Dec)) for HE-based methods), and semi-honest security is proven via a hybrid argument in the UC framework showing the mixed model is computationally indistinguishable from random.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with 20% Min-Max attackers (α=0.5), FLSeg(DnC) achieves 64.59% top-1 accuracy, substantially outperforming ACORN (48.21%), ShieldFL (38.51%), and PEFL (12.36%), while FedAvg drops to 35.89%. Under no attack, FLSeg(DnC) reaches 63.99% on CIFAR-10, 77.88% on FEMNIST, and 53.55% on CIFAR-100, exceeding the FedAvg baselines (52.16%, 70.17%, 35.92%) by 11.8, 7.7, and 17.6 percentage points respectively. Computationally, FLSeg requires only 233 ms client-side and 78 ms/server-side per client, an 8× and 2× speedup over the fastest SOTA method (ACORN at 1696 ms and 181 ms/c), and orders of magnitude faster than HE-based methods (ShieldFL: 231,541 ms client; PEFL: 790,032 ms/c server).

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLSeg(DnC) dominates all baselines across every dataset–attack combination in Table 2. Against the strongest privacy-preserving robust FL competitor ACORN, FLSeg(DnC) gains +16.4 pp on CIFAR-10 Min-Max (64.59 vs 48.21), +6.5 pp on FEMNIST Min-Max (77.74 vs 71.29), and +30.1 pp on CIFAR-100 Min-Max (51.11 vs 21.04). HE-based methods ShieldFL and PEFL collapse under adaptive attacks: PEFL drops to 12.36% on CIFAR-10 Min-Max and 3.20% on CIFAR-100 Min-Max, while ShieldFL reaches only 38.51% and 34.39% respectively. Among pure RAR baselines, DnC alone achieves 50.62% on CIFAR-10 Min-Max, but wrapping it in FLSeg lifts accuracy to 64.59% (+14.0 pp); Krum alone scores 45.46% but FLSeg(Krum) reaches 60.82% (+15.4 pp). On the no-attack setting, FLSeg also outperforms FedAvg by 11.8 pp (CIFAR-10), 7.7 pp (FEMNIST), and 17.6 pp (CIFAR-100), confirming that segment-level aggregation and personalized training improve utility even without adversaries. Computationally, FLSeg's 233 ms client time is 7.3× faster than ACORN (1696 ms) and ~1000× faster than ShieldFL (231,541 ms), while its 78 ms/c server cost is 2.3× lower than ACORN (181 ms/c) and ~1000× lower than PEFL (790,032 ms/c).

## Relation to Prior Work

FLSeg sits at the intersection of three established lines of work: privacy-preserving FL (HE/SMPC-based methods like ShieldFL, PEFL, and ACORN), robust aggregation rules (Krum, DnC, RFA), and personalized FL (Ditto, Moreau envelopes). It departs from prior PRFL frameworks by replacing heavyweight encryption or SMPC protocols with a lightweight XOR-based segment exchange for privacy, and by applying RAR at the segment level rather than on the full model—conceptually similar to gradient splitting (GAS) but extended with a privacy mechanism. The personalization component directly adapts the Ditto-style regularization objective, while the segment-level RAR application generalizes the dimensionality-reduction insight from DnC and GAS into a privacy-compatible setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLSeg is the first PRFL framework that simultaneously addresses computational efficiency, unrestricted RAR choice, and data heterogeneity—three limitations that prior methods (ACORN, ShieldFL, PEFL) could not handle together. The 8× client-side speedup over the fastest baseline and the ability to plug in any RAR make it practically attractive for large-scale deployments. However, the threat model is limited to a semi-honest server (not a malicious one), the segment size is a sensitive hyperparameter with a non-trivial accuracy-privacy tradeoff, and the reputation system for partner selection is heuristic. Open questions include extending to malicious-server settings, decentralized architectures, and whether the segment-level RAR advantage persists at much larger model scales.
