## Problem

Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) and neighbor search (Ball Query, k-NN) dominate inference latency in PointNet++-based 3D point cloud models, consuming 88–94% of total execution time. FPS is inherently sequential—only one point is selected per iteration—making it difficult to parallelize, while neighbor search redundantly recomputes distances already calculated during FPS. Existing software-based accelerations either require specialized hardware or introduce significant accuracy loss (up to 2% mIoU), leaving a gap for a practical, accuracy-preserving software solution.

## Input / Output

FastPoint operates as a drop-in software acceleration layer on existing PointNet++-based models (PointMetaBase-L, PointVector-L) that take raw 3D point clouds (indoor scenes from S3DIS/ScanNet, outdoor LiDAR sequences from SemanticKITTI) and output per-point semantic segmentation labels. It is applied only to the first Set Abstraction layer, where FPS and neighbor search account for over 90% of total computation. Critically, the models are trained with standard FPS and FastPoint is applied at inference time without any retraining, since MDPS closely replicates the FPS sampling pattern.

## Method

The core insight is that the minimum distance curve in FPS follows a smooth, predictable decreasing trend, and the first few sample points capture the overall point cloud structure. FastPoint trains a 3-layer MLP to predict the remaining 90% of this curve from just the first 10% of FPS iterations (MAPE ~1–2%). The predicted curve is divided into 6 segments, and a fully parallelizable exclusion list is built by computing all pairwise distances once, encoding which points fall within each segment's radius threshold. Sampling then proceeds by randomly selecting from available (non-excluded) points per segment, eliminating per-iteration sequential distance computation. The same exclusion list is reused for Ball Query (directly) and k-NN (as a search-space filter), eliminating redundant distance calculations. An early-termination mechanism handles curve overestimation by falling back to original FPS for remaining iterations. This yields a 2.55× end-to-end speedup on RTX 3090 with negligible accuracy change (≤0.12% mIoU).

## Results

FastPoint achieves a geomean 2.55× end-to-end speedup over baseline FPS on NVIDIA RTX 3090 across S3DIS, ScanNet, and SemanticKITTI, with per-dataset speedups ranging from 1.98× (PV-L, S3DIS) to 3.38× (PMB-L, ScanNet). Accuracy is preserved with mIoU differences within ±0.12% of the FPS baseline across all model-dataset combinations. Sampling quality (average minimum distance) reaches 99.45% (S3DIS), 99.35% (ScanNet), and 98.36% (SemanticKITTI) of the FPS baseline. Combining FastPoint with QuickFPS further boosts the geomean speedup to 2.76×.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the FPS baseline, MDPS matches accuracy within ±0.12% mIoU across all six model-dataset combinations while delivering 2.55× end-to-end speedup, confirming that the predicted-distance sampling faithfully replicates FPS behavior without retraining. Compared to Random Sampling (from RandLA-Net), MDPS substantially outperforms, with gaps of 6.5–12.5 mIoU (e.g., 71.37 vs. 64.83 on PV-L/S3DIS), demonstrating that random sampling's low coverage is not recoverable by downstream aggregation alone. Against Grid Sampling (from KPConv), MDPS is consistently equal or better: it wins on 5 of 6 configurations and ties within 0.08 mIoU on the one exception (PMB-L/SemanticKITTI), while grid sampling shows larger and less consistent accuracy drops (up to −1.09 mIoU). Against QuickFPS (software k-d-tree FPS), FastPoint alone achieves 2.55× geomean speedup versus QuickFPS's lower standalone speedup; the two methods are complementary, and their combination yields 2.76× geomean speedup (1.41× over QuickFPS alone), showing FastPoint's compatibility with existing FPS optimizations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the PointNet++ architecture, targeting its two dominant latency bottlenecks—Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) and neighbor search (Ball Query, k-NN)—with a purely software-based approach. It departs from hardware-assisted accelerators (QuickFPS, MARS, PTrAcc) that require specialized silicon, and from approximation-based software methods (random sampling in RandLA-Net, grid sampling in KPConv, EdgePC, Adjustable FPS) that sacrifice accuracy. The key conceptual departure is the observation that the minimum-distance curve in FPS is smooth and predictable, enabling a small MLP to estimate the full curve from just 10% of iterations, thereby decoupling sampling from distance computation and opening new parallelism opportunities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 2.55× end-to-end speedup with negligible accuracy loss (≤0.12 mIoU) makes real-time 3D point cloud inference more practical on commodity GPUs without retraining existing models. The decoupling of sampling from distance computation is a structural insight that could inform future optimizations beyond PointNet++ architectures. Limitations include evaluation only on the first layer (where >90% of coordinate-based cost resides), restriction to PointNet++-based models, and the need for a per-dataset trained MLP estimator. Open questions include whether the distance-curve prediction generalizes to non-PointNet++ architectures (e.g., voxel-based or transformer-based models) and whether the approach can be extended to the training phase.
