## Problem

Existing differentiable rendering methods are limited to first-order gradients, leaving the curvature information (Hessians and Hessian-vector products) needed by higher-order optimizers like Newton or Conjugate Gradient entirely unexplored for inverse rendering. The rendering equation's discontinuities prevent naive differentiation, and no prior work has derived or sampled second-order differentials of the rendering operator.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252134/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a rendered image and a reference image, treating the rendering operator as a black box that only requires point evaluations (no analytical gradients or AD graphs). It produces Hessian matrices (n×n), Hessian-vector products (n-vectors), and optimized scene parameters (geometry, reflectance, light, or CNN weights). The key constraint is that each function evaluation corresponds to one full render call, so the sampling strategy must minimize the number of renders per iteration.

## Method

The approach convolves the rendering equation's integrand with a Gaussian kernel to enforce smoothness, then applies second-order differential operators to the combined kernel, yielding closed-form expressions for Hessian and HVP sampling densities. For the Hessian, diagonal elements require inverting a transcendental CDF (tabulated in 1D), while off-diagonals factor into products of independent gradient samplers. The HVP is computed as central differences of the gradient estimator along a given direction. The key efficiency innovation is 'aggregate' importance sampling: instead of sampling each of the n² Hessian elements independently (requiring n² renders), the method samples the average of all kernel dimensions simultaneously, reducing function evaluations to O(1) per iteration while remaining unbiased as a linear combination of unbiased estimators.

## Results

The paper's primary metric is convergence time (wall-clock) to reach 90% and 99% error reduction, reported as ratios to the best method (OurHVPA = 1.00). Across all tasks, OurHVPA achieves an average 2.71× speedup over the next-best method. Gains are largest on the MUG task (~7× faster than FR22) and SHADOW (~60% faster), while on the simple QUAD quadratic, CMA-ES actually outperforms all proposed methods. On the high-dimensional TEXTURE task (1024 params), the full Hessian (OurH) is 40× slower than the HVP variant due to matrix storage, confirming the importance of the HVP formulation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against FR22 (Fischer & Ritschel, CVPR 2023), the proposed OurHVPA consistently outperforms across all tasks where both converge: on MUG, FR22 requires 3.28× the time to reach 90% parameter error reduction vs. OurHVPA's 1.00×, and on TEXTURE the gap widens to 4.04×. FR22 is competitive only on BUNNY (0.78× vs 1.00×), a task specifically designed for Mitsuba to converge without plateaus. Against CMA-ES, the comparison is more nuanced: CMA-ES wins on the trivial QUAD quadratic (0.13× vs 1.00×) where its covariance adaptation is well-suited, but OurHVPA dominates on all non-trivial rendering tasks (e.g., 8.13× slower on BOX2, 4.38× on SHADOW, 11.16× on TEXTURE). The paper also notes that OurG (their first-order gradient) typically cannot outperform FR22, indicating that the benefit of their sampling framework materializes only at higher order. On the highest-dimensional task (CNN5, 268K parameters), even OurHVPA cannot reduce error below 1:1000, though it remains the fastest method that does converge.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the MC-based gradient estimation framework of Fischer and Ritschel (CVPR 2023), which itself builds on the smoothing-for-differentiability idea from Chaudhuri and Solar-Lezama (2010), by deriving analogous importance-sampling estimators for Hessians and Hessian-vector products. While virtually all prior differentiable renderers (Mitsuba, OpenDR, etc.) are limited to first-order AD through the rendering pipeline, this work treats the renderer as a black box and samples a combined smoothing-and-differentiation convolution kernel, enabling any-order differentials without modifying the renderer. The aggregate sampling strategy—importance-sampling the average of all kernel dimensions simultaneously—repurposes the mixture-sampling idea from multiple importance sampling in rendering to reduce function evaluations from O(n²) to O(1) for Hessians.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first published method to compute Hessians and HVPs for inverse rendering, opening the door to Newton and Conjugate Gradient optimizers in a domain previously locked to gradient descent. The 2.71× average speedup demonstrates that the per-iteration overhead of higher-order methods is negligible when paired with efficient MC estimation, challenging the assumption that such methods are impractical for rendering. However, performance degrades noticeably with dimensionality (e.g., 40× slowdown for full Hessians on the 1024-parameter TEXTURE task), and the most demanding CNN tasks still fail to reach 1:1000 error reduction, leaving open the question of how to scale higher-order inverse rendering to high-dimensional parameter spaces.
