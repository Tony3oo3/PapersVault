## Problem

Despite the proliferation of jailbreak attacks and defense mechanisms for text-to-image diffusion models, there is no standardized benchmark to systematically evaluate pipeline robustness. Existing safety datasets like I2P contain significant labeling errors (36.3% of prompts reclassified as safe upon human re-annotation), and widely deployed moderation tools have never been compared under a unified protocol, making it impossible to assess the true security posture of real-world diffusion pipelines.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes three categories of text prompts—10,000 benign (from DiffusionDB), 3,750 NSFW (aggregated from I2P, 4chan, Civitai, VBCDE, and online sources), and 2,000 adversarial (MMA-based)—each routed through a formalized pipeline of prompt checker → diffusion model → image checker. For image-level evaluation, 6,000 benign and 5,439 NSFW AI-generated images across 10 diffusion models serve as ground truth. Outputs include binary moderation decisions, generated images, and a suite of quantitative metrics (Bypass Rate, Attack Success Rate, Human-Verified ASR, BLIP Alignment Score, F1, AUROC, MCC) computed per content category and per attack method.

## Method

JailbreakDiffBench formalizes a diffusion safety system as a triplet (C_p, D, C_i) and defines jailbreak success as simultaneously bypassing both checkers while producing an image that a human judger confirms as harmful and an alignment checker confirms as semantically matching the original prohibited intent. The benchmark comprises three components: (1) a standardized evaluation protocol that benchmarks 11 prompt checkers (rule-based, BERT-based, commercial APIs, LLM-based) and 7 image checkers (CLIP-based classifiers, commercial APIs, multimodal LLMs) against the human-annotated JDBench dataset; (2) an attack assessment module that tests five jailbreak strategies (PGJ, DACA, SneakPrompt, MMA, Ring-A-Bell) against eight diffusion models (SD-v1.5 through FLUX.1-dev, Hunyuan-Distill, CogView3); and (3) a safety analysis toolkit combining ASR, BPR, AlignS, and HASR to disentangle detection evasion from actual harmful content generation. A key empirical finding is that high bypass rates (DACA and PGJ exceed 50%) do not translate to high attack success rates (typically <10%), revealing that evading automated filters does not guarantee the diffusion model will produce the intended harmful output.

## Results

Among prompt checkers, GPT-4o-mini(IC) achieves the best F1 score (0.8137) and accuracy (0.872), while GPT-4o reaches the highest TPR (0.908) but suffers a high FPR (0.548). For image checkers, GPT-4o dominates with 92.03% accuracy, 86.43% TPR, and 0.9116 F1, far outperforming Multihead Detector (75.71% ACC) and commercial APIs (Azure: 62.56%, OpenAI: 54.19%). In jailbreak evaluations, DACA and PGJ achieve the highest bypass rates (54–58.5% and 50–52.25% respectively), yet actual Attack Success Rates remain low (3–8%) and Human-Verified ASR is even lower (2–8%), indicating that evading automated checks does not reliably produce harmful outputs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among prompt checkers, GPT-4o-mini(IC) (F1=0.8137) outperforms NSFW-Text-Classifier (F1=0.7991), Google Text Moderation (F1=0.7878), and all BERT-based variants, while Llama-Guard-3 fails entirely (TPR=0.0, F1=0.0). For image checkers, GPT-4o (ACC=92.03%, F1=0.9116) substantially outperforms Multihead Detector (ACC=75.71%), Llava Guard (ACC=72.83%), and both commercial APIs (Azure: 62.56%, OpenAI: 54.19%). In jailbreak attacks, DACA achieves the highest BPR (54–58.5%) and ASR (4.75–8.25%) across models, edging out PGJ (BPR 49.75–52.25%, ASR 1.75–6.00%); SneakPrompt and MMA have much lower BPR (4.25–8.25%) but MMA achieves the highest AlignS (up to 67.50% on SD-XL-Base-0.9). Ring-A-Bell (RABell) consistently shows the lowest BPR (0.50–0.75%) due to its random tokens being easily flagged by LLM-based prompt checkers. Notably, even the best attacks (DACA, PGJ) achieve ASR below 10% and HASR below 8%, revealing a large gap between bypassing automated filters and actually generating verified harmful content.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper consolidates the fragmented landscape of diffusion model safety evaluation into a unified benchmark, drawing on the jailbreak evaluation paradigm established for LLMs and adapting it to the multi-stage diffusion pipeline (prompt checker → diffusion model → image checker). It repurposes existing attack methods (MMA-Diffusion, SneakPrompt, DACA, PGJ, Ring-A-Bell) and defense tools (BERT classifiers, commercial APIs, LLM-based checkers, CLIP-based NSFW detectors) under a common protocol, rather than proposing new attack or defense techniques. The key departure from prior work is the addition of semantic alignment verification (via BLIP and GPT-4o) to confirm that bypassed outputs actually match the original prohibited intent, and the systematic human re-annotation that exposes significant labeling errors in the widely-used I2P dataset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that 36.3% of I2P prompts were mislabeled and that the Copyright category was entirely absent from original annotations undermines a commonly used evaluation resource and motivates the need for the proposed JDBench dataset. The large gap between bypass rate (50%+ for PGJ/DACA) and actual attack success rate (4–8%) reveals that evading automated filters does not reliably produce harmful content, reframing the threat model for practitioners. Open questions include whether the benchmark's findings generalize to text-to-video models (which the authors note as an extension goal), whether the alignment verification step introduces its own biases, and how the evaluation scales as new diffusion architectures and moderation tools emerge rapidly.
