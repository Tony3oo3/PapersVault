## Problem

JSMA provides interpretable, pixel-level adversarial attacks through Jacobian-based saliency maps, but its O(M²×N) computational complexity makes it infeasible for large-scale datasets like ImageNet, where both memory and processing time become prohibitive. The paper asks whether JSMA's core mathematical structure can be reformulated to preserve its interpretability and pixel-selection capability while reducing complexity to a tractable level.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251141/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image (32×32×3 for CIFAR-10/100, 224×224×3 for ImageNet) and a pretrained ResNet18 classifier, and produces an L0-norm-constrained adversarial perturbation that modifies a fixed number of pixels (8 for 32×32 images, 196 for 224×224 images, ~0.78% of total pixels). The perturbation is untargeted—its goal is to flip the predicted class to any incorrect class—and is generated by selecting the highest-saliency pixels and adjusting their values in the direction indicated by the decoupled gradients.

## Method

FastJSMA replaces the full Jacobian matrix computation with a gradient decoupling strategy that constructs two virtual labels and computes only two gradient vectors via automatic differentiation: a class suppression gradient (g⁻) that decreases the current class score and optionally boosts a specific attempt class by √N, and a class excitation gradient (g⁺) that uniformly increases all non-target class scores. After mean-normalization, g⁻ is additionally scaled by N to amplify its influence, and the two are summed into a gain matrix G that ranks pixels by their bidirectional impact on the prediction. A class probing mechanism then explores √N candidate classes (selected from the top predicted classes) to identify the most effective gradient direction, and a significance threshold filters out low-impact pixels before perturbation is applied. This reduces complexity from O(M²×N) to O(M√N), enabling execution on ImageNet where traditional JSMA and WJSMA fail entirely due to memory constraints.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, FastJSMA achieves 43.98% ASR in 339 μs per sample, matching JSMANT's 43.79% ASR while running 34.7× faster (11,769 μs). On CIFAR-100, FastJSMA reaches 45.30% ASR in 751 μs versus JSMANT's 43.87% in 61,166 μs (81.4× speedup). On ImageNet (1,000 classes, 224×224), FastJSMA achieves 54.89% ASR in 7,908 μs per sample, while both JSMANT and WJSMA fail entirely due to memory constraints. The L2 perturbation norms remain comparable across methods (1.53–1.75 on CIFAR, 9.19 on ImageNet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against JSMANT (the original non-targeted JSMA), FastJSMA matches or slightly exceeds ASR on both CIFAR-10 (43.98% vs. 43.79%) and CIFAR-100 (45.30% vs. 43.87%) while reducing per-sample computation time by 34.7× and 81.4× respectively. Against WJSMA, FastJSMA trades some ASR (43.98% vs. 51.56% on CIFAR-10; 45.30% vs. 49.78% on CIFAR-100) for a 25–68× speedup, and WJSMA's lower L2 norm (1.29 vs. 1.53) indicates slightly smaller perturbations. The most decisive comparison is on ImageNet: FastJSMA is the only JSMA-family method that completes at all, achieving 54.89% ASR in 7,908 μs, whereas both JSMANT and WJSMA crash due to the O(M²×N) memory requirement of the full Jacobian. A random-pixel baseline (7.55% on CIFAR-10, 15.31% on CIFAR-100, 2.97% on ImageNet) confirms that the gradient-decoupling saliency signal is essential for the observed attack success.

## Relation to Prior Work

FastJSMA directly extends the JSMA lineage (Papernot et al., 2016; WJSMA, Combey et al., 2020) by replacing the prohibitively expensive full Jacobian matrix computation with a dual-gradient approximation that decomposes saliency into class suppression (g⁻) and class excitation (g⁺) components. Rather than computing N×M partial derivatives per pixel pair, the method leverages automatic differentiation on two carefully constructed virtual labels, reducing complexity from O(M²×N) to O(M√N). The class probing mechanism (square-root sampling of candidate classes) and adaptive significance threshold are new engineering additions that further optimize the search without altering the fundamental L0-constrained, untargeted attack paradigm inherited from JSMA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The primary practical impact is making interpretable, pixel-level saliency-based attacks feasible on ImageNet, where both JSMA and WJSMA fail entirely due to memory constraints—a barrier that had effectively removed JSMA from consideration in large-scale adversarial robustness evaluations. The gradient decoupling principle (approximating a full Jacobian via two directional gradients) could inform efficiency improvements for other Jacobian-dependent analyses in explainable AI. Limitations include evaluation on a single architecture (ResNet18), an empirically motivated N-scaling factor for g⁻ lacking theoretical justification, and the question of whether approximation quality degrades with deeper or more complex models. Whether the method extends to targeted attacks or other norm constraints remains open.
