## Problem

Existing temporal action segmentation models are trained on a fixed set of procedural tasks and cannot adapt to novel tasks without retraining, which is impractical given the vast number of tasks and high annotation cost of long videos. Prior few-shot video methods handle only single-action clips and ignore temporal dependencies among actions, while direct multi-modal feature fusion causes models to over-rely on textual features and underutilize visual ones, degrading performance on novel tasks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251308/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model receives a query video and K support videos (V per task) with framewise action labels, plus textual action names, following a standard K-way V-shot protocol. Uniquely, the framework supports flexible inference scenarios beyond full-label few-shot: zero-shot (action names only, no support videos), unlabeled few-shot (support videos without labels, where the textual PGNet first predicts support labels), and weakly-labeled few-shot (one labeled frame per action instance). Output is framewise action labels for the query video plus a task label, obtained by masking the final SHCA attention map to the predicted task's action prototypes and applying argmax.

## Method

PGNet follows a summarize-and-match strategy with two components. The Prototype Building Block constructs an Action Relation Graph with task, action, and instance nodes connected by typed relational edges (task, action, prototype, video, and two context edges), then processes it with a Dynamic Graph Transformer that learns per-head dynamic edge weights α to specialize attention heads to different edge types without pre-assigning head counts. The Matching Block uses Single-Head Cross-Attention with multi-stage refinement (alternating query/key roles) and a skip-connection mechanism that fuses the current attention with the previous layer's attention via a learnable factor τ. A key design choice is maintaining separate visual and textual PGNets and fusing at the prediction level (the first SHCA layer of one modality receives the other modality's initial attention as reference) rather than at the feature level, with an L2 penalty on τ₁ to prevent over-reliance on the textual modality.

## Results

On CrossTask (few-shot 3way-3shot, full-label), PGNet achieves F1@50 of 18.3% and Edit of 37.7, surpassing the best baseline MUPPET (F1@50 9.5%, Edit 18.1) by +8.8% F1@50. On COIN under the same setting, PGNet reaches F1@50 of 15.0% and Edit of 46.6, outperforming MUPPET (F1@50 8.1%, Edit 13.1) by +6.9% F1@50. In the zero-shot setting (3way-3shot), PGNet achieves F1@50 of 15.4% on CrossTask and 8.6% on COIN, exceeding the best VLM baseline (ProceduralVRL: 5.8% and 5.3% respectively) by roughly 2–3×. The 5way-3shot few-shot setting shows similar margins: +8.9% F1@50 on CrossTask and +6.7% on COIN over MUPPET.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PGNet substantially outperforms all five baselines across every setting and dataset. Against the zero-shot VLM baselines, PGNet (zero-shot) achieves F1@50 of 15.4% on CrossTask vs. ProceduralVRL's 5.8% and LanguageBind's 4.3%, and 8.6% on COIN vs. 5.3% and 4.9% respectively; the VLMs suffer from over-segmentation due to clip-level processing that misses temporal dependencies. Among few-shot baselines, MUPPET [44] is the strongest competitor but still trails PGNet by +8.8–9.3% F1@50 on CrossTask and +6.9–7.6% on COIN, primarily because MUPPET models each action in isolation (ignoring action dependencies) and fuses modalities at the feature level, causing over-reliance on textual features. Linear Probe performs worst among few-shot methods (F1@50 5.4–6.0% on CrossTask) due to its inability to capture inter-action relations. Notably, PGNet's weakly-labeled variant (one label per action instance) nearly matches the fully-labeled version (e.g., F1@50 17.3% vs. 18.3% on CrossTask 3way-3shot), demonstrating that the framework is robust to annotation budget constraints.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior few-shot video methods (MUPPET, [19, 25, 43, 46, 67]) that treat actions as isolated single-action instances, instead modeling action dependencies through an Action Relation Graph within a Dynamic Graph Transformer. It also departs from the standard multi-modal few-shot paradigm of fusing visual and textual features at the feature level, which the authors show causes over-reliance on textual modality; instead, they fuse at the prediction level via a reference-attention mechanism in Single-Head Cross-Attention. The multi-stage refinement in the Matching Block is adapted from FACT [40], and the overall summarize-and-match strategy follows the prototype-learning lineage of few-shot classification, extended here to the temporal and multi-action setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By framing MMF-TAS as a new problem, the paper addresses a practical gap: closed-set TAS models cannot adapt to novel procedural tasks without retraining, and the high annotation cost of long procedural videos makes full supervision infeasible. The framework's support for zero-shot, unlabeled, and weakly-labeled inference scenarios offers a practical path to reducing annotation budgets, and the prediction-fusion insight (that feature-level multi-modal fusion can be harmful when modalities have complementary strengths on different task splits) is broadly applicable. Open questions include scalability to very long videos with many actions, whether the dense Action Relation Graph remains tractable as task complexity grows, and whether the approach generalizes beyond the two procedural datasets evaluated.
