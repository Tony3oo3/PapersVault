## Problem

Existing image-to-video generation methods lack precise 3D controllability: text prompts, bounding boxes, and trajectories operate in 2D space and cannot express motions like rotating an object 180° or executing complex camera paths. Traditional CG pipelines (Blender, Maya) provide this fine-grained 3D control but require high-quality 3D models that single-image reconstruction methods cannot yet produce at photorealistic quality.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252282/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a single RGB image and a user-authored 3D animation (camera trajectory, object/character motion) created in Blender from the reconstructed scene meshes, plus a simple text prompt describing the scene. The input image need not be the first frame of the output video, allowing arbitrary animation start points. Outputs include a high-quality video at 1024×576 resolution (≈20 s per keyframe, ≈310 s per 16-frame clip on a 3090Ti), along with intermediate 3D foreground/background meshes and depth maps used as geometric guidance.

## Method

The pipeline has three stages. First, 3D scene reconstruction: the foreground is segmented (SegAnything) and meshed via InstantMesh (sparse-view feed-forward), while the background is inpainted (SDXL), expanded with novel views from ViewCrafter, and meshed via Dust3r MVS; both meshes are imported into Blender for user animation and rendered to coarse RGB + depth sequences. Second, 3D-guided keyframe generation: SDXL is customized with LoRA using multi-view augmentation (InstantMesh's six novel views) to avoid frontal-view overfitting, then keyframes are generated under joint depth ControlNet and rendered-feature injection (self-attention and convolutional features extracted via DDIM inversion), with an Extended Attention mechanism that lets each keyframe attend to all others for temporal consistency. Third, 3D-guided video interpolation: a training-free dual-trajectory denoising scheme (forward conditioned on the first keyframe, time-reversed conditioned on the last) is applied within the pretrained SVD backbone, with EDM inversion of the rendered video providing initial latents and the same depth + rendered-feature guidance ensuring geometric alignment, producing smooth intermediate frames without additional training.

## Results

On nonhuman-like objects, I2V3D achieves the best temporal consistency (0.994), matches ISculpting on CLIP-I (0.906), and substantially outperforms all baselines on geometric alignment: SSIM of 0.757 (vs. 0.570 for ISculpting, the next best) and D-RMSE of 0.102 (vs. 0.161 for ISculpting). On human-like characters, I2V3D again leads in consistency (0.991) and geometry (SSIM 0.735, D-RMSE 0.117), while ISculpting edges it slightly on CLIP-I (0.916 vs. 0.901). The evaluation uses 30 animations (15 per category), each with at least 61 frames, at 1024×576 resolution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

I2V3D wins decisively on geometric alignment metrics across both categories: SSIM improves by 0.187 over ISculpting (0.757 vs. 0.570) and 0.228 over AnimateAnyone (0.735 vs. 0.507) on nonhuman and human subjects respectively, while D-RMSE drops from 0.161 to 0.102 (nonhuman) and 0.155 to 0.117 (human). On temporal consistency, I2V3D leads all baselines (0.994 nonhuman, 0.991 human), with the closest competitor being DragAnything at 0.991 for nonhuman objects. On visual similarity (CLIP-I), I2V3D ties ISculpting on nonhuman objects (0.906) but trails slightly on human characters (0.901 vs. 0.916), which the authors attribute to ISculpting directly blending the input background during denoising. MotionBooth and DragAnything show the weakest geometry scores (D-RMSE 0.256 and 0.167), reflecting their 2D-only control signals. The skeleton-based methods (AnimateAnyone, MagicPose) struggle most with geometry (SSIM ≤ 0.425) and cannot handle camera movement at all.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: traditional CG pipelines (Blender-based rigging, keyframe animation, camera paths) that offer precise 3D control but require high-quality 3D assets, and diffusion-based image-to-video models (SVD, DynamicRafter) that produce photorealistic output but lack fine-grained 3D controllability. It builds directly on ISculpting's coarse-to-fine 3D-guided refinement strategy but extends it from single-image editing to video generation and from foreground-only reconstruction to full scene geometry (foreground + background). The 3D reconstruction stack (InstantMesh for foreground, ViewCrafter + Dust3r for background) and the two-stage generation pipeline (LoRA-customized keyframe generation with Extended Attention, followed by training-free bidirectional interpolation with ControlNet depth and rendered-feature guidance) are assembled from existing components but integrated in a way that no prior controllable video generation method (bounding-box, trajectory, or skeleton-based) has attempted.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework significantly lowers the barrier for professional CG users to produce photorealistic animations from a single image, automating the modeling and rendering steps while preserving the familiar Blender workflow for motion design. The two-stage keyframe-then-interpolation design addresses the temporal error accumulation that limits video diffusion models to short windows, enabling longer sequences. Key limitations include dependence on the quality of single-image 3D reconstruction (still coarse for complex scenes), the need for Blender proficiency, and the current restriction to relatively simple object/character animations. Open questions include whether the approach scales to multi-object interactive scenes, handles non-rigid deformations beyond skeleton-based rigging, and can be made more accessible to non-technical users.
