## Problem

MLLMs are increasingly deployed as automated process judges to evaluate step-by-step scientific reasoning, yet no comprehensive benchmark exists to assess their reliability. Existing process-evaluation benchmarks are limited to single modality, single discipline, or synthetic error data, and focus only on error detection while neglecting fine-grained error classification and diagnosis. This leaves the accuracy and generalizability of model-based judges largely unverified.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252677/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The judge model receives a scientific problem P, its ground-truth final answer A, and a student model's step-by-step solution S = {s_0, …, s_{n-1}} (averaging 20.8 steps, up to 470). For each step it must output a structured tuple (c_i, e_i, r_i): a binary correctness label, one of seven error types (reasoning, visual interpretation, numerical calculation, symbolic calculation, knowledge, question understanding, no solution provided), and a free-text root-cause explanation. Problems span four disciplines (math, physics, chemistry, biology) across K12 and competition difficulty, with modalities ranging from pure text to single or multiple images.

## Method

The paper introduces ProJudgeBench (2,400 cases, 50,118 step-level labels) built from solutions generated by 10 diverse MLLMs and human-annotated for correctness, error type, and explanation, plus ProJudge-173k, a 173k-sample instruction-tuning dataset constructed via two complementary paths: controlled error injection by GPT-4o into correct solutions, and realistic error collection from 9 MLLMs solving OlympiadBench/K12 problems. The key training innovation is a Dynamic Dual-Phase (DDP) fine-tuning strategy that alternates between (1) Direct Evaluate, where the model scores each step directly, and (2) Synthesize-then-Evaluate, where the model first generates its own reference solution to the problem before assessing the student's steps, mimicking human expert behavior. The two phases are sampled dynamically with probability p during training, and the model is optimized with separate cross-entropy losses for solution synthesis and process evaluation. Fine-tuning open-source models (InternVL2.5-8B, Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B) with DDP on ProJudge-173k via LoRA yields 50–70% absolute gains in step-correctness accuracy, closing the gap with proprietary judges like GPT-4o.

## Results

On ProJudgeBench (2,400 test cases, 50,118 step-level labels), GPT-4o achieves the highest step correctness accuracy at 85.10% and overall error type classification accuracy at 44.89%, followed by Gemini-2.0-thinking-exp (72.61% / 35.27%) and Qwen2-VL-72B (77.93% / 33.87%). After fine-tuning on ProJudge-173k with the Dynamic Dual-Phase strategy, InternVL2.5-8B jumps from 25.58% to 84.50% step correctness (+58.92 pp), and Qwen2.5-VL-3B surges from 11.47% to 81.29% (+70.45 pp), bringing small open-source models within ~4 pp of GPT-4o. On the unseen OlympicArena subset, DDP-enhanced models show the largest generalization gains (e.g., InternVL2.5-8B: 81.71% → 85.07%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4o leads all baselines with 85.10% step correctness and 44.89% overall error type accuracy, outperforming Gemini-2.0-thinking-exp (72.61% / 35.27%) by ~12.5 pp on step correctness. Among open-source models, Qwen2-VL-72B (77.93%) and InternVL2.5-38B (78.85%) are the strongest, but both trail GPT-4o by 6–7 pp on step correctness and by 11–18 pp on overall error type classification. The fine-tuned small models dramatically close the gap: InternVL2.5-8B† reaches 84.50% (within 0.6 pp of GPT-4o) and even surpasses it on reasoning error detection (53.97% vs. 52.94%), while Qwen2.5-VL-3B† (81.29%) exceeds the much larger Qwen2-VL-72B (77.93%) by 3.4 pp. Gemini-2.0 models show a distinctive strength in visual interpretation error detection (26.6–27.7%) compared to GPT-4o (9.61%), but are weaker on reasoning and knowledge errors. The ablation confirms DDP adds 1.1–3.4 pp over standard fine-tuning, with the largest gains on the unseen OlympicArena subset (up to +4.79 pp for Qwen2-VL-7B), indicating improved generalization to novel competition-level problems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the process-evaluation line of work (ProcessBench, PRMBench, MR-GSM8K) from text-only, single-discipline, error-detection-only settings to a multi-modal, multi-discipline (math, physics, chemistry, biology) framework with fine-grained error classification and diagnosis. It departs from prior MLLM-as-judge studies (MM-Math, OlympicArena, MathVerse) by shifting the evaluation target from student models to the judges themselves, asking how reliably MLLMs can assess reasoning steps. The Dynamic Dual-Phase fine-tuning strategy is novel in explicitly requiring the judge to synthesize its own solution before evaluating the student's, a mechanism not present in prior instruction-tuning approaches for process reward models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ProJudgeBench and ProJudge-173k fill a critical gap: there was no comprehensive, multi-modal benchmark for assessing the reliability of MLLM-based process judges, and no large-scale dataset for training open-source judges. The finding that a fine-tuned 3B model can match or exceed a 72B model on this task suggests that domain-specific process-evaluation data is a more effective lever than raw scale, which has practical implications for deploying affordable judges. Open questions include whether the 7 error-type taxonomy generalizes beyond the four disciplines covered, whether the self-bias observed in cross-model evaluation (models scoring their own series higher) undermines the objectivity of LLM-as-judge pipelines, and how well these judges transfer to entirely new domains or longer reasoning chains beyond the ~20-step average.
