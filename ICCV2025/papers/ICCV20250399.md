## Problem

Visual robot manipulation requires costly multimodal robot data, and existing vision-language pretraining approaches either use web data irrelevant to manipulation tasks or learn human action knowledge implicitly (pixel-level prediction, contrastive learning), which introduces irrelevant background noise and limits generalization under data scarcity. The paper asks: can we explicitly extract action knowledge from human videos via hand keypoints and transfer it to robot manipulation through a learned correspondence between human hand structure and robotic arm components?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250399/cropped_page0_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a natural language instruction, a sequence of RGB visual observations, and robot states (6D end-effector pose plus binary gripper state) at each timestep, and outputs the next robot action as the delta of state parameters. During fine-tuning, the system additionally retrieves top-J similar human action video clips from an Ego4D database (matched by cosine similarity of language and visual embeddings) and processes their 3D hand keypoints (K keypoints per frame, extracted offline by InterHand) as auxiliary supervision signals through the analogical reasoning module.

## Method

AR-VRM uses a two-stage pipeline. In pretraining, a keypoint VLM (CLIP text encoder + MAE-pretrained ViT image encoder with Perceiver Resampler + HandFormer keypoint encoder, all projected to a shared dimension and fed into a 12-layer causal-attention Transformer) is trained on 800K Ego4D clips to predict future 3D hand keypoints via MSE loss, explicitly encoding action knowledge without pixel-level noise. In fine-tuning on robot data, the model retrieves similar human action videos and introduces a learnable analogical map matrix m ∈ R^{S×K} that weights the influence of each hand keypoint on each robot arm component; the imitated robot state feature is a convex combination (controlled by a learnable α) of the mapped keypoint features and the actual robot state feature, trained with an additional MSE loss. The keypoint encoder and head are frozen during fine-tuning to preserve pretrained action knowledge while the Transformer layers adapt, serving simultaneously as a data-replay mechanism against overfitting to small robot datasets.

## Results

On the CALVIN benchmark (ABCD→D, full dataset), AR-VRM achieves 85.4% average success rate and 4.27 average task length, outperforming the previous SOTA GR-1 (84.2%, 4.21) by +1.2%. Under the more challenging unseen scene setting (ABC→D), AR-VRM reaches 65.9% average success rate versus GR-1's 61.2% (+4.7%). In the data-efficient 10% training data setting, AR-VRM improves the 5-task success rate from 40.0% (GR-1) to 45.6%, and in unseen language generalization from 43.3% to 46.6%. On real-robot experiments, AR-VRM achieves 0.95 success rate on seen-object transportation and 0.82 on articulated manipulation, surpassing all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AR-VRM outperforms all five baselines across every CALVIN evaluation setting. Against the closest competitor GR-1 [31], which also uses Ego4D pretraining but with implicit pixel-level prediction, AR-VRM gains +1.2% average rate on full-data (ABCD→D) and a more substantial +4.7% on unseen scene (ABC→D), with the gap widening further under 10% data (45.6% vs 40.0%) and unseen language (46.6% vs 43.3%). The earlier baselines MCIL [15], RT-1 [1], HULC [16], and MT-R3M [25] are all substantially outperformed, with average rates ranging from 6.4%–61.3% (ABCD→D) and 6.4%–18.6% (ABC→D) compared to AR-VRM's 85.4% and 65.9%. On real-robot experiments (Table 2), AR-VRM achieves 0.95/0.91/0.53 success rates on seen/unseen-instance/unseen-category transportation versus GR-1's 0.79/0.73/0.30, and 0.82 on articulated manipulation versus GR-1's 0.75, confirming the advantage transfers beyond simulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of implicit human-action pretraining for robot manipulation (pixel-level future-frame prediction in GR-1 [31], contrastive feature learning in MT-R3M [25], or web-scale VQA in RT-2 [2]) by proposing explicit keypoint-based learning from human hand poses. It builds on the Ego4D pretraining pipeline and VLM architecture (CLIP + ViT + Perceiver Resampler) established by GR-1, but replaces the generative pixel head with a HandFormer-based keypoint prediction head and introduces a novel Analogical Reasoning module that learns a soft mapping between human hand keypoints and robot arm components during fine-tuning. The retrieval-augmented fine-tuning strategy, where similar human action videos are fetched and replayed alongside robot data, extends the data-replay idea from continual learning into the cross-embodiment setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The explicit keypoint formulation offers a clean conceptual alternative to pixel-level pretraining, potentially reducing the irrelevant background noise that limits generalization under data scarcity, and the few-shot gains (40.0% → 45.6% at 10% data) are practically meaningful for deployment where robot demonstrations are expensive. However, the approach is constrained by the quality of offline hand pose estimation (InterHand), the assumption that human hand keypoints are a meaningful proxy for robot end-effector trajectories, and the relatively modest absolute improvements over the strong GR-1 baseline on full-data settings (+1.2%). Open questions include whether the analogical map generalizes to fundamentally different robot morphologies (multi-arm, mobile bases) or to tasks where human and robot kinematics diverge substantially, and whether the retrieval step scales efficiently to very large human video databases at inference time.
