## Problem

CLIP's latent space is known to fail at compositional understanding, spatial reasoning, and negation, but prior remedies have focused on data curation or architectural tweaks. This paper asks whether the failure is more fundamental: it proves that no CLIP-like joint embedding space using cosine similarity on unit vectors can simultaneously represent basic descriptions, attribute binding, spatial relationships, and negation, establishing that the geometry itself is the bottleneck.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251885/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB image and a text prompt (sentence or caption) and produces a single scalar text-image similarity score. Unlike standard CLIP which reduces each modality to one vector (CLS token and EOS token) and computes a dot product, the pipeline retains all per-token and per-patch embeddings from frozen CLIP encoders, forming a dense matrix of pairwise cosine similarities. The final score is produced by a lightweight CNN that reads this matrix as a 2D pattern, meaning the model never sees the raw image pixels or text characters directly.

## Method

The paper first proves geometric impossibility results showing that linear superposition (required for multi-object scenes under Condition 1) makes attribute bindings, spatial arrangements, and negation indistinguishable in any unit-hypersphere embedding. To rescue CLIP, it introduces Dense Cosine Similarity Maps (DCSMs): a matrix where each entry is the cosine similarity between one text token embedding and one image patch embedding, preserving spatial and syntactic topology that a single scalar discards. Rows corresponding to compositional 'functional words' (e.g., 'left of', 'above') are replaced with fixed constant vectors (Functional Rows) since these words lack direct visual counterparts in CLIP's contrastive training. A two-layer CNN with hidden dimension 128 (~20× fewer parameters than CLIP) is trained on only ~20k hard positive/negative pairs to score the DCSM, learning to recognize correct vs. incorrect syntactic-spatial patterns without retraining the base CLIP encoders.

## Results

DCSM (trained on COCO) achieves the best scores on 7 out of 8 benchmarks, with particularly large gains on CLEVR-bind (39.9% vs. 22.2% for CLIP ViTB/32), NCD (95.7% vs. 79.2% for NegCLIP), and WhatsUp spatial reasoning (63.7% vs. 33.2% for NegCLIP). On COCO and VG spatial QA, DCSM coco reaches 72.4% and 67.0% respectively, surpassing the best baseline (CLIP ViTB/16 at 51.5%) by over 15 points. The only benchmark where DCSM coco does not lead is VG_attribution (68.1% vs. NegCLIP's 72.4%), which the authors attribute to NegCLIP's use of similar hard-negative mining. The DCSM model trained on synthetic Objaverse data also outperforms all baselines on 6 of 8 benchmarks, demonstrating the method's data efficiency with only ~20,000 training samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCSM coco outperforms all five CLIP-like baselines on 7 of 8 benchmarks. Against CLIP ViTB/16 (its base encoder), DCSM coco gains +19.7 on CLEVR-bind (39.9 vs. 20.2), +32.6 on WhatsUp (63.7 vs. 30.5), and +23.4 on COCO spatial QA (72.4 vs. 48.9). Against NegCLIP, the strongest baseline on attribute binding, DCSM coco leads on NCD (95.7 vs. 79.2) and WhatsUp (63.7 vs. 33.2) but trails on VG_attribution (68.1 vs. 72.4), where NegCLIP's hard-negative mining provides an advantage. CoCa, BLIP, and SigLIP are consistently outperformed by DCSM coco across all benchmarks, with the largest gaps on CLEVR-bind (39.9 vs. 19.2/11.1/13.3) and NCD (95.7 vs. 48.7/56.2/53.6). The synthetic-data variant (DCSM synth) also beats every baseline on 6 of 8 benchmarks, confirming that the gains come from the DCSM scoring mechanism rather than large-scale training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's frozen encoders but departs fundamentally from prior remediation efforts (hard-negative mining, autoregressive VLMs, neurosymbolic decomposition) by arguing the problem is geometric rather than data- or architecture-centric. It extends Brody's [7] formalization of CLIP as a mapping to a unit hypersphere with rigorous impossibility proofs showing no such space can simultaneously satisfy concept categorization, attribute binding, spatial relationships, and negation. The proposed DCSM scoring mechanism repurposes token- and patch-level embeddings that standard CLIP discards, positioning itself as a lightweight post-hoc fix rather than a retraining or re-architecture effort, and contrasts with hyperbolic CLIP approaches [13, 46] by providing formal logical analysis rather than empirical geometry swaps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility proof provides a clear theoretical ceiling for all CLIP-like joint embedding spaces, redirecting future work away from incremental data or loss-function tweaks toward fundamentally different scoring or representation paradigms. The DCSM approach demonstrates that a 20-parameter-reduction CNN trained on ~20k samples can outperform much larger models on compositional benchmarks, suggesting the information is already in CLIP's intermediate representations. Key limitations include reliance on templated training captions, a small static functional-word dictionary, and evaluation on relatively small benchmarks; the open-vocabulary LLM-in-the-loop extension is promising but still a bottleneck. Open questions include whether alternative manifolds (hyperbolic, projective) can escape the impossibility, whether the approach scales to multi-object scenes with complex spatial layouts, and whether more sophisticated scoring architectures can close the remaining gap on natural-language captions.
