## Problem

Most existing studies on LMM classification performance assume a closed-world setting with a predefined category list, or evaluate open-world performance using only a single string-matching metric (text inclusion). This provides a limited view of classification quality, failing to account for semantically equivalent alternatives (e.g., sofa vs. couch), granularity mismatches (e.g., dog vs. pug), or annotation ambiguities. The paper formalizes the open-world classification task and introduces a comprehensive multi-metric evaluation protocol to properly assess LMMs in this unconstrained setting.

## Input / Output

Each LMM receives an RGB image paired with a fixed text prompt ('What type of object is in this image?') and produces a free-form, unconstrained text generation as its class prediction. There is no predefined vocabulary or multiple-choice constraint; the model may output a single word, a phrase, or a full sentence. Evaluation then compares this free-form output against a single ground-truth label per image using four complementary metrics that capture different levels of alignment from strict string matching to semantic and conceptual proximity.

## Method

The paper introduces four evaluation metrics: Text Inclusion (binary string containment of ground truth in prediction), Llama Inclusion (binary LLM-as-a-judge using Llama 3.2 3B to assess semantic correctness), Semantic Similarity (cosine similarity of Sentence-BERT embeddings between prediction and ground truth), and Concept Similarity (maximum cosine similarity over spaCy-split sentence parts of the prediction against the ground truth embedding). Using these metrics, the authors benchmark 13 LMMs from 8 families across 10 datasets spanning prototypical, non-prototypical, fine-grained, and very fine-grained categories, alongside contrastive baselines (CLIP, SigLIP, CaSED, CLIP retrieval). By jointly analyzing inclusion and similarity scores, they categorize predictions into four error types (correct-specific, correct-generic, wrong-specific, wrong-generic) and identify root causes such as granularity mismatch, fine-grained confusion, and wrong-object focus. They further demonstrate that domain-specific prompting converts generic predictions into specific ones, and that chain-of-thought reasoning at test time reduces wrong-but-specific errors more effectively than reasoning-tailored model variants.

## Results

Across 13 LMMs and 10 benchmarks, the best-performing model (Qwen2VL 7B) achieves 46.4% Text Inclusion and 78.7% Llama Inclusion on prototypical classes, but drops to 0.8% TI and 63.0% LI on very fine-grained classes (Stanford Cars, FGVC Aircraft). LMMs lead open-world baselines (CaSED, CLIP retrieval) in 11 out of 16 metric/group combinations, yet remain far below closed-world CLIP (76.4% TI, 91.5% CS on prototypical) and SigLIP (81.8% TI, 90.5% CS). On very fine-grained tasks, most LMMs score 0.0–2.3% TI, with only Qwen2VL 2B reaching 12.9%, highlighting a severe granularity gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the open-world baseline CaSED [17], LMMs consistently outperform on both inclusion metrics (TI and LI) across all four dataset groups, with Qwen2VL 7B achieving +21.9 LI on prototypical (78.7 vs 46.3). However, CaSED remains competitive on Semantic Similarity (58.9 SS on prototypical vs 51.9 for Qwen2VL 7B) due to its concise single-word outputs. Against CLIP retrieval [49], LMMs win on LI across all groups (e.g., 78.7 vs 42.9 on prototypical) but CLIP retrieval outperforms 9 of 13 LMMs on TI, and is competitive on CS for very fine-grained tasks (56.1 vs 43.4 for Qwen2VL 7B). Against closed-world CLIP [49] and SigLIP [68], all LMMs fall substantially short: the best LMM reaches 46.4% TI vs 76.4% (CLIP) and 81.8% (SigLIP) on prototypical, and 63.0% LI vs 69.2% (SigLIP) on very fine-grained. For reasoning-tailored variants (InternVL2.5 [11], Qwen2.5VL [4]), replacing base models yields +6.6% average correct predictions but also +3.7% wrong-but-specific errors, suggesting test-time CoT prompting is more effective than architecture-level reasoning for fine-grained discrimination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work evaluating LMMs for image classification (Zhang et al. [69], Liu et al. [42], Yue et al. [66]) by shifting from closed-world or single-metric evaluation to a properly formalized open-world setting with a four-metric protocol. It draws on vocabulary-free classification [17] (CaSED) and contrastive retrieval baselines as related but distinct formulations, positioning LMM-based open-ended prediction as a natural successor that removes the need for a predefined vocabulary. The multi-metric evaluation (text inclusion, LLM-as-judge inclusion, semantic similarity, concept similarity) departs from the single text-inclusion metric used in prior OW studies, enabling a finer-grained error taxonomy that no previous work provided.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first large-scale (13 models × 10 datasets) diagnostic study of LMMs in truly open-world classification, and its four-metric framework reveals that LMM errors are not monolithic but decompose into granularity failures, fine-grained confusion, and wrong-object focus—each addressable by different interventions (domain-specific prompting, CoT reasoning, multi-label awareness). The finding that 30–60% of 'wrong' predictions are actually correct for other objects in the image highlights a fundamental mismatch between single-label benchmarks and LMM behavior, suggesting that evaluation protocols for generative models need to account for multi-object scenes. Open questions include how to close the substantial gap with closed-world CLIP/SigLIP, whether the granularity issue is inherent to LMM training data or fixable with targeted fine-tuning, and whether the proposed metrics generalize to video or multi-modal classification settings.
