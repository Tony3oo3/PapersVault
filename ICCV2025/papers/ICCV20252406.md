## Problem

Existing multi-sensory object-centric datasets suffer from four key deficiencies: they often target scenes rather than objects, rely on simulated or controlled-environment data that creates a domain gap to real-world use, require expensive rigs that limit accessibility and scalability, and typically correlate only two sensory modalities rather than three or more. This hinders the development of cross-sensory representations that can form direct alignment among multiple modalities for real, in-the-wild objects.

## Input / Output

The device probes a single point on a physical object at a time, capturing an RGBD image (RealSense D405, 8–13 cm depth range), a tactile image from a DIGIT sensor pressed at 10, 15, and 20 N (with calibrated normal force and device angle recorded via load cell and accelerometer), and an impact audio recording from a piezo-stack hammer released by an electromagnet, captured by a Dayton EMM6 microphone through a HiFiBerry DAC2 board. Each object yields six such multi-sensory points, producing a dataset of 3,600 correlated samples across 600 objects. Downstream, the trained encoders map each modality to a shared embedding space used for cross-sensory retrieval rankings, contact-point classification, CLIP-aligned generation prompts (feeding Shap-E and Stable Diffusion), and Detic object detection.

## Method

X-Capture is a 3D-printed, sub-$1,000 handheld device that rigidly constrains six sensors into fixed relative poses on an ergonomic chassis, ensuring independent sensor readings (e.g., the tactile sensor is occluded from the camera). A key design choice is explicit input-output measurement: a load cell and accelerometer calibrate the pressing force and angle for tactile readings, while a piezo stack in the impact hammer measures the striking force for audio, eliminating the need for expensive force sensors or uncontrolled manual probing. The hammer is held by an electromagnet and released after a configurable delay for repeatable, silent impacts, and the HiFiBerry board provides time-synchronized, dynamically gain-adjusted audio recording. For the empirical study, the authors fine-tune both ImageBind and an ensemble of modality-specific encoders (CLIP ViT-L for RGB, TVL ViT-L for tactile, AST for audio, PointBERT for point clouds) using symmetric InfoNCE contrastive loss in two formulations—Image Loss (image-to-each-modality) and Cross-Sensory Loss (all pairwise modality alignments)—with RGB encoders frozen, then evaluate on cross-sensory retrieval, contact-point localization, CLIP-aligned 2D/3D generation, and zero-shot audio-based object detection.

## Results

On the X-Capture dataset (100 test objects), fine-tuning ImageBind with a Cross-Sensory Loss achieves 47.2% average top-5 retrieval accuracy, a massive improvement over the out-of-the-box pretrained weights (6.9%) and the Image Loss formulation (45.8%). For contact point localization (6 points per object), the Cross-Sensory Loss fine-tuned model reaches 28.5% average top-1 accuracy versus 18.0% for out-of-the-box ImageBind and 16.7% random chance. When used as a pretraining set for ObjectFolder Real evaluation (30 test objects), X-Capture pretraining plus fine-tuning achieves 36.1% average top-5 accuracy, outperforming fine-tuning on ObjectFolder alone (32.8%), with the largest gain in the audio modality (40.8% vs. 32.5%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is ImageBind's out-of-the-box pretrained weights, which perform near random chance (6.9% avg top-5 vs. 5.0% random) on the X-Capture object-centric data, indicating poor zero-shot generalization to real-world in-the-wild objects. Fine-tuning with a Cross-Sensory Loss (symmetric InfoNCE across all modality pairs) consistently outperforms the Image Loss formulation (symmetric InfoNCE only between images and each other modality) by 1.4 points on average top-5 retrieval and 1.0 point on contact localization, with the largest gap in the Depth→Audio direction (28.0% vs. 25.4%). The cross-sensory encoder ensemble (CLIP ViT-L + TVL ViT-L + AST + PointBERT) shows a smaller advantage for Cross-Sensory Loss over Image Loss compared to ImageBind, suggesting the benefit of full cross-modal alignment is more pronounced when a single shared embedding space is used. For the pretraining ablation on ObjectFolder Real, X-Capture pretraining plus fine-tuning (36.1% avg) beats fine-tuning on ObjectFolder alone (32.8%), confirming that the X-Capture dataset provides transferable multi-sensory knowledge, though the tactile modality shows minimal improvement (32.5% vs. 32.5%), likely due to the domain gap between DIGIT and GelSight sensors.

## Relation to Prior Work

X-Capture repurposes commercially available sensors (DIGIT tactile sensor, RealSense D405 depth camera, piezo-stack impact hammer, measurement microphone) into a unified portable chassis, departing from prior multi-sensory collection rigs (UBC ACME, RealImpact, ObjectFolder Real) that were expensive, non-portable, or limited to controlled/simulated environments. Conceptually, it extends the line of work on multi-sensory representation learning (ImageBind, ULIP, TVL) by addressing the data bottleneck: prior models were trained on datasets that lacked breadth in correlated modalities or were collected in domains far from in-the-wild use. The explicit input-output measurement (calibrated force via load cell, impact impulse via piezo stack) distinguishes it from prior in-the-wild efforts like Touch and Go and Greatest Hits, which captured outputs without measuring the physical inputs that produce them.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The open-source, sub-$1000 device lowers the barrier to creating multi-sensory object datasets, making it feasible for labs without dedicated scanning facilities to contribute to or scale such collections. The 600-object sample dataset provides the first in-the-wild benchmark correlating all four modalities (RGB, depth, audio, touch) at matched points, enabling direct comparison of cross-sensory loss formulations. A key limitation is that all captures are from static configurations, whereas real embodied agents interact with objects dynamically; bridging this gap (e.g., through automated or interactive collection) is an open direction. The scaling-law results suggest substantial headroom, but whether the device's ~9-minute-per-object throughput is sufficient for the millions-of-examples scale that foundation models require remains to be seen.
