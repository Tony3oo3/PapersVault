## Problem

Real-time, user-controllable soccer motion generation remains unsolved: existing approaches either rely on motion matching (producing visual artifacts), reinforcement learning (restricted to isolated skills like dribbling or juggling), or diffusion-based HOI synthesis (requiring time-consuming post-optimization incompatible with interactive latency). No prior framework covers the full spectrum of soccer actions while maintaining physical plausibility in ball-foot contacts at interactive frame rates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20251573/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes a discrete soccer skill label (dribble, trick, shoot, stand, celebrate, off-the-ball move), continuous direction/speed from keyboard input, a past trajectory window (P=10 frames), and a past soccer motion window, all at 30 Hz. It produces a 45-frame future trajectory (root position + ground-projected orientation) and a 45-frame future soccer motion comprising SMPL 24-joint rotations with 6-DOF representation, a ball state (relative position via a distance-weighted transform, global velocity, rotation, and control weight), and binary foot-ground and foot-ball contact labels per frame.

## Method

SMGDiff is a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 uses a lightweight single-step diffusion transformer encoder to map coarse user controls (skill label, target point, past trajectory) to a refined future trajectory, with Heuristic Future Trajectory Extension blending to prevent abrupt direction/speed changes. Stage 2 employs a transformer-based autoregressive diffusion model (adapted from CAMDM) that denoises a 45-frame soccer motion sequence conditioned on the trajectory, skill label, and past motion, using x0-prediction with auxiliary losses for joint position, velocity, and foot-ground contact. A novel contact guidance module, activated only in the final two of eight denoise steps, detects physically implausible ball-foot interactions by thresholding ball acceleration, selects the appropriate foot joint (penalizing grounded joints), and applies a gradient-based correction to the diffusion sampling direction to enforce realistic contact geometry.

## Results

On the Soccer-X test set, SMGDiff achieves the best FID of 0.1813 (vs. 0.2494 for CM, 0.3541 for LMP, and 0.3593 for MANN-DP), the lowest foot sliding distance of 0.8543 m, and the highest diversity score of 0.6177. For condition alignment, SMGDiff attains the lowest trajectory error (2.41°), lowest orientation error (4.94°), and the highest skill accuracy (93.3%), substantially outperforming all baselines. The method uses 8 denoise steps for real-time inference (~12 ms per frame on an RTX 3080 Ti).

## Baselines & Comparisons

SMGDiff outperforms all three baselines on every condition-alignment metric: skill accuracy reaches 93.3% versus 73.3% (LMP), 69.1% (MANN-DP), and 52.9% (CM), and trajectory error drops to 2.41° versus 4.12° (LMP), 4.07° (MANN-DP), and 3.10° (CM). In terms of motion quality, SMGDiff achieves the lowest FID (0.1813) and foot sliding (0.8543 m), while CM produces the second-best FID (0.2494) but suffers from the worst foot sliding (1.6498 m) and the lowest skill accuracy (52.9%), indicating it generates smoother but less skill-accurate motions. LMP and MANN-DP show comparable FID scores (~0.35) but exhibit higher foot sliding and significantly worse skill accuracy than SMGDiff. The only metric where SMGDiff is not the absolute best is acceleration, where CM (1.1752) and MANN-DP (1.5652) are marginally lower or higher respectively, though the differences are small. Overall, SMGDiff dominates on 6 out of 7 metrics, with the sole exception being a near-tie on acceleration.

## Relation to Prior Work

SMGDiff builds directly on the autoregressive diffusion framework of CAMDM [5] for real-time character control, adapting it to the soccer domain by jointly modeling skeletal motion, rigid ball dynamics, and contact labels. It departs from physics-based soccer control methods (Hong et al. [24], Xie et al. [69]) that are restricted to individual skills like dribbling or juggling, and from motion-matching pipelines (EA SPORTS FC) that rely on pre-recorded clips. The trajectory generation stage borrows the single-step diffusion and HFTE blending strategy from CAMDM, while the contact guidance module adapts the loss-guidance mechanism from DSG [73] with a novel physics-inspired contact loss specific to ball-foot interactions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The Soccer-X dataset (1.08M frames, 30 players, 6 skill categories) is the first large-scale resource for data-driven soccer motion generation, filling a clear gap that previously forced researchers to rely on small, skill-specific captures. The real-time, user-controllable pipeline addresses a practical need in gaming and VR/AR where deterministic or post-optimization-based methods are infeasible. Key open questions remain: the model lacks true physics simulation (no gravity, collision, or multi-player interaction), and whether the contact guidance approach generalizes to other human-object interaction domains beyond soccer. The paper explicitly identifies multi-person physically realistic soccer as a future direction.
