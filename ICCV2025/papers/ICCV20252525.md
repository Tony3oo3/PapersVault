## Problem

Standard linear assignment-based cell tracking algorithms produce single point-estimate solutions without reporting any uncertainty, even when the tracking problem is inherently ambiguous due to low temporal resolution and visually similar cells. This overconfidence is problematic for fully automated high-throughput live-cell microscopy analysis, where the data volume exceeds human oversight capacity and unreliable tracking propagates errors downstream. The paper addresses this gap by providing a general, plug-and-play framework for uncertainty quantification applicable to any frame-to-frame linear assignment tracker.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20252525/cropped_page0_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes cell detections (positions and optional features) from two consecutive frames of a microscopy time-lapse, along with a user-specified cost function w(x_i, x_j') defining pairwise association costs. It outputs the standard tracking assignment (mother-daughter pairs) augmented with per-edge probability confidence scores P_ij and, optionally, daughter-wise entropy H_j as an alternative uncertainty measure. The framework is agnostic to the underlying tracker: it wraps around distance-based, overlap-based, activity-based, or Transformer-based (TrackASTRA) cost functions without modification, requiring only the cost matrix as input.

## Method

The paper formalizes cell tracking as a Bayesian inference problem over the finite set of biologically feasible assignments A*, where the uniform prior encodes constraints (each mother has ≤2 daughters, each daughter has ≤1 mother) and the likelihood is the product of pairwise costs. From this posterior, it derives four uncertainty estimation methods: Softmax (SM) via daughter-wise softmax normalization of costs, Feature Perturbation (FP) via test-time augmentation of cell features with Gaussian noise, Feature Perturbation with Assignment (FP+A) by solving K perturbed assignment problems and counting edge frequencies, and Assignment Sampling (AS) by enumerating top-K LAP solutions and applying self-normalized importance weighting. A second perspective reframes tracking as daughter-based mother classification (DBMC), enabling standard calibration tools—most notably temperature scaling—to post-hoc correct overconfident predictions. The key insight is that the temperature parameter in distance-based tracking corresponds to the variance of the implicitly assumed Brownian motion, explaining why calibration degrades at low temporal resolution and motivating the use of temperature scaling as a cheap, effective remedy when annotated tracking data is available.

## Results

The paper evaluates uncertainty quantification methods (SM, FP, FP+A, AS, each optionally with temperature scaling) across four tracking algorithms at decreasing temporal resolutions (every 10th, 30th, 50th frame). Temperature scaling (TS) almost always reduces Expected Calibration Error (ECE), with AS+TS yielding the best calibration across all tested algorithms and resolutions. For sparsification (accuracy improvement from removing uncertain predictions), entropy-based uncertainty is less dependent on TS, while edge-wise probability requires TS to achieve any accuracy gain for FP+A and AS methods. The Transformer-based tracker (TRACKASTRA) with SM or FP is the only non-tempered setup that maintains or improves calibration as temporal resolution decreases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper applies its uncertainty framework to four existing tracking algorithms: distance-based (Crocker & Grier [6]), overlap-based (LapTrack [10]), activity-based [32], and Transformer-based (TRACKASTRA [12]). Across all four, vanilla (non-tempered) tracking tends to be overconfident, especially at low temporal resolution, with ECE increasing as frames are subsampled. Temperature scaling consistently improves calibration for all four algorithms, with the effect most pronounced at the lowest resolutions. The Transformer-based tracker (TRACKASTRA [12]) stands out: its SM and FP variants are the only non-tempered methods that do not degrade in calibration at lower resolution, likely because its attention mechanism already encodes multi-frame context into the cost matrix. For sparsification, the activity-based and distance-based trackers show similar patterns, while the overlap-based tracker requires TS for SM/FP to yield accuracy improvements. The approximate Bayesian methods (FP+A, AS) show the strongest sparsification gains but at higher computational cost (K=10 assignment solves per frame pair).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the standard linear-assignment cell tracking problem (rooted in Crocker & Grier's maximum-likelihood formulation) as a Bayesian MAP estimation problem, thereby unlocking the full posterior over biologically feasible assignments for uncertainty quantification. It draws on joint probabilistic data association for sampling-based inference, borrows feature perturbation from test-time augmentation in segmentation, and applies temperature scaling (originally for neural network calibration) to tracking for the first time. The classification perspective (daughter-based mother classification) builds directly on TrackASTRA's parental softmax, while the concurrent work of Betjes et al. addresses a narrower local-enumeration variant of the same calibration idea. Unlike Theorell et al. and Kaiser et al., who overcome the Markov assumption with multi-hypothesis tracking across all prior frames, this paper deliberately works under the frame-to-frame Markov assumption to maintain a plug-and-play framework applicable to any LAP-based tracker.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a general, framework-like recipe for adding calibrated uncertainty to any existing linear-assignment tracker, which is practically important for high-throughput live-cell microscopy where human oversight is infeasible. The finding that vanilla trackers are systematically overconfident at low temporal resolution—and that a single scalar (temperature) can largely fix this—gives practitioners a cheap, interpretable diagnostic. Open questions include whether the Markov assumption is sufficient for highly dynamic populations, how to automatically tune the perturbation noise distribution, and whether the computational overhead of Bayesian sampling (K=10 LAPs) is acceptable at the scale of microbial colony tracking (millions of cells). The framework also naturally extends to 3D tracking and to incorporating segmentation uncertainty, which the paper leaves for future work.
