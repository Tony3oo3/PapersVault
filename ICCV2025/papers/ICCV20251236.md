## Problem

With over 300 MLLM benchmarks available, developers lack a principled way to determine which benchmarks actually provide useful insight for model development. The evaluation mechanism itself has never been evaluated: benchmarks may contain fallacious questions, be too easy to discriminate, leak answers through redundant information, or suffer from low sample diversity. This paper asks: how much meaningful information does a given benchmark actually convey per sample?

## Input / Output

The pipeline ingests MCQ benchmark datasets (each sample comprising an RGB image, a text question, and multiple-choice options) along with MLLM inference results from three models (GPT-4o, InternVL-2.5, QwenVL-2.5). It outputs a four-dimensional information density score (Fallacy, Difficulty, Redundancy, Diversity) with sub-dimension breakdowns, plus SRCC/PLCC correlation coefficients validating automated scores against human expert ratings. A key constraint is that 1,000 samples are randomly sampled per benchmark for scale alignment, and a circular evaluation protocol (5 random seeds, all must agree) prevents random guessing from inflating difficulty scores.

## Method

The core contribution is an information-entropy-based theoretical decomposition showing that information density E(I) is proportional to (1−D_fal)·D_dif·(1−D_red)·D_div, where each factor captures a distinct defect. Operationally, a three-tier Human-Model-Data pipeline is designed: Model Eval uses a Mix-of-Expert of three MLLMs to classify difficulty (Extreme/Junior/Ambiguity), removes modalities to detect redundancy, and applies CLIP embeddings with k-means clustering plus semantic deduplication for diversity; Data Eval bypasses MLLM inference entirely, using low-level image features (Laplacian texture, Sobel blur, luminance, chrominance, structural information) and text features (syntax tree depth, CLIP option distances, 10 question-format categories) fitted via random forest to match Model Eval scores. Fallacy is assessed exclusively by human experts on samples flagged as difficult. The pipeline is validated by showing SRCC/PLCC correlations above 0.7 between automated and human evaluations across all dimensions, then applied to 19 benchmarks (17,912 instances) to reveal temporal trends and inter-dimensional trade-offs.

## Results

The paper evaluates 19 MLLM benchmarks (17,912 instances total) across four information density dimensions. Difficulty shows a clear upward trend, rising from ~0.12 (POPE, May-2023) to 0.67 (MME-Realworld, Aug-2024), while Fallacy drops from ~0.60 (A-okvqa) to 0.13 (MMStar) and Redundancy falls from ~0.56 (POPE) to 0.04 (MME-Realworld). Diversity, however, remains largely stagnant across the 2022–2024 period. Model Eval and Data Eval both correlate with Human Eval at SRCC/PLCC > 0.7 across all dimensions, with Difficulty and Diversity-Img reaching ~0.9, validating the automated pipeline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare competing methods but rather benchmarks 19 existing MLLM evaluation suites against each other on four information-density dimensions. MMStar (Mar-2024) achieves the lowest Fallacy (0.135) and second-lowest Redundancy (0.054), making it the most reliable benchmark, while MME-Realworld (Aug-2024) is the most difficult (0.666) and least redundant (0.040) but suffers from high Fallacy (0.480), illustrating the trade-off between difficulty and question quality. Early benchmarks (A-okvqa, POPE, MME) are saturated with low Difficulty (0.12–0.21) and high Fallacy (0.53–0.60), rendering them largely uninformative for current MLLMs. Diversity scores show no meaningful temporal improvement: specialized benchmarks like HallusionBench (0.191) and HR-Bench (0.205) have the lowest diversity due to narrow image/text distributions, while general benchmarks like Q-Bench (0.951) and A-Bench (0.941) maintain high diversity. Inter-dimensional correlations are low (<0.32), confirming that the four dimensions capture independent aspects of benchmark quality, and no single benchmark excels across all four simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends the fragmented line of benchmark-quality work: LIME [52] addressed the Fallacy–Difficulty trade-off via human annotation, while Zerobench [30] and Enigmaeval [39] used model performance to proxy Difficulty and Redundancy. The key departure is the introduction of a single information-entropy-based framework (Information Density) that simultaneously captures four dimensions—Fallacy, Difficulty, Redundancy, and Diversity—and adds a Data Eval paradigm (content-level analysis without MLLM inference) that prior works lacked. By positioning itself as a 'benchmark for benchmark,' it repurposes standard NLP/CV tools (CLIP embeddings, syntax trees, Laplacian/Sobel operators, k-means clustering) into a unified quality-scoring pipeline rather than proposing a new model architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With 300+ MLLM benchmarks in circulation and no prior systematic quality assessment, this work gives developers a concrete, multi-dimensional rubric for selecting benchmarks and gives benchmark creators a validation pipeline before release. The finding that Diversity has remained nearly flat over two years while Fallacy and Difficulty improved sharply, and that the most difficult benchmark (MME-Realworld) simultaneously exhibits the highest Fallacy (~0.5), challenges the implicit assumption that 'harder is better.' Open questions include whether the four-dimension decomposition generalizes beyond multiple-choice multimodal benchmarks (e.g., open-ended VQA, video), whether the information-entropy formulation is the tightest possible bound, and whether the mutual antagonism between dimensions is a fundamental constraint or an artifact of current data-collection practices.
