## Problem

Existing automated T2I evaluation metrics fail to handle complex, multi-object prompts: detection-based methods like GenEval are restricted to 80 COCO classes and only check color attributes, while QA-based methods (TIFA, DSG) suffer from ambiguous question generation and VLM yes/no bias (human agreement below 70%). Additionally, no existing metric penalizes extra foreground objects that appear in generated images but are absent from the prompt, leading to inflated scores for models that hallucinate content.

## Input / Output

The method takes a generated RGB image (from any T2I model) and a corresponding text prompt (averaging 113 words in PSG-Bench, far longer than the 8–17 words typical of prior benchmarks). It produces a predicted scene graph with object nodes (each carrying multiple attributes extracted by GPT-4o) and relationship edges, which is then matched against a manually verified ground-truth scene graph. The final output is a triple of precision, recall, and F1 (PSG-Score-Overall), where false positives only count extra foreground objects—background extras are explicitly ignored to avoid penalizing plausible scene context.

## Method

PSG-Score converts each generated image into a panoptic scene graph via a two-stage pipeline: (1) panoptic segmentation (FC-CLIP for synthetic prompts, kMaX-DeepLab for real-world prompts) yields object instances and scene regions, and (2) the Set-of-Mark technique overlays numbered masks on the image so that GPT-4o can extract a structured JSON scene graph of objects, attributes, and spatial/action relationships. Matching between the predicted and ground-truth graphs uses BERT word embeddings with a cosine-distance threshold of 0.5 for both node and edge similarity, followed by a Graph Edit Distance optimization to find the globally optimal node-edge correspondence rather than greedy matching. The final score is an F1 over matched nodes and edges, with a saliency-based foreground/background split ensuring that only unrequested foreground objects incur a false-positive penalty—a design choice absent from all prior detection- and QA-based metrics.

## Results

On PSG-Bench (5K prompts), DALL-E3 achieves the highest PSG-Score-Overall of 0.64 (Precision 0.70, Recall 0.67), followed by FLUX.1[dev] (0.62), SD3.5-Large (0.60), PixArt-α (0.57), and FLUX.1[schnell] (0.50). Human Likert ratings (1–5) confirm this ordering: DALL-E3 (3.9) > FLUX.1[dev] (3.6) > SD3.5-Large (3.4) > PixArt-α (3.3) > FLUX.1[schnell] (2.3). PSG-Score maintains the strongest Spearman's ρ correlation with human ratings across all three difficulty levels (0.64/0.57/0.54 for easy/medium/hard), whereas GenEval and TIFA correlations degrade sharply on harder prompts (e.g., GenEval drops to 0.16 on hard prompts).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PSG-Score outperforms GenEval in human alignment: GenEval ranks SD3.5-Large (0.50) above DALL-E3 (0.48), directly contradicting human Likert scores where DALL-E3 (3.9) clearly leads SD3.5-Large (3.4); PSG-Score correctly places DALL-E3 first (0.64 vs. 0.60). Against TIFA, PSG-Score provides stronger model differentiation (range 0.50–0.64) versus TIFA's compressed scores (82.1–85.7), and human agreement on TIFA's yes/no QA pairs is only 64%, barely above random. DSG shows a similar limitation with flat scores (80.1–84.3) and 67% human agreement on yes/no pairs, while PSG-Score avoids QA entirely by using scene-graph matching. On the simpler GenEval dataset (533 prompts, avg. 10 words), both GenEval and PSG-Score produce nearly identical scores (0.57–0.68 range), confirming that the existing prompts lack the complexity to differentiate modern T2I models. Across difficulty levels, PSG-Score's Spearman's ρ with human ratings remains above 0.54 even on hard prompts, while GenEval drops to 0.16 and TIFA to 0.24.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on panoptic scene graph representations [44] and graph edit distance [26] to formulate T2I evaluation as a structured graph matching problem, departing from both detection-only metrics (GenEval, which is restricted to 80 COCO classes and color attributes) and QA-based metrics (TIFA, DSG, which suffer from VLM answer bias and ambiguous question generation). The Set-of-Mark technique [45] is repurposed here not for grounding but for enabling VLM-based attribute and relationship extraction over panoptic segmentation masks, replacing CLIP's limited color-only extraction. Conceptually, the paper bridges the detection-based and VLM-based evaluation paradigms by using detection/segmentation for object localization and VLMs for semantic enrichment, then unifying both through scene graph matching.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PSG-Bench (5K prompts, 113 avg. words) is substantially more complex than existing benchmarks (8–17 avg. words) and includes background/scene-level content, making it a more realistic stress test for modern T2I models. The finding that QA-based evaluation achieves only 64–67% human agreement on yes/no questions—barely above random—challenges the reliability of a widely-adopted evaluation paradigm and motivates detection+graph-based alternatives. Open questions include the metric's dependence on GPT-4o for both scene graph extraction and (implicitly) ground truth annotation, whether the graph matching approach scales to even longer or more abstract prompts, and whether the 300-class vocabulary sufficiently covers the open-ended nature of real-world generation.
