## Problem

Large vision-language models (LVLMs) produce image captions that suffer from both hallucination (low precision) and missed details (low recall), and prior work shows that simply prompting these models to self-correct their outputs fails to improve quality. No prior study has applied self-correction training specifically to image captioning in multimodal LLMs, and existing preference-based approaches like DPO can teach the model to *make* corrections but not to make *correct* ones.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/ICCV20250311/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a simple captioning instruction as input and produces a two-turn output: an initial caption generated in the first turn, followed by a self-corrected caption generated in the second turn after the model is prompted to refine its own output. The self-correction is performed in-context by concatenating the first-turn instruction, the initial caption, and a self-correction instruction back into the same model, so no separate refinement model is needed. Training uses LoRA fine-tuning on 7B-parameter LVLMs (LLaVA-1.5-7B, Qwen2-VL-7B) with the 6.5K-image RefinedCaps dataset.

## Method

SC-Captioner is a policy-gradient-based multi-turn reinforcement learning framework where the reward function is the core innovation. Given initial caption y₁, corrected caption y₂, and ground-truth caption y*, the FACTUAL scene-graph parser extracts objects, attributes, and relations from each; set differences between y₁ and y₂ identify added and removed elements, which are then matched against the ground-truth sets via Sentence Transformer cosine similarity. The reward combines a correctness bonus (rewarding additions that match GT and removals of hallucinated content) with a mistake punishment (penalizing hallucinated additions and removal of correct content), directly reflecting the effectiveness of each self-correction action. The training objective uses a KL-regularized policy gradient that updates the model to increase the probability of well-corrected second-turn captions while preserving initial caption generation quality. The paper also introduces refined CAPTURE evaluation metrics (object-level precision with GPT-4o-expanded reference sets, object-conditioned attribute matching, and QA-based relation evaluation) and the RefinedCaps dataset of 6.5K human-refined captions from COCO.

## Results

On DOCCI500 with Qwen2-VL-7B, SC-Captioner (SFT+Ours*) achieves a CAPTURE score of 63.34, Objects F1 of 71.63, Attributes F1 of 57.67, and Relations QA of 30.51, outperforming SFT+DPO* (62.51, 70.67, 55.60, 27.33) and SFT (62.05, 69.50, 55.50, 27.65). On COCO-LN500 with the same base model, SC-Captioner reaches CAPTURE 47.51, Objects F1 76.37, Attributes F1 57.56, and Relations QA 38.51, again surpassing SFT+DPO* (46.44, 75.14, 55.79, 37.21). The self-corrected captions from SC-Captioner consistently improve over their initial counterparts, whereas baseline and SFT models show no such improvement, confirming that the RL training effectively activates self-correction. Gains are most pronounced on Relations QA (up to +3.2 over DPO) and Attributes F1 (up to +2.1), while Objects Precision remains comparable to SFT, indicating the method adds missing content without introducing hallucinations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares SC-Captioner against three baselines: zero-shot LVLMs (LLaVA-1.5-7B and Qwen2-VL-7B), SFT on RefinedCaps, and SFT+DPO for self-correction training. SC-Captioner consistently outperforms SFT+DPO on all four proposed metrics (CAPTURE, Objects F1, Attributes F1, Relations QA) across both test sets and both base models; for example, on DOCCI500 with Qwen2-VL-7B, the gap is +0.83 CAPTURE, +0.96 Objects F1, +2.07 Attributes F1, and +3.18 Relations QA. DPO achieves slightly higher Objects Recall than SC-Captioner (e.g., 65.44 vs. 66.00 on DOCCI500-Qwen2-VL) but at the cost of lower Objects Precision (78.78 vs. 80.20), confirming that DPO teaches the model to make corrections but not to make *correct* ones. Against the SFT baseline, SC-Captioner improves CAPTURE by +1.29 (Qwen2-VL, DOCCI500) and +0.37 (Qwen2-VL, COCO-LN500), with the largest relative gains on Relations QA (+2.92 and +2.12 respectively). The zero-shot baselines are substantially weaker (CAPTURE 57.96 and 46.52 for Qwen2-VL on the two test sets), and their self-corrected captions do not improve over initial ones, underscoring that intrinsic self-correction without training is ineffective for captioning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the multi-turn policy-gradient reinforcement learning framework for self-correction from Kumar et al. [20] (originally developed for math reasoning in pure LLMs) to the multimodal image captioning setting, making it the first work to apply RL-based self-correction training to LVLMs for caption generation. For evaluation, it refines the CAPTURE metric [8] by fixing its incomplete precision calculation, globally-matched attributes, and inefficient relation matching. The reward function design departs from both simple metric-difference rewards and DPO-style preference learning, instead decomposing captions into scene-graph elements (objects, attributes, relations) via FACTUAL parsing to compute targeted correctness bonuses and mistake punishments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The RefinedCaps dataset (6.5K human-refined captions) and the refined evaluation metrics provide practical tools for the image captioning community, and the finding that DPO teaches models to perform correction actions without teaching them to make *correct* corrections (high recall, low precision) offers a useful diagnostic for preference-based training in structured generation tasks. However, the improvements over SFT are modest (1–3 points on most metrics), experiments are limited to 7B models and two 500-image test sets, and the reward function's reliance on scene-graph parsing quality and Sentence Transformer similarity introduces a non-trivial engineering dependency. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models, whether the reward can be simplified or learned end-to-end, and how it interacts with other hallucination-mitigation strategies.
