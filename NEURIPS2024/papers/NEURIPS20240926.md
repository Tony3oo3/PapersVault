## Problem

Marginalizing random effects in linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) is known to improve HMC sampling by reducing dimensionality and alleviating funnel pathologies, but the straightforward vectorized marginalization introduces a dense N×N covariance matrix, causing cubic-time log-density evaluations that negate the benefits. The paper addresses how to perform vectorized marginalization of random effects in LMMs while preserving the linear-time complexity of the original model's density evaluation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an LMM specified by sparse design matrices A_i (N×M_i with at most Nd nonzeros per matrix due to tree structure), a diagonal observation covariance Σ_y, block-diagonal random-effect covariances Σ_u with d×d blocks, group assignment indices, and prior specifications for fixed effects and variance parameters. It produces posterior samples of the remaining (non-marginalized) variables via HMC, plus recovered random effects obtained by ancestral sampling from the analytical conditional p(u_i | Θ, y, u_{-i}) after the HMC run. The key structural constraint is that each observation belongs to exactly one group per random-effect class, giving A its tree structure.

## Method

The core technique applies the matrix determinant lemma and Woodbury (matrix inversion) lemma to replace the expensive N×N matrix E = AΣ_u A^T + Σ_y with the M×M matrix F = Σ_u^{-1} + A^T Σ_y^{-1} A. The key theoretical result (Theorem 1) shows that F inherits a block-diagonal structure with d×d blocks from the tree structure of A, making its determinant and inverse computable in O((M+N)d²) rather than O(M³). This reduces the per-step log-density cost from O(N³) to O(Md² + NLd + Nd²), matching the original model's complexity. For the ancestral recovery step, the same lemmas express the conditional mean and covariance in terms of block-diagonal matrices, enabling O(Md²) Cholesky factorization. When marginalizing all L classes simultaneously, an additional scaled-identity covariance assumption allows a one-time O(D³) eigendecomposition of B^T B as preprocessing, reducing per-step cost to O(D² + NLd).

## Results

On the ETH instructor evaluation model (N=73,421 observations, 4,114 random effects), marginalizing all random effects reduces HMC running time from 13,417 s to 631 s (≈21× speedup), while marginalizing a single effect (u₂) already cuts time to 2,607 s. On the grouse ticks model, marginalizing u₂ eliminates all divergent transitions (0 vs. 42.6 without marginalization), whereas reparameterizing both effects (R1+R2) actually increases divergences to 431.6. Vectorized marginalization is ~5× faster in running time than the scalar marginalization of Lai et al. [35] (e.g., 252 s vs. 1,249 s on the electric company model), with compilation time reduced from ~550 s to ~7 s. Across nine cognitive-science LMMs, marginalization consistently improves both iterations per second and ESS per iteration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the scalar automatic marginalization of Lai et al. [35], the paper's vectorized approach with fast linear algebra achieves roughly 5× lower running time (252 s vs. 1,249 s on electric company; 178 s vs. 2,208 s on pulmonary fibrosis) and ~80× lower compilation time (7 s vs. 552 s; 10 s vs. 727 s), because it exploits the block-diagonal and sparse structure of LMM design matrices rather than expanding each scalar effect into a separate node. Against non-centered reparameterization [49] on the grouse ticks model, marginalizing u₂ (M2) yields zero divergent transitions out of 10,000 samples, while reparameterizing both effects (R1+R2) produces 431.6 divergences—far worse than the 42.6 seen with no transformation at all. Marginalization also speeds up HMC by ~20% on this model, whereas reparameterization does not improve running time. The paper's key empirical claim is that marginalization is always beneficial when applicable: across all 13 evaluated models, it never hurts ESS or running time, and it consistently outperforms both the untransformed model and reparameterization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the automatic marginalization framework of Lai et al. (ICML 2023), which was limited to scalar random variables and required users to write models as explicit graphical models, to a vectorized setting for LMMs that exploits the tree structure of design matrices. The key technical departure is showing that the matrix F = Σ_u^{-1} + A^T Σ_y^{-1} A inherits block-diagonal structure from the LMM's group structure, enabling the matrix inversion and determinant lemmas to reduce log-density evaluation from O(N³) to O(Nd²). The paper also positions itself against non-centered parameterization (reparameterization) as an alternative geometry-fixing transformation, and against collapsed Gibbs sampling for LMMs, which operates in a different MCMC context (Gibbs vs. HMC).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that practitioners should marginalize group-level random effects whenever applicable in Bayesian LMM inference—is strongly supported across 11+ real datasets spanning cognitive science, ecology, and education, with marginalization never hurting ESS and consistently improving speed. Open questions include generalizing multi-effect marginalization beyond the scaled-identity covariance assumption (e.g., via conjugate gradient solvers or balanced-levels assumptions), extending to non-normal likelihoods like probit, and automating the transformation within probabilistic programming languages. The finding that reparameterization can introduce new pathologies (divergences) while marginalization eliminates them provides a clear practical guideline for choosing between the two transformations.
