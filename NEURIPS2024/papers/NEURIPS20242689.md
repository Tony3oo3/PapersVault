## Problem

Existing medical LLM benchmarks (MedQA, PubMedQA, MedMCQA) evaluate qualitative reasoning through multiple-choice questions but do not test the quantitative calculation capabilities that clinicians rely on daily via medical calculators. No prior benchmark systematically assessed whether LLMs can recall specific medical equations or scoring rules, extract relevant parameters from long patient notes, and perform the resulting arithmetic correctly.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242689/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each instance pairs a patient note (averaging ~530 words of clinical text) with a question requesting a specific medical value. The model must produce a single numerical score, date, or time, optionally preceded by a step-by-step rationale. Evaluation uses exact match for rule-based and date-based calculators, and a 5% relative tolerance for equation-based lab, physical, and dosage calculators. In the one-shot setting, a manually curated exemplar (patient note + question + full explanation + answer) for the same calculator is prepended to the prompt.

## Method

MEDCALC-BENCH is constructed via a three-step pipeline: (1) 55 popular calculators from MDCalc are split into 19 rule-based and 36 equation-based tasks; (2) over 180k patient notes from Open-Patients (PMC-Patients, MedQA-USMLE, TREC CDS/CT tracks) are filtered by GPT-3.5-Turbo for eligibility and attribute values are extracted by GPT-4, then verified by a medical professional, with 21 calculators lacking eligible notes filled by template synthesis or handwritten vignettes; (3) template-based generators produce step-by-step natural-language explanations and ground-truth answers, yielding 1047 instances. Evaluation tests eight LLMs (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Llama 3-8B/70B, Mistral-7B, Mixtral-8x7B, PMC-LLaMA-13B, MEDITRON-70B) under zero-shot direct, zero-shot CoT, and one-shot CoT prompting. A GPT-4-based error classifier (89% human-verified accuracy) categorizes failures into knowledge, extraction, computation, and other error types to diagnose where models break down.

## Results

On MEDCALC-BENCH (1047 instances across 55 medical calculation tasks), GPT-4 achieves the best overall accuracy of 50.9% with one-shot chain-of-thought prompting, 37.9% with zero-shot CoT, and 20.8% with zero-shot direct prompting. Llama 3-70B is the strongest open-source model, reaching 39.5% (one-shot CoT) and 35.5% (zero-shot CoT), but trails GPT-4 by 11.4 points in the one-shot setting. All models perform well below clinical usability thresholds, with the best subtask accuracy (GPT-4 on Physical calculations, one-shot CoT) reaching only 77.5%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 outperforms all seven baselines across all three prompting settings. In the one-shot CoT setting, GPT-4 (50.9%) leads Llama 3-70B (39.5%) by 11.4 points, GPT-3.5 (34.9%) by 16.0 points, and Mixtral-8x7B (29.2%) by 21.7 points. In zero-shot CoT, GPT-4 (37.9%) edges out Llama 3-70B (35.5%) by only 2.4 points, but substantially outperforms GPT-3.5 (23.7%) and Mixtral (22.4%). Notably, MEDITRON-70B performs poorly in zero-shot settings (6.2% direct, 0.4% CoT) due to lack of instruction tuning, but recovers to 26.3% with one-shot prompting, approaching Mixtral-8x7B. PMC-LLaMA-13B is essentially non-functional in zero-shot settings (0.0%) and reaches only 7.0% even with one-shot CoT, indicating severe gaps in medical calculation knowledge. The gap between GPT-4 and Llama 3-70B widens from 0 points (zero-shot direct) to 11.4 points (one-shot CoT), highlighting GPT-4's superior in-context learning for medical equations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first benchmark specifically targeting LLMs' medical calculation capabilities, a gap the authors identify between existing medical QA benchmarks (MedQA, PubMedQA, MedMCQA) that test qualitative reasoning and general math benchmarks (GSM8k, MATH) that lack domain grounding. It draws on the MDCalc platform's 55 popular calculators as task sources and the Open-Patients corpus for realistic patient notes, while building on prompting paradigms (zero-shot, CoT, one-shot) established in the broader LLM evaluation literature. Prior work on medical calculators with LLMs (OpenMedCalc, CalcQA, AgentMD) relied on small-scale or automatically constructed datasets; MEDCALC-BENCH departs by providing 1047 manually verified instances with step-by-step explanations across both rule-based and equation-based paradigms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even GPT-4 achieves only 50.9% accuracy with one-shot CoT prompting provides a concrete, evidence-based argument that current LLMs are not safe for clinical calculation tasks, with direct implications for healthcare AI deployment. The error decomposition (knowledge vs. extraction vs. computation) gives researchers a clear roadmap for targeted improvements rather than a single aggregate score. Key limitations include the modest dataset size (1,047 instances), reliance on a single medical annotator for verification, and the absence of few-shot or tool-augmented evaluations, leaving open whether scaling demonstrations or integrating external calculators could close the gap to clinically acceptable performance.
