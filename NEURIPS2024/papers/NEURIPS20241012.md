## Problem

Existing jailbreak attack studies use inconsistent hyperparameter settings (model size, safety prompts, templates, attacker capability, suffix length, budget) and focus almost exclusively on unprotected LLMs, making cross-paper comparisons unreliable and leaving the impact of defense-enhanced models largely unexplored. This paper identifies that these overlooked implementation details can dramatically shift attack success rates, obscuring the true effectiveness of both attack and defense methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241012/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes malicious instruction prompts from three datasets (AdvBench with 50 deduplicated samples, MaliciousInstruct with 100 instructions across 10 categories, and HEx-PHI with 330 examples across 11 intention categories) and applies them to target LLMs (Llama-2 7B/13B/70B, Llama-3 8B/70B, Vicuna 7B/13B, Qwen1.5, Mistral-7B) under configurable defense settings. Outputs are scored via two complementary ASR metrics: a prefix-based method matching against a curated list of ~80 rejection phrases, and a GPT-4 agent-based method that scores each response on a 1–10 scale with a threshold of 5 to classify jailbroken vs. safe outputs. The full experimental matrix spans approximately 354 configurations consuming ~55,000 GPU hours on A800-80G hardware.

## Method

JailTrickBench systematically isolates and evaluates eight key factors split across target-model level (model size, fine-tuning alignment, safety system prompt, template type) and attacker level (attacker LLM ability, adversarial suffix length, attack budget, attack intention), then combines them into weakest/normal/strongest configurations to measure their joint effect. Seven representative attacks are deployed: four token-level (GCG, AutoDAN, AmpleGCG, AdvPrompter) and three prompt-level (PAIR, TAP, GPTFuzz), each tested against six defenses spanning system-level (Self-Reminder, RPO, SmoothLLM) and model-level (Adversarial Training, Unlearning, Safety Training) strategies. The key empirical finding is that improper settings—such as removing safety system prompts, using zero-shot templates, or under-budgeting token-level attacks—can swing ASR by tens of percentage points, meaning prior results are not directly comparable without standardizing these factors. The paper distills these findings into a 'bag of tricks' that prescribes which configurations most strongly influence attack and defense outcomes, providing a reproducible baseline for future jailbreak research.

## Results

On AdvBench (ASR_Agent), GPTFuzz is the most effective attack, achieving 92% ASR on Vicuna-13B and 22% on LLaMA-2-7B with no defense. SmoothLLM is the strongest defense overall, reducing GPTFuzz's ASR from 92% to 4% on Vicuna-13B and from 22% to 4% on LLaMA-2-7B. RPO achieves 0% ASR against both AmpleGCG and AdvPrompter on Vicuna-13B, while Unlearning paradoxically *increases* ASR for PAIR (36%→96%) and TAP (32%→96%) on Vicuna-13B. Across ~354 experiments (~55,000 GPU hours on A800-80G), the paper shows that implementation details (safety prompts, templates, attacker ability, suffix length) can shift ASR by 20–80 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 7 attacks (GCG, AutoDAN, AmpleGCG, AdvPrompter, PAIR, TAP, GPTFuzz) against 6 defenses (Self-Reminder, RPO, SmoothLLM, Adversarial Training, Unlearning, Safety Training) on two models. GPTFuzz dominates as the strongest attack (92–93% ASR on Vicuna-13B, no defense), followed by AutoDAN (88–92%), while GCG is the weakest (2–14%). Among defenses, SmoothLLM is the most consistently effective, reducing ASR to 0–20% across most attack–model pairs; RPO achieves 0% ASR against AmpleGCG and AdvPrompter on Vicuna-13B but fails against GPTFuzz (60% ASR). Unlearning is the most problematic defense: it *increases* ASR for PAIR (36%→96%) and TAP (32%→96%) on Vicuna-13B, suggesting it removes safety-relevant knowledge without adding robustness. LLaMA-2-7B is substantially more resistant than Vicuna-13B (e.g., GPTFuzz: 22% vs. 92% ASR), and the paper shows this gap is not explained by model size alone. The key comparative takeaway is that no single defense is universally effective—SmoothLLM excels against token-level attacks but is less reliable against GPTFuzz, while RPO neutralizes token-level attacks but is bypassed by prompt-level ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a systematic benchmarking effort that departs from prior jailbreak evaluation work (JailbreakBench, HarmBench, Latent Jailbreak) by explicitly accounting for the diverse implementation details—model size, fine-tuning alignment, safety system prompts, template type, attacker capability, suffix length, attack budget, and attack intention—that have been inconsistently handled across existing studies. Rather than introducing new attack or defense methods, it repurposes seven established attacks (GCG, AutoDAN, AmpleGCG, AdvPrompter, PAIR, TAP, GPTFuzz) and six defenses (Self-Reminder, RPO, SmoothLLM, Adversarial Training, Unlearning, Safety Training) under a unified protocol to isolate and quantify each factor's contribution. The key conceptual departure is treating these 'tricks' as first-class evaluation variables rather than fixed hyperparameters, and extending the evaluation to defense-enhanced LLMs where prior benchmarks largely stopped.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a much-needed standardized reference (JailTrickBench) that makes cross-paper comparisons of jailbreak attacks meaningful, addressing a real reproducibility gap in the field. Its findings—particularly that model robustness is largely independent of size, that fine-tuning substantially degrades safety alignment, and that safety system prompts and template choices can swing ASR by tens of percentage points—give practitioners concrete levers for both attack and defense design. Open questions remain: the evaluation is limited to Llama-2, Llama-3, Vicuna, Qwen1.5, and Mistral families; the ~$10,000 API cost and 55,000 GPU-hour budget constrain coverage; and the paper acknowledges that its controlled settings may not fully replicate real-world deployment scenarios where multi-turn interactions, multimodal inputs, and evolving attack strategies are common.
