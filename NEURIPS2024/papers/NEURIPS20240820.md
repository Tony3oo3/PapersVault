## Problem

When deep learning models are used to estimate causal treatment effects from high-dimensional observations (e.g., video frames) in randomized controlled trials, common pipeline choices—annotation sampling, encoder selection, prediction discretization, and accuracy-based model selection—can introduce systematic bias into the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) estimate. The paper shows that even in the ideal RCT setting, these seemingly innocuous design decisions can invalidate causal conclusions, and that standard prediction metrics like accuracy do not proxy the quality of the downstream causal estimate.

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB video frames (770×770 pixels, sampled at 2 fps) of three-ant groups in a controlled 3×3 grid setup, where one focal ant is treated with one of two microparticle types. Only a subset of frames is annotated by human experts (binary grooming labels per nestmate), and the model must predict grooming probability on the unannotated majority. The downstream output is not a per-frame label but the ATE: the difference in mean predicted grooming probability between the two treatment arms, which is compared against the ground-truth ATE computed from expert annotations on the full RCT.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a theoretical characterization of Treatment Effect Bias (TEB) for ML pipelines in RCTs, combined with a large-scale empirical validation. Theoretically, they prove (Lemma 3.1) that a model with accuracy 1−ε can have TEB as large as ε/min_t P(T=t), and (Theorem 3.1) that discretizing a consistent probability estimator to hard labels converges to a different quantity, biasing the ATE. Empirically, they introduce ISTAnt—the first real-world RCT dataset for causal inference from high-dimensional observations (44 videos, 52,800 frames of Lasius neglectus ants)—and CausalMNIST, a synthetic benchmark with a controlled structural causal model. They fine-tune 6,480 models (six frozen ViT/CLIP/MAE/DINOv2 encoders × three token representations × two task formulations × two MLP depths × three learning rates × five seeds × six annotation-splitting criteria) and demonstrate that biased annotation sampling, non-minimal encoder representations (especially MAE's background sensitivity), and prediction discretization each independently degrade TEB, while TEB on a validation set is a far better model-selection criterion than accuracy.

## Results

Across 6480 fine-tuned models on ISTAnt, the paper finds that discretizing predictions significantly worsens treatment effect estimation (t = −10.42, p = 1.07×10⁻²⁵), confirming the theoretical discretization bias. Even among the 200 best models per annotation criterion (by balanced accuracy), statistical tests reject unbiasedness for nearly all criteria (e.g., position annotation: t = 14.982, p ≈ 0 in many-shot; t = −11.250, p ≈ 0 in few-shot). Among six pre-trained encoders, MAE significantly underperforms the others, with a Fréchet Distance of 760.9±122.4 on position splitting versus 211.8–681.7 for the remaining encoders, suggesting its masked-reconstruction training captures spurious background correlations. Models with balanced accuracy >0.95 still exhibit TERB up to ±50%, and TEB computed on a small validation set is a better predictor of full-dataset TEB than any standard accuracy metric.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares six pre-trained Vision Transformer encoders (ViT-B, ViT-L, CLIP-ViT-B, CLIP-ViT-L, MAE, DINOv2) under three annotation strategies (random, experiment/batch, position) in both few-shot and many-shot regimes. MAE is the clear outlier, significantly underperforming all other encoders in TERB; its Fréchet Distance on position splitting (760.9±122.4) is roughly 2–3× higher than the others, indicating it relies on spurious background correlations rather than ant-relevant features. Among the remaining five encoders, TERB ranges are similar (up to ±50%) despite differences in downstream prediction accuracy, confirming that classification accuracy is not a proxy for causal estimation quality. Random annotation is the only criterion that does not produce strongly biased ATE estimates in the few-shot regime (t = −4.46 vs. t ≈ −11 to −13 for biased criteria), though even random annotation shows a small but significant bias. Discretizing model outputs (thresholding at 0.5) consistently increases |TEB| across all 9720 evaluations (t = −10.42, p = 1.07×10⁻²⁵), and selecting models by validation TEB rather than validation accuracy reduces underestimation of the effect from 18% to 11% in the most realistic few-shot/experiment setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of representation learning and causal inference, departing from prior AI-for-science benchmarks (MABE22, MammalNet, Terra Incognita) that evaluate models solely on prediction accuracy. It also distinguishes itself from the causal representation learning literature (Ahuja et al., Brehmer et al., Locatello et al.), whose identifiability assumptions (interventions on the outcome, multi-view pairs, non-degeneracy) are inapplicable to the high-dimensional, partially-annotated RCT setting considered here. Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper repurposes frozen state-of-the-art vision backbones (ViT, CLIP, MAE, DINOv2) with simple MLP heads and formally shows—via a new Treatment Effect Bias (TEB) definition and a discretization bias theorem—that standard ML pipeline choices (annotation sampling, encoder selection, thresholding, accuracy-based model selection) systematically distort downstream ATE estimates even under the ideal Ignorability Assumption.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that classification accuracy is not a proxy for causal estimation quality (TERBs up to ±50% among models with balanced accuracy >0.95) challenges a core assumption in AI-for-science benchmarks and provides concrete, actionable guidelines for representation learning in causal contexts. The ISTAnt dataset is the first real-world benchmark for treatment effect estimation from high-dimensional observations, and CausalMNIST offers a controlled synthetic complement, together enabling future work on debiasing, semi-supervised adaptation, and causal representation learning with realistic assumptions. Key open questions include how to mitigate encoder bias during fine-tuning, whether fairness-metric tools (e.g., Treatment Equality) can be extended to bound TEB on validation data, and how these results generalize beyond RCTs to observational settings where confounding compounds the biases identified here.
