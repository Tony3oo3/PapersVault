## Problem

Prior retrieval benchmarks treat textual (unstructured) and relational (structured/knowledge graph) retrieval as separate problems, leaving no unified testbed for semi-structured knowledge bases (SKBs) where queries simultaneously demand multi-hop relational reasoning and nuanced textual understanding. This gap is especially acute for LLM-driven retrieval systems, whose ability to handle the interplay of private, domain-specific relational and textual knowledge remains uncharacterized.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244036/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Given a natural-language query Q, a knowledge graph G=(V,E) with typed nodes and edges, and a collection of free-text documents D_i attached to each node, the system must return a ranked set of nodes A⊆V satisfying both the relational constraints encoded in G and the textual constraints in D_i. Queries are conversation-like and context-rich (averaging ~16 words, up to 50), and ground-truth answer sets are multi-entity (averaging 2.5–18 items per query across the three SKBs, which span 129K–1.9M entities and 8M–40M relations).

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a four-step automated pipeline for synthesizing realistic SKB queries with verified ground-truth answers: (1) sample a domain-specific relational template and ground it with entities to yield a candidate set; (2) use an LLM to extract role-specific textual properties from a randomly chosen gold answer's document; (3) use two different LLMs to fuse relational and textual requirements into natural, diverse queries with contextual framing; and (4) verify all remaining candidates against the extracted properties using multiple LLMs, retaining only those passing all checks (86–99% gold-answer recall). The benchmark is then used to evaluate five classes of retrievers—sparse (BM25), small dense (DPR, ANCE, QAGNN), LLM-based dense (ada-002, voyage-l2-instruct, LLM2Vec, GritLM-7b), multivector (multi-ada-002, ColBERTv2), and LLM rerankers (Claude3, GPT-4)—on Hit@k, Recall@20, MRR, and latency, revealing that even the strongest rerankers achieve only ~18–41% Hit@1 and <60% Recall@20, highlighting substantial room for improvement in semi-structured retrieval.

## Results

On the full synthesized test sets, the GPT-4 Reranker achieves the best Hit@1 scores of 44.79% (STARK-AMAZON), 40.90% (STARK-MAG), and 18.28% (STARK-PRIME), while the best dense retriever GritLM-7b reaches 42.08%, 37.90%, and 15.57% respectively. BM25, despite being a simple sparse method, is a surprisingly strong baseline, achieving 44.94% Hit@1 on STARK-AMAZON and outperforming several dense retrievers. Even the strongest systems remain far from solving the task: Recall@20 stays below 60% across all three datasets, and MRR for STARK-PRIME peaks at only ~27% for the GPT-4 Reranker, indicating that top-ranked answers are frequently incorrect.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BM25 [39] is a surprisingly competitive sparse baseline, achieving the highest Hit@1 (44.94%) on STARK-AMAZON among non-reranking methods and outperforming DPR, ANCE, and QAGNN across all three datasets. Among small dense retrievers, DPR [26] and ANCE [52] underperform significantly (Hit@1 of 15.29% and 30.96% on AMAZON), likely due to their small model sizes and overfitting risk on SKBs with hard-to-differentiate entity documents. QAGNN [56] shows intermediate performance, with its graph-aware architecture helping on MAG (R@20 = 46.97%) but still trailing BM25 on AMAZON. Among LLM-based dense retrievers, GritLM-7b [33] is the clear winner (Hit@1: 42.08/37.90/15.57), surpassing ada-002 [36] (39.16/29.08/12.63) and voyage-l2-instruct [1] (40.93/30.06/10.85), while LLM2Vec [3] underperforms all others due to its limited context length being insufficient for lengthy SKB documents. ColBERTv2 [41] benefits from fine-grained token-level matching, achieving the best Hit@1 (46.10%) among non-reranking methods on AMAZON, but trails GritLM-7b on MAG and PRIME. The LLM rerankers (GPT-4 and Claude3) [62] consistently achieve the best Hit@1 and MRR scores across all datasets, with GPT-4 reaching 40.90% Hit@1 and 49.00% MRR on STARK-MAG, yet even these top systems remain far from adequate, with Recall@20 below 60% everywhere, confirming that STARK poses substantial challenges to current retrieval technology.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by unifying two previously separate research threads: purely textual retrieval (SQuAD, HotpotQA, TriviaQA) and structured knowledge graph querying (WikiSQL, Spider, ComplexWebQuestions, GraphQA). Prior semi-structured QA datasets (WikiTableQuestions, TabFact, HybridQA) combined tabular and textual data but lacked rich multi-hop relational dynamics among entities. STARK departs from all of these by formulating retrieval over semi-structured knowledge bases where queries must simultaneously satisfy multi-hop relational constraints on a knowledge graph and nuanced textual requirements over free-form documents, a combination the authors explicitly identify as absent from prior literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STARK provides the first comprehensive testbed for evaluating retrieval systems on semi-structured knowledge bases spanning e-commerce, academic search, and precision medicine, filling a clear gap between purely textual and purely structured benchmarks. The finding that even GPT-4 and Claude3 rerankers achieve only ~18–41% Hit@1 across domains reveals a substantial capability gap in current LLM-based retrieval for real-world complex queries. Open questions include how to reduce the 25–29 second latency of LLM rerankers while preserving accuracy, how to extend SKBs to multimodal content (images, video), and how to handle privacy when operating on private knowledge bases. The benchmark's automated query synthesis pipeline is also a reusable contribution for constructing similar evaluation sets on other SKBs.
