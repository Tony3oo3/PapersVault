## Problem

Despite extensive study of in-context learning in LLMs from theoretical and accuracy-based perspectives, the qualitative behavior of the learned decision function remains unexplored. This paper asks: what do the decision boundaries of LLMs look like when performing in-context binary classification, and what factors control their smoothness? The surprising finding is that even state-of-the-art models like GPT-4o produce fragmented, non-smooth boundaries on trivially linearly separable tasks where classical classifiers (e.g., SVM) achieve clean boundaries with far fewer examples.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 2D numerical feature-label pairs (generated from scikit-learn's make_classification, make_circles, or make_moons) formatted as text prompts with a fixed template (e.g., 'Input: 64 24 / Label: Bar'), and a query point appended at the end. The LLM's next-token logit distribution over the two label tokens determines the predicted class. Decision boundaries are rendered by querying a 50×50 grid (2500 points) spanning the bounding box of the in-context examples, producing a colored 2D map. Test accuracy is additionally measured on a held-out set of 100 points. For closed-source models (GPT-4o, GPT-3.5-turbo), next-token generation is used instead of logit extraction.

## Method

The core technique is to treat in-context learning as a black-box classifier and visualize its decision boundary by prompting the LLM with n in-context examples plus a grid of query points, recording the predicted class at each location. The paper then systematically ablates factors affecting boundary smoothness: model size (1.3B–13B), quantization level (8-bit vs. 4-bit), label semantics (e.g., 'Foo/Bar' vs. reversed), and example ordering. To improve smoothness, the authors fine-tune Llama models on 1000 synthetic binary classification tasks using LoRA, finding that updating earlier layers (embeddings, attention) yields smoother boundaries than updating only the prediction head. They also introduce CustomLLM, which freezes the transformer backbone and trains new input-embedding and prediction-head MLPs, and demonstrate that uncertainty-aware active sampling—iteratively adding the most uncertain grid points as new in-context examples—produces smoother boundaries and higher test accuracy than random sampling. Finally, TNP models trained from scratch on the same task family achieve smooth boundaries, confirming that the non-smoothness is specific to pretrained LLMs rather than the transformer architecture itself.

## Results

The paper's primary finding is qualitative: all tested LLMs (Llama2-7B/13B, Llama3-8B, Mistral-7B, Sheared-Llama-1.3B, GPT-4o, GPT-3.5-turbo) exhibit non-smooth, fragmented decision boundaries even on simple linearly separable binary classification tasks, regardless of model size or number of in-context examples (up to 256). On test accuracy (Figure 3), Llama-3-8B and Mistral-7B scale comparably to SVM (polynomial kernel) and MLP (two hidden layers) baselines as in-context examples increase, while Sheared-Llama-1.3B consistently performs worst. Fine-tuning earlier layers (embedding, attention) via LoRA on 1,000 synthetic classification tasks produces visibly smoother boundaries than fine-tuning only the prediction head, and uncertainty-aware active sampling yields smoother boundaries and higher test accuracy than random sampling with the same number of additional examples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares LLMs against two classical ML baselines (SVM with polynomial kernel and MLP with two hidden layers of 256 neurons) on test accuracy across linear, circle, and moon classification tasks. Llama-3-8B and Mistral-7B achieve accuracy comparable to these baselines as the number of in-context examples grows, but their decision boundaries remain fragmented while SVM and MLP produce smooth boundaries with far fewer examples. The key contrast is with Transformer Neural Processes (TNP) trained from scratch [Nguyen & Grover, ICML 2022]: TNP models of all four sizes (0.1M–9.7M parameters) learn smooth decision boundaries on the same moon-shaped non-linear task, demonstrating that the non-smoothness is specific to pretrained LLMs rather than an inherent limitation of transformer architectures. Notably, smaller TNP models generalize better than larger ones, inverting the typical scaling trend observed in LLMs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the classical ML diagnostic of decision boundary visualization as a lens for probing in-context learning in LLMs, a perspective absent from prior ICL analyses that focus on accuracy, prompt sensitivity, or gradient-descent equivalences (Von Oswald et al. 2023; Min et al. 2022b; Wei et al. 2023). It builds on the TNP framework (Nguyen and Grover 2022) for training transformers from scratch as a controlled comparison, and on MetaICL-style fine-tuning (Min et al. 2022a) as a baseline for improving ICL behavior. The key departure is treating the LLM's ICL as a black-box classifier whose geometric inductive bias can be inspected, rather than reducing it to an optimization or statistical generalization problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that high test accuracy coexists with fragmented, non-smooth decision boundaries—even for GPT-4o on trivially separable tasks—raises a practical reliability concern: LLMs may be confidently wrong in small neighborhoods of the input space, undermining deployment in safety-critical classification. The paper leaves open why this non-smoothness arises (architecture, pretraining objective, or tokenization of numerical inputs) and whether it persists in higher-dimensional or non-synthetic settings beyond the 2D t-SNE projections shown. The practical takeaway that fine-tuning earlier embedding layers or using uncertainty-aware active sampling improves boundary quality is actionable but narrow in scope, and the analysis remains primarily qualitative (visual inspection) rather than supported by a quantitative smoothness metric.
