## Problem

Standard RLHF assumes all users share a single utility function, so when preferences are multi-modal (e.g., one group prefers detailed responses, another prefers concise ones), the BTL reward model averages over divergent modes and fails to serve any individual subgroup. No existing method can infer a user-specific preference context from a few annotations and adapt both the reward model and downstream policy to that user without requiring additional user-specific training data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes binary preference labels over pairs of states (or prompt+response pairs), critically requiring that N annotations come from the same annotator to form the 'context' for latent inference. For LLMs, prompt+response pairs are first encoded into fixed embeddings by a frozen LLM (last-token representation), and the encoder operates over these compressed vectors rather than raw text. The output is a Gaussian latent distribution z per user, a latent-conditioned reward function r(s,z), and a personalized policy π(a|s,z); the probabilistic nature of z additionally enables uncertainty quantification and active query selection at test time.

## Method

VPL formulates multi-modal reward learning as a latent variable problem: a variational encoder q_ψ(z | {annotations}) infers a user-specific latent from a few same-user preference pairs, and a latent-conditional BTL reward model r_φ(s,z) explains preferences given z. Training optimizes an ELBO combining the BTL reconstruction likelihood with a KL divergence against a learned Gaussian prior. A key practical contribution is addressing the reward scaling problem—since BTL only constrains reward differences, different z values produce rewards at wildly different scales that destabilize multi-task policy optimization; VPL+SPO resolves this by replacing raw rewards with expected pairwise preference likelihoods (normalized to [0,1]). For LLMs, the architecture uses a self-attention encoder over context pair embeddings, LoRA fine-tuning of the reward model, and data augmentation by sampling multiple context subsets per data point. At test time, active learning selects the most informative query set by maximizing mutual information between labels and the latent, enabling personalization with as few as 2–4 preference queries.

## Results

On the LLM reward modeling benchmark (Table 1), VPL achieves 100% accuracy on Pets(Divergent) with GPT2, compared to 63.27% for BTL and 70.62% for DPL. On the more challenging UltraFeedback-P-2 dataset, VPL reaches 74.75% (GPT2) and 76.41% (Llama2-7b), outperforming BTL (49.84% / 47.17%) and DPL (49.57% / 49.51%) by 25–29 percentage points. On UF-P-4 (four divergent users), VPL achieves 61.49% versus 53.48% (BTL) and 52.92% (DPL). In simulated control tasks, VPL matches or approaches oracle-level task success rates while all baselines fail to adapt to individual user preferences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VPL decisively outperforms the standard BTL baseline [52, 17] across all datasets: by 36.7 points on Pets(Divergent), 24.9 points on UF-P-2 (GPT2), and 29.2 points on UF-P-2 (Llama2-7b), confirming that the unimodal BTL assumption causes severe model misspecification under divergent preferences. DPL [58], which models the mean and variance of the reward distribution, improves slightly over BTL on Pets(Divergent) (70.62% vs 63.27%) but still falls 29.4 points short of VPL, because DPL cannot recover individual user-specific reward functions. On UF-P-2 and UF-P-4, DPL performs at or below chance level (≈50%), indicating it fails to disambiguate between users with conflicting objectives. In control tasks (Figure 4), VPL approaches the Oracle [47] upper bound (which has access to true per-user reward functions), while BTL and DPL policies converge to averaged or majority-preferred goals and fail to personalize. The VPL+SPO variant further improves multi-task policy optimization by normalizing reward scales across latents, yielding additional gains over plain VPL in Maze-Navigation and Ravens-Manipulation.

## Relation to Prior Work

VPL extends the standard RLHF pipeline (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) by replacing the unimodal BTL reward model with a latent-conditional variant, where a variational encoder infers a user-specific latent z from a few preference annotations and the reward model is conditioned on z. This is conceptually closest to Distributional Preference Learning (DPL, Siththaranjan et al., 2023), which also addresses hidden context in RLHF, but DPL only models the mean/variance or a categorical approximation of the reward distribution and cannot recover individual user-specific reward functions. The reward scaling technique (VPL+SPO) borrows from Swamy et al. (2024) to address the practical issue that BTL rewards have inconsistent scales across latent values, which destabilizes multi-task policy optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Pluralistic alignment is a recognized but under-addressed gap in RLHF: current deployed systems assume a single utility function and silently average over divergent user preferences, disadvantaging minority groups. VPL's latent variable framework enables personalization at test time, active preference elicitation (halving required queries), and uncertainty estimation, all from a single unified model. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic or constructed preference datasets (no large-scale real-world diverse-annotator data exists yet), the assumption that a user can be queried in batches on a fixed set of survey questions, and sensitivity to context length. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to naturally occurring preference diversity in open-ended conversations, how to handle adversarial or sycophantic personalization, and when a system should override user-specific preferences in favor of universal safety values.
