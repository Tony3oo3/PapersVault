## Problem

In intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), the large dose influence matrix (100K–500K rows × 1K–20K columns) is currently sparsified in clinical practice by simply discarding all entries below a fixed threshold, a 'naive' approach that can degrade treatment quality by misestimating delivered doses. Existing randomized sketching techniques from machine learning reduce the number of rows, which is incompatible with radiotherapy because the full dose vector Ax is needed to evaluate clinical constraints. This paper is the first to apply matrix sparsification (preserving all rows) to radiotherapy optimization, demonstrating that randomized methods can match or exceed the naive approach while providing formal guarantees on feasibility and optimality gaps.

## Input / Output

The method takes the dense dose influence matrix A ∈ ℝ^{m×n} (where m is the number of patient voxels and n is the number of machine beamlets) and a scalar sparsification threshold ε, and produces a sparse matrix S with O(m + ‖A‖₁/ε) non-zero entries that approximates A in spectral norm. Unlike row-reduction sketching, S retains all m rows so that the full dose vector Sx can be evaluated against per-voxel clinical constraints (maximum and mean dose limits per organ). The sparse matrix S is then plugged into the quadratic constrained optimization problem (solved via CVXPY/MOSEK interior-point methods) to yield optimized beamlet intensities x_S, which are evaluated against the original matrix A to compute dose discrepancy, feasibility gap, and optimality gap.

## Method

The proposed Randomized Minor-value Rectification (RMR) algorithm operates row-by-row in two phases: it deterministically retains all entries with |a_ij| > ε, then for the remaining minor entries it computes their row-wise sum Σ_i and performs k_i = ⌈Σ_i/ε⌉ random draws, each selecting a column j with probability proportional to |a_ij| and adding (Σ_i/k_i)·sign(a_ij) to the corresponding entry of S. This hybrid design combines the deterministic large-entry retention of Arora et al. (AHK06) with an ℓ₁-sampling redistribution step, avoiding AHK06's substitution of all minor entries with a uniform ±c that disproportionately distorts tumor-voxel rows. Theoretical analysis via the Matrix Bernstein inequality yields an ℓ₂-norm error bound with non-zero count scaling as 1/τ (rather than 1/τ² for sampling-based methods), and further lemmas bound the feasibility gap at (19+5 log m)ε‖x‖₂ and the sub-optimality gap at O(ε) with probability ≥ 0.95. Experiments on 10 lung and 5 prostate patients from PortPy show RMR achieves the smallest feasibility and optimality gaps among all compared methods (AHK06, DZ11, AKL13, BKKS21, naive) at 96–99% sparsity, with the dose discrepancy between Sx_S and Ax_S being negligible in DVH plots.

## Results

At 98% sparsity across 10 lung patients, RMR achieves a mean relative optimality gap of 19.5%, dramatically outperforming BKKS21 (116.6%), AHK06 (208%), DZ11 (392%), AKL13 (507%), and the naive thresholding approach (8,450%). The mean feasibility gap for RMR is 0.098, comparable to BKKS21 (0.11) and far below DZ11 (2.74) or the naive method (6,570). On relative ℓ₂-norm error, RMR (9.91%) is slightly behind AHK06 (9.16%) and BKKS21 (9.45%) but still far superior to the naive approach (24.97%). RMR's sparsification runtime averages 36.6 s, competitive with AHK06 (30.2 s) and substantially faster than DZ11 (202.4 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RMR decisively outperforms all four randomized baselines on the two clinically most relevant metrics: optimality gap and feasibility gap. On optimality gap, RMR (19.5% mean) is roughly 6× better than the next-best method BKKS21 (116.6%) and over 10× better than AHK06 (208%), DZ11 (392%), and AKL13 (507%). On feasibility gap, RMR (0.10) ties or slightly beats BKKS21 (0.11) and is 2.4× better than AHK06 (0.24), 5× better than AKL13 (0.51), and 28× better than DZ11 (2.74). On the ℓ₂-norm error metric, AHK06 (9.16%) and BKKS21 (9.45%) marginally outperform RMR (9.91%), a gap the authors attribute to RMR's row-wise redistribution strategy prioritizing dose accuracy over global spectral fidelity. In terms of runtime, RMR (36.6 s) is slightly slower than AHK06 (30.2 s) but 5.5× faster than DZ11 (202.4 s) and 1.4× faster than BKKS21 (51.5 s); the naive method is fastest (13.6 s) but produces infeasible and highly sub-optimal treatment plans. The paper also notes that RMR's sparser output (fewer non-zeros at the same accuracy level) translates into faster downstream optimization: for patient 1, solving the constrained problem takes 57 s with RMR's sketch versus 200 s with AHK06's.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the matrix sparsification literature (Arora et al. 2006, Drineas & Zouzias 2011, Achlioptas et al. 2013, Braverman et al. 2021) to a setting where the matrix appears in both the objective and the constraints of a large-scale nonlinear optimization problem, a regime where standard row-reduction sketching is inapplicable. The proposed RMR algorithm is a hybrid that borrows the deterministic retention of large entries from Arora et al. but replaces their uniform ±c substitution with a row-wise ℓ1-sampling redistribution of minor values, motivated by the heterogeneous row structure of dose influence matrices. The theoretical analysis (Theorems 3.2, 3.6, 3.9) provides the first end-to-end guarantees bounding feasibility and optimality gaps for the surrogate constrained problem, going beyond the ℓ2-norm approximation guarantees of prior sparsification work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first application of randomized matrix sparsification to radiotherapy treatment planning with a public benchmark (PortPy), demonstrating that the naive thresholding approach currently used in clinical practice produces feasibility and optimality gaps orders of magnitude larger than RMR at the same sparsity level. The practical impact is significant: at 98% sparsity, RMR reduces optimization time from ~2800s (full matrix on HPC) to ~87s on a standard workstation while keeping dose discrepancies clinically small. Open questions include whether the theoretical bounds extend to the general non-quadratic formulation (Problem 1), whether the approach generalizes to other modalities (proton therapy, 4π planning), and whether the observed DVH improvements translate to measurable clinical outcomes in prospective trials.
