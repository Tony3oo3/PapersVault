## Problem

Interpretable models like GAMs are not well-defined when feature values are missing, and the two standard remedies each destroy interpretability: imputation creates multivariate shape functions that are hard to visualize, while naively adding missingness indicators and their interactions causes a combinatorial explosion of terms that sacrifices sparsity. The paper asks whether a model can reason directly about missingness in a way that preserves the univariate, additive structure that makes GAMs interpretable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240380/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabular dataset where each feature may be missing, with missingness optionally encoded into c distinct categories (e.g., FICO uses three codes: no information, no usable information, no report found). Each feature is binned into 8 quantile thresholds, and the model produces a binary prediction as a sum of univariate step-function shape curves plus sparse missingness indicator and interaction terms. The output is fully visualizable: for any sample, one can see which shape curves are active and how they are adjusted by missingness, unlike imputation-based models whose effective mapping becomes a high-dimensional surface.

## Method

M-GAM extends a standard GAM by adding two types of augmentation terms per feature: (1) missingness indicator terms that shift the shape function for feature j when j itself is missing, and (2) missingness interaction terms that reshape the curve for feature j' when a different feature j is missing, preserving the univariate additive structure. The key theoretical insight (Proposition 3.1) is that even perfect imputation can be strictly worse than using missingness as a value, because missingness can carry information about the label that no imputed value recovers. To control the combinatorial explosion of interaction terms (scaling as d·Σ len(t_j) when c=1), M-GAM applies ℓ0 regularization directly during optimization using the FastSparse framework with exponential loss, yielding models with fewer than 40 nonzero coefficients that match or exceed impute-then-predict accuracy. Theorem 3.4 further shows that M-GAM can exactly recover the expected score of any affine-imputation-plus-GAM pipeline, establishing that the model class is at least as expressive as impute-then-predict while being strictly more expressive in informative-missingness settings.

## Results

On the FICO dataset, M-GAM achieves superior accuracy to all impute-then-predict baselines using only 20 non-zero coefficients, while matching the accuracy of dense GAMs with multiple imputation using fewer than 40 total coefficients. Under synthetic MAR missingness (up to 50% of entries), M-GAM with interaction terms substantially outperforms all imputation-based methods, with the gap widening as missingness increases. On real datasets (FICO, Breast Cancer, MIMIC-III, Pharyngitis), no baseline method substantially outperforms M-GAM in test accuracy/AUC. In runtime, M-GAM is at least an order of magnitude faster than any multiple imputation method (MICE, MIWAE, MissForest) across all six datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MICE (the primary imputation baseline), M-GAM matches test accuracy on all four real datasets (Figure 6) but is at least 10x faster in runtime (Figure 5); under synthetic MAR missingness, M-GAM with interactions clearly outperforms MICE-ensembled GAMs, with the advantage growing from 25% to 50% missingness (Figure 3). MIWAE and MissForest show the same pattern: comparable accuracy on real data but substantially longer runtimes, and inferior accuracy under informative missingness. Against XGBoost and Random Forest (both with and without imputation), M-GAM achieves comparable or better accuracy while providing global interpretability through sparse shape functions, whereas tree-based methods remain opaque. The SMIM baseline (Van Ness et al., 2023), which selectively adds missingness indicators without ℓ0 regularization, produces denser models; M-GAM achieves similar or better accuracy with far fewer non-zero coefficients. Decision trees without imputation are faster than M-GAM but consistently lower in accuracy. Overall, M-GAM wins on sparsity and runtime across all comparisons, ties on real-data accuracy, and wins decisively under informative (MAR) missingness.

## Relation to Prior Work

M-GAM extends the sparse GAM framework of Liu et al. (2022) by incorporating missingness indicators and their interaction terms with shape functions, while retaining the ℓ0 regularization that enforces sparsity. It departs from the missingness indicator approach of Van Ness et al. (2023), which uses hypothesis testing for variable selection in linear models, by directly optimizing for sparsity and by adding interaction terms that adjust shape curves when other features are missing. The paper also builds on the impute-then-predict literature (MICE, MIWAE, MissForest) primarily as a baseline to compare against, and on tree-based missingness handling (surrogate splits, missingness-as-value splits) as a conceptual precursor to its missingness interaction terms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

M-GAM provides a practical path to deploying interpretable models in high-stakes domains (medicine, justice) where missing data is ubiquitous and regulatory frameworks increasingly demand transparency. The theoretical result that perfect imputation can reduce the best achievable predictive performance (Proposition 3.1, Corollary 3.2) challenges the common assumption that better imputation always helps downstream prediction, reframing imputation as a trade-off rather than a pure benefit. Key open questions include whether differentiated ℓ0 penalties (lighter regularization for interaction terms on already-selected features) would improve interpretability, and how robust missingness-based models are to distribution shift in deployment settings.
