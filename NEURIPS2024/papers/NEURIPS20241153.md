## Problem

Web-scale vision-language datasets exhibit extreme class distribution imbalance, yet CLIP models pre-trained on them show notably weaker correlation between class frequency and per-class performance compared to supervised learning. Despite this observation, the underlying mechanisms responsible for CLIP's relative robustness to data imbalance remain unexplored, with prior work focusing primarily on data curation strategies rather than the model's internal training dynamics.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241153/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB images (resized to 224×224) paired with text captions of controllable descriptiveness—ranging from single-template class names to concatenated title, description, and tags—as input, and produces class labels via zero-shot nearest-prototype matching or feature representations for linear probing. A key design choice is that the training vocabulary (set of candidate classes per mini-batch) is dynamically subsampled to a smaller size, forming a dynamic classification task rather than a fixed 1000-class problem. For the DINO extension, only images are consumed, and the output is a set of feature representations evaluated via ℓ₂-regularized logistic regression on 12 downstream datasets.

## Method

The paper conducts a controlled study isolating five factors—language supervision descriptiveness, pretext task structure, data distribution, data scale, and open-world concept coverage—using ResNet-50 backbones with matched training protocols for CLIP and supervised learning. The central finding is that CLIP's contrastive pretext task inherently forms a dynamic classification problem where each mini-batch contains only a subset of classes, which isolates the bias from dominant head classes and implicitly balances the learning signal. This insight is made transferable through a simple technique: randomly subsampling the vocabulary (candidate classes for SL, or prototypes for DINO) during training, which acts as a form of classifier regularization analogous to dropout, preventing over-optimization toward head classes. Combined with fixed class prototypes from a pre-trained CLIP text encoder, this approach enables vanilla supervised and self-supervised models trained on extremely imbalanced data to achieve CLIP-level generalization on tail and open-world classes.

## Results

The paper's central finding is that CLIP's zero-shot classification accuracy is significantly less correlated with pre-training class frequency than supervised learning (SL), with the correlation narrowing further as data scales from 12M to 2.5B pairs. Under an extreme imbalance setting (tail classes reduced to 0-shot), CLIP with a frozen pre-trained text encoder retains strong generalization on tail and open-world classes, while a vanilla SL model with fixed CLIP prototypes plus vocabulary subsampling (size ~100) recovers comparable CLIP-level generalization. For self-supervised learning, vocabulary subsampling of DINO's prototypes (16,384 per iteration out of 65,536 total) on the imbalanced LAIONet dataset narrows the transfer-learning gap versus ImageNet-pretrained DINO (60.8 avg) from ~12 points to a few points across 12 downstream datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against CLIP (Radford et al., 2021), the paper demonstrates that the dynamic classification mechanism inherent to CLIP's contrastive pretext task can be replicated in a supervised setting: an SL model with fixed class prototypes from CLIP's text encoder plus random vocabulary subsampling (size ~100) achieves CLIP-level generalization on tail and open-world classes under extreme 0-shot imbalance (Fig. 8), whereas using the CLIP prototypes alone without subsampling yields only marginal improvements. Against DINO (Caron et al., 2021), the paper shows that vanilla DINO pre-trained on the imbalanced LAIONet dataset suffers a notable drop in transfer accuracy (e.g., 24.6% on Birds vs. 37.1% for ImageNet-pretrained DINO, 48.6% on Food vs. 55.8%), while applying vocabulary subsampling to DINO's prototypes during self-distillation narrows this gap substantially, recovering performance close to the ImageNet-pretrained baseline across 11 of 12 evaluation datasets (Fig. 10, Table 2). The paper also notes that reducing the total vocabulary size to 16,384 without subsampling is notably weaker than subsampling 16,384 per iteration from a pool of 65,536, confirming the benefit comes from the dynamic sampling rather than simply fewer prototypes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) and DINO (Caron et al., 2021) as the two primary systems under analysis, but its conceptual contribution is a mechanistic decomposition of *why* CLIP resists data-imbalance bias. Prior work on CLIP's distributional robustness (Fang et al. 2022; Nguyen et al. 2022) attributed the effect to diverse training distributions or data quality, while work on uncurated data (Shirali & Hardt 2023; Xu et al. 2024) focused on curation strategies. This paper departs from that line by isolating the pretext-task structure—specifically, that CLIP's in-batch contrastive loss forms a *dynamic classification* problem where only a random subset of classes is present per iteration—and showing this alone, via vocabulary subsampling, can replicate CLIP-level robustness in vanilla supervised and self-supervised models. The analysis also draws on neural-collapse metrics (Papyan et al. 2020; Han et al. 2022) to characterize feature-space effects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that a single, simple intervention—randomly subsampling the classification vocabulary during training—transfers CLIP-level tail-class generalization to both supervised ResNet classifiers and DINO self-distillation on extremely imbalanced web data, without requiring language supervision or contrastive objectives. This gives practitioners a low-cost tool for long-tailed and open-world recognition that is orthogonal to existing re-weighting or re-sampling strategies. Open questions the authors flag include whether the findings extend to detection and segmentation, whether generative vision-language models (LLaVA, BLIP-2) share the same implicit rebalancing property (evidence suggests they do not), and whether the rule-based frequency estimation used here would change conclusions under more accurate LLM-based counting. The study is limited to classification tasks and ResNet-50 backbones, and the frequency pipeline may undercount tail classes.
