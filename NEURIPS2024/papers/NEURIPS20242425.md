## Problem

While no-swap-regret algorithms guarantee time-averaged convergence to correlated equilibria in general games, it was unclear whether this could be strengthened to Nash convergence in the important special case of symmetric zero-sum games under self-play. The paper also asks whether the stronger guarantees of swap regret come at a structural cost to the learning algorithm itself, specifically whether time-symmetry (invariance to permutation of past observations) is compatible with swap regret minimization.

## Input / Output

The convergence result takes as input a generic symmetric zero-sum game (represented by a skew-symmetric N×N payoff matrix), identical symmetric initializations for both agents, and any no-swap-regret learning algorithm, and produces frequent-iterate convergence to the unique symmetric Nash equilibrium — meaning that in all but a vanishing fraction of rounds, the joint strategy profile is ε-close to Nash. The impossibility result takes any learning algorithm whose action at round t is a symmetric (permutation-invariant) function of the opponent's past mixed strategies and shows it must incur Ω(T) swap regret in some game, establishing that no-swap-regret dynamics fundamentally require time-asymmetric dependence on history.

## Method

The convergence proof combines two key lemmas: (1) a geometric lemma showing that time-averaged convergence of symmetric tensor products x_t⊗x_t to a product distribution y⊗y implies frequent-iterate convergence, proven by constructing a linear functional L_y (a weighted sum of squared coordinates) that is uniquely minimized at y⊗y over the manifold {x⊗x : x∈Δ_N}; and (2) a genericity lemma showing that almost all symmetric zero-sum games have a unique correlated/Nash equilibrium, proven via quasi-strict equilibrium theory and the fact that determinants of even-dimension skew-symmetric matrices are non-trivial polynomials. The time-asymmetry impossibility result couples the learning algorithm to a card-guessing game (using the Diaconis–Graham bound on expected correct guesses) to show that any symmetric function of past rewards must approximate Follow-the-Leader, then constructs a three-phase adversarial sequence (actions 1→2→3) where this forced leader-following creates linear swap regret.

## Results

The paper establishes that in almost all (generic) symmetric zero-sum games, any no-swap-regret algorithm in self-play with symmetric initialization achieves frequent-iterate convergence to the unique Nash equilibrium (Theorem 3), meaning that in all but a vanishing fraction of rounds, players play a strategy profile arbitrarily close to Nash. This result is tight: last-iterate convergence cannot be guaranteed even under these conditions. A complementary impossibility result (Theorem 6) shows that no no-swap-regret algorithm can be a symmetric (permutation-invariant) function of the observed reward history, proving that swap regret minimization inherently requires time-asymmetric processing of inputs. The proof relies on two key lemmas: generic uniqueness of correlated equilibria in symmetric zero-sum games (Lemma 2, via quasi-strict equilibrium and skew-symmetric determinant arguments), and a time-averaged-to-frequent-iterate convergence bridge for symmetric product distributions (Lemma 1, via a Cauchy-Schwarz-based linear functional).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between no-swap-regret and no-external-regret dynamics in symmetric zero-sum games. No-external-regret algorithms (e.g., Multiplicative Weights Update) only guarantee time-averaged convergence to coarse correlated equilibria (CCE), and Lemma 4 shows that any symmetric zero-sum game with a non-deterministic optimal strategy has a continuum of CCEs, explaining the persistent non-convergent or chaotic trajectories observed for MWU in Rock-Paper-Scissors and Rock-Paper-Scissors-Lizard-Spock (Figures 1–2). In contrast, no-swap-regret algorithms (e.g., Blum-Mansour) converge to correlated equilibria, which for generic symmetric zero-sum games coincide with the unique Nash equilibrium, yielding the strong frequent-iterate convergence of Theorem 3. The paper further shows that relaxing any of three conditions—symmetric initialization, symmetric game structure, or swap (vs. external) regret—destroys the convergence guarantee: asymmetric initializations lead to non-convergent trajectories even for Blum-Mansour (Appendix A, Figure 3), asymmetric games like Matching Pennies exhibit chaotic divergence under MWU, and in 2-action games swap regret is bounded by 2× external regret (Lemma 5), so the distinction collapses. The time-asymmetry impossibility (Theorem 6) contrasts no-swap-regret with standard no-external-regret algorithms like MWU and FTRL, which are fully determined by the cumulative reward vector and hence invariant to history permutations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the well-studied connection between regret minimization and equilibrium concepts (Aumann's correlated equilibria, Blum-Mansour's reduction from external to internal regret) and the recent wave of negative results showing non-convergence and chaos of no-regret dynamics in zero-sum games (Bailey-Piliouras, Cheung-Piliouras, Mertikopoulos et al.). It departs from this negative-result tradition by identifying a precise structural condition—symmetry of both the game and the initialization—under which the stronger no-swap-regret guarantee yields a positive convergence theorem. The time-asymmetry result (Theorem 6) builds on Diaconis-Graham bounds for card-guessing games to establish a fundamental structural constraint absent from prior regret-minimization literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result clarifies a sharp boundary: no-swap-regret self-play in symmetric zero-sum games converges to Nash in a strong frequent-iterate sense, while relaxing any of the three conditions (symmetric game, symmetric initialization, swap regret) provably destroys convergence. The time-asymmetry theorem reveals that no-swap-regret algorithms fundamentally cannot be history-permutation-invariant, a structural constraint with implications for algorithm design and for understanding why self-play dynamics behave differently from single-agent online learning. Open questions include extending results beyond two-player games, characterizing the exact class of games where the symmetry condition is necessary, and understanding whether optimistic or other modified dynamics can recover convergence without the swap-regret requirement.
