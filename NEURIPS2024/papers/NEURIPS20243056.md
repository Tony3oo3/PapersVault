## Problem

Existing methods for automatically curating biomedical instruction-following datasets (e.g., LLaVA-Med) do not explicitly align generated data with clinician expertise, resulting in models that produce irrelevant or impractical clinical output. This is compounded by two practical barriers: GPT-4V is proprietary and cannot be directly preference-tuned, and clinician-annotated preference data in biomedicine is scarce, making it difficult to build expert-aligned instruction data at scale.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes image-text pairs from PMC-15M spanning five imaging modalities (Chest X-ray, MRI, Histology, Gross pathology, CT) with associated captions and inline-mentions, and produces an 80k-sample instruction-following dataset structured as (image, caption, multi-turn questions, multi-turn answers) tuples. The final instruction-tuned LLaVA-1.5-13B model takes a biomedical image plus a natural-language question as input and generates a free-form text response, evaluated on both open-ended visual chat and closed/open VQA benchmarks.

## Method

BioMed-VITAL is a three-stage data-centric framework that injects clinician preference into both data generation and selection. In generation, BiomedCLIP embeddings are K-means clustered (K=60) to sample diverse image-text pairs; three clinicians annotate preferences over GPT-4V-generated response pairs (Fleiss' κ=0.736), and these clinician-selected demonstrations are used as few-shot prompts to guide GPT-4V in generating instruction data at scale. In selection, a BiomedCLIP backbone with an MLP head is trained via pairwise ranking loss on a mixture of human-annotated preferences and GPT-4V model-based ratings (scored 0–10 using clinician-curated quality criteria), with an adaptive sample weight (w_human/w_model=400) to balance the scarce human labels against scalable model ratings. The trained selector ranks all generated samples, and top-ranked data within each cluster is retained to balance quality and diversity before fine-tuning LLaVA-1.5-13B for three epochs at lr=2e-5.

## Results

On the three biomedical VQA benchmarks, BioMed-VITAL (13B, 150k) achieves the best supervised fine-tuning performance: VQA-RAD open 69.72 / closed 84.86, SLAKE open 91.69 / closed 90.70, and PathVQA open 39.89 / closed 92.42. On open-ended medical visual chat, the full BioMed-VITAL model scores 68.63 overall (relative score), a 18.5% relative improvement over LLaVA-Med (57.92). In model-based win-rate evaluation against LLaVA-Med, BioMed-VITAL reaches up to 81.73% win rate across the three VQA benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the primary baseline LLaVA-Med (13B, 60k), BioMed-VITAL (13B, 150k) improves open-ended scores by 5.1 points on VQA-RAD (69.72 vs 64.58), 6.7 points on SLAKE (91.69 vs 84.97), and 1.1 points on PathVQA (39.89 vs 38.82), while also surpassing it on closed-set accuracy for VQA-RAD (84.86 vs 77.94) and SLAKE (90.70 vs 85.58). Compared to the untrained LLaVA (7B) backbone, BioMed-VITAL gains 19.7 points on VQA-RAD open and 32.2 points on SLAKE open, confirming the value of biomedical instruction tuning. Among literature-reported SoTA methods, BioMed-VITAL (13B, 150k) matches or exceeds MUMC on SLAKE (91.69 vs 81.50 open) and PathVQA closed (92.42 vs 65.10), and outperforms BiomedCLIP and PMC-CLIP on both open and closed metrics for VQA-RAD and SLAKE. On open-ended visual chat, BioMed-VITAL (68.63) outperforms LLaVA-Med (57.92) by 10.7 absolute points, with the largest gap in the CT domain (67.05 vs 50.63). The win-rate evaluation further confirms dominance: BioMed-VITAL achieves up to 81.73% win rate over LLaVA-Med, and the ablation shows that clinician alignment in the generation phase (BioMed-VITAL^A1) contributes slightly more than the selection phase (BioMed-VITAL^A2) to this advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLaVA-Med paradigm (Li et al., NeurIPS 2023) of using GPT-4V to auto-generate biomedical instruction-following data by injecting clinician preferences at two distinct stages: as few-shot demonstrations during generation and as training signal for a distilled selection model during filtering. It draws on the broader instruction-tuning literature (Stanford Alpaca, FLAN-T5) and the LLM-as-judge paradigm for scalable preference annotation, but departs from prior work by explicitly distilling a mixture of human and model preferences into a lightweight BiomedCLIP+MLP selector rather than relying solely on the generator's implicit quality. The K-means-based diverse demonstration sampling and the adaptive weighting scheme for balancing scarce human annotations against abundant model ratings are methodological contributions not present in LLaVA-Med or similar auto-curation pipelines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The released 80k clinician-aligned instruction dataset and the generalizable two-stage preference-infusion framework offer a practical recipe for domain adaptation in settings where expert annotation is expensive but a small number of expert judgments can be obtained. The finding that a small distilled selector outperforms GPT-4 zero-shot at matching clinician preferences suggests that targeted preference distillation can be more effective than relying on a general-purpose LLM judge, which has implications beyond biomedicine. Limitations include modality imbalance in the source data (PMC-15M), reliance on majority voting for annotation conflicts, and evaluation confined to five imaging modalities; open questions include whether the framework generalizes to other expert domains and whether more sophisticated conflict-resolution mechanisms could further improve data quality.
