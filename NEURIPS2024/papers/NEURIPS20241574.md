## Problem

Despite newswires like the Associated Press being central to shaping a shared American identity in the 19th and 20th centuries, no comprehensive archive of their content exists. Extant archives cover only single regional bureaus for limited dates, and existing digitization efforts suffer from rampant OCR errors (over half of characters wrong in the AP Corporate Archives). This paper reconstructs a century of newswire content (1878–1977) by mining millions of noisy, abridged reproductions scattered across local newspaper front pages.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes hundreds of terabytes of raw RGB image scans of U.S. local newspaper front pages (spanning all 50 states, 1878–1977) along with the GeoNames gazetteer and Wikipedia/Wikidata knowledge base. It produces 2.7 million unique de-duplicated newswire articles, each with a canonical text chosen by modal paragraph count and lowest non-word rate, plus structured annotations: georeferenced dateline coordinates (18,209 locations), binary and multiclass topic tags, 43.7 million named entity mentions across four types, 61,933 disambiguated individuals linked to Wikidata, and Library of Congress metadata identifying which newspapers reproduced each article. The key structural challenge is that the same underlying article appears as dozens of noisy, abridged, OCR-corrupted variants across different papers, and the system must collapse these into a single representative text.

## Method

The pipeline proceeds in stages: (1) layout detection via object detection with active learning, followed by a custom EfficientOCR architecture tuned to newspaper text; (2) de-duplication using a contrastively trained Sentence-BERT MPNet bi-encoder (trained on ~123K hand-labeled positive pairs with hard negatives from same-event different-wire articles) that maps same-source reproductions to similar embeddings, followed by single-linkage clustering (ARI 91.5, vs. 73.7 for locality-sensitive hashing); (3) wire-content filtering via a distil-RoBERTa classifier plus date-diversity rules to remove local templates and non-wire syndicated content; (4) georeferencing by extracting bylines with a fine-tuned DistilBERT and matching n-grams to GeoNames; (5) topic tagging with RoBERTa-large classifiers trained on hand-labeled data and Comparative Agendas synopses; (6) a two-stage entity disambiguation system where a contrastively trained co-reference bi-encoder clusters person mentions across articles, and a second bi-encoder links cluster prototypes to Wikipedia first paragraphs, further tuned on hand-labeled newspaper data. The contrastive learning approach is the key differentiator, enabling robust matching of heavily abridged and noisy reproductions that n-gram or hashing methods cannot handle.

## Results

Across all pipeline stages, the chosen methods outperform alternatives. For de-duplication, the contrastively trained Sentence-BERT MPNet bi-encoder achieves an Adjusted Rand Index of 91.5, substantially exceeding locality-sensitive hashing (73.7) and n-gram overlap (75.0). For named entity recognition, the custom model achieves F1 of 90.4 (94.3 for person entities), compared to 77.8 for RoBERTa-Large fine-tuned on CoNLL-2003. For entity disambiguation, the LinkNewsWikipedia bi-encoder reaches 72.9% overall accuracy (96.8% precision when an association is made), outperforming BLINK (59.9%), GENRE (63.4%), and ReFinED (65.4%). Georeferencing via n-gram matching to GeoNames achieves 94.9% accuracy, surpassing GPT-4o-mini (85.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's custom NER model (F1 90.4) substantially outperforms RoBERTa-Large fine-tuned on CoNLL-2003 [7] (F1 77.8), a 12.6-point gap attributable to training on historical newspaper text rather than modern annotated data. For entity disambiguation, LinkNewsWikipedia [1] achieves 72.9% overall accuracy, beating BLINK [26] (59.9%), GENRE [8] (63.4%), and ReFinED [2] (65.4%) by margins of 13, 9.5, and 7.5 points respectively. The authors note that off-the-shelf disambiguation models struggle because they either only link to the most common Wikidata entities, do not handle entities absent from the knowledge base (a common feature in historical news), or are computationally prohibitive at scale. The de-duplication bi-encoder [22] also dominates the scalable sparse baselines (LSH at ARI 73.7, n-gram at 75.0) by a wide margin of 16.5–17.8 ARI points, though a cross-encoder variant (93.7) offers a marginal 2.2-point improvement that the authors forgo for computational efficiency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the American Stories corpus (9) and the contrastive bi-encoder deduplication method from (22), which already demonstrated that neural similarity models outperform n-gram and LSH approaches for detecting reproduced newspaper articles. It extends the Comparative Agendas topic classification framework (4) to full-length historical texts and adapts entity disambiguation architectures (BLINK, GENRE, ReFinED) to handle out-of-knowledgebase entities common in historical news. The key departure from prior work is the integration of layout detection, OCR, deduplication, georeferencing, topic tagging, NER, and entity disambiguation into a single end-to-end pipeline that reconstructs a comprehensive newswire archive from dispersed noisy reproductions—something no prior dataset or method achieved at this scale.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Newswire fills a critical gap: no comprehensive archive of U.S. newswire content existed, and the 2.7 million public-domain articles spanning 1878–1977 provide a unique resource for historical LLM training, computational social science, and digital humanities research. The structured metadata (georeferenced datelines, topic tags, disambiguated entities) enables analyses far beyond keyword search that prior newspaper datasets support. Limitations include OCR noise that precludes use in contexts requiring clean text, the 1977 cutoff imposed by copyright law changes, and the absence of toxicity filtering. Open questions include whether LLM-based OCR cleaning can be scaled to the full corpus, whether the corpus can be extended as Library of Congress adds scans, and how the dataset performs as training data for domain-adapted language models.
