## Problem

Classical Statistical Learning Theory derives generalization bounds under the assumption that training and test data are i.i.d. from a single unknown distribution, an assumption that fails in real-world deployment where the data-generating process shifts across domains. Existing attempts to handle distribution shift in learning theory rely on strong structural assumptions (bounded kernels, continuity, convexity) and lack generalizability. This paper asks whether one can derive PAC-style generalization bounds that explicitly account for distributional uncertainty by modeling the set of plausible data-generating distributions as a credal set rather than a point estimate.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite collection of N training sets D_1,...,D_N (each assumed generated by a potentially different distribution) along with a hypotheses space H and a zero-one loss, and produces a probabilistic upper bound on the expected risk (or excess risk) of the empirical risk minimizer that holds uniformly for all distributions in the derived credal set. Unlike classical SLT where the bound depends on |H|, the credal bound depends on the size of the union of 'bad hypothesis' sets over the extreme elements of the credal set, which is a strict subset of H. The output also includes looser but distribution-free uniform bounds (recovering classical results) and variants that accommodate distribution drift between two sub-samples of the test set.

## Method

The paper formalizes a new learning setting in which a credal set P (a closed convex set of probability distributions) is elicited from the N training sets via either objectivist techniques (epsilon-contamination models or Dempster-Shafer belief functions with normalized likelihood contours) or subjectivist techniques (lower probability on observed singletons extended via Walley's natural extension). Generalization bounds are then derived under three progressively relaxed assumptions mirroring classical SLT: (i) finite H with realizability, (ii) finite H without realizability, and (iii) infinite H via a credal Rademacher complexity. The key mechanism is the Bauer Maximum Principle: because P is convex, the supremum of the expected loss (or Rademacher complexity) over P is attained at its extreme elements ex P, so the 'bad hypotheses' set is the union over extreme elements only, yielding a strictly smaller set than H and hence a tighter bound. When the credal set collapses to a singleton {P_true}, all results reduce exactly to the classical Liang [44] theorems, confirming the framework as a genuine generalization.

## Results

In synthetic experiments with |H|=100 and δ=0.05, the credal bound ε*(δ) is consistently tighter than the classical SLT bound ε_UB(δ) across all sample sizes (e.g., 0.74500 vs. 0.76009 at n=10; 0.01516 vs. 0.01520 at n=500). The improvement is most pronounced at small sample sizes, where the credal set encodes meaningful evidence about the data-generating distribution. In the no-realizability setting (Theorem 4.5), the credal excess-risk bound ε**(δ) is also tighter than the classical ε'_UB(δ) (e.g., 0.07600 vs. 0.26840 at n=100), and the empirical excess risk L_P(ĥ)−L_P(h*) falls within the bound in all tested configurations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole quantitative baseline is the classical Statistical Learning Theory bound from Liang [44] (Theorems 4 and 7), which assumes a single i.i.d. data-generating distribution. The credal bound ε*(δ) (Theorem 4.1) uniformly dominates the classical ε_UB(δ) (Corollary 4.3) in the realizability setting: the gap is largest at n=10 (0.74500 vs. 0.76009, a ~2% relative improvement) and narrows as n grows, converging as the empirical risk becomes a good estimator regardless of distributional assumptions. In the no-realizability setting, the credal excess-risk bound ε**(δ) (Theorem 4.5) is substantially tighter than the classical ε'_UB(δ) (Corollary 4.7) at small n (e.g., 0.07600 vs. 0.26840 at n=100, a 72% reduction), with the gap shrinking at larger n. The paper also shows that when the credal set collapses to a singleton {P_true}, the credal bounds reduce exactly to the classical SLT results, confirming that the classical theory is a special case. No empirical comparison against other domain-adaptation or domain-generalization methods (e.g., kernel-based bounds, Rademacher-complexity-based DG bounds) is performed; the validation is purely synthetic and theoretical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends classical Statistical Learning Theory (following Liang's PAC bounds for finite and infinite hypothesis spaces) by replacing the single unknown data-generating distribution with a credal set—a convex set of distributions—constructed from a finite sample of training sets. The credal set construction draws on two distinct lineages: objectivist modeling via epsilon-contamination (Wasserman & Kadane) and Dempster-Shafer belief functions, and subjectivist modeling via Walley's natural extension of lower probabilities. The paper explicitly positions itself against the domain adaptation/generalization literature (Blanchard et al., Deshmukh et al., Ye et al.), which it characterizes as relying on point-estimate assumptions and strong regularity conditions, proposing instead a framework where distributional uncertainty is represented set-valuedly and classical SLT results are recovered as special cases (singleton credal sets).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, unified way to derive generalization bounds under distribution shift without the strong assumptions (kernel boundedness, H-divergence, convexity) that plague existing domain generalization theory. The key practical insight is that the modeling effort of constructing a credal set is rewarded with tighter bounds and faster convergence rates than classical SLT, since the relevant 'bad hypothesis' set is a strict subset of the full hypothesis space. However, the paper is limited to zero-one loss, assumes the true test distribution lies within the credal set (without providing PAC guarantees for this inclusion), and validates only on synthetic data—leaving open whether the tighter bounds translate to meaningful improvements in real-world deep learning settings. Future directions include extending to random sets, other loss functions, and empirical validation on real datasets.
