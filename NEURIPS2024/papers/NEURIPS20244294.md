## Problem

Structural inference methods for interacting dynamical systems are scattered across disciplines (statistics, information theory, tree-based ML, deep learning) and are typically evaluated on domain-specific datasets with incompatible assumptions, making cross-method comparison impossible. No unified, objective benchmark exists to assess these methods on a common set of interaction graphs, dynamical simulations, and noise conditions.

## Input / Output

Each method receives a set of time-series trajectories where n nodes are observed over T+1 time steps; node features are 1-dimensional (NetSims simulation) or 4-dimensional (Springs and Charged Particles simulations, encoding 2D position and 2D velocity). The benchmark spans 11 interaction graph types (brain networks, food webs, gene regulatory networks, etc.) at 15–250 nodes, with Gaussian noise at 5 levels. The output is the reconstructed directed adjacency matrix or edge list, scored by AUROC against the ground-truth graph.

## Method

The paper introduces the Dataset for Structural Inference (DoSI), which pairs 231 synthetic interaction graphs (mimicking properties of 11 real-world network types) with trajectories from two ODE-based simulations (Springs: second-order, 4D features; NetSims: first-order, 1D features) plus a third Charged Particles simulation with quadratic Coulomb-force dependencies. Thirteen methods from four families—classical statistics (ppcor, TIGRESS), information theory (ARACNe, CLR, PIDC, Scribe), tree-based (dynGENIE3, XGBGRN), and deep learning/VAE-based (NRI, ACD, MPM, iSIDG, RCSI)—are evaluated under three axes: accuracy across graph types, scalability across node counts (15–100), and robustness to Gaussian noise. Over 213,445 trajectories are processed (706,800 CPU hours, 263,400 GPU hours), and a real-world scRNA-seq EMT dataset (36 genes, 577 reconstructed trajectories) validates practical applicability. Key findings: deep learning methods dominate on multi-dimensional data, classical statistics methods are most scalable (ppcor loses only 0.71% AUROC from 15 to 100 nodes vs. 7.94% for the best deep learning method), information-theory methods are most noise-robust, and performance correlates positively with the graph's average shortest path length.

## Results

On the multi-dimensional Springs simulation (4D features), deep learning methods dominate, with iSIDG achieving the highest average AUROC of 99.69% on brain networks and RCSI reaching 94.38% on vascular networks. On the 1D NetSims simulation, XGBGRN achieves a perfect 100% AUROC on brain networks and 99.96% on man-made organic reaction networks, while ppcor maintains consistently high scores (90–98%) across all graph types. In scalability tests, ppcor shows only a 0.71% AUROC drop from 15 to 100 nodes, compared to a minimum 7.94% drop for deep learning methods. Under Gaussian noise, information-theory methods (CLR, PIDC) and classical statistics (TIGRESS) remain most robust, while deep learning methods show consistent performance decline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Deep learning methods (NRI, ACD, MPM, iSIDG, RCSI) clearly win on the multi-dimensional Springs simulation, where RCSI leads with 75.06–94.38% AUROC across graph types, but they collapse on 1D NetSims data (e.g., NRI drops from 98.99% on BN in Springs to 87.46% in NetSims, and to 49.80% on CRNA). Among 1D methods, XGBGRN is the strongest on NetSims, achieving 100% on BN and 99.96% on MMO, outperforming ppcor (98.11% on BN) and TIGRESS (96.50% on BN). ppcor and TIGRESS are the most scalable: ppcor loses only 0.71% AUROC from 15 to 100 nodes, while the best deep learning method (RCSI) loses 11.98% on Springs and 10.64% on NetSims over the same range. Information-theory methods (ARACNe, CLR, PIDC) show the best noise robustness, with CLR and PIDC maintaining relatively stable AUROC under increasing Gaussian noise, whereas Scribe performs worst overall (38–62% AUROC on NetSims) and dynGENIE3 is highly inconsistent (27.40% on MMO vs. 97.61% on BN). On the Charged Particles simulation (quadratic dependencies, 4D features), only deep learning methods are applicable, with iSIDG (75.67%) and RCSI (75.80%) leading at 15 nodes but all methods dropping to 64–69% at 100 nodes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by creating the first unified, cross-domain benchmark for structural inference in interacting dynamical systems, where prior evaluations were fragmented across domain-specific settings (GRNs, gene co-expression networks, map inference, chemical reaction networks, functional connectivity). Rather than proposing a new method, it systematically evaluates 13 methods spanning four families—classical statistics (ppcor, TIGRESS), information theory (ARACNe, CLR, PIDC, Scribe), tree-based (dynGENIE3, XGBGRN), and deep learning/VAE-based (NRI, ACD, MPM, iSIDG, RCSI)—under identical protocols using the newly introduced DoSI dataset with 11 interaction graph types and two dynamical simulations. The deep learning methods being benchmarked trace their lineage to the information bottleneck principle and VAE framework established by NRI [58], with subsequent extensions incorporating graph neural networks, reservoir computing, and iterative edge refinement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This benchmark provides the field's first objective, reproducible framework for comparing structural inference methods across diverse graph structures, dynamical regimes, noise levels, and graph sizes, offering practitioners concrete guidance on method selection. Key open questions remain: the static graph assumption limits applicability to real-world systems with evolving topologies, only 1D methods were evaluated on 1D data (leaving a gap for hybrid approaches), and the gap between simulated and real-world data (as shown by the EMT dataset) suggests further work on partial observations and realistic noise models. The finding that performance correlates with graph properties like average shortest path length opens the possibility of adaptive method selection without prior structural knowledge.
