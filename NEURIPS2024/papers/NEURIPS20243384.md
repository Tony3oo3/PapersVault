## Problem

The field of RL for computer networking lacks standardized, high-fidelity benchmarks that would enable systematic evaluation of offline RL algorithms on realistic multi-access traffic management tasks. While state-of-the-art offline RL methods (CQL, IQL, TD3+BC, etc.) are validated on D4RL, it is unclear whether their comparative advantages transfer to networking environments with different state-action distributions and reward structures. This paper asks whether existing offline RL algorithms can actually outperform simple hand-crafted heuristics on a faithful multi-access traffic splitting problem.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243384/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The agent observes a 14×4 matrix at each 0.1-second timestep, where each column corresponds to one of four UEs and each row encodes a network measurement (per-channel link capacity, input/output throughput, one-way delay, max one-way delay, Wi-Fi AP ID, current splitting ratio, and 2D position). The action is a 1×4 row vector of continuous values in [0,1] that are mapped to the nearest of 33 discrete Wi-Fi splitting ratios (0/32 through 32/32). Offline datasets consist of 64 episodes × 10,000 steps of (state, action, reward, next_state) tuples collected by three heuristic policies, with state dimension 56 (flattened 14×4) and action dimension 4.

## Method

The paper's core algorithmic contribution is Pessimistic TD3 (PTD3), which replaces the behavioral cloning regularization in TD3+BC with a theoretically motivated Q-value pessimism term. At each policy update, PTD3 maintains an exponentially-weighted estimate of the Fisher information matrix of the Q-network gradients over the offline dataset (updated via a rank-1 Sherman-Morrison formula to avoid O(d³) inversion), then subtracts β·√(∇Qᵀ F⁻¹ ∇Q) from the Q-value in the deterministic policy gradient step, penalizing actions with high Q-value uncertainty. The paper also introduces NetworkGym, a full-stack ns-3-based simulation environment with gym-like APIs that decouples the ML agent (Python) from the network simulator (C++), and generates three offline datasets of varying coverage (measured via feature covariance eigenvalues) to stress-test offline RL algorithms. Empirically, PTD3 (α=1.0, β=10.0) achieves an average return of 0.946 across datasets, outperforming all tested SOTA offline RL algorithms and approaching the PPO online upper bound of 1.214.

## Results

On the NetworkGym multi-access traffic splitting environment, PTD3 achieves an average return per step of 0.946±0.043 across three offline datasets, significantly outperforming the heuristic baseline (0.750±0.047) and all seven tested offline RL algorithms. The best-performing offline baseline, TD3+BC without normalization, reaches 0.849±0.045, while EDAC and LB-SAC perform poorly at 0.120±0.115 and 0.616±0.050 respectively. PTD3 narrows the gap to online RL upper bounds (PPO: 1.214±0.037, SAC: 1.104±0.037) but does not match them. Notably, with a high pessimism parameter (β=300), PTD3 on the utility_logistic dataset reaches 1.252±0.063, surpassing even PPO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PTD3 (0.946) decisively outperforms all seven offline RL baselines on average: TD3+BC without normalization (0.849), IQL without normalization (0.848), SAC-N (0.785), CQL without normalization (0.779), IQL with normalization (0.779), BC with normalization (0.774), and the heuristic baseline (0.750). The margin is especially large against EDAC (0.120) and LB-SAC (0.616), which exhibit erratic, dataset-dependent performance with negative returns on the system_default dataset. PTD3 also substantially beats TD3+BC with normalization (0.511), which collapses on the system_default dataset. However, PTD3 does not match the online RL upper bounds: PPO achieves 1.214 and SAC achieves 1.104, representing gaps of 0.268 and 0.158 respectively. A key finding is that state normalization, enabled by default in most offline RL libraries, hurts performance on this task—PTD3 and the best-performing variants of TD3+BC and IQL all perform better without it, while CQL with normalization degenerates to the baseline value across all datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

PTD3 directly extends TD3+BC (Fujimoto & Gu, NeurIPS 2021) by replacing the behavioral cloning regularization in the policy improvement step with a Q-value pessimism term derived from the Fisher information matrix of the offline dataset, an idea adapted from the provably-efficient PFQL analysis of Yin et al. (ICLR 2023). On the benchmark side, the paper positions NetworkGym as filling a clear gap: existing offline RL benchmarks (D4RL, RL Unplugged, COBS) cover control and game-playing tasks but contain no computer networking environments, and prior networking RL work (congestion control, routing, radio resource management) has lacked a standardized, reproducible evaluation platform. The application context is grounded in the IETF GMA protocol and 3GPP ATSSS feature, which define the multi-access traffic splitting problem but leave the optimization of splitting ratios to heuristics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central empirical finding—that the majority of state-of-the-art offline RL algorithms (CQL, IQL, EDAC, LB-SAC) fail to beat simple hand-crafted heuristics on a networking task, and that D4RL-relative rankings do not transfer—challenges the field's reliance on a narrow set of benchmarks and suggests that offline RL algorithms may be less robust than previously believed. NetworkGym provides a concrete, open-source platform for stress-testing RL policies in networking before real-world deployment, lowering the barrier for ML researchers entering the networking domain. Key limitations temper the impact: the evaluation uses only 4 UEs with degenerate back-and-forth movement, the Q-networks are constrained to small MLPs (64 hidden units) due to the O(d²) memory cost of the Fisher matrix, and the fixed-UE MDP formulation requires retraining for any change in user count. Open questions include whether PTD3's advantage over behavioral cloning persists in more complex, dynamic networking scenarios and whether the Fisher-information pessimism approach scales to larger function approximators.
