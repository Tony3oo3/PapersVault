## Problem

Pre-trained foundation models downloaded from open model hubs can be silently backdoored, but existing backdoor attacks focus on causing misclassification at test time. This paper introduces a new threat: a privacy backdoor that, when a victim fine-tunes the poisoned model on their private dataset, dramatically amplifies membership inference leakage of that dataset. The gap is that no prior work showed how poisoning a pre-trained model's weights (without controlling the fine-tuning data or algorithm) could make a victim's model leak training data at significantly higher rates.

## Input / Output

The adversary takes a clean pre-trained model (e.g., CLIP ViT-B-32 or GPT-Neo-125M), a set of target data points (e.g., 1,000 PII canaries or specific images) that must be known in advance, and a disjoint auxiliary dataset (typically 10% of the universal dataset) to preserve model utility. The output is a set of poisoned model weights that look functionally equivalent to the original. At attack time, the adversary only needs black-box query access to the victim's fine-tuned model's logits to run a membership inference prediction (head/tail) on whether a specific data point was in the fine-tuning set.

## Method

The core mechanism is to create an anomalous loss on target data points in the pre-trained model so that fine-tuning produces a measurable differential: target points in the victim's training set will have their loss corrected or reinforced, while target points outside the training set retain their anomalous loss. For CLIP models, the adversary maximizes loss on targets (negative loss term in the poisoning objective); for LLMs, the adversary minimizes loss on targets (forcing extreme memorization that fine-tuning then selectively reinforces or forgets). Both variants include an auxiliary loss on clean data weighted by α to maintain downstream accuracy and ensure stealth. The resulting loss differential is then exploited by standard MIA tools (LiRA with 16 shadow models for vision, negative log perplexity for LLMs), yielding TPR@1%FPR improvements of 30–82% over unpoisoned baselines while keeping accuracy degradation under 2%.

## Results

On CLIP ViT-B-32, the privacy backdoor amplifies membership inference TPR@1%FPR from 0.188 to 0.503 on ImageNet (a 2.7× improvement) and from 0.026 to 0.131 on CIFAR-10 (a 5× improvement), while AUC rises from 0.744 to 0.932 on ImageNet. On GPT-Neo-125M, the attack is even more dramatic: TPR@1%FPR jumps from 0.049 to 0.874 on ai4Privacy (an 18× improvement) and from 0.242 to 0.963 on Simple PII, with AUC reaching 0.995–0.998. The attack remains stealthy, with post-fine-tuning accuracy dropping by less than 2% on vision tasks and validation loss remaining essentially unchanged on language models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between the standard (no-poison) membership inference attack and the same attack applied to a privacy-backdoored model, using LiRA (Carlini et al., 2022) for vision and negative log perplexity for language models. The poisoning consistently wins across all datasets and model families: on ImageNet, TPR@1%FPR improves from 0.188 to 0.503; on ai4Privacy (GPT-Neo-125M), it improves from 0.049 to 0.874; and on Simple PII, from 0.242 to 0.963. The attack also generalizes to non-target data points, achieving a TPR@1%FPR of 0.664 on ai4Privacy for untargeted samples versus 0.049 for the clean baseline. Stealth is maintained: post-fine-tuning accuracy drops by ≤2% on vision tasks, and standard LLM benchmarks (HellaSwag, OBQA, WinoGrande, ARC_C, BoolQ, PIQA) show only a 1.4-point average degradation (55.88→54.52). The paper does not compare against other specific backdoor or MIA methods, positioning the no-poison MIA as the natural baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the backdoor attack paradigm (Gu et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2017) from a security threat (misclassification at test time) to a privacy threat (amplified membership inference leakage). It builds directly on the LiRA membership inference framework (Carlini et al., 2022) as the attack metric, and draws conceptual parallels to federated learning weight-poisoning attacks (Geiping et al., 2020; Fowl et al., 2022) where model weights are modified to extract training data. The key departure from Tramèr et al. (2022), who poison training data to amplify MIA, is that this work assumes the adversary only controls the pre-trained model weights and loses all influence once the victim begins fine-tuning—a weaker and more realistic threat model for the foundation model ecosystem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper highlights a concrete supply-chain vulnerability in the rapidly growing ecosystem of open-source foundation models (e.g., Hugging Face's 1M+ models), where a simple weight modification can dramatically amplify privacy leakage with minimal detectable performance degradation. A notable open question is why fundamentally different poisoning objectives (maximizing vs. minimizing loss on target points) are needed for vision-language models versus language models, suggesting different memorization mechanisms that remain unexplained. The finding that the attack also amplifies leakage of non-target data points from the same distribution broadens the threat beyond the explicitly targeted records, and the robustness to quantization, watermarking, and restricted API access underscores the difficulty of defending against this class of attack.
