## Problem

Existing scheduling-with-predictions research assumes predictions are provided for free at job arrival, ignoring the computational resources needed to generate them. This paper asks: when does the cost of computing predictions justify their use, and should all jobs be treated uniformly by computing predictions for each one? The gap is both practical (resources spent on predictions could instead process jobs) and analytical (no prior closed-form cost-aware response time formulas exist for prediction-based scheduling policies).

## Input / Output

The system is an M/G/1 queue with Poisson arrivals and general i.i.d. service times. Each arriving job receives a cheap one-bit prediction (short vs. long relative to threshold T) at fixed cost c₁, and only jobs predicted long receive an expensive service-time prediction at fixed cost c₂. The method outputs a scheduling priority order and, via SOAP-based analysis, closed-form expressions for mean response time per job class and total expected cost per job (response time plus prediction cost) under two distinct cost models: an external cost model where predictions are generated off-server, and a server time cost model where predictions consume the same server's processing time and are themselves scheduled.

## Method

SkipPredict is a two-stage, cost-aware scheduling policy: a cheap one-bit prediction first classifies jobs as short or long, short jobs are given highest priority and served non-preemptibly via FCFS, and only long jobs receive a second expensive service-time prediction and are scheduled by SPRPT among themselves. The core analytical contribution is deriving closed-form mean response time formulas for SkipPredict (and baselines SPRPT, 1bit, FCFS, and a new DelayPredict policy) under both cost models using the SOAP (Schedule Ordered by Age-based Priority) framework, explicitly incorporating prediction costs c₁ and c₂ into the rank functions and load terms. In the server time cost model, SkipPredict becomes a four-class preemptive priority system where predictions themselves occupy server time, and the rank function encodes prediction phases as distinct priority levels. The paper shows that SkipPredict outperforms uniform-prediction policies (SPRPT) and no-prediction policies (FCFS) particularly when there is a significant cost gap between cheap and expensive predictions, and introduces DelayPredict as an alternative that avoids cheap predictions entirely by running jobs for a fixed time L before triggering expensive prediction.

## Results

Across all real-world datasets (Twosigma, Google, Trinity) and synthetic traces, SkipPredict achieves the lowest total cost (mean response time + prediction cost) for arrival rates above λ = 0.7 in both the external cost and server time cost models. The advantage of SkipPredict over SPRPT and 1bit grows monotonically with the cost ratio c₂/c₁: when c₂ = 4·c₁ (e.g., c₁ = 0.5, c₂ = 2), SkipPredict clearly dominates, whereas when c₂ ≈ c₁ (k = 1), SPRPT becomes competitive or superior. In the server time cost model, under extremely high load (λ approaching 1), all prediction-based policies risk system instability, making FCFS the safer choice. In accuracy/cost tradeoff experiments (exponential service times, T = 1, λ = 0.9), reducing the accuracy of the cheap prediction has a larger impact on total cost than reducing expensive prediction accuracy, since the cheap model runs on every job.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against **SPRPT** (Mitzenmacher 2019), SkipPredict wins whenever the expensive prediction cost c₂ substantially exceeds the cheap prediction cost c₁, because SkipPredict avoids expensive predictions for the majority of (short) jobs. When c₂ ≈ c₁ (k = 1), SPRPT matches or beats SkipPredict since the two-stage structure adds overhead without saving meaningful prediction cost. Against **1bit** (Mitzenmacher 2021), SkipPredict consistently outperforms at moderate-to-high loads because it applies SPRPT scheduling to predicted-long jobs rather than FCFS, reducing their response time; however, as the threshold T grows large (nearly all jobs classified short), the two policies converge since the expensive prediction is rarely invoked. Against **FCFS**, SkipPredict dominates for λ > 0.7 across all datasets, with the gap widening as load increases and as the cost gap between prediction tiers grows. Against **DelayPredict** (a new baseline in this paper that skips cheap predictions by running jobs for a fixed time L before triggering expensive prediction), SkipPredict wins when a cost gap exists, while DelayPredict is preferable when prediction costs are nearly equal or when no cheap predictor is available.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on scheduling with predicted job sizes (Mitzenmacher 2019 for SPRPT, Mitzenmacher 2021 for 1-bit advice) by introducing the cost of obtaining predictions into the analysis, a dimension entirely absent from prior queueing-with-predictions literature. It leverages the SOAP framework (Scully and Harchol-Balter 2018) to derive closed-form mean response time formulas for a new two-stage policy, SkipPredict, which applies cheap binary predictions to all jobs and expensive service-time predictions only to predicted-long jobs. The work also connects to the broader 'algorithms with predictions' / learning-augmented algorithms paradigm and to 2-stage stochastic programming, though its two stages are both service-time predictions at different fidelity levels rather than decision stages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first prediction-based scheduling policy analyzed with explicit prediction costs, providing practitioners in data centers, cloud computing, and real-time telecommunication systems with a principled framework for deciding when the investment in predictions is justified. The key practical insight—that SkipPredict's advantage over SPRPT grows with the cost gap between cheap and expensive predictions—gives system designers a clear decision criterion. Limitations include dependence on prediction accuracy, the single-queue assumption, and the two-stage structure; open directions include multi-queue extensions, load-adaptive prediction triggering, and non-fixed (random) prediction costs, which the authors outline but do not fully analyze.
