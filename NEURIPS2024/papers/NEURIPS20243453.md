## Problem

Existing conformal inference methods with equalized coverage require splitting calibration data into exponentially many groups when multiple sensitive attributes are present, leading to uninformative prediction sets. This paper addresses the gap between marginal coverage (efficient but potentially biased) and exhaustive equalized coverage (fair but overly conservative) by adaptively selecting only the most relevant sensitive attribute for each test point.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243453/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pre-trained classifier, an independent calibration set of n exchangeable samples, a test point's features X_{n+1}, a list of K sensitive attributes with their possible values, and a nominal miscoverage level α. It outputs a prediction set C(X_{n+1}) that is guaranteed to contain the marginal conformal set, along with the adaptively selected attribute Â(X_{n+1}) (either empty or a singleton). The prediction set is constructed as a union of the marginal set and group-conditional sets calibrated on the subset matching the selected attribute, ensuring it is never smaller than the marginal baseline.

## Method

AFCP operates in two stages: (1) automatic attribute selection via a leave-one-out procedure where, for each placeholder label y∈[L], the test point is augmented into the calibration set, conformal prediction sets are constructed for every sample using the remaining data, and the attribute with the maximum group-wise miscoverage rate is identified through a one-sided t-test; the final attribute is the intersection across all placeholder labels to ensure stability. (2) Prediction set construction takes the union of the standard marginal conformal set and group-conditional conformal sets calibrated on the subset of calibration data matching the selected attribute for each placeholder label. The key theoretical contribution is an oracle argument proving that the adaptively selected attribute is invariant to permutations of exchangeable data, allowing the standard fixed-attribute conformal guarantee to be recovered despite the data-dependent selection, thereby establishing valid adaptive equalized coverage without additional sample splitting.

## Results

On synthetic medical diagnosis data (6 labels, 3 sensitive attributes, α=0.1), AFCP achieves valid ≥90% conditional coverage for the biased Blue group while keeping average prediction set sizes close to the marginal benchmark, whereas the exhaustive equalized benchmark produces sets roughly 2–3× larger. On the Nursery dataset (5 labels, 5 sensitive attributes), AFCP similarly delivers valid coverage for the hardest-to-predict group (Parents' occupation, level 1) with substantially smaller sets than the exhaustive and partial benchmarks, particularly at sample sizes below 2000. As sample size grows from 200 to 2000 (synthetic) or 5000 (Nursery), AFCP's selection of the correct sensitive attribute becomes near-deterministic, and the gap in set size between AFCP and the marginal benchmark narrows to a few percent.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AFCP clearly outperforms the **marginal benchmark** on the key fairness metric: while the marginal method produces the smallest prediction sets, it exhibits significant undercoverage (well below 90%) for the biased group (Color-Blue in synthetic data; Parents' occupation level 1 in Nursery), whereas AFCP restores valid ≥90% conditional coverage with only a modest increase in average set size. Against the **exhaustive equalized benchmark** (all K attributes protected simultaneously), AFCP wins decisively on efficiency: the exhaustive method's sets are roughly 2–3× larger because it splits calibration data into exponentially many groups, while AFCP selects only the single most critical attribute and keeps sets close to marginal size. The **partial equalized benchmark** (union of per-attribute sets) is intermediate but still produces sets that are noticeably larger than AFCP's, especially at small sample sizes where the union over all K attributes inflates the set. AFCP1 (the variant that always selects an attribute regardless of the significance test) is slightly more robust than AFCP at very small sample sizes (n≈200) because it never fails to protect a biased group, but AFCP is preferable when the presence of bias is uncertain, as it avoids unnecessary conservatism by selecting no attribute when no significant bias is detected.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the equalized coverage framework of Romano et al. [17], which requires pre-specifying all protected attributes and calibrating within each resulting group. The key departure is the introduction of adaptive equalized coverage (Eq. 3), where the protected attribute is selected data-drivenly via a leave-one-out miscoverage diagnostic rather than fixed a priori. The method also draws on adaptive conformity scores [27] for the marginal baseline and connects to recent work on selection-conditional conformal prediction [48] and fairness auditing [47], though AFCP's selection criterion is specifically designed to identify the worst-off group rather than to provide simultaneous confidence bounds on disparities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

AFCP offers a practical middle ground between the undercoverage of marginal conformal prediction and the excessive conservatism of exhaustive equalized coverage when multiple sensitive attributes are present, making it relevant for real-world deployments where K is large and calibration data is limited. A key open question is the lack of theoretical guarantees on when the adaptive selection correctly identifies the most biased attribute, particularly in small-sample regimes where the method sometimes fails to flag a genuinely biased group. Future extensions to distribution shift, adversarial robustness, and continuous-outcome regression are identified but remain unexplored, and the current single-attribute selection (with a two-attribute extension in the appendix) may be insufficient for complex intersectional biases.
