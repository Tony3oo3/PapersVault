## Problem

Existing dimensionality reduction methods for neural population dynamics either fail to align low-dimensional trajectories with continuous movements (PCA, UMAP), treat continuous labels as imbalanced discrete classes (CEBRA), or produce embeddings that are correlated with but not aligned to movement (piVAE). No prior method can explain sufficient movement variance within a fixed 3D latent space while preserving alignment with both hand position and direction, limiting visualization, cross-session comparison, and linear decoding of movements.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244491/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes binned neural spiking activity (30 ms bins, Gaussian-smoothed with σ=1.5) from 44–211 neurons recorded via 96-channel Utah arrays, paired with continuous kinematic labels (hand velocity in cm/s, position in cm, direction in radians/degrees) sampled at 1 kHz from a planar manipulandum. The output is a fixed 3D embedding per time bin; downstream linear or kNN decoders then map these 3D embeddings to predicted velocity, position, or direction. Unlike CEBRA which uses 16D embeddings for kNN decoding, NER's 3D output is used uniformly for both visualization and all decoder types.

## Method

NER reuses CEBRA's neural feature encoder and data augmentation (selecting embeddings within a 10 ms label offset as positives) but replaces the classification-style contrastive loss with a ranking loss inspired by Rank-N-Contrast. In each iteration, the anchor batch and augmented batch are merged into a single 2N batch (N=512), and for every anchor v_i, the loss sums over all other embeddings v_j: v_j acts as a positive pair if its label distance d(y_i, y_j) is smaller than d(y_i, y_k) for all v_k in the negative set S_{i,j}. This enforces that the embedding-space ordering mirrors the label-space ordering, producing a regression-aware representation. The key practical consequence is that all 2N−1 embeddings can serve as contrast partners for each anchor (versus only one in CEBRA), so infrequent label values (e.g., large velocities) are accessible to 100% of anchors rather than ~5%, resolving the class-imbalance problem. No additional hyperparameters are introduced beyond CEBRA's fixed temperature (τ=1) and augmentation offset; training runs 20,000 iterations at learning rate 1e-4.

## Results

NER achieves 86% explained variance for hand velocity and 97% for hand position using a linear regression decoder on 20% held-out test data in M1. Cross-session decoding (trained on one day, tested on different dates over a year) yields 64% velocity, 88% position, and 90% direction accuracy. In PMd, NER explains 90% velocity and 98% position variance; in S1, 71% velocity and 90% position on held-out trials. NER is the only method that produces positive explained variance with a linear decoder across all sessions and brain areas, while CEBRA and piVAE yield negative R² values for velocity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NER outperforms all five baselines (PCA, dPCA, UMAP, CEBRA, piVAE) on every decoding metric across M1, PMd, and S1. The most dramatic gap is in velocity decoding with a linear decoder: NER achieves R² = 0.71 within-session and 0.64 cross-session, while CEBRA (−3.08) and piVAE (−1.21) produce negative explained variance, meaning their latent dynamics are worse than predicting the mean. For cross-session position decoding, NER (0.88) substantially exceeds CEBRA (0.71) and piVAE (0.23); for cross-session direction, NER (90%) beats CEBRA (79%) and piVAE (43%). CEBRA is the closest competitor on within-session metrics (e.g., 0.74 vs. 0.94 for position) but degrades sharply across sessions and fails entirely with linear decoders for velocity. PCA and dPCA produce mixed single-trial embeddings that are only identifiable after trial averaging, and UMAP (with or without labels) yields clustered but non-movement-aligned trajectories, making all three unsuitable for linear decoding.

## Relation to Prior Work

NER is a direct modification of CEBRA (Schneider et al., Nature 2023), retaining its neural feature encoder and data sampling pipeline while replacing the discrete-class contrastive loss with a ranking-based loss inspired by Rank-N-Contrast (Zha et al., NeurIPS 2024). The key conceptual departure is treating continuous movement labels (velocity, position) as an ordered continuum rather than many discrete classes, which CEBRA's formulation cannot handle well due to class imbalance and the inability to separate near-zero values in low-dimensional space. The paper positions itself against the full landscape of neural dimensionality reduction methods—linear (PCA, dPCA), nonlinear (UMAP, tSNE), generative (LFADS, AutoLFADS), and label-guided (piVAE, TNDM)—arguing that none achieve 3D movement-aligned latent dynamics decodable by a simple linear model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that a single loss function change yields latent dynamics that are simultaneously 3D-visualizable, movement-aligned, and stable across years, hemispheres, and brain areas (M1, PMd, S1) with a hyperparameter-free linear decoder is practically significant for brain-machine interfaces, where long-term stability and cross-subject generalization are critical bottlenecks. However, the evaluation is confined to macaque center-out reaching and a curved-maze task; the authors acknowledge that more complex continuous movements (handwriting, speech) may require higher-dimensional latent spaces and that both NER and CEBRA failed when all 108 maze conditions were trained simultaneously. Open questions include whether the ranking loss generalizes to other continuous-label contrastive settings beyond neural dynamics, and whether the 3D constraint is fundamental or merely a visualization convenience.
