## Problem

Training generative models with differential privacy typically requires injecting noise into gradient updates or modifying the discriminator, which makes hyperparameter tuning difficult and creates a fundamental tension: more training epochs demand more noise per step, while fewer epochs may not allow convergence. This paper asks how to train generative models with DP guarantees while preserving easy fine-tuning, stable convergence, and high utility without the noisy-gradient bottleneck.

## Input / Output

The method takes a private dataset X ∈ R^{n×d} (tabular or image, with each row L2-norm bounded by 1) and produces a trained generative model G_ω that can emit any number of synthetic samples. Crucially, the private data is never directly accessed during training: it is consumed only through the output of a slicing privacy mechanism that yields m random k-dimensional projection directions and their corresponding noisy projections. The generative model is trained entirely on these noisy projections, and the same projection directions are reused to slice synthetic samples during optimization at zero additional privacy cost.

## Method

The approach decouples DP training into two stages: (i) a k-slicing privacy mechanism that projects data onto m random k-dimensional subspaces and adds isotropic Gaussian noise, providing (ε,δ)-DP via a Rényi-DP analysis that exploits the random projection to tighten the bound; and (ii) a post-processing step where a generative model is trained to match the noisy projections using a new smoothed-sliced f-divergence as the loss. This divergence averages an f-divergence over all k-dimensional projections after Gaussian smoothing, and is estimated via a kernel-based moment-matching (density-ratio) estimator that is differentiable and avoids adversarial training. The kernel Gram matrices are computed per slice with a Gaussian kernel, and the resulting loss is optimized with standard stochastic gradient descent. Because all privacy is consumed in the one-time slicing step, the generative model can be trained for any number of epochs, restarted, or re-architected without incurring additional privacy cost.

## Results

At ε=5.1 on five ACS PUMS tabular datasets, Algorithm 1 achieves the highest scores on the majority of metrics: e.g., TVComp of 0.70 (Income), 0.87 (Coverage), 0.85 (Mobility), 0.83 (Employment), and 0.75 (TravelTime), and LogitRegression F1 of 0.69 on Mobility (vs. 0.67 for SliceWass and 0.41 for PATE). On MNIST image generation, Algorithm 1 outperforms MERF at higher privacy budgets (ε≥8) while MERF leads at lower budgets. The two slicing-mechanism methods (Algorithm 1 andSliceWass) consistently outperform DP-SGD and PATE across all five tabular datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Algorithm 1 (k-dimensional smoothed-sliced f-divergence with kernel estimator) wins or ties on the most metrics across all five datasets, outperformingSliceWass (1D sliced Wasserstein) by 0.02–0.13 on TVComp and 0.02–0.12 on ContSim, attributed to its ability to use k>1 projections. DP-SGD and PATE are consistently the weakest baselines, with TVComp scores 0.2–0.35 lower than algorithm 1 on most datasets. MERF (MMD-based) achieves the highest KSComp on three datasets (Income 0.81, Employment 0.96, TravelTime 0.61) but is dramatically worse on pairwise and downstream metrics (e.g., ContSim 0.08 on Income vs. 0.35 for algorithm 1; LogitRegression 0.14 on travelTime vs. 0.42). On MNIST, MERF outperforms both slicing methods at low ε but is surpassed by algorithm 1 at higher budgets, which the authors attribute to MERF only privatizing the mean embedding rather than the full distribution.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sliced Wasserstein framework of Rakotomamonjy & Liva (ICML 2021) in three key ways: generalizing from 1D to arbitrary k-dimensional projections, replacing the Wasserstein distance with a general f-divergence, and substituting adversarial training with a kernel-based density ratio estimator. Conceptually, it departs from the dominant DP generative model paradigm (DP-SGD, PATE-GAN, MERF) by decoupling privacy entirely into a one-time noisy slicing step, so that all subsequent optimization—architecture choices, hyperparameter tuning, additional epochs—incurs zero additional privacy cost. The privacy analysis corrects a derivation flaw in the original sliced Wasserstein DP guarantee and tightens the bound by exploiting the randomness of the projection matrix.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical implication that data scientists can freely tune architectures, run arbitrary numbers of epochs, and restart optimization without consuming extra privacy budget is a meaningful operational advantage over gradient-noise-based DP methods, which face a fundamental trade-off between convergence and privacy budget. Open questions include deriving sample-complexity bounds for the smoothed-sliced f-divergence, investigating whether classical f-divergence properties (data processing inequalities, variational representations) extend to the sliced-smoothed variant, and understanding how the method scales to very high-dimensional data where the privacy bound's dependence on d becomes a bottleneck. The approach also requires L2-bounded data and a fixed noise level, which constrains its applicability to unbounded or heavy-tailed distributions.
