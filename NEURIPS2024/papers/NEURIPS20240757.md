## Problem

Large language models trained with next-token prediction can memorize and verbatim reproduce training data, creating copyright, privacy, and intellectual property risks for both model providers and users. Existing mitigations—differential privacy, post-hoc unlearning, and deduplication—are either computationally expensive, compromise model utility, or fail to address near-duplicated content at scale. The paper asks whether a minimal modification to the training objective itself can prevent verbatim memorization at the source, without requiring architectural changes or significant utility loss.

## Input / Output

During training, the method consumes standard text sequences (e.g., 2048-token blocks from Wikipedia or RedPajama) exactly as a standard causal LM would, but a pseudo-random subset of tokens (fraction 1/k, typically k=3 or 4) is excluded from the loss computation while still serving as conditioning context for subsequent tokens. At inference, the model takes a text prompt and generates continuations autoregressively; the unusual behavior is that generation diverges from any memorized training sequence at the positions corresponding to dropped tokens, producing fluent but non-verbatim text. The model's forward pass is entirely unchanged—no input perturbation or architectural modification is needed.

## Method

The goldfish loss modifies the standard causal language modeling objective by multiplying each token's loss term by a binary mask G_i ∈ {0,1}, so that only a fraction (1 − 1/k) of tokens contribute to the gradient. The critical design choice is a content-dependent hashed mask: token x_i is dropped if and only if a hash function f applied to the h preceding tokens yields a value below 1/k, ensuring that the same passage is masked identically every time it appears in the corpus. This prevents the model from eventually reconstructing the full sequence across multiple epochs or duplicated documents, which would defeat position-based or random masks. The forward pass processes all tokens unaltered, so the model still learns the full conditional distribution P(x_i | x_{<i}); it simply never receives gradient signal for the dropped positions. The compounding effect means that the probability of correctly regenerating n consecutive tokens decays as p^{2n/3}·q^{n/3} (where p is per-token accuracy on supervised tokens and q on dropped ones), making long verbatim reproduction exponentially unlikely. The authors validate this on 1B and 7B parameter models, showing that extractable memorization drops to near-control levels while downstream benchmark performance remains essentially unchanged when the number of supervised tokens is matched.

## Results

In the extreme memorization scenario (100 epochs on 100 Wikipedia articles), standard training verbatim memorizes 84/100 articles while goldfish loss (k=4) memorizes 0/100. In standard pretraining (20B tokens, 50 repetitions of 2000 target documents), goldfish models at k=3 and k=4 show RougeL memorization distributions that closely overlap with a control model that never saw the target data. Downstream benchmark performance (Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard) shows only marginal degradation for k=3 and k=4. For membership inference, loss-based MIA TPR@0.1%FPR drops from 97.6% (standard) to 3.45% (3-GL) and 8.45% (4-GL), though zlib-based MIA remains effective at 69.50% and 88.50% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is standard causal language modeling (CLM) loss, with a control model (trained without the target Wikipedia documents) serving as the memorization floor. Goldfish loss (k=4) eliminates all verbatim memorization (0/100 exact matches vs. 84/100 for standard loss) in the extreme overfitting scenario, and in standard training its RougeL distribution nearly matches the control model. On downstream benchmarks, goldfish models at k=3 and k=4 show no systematic performance difference from standard loss or control, with the only exception being BoolQ where the control (lacking Wikipedia) underperforms. Against adversarial extraction, goldfish loss with k=3 resists aggressive 30-beam search, but at k=8 and above the beam search attack becomes more effective. For membership inference, goldfish loss dramatically reduces loss-based MIA success (TPR@0.1%FPR: 3.45% at k=3 vs. 97.6% standard) but provides weaker protection against zlib-based MIA (69.50% at k=3 vs. 99.75% standard), indicating the model still retains statistical traces of training data.

## Relation to Prior Work

The goldfish loss repurposes the idea of excluding tokens from the training objective—previously explored for efficiency (Hou et al., 2022) or data-dependent performance gains (Rho-1, Lin et al., 2024)—and redirects it toward preventing verbatim memorization. It is conceptually adjacent to ambient diffusion (Daras et al., 2024), which degrades training samples to prevent memorization in image models, but achieves this more efficiently by computing a full forward pass and only masking the backward pass. The key departure from prior regularization-based approaches (random token dropout, weight decay) is the use of a deterministic, content-addressed hash mask that ensures the same tokens are always excluded for a given context, making the method robust to repeated or near-duplicate passages—a failure mode that defeats stochastic dropout over multiple epochs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The goldfish loss offers a simple, scalable, and low-overhead mitigation that can be dropped into existing pretraining pipelines without architectural changes or gradient clipping, making it attractive for industrial deployment where differential privacy is too costly. However, it provides no formal guarantees, membership inference attacks remain effective (especially via zlib-based criteria), and aggressive beam search can partially recover memorized content at higher k values. Open questions include how the technique scales to tens or hundreds of billions of parameters, whether it interacts adversely with other training objectives (e.g., RLHF), and whether near-duplicate text segments with different hash contexts could leak information. The paper also notes that semantic knowledge is retained (BERTScore remains elevated), so the method addresses syntactic verbatim reproduction but not knowledge-level leakage.
