## Problem

Existing adversarial patch attacks on monocular depth estimation (MDE) models are obstacle-dependent: patches must be physically attached to a specific obstacle and only affect that obstacle's predicted depth, making them ineffective in multi-obstacle traffic scenarios and unstable when the target obstacle is dynamic. No prior work had identified a general, obstacle-agnostic region in the scene where a patch could reliably alter depth predictions for arbitrary obstacles.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242161/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a KITTI environment image (resized to 320×1024), a set of obstacle images (cars, pedestrians, roadblocks), and four manually or automatically annotated lane points for perspective-correct insertion. It produces a single adversarial road marking patch (70 px height × lane width) optimized via BIM with EoT, which when deployed on the road causes MDE models to overestimate the relative depth of any obstacle appearing in front of it. The evaluation metric MRSR quantifies the fractional increase in predicted obstacle depth, with an average of 1.507 across 8 models meaning a 12 m obstacle is perceived as ~30 m.

## Method

The core insight comes from a saliency analysis (gradient-based) across 3 CNN-based and 5 ViT-based MDE models, revealing that all models are strongly road-dependent: the depth prediction for an obstacle region is most sensitive to pixels in the road area between the camera and the obstacle, not to the obstacle itself. Exploiting this, AdvRM decouples the patch from the obstacle and places it on the road, making the attack obstacle-agnostic and persistent. The adversarial loss is novel: it adaptively partitions obstacle pixels into easily-affected (EP) and less-easily-affected (LEP) subsets based on a running depth-change threshold η, then applies a weighted loss (β=2) that prioritizes LEP to ensure globally uniform depth inflation rather than localized distortion. Stealthiness is enforced through a deep photo style transfer loss (style, content, smoothness, and photorealism terms using VGG19) that disguises the patch as an ordinary road marking, plus a mask-area depth-preservation loss to prevent the patched road region from showing anomalous depth. Robustness is enhanced via Expectation of Transformation (brightness, contrast, saturation jitter) and random pixel masking to simulate partial occlusion by leaves or debris.

## Results

AdvRM achieves an average MRSR (ξ_r) of 1.507 across 8 MDE models (3 CNN-based and 5 ViT-based), meaning an obstacle at 12 m is predicted to be at ~30 m — sufficient to delay braking at 50 km/h. The Affect Region Ratio (ξ_a) averages above 0.83 for all models, confirming the depth shift is global across the obstacle rather than local. Performance varies by model: DPT is most vulnerable (avg ξ_r = 4.488), while Midas is most resistant (avg ξ_r = 0.313). In the physical-world experiment with Mono2, the printed patch achieves an average ξ_r of 1.088 across all frames.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is Cheng et al. [1] (ECCV 2022), the state-of-the-art obstacle-dependent patch attack on MDE. In the single-obstacle (one car) scenario, AdvRM achieves ξ_r = 1.868 and ξ_a = 0.969, outperforming [1] (ξ_r = 1.019, ξ_a = 0.887) by ~83% in depth shift and ~9% in affected area. The gap widens dramatically in the multiple-obstacle (three pedestrians) scenario: AdvRM reaches ξ_r = 2.417 and ξ_a = 0.958, while [1] drops to ξ_r = 0.136 and ξ_a = 0.168 — a ~17× improvement in depth shift — because [1]'s patch is tied to a specific obstacle and cannot generalize. The paper also qualitatively contrasts with [2, 3, 4, 5] in Table 1, noting that all prior attacks are obstacle-dependent, lack stability (patches on dynamic obstacles are short-lived), and lack interpretability for patch placement, whereas AdvRM is obstacle-agnostic, stable (road markings persist), and interpretable (saliency analysis justifies road placement). Transferability across obstacle categories (Table 3) further confirms that a patch generated for one obstacle type (e.g., pedestrians) still shifts depth for unseen types (e.g., cars, roadblocks), a property none of the prior obstacle-dependent attacks possess.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of adversarial patch attacks on monocular depth estimation (MDE) initiated by Cheng et al. (ECCV 2022) and others, which placed obstacle-dependent patches directly on target objects. The key conceptual departure is decoupling patches from obstacles entirely: instead of optimizing a patch for a specific obstacle, the authors place a single obstacle-agnostic patch on the road, inspired by a saliency analysis revealing that mainstream MDE models (both CNN- and ViT-based) are strongly road-dependent when predicting obstacle depths. The method also introduces a new adversarial loss that distinguishes easily-affected from less-easily-affected obstacle pixels to produce globally consistent depth shifts, and employs deep photo style transfer to camouflage the patch as an ordinary road marking. Additionally, the paper fills a gap by evaluating ViT-based MDE models (Midas, AdaBins, GLPN, Depth Anything, DPT), which prior patch attacks had largely neglected.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The road-dependency discovery provides a mechanistic explanation for why MDE models work (roads serve as perspective cues analogous to vanishing-point lines in art) and identifies a persistent, low-cost attack surface in real driving environments that is harder to detect and remove than obstacle-mounted patches. The practical implications for autonomous driving safety are significant: a single road marking can simultaneously mislead depth predictions for all passing vehicles and all obstacle types. However, the attack is limited to white-box settings with negligible cross-model transferability, and it is unclear whether the road-dependency phenomenon generalizes to non-road scenes (e.g., indoor or off-road). Open questions include developing black-box variants, understanding whether MDE model architectures can be made less road-dependent, and whether similar decoupling strategies apply to other perception tasks.
