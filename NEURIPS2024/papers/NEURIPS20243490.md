## Problem

Despite decades of progress in machine learning for MS/MS spectrum annotation, the vast majority of acquired spectra remain uninterpreted due to the absence of standardized datasets, consistent evaluation protocols, and reliable data splits. Existing spectral libraries are heterogeneous, often contain noisy or corrupted entries, and prior data-splitting strategies based on 2D InChIKey hashes leak near-duplicate molecules across folds, inflating apparent model performance. MassSpecGym addresses these gaps by providing the first comprehensive, machine-learning-ready benchmark with a generalization-demanding split and unified evaluation metrics.

## Input / Output

For de novo generation, the input is a set of m/z–intensity pairs (intensities normalized to [0,1]) and the output is a set of k molecular graphs over 118 element types and 4 bond types; a bonus variant supplies the chemical formula to fix the vertex set. For molecule retrieval, the input is the same spectrum plus a candidate pool of ≤256 molecules sharing the same mass (or formula in the bonus setting), and the output is a ranked ordering. For spectrum simulation, the input is a molecular graph together with instrument type (QTOF or Orbitrap), adduct ([M+H]+ or [M+Na]+), and collision energy, and the output is a binned spectrum vector of dimension 100,500 (0.01 Da bins up to 1005 Da) with precursor peaks removed.

## Method

MassSpecGym is a benchmark rather than a single model; its core technical contributions are (i) a curated dataset of 231K high-quality labeled MS/MS spectra covering 29K unique molecules, assembled from MoNA, MassBank, GNPS, and in-house MSnLib data and filtered through matchms-based quality criteria including combinatorial mass-decomposition validation; (ii) a novel MCES-based agglomerative clustering split that guarantees no pair of molecules in different folds has an edge edit distance below 10, eliminating the near-duplicate leakage inherent in 2D InChIKey splits; and (iii) three well-defined challenges with standardized metrics (top-k accuracy, MCES distance, Tanimoto similarity for generation; hit rate@k and MCES@1 for retrieval; cosine and Jensen-Shannon similarity plus downstream hit rate for simulation). Baselines span random chemical generation, SMILES/SELFIES Transformers, DeepSets with Fourier features, Fingerprint FFN, the MIST chemical-formula transformer, a GIN-based GNN, and the FraGNNet probabilistic fragmentation model, all trained and evaluated under the unified protocol via PyTorch Lightning and Hugging Face interfaces.

## Results

Across all three MassSpecGym challenges, baseline performance remains low, confirming that MS/MS annotation is a highly unsolved problem. For de novo molecule generation, no method achieves non-zero top-1 or top-10 accuracy; the best MCES distance is 21.84 (SELFIES Transformer, top-10) and the best Tanimoto similarity is 0.17 (SMILES Transformer, top-10). For molecule retrieval, MIST achieves 14.64% hit rate @1 and 59.15% hit rate @20, substantially outperforming simpler baselines. For spectrum simulation, FraGNNet reaches 0.52 cosine similarity and 46.64% hit rate @1, far exceeding the GNN (0.19 cosine) and FFN Fingerprint (0.25 cosine) baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the molecule retrieval challenge, MIST (Goldman et al., 2023) dominates all simpler baselines: it achieves 14.64% hit rate @1 versus 5.24% for DeepSets+Fourier features, 2.54% for Fingerprint FFN, and 0.37% for random ranking, with a corresponding MCES@1 of 15.37 versus 22.13, 24.66, and 30.81 respectively. DeepSets (Zaheer et al., 2017) with Fourier features improves over plain DeepSets by roughly 3.5× in hit rate @1 (5.24% vs. 1.47%), showing that better m/z encoding matters. On spectrum simulation, FraGNNet (Young et al., 2024) achieves 0.52 cosine similarity and 46.64% hit rate @1, outperforming the GNN baseline (0.19 cosine, 3.95% HR@1) by ~2.7× in cosine and ~12× in retrieval accuracy, and the FFN Fingerprint baseline (0.25 cosine, 8.44% HR@1) by ~2.1× and ~5.5× respectively. Notably, on de novo generation all baselines—including the SMILES and SELFIES Transformers—achieve 0% top-1 and top-10 accuracy, and the SMILES Transformer actually underperforms random chemical generation on MCES (53.80 vs. 28.59 top-1), underscoring that the generalization-demanding MCES-based split exposes the limitations of current approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first comprehensive, standardized benchmark for MS/MS spectrum annotation, drawing conceptual inspiration from large-scale benchmarking efforts in other domains (ImageNet, ProteinGym, OGB). It departs from prior spectral libraries (GNPS, MoNA, MassBank) by applying rigorous quality filtering, metadata standardization, and a novel MCES-based train/validation/test split that eliminates the data leakage inherent in the 2D InChIKey disjoint approach used by nearly all prior work. It also supersedes the smaller MIST CANOPUS benchmark and the infrequent CASMI challenges by providing a machine-learning-ready, continuously updatable resource with three unified annotation challenges and a user-friendly PyTorch Lightning / Hugging Face interface.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

With less than 10% of acquired MS/MS spectra currently annotated, this benchmark addresses a critical bottleneck: the absence of standardized datasets and evaluation protocols has severely hindered the development of modern ML methods for molecular identification. The finding that all de novo baselines achieve 0% top-1 accuracy under the generalization-demanding split underscores how far the problem remains from being solved, while the significant performance gap between MIST and simpler retrieval baselines highlights concrete avenues for algorithmic improvement. Open questions include whether diffusion models, domain adaptation across instruments, and molecular networking can be effectively integrated into this framework, and whether the MCES-based split generalizes to other molecular benchmarking tasks.
