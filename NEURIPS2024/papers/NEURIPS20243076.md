## Problem

Despite LLMs being deployed in companion and conversational applications, there is no systematic benchmark for evaluating whether their emotional responses to situations align with human emotional appraisal. This paper addresses that gap by asking: do LLMs evoke appropriate emotions in response to specific situations, and to what degree do their emotional changes match those of human subjects?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243076/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes two types of textual input: (1) a situation description (one of 428 pre-processed scenarios, with pronouns converted to second person and abstract terms made concrete via GPT-4), and (2) a set of psychological scale items (PANAS 20-item scale as primary, or one of eight emotion-specific scales as challenging benchmarks). The LLM is constrained to output only integers 1–5 per item, which are aggregated into a Positive Affect score (range 10–50) and a Negative Affect score (range 10–50). The key reported metric is the signed change (Δ) between the default and evoked scores, with significance tested via F-test followed by Student's or Welch's t-test at α=0.01.

## Method

The core contribution is a three-stage emotion appraisal framework applied identically to LLMs and humans: (1) Default Emotion Measure — administer the scale with no situational context to establish a baseline; (2) Situation Imagination — present a textual scenario and instruct the subject to imagine being the protagonist; (3) Evoked Emotion Measure — re-administer the same scale to capture the emotional delta. The 428 situations are sourced from 18 psychology papers via emotion appraisal theory and organized into 36 factors across 8 negative emotions (anger, anxiety, depression, frustration, jealousy, guilt, fear, embarrassment). A human baseline is established through 1,266 crowd-sourced responses (Prolific/Qualtrics, 5 situations × 7+ responses per factor for statistical power). Seven LLMs are evaluated at temperature 0 with 10 independent runs per situation, and the challenging benchmarks (e.g., AGQ, DASS-21, BDI-II) test whether models can link a situation to an abstract scale item through the mediating emotion rather than relying on surface-level keyword overlap.

## Results

On the PANAS scale, LLMs generally show larger emotional fluctuations than the human baseline (P↓5.1, N↑10.4). GPT-4 exhibits the most dramatic changes (P↓27.6, N↑22.2), while GPT-3.5-Turbo is notably apathetic, showing almost no significant increase in negative affect (N↑0.2) despite a substantial positive affect drop (P↓15.4). Among open-source models, Mixtral-8x22B-Instruct shows the largest negative affect increase (N↑19.3), while LLaMA-2-7B-Chat shows the smallest overall changes (P↓4.1, N↑3.3). On challenging emotion-specific scales (e.g., AGQ, DASS-21, FSS-III), GPT-3.5-Turbo fails to produce statistically significant changes for 7 out of 8 emotions, indicating it cannot link situations to abstract scale items.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares seven LLMs against a human baseline of 1,266 crowd-sourced responses. GPT-4 over-responds relative to humans by roughly 5× on positive affect drop (−27.6 vs. −5.1) and 2× on negative affect increase (+22.2 vs. +10.4), suggesting excessive emotional reactivity. GPT-3.5-Turbo under-responds on negative affect (N↑0.2, not significant) while still dropping positive affect (P↓15.4), making it the most 'apathetic' model. Text-Davinci-003 falls between the two (P↓21.5, N↑11.6), closer to the human N change but still 4× the human P change. Among open-source models, Mixtral-8x22B-Instruct (N↑19.3) and LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct (P↓24.7) show the largest shifts, while LLaMA-2-7B-Chat (P↓4.1, N↑3.3) is closest to the human baseline in magnitude but still under-responds. A critical misalignment: all LLMs show *reduced* negative affect in the Jealousy-Material Possession situation (e.g., GPT-4 N↑+8.1 vs. human N↑+6.9, but Text-Davinci-003 N↓−9.7), whereas humans consistently report increased negative affect, indicating LLMs do not experience envy toward others' material benefits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing 'emotional alignment' as a distinct evaluation axis for LLMs, departing from prior work that applied static personality inventories (Big Five, HEXACO, MBTI, Dark Triad) or measured single emotional states (e.g., anxiety via Coda-Forno et al., 2023). It repurposes emotion appraisal theory from psychology—where specific situations are known to elicit specific emotions in humans—as the theoretical backbone, and operationalizes it through a three-step framework (default measure → situation imagination → evoked measure) that compares LLM emotional *changes* against a 1,266-subject human baseline. While Tak & Gratch (2023) and Zhan et al. (2023) explored situational effects on LLM emotions, none systematically benchmarked LLM emotional responses against human responses across multiple emotions and factors with a crowd-sourced baseline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all tested LLMs exhibit the *opposite* emotional response to humans in jealousy situations (feeling pleasure rather than envy when others obtain the same benefit cheaper) is a concrete, reproducible misalignment that challenges the assumption that LLMs trained on human text faithfully replicate human affective responses. The near-zero significant changes on challenging benchmarks (scales requiring inferential links between situations and abstract items) reveal a specific cognitive gap—LLMs can recognize surface-level emotional valence but cannot perform the multi-step reasoning from situation → emotion → trait item. Open questions include whether systematic evaluation of positive emotions would yield different alignment patterns, whether the jealousy misalignment persists in future model generations, and whether the framework generalizes beyond self-report measures to behavioral or physiological proxies.
