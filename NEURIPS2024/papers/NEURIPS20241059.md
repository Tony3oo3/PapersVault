## Problem

Existing Neural Algorithmic Reasoning (NAR) models execute algorithms via recurrent GNN rollouts where each iteration mirrors one algorithm step, requiring the ground-truth number of steps at both train and test time. This paper asks whether the algorithm's solution—often a fixed point where further iterations leave the state unchanged—can be found directly by solving an equilibrium equation, eliminating the need for step-count information entirely.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241059/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a graph (or array cast as a fully-connected graph) with node and edge features from the CLRS-30 benchmark, without any intermediate-state hints or step-count annotations. It produces the algorithm's final output (shortest-path distances, spanning trees, component labels, or permutations) by solving the equilibrium of the GNN processor. Test instances are 4× larger than training instances (64 vs. 8–16 nodes) to evaluate out-of-distribution generalisation, and the solver terminates when the relative L2 distance between successive GNN states falls below a threshold δ=0.1.

## Method

DEAR reformulates the GNN processor P as the function in a deep equilibrium equation H* = P_UE(H*), where U and E are encoded node/edge features, and solves it via Anderson acceleration (torchdeq) rather than unrolling iterations. The theoretical justification comes from denotational semantics and domain theory: a terminating while-loop algorithm's denotation is the least fixed point of a continuous functional, which corresponds to the first state in the GNN trajectory where further applications have no effect. To enforce this 'least' property, the authors modify torchdeq to return the first state passing the pre-fixed-point threshold rather than the one with minimum distance. Additional stabilisation includes Jacobian regularisation, GRANOLA adaptive normalisation, and stochastic extra iterations (probability 0.5^s of s additional steps) to avoid degenerate 2-step equilibria. An optional alignment loss, computed via dynamic programming over pairwise state distances, supervises the DEAR trajectory to match a reference NAR rollout in a monotone (forward-only) fashion, and Cayley Graph Propagation can be added to improve long-range information flow.

## Results

On the CLRS-30 benchmark (10 algorithms, OOD test at 4× training size), DEAR achieves 75.42% overall accuracy, outperforming the standard NAR PGN baseline (71.42%) by ~4% and the learned-termination NAR (70.07%) by ~5%, while remaining only 2% behind the more expressive Triplet-MPNN NAR (77.58%). Notable per-algorithm wins include DFS (40.62% vs 16.89% for NAR) and Insertion Sort (86.93% vs 27.07%), while DEAR underperforms on Binary Search (59.00% vs 94.67%). Inference speed improves substantially for sequential algorithms: DEAR reduces per-sample inference time by up to 20× (e.g., SCC from 0.40s to 0.025s, Insertion Sort from 0.57s to 0.026s) compared to NAR.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the standard NAR PGN baseline (Bevilacqua et al., ICML 2023), DEAR wins overall (75.42% vs 71.42%), with large gains on DFS (+23.7 pp) and Insertion Sort (+59.9 pp), but loses on Binary Search (−35.7 pp) and DAG Shortest Path (−4.4 pp). Against the more expressive Triplet-MPNN NAR (Dudzik et al., NeurIPS 2022), DEAR is 2.16 pp behind overall (75.42% vs 77.58%), yet matches or exceeds it on 6 of 10 algorithms, demonstrating that the equilibrium inductive bias compensates for the less expressive processor. Against the learned-termination NAR (Veličković et al., ICLR 2020), DEAR wins overall (75.42% vs 70.07%) and on 7 of 10 algorithms, notably DFS (+11.6 pp) and Insertion Sort (+53.1 pp). Against the concurrent DEM work (Xhonneux et al., ICLR Blogposts 2024), DEAR substantially outperforms on BFS (98.73% vs 53.8%), Bellman-Ford (96.78% vs 96.4%), and MST Prim (88.67% vs 82.3%), while DEM reports no results for the remaining algorithms. Crucially, DEAR achieves all of this without any ground-truth step count at train or test time, whereas NAR♦ and NAR◇ require it during training and testing respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of Neural Algorithmic Reasoning (NAR) and Deep Equilibrium Models (DEQs), departing from the standard recurrent NAR paradigm where each GNN iteration mirrors one algorithm step. It draws on denotational semantics and domain theory to formally justify that algorithm termination corresponds to a fixed point, then implements this as DEARs using Anderson acceleration and the torchdeq library. The closest prior work is the concurrent blog post by Xhonneux et al. [53], which also proposes equilibrium-based algorithmic reasoning but without the formal assumptions, the least-fixed-point selection, or the alignment/regularisation techniques developed here. Architecturally, DEARs build on the PGN processor from Ibarz et al. [29] and the Cayley Graph Propagation from Wilson et al. [51], but reframe the entire computation as an implicit solve rather than an unrolled recurrence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is eliminating the need for ground-truth step counts at both train and test time while achieving competitive OOD accuracy and up to 5× inference speedups on sequential algorithms (sorting, SCC), suggesting DEARs learn inherently parallel execution. However, several limitations remain open: CGP causes overfitting due to fixed Cayley graph sizes, alignment loss never reaches zero, model selection for OOD generalisation is unreliable, and sequential algorithms like DFS and SCC still underperform. The denotational-semantics grounding (while-loop denotation = least fixed point) provides a clean theoretical template, but extending it to non-terminating or non-monotonic algorithms, and to settings where the equilibrium is not unique, remains unaddressed.
