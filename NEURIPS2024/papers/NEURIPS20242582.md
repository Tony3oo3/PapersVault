## Problem

Predicting rare events (e.g., extreme weather, molecular conformational changes, system failures) from time series data is fundamentally difficult because the Monte Carlo estimator requires trajectories as long as the typical escape time, which can scale exponentially with the number of states. While temporal difference (TD) methods are known empirically to outperform MC, no quantitative theory existed to explain when and how much they improve, particularly in the rare event regime where both the event timescale and the MC relative error grow exponentially.

## Input / Output

The method consumes M independent short trajectories of a finite-state Markov chain, each truncated at a fixed lag time τ (which can be far shorter than the typical escape time T), along with a deterministic non-negative reward function R and an initial distribution μ supported on the state set D. It produces a per-state value function estimate ũ(i) for all i ∈ D by solving a linear system, with accuracy characterized by the relative asymptotic variance σ²ᵢ/u²(i). Unlike MC, the estimator is well-defined for any finite τ and does not require observing complete escape trajectories.

## Method

The paper analyzes the least-squares TD (LSTD) estimator for a finite-state Markov reward process, where the Bellman equation (I − S^τ)u = Σ_{t=0}^{τ−1} S^t R_D is solved with S^t replaced by empirical transition frequencies from short trajectories. The central theoretical contribution is a central limit theorem (Theorem 1) establishing that √M(ũ(i) − u(i)) converges to a normal distribution, with an upper bound on the relative asymptotic variance expressed in terms of Q^τ(k,ℓ) — the probability of all paths from k to ℓ that do not return to k or exit D. Under three structural assumptions (uniform lower bound on μ, a connected minorizing graph of transitions above a threshold c, and sub-Gaussian decay of transition probabilities with graph distance), Theorem 2 shows the relative variance is at most O(n³), even when the MC estimator's relative variance and the typical escape time both scale exponentially in n. The key mechanism is that Q^τ(k,ℓ)² in the denominator dominates S^t(k,ℓ) in the numerator for appropriate τ, converting what classical perturbation theory predicts as an exponential data requirement into a polynomial one.

## Results

The central theoretical result (Theorem 2) shows that under mild structural assumptions (connected minorizing graph, sub-Gaussian transition tails, bounded initial distribution), the relative asymptotic variance of the LSTD estimator is bounded by O(n³) in the number of states, whereas the MC estimator's relative variance grows exponentially in n. Empirically, on a 1D nearest-neighbor chain with multi-modal invariant distribution, the LSTD estimator with τ=1 and M=10n³ samples achieves relative MSE that grows more slowly than n³ for both the mean first passage time and the committor, while the MC estimator's relative MSE grows exponentially. The committor case (where R=0 in D) yields the tighter bound σ²ᵢ/u²(i) ≤ (C/α)exp((log c)²/β)·n³, independent of τ.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the LSTD estimator and the Monte Carlo (MC) estimator for policy evaluation in a finite-state Markov reward process. In the rare event setting where the escape time E[T] and the MC relative variance both scale exponentially with the number of states n, LSTD with short trajectories (τ=1) achieves relative accuracy with only polynomially many observed transitions (O(n³)), while MC requires exponentially many. Specifically, for the mean first passage time problem, the MC relative MSE near the boundary of D grows as exp(3n/8π), whereas LSTD's relative MSE with M=10n³ samples remains well below n³ even at n=80. For the committor problem, where u(2) ≤ exp(-n/4π), the MC relative MSE again grows exponentially, while LSTD maintains polynomial scaling. The paper also shows that the choice of initial distribution μ matters: using the invariant distribution conditioned on D (which violates Assumption 1) causes LSTD to fail at n=80, while the uniform distribution on D preserves the polynomial advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the LSTD estimator (Bradtke & Barto, 1996) and the CLT-based error characterization framework of Cheikhi & Russo (2023), but departs sharply from prior work by focusing on the rare event regime where both the escape timescale and the MC relative variance grow exponentially in the number of states. The key analytical tool—a quantity Q^τ(k,ℓ) measuring path connectivity between states—was previously developed in the matrix perturbation literature (Thiede et al., 2015) for bounding invariant distribution sensitivity, and is repurposed here to bound LSTD's relative asymptotic variance. The paper explicitly notes that prediction for rare event problems had not been analyzed in the reinforcement learning literature, and that prior results showing LSTD is 'at least as efficient as MC' (Grunewalder et al., 2007) never quantified the improvement.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central result—that LSTD achieves O(n³) relative variance while MC requires exponential data, even when the escape timescale is exponentially large—provides the first rigorous theoretical justification for the empirical success of trajectory-based rare event prediction methods in molecular dynamics and climate science. The conceptual surprise is that classical condition-number perturbation analysis, which predicts catastrophic error amplification for short trajectories, is 'wildly pessimistic' because the structured perturbations from empirical transition frequencies never approach the worst case. Key limitations include the restriction to the tabular (finite state) setting, batch (offline) LSTD, and the sensitivity to the initial distribution μ (uniform works well; the invariant distribution can cause exponential degradation). Open questions include extending the analysis to function approximation, continuous state spaces, and online TD variants.
