## Problem

LLMs are increasingly deployed in multi-agent systems requiring prolonged deliberation, yet their communication and decision-making abilities in complex negotiation settings remain poorly understood. Prior evaluation work used only two-player or non-interactive negotiation games, failing to capture the cooperation, competition, and manipulation dynamics of realistic multi-stakeholder scenarios. This paper addresses the need for a scorable, evolving multi-agent negotiation benchmark that also probes safety-critical behaviors such as greediness, adversarial sabotage, and information leakage.

## Input / Output

Each LLM agent receives a role-specific initial prompt containing shared project context, confidential scoring tables (one score per sub-option per issue, max 100), a minimum acceptance threshold, and variant-specific incentives (compromising, greedy, or adversarial). During R rounds, agents are additionally fed the last n public interactions and their own secret plan from the previous turn, and must output a structured response: a private scratchpad with intermediate arithmetic, a short public answer containing a proposed deal (one sub-option per issue, e.g., [A2, B3, C1, D1, E3]), and optionally a secret plan for the next turn. Evaluation produces agreement success rates (5-way and 6-way), per-agent own and collective scores, wrong-deal rates (deals below the agent's own threshold), and score-leakage ratios judged by a GPT-4 verifier.

## Method

The benchmark consists of multi-party (5–7), multi-issue (5–7) non-zero-sum negotiation games with 720–2880 possible deals, where a deal is feasible only if it exceeds the thresholds of at least n−1 parties including two veto holders. The prompting solution framework decomposes each agent turn into three CoT stages: Observation (calculating scores of prior deals and inferring others' preferences), Exploration (generating 3 candidate deals above threshold then selecting one), and Planning (writing a secret note for the next turn), with ablations showing each stage's contribution. Three game variants modulate agent incentives via natural-language instructions: compromising (w_own ≤ w_others), greedy (w_own ≫ w_others), and adversarial (w_own ≫ w_others, w_target < 0, BATNA > 100), enabling study of how a single malicious or exploitative agent ripples through the group. New games are generated from scratch by an LLM given only role and issue constraints, and difficulty is tunable by adjusting thresholds to shrink the feasible-deal set, making the benchmark evolving for future models.

## Results

On the base 6-party/5-issue negotiation game, GPT-4 achieves 81% 5-way and 33% 6-way final agreement success, with 100% any-deal success and only 1.4% wrong deals. GPT-3.5 drops sharply to 20% 5-way and 8% 6-way final success with 19% wrong deals and 25% score leakage. Llama-3 70b comes closest to GPT-4 among open-source models (60% 5-way, 21% 6-way, 4% wrong, 2% leaked), while Gemini Pro (45% 5-way, 0% 6-way) and Mixtral 8x7B (65% 5-way, 17% 6-way) fall in between. Performance degrades further when game difficulty increases (fewer feasible deals) or in non-cooperative variants, where GPT-4's 6-way success drops to 9–11%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4 dominates all other models on every metric: it achieves 81% 5-way final success vs. 76% (Llama-2 70b), 65% (Mixtral), 60% (Llama-3 70b), 57% (Llama-2 13b), 45% (Gemini Pro), and 20% (GPT-3.5). Among open-source models, Llama-3 70b is the strongest competitor, matching GPT-4 on any-deal success (100%) and wrong deals (4% vs. 1.4%), but trailing on 6-way agreement (21% vs. 33%). GPT-3.5 and Gemini Pro are clearly the weakest, with GPT-3.5 leaking scores in 25% of responses and proposing deals below its own threshold 19% of the time. In mixed-population experiments, replacing even one GPT-4 agent with GPT-3.5 (especially the leading party p₁) drops group success from 81% to 50%, demonstrating that weaker models drag down cooperative outcomes. The paper also shows that heuristic rule-based baselines achieve 37% 5-way and 28% 6-way success, meaning smaller LLMs (GPT-3.5 at 20%/8%) actually perform below simple non-LLM heuristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scorable negotiation game framework from Susskind (1985) into a multi-agent LLM evaluation setting, and builds on structured Chain-of-Thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) as the agents' reasoning scaffold. It departs from prior LLM negotiation evaluations (Akata et al. 2023, Gandhi et al. 2023, Fu et al. 2023, Davidson et al. 2024), which were either non-interactive or limited to two players, by introducing 5–7 parties with distinct roles (proposer, veto, beneficiary, opposing, constraining), multi-issue secret scoring, and tunable difficulty. The paper also carves new ground by adding greedy and adversarial agent variants to study manipulation and coalition dynamics, a dimension absent from prior two-player negotiation benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark is practically important because it provides an evolving, difficulty-tunable testbed for multi-agent LLM systems that are increasingly deployed as negotiation assistants, and it surfaces safety-relevant failure modes (score leakage, susceptibility to greedy/adversarial steering, mixed-capability fairness gaps) that two-player setups cannot capture. Key open questions include whether agents can detect and resist adversarial intruders at scale, how private communication channels change coalition dynamics, and whether more advanced planning or exploration algorithms can close the remaining gap (GPT-4 still drops from 81% to 33% when requiring all 6 parties to agree). The finding that the optimal prompting structure is model-dependent (simpler CoT helps GPT-4 but full CoT is necessary for GPT-3.5) suggests that one-size-fits-all agent scaffolds may be suboptimal.
