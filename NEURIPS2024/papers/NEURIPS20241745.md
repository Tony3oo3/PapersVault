## Problem

In performative prediction, the distribution map that characterizes how a deployed model shifts the data distribution is inevitably misspecified, causing the standard performative optimum (PO) to incur high true performative risk. This paper introduces a distributionally robust performative prediction framework that provides provable guarantees against a spectrum of plausible distribution maps, rather than relying on a single nominal specification.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular data samples (x, y), a nominal distribution map D(θ) (e.g., parameterized by a response map T_θ and base distribution), and a critical radius ρ (or equivalently a tilt parameter α = 1/μ) that controls the KL-divergence ball of admissible distribution maps. It outputs model parameters θ_DRPO that minimize the worst-case performative risk over the uncertainty collection, along with predictions ŷ on the model-induced distribution. The radius ρ can be calibrated post-fitting via bisection search on estimated KL divergences, or via a small calibration set with demographic information.

## Method

The DRPO is defined as the minimizer of the distributionally robust performative risk (DRPR), which takes the supremum of performative risk over all distribution maps within a KL-divergence ball of radius ρ around the nominal map. A strong duality result (Proposition 3.1) reduces the infinite-dimensional supremum to a one-dimensional infimum over a dual variable μ, yielding DRPR(θ) = inf_{μ≥0} {μ log E[e^{ℓ(Z;θ)/μ}] + μρ}. Algorithm 1 solves this via alternating minimization: the θ-update is a standard performative risk minimization (solvable by any existing PO solver), while the μ-update is a convex 1-D problem solvable by line search or Newton's method. An equivalent tilted performative risk formulation (Algorithm 2) replaces ρ-tuning with α-tuning, minimizing E_{Z~D(θ)}[e^{αℓ(Z;θ)}] directly. Theoretically, the excess risk of DRPO is localized to the true PO (scaling as √ρ · Var[ℓ]) whereas the PO's excess risk is entangled with the full parameter space, and in the Gaussian toy example DRPR reduces to PR plus an L1-regularization penalty √(2ρσ²)|θ|.

## Results

The paper's experiments are presented as curves (Figures 1–3, 5–7) rather than a single numerical table. In the strategic classification experiment (5.1), the DRPO with ρ=0.04 outperforms the PO for all ε_true ∈ [0,1] in performative balanced error rate, and the relative worst-case improvement grows with the misspecification range η. In the partially identifiable distribution map experiment (5.2), the TPO with α=0.5 outperforms the PO for all ε_true ∈ [−1,1] in performative BER when η ≥ 2/3, while the PO remains superior at η=0. In the fairness experiment (5.3), increasing the tilt α reduces the majority–minority performance gap at the cost of slightly higher population performative risk, and a calibrated α_cal satisfies the four-fifth rule with minimal population risk.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the standard Performative Optimum (PO), which minimizes nominal performative risk under a single (potentially misspecified) distribution map. The proposed DRPO (or equivalently TPO) is compared against PO across three misspecification scenarios. When misspecification is small (η=0), the PO is strictly better or comparable, as expected since the nominal map is correct. As misspecification grows to moderate or large levels (η ∈ {2/3, 4/3, 2}), the DRPO/TPO achieves meaningful relative improvements in worst-case performative risk and worst-case performative BER, with the gap widening as η increases. In the fairness setting, the TPO trades a small increase in population performative risk for a substantial reduction in the majority–minority performance disparity, effectively mitigating disparate impact without requiring demographic labels during training. The paper also notes that the DRPO's excess risk bound is localized to the true PO (scaling as √ρ · Var), whereas the PO's bound is entangled with the full parameter space Θ, providing a theoretical explanation for the empirical robustness gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the performative prediction framework (Perdomo et al., 2020) by incorporating distributionally robust optimization to handle inevitable misspecification of the distribution map. The strong duality result for the distributionally robust performative risk (DRPR) is inherited directly from KL-divergence DRO (Hu & Hong, 2013), while the performative structure (distribution map, performative risk, response maps) follows the standard performative prediction setup. The paper departs from prior DRO work, which ignores performativity, and from prior performative prediction work, which assumes a correctly specified distribution map; it also distinguishes itself from Peet-Pare et al. [28], who study distributionally robust performative *stability* rather than the distributionally robust performative *optimum*.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically critical gap: distribution maps are never perfectly specified, yet standard performative prediction methods silently assume they are. The excess risk bound showing DRPO's suboptimality is localized to the true PO (rather than entangled with the full parameter space as in the PO bound) provides a principled argument for when robustness helps. Open questions include extending the framework to Wasserstein-based uncertainty sets (sketched but not developed), establishing convergence guarantees for the alternating minimization algorithm, and understanding how the calibration of the critical radius ρ behaves in high-dimensional or nonparametric settings. The fairness application—mitigating disparate impact without demographic labels—suggests broader utility beyond the core performative prediction community.
