## Problem

Reinforcement learning has achieved strong results in game playing, robotics, and control, yet no unified benchmark exists for evaluating algorithmic and logical reasoning in single-agent, perfect-information RL settings. Existing reasoning benchmarks (CLRS, BIG-bench) target classical ML or LLMs, while game benchmarks like Chess and Go involve multi-agent play with imperfect information. This gap leaves the community without a standardized platform to measure whether RL agents can learn and generalize algorithmic strategies.

## Input / Output

The environment offers two observation modalities: a puzzle-specific discrete internal game state (a dict of NumPy arrays and scalars whose structure varies per puzzle) and a uniform RGB pixel observation of shape (3 × width × height) with padding for aspect-ratio normalization. The action space is discrete with cardinality 4–14, composed of selector-movement actions (UP/DOWN/LEFT/RIGHT) and game-state-change actions (SELECT, digit entry 1–9, UNDO, etc.), and is independent of puzzle size for most puzzles. The agent's performance is measured by episode length (number of steps to solve, lower is better) and success rate over 1000 random episodes, with an upper-bound 'Optimal' step count and a human-expert reference for each configuration.

## Method

PUZZLES wraps Simon Tatham's 40 logic puzzles into a Gymnasium-compatible environment by extending the original C source code with a Pygame front-end and a Python PuzzleEnv class that exposes reset() and step() following the standard RL API. Each puzzle supports arbitrarily scalable size and difficulty parameters, two observation spaces, a discrete action space, optional action masking (which removes no-op actions to improve exploration), and early episode termination upon repeated state visits to counter the sparse-reward, unbounded-horizon nature of most puzzles. Custom rewards can be injected via a Gymnasium Wrapper that accesses the internal game state through the info dict. The empirical evaluation trains eight RL algorithms (PPO, RecurrentPPO, A2C, A3C, TRPO, DQN, QRDQN, MuZero, DreamerV3) with default hyperparameters from Stable-Baselines3 or official implementations, using ~10,000 GPU hours on NVIDIA 3090s, and additionally tests a transformer-based feature extractor to probe generalization from small to large puzzle instances.

## Results

Across 40 logic puzzles (excluding 5 intractable ones: Loopy, Pearl, Pegs, Solo, Unruly), DreamerV3 achieves the best overall success rate at 62.7% of puzzle instances solved, with TRPO at 70.8% and PPO at 61.6%. DreamerV3 solves 14/40 puzzles within the Optimal upper bound, compared to 11 for TRPO and 8 for PPO. A2C, RecurrentPPO, DQN, and QRDQN all perform worse than a random policy, while MuZero suffers from unstable training and only learns successful strategies for a small subset of puzzles. All algorithms fall short of the human expert, who solves every puzzle within the optimal bound.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DreamerV3 (model-based, implicit world model) outperforms all other algorithms on both success rate and episode length, solving 62.7% of instances and 14/40 puzzles within the optimal bound. TRPO achieves the highest raw success rate (70.8%) but solves fewer puzzles within the optimal bound (11/40) compared to DreamerV3. PPO is competitive with TRPO (61.6% success, 8/40 within optimal) and benefits most from action masking, which significantly reduces episode length. MuZero, despite being a model-based planner, suffers from unstable training and only learns viable policies for a small number of puzzles without puzzle-specific hyperparameter tuning. A2C, RecurrentPPO, DQN, and QRDQN all underperform a random policy, indicating that the sparse-reward, logic-heavy nature of PUZZLES is particularly challenging for value-based and simpler policy-gradient methods. The gap between the best RL agent (DreamerV3) and the human expert (who solves all puzzles within the optimal bound) remains substantial, especially at higher difficulty levels where success rates drop sharply (e.g., Netslide 3x3: 22.4% for DreamerV3 vs. 100% for humans).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Simon Tatham's Portable Puzzle Collection (2004), extending its C source code to interface with Pygame and the Gymnasium framework, creating a standardized RL environment. Conceptually, it departs from existing RL benchmarks (Atari, MuJoCo, OpenSpiel) by focusing on single-agent, perfect-information, deterministic puzzles with known solutions rather than stochastic multi-agent games or continuous control. It also distinguishes itself from prior algorithmic-reasoning benchmarks (CLRS, IsarStep, BIG-bench) which target supervised learning or LLMs, by providing the first unified benchmark specifically for algorithmic and logical reasoning within the RL paradigm. The scalable puzzle design (adjustable size and difficulty) extends the fixed-input-size paradigm of prior benchmarks, enabling explicit generalization testing.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PUZZLES fills a clear gap: no prior benchmark offered a diverse, scalable, single-agent perfect-information RL environment for evaluating algorithmic reasoning. The empirical results reveal that even strong model-based algorithms (DreamerV3) solve only ~63% of instances, and several standard algorithms (DQN, A2C, QRDQN) perform at or below random, highlighting that current RL methods lack robust logical reasoning capabilities. Key open questions include whether intermediate rewards, GNN architectures, or better exploration mechanisms can close the gap to optimal/human performance, and whether agents can truly generalize to unseen puzzle sizes. The benchmark also opens a path for evaluating LLMs and VLMs on structured reasoning tasks with true out-of-distribution testing.
