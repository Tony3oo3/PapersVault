## Problem

Most LLM benchmarks are anglocentric or built by translating English datasets, leaving a critical gap in measuring how well models handle knowledge specific to minority cultures whose web presence is limited. This paper asks whether state-of-the-art LLMs can answer questions about a specific local culture (Basque) at the same level they answer global-knowledge questions, and whether training in a low-resource language can transfer cultural knowledge to a high-resource one.

## Input / Output

Each question is a multiple-choice item with one correct answer and two distractors, presented with 5 few-shot examples in a fixed prompt template. Open models are scored by computing log-probabilities over all three candidate letters and selecting the highest; commercial models are queried via API with a system prompt enforcing single-letter output. The dataset is fully parallel in Basque and English (natively authored in Basque, professionally translated to English), with 4,756 questions split into a local subset (~2,364) and a global subset (~2,392), each further divided into 8 categories and 3 difficulty levels.

## Method

The core contribution is the BERTAQA benchmark itself: 4,756 multiple-choice trivia questions natively compiled in Basque from crawled public sources, professionally translated into English, and explicitly partitioned into local (Basque-culture-specific) and global (broad-interest) subsets to enable controlled comparison. The experimental protocol evaluates 15+ open and commercial models (Llama 2/3, Mistral, Qwen, Yi, Gemma, GPT-3.5/4, Claude 3) under identical 5-shot conditions. A key experiment continues pretraining Llama 2 on Basque corpora (producing Latxa) and evaluates the resulting models in both English and Basque, demonstrating that local cultural knowledge acquired in Basque transfers to English queries while global knowledge degrades slightly. The paper further tests translate-test (NLLB-200) and self-translate pipelines to assess whether cross-lingual inference strategies close the local-vs-global gap, finding they help primarily on global questions.

## Results

On the English version of BERTAQA, state-of-the-art commercial models achieve the highest accuracy: GPT-4 Turbo scores 91.68% on global and 72.17% on local questions, while Claude 3 Opus scores 91.85% and 71.91% respectively. The average gap between global and local performance across all 19 model variants is 26.66 points (79.91% vs. 53.25%). Continued pretraining Llama 2 70B on Basque data (Latxa) improves local accuracy by 13.46 points (49.15% → 62.61%) but degrades global accuracy by 4.06 points (77.68% → 73.62%), demonstrating knowledge transfer from a low-resource to a high-resource language.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 19 model variants show a consistent performance gap between global and local questions, with the average Δ of 26.66 points. Among commercial models, GPT-4 Turbo (72.17% local) and Claude 3 Opus (71.91% local) lead on local questions, while Claude 3 Opus (91.85%) edges out GPT-4 Turbo (91.68%) on global. Among open models, Llama 3 70B is the strongest, reaching 59.56% local and 84.74% global, but still trails GPT-4 Turbo by 12.6 points on local. The continued-pretraining variant Latxa 70B (Llama 2 70B + Basque) outperforms the original Llama 2 70B by 13.46 points on local (62.61% vs. 49.15%) but loses 4.06 points on global (73.62% vs. 77.68%), confirming that Basque-specific knowledge transfers to English queries. Translation-based approaches (translate-test, self-translate) improve global scores for non-Basque models (e.g., Llama 2 7B global: 37.08% → 55.35% with translate-test) but harm local scores for Basque-trained models (e.g., Latxa 70B local: 64.85% → 46.28% with translate-test), showing these methods are less effective for culturally specific content.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself within the growing effort to create native, culture-specific evaluation benchmarks (C-Eval, HAE-RAE Bench, COPAL-ID, RoCulturaBench, native MMLU variants) that go beyond translated-from-English datasets. It departs from prior work in two key ways: the dataset is natively authored in Basque and then professionally translated into English (the reverse of the typical direction), and it explicitly partitions questions into local and global subsets, enabling a controlled comparison that prior benchmarks lack. The experimental design builds on the Latxa continued-pretraining models (Etxaniz et al., 2024) and re-examines the translate-test (Conneau et al., 2018) and self-translate (Etxaniz et al., 2023) paradigms under a local-knowledge lens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides what the authors claim is the first solid evidence of knowledge transfer from a low-resource language (Basque) to a high-resource language (English), directly challenging the widely held 'curse of multilinguality' narrative. It also reveals that prior positive results for translate-test and self-translate were likely inflated by evaluating only on global topics, prompting a re-examination of cross-lingual evaluation methodology. Key limitations include the restriction to a single minority culture (Basque) and the fact that the English version was produced via professional translation, which may introduce artifacts; extending this framework to other cultures and authoring questions natively in English remain important open questions.
