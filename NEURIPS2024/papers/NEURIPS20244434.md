## Problem

Existing video anomaly detection systems rely on binary or multi-class classifiers that produce limited outputs, lack deep semantic understanding of scenes, and offer no interactive capability for users. Moreover, the scarcity of detailed language annotations for anomaly videos severely restricts generalization to open-world scenarios, and prior LLM-based approaches depend on rigid pseudo-labels that provide only superficial supervision.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244434/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a video clip (up to 32 frames) and a text prompt specifying either a description instruction (from a pool of 20 anomaly-focused prompts) or an open-ended user question (from 100 generated 5W2H-style questions). The video is consumed through two parallel branches: raw appearance frames and motion frames (optical-flow-magnitude-masked video). Output is free-form natural language — either an anomaly description or a question answer — generated autoregressively by LLaMA-2-7B.

## Method

HAWK employs a dual-branch architecture where two BLIP-2 encoders (EVA-CLIP + Video Q-Former) with separate parameters process appearance and motion inputs independently, then project into a shared language space via learnable projection networks before feeding LLaMA-2. Motion is extracted via Farneback optical flow, whose magnitude serves as a spatial mask multiplied onto the original frames to isolate moving regions. Three losses jointly optimize the model: a standard video-to-language cross-entropy loss, a video-motion consistency loss (1 − cosine similarity in a tightly compressed feature space) that implicitly steers the appearance branch toward motion-relevant regions, and a motion-to-language matching loss that supervises the motion branch to generate dependency-parsed verb-entity phrases extracted from ground-truth descriptions. Training proceeds in two stages: pre-training on WebVid for general video understanding, then fine-tuning on 8,000+ manually verified anomaly descriptions and QA pairs spanning seven datasets (UCF-Crime, ShanghaiTech, CUHK Avenue, UCSD Ped1/Ped2, DoTA, UBnormal).

## Results

On anomaly video description generation (Table 1A), HAWK achieves BLEU-1 of 0.270, BLEU-4 of 0.043, and GPT-Guided Consistency of 0.218, roughly doubling the best baseline (LLaMA-Adapter at BLEU-1=0.132) across all Text-Level and GPT-Guided metrics. On anomaly video question-answering (Table 1B), HAWK reaches BLEU-1 of 0.319, Reasonability of 0.840, and Consistency of 0.753, outperforming the strongest baseline (VideoChat at BLEU-1=0.261, Reasonability=0.699) by 22–21% relative improvement. Ablation studies confirm each component contributes: removing motion information drops BLEU-1 from 0.270 to 0.249 (description) and from 0.319 to 0.309 (QA).

## Baselines & Comparisons

HAWK achieves state-of-the-art performance across all seven metrics (BLEU-1 through BLEU-4, Reasonability, Detail, Consistency) in both description generation and question-answering, outperforming every baseline by a wide margin. The closest competitor on description generation is LLaMA-Adapter (BLEU-1=0.132), which HAWK surpasses by 105% relative (0.270 vs. 0.132); on GPT-Guided Consistency, HAWK leads by 5.7× (0.218 vs. 0.038). For question-answering, VideoChat is the strongest baseline (BLEU-1=0.261, Reasonability=0.699), yet HAWK still leads by 22% on BLEU-1 (0.319) and 20% on Reasonability (0.840). Video-LLaVA and Video-ChatGPT consistently rank at or near the bottom across both tasks, with Video-LLaVA scoring the lowest on nearly every metric. In a direct comparison with GPT-4's raw data-engineering pipeline (Table 6), HAWK's 7B model outperforms GPT-4 on all metrics (e.g., BLEU-1: 0.270 vs. 0.188; Consistency: 0.218 vs. 0.158), demonstrating that the motion-aware architecture adds value beyond what a general-purpose LLM can produce from the same annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

HAWK extends the vision-language model paradigm for video understanding (BLIP-2 encoders, LLaMA-2 backbone, Video Q-Former) into the video anomaly detection domain, where prior work relied on binary or multi-class classifiers that offered no semantic explanation or user interaction. It builds most directly on Lv et al.'s video-based LLM for anomaly explanation but replaces their pseudo-label training with a curated 8,000+ video dataset featuring human-verified language descriptions and QA pairs. The key architectural departure is the explicit dual-branch motion modality (Farneback optical flow) coupled with two auxiliary losses (video-motion consistency and motion-language matching via dependency parsing), which prior video-LLM frameworks (VideoChat, Video-LLaMA, Video-LLaVA) did not incorporate.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HAWK bridges a practical gap between anomaly detection (localizing *that* something is wrong) and anomaly understanding (explaining *what* is wrong in natural language and answering open-ended user questions), making VAD systems more actionable in surveillance, autonomous driving, and crime analysis. The annotated multi-scenario dataset (7 datasets, 8,000+ videos with descriptions and QA pairs) is a reusable resource for the community. Open questions remain: the system operates at video-level without spatial/temporal localization, cannot handle streaming data, still suffers from residual hallucinations, and the dataset—while diverse—remains limited for truly open-world generalization. The finding that a 7B model with motion-specific supervision outperforms GPT-4's zero-shot data generation pipeline on this task suggests targeted architectural inductive biases can beat raw scale for narrow-domain understanding.
