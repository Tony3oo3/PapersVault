## Problem

Selecting the privacy budget ε in differential privacy is difficult for agencies because the parameter lacks intuitive interpretation, and existing guidance either relies on ad hoc values or requires access to the confidential data or full population values. Prior Bayesian approaches to setting ε (e.g., Lee and Clifton) are restricted to inclusion-only disclosure with public populations, and the naive strategy of bounding relative risk by a constant is overly conservative, yielding unnecessarily small ε. This paper asks: can an agency specify its acceptable posterior-to-prior disclosure risk as a function of prior probabilities and derive a corresponding ε without using the confidential data?

## Input / Output

The agency specifies a risk profile function r*(p_i, q_i) giving the maximum tolerable posterior-to-prior risk ratio at each pair of prior probabilities (inclusion probability p_i and value-disclosure probability q_i), along with a privacy violation set S. The framework outputs a single recommended ε via a minimization over all (p_i, q_i) ∈ (0,1]×(0,1], with closed-form solutions available for several common risk profile shapes (constant bound, max of absolute and relative risk thresholds). Crucially, all computations are theoretical and data-free, so they do not consume part of the overall privacy budget.

## Method

Under two assumptions (the adversary knows the release mechanism, and the adversary's beliefs about other individuals' data are independent of individual i's inclusion and values), the paper derives a tight upper bound on the relative disclosure risk r_i(p_i, q_i, t*) in terms of ε, p_i, and q_i (Theorem 1). Inverting this bound yields ε_i(p_i, q_i), the largest ε that still satisfies a given risk bound at that prior pair (Theorem 2). The agency then sets ε as the minimum of ε_i over all (p_i, q_i), which for specific risk profile forms (e.g., max{ã/(p_i q̃), r̃}) admits closed-form solutions. The key insight is that allowing the risk profile to vary with prior probabilities—tolerating larger relative risks when priors are small—can yield ε values substantially larger (sometimes by a factor of two or more) than the constant-bound baseline, directly translating to less noise and higher data utility.

## Results

For three risk profiles of the form max{ã/(p_i·q̃), r̃} with ã=0.25, the framework recommends ε=0.51 (r̃=1.5), ε=1.30 (r̃=3), and ε=2.04 (r̃=6), corresponding to geometric-mechanism noise standard deviations of 2.74, 1.02, and 0.59, and exact-value release probabilities of 25%, 57%, and 77%. These recommendations are more than double the ε values (≈0.20, 0.55, 0.90) from the naive constant-baseline r*(p_i,q_i)=r̃. In the Durham county infant-mortality example, the most aggressive risk profile (ã=0.5, r̃=5) yields ε≈2.20 with RMSE≈0.53, keeping the probability of switching the 6.0 target below 10% for a 0.25 rate deviation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the constant relative-risk bound r*(p_i,q_i)=r̃, which yields ε=log(r̃)/2. The proposed framework's risk profiles (which allow larger relative risks for low prior probabilities and cap absolute risk) consistently produce ε values more than double the baseline—for example, ε=1.30 vs. ≈0.55 at r̃=3, and ε=2.04 vs. ≈0.90 at r̃=6. Against Lee and Clifton [29], the most closely related prior work, the paper shows in Appendix D.1 that [29]'s method can recommend a larger ε for specific statistics and populations (ranging from 0.34 to 1.62 in their examples vs. ε≈0.41 from the proposed method for n=4, ã=1/3), but [29] requires the full population to be public and only addresses inclusion disclosure, whereas the proposed framework handles non-public populations and value-level disclosure. The paper also notes that the gap between the baseline and the proposed method can be arbitrarily large: for a 'point' risk profile with r'=2, the baseline gives ε<0.35 while the proposed method's ε diverges as r'→2.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian semantics of differential privacy (Kifer & Machanavajjhala 2014; Kasiviswanathan & Smith 2014) from characterizing what ε means to actively using it to select ε. The most directly related prior work is Lee & Clifton (2011), which also uses a Bayesian approach to set ε but is restricted to public populations and inclusion-only disclosure. The paper departs from that line by handling non-public populations, value-level disclosure (not just inclusion), and a data-free computation that does not consume privacy budget. It also builds on the relative disclosure risk framework of McClure & Reiter (2012) as the core risk quantity, and on the geometric mechanism (Ghosh et al. 2012) as the primary release mechanism in examples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives agencies a principled, data-free procedure for setting ε that can yield substantially larger (less noisy) releases than the naive constant-bound strategy—the gap is shown to be arbitrarily large in the worst case. This matters practically because current DP tools (e.g., DP Creator) leave users without clear guidance on ε selection, and the paper's risk-profile formulation translates an abstract parameter into interpretable posterior-to-prior risk language. Open questions include extending to multiple releases via composition theorems, other DP variants (zCDP, approximate DP), and the ethical implications of assigning different risk profiles to different subpopulations, which raises data equity concerns the authors flag but do not resolve.
