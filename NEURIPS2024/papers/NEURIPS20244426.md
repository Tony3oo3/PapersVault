## Problem

Existing disentanglement methods encode compositional structure via dimension-wise concatenation of FoV tokens, a symbolic/localist scheme that the authors argue misaligns with the continuous, distributed nature of deep learning by restricting gradient flow, limiting expressivity, and increasing noise sensitivity. Tensor Product Representations (TPRs) offer a distributed alternative but are too rigid: they require representations to lie on a discrete subset of the representational space and enforce a strict algebraic form that cannot capture quasi-compositional structures in non-formal domains like vision. No prior framework learns distributed, flexible compositional representations in the visual domain under weak supervision.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single RGB image x (from Cars3D, Shapes3D, or MPI3D) and a paired image x' that differs from x in exactly one known FoV type (match-pairing weak supervision). The encoder E maps x to a (D_F · D_R)-dimensional vector z serving as the Soft TPR; the TPR decoder then unbinds soft fillers via tensor inner products with semi-orthogonal role embeddings, quantises them via VQ-VAE into a learned filler codebook, and reconstructs an explicit TPR ψ*_tpr. A standard decoder D reconstructs the input image from ψ*_tpr, and the final loss combines the Soft TPR form penalty, reconstruction, VQ commitment, cross-entropy on the changed role, and swap-reconstruction terms.

## Method

The core contribution is the Soft TPR, a continuous relaxation of Smolensky's TPR in which any vector z within Frobenius distance ε of an explicit TPR qualifies, replacing the discrete set of valid TPRs with continuous neighbourhoods. The Soft TPR Autoencoder enforces this property by penalising ‖z − ψ*_tpr‖₂, where ψ*_tpr is constructed greedily: a TPR decoder first unbinds soft fillers from z using a fixed semi-orthogonal role embedding matrix (so unbinding vectors equal role embeddings), then quantises them via VQ-VAE into a learned filler codebook, and finally rebinds quantised fillers to roles via tensor products and summation. Weak supervision is injected through a match-pairing cross-entropy loss that encourages the quantised filler distance for the known changed role to be maximal relative to all other roles, plus a swap-reconstruction loss that enforces consistency when exchanging quantised fillers between paired images. This design lets the encoder produce arbitrary vectors in the full representational space while the quantisation bottleneck implicitly enforces the TPR algebraic structure, combining the flexibility of distributed encoding with the recoverability of explicit role-filler bindings.

## Results

On the three standard disentanglement benchmarks, Soft TPR achieves state-of-the-art DCI scores of 0.863 (Cars3D), 0.926 (Shapes3D), and 0.828 (MPI3D), with FactorVAE scores of 0.999, 0.984, and 0.949 respectively. This represents a 29% DCI improvement over the best symbolic scalar-tokened baseline (Ada-GVAE-k, 0.664) on Cars3D and a 74% improvement over the best vector-tokened baseline (VCT, 0.475) on MPI3D. In downstream tasks, Soft TPR achieves 93% sample-efficiency improvement at 100 samples and 138–168% raw performance gains in the 100–200 sample regime on MPI3D FoV regression.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Soft TPR outperforms all seven baselines across every dataset and metric. Against the strongest scalar-tokened baseline Ada-GVAE-k [33], Soft TPR gains +0.199 DCI on Cars3D (0.863 vs 0.664) and +0.485 DCI on MPI3D (0.828 vs 0.343). Against the strongest vector-tokened baseline VCT [47], Soft TPR leads by +0.485 DCI on MPI3D (0.828 vs 0.475) and +0.481 on Cars3D (0.863 vs 0.382), though VCT is competitive on Shapes3D DCI (0.884 vs 0.926). The GAN-based Shu [35] and energy-based COMET [36] baselines perform substantially worse, with DCI scores below 0.05 on most datasets. GVAE [22] and ML-VAE [13] are mid-tier, with DCI scores of 0.245–0.842 depending on dataset. Slow-VAE [39] is competitive on Shapes3D (DCI 0.850) but lags significantly on Cars3D and MPI3D. In downstream evaluation, Soft TPR also dominates: at 100 training iterations it achieves 0.732 R² on MPI3D FoV regression (vs 0.557 for Slow-VAE) and 80.4% abstract visual reasoning accuracy (vs 63.1% for Ada-GVAE-k).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Smolensky's Tensor Product Representation (TPR) by introducing a continuous relaxation (Soft TPR) that permits approximate rather than exact adherence to the rigid role-filler binding algebra, and operationalises this via a VQ-VAE-style quantisation module within an autoencoder. It departs fundamentally from the dominant disentanglement literature (β-VAE, GVAE, Ada-GVAE, ML-VAE, Shu, etc.), which encodes compositional structure as a dimension-wise concatenation of scalar or vector FoV tokens—a symbolic, localist scheme the authors argue is misaligned with deep learning's distributed, continuous nature. Prior TPR-based work has been confined to formal, algebraically-structured domains (language, mathematics) with strong supervision; this paper is the first to learn distributed compositional representations in the non-formal visual domain using only weak (match-pairing) supervision.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a theoretically principled path to compositional representations that are both explicitly structured and inherently distributed, potentially resolving a long-standing tension between symbolic compositionality and connectionist learning. The ablation showing that both relaxed constraints and distributed encoding are necessary, and the finding that the flexible Soft TPR outperforms its rigid quantised counterpart for downstream tasks, suggest that representational 'noise' around a compositional structure can be beneficial rather than harmful. However, all empirical validation is on simple synthetic datasets (Cars3D, Shapes3D, MPI3D) with a limited number of FoVs; whether the approach scales to real-world images with hundreds of interacting factors, hierarchical compositionality, or open-vocabulary scenes remains an open question. The paper also notes future work on hierarchical TPRs where fillers recursively decompose into further role-filler bindings.
