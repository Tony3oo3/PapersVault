## Problem

Existing long-term time series forecasting (LTSF) models rely on complex deep architectures (Transformers, TCNs, RNNs) to implicitly capture periodic patterns through long-range dependency modeling, yet the fundamental basis for accurate long-horizon prediction is the stable periodicity inherent in the data. This paper asks: if the purpose of deep models is to extract periodic features, why not explicitly model the periodic patterns directly rather than hoping a complex architecture discovers them?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243372/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a multivariate time series window of length L (typically 96 steps) across D channels, along with a critical hyperparameter W that must be set to the maximum stable cycle length in the data (e.g., 24 for hourly daily cycles, 168 for hourly weekly cycles, 144 for 10-min daily cycles). It produces H future steps (96–720) for all D channels. The channel-independent strategy means a single shared backbone processes each channel independently, and W must be correctly inferred (via domain knowledge or ACF analysis) for the method to function; an incorrect W renders the RCF component essentially inert.

## Method

The core contribution is the Residual Cycle Forecasting (RCF) technique, which introduces learnable recurrent cycles Q ∈ R^{W×D} (initialized to zeros) that are globally shared within each channel and trained end-to-end via gradient backpropagation alongside the backbone. At inference, Q is left-shifted by t mod W and cyclically repeated to produce aligned cyclic components for both the input window and the prediction horizon; the input residuals (original minus cyclic) are passed through a lightweight backbone (single-layer Linear or dual-layer MLP), and the predicted residuals are added back to the future cyclic components. Unlike existing seasonal-trend decomposition methods that estimate trends via sliding averages within a limited look-back window, RCF learns the periodic component globally from the entire training set, enabling it to surpass the constraints of finite-length windows and go beyond what standard linear regression can achieve. The RCF module adds only W×D parameters and zero MACs, making it a plug-and-play enhancement that also improves existing models like PatchTST and iTransformer.

## Results

On the standard LTSF benchmark (L=96, averaged over H∈{96,192,336,720}), CycleNet/MLP achieves the best overall MSE across 7 of 8 datasets, with standout results on Electricity (MSE 0.168, MAE 0.259), Solar-Energy (MSE 0.210, MAE 0.261), and Weather (MSE 0.243, MAE 0.271). CycleNet/Linear ranks second overall, leading on ETTh1 (MSE 0.432) and ETTh2 (MSE 0.383). The only dataset where CycleNet does not rank first is Traffic, where iTransformer (MSE 0.428) outperforms CycleNet/MLP (MSE 0.472) due to strong spatiotemporal inter-channel dependencies. In terms of efficiency, CycleNet/MLP uses only 472.9K parameters and 134.84M MACs on Electricity, reducing parameters by over 90% compared to iTransformer (5.15M) and PatchTST (10.74M).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CycleNet/MLP outperforms all 14 baselines on 7 of 8 datasets in MSE, with the largest margins on Electricity (0.168 vs. iTransformer's 0.178, a 5.6% improvement) and Solar-Energy (0.210 vs. iTransformer's 0.233, a 9.9% improvement). On ETTh1 and ETTh2, CycleNet/Linear edges out iTransformer (0.432 vs. 0.454 and 0.383 vs. 0.383 MSE respectively), while CycleNet/MLP leads on ETTm1 (0.379 vs. 0.407) and ETTm2 (0.266 vs. 0.288). The sole exception is Traffic, where iTransformer's inverted Transformer architecture for inter-channel modeling yields MSE 0.428 versus CycleNet/MLP's 0.472, a gap the authors attribute to Traffic's strong spatiotemporal dependencies that a channel-independent strategy cannot capture. Against the lightweight DLinear baseline, CycleNet/MLP improves MSE by 20.7% on Electricity (0.168 vs. 0.212) and 36.4% on Solar-Energy (0.210 vs. 0.330). Compared to the recent TimeMixer (ICLR 2024), CycleNet/MLP achieves lower MSE on all datasets except Traffic (0.472 vs. 0.484), with the gap widening on high-dimensional datasets like Electricity (0.168 vs. 0.182). The RCF technique also serves as a plug-and-play module, improving PatchTST by ~10% and iTransformer by ~5–14% MSE on Electricity when added as an auxiliary component.

## Relation to Prior Work

CycleNet positions the Residual Cycle Forecasting (RCF) technique as a novel form of Seasonal-Trend Decomposition (STD), departing from existing window-based methods (MOV in DLinear, LD in Leddam, Sparse in SparseTSF) by learning global periodic patterns via learnable recurrent cycles trained end-to-end over the full training set rather than decomposing within a finite look-back window. It inherits the channel-independent strategy from DLinear/PatchTST and the RevIN-style instance normalization from recent LTSF work, but the core conceptual contribution is treating periodicity as an explicit, globally shared, learnable parameter rather than an implicit feature to be extracted by deep architectures. The paper explicitly frames RCF as a plug-and-play module that can augment existing backbones (PatchTST, iTransformer, DLinear), distinguishing it from prior STD methods that are tightly coupled to specific model designs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single-layer Linear or dual-layer MLP combined with explicit periodic modeling can match or exceed complex Transformer-based models on most LTSF benchmarks challenges the prevailing assumption that architectural depth and attention mechanisms are necessary for long-horizon forecasting, and offers a 90%+ parameter reduction with minimal computational overhead. However, the approach requires a priori knowledge of the cycle length W, struggles with spatiotemporal dependencies (notably on Traffic), is sensitive to outliers that distort learned average cycles, and cannot handle varying or multi-scale cycle lengths across channels. Open questions include how to automatically discover cycle lengths, how to extend RCF to model inter-channel relationships in spatio-temporal settings, and whether the technique generalizes to domains without stable periodicity (e.g., ECG, financial data).
