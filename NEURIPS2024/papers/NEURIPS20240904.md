## Problem

Diffusion-based generative models and stochastic optimal control (SOC) theory are well-established in finite-dimensional spaces, but their theoretical foundations for infinite-dimensional function spaces remain underexplored. The central obstacle is that infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces lack a Lebesgue-equivalent measure, so the Radon-Nikodym densities needed for Doob's h-transform and diffusion bridges have no closed form, making it nontrivial to construct conditioned SDEs or define terminal costs for sampling.

## Input / Output

Data is consumed as a field representation — a finite collection of function evaluations {Y[p_i], p_i} mapping coordinate points to signal values — rather than as a fixed-resolution tensor. For bridge matching, the method takes two distributions over functions (e.g., two image distributions or two density functions) and produces a learned stochastic process whose time-marginals interpolate between them; for Bayesian inference, it takes observed function evaluations on a grid O and an energy functional U, and produces posterior samples on unobserved grid points T. A key property is resolution-invariance: the model is trained on evaluations at a coarse grid (e.g., 32²) but can generate accurate function evaluations at finer, unseen resolutions (e.g., 256²) without retraining.

## Method

The paper derives Doob's h-transform in infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces by showing, via the Hopf-Cole transform, that the Kolmogorov backward equation for h is equivalent to the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation of an SOC problem with running cost R(α) = ½‖α‖², yielding the optimal control α* = σQ^{1/2} D_x log h. To overcome the absence of a Lebesgue measure, the h function is expressed as a Radon-Nikodym derivative q_t(x,·) with respect to the invariant Gaussian measure N(0, Q_∞), giving an explicit density formula (Theorem 2.3) that enables computation of D_x log h. Two learning algorithms follow: (1) Bridge Matching trains a neural-network-parameterized control α^θ to match a mixture of diffusion bridge paths between two distributions, minimizing a cross-entropy loss derived via the infinite-dimensional Girsanov theorem; (2) Bayesian Learning trains α^θ to steer a process from a deterministic initial point to a target posterior π_T ∝ exp(−U)·μ_prior, minimizing a relative-entropy loss whose terminal cost is computed by a chain rule of Radon-Nikodym derivatives through the invariant measure.

## Results

On the Physionet imputation task, DBFS achieves the lowest RMSE across all missingness levels (0.50 at 10%, 0.61 at 50%, 0.77 at 90%), outperforming both CSDI and DSDP-GP. On 1D function generation (kernel two-sample test power, lower is better), DBFS achieves 5.1 on Quadratic and 3.9 on Gridwatch, competitive with or better than SP-SGM, though it trails on Melbourne (9.67 vs. 5.3). On unpaired image transfer (FID, lower is better), DBFS scores 9.1 on EMNIST→MNIST and 44.4 on AFHQ-64 Wild→Cat, slightly behind the finite-dimensional DSBM baseline (6.0 and 25.4 respectively). On GP regression, DBFS achieves the best context and target log-likelihoods on RBF and Matérn 5/2 kernels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DBFS clearly outperforms both CSDI [68] and DSDP-GP [6] on Physionet imputation at all three missingness levels (e.g., 0.77 vs. 0.81 vs. 0.84 RMSE at 90% missing), demonstrating that forward-only controlled SDEs can match or beat denoising-based diffusion imputation. On 1D function generation, DBFS beats SP-SGM [52] on Quadratic (5.1 vs. 5.4) and Gridwatch (3.9 vs. 4.7) kernel two-sample test power but loses on Melbourne (9.67 vs. 5.3), while both substantially outperform NDP [22] on Quadratic and Gridwatch. On unpaired image transfer, DBFS trails the finite-dimensional DSBM [61] on both EMNIST→MNIST (FID 9.1 vs. 6.0) and AFHQ-64 Wild→Cat (44.4 vs. 25.4), which the authors attribute to the inherent trade-off of resolution-agnostic methods. For GP regression, DBFS surpasses both CNP [27] and NP [28] on RBF and Matérn 5/2 kernels in both context and target log-likelihood, though it underperforms NP on the Periodic kernel's target likelihood (−1.88 vs. −1.34). Overall, the method shows its strongest advantage in Bayesian inference tasks where the infinite-dimensional formulation provides a natural fit, while finite-dimensional baselines retain an edge in pixel-space image generation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate threads—Doob's h-transform for constructing diffusion bridges and stochastic optimal control (SOC) for generative modeling—within a single infinite-dimensional Hilbert space framework. It extends finite-dimensional bridge matching algorithms (Peluchetti [51], Shi et al. [61]) and simulation-based Bayesian inference via Schrödinger-Föllmer flows (Vargas et al. [73], Zhang & Chen [77]) to function spaces by deriving an explicit Radon-Nikodym density with respect to a Gaussian reference measure, circumventing the absence of a Lebesgue measure in infinite dimensions. While the infinite-dimensional Doob's h-transform itself has appeared in prior work [2, 26, 31], the paper's positioning is that the SOC perspective provides the natural unifying lens under which diverse finite-dimensional sampling problems (bridge matching, Bayesian posterior sampling) generalize coherently to Hilbert spaces, rather than treating each as an isolated extension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled, resolution-free alternative to pixel-grid diffusion models, with demonstrated ability to generate images at unseen resolutions and sample Bayesian posteriors over continuous functions (e.g., Gaussian processes). However, the authors acknowledge that time-independent coefficients preclude noise schedules, the Bayesian learning gradient is computationally expensive, and FID scores are slightly worse than finite-dimensional baselines—suggesting that the theoretical elegance has not yet translated into clear empirical dominance. Open questions include whether the framework can accommodate time-dependent coefficients, scale to irregular domains (graphs, manifolds), and whether the resolution-invariance property can be leveraged for data-efficient training across resolutions.
