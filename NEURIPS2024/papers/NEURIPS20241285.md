## Problem

Patient motion during long 3D MRI acquisitions causes severe artifacts, yet existing deep learning motion-correction methods are limited to 2D in-plane motion, rely on simulated motion during training (making them specific to the trained motion type), and require scarce 3D motion-corrupted data. This paper addresses the gap by proposing the first deep learning-based method for 3D rigid motion estimation that requires only 2D motion-free training data and no motion simulation.

## Input / Output

The method takes undersampled 3D multi-coil k-space data (C=12 coils, 218×170×256 in experiments), a sampling trajectory mapping k-space lines to shots, and coil sensitivity maps as input. It outputs 6-DOF rigid motion parameters (3 translations, 3 rotations) per shot and a motion-corrected 3D volume. Unusually, the 3D volume is processed by a 2D U-Net slice-wise along randomly chosen axes, with gradients computed for only 5 randomly selected slices per iteration to keep GPU memory manageable.

## Method

MotionTTT exploits the insight that a U-Net trained for motion-free 2D reconstruction yields a small data-consistency loss only when its input is motion-free, so optimizing motion parameters through the forward model at test time recovers the correct motion. The three-step pipeline is: (1) pre-train a 17.5M-parameter U-Net on 2D motion-free data with a combined image-domain and k-space L1 loss; (2) freeze the network and optimize motion parameters via Adam to minimize the data-consistency loss ‖A(T,m) f_θ(A†(T,−m)y) − y‖₁/‖y‖₁, using a three-phase schedule (explore with large LR, reset/split poorly-estimated states for intra-shot motion, refine jointly with small LR); (3) reconstruct the volume using the estimated motion parameters with L1-minimization or a U-Net with a data-consistency layer. A theoretical result proves that for a random-subspace signal model, the TTT loss has a unique global minimum at the true motion parameters when the subspace dimension and number of shots are sufficiently small relative to the number of measurements.

## Results

On simulated inter-shot motion (Calgary Campinas Brain MRI, 12 coils, acceleration factor 4, 50 shots), MotionTTT achieves reconstruction PSNR matching the oracle KnownMotion-L1 across all 10 severity levels (1–10 motion events, up to 10°/mm), indicating near-perfect motion parameter estimation. For severe motion (severity 9–10), MotionTTT+Th-L1 (with DC loss thresholding) essentially closes the remaining gap to KnownMotion-L1, while AltOpt degrades significantly. MotionTTT runs in ~25 minutes on an A100 GPU, approximately 6× faster than AltOpt with early stopping (~2 h 39 min) and ~10× faster than AltOpt without early stopping (~4 h 15 min). On prospectively acquired real motion data (3D T1-TFE, 52 shots, undersampling factor 4.94), MotionTTT yields significant visual quality improvements over no motion correction for both mild and strong motion.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against alternating optimization (AltOpt, Cordero-Grande et al. [7]), MotionTTT matches or exceeds reconstruction PSNR at all motion severities, with the gap widening substantially for severe motion (severity ≥ 7) where AltOpt fails to recover motion parameters accurately. MotionTTT is also ~6× faster in wall-clock time. Against the end-to-end stacked U-net with self-assisted priors (Al-Masni et al. [1]), MotionTTT achieves significantly higher PSNR across all severities; the E2E method suffers from overly smooth reconstructions as motion severity increases because it cannot resolve the degraded network input without explicit motion knowledge. Against plain L1-minimization without motion correction, MotionTTT provides clear gains whenever motion is present, with the improvement growing with severity. Critically, MotionTTT's estimated-motion PSNR matches the oracle KnownMotion-L1 PSNR at every severity level, confirming that the test-time-training loss reliably recovers the true motion parameters rather than merely producing a plausible reconstruction.

## Relation to Prior Work

MotionTTT repurposes the test-time-training (TTT) paradigm from Sun et al. [39] and Darestani et al. [9] in a novel way: rather than adapting network weights to distribution shift, it optimizes the motion parameters embedded in the forward model (treated as part of the network) so that the input to a frozen, motion-free-trained U-Net becomes consistent with the measurements. This departs from prior deep learning motion-correction methods (Al-Masni [1], Singh [34], Haskell [14,16]) that either learn end-to-end mappings requiring motion-corrupted training data or predict motion in the high-dimensional measurement space. It also contrasts with classical alternating optimization [7], which jointly optimizes motion and reconstruction without any learned prior, and with 2D-only DL methods that cannot handle the 3D rigid motion that occurs in practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first deep learning-based method for 3D rigid motion estimation in MRI, requiring only 2D motion-free training data (no 3D or motion-corrupted data), and achieving a 6× speedup over the classical alternating optimization baseline while outperforming it under severe motion. Key limitations include a distribution shift in the effective undersampling mask under motion (which degrades U-net-based reconstruction relative to L1), reliance on fully-sampled training data that is scarce at 3D resolution, and restriction to rigid motion. Open questions include whether self-supervised or diffusion-based reconstruction modules can close the remaining gap to oracle motion, and whether the TTT framework extends to non-rigid or more complex motion models.
