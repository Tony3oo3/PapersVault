## Problem

Testing RL policies for safety is infeasible by exhaustive evaluation, yet existing model-free testing approaches lack formal verification guarantees and do not concentrate effort where policy decisions most affect safety. The paper addresses this by proposing a model-based testing framework that rigorously ranks states by decision importance and provides provable safety guarantees over the entire state space by sampling only a fraction of the policy.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240881/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite MDP (state set, action set, probabilistic transition function, initial distribution), a deterministic memoryless policy π: S→A, and a safety objective as a CTL formula φ with a probability threshold δ_φ. It outputs a partition of the state space into verified-safe (S_s) and verified-unsafe (S_f) states, per-state optimistic and pessimistic safety probability estimates in [0,1], and an importance ranking. The MDP is progressively restricted as policy decisions are sampled, so estimates tighten monotonically: e_pes increases and e_opt decreases across iterations until convergence.

## Method

The core algorithm (IMT) iteratively computes optimistic (P_max) and pessimistic (P_min) safety estimates via probabilistic model checking (value iteration) over the current MDP. At each iteration, an importance ranking is computed where each state's rank equals the maximum difference in optimistic safety probability across available actions, identifying states where the policy's decision most affects the safety outcome. The policy is then sampled at the top-ranked states and the MDP is restricted by fixing those state-action pairs, which monotonically tightens the estimates. The algorithm terminates when the maximum gap between optimistic and pessimistic estimates falls below a user-defined threshold ε_φ, at which point the state space is fully partitioned into safe and unsafe regions. A scalability extension (IMTc) clusters high-ranked states via k-means, tests only a fraction κ of each cluster, and assigns conservative safe/unsafe verdicts to entire clusters, reducing the testing budget by up to a factor of 5.

## Results

On the Slippery Gridworld (196 states), IMT fully verifies the policy over the entire state space after only 33 policy samples (145 safe, 51 unsafe, 0 undetermined), while MT requires sampling nearly the entire state space. On the UAV Reach-Avoid task (25,517 states), IMT reduces verification time by 6.6× compared to MT (269.8s vs. 1793s at η=0.25) and discovers safety violations in 775 additional states for η=1.0 that random testing (50,000 queries) only partially captures. On Atari Skiing (~2.2M states), the clustering extension (IMTc) reduces the testing budget by up to a factor of 5 (ζ=25) while still covering the full state space. Across all experiments, IMT consistently outperforms both MT (random model-based sampling) and RT (model-free random testing) in sample efficiency and runtime.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares IMT against two baselines: MT (model-based testing with random policy sampling, no importance ranking) and RT (model-free random testing). Against MT, IMT consistently requires fewer policy samples and less wall-clock time to converge: on the Gridworld, IMT needs 33 samples vs. nearly the full 196-state space for MT; on UAV, IMT is 1.7–7× faster depending on noise level. Against RT, the advantage is qualitative rather than a single metric: IMT/MT provide formal verification guarantees over the entire state space (partitioning all states into safe/unsafe), while RT can only report individual failing test cases (e.g., RT found only 13.25±2.75 failing states on Urban Navigation with 2000 queries, versus full-state-space verification by IMT). On Atari Skiing, the clustering extension (IMTc) further reduces the testing budget by up to 5× compared to plain IMT (ζ=1), at the cost of conservative cluster-level verdicts that may over-approximate unsafe regions. IMT wins on all quantitative comparisons; no baseline outperforms it on any reported metric.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on probabilistic model checking (Baier & Katoen) as its computational engine, extending it from full-state-space verification to an iterative, importance-guided testing procedure for RL policies. It departs from off-policy evaluation, which estimates performance from logged data without formal guarantees, and from fuzz/search-based RL testing, which finds violations but cannot certify safety from non-failing tests. The importance ranking draws motivation from Chockler et al.'s statistical fault-localization approach to ranking policy decisions, but replaces the empirical, execution-based ranking with a rigorous MDP-based computation that considers all possible policy behaviors. The clustering extension repurposes standard k-means to balance scalability against the risk of missing unsafe behavior within a cluster.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key contribution is that a small number of well-chosen policy samples (e.g., 33 for a 196-state GridWorld) can yield formal verification guarantees over the entire state space, which is a significant efficiency gain over exhaustive or random testing. However, the approach requires a known MDP model of the environment and assumes deterministic policies with discrete state spaces, limiting immediate applicability to many real-world RL deployments. Open questions include extending to stochastic policies, continuous state/action spaces, and learning the MDP online during training. The clustering trade-off—reduced testing budget at the cost of potentially missing unsafe behavior within a cluster—remains a practical concern for safety-critical applications where missed failures are unacceptable.
