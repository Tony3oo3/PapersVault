## Problem

CLIP and similar pretrained vision-language models struggle with downstream generalization in specialized domains (satellite imagery, fine-grained car models) where visual concepts are under-represented during pretraining. Existing prompt learning methods either ignore textual knowledge entirely or rely on LLM-generated prompts that incur high inference cost at test time and may be noisy or task-irrelevant. The paper asks how to distill rich textual priors from natural language prompts into CLIP's prompt space to improve generalization while eliminating LLM inference cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242576/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For classification, the method takes an RGB image and produces a class label by matching the image embedding against projected combinations of a template embedding and the generated AAPE. For VQA and captioning, the AAPE (a single d-dimensional vector) is appended as a prefix to the LiMBeR prompt sequence fed to an LLM, producing a text answer or caption. For image-to-text retrieval, the AAPE serves directly as the query embedding. The natural language prompts used during training are GPT-3-generated class descriptions (20–90 per class) for classification and 5 human-annotated COCO captions per image for the other tasks.

## Method

The method has three stages: (1) collecting reference prompts per class (via GPT-3) or per image (COCO captions); (2) learning an input-adapted prompt aggregator — a single attention layer that condenses n prompt embeddings into one image-aligned embedding p^a, optimized with the CLIP reward to suppress redundant or irrelevant prompts; (3) training a lightweight two-layer FC prompt generator h that maps image features x to an AAPE embedding, jointly minimizing a distillation loss (L2 distance to p^a) and a task loss (cross-entropy for classification, CLIP contrastive loss for retrieval/captioning/VQA). At test time, only h is retained (the aggregator is discarded), so no LLM query is needed. For classification, h(x) is concatenated with a template embedding and projected through a ReLU layer to form the classifier weight, avoiding token-wise prompt prediction.

## Results

On the primary task of few-shot image classification (16-shot, base-to-new class generalization), AAPE achieves a new SOTA harmonic mean of 80.97% averaged across 11 datasets, outperforming the previous best OGEN (80.34%) and the LLM-based but learning-free CuPL (74.78%). Gains are most pronounced on fine-grained and OOD datasets: StanfordCars (77.44% H vs. 76.38% for OGEN), FGVC-Aircraft (40.91% vs. 40.75%), DTD (72.41% vs. 71.60%), and EuroSAT (84.79% vs. 84.25%). For image captioning, LiMBeR+AAPE (finetuned) reaches 57.8 CIDEr-D on COCO and 47.3 on NoCaps, surpassing both LiMBeR (54.9/43.9) and fully-finetuned MAGMA (47.5/38.7). Zero-shot AAPE also generalizes to VQA (43.1% at 4-shot) and image-to-text retrieval (90.8 R@1 on Flickr30k), competitive with CLIP and SigLIP.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AAPE outperforms all prompt-learning baselines that do not use language priors (CoOp, CoCoOp, MaPLe, CLIPood, PromptSRC, OGEN) by 0.6–9.3 points in harmonic mean, with the largest margin over CoOp (+9.31 H). It also beats methods that align with hand-written prompt templates (ProGrad, KgCoOp, LASP-V) by 1.5–4.8 points, demonstrating that LLM-generated natural language prompts provide richer priors than short templates like 'a dark photo of a {class}'. Against the closest LLM-based baseline CuPL (which averages GPT-3 prompts without learning), AAPE gains +6.19 H, confirming that the aggregate-and-adapt learning stage is essential. On domain generalization (Table 8), AAPE leads on 3 of 4 ImageNet variants (e.g., +0.67 on ImageNet-A, +0.87 on ImageNetV2) but is slightly behind PromptSRC on ImageNet-R (77.52 vs. 77.80). For image captioning and VQA, LiMBeR+AAPE (finetuned) surpasses both LiMBeR and the fully-finetuned MAGMA on all metrics (e.g., +3.9 CIDEr-D on COCO, +8.6 on NoCaps), while zero-shot AAPE already exceeds MAGMA's finetuned performance on NoCaps (45.1 vs. 38.7 CIDEr-D). On zero-shot image-to-text retrieval, AAPE (90.8 R@1) trails BLIP-2 (96.9) and Llip (93.2) but exceeds CLIP (88.0) and SigLIP (91.5) on R@1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CuPL line of work [39], which queries GPT-3 for class-level natural language prompts but uses them in a learning-free manner (simple averaging for zero-shot classification), by introducing a learned 'aggregate-and-adapt' pipeline: an attention-based prompt aggregator condenses noisy LLM prompts into an image-aligned summary, and a lightweight prompt generator distills from that summary while minimizing task loss. It also builds on LiMBeR [35] for generative tasks (captioning, VQA), where AAPE is appended as a prefix to the linearly-mapped image features. Conceptually, the paper departs from both standard prompt learners (CoOp, CoCoOp, MaPLe) that learn prompts without any external text knowledge, and from template-alignment methods (ProGrad, KgCoOp, LASP-V) that distill only from short hand-written templates, by showing that distilling from rich LLM-generated or human-annotated natural language prompts yields substantially better generalization, especially for under-represented and OOD concepts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: AAPE eliminates the LLM inference cost at test time that plagues prior LLM-augmented methods, while retaining the benefits of rich textual priors, making it deployable in latency-sensitive settings. The finding that 1-shot AAPE outperforms 16-shot baselines without any language knowledge highlights a data-efficiency advantage that could reduce annotation costs in specialized domains. Key limitations include the restriction to a single aggregated prompt embedding (m=1), which limits modeling of text diversity, and the current scope to CLIP-based models. Open questions include whether the multi-task distillation framework transfers to generative VLMs (e.g., PaLI, Flamingo), how to scale to diverse prompt sets without data-deficiency issues, and whether the observed modality-gap behavior (sometimes increasing, sometimes decreasing) reveals a deeper mechanism for generalization.
