## Problem

Emergent abilities in language models have been defined as capabilities that appear only in large models, but this framing is challenged by evidence that smaller models trained on more data can match or exceed larger ones, and by claims that apparent emergence is an artifact of discontinuous evaluation metrics. The fundamental relationship between pre-training loss and downstream task performance—particularly whether a loss threshold governs the onset of certain abilities—remains unexplored, leaving the mechanism behind emergent abilities unclear.

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-trained Transformer LMs of varying sizes (300M–32B parameters) trained on different token counts (33B–500B) with a fixed English-Chinese corpus (4:1 ratio), BPE tokenization (65k vocabulary), and LLaMA-like architecture, and evaluates them on 12 downstream tasks spanning six task types, two languages, three prompting types (zero-shot, few-shot, few-shot CoT), and two answer forms (multi-choice, open-formed). The key output is a performance-vs-loss curve where the x-axis is the scalar pre-training cross-entropy loss rather than model size or compute, revealing that all models collapse onto a single trending curve per task and that four tasks (MMLU, C-Eval, GSM8K, GSM8K-Chinese) exhibit a sharp threshold at loss ≈ 2.2 below which performance departs from random chance.

## Method

The paper pre-trains over 30 LMs from scratch using Megatron-LM with grouped-query attention and rotary position embeddings, varying model size and token count while holding corpus, tokenizer, and architecture fixed, then evaluates intermediate checkpoints (~every 43B tokens) on 12 English and Chinese benchmarks. The central empirical finding is that pre-training loss, not model size or training compute, is the predictive variable for downstream performance: data points from different-sized models at the same loss are indistinguishable on the performance-vs-loss plot, with Spearman correlations reaching −0.996 on most tasks. For four harder tasks (MMLU, C-Eval, GSM8K, GSM8K-Chinese), performance remains at random-guess level until loss drops below a shared threshold of approximately 2.2, after which it improves smoothly—behavior the authors confirm persists under continuous metrics (CorrectChoiceProb and Brier Score) rather than only discontinuous accuracy. The authors then formalize this as a new definition of emergent abilities: an ability is emergent if its normalized performance is zero for loss above a threshold η and follows a monotonically decreasing function f(L) below it, and they derive (via the Kaplan scaling law) how this loss-based definition reduces to the previously observed model-size-based emergence, explaining why smaller models trained on more tokens can also cross the threshold.

## Results

Across 30+ models (300M–32B parameters, 33B–3T tokens) evaluated on 12 English and Chinese datasets, pre-training loss is a strong predictor of downstream performance: Spearman correlations range from −0.996 (TriviaQA, HellaSwag) to −0.804 (MMLU), and Pearson correlations from −0.994 to −0.829. On four harder tasks (MMLU, C-Eval, GSM8K, GSM8K-Chinese), performance remains at random-guess level (≈25% accuracy for 4-choice, ≈0% for open-ended) until the pre-training loss drops below a threshold of approximately 2.2, after which it improves smoothly. This threshold is consistent across languages, task types, prompting formats, and answer forms, and persists under continuous metrics (CorrectChoiceProb, Brier Score), refuting the claim that emergent abilities are an artifact of discontinuous evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing methods but instead validates its loss-predicts-performance hypothesis against two external model families. LLaMA (7B–65B, English-only corpus, fairseq framework) and Pythia (multiple sizes, The Pile corpus) both show data points falling on the same performance-vs-loss curves as the authors' models, confirming generality across architectures, corpora, and training frameworks. Against Schaeffer et al.'s claim that emergent abilities are a mirage caused by discontinuous metrics, the authors demonstrate that the tipping point at loss ≈ 2.2 persists under continuous metrics (CorrectChoiceProb and Brier Score), and argue that Brier Score decreases above the threshold merely reflect the model learning the uniform answer distribution rather than genuine task improvement. Against Hoffmann et al.'s (Chinchilla) observation that smaller models with more data can outperform larger undertrained models, the authors show this is fully explained by the loss-based view: different model/data combinations reaching the same loss yield the same downstream performance, so the apparent 'emergence' with model size is simply a monotonic proxy for decreasing loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., Henighan et al., Hoffmann et al./Chinchilla) that established pre-training loss as a predictable function of model and data sizes, and on Wei et al.'s original formulation of emergent abilities as properties that appear only in sufficiently large models. It departs from both the model-size framing of emergent abilities and Schaeffer et al.'s claim that such abilities are artifacts of discontinuous metrics, by showing that a loss-based threshold (~2.2) universally separates random-guess performance from above-random performance across tasks, languages, and both continuous and discontinuous metrics. The paper also distinguishes itself from transfer-learning studies (Tay et al., Liu et al.) that examine pre-training loss in the finetuning paradigm, focusing instead on prompted (zero/few-shot) performance of under-trained LLMs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The loss-based definition of emergent abilities provides a more precise and architecture-agnostic characterization of the tipping points in training trajectories, with practical implications for estimating the compute required to unlock specific capabilities. The universality of the threshold across MMLU, C-Eval, GSM8K, and GSM8K-Chinese—spanning different languages, task types, and prompting strategies—is a strong empirical finding that unifies previously disparate observations. Key limitations include the restriction to a single architecture family (LLaMA-like Transformers with AdamW), a fixed tokenizer and corpus, and the absence of error bars due to computational cost. Open questions include whether fundamentally different architectures (routed Transformers, state-space models) or optimizers shift the threshold, and whether new tipping points emerge at scales beyond those studied.
