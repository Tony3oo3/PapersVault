## Problem

Diffusion models trained on finite datasets produce high-quality novel images that go well beyond memorizing training samples, yet the mechanism enabling this generalization remains unexplained. Existing theoretical analyses either assume infinite data or simplified distributions, failing to account for the finite-sample regime where real models operate. This paper asks: what hidden structural properties do learned score functions (diffusion denoisers) possess that drive generalization?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241832/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained EDM diffusion model (typically channel size 128, trained on FFHQ, CIFAR-10, AFHQ, or LSUN-Churches) and the corresponding training dataset. For each of 10 discrete noise levels from the EDM schedule, it trains a linear model W_{σ(t)}x + b_{σ(t)} via gradient descent to approximate the nonlinear denoiser D_θ on in-distribution inputs (clean images plus calibrated Gaussian noise). Outputs include the distilled weight matrices, linearity scores (cosine similarity of additivity/homogeneity), score approximation errors (RMSE) comparing D_θ against D_L, D_G, and D_M, and generated images from each model for qualitative comparison.

## Method

The paper first quantifies linearity of diffusion denoisers via a linearity score measuring additivity and homogeneity, revealing that denoisers become progressively more linear as models transition from memorization to generalization. It then extracts the hidden linear structure through linear distillation: for each noise level, a linear map W_{σ(t)}x + b_{σ(t)} is trained to minimize MSE against the nonlinear denoiser on in-distribution inputs. The key finding is that these distilled linear models closely match the optimal Gaussian denoiser D_G = μ + UΛ̃_{σ(t)}U^T(x−μ) (a Wiener filter defined by the empirical mean and covariance of the training data), both in weight matrices, score functions, and generated images. Theorem 1 proves that under a linear architecture constraint, the optimal solution to the denoising score matching objective is exactly this Gaussian denoiser. The paper further shows this Gaussian inductive bias is governed by the ratio of model capacity to dataset size and emerges during early training of overparameterized models, providing a mechanistic explanation for strong generalization across non-overlapping datasets.

## Results

In the generalization regime, diffusion denoisers exhibit linearity scores (cosine similarity) exceeding 0.96, with the distilled linear model D_L achieving score approximation error (RMSE) below 0.05 relative to the nonlinear denoiser D_θ in both the high-noise [20, 80] and low-noise [0.002, 0.1] regimes. In the critical intermediate-noise regime [0.1, 20], D_L and the Gaussian denoiser D_G maintain substantially lower approximation errors than the multi-delta denoiser D_M, and D_L and D_G achieve nearly identical score differences (RMSE < 0.05) across all noise levels. The normalized MSE between the distilled linear weights W_{σ(t)} and the Gaussian weights UΛ̃_{σ(t)}U^T converges below 0.2 for most noise levels, confirming that the optimal linear approximation of D_θ closely aligns with the Gaussian denoiser defined by the empirical mean and covariance of the training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the learned nonlinear diffusion denoiser D_θ against two analytical baselines: the multi-delta denoiser D_M (Gu et al. [24]) and the Gaussian denoiser D_G (Wang & Vastola [25]). Against D_M, the key finding is that D_M exhibits high score approximation error in the intermediate-noise regime [0.1, 20] where realistic image content is generated, because D_M rapidly converges to training samples (a memorization effect), while both D_L and D_G maintain substantially lower errors in this regime. Against D_G, the paper's central empirical result is that the optimal linear approximation D_L (obtained via linear distillation) is nearly identical to D_G across all noise levels, with score differences below 0.05 RMSE and normalized weight MSE below 0.2. This extends the finding of Wang & Vastola [25], who showed D_G approximates learned scores only at high noise variances, to the full noise range including the critical intermediate regime. The paper further shows that this D_L ≈ D_G alignment is unique to the generalization regime: in the memorization regime (small datasets), D_L significantly diverges from D_G, and both provide considerably poorer approximations of D_θ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the memorization-vs-generalization framework for diffusion models established by Kadkhodaie et al. [20] and Gu et al. [24], but departs from their mechanistic explanations: where [20] attributes strong generalization to geometry-adaptive harmonic bases and [24] characterizes memorization via multi-delta optimal denoisers, this work identifies the empirical Gaussian covariance structure as the key inductive bias. It also builds on Wang & Vastola [25], who showed learned scores approximate Gaussian scores at high noise, by demonstrating that the optimal *linear* approximation of learned denoisers matches Gaussian denoisers across a much wider noise range and providing a formal proof (Theorem 1) that the Wiener filter is the unique optimal linear denoiser under score matching. The linear distillation methodology is novel as an analytical tool for probing nonlinear diffusion denoisers.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, testable mechanistic account of why diffusion models generalize: they implicitly learn the empirical covariance structure of training data, and this Gaussian inductive bias is what enables generation of novel images rather than memorized replicas. Practically, it suggests early stopping and capacity reduction as effective strategies to promote generalization in overparameterized diffusion models, and it challenges the claim that strong generalization requires datasets of 10^5+ images by showing that hundreds of images already capture most of the relevant Gaussian structure. The central open question—why gradient descent on highly nonlinear networks converges to this particular linear/Gaussian solution rather than the true multi-delta optimum—remains unresolved, and the authors acknowledge that Gaussian structure only partially explains generalization, with nonlinearity still critical for perceptual quality.
