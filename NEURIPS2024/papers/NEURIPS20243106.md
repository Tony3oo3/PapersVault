## Problem

The widely used incremental-deletion protocol for evaluating attribution maps suffers from two fundamental limitations: deleting pixels introduces out-of-domain shifts that confound whether output changes reflect removed information or domain artifacts, and the protocol's dependence on raw model outputs prevents fair inter-model comparisons. As a result, how different architectural design choices (depth, width, batch normalization, bias terms, softmax placement) affect attribution quality has remained largely unexplored at scale.

## Input / Output

The protocol takes a pre-trained image classification model (ResNet, VGG, ViT, or intrinsically explainable variants), an RGB image from ImageNet, an attribution map computed for that image, and a baseline image (default: zeros after normalization). The image is divided into P=16 non-overlapping patches; each patch is individually replaced by the baseline, the model's target-class logit drop is measured, and the Spearman rank correlation between per-patch attribution sums and per-patch output drops is averaged over the full ImageNet validation set to yield a single scalar IDSDS score in [-1, 1]. A critical prerequisite is fine-tuning the model for 30 epochs with a data augmentation that replaces one random patch in 50% of training images with the baseline, ensuring train/test domain alignment.

## Method

IDSDS evaluates attribution correctness by measuring how well an attribution map's per-patch importance sums rank the true causal impact of each patch, as revealed by single-patch deletion. The key innovation over prior single-deletion protocols is an exact, attribution-method-independent alignment of train and test domains: the model is fine-tuned with a simple augmentation (one random patch replaced by the baseline in half the training images), which guarantees that the model has seen the same intervention distribution at test time. Because the masking is content-independent and class-independent, a formal proof shows zero mutual information between the mask and the class label, eliminating information leakage. This design makes the score invariant to model calibration (unlike incremental-deletion), enabling direct inter-model comparison. The paper uses this protocol to rank 23 attribution methods on ImageNet and to systematically vary backbone design choices (architecture, depth, width, BN, bias, pre/post-softmax), revealing that intrinsically explainable models (BagNet-33: 0.797) far outperform post-hoc methods (best: IG-U at 0.255), that raw attributions beat absolute ones, and that an accuracy-explainability trade-off exists.

## Results

On ImageNet with a ResNet-50 backbone, the proposed IDSDS protocol ranks 23 attribution methods. The intrinsically explainable BagNet-33 achieves the highest IDSDS of 0.797, followed by B-cos ResNet-50 at 0.314, both substantially outperforming the best standard attribution method (IG-U at 0.255). Among model-agnostic methods, Integrated Gradients with a uniform baseline (IG-U) leads at 0.255, while the best CAM-based methods (Grad-CAM and XGrad-CAM) reach 0.236. Notably, raw attribution values consistently outperform their absolute-value counterparts, contradicting prior findings from the incremental-deletion protocol.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central finding is that intrinsically explainable models dominate all post-hoc attribution methods: BagNet-33 (0.797) and B-cos ResNet-50 (0.314) far exceed the best standard method IG-U (0.255) by 0.54 and 0.06 IDSDS respectively. Among the 18 standard attribution methods, IG-U outperforms all other model-agnostic methods (IG at 0.196, I×G at 0.070) and all perturbation-based methods (RISE at 0.131, RISE-U at 0.065). Grad-CAM and XGrad-CAM tie as the best CAM-based methods (both 0.236), narrowly beating Grad-CAM++ (0.213) and Layer-CAM (0.192). SmoothGrad consistently degrades performance across all methods it is applied to (e.g., IG-SG drops from 0.196 to 0.049; SG-CAM++ drops to 0.102). In the protocol comparison (Figure 2c), IDSDS and the OOD single-deletion score (SDS) show a rank correlation of 0.89, but aligning train/test domains shifts rankings: IG-U becomes the top method under IDSDS while CAM-based methods lead under SDS and IDS. The incremental-deletion score (IDS) is the only protocol where absolute attributions strictly outperform raw ones, explaining the apparent contradiction with prior work [66].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the single-deletion evaluation lineage from Alvarez-Melis & Jaakkola [3] and Selvaraju et al. [55], which measure rank correlation between deleted-region output drops and attribution scores, by adding exact train/test domain alignment via a simple data augmentation (randomly zeroing one patch in half of training images). It departs from the dominant incremental-deletion protocol [54] and its domain-alignment variant ROAR [29] by showing that a single fine-tuned model can fairly compare multiple attribution methods in-domain, without the per-method retraining or class-information leakage that plagues prior approaches. The protocol is positioned as the first to simultaneously achieve domain alignment, zero information leakage, inter-model comparability, and operation on natural images (Table 1).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first large-scale empirical confirmation of the accuracy-explainability trade-off and actionable design guidance (remove BN and bias terms, use pre-softmax attributions, prefer shallower/narrower networks) for practitioners seeking more explainable vision models. The finding that intrinsically explainable architectures (BagNet-33 at 0.797 IDSDS) outperform the best post-hoc method (IG-U at 0.255) by over 3× suggests the field should invest more in explainable-by-design models rather than improving post-hoc attribution. Open questions include whether the patch-level granularity (P=16) captures sufficient spatial detail, whether the fine-tuning requirement limits adoption, and whether the accuracy-explainability trade-off can be mitigated by the specific architectural choices identified here.
