## Problem

Dynamic Bayesian Optimization (DBO) must track a moving optimum over time, but past observations become progressively less informative, creating a tension between maintaining predictive accuracy (which requires a large dataset) and achieving a high sampling frequency (which requires a small dataset to keep O(n³) GP inference fast). Existing DBO algorithms either retain all observations indefinitely or reset the entire dataset periodically, and no method exists to quantify the individual relevancy of each observation in an online setting for selective, fine-grained removal.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes a time-varying black-box function f(x,t) queried only at the current time t₀, a GP surrogate with a decomposable isotropic spatio-temporal covariance k((x,t),(x',t')) = λ·k_S(‖x−x'‖₂, l_S)·k_T(|t−t'|, l_T), a standard acquisition function (e.g., GP-UCB), and a single hyperparameter α that controls the exponential removal budget. At each iteration it outputs the next spatial query x_{n+1} at time t₀, an updated (possibly pruned) observation dataset, and the re-estimated GP posterior with MLE hyperparameters (λ, l_S, l_T, σ²). The removal criterion is evaluated in parallel over all observations at every step, making the per-iteration cost scale linearly with dataset size rather than cubically.

## Method

The paper introduces a normalized 2-Wasserstein distance criterion R(GP_D, GP_D̃) = W₂(GP_D, GP_D̃) / W₂(GP_D, GP_∅) that measures the relative drift in the GP posterior over the future prediction domain F_{t₀} = S × [t₀, ∞) caused by removing one observation, normalized by the drift from resetting the entire dataset to cancel out hyperparameter dependence. To make this computable online, they derive an explicit upper bound (Theorem 4.1) that replaces the intractable integrals over F_{t₀} with self-convolutions of the spatial and temporal covariance functions (k_S * k_S and k_T * k_T restricted to [t₀−t_i, ∞)), for which closed-form expressions are provided for both Squared-Exponential and Matérn kernels. The W-DBO algorithm (Algorithm 1) then applies a greedy removal policy: at each iteration it computes the approximated relevancy R̂_i for every observation in parallel, removes the least relevant observation if its cost fits within the current removal budget b, and updates b multiplicatively. The budget grows as b ← b·(1+α)^{(t−t')/l_T} between iterations, allowing a controlled relative drift of α per temporal lengthscale, which the sensitivity analysis shows is robust across diverse benchmarks with α = 1/4.

## Results

The primary metric is average regret (lower is better) over 10 independent replications, evaluated on 12 benchmarks (10 synthetic functions + 2 real-world: Temperature and WLAN). W-DBO achieves a normalized average performance of 0.01, compared to 0.29 for the next-best method (TV-GP-UCB), 0.47 for R-GP-UCB, 0.48 for vanilla GP-UCB, 0.54 for ET-GP-UCB, and 0.62 for ABO. On the most challenging benchmarks the gap is largest: on Powell(4), W-DBO achieves 428.1 regret versus 1223.4 (TV-GP-UCB) and 9888.6 (ABO); on Rosenbrock(3), W-DBO achieves 3.81 versus 17.55 (TV-GP-UCB) and 171.04 (ABO). W-DBO obtains the lowest average regret on 11 out of 12 benchmarks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

W-DBO outperforms all four baseline families across nearly every benchmark. Against TV-GP-UCB (Bogunovic et al., AISTATS 2016), the closest competitor, W-DBO reduces average regret by roughly 3–10× on the hardest functions (e.g., 428.1 vs. 1223.4 on Powell, 3.81 vs. 17.55 on Rosenbrock) and by ~30% on easier ones (e.g., 2.24 vs. 3.27 on Ackley). Against R-GP-UCB (same paper, periodic dataset reset), W-DBO is consistently better, with the gap widening on high-dimensional or rapidly changing functions (e.g., 290.34 vs. 954.03 on Schwefel). Against ET-GP-UCB (Brunzema et al., 2022, event-triggered reset), W-DBO dominates on 11/12 benchmarks; ET-GP-UCB's event-triggered mechanism fails to adapt when the function changes gradually. Against ABO (Nyikosa et al., 2018, adaptive sampling frequency), W-DBO is dramatically superior on most benchmarks (e.g., 428.1 vs. 9888.6 on Powell, 1.19 vs. 12.94 on WLAN), because ABO only reduces the rate of adding observations but never removes stale ones, causing its dataset to grow unboundedly and its response time to become prohibitive. Vanilla GP-UCB (Srinivas et al., 2012), which ignores temporal structure entirely, is outperformed by W-DBO on all 12 benchmarks, confirming that explicit spatio-temporal modeling plus observation pruning is essential in dynamic settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dynamic Bayesian optimization (DBO) line of work initiated by ABO [20], R-GP-UCB/TV-GP-UCB [21], and ET-GP-UCB [22], all of which handle data staleness through periodic or event-triggered dataset resets or decreasing covariance weights. The key departure is the introduction of a Wasserstein-distance-based criterion that quantifies the individual relevancy of each observation with respect to future predictions, drawing on the 2-Wasserstein metric for GPs [29] and the self-convolution structure of spatio-temporal covariance functions. Unlike prior DBO methods that work in discrete time with fixed hyperparameters, W-DBO operates in continuous time with unknown horizon and estimates all covariance hyperparameters on the fly, making it applicable to arbitrarily long optimization tasks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ability to selectively remove stale observations on the fly is essential for long-horizon DBO tasks where unbounded dataset growth would make GP inference (O(n³)) prohibitively slow, directly degrading the sampling frequency needed to track a moving optimum. The method is agnostic to the underlying acquisition function and GP model, making it a drop-in post-processing stage for any existing BO pipeline. Open questions include deriving regret bounds in continuous time that account for both sampling frequency and observation deletion, and extending the approach to real-world DBO applications (networks, robotics). A limitation is that the greedy removal policy overestimates the cumulative impact of deleting multiple observations, and the approximation (12) introduces a small but non-zero error relative to the exact criterion (9).
