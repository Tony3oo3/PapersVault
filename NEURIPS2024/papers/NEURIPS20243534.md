## Problem

Foundation models are increasingly deployed for medical image classification and segmentation, yet no existing benchmark provides a standardized pipeline to evaluate their fairness across comprehensive model families, data modalities, downstream tasks, and sensitive attributes. Prior fairness studies in medical imaging focus on traditional deep learning models or examine only a narrow subset of FMs, modalities, or evaluation metrics, leaving the fairness landscape of modern FMs largely uncharacterized.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243534/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For classification, 2D images are resized to 224×224 (ImageNet-normalized) and 3D volumes are processed as 2.5D slices (32 slices along the longitudinal axis) fed into 2D FMs, with volume-wise predictions obtained by maximizing slice-level outputs. For segmentation, 2D images are resized to 1024×1024 and 3D volumes are normalized to [0,1]; prompts (center, rand, rands, bbox) are generated from ground-truth masks and fed to SAM-family models. Sensitive attribute labels (sex, age, race, language, BMI, handedness, marital status) define binary subgroups, and the pipeline outputs per-subgroup utility (AUC, DSC), fairness gaps (AUC_Δ, DSC_Δ, EqOdds, ECE_Δ, DP), and equity-scaled tradeoff scores (AUC_ES, DSC_ES).

## Method

FairMedFM is a standardized benchmarking pipeline rather than a new model architecture. It integrates 17 public medical imaging datasets (9 classification, 8 segmentation) spanning 2D/2.5D/3D dimensions and seven modalities, with 20 FMs: 11 classification models (5 vision models like DINOv2, MedMAE, C2L and 6 vision-language models like CLIP, MedCLIP, BiomedCLIP) evaluated under zero-shot, linear probing, CLIP-Adapt, and PEFT (LoRA, pruning) protocols, and 9 segmentation models (3 general 2D, 3 medical 2D, 3 medical 3D SAM-family variants) evaluated under four prompt types. The framework incorporates 9 fairness metrics organized into outcome-consistency, predictive-alignment, positive-parity, and representation fairness categories, plus 6 bias mitigation algorithms (group rebalancing, adversarial training, fairness constraints, subgroup-tailored modeling, domain generalization, and resampling). The key empirical contribution is a systematic analysis across all combinations, revealing that bias is prevalent (average AUC_Δ > 5%), that fairness-utility tradeoffs depend on both model choice and usage protocol (e.g., CLIP-Adapt outperforms CLIP-ZS), that dataset-specific SA disparities are consistent across FMs, and that existing mitigation strategies show limited or inconsistent effectiveness in PEFT settings.

## Results

Across 17 medical imaging datasets and 20 foundation models, bias is pervasive: the average AUC_Δ exceeds 5% in classification, and DSC_Δ reaches up to 10% for SegVol and FT-SAM in segmentation. In the bias mitigation evaluation (Table 2), LoRA achieves the highest classification utility (AUC_Avg = 82.52%) but with the worst fairness gap (AUC_Δ = 1.95%), while LP is fairer (AUC_Δ = 0.53%) at lower utility (76.07%). For segmentation, SAMed+FEBS achieves the best fairness-utility trade-off (DSC_ES = 91.62%, DSC_Δ = 0.02%), whereas SAMed+InD degrades both utility (88.85% vs. 90.92%) and fairness (DSC_Δ = 1.42 vs. 0.10) relative to the SAMed baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This benchmark paper does not propose a new method but systematically compares 20 FMs across multiple usages and 6 mitigation strategies. In classification, LoRA substantially outperforms LP in utility (AUC 82.52% vs. 76.07%) but introduces a 3.7× larger fairness gap (AUC_Δ 1.95% vs. 0.53%); adding GroupDRO to LoRA reduces the gap to 1.22% but drops AUC_ES from 81.72 to 79.06, showing the mitigation is counterproductive in the PEFT setting. CLIP-Adapt consistently improves the fairness-utility trade-off over CLIP-ZS across all VLMs. In segmentation, SAMed+FEBS achieves near-perfect group parity (DSC_Δ = 0.02%) while also improving utility over SAMed (91.63% vs. 90.92%), making it the only mitigation that wins on both axes; conversely, SAMed+InD (domain generalization) degrades both utility (−2.07%) and fairness (DSC_Δ 1.42 vs. 0.10). For prompts, rands and bbox yield fairer segmentations than center or rand, and general-purpose SegFMs (SAM, MobileSAM, TinySAM) achieve better fairness-utility trade-offs than medical-specific ones (MedSAM, SAM-Med2D, FT-SAM). Statistical tests (Friedman/CD) confirm no single FM significantly outperforms others in overall utility or fairness, indicating that dataset-specific biases dominate over model-specific ones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the medical imaging fairness benchmarking line (MedFAIR, CXR-Fairness, RadFusion, Fair-Tune) by shifting the focus from traditional deep learning models to Foundation Models, which prior benchmarks largely excluded. It repurposes existing FM architectures (SAM family for segmentation, CLIP/BLIP/DINOv2 families for classification) and established fairness metrics and mitigation algorithms into a unified, extensible pipeline. The key conceptual departure is treating FM-specific usages (zero-shot, linear probing, PEFT, prompt-based segmentation) as first-class evaluation axes, rather than evaluating a single fine-tuned model as in prior fairness benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As FMs become the default architecture for medical imaging deployment, this benchmark provides the first comprehensive, reproducible pipeline to audit their fairness across 17 datasets, 20 models, and multiple sensitive attributes before clinical use. The finding that established bias-mitigation strategies (GroupDRO, LAFTR, InD) fail to improve utility-fairness trade-offs in FM fine-tuning settings is a practical warning that the field cannot simply port small-model fairness solutions to FMs. Open questions include whether the consistent dataset-level disparities observed across FMs point to irreducible data bias, how findings generalize to emerging FM architectures, and whether segmentation-specific fairness (prompt sensitivity, 3D volume effects) admits effective mitigation.
