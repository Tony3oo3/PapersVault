## Problem

Existing conformal factuality methods for LLMs suffer from two practical deficiencies: their validity guarantees hold only marginally over random prompts (so trustworthiness varies substantially by topic), and their threshold-based filtering removes too many accurate claims because the underlying scoring functions are only weakly correlated with ground truth. This paper addresses both gaps by introducing conditionally valid, level-adaptive conformal filtering and a differentiable procedure for automatically improving the scoring function.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243644/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an LLM response parsed into self-contained sub-claims (via GPT-4o), a prompt, per-claim confidence scores from one or more scoring functions, and a calibration set of prompt-response-claim-annotation tuples. It outputs a filtered subset of claims together with an output-adaptive probability of correctness (e.g., 63% rather than a fixed 90%) and a group-conditional calibration guarantee stating that among similar prompts, the issued probability matches the realized factuality rate. The adaptive level is learned from data to meet a user-specified quality criterion such as retaining at least 70% of original claims.

## Method

The paper contributes two complementary techniques built on the conditional conformal framework of Gibbs et al. (2023). First, level-adaptive conformal prediction replaces the fixed pinball loss in the augmented quantile regression with a data-dependent loss ℓ_{α(X_i)}, allowing the error rate to vary per prompt while preserving a group-conditional calibration guarantee over a user-specified function class. Second, conditional boosting optimizes a parameterized scoring function by differentiating through the conditional conformal quantile regression: the key technical result (Proposition 3.1) shows that the filtering threshold is locally linear in the score parameters via the LP optimal basis, enabling backpropagation through the conformal procedure. The boosting objective maximizes retained claims on a hold-out set, with the non-differentiable indicator approximated by a sigmoid, and the entire conformal calibration is replicated at each gradient step. Together, these methods retain substantially more claims (39% vs. 24% mean retention on MedLFQA) while issuing well-calibrated, topic-adaptive validity guarantees.

## Results

On the MedLFQA medical QA benchmark, conditional boosting improves mean claim retention from 24% (unboosted frequency score) to 39% at a fixed error rate of α=0.1, while the level-adaptive method retains at least 70% of original claims with issued correctness probabilities ranging from 50% to 85%. On the Wikipedia biographies dataset, the level-adaptive method retains at least 80% of claims while guaranteeing at most 3 factual errors, and the conditional conformal method achieves accurate coverage across all Wikipedia page-popularity groups. On a synthetic heteroskedastic regression task, conditional boosting attains near-perfect conditional coverage, whereas marginal boosting (Stutz et al.) exhibits severely degraded coverage on high-variance subsets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the split conformal factuality method of Mohri and Hashimoto [21], the paper's conditional conformal method wins decisively on conditional validity: Figure 4 shows that the split conformal baseline exhibits substantially higher miscoverage for rare (low-view-count) Wikipedia topics, while the conditional method maintains accurate coverage uniformly across all popularity groups. The conditional method also retains more claims for popular topics and fewer for rare ones, adapting the filtering threshold to the prompt's characteristics. Against the marginal boosting procedure of Stutz et al. [27] (ConfTr), the paper's conditional boosting method wins on conditional coverage: Figure 7 shows that ConfTr produces prediction sets with extremely poor coverage on high-variance data subsets, while conditional boosting achieves near-perfect conditional coverage at comparable or slightly larger set sizes. On the MedLFQA benchmark, the boosted scores outperform the unboosted frequency score by 15 percentage points in mean claim retention (39% vs. 24%) at the same fixed error rate, and when combined with level-adaptive CP, the boosted method issues stronger (higher-confidence) guarantees at the same 70% retention target.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conditional conformal prediction framework of Gibbs et al. (2023) from prediction sets to LLM claim filtering, generalizing the conformal factuality method of Mohri and Hashimoto (2024) from marginal to group-conditional guarantees. It also extends the ConfTr score-optimization procedure of Stutz et al. (2021) by proving differentiability of the conditional conformal cutoff, enabling gradient-based improvement of scoring functions under conditional (rather than marginal) coverage constraints. A novel level-adaptive conformal prediction procedure is introduced, allowing the validity level to vary per prompt to preserve output utility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses two practical barriers to deploying conformal factuality guarantees: marginal-only guarantees that degrade on underrepresented topics, and excessive claim removal that renders filtered outputs useless. The conditional boosting and level-adaptive methods together enable calibrated, topic-aware guarantees while retaining 70%+ of original claims, making conformal LLM safety more deployable. Key limitations include the i.i.d. assumption (no robustness to distribution shift), dependence on the quality of underlying claim scores, and sensitivity to the function class choice for conditional calibration. Open questions include extending guarantees to non-i.i.d. settings, discovering better hallucination-predictive features, and understanding the trade-off between function class complexity, calibration quality, and computational cost.
