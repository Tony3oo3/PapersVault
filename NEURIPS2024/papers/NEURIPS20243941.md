## Problem

Standard RLHF assumes human preference labels are correct, but annotators may provide inconsistent or intentionally misleading labels due to bias, inexperience, or malicious intent. This corruption can distort the learned reward and degrade policy performance, yet the robustness of RLHF to corrupted preference data has received limited attention in the literature.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243941/cropped_page6_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes preference data pairs—either trajectory segments (for robotic control) or state-action pairs with binary preference labels (for NLP tasks)—where a sparse subset of labels may be corrupted. It outputs a learned reward function, a sparse perturbation vector δ that flags outlier samples (nonzero entries correspond to corrupted labels), and an optimized policy. The perturbation factors are deterministic and instance-specific, and the ℓ1 norm of δ controls how many samples are identified as outliers via the tuning parameter λ.

## Method

R³M introduces an instance-specific perturbation factor δ_i into the Bradley-Terry preference model, so that p(z_w ≻ z_l) = σ(r(z_w) − r(z_l) + δ_i), and jointly learns the reward and the sparse δ vector by minimizing an ℓ1-regularized negative log-likelihood. The key computational insight is that the log-likelihood is strictly convex and univariate in each δ_i, yielding a closed-form soft-thresholding update δ_i = max{log(1/λ − 1) − r(z_w) + r(z_l), 0} at every iteration, so the alternating optimization incurs negligible overhead over standard RLHF. The approach extends naturally to DPO by substituting the implicit reward r_π(a|s) = log(π(a|s)/π_ref(a|s)) into the same regularized objective. Theoretically, under a sparsity assumption (‖δ*‖₀ ≤ s with s sublinear in n) and bounded reward, the estimator achieves the same convergence rate as clean-data MLE for reward recovery while consistently identifying the outlier set.

## Results

On the dialogue task, R³M-DPO achieves a 63.5% win rate (winning score 1.506) against the instruction-tuned reference model, outperforming DPO (57.2%), SLiC-HF (62.58%), and IPO (53.62%). On the summarization task, R³M-DPO reaches 62.29% win rate (winning score 1.504), surpassing DPO (59.95%), SLiC-HF (59.5%), and IPO (51.91%), though slightly below the Data Filtering baseline (63.20%) which incurs double the training cost. On robotic control (HalfCheetah, Ant, Hopper), R³M consistently outperforms the standard cross-entropy loss baseline across all three noise models (stochastic, myopic, irrational) and all noise rates, with the sole exception being the mildest irrational noise setting (p=1/3, ~6.25% corruption).

## Baselines & Comparisons

R³M-DPO outperforms DPO on both tasks: by 6.3 percentage points in win rate on dialogue (63.5% vs. 57.2%) and by 2.3 points on summarization (62.29% vs. 59.95%). Against SLiC-HF, R³M-DPO wins on dialogue (63.5% vs. 62.58%) and summarization (62.29% vs. 59.5%), with the summarization gap being more substantial. Against IPO, R³M-DPO achieves the largest margins, exceeding it by roughly 10 points on dialogue (63.5% vs. 53.62%) and 10.4 points on summarization (62.29% vs. 51.91%). The Data Filtering baseline slightly edges out R³M-DPO on summarization (63.20% vs. 62.29%) but requires twice the computational cost, and underperforms on dialogue (53.33% vs. 63.5%). The paper also reports that the learned sparsity rates are 1.2% for dialogue and 10.8% for summarization, suggesting the summarization dataset contains more noisy preferences, consistent with the larger improvement R³M-DPO shows over DPO on that task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bradley-Terry preference model by introducing instance-specific deterministic perturbation factors to capture corrupted human labels, then applies ℓ1-regularized maximum likelihood estimation (analogous to Lasso) to jointly recover the reward and identify sparse outliers. It departs from prior robust RLHF work [12, 25], which filters corrupted data based on label confidence, by instead modeling corruption explicitly within the likelihood and solving via alternating optimization with a closed-form update for each perturbation factor. The extension to DPO [29] connects the robust reward-learning framework to the increasingly popular preference-optimization paradigm that bypasses explicit reward modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As RLHF becomes the dominant alignment mechanism for large language models, robustness to imperfect human annotations is a practical necessity that has received limited theoretical treatment; this paper provides the first consistency guarantees showing that reward recovery is unaffected by sparse corruption. The method's negligible computational overhead (a univariate closed-form step per sample) makes it drop-in compatible with existing PPO and DPO pipelines. Open questions include what happens when corruption is dense or non-sparse, how to handle non-smooth reward regions (discussed but not solved), and whether the deterministic-perturbation assumption holds in adversarial annotation settings.
