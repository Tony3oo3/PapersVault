## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks evaluate vision-language models on adult-level tasks without direct comparison to child behavioral data, making it impossible to determine whether models trained on developmentally realistic data actually learn like children. No prior benchmark simultaneously spans multiple linguistic levels (lexicon, syntax, semantics), includes corresponding child data across a wide age range, and uses evaluation methods that match how children are tested (e.g., nonverbal looking responses).

## Input / Output

For each of the seven tasks, models receive image-text pairs (2 or 4 images with a verbal cue for lexicon/syntax), image pairs (visual semantic tasks), or text cues (word association), and produce per-item logits or embedding similarity scores. These are converted into model-human similarity metrics: a softmax-optimized KL divergence (lower = more human-like) for lexicon, syntax, and word association tasks, and a Spearman RSA correlation (higher = more human-like) for visual semantic tasks. Human response distributions are available at item level for children aged 4 months through 12 years and adults, enabling age-binned comparisons that reveal developmental trends.

## Method

DEVBENCH comprises seven tasks across three linguistic domains—two lexical (looking-while-listening, visual vocabulary), two syntactic (TROG, Winoground-NoTag), and three semantic (word association, visual object categorization, THINGS similarity)—each with item-level human behavioral data from children and adults. The key methodological innovation is the softmax-optimized KL divergence, which finds the temperature β that minimizes KL divergence between human response probability distributions and softmaxed model logits, capturing response-pattern similarity rather than absolute accuracy. For visual semantic tasks, representational similarity analysis (RSA) compares pairwise cosine-similarity matrices between humans and models. The paper evaluates nine similarity models (CLIP, OpenCLIP-H, SigLIP, CVCL, etc.) and five generation models (LLaVA, TinyLLaVA, Kosmos-2, etc. via next-token prediction and log-likelihood), and traces OpenCLIP-H's developmental trajectory across 32 training checkpoints to identify when models become more or less human-like across tasks.

## Results

Across seven developmental language tasks, CLIP-large and OpenCLIP-H achieve the lowest (most human-like) softmax-optimized KL divergences on lexicon and syntax tasks (e.g., CLIP-large: 0.013 on LWL, 0.179 on VV, 0.692 on TROG; OpenCLIP-H: 0.012, 0.188, 0.683 respectively), while CVCL—trained on a single child's egocentric video—achieves the highest RSA similarity to infant visual object categorization (0.138 vs. 0.031 for OpenCLIP-H). Model-human similarity correlates most strongly with task accuracy (r ≈ 0.82–0.99 across tasks), followed by model size (r ≈ 0.25–0.85), and weakest with training data size (r ≈ −0.07 to 0.69). OpenCLIP-H training trajectories show increasing human-likeness over 32 checkpoints, recovering developmental trends where earlier checkpoints resemble younger children and later checkpoints resemble adults.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among similarity-based models, CLIP-large and OpenCLIP-H are the most human-like on lexicon and syntax tasks, with CLIP-large achieving the lowest VV divergence (0.179) and OpenCLIP-H the lowest TROG divergence (0.683); BridgeTower edges out on LWL (0.008) and WG (0.250). CVCL, trained on only 600K frames from a single child's head-mounted camera, is the worst-performing model on lexicon and syntax (VV = 0.740, TROG = 0.911) yet uniquely achieves the highest RSA similarity to infant visual object categorization (0.138), suggesting its visual space aligns with preverbal infants despite poor language performance. SigLIP is a clear outlier (VV = 0.612, TROG = 0.888) and was excluded from further analyses, potentially due to its sigmoid loss objective. Among generation models evaluated via next-token prediction, TinyLLaVA (3.1B) substantially outperforms LLaVA (7B), Kosmos-2 (1.6B), moondream2 (1.9B), and CogVLM (17B) on both VV (0.246 vs. 0.447–0.468) and TROG (0.410 vs. 0.757–0.910), indicating that model size does not guarantee human-likeness in generative settings. Overall, no model approaches the human baseline (VV = 0.091, TROG = 0.028), and the gap between the best model and human performance remains large, particularly on syntax tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of developmentally-inspired ML evaluation (LRS, InfLevel, MEWL, ModelVsBaby, Zorro) by being the first benchmark to simultaneously combine multimodality, child behavioral data, multiple linguistic levels (lexicon, syntax, semantics), and direct human-model response distribution comparison. It departs from prior multimodal benchmarks (MMBench, GEM, MME) that focus on perception and reasoning rather than linguistic ability specifically. Methodologically, it builds on representational similarity analysis (Kriegeskorte et al.) for visual semantic tasks and introduces a novel softmax-optimised KL divergence metric for comparing model logits to human choice distributions, while extending model trajectory analyses from Chang & Bergen and Evanson et al. to span multiple linguistic domains simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DEVBENCH provides the first framework for asking whether models trained on developmentally realistic data actually learn like children, rather than merely achieving adult-level accuracy. The finding that model-human similarity tracks accuracy (not size or training data volume) and that better models match older children while worse models match younger children suggests a potential developmental correspondence, but the THINGS result—where contrastive training actually decreases visual representational similarity to humans—challenges the assumption that more training universally improves human-likeness. Key limitations include small participant samples (e.g., N=73 for VOC), English-only data, and inability to hold model architectures constant, leaving open whether the observed patterns generalize across languages, architectures, and training paradigms. The benchmark also highlights the unresolved methodological challenge of evaluating generation models (LLaVA, CogVLM, etc.) on tasks designed for similarity models.
