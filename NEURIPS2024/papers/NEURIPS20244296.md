## Problem

Selecting the best CATE estimator from a pool of candidates is impractical with conventional validation because ground-truth CATE labels are unavailable. Existing plug-in and pseudo-outcome metrics require fitting nuisance parameters (outcome models, propensity scores, plug-in learners) whose choice is itself a model-selection problem, and neither metric explicitly targets robustness to the distribution shift between factual and counterfactual covariate distributions.

## Input / Output

The DRM takes J pre-trained CATE estimators and a validation set of observational samples (X, T, Y) as input, requiring no additional nuisance model fitting. The ambiguity radius ε is set from the empirically estimated KL divergence between covariate distributions in the treated and control groups (under unconfoundedness), and the method outputs a per-estimator robustness score R^DRM(τ̂) from which the selected estimator is the argmin. The only tuning parameters are the ambiguity radii ε₀, ε₁ and the Newton-Raphson iteration count K.

## Method

The core idea decomposes PEHE into empirically computable terms and two uncomputable cross-distribution expectations, then bounds the latter via KL ambiguity sets centered at the factual distribution. Theorem 4.4 exploits the KL-DRO duality (Hu & Hong, 2013) to convert the infeasible supremum over the ambiguity set into a one-dimensional convex minimization over a temperature parameter λ, solved by Newton-Raphson. The final DRM metric (Eq. 15) combines the sample-mean squared prediction, the factual cross-terms, and the two distributionally robust values V̂⁰ and V̂¹, yielding a nuisance-free score that penalizes estimators whose predictions are sensitive to distribution shift. Proposition 4.6 further shows that under unconfoundedness the ideal ambiguity radius equals the KL divergence between treated and control covariate distributions, making the radius data-driven without any nuisance estimation. A finite-sample bound (Theorem 4.5) guarantees the empirical robust value converges to its population counterpart at rate n^{-1/2}.

## Results

On the ACIC2016 semi-synthetic benchmark (36 CATE estimators, 14 selectors, 100 runs), DRM achieves the lowest Regret across nearly all settings: 0.96±3.67 (Setting A, ρ=0), 0.84±4.83 (ρ=0.1), 1.25±5.97 (ρ=0.3), 0.38±1.39 (Setting B, ξ=0), and 1.56/1.40/1.26 (Setting C, m=0.1/0.5/0.9). In the hidden-confounder setting (C), DRM's advantage is most dramatic, outperforming the best baseline (Plug-R) by roughly 20–70% in Regret. In Spearman rank correlation (Table 2), DRM achieves 0.77–0.85, competitive with but slightly below the best nuisance-based selectors (Plug-R at 0.93–0.96, Plug-S at 0.91–0.95), reflecting its worst-case (robust) rather than average-performance objective.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DRM outperforms all 14 baselines in Regret across the majority of settings. Against the strongest nuisance-based baselines (Plug-R from Nie & Wager [55], Plug-S/PS from Curth & van der Schaar [20]), DRM achieves roughly 2× lower Regret in Setting A (0.96 vs. 1.92 for Plug-R at ρ=0) and a 3–4× advantage in Setting C with hidden confounders (1.26 vs. 2.12 for Plug-R at m=0.9). Against the X-learner [47], DR-learner [41], and RA-learner [18] plug-in selectors, DRM's advantage is even more pronounced (e.g., 0.96 vs. 8.10 for Plug-X, 0.96 vs. 43.11 for Plug-DR, 0.96 vs. 56.60 for Plug-RA at Setting A ρ=0). Pseudo-outcome baselines (Pseudo-DR from Saito & Yasui [65], Pseudo-IF from Alaa & van der Schaar [5]) and the Nearest-Neighbor Matching baseline [62] are consistently dominated by DRM by large margins (e.g., 0.96 vs. 61.35 for Pseudo-DR, 0.96 vs. 64.54 for Pseudo-IF). The one setting where DRM does not clearly win is Setting B with ξ=0 (no selection bias), where the simple Factual selector (14.33) and Plug-S (0.22) perform comparably or better, since there is no distribution shift to be robust against. In Spearman rank correlation, DRM (0.77–0.85) trails Plug-R (0.93–0.96) and Plug-S (0.91–0.95), which the authors attribute to DRM optimizing worst-case rather than average performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the KL-divergence-constrained distributionally robust optimization framework of Hu and Hong (2013), directly applying their duality result (Theorem 1) to reformulate intractable supremum problems over ambiguity sets into tractable minimizations. It departs from the established CATE estimator selection literature (Curth & van der Schaar 2023, Schuler et al. 2018, Mahajan et al. 2024), which relies on plug-in or pseudo-outcome metrics requiring fitted nuisance parameters (outcome models, propensity scores, plug-in learners). The key conceptual departure is making the selection criterion nuisance-free while explicitly targeting worst-case performance under distribution shift between factual and counterfactual distributions, rather than average-case PEHE.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The nuisance-free property eliminates a major practical bottleneck in CATE estimator selection, where choosing appropriate ML models for nuisance parameters can be as difficult as the original selection problem. The demonstrated robustness under hidden confounders (Setting C) is practically significant for observational studies where unconfoundedness is often violated. However, the method's ranking ability is notably weaker than several plug-in baselines (the authors acknowledge this tension between worst-case and average-case optimization), the ambiguity radius requires KL-divergence approximation that may be biased, and all experiments are on semi-synthetic data from a single source (ACIC2016). Open questions include whether Wasserstein-based ambiguity sets yield tighter bounds, how the method performs with more complex CATE functions and larger sample sizes, and its applicability to real-world healthcare or economics data.
