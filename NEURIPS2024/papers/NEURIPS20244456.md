## Problem

Accuracy-fairness trade-offs are inherently dataset-dependent, yet existing methods impose uniform fairness thresholds and require training hundreds of models to approximate the trade-off curve, making them computationally infeasible for large-scale settings. Moreover, these methods ignore finite-sampling uncertainty, so apparent suboptimality in a baseline's empirical trade-off may be an artifact of the particular test split rather than a genuine deficiency. The paper asks: given a dataset, what is the statistically valid range of permissible fairness violations at each accuracy level for a given model class?

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training set of (X, A, Y) triples (features, sensitive attribute, binary label) and a disjoint held-out calibration set D_cal (typically a 10% split) that must include sensitive attributes for CI construction. At inference time, the user specifies an accuracy threshold ψ and receives a confidence interval [l(ψ), u(ψ)] on the fairness violation, valid with probability ≥ 1−α over draws of D_cal. The framework is modality-agnostic, demonstrated on tabular (Adult, COMPAS), image (CelebA), and text (Jigsaw) datasets, and extends to settings where sensitive attributes are partially missing via a predictive imputation model.

## Method

The approach is a two-step pipeline. Step 1 adapts the YOTO framework to fairness: a single neural network h_θ(X, λ) is trained with FiLM conditioning layers on the regularized loss L_λ = CE + λ·L_fair, where λ is sampled from a distribution P_λ during training, so that at inference time plugging in different λ values recovers the full trade-off curve without retraining (≈40× speedup over grid-based training). Step 2 constructs CIs on τ_fair*(ψ) using the calibration data: upper CIs follow from Proposition 3.2 (a union-bound argument requiring no optimality assumption on the trained model), while lower CIs follow from Proposition 3.3, which introduces a Δ(h_λ) gap term between the YOTO model's achieved fairness and the true optimum. This unknown Δ is handled via a lightweight sensitivity analysis that trains only 2–5 additional standard models at random λ values and shifts the lower CI down by the maximum observed empirical gap, leaving CIs unchanged when YOTO is already Pareto-optimal.

## Results

Across four datasets (Adult, COMPAS, CelebA, Jigsaw) and three fairness metrics (DP, EO, EOP), the YOTO method places 100% of its empirical trade-offs in the 'Permissible' region with 0% in 'Sub-optimal' or 'Unlikely' regions, while requiring only 1 model (105 min) versus ~40 models (~100 min each) for all other baselines — a ~40-fold reduction in training cost. Among baselines, KDE-fair (97% permissible, 3% sub-optimal) and separate training (98% permissible, 2% sub-optimal) perform best, while adversarial learning (45% sub-optimal) and RTO post-processing (35% sub-optimal) show the most suboptimal trade-offs. The confidence intervals constructed via Hoeffding's, Bernstein's, bootstrap, and CLT-based methods all yield reliable coverage, with Bernstein's CIs used as the primary evaluation in Table 1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares YOTO against five baselines: adversarial fairness [45], regularization-based methods (logsig and linear) [8], the reductions approach [1], KDE-fair [12], and RTO post-processing [2]. YOTO matches or exceeds all baselines in trade-off quality (100% permissible vs. 51–98% for others) while reducing training cost by ~40× (1 model vs. 40). Among baselines, KDE-fair and separate training are closest to YOTO in trade-off quality (97–98% permissible), while adversarial learning and RTO post-processing show the most suboptimal trade-offs (45% and 35% sub-optimal respectively). The regularization-based methods (logsig, linear) and reductions approach fall in the middle, with 14–33% of trade-offs flagged as sub-optimal. Notably, YOTO's trade-off curves are smoother and lower-variance than those from baselines, which exhibit high variance as accuracy increases (especially visible on Jigsaw). The sensitivity analysis (using 2 additional models) only shifts lower CIs for 2 out of 12 trade-off curves, confirming that YOTO achieves near-optimal trade-offs in most settings without unnecessary conservatism.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the You-Only-Train-Once (YOTO) framework from Dosovitskiy and Djolonga [15]—originally designed for loss-conditional training of deep networks—to the fairness setting, replacing the need to train hundreds of models over a grid of regularization parameters with a single FiLM-conditioned model. It builds on the regularization-based fairness training paradigm (Bendekgey & Sudderth [8], Agarwal et al. [1]) but departs from it in two key ways: (1) it provides the first confidence intervals on the accuracy-fairness trade-off curve with finite-sample guarantees, and (2) it introduces a sensitivity analysis procedure to account for the unknown gap between a trained model's fairness and the true optimum. The paper positions itself against both in-processing methods that impose uniform fairness constraints and post-processing approaches, arguing that dataset-specific trade-off characterization is more principled than one-size-fits-all mandates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides practitioners with a statistically grounded, dataset-specific tool for determining acceptable fairness ranges at any given accuracy level, moving the field away from arbitrary uniform thresholds. The 40-fold computational reduction (one YOTO model vs. ~40 separately trained models) makes trade-off curve estimation feasible for large-scale models, and the confidence intervals enable reliable auditing of fairness interventions without false conclusions from finite-sample noise. Key limitations include the requirement for a separate calibration dataset, the dependence of lower CIs on the unknown Δ(h_λ) term (mitigated but not eliminated by sensitivity analysis), and the fact that the approach assumes access to sensitive attributes in calibration data. Open questions include tighter bounds on Δ(h_λ) under weaker assumptions and extending the framework to multi-class or multi-attribute settings.
