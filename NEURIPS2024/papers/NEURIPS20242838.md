## Problem

All known Thompson Sampling algorithms for linear combinatorial semi-bandits have finite-time regret bounds that scale exponentially with the action size m, making them impractical in high-dimensional regimes. This paper addresses that gap by proposing the first TS variant with polynomial regret in m, and further reveals a counterintuitive 'mismatched sampling paradox' where deliberately using an incorrect (Gaussian) likelihood outperforms the statistically correct (Bernoulli) likelihood by an exponential factor.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a known combinatorial action set A ⊂ {0,1}^d, a subgaussian parameter σ, and an exploration parameter λ > 0. At each round it selects a binary action vector and observes only the reward components corresponding to the selected items (semi-bandit feedback via Hadamard product). The output is a sequence of action selections whose cumulative regret is bounded polynomially in m, d, 1/Δ_min, Δ_max, and σ, with leading terms O(σ²d ln m / Δ_min · ln T). Notably, the algorithm uses an improper uniform prior over all of R^d rather than the true parameter space [0,1]^d, and a Gaussian likelihood even when rewards are Bernoulli.

## Method

BG-CTS (Boosted Gaussian-Combinatorial Thompson Sampling) modifies standard Gaussian TS by inflating the posterior variance with a time-varying boost g(t) = f(t)/ln t, where f(t) is derived from self-normalized concentration inequalities and g(t) → 1+λ asymptotically. The key proof strategy introduces 'clean runs'—events where all Thompson samples remain within confidence bounds and the optimal action's sample exceeds its true mean at least Ω(t^α) times—and shows these occur with probability ≥ 1 − O(dt^{-2} + t^{-1}(ln t)^{-2} + e^{-Ct^α}). On clean runs, the optimal action is selected at least C_6 t^α times, which drives the Thompson sample of the optimal action to within a vanishing h(t) of its true mean, thereby controlling the transient phase that previously produced exponential regret. The regret decomposition then bounds each suboptimal-play event (empirical mean deviation, random sample deviation, or both) using concentration inequalities from CUCB, ESCB, and Perrault (2023), yielding the polynomial bound.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 1) establishes that BG-CTS achieves a regret upper bound of O(σ²d ln m / Δ_min · ln T + σ²d²m ln m / Δ_min · ln ln T + P(m, d, 1/Δ_min, Δ_max, σ)), where P is a polynomial (degree at most 30 in m, 10 in d, 20 in 1/Δ_min). This replaces the exponentially scaling term m(σ(m²+1)/Δ_min)^(2+4m) from the prior analysis of TS in [20]. In the numerical experiments (T=2×10⁴, m=50, Bernoulli rewards), Beta-based TS exhibits nearly linear regret over time, while BG-CTS and ESCB achieve regret several orders of magnitude lower. As m increases from 5 to 65 (T=10⁴), the final regret of Beta-based TS grows rapidly with high variance, whereas BG-CTS and ESCB remain essentially unaffected.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares BG-CTS against two families of baselines: (1) Beta-based combinatorial Thompson Sampling (B-CTS) with uniform or Jeffreys prior and Bernoulli likelihood, which corresponds to the natural TS analyzed in [20], and (2) ESCB [10], a UCB-style combinatorial algorithm. Against B-CTS, BG-CTS wins decisively: in the 2-action Bernoulli problem with m=d/2, B-CTS exhibits regret that scales exponentially with m (confirmed both theoretically by [24] and empirically in Figure 1b), while BG-CTS maintains polynomial regret and its final regret remains flat as m grows from 5 to 65. The improvement is described as 'several orders of magnitude.' Against ESCB, BG-CTS performs comparably in the numerical experiments — both algorithms show low, stable regret that is insensitive to m — but BG-CTS has the advantage of lower computational complexity (a single linear maximization per step versus the more complex UCB computation in ESCB). The mismatched sampling paradox further shows that BG-CTS (Gaussian likelihood, improper prior) outperforms B-CTS (Bernoulli likelihood, Beta prior) even when the learner knows the true reward distribution is Bernoulli, demonstrating that exploiting statistical knowledge can be counterproductive for regret minimization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of Thompson Sampling analysis for combinatorial semi-bandits initiated by Perrault et al. [20] (NeurIPS 2020), which established the first finite-time regret bounds for TS in this setting but suffered from an exponential dependency on the action size m. The authors build on concentration inequalities from Degenne & Perchet [10] (ESCB) and a combinatorial counting lemma from Perrault [19] (NeurIPS 2023), but introduce a fundamentally new proof strategy centered on controlling the sample-path behavior of the optimal action's Thompson sample. The key departure from prior work is the introduction of a time-varying exploration boost (BG-CTS) that replaces the exponential transient term with a polynomial one, and the demonstration that this boost is most effective when the likelihood is deliberately mismatched to the true reward distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The mismatched sampling paradox — that a learner who correctly models Bernoulli rewards with a Beta posterior can perform exponentially worse than one who naively uses a Gaussian posterior — challenges a core intuition in Bayesian bandit algorithms that using the correct statistical model should always be advantageous. This has practical implications: practitioners implementing TS for combinatorial problems with bounded rewards may be better off ignoring their knowledge of the reward distribution. Open questions include whether the paradox extends beyond the two-action Bernoulli example, whether the polynomial regret bound is tight, and whether the exploration boost can be made adaptive to the problem structure rather than requiring knowledge of m and σ.
