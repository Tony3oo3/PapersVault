## Problem

World models in goal-conditioned RL trained on replay buffers generalize poorly to state transitions that go backward along recorded trajectories or jump between different trajectories, causing hallucinated rollouts and a large model-to-reality gap. This limits exploration in long-horizon, sparse-reward environments where the agent must navigate between arbitrary key states (e.g., unstacking blocks after learning to stack them). The paper asks how to train world models that capture bidirectional and cross-trajectory dynamics to enable robust, generalizable goal-conditioned policies.

## Input / Output

The method consumes low-dimensional state vectors (ranging from 9-dim in Walker to 61-dim in Block/Pen Rotation) and goal vectors of matching dimensionality, with the assumption S = G and an identity mapping η. The goal-conditioned policy π^G(a|s, g) outputs a 4- to 8-dimensional continuous action vector per timestep. A key intermediate output is the set of key subgoal states G_subgoals, identified by applying Farthest Point Sampling to action vectors extracted from replay buffer episodes, which then serve as navigation targets for collecting bidirectional transition data.

## Method

MUN introduces a bidirectional replay buffer strategy: instead of only collecting forward transitions along a single trajectory, the agent is directed to sequentially navigate between pairs of key subgoal states (N_s = 2) sampled from the buffer, recording transitions in both directions and across trajectories. Key subgoals are identified via DAD (Distinct Action Discovery), which applies Farthest Point Sampling to action vectors in a batch of episodes to select the N most distinct actions and maps their corresponding states to subgoal commands. The world model (an RSSM following Dreamer) is trained jointly on trajectories from both subgoal navigation (D_DAD) and standard environment-goal sampling (D_egc), yielding a richer set of dynamic transitions. The goal-conditioned policy is then trained on imagined rollouts from this improved world model using a self-supervised temporal distance reward r^G = -D_t(s, g), closing the generalization gap between model-imagined and real-environment behavior.

## Results

MUN achieves over 95% success rate on the most challenging 3-Block Stacking task, while all baselines (GC-Dreamer, MEGA-G, PEG-G) plateau around 60% within 2.5M environment steps. In unconstrained navigation experiments between arbitrary subgoal pairs, MUN reaches 95% success on 3-Block Stacking (vs. 56% GC-Dreamer, 42% MEGA-G, 47% PEG-G), 96% on Ant-Maze, and 89% on Walker. MUN's world model also exhibits substantially lower one-step prediction error (e.g., 0.0070 vs. 0.0392–0.0853 on 3-Block Stacking) and compound prediction error (e.g., 0.45 vs. 0.70–0.95) compared to all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MUN outperforms all three baselines across all six environments in both task success rate and world model prediction quality. Against GC-Dreamer (LEXA), MUN achieves a 39-point gap on 3-Block Stacking navigation (95% vs. 56%) and a 21-point gap on Walker (89% vs. 67%), with GC-Dreamer's lack of any exploration strategy limiting its replay buffer diversity. Against MEGA-G, MUN leads by 53 points on 3-Block Stacking (95% vs. 42%) and 8 points on Walker (89% vs. 81%), though MEGA-G is competitive on Ant-Maze (94% vs. 96%). Against PEG-G, MUN wins by 48 points on 3-Block Stacking (95% vs. 47%) and 27 points on Walker (89% vs. 62%), with PEG-G's exploration-potential-based goal selection proving less effective than MUN's DAD-driven subgoal navigation. MUN's world model consistently shows the lowest one-step and compound prediction errors across all environments, confirming that the bidirectional replay buffer strategy yields a more accurate model of real-world dynamics.

## Relation to Prior Work

MUN builds directly on the Dreamer RSSM world model architecture (Hafner et al., 2019a) and the LEXA goal-conditioned framework (Mendonca et al., 2021), which uses a temporal distance network for self-supervised goal-reaching rewards. Its key departure is from the Go-Explore exploration paradigm (Ecoffet et al., 2019; Pitis et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2023): rather than pairing a goal-conditioned 'Go-phase' with a separate undirected 'Explore-phase' policy, MUN replaces the Explore-phase with a second Go-phase directed at a different subgoal, creating a bidirectional replay buffer that captures transitions both forward and backward along trajectories and across separate trajectories. The DAD subgoal identification strategy, which applies Farthest Point Sampling in action space, is a novel but lightweight alternative to state-space clustering or density-based goal selection used by MEGA and PEG.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bidirectional replay buffer addresses a concrete and previously underappreciated failure mode in model-based GCRL: world models trained only on forward trajectory transitions generalize poorly to reverse or cross-trajectory dynamics, producing hallucinated rollouts that mislead policy training. The practical impact is demonstrated by MUN's ability to generalize to reverse tasks (e.g., unstacking blocks) without explicit training on those goals. However, the DAD strategy's reliance on action-space distinctiveness limits its applicability to environments with highly complex or weakly correlated action-goal spaces, and the lack of infeasible-goal filtering remains an open efficiency concern. The authors note that the framework could extend to model-free GCRL, suggesting broader applicability beyond the Dreamer-based MBRL setting.
