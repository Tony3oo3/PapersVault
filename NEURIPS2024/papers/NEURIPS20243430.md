## Problem

General-purpose AI systems depend on massive web-crawled training corpora, but no prior work had systematically measured how consent signals (robots.txt, Terms of Service) on the underlying web domains are evolving over time in response to AI data collection. This paper asks: at what rate are web sources restricting AI crawlers, how do restrictions differ across AI developers, and how consistent are the various consent mechanisms with each other?

## Input / Output

The audit consumes 14,000 web domains sampled from C4, RefinedWeb, and Dolma (a 3.95k head set of top-token domains plus a 10k random sample from the corpora's intersection), along with their monthly Wayback Machine snapshots of robots.txt and ToS pages spanning January 2016 to April 2024. Outputs are token-weighted restriction percentages over time for nine specific crawler agents, SARIMA-based one-year forecasts, a cross-tabulation of robots.txt vs. ToS policy categories, and human-annotated content characteristics (modality, monetization, sensitivity, purpose) for each domain. A secondary analysis compares WildChat user conversation logs against the service types of training-data domains to measure real-use misalignment.

## Method

The paper constructs a longitudinal consent audit by (1) sampling web domains from three major open AI corpora, (2) retrieving historical robots.txt and ToS pages at monthly intervals via the Wayback Machine, (3) classifying robots.txt restrictions in ascending severity (no file → sitemap → pattern-based → full disallow) for nine named crawler agents (Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Cohere, Meta, Common Crawl, Internet Archive, plus two search agents), and (4) auto-annotating ToS pages for crawling bans, AI-specific prohibitions, non-compete clauses, and content-use restrictions. Human annotators (compensated at $25–30/hr) label each domain's content modalities, monetization, sensitivity, and service type. SARIMA time-series models are fitted to the monthly restriction proportions to forecast trends through April 2025. A key analytical contribution is the cross-tabulation of robots.txt and ToS categories, which reveals that 35.1% of domains with anti-crawling ToS lack any robots.txt, and 20.3% have restrictive robots.txt without corresponding ToS—exposing systematic inconsistencies in how consent is expressed.

## Results

In one year (Apr 2023–Apr 2024), ~5%+ of all tokens across C4, RefinedWeb, and Dolma became fully restricted by robots.txt, while 28%+ of tokens from the most critical head domains (HEAD_All) are now restricted. For Terms of Service, 45–55% of all tokens in the three corpora carry some form of data-use restriction. OpenAI's crawlers face the heaviest restrictions at 25.9% of HEAD_C4 tokens, compared to 9.8% for Google's AI crawler, 4.9% for Cohere, 4.1% for Meta, and just 1.0% for Google Search. SARIMA forecasts predict an additional 2–4% of full-corpus tokens and 7–11% of head-distribution tokens will become restricted by April 2025.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical audit paper with no competing methods to benchmark against, so traditional baseline comparisons do not apply. Instead, the paper's key comparative findings are: (1) OpenAI's GPTBot is restricted in 91.5% of cases where any AI organization is blocked, while Google Search's crawler is restricted in only 17.1% of such cases—a 74-percentage-point gap that reflects website administrators' willingness to block AI training while preserving search and archival access. (2) Robots.txt and Terms of Service frequently contradict each other: 35.1% of sites have anti-crawling ToS but no robots.txt, and 20.3% have a restrictive robots.txt but no ToS, indicating the two signaling mechanisms are poorly aligned. (3) The head distribution (top 2k domains) shows dramatically higher restriction rates (26.5% restrictive robots.txt, 51.2% restrictive ToS) compared to the random tail sample (3.4% and 15.7% respectively), meaning the most valuable training data is also the most heavily restricted. (4) News/periodical sites dominate the head (45.6% of top-100 domains) yet face the steepest restriction growth (~45% of news tokens fully restricted vs. 3% in 2023), signaling a compositional shift away from fresh, curated content in future crawls.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior dataset audits (e.g., Dodge et al. on C4, Kreutzer et al. on multilingual corpora, Baack on Common Crawl) that assess quality, provenance, or biases in curated snapshots, by instead tracing the longitudinal evolution of consent signals (robots.txt, Terms of Service) at the source web-domain level. It also extends the line of work on web crawler behavior and robots.txt analysis (Sun et al., Calzarossa & Massari) by applying it specifically to AI training data provenance and introducing SARIMA-based forecasting of consent trends. The WildChat comparison to training data composition is a novel analytical angle not present in prior dataset audits, connecting real user behavior to the data that models were trained on.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The documented 500–1000%+ year-over-year increase in restrictions on critical training domains, combined with the 45% ToS restriction rate, signals that the open web data commons underpinning general-purpose AI is contracting rapidly, with direct implications for data scaling laws, model freshness, and representativeness. The protocol-level inconsistencies (unrecognized agents, robots.txt/ToS contradictions, asymmetric restrictions across AI developers) highlight that existing web infrastructure is fundamentally ill-suited for expressing AI-specific consent, pointing toward a need for new standardized protocols. Open questions include how legal enforcement of ToS restrictions will play out, whether non-commercial and archival uses can be preserved, and how the composition shift away from news/forums toward e-commerce/organizational sites will affect model capabilities.
