## Problem

Nonlinear PDEs with multiple solutions are ubiquitous in physics, biology, and engineering, yet existing neural network approaches fail to handle them: function-learning methods (PINNs, Deep Ritz) are ill-posed when multiple solutions exist and can only recover one solution per training run, while operator-learning methods require solution uniqueness to be well-defined. Classical Newton solvers, though well-posed locally, become computationally prohibitive for large-scale systems. The paper asks whether a neural operator can learn the Newton step itself, enabling efficient multi-solution discovery with limited supervised data.

## Input / Output

The neural operator takes a spatial field u (the current Newton iterate, defined on a regular grid over domain Ω) and outputs the Newton correction δu = G(u) at that iterate. In practice, the input is a grid-valued function (e.g., 63×63 for the Gray-Scott model) and the output is the same-shape correction that, when added to u, advances one Newton step. The operator is applied iteratively as (O + Id)^(n)(u₀) to converge to a solution; different initial conditions u₀ route to different solutions, enabling multi-solution discovery in a single trained model.

## Method

The Newton Informed Neural Operator (NINO) uses a shallow DeepONet (convolutional branch network with 5 Conv2D layers + GELU, and a static POD-based trunk) to approximate the local Newton step operator G(u) = δu that solves the linearized system (L − f′(u))δu = −Lu + f(u). The key innovation is a hybrid loss: a supervised MSE term on (u, G(u)) pairs combined with an unsupervised Newton loss that penalizes the PDE residual (L − f′(u))O(u;θ) + (Lu − f(u)), encoding the physics of the linearized equation directly into training. This allows effective learning even when supervised data is scarce (e.g., 500 samples) by leveraging 5000–50000 unsupervised initial states. Theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–2) establishes approximation rates in Sobolev spaces and generalization bounds, showing the operator can be learned with error O(m^{-1/n} + ε + p^{-2/d}). Once trained, the operator replaces the expensive linear solve at each Newton iteration, yielding ~10⁵× speedup over CUDA-parallelized Newton for batched systems, and can discover steady states (e.g., in the Gray-Scott model) absent from the supervised training set.

## Results

The headline quantitative result is a dramatic speedup over the classical Newton solver: NINO solves 500 independent Newton linear systems in 1.1×10⁻⁴ s versus 31.52 s for the CUDA-parallelized Newton method (~286,000× faster), and 5000 systems in 1.4×10⁻⁴ s versus 321.15 s (~2.3 million× faster). In the convex problem (Case 1), the Newton-loss-augmented training (Method 2) yields substantially lower test L₂ and H₁ errors than MSE-only training (Method 1) with the same 500 supervised samples. In the Gray-Scott model (Case 3), NINO successfully converges to steady-state patterns (e.g., a ring-like pattern) that are absent from the supervised dataset, demonstrating generalization beyond the training distribution.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole quantitative baseline is the classical Newton method, parallelized on an NVIDIA A100 GPU using 10 CUDA streams and CuPy's sparse solver. NINO wins decisively on execution time: for 500 Newton linear systems, NINO completes in 1.1×10⁻⁴ s compared to 31.52 s for the parallelized Newton solver, and for 5000 systems, 1.4×10⁻⁴ s versus 321.15 s. Critically, NINO's inference time scales nearly flatly (1.1→1.4×10⁻⁴ s) as the batch size grows 10×, whereas the classical solver's time increases 10× (31.52→321.15 s), highlighting the advantage of GPU-native batching in the neural operator. The comparison is framed as a surrogate-model speedup rather than an accuracy trade-off: NINO approximates one Newton step, so accuracy depends on the learned operator's fidelity, which the paper validates through convergence curves in the Gray-Scott model. No comparison is made against other neural PDE solvers (PINN, FNO, etc.) in terms of solution accuracy; the paper positions NINO as a drop-in replacement for the Newton linear solve within the iterative scheme.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of neural operator learning (DeepONet, FNO, MgNO) and classical Newton iteration for nonlinear PDEs. Rather than learning the full solution operator—which is ill-posed when multiple solutions exist—it learns the local Newton step operator G(u) that maps a current iterate to its correction δu, making the learning problem well-defined. The hybrid loss function (MSE + Newton residual) extends the physics-informed loss paradigm from PINNs into the operator-learning setting, enabling semi-supervised training with far fewer ground-truth samples. Theoretically, the paper provides the first approximation and generalization bounds specifically for a neural operator learning the Newton linearization in Sobolev spaces, building on DeepONet approximation theory (Lanthaler et al.) and covering-number arguments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 300× speedup over a CUDA-parallelized Newton solver for batched linear systems, combined with the ability to discover steady states absent from supervised data (demonstrated on the Gray-Scott model), makes this approach practically relevant for pattern-discovery workflows in reaction-diffusion and materials science. Key limitations include the local well-posedness assumption (the operator is only guaranteed near a solution basin), the restriction to second-order elliptic operators with bounded nonlinearities, and the fact that multi-solution discovery still depends on sampling diverse initial conditions rather than being guaranteed by the method itself. Open questions include whether the approach extends to higher-dimensional PDEs, time-dependent problems, and whether the POD-based trunk basis generalizes beyond the tested grid resolutions.
