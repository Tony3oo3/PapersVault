## Problem

Binarizing LLM weights to 1-bit achieves 16× compression but causes severe accuracy degradation, and existing remedies (PB-LLM, BiLLM) either inflate memory overhead to near 2-bit levels or complicate inference with sparse weight management. The paper identifies that scaling factors—though occupying a negligible fraction of model size—are the critical lever for reducing binarization error, yet prior methods use only static, context-independent values.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244366/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained LLM (LLaMA-1/2 7B/13B/30B, OPT 125M/1.3B) and produces a 1-bit binarized model where each linear layer stores binary weights plus four scaling expert vectors per dimension and a small router matrix. At inference, the binarized model consumes a text token sequence and, for each token, the router computes gating scores from the input activations to produce token-adaptive scaling factors that modulate the binary weight matrix before and after the 1-bit GEMV. The overall memory footprint is only ~2% larger than OneBit (e.g., 1.40 GB vs 1.37 GB for LLaMA-7B), preserving the ~10× compression ratio over Float16.

## Method

BinaryMoS extends OneBit's dual-dimension (input and output) scaling framework by replacing each static scaling vector with a set of four scaling experts per dimension. A lightweight router (single linear layer followed by softmax) computes per-token gating scores from the input activations, which are then used to linearly combine the scaling experts into token-adaptive scaling factors (Equations 3–4). Because the combination is linear, all experts can be merged before the 1-bit matrix multiplication, avoiding the per-expert processing overhead of conventional MoE. Training employs quantization-aware training with knowledge distillation from the full-precision teacher, combining cross-entropy loss on output logits with an MSE-based layer-to-layer loss (α=10) to align intermediate representations. The key insight is that four experts per dimension add only ~0.2% extra parameters yet create effectively infinite token-adaptive scaling combinations, closing most of the perplexity and zero-shot accuracy gap to Float16 while even outperforming 2-bit PTQ methods like GPTQ and OmniQuant.

## Results

On LLaMA-1-7B, BinaryMoS achieves 7.97 WikiText2 perplexity and 54.48% average zero-shot accuracy, compared to 8.48/52.36% for OneBit, 41.66/44.24% for BiLLM, and 198.37/40.66% for PB-LLM. On LLaMA-2-13B, BinaryMoS reaches 7.08 WikiText2 perplexity and 57.09% average zero-shot accuracy, narrowing the gap to the Float16 model (4.88/66.10%) to within 2.2 perplexity points. BinaryMoS also outperforms 2-bit quantization methods: on LLaMA-1-7B it achieves 7.97 WikiText2 perplexity versus 9.75 for OmniQuant and 45.73 for GPTQ, while using half the inference memory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

BinaryMoS decisively outperforms PB-LLM and BiLLM across all models and metrics; on LLaMA-1-7B, BinaryMoS achieves 7.97 WikiText2 perplexity versus 198.37 (PB-LLM) and 41.66 (BiLLM), with average zero-shot accuracy of 54.48% versus 40.66% and 44.24% respectively. Against the closest binarization baseline OneBit, BinaryMoS consistently wins by a moderate margin: 7.97 vs. 8.48 WikiText2 perplexity and 54.48% vs. 52.36% average zero-shot accuracy on LLaMA-1-7B, with similar gaps on other models (e.g., 7.08 vs. 7.56 perplexity on LLaMA-2-13B). Notably, BinaryMoS also surpasses 2-bit quantization methods despite using half the inference memory: on LLaMA-1-7B it achieves 7.97 WikiText2 perplexity versus 9.75 for OmniQuant and 45.73 for GPTQ, and 54.48% average zero-shot accuracy versus 51.58% (OmniQuant) and 43.75% (GPTQ). The latency overhead of BinaryMoS over OneBit is minimal (34.5 vs. 32.7 µs for a 4096×4096 layer in LLaMA-7B), confirming that the additional scaling-expert routing adds negligible computational cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

BinaryMoS directly extends OneBit's dual-dimension scaling framework by replacing its static per-dimension scaling vectors with a mixture of multiple scaling experts combined via a token-adaptive router. Conceptually, it transplants the Mixture-of-Experts idea into the scaling-factor space rather than duplicating full layers, thereby avoiding the proportional memory blowup that makes standard MoE incompatible with 1-bit compression. It departs from PB-LLM and BiLLM, which recover accuracy by storing additional high-precision or multi-bit weight information, by keeping the weight matrix strictly 1-bit and instead enriching the tiny scaling vectors that already exist in every binarization scheme.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BinaryMoS demonstrates that the representational bottleneck of 1-bit LLMs can be partially alleviated by making the scaling factors context-dependent, achieving perplexity and zero-shot accuracy that surpass 2-bit PTQ methods (GPTQ, OmniQuant) while maintaining a ~10× compression ratio suitable for edge deployment. However, the QAT-vs-PTQ asymmetry in the 2-bit comparison limits the strength of that claim, and the residual gap to Float16 (e.g., ~2.3 perplexity points on LLaMA-2-13B) remains substantial for production use. Open questions include whether the mixture-of-scales idea transfers to multi-bit quantization, whether advanced MoE training techniques (load balancing, auxiliary losses) further improve routing, and whether the QAT training cost can be reduced to make the approach viable for 70B+ models.
