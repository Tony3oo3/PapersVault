## Problem

Standard GNN-based graph classifiers rely on global pooling (sum, mean, max) to collapse variable-sized node embedding matrices into fixed-length vectors, inevitably discarding higher-order structural and semantic information. Graph kernel methods avoid pooling but suffer from high computational cost and manual feature engineering. The paper asks whether graphs can be classified directly from their node embeddings without any aggregation step, while retaining theoretical guarantees on generalization.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244373/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a graph's adjacency matrix A_i ∈ R^{n_i×n_i} and node feature matrix X_i ∈ R^{n_i×d_0} as input, where n_i varies across graphs. Rather than producing a single graph-level vector, the GNN encoder outputs a node embedding matrix H_i ∈ R^{n_i×d} that is treated as a discrete distribution. The reference module then maps this distribution to a K-dimensional similarity vector s_i ∈ R^K (one score per class), which is softmaxed into class probabilities; the predicted label is argmax_k s_ik. The Gaussian kernel bandwidth θ is learned jointly with all other parameters.

## Method

GRDL replaces the global pooling step with a reference distribution layer: K learnable reference matrices D_k ∈ R^{m×d} (one per class) are compared against each graph's node embedding distribution H_i via negative squared Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with a Gaussian kernel, yielding class scores without any aggregation. The GNN encoder (an L-layer GIN with r-layer MLPs per layer) and the reference distributions are trained end-to-end using cross-entropy loss plus a discrimination loss that penalizes inter-reference MMD similarity. Theoretically, the paper derives a Rademacher-complexity-based generalization bound showing the gap scales as Õ(μb̄‖X‖₂ c^L (Lr)^{3/2} κ̄^{Lr} √(θK/n)/N + γ√(Kmd/N)), and proves this bound is strictly tighter than the analogous bound for GIN with mean readout, establishing that GRDL generalizes better in the worst case. Empirically, GRDL achieves top-3 accuracy on 11 benchmarks while being at least 10× faster than OT-based competitors in both training and inference.

## Results

GRDL achieves the best average classification accuracy of 77.9% across 8 standard graph benchmarks, outperforming the next-best methods SAT (76.1%) and WITTopoPool (75.9%). On three large imbalanced datasets (PC-3, MCF-7, ogbg-molhiv), GRDL attains AUC-ROC scores of 85.1%, 81.4%, and 79.8% respectively, surpassing GIN by 0.5–2.0 points. In terms of efficiency, GRDL is at least 10× faster than OT-GNN and TFGW in both training and inference (1.4 ms vs. 21.6 ms vs. 64.6 ms average prediction time per graph), and uses ~45× fewer parameters than graph transformers (15k vs. 659–675k).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GRDL achieves the highest average accuracy (77.9%) among all 18 baselines, winning on PROTEINS (82.6% vs. 80.0% WITTopoPool), IMDB-B (74.8% vs. 74.1% SEP), and BZR (92.0% vs. 91.7% SAT). It trails on NCI1 (80.4% vs. 85.7% WWL and 85.6% WL) and PTC-MR (68.3% vs. 70.2% GMT), and is roughly tied with WITTopoPool on IMDB-M (52.9% vs. 52.9%). Against OT-based methods, GRDL dominates on both accuracy and speed: it outperforms OT-GNN (75.7% avg) and WEGL (73.4% avg) while being ~10× faster in prediction (1.4 ms vs. 21.6 ms) and ~46× faster than TFGW (64.6 ms). Against graph transformers, GRDL matches or exceeds SAT (76.1%) and Graphormer (75.1%) in accuracy while using ~45× fewer parameters (15k vs. 659–675k) and training ~5–6× faster per epoch. On the three large imbalanced datasets, GRDL consistently leads: 85.1% AUC on PC-3 (vs. 84.6% GIN), 81.4% on MCF-7 (vs. 80.6% GIN), and 79.8% on ogbg-molhiv (vs. 77.8% GIN).

## Relation to Prior Work

GRDL carves new ground by repurposing the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (a two-sample test from kernel statistics) as a classification mechanism for graph node embeddings, treating them as discrete distributions rather than aggregating them via global pooling. Conceptually, it is closest to OT-GNN's 'learned point clouds' and TFGW's 'template graphs,' but replaces the expensive Wasserstein/Gromov-Wasserstein distances with a cheaper MMD-based similarity, and frames the comparison as distribution-to-distribution rather than point-cloud-to-point-cloud. The paper also departs from the standard GNN+pooling+MLP pipeline by eliminating the readout step entirely, and provides the first Rademacher-complexity-based generalization bound for this reference-distribution architecture, contrasting it with a matching bound for GIN with mean pooling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: GRDL is at least 10× faster than OT-based competitors in both training and inference while matching or exceeding their accuracy, making distribution-based graph classification viable at scale. The theoretical analysis yields actionable design guidance (moderate network depth, moderate reference size, no need for reference-norm regularization) and a provable generalization advantage over pooled GNNs. However, the accuracy gains over the best baselines (SAT, WITTopoPool) are modest (~1–2 points on average), and the method does not outperform GIN on NCI1, suggesting the distributional framing may not always capture the most discriminative structural cues. Open questions include whether the approach extends naturally to graph regression, link prediction, or heterogeneous/multi-relational graphs, and whether the single-reference-per-class finding holds in highly imbalanced or fine-grained classification settings.
