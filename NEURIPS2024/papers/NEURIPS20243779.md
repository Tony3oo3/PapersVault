## Problem

Existing evidential learning methods for uncertainty quantification are restricted to neural networks and require closed-form individual-level posteriors, limiting their applicability to tree-based models and general posterior forms. The paper asks whether a gradient-boosting framework can solve distribution-valued supervised learning—where each training output is a probability distribution—without requiring closed-form posteriors or neural network architectures.

## Input / Output

The method takes tabular input vectors x_i and scalar or categorical response variables y_i, from which individual-level posteriors p(θ|y_i) are computed via Bayes' theorem using a user-specified response likelihood and prior. For each new input x, the model outputs N particles (default 10) whose empirical distribution approximates the target posterior; from these, a predictive distribution p(y|x) is obtained by marginalizing the response likelihood over particles, a point prediction via the Bayes action, and an OOD score from the inverse maximum-norm particle variance. The algorithm requires only the log-gradient of the individual-level posterior, so no normalizing constant is needed.

## Method

WGBoost extends gradient boosting by maintaining N parallel boosting ensembles (one per particle) and, at each iteration, fitting a new weak learner for each particle to the negative smoothed Wasserstein gradient of the KL divergence between the current particle empirical distribution and the target output distribution. The Wasserstein gradient is made well-defined for empirical distributions via a Gaussian kernel smoothing operator (SVGD-style), yielding a finite-dimensional target for each particle. The default second-order variant (WEvidential) replaces the gradient with a diagonal approximate Wasserstein Newton direction—dividing the smoothed gradient elementwise by the diagonal of the smoothed Wasserstein Hessian—mirroring the Newton-direction practice in XGBoost and LightGBM. Decision tree regressors serve as weak learners, and the entire procedure operates on tabular data without requiring closed-form posteriors or neural network parameterization.

## Results

On the probabilistic regression benchmark (10 UCI datasets), WEvidential achieves the best NLL on energy (0.53), yacht (0.16), power (2.60), and concrete (2.83), and the best RMSE on energy (0.42), yacht (0.48), power (3.19), and concrete (4.15). On classification and OOD detection, WEvidential achieves 99.67 PR-AUC on segment (best, vs. 96.74 for PNetwork) and 81.13 PR-AUC on sensorless (second to PNetwork's 88.65, but far above DEnsemble's 50.62 and DDistillation's 31.17). Overall, WEvidential achieves the best or near-best score on the majority of datasets across both regression and classification tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DEvidential (the closest tree-free evidential baseline), WEvidential wins decisively on energy (NLL 0.53 vs. 1.39), yacht (0.16 vs. 1.03), power (2.60 vs. 2.81), and concrete (2.83 vs. 3.01), but loses on kin8nm (-0.44 vs. -1.24), naval (-5.47 vs. -5.73), protein (2.70 vs. 2.63), and wine (0.95 vs. 0.89). Against NGBoost (the closest tree-based probabilistic baseline), WEvidential wins on energy, yacht, power, and concrete while losing on kin8nm, naval, wine, and year msd. Against DEnsemble, the gap is similar: WEvidential dominates on energy, yacht, power, and concrete but trails on kin8nm and year msd. MCDropout and CDropout are consistently outperformed by WEvidential on most datasets, with CDropout being the strongest among the dropout-based methods on boston and protein. On OOD detection (Table 2), WEvidential achieves 99.67 PR-AUC on segment (beating PNetwork's 96.74 and DEnsemble's 58.13) but trails PNetwork on sensorless (81.13 vs. 88.65), though it still substantially outperforms DEnsemble (50.62) and DDistillation (31.17).

## Relation to Prior Work

WGBoost unifies two previously separate lines of work: the sequential weak-learner fitting paradigm of gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001) and the particle-based Wasserstein gradient flow framework from Stein variational gradient descent (Liu & Wang, 2016) and the Stein variational Newton method (Detommaso et al., 2018). In standard gradient boosting, a weak learner is fitted to the negative gradient of a scalar loss; here, the 'loss' is a functional over probability distributions (KL divergence) and the 'gradient' is a Wasserstein gradient acting on distributions, with each weak learner trained to approximate the smoothed Wasserstein gradient evaluated at each particle. The paper departs from existing evidential learning (Sensoy et al., 2018; Amini et al., 2020), which is restricted to neural networks and requires closed-form individual-level posteriors, by enabling tree-based models and handling posteriors known only up to normalizing constants via particle representations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

WGBoost provides the first framework for evidential learning with boosted tree models and for cases where individual-level posteriors lack closed form, filling a gap between the strong tabular-data performance of gradient boosting and the distributional output requirements of uncertainty quantification. The competitive NLL/RMSE results against neural-network-based methods (MCDropout, Deep Ensembles, DEvidential) and the notably strong OOD detection (99.67% PR-AUC on segment) suggest tree-based particle methods are a viable alternative for safety-critical tabular applications. Open questions include convergence guarantees, robustness of the predictive uncertainty under distribution shift, and whether the framework extends beyond tabular data where gradient boosting is most competitive.
