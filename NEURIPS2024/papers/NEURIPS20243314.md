## Problem

Trained transformers implement interpretable circuits for tasks like arithmetic and associative recall, but it is unclear whether these circuits emerge from the supervisory signal or are already latent in randomly initialized models. This paper asks: to what extent can a randomly initialized transformer perform meaningful algorithmic tasks if only its input/output encoding is learned, with all internal computation left at random initialization?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243314/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes token sequences (integer pairs for modular addition, marker-value sequences for needle-in-a-haystack, reversed digit strings for decimal addition, parenthesis strings, or natural language text) and produces next-token predictions. The critical constraint is that the input is mapped into the fixed random computation solely through learned token embeddings, positional embeddings, and a learned unembedding matrix—no intermediate attention or feed-forward weights are updated. For algorithmic tasks the vocabulary is small (e.g., 4 tokens for parenthesis balancing), while language modeling uses a 10,000-token BPE vocabulary over TinyStories.

## Method

The paper introduces embedding-only training: a decoder-only transformer (GPT-2 architecture) is randomly initialized, then only the embedding layer E (token + positional) and unembedding layer U are optimized via gradient descent while all m intermediate layers F remain frozen at their random initialization. The key mechanism is 'subspace selection'—embedding-only training steers both inputs and hidden representations into low-dimensional subspaces (often <10 principal components explaining >50% of variance) where the random intermediate layers already implement the target computation. This is distinct from lottery-ticket sparsification because the active subspaces are distributed across neurons rather than confined to sparse sub-networks. The paper demonstrates that this approach achieves 100% accuracy on modular addition, needle-in-a-haystack, decimal addition, and parenthesis balancing (outperforming fully trained LSTMs on several tasks), and produces grammatical natural language text on TinyStories, with success scaling with the ratio of task-relevant subspace dimensionality to model hidden width.

## Results

Random transformers (1024 hidden width, only embedding and unembedding layers trained) achieve 100% test accuracy on all four algorithmic tasks—modular addition, needle-in-a-haystack, decimal addition, and parenthesis balancing—matching or exceeding fully trained transformers of the same width. On the TinyStories language modeling task, a 512-width random transformer (~10M trainable parameters) reaches a cross-entropy loss of 2.64, comparable to a fully trained 32-width model (~0.7M parameters) which achieves 1.35. On the memorization task, random transformers store only 0.41 bits per trainable parameter versus 2.88 for fully trained models, revealing a significant capacity gap. The key finding is that embedding-only training steers hidden representations into low-dimensional subspaces where the target computation is already implemented by the random initialization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is a fully trained 1024-width LSTM (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997). Random transformers match or outperform the LSTM on all four algorithmic tasks: they tie on modular addition (100% vs 100%), needle-in-a-haystack (100% vs 99.5%), and parenthesis balancing (100% vs 100%), and substantially outperform on decimal addition (100% vs 53%). This gap on decimal addition is particularly striking, as it requires distributing arithmetic across multiple token positions—a task where recurrent architectures are known to struggle. Compared to fully trained transformers of the same 1024 width, random transformers match on three of four tasks but fall slightly short on parenthesis balancing (100% vs 92.3% for normal, though the normal model's sub-100% result is attributed to imperfect hyperparameters). The critical contrast is with the 16-width random transformer, which drops to 1.3–87.3% accuracy, demonstrating that the capability is highly sensitive to hidden dimensionality and only accessible when the model is wide enough to host the required low-dimensional subspaces.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the random feature extractor literature (Jarrett et al., Saxe et al.) from vision to language modeling, but departs fundamentally by showing that random transformers implement algorithmic computations (not just feature extraction) accessible through embedding-only training. It is conceptually adjacent to the lottery ticket hypothesis and reservoir computing, but shows that useful capabilities can be surfaced without any pruning or sparsification—only by finding the right input/output encoding that confines computation to low-dimensional subspaces. The mechanistic analysis connects to circuit interpretability work on modular addition (Nanda et al., Zhong et al.) and induction heads (Olsson et al.), demonstrating that random models exhibit the same attention patterns and embedding geometries as fully trained ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that algorithmic capabilities are present in transformers at initialization, requiring only a learned encoding to access, has direct implications for circuit-level interpretability: if random models already implement these circuits, then inspecting parameter matrices of trained models may be fundamentally limited in characterizing learned behavior. The paper leaves open whether full training gradually evolves these initialization-time circuits into their trained forms or uses entirely different pathways, and whether the subspace-selection mechanism generalizes beyond the small-scale (≤1024 width, 2-layer) models studied here. The observation that random transformers can produce grammatical text, while clearly inferior to fully trained models, suggests that a non-trivial fraction of linguistic structure is architecturally pre-wired rather than learned.
