## Problem

Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs) are the de facto standard for graph representation learning, yet they cannot capture joint structural features like Common Neighbor (CN) for link prediction because permutation invariance assigns identical representations to isomorphic nodes, making it impossible to distinguish structurally different links. This paper asks whether the pure message-passing paradigm, without any subgraph extraction or node-labeling trick, can in principle estimate such link-level structural features, and if so, how to do so efficiently.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242323/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph (with optional node attributes) and a target node pair (u,v) as input. Node signatures are initialized as quasi-orthogonal random vectors sampled from the vertices of an F-dimensional hypercube (F=1024 by default), with top-b high-degree hubs assigned one-hot vectors in a reserved subspace for variance reduction. The output is a link prediction score produced by a classifier over a concatenated feature vector: the Hadamard product of GNN-encoded node representations and a set of estimated structural features #(p,q) counting nodes at specific shortest-path distances from the target pair.

## Method

The core insight is that quasi-orthogonal (QO) vectors, when propagated via sum-aggregation message passing (no self-loop, no degree normalization), yield inner products between node pairs that are unbiased estimators of Common Neighbor (Theorem 3.2), and multi-hop propagation estimates the number of walks connecting the pair to all other nodes (Theorem 3.3). MPLP leverages this by: (1) encoding node attributes with a standard GNN, (2) constructing QO signatures rescaled by an MLP of the GNN representation and node degree to enable weighted counts (RA/AA), (3) estimating Distance Encoding labels #(p,q) via single-layer message passing over shortest-path neighborhoods to capture richer local topology beyond CN, and (4) applying shortcut removal (excluding target links from the graph during training) to prevent distribution shift between positive and negative instances. A more scalable variant, MPLP+, replaces shortest-path neighborhood propagation with standard one-hop message passing, trading some structural precision for O(td^r F) complexity identical to conventional GNNs.

## Results

On 8 non-attributed benchmarks, MPLP achieves Hits@50 scores of 92.12 (USAir), 90.02 (NS), 52.55 (PB), 85.36 (Yeast), 74.28 (C.ele), 32.66 (Power), 64.68 (Router), and 86.11 (E.coli), outperforming the best baseline by 1.6–10.3 points across all datasets. On attributed benchmarks, MPLP leads on CS (76.40), Physics (76.46), Computers (43.47), Photo (58.08), and Collab (67.05) Hits@50. On large-scale OGB datasets under the challenging HeaRT setting, MPLP+ achieves MRR of 6.79 (Collab), 41.40 (PPA), and 23.11 (Citation2), setting new state-of-the-art results while also matching GCN-level inference speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MPLP outperforms all nine baselines across all 15 evaluated benchmarks. Against heuristic methods (CN, AA, RA), MPLP gains 6–63 points on Hits@50, with the largest margins on sparse graphs like Power (32.66 vs. 11.57) and Router (64.68 vs. 9.38). Node-level GNNs (GCN, SAGE) are consistently outperformed; SAGE is the closest node-level competitor on PB (47.26 vs. 52.55) and E.coli (75.60 vs. 86.11), while GCN lags most severely on C.ele (40.68 vs. 74.28). Among link-level baselines, NCNC is the strongest competitor on E.coli (83.94 vs. 86.11) and Physics (75.96 vs. 76.46), while ELPH is closest on NS (88.49 vs. 90.02) and Router (61.07 vs. 64.68). On the HeaRT evaluation (Table 3), MPLP+ dominates all methods by a wide margin, e.g., 41.40 MRR on PPA vs. 33.52 for NCNC and 29.71 for SEAL, confirming robustness under harder negative sampling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DotHash's quasi-orthogonal (QO) vector sampling and sum-aggregation message passing, extending it from a set-similarity estimator into a full link prediction framework. It also adapts Distance Encoding's shortest-path labeling scheme, but repurposes it not as node labels for a GNN encoder, but as a target for probabilistic counting via inner products of propagated QO vectors. Conceptually, the paper departs from the SEAL/ELPH/NCNC lineage of link-level models that require subgraph extraction or specialized aggregation, arguing instead that the pure message-passing paradigm—when fed QO signatures rather than learned embeddings—already contains the information needed to estimate joint structural features like CN, AA, and RA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a recognized tension in the GNN-for-link-prediction literature: Zhang et al. showed permutation invariance prevents node-level MPNNs from distinguishing isomorphic endpoints, yet here pure message passing with QO vectors provably recovers CN and multi-hop walk counts. This reframes the limitation as one of input representation (learned embeddings vs. random signatures) rather than architectural expressiveness, opening a path to link-level structural features at node-level computational cost. Open questions include whether the variance reduction strategy (one-hot hubs) generalizes beyond power-law graphs, whether the finding that random vectors can match or exceed node attributes (Appendix E) holds in domains where semantic features are genuinely informative, and whether the walk-level approximation (MPLP+) can be tightened to recover exact node-level counts without the BFS preprocessing overhead.
