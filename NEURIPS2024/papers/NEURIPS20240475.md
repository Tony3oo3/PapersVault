## Problem

Existing implicit bias analyses of SGD assume data from a single distribution, yet real-world data is inherently heterogeneous across multiple environments (e.g., hospitals, web sources). While invariance learning has been studied through dedicated algorithms (IRM, EILLS), it remains unexplored whether standard training procedures on heterogeneous data can implicitly drive models toward invariant solutions without any explicit regularization.

## Input / Output

The method consumes m linear measurements X_i^(e_t) ∈ R^{d×d} and scalar responses y_i^(e_t) ∈ R at each step, where the batch is drawn entirely from one environment e_t sampled from a distribution D over environments. The response decomposes as y = ⟨X, A*⟩ + ⟨X, A^(e)⟩, with A* a rank-r1 invariant matrix and A^(e) a rank-r2 environment-varying spurious matrix. The output is the over-parameterized estimate U_T U_T^T ∈ R^{d×d}, which provably converges to the invariant matrix A* under HeteroSGD, while PooledSGD converges to A* + A^s (the spurious mean).

## Method

The core algorithm (HeteroSGD) runs gradient descent on the over-parameterized model y = ⟨X, UU^T⟩ with small initialization U_0 = αI_d and large constant step size η = Θ(1), where each batch is sampled from a single environment rather than a mixture. The key mechanism is that the environment-varying spurious signal Σ^(e) induces multiplicative oscillations in the spurious component Q_t of the iterate: when the step size is large enough relative to the variance of Σ^(e), a concave-function supermartingale argument (φ(x) = x^γ, γ ∈ (0,1)) shows that E[‖Q_t‖^γ] contracts, preventing the model from fitting spurious directions. The proof decomposes U_t into invariant (R_t), spurious (Q_t), and residual (E_t) components, showing R_t grows to Θ(1) in O(η^{-1} log(1/α)) steps while Q_t and E_t remain bounded by the RIP error. In contrast, PooledSGD removes the oscillation by averaging over environments, causing the model to converge to the association-based solution A* + E_e[A^(e)] rather than the invariant A*.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 2) shows that HeteroSGD with large step size η=Θ(1) and T=Θ(log(α⁻¹)/η) iterations recovers the invariant matrix A* with Frobenius error bounded by C·max{δ*²√r₁·M₁², δ*·M₁}·log²(d) with probability ≥0.99, where δ*=(c_v·M₂·log(d))^{1.5}·δ·r₂²·√(r₁+r₂). In the regime d·log²(d)≪m, this simplifies to error O(log(d)·√(d/m)). The negative result (Theorem 3) proves that Pooled SGD instead converges to A*+A^s (invariant plus spurious), with ‖U_T U_T^T − A*‖_F ≳ √(r₁∧r₂) for all t. Simulations confirm a phase transition: invariance learning is achievable only when heterogeneity M≳5 or step size η≥0.05.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between HeteroSGD (batches sampled from a single environment at each step) and Pooled SGD (batches sampled from all environments simultaneously). Theoretically, HeteroSGD provably recovers only the invariant component A*, while Pooled SGD converges to A*+A^s, retaining the spurious signal (Theorem 3). In simulations (Figure 5), HeteroSGD with (r₁,r₂,d,Es^(e))=(3,2,40,0.5) and m=2800 successfully eliminates the spurious signal, whereas Pooled SGD with m=5600 (double the data) fits both invariant and spurious signals simultaneously without distinction. The paper also shows that increasing heterogeneity (M) or step size (η) are both sufficient to trigger invariance learning, with phase transitions around M≈5 and η≈0.05 respectively (Figures 3–4). No other external baselines (e.g., IRM, EILLS) are directly compared; the contribution is to show that standard SGD with heterogeneous batching already achieves invariance without any explicit regularization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the implicit bias literature (which has focused on single-distribution settings and simplicity/low-rank preferences) to multi-environment heterogeneous data, showing that SGD's implicit bias can drive learning toward invariant solutions without any explicit regularization. It builds directly on the over-parameterized matrix sensing framework of Li et al. [31] (U U^T parameterization, small initialization) and the concave-function analysis of HaoChen et al. [20] for controlling signal dynamics. The key departure from prior invariance learning work (IRM, EILLS, etc.) is that no specialized algorithm is needed—standard SGD with large step size on per-environment batches suffices, positioning heterogeneity itself as a source of implicit regularization rather than a nuisance to be corrected.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides a theoretical explanation for why models trained on heterogeneous data may naturally acquire robust, even causal, predictions without explicit invariance objectives—potentially reducing the need for specialized algorithms like IRM in practice. However, the guarantees require restrictive conditions (RIP, diagonal dominance of spurious signals, near-orthogonality of invariant and spurious subspaces, large step size in a narrow range), and the empirical validation is limited to small-scale matrix sensing and two-layer quadratic networks. Open questions include whether over-parameterization is truly necessary, whether both heterogeneity and stochasticity are indispensable, and whether the phenomenon extends to deeper networks or real-world heterogeneous data distributions.
