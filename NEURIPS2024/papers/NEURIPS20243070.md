## Problem

While global remapping of hippocampal place cells is known to encode environmental context, no prior work had explicitly calculated how the number of storable contexts depends on place field firing properties such as width, noise level, and neuron count. The paper also identifies a fundamental trade-off between high-resolution position encoding and contextual capacity that had not been formally quantified or linked to the observed dorsal-ventral gradient in field sizes.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243070/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The model consumes a population of N place cells with Gaussian firing fields of width W, placed via gamma-poisson statistics in 1D or 2D environments, under either constant Gaussian noise (σ) or rate-dependent Poisson-like noise (φ), with optional beta-distributed boundary bias (parameter α). It outputs the number of storable contexts M(N) scaling as N^(1/4)·e^(γN), the exponent γ (a function of field width, noise, and room geometry but independent of N at large N), pairwise separability probability P₂, and the Cramer-Rao bound on spatial decoding error. The key structural feature is that γ is fully determined by the mean and variance of the minimum rate-space distance distribution, making it computable from just two summary statistics of the manifold geometry.

## Method

The core technique embeds each context as a low-dimensional manifold in N-dimensional firing rate space, where position parametrizes points along the manifold, and noise thickens these manifolds into tubes (constant noise) or ellipsoidal neighborhoods (Poisson-like noise). Context separability is then a geometric non-overlap condition: for constant noise, the minimum inter-manifold distance must exceed 2σ(√N+q); for Poisson noise, the paper applies a convex function K(s) to test ellipsoid intersection, reducing the condition to min_{x_A,x_B} Nφ*(x_A,x_B) > (√N+q)²φ. By invoking the central limit theorem to show that minimum distances are asymptotically Gaussian, the paper derives via a union bound that M(N) ~ N^(1/4)·e^(γN), with γ set by the signal-to-noise ratio of the distance distribution. Spatial resolution is quantified through the Fisher Information and Cramer-Rao bound, revealing that narrow fields maximize position decoding while wide fields maximize context capacity, and that the optimal width interpolates between these extremes depending on the relative weighting of the two objectives.

## Results

The paper's central quantitative result is that the number of storable contexts M(N) scales as N^(1/4)·e^(γN) at large N, where the exponent γ is determined by the mean and variance of the minimum inter-manifold distance distribution and is independent of N. For 1 m environments with the Poisson-like noise model, γ is maximized when firing field widths are approximately the size of the environment (~1 m), while for larger environments (≥4 m) smaller relative widths become preferable. The Poisson-like (rate-dependent) noise model is substantially more robust to noise than the constant-variance Gaussian model, maintaining positive γ over a wider range of noise levels. Boundary clustering of place field centers (parameterized by α < 1 in a symmetric beta distribution) increases the minimum inter-context distance near boundaries, which is where confusion is most likely, and the optimal α increases with firing field width.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical framework paper with no external method baselines to compare against; the 'comparisons' are internal to the model. The two noise models (constant-variance Gaussian vs. rate-dependent Poisson-like) are compared throughout: the Poisson-like model consistently yields higher γ values and greater robustness to noise, particularly in 2D environments (Fig. 3, Supplemental Fig. 8). The paper also compares uniform vs. boundary-biased place field distributions, showing that boundary clustering (α < 1) raises the minimum of the inter-context distance surface near boundaries until it discontinuously jumps to the center (Fig. 5D). The trade-off analysis (Fig. 4) compares two objective function formulations (using ln M vs. ln P₂ for context capacity), showing that the former produces a sharp jump in optimal field width while the latter yields a smooth transition, but both confirm the fundamental tension between spatial resolution and context segregation. The geometric framework is validated against the known experimental observation that place field widths increase along the dorsal-ventral axis, with the model predicting that ventral (wider-field) cells are optimized for context storage while dorsal (narrower-field) cells are optimized for spatial precision.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the geometric/manifold framework for neural population coding (Kriegeskorte et al. 2008; Chung et al. 2018) to the specific problem of hippocampal place cell context storage, combining it with the ellipsoid intersection test of Gilitschenski & Hanebeck (2012) and the gamma-Poisson firing field statistics from Lee et al. (2020) and Tanni et al. (2022). While prior work (Moser et al. 2015; Yim et al. 2021) speculated that global remapping enables storage of many contexts, the authors note that 'precise calculations backing these speculations are lacking' and provide the first explicit geometric derivation of contextual capacity as a function of firing field width, noise model, and environment size. The capacity-resolution trade-off formalized here parallels the analogous trade-off in recurrent neural networks (Battista & Monasson 2020), but is grounded in biologically constrained place cell statistics rather than generic network architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a mechanistic, quantitative explanation for the dorsal-ventral gradient in place field width: dorsal cells (narrow fields) optimize spatial resolution while ventral cells (wide fields) optimize context segregation, consistent with lesion studies showing dissociable spatial vs. contextual impairments. The prediction that boundary clustering of place fields systematically improves context discrimination—and that this bias should vary along the dorsal-ventral axis—is a concrete, testable hypothesis. Key open questions include the effects of partial remapping, the role of decoder architecture and network connectivity constraints, interactions with border and grid cells, and whether the geometric framework extends to abstract (non-spatial) cognitive maps. The strict separability criterion, while conservative, may overestimate the difficulty of real-world context discrimination, and the rate-coding assumption excludes spike-timing information.
