## Problem

Existing human feedback datasets for LLM alignment rely on binary A/B comparisons from small, demographically narrow samples (crowdworkers, tech communities), with limited participant metadata and no documentation of how values are operationalised. This makes it impossible to study how subjective, multicultural, and individualised preferences shape alignment, particularly on value-laden and controversial topics where interpersonal disagreement is expected. PRISM addresses this by linking detailed participant profiles to fine-grained contextual preferences across 1,500 diverse participants from 75 countries.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243341/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Participants first complete a survey capturing demographics, LLM familiarity, a self-written 'constitution' (system string), structured importance ratings on behaviour attributes (0–100 VAS), and a free-text self-description. They then engage in multi-turn conversations with 21 LLMs, rating each response on a 1–100 cardinal visual analogue scale (numeric value hidden to avoid anchoring), providing fine-grained performance and choice attribute ratings, and open-ended natural language feedback. The resulting 8,011 conversations (68,371 utterances) are linked to pseudonymous participant profiles, enabling both personalised alignment studies and aggregate welfare analyses across sub-populations.

## Method

The dataset employs a two-stage protocol combining stated preferences (survey) with contextual preferences (live conversations) to track how abstract values translate into in-situ judgments. Conversations follow a structured format: the first turn presents four model responses to a participant-chosen prompt, and subsequent turns continue with the highest-rated model generating two responses at non-deterministic temperature, with participants rating on a cardinal VAS. For model ranking, the paper applies Pairwise Rank Centrality and Convergence Voting (PageRank-inspired social choice methods) over pairwise battle wins to produce collective preference scores sensitive to sample composition and conversational context. Topic analysis embeds opening prompts with a sentence transformer, reduces dimensionality via UMAP, and clusters with HDBSCAN to identify 22 topics. Welfare experiments use first-order stochastic dominance to compare how different sampling schemes (representative vs. demographically restricted, varying sample sizes) affect the alignment of the chosen LLM to sub-populations.

## Results

PRISM contains 8,011 conversations (27,172 interactions, 68,371 utterances) between 1,500 participants from 75 countries and 21 LLMs. Topic clustering (HDBSCAN on UMAP-reduced sentence-transformer embeddings) identifies 22 topic clusters covering 70% of prompts, with 84% of local prompt neighbourhoods meeting or exceeding intersectional demographic entropy expected under random sampling. Model rankings via Pairwise Rank Centrality are highly sensitive to sample composition: gpt models rank significantly worse and open models like zephyr-7b rank significantly better in PRISM compared to Chatbot Arena (95% CI over 1,000 bootstraps). In the welfare analysis, the model maximising MEANCHOICE achieves only 45% selection probability, and sampling exclusively from a demographic group (e.g., US males) is first-order stochastically dominated by sampling from the full representative population for out-group welfare.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not propose a new alignment method to benchmark against baselines; instead, it uses PRISM as a diagnostic lens on existing model rankings. The primary external comparison is against Chatbot Arena: applying the same Pairwise Rank Centrality aggregation to Chatbot Arena data shows that gpt models fare significantly worse in PRISM while open models like zephyr-7b do significantly better (95% CI over 1,000 bootstraps), attributed to domain shift (task-oriented/coding prompts vs. controversial/cultural prompts), sample diversity, and task incentives. Within PRISM, the authors test whether formatting and refusal language explain score differences via OLS regression, finding significant effects (additional characters, ending in a question mark, enumeration positive; line breaks and de-anthropomorphic phrases negative) but only R²=0.06 explained variance, indicating that content and participant-level factors dominate. In the welfare case study, sampling 100 individuals from a single demographic group (male, white, or ≥45) is first-order stochastically dominated by sampling 100 from the full representative population for out-group welfare, and no single model achieves majority preference (max 45% MEANCHOICE), underscoring that no universal 'best' LLM exists across diverse populations.

## Relation to Prior Work

PRISM extends the human-feedback-for-alignment literature (RLHF, Constitutional AI, DPO) by introducing a dataset that links individual-level survey profiles—demographics, stated preferences, self-written 'constitutions'—to contextual preference ratings in live multi-turn conversations with 21 LLMs. Its aggregation methodology draws on Pairwise Rank Centrality and Convergence Voting (both PageRank-inspired social-choice methods) rather than the binary A/B comparisons dominant in prior datasets like Chatbot Arena or LMSYS-1M. The paper deliberately departs from existing resources by targeting subjective, value-laden, and cross-cultural controversial topics where interpersonal disagreement is expected, and by including census-representative samples for the UK and US alongside a geographically balanced global sample, addressing gaps in representativeness and individual-level attribution that the authors identify in prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PRISM is the first alignment dataset to permit individualised, multicultural, and participatory analysis of human feedback, opening concrete research avenues for personalised alignment, consensus-seeking across opinion distributions, and democratic input on AI-citizen interactions. The welfare analysis demonstrates that sampling alignment data from demographically narrow groups systematically reduces welfare for out-groups, and that no single LLM achieves majority preference (max 45% MEANCHOICE), challenging the implicit assumption that a 'best' aligned model exists for a population. Key limitations—English-only, crowdworker bias, cardinal-scale measurement noise, and the low explanatory power of surface formatting features (R²=0.06)—leave open questions about generalisability and the relative importance of content versus style in preference formation.
