## Problem

Prior theoretical work on double descent for linear models has established that the peak in generalization error occurs at or beyond the interpolation point (c = d/n = 1), with the under-parameterized regime assumed to be monotonic. This paper challenges that assumption by showing the peak location is not universally fixed at c = 1 but depends on two spectral properties of the data: the alignment between the target vector and the right singular vectors of the training matrix, and the behavior of the Stieltjes transform of the limiting empirical spectral distribution. The gap is that no prior theory explains how these properties can shift the peak into the under-parameterized regime.

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a training data matrix X ∈ R^{d×n}, a target vector y, a ridge parameter μ ≥ 0, and the aspect ratio c = d/n as the free variable. It produces closed-form asymptotic expressions for the generalization error R(c, μ) and identifies the exact c-value at which the risk curve attains a local maximum. Unusually, the peak location is a function of model hyperparameters: in the first example it is c = 1/(1+μ²), and in the second it is c = π₁ (the isotropic mixture weight), both strictly less than 1. The paper also outputs the expected norm of the estimator ‖W_opt‖_F², showing it can peak in the under-parameterized regime even when the risk does not.

## Method

The paper constructs two minimal models that provably exhibit under-parameterized double descent. The first is a spiked covariate model where the signal matrix is rank-1 (X_trn = σ_trn · u v_trn^T) and the target depends only on the dominant eigendirection; ridge regularization with parameter μ creates a misalignment between y and the right singular vectors of the augmented matrix, shifting the peak to c = 1/(1+μ²) (Theorem 2). The second is a mixture model where data points are drawn from N(0, I/d) with probability π₁ and from a fixed direction z with probability π₂; the rank-1 spike does not affect the smallest eigenvalue of the covariance, so the Stieltjes transform at 0 peaks at c = π₁ rather than c = 1 (Theorem 4). The proofs rely on the Sherman-Morrison formula for pseudoinverses to derive explicit estimator formulas, then use random matrix theory (Marchenko-Pastur limits, Stieltjes transforms) and concentration of measure to evaluate the resulting trace terms asymptotically. Ablation experiments confirm that the peak is caused by alignment mismatch (not spectrum shift) in the first model, and by the shifted Stieltjes transform peak in the second.

## Results

The paper proves two main theorems establishing under-parameterized double descent. Theorem 2 shows that for a spiked covariate model with ridge parameter μ, the generalization risk has a local maximum at c = 1/(1+μ²) < 1 (e.g., c ≈ 0.5 for μ=1, c ≈ 0.2 for μ=2). Theorem 4 shows that for a mixture of isotropic Gaussian and directional data with mixture weight π₁, the risk peaks at c = π₁ < 1. Numerical verification with d=1000, n_tst=1000, and 100+ trials per point confirms the theoretical risk curves match empirical values. Additionally, Theorem 3 demonstrates that the norm of the estimator can exhibit a peak at c = (μ²+1)⁻¹ even when the risk curve is monotonic, decoupling the two phenomena.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are against prior theoretical predictions rather than competing methods. All prior work on linear regression (e.g., [5, 8, 11, 23]) predicts the peak occurs at c=1 (the interpolation point) or in the over-parameterized regime, and attributes it to the norm of the estimator blowing up. This paper's two examples directly contradict both predictions: the peak shifts into the under-parameterized regime (c < 1) and, in the parameter scaling regime, the risk can be monotonic while the estimator norm still peaks. The ablation experiments (Figure 3) isolate the cause: modifying only the spectrum (not the alignment) keeps the peak at c=1, while randomizing the alignment moves the peak to the over-parameterized side, confirming that alignment mismatch—not spectral properties alone—drives the under-parameterized peak. The mixture model (Theorem 4) further shows that even with isotropic targets (no alignment issue), modifying the spectrum via a low-rank spike shifts the peak to c=π₁, violating the Stieltjes transform peak assumption. These results collectively show that the two standard explanations (norm blow-up and interpolation threshold) are insufficient to determine peak location.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the random matrix theory framework of Sonthalia and Nadakuditi [24] for analyzing denoising and linear regression, and on Meyer's [47] Sherman-Morrison formula for pseudoinverses, to derive explicit risk formulas in two new model settings. It departs from the prevailing double descent literature (Hastie et al. [11], Dobriban and Wager [5], Nakkiran et al. [23]) which establishes that peaks occur at the interpolation point c=1 or in the over-parameterized regime, and from Chen, Min, Belkin, and Karbasi [27] who proved under-parameterized double descent is impossible for isotropic Gaussian data. The paper identifies two specific violations of implicit assumptions—alignment between targets and singular vectors, and the location of the Stieltjes transform peak—that shift the peak into the under-parameterized regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results challenge the field's working assumption that the under-parameterized regime always exhibits monotonic variance and that peaks are pinned to the interpolation threshold, suggesting the double descent narrative is incomplete even for simple linear models. The finding that the estimator norm can peak without the risk peaking (Section 4.4) further decouples two quantities that prior theory treated as linked. However, the constructions are quite specific (rank-1 spiked covariance, two-component mixtures), and the paper itself notes that understanding whether analogous phenomena arise in deep neural networks remains an open question. The practical impact is currently limited to refining theoretical understanding rather than guiding model selection.
