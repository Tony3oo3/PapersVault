## Problem

Efficient influence function (EIF)-based estimators such as debiased/double ML and TMLE achieve optimal √N convergence rates but require case-by-case manual derivation of the EIF for each model-functional pair, a process that is time-consuming, error-prone, and inaccessible to most practitioners. Unlike automatic differentiation and probabilistic programming systems that automate numerical inference, EIF computation has resisted similar automation because exact recovery requires solving high-dimensional integral equations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a differentiable parametric probabilistic model P_φ, a target functional Ψ (expressible as a differentiable function ψ of φ), N observed data points split into training and held-out halves, and an initial parameter estimate φ̂ obtained via MLE or MAP. It requires M Monte Carlo samples drawn from P_φ to approximate the Fisher information matrix, and the functional gradient ∇_φ ψ(φ) must be estimable via a sequence of bounded-error MC estimators. The output is a pointwise MC-EIF approximation φ̂_{φ,M}(x) that serves as a drop-in replacement for the analytic EIF in any of three estimator templates (one-step, DML, TMLE), yielding a corrected estimate θ̂ with the same asymptotic guarantees as the oracle EIF-based estimator.

## Method

The key insight is that the EIF for a parametric model admits the closed-form expression [∇_φ ψ(φ)]^T I(φ)^{-1} ∇_φ log P_φ(x̃) (Theorem 3.4), where all three factors are computable from quantities already available in AD and PPL systems. MC-EIF replaces the unknown functional gradient and Fisher information with Monte Carlo approximations: the Fisher information inverse-times-score is computed via conjugate gradient iterations using Pearlmutter's trick for Jacobian-vector products (avoiding explicit p×p matrix storage), and the functional gradient is estimated via automatically differentiable MC estimators. The paper proves a non-asymptotic error bound (Theorem 3.8) showing M = Ω(Np log p) Monte Carlo samples suffice to ensure the MC-EIF approximation error is o_p(1/√N), preserving the optimal convergence rate of the analytic EIF estimator. This modular design means MC-EIF integrates directly into existing differentiable PPLs like Pyro and ChiRho, and advances in general-purpose probabilistic inference translate directly into advances in efficient estimation.

## Results

On the Markowitz optimal portfolio optimization task (D=25 assets, N=1000), the MC-EIF one-step estimator achieves a relative expected volatility (REV) of 1.86±0.35 and RMSE of 0.08±0.02, substantially outperforming the plug-in estimator (REV 2.60±0.35, RMSE 0.14±0.02). For ATE estimation with 200 confounders and 500 training points, MC-EIF produces estimates at parity with the analytic EIF oracle across all three estimator types (One Step, TMLE, and Double ML). MC-EIF approximates the true EIF with M=10⁴ Monte Carlo samples, while the empirical Gateaux baseline is highly sensitive to kernel hyperparameters and becomes numerically infeasible for p>2.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MC-EIF against two baselines. Against the empirical Gateaux derivative method (Jordan et al. 2022; Carone et al. 2019), MC-EIF wins decisively: it requires only a single tunable parameter (M) versus two kernel hyperparameters (ε, λ) that are highly sensitive and whose required precision degrades exponentially with input dimension, making the Gateaux approach infeasible for D≈10 and beyond. The authors were unable to obtain numerically stable Gateaux solutions for any p>2 without prohibitively long run-times, whereas MC-EIF scales cleanly. Against the analytic EIF (the oracle), MC-EIF achieves parity: in the ATE experiments with 200 confounders, MC-EIF-based One Step, TMLE, and Double ML estimators produce estimates that lie on or very near the diagonal of the oracle comparison plots (Figure 3), confirming that the Monte Carlo approximation error is negligible relative to statistical estimation error. The portfolio optimization capstone (Table 1) further demonstrates that MC-EIF enables a ~28% reduction in REV and ~43% reduction in RMSE over the uncorrected plug-in estimator for a problem (global minimum variance portfolio) for which no efficient estimator previously existed in the literature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes a decades-old formula from semiparametric theory (Tsiatis, 2006, Theorem 3.5) — the EIF as a product of the functional gradient, inverse Fisher information, and score — by showing it can be computed end-to-end via Monte Carlo sampling and automatic differentiation within a probabilistic programming framework. It departs from the empirical Gateaux derivative approach (Jordan et al., 2022; Carone et al., 2019), which approximates influence functions via finite differences on kernel-smoothed distributions and suffers from numerical instability in moderate dimensions, and from the traditional workflow of manually deriving closed-form EIFs case by case. The conjugate-gradient Fisher-vector-product trick is borrowed from the empirical influence function literature (Koh & Liang, 2017), but is repurposed here for the semiparametric EIF rather than the parametric empirical influence function, a distinction the paper emphasizes is non-trivial.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is that practitioners can now plug a differentiable probabilistic program and a target functional into MC-EIF to obtain an efficient estimator without deriving the EIF by hand — a task that has historically required expert-level semiparametric analysis and is error-prone. The portfolio optimization capstone demonstrates that MC-EIF can produce efficient estimators for functionals (e.g., the global minimum variance portfolio) for which no analytic EIF is known in the literature. Key limitations include the requirement of finite-dimensional differentiable parameters, the computational cost of Fisher information matrix operations at scale, and the need for M = Ω(Np log p) Monte Carlo samples, which may be prohibitive for very large models. Open questions include extending MC-EIF to latent-variable models, infinite-dimensional targets, and more scalable approximations to the Fisher matrix (e.g., Kronecker-factored approximations).
