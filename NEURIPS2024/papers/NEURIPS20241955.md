## Problem

3D Gaussian Splatting achieves state-of-the-art real-time rendering quality but relies on low-order spherical harmonics (SH) for view-dependent appearance, which fundamentally cannot represent the high-frequency information needed for specular highlights and anisotropic surfaces. Additionally, 3D-GS overfits in real-world scenes, producing floaters in novel views due to sparse COLMAP initialization and gradient cancellation during densification.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241955/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of posed RGB images and a COLMAP SfM point cloud as initialization. Each 3D Gaussian carries an additional 24-dimensional local feature vector that feeds the ASG appearance MLP, and the method optionally uses anchor Gaussians (from Scaffold-GS) to compress these features into a shared 32-dim anchor feature. During training, a coarse-to-fine schedule progressively increases rendering resolution from 4× downsampled to full resolution over 5k iterations. The output is a differentiable 3D Gaussian scene rendered in real-time (33–180 FPS depending on variant) that faithfully reproduces specular and anisotropic appearance from novel viewpoints.

## Method

Spec-Gaussian replaces the standard 3-order SH appearance model with an ASG appearance field: each 3D Gaussian stores 24-dim local features that a compact MLP decodes into parameters (λ, μ, ξ) for N=32 anisotropic spherical Gaussians. Rather than using ASG to directly output color, the method concatenates the N ASG responses queried along the reflect direction ω_r (computed from an unsupervised normal estimated as the Gaussian's shortest axis) into a latent feature κ, which a 3-layer feature-decoding MLP Ψ maps—along with positional encoding of the view direction and the normal–view dot product—to the specular color c_s. The total color is decomposed as c = c_d + c_s, where c_d is the diffuse component still modeled by 3-order SH, enabling the network to separate low- and high-frequency appearance. A coarse-to-fine training strategy applies an L1 norm to per-pixel gradient contributions for densification (preventing negative-gradient cancellation) and ramps rendering resolution from 4× downsampled to full over 5k iterations, eliminating floaters without increasing Gaussian count. An anchor-based variant (Ours-w/anchor) further compresses ASG parameters by sharing a single anchor feature across k neural Gaussians, reducing storage to 19–27 MB while maintaining competitive quality.

## Results

On Mip-NeRF 360 (real-world), Spec-Gaussian achieves 28.18 PSNR / 0.835 SSIM / 0.176 LPIPS, outperforming 3D-GS (27.79/0.826/0.202) and Scaffold-GS (27.98/0.824/0.207) while running at 33 FPS. On the NeRF synthetic benchmark, it reaches 34.19 PSNR / 0.971 SSIM / 0.028 LPIPS vs. 33.32/0.969/0.031 for 3D-GS. On the authors' new Anisotropic Synthetic dataset, the improvement is most dramatic: 37.70 PSNR vs. 33.82 for 3D-GS (+3.88 dB), demonstrating the ASG appearance field's strength on specular and anisotropic surfaces. The method trades rendering speed (33–145 FPS vs. 306–325 FPS for 3D-GS) for substantially better perceptual quality, especially on indoor scenes with specular highlights.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Spec-Gaussian consistently outperforms 3D-GS [23] across all four main benchmarks: +0.39 dB PSNR on Mip-NeRF 360, +0.87 dB on NeRF Synthetic, +1.33 dB on NSVF, and +3.88 dB on the Anisotropic Synthetic dataset, with the largest gains on scenes containing specular highlights and anisotropic materials. Against Scaffold-GS [32], the advantage is similar in magnitude (+0.20 dB on Mip-360, +0.51 dB on NeRF, +1.97 dB on Anisotropic Synthetic), and the anchor-based variant (Ours-w/anchor) further reduces memory to 260 MB while maintaining competitive quality. Compared to NeRF-based methods (iNGP [38], Mip-NeRF 360 [2], Plenoxels [68]), Spec-Gaussian achieves 2.6–5.1 dB higher PSNR on Mip-360 while rendering 3–450× faster (33 FPS vs. 0.06–9.43 FPS). On the NSVF dataset, Spec-Gaussian surpasses NeuRBF [8] (38.40 vs. 37.80 PSNR) and TensoRF [5] (38.40 vs. 36.52 PSNR), confirming that the ASG appearance field enables Gaussian splatting to match or exceed dedicated NeRF methods on metallic and reflective scenes. The primary trade-off is rendering speed: the full model runs at 33–145 FPS versus 306–325 FPS for vanilla 3D-GS, though the light and anchor variants recover 44–172 FPS at a small quality cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends 3D Gaussian Splatting (3D-GS) by replacing its low-order spherical harmonics (SH) appearance model with an anisotropic spherical Gaussian (ASG) field, a classical rendering primitive from 2013 that was previously used in traditional lighting pipelines. It also adapts the anchor-based structured representation from Scaffold-GS to reduce storage overhead introduced by the additional ASG parameters, and introduces a coarse-to-fine training schedule to mitigate floaters in real-world scenes. Conceptually, the work bridges classical anisotropic shading theory with the modern differentiable rasterization pipeline, positioning ASG as a drop-in replacement for SH that captures high-frequency view-dependent effects without increasing the number of Gaussians.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a well-known and practically important limitation of 3D-GS: its inability to faithfully render specular highlights and anisotropic surfaces, which restricts its use in photorealistic real-time applications. The introduction of a dedicated anisotropic synthetic benchmark is a useful contribution for the community to evaluate such capabilities. However, the authors explicitly note that reflections remain out of scope because 3D-GS lacks explicit geometry to separate material from environment, leaving a significant gap for fully reflective scenes. The coarse-to-fine training strategy, while effective, is a relatively simple heuristic whose generalizability across scene types and optimization schedules is not deeply explored.
