## Problem

While extensive work has shown that LLMs store factual information in mid-layer MLP parameters, no equivalent understanding exists for Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs), which process both continuous visual tokens and discrete text tokens through additional vision-encoder and projection modules. Standard causal tracing methods fail in the multi-modal setting because injecting Gaussian noise into hundreds of visual token embeddings makes it impossible to recover causal states. This paper asks: where in an MLLM's internal layers is factual visual information stored, and how does it flow from the image to the final answer?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a natural RGB image paired with a short factual question (e.g., 'Which city is this building located in?') and decomposes the question into visual constraints (words referring to an image entity, e.g., 'this building') and optional textual constraints (e.g., a year or award name). The method produces three types of outputs: (1) per-layer indirect estimation effect scores identifying which MLP and self-attention blocks are causally responsible for retrieving the answer, (2) attention contribution matrices quantifying how specific visual tokens feed information into causal layers and how mid-layer self-attention propagates it to the final token position, and (3) for MULTEDIT, a closed-form weight update to a single early causal MLP's projection matrix that changes the model's generated answer. The VQA-Constraints test-bed provides 9.7K such image-question pairs with annotated constraints across OK-VQA, WikiMovies, and Known.

## Method

MULTIMODALCAUSALTRACE extends causal tracing to MLLMs by corrupting the model not with Gaussian noise (which fails due to the large number of visual tokens) but by replacing the visual constraint's token IDs with an unrelated phrase, so the image information becomes irrelevant to the question. Layer activations (MLP and self-attention outputs) are then iteratively copied from the clean model to the corrupted model over a sliding window, and the indirect estimation effect (restored output probability minus corrupted probability) identifies which layers are causally necessary. For information transfer, the paper computes attention contributions (Eq. 1) to show that only the last ~36 visual tokens from LLaVA's CLIP encoder feed the early causal MLPs via the first self-attention layer, while mid-layer self-attention (layers 16–17) shuttles the stored information to the final token. MULTEDIT then exploits these findings by applying a closed-form rank-one update (Eq. 7) to the W_proj matrix of a single early causal MLP, mapping the constraint token's input embedding to an optimized output embedding that increases the probability of the correct answer, with an L2 regularization term to preserve performance on unrelated questions.

## Results

MULTEDIT corrects erroneous VQA answers in LLaVA-7B, improving the average probability of the correct answer from 0.07 to 0.82 (editing efficacy), with 0.76 generalization on paraphrased questions and only a 1.5% drop in VQA accuracy on unrelated questions (specificity). For inserting long-tailed knowledge from Encyclopedia-VQA, MULTEDIT achieves an editing efficacy of 0.83 and generalization of 0.79, with a 1.4% specificity drop. On the interpretability side, MULTIMODALCAUSALTRACE identifies early MLP layers (1–4) as causal in LLaVA-7B (vs. mid-layers 4–7 in LLaMA-7B), with a minimum window size of 1 sufficient for MLLMs versus 5 for LLMs. Mid-layer self-attention contributions (layers 16–17) predict correct answers with AUROC 0.63, compared to 0.76 for model confidence.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares MULTEDIT against two fine-tuning baselines: (i) full fine-tuning from Meng et al. [23], which updates all layers with a language modeling objective, and (ii) constrained fine-tuning from Zhu et al. [38], which adds a weight-continuity constraint. MULTEDIT outperforms both baselines on all three metrics (editing efficacy, generalization, and specificity) in both the error-correction and long-tailed knowledge insertion settings. Specifically, for fixing incorrect answers, MULTEDIT achieves 0.82 editing efficacy versus lower values for both fine-tuning baselines, while incurring only a 1.5% specificity drop compared to larger drops for the baselines. For long-tailed insertion, MULTEDIT reaches 0.83 efficacy and 0.79 generalization with a 1.4% specificity drop, again surpassing both baselines. The key advantage of MULTEDIT is its surgical nature: it edits only a single early causal MLP layer via a closed-form update, whereas both baselines modify many or all layers, leading to greater collateral damage on unrelated questions.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal tracing methodology developed for LLMs (Meng et al., 2022/2023) to the multi-modal setting, introducing a novel corruption strategy that replaces visual constraint tokens rather than injecting Gaussian noise, which the authors show is necessary to recover causal states in MLLMs. It also adapts the constraint-satisfaction framework from Yuksekgonul et al. (2023) by introducing visual constraints alongside textual ones, and leverages attention contribution analysis from Elhage et al. (2021) to trace information flow. For model editing, MULTEDIT repurposes the closed-form linear-associated memory update from Kohonen (1972) and Basu et al. (2023, text-to-image models) to target early causal MLPs in MLLMs, positioning itself as the first to apply this update to the projection-layer MLLM family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic study of where and how MLLMs store and transfer factual information, revealing that the mid-layer MLP storage pattern well-established in LLMs does not carry over to MLLMs. The practical implication is that targeted single-layer edits (MULTEDIT) can correct errors and insert long-tailed knowledge with minimal collateral damage, offering a lightweight alternative to full fine-tuning. However, the scope is limited to factual short-answer VQA on natural images and the projection-layer MLLM family; open questions include whether findings generalize to cross-attention MLLMs, subjective questions, non-natural image domains, and whether the consistent subset of late visual tokens can be mapped to specific image concepts.
