## Problem

Prior shared autonomy systems keep the copilot constantly active at every time step, using a fixed hyperparameter (e.g., diffusion ratio) to trade off assistance against human autonomy. This limits pilot autonomy and can degrade performance, especially when the required level of help varies with task dynamics. The paper asks how to dynamically and selectively intervene so the copilot only takes control when the pilot's action is universally bad across all possible goals, preserving autonomy while preventing entry into deleterious states.

## Input / Output

The intervention function receives three inputs at each timestep: the goal-agnostic environment state s̃ (with all goal-specific information masked out), the pilot action a_p sampled from the human or surrogate policy, and the copilot action a_c generated by a diffusion model conditioned on (a_p, s̃). It outputs a binary decision T ∈ {0,1}: if T=1 the copilot action is played in the environment, otherwise the pilot action is played. The goal-agnostic constraint means the system never observes the pilot's intended goal, so the intervention must be justified across the entire goal space G* rather than for a single inferred goal.

## Method

IDA (Interventional Diffusion Assistance) combines three components: (1) a SAC expert trained to solve the environment, providing a Q-value estimator Q^{πe}(s,a); (2) a DDPM diffusion copilot trained on 10M expert state-action pairs with goal information removed, which denoises the pilot action toward the expert action distribution; and (3) a trajectory-based goal-agnostic intervention function that computes a copilot advantage A(s̃, a_c, a_p) by marginalizing the sign of Q^{πe}(s̃, a_c|ĝ) − Q^{πe}(s̃, a_p|ĝ) over the full goal space G*. The copilot intervenes only when A=1, i.e., when its action has strictly higher expected return than the pilot's action for every possible goal simultaneously. This makes the framework hyperparameter-free (no diffusion ratio or penalty weight to tune) and modular (compatible with any copilot architecture). The paper proves a lower bound: for a near-optimal pilot the IA policy guarantees J(π_I) ≥ J(π_p), and for a poor pilot J(π_I) ≥ J(π_c), ensuring the system never degrades performance relative to the better of the two policies.

## Results

In Lunar Lander with surrogate pilots, IDA achieves 100% success rate for the laggy pilot (vs. 61% pilot-only and 92% copilot) and 83% for the noisy pilot (vs. 21% pilot-only and 75% copilot), while reducing crash rates to 0% and 3.3% respectively. In the human-in-the-loop Lunar Lander experiment, IDA enables 91.7% successful landings compared to 14.0% for pilot-only and 68.2% for copilot control, with significantly fewer timeouts (0.1% vs. 13.8%). In Reacher, IDA approximately doubles the noisy pilot's hit rate from 1.6 to 3.3 targets per minute, while the copilot achieves 0 targets. IDA also preserves expert performance (100% success in Lunar Lander, 18.8 hits/min in Reacher), consistent with the theoretical guarantee that near-optimal pilots are not degraded.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IDA outperforms the diffusion copilot baseline (Yoneda et al., 2023) across all surrogate pilot conditions in both environments: in Lunar Lander, IDA achieves 83% vs. 75% success for the noisy pilot and 100% vs. 92% for the laggy pilot; in Reacher, IDA doubles the noisy pilot's hit rate (3.3 vs. 0 targets/min) while the copilot fails entirely. Against the penalty-based intervention method (Tan et al., 2022), IDA consistently achieves higher success rates (83% vs. 58% noisy; 100% vs. 76% laggy in Lunar Lander) and lower crash rates (3.3% vs. 4% noisy; 0% vs. 8% laggy). A key advantage over the copilot baseline is that IDA avoids the 13.8% timeout rate observed in human experiments, where the always-active copilot frequently prevented landings entirely. IDA also preserves expert performance (100% success, 0% crash) where the copilot degrades it (93.3% success, 0.3% crash), validating the theoretical guarantee that near-optimal pilots are not harmed. In the human study, participants subjectively rated IDA as significantly easier, more controllable, and more autonomous than copilot control (p<0.01, Wilcoxon signed-rank), while reporting autonomy levels comparable to pilot-only.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the diffusion-based shared autonomy framework of Yoneda et al. (2023), which uses a DDPM copilot to map human actions toward expert actions but requires a fixed, empirically tuned diffusion ratio to trade off assistance and autonomy. It also draws on the value-based intervention idea from the Teacher-Student Framework (Xue et al., 2023) and departs from Tan et al. (2022), which uniformly penalizes all copilot interventions. The key conceptual departure is replacing a fixed blending hyperparameter or a uniform intervention penalty with a goal-agnostic, value-based intervention function that activates the copilot only when its action dominates the pilot's action across the entire goal space, making the system hyperparameter-free and generalizable to unseen goals.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

IDA provides a clean theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) that the intervention policy never degrades a near-optimal pilot and is lower-bounded by the copilot for a poor pilot, addressing a practical concern that always-active copilots can harm performance. The human-in-the-loop results (91.7% success vs. 14% pilot-only and 68.2% copilot, with participants reporting greater autonomy under IDA) demonstrate that selective, value-based intervention can simultaneously improve task performance and preserve perceived user control. Key limitations include the requirement to train an expert Q-function in simulation and the assumption that the goal space (or a reasonable approximation thereof) is known; open directions include offline Q-function learning, belief-based goal weighting, and extension to higher-dimensional or real-world control tasks.
