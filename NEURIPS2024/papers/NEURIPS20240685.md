## Problem

AI benchmarks are the primary tool for comparing model performance, yet no structured framework exists to assess their quality, and no comparative analysis has examined quality differences across widely used benchmarks. This leaves model developers, users, and regulators without guidance on which benchmarks are reliable, reproducible, and well-documented versus those with significant methodological gaps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240685/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The assessment consumes heterogeneous, non-standardized information sources per benchmark—official websites, published papers, and GitHub repositories—and manually evaluates them against 46 criteria. Output is a set of discrete scores (0/5/10/15 per criterion) aggregated into four lifecycle-stage subscores (design, implementation, documentation, maintenance), which are further combined into a single design score and a weighted usability score. A developer-facing checklist with YES/NO/N/A fields and justification prompts accompanies the scoring for pre-deployment self-assessment.

## Method

The framework is derived through a six-step methodology: mapping the benchmark landscape, conducting unstructured interviews with 20+ stakeholders across five groups (benchmark developers, model developers, model users, AI researchers, regulators), translating objectives into 46 concrete criteria organized along a five-stage benchmark lifecycle, calibrating scoring via independent multi-annotator review, and validating with stakeholder feedback. Scoring uses a discrete 0/5/10/15 scale (not addressed / acknowledged / partially met / fully met) with an N/A option, and at least two authors must independently score each benchmark and reach consensus. The usability score is a weighted average of implementation, documentation, and maintenance subscores, weighted by the number of applicable criteria in each stage. The authors apply the framework to 24 benchmarks (16 foundation-model, 8 non-foundation-model) and publish results on a living repository (betterbench.stanford.edu) with a community feedback channel for continuous updates.

## Results

Across 24 assessed benchmarks (16 FM, 8 non-FM), the implementation stage is the weakest area for both categories (FM: 5.5/15, Non-FM: 7.4/15, All: 6.1/15), while design scores are highest (FM: 10.6, Non-FM: 11.1, All: 10.7). The widely-used MMLU benchmark scored the lowest overall usability (weighted average: 5.5), while GPQA scored significantly higher (11.0). Critically, 14 of 24 benchmarks did not report statistical significance or uncertainty of results, and 17 of 24 did not provide scripts to replicate published results (average score: 3.75/15 for replication scripts; 5.62/15 for statistical significance). A statistically significant positive correlation exists between design and usability scores for FM benchmarks (Pearson ρ=0.721, p=0.001) and all benchmarks combined (ρ=0.655, p=0.001), but not for non-FM benchmarks (ρ=0.318, p=0.487).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare methods against baselines in the traditional sense; instead, it assesses 24 existing AI benchmarks against a unified 46-criteria framework. The key comparison is between foundation model (FM) and non-FM benchmarks: non-FM benchmarks score higher on implementation (7.4 vs. 5.5) and maintenance (10.8 vs. 9.1), while FM benchmarks score slightly higher on design (10.6 vs. 11.1) and documentation (10.3 vs. 9.9). The difference in mean design and usability scores between FM and non-FM benchmarks is not statistically significant at the 95% confidence level. Among individual benchmarks, GPQA (usability: 11.0) substantially outperforms MMLU (usability: 5.5) despite both being widely reported by model developers (GPT-4, Claude-3, Gemini). The strong design–usability correlation for FM benchmarks (ρ=0.721) suggests that poorly designed FM benchmarks tend to also be less usable, a pattern not observed for non-FM benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first structured, multi-criteria assessment framework for AI benchmark quality, spanning the full benchmark lifecycle (design, implementation, documentation, maintenance). It draws on data documentation frameworks (data cards, FAIR principles) and benchmarking best practices from non-AI fields (transistor hardware, environmental quality, bioinformatics), but explicitly argues these are insufficient for benchmarks, which extend data with infrastructure and evaluation methods. Unlike prior critiques of AI benchmarks (e.g., McIntosh et al. 2024, Liao & Xiao 2024, Raji et al. 2021) that identify limitations in narrow contexts, this work provides a concrete 46-criteria scoring rubric and a living repository for continuous assessment, positioning itself as a practical tool rather than a diagnostic survey.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that the most widely-used benchmark (MMLU) scored lowest (5.5/15) while GPQA scored significantly higher (11.0/15), yet both are reported interchangeably in major model releases (GPT-4, Claude-3, Gemini) without quality caveats, raises serious concerns for model selection and AI policy. The fact that 14/24 benchmarks omit statistical significance and 17/24 lack replication scripts undermines the evidentiary basis for inter-model comparisons. Open questions include standardizing benchmark reporting, addressing contamination in web-scraped training data, and extending the framework to private benchmarks; the equal-weighting and 4-level scoring also limit granularity, and construct validity remains outside the assessment's scope.
