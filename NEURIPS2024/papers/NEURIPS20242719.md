## Problem

Standard Bellman updates in value-based RL take the maximum over all actions, including unseen ones, causing bootstrapping errors that propagate back into the replay memory and are difficult to correct even with techniques like Double Q-learning. The paper identifies that restricting the max operator to only in-sample actions (those present in the replay memory) yields a unique, well-defined solution for the empirical MDP, and proposes an iterative framework that alternates between solving this empirical MDP and exploring to grow it toward the full MDP.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242719/cropped_page1_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes environment observations (84×84 grayscale frames for Atari, low-dimensional state vectors for MuJoCo) and a growing replay memory of (s, a, r, s') transitions. It maintains two parallel value networks: a standard Q-network trained with the full Bellman update and a Q̂-network trained with the in-sample Bellman update. The output is an action (discrete via modified ε-greedy for DQN, or continuous via clipped Gaussian noise for TD3) plus the two Q-value estimates whose absolute difference δ(s,a) drives targeted exploration toward under-explored state-action pairs.

## Method

EMIT constructs a sequence of empirical MDPs from the growing replay memory, where transitions absent from the data are assigned reward −∞ and zero transition probability. For each empirical MDP, it learns Q̂ via the in-sample Bellman update (Eq. 2), which restricts the max to actions present in the data, guaranteeing a unique fixed point (proven via a γ-contraction argument). The standard Q-network is then regularized by adding α·MSE(Q, Q̂) to its Bellman loss (Eq. 4), pulling Q toward the accurate in-sample estimate. Exploration is driven by the value gap δ(s,a) = |Q(s,a) − Q̂(s,a)|: for DQN, δ is added to the greedy action score in ε-greedy selection; for TD3, δ serves as a state-action-dependent standard deviation for clipped Gaussian action noise. Theoretically, the paper proves monotonic improvement of Q̂* as the replay memory grows (under deterministic dynamics), and empirically shows that Q̂-based policies match or exceed active Q-based policies even when learned passively from the same data.

## Results

On 20 Atari games and 8 MuJoCo tasks, EMIT achieves the best average normalized performance across all 28 tasks, outperforming or matching (within 10%) the best baseline on 19 of 28 tasks. On MuJoCo continuous control tasks, EMIT-TD3 significantly surpasses all baselines including SAC, TD3, XQL, TRPO, and PPO. The regularization of Q with Q̂ reduces the estimation error (V_diff) to near zero throughout training, and policy churn is measurably reduced compared to DQN. Ablation shows the regularization term (α·MSE(Q, Q̂)) contributes more to performance gains than the exploration bonus δ(s,a).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On Atari (discrete actions), EMIT-DQN outperforms DQN, C51, IQN, and Rainbow across the 20 games, with the largest gains on tasks like Breakout and Seaquest where estimation error is most pronounced. On MuJoCo (continuous control), EMIT-TD3 significantly surpasses SAC, TD3, XQL, TRPO, and PPO, with particularly large margins on HalfCheetah, Hopper, and Walker2D. The paper also compares against two ablated variants: Q̂-online (IQL solving the empirical MDP without exploration) and Q-only (standard DQN/TD3 without regularization), showing that both the in-sample learning and the exploration mechanism are necessary for full performance. The Double Q technique, while mitigating overestimation on some tasks (e.g., Breakout), still underestimates on others (e.g., HalfCheetah), whereas EMIT's Q̂ regularization maintains near-zero estimation error across both task types. EMIT is positioned as a drop-in regularizer rather than a replacement, preserving the backbone algorithm's architecture while adding the Q̂ loss term and δ-based exploration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: online value-based RL (DQN, TD3) that solves the full MDP via the Bellman optimality equation, and offline RL methods (IQL, InAC, XQL) that restrict bootstrapping to in-sample actions. The key conceptual departure is framing the growing replay memory as a sequence of empirical MDPs to be solved iteratively, rather than treating the Bellman equation over the full MDP as the sole learning objective. EMIT repurposes IQL's in-sample Bellman update as a regularizer for standard Q-learning and uses the Q–Q̂ gap as a targeted exploration signal, positioning itself as a modular enhancement rather than a new algorithm. The theoretical analysis (unique convergence of in-sample updates, monotonic improvement under deterministic dynamics) extends prior offline RL guarantees into an online iterative setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EMIT provides a principled, drop-in framework for reducing the estimation error that plagues value-based RL with function approximation, with clean theoretical guarantees in the tabular case and consistent empirical gains across 28 Atari and MuJoCo tasks. The counterintuitive finding that a passive IQL agent matches or exceeds the active DQN/TD3 policy on identical data challenges the assumption that active interaction is necessary for good policy learning. A key limitation is the doubled wall-clock cost from maintaining two Q-networks, and the monotonic improvement guarantee holds only for deterministic MDPs. An open direction the authors identify is designing a single learning process that directly uses in-sample bootstrapping while incorporating exploration, eliminating the need for a separate Q̂ network.
