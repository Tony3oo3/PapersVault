## Problem

Existing LLM+GNN integration works target either conventional graph tasks (node/edge/graph classification) or simple structural queries on small synthetic graphs, leaving no framework for answering complex, open-ended questions about real-world textual graphs. A comprehensive benchmark for graph question answering across diverse domains (commonsense reasoning, scene understanding, knowledge graphs) was absent, and no retrieval-augmented generation approach existed for general textual graphs that simultaneously mitigates hallucination and scales beyond the LLM's context window.

## Input / Output

The input is a textual graph in a CSV-like format (node_id,node_attr rows and src,edge_attr,dst rows) paired with a free-form natural language question. Graphs range from ~5 nodes (ExplaGraphs) to ~1,370 nodes and ~4,250 edges (WebQSP), the latter far exceeding any LLM context window, making selective retrieval a hard requirement rather than an optimization. The output is a natural-language answer accompanied by the retrieved connected subgraph, which serves as a human-inspectable explanation of which graph elements grounded the response.

## Method

G-Retriever is a four-stage pipeline: (1) SentenceBERT encodes all node/edge attributes into a nearest-neighbor index; (2) k-nearest-neighbor retrieval on cosine similarity selects top-k relevant nodes and edges; (3) a Prize-Collecting Steiner Tree (PCST) optimization constructs a connected subgraph that maximizes relevance prizes while penalizing edge count, with edge prizes handled via virtual-node substitution; (4) a frozen Llama2-7b generates the answer conditioned on two complementary graph representations—a structural graph token produced by a Graph Transformer encoder followed by an MLP projection, and a textualized flattening of the retrieved subgraph. Only the GNN encoder and projection layer receive gradients (soft prompt tuning), keeping the LLM frozen. The PCST formulation is the key architectural choice: it guarantees the returned subgraph is connected (preserving neighborhood context), allows size control via an adjustable per-edge cost, and outperforms alternatives like top-k triples or shortest-path retrieval by 10–16 points in Hit@1.

## Results

Under the Frozen LLM with prompt tuning setting, G-Retriever achieves 0.8516 accuracy on ExplaGraphs, 0.8131 on SceneGraphs, and 70.49 Hit@1 on WebQSP, outperforming all baselines in each configuration. With LoRA fine-tuning, G-Retriever further improves to 0.8705, 0.8683, and 73.79 respectively. The method also dramatically improves efficiency: retrieval reduces token count by 83% (SceneGraphs) and 99% (WebQSP), and cuts training time by 29% and 67% respectively.

## Baselines & Comparisons

G-Retriever (Frozen LLM w/PT) decisively outperforms all inference-only baselines: Zero-CoT (0.5704/0.5260/51.30), CoT-BAG (0.5794/0.5680/39.60), and KAPING (0.6227/0.4375/52.64) on ExplaGraphs/SceneGraphs/WebQSP respectively. Against the closest prompt-tuning competitor GraphToken [31], G-Retriever is essentially tied on ExplaGraphs (0.8516 vs. 0.8508) but dominates on SceneGraphs (0.8131 vs. 0.4903, +65.9% relative) and WebQSP (70.49 vs. 57.05, +23.6% relative). Compared to standard prompt tuning [20], G-Retriever gains 47.77%, 28.23%, and 45.81% relative improvement across the three datasets. In the tuned-LLM setting, G-Retriever w/LoRA surpasses plain LoRA [10] by 1.95% on ExplaGraphs, 11.74% on SceneGraphs, and 10.44% on WebQSP, confirming that the retrieval component provides complementary benefits even when the LLM itself is fine-tuned.

## Relation to Prior Work

G-Retriever sits at the intersection of three research threads: retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020), which is adapted here from text to general textual graphs for the first time; the GNN-to-soft-prompt paradigm popularized by GraphToken (Perozzi et al., 2024), where a trainable GNN encodes graph structure as a prompt token for a frozen LLM; and the Prize-Collecting Steiner Tree optimization (Bienstock et al., 1993), repurposed as a subgraph retrieval mechanism that respects graph connectivity. The paper departs from prior LLM+GNN work, which targets conventional classification tasks or simple queries on small synthetic graphs, by formulating a unified conversational QA framework over real-world textual graphs. It also extends RAG beyond knowledge graphs (where retrieval is typically triple-based and structure-agnostic) to general graphs where neighborhood and connectivity matter during retrieval.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GraphQA benchmark standardizes three heterogeneous datasets into a uniform format, filling a gap for evaluating models on complex, real-world graph QA rather than toy problems. The 54% hallucination reduction and 99% token reduction on WebQSP demonstrate that RAG over graphs is both a faithfulness and a scalability solution, making LLM-based graph reasoning practical for large knowledge bases. A key limitation is the static, non-trainable retrieval component; making retrieval end-to-end learnable (e.g., via differentiable PCST or learned scoring) is a natural next step. The framework's reliance on a frozen LLM also means it inherits the LLM's reasoning ceiling, though the Llama2-7b→13b comparison confirms that stronger backbones help.
