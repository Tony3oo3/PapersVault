## Problem

The existing causal clustering framework (Causal k-Means, Kim et al. 2024) is limited to k-means, which assumes roughly spherical clusters, is sensitive to outliers, and requires a margin condition on the outcome process. This paper addresses the gap by extending the framework to hierarchical and density-based clustering, which handle arbitrary cluster shapes, varying densities, and nested structures without such strong structural assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method takes observational data (Y, A, X) with finite treatment levels A ∈ {1,...,q} and covariates X ∈ R^d, first estimating the q-dimensional counterfactual mean vector μ(X) = [μ₁(X),...,μ_q(X)] via flexible nonparametric methods (e.g., Super Learner ensemble). Clustering is then performed on the estimated points μ̂(X_i) in R^q, producing either a dendrogram with a pruning (hierarchical) or a collection of level sets at various thresholds t (density-based). The key distinction from standard clustering is that the points being clustered are plug-in estimates of unobserved counterfactual quantities, not directly observed features.

## Method

For hierarchical causal clustering, the paper adapts Balcan et al.'s robust agglomerative algorithm to the causal setting, showing that the linkage distance error between true and estimated counterfactual points is bounded by the sup-norm regression estimation error (Proposition 3.1). Under a (α,ν)-good neighborhood property on the population distribution, the inductive algorithm achieves classification error at most ν′+δ with a sample size independent of the full dataset, where the natural misclassification rate α is inflated only by O_P(Σ‖μ̂_a − μ_a‖_∞). For density-based causal clustering, the paper constructs a plug-in kernel density estimator on the estimated counterfactual mean space and targets upper level sets L_{h,t} = {μ: p_h(μ) > t}. Under a level-set stability condition, the Hausdorff distance between estimated and true level sets is bounded by the standard KDE rate plus a term involving the L1 regression estimation error scaled by h^{-(q+1)} (Theorem 4.1). Crucially, unlike causal k-means, neither method requires a margin condition, and the additional cost of causal clustering is essentially just the nuisance estimation error.

## Results

The paper's main contribution is theoretical: Theorem 3.2 shows that inductive robust hierarchical causal clustering achieves misclassification error at most ν′ + δ, where the natural noise parameter α is inflated only by O_P(Σ_a ‖μ̂_a − μ_a‖_∞) due to nuisance estimation. Theorem 4.1 bounds the Hausdorff distance between estimated and true level sets by the standard kernel density estimation rate √(log(1/h_n)/(nh_n^q)) plus a nuisance error term scaled by 1/h_n^{q+1}. Simulation studies (Figure 2) empirically confirm that classification error and Hausdorff distance increase monotonically with the nuisance estimation error rate n^{−β}, validating the theoretical predictions. In the voting study case study, density-based causal clustering identifies two clearly separable clusters (one with near-zero effect, one with negative effect) with ~4% of points classified as noise; in the BLS employment data, four distinct clusters are recovered across eight education-level treatments.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense; its `compares_to` list is empty. Instead, it extends the causal k-means clustering framework of Kim et al. [39] by adding hierarchical and density-based alternatives. The key practical advantage over causal k-means is that the proposed estimators do not require the margin condition that k-means clustering necessitates, making them applicable under weaker structural assumptions on the outcome process. The simulation in Figure 2(a)–(b) implicitly contrasts the hierarchical causal clustering error against the theoretical bound from Theorem 3.2, confirming that the inflation of the good-neighborhood parameter α is driven by the nuisance estimation error as predicted. The density-based results in Figure 2(c)–(d) similarly validate Theorem 4.1's rate. The case studies (Figures 3–5) demonstrate that the new methods recover interpretable subgroup structures (e.g., clusters with different baseline risk levels in the voting data, or occupations with substantially different education-level effects in the BLS data) that would be difficult to capture with k-means alone due to its spherical-cluster assumption.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Kim et al.'s (2024) Causal k-Means Clustering framework, which projected units onto the counterfactual mean vector space and applied k-means to discover treatment-effect subgroups. The key departure is replacing k-means with hierarchical (agglomerative, adapting Balcan et al.'s robust algorithm) and density-based (level-set kernel density estimation following Rinaldo et al.) clustering, thereby eliminating the margin condition required by the k-means variant. The plug-in estimation strategy for the nuisance outcome regression functions draws on standard nonparametric and machine learning tools (Super Learner, random forests, splines), and the theoretical analysis builds on empirical process theory and topological data analysis (Rips graphs, persistent homology).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The clean theoretical result—that the additional cost of causal clustering over standard clustering is essentially the L∞ (or L1) estimation error of the outcome regression functions—provides a clear roadmap for practitioners: invest in better CATE/nuisance estimation and clustering performance follows. The removal of the margin condition broadens applicability to settings where k-means causal clustering would be theoretically unjustified. Open questions include developing semiparametric (second-order bias) estimators tailored to hierarchical and density-based causal clustering to handle high-dimensional covariates, extending to other identification strategies (instrumental variables, proximal causal inference), and exploring whether the discovered subgroup structures can serve as inputs for downstream precision-medicine or optimal-policy tasks.
