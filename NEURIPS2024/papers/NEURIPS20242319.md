## Problem

Existing availability attacks (unlearnable examples) are designed to protect data against supervised learning but largely fail to prevent contrastive learning from extracting useful features from the same poisoned data. A malicious data collector can simply switch to contrastive algorithms like SimCLR or MoCo to bypass the protection. While CL-based attacks (CP, TUE, TP) address this gap, they are computationally expensive due to large batch sizes and long training, making them impractical for protecting large-scale datasets in real-world scenarios.

## Input / Output

The method takes labeled RGB training images (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, 84×84 for Mini-ImageNet, 224×224 for ImageNet-100) along with their class labels, and produces sample-wise L∞-bounded perturbations (ε = 8/255) that are added to each image. The resulting poisoned dataset is designed to simultaneously degrade the generalization performance of both supervised classifiers and contrastive encoders followed by linear probing, evaluated under a worst-case unlearnability metric that takes the maximum accuracy across all target algorithms.

## Method

The core insight is that training a supervised model with contrastive-like strong data augmentations (ResizedCrop, ColorJitter, Grayscale) implicitly optimizes the contrastive InfoNCE loss, as empirically verified and supported by a theoretical toy example showing the contrastive loss is upper-bounded by an increasing function of the supervised loss. Building on this, the paper proposes two attacks: AUE (Augmented Unlearnable Examples) replaces the mild augmentations in the UE error-minimization loop with contrastive augmentations of controllable strength s, and AAP (Augmented Adversarial Poisoning) applies the same augmentation enhancement to both the reference model pretraining and the adversarial perturbation generation in the AP error-maximization framework. Perturbations are updated via PGD (5 steps for AUE, 250 steps for AAP) with the augmented supervised loss, and the strength parameter s is tuned per dataset (e.g., s=0.6 for CIFAR-10, s=1.0 for CIFAR-100 in AUE). This keeps the entire generation pipeline within the supervised learning framework, avoiding the large batches and long training of CL-based attacks while still producing perturbations that create large alignment and uniformity gaps in contrastive feature spaces.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, AAP achieves the best worst-case unlearnability of 35.5% (vs. 44.0% for AP and 61.8% for TP), while AUE achieves 58.6%. On CIFAR-100, AUE achieves the best worst-case unlearnability of 19.2% (vs. 22.3% for TUE and 27.0% for TP), with AAP close behind at 21.3%. On higher-resolution datasets, AUE dominates: 20.4% worst-case on Mini-ImageNet and 11.7% on Tiny-ImageNet, outperforming TUE (21.2% and 15.9% respectively). In terms of efficiency, AUE/AAP are 3×, 6×, and 17× faster than TUE, CP, and TP in poisoning generation time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AAP outperforms AP by 8.5% in worst-case unlearnability on CIFAR-10 (35.5% vs. 44.0%) and by 7.5% on CIFAR-100 (21.3% vs. 28.8%), confirming that enhanced contrastive augmentations improve the error-maximization framework. AUE dramatically surpasses UE in contrastive unlearnability: SimCLR accuracy drops by 38.9% under AUE versus only 2.3% under UE on CIFAR-10. Against CL-based attacks, AAP beats TP (61.8% → 35.5% worst on CIFAR-10) and TUE (60.1% → 35.5%), while AUE beats TUE on CIFAR-100 (19.2% vs. 22.3%). CP completely fails on supervised learning for CIFAR-100 (74.4% SL accuracy), and TUE-SimSiam is nearly ineffective (84.3% worst), highlighting the algorithm-transferability weakness of CL-based generation. On higher-resolution datasets (Mini-ImageNet, Tiny-ImageNet), AUE achieves the best worst-case unlearnability (20.4% and 11.7%), beating TUE (21.2% and 15.9%) and AP (50.1% and 34.7%), while AAP trails TUE on Mini-ImageNet (43.8% vs. 21.2%) due to the difficulty of generating adversarial perturbations with strong augmentations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the design philosophy of recent contrastive availability attacks (CP, TUE, TP), which start from contrastive error minimization and bolt on supervised components. Instead, it starts from the simpler and more efficient supervised error-minimization (UE) and error-maximization (AP) frameworks, and achieves contrastive unlearnability by simply replacing mild supervised augmentations with strong contrastive-like augmentations during poison generation. The key conceptual departure is the observation that supervised training with contrastive augmentations implicitly optimizes the contrastive loss, so the resulting perturbations naturally adapt to contrastive training dynamics without ever computing a contrastive gradient. This also sidesteps the algorithm-transferability problem inherent in CL-based attacks, where the generation algorithm must match the evaluation algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance is that data publishers need protection against both supervised and contrastive learning algorithms simultaneously, yet most existing methods fail at one or the other, leaving a security gap that a determined data collector can exploit. The 3×–17× speedup in poison generation and reduced memory footprint make availability attacks viable for large, continually updated datasets in real-world deployment. Open questions remain around robustness to adversarial training defenses, sensitivity to partial poisoning ratios, and whether the augmentation-strength trade-off (stronger augmentations help CL unlearnability but can hurt SL unlearnability) can be resolved more gracefully.
