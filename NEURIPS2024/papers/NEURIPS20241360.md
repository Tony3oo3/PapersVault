## Problem

Existing OOD detection methods for generative language models rely on uncertainty estimation or static embedding distance (e.g., Mahalanobis distance in input/output space), but these fail in mathematical reasoning where the output space exhibits 'pattern collapse'—high-density, overlapping embeddings across domains due to the scalar, token-sparse nature of mathematical expressions. This paper identifies that while static embeddings converge, the dynamic embedding trajectory across hidden layers retains discriminative signal between ID and OOD samples, and formalizes why this is the case.

## Input / Output

The method takes a mathematical reasoning question as input to a fine-tuned GLM (Llama2-7B or GPT2-XL) and extracts the token-averaged hidden embedding at each of L layers, forming a trajectory y_0 → y_1 → … → y_L. A per-layer Gaussian distribution is pre-fitted from ID training samples (MultiArith, 360 samples). The output is a single scalar TV Score computed from the volatility of per-layer Mahalanobis distances, which is thresholded for binary ID/OOD classification or used as a continuous quality signal for OOD quality estimation.

## Method

TV Score measures the trajectory volatility of a new sample's per-layer Mahalanobis distances relative to ID-fitted Gaussians: for each layer l, the sample embedding is mapped to its MaDis f(y_l), and the score is the mean absolute difference |f(y_l) − f(y_{l−1})| across adjacent layers. The key insight is that 'pattern collapse' (convergent trajectory endpoints in mathematical reasoning) forces ID and OOD samples to diverge in their intermediate-layer trajectories even when their final embeddings are similar, a property proven via a main theorem showing that fixing the endpoint increases the probability of volatility differences. An optional differential smoothing variant (TV Score w/ DiSmo) applies k-order backward differences to both embeddings and Gaussians before computing MaDis, reducing sensitivity to trajectory outliers. The method is training-free at inference time—only the ID Gaussian fitting requires the training set—and runs in milliseconds per sample.

## Results

On the far-shift OOD setting, TV Score achieves 98.76 AUROC and 5.21 FPR95 with Llama2-7B, and 93.47 AUROC and 24.10 FPR95 with GPT2-XL, surpassing the best baseline by over 10 AUROC points and reducing FPR95 by more than 80%. On the more challenging near-shift OOD setting, TV Score maintains 92.64 AUROC / 28.39 FPR95 (Llama2-7B) and 94.86 AUROC / 13.82 FPR95 (GPT2-XL), while all baselines degrade sharply (AUROC dropping below 60–83). In online detection, TV Score reaches ~94.7% accuracy (far-shift) and ~90.3% (near-shift) with robustness (sampling variance) of 1.60 and 2.51, compared to ~73% and ~71% for embedding-based methods. For OOD quality estimation, TV Score achieves Kendall τ of 0.161 and Spearman ρ of 0.147 (Llama2-7B, far-shift), roughly doubling the best baseline correlations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TV Score decisively outperforms all five training-free baselines across every setting. Against the strongest baseline (Perplexity for Llama2-7B far-shift at 85.64 AUROC; Input Embedding for GPT2-XL far-shift at 86.26 AUROC), TV Score gains +13.12 and +10.28 AUROC points respectively, while reducing FPR95 from 53.06 to 5.21 (Llama2-7B) and from 49.33 to 24.10 (GPT2-XL). The embedding-based methods (Input/Output Embedding from Ren et al., ICLR 2023) suffer the most severe degradation on near-shift OOD, with Output Embedding dropping to 44.50 AUROC on Llama2-7B, whereas TV Score retains 92.64. Monte-Carlo Dropout and Max Softmax Probability are consistently the weakest baselines, with AUROC values often near or below 70. In online detection (Table 2), TV Score achieves ~20-point accuracy gains over both Input and Output Embedding methods on average, with the largest gap (over 40 points) on the Counting & Probability domain. For quality estimation (Table 3), TV Score roughly doubles the Kendall τ of the best baseline (0.161 vs. 0.078 for Input Embedding on Llama2-7B far-shift), confirming that trajectory volatility captures generation quality beyond binary ID/OOD discrimination.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the embedding-based OOD detection framework for GLMs established by Ren et al. (ICLR 2023), which uses Mahalanobis Distance in static input/output spaces, by shifting from static embedding distance to dynamic embedding trajectory volatility. The trajectory concept itself is borrowed from Wang et al.'s latent space chain-of-embedding work. The key conceptual departure is identifying 'pattern collapse' in mathematical reasoning output spaces as a fundamental failure mode of static embedding methods, and providing a theoretical proof (via constrained endpoint distributions) that trajectory volatility differences become more probable under such collapse. The paper also positions itself against uncertainty-estimation baselines (MC Dropout, softmax probability) and perplexity-based methods, showing all are inadequate for the mathematical reasoning setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Mathematical reasoning is a critical and growing application domain for LLMs, yet OOD detection there was previously unaddressed; this paper provides the first systematic study and a lightweight, training-free solution (TV Score) that generalizes to other high-density output tasks like multiple-choice questions. The 'pattern collapse' diagnosis is a useful conceptual tool for understanding when and why embedding-based methods fail, potentially guiding future OOD detection design beyond mathematics. Limitations include reliance on small datasets (hundreds to low thousands of samples), evaluation on only two relatively small models (Llama2-7B, GPT2-XL), and inconsistent performance of the differential smoothing variant across settings. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger models (70B+), whether it holds for non-symbolic high-density outputs, and whether the theoretical assumptions (independent layer embeddings, uniform distribution) hold in practice.
