## Problem

Secure aggregation for privacy and robust aggregation for poisoning defense in federated learning have been developed in isolation, but combining them is fundamentally difficult: anomaly detection requires access to unencrypted model updates that secure aggregation deliberately obscures. Existing joint solutions either rely on impractical non-colluding two-server assumptions that disrupt FL's single-server topology, use three-party computation with scalability issues, or add differential privacy noise that degrades model accuracy. The paper asks how to simultaneously protect privacy and detect poisoned models without introducing new participant roles or altering the standard single-server, multiple-clients FL architecture.

## Input / Output

Each client normalizes its local model update (specifically the last layer for similarity computation) and encrypts it with a shared FHE public key before transmission; the server also retrieves the previous round's encrypted global model last layer. The server produces an encrypted similarity score vector sent back to all clients for collaborative decryption and threshold-based benign-client selection, followed by an FHE-aggregated encrypted global model distributed to all clients. Unusually, clients hold the FHE private key rather than the server, so the server performs all operations 'in the dark' without ever seeing plaintext model parameters.

## Method

DDFed integrates CKKS-based fully homomorphic encryption for both secure aggregation and a novel two-phase anomaly detection mechanism over encrypted models. In the first phase (secure similarity computation), the server extracts the last layer of each encrypted update, adds Gaussian DP perturbation (ε,δ-differential privacy) to prevent malicious clients from inferring information via decrypted scores, and computes encrypted inner products with the previous global model's last layer—circumventing FHE's inability to perform division by having clients pre-normalize their inputs. In the second phase (feedback-driven collaborative selection), each client decrypts the similarity scores, applies a mean-threshold filter to identify trustworthy peers, and submits its selection; the server resolves conflicts via majority voting to form the final aggregation group. The server then performs FHE-based weighted aggregation (data-size-proportional, FedAvg-style) over the selected clients' encrypted updates, and benign clients apply a post-aggregation clipping step before the next training round to counter sophisticated poisoning attacks that pure similarity-based filtering misses.

## Results

On FMNIST with cold-start attacks (100 clients, non-IID, 30% attackers), DDFed achieves 83.32% accuracy under IPM, 80.97% under ALIE, and 83.05% under SCALING attacks, outperforming all baselines on IPM and SCALING. Under the SCALING attack, FedAvg and Trimmed Mean collapse to 0% accuracy, while DDFed and Clipping Median are the only methods that retain meaningful performance. On CIFAR-10 (60 rounds), DDFed (Last Layer) reaches 70.3% under IPM versus FedAvg's 0%, with a time overhead of ~2 seconds per round (~20% increase over standard FedAvg's ~10 s/round).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDFed outperforms FedAvg and Trimmed Mean by a wide margin on all three attacks, as both baselines are completely defeated by IPM and SCALING (0% accuracy). Against Krum, DDFed gains 14.3 points on IPM (83.32% vs 69.05%) and 13.1 points on SCALING (83.05% vs 69.95%), while also leading on ALIE (80.97% vs 73.69%). Compared to Cos Defense—the closest non-privacy-preserving baseline—DDFed wins on IPM (+1.45 pts) and SCALING (+1.94 pts) but trails slightly on ALIE (80.97% vs 82.97%, a ~2-point gap). Against Median and Clipping Median, DDFed leads on IPM (83.32% vs 67.57%/61.1%) and SCALING (83.05% vs 74.03%/75.49%), while remaining competitive on ALIE. The key differentiator is that DDFed is the only method combining FHE-based cryptographic privacy with robust poisoning defense, whereas all baselines operate on unencrypted model updates.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Cosine Defense client-filtering mechanism (Yaldiz et al., 2023) and the FedAvg aggregation protocol (Konecny et al., 2016), adapting both to operate under full homomorphic encryption (CKKS). It departs from prior joint privacy-and-robustness approaches (e.g., 3PC-based, two-trapdoor HE, additive secret sharing) that require non-colluding dual servers or introduce scalability bottlenecks, instead maintaining the standard single-server multi-client FL topology. The key methodological departure is a two-phase anomaly detection mechanism—secure similarity computation over encrypted last-layer weights followed by client-side decryption and majority-vote selection—that circumvents FHE's inability to perform division and comparison natively.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DDFed offers a deployable dual-defense solution that avoids the impractical topology changes (extra servers, 3PC) that have limited prior joint approaches to theoretical interest, making it relevant to practitioners who need both privacy and robustness without architectural overhaul. However, the r_ATTACK < 0.5 assumption, reliance on similarity-based detection only (not extending to more recent defenses like FLAME or FLIP), and the open question of handling dropout or dynamic participant groups limit its generality. The modest 20% per-round overhead is encouraging but the FHE computation cost will grow with model size, raising scalability questions for larger architectures beyond the 225k-parameter CNN tested here.
