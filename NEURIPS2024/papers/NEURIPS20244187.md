## Problem

Interactive teaching methods like co-teaching have demonstrated practical efficacy for learning with noisy labels, yet their underlying optimization principles and convergence guarantees remain theoretically unexplored. Specifically, it is unclear how the mutual low-loss sample selection between two networks relates to loss landscape geometry, and whether the iterative process has a principled convergence mechanism analogous to established probabilistic frameworks.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244187/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard image classification datasets (MNIST 28×28 grayscale, Fashion-MNIST 28×28 grayscale, CIFAR-10/100 32×32 RGB, SVHN 32×32 RGB) with artificially injected label noise (symmetric, pairflip, tridiagonal, or instance-dependent at 20% or 40% corruption rates). Two structurally identical 9-layer CNNs with dropout and batch normalization are trained jointly; each network's output is a class probability vector, and the final prediction is the argmax class label. The key operational detail is that during training, each network does not update on its own mini-batch but rather on the subset of samples the peer network identifies as low-loss, and in SRIT additionally on a SAM-perturbed gradient computed at that subset.

## Method

The paper establishes that co-teaching's mutual low-loss selection is equivalent to iteratively carving away high-loss peaks from the loss landscape, drawing a structural parallel to SAM's sharpness reduction. It then formalizes co-teaching as an EM algorithm where data cleanliness (clean vs. noisy) is a latent variable, proving convergence by showing each iteration monotonically increases a variational lower bound on the log marginal likelihood. Building on this, the authors propose Sharpness Reduction Interactive Teaching (SRIT), a dual-level sequential optimization: the first level performs the standard loss-information exchange (each network selects R(T)·|D̄| lowest-loss samples from the peer's perspective), and the second level performs a sharpness-knowledge exchange where each network computes a SAM-style perturbation ε̂ = ρ∇L/‖∇L‖² on the peer-selected subset and evaluates the gradient at the perturbed point θ+ε̂ before the parameter update. This two-level design filters harmful data (level 1) while simultaneously flattening the optimized loss landscape to escape local optima (level 2), yielding consistent accuracy gains over co-teaching and CNLCU across all tested datasets and noise conditions.

## Results

SRIT consistently outperforms Co-teaching across all five datasets (MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Fashion-MNIST, SVHN) and all four noise types (symmetric, pairflip, tridiagonal, instance) at both 20% and 40% noise ratios. The most dramatic gains appear on CIFAR-100, where SRIT achieves 59.66% test accuracy under 20% symmetric noise versus 50.21% for Co-teaching (+9.45 pp), and 50.57% vs. 42.40% under 40% symmetric noise (+8.17 pp). On MNIST, SRIT reaches 99.42% (20% symmetric) and 99.19% (40% symmetric), surpassing Co-teaching's 97.50% and 94.96% respectively. The SAM-enhanced variant SRCNLCU-H similarly improves over CNLCU-H, e.g., 55.84% vs. 46.27% on CIFAR-100 under 20% symmetric noise.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SRIT (the proposed method) is compared against Co-teaching [Han et al., NeurIPS 2018] as the primary baseline. SRIT wins on every dataset–noise combination tested: on CIFAR-100 under 20% symmetric noise, SRIT achieves 59.66% vs. Co-teaching's 50.21% (+9.45 pp); on CIFAR-10 under 20% symmetric noise, 85.64% vs. 82.15% (+3.49 pp); and on MNIST under 40% symmetric noise, 99.19% vs. 94.96% (+4.23 pp). The second comparison is SRCNLCU-H vs. CNLCU-H [Xia et al., ICLR 2022] on three datasets. SRCNLCU-H again wins across the board, with the largest margin on CIFAR-100 (55.84% vs. 46.27% under 20% symmetric noise, +9.57 pp) and CIFAR-10 (85.43% vs. 83.03%, +2.40 pp). The gains are most pronounced on harder datasets (CIFAR-100) and under higher noise ratios, suggesting that the SAM-based sharpness reduction is particularly beneficial when the loss landscape is more complex and noisy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three research threads: interactive teaching (co-teaching, Han et al. 2018), sharpness-aware minimization (Foret et al. 2021), and the EM algorithm (Dempster et al. 1977). It departs from prior co-teaching work by providing the first theoretical convergence analysis via an EM formulation where data cleanliness is treated as a latent variable, and by connecting the loss-landscape reduction in co-teaching to SAM's sharpness minimization. The proposed SRIT introduces a dual-level optimization—first exchanging loss information (as in co-teaching), then exchanging sharpness/gradient information (as in SAM)—which is a conceptual departure from the single-level loss exchange in all prior interactive teaching variants (co-teaching+, JoCoR, CNLCU).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The EM formalization gives a principled convergence guarantee for interactive teaching, which previously lacked theoretical grounding, and the SAM integration addresses the well-known local-optima limitation of EM. However, the practical gains, while consistent across five datasets and four noise types, are largely attributable to SAM's known generalization benefits rather than a fundamentally new mechanism. Open questions include whether the EM interpretation extends to more complex multi-agent interactive teaching paradigms, how the method scales to larger architectures and datasets beyond 9-layer CNNs, and whether the computational overhead of the dual-level optimization can be reduced without sacrificing the sharpness benefit.
