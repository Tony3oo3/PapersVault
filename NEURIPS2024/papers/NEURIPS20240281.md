## Problem

Rotting bandits with infinitely many arms are fundamentally harder than their finite-arm counterparts because near-optimal arms always exist outside the explored set, so the mean reward gap cannot be confined within confidence bounds. Prior work (Kim et al., 2022) addressed only a maximum rotting rate constraint with uniform initial reward distribution (β=1), leaving the more general slow-rotting (V_T) and abrupt-rotting (S_T) scenarios with arbitrary β>0 unexplored.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives the time horizon T, a threshold parameter δ (tuned from V_T, S_T, and β), and access to an infinite arm set A from which it sequentially samples arms. At each time step it pulls one arm and observes a 1-sub-Gaussian stochastic reward. The sliding window mechanism restricts window start times to powers-of-two offsets (s = t − 2^{i−1}), reducing per-step memory from O(t) to O(log t). The output is an arm-selection policy with provable regret upper bounds matching lower bounds for β ≥ 1.

## Method

The proposed UCB-Threshold with Adaptive Sliding Window algorithm pulls a newly selected arm consecutively until the minimum window-UCB index over a set of doubling-length sliding windows falls below 1−δ, at which point the arm is declared suboptimal and withdrawn. The adaptive sliding window is the key mechanism: smaller windows track recent mean rewards with low bias but high variance, while larger windows reduce variance at the cost of bias, and taking the minimum over all doubling-length windows lets the algorithm adapt to arbitrary rotting rate profiles without knowing them in advance. The threshold δ is tuned as δ_V(β) for slow rotting and δ_S(β) for abrupt rotting, yielding tight regret bounds of Õ(max{V_T^{1/(β+2)} T^{(β+1)/(β+2)}, T^{β/(β+1)}}) and Õ(max{S_T^{1/(β+1)} T^{β/(β+1)}, V̄_T}) respectively. For abrupt rotting, the analysis treats each rotting event as spawning a new arm, and the sliding window starting immediately after the rotting event enables efficient detection of substantially rotted arms. A Bandit-over-Bandit variant (Algorithm 2) handles the case where V_T, S_T, and β are unknown by estimating the optimal δ directly.

## Results

The paper establishes tight regret bounds for infinitely many-armed bandits under two rotting constraints. For slow rotting (V_T), Algorithm 1 achieves Õ(max{V_T^{1/(β+2)} T^{(β+1)/(β+2)}, T^{β/(β+1)}}) for β≥1, matching the lower bound Ω(max{V_T^{1/(β+2)} T^{(β+1)/(β+2)}, T^{β/(β+1)}}). For abrupt rotting (S_T), the upper bound Õ(max{S_T^{1/(β+1)} T^{β/(β+1)}, V̄_T}) is also tight for β≥1. In numerical experiments with β=1 and V_T=O(1), Algorithms 1 and 2 achieve Õ(T^{2/3}) and Õ(T^{3/4}) regret respectively, outperforming UCB-TP's Õ(T/log(T)^{1/3}).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two baselines: UCB-TP [Kim et al., ICML 2022], the state-of-the-art algorithm for rotting infinitely many-armed bandits under a maximum rotting rate constraint, and SSUCB [Bayati et al., 2020], a near-optimal algorithm for stationary infinitely many-armed bandits. In the theoretical comparison (Remark 3.2), with β=1 and an oblivious adversary, Algorithm 1's regret bound Õ(max{V_T^{1/3} T^{2/3}, √T}) is strictly tighter than UCB-TP's Õ(max{ρ^{1/3} T, √T}) since V_T ≤ Tρ. In the numerical experiments (Figure 3) with ρ_t = 1/(t log T), V_T = O(1), and S_T = T, both Algorithm 1 and Algorithm 2 outperform UCB-TP and SSUCB in cumulative regret, with Algorithm 1 (knowing V_T, S_T, β) performing best. The theoretical advantage is confirmed empirically: Algorithm 1 achieves Õ(T^{2/3}) regret versus UCB-TP's Õ(T/log(T)^{1/3}), a meaningful gap that grows with T.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the rotting bandit framework from finite arms (Seznec et al., where rotting is provably no harder than the stationary case) to infinitely many arms, where the problem becomes fundamentally harder due to the persistent existence of unexplored near-optimal arms. It generalizes Kim et al. [13] (ICML 2022), which considered only a maximum rotting rate constraint and uniform initial reward distribution (β=1), to generalized slow-rotting (V_T) and abrupt-rotting (S_T) constraints with arbitrary β>0. The adaptive sliding window mechanism is borrowed from nonstationary bandit literature (Auer et al. [4], Seznec et al. [21]) and repurposed here to manage the bias-variance tradeoff specific to rotting rewards, while the generalized distribution analysis draws on techniques from Bayati et al. [5] for stationary infinite-armed bandits.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to establish tight regret bounds (matching lower bounds for β≥1) for rotting bandits with infinitely many arms under both slow and abrupt rotting constraints, filling a gap between the finite-armed rotting literature and the stationary infinite-armed bandit literature. The key conceptual contribution is showing that rotting fundamentally changes the exploration dynamics in the infinite-armed setting—unlike the finite case, the agent must continuously seek new near-optimal arms as explored ones rot, creating an additional cost absent in stationary or finite-armed settings. The gap between upper and lower bounds for 0<β<1 remains open, and the empirical validation is limited to a single synthetic setting with β=1, leaving practical performance on real recommendation or clinical trial data unexplored.
