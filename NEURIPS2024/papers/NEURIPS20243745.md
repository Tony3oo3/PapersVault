## Problem

Iteratively refining code with LLMs (prompting the model to fix its own buggy outputs) is a popular strategy for solving complex programming problems, but prior work relies on simple greedy or breadth-first expansion policies with only marginal gains over i.i.d. resampling. The paper identifies that refinement exposes a fundamental exploration-exploitation tradeoff—whether to refine the program closest to correct or to explore less-refined programs—and that this tradeoff is complicated by the fact that every refinement creates a brand-new candidate, yielding an ever-growing action space.

## Input / Output

The method takes a programming problem with an efficiently checkable specification Φ (decomposed into K conjuncts such as test cases, verification conditions, or input-output image pairs) and an LLM as the refinement oracle. At each step it selects one program from the growing search tree, prompts the LLM with a randomly sampled counterexample to produce a refined program, and checks the result against Φ. The output is the first program ρ′ that satisfies all conjuncts (ρ′ ⊢ Φ). The heuristic h(ρ) used to guide search is simply the fraction of the K conjuncts that ρ satisfies, and the only tunable hyperparameter is C, which controls how strongly the heuristic biases the prior.

## Method

REx (REfine, Explore, Exploit) frames iterative code refinement as an arm-acquiring bandit problem: each program is an arm, refining a program is pulling that arm, and the reward is 1 if the refined program satisfies the specification (Bernoulli). The algorithm uses Thompson Sampling with a Beta-Bernoulli conjugate model, where the prior for each program ρ is Beta(1 + C·h(ρ), 1 + C·(1−h(ρ))), injecting heuristic knowledge so that programs closer to correct start with higher expected reward. After N_ρ unsuccessful refinements, the posterior becomes Beta(1 + C·h(ρ), 1 + C·(1−h(ρ)) + N_ρ), which both lowers the expected reward and shrinks the variance as O(N_ρ^{−3}), automatically penalizing over-refined programs. At each step, REx samples θ_ρ from every program's posterior and refines the program with the highest sampled value, then adds the new program as a fresh arm with N=0. The entire algorithm is roughly ten lines of Python, and the hyperparameter C (C=20 works well across domains) trades off greediness: larger C makes the prior more concentrated around h(ρ), favoring exploitation of near-correct programs.

## Results

Across three code-generation domains, REx consistently solves more problems with fewer LLM calls than all baselines. On the NLA loop-invariant benchmark, REx solves 28/38 problems (73.7%), outperforming G-CLN (24/38, 63.2%), Loopy-GPT4 (11/38), and Z3-GSpacer (5/38). REx achieves speedups of ~2× on APPS-Competition, 2–5× on loop invariants, and 1.1–1.6× on ARC relative to the best baseline. REx is also the most robust to hyperparameter choice, with C=20 performing well across all datasets, whereas baselines degrade significantly when their hyperparameters are not tuned per-dataset.

## Baselines & Comparisons

REx outperforms all three search-policy baselines (Greedy, BFS, Fixed-Width) on the hardest datasets: on APPS-Competition and NLA loop invariants, REx achieves the highest AUC and final success rate, while Fixed-Width is the worst-performing method on those domains. On easier benchmarks (APPS-Introductory, APPS-Interview), Greedy is competitive with REx and the speedup advantage disappears. Against domain-specific solvers, REx beats G-CLN by 13.5 percentage points on NLA (73.7% vs. 60.5%) without requiring per-instance feature engineering or runtime traces, and beats Z3-GSpacer by a wide margin (73.7% vs. 13.2%). On ARC, REx surpasses the state-of-the-art Hypothesis Search [23] in both problems solved and LLM calls needed. On APPS-Competition, REx with GPT-4 sets a new state-of-the-art, outperforming Parsel [25], AlphaCode [14], and the fixed-width approach of Olausson et al. [3].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Thompson Sampling framework (Russo et al., 2018) and the arm-acquiring bandit formulation (Whittle, 1981), applying them to the novel setting of LLM-guided code refinement. It departs from prior refinement work (Chen et al. 2023, Olausson et al. 2023, Shinn et al. 2023) that employed fixed greedy or breadth-first expansion policies, and explicitly distinguishes itself from Monte Carlo Tree Search by noting that infinite branching, stochastic transitions, and prohibitively expensive rollouts make MCTS inapplicable. The paper positions itself as the first to systematically study the exploration-exploitation tradeoff in iterative LLM code repair and to introduce a bandit-based policy for it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REx provides a simple (~10-line) and broadly applicable search strategy that reduces LLM API calls by 1.5×–5× across competition programming, visual reasoning, and loop invariant synthesis, with robust hyperparameter sensitivity (C=20 works across all domains). However, the paper candidly notes that the improvement in total problems solved in the large-compute limit is modest, and that cracking the hardest problems is more likely to come from base-model improvements than from better search organization. Open directions include richer heuristics beyond pass-rates, contextual bandits conditioned on problem statements and refinement history, and integration with fine-tuned refinement models.
