## Problem

Unsupervised skill discovery (USD) methods face two critical limitations in complex continuous control environments: they struggle to learn intricate behaviors like walking and running (settling for simple static skills like standing), and they frequently acquire unsafe or undesirable behaviors such as tripping, rolling, or navigating into hazardous areas. The paper asks whether the purely unsupervised assumption of USD is sufficient for learning practical foundational policies, and proposes incorporating minimal human intention via a small set of instruction videos.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241511/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of action-free instruction videos (typically 4 'Do's' and 4 'Don'ts', fewer than 8 total) that demonstrate desirable and undesirable behaviors, plus online state observations (pixel or state-based) during skill learning. Videos are decomposed into adjacent state pairs (s_t, s_{t+1}) with binary labels (y=1 for Do's, y=0 for Don'ts) to train the instruction network. The output is a skill-conditioned policy π(a|s,z) that produces diverse behavior trajectories aligned with human intentions, where the skill z is sampled from a standard normal prior. The instruction network itself is a 4-layer CNN backbone with a 3-layer FC head that maps a pair of adjacent states to a probability in [0,1] via Tanh bounding.

## Method

DoDont is a two-stage algorithm. In the instruction learning stage, a binary classification network (instruction network) is trained on the Do's/Don'ts video dataset to assign high values to desirable transitions and low values to undesirable ones. In the skill learning stage, the frozen instruction network is integrated as the distance metric into the Wasserstein Dependency Measure (WDM) objective of METRA, a distance-maximizing skill discovery algorithm. A key rephrasing (Eq. 7) separates the instruction network from the representation function training, so that p̂_ψ(s,s') directly multiplies the intrinsic reward r^METRA, providing immediate instruction signals from the first training step rather than waiting for the Lipschitz-constrained representation φ to converge. This multiplicative integration (rather than additive) effectively controls the scale of the total reward and requires only one additional hyperparameter (the instruction network coefficient) on top of METRA, making it a single-line code addition.

## Results

With fewer than 8 instruction videos (4 Do's + 4 Don'ts), DoDont learns complex locomotion skills (e.g., upright running) that METRA fails to acquire, achieving higher state coverage and zero-shot running rewards in both Cheetah and Quadruped environments. In the Kitchen manipulation task, DoDont completes more of the six target tasks than METRA, SMERL, or DGPO. In offline zero-shot RL (aggregated over 4 environments, 4 tasks, 2 datasets, 4 seeds via IQM), DoDont outperforms HILP, FB, and FDM by a large margin, e.g., achieving 953±22 on Walker-Stand (APS) vs. HILP's 883±42, and 984±2 on Cheetah-Walk (APS) vs. HILP's 939±55. Even with a single Do's video, the agent still learns multi-directional running behavior.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DoDont consistently outperforms METRA across all online skill discovery experiments: in Quadruped, METRA primarily learns rolling locomotion while DoDont learns upright running with higher state coverage; in the hazardous-area experiment, METRA covers the entire state space including dangerous zones while DoDont avoids them; and in Kitchen, DoDont completes more of the six manipulation tasks. Against METRA† (hand-designed reward as distance metric), DoDont wins in high-dimensional action spaces (Quadruped) where crafting a single reward capturing multiple behavioral intentions becomes intractable, though METRA† performs competitively in simpler Cheetah environments. SMERL and DGPO both fail to learn diverse behaviors in all tested environments, reducing to simple standing-at-start-point policies, likely due to the insufficiency of the MI-based diversity objective in complex continuous control. In offline zero-shot RL, DoDont surpasses HILP (the strongest baseline) on the majority of task-environment combinations, with particularly large margins on Quadruped-Run (489 vs. 411 on APS; 494 vs. 393 on RND) and Cheetah-Walk (984 vs. 939 on APS; 944 vs. 823 on RND), while FB and FDM trail both HILP and DoDont across nearly all settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

DoDont builds directly on the distance-maximizing skill discovery (DSD) framework, specifically METRA's Wasserstein Dependency Measure objective, by replacing its fixed distance metric with a learned instruction network. This instruction network is trained as a binary classifier on action-free video pairs (Do's vs. Don'ts), a departure from prior work that either relies on hand-designed reward functions (SMERL, DGPO) or purely unsupervised objectives (METRA, LSD, CSD). The paper also extends the approach to the offline setting by integrating the same instruction network into HILP's Hilbert representation framework, unifying online and offline skill discovery under a single video-guided mechanism.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a critical practical limitation of unsupervised skill discovery: agents learning unsafe or undesirable behaviors (rolling, flipping, navigating hazards) that make them unsuitable for real-world deployment. The finding that fewer than 8 action-free videos suffice to redirect skill learning toward complex behaviors like quadruped running—while the agent generalizes beyond the specific directions shown in videos—suggests a scalable path toward safety-aware foundation policies. Key open questions include whether the instruction network can be trained on general in-the-wild video data rather than in-domain simulator rollouts, and whether the multiplicative integration strategy generalizes beyond the DSD framework to other skill discovery objectives.
