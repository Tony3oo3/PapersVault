## Problem

It remains unclear whether transformers learn the underlying compositional primitives on unseen tasks or merely memorize input-output mappings, and how this behavior is controlled. This paper identifies the parameter initialization scale as a critical, previously unexplored factor that determines whether a transformer fits composite functions by reasoning (capturing individual single-anchor operations) or by memorizing (treating symmetric anchor pairs as equivalent).
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240451/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For the synthetic anchor-function task, each input is a 9-token sequence containing one anchor pair (two tokens from {1,2,3,4}), a key item immediately preceding the pair, and noise items; the target is the output of sequentially applying the two anchor-specific arithmetic operations to the key item. For real-world validation, inputs are standard text sequences from SCAN (primitive commands), COGS (semantic interpretation), PrOntoQA (hierarchical reasoning chains), addition problems (a+b=c with a,b∈[0,999]), and SlimPajama (GitHub/Wikipedia text), with outputs being the next predicted token or a binary correctness judgment.

## Method

The paper uses the anchor-function framework to construct controlled compositional data where 14 of 16 anchor pairs follow inferential mappings, one pair (3,4) is assigned a non-inferential mapping, and pair (4,3) is held out as an unseen task. By sweeping the initialization rate γ (where parameter std = 1/d_in^γ) across model depths, the authors discover a phase diagram: small γ (large initialization) yields symmetric solutions that directly combine anchor information without learning primitives, while large γ (small initialization) yields inferential solutions that learn individual single-anchor mappings and compose them. Mechanistic analysis via attention-matrix information flow, cosine-similarity heatmaps of fused vector representations, and parameter condensation metrics (eigenvalue spectra, t-SNE of embedding spaces) reveals that inferential solutions exhibit low complexity with weights condensing in few directions and tokens arranged in ordinal structure, whereas symmetric solutions show high complexity with no such structure. The authors propose γ as a convenient tunable hyperparameter in standard deep learning frameworks and validate the findings on GPT-2 and real-world datasets, showing that small initialization with large weight decay consistently improves OOD generalization on compositional and reasoning tasks.

## Results

On the synthetic anchor-function task, the model exhibits a clear phase transition: small initialization (γ=0.8) yields ~100% inferential accuracy on the unseen anchor pair (4,3), while large initialization (γ=0.3–0.5) yields ~100% symmetric accuracy, with a shadow zone of poor generalization at very large scales for deep models. On GPT-2, the same trend holds with a narrower γ range. On real-world compositional tasks (SCAN, COGS), small initialization (γ=0.7) with large weight decay (WD=0.1) consistently outperforms large initialization (γ=0.5) with small weight decay (WD=0.01) across all data scales; notably on COGS, even when in-distribution accuracy exceeds 99% for both settings, the OOD gap remains significant. On PrOntoQA, small-init models maintain faster convergence and better generalization, while large-init models show a non-monotonic convergence pattern (slowing down then speeding up) as data size increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it contrasts different initialization scales (parameterized by the initialization rate γ) and weight decay coefficients as the primary experimental variable. Across all evaluated tasks—synthetic anchor functions, SCAN, COGS, PrOntoQA, addition, and SlimPajama—small initialization with large weight decay consistently outperforms large initialization with small weight decay on out-of-distribution and compositional generalization metrics. On the anchor-function task, the two settings produce qualitatively different solutions (inferential vs. symmetric) rather than a gradual performance gap. On COGS, the OOD generalization gap persists even when both settings achieve >99% in-distribution accuracy, indicating the difference is structural rather than a matter of training duration. On PrOntoQA, large-init models exhibit a non-monotonic convergence pattern (initially memorizing, then shifting to reasoning as data grows), while small-init models converge monotonically through reasoning from the start. The GPT-2 experiments confirm that these findings generalize beyond the single-head architecture used for mechanistic analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the anchor function framework (Zhang et al., 2024) for controlled synthetic compositional tasks, extending it by varying the parameter initialization scale to probe how transformers generalize to unseen compositional pairs. It connects to the broader initialization literature (NTK/linear regime vs. condensed regime for ReLU networks, grokking and initialization scale in Liu et al., 2022) but reframes initialization not merely as a training-stability concern but as a mechanism that selects between qualitatively different solution types (reasoning vs. memorization). The mechanistic analysis draws on attention-flow visualization and parameter condensation diagnostics from prior interpretability work, applying them to a compositional generalization setting where prior studies had not isolated the role of initialization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper introduces the initialization rate γ as a simple, practical hyperparameter that practitioners can tune to bias a transformer toward reasoning (small γ) or memorization (large γ), with validation on GPT-2 and real-world compositional/reasoning benchmarks (SCAN, COGS, PrOntoQA, SlimPajama). The clear phase diagram and mechanistic explanation (information flow, vector-space structure, parameter condensation) provide an interpretable account of why the two regimes arise, which is valuable for understanding transformer behavior beyond this specific task. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic anchor-function data for the main mechanistic analysis and single-head attention for the core experiments; whether the findings transfer to very large multi-head models or to tasks without clean compositional structure remains open.
