## Problem

No prior emergent communication work has demonstrated the emergence of spatial references (spatial deixis) in agent languages, despite their importance for efficient, interpretable, and generalisable communication. While emergent languages have achieved efficiency and compositionality, they lack fundamental properties like positional referencing that allow agents to describe objects relative to one another rather than through unique descriptions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244445/cropped_page5_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The sender receives a 5-integer sliding window into a longer sequence of 60 unique integers, where a single -1 marks the target position; the window shifts at sequence boundaries, creating five observation types (begin, begin+1, end-1, end, middle) that carry implicit positional information. The receiver takes the sender's 3-integer message (vocabulary of 26, yielding ~17k possible messages), the full 60-integer sequence, and a 4-element target-and-distractor vector, and must output the index of the target within that vector. The NPMI analysis pipeline additionally produces a human-readable dictionary mapping message n-grams to their spatial and integer meanings.

## Method

The architecture follows the standard EGG GRU design with a key modification: the receiver uses an additional GRU to process the full sequence after encoding the message, allowing it to use message-derived information to locate the target within the sequence. Training is end-to-end via Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization with cross-entropy loss, requiring no explicit supervision on message meanings. The core analytical contribution is a two-algorithm NPMI framework: PMI_nc measures non-compositional monolithic messages (e.g., dedicated codes for begin/end observations), while PMI_c measures trivially compositional messages by computing NPMI between n-grams and both integers and referent positions, accounting for position-variant and position-invariant usage. This framework segments messages and maps segments to meanings without requiring pre-existing labels, revealing that agents use a mixture of non-compositional codes for sparse observation types and compositional position+integer messages for the common middle case, with positional components assigned fixed message slots constituting a first hint of syntactic structure.

## Results

All 16 agent pairs achieve above 98% accuracy on the spatial referential task, confirming that agents successfully develop communication about spatial relationships. Using the NPMI-based dictionary to query the receiver agent, non-compositional positional messages (e.g., 'begin', 'end') yield 90%±3% average accuracy (94% max), far above the 20% chance level. Compositional messages with both integer and positional components (Compositional-P) reach up to 78% accuracy, while removing the positional component (Compositional-NP) drops accuracy to near-chance (22%±2%), confirming that positional n-grams carry meaningful spatial information. Over 90% of agent pairs develop dedicated non-compositional messages for boundary observations, and 25% develop compositional spatial references.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper presents the first demonstration of emergent spatial references, so no external baseline methods exist for direct comparison. The internal validation is structured around two hypotheses tested against a 20% chance baseline (4 distractors). H1 is confirmed: non-compositional positional messages achieve 90%±3% accuracy, significantly above chance, showing the NPMI method captures their meaning without requiring complex compositionality. H2 is confirmed by the gap between Compositional-NP (22%±2%, near chance) and Compositional-P (up to 78%), demonstrating that the identified positional n-grams carry genuine spatial information that the receiver can exploit. The non-compositional integer messages (36%±0.4%) remain only modestly above chance, suggesting additional pragmatic or context-dependent structure not captured by the current NPMI analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the referential game framework of Lazaridou et al. (2018) by designing an environment where spatial references are necessary for successful communication, and adapts the GRU-based sender/receiver architecture from Lipinski et al. (2023), who investigated temporal deixis in emergent communication. For language analysis, it repurposes Normalised Pointwise Mutual Information (NPMI) from Bouma (2009), a standard collocation measure in computational linguistics, as a tool to segment and interpret emergent messages without requiring natural-language labels. The work positions itself within the broader push (Rita et al., 2024; Boldt and Mortensen, 2024) to align emergent languages with fundamental properties of human language, specifically addressing the gap that no prior work has demonstrated spatial deixis in emergent communication.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first demonstration that agents can develop spatial references in an emergent language, and it challenges the assumption (Rita et al., 2024) that complex settings are required for such properties to emerge, showing that a modified simple referential game suffices. The NPMI-based interpretation method is potentially broadly applicable to other emergent communication settings where natural-language labels are unavailable, offering a path toward human-interpretable agent languages. However, the moderate average accuracy (~33%) for compositional messages, the 1D sequence environment, and the assumption of trivial compositionality limit generalisability; open questions include whether the approach scales to 2D/3D observations, non-trivially compositional messages, and larger vocabulary spaces.
