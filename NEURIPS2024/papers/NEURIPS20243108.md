## Problem

Prior learning-augmented algorithms treat predictors as black boxes with ad-hoc, problem-specific predictions, lacking standardized quality measures and ignoring how predictions are generated relative to the algorithmic process. This paper argues that unpacking the predictor and integrating the learning problem it gives rise to within the algorithmic challenge yields substantially better performance guarantees, since the algorithm can adapt its strategy as it observes the input prefix rather than relying on a fixed, pre-computed prediction.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a hypothesis class H of size ℓ (each hypothesis being a full input instance or a compact summary such as job-type frequencies) and processes the online input (page request sequence, job arrivals with m-dimensional type vectors) sequentially. Unlike black-box settings where a single prediction is given upfront, here the predictor learns adaptively from the input prefix, and the algorithmic component applies an offline optimal solver (Belady's FitF, Lenstra-Shmoys-Tardos LP, or Shortest-Job-First) to the current predicted instance at each decision point. The predictor's total loss—comprising per-round prediction errors plus a switching cost proportional to the cost of recomputing the offline solution—directly upper-bounds the algorithm's additive regret or multiplicative competitive ratio.

## Method

The paper introduces a modular framework that decouples the problem into a predictor (solving a problem-specific online learning task with two cost types: prediction errors and switching costs) and an online algorithm (applying the offline optimal solver to the predicted instance). For caching, the learning task is sequence prediction with switching cost, solved by a halving/majority-vote rule in the realizable setting (yielding k log ℓ regret) and a multiplicative-weights rule in the agnostic setting (yielding O(μ* + k log ℓ) regret). For load balancing, the predictor forecasts job-type frequencies and the algorithm applies the Lenstra-Shmoys-Tardos 2-approximation to the predicted instance, achieving O(log ℓ) competitive ratio. For non-clairvoyant scheduling, the predictor learns job-length orderings and the algorithm always processes the job with the shortest predicted length, achieving OPT + ℓ√(2·OPT) regret. A key technical subtlety is that the caching predictor is improper (predicting sequences not in H), requiring the offline solver to be monotone—FitF satisfies this property.

## Results

For caching in the realizable setting, the paper achieves additive regret of OPT(I) + k log ℓ (where k is cache size and ℓ = |H|), improving upon the previous O(μ* + k + √(Tk log ℓ)) bound of Emek et al. whenever T = ω(k log ℓ). In the agnostic caching setting, the bound is OPT(I) + (5+6/k)μ* + (2k+1) ln ℓ. For load balancing, the paper achieves O(log ℓ) competitive ratio (deterministic: O(log ℓ log τ) OPT; randomized: O(log ℓ) OPT in expectation), matching Dinitz et al.'s ratio but producing integral solutions directly without the costly Θ(log log m / log log log m) rounding factor. For non-clairvoyant scheduling, the realizable bound is OPT(I) + ℓ√(2 OPT(I)) and the agnostic bound is OPT(I) + μ* + O(n^(5/3) log^(1/3) ℓ), both achieving additive regret rather than the multiplicative (1+ε)OPT + O(ε^{-5})μ* of prior work.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Emek et al. [2021] on caching, the paper's agnostic bound OPT + O(μ* + k log ℓ) strictly improves upon their O(μ* + k + √(Tk log ℓ)) whenever the sequence length T = ω(k log ℓ), eliminating the √(Tk log ℓ) term that grows with instance length. The paper also achieves additive regret (OPT + additive term) rather than a multiplicative competitive ratio, which is a qualitatively stronger guarantee. Against Dinitz et al. [2022] on load balancing, the paper matches the O(log ℓ) competitive ratio but avoids the costly online rounding procedure that incurs an additional Θ(log log m / log log log m) factor, producing integral solutions directly. On non-clairvoyant scheduling, the paper's additive regret bound OPT + μ* + O(n^(5/3) log^(1/3) ℓ) is strictly better than Dinitz et al.'s (1+ε)OPT + O(ε^{-5})μ*, which always has a regret component linear in OPT and a much worse polynomial dependence on the prediction error μ*. The paper's approach is also conceptually simpler, separating the learning and algorithmic components into independent modules rather than treating prediction selection as an integral part of the algorithm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant black-box predictor paradigm in learning-augmented algorithms (Lykouris & Vassilvitskii 2021; Dinitz et al. 2022; Emek et al. 2021) by making the predictor an explicit, co-designed component of the algorithm. Rather than treating predictions as fixed inputs or aggregating outputs of multiple prediction-based algorithms, the authors formulate a tailored online learning problem (sequence prediction with switching cost for caching, demand forecasting for load balancing, job-duration comparison for scheduling) and solve it using classical tools—halving/majority vote, multiplicative weights, and median-based rules—whose parameters are chosen to match the algorithmic cost structure. The modular separation of the learning component from the algorithmic component (FitF, Lenstra-Shmoys-Tardos LP, shortest-predicted-job-first) is the key methodological departure, yielding simpler analyses and tighter bounds than the portfolio-aggregation approaches of Dinitz et al. and Emek et al.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a principled recipe for designing learning-augmented algorithms: identify the natural learning task, design a predictor with costs calibrated to the algorithmic impact of errors, and plug it into a simple offline-optimal subroutine. This modularity makes it straightforward to robustify, extend to agnostic settings, and adapt to new problems. The unexpected separation in non-clairvoyant scheduling (O(n log ℓ) regret for two job lengths vs. a lower bound of Ω(ℓn) for three) reveals a genuine structural threshold that prior black-box analyses could not capture. Open questions include extending the framework to problems where the offline optimum is not efficiently computable, handling streaming or distributed settings, and understanding whether the co-design principle yields further gains in more complex hypothesis classes.
