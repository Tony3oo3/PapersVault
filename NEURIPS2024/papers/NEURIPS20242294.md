## Problem

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods for image segmentation lack a shared benchmark that simultaneously offers ground-truth uncertainty and realistic complexity. Existing evaluations rely on either overly simplistic synthetic datasets where uncertainty is trivially known but the task is unrepresentative, or on real-world datasets where true uncertainty is unattainable, making it impossible to systematically compare how well different UQ methods capture epistemic versus aleatoric uncertainty.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242294/cropped_page2_idx19.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides 50,000 pre-rendered 512×512 RGB images of synthetic H&E-stained colon tissue, each accompanied by exact 2D and 3D instance and semantic masks, per-cell metadata (type, position, shape parameters), and full Blender scene files for regeneration. A companion data loader applies controlled label noise (class swaps, geometric distortions, missing labels) and image-level manipulations (nuclei-intensity reduction, blood-stain increase) as post-processing, enabling independent tuning of label and image uncertainty without re-rendering. The UQ evaluation pipeline consumes these images through a UNet backbone and outputs pixel-wise predictive, epistemic, and aleatoric uncertainty maps aggregated to image-level scores.

## Method

Arctique is generated via a five-stage Blender ray-tracing pipeline that mirrors the physical H&E colon biopsy protocol: (1) constructing a 3D macro-structure of hexagonally packed epithelial crypts, (2) populating five cell types (plasma cells, lymphocytes, eosinophils, fibroblasts, epithelial/goblet cells) using a 3D Voronoi-based placement algorithm with controllable shape parameters, (3) digitally sectioning the 3D scene to replicate tissue-slicing artifacts, (4) applying H&E staining via custom Blender shaders with tunable hue, intensity, and noise, and (5) rendering with a virtual camera whose focal plane produces depth-of-field blur characteristic of light microscopy. Two orthogonal uncertainty-control mechanisms are provided: a Blender-based framework for targeted image-level manipulations (e.g., reducing nuclei staining or increasing blood-cell density) and a data loader for label-level noise injection (class swaps, elastic transforms, missing labels). The paper benchmarks four UQ methods—Maximum Softmax Response, Test-Time Augmentation, Monte Carlo Dropout, and Deep Ensembles—on a UNet backbone for both foreground-background and semantic segmentation, decomposing predictive uncertainty into epistemic and aleatoric components via the information-theoretic identity of Kendall & Gal, and validates realism through zero-shot HoVer-NeXt inference on the real Lizard colon dataset.

## Results

The paper does not report a single headline accuracy number; instead, its primary results are trend-based. Across both FG-BG-Seg and Sem-Seg tasks, predictive uncertainty (PU) increases monotonically with both label noise and image noise for all four UQ methods (MSR, TTA, MCD, DE). The information-theoretic decomposition reveals that aleatoric uncertainty (AU) predominantly tracks label-level manipulations while epistemic uncertainty (EU) predominantly tracks image-level manipulations, validating the dataset's controllability. In the zero-shot transfer experiment, a HoVer-NeXt model trained solely on Arctique achieves competitive F1 and Hausdorff distance on the real Lizard dataset, with performance improving as synthetic data heterogeneity increases (depth-only < default < noisy variants). The blood-stain manipulation exposes a critical miscalibration: DE uncertainty actually *decreases* slightly as blood-cell prevalence rises, while error rates increase substantially.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The four UQ methods (MSR, TTA, MCD, DE) are not competing baselines but rather the subjects of the benchmark; the paper evaluates how each responds to controlled perturbations. All four methods show increasing PU with label noise, and the majority of that uncertainty is aleatoric, consistent with theory. For image-level manipulations (nuclei-intensity reduction), all methods correctly show rising EU, confirming they detect OOD-like shifts. However, under the blood-stain manipulation, all methods fail to flag the growing misclassification of blood cells as eosinophils: uncertainty remains flat or even decreases (notably for DE), revealing a systematic miscalibration that would be invisible on real data without ground-truth control. MSR, being deterministic, cannot be decomposed into AU/EU and thus provides only the PU signal. The paper's key comparative insight is that Arctique's independent control of image vs. label uncertainty allows disentangling which component (AU vs. EU) each method is actually sensitive to—something impossible on real histopathological data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of two underdeveloped areas: synthetic data generation for segmentation benchmarking and systematic UQ validation. Prior synthetic datasets for microscopy (e.g., SimuCell [30]) offer controllability but lack biological complexity, while generative-model-based approaches [8, 40] achieve realism at the cost of full parameter control. Arctique departs from both by using Blender-based 3D ray-tracing to replicate the physical H&E staining and tissue-slicing protocol, yielding a dataset where every source of uncertainty (staining intensity, cell overlap, label corruption) is an explicit, independently adjustable parameter. For UQ evaluation, it extends the VALUES framework [20] by providing ground-truth uncertainty at both the image and label level simultaneously, rather than relying on empirically induced distribution shifts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Arctique fills a concrete gap: UQ methods for segmentation have lacked a benchmark where true uncertainty is known and independently controllable, making it impossible to verify whether aleatoric and epistemic components actually capture what they claim to. The proof-of-concept results (AU tracking label noise, EU tracking image noise) validate the dataset's design, while the blood-stain manipulation exposing confident miscalibration in all four UQ methods highlights a practical risk in clinical deployment. Open questions include whether the clean AU/EU separation holds for more complex manipulations (e.g., combined image+label noise), whether the findings generalize beyond colon H&E tissue, and how the benchmark performs for newer UQ methods (e.g., deep deterministic uncertainty [27]) or active learning sampling strategies.
