## Problem

Standard causal language modeling applies next-token prediction loss uniformly to every token in a pretraining corpus, yet token-level training dynamics reveal that only ~26% of tokens actually show meaningful loss reduction, while the majority are either already learned, persistently noisy, or exhibit non-convergent fluctuations. This uniform treatment wastes gradient updates on uninformative tokens and fails to align the training signal with the distribution most relevant to downstream tasks.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a large pretraining text corpus (e.g., 15B OpenWebMath tokens or 80B mixed general tokens) and a reference model that provides per-token logprob scores. The reference model is a same-architecture LM trained for 3 epochs on a small curated high-quality dataset (0.5B math tokens or 1.9B general tokens) that encodes the desired target distribution. The output is a language model trained with loss applied only to the top k% of tokens ranked by excess loss (typically 60–70%), effectively discarding gradients for the remaining tokens without any architectural change.

## Method

Selective Language Modeling (SLM) replaces the uniform cross-entropy loss with a masked loss that retains gradients only for tokens with the highest excess loss L_Δ(x_i) = L_θ(x_i) − L_RM(x_i), where L_θ is the current training model's loss and L_RM is the reference model's loss. The pipeline has three stages: (1) train a reference model on a curated high-quality corpus reflecting the target distribution, (2) score every token in the pretraining corpus using the reference model's per-token loss, and (3) at each training step, rank tokens within a batch by excess loss and apply cross-entropy only to the top k% (Eq. 4–5). The key insight is that high excess loss identifies tokens that are both learnable (the training model hasn't yet captured them) and aligned with the desired distribution (the reference model finds them informative), naturally filtering out noisy, irrelevant, or already-mastered tokens. Unlike RHO-LOSS, SLM operates at token granularity rather than sample level, uses a distribution-matched reference model rather than a random holdout proxy, and scales to 80B-token pretraining.

## Results

On math continual pretraining, RHO-1-7B (trained with SLM on 10.5B selected tokens from 15B OpenWebMath) achieves 66.2% average few-shot accuracy across 9 math benchmarks, outperforming the CLM baseline Mistral-CT (55.8%) by +10.4% and matching DeepSeekMath-7B (68.4%) which used 500B tokens — a 33× data reduction. After fine-tuning with ToRA, RHO-1-1B and RHO-1-7B achieve 40.6% and 51.8% on MATH respectively, with RHO-1-7B matching DeepSeekMath-7B's 52.0%. In general continual pretraining on 80B tokens, SLM yields a 6.8% average improvement across 15 diverse benchmarks over direct CLM training, with gains exceeding 10% on code and math tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RHO-1-7B (SLM, 10.5B tokens) achieves 66.2% average few-shot accuracy, surpassing the CLM baseline Mistral-CT (55.8%) by +10.4% and approaching DeepSeekMath-7B (68.4%) which required 500B tokens — a 33× reduction in pretraining data. On the 1B scale, RHO-1-Math (9B selected tokens) improves over Tinyllama-CT (21.6%) by +16.5% to reach 38.1%, exceeding Phi-1.5 (31.0%) and Gemma (38.4%) at comparable or larger model sizes. After ToRA fine-tuning, RHO-1-7B reaches 51.8% on MATH, essentially matching DeepSeekMath-7B (52.0%) while using only 3% of its pretraining tokens, and outperforming ToRA-7B (40.1%) and MAmmoTH-70B (41.8%). RHO-1-1B becomes the first 1B model to exceed 40% on MATH (40.6%), approaching early GPT-4's CoT performance of 42.5%. In the self-reference setting (no high-quality reference data), SLM still yields +3.3% average improvement with 40% fewer tokens, confirming the method's robustness beyond the primary experimental setup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the excess-loss formulation from RHO-LOSS (Mindermann et al., 2022), which selects samples for fine-tuning, to token-level selection during large-scale pretraining. It departs from prior document-level filtering (CCNet, GPT-3 heuristics) and BERT-era token strategies (selective masking, token dropping) by operating at the granularity of individual tokens in causal LM pretraining, using a reference model trained on a curated distribution to score and gate the loss. The training-dynamics analysis builds on Xia et al. (2022) but identifies a richer taxonomy of token behavior (four categories with persistent fluctuations) that motivates the selective objective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that only ~26% of tokens exhibit meaningful loss reduction during pretraining challenges the default assumption of uniform next-token prediction and offers a simple, drop-in mechanism (rank by excess loss, apply loss to top k%) that could be widely adopted in pretraining pipelines. The practical impact is significant: matching DeepSeekMath-7B's SOTA with 3% of its pretraining tokens suggests substantial compute savings. However, the method is validated only on ≤7B models and <100B tokens, leaving open whether very large models trained on extensive corpora naturally develop the same inductive bias, making the scalability question the most important open problem.
