## Problem

Visually-grounded language models (VLMs) such as GPT-4V and LLaVA, despite commonly using CLIP as their vision encoder and possessing 20× more total parameters, significantly underperform CLIP on standard image classification benchmarks like ImageNet (60.6% vs 79.2% in closed-world). This paper investigates the root cause of this gap by systematically testing hypotheses across inference, training, and data dimensions, and identifies a data-centric explanation that prior work had overlooked.

## Input / Output

In the classification evaluation, each RGB image is paired with a text prompt; in the closed-world setting, all class names are concatenated into the prompt (e.g., 'What type of object is in this photo? Choose one from <class A>, <class B>, ...'), while the open-world setting omits the label list. Success is defined as whether the ground-truth class name appears anywhere in the VLM's free-form generation. For the newly introduced ImageWikiQA benchmark, input is an image plus a four-option multiple-choice question about the depicted object (e.g., 'What is the native region of this object?'), and output is a single letter answer.

## Method

The paper first rules out inference-level causes (prompt variation, label set size, probabilistic inference with classifier-free guidance) and training-level causes (information loss in latent space, inadequacy of the text-generation objective), showing via linear probing that 77–81% of classification information is preserved in VLM hidden states and that fine-tuning with next-token prediction matches CLIP's cross-entropy performance. The key finding is a strong Spearman correlation (0.76 on ImageNet) between the frequency of each class in the VLM's training/instruction-tuning data and per-class zero-shot accuracy, pinpointing data coverage as the primary bottleneck. The proposed fix is to fine-tune only the small projector layer (e.g., a 2-layer MLP in LLaVA) on 1.28M ImageNet classification samples combined with the original 665K instruction-tuning data, using the standard text-generation template. This yields 84.4% ImageNet accuracy (vs. 22.8% unfine-tuned) and, crucially, transfers to an 11.8% absolute improvement on the new ImageWikiQA benchmark, demonstrating that classification capability is foundational for advanced object-centric visual reasoning.

## Results

On ImageNet (closed-world), the best proprietary VLM GPT-4 achieves 60.6% accuracy, while CLIP-L and EVA-G reach 74.8% and 79.2% respectively — a gap of 14–19 points. In the open-world setting, the best public VLM (LLaVANeXT-M7B) scores only 32.3% on ImageNet. After fine-tuning LLaVA1.5-7B on ImageNet classification data, it reaches 85.7% on ImageNet, matching CLIP-L's 85.2% and surpassing EVA-G's 86.5% on Flowers102. On the newly introduced ImageWikiQA benchmark, the fine-tuned model (ImageNet + LLaVA data) achieves 49.8% accuracy, an 11.8-point improvement over the non-fine-tuned LLaVA1.5-7B (38.0%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All ten evaluated VLMs (public and proprietary) underperform both CLIP-L and EVA-G on every closed-world benchmark, with the gap most severe on fine-grained datasets (e.g., StanfordCars: best VLM GPT4 at 58.2% vs EVA-G at 90.2%). Among public VLMs, LLaVANeXT-M7B is the strongest (32.3% open-world ImageNet), yet still trails CLIP-L by 42.5 points in closed-world. The paper's fine-tuning approach closes this gap entirely: LLaVA1.5-7B fine-tuned on ImageNet reaches 85.7%, matching CLIP-L (85.2%) and BLIP2-2.7B fine-tuned reaches 88.0%, exceeding EVA-G's 86.5% on ImageNet. On ImageWikiQA, the fine-tuned LLaVA1.5-7B (ImageNet + LLaVA data) at 49.8% surpasses all public VLMs (best: LLaVANeXT-M7B at 41.9%) and approaches GeminiPro (49.1%) and Claude3 (54.3%), though it still trails GPT4 (61.2%). Notably, fine-tuning on ImageNet alone causes catastrophic forgetting (30.6% on ImageWikiQA), confirming that combined training with original instruction data is essential.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of VLM architecture research (LLaVA, BLIP2, InstructBLIP) and data-centric AI, but carves new analytical ground by systematically testing hypotheses about why VLMs underperform their own vision encoders on classification. It builds on prior observations of VLM limitations (Tong et al. on visual shortcomings, Karamcheti et al. on prismatic VLMs, Udandarao et al. on concept frequency determining performance) but is the first to isolate data as the primary cause through controlled experiments across inference, training, and data dimensions. The introduction of ImageWikiQA extends the evaluation landscape beyond pure classification or pure VQA, bridging the two in an object-centric knowledge-intensive setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that VLMs encode classification information in their latent space but cannot decode it without sufficient class-exposure data has direct practical implications: simply adding classification datasets to VLM training closes the gap with CLIP and improves general capabilities (11.8% on ImageWikiQA). Open questions include whether zero-shot methods can decode this latent information without extensive fine-tuning, how to prevent catastrophic forgetting when adding task-specific data, and whether the data-centric finding generalizes to other visual capabilities beyond classification. The ImageWikiQA benchmark provides a useful tool for evaluating the classification-to-reasoning pipeline in VLMs.
