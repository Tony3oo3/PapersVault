## Problem

While biased belief updating is well-documented in political and scientific contexts, no formal framework exists to algorithmically measure the magnitude of an agent's bias in how they update beliefs given new evidence. This paper is the first to formalize bias detection as a threshold-testing problem and provide analytical tools to determine whether an agent's bias level w is above or below a given threshold τ.

## Input / Output

The principal is given the common prior μ₀, the agent's utility function U over a finite action set A and state space Θ, and a threshold τ ∈ (0,1). At each time step the principal observes a realized state θ_t drawn from μ₀ and commits to a signaling scheme; the agent's resulting action a_t is the only observable feedback. The algorithm outputs an optimal signaling scheme (via a linear program) whose sample complexity is 1/p*, where p* is the LP's optimal objective value representing the per-step probability of a useful (boundary) signal.

## Method

The approach models bias linearly (ν_s = wμ₀ + (1−w)μ_s) and uses information-design-style signaling schemes to probe the agent. Three key reductions simplify the problem: (1) constant signaling schemes are provably as powerful as adaptive ones, so the principal need only commit to a single scheme; (2) a revelation principle shows optimal schemes can be restricted to direct action-recommendation signals where the agent is exactly indifferent between the recommended action and the default action a₀ at bias level τ; (3) the optimal scheme is then obtained by solving a polynomial-size linear program that maximizes the total probability of such boundary signals subject to optimality and indifference constraints. A geometric characterization (Theorem 5.2) further identifies when a single sample suffices (μ₀ lies in the convex hull of translated indifference sets I_{a,τ}), when finite samples suffice, and when bias is fundamentally untestable at threshold τ.

## Results

The paper's main quantitative result is a polynomial-time linear program (Algorithm 1) that computes the optimal signaling scheme for testing whether an agent's bias w ≥ τ or w ≤ τ, with sample complexity 1/p* where p* is the LP's optimal objective value. In the two-state, two-action warm-up example, the optimal sample complexity is (μ* − μ₀)/(μ₀(1−τ)) + 1, which increases with τ. The geometric characterization (Theorem 5.2) identifies three regimes: single-sample detection (sample complexity = 1) when μ₀ lies in the convex hull of translated indifference sets I_{a,τ}; finite but >1 sample complexity when at least one I_{a,τ} is non-empty; and impossibility when all I_{a,τ} are empty. A key structural result is that constant signaling schemes are as powerful as adaptive ones (Lemma 4.1), and a revelation principle (Lemma 4.5) restricts the search to direct action-recommendation signals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or quantitative comparisons against prior methods. The paper's closest prior work is de Clippel and Zhang [5] on non-Bayesian persuasion, which studies the same linear bias model but assumes the agent's bias is fully known and aims to maximize the principal's utility, whereas this paper treats bias as unknown and aims to infer it. Tang and Ho [18] provide real-world experiments supporting the linear bias model assumption. The paper's internal 'comparison' is between constant and adaptive signaling schemes, proving they are equivalent in power (Lemma 4.1), and between the proposed LP-based algorithm and the infeasibility of the LP (which corresponds to the impossibility case in Theorem 5.2). No head-to-head numerical benchmarks against competing algorithms are presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the mathematical machinery of Bayesian persuasion (the splitting lemma, revelation principle) from Kamenica & Gentzkow (2011) and Dughmi & Xu (2016), but inverts the principal's objective: rather than maximizing utility against a known Bayesian agent, the principal designs signals to *infer* an unknown bias parameter. The linear bias model (ν_s = wμ₀ + (1−w)μ_s) is drawn from the economics literature on non-Bayesian updating (Epstein et al. 2010, de Clippel & Zhang 2022), but those works assume the bias is known and focus on persuasion, not detection. The key conceptual departure is that the revelation principle here requires the agent to be *indifferent* at the threshold bias level rather than strictly preferring the recommended action, a structural difference that enables the clean LP formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first formal algorithmic treatment of detecting non-Bayesian belief updating, a problem with direct relevance to understanding political polarization, scientific disagreement, and the design of collective decision-making systems that must discount biased agents. The geometric characterization (Theorem 5.2) cleanly separates the problem into three regimes—single-sample, finite-sample, and fundamentally impossible—providing a useful diagnostic for when bias detection is even feasible. Key limitations include the linear bias model assumption, the requirement that the agent's prior matches the true prior, and the risk-neutral agent assumption; extending to general continuous bias functions φ or unknown priors is explicitly left open and would be substantially harder.
