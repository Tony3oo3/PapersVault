## Problem

No comprehensive benchmark or dataset exists for zero-shot material state segmentation across diverse domains (food, plants, rocks, construction, liquids), and top foundation models like SAM and Materialistic perform poorly on this task. The fundamental challenge is that material states exhibit scattered shapes, soft boundaries, and gradual transitions that make manual polygon annotation infeasible, while purely synthetic data fails to capture the vast complexity of real-world material patterns.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241924/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At inference, the network receives a single RGB image and one or more input points marking a target material, and outputs a per-pixel material similarity map (soft) indicating how similar each pixel's material is to the queried point. The MatSeg benchmark uses point-based annotations with partial similarity groups rather than full polygon masks, since many material states (stains, infections, corrosion) have scattered or fuzzy boundaries that cannot be cleanly segmented. Evaluation on the main benchmark uses a triplet metric comparing relative similarity rankings between point pairs, while additional benchmarks use IoU with an optimal threshold applied to the similarity map.

## Method

The key contribution is an unsupervised 'patterns infusion' pipeline that extracts textures and spatial maps from real-world images by randomly selecting image channels (RGB, HSV), applying fuzzy thresholding to create soft binary maps, and identifying uniform texture regions via Jensen-Shannon distance on color and gradient distributions. These extracted patterns are then mapped onto synthetic 2D scenes (as alpha-blended textures over random backgrounds) or 3D scenes (as UV maps controlling PBR material distribution on object surfaces rendered in Blender with Cycles and HDRI illumination). The segmentation network is a U-Net-style architecture that predicts 128-dimensional L2-normalized descriptor vectors per pixel; material similarity is computed via cosine similarity between pixel descriptors, and training uses cross-entropy loss against batch-averaged material embeddings with a softmax temperature of 0.2. This decouples the learned material patterns from the specific image-channel correlation that produced them, allowing the network to generalize across domains while retaining the precision and scalability of synthetic data.

## Results

On the MatSeg benchmark (triplet accuracy, where 50% = random and 100% = perfect), the Mixed MatSeg net trained on infused synthetic data achieves 0.92 (all triplets) and 0.84 (soft/gradual triplets) with a single input point, substantially outperforming Materialistic (0.76 / 0.72) and SAM (0.69–0.74 / 0.62–0.65 at 1–8 points). On 12 additional domain-specific benchmarks (Table 2, IoU), MatSeg leads on all tasks involving scattered shapes or gradual transitions (e.g., corrosion 0.69 vs 0.49/0.48, leaf disease 0.56 vs 0.47/0.47, microscopy 0.38–0.45 vs 0.17–0.28), while SAM and Materialistic retain the edge on structured, object-aligned segments (e.g., Soil type 0.88 vs 0.69, Materialistic test set 0.85–0.87 vs 0.75).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MatSeg decisively outperforms both baselines on its primary benchmark: the Mixed MatSeg net scores 0.92 triplet accuracy vs. Materialistic's 0.76 and SAM's 0.74 (even with 8 guiding points), a gap of 16–23 points. On the 12 cross-domain benchmarks (Table 2), MatSeg wins every task marked with scattered shapes or gradual transitions—corrosion (0.69 vs. 0.49/0.48), leaf disease (0.56 vs. 0.47/0.47), copper/iron ore (0.52–0.62 vs. 0.36–0.37/0.36–0.37), and microscopy (0.38–0.45 vs. 0.17–0.28/0.23–0.26). Conversely, SAM and Materialistic outperform MatSeg on structured, object-aligned benchmarks: Materialistic leads on its own test set (0.87 vs. 0.75) and Soil type (0.71 vs. 0.69), while SAM with 8 points reaches 0.88 on Soil type and 0.85 on Materialistic. The authors interpret this split as evidence that MatSeg's infusion method captures raw material-state patterns (scattered, soft, gradual) that are underrepresented in both SAM's polygon annotations and Materialistic's asset-based synthetic training, while the baselines remain stronger on clean, bulk-region segmentation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Drehwald et al. (ICCV 2023), adopting their vector-embedding + cosine-similarity framework for material matching and their Blender-based 3D scene generation pipeline, but departs fundamentally in how training data is created: rather than relying on manually crafted PBR assets or procedural rules, it extracts patterns and textures from real-world images using simple unsupervised methods (channel thresholding, grid-based Jensen-Shannon uniformity detection) and injects them into synthetic scenes. This repurposes 'ancient' texture-segmentation techniques (50+ years old) as a data-generation strategy, positioning them as a bridge between the precision of synthetic data and the diversity of real-world patterns. The evaluation is framed against SAM (real-image, polygon-annotated) and Materialistic (synthetic, asset-based), isolating the contribution of the infusion method from the underlying network architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a concrete and substantial gap: even SAM (11M images, 1B segments) and Materialistic struggle with scattered, soft-boundary, and gradual-transition material states, while a net trained on pattern-infused synthetic data closes much of this gap across 12+ benchmarks. The released 300K extracted textures and PBR materials, combined with the MatSeg benchmark, provide infrastructure for future work on open-world material understanding. Key limitations include weaker performance on microscopy (due to under-representation in source images), the inherent noise in unsupervised extraction, and the subjective nature of point-based similarity annotation, leaving open whether the infusion strategy generalizes to domains with fundamentally different visual statistics.
