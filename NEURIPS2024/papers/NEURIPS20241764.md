## Problem

Amortized explainers approximate expensive feature attribution methods (e.g., SHAP) with a single inference, making them practical for large models, but they can produce highly divergent explanations from their reference. Existing approaches offer a binary choice between fast-but-inaccurate amortized explanations and accurate-but-expensive Monte Carlo explanations, with no principled mechanism to detect when the cheap explanation is unreliable or to selectively invest additional computation where it is most needed.

## Input / Output

The selective explainer takes an input feature vector x (raw features for tabular data, mean-pooled token embeddings for text), a target output y, a pre-trained amortized explainer, and a Monte Carlo explainer, and outputs a d-dimensional feature attribution vector. A coverage parameter α (set by the practitioner) determines the fraction of inputs that receive only the single-inference amortized explanation; the remaining 1−α fraction receive an 'explanation with initial guess' requiring n+1 inferences. The selection is governed by a learned uncertainty score s_h(x) thresholded at the α-quantile, and the combination weight λ_h(x) is looked up from pre-computed per-bin constants.

## Method

The selective explanation framework has three components: (i) an uncertainty metric (either Deep Uncertainty, the average variance across k independently trained amortized explainers, or Learned Uncertainty, an MLP regressing the MSE between amortized and Monte Carlo explanations), (ii) a selection function τ_α that thresholds the uncertainty score to route each input to either the cheap amortized path or the expensive recourse path, and (iii) an 'explanation with initial guess' that linearly combines the amortized and Monte Carlo explanations as λ_h(x)·Amor(x,y) + (1−λ_h(x))·MC^n(x,y). The combination weight λ_h is optimized per uncertainty bin via a closed-form shrinkage-estimator solution (Theorem 1) that minimizes MSE to a higher-quality Monte Carlo reference, and Theorem 2 shows this approximates the oracle weight with high probability. The key insight is that even a poor amortized explanation carries signal that, when blended with Monte Carlo at the optimal shrinkage coefficient, yields explanations superior to either method alone.

## Results

On the four evaluated datasets (UCI-Adult, UCI-News, Yelp Review, Toxigen), the Learned uncertainty metric achieves Spearman's correlation of 0.93 with MSE on Toxigen and 0.77 on Yelp, closely matching the Oracle selector. Selective explanations with initial guess Pareto-dominate pure Monte Carlo explanations in the inference-vs-MSE trade-off (Figure 6), closely approximating the Oracle curve. With only 20% of samples receiving additional computations, the MSE for the worst 5% of explanations on Yelp decreases by approximately 30% compared to the amortized explainer alone (Figure 5). Explanations with initial guess outperform both amortized-only and Monte Carlo-only recourse in all tested settings, even when the Monte Carlo explanation itself has higher MSE than the amortized one (Figure 4, Cases 1 and 2).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares selective explanations against four baselines: the amortized explainer (Stochastic Amortization [6] for tabular data, Amortized Shap for LLMs [38] for text), Monte Carlo explainers (SVS [24] and Kernel Shap [22]), and an Oracle that knows a priori which samples to route to which method. Selective explanations consistently outperform the amortized-only baseline: with 20% coverage, worst-5% MSE drops by ~30% on Yelp and even more on UCI datasets (Figure 5). Against pure Monte Carlo recourse (λ_h=0, 'Naive'), explanations with initial guess achieve lower MSE in all four datasets (Figure 4), and this advantage holds even when the Monte Carlo explanation is itself worse than the amortized one (Case 2 in Figure 4). In the inference-vs-MSE trade-off (Figure 6), selective explanations Pareto-dominate the Monte Carlo curve (SVS-12→25→50) and closely track the Oracle, indicating near-optimal routing. The Learned uncertainty selector approaches Oracle-level performance on language models (Figure 3c,d), while Deep uncertainty performs comparably on tabular data (Figure 3a,b).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper applies the selective prediction framework (Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017) to the feature attribution setting, a combination the authors note has not been previously explored. It builds on amortized explainers (Stochastic Amortization for tabular data, Amortized SHAP for LLMs) and Monte Carlo approximations (SVS, Kernel SHAP) as the two endpoints of a quality-cost spectrum, and introduces a shrinkage-estimator-inspired linear combination ('explanations with initial guess') to blend them. The uncertainty metrics extend deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) to the high-dimensional regression problem of d-dimensional attribution vectors, a setting not addressed by prior selective prediction work which targets classification or scalar regression.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives practitioners a principled, tunable knob (coverage α) to trade explanation quality against inference cost, and the near-Oracle performance in the inference-vs-MSE trade-off suggests the approach is close to optimal routing of computational budget. Key limitations include restriction to SHAP values, absence of image classifiers and generative language models, and reliance on Monte Carlo explanations as a proxy for the unavailable target. Open questions include whether the high-dimensional uncertainty metrics generalize to other attribution methods (LIME, integrated gradients) and whether the approach extends to the increasingly important setting of explaining generative LLM outputs.
