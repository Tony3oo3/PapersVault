## Problem

Prior theoretical work on transformer in-context learning has focused on expressive power via hand-designed parameter constructions, leaving the actual working mechanisms of trained multi-head transformers largely opaque. This paper asks how a trained transformer distributes its attention heads across layers when solving sparse linear regression in-context, and whether different layers serve fundamentally different computational roles.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243798/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The transformer receives a (d+1) × (n+q) input matrix E where the first n columns are in-context examples (x_i, y_i) and the remaining q columns are queries (x_i, 0), with x ~ N(0, Σ) and y = ⟨w*, x⟩ + N(0, σ²) under a sparse ground truth ‖w*‖₀ ≤ s ≪ d. The model outputs a scalar prediction ŷ for each query position. Experiments use d=16, s=4, d_hid=256, an encoder-only linear-attention architecture without positional encodings, and n=12 training examples with q=4 queries per batch.

## Method

Head-masking experiments reveal that the first attention layer uses all heads roughly equally while subsequent layers rely dominantly on a single head, motivating a 'preprocess-then-optimize' mechanism. The first layer's d-head attention estimates per-feature label correlations r̂_j = (1/n)Σᵢ x_ij·y_i and reweights features as x̃ = R̂x, effectively emphasizing the sparse support of w*; subsequent single-head layers then execute gradient descent on the preprocessed data. The authors prove this algorithm is exactly implementable by a transformer with d heads (d_hid=3d) in layer 1 and 1 head in all remaining layers, and derive excess risk bounds showing Õ(s/n) in the under-parameterized regime versus Õ(d/n) for OLS, ridge regression, and standard GD. Empirical validation includes pruning (retaining all first-layer heads, one subsequent head, then fine-tuning) and P-probing (extracting first-layer hidden states and running GD on them to confirm the preprocessing step).

## Results

The paper's main quantitative contribution is theoretical: Theorem 5.3 shows that the preprocess-then-optimize algorithm (multi-head first layer + single-head subsequent layers) achieves excess risk of $\tilde{O}(s/n + ds^2/n^2)$, strictly improving over the $\tilde{\Omega}(d/n)$ lower bound for OLS, ridge regression, and finite-step gradient descent in the under-parameterized regime ($d \lesssim n$). In the over-parameterized regime ($d \gtrsim n$), the method achieves $\tilde{O}(ds/n^2)$ versus an $\Omega(1)$ lower bound for all baselines. Empirically, head-masking experiments (Figure 1c, Figure 4) confirm that in the first attention layer all heads contribute roughly equally (normalized importance $\mathcal{W}_{i,j} \approx 1/h$), while in subsequent layers a single head dominates with importance $\mathcal{W}_{i,j} \gg 1/h$. P-probing experiments (Figure 2a) show that query representations extracted from multi-head transformers ($h=4,8$) yield lower excess risk after gradient descent than those from single-head transformers ($h=1$).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to Bai et al. [8] (Transformers as Statisticians), this paper extends the sparse linear regression ICL setting by revealing *how* multi-head attention is structured across layers: while [8] demonstrates that transformers can select among algorithms (e.g., ridge vs. OLS), this work shows that the first layer's multi-head attention performs feature reweighting (preprocessing) and subsequent layers' single-head attention performs gradient descent on the preprocessed data. The empirical head-assessment experiments build directly on [8]'s experimental protocol but add per-head masking to expose the layer-dependent utilization pattern. Compared to Garg et al. [20] (What Can Transformers Learn In-Context?), this paper uses the same ridge/OLS/lasso baselines and tuning protocol but demonstrates that multi-head transformers ($h=4,8$) consistently achieve lower excess risk than single-head transformers ($h=1$) across noise levels and layer counts (Figure 3), and that the preprocess-then-optimize algorithm provably outperforms all three baselines by a factor of $s/d$ in the under-parameterized regime. The P-probing and pre-GD experiments (Figure 2) further validate that the preprocessing step is what drives the improvement, not simply having more parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work connecting multi-layer transformers to iterative optimization algorithms (von Oswald et al. [47], Ahn et al. [3], Zhang et al. [53]), which showed that single-head transformers can implement gradient descent steps for in-context linear regression. It also builds on prior studies of transformers for sparse linear regression (Bai et al. [8], Garg et al. [20]) that demonstrated in-context learning performance but left the role of individual attention heads opaque. The key departure is a mechanistic, post-training analysis: rather than constructing parameters to show expressive power, the paper uses head-masking, pruning, and probing on trained models to reveal a 'preprocess-then-optimize' division of labor, where multi-head attention in the first layer performs correlation-based feature reweighting and single-head attention in subsequent layers executes gradient descent on the preprocessed data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic explanation for why multi-head attention specifically benefits in-context learning: the benefit is concentrated in the first layer's preprocessing step, not distributed across all layers. This reframes how one should think about head allocation in transformer architectures for ICL tasks. However, the analysis is limited to sparse linear regression with linear (non-softmax) attention, d=16, and ignores MLPs and training dynamics, so whether the preprocess-then-optimize pattern generalizes to real-world tasks, deeper architectures, or softmax attention remains an open question. The theoretical excess risk bounds showing the algorithm outperforms OLS, ridge regression, and standard GD (comparable to Lasso up to log factors in the under-parameterized regime) are a useful benchmark for future mechanistic analyses.
