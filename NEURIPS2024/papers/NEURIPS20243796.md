## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning benchmarks focus on final-answer accuracy, which is saturating and masks underlying logical errors, unnecessary steps, and flawed intermediate reasoning. Prior meta-reasoning efforts (MR-GSM8K, MR-Math, BIG-Bench Mistake) extend evaluation to error identification but are confined to narrow domains (mathematics or symbolic logic) and small scale. MR-Ben addresses this gap by introducing a comprehensive, multi-subject meta-reasoning benchmark that demands models to locate, explain, and correct errors in generated reasoning chains across physics, chemistry, biology, medicine, math, logic, and coding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243796/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation instance pairs a multi-subject question (sourced from MMLU, LogiQA, or MHPP) with a numbered Chain-of-Thought solution generated by GPT-3.5-Turbo, Claude2, or Mistral-Medium at temperature 1. The evaluated model must produce a structured judgment: a binary correctness label, the index of the first erroneous step, a natural-language explanation of why that step is wrong, and a correction. Scoring is progressive—ACC_step and ACC_reason are only computed for solutions the model correctly flags as incorrect—and the error-reason component is scored by GPT-4-Turbo as a proxy (92% agreement with human annotators on a 100-sample holdout).

## Method

MR-Ben employs a meta-reasoning paradigm in which LLMs assume the role of a teacher critiquing a student's solution, requiring them to scrutinize assumptions, conditions, calculations, and logical steps—a process the authors align with System-2 slow thinking. The 5,975-instance dataset is built through a three-stage annotation pipeline: (1) determining whether the final answer is correct and whether the reasoning path is valid even when the answer matches ground truth, (2) identifying the first step where the correct result becomes unreachable, and (3) writing the error reason and correction. Quality control involves dual annotation, secondary review by independent annotators, and 10% meta-controller sampling. Evaluation uses a step-wise chain-of-thought prompt that guides models to reason through the solution trace before deciding, and performance is quantified by MR-Score, a weighted combination of MCC (solution correctness), ACC_step (first-error-step localization), and ACC_reason (error-reason accuracy), with weights chosen via grid search to maximize inter-model differentiation.

## Results

On MR-Ben, the metric is MR-Score (a weighted combination of MCC for solution-correctness, ACC_step for first-error-step identification, and ACC_reason for error-reason explanation). o1-preview achieves the highest average MR-Score of 59.8 (1-shot), followed by o1-mini at 50.8 and GPT-4o at 45.5. Among open-source models, Qwen2-72B (34.2) and DeepSeek-V2-236B (32.3) lead, while most other open-source models score below 20. Small models (Gemma-2B, Qwen1.5-1.8B) score near zero, and even LLaMA3-70B (20.2) trails GPT-4-Turbo (44.7) by over 24 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

o1-preview and o1-mini dominate all other models by a wide margin (59.8 vs. 45.5 for GPT-4o), attributed to their test-time compute scaling and solution-space exploration. GPT-4-Turbo (44.7) and GPT-4o (45.5) are the strongest non-o1 closed-source models, but both trail o1-preview by ~14 points. Among open-source models, Qwen2-72B (34.2) and DeepSeek-V2-236B (32.3) are competitive with mid-tier closed-source models like Moonshot-v1-8k (33.0) and Yi-Large (32.3), but fall well short of GPT-4o. Notably, Phi3-3.8B (11.0) outperforms several models 10× its size (e.g., DeepSeek-Coder-33B at 6.3), suggesting effective synthetic data training. DeepSeek-Coder-7B/33B do not show a coding advantage on the Coding subject despite specialized pretraining, scoring only 5.8/4.9 respectively. LLaMA3-70B (20.2) lags GPT-4-Turbo by over 24 points, and small models (Gemma-2B, Qwen1.5-1.8B) are essentially non-functional on this benchmark.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the meta-reasoning evaluation paradigm established by MR-GSM8K and MR-Math, which asked LLMs to locate and explain the first error in a chain-of-thought solution, but which were confined to mathematical reasoning. It also builds on the step-level positive/negative/neutral categorization from PRM800K and the error-identification focus of BIG-Bench Mistake (symbolic logic). The key departure is scope: MR-Ben generalizes the meta-reasoning task from a single or narrow domain to seven subjects (math, physics, biology, chemistry, medicine, logic, coding) at 5,975 questions, spanning high-school to professional difficulty, while retaining the same MR-Score metric (MCC + step accuracy + reason accuracy).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MR-Ben reveals that meta-reasoning—scrutinizing assumptions, calculations, and logical steps in another's solution—is a fundamentally underdeveloped capability in most current LLMs, with even GPT-4-Turbo achieving only ~45 MR-Score. The large gap between o1-preview (~59) and all other models highlights the potential of test-time compute scaling and iterative search through the solution space, while the finding that coding-specialized pretraining (DeepSeek-Coder) does not confer superior algorithmic reasoning challenges the assumption that domain-specific training yields broad cognitive gains. Open questions include how to systematically develop system-2 thinking in LLMs beyond test-time scaling, whether the benchmark's reliance on three LLMs for generating CoT answers limits generalizability, and whether the GPT-4-based proxy scoring of error reasons introduces systematic bias.
