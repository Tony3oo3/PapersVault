## Problem

Existing AI alignment methods are entirely a posteriori—a system is deemed aligned only if it does not exhibit misaligned behavior—providing no formal, verifiable guarantee that an autonomous agent's objective faithfully represents social interest before deployment. For critical social decision processes (e.g., setting tax rates or public spending), this is insufficient because a single destructive action by a brittle agent could outweigh all benefits, and holding a deceptive AI accountable remains unresolved. The paper asks whether one can define alignment quantitatively in the context of social choice and prove the existence of policies with a priori, probabilistic alignment guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a current social state, an approximate world model (transition dynamics characterized by its worst-case KL divergence from the true model), utility reports from a subset of n assessors (out of N total individuals), and optionally a black-box policy to be safeguarded. It produces a deterministic PAA policy (a state-to-action mapping via greedy selection on recursively estimated Q-values) or a safe policy (the original policy restricted to a verifiably safe action subset), together with explicit probabilistic guarantees: with probability at least 1−δ, the policy's expected future discounted social welfare is within ε of optimal (PAA) or never transitions to a state where even the optimal policy falls below a welfare threshold ω (safe). The sample complexity is two-faceted—K Monte Carlo calls to the world model and n assessor feedbacks—and is designed to be independent of the (potentially infinite) state space size.

## Method

The paper introduces Social Markov Decision Processes (SMDPs) where the reward is the power-mean aggregation of individual utilities (W_q) rather than a scalar, and proves that the expected future discounted social welfare is exactly the MDP state value function (Lemma 1), reducing alignment to a standard planning problem. The PAA policy is constructed by amending the sparse sampling algorithm of Kearns et al. to handle two sources of approximation: an inexact dynamics model (bounded via KL divergence and Pinsker/Bretagnolle-Huber inequalities) and an inexact social welfare estimate (bounded via a novel Hoeffding-Serfling concentration inequality for the power mean, yielding the function Γ that governs how many assessors n are needed). The recursive Q̂^h estimates use K samples from the approximate model for the immediate reward and C samples for the value backup, with planning depth H controlling the horizon. For safe policies, the method applies action masking: the black-box policy is restricted to actions whose estimated Q̂_ω(s,a) exceeds γω + U_max + α, where α is a closed-form error bound aggregating all approximation sources (model inaccuracy, Monte Carlo noise, assessor sampling, and finite-horizon truncation), guaranteeing with probability ≥ 1−δ that no action leads to a destructively low-welfare state.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical evaluations. The main quantitative result (Theorem 2) establishes that PAA policies exist whenever the KL-divergence between the true and approximate world model satisfies sup_{(s,a)} D_KL(p(·|s,a)‖p̂(·|s,a)) < ε²(1−γ)⁴/(8ΔW²). Theorem 3 provides explicit sample complexity bounds: the number of Monte Carlo samples K scales as O(ΔU²/β² · H·log(1/δ)), the number of successor samples C scales as O(γ²/(1−γ)² · K), and the number of assessors n scales as O(N/(1 + ΔU²NΓ/(2K))). Crucially, all complexities are independent of the state space size |S|, making the policies 'computable' even for infinite state spaces. Theorem 8 shows that any black-box policy can be made δ-ω-safe by restricting its action set to those with estimated Q-value ≥ γω + U_max + α, where α captures all approximation errors.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a theoretical contribution and does not include empirical comparisons against other methods. The closest methodological relationship is to the sparse sampling algorithm of Kearns et al. [19], which the authors modify to handle approximate dynamics (p̂) and approximate rewards (r̂_{I_n}) rather than assuming both are known. The paper positions itself against existing alignment approaches (RLHF, red-teaming, value alignment) by arguing they provide only a posteriori guarantees, whereas the PAA framework offers a priori, quantitative alignment guarantees tied to the statistical accuracy of the world model. No head-to-head numerical comparisons are made, as the contribution is the existence proof and the sample complexity characterization rather than an algorithmic improvement over a baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse sampling algorithm of Kearns et al. [19] by adapting it to handle approximate dynamics models and approximate social welfare rewards, rather than assuming both are known. It draws heavily on classical utility theory (Debreu's representation theorem) and social choice theory (power means as social welfare functions under various informational basis invariance axioms) to construct a quantitative alignment metric that prior AI alignment work has not provided. The paper explicitly positions itself against the a posteriori alignment paradigm (red-teaming, behavioral checks) by offering a priori, probabilistic guarantees, and contrasts with safe RL by avoiding exploration in favor of model-based planning with action masking.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first formal, quantitative definition of alignment for social decision-making with provable existence conditions, filling a gap the authors identify between AI alignment (which lacks formal guarantees) and social choice theory (which lacks a dynamic, sequential decision framework). The safe policy concept is practically relevant: it allows leveraging powerful black-box agents (e.g., LLM-based policymakers) while bounding the probability of destructive decisions, even when full PAA is computationally infeasible. Key open questions include the practical difficulty of building world models satisfying the KL divergence condition, the poor sample complexity for non-utilitarian welfare functions (q≠1) and high discount factors (γ≈1), and the assumption of static preferences and full state observability.
