## Problem

The prevalent practice of filtering contrastive VLM training data to English-only image-text pairs systematically disadvantages lower-income and non-Western communities, yet this cultural disparity remains invisible to the standard Western-centric benchmarks (ImageNet, COCO) that dominate model evaluation. The paper asks whether this filtering is truly necessary for strong benchmark performance and whether a training strategy can simultaneously preserve cultural diversity and competitive standard-benchmark scores.

## Input / Output

The models consume 256×256 RGB images and text prompts (up to 64 tokens from a 250k-vocabulary mC4 tokenizer spanning 101 languages), producing 768-dimensional embeddings for contrastive alignment. Evaluation outputs span zero-shot class labels, cross-modal retrieval similarity scores, and—newly introduced—few-shot geographic location predictions (country or region) obtained via a closed-form linear probe on frozen image embeddings.

## Method

The paper trains SigLIP models (ViT-B/16 image encoder + Transformer-B text encoder, sigmoid loss, 10B WebLI pairs, ~40k TPUv2 core-hours) in three data variants: English-only (en), full multilingual (globe), and multilingual with machine translation to English (globe-tl). The central finding is an asymmetry in fine-tuning order: pretraining on globe-tl then fine-tuning on en recovers ImageNet/COCO performance while retaining cultural diversity gains, whereas the reverse (en pretraining → globe-tl fine-tuning) fails to close the cultural gap. To make this disparity measurable, the authors introduce few-shot geo-localization (5/10/25-shot linear probes on image embeddings over Dollar Street, XM3600, and GeoDE), which reveals substantially larger en-vs-globe-tl gaps than zero-shot classification. Statistical significance is confirmed with 3 seeds per configuration and two-sample t-tests at 95% confidence, and quality-filtered ablations verify that findings persist after removing ~60% of low-quality pairs.

## Results

On culturally diverse zero-shot benchmarks, globe-tl (global data with English translation) consistently outperforms en (English-only): +5.62% on GLDv2 (49.46% vs 43.84%), +1.66% on MaRVL (69.96% vs 68.30%), +1.44% on Dollar Street (49.96% vs 48.52%), and +1.02% on GeoDE (92.84% vs 91.82%). Conversely, en achieves substantially higher scores on Western-oriented benchmarks: 70.36% on ImageNet 0-shot (vs 68.23% for globe-tl), 59.28% on COCO I→T R@1 (vs 54.00%), and 42.91% on COCO T→I R@1 (vs 37.78%). In few-shot geo-localization, globe-tl improves over en by up to +13.30% (GeoDE country, 25-shot: 36.54% vs 23.24%), demonstrating that English-filtered models learn significantly less geographically informative image features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is an empirical study comparing three training data configurations for SigLIP-B/16 models rather than different architectures: en (English-only), globe (full multilingual), and globe-tl (global data with machine translation to English). On all four culturally diverse zero-shot benchmarks, globe-tl significantly outperforms en, with the largest gap on GLDv2 (+5.62%) and MaRVL (+1.66%), while globe sits in between. On Western-oriented benchmarks (ImageNet, COCO), en dominates by 2.13–5.28% over globe-tl, confirming the trade-off. Notably, XM3600 retrieval shows no statistically significant differences among the three variants, leading the authors to argue that multilinguality benchmarks are insufficient proxies for cultural diversity. In few-shot geo-localization (Table 2), globe and globe-tl both substantially outperform en across all datasets and shot counts (e.g., +13.30% on GeoDE country 25-shot), and the gap between globe and globe-tl narrows considerably compared to zero-shot, indicating the image encoder itself carries the geographic signal. The authors further show that fine-tuning globe-tl on en for as few as 50k steps recovers most of the ImageNet/COCO gap while retaining cultural diversity gains, whereas the reverse (fine-tuning en on globe-tl) fails to close the cultural diversity gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) as its base architecture, extending the CLIP/ALIGN/SigLIP lineage of contrastive vision-language models. It departs from prior bias-focused work (Agarwal et al., Birhane et al., Hall et al.) that examined gender, race, and age stereotypes by instead studying the impact of English-only data filtering on cultural and socioeconomic diversity. The closest prior work is Richards et al. (2024), which showed slower progress on global benchmarks versus ImageNet, but this paper goes further by isolating the specific effect of language filtering versus translation, introducing geo-localization as a new evaluation metric, and demonstrating that pretraining on global data followed by English fine-tuning can reconcile the competing objectives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard ImageNet/COCO benchmarks are not merely blind to but actively at odds with cultural diversity performance challenges a core assumption in how the field evaluates VLMs, and the practical recommendation (pretrain on unfiltered global data, then fine-tune on English) offers a concrete path forward for practitioners. The geo-localization task provides a new, sensitive metric that reveals image-encoder-level cultural gaps invisible to zero-shot classification. Key limitations include the restriction to encoder-only SigLIP models (not generative VLMs), the lack of a precise definition of 'cultural diversity,' and the unresolved question of how cultural diversity intersects with the social biases (stereotypes, fairness) that prior work has documented.
