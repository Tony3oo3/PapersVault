## Problem

Existing end-to-end ASR systems (CTC, RNN-T, AED) require complex alignment mechanisms—dynamic programming over all possible alignments or full-sequence cross-attention at every decoding step—because their encoders were designed to preserve temporal position. The paper asks whether modern transformer-based encoders can instead learn to perform audio-to-text alignment internally during the forward pass, eliminating both dynamic programming and cross-attention from the model.

## Input / Output

The model consumes log-mel spectrograms (80 or 128 bins, 32 ms window, 10 ms stride) and produces a sequence of word-piece tokens (vocabulary 1024–4096) decoded one token per encoder frame until an <EOS> token is emitted. The encoder applies 2-D convolutional subsampling (kernel 3, stride 2) before the Conformer layers, so the embedding sequence is shorter than the input; the decoder then reads frames strictly left-to-right with a one-to-one correspondence between embedding positions and output tokens, with no blank symbols or skipping.

## Method

The Aligner-Encoder uses a Conformer encoder that, through self-attention, learns to move relevant acoustic information to the front of the embedding sequence in a label-aligned order (a 'self-transduction'), so that frame i of the embedding corresponds to token i of the output. Training uses a simple frame-wise cross-entropy loss over the first U frames (matching the label length), with no dynamic programming, no blank tokens, and no cross-attention. The decoder is a small LSTM prediction network (e.g., 1×640) that feeds the previous token into a joint network alongside the corresponding encoder frame, producing exactly one token per frame until <EOS>. This yields O(U) decoder complexity versus O(T+U) for RNN-T and O(U×T) for AED, and eliminates the need for path merging during beam search. The paper demonstrates that the alignment is visible in the self-attention weights of specific layers (e.g., layers 14–15 of a 17-layer encoder) and that the model can handle reversed alignments, suggesting applicability to non-monotonic tasks.

## Results

On LibriSpeech, the Aligner-Encoder achieves 2.3% WER on Test-Clean and 5.1% WER on Test-Other, closely matching RNN-T (2.1% and 4.6%) and outperforming AED (2.4% and 5.5%). On the Voice Search dataset, the Aligner matches RNN-T at 3.7% WER (vs. 3.6%) on the main test set. In a representative inference scenario (11.5s audio, beam size 6), the Aligner's total inference time is 2× faster than RNN-T (51 ms vs. 108 ms) and 16× faster than AED (51 ms vs. 832 ms).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RNN-T, the Aligner is slightly worse on LibriSpeech (2.3% vs. 2.1% Test-Clean; 5.1% vs. 4.6% Test-Other) and on YouTube long-form unsegmented (7.3% vs. 6.8%), but essentially matches it on Voice Search (3.7% vs. 3.6% main test) and ties on 15-second segmented YouTube (both 7.6%). Against AED, the Aligner wins on LibriSpeech Test-Other (5.1% vs. 5.5%) and is marginally better on Test-Clean (2.3% vs. 2.4%), while being 16× faster at inference. Against CTC, the Aligner clearly outperforms on every dataset: 2.3% vs. 2.8% on LibriSpeech Test-Clean, 3.7% vs. 4.3% on Voice Search, and the non-AR Aligner variant (which is closest to CTC in formulation) still trails CTC on LibriSpeech and YouTube. The paper does not claim a new SOTA in WER; the primary contribution is matching near-SOTA accuracy with substantially simpler training (no dynamic programming) and faster decoding (O(U) vs. O(T+U) for RNN-T, O(U×T) for AED).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines the decoder architecture of RNN-T (a small LSTM prediction network plus a joint network) with the frame-wise cross-entropy training loss of AED, but departs fundamentally from both by requiring the Conformer encoder itself to learn audio-to-text alignment internally via self-attention during the forward pass. Prior ASR systems (CTC, RNN-T, AED) all assumed the encoder would preserve temporal positions and delegated alignment to the decoder—either through dynamic programming over a label-frame lattice (RNN-T) or learned cross-attention (AED). The Aligner-Encoder repurposes the transformer encoder's self-attention mechanism to perform what the paper calls 'self-transduction,' collapsing the alignment step into the encoder and reducing the decoder to a minimal O(U) auto-regressive scan.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 16× inference speedup over AED and 2× over RNN-T, combined with a simpler training objective (no dynamic programming, no cross-attention), makes this a practically attractive alternative for production ASR systems. The key open limitation is length generalization: the encoder can only align sequences up to a trained maximum, requiring a chunking-and-priming inference strategy for long-form audio that still trails RNN-T by ~0.5 WER. The reverse-alignment experiment and the visibility of alignment in specific self-attention layers suggest the approach could extend to non-monotonic tasks like machine translation and speech translation, though those applications remain unexplored. Streaming operation, multilingual support, and integration with pretrained encoders are explicitly flagged as future work.
