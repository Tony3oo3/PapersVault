## Problem

High-dimensional linear bandits suffer from the curse of dimensionality, and while sparsity has been widely exploited as a low-dimensional inductive bias, many practical reward functions lack sparsity but possess symmetry (invariance under hidden groups of coordinate permutations). This paper asks whether a learner can exploit such hidden symmetry to break the curse of dimensionality without prior knowledge of the symmetry group, and establishes that no algorithm can benefit from merely knowing the group is a subgroup of S_d — further structural constraints on the partition class are necessary.

## Input / Output

At each round the learner selects an arm x_t from a set X ⊂ R^d (d potentially very large) and receives a stochastic reward y_t = ⟨x_t, θ*⟩ + η_t. The learner also has access to a collection M of candidate fixed-point subspaces (each of dimension ≤ d_0 ≪ d) derived from a constrained partition class Q_{d,≤d_0} with sub-exponential cardinality. The algorithm outputs a selected arm per round, an estimated parameter vector θ̂ constrained to the chosen subspace, and the identified model subspace m̂; the key unusual aspect is that the true symmetry group G is hidden and must be inferred online from the data.

## Method

The paper introduces the Explore-Models-then-Commit (EMC) algorithm, which casts the hidden-symmetry problem as model selection over a collection of low-dimensional fixed-point subspaces (via a bijection between set partitions and fixed-point subspaces). During an exploration phase of t_1 rounds, arms are sampled i.i.d. from an exploratory distribution; the algorithm then selects the subspace minimizing least-squares error and commits to greedy play. The key technical departure from prior LSB model-selection work is replacing model aggregation with explicit model selection combined with the restricted isometry property on the union of subspaces: this guarantees the estimator θ̂ lies in one of the candidate subspaces, enabling risk-error recovery with only O(log M) samples and eliminating polynomial dependence on the number of models M and arms K. The regret analysis proceeds in two steps — bounding prediction error via Gaussian model selection concentration, then converting to risk error via RIP — yielding O(d_0^{2/3} T^{2/3} log d) regret, improvable to O(d_0 √T log d) under a well-separated partition assumption.

## Results

The main theoretical contribution is a regret bound of Õ(d₀^{2/3} T^{2/3}) for the EMC algorithm (Algorithm 1), which matches the minimax lower bound Ω(C_min(X)^{-1/3} d₀^{2/3} T^{2/3}) in the data-poor regime when K_x = O(d₀). Under an additional well-separated partition assumption (ε₀ ≥ T^{-1/4}), the regret improves to Õ(d₀√T). In simulations with d=100, d₀=15, σ=0.1, and arms on √d·S^{d-1}, EMC achieves competitive regret in the sparsity case and significantly lower regret than the sparse-bandit ESTC baseline in non-crossing and non-nesting partition settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its EMC algorithm (Algorithm 1) against the ESTC sparse-bandit algorithm of Hao et al. [23] in three partition settings: sparsity (interval partitions), non-crossing partitions, and non-nesting partitions. In the sparsity case, EMC performs competitively with ESTC, as expected since sparsity is a strict subset of both non-crossing and non-nesting partitions. In the non-crossing and non-nesting partition settings, EMC significantly outperforms ESTC, as shown by the regret curves in Figure 2, because ESTC is designed specifically for the sparsity structure and cannot exploit the broader symmetry. The theoretical advantage is that EMC's regret depends only on log(M) (the number of candidate subspaces) and d₀ (the true subspace dimension), rather than on the ambient dimension d or the number of arms K, whereas prior model-selection approaches in linear bandits [25, 35] incur polynomial dependence on M or K.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sparse linear bandit literature (Hao et al. 2020, Abbasi-Yadkori et al. 2012) by showing that sparsity is a special case of a broader hidden symmetry structure (interval partitions), and generalizes the Explore-then-Commit framework to the setting where the symmetry group is unknown. It departs from prior model-selection-in-bandits approaches (Kassraie et al. 2023, Moradipari et al. 2022) that rely on model aggregation, instead borrowing techniques from Gaussian model selection (Giraud 2021) and restricted isometry on unions of subspaces (Blumensath & Davies 2009) to achieve logarithmic dependence on the number of candidate subspaces. The symmetry-in-MDPs literature (Givan et al. 2003, Ravindran & Barto 2004) requires the symmetry group to be known a priori, whereas this paper addresses the harder hidden-symmetry setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result (Proposition 3) is a key conceptual contribution: even knowing the parameter lies in a 2-dimensional subspace among a finite pool yields no regret improvement without further structural constraints on the partition class, clarifying exactly what additional information is needed. The sub-exponential cardinality condition (Assumption 5) cleanly delineates tractable from intractable symmetry classes, encompassing non-crossing, non-nesting, and hierarchy-respecting partitions. Open questions include whether an adaptive algorithm can simultaneously achieve O(T^{2/3}) worst-case regret and O(√T) regret under well-separatedness without knowing the separation constant in advance (currently only O(T^{3/4}) is achieved), and whether convex relaxations can resolve the inherited NP-hardness of the model selection step.
