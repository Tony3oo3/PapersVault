## Problem

Bi-Lipschitzness is a valuable inductive bias for uncertainty estimation, invertible models, and inverse problems, yet existing architectures either impose soft regularization without theoretical guarantees, rely on layer-wise control that produces loose and overly conservative bounds, or lack proofs of expressive power. No prior model achieves a direct, tight, and simple control of the overall (not per-layer) bi-Lipschitz constants through a minimal number of hyperparameters while retaining provable universality.

## Input / Output

The forward pass takes a data vector x, an ICNN G_θ with parameters θ, and two non-negative scalars α and β, and produces a bi-Lipschitz-transformed vector f*(x) = y*(x) + αx of the same dimension as x. Unusually, computing y*(x) requires solving an inner strongly convex optimization (the Legendre-Fenchel transform) via steepest gradient descent with step size η_t = 1/(μ(t+1)), making the model an implicit/DEQ-style architecture rather than a standard feedforward network. In downstream tasks the transformed vector feeds into a classifier or uncertainty head, yielding class labels or OOD detection scores.

## Method

The core construction exploits the Legendre-Fenchel duality between strong convexity and smoothness: a 1/β-strongly convex ICNN F_θ(y) = G_θ(y) + (1/2β)||y||² is built, its convex conjugate F_θ* is β-smooth, and adding (α/2)||x||² yields a function that is simultaneously α-strongly convex and (α+β)-smooth, so its gradient is (α, α+β)-bi-Lipschitz by design. Only two scalar hyperparameters (α for inverse Lipschitzness, β for the Lipschitz gap) control the overall constants, eliminating the need for per-layer spectral normalization or orthogonalization. The LFT is evaluated by steepest gradient descent whose bi-Lipschitz behavior is characterized non-asymptotically (Theorem 3.5), and the backward pass avoids unrolling the optimization loop by applying an implicit-function-theorem identity (Theorem 3.7) that expresses the parameter gradient through the Hessian of F_θ at the fixed point. A partially bi-Lipschitz variant (PBLNN) replaces the ICNN with a PICNN to constrain bi-Lipschitzness on a subset of variables, improving scalability and expressive power while preserving the same two-parameter control.

## Results

On the Lipschitz tightness benchmark (fitting a piecewise-linear function with a discontinuity), BLNN achieves near-perfect tightness of 100.0%, 99.4%, and 99.9% at L=5, 10, and 50 respectively, while all seven baselines degrade substantially (e.g., BiLipNet drops to 6.8% at L=50, SN to 32.5%). On the Fashion-MNIST OOD detection task, DUQ+BLNN improves AUROC from 0.862 to 0.896 (vs MNIST) and from 0.900 to 0.964 (vs NotMNIST) over DUQ, while also increasing classification accuracy from 0.889 to 0.899. On monotone benchmark datasets, PBLNN is competitive with LMN and SMNN, achieving the best HeartDisease accuracy (90.2%) and AutoMPG MSE (7.13).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BLNN decisively outperforms all seven Lipschitz-construction baselines on the tightness benchmark: at L=50, it achieves 99.9% tightness versus 32.5% (SN), 14.6% (Orthogonal), 15.4% (AOL), 15.7% (SLL), 16.4% (Sandwich), 26.0% (LMN), and 6.8% (BiLipNet), demonstrating that direct parameterization via strong convexity avoids the compounding looseness of layer-wise approaches. On the Fashion-MNIST OOD detection task, DUQ+BLNN beats DUQ (Van Amersfoort et al., 2020) on every metric: accuracy 0.899 vs 0.889, AUROC vs MNIST 0.896 vs 0.862, and AUROC vs NotMNIST 0.964 vs 0.900, confirming that hard bi-Lipschitz constraints via architecture outperform soft gradient-penalty regularization. On monotone benchmarks, PBLNN matches or slightly exceeds LMN (Nolte et al., 2023) on COMPAS (69.4% vs 69.3%), LoanDefaulter (65.5% vs 65.4%), and HeartDisease (90.2% vs 89.6%), and outperforms SMNN (Kim and Lee, 2024) on HeartDisease (90.2% vs 88.0%) and AutoMPG (7.13 vs 7.44 MSE), while BlogFeedBack RMSE is slightly worse than SMNN (0.157 vs 0.150). On the two-moons uncertainty estimation task, DUQ+BLNN with (α,β)=(2,4) produces a tighter certainty region than both DUQ and deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017), with the advantage attributable to the direct control of the inverse Lipschitz constant preventing feature collapse.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the inverse Lipschitz architecture of Kinoshita et al. (2023), which controlled only the lower bound via strong convexity, to full bi-Lipschitzness by combining it with the Legendre-Fenchel duality: the LFT of a 1/β-strongly convex function is β-smooth, so adding a quadratic term at the output yields a function whose gradient is simultaneously α-inverse Lipschitz and (α+β)-Lipschitz. Unlike prior bi-Lipschitz models (i-ResNet, BiLipNet, LMN) that impose layer-wise constraints leading to loose overall bounds, this approach controls both constants through just two global parameters (α, β) without dissecting individual layers. The paper also identifies a structural equivalence between the BLNN forward pass and deep equilibrium models (DEQs), positioning it within that broader implicit-model family while noting the guaranteed convergence of the fixed-point iteration.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first bi-Lipschitz architecture that simultaneously achieves tight certified bounds, direct parameterization of both constants, and a universality theorem on expressive power — properties that no prior model satisfies jointly. The practical impact is demonstrated in uncertainty estimation (DUQ+BLNN outperforms DUQ on OOD detection) and monotone classification benchmarks, where the simple two-knob control enables principled hyperparameter selection. Key limitations remain: the LFT optimization in the forward pass is computationally expensive (mitigated but not eliminated by the PBLNN variant and Theorem 3.7 backward pass), and the model is restricted to gradients of convex functions, excluding rotation-type bi-Lipschitz maps. Open questions include scaling to very high-dimensional inputs, extending to non-gradient bi-Lipschitz functions via Hodge decomposition, and amortizing the LFT for real-time applications.
