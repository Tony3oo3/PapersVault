## Problem

Despite a large body of ICA algorithms (FASTICA, JADE, PFICA, PEGI, etc.), no single method works universally across all source distributions—cumulant-based methods fail on zero-kurtosis or heavy-tailed sources, characteristic-function methods struggle with small-probability Bernoulli sources, and entropy-based approximations can yield spurious solutions. The literature has lacked a data-driven diagnostic to select the best algorithm for a given dataset, which is the primary gap this paper addresses.

## Input / Output

The Meta algorithm takes a mixed signal matrix X ∈ R^{n×k} (n samples, k sources) and a list of candidate ICA algorithms as input. For each candidate, it extracts an estimated mixing matrix B_j and computes a corrected independence score by averaging |Δ(t, B_j^{-1}|P̂)| over t ~ N(0, I_k), where the score uses only the sample covariance S (no knowledge of the noise covariance Σ is required). The output is the candidate with the lowest score, along with its estimated B and the per-candidate scores. The score requires only a finite second moment of the data, making it applicable to heavy-tailed sources where higher-moment methods break down.

## Method

The paper introduces a corrected independence score that adapts the CHFICA characteristic-function objective to noisy ICA by subtracting Gaussian noise contributions using the sample covariance S, so that Δ(t,F|P)=0 iff F is a permutation-scaled version of B^{-1} (Theorem 1). Two new contrast functions are proposed: a CHF-based one (log of |E exp(iu^T x)|² + u^T S u) requiring only a finite second moment, and a CGF-based one (log E exp(u^T x) − ½ u^T S u), both of which vanish exactly for Gaussian data and satisfy additivity over independent components. These are optimized via a pseudo-Euclidean power iteration using a quasi-orthogonalization matrix C = BDB^T constructed from the Hessian of the contrast function. The Meta algorithm (Algorithm 1) runs all candidate algorithms, scores each solution with the independence score, and selects the best. Theoretical contributions include a uniform convergence bound for the empirical score (Theorem 2), a global convergence characterization showing that all fixed points of the constrained optimization are either maxima at source directions, minima, or saddle points (Theorem 3), and a local linear convergence guarantee for the power iteration (Theorem 4).

## Results

The primary metric is the Amari error (lower is better) for estimating the mixing matrix B. In Table 1 (Bernoulli(p) sources, k=5, n=10⁵, ρ=0.2), the Meta algorithm achieves median Amari errors ranging from 0.007 (high kurtosis) to 0.023 (zero kurtosis), matching or exceeding the best individual method at every kurtosis level. At zero kurtosis, PEGI and JADE fail catastrophically (errors 1.802 and 1.909), while Meta maintains 0.023. In the variance-reduction experiment (Figure 2c), using the independence score to select the best of 30 random initializations reduces the mean Amari error from 0.51 to 0.39 and the standard deviation from 0.51 to 0.34.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Meta algorithm is compared against four families of ICA methods: PEGI-κ₄ (cumulant-based, [49]), PFICA (characteristic-function-based, [11]), JADE (cumulant-based, [9]), and FastICA (negentropy/tanh-based, [27]). The Meta algorithm wins or ties at every kurtosis level: at high kurtosis it matches PEGI and CGF (0.007), at moderate kurtosis it matches CHF (0.010–0.011), and at zero kurtosis it dramatically outperforms PEGI (0.023 vs. 1.802) and JADE (0.023 vs. 1.909), which both fail. CHF and PFICA, the two characteristic-function-based methods, suffer at small p (kurtosis 994: errors 1.524 and 1.525 respectively) because the characteristic function is near-constant for rare events; the Meta algorithm avoids this by selecting CGF or PEGI instead. CGF degrades monotonically as kurtosis decreases (0.007 → 0.071) due to heavy-tailed sensitivity, while the Meta algorithm caps its worst-case error at 0.023. The Uncorrected Meta (without the noise correction term) performs identically to PFICA, confirming that the Gaussian-noise correction in the independence score is essential for reliable algorithm selection.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the noisy ICA literature initiated by Arora et al. [4] (quasi-orthogonalization) and Voss et al. [48, 49] (pseudo-Euclidean power iteration) by introducing a nonparametric independence score that evaluates any ICA solution using only the sample covariance, without requiring knowledge of the unknown noise covariance Σ. The corrected score adapts the CHFICA objective of Eriksson & Koivunen [21] by canceling Gaussian noise contributions via the data covariance S, and the new CHF/CGF contrast functions are optimized using the same pseudo-Euclidean power method from [49]. The key departure is treating algorithm selection as a first-class problem: rather than proposing yet another ICA algorithm, the paper provides a diagnostic (the Meta algorithm) that adaptively picks the best solution from a pool of candidates, addressing a gap the authors identify in the literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical value is clear: no single ICA algorithm dominates across all source distributions (kurtosis-based methods fail at zero kurtosis, characteristic-function methods fail for small-p Bernoulli sources, CGF fails under heavy tails), and this paper gives practitioners a principled, nonparametric way to choose. The theoretical contribution—characterizing the loss landscape in pseudo-Euclidean space and proving global/local convergence for a broad class of contrast functions—is the first such analysis for noisy ICA. Limitations include the fact that the independence score itself is not easily optimizable (motivating the Meta-algorithm workaround), the subgaussianity assumption in the uniform convergence theorem, and the fact that the Meta algorithm's performance is bounded by the quality of its candidate pool. Open questions include extending the score to sequential/deflation settings, relaxing the subgaussian assumption, and whether the framework generalizes to d > k with unknown Σ.
