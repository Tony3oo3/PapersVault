## Problem

Regret-based Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) methods rely on heuristic score functions (MaxMC, PVL) to approximate the intractable regret of a policy on a level. The paper shows these approximations do not correlate with actual regret but instead with success rate, causing agents to waste most of their training experience on already-mastered levels rather than on the learning frontier where improvement is possible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240512/cropped_page2_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

SFL takes the current policy π_φ and a set of N randomly sampled environment levels as input, rolling out the policy for L timesteps on each (without gradient computation) to estimate per-level success rates. It outputs a curated buffer of the top-K levels ranked by learnability p(1−p), which is then mixed with freshly generated random levels at ratio ρ during PPO training. In multi-agent settings, learnability is computed per-agent and summed across agents, and the buffer is refreshed every T PPO updates to track the shifting learning frontier.

## Method

The core contribution is Sampling For Learnability (SFL), which replaces flawed regret approximations with a direct empirical estimate of learnability: for each level, the agent's success rate p is measured via rollouts, and the level is scored by p(1−p) (the variance of a Bernoulli outcome), which peaks at p=0.5 and vanishes at p=0 or p=1. This avoids the noisy value-function estimation that corrupts MaxMC and PVL scores in partially observable settings. The training loop periodically (every T PPO updates) samples N random levels, computes learnability scores, retains the top-K in a buffer, and then trains on a mixture of buffer levels (fraction ρ) and fresh random levels. The paper also introduces a CVaR-based adversarial evaluation protocol that measures performance on the worst α% of 10,000 newly sampled solvable levels, providing a principled robustness metric that replaces arbitrary hand-designed holdout sets.

## Results

The paper's primary evaluation metric is the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) of success at various α levels, measuring worst-case performance over 10,000 sampled solvable levels. SFL achieves superior CVaR across a wide range of α values on all four domains (single-agent JaxNav, multi-agent JaxNav, Minigrid, and XLand-Minigrid), while all methods perform similarly at α=100% (mean success rate). On single-agent JaxNav, SFL matches the oracle 'Perfect Regret' method (which uses the true 1−p score restricted to solvable levels), confirming that the flawed regret approximations—not the underlying theory—are responsible for UED's underperformance. Domination heatmaps further show that SFL consistently solves more environments more often than every baseline, with the density below the diagonal indicating SFL's robustness advantage.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SFL outperforms Domain Randomisation (DR) on all four domains, with the gap most pronounced at low α (worst-case levels); on easier level subsets, DR becomes more competitive, particularly in Minigrid where deterministic dynamics make obstacle count the primary difficulty factor. Against Prioritised Level Replay (PLR, both PVL and MaxMC variants), SFL wins decisively on CVaR; buffer analysis (Table 5) reveals PLR buffers contain levels with mean learnability of only 0.01–0.02 and success rates of 0.84–0.98, confirming these methods prioritize already-mastered levels. ACCEL (MaxMC) is similarly outperformed, with its buffer showing even higher success rates (0.94–0.97) and near-zero learnability, indicating its mutation operator fails to maintain the learning frontier. The 'robust' variants (Robust PLR, Robust ACCEL), which use 2–3× more environment interactions and are 80%–2× slower, are also beaten by SFL, which uses fewer total compute. The oracle Perfect Regret (using true 1−p on solvable levels only) matches SFL's performance, providing strong evidence that the core issue is the score function rather than the buffer-based training paradigm itself.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits within the Unsupervised Environment Design (UED) lineage, which frames curriculum discovery as a two-player zero-sum game between a level generator and an agent. It builds directly on the buffer-based level selection architecture of Prioritised Level Replay (Jiang et al., 2021) and the learnability score p·(1-p) from ProCuRL (Tzannetos et al., 2023), but departs from the dominant regret-maximisation paradigm by showing that the practical regret approximations (MaxMC, PVL) used by PLR and ACCEL actually correlate with success rate rather than regret. SFL replaces these flawed proxies with a direct learnability estimate, and the paper also introduces JaxNav as a new hardware-accelerated robotic navigation benchmark and a CVaR-based adversarial evaluation protocol that goes beyond the standard hand-designed holdout sets.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The diagnostic finding that UED's core scoring functions are misaligned with their theoretical objective undermines the justification for the most widely used curriculum methods and suggests that much of the collected training experience is wasted on already-mastered levels. SFL's simplicity (a few lines of code on top of any RL algorithm) and its consistent outperformance across four domains make it a practical drop-in replacement, while the CVaR evaluation protocol offers a more rigorous and less arbitrary measure of robustness. Key limitations include restriction to deterministic binary-outcome domains, dependence on JAX's parallelism for the additional rollouts, and the open question of how to extend the learnability score to continuous-reward or vast environment spaces where random generation becomes infeasible.
