## Problem

The conditions under which classical optimization-based methods outperform deep learning-based methods (and vice versa) in deformable image registration remain unclear and are not explicitly outlined in the literature. Prior comparisons are confounded by instrumentation bias—suboptimal hyperparameters or early stopping of classical baselines—leading to inconsistent and often contradictory conclusions about the relative merits of each paradigm.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243438/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Both classical and DLIR methods take a pair of T1-weighted brain MRI volumes (fixed and moving) as input and produce a dense displacement field that warps the moving image into alignment with the fixed image. Evaluation is performed on community-standard neuroimaging datasets (OASIS, LPBA40, MGH10, IBSR18, CUMC12) acquired under different scanners, resolutions, and preprocessing protocols, with performance measured by Dice score over 35 subcortical structures. The paper distinguishes between an unsupervised image-matching objective (intensity similarity only) and a weakly-supervised label-matching objective that additionally enforces alignment of segmentation maps during training.

## Method

Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper performs a structured empirical and theoretical analysis to delineate the performance boundaries of classical versus DLIR registration. The key theoretical insight is that label-matching performance under an unsupervised image-matching objective is fundamentally bounded by the mutual information between per-pixel intensity and label maps, MI(i;s), and that the Jacobian projection ∂φ/∂θ in DLIR methods cannot exceed this bound. Empirically, the authors validate this with a Pearson correlation (r=0.886 linear, r=0.933 logarithmic) between ANTs Dice scores and MI across four brain datasets, then confirm that no unsupervised DLIR method (SynthMorph, LapIRN, SymNet, LKU-Net, TransMorph) outperforms classical baselines (ANTs, NiftyReg, Symmetric Log Demons, Greedy, FireANTs) on the OASIS dataset. When label-matching supervision is added, DLIR methods do surpass classical methods in-distribution, but this advantage vanishes under domain shift to other datasets, revealing that learned anatomical features do not confer generalization. The paper also quantifies instrumentation bias by re-evaluating classical baselines with recommended parameters, showing 2–10 Dice point underestimation in prior literature.

## Results

On the OASIS dataset, a strong correlation (r=0.886 linear, r=0.933 logarithmic) is found between per-pixel mutual information MI(i;s) and Dice score of classical registration, indicating that image matching can only achieve label alignment up to the information content of intensities. In the unsupervised setting, classical methods (Greedy, ANTs, NiftyReg, FireANTs) significantly outperform all DLIR baselines (p<0.01 for most pairs), with the top classical (Greedy) and top DLIR (TransMorph) achieving statistically indistinguishable performance (p=0.161). With label supervision added, supervised DLIR methods (LKU-Net: 0.904 mean Dice, TransMorph-Regular: 0.855) significantly outperform classical methods (~0.787), but this advantage does not transfer to other datasets (CUMC12, LPBA40, MGH10, IBSR18), where classical methods again dominate. The paper also documents a 2–10 Dice point instrumentation bias in prior literature, where classical baselines were underreported due to suboptimal hyperparameters.

## Baselines & Comparisons

In the unsupervised setting on OASIS, classical methods (Greedy, ANTs, NiftyReg, FireANTs, Symmetric Log Demons) significantly outperform all five DLIR baselines (SynthMorph, LapIRN, SymNet, LKU-Net, TransMorph) in pairwise t-tests (p<0.01 for most pairs), with the key finding that DLIR methods show no improvement on the training split over the validation split—indicating the Jacobian projection does not add label-matching information beyond MI(i;s). In the supervised setting, LKU-Net (0.904 mean Dice) and TransMorph-Regular (0.855) significantly outperform all classical methods (~0.787), but SymNet diverges under Dice loss and only marginally improves over its unsupervised counterpart. Across four external datasets (CUMC12, LPBA40, MGH10, IBSR18), FireANTs, Greedy, ANTs, and NiftyReg consistently outperform all DLIR methods, including their supervised variants trained on OASIS, revealing that the supervised DLIR advantage is dataset-specific and does not generalize. Among DLIR methods, SynthMorph (trained on synthetic data) shows the best cross-dataset robustness, while TransMorph is the strongest unsupervised DLIR baseline, matching the top classical method (Greedy) in performance. The paper also shows that prior literature underreported classical baselines by 2–10 Dice points due to instrumentation bias (suboptimal hyperparameters, early stopping), which had artificially inflated the apparent DLIR advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not propose a new registration algorithm but instead provides a structured, systematic evaluation that resolves the long-standing 'tug-of-war' between classical optimization-based methods (ANTs, NiftyReg, Demons, FireANTs) and deep learning-based methods (Voxelmorph, SymNet, TransMorph, LapIRN, SynthMorph, LKU-Net). Conceptually, it departs from prior DLIR papers that attributed performance gains to architectural design by introducing a mutual information bound: the per-pixel intensity-to-label mutual information sets a ceiling on label matching performance that no Jacobian projection (i.e., no network architecture) can exceed in the unsupervised setting. It also explicitly identifies and quantifies instrumentation bias in prior literature, where classical baselines were evaluated with suboptimal hyperparameters, leading to underrepresentation of their true performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper recalibrates practitioner expectations: classical methods remain the more robust and generalizable choice across domain shifts, while supervised DLIR methods offer a genuine but narrow advantage in label matching only within their training distribution. The observed performance hierarchy (Supervised DLIR ID > Classical > Supervised DLIR OOD) challenges the assumption that label supervision in DLIR transfers across datasets, implying that large labeled datasets do not guarantee robust generalization in registration. Open questions include whether hybrid methods, optimal transport formulations, or foundation-model approaches can break the mutual information ceiling while retaining domain invariance, and whether the findings extend beyond inter-subject brain MRI to other anatomies and modalities.
