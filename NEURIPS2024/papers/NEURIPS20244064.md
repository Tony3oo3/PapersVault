## Problem

Applying too many graph convolutions causes oversmoothing, where node representations converge to identical values and classification accuracy degrades. While empirical work has documented this phenomenon, there was no rigorous theoretical characterization of how many convolutions are safe or a principled correction that prevents degradation. This paper provides the first spectral analysis showing that removing the principal eigenvector from the convolution matrix eliminates oversmoothing while preserving exponential error reduction.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph with adjacency matrix A and n×m node feature vectors (drawn from a Gaussian mixture model in the theoretical setting) and produces binary or multi-class node labels via a linear (or quadratic) classifier. The key modification is applied to the adjacency matrix before convolution: the principal eigenvector component is subtracted, yielding corrected matrices \tilde{A} = A/d − (1/n)11ᵀ or \hat{A} = D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2} − (D^{1/2}11ᵀD^{1/2})/(1ᵀD1). The classifier threshold is fixed at zero in the 1-dimensional reduction, making the output invariant to the scaling of the convolved features.

## Method

The core mechanism is to subtract the rank-1 principal eigenvector projection from the standard GCN convolution matrix, so that repeated application projects features onto the second eigenvector (which captures class structure) rather than the all-ones vector. Under the contextual stochastic block model, the corrected matrix \tilde{A} decomposes as ηssᵀ + (1/d)R + d′11ᵀ, where ssᵀ is the rank-1 signal and R is a zero-mean noise matrix with spectral norm O(√(np)). Spectral analysis then shows each convolution reduces the feature-variance error term by a factor of (C/(γ√(np)))² until it saturates at the graph-signal floor O(1/(γ²d)), after which further convolutions neither help nor hurt. For exact classification, a combinatorial moment analysis bounds the message-passing error through incorrect paths, proving linear separability after O(log n / log log n) corrected convolutions under mild density and signal-strength assumptions. The analysis extends to the multi-class L-block setting where the second eigenspace has multiplicity L−1 and captures all cluster structure.

## Results

The paper's main contributions are theoretical: for partial classification (Theorem 4.1), each round of corrected convolution reduces misclassification error by a multiplicative factor of approximately 1/(γ²d) until saturation at O(1/(γ²d)), where γ = |p−q|/(p+q) is the graph signal strength and d is the expected degree. For exact classification (Theorem 4.2), the required feature signal-to-noise ratio for linear separability decreases exponentially with k convolutions, reaching the optimal bound after O(log n / log log n) rounds. Empirically, on synthetic CSBM data (n=2000, 20 features), the corrected GCN variants maintain high accuracy at low signal-to-noise ratios even at 16 convolutions, while the vanilla GCN degrades significantly. On real citation networks (CORA, CiteSeer, PubMed), corrected convolutions converge to a higher asymptotic accuracy than the uncorrected GCN as layers increase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the original GCN (Kipf and Welling, 2017) without principal eigenvector removal. On synthetic CSBM data (Figure 1, γ=2/3), the vanilla GCN (blue) shows a sharp drop in accuracy as the number of convolutions increases from 1 to 16, particularly at low signal-to-noise ratios, while both corrected variants — normalized (removing vvᵀ) and unnormalized (removing 11ᵀ) — retain near-optimal accuracy across all convolution depths. The same pattern holds when varying γ at fixed SNR=1 (Figure 2): the uncorrected GCN's accuracy degrades with more layers, whereas the corrected versions remain stable. On real multi-class benchmarks (Figure 3: CORA, CiteSeer, PubMed), all methods see accuracy decrease with more convolutions, but the corrected versions converge to a substantially higher plateau than the vanilla GCN. The paper notes that for multi-class problems, the second eigenvector only captures partial class structure, so the corrected method's advantage, while consistent, is less dramatic than in the binary setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the GCN framework of Kipf and Welling (2017) by proposing a corrected convolution matrix that removes the principal eigenvector component, and on the 1-dimensional CSBM reduction of Baranwal et al. (2021) for its analytical machinery. It departs from Wu et al. (2023), which rigorously characterized oversmoothing in vanilla convolutions by identifying competing mixing and denoising effects, by showing that the corrected convolution eliminates the mixing effect entirely, yielding exponential error reduction up to a saturation point rather than eventual degradation. The approach is conceptually related to PairNorm (Zhao and Akoglu, 2020) but differs in explicitly using the principal eigenvector with a full spectral justification rather than a heuristic normalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first rigorous theoretical guarantees showing that a simple spectral modification (removing one eigenvector) fundamentally changes the asymptotic behavior of deep graph convolutions from destructive oversmoothing to stable convergence toward class-relevant information. This gives practitioners a principled, low-cost fix for oversmoothing with clear quantification of how many layers are beneficial. Open questions include extending the analysis to unbalanced class sizes, non-Gaussian feature distributions, and settings where graph and feature clusters are misaligned, all of which would require more sophisticated architectures beyond the linear classifier analyzed here.
