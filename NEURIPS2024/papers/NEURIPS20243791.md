## Problem

Long video understanding with LLMs is bottlenecked by the enormous token count from dense video frames, forcing prior methods to rely on sparse temporal sampling or frame-wise compression that either discard long-range temporal dynamics or sacrifice spatial detail. Existing memory-bank approaches depend on explicit timestamps for retrieval, limiting their ability to answer questions without specific time indicators, while frame-compression pipelines like LLaMA-VID are question-dependent and cannot produce a general video representation reusable across diverse queries.

## Input / Output

The model takes an arbitrarily long video (sampled at 1 FPS into 16-frame clips at 224×224 resolution) and a free-form text question as input. It produces a natural-language answer along with an implicit temporal grounding: exactly V=4 clip memories (256 tokens total) are adaptively selected and fed to the LLM, so the same encoded video can answer multiple questions without re-encoding. The streaming encoder maintains a fixed-length memory (64 tokens per clip) regardless of video duration, and the final LLM input is always 256 visual tokens plus the question text.

## Method

VideoStreaming introduces two coupled mechanisms. First, Memory-Propagated Streaming Encoding segments the video into 16-frame clips and sequentially encodes each with a small decoder-only LM (Phi-2, first 16 of 32 layers), concatenating the previous clip's condensed memory (H_{k-1}) with current clip features and summarization tokens so that autoregressive attention accumulates long-range temporal context into a fixed 64-token representation per clip. A modified attention mask (prefix task) forces the LM to consolidate all clip information into the summarization tokens during training. Second, Adaptive Memory Selection computes a question indicator by passing the final global memory plus the question through the same LM, then uses Gumbel-Top-k over cosine similarities with per-clip indicator tokens to differentiably select the V=4 most relevant clip memories for the LLM (Vicuna-7B). Training is two-stage: single-clip encoding with a prefix task on 790K captions + 763K QA pairs, followed by joint end-to-end fine-tuning of ViT, streaming encoder, and LLM on ~340K long-video QA pairs with a KL-divergence warm-up for temporal grounding supervision.

## Results

On VideoChatGPT, VideoStreaming (7B+1.3B) achieves the best scores across all five metrics: CI=3.33, DO=3.27, CU=3.73, TU=2.74, CO=3.15, outperforming the strongest 13B baselines (LLaMA-VID, PLLaVA). On EgoSchema (zero-shot), it reaches 44.1% fullset accuracy, surpassing all zero-shot methods and matching the finetuned MC-ViT (44.4%). On MovieChat-1K, it achieves a dramatic 90.4% global accuracy (vs. 71.2% for MovieChat+) and 54.9% breakpoint accuracy. On MovieNet-QA (hour-long movies), it uses only 256 tokens with 5.32 s latency per question, compared to 5,477 tokens and ~10.4 s for LLaMA-VID/MovieLLM, while achieving higher overview (2.65), plot (3.13), and temporal (1.88) scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

VideoStreaming consistently outperforms all compared baselines across every benchmark. On VideoChatGPT, it beats the strongest 13B baselines (LLaMA-VID, PLLaVA) on all five metrics with only 8.3B parameters, with the largest margin in Temporal Understanding (2.74 vs. 2.58 for LLaMA-VID). On EgoSchema zero-shot, it surpasses LangRepo 8×7B (41.2%) by 2.9 points and is within 0.3 points of the finetuned MC-ViT (44.4%). On Next-QA zero-shot, it improves over LangRepo 8×7B by 5.3 points overall (66.2% vs. 60.9%), with particularly large gains in Temporal (+10.8%) and Descriptive (+9.0%) subsets. On MovieChat-1K, the global accuracy jump from 71.2% (MovieChat+) to 90.4% is the most striking result, while breakpoint accuracy also improves from 49.6% to 54.9%. On Next-GQA, it achieves the highest mIoP (32.2) and Acc@GQA (17.8) without any specialized grounding module, surpassing SeViLA (mIoP=29.5) which uses one. On MovieNet-QA, it matches or exceeds LLaMA-VID and MovieLLM on all three GPT-scored dimensions while using 21× fewer tokens (256 vs. 5,477) and achieving 2× lower inference latency (5.32 s vs. 10.43 s).

## Relation to Prior Work

VideoStreaming carves new ground at the intersection of three prior lines of work: (1) sparse-sampling and frame-compression VLMs (VideoChatGPT, LLaMA-VID) that reduce tokens but discard temporal dynamics; (2) memory-bank approaches (MovieChat, MC-ViT) that accumulate history via handcrafted rules but cannot be trained end-to-end; and (3) language-as-bridge pipelines (LangRepo, LLoVi) that generate per-clip captions for an LLM to aggregate, sacrificing end-to-end optimization. The paper's key departure is repurposing a small decoder-only language model (Phi-2) as a trainable streaming encoder whose autoregressive attention naturally consolidates clip information into fixed-length memory tokens, combined with a differentiable Gumbel-Top-k selection mechanism that retrieves question-relevant memories without re-encoding the video.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: feeding only 256 tokens to the LLM (vs. 5,000–18,000 for competitors) while matching or exceeding their accuracy, and enabling a single encoding pass to answer multiple questions without reprocessing the video, makes long-video understanding substantially cheaper and more deployable. The 90.4% global accuracy on MovieChat-1K (vs. 71.2% for MovieChat+) and 5.32 s per-question latency on hour-long movies demonstrate that the streaming-memory paradigm can handle very long contexts efficiently. Open questions include whether uniform clip sampling is optimal for videos with heterogeneous information density (acknowledged as a limitation), how the approach scales to multi-hour or multi-day streams, and whether the language-model-as-encoder insight generalizes beyond the specific Phi-2/Vicuna combination tested here.
