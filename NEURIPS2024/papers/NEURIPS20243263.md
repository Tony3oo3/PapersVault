## Problem

Synthesis-based data expansion methods using diffusion models generate novel content but risk producing samples that deviate from the real data distribution, degrading downstream classifier performance. Existing mitigations either require costly fine-tuning of the diffusion model (risking overfitting on small datasets) or apply post-hoc filtering that discards useful samples. The paper addresses how to guide diffusion-based generation to remain distribution-consistent with the original dataset in a training-free manner.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243263/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a reference image from the original dataset, a class-specific text prompt (e.g., 'A photo of a [class]'), and pre-computed hierarchical prototypes (class-level and group-level feature vectors extracted via a ResNet-50 encoder) as inputs. It produces 512×512 synthetic images using a 50-step DDIM sampling process with noise strength 0.5 and classifier-free guidance scale 7.5. The entire pipeline is training-free: no parameters of the Stable Diffusion 1.4 base model are updated, and the only learned component is the lightweight guidance encoder trained from scratch on the original dataset.

## Method

DistDiff constructs two levels of prototypes to approximate the real data distribution: class-level prototypes (mean feature per class) and group-level prototypes (K=3 sub-cluster centroids per class via agglomerative hierarchical clustering), capturing distribution structure at complementary scales. During diffusion sampling, at an intermediate denoising step M=20 (the semantic stage), the method applies a residual multiplicative transformation to the latent point with an ε-ball constraint, then predicts a clean estimate z_{0|t} from the noisy state. A composite energy function measuring the L2 distance from z_{0|t} to both the class prototype and the nearest group prototype is minimized via gradient descent on the transformation parameters (e, b) over 20 optimization steps. This energy guidance is applied at intermediate steps rather than only on the final output, which the ablations show is critical: the semantic stage is where the image structure is still malleable enough for meaningful distributional correction, while the early chaos stage lacks stable structure and the late refinement stage offers too little room for change.

## Results

On Caltech-101 with 5× data expansion, DistDiff achieves 83.09% top-1 accuracy (ResNet-50), surpassing the original dataset (66.71%) by 16.38% and the direct Stable Diffusion baseline by 6.25%. The method also reduces FID-3K from 72.56 (no guidance) to 67.72 (full hierarchical prototypes), confirming tighter distribution alignment. On fine-grained StanfordCars with a pre-trained ResNet-50, DistDiff yields a +3.56% accuracy gain over the original, and on PathMNIST with CLIP-ViT-B/32, it delivers a +5.18% improvement, demonstrating effectiveness under significant distribution shift.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DistDiff outperforms all synthesis-based baselines on Caltech-101: it beats direct Stable Diffusion by 6.25% and GIF-SD (which uses CLIP-guided class-maintained optimization plus KL-divergence diversity) by 5.46%. LECF, which relies on prompt enhancement and confidence-based filtering, fails to surpass the raw Stable Diffusion baseline, while DistDiff requires no post-hoc filtering. Against transformation-based methods, DistDiff with default augmentation (crop/flip/rotate) reaches 83.38%, exceeding AutoAug (74.34%), RandAug (74.07%), Random Erasing (74.22%), GridMask (73.88%), MixUp (78.64%), and CutMix (70.13%) by 3–15 points. In the appendix, DistDiff also surpasses DA-Fusion (79.14%) by 3.95% and DiffuseMix (82.33%) by 0.76% on the same 5× Caltech-101 protocol. Notably, DistDiff is compatible with transformation-based augmentations—combining it with CutMix yields 85.27%, the highest single configuration reported—indicating the two paradigms are complementary rather than competing.

## Relation to Prior Work

DistDiff builds on Stable Diffusion as its base generative model and adapts the residual multiplicative latent transformation from GIF-SD, but departs fundamentally from prior synthesis-based augmentation methods in where and how it intervenes. While LECF and GIF-SD treat the diffusion model as a black box—filtering or optimizing only the final generated output—DistDiff injects hierarchical energy guidance at intermediate denoising steps, using class-level and group-level prototypes from agglomerative clustering to steer the sampling trajectory toward the real data distribution. This positions it as a training-free alternative to fine-tuning-based approaches (DreamBooth, DiffFit) that avoid overfitting on small datasets, and as a distribution-aware upgrade to post-hoc filtering strategies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical bottleneck in data-scarce classification: synthetic samples that drift from the real distribution can degrade rather than help downstream models. By showing that 5× DistDiff expansion outperforms 20× expansion via AutoAugment or raw Stable Diffusion, it offers a compelling efficiency argument for distribution-aware generation over brute-force scaling. Limitations include the need for an auxiliary guide model (ResNet-50 trained from scratch), modest per-sample overhead, and evaluation primarily on small-to-medium datasets; open questions include whether the hierarchical prototype approach generalizes to video, 3D, or other modalities, and whether faster samplers (e.g., consistency models) can reduce the computational cost further.
