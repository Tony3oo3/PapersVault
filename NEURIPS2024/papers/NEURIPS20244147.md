## Problem

In mathematics, compactness principles (e.g., De Bruijn–Erdős for graph coloring, finite intersection property for compact sets) allow an infinite system to be fully characterized by its finite subsystems. No analogous exact compactness result was known for the sample complexity of supervised learning: it was unclear whether the transductive (or PAC) sample complexity of a hypothesis class H could be determined entirely by inspecting its finite projections, and whether such a characterization could hold exactly rather than up to asymptotic or constant factors.

## Input / Output

The learning instance consists of an unlabeled data sequence S = (x₁,…,xₙ) drawn from an arbitrary domain X, with one point xᵢ hidden and the labels of the remaining n−1 points revealed under a hypothesis h ∈ H. The learner outputs a prediction for xᵢ, and the transductive error is the average loss over the uniformly random choice of i. The compactness result then takes as its 'input' the entire hypothesis class H ⊆ Y^X (potentially infinite) and as its 'output' a certificate that H is learnable with sample complexity m, verified solely by checking that every finite projection H' ⊆ H|_X (for finite X ⊆ X) is learnable with the same m.

## Method

The central technical tool (Theorem 3.3) is a compactness theorem for variable-assignment problems: given infinitely many variables taking values in proper metric spaces and infinitely many proper functions each depending on finitely many variables, a global assignment keeping all functions below ε exists iff every finite subcollection admits such an assignment. The proof proceeds via Zorn's lemma over finitely completable partial assignments, with a key intermediate lemma (Lemma 3.4) showing that one can always assign an additional variable while preserving finite completability, using compactness of the relevant preimage sets. Transductive learning is then encoded as such a system: variables correspond to learner responses at each leave-one-out query, and functions correspond to hypotheses in H|_S, with the function output equal to the learner's error when that hypothesis is ground truth. This yields exact compactness for proper metric losses (Theorem 3.6) and continuous losses on compact spaces (Theorem 3.7); for improper metric losses, a tight factor-of-2 gap is established (Theorems 3.8–3.9), while the 0-1 loss recovers exact compactness via M. Hall's infinite matching theorem (Corollary 3.14). Results extend to agnostic learning (Theorem 3.16) and transfer to the PAC model up to a logarithmic factor in δ via the transductive–PAC equivalence of Asilis et al. [2024].

## Results

The paper establishes exact compactness for transductive learning: a hypothesis class H is learnable with transductive sample complexity m if and only if all of its finite projections are learnable with the same complexity m (Theorems 3.6–3.7), for proper metric losses (e.g., any norm on R^d) and continuous losses on compact spaces (e.g., cross-entropy, squared loss). For improper metric losses, the gap between the sample complexity of H and its finite projections is bounded by a factor of 2, with matching upper and lower bounds (Theorems 3.8–3.9). For the 0-1 loss (multiclass classification over arbitrary label sets), exact compactness is recovered via M. Hall's matching theorem (Corollary 3.14). Transferring to the PAC model via the transductive–PAC equivalence yields an almost-exact compactness result, with sample complexities differing by at most an O(log(1/δ)) factor (Corollary 3.20).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical experiments, datasets, or method comparisons against baselines. The 'comparisons' are between different theoretical settings: (i) proper metric losses yield exact compactness, while improper metric losses admit a factor-of-2 gap (tight); (ii) realizable and agnostic learning both enjoy exact compactness under proper/continuous losses, though the agnostic case with improper metric losses may admit larger gaps (conjectured); (iii) the transductive model gives exact compactness, while the PAC model inherits only approximate compactness (up to a logarithmic factor in δ). The paper also notes that requiring learners to be proper (restricted to H) breaks compactness entirely, per Ben-David et al. [2019], highlighting a structural distinction between proper and improper learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the classic marriage/matching theorems of P. Hall (1935) and M. Hall (1948) into a variable-assignment compactness principle (Theorem 3.3) that subsumes both supervised learning and bipartite matching as special cases. It builds on the transductive learning framework of Vapnik and Haussler et al. (1994, via the one-inclusion graph) and the recent γ-OIG dimension of Attias et al. (2023), which established qualitative compactness for realizable regression but did not tightly characterize sample complexity. The transductive-to-PAC transfer leverages the equivalence results of Asilis et al. (2024) and Dughmi et al. (2024), and the paper recovers M. Hall's infinite matching theorem as a direct corollary, unifying combinatorial and learning-theoretic compactness under one framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The exact compactness result (no asymptotic dilution, no constant factors) means that learnability and sample complexity of an infinite hypothesis class can be certified by checking only its finite projections — a powerful structural guarantee that could simplify future characterizations of learnability across broad loss-function classes. The matching factor-of-2 gap for improper metric losses (Theorems 3.8–3.9) is a clean negative result showing the properness assumption is essential, and the conjecture that larger gaps exist in the agnostic case opens a concrete direction. Open questions include whether the transductive-PAC gap in the agnostic setting can be tightened, whether compactness extends to non-proper/non-continuous losses, and whether analogous results hold in online or unsupervised learning.
