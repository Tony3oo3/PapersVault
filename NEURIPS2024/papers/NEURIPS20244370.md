## Problem

Existing complex instruction-following benchmarks model only simple coordination (And) between constraints, neglecting the structural composition types—Chain (sequential task completion) and Selection (conditional branch execution)—that are essential in real-world complex instructions. This omission leads to both incomplete dataset coverage and biased evaluation, since simple averaging of per-constraint scores ignores the dependency structure that composition types impose among constraints.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes a complex instruction containing multiple constraints organized under one or more composition types (And, Chain, Selection, or nested combinations) and a model-generated response as input. For each constraint dimension and composition type in the instruction, a manually crafted yes/no scoring question is posed; the evaluation produces per-question binary verdicts that are then aggregated via a dependency graph (where, e.g., all questions in a later Chain task depend on the preceding task's questions, and all questions in a Selection branch depend on correct branch selection) into a single DRFR score in [0, 1].

## Method

COMPLEXBENCH introduces a hierarchical taxonomy of 4 constraint types (Lexical, Format, Semantic, Utility) spanning 19 fine-grained dimensions, combined with 4 composition types (Single, And, Chain, Selection) that can be nested to arbitrary depth. The evaluation uses a Rule-Augmented LLM-based (RAL) protocol: for rule-verifiable scoring questions (e.g., word count, keyword inclusion, JSON validity), an LLM first extracts the relevant segment from the response, then a deterministic rule computes the verdict; for open-ended questions (e.g., topic relevance, sentiment), the LLM judge answers directly. Crucially, the final score is not a simple average but a dependency-based aggregation: each scoring question's result is AND-ed with the results of all questions it depends on (as determined by the composition structure), so a failure in a prerequisite constraint cascades to invalidate all downstream constraints. The benchmark comprises 1,150 manually constructed instructions and 5,306 scoring questions, with a Selection Branch Expansion step ensuring uniform coverage of all conditional branches.

## Results

On COMPLEXBENCH, GPT-4-1106 achieves the highest overall DRFR of 0.800, still failing ~20% of complex instructions. Performance degrades sharply with composition complexity: GPT-4 drops from 0.881 (And, depth 1) to 0.626 (Selection, depth ≥3) and 0.694 (Chain, depth ≥3). Among open-source models, Llama-3-70B-Instruct leads at 0.757 DRFR, while the weakest model (ChatGLM3-6B-Chat) scores only 0.546. The proposed RAL evaluation method achieves 87.82% question-level agreement with human annotators, substantially outperforming Direct Scoring (75.18%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Direct Scoring baseline (MT-Bench-style 1–10 scoring [5]), the proposed RAL method improves question-level human agreement from 75.18% to 87.82% and pairwise agreement from 0.512 to 0.614, with Dependency Aggregation contributing an additional +0.040 pairwise gain. In cross-benchmark comparisons (Table 7), COMPLEXBENCH rankings do not fully align with IFEval, HumanEval, or MATH: Llama-3-70B-Instruct surpasses GPT-4-1106 on IFEval (78.9 vs. 75.4) yet trails it on COMPLEXBENCH (0.757 vs. 0.800), and ChatGLM3-6B-Chat ranks competitively on HumanEval (64.0) and MATH (25.7) among 6–8B models but scores only 0.546 on COMPLEXBENCH. Pearson correlations between COMPLEXBENCH and the other benchmarks are moderate (IFEval 0.814, HumanEval 0.715, MATH 0.895), confirming that COMPLEXBENCH captures a distinct dimension of LLM capability—particularly around multi-constraint composition (Chain, Selection, nested structures)—that existing benchmarks like IFEval do not cover.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of instruction-following benchmarks (WizardLM, CELLO, FollowBench, IFEval, InfoBench) by introducing constraint *composition*—how constraints are structurally combined (And, Chain, Selection, and nested variants)—as a first-class evaluation dimension that prior benchmarks largely ignored, restricting themselves to simple coordination (And). It adopts the DRFR metric from InfoBench [7] for score aggregation but augments it with a dependency-aware aggregation that respects the compositional structure. The evaluation methodology draws from both LLM-as-judge paradigms and rule-based verification, combining them into a Rule-Augmented LLM-based (RAL) approach, while the composition taxonomy is informed by compositionality research in semantic parsing, machine translation, and data-to-text generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COMPLEXBENCH reveals that even GPT-4 fails roughly 20% of complex instructions, with performance dropping sharply as composition complexity increases—particularly for Selection and Chain types. The coherent test showing GPT-4 achieves only 14.9% on multi-layer nested Selection, and the finding that instruction decomposition *worsens* performance due to cumulative errors, highlight a fundamental gap in current LLM capabilities that existing benchmarks (e.g., IFEval, which covers only And-composition) cannot detect. Open questions include whether the benchmark generalizes beyond Chinese-origin instructions, whether the GPT-4-as-judge dependency introduces systematic biases, and whether future models can learn to handle tree-structured conditional instructions without decomposition.
