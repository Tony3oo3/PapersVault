## Problem

Existing functional causal model (FCM) methods for global causal discovery face a fundamental trade-off: they either impose strong parametric assumptions on noise or functional form, or rely on high-dimensional nonparametric regressions that scale poorly and require large sample sizes. Moreover, current FCM-based approaches discover causal structure bottom-up from leaves, using global conditioning sets that grow with graph size, making them ill-suited for sparse high-dimensional graphs common in applications like genomics.

## Input / Output

The method takes i.i.d. observational samples of d continuous variables (tabular data) as input. LHTS and NHTS each produce a hierarchical topological sort — a layering of vertices where each layer contains vertices at the same maximum directed-path distance from the roots — which encodes more causal structure than a flat linear ordering. ED takes a topological ordering (linear or hierarchical) plus the same observational samples and outputs the full set of parent sets, i.e., the directed edges of the causal DAG. All three algorithms are nonparametric in their independence tests and require no parametric assumptions on the functional form of causal mechanisms.

## Method

The paper introduces three complementary algorithms that exploit local causal substructures in a top-down fashion. LHTS (linear setting) classifies every vertex pair into one of four active causal ancestral path relations (AP1–AP4) using marginal independence tests and sequential univariate regressions, then peels off layers via a recursive ancestor-sort subroutine to produce a compact hierarchical sort. NHTS (nonlinear setting) shifts from ancestral to parent-child relations, identifying a superset of roots via local nonparametric regressions (PP2 detection) and pruning non-roots using d-separation asymmetry, then recovering remaining layers by regressing unsorted vertices onto all sorted vertices and selecting those with independent residuals. ED prunes spurious edges from any topological ordering using a local conditioning set Z_ij consisting only of potential confounders and mediators between a candidate parent–child pair, rather than all potential ancestors, enabling a nonparametric conditional independence test with far fewer variables. The key architectural insight is that searching from roots downward, combined with local (rather than global) conditioning sets, reduces both the number and dimensionality of regressions needed, yielding polynomial worst-case complexity and lower sample complexity than prior methods like RESIT and NoGAM.

## Results

On synthetic Erdős–Rényi DAGs (d=10, n=300, quadratic mechanisms), NHTS achieves the highest median A_top across all three noise distributions (Gaussian, Laplace, Uniform), outperforming all six baselines. For edge pruning, ED achieves the highest median F1 score in all trials (n=300, d=10, uniform noise), significantly outperforming CAM-pruning, Lasso, and RESIT. LHTS demonstrates asymptotic correctness, reaching near-perfect A_top at n=2000, and produces hierarchical sorts with roughly 70% fewer layers than DirectLiNGAM. NHTS runs up to 4× faster than NoGAM, and ED is significantly faster than both CAM-pruning and RESIT (RESIT was excluded from higher-dimensional tests due to runtime issues).

## Baselines & Comparisons

For linear topological sorting, LHTS consistently achieves lower A_top than DirectLiNGAM but encodes substantially more causal structure (~70% fewer layers), while both significantly outperform the heuristic R²-Sort. For nonlinear topological sorting, NHTS outperforms all six baselines (GES, GRaSP, GSP, DirectLiNGAM, NoGAM, R²-Sort) on median A_top across Gaussian, Laplace, and Uniform noise, and is up to 4× faster than NoGAM. For edge pruning, ED significantly outperforms CAM-pruning, Lasso regression, and RESIT on median F1 in all trials; ED is slower than Lasso but much faster than the other nonlinear methods, with RESIT failing to scale beyond low dimensions. The paper notes that PC and RESIT were excluded from the nonlinear sorting comparison because they perform much worse than the other baselines [20].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of FCM-based causal discovery (LiNGAM, DirectLiNGAM, RESIT, NoGAM) and constraint-based methods (PC, GES), departing from both by adopting a top-down (root-to-leaf) strategy rather than the bottom-up (leaf-to-root) approach used by RESIT and NoGAM. It builds directly on DirectLiNGAM's root-identification logic and Peters et al.'s residual independence framework for nonlinear ANMs, but restructures the search around local ancestral and parent-child substructures to reduce conditioning set sizes and the number of high-dimensional regressions. The edge discovery algorithm ED introduces a local sufficient conditioning set (confounders + mediators only) that is smaller than the full ancestor set used by traditional sparse regression pruning, drawing on d-separation logic from the constraint-based tradition.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a genuine scalability bottleneck in FCM-based causal discovery: high-dimensional nonparametric regressions that do not scale to large or sparse graphs common in genomics and healthcare. The hierarchical sort encoding more causal structure than a linear ordering, combined with the local conditioning set for edge discovery, offers a principled path toward polynomial-time global discovery under ANM assumptions. However, validation is limited to synthetic Erdős-Rényi DAGs with specific noise distributions, and the methods do not yet handle unmeasured confounding, mixed linear/nonlinear mechanisms, or provide finite-sample guarantees—key open questions for real-world applicability.
