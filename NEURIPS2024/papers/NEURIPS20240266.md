## Problem

MIL explanation methods in histopathology are limited to small bag sizes, disregard instance interactions, or cannot distinguish positive from negative evidence, making them unreliable for complex tasks like biomarker prediction. No formal framework existed to define what properties a good MIL explanation should satisfy, leaving practitioners without principled guidance for interpreting heatmaps.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240266/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a trained MIL model (AttnMIL or TransMIL) operating on bags of 256×256 patch features extracted at 20× magnification via the frozen CTransPath foundation model, along with the model's scalar prediction score. It produces per-instance relevance scores that are real-valued (positive or negative), summing to the prediction via conservation, and are rendered as a zero-centered red-blue heatmap where red indicates evidence supporting and blue evidence contradicting the predicted class. Unlike attention scores which are bounded in [0,1], the relevance scores can be negative, enabling the distinction between supportive and refuting tissue regions.

## Method

xMIL-LRP adapts layer-wise relevance propagation to MIL by backpropagating the prediction score through the aggregation module to the instance feature vectors, using the AH-rule for attention layers (treating attention scores as constant weightings during backpropagation), the LRP-ε rule for linear+ReLU layers, and the LN-rule for LayerNorm. The instance-level evidence score is the sum of feature-level relevance scores, yielding a context-sensitive decomposition that jointly considers all instances in the bag. This design satisfies three key properties: context sensitivity (relevance flow captures inter-instance dependencies), positive/negative evidence distinction (real-valued scores with zero for irrelevant instances), and conservation (instance scores sum exactly to the prediction). The method is architecture-agnostic, requiring no model modifications, and scales linearly with bag size, making it feasible for the 10,000+ patch bags typical in whole-slide histopathology.

## Results

On four real-world histopathology datasets (CAMELYON16, TCGA NSCLC, TCGA HNSC HPV, TCGA LUAD TP53), xMIL-LRP achieves the lowest AUPC (faithfulness) scores across both AttnMIL and TransMIL architectures, with the largest margins on the challenging biomarker prediction tasks (e.g., AUPC 0.24 vs. 0.73 for 'single' on LUAD TP53 with TransMIL). On the three toy datasets, xMIL-LRP attains the highest AUPRC-2 scores in 7 of 9 model-dataset combinations, reaching 0.91–0.92 on 4-Bags and Pos-Neg. Model test AUROCs range from 0.71 (AttnMIL on LUAD TP53) to 0.96 (TransMIL on NSCLC), confirming that the explanation quality gains are not driven by model performance differences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

xMIL-LRP significantly outperforms all baselines on AUPC across all four histopathology datasets and both architectures. Against attention scores (the most widely used MIL explanation), xMIL-LRP achieves substantially lower AUPC on every dataset except CAMELYON16 where attention is competitive (0.65 vs. 0.51 for AttnMIL); on the harder biomarker tasks, attention scores are nearly as unfaithful as random (AUPC ≈ 0.94–1.00). Integrated Gradients collapses catastrophically on TransMIL, yielding AUPC ≈ 1.0 on NSCLC, HNSC HPV, and LUAD TP53, while xMIL-LRP maintains AUPC of 0.45, 0.75, and 0.24 respectively. The 'single' perturbation method is competitive on simpler AttnMIL tasks (e.g., 0.34 vs. 0.31 on LUAD TP53) but degrades sharply with TransMIL on biomarker prediction (0.92 on HNSC HPV vs. 0.75 for xMIL-LRP). G×I is consistently outperformed by xMIL-LRP, particularly on TransMIL where it shows high variance (AUPC 0.66–0.87). AddMIL's intrinsic logits are not competitive because the model itself achieves lower test AUROC (e.g., 0.77 vs. 0.92 on Adjacent Pairs), confirming that architectural constraints for interpretability come at a performance cost.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) to the multiple instance learning setting, building directly on the LRP framework of Bach et al. and Montavon et al., and specifically on the AH-rule for attention propagation introduced by Ali et al. (ICML 2022). It departs from the standard MIL formulation (binary instance labels with a max-aggregation bag label) by introducing xMIL, a more general framework that replaces instance labels with context-aware, signed evidence scores. The paper positions itself against the dominant practice in histopathology MIL of using raw attention scores as explanations, as well as against perturbation-based methods (Early et al.) and architecturally constrained additive MIL (Javed et al.), arguing that all of these fail to capture instance interactions and positive/negative evidence simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first systematic, quantitative evaluation of MIL explanation methods on real histopathology datasets, demonstrating that attention scores—the de facto standard in the field—are unfaithful for all but the simplest tumor detection tasks. The xMIL-LRP method and the xMIL framework offer a principled path toward trustworthy model debugging and, more ambitiously, scientific knowledge discovery from histopathology models (e.g., identifying visual correlates of HPV status or TP53 mutation). Open questions include whether the approach extends to multi-modal inputs, whether the evidence function can be learned end-to-end rather than estimated post-hoc, and how to validate explanations at scale in clinical deployment settings.
