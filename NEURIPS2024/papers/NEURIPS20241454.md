## Problem

Compound AI systems that aggregate multiple LM calls (e.g., Gemini's CoT@32, AlphaCode 2) achieve state-of-the-art results, yet there is no systematic understanding of how the number of LM calls affects their performance. This paper initiates the study of inference-time scaling for compound systems, revealing the surprising phenomenon that performance can first increase and then decrease as more LM calls are made, contradicting the intuitive expectation of monotonic improvement.

## Input / Output

The system takes a multiple-choice text query and a budget K of LM calls, generating K candidate answers (optionally filtered by an LM judge that labels each as correct or wrong) before selecting the final answer via majority vote. The analytical scaling model is fit from performance measurements at a small set of K values (e.g., 2, 5, 10, 50, 100) and then predicts accuracy at arbitrary K, enabling practitioners to identify the optimal number of calls without exhaustive search. The framework is restricted to tasks with a small, discrete answer space that supports majority voting, such as multiple-choice QA and fact verification.

## Method

The paper analyzes two compound system designs—Vote (majority vote over K generations) and Filter-Vote (LM-filtered majority vote)—and introduces a formal query difficulty indicator d(x) that partitions queries into easy (d(x)<0) and hard (d(x)>0) based on whether infinitely many LM calls converge to the correct answer. The key theoretical result (Theorem 2) shows that non-monotonic scaling emerges when a dataset mixes easy and hard queries: more calls improve easy queries but degrade hard ones, producing inverse-U or U-shaped performance curves depending on the difficulty distribution parameters (p₁, p₂, α). For the binary answer space case, an exact closed-form is derived via the regularized incomplete beta function (Theorem 3), and a general exponential scaling model G(K,x) is proposed for arbitrary answer spaces. A closed-form expression for the optimal K* is derived (Theorem 4), and the model is validated empirically on four real-world datasets using GPT-3.5-turbo, showing accurate prediction of both performance curves and optimal call counts.

## Results

The paper's central empirical finding is that Vote and Filter-Vote exhibit non-monotonic scaling: on AVERITEC, both systems' accuracy first increases then decreases as LM calls grow from 2 to 1000, and on MMLU PHYSICS, Filter-Vote shows a U-shape (initial decrease then increase). The proposed analytical scaling model G(K,D) accurately predicts empirical performance across all four real-world datasets (MMLU PHYSICS, TRUTHFULQA, GPQA, AVERITEC), with prediction accuracy on Vote higher than on Filter-Vote. On the synthetic bi-level difficulty dataset, the analytically predicted optimal number of LM calls (Theorem 4) exactly matches the empirically observed optimum across all 15 tested difficulty configurations (Table 2). Notably, on TRUTHFULQA, Vote's peak accuracy exceeds Filter-Vote's peak, showing that adding a filter does not guarantee better performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against external baseline methods; instead, it contrasts two compound system designs (Vote and Filter-Vote) and validates an analytical scaling model against empirical performance. Vote and Filter-Vote show different non-monotonic shapes depending on the dataset: on MMLU PHYSICS, Vote peaks then declines while Filter-Vote initially drops then recovers; on AVERITEC, both show an inverse-U shape. Importantly, Filter-Vote does not uniformly dominate Vote — on TRUTHFULQA, Vote's best accuracy exceeds Filter-Vote's best, demonstrating that the filter can hurt performance on certain query distributions. The analytical scaling model G(K,D) is validated by fitting on 5 data points (K = 2, 5, 10, 50, 100) and predicting performance at 100 random K values; the correlation is strong across all four real-world datasets, with Vote predictions slightly more accurate than Filter-Vote predictions due to the latter's added complexity. On the synthetic dataset, the closed-form optimal K from Theorem 4 matches the empirically observed optimum exactly in all 15 tested configurations, confirming the theoretical analysis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by applying scaling-law analysis to inference-time compute in compound AI systems, a dimension orthogonal to the well-studied training-time scaling laws (Kaplan et al. 2020, Bahri et al. 2021). It takes existing compound system designs—majority voting (as in Gemini's CoT@32 and self-consistency) and filter-then-vote (as in AlphaCode 2)—and asks a question the authors explicitly note has not been systematically studied: how does the number of LM calls affect aggregate performance? Rather than proposing a new architecture or training method, the paper repurposes the scaling-law framework to the inference-time setting and introduces a query-difficulty decomposition to explain non-monotonic behavior.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway—that practitioners should not blindly scale up LM calls and can instead predict the optimal number from a small sample—directly affects cost and latency decisions in production compound systems. However, the scope is narrow: only two system designs (Vote, Filter-Vote), only objective multiple-choice tasks, and only GPT-3.5 are studied. Open questions include extending to open-ended generation, predicting query difficulty without querying the LM, and incorporating cost/latency into the optimization. The theoretical results also rely on a simplified binary-answer setting, and the generalization to larger answer spaces is an approximation rather than an exact result.
