## Problem

Classical causal effect identification methods (do-calculus, ID algorithm) treat all hidden variables as having arbitrary conditional distributions, missing the opportunity to exploit qualitative knowledge that certain variables are functionally determined by their parents. This paper shows that such functional dependencies can render unidentifiable causal effects identifiable and can reduce the number of variables that must be observed, but no systematic framework existed for testing identifiability under these constraints.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242760/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a causal graph (DAG), a designated set of observed variables, a set of positivity constraints (e.g., strict positivity Pr(V)>0 or weaker per-variable constraints), and a set of functional variables whose CPTs are known to be deterministic but whose specific functions are unknown. It outputs a binary identifiability determination, an identifying formula (a function of the observational distribution Pr(V)) when the effect is F-identifiable, and a reduced causal graph from which the formula is derived. A key subtlety is that positivity constraints and functional dependencies can contradict each other, so the method explicitly handles their interaction through consistency and separability conditions.

## Method

The core mechanism is functional elimination: removing a functional variable X from a DAG by adding edges from each parent of X to each child of X, then deleting X. Unlike classical projection, this operation preserves D-separation (a refined independence notion that accounts for functional variables), yielding a projected graph with more independencies visible to existing algorithms. The paper proves that eliminating hidden functional variables always preserves F-identifiability (Theorem 13), while eliminating observed functional variables requires them to be non-treatment/non-outcome, separable from positivity constraints, and to have observed parents (Theorem 15). By iteratively applying these eliminations followed by classical projection, the problem reduces to standard identifiability testable by the ID algorithm or do-calculus. A complementary technique (Theorem 18) reduces F-identifiability to identifiability by pretending hidden functional variables are observed, operating under only the weakest positivity constraints (Pr(X)>0 for treatments).

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental benchmarks or quantitative metrics. The key results are a series of theorems establishing when functional dependencies render unidentifiable causal effects identifiable: Theorem 13 shows that eliminating hidden functional variables preserves F-identifiability; Corollary 14 reduces F-identifiability to classical identifiability (via the ID algorithm) when all functional variables are hidden; Theorem 15 extends elimination to observed functional variables under separability conditions; and Theorem 18 provides a complete reduction under the weakest positivity constraint Pr(X)>0. The paper demonstrates through worked examples (Figures 3–4) that causal effects declared unidentifiable by the classical project-ID pipeline become identifiable once functional dependencies are incorporated.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not run empirical comparisons against competing methods; instead, it contrasts its theoretical framework with classical identifiability tools. The primary 'baseline' is the project-ID pipeline (classical projection followed by the ID algorithm [10]), which the paper shows to be incomplete in the presence of functional dependencies: in the Figure 3 example, project-ID returns FAIL while the functional-projection-based approach yields a valid identifying formula. Functional projection (Definition 12) is shown to produce a strictly sparser graph than classical projection (Figure 2b vs. 2d), exposing additional independencies (D-separations) that classical d-separation misses. The do-calculus [2] and ID algorithm [10] are not replaced but rather reused as downstream tools after the functional elimination/projection preprocessing step. The paper also notes that Theorem 18, while more general, cannot fully exploit the ID algorithm due to its stronger positivity assumptions, suggesting a gap that future work could address.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical causal effect identification framework (Pearl's do-calculus, the ID algorithm of Shpitser & Pearl, and the projection operation of Verma/Tian) by incorporating qualitative functional dependencies as additional structural knowledge. It follows the direction of Tikka et al. (2019), who showed that context-specific independence can render unidentifiable effects identifiable, but targets a different type of qualitative knowledge. The key technical contribution is a new elimination operation (functional elimination) and a refined separation criterion (D-separation) that are stronger than classical projection and d-separation, enabling the reuse of existing identification algorithms like ID on functionally-projected graphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is twofold: (1) unidentifiable causal effects can become identifiable with only qualitative knowledge that certain variables are deterministic functions of their parents, and (2) some functional variables need not be observed at all, reducing data collection costs or privacy concerns. A notable limitation is that the elimination-based method is complete only when observed functional variables satisfy specific conditions (separability, observed parents, not being treatments/outcomes), and Theorem 18's full bidirectional guarantee requires identifiability algorithms operating under the weakest possible positivity constraints, which do not yet exist. Open questions include extending the framework to quantitative (known) functional dependencies and to settings with multiple types of qualitative knowledge simultaneously.
