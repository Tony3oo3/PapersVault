## Problem

Scaling Mixture of Experts to tens of thousands of experts for fine-grained specialization is blocked by a fundamental trade-off: dense MoEs incur prohibitive inference costs, while the dominant sparse (top-K) MoE paradigm suffers from non-differentiable routing, training instability, and parameter inefficiency at large expert counts. No existing MoE formulation simultaneously achieves differentiability, parameter efficiency, and FLOP efficiency when expert counts grow large.

## Input / Output

The μMoE layer consumes a per-token hidden representation vector z ∈ R^I (e.g., a ViT patch embedding or a GPT-2 token embedding) and produces an output vector y ∈ R^O, with expert coefficients computed via entmax over a gating projection. In the full architectures, RGB images (224×224 for ImageNet/CelebA) or text sequences (100-token windows for GPT-2) are processed through stacks of μMoE blocks replacing standard MLPs, yielding class labels, multi-label attribute predictions, or generated text respectively. The layer is drop-in replaceable for any linear/MLP block, requiring only the input and output dimensions plus a chosen expert count N and factorization rank.

## Method

The μMoE layer parameterizes N experts' weight matrices as a single tensor W ∈ R^{N×I×O} and computes a sparse convex combination of all N experts' affine transformations, gated by entmax-activated coefficients. The key insight is that this dense computation over all N experts simultaneously can be executed entirely in factorized form without ever materializing W: the CP μMoE variant imposes a rank-R CP decomposition (reducing parameters from NIO to R(N+I+O)), while the TR μMoE variant uses a Tensor Ring factorization with ranks R1, R2, R3 where N enters the FLOP count only through R1·N·R2, enabling even greater efficiency at large N. Both variants yield closed-form fast forward passes expressed as a few matrix-vector products and outer products over the factor matrices/cores. Unlike sparse MoEs, the entmax gating is fully differentiable, eliminating the need for load-balancing losses or stochastic gradient masking, and the layer recovers dense MoEs as a special case when the weight tensor is materialized.

## Results

On ImageNet1k, parameter-matched TR μMoE MLP-Mixer S-16 achieves 71.26% validation accuracy (vs. 70.31% for the MLP baseline), and fine-tuned CLIP ViT-B-32 with a TR μMoE final layer reaches 78.71% (vs. 77.99% MLP). On OpenWebText, GPT-2 with CP μMoE blocks achieves 2.893 validation loss, marginally behind the MLP baseline of 2.876. Crucially, μMoE layers with N=256 experts require only 57M MLP parameters versus 14.5B for dense/sparse MoEs at the same expert count, while maintaining comparable accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the MLP-Mixer S-16 baseline [Tolstikhin et al., 2021], both μMoE variants improve ImageNet1k validation accuracy by ~1% (71.26–71.29% vs. 70.31%) at matched parameter counts. Against the GPT-2 NanoGPT baseline [Radford et al., 2019], μMoE blocks are slightly worse on OpenWebText validation loss (2.886–2.893 vs. 2.876), a gap of less than 0.02 nats. Against fine-tuned CLIP ViT-B-32 [Radford et al., 2021], TR μMoE improves ImageNet1k accuracy by 0.72% (78.71% vs. 77.99%). Against dense/sparse MoEs [Shazeer et al., 2017], μMoEs are dramatically more parameter-efficient: 57M vs. 14.5B MLP parameters for GPT-2 with N=256 experts (a 254× reduction). For the CelebA fairness task, the proposed expert thresholding method outperforms both adversarial debiasing [Alvi et al., 2018] and blind thresholding [Hardt et al., 2016] on most fairness metrics (e.g., target subpopulation accuracy of 0.866 vs. 0.424 and 0.843 for the 'Old females' age bias experiment), while retaining higher overall test accuracy than blind thresholding.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Mixture of Experts lineage from Jacobs et al. (1991) and the sparse top-K gating of Shazeer et al. (2017) by proposing a dense, differentiable MoE whose forward pass is computed entirely in factorized form via CP or Tensor Ring decompositions. It departs from the dominant sparse MoE paradigm (which suffers from non-differentiable routing and training instability) and from the DFC layer of Babiloni et al. (2023), which also uses CP decomposition but applies an MLP to input vectors rather than taking a sparse convex combination of explicit expert factors—thereby preserving the interpretability and editability of individual experts. The work also draws on tensor factorization literature for parameter-efficient training and multi-task learning, but uniquely leverages the factorization to scale expert count to tens of thousands while keeping FLOPs and parameters at the level of a single dense MLP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The μMoE layer offers a concrete path toward interpretable, editable foundation models by making fine-grained expert specialization computationally tractable at scale, demonstrated through counterfactual intervention and manual bias correction on CelebA. However, all quantitative results are limited to models of roughly 100M parameters, and expert specialism in large pre-trained models is shown only qualitatively, leaving open whether the approach scales to billion-parameter LLMs without degrading performance. A key open question is how to objectively quantify fine-grained (sub-class) expert specialism beyond the ImageNet class-level proxy, and whether the low-rank constraint on expert matrices becomes a bottleneck at larger scales.
