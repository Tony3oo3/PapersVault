## Problem

Node individualization schemes (e.g., random noise, relational pooling) are a standard way to make message-passing GNNs universally expressive, but their sample complexity—how much training data is needed for generalization—remained uncharacterized. This paper bridges the gap between GNN expressivity theory and statistical learning by deriving VC-dimension and covering-number bounds for GNNs endowed with various individualization schemes, revealing that equivariant schemes generalize far more efficiently than randomized ones.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph with finite node labels (and, for substructure identification, a query pattern P of radius Δ_P) plus a pseudo-random seed ω that drives the relabeling function. The relabeling produces an individualized (or k-weakly individualized) graph that is fed to a message-passing GNN, which outputs a binary class score in [0,1] for graph classification or a per-ego-net binary indicator aggregated by max-pooling for substructure detection. Graphs are bounded in size (|V| ≤ n_max), and the GNN depth is set to n_max to guarantee full WL-equivalent expressivity.

## Method

The paper derives two complementary sample complexity frameworks. First, a VC-dimension bound (Theorem 1) shows that the VC dimension of GNN_K^bin ∘ Rel equals the number of WL-isomorphism classes in Rel(G), implying that equivariant, deterministic schemes like Tinhofer (which produce a canonical ordering) have VC dimension equal to |G|/≃, while randomized schemes like RNI and RP inflate it by factors of |C|^n or n! respectively. Second, a covering-number bound (Theorem 4) based on a novel Lipschitz-function covering lemma (generalizing Gottlieb et al.) shows that schemes preserving pairwise graph distances (e.g., the proposed weak Tinhofer Tinhofer_W, which only relabels nodes in non-singleton WL color classes) yield tighter Rademacher-complexity bounds. As a concrete architecture, EGONN extracts Δ_P-ego-nets, applies a 1-layer GNN to each individualized ego-net, and max-pools the results, achieving provable substructure identification with VC dimension bounded by |Rel(G_Δ)/≃|—substantially smaller than the full-graph bound.

## Results

On the substructure identification task (Table 1), EGONN with Tinhofer individualization achieves 100% test accuracy on all four subgraph patterns (C3, C4, C5, K2,3) on 3-regular graphs, while standard GNNs with RP or Tinhofer individualization only reach 47–62% test accuracy. On real-world classification (Table 2), the proposed weak Tinhofer (Tinhofer_w) scheme achieves the lowest covering-number proxy N̂ across all four datasets (e.g., 0.203 on NCI1 vs. 0.719 for RP and 0.677 for full Tinhofer), yielding a generalization gap of 18.0% on NCI1 (GNN_5) compared to 32.2% for RP and 28.4% for Tinhofer. On the unlabeled COLLAB-b dataset, Tinhofer_w with GNN_5 reaches 79.5% test accuracy versus 56.1% for the un-augmented GNN, while maintaining a smaller gap (13.6%) than RP (3.1% gap but only 67.5% test) or LPE (21.6% gap).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four individualization/encoding baselines—Relational Pooling (RP), Random Node Initialization (RNI), full Tinhofer, and Laplacian Positional Encoding (LPE)—against the proposed weak Tinhofer (Tinhofer_w) scheme. Tinhofer_w consistently achieves the lowest covering-number proxy N̂ across all four real-world datasets (e.g., 0.203 vs. 0.719 for RP on NCI1), confirming that it perturbs the graph metric space least while still providing individualization. On molecular datasets (NCI1, Mutagenicity) where WL already distinguishes graphs, Tinhofer_w matches the un-augmented GNN's test accuracy (81.9% vs. 83.8% on NCI1) with a comparable generalization gap, whereas RP and RNI degrade test accuracy by 14–16 points. On the unlabeled COLLAB-b dataset, Tinhofer_w with GNN_5 reaches 79.5% test accuracy, substantially outperforming the un-augmented GNN (56.1%) and LPE (74.6%), while RP (67.5%) and RNI (56.8%) remain far behind. For substructure identification (Table 1), EGONN with Tinhofer achieves 100% test accuracy on all four patterns, while standard GNNs with RP or Tinhofer individualization overfit (100% train, 47–62% test), and EGONN without individualization fails to fit training data on C4 and K2,3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate research threads: the expressivity analysis of GNNs (bounded by the 1-WL test, with individualization schemes like RNI, RP, and CLIP providing universal approximators) and statistical learning theory for GNNs (VC-dimension bounds from Morris et al. [37], Rademacher complexity from Garg et al. [23]). The key conceptual move is to analyze how the *choice* of individualization scheme affects sample complexity, building on the Tinhofer canonical ordering [59] and the individualization-refinement framework [20], and on a covering bound for Lipschitz functions [24] that the authors generalize. The EGONN architecture for substructure identification extends the approach of Chen et al. [12] by pairing ego-net decomposition with individualization to achieve provable expressivity at lower VC dimension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a principled criterion for choosing individualization schemes: permutation-equivariant and deterministic schemes (Tinhofer, weak Tinhofer) yield lower VC dimension and covering numbers than randomized ones (RNI, RP), meaning less training data is needed for generalization. The weak Tinhofer scheme is a practical contribution that reduces covering numbers while preserving expressivity, validated on molecular and social network datasets. Open questions include the theoretical analysis of data augmentation via resampling individualizations at each epoch, relaxing the strong Lipschitzness assumption in the covering-number bounds, and extending the analysis to non-binary or multi-graph tasks.
