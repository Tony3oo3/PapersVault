## Problem

Linear switching state-space models (HMM, SLDS, rSLDS) are the standard tools for identifying discrete neural states, but they cannot capture the nonlinear dynamics of neural propagation. While RNNs model nonlinear dynamics effectively, they typically assume a single static dynamical system rather than switching between distinct regimes. The paper asks whether a switching RNN framework can simultaneously recover behaviorally-relevant discrete states and the distinct nonlinear dynamics within each state from raw neural recordings.

## Input / Output

The model consumes trial-averaged neural time-series of shape N×T×R (N sequences, T timepoints, R neural dimensions), where R ranges from 16 (lever-pull WFCI) to 180 (monkey electrophysiology) or 210 (decision-making WFCI), with T spanning 2–11 seconds depending on the task. It produces reconstructed neural activity, K discrete hidden states with learned Markov transition probabilities, P-dimensional continuous latent states, and forward predictions of future neural activity over 10–40 timepoints. The inference network is a bidirectional RNN that maps the full observation sequence to per-timepoint Gaussian parameters for the latent states, while the generative model uses tanh-activated RNNs with state-dependent weight matrices.

## Method

SRNN is a switching state-space model where each discrete state k has its own RNN dynamics (tanh-activated recurrent layers), and transitions between states follow a Markov process conditioned on the previous continuous hidden state (as in rSLDS). The key architectural distinction from SNLDS is that the dynamics are explicitly RNNs rather than feedforward networks with nonlinear activations, and the transition depends on the hidden state rather than the observation, enabling forward prediction. Training uses amortized variational inference: a bidirectional RNN inference network produces per-timepoint Gaussian means for the continuous latent states, while discrete states are marginalized out using Baum-Welch posterior probabilities obtained from the generative model. To prevent the common VI failure mode of collapsing to a single state, the model is initialized with HMM states and trained in two phases with a state-utilization penalty in the first phase. The final loss combines the expected log-likelihood under the generative model (transition, dynamics, and emission terms weighted by discrete state posteriors) with the entropy of the continuous latent posterior, optimized via Adam on A100 GPUs.

## Results

Across three experimental datasets (monkey reaching electrophysiology, mouse decision-making WFCI, and mouse lever-pull WFCI), SRNNs consistently achieve the lowest state-recovery error (1-recall on imbalanced multi-class classification), the lowest reconstruction MSE, and the lowest forward-prediction MSE among all compared switching models. On the reaching dataset, SRNNs recover all 5 behaviorally-relevant states (rest, delay, reaction time, movement, hold) purely from neural activity, with a cross-condition variability of 0.098 at K=5 (vs. 0.384 at K=4 and 0.282 at K=6). On the lever-pull dataset, SRNNs detect inhibitory dynamics ~3 s before behavior onset—far earlier than SLDS or rSLDS—and achieve superior prediction even 1–1.33 s ahead despite slightly higher reconstruction MSE. Training one SRNN session on curved reaching takes ~6.5 h on an A100 GPU, compared to <1 h for SLDS/rSLDS.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SRNNs outperform all four switching baselines (SLDS, rSLDS, SNLDS, mrSDS) on state-recovery error across every dataset and every tested latent dimensionality P, with the gap widening as P increases. On reconstruction MSE, SRNNs lead on the reaching and decision-making datasets, though the advantage narrows at higher P; on the lever-pull dataset, SRNNs are slightly worse than SLDS/rSLDS on reconstruction but substantially better on forward prediction. SNLDS and mrSDS, which also use nonlinear dynamics, are the closest competitors on reconstruction but still trail SRNNs on both state recovery and prediction. In the appendix comparison with the non-switching LFADS model (Pandarinath et al., 2018), all three switching models (SRNN, SLDS, rSLDS) achieve lower reconstruction error than LFADS at matched parameter budgets, confirming that the switching structure itself—beyond nonlinearity alone—contributes to better neural activity modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the switching state space model lineage (HMM → SLDS → rSLDS → SNLDS) by replacing the general nonlinear dynamical system of SNLDS (Dong et al., 2020) with a dedicated RNN architecture, while retaining the hidden-state-dependent transition mechanism from rSLDS (Linderman et al., 2017). The key conceptual departure is that prior switching models either assume linear dynamics (SLDS, rSLDS) or use unconstrained neural networks for the dynamics (SNLDS), whereas SRNNs impose the recurrent structure that is natural for temporal neural data and add architectural constraints that enable forward prediction. The inference framework draws on amortized variational inference with a bidirectional RNN encoder, building on the reparameterization-based VI approach of Kingma and Welling (2013) and the collapsed VI of Dong et al. (2020).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By capturing nonlinear dynamics within each discrete state, SRNNs reveal qualitatively different flow-field structures (stable, unstable, oscillatory) across behavioral phases that linear switching models cannot distinguish, offering a more faithful characterization of how neural circuits reconfigure during behavior. The consistent outperformance across three recording modalities (electrophysiology, widefield calcium imaging) and two species (monkey, mouse) strengthens the generality of the approach. Key limitations include the need for manual specification of the number of discrete states, sensitivity to initialization (mitigated by HMM pre-training), and substantially longer training times compared to linear baselines. Open questions include whether the framework can incorporate external inputs or control signals, and whether the approach scales to larger-scale recordings with more complex state structures.
