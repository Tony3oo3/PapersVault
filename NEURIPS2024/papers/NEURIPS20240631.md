## Problem

Prior work on input loss curvature (the trace of the Hessian of loss w.r.t. input) has studied its properties exclusively on training data, leaving its behavior on unseen test examples unexplored. This gap prevents a principled understanding of how curvature scores distinguish train from test samples, and no theoretical framework connects curvature-based train-test distinguishability to differential privacy parameters or dataset size.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240631/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The attack consumes an image-label pair and queries only the target model's forward pass (black-box, no parameter access), using a zero-order finite-difference scheme that requires 4 function evaluations per iteration (n_iter=10, h=0.001), yielding roughly 4× more queries than Carlini et al.'s LiRA. Shadow models (64 for CIFAR, 52 for ImageNet) are trained with and without the target example to build Gaussian parametric distributions of curvature scores. The output is a membership decision via either a parametric likelihood ratio (LR) or a negative log-likelihood ratio (NLL) score, with LR excelling at very low FPR and NLL achieving higher AUROC.

## Method

The paper derives an upper bound on the KL divergence between train and test curvature score distributions (Theorem 4.2), showing it scales with dataset size m and the DP parameter ε, and proves (Theorem 4.3) that curvature-based MIA outperforms probability-score-based MIA once m exceeds a threshold dependent on σ, ε, and the loss bound L. To enable black-box operation, the authors replace Hutchinson's trace estimator (which requires gradient access) with a zero-order finite-difference Hessian estimator using Rademacher random vectors, computing the trace of ∇²ℓ without any parameter access. The MIA procedure trains shadow models on subsets with and without the target example, computes zero-order curvature scores for each, fits Gaussian distributions to the IN and OUT score sets, and applies a likelihood ratio test to classify the target's membership. Experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ImageNet with ResNet-18/50 (and ablations on Inception, VGG-11, ViT-B16) confirm that curvature-based MIA surpasses all prior state-of-the-art methods on datasets with ≥10% of CIFAR-100 training samples, validating the theoretical size threshold.

## Results

The proposed zero-order curvature-based MIA (Curv ZO NLL) achieves the highest AUROC across all three datasets: 77.45% on ImageNet, 93.49% on CIFAR-100, and 68.82% on CIFAR-10, outperforming all six baselines. On CIFAR-100, the method reaches 84.47% balanced accuracy, surpassing the closest competitor (Ye et al., 80.73%) by nearly 4 points. The parametric likelihood-ratio variant (Curv ZO LR) achieves the best TPR at very low FPR (23.92% at 0.1% FPR on CIFAR-100 with 2 augmentations), exceeding Carlini et al. (15.80%) by ~8 points. Results are reported as mean ± std over 3 seeds using 64 shadow models (CIFAR) and 52 (ImageNet).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed Curv ZO NLL method outperforms all six baselines on every dataset and metric. Against the strongest baseline, Ye et al. [2022], it gains +2.61 AUROC on CIFAR-100 (93.49 vs. 90.88) and +1.52 on CIFAR-10 (68.82 vs. 67.30). Against Carlini et al. [2022] (LiRA), the closest competitor on ImageNet, it achieves +3.99 AUROC (77.45 vs. 73.46) and +4.62 balanced accuracy (69.16 vs. 66.14). Sablayrolles et al. [2019] is the second-strongest on ImageNet (76.50 AUROC) but is surpassed by +0.95 AUROC; on CIFAR-100 it lags significantly at 81.11 AUROC (−12.38). Yeom et al. [2018] and Song and Mittal [2021] are the weakest baselines, trailing by 10+ AUROC points on all datasets. Watson et al. [2022] underperforms most notably on CIFAR-100 (71.66 AUROC, −21.83 vs. Curv ZO NLL), indicating that difficulty-calibration-based attacks struggle on larger-class datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the input loss curvature line of research (Garg & Roy 2023, Garg et al. 2024, Ravikumar et al. 2024), which had previously studied curvature only on the training set for prototypicality, adversarial robustness, and memorization, by analyzing curvature on unseen test examples and exploiting the systematic train-test gap for membership inference. Methodologically, it adapts the shadow-model + Gaussian parametric model + likelihood ratio framework of Carlini et al. (2022, LiRA) but replaces probability scores with curvature scores, and introduces a zero-order finite-difference estimator to make curvature computation feasible in a black-box setting where prior curvature methods (Hutchinson's trace estimator) required white-box parameter access. The theoretical contribution—deriving a KL-divergence upper bound on train-test curvature distinguishability that depends on dataset size m and privacy parameter ε—builds directly on the stability and generalization bounds from Ravikumar et al. (2024) and the differential privacy framework of Dwork et al. (2006).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical characterization of when curvature-based MIA outperforms probability-based MIA (Theorem 4.3: when m exceeds a threshold depending on σ, ε, and L), and validates this empirically on ImageNet, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100, showing that the threshold is met by real-world dataset sizes. The zero-order estimation is practically important because it enables curvature-based MIA in the black-box threat model, which is the more realistic adversarial setting. Limitations include the 4× query overhead compared to Carlini et al., the computational cost of training shadow models, and the gap between zero-order and white-box curvature estimation (particularly for the NLL variant at low FPR). Open questions include whether the dataset-size threshold can be tightened, whether the approach generalizes to non-vision modalities or generative models, and whether the counterintuitive finding that curvature-based coresets make models more MIA-susceptible can be leveraged as a defense signal.
