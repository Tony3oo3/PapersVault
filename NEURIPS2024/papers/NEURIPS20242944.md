## Problem

Learning from multiple sets of inaccurate labels (e.g., from rule-based annotators in finance or healthcare) has been approached primarily through label aggregation or end-to-end training on all noisy labels, but these methods largely overlook the significance of data refinement during training. This paper argues that refining a relatively clean dataset is the central lever for improving performance, and that annotator agreement/disagreement provides a natural instrument for identifying which labels are reliable and which samples are trustworthy.

## Input / Output

The method takes pre-extracted feature vectors (768-dim BERT embeddings for NLP, 512-dim ResNet-18 embeddings for images, or raw tabular features) paired with R sets of binary labels from R annotators, and produces a single binary class probability from the (R+1)-th submodel. The R annotator-specific submodels are trained on their respective label sets with class-imbalance weighting, while the final submodel is trained only on the refined subset of samples. Refined labels from LRD are frozen after a few epochs to prevent overfitting, and RUS selects a fixed proportion (p=0.8) of samples from the agreement set each epoch.

## Method

CRL partitions the dataset by annotator agreement into D_d (some annotators disagree) and D_a (all agree), then applies two complementary refinement modules. For D_d, LRD compares the per-sample loss values across R submodels and selects the label with the minimum loss as the most reliable, grounded in a theorem showing that under class-conditional noise with diagonal transition probabilities > 0.5, the lower-loss label corresponds to the true label. For D_a, RUS computes a robust average loss using a smooth non-decreasing function φ(z)=log(1+z+z²/2) across R submodels and multiple historical epochs, then subtracts a variance-dependent lower-bound term (from Theorem 2) to give hard or annotator-biased samples a chance to be retained. All R+1 submodels (R annotator-specific + 1 final) share embedding layers and are trained simultaneously, with the final submodel learning from the union of LRD-refined and RUS-selected samples via standard binary cross-entropy.

## Results

CRL is evaluated on 13 benchmark datasets (NLP, image, tabular) under both class-dependent and instance-dependent noise, plus 2 real-world datasets (CIFAR-10N, Sentiment), using AUC as the metric. Under class-dependent noise, CRL achieves the best AUC on 12 of 13 datasets, with particularly large margins on Backdoor (0.937 vs. 0.816 for SLF) and Celeba (0.891 vs. 0.859 for CoNAL). Under instance-dependent noise, CRL leads on 11 of 13 datasets, e.g., 0.916 on AgNews (vs. 0.882 CoNAL) and 0.964 on Waveform (vs. 0.959 CoNAL). On real-world data, CRL reaches 0.866 AUC on CIFAR-10N and 0.753 on Sentiment, outperforming all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRL outperforms all 9 compared baselines on the vast majority of 26 dataset-noise combinations. Against the strongest end-to-end baselines, CRL leads CoNAL by 3.4 AUC points on AgNews (class) and 3.4 on AgNews (instance), and beats SLF by 12.1 points on Backdoor (class: 0.937 vs. 0.816). Against label-aggregation baselines, CRL surpasses NN-Mjv (majority voting) by 22.1 points on Backdoor (class) and 18.9 on Campaign (instance), and outperforms WeaSEL by 2.2 points on AgNews (class). Against the MoE-based ADMoE, CRL gains 12.1 points on Backdoor (class) and 3.4 on AgNews (instance). The only near-ties occur on Waveform (class: 0.840 vs. 0.840 WeaSEL) and CIFAR-10 (instance: 0.790 vs. 0.799 ADMoE), where CRL is marginally behind or matched. On real-world data, CRL leads the best baseline (NN-Mjv) by 11.3 points on CIFAR-10N (0.866 vs. 0.853) and 2.6 points on Sentiment (0.753 vs. 0.727).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in multi-annotator noisy-label learning, where prior work either aggregates labels (Majority Voting, EBCC, EM-based truth inference) or trains end-to-end models that map all noisy label sets into shared model components (CrowdLayer, DoctorNet, CoNAL, SLF, ADMoE). Instead, CRL positions data refinement as the central mechanism, using annotator agreement to partition samples and applying targeted strategies: a loss-comparison rule (LRD) grounded in a new theorem about noise transition matrices for disagreeing samples, and a robust loss-based selection criterion (RUS) for agreeing samples. The theoretical foundation extends the small-loss criterion of Gui et al. [11] from single-annotator to multi-annotator settings via Theorem 1 and Corollary 1, while RUS builds on the lower-bound analysis of Xia et al. [39] with a robustifying transformation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The architecture-agnostic design (a simple MLP backbone suffices) makes the framework broadly deployable in industrial settings where rule-based annotators are common (finance, healthcare), and the cooperation experiments confirm it can boost existing methods like SLF and CoNAL by 2–6% AUC without modifying their internals. Key limitations include the restriction to binary classification—extending LRD to multi-class is non-trivial because all annotators can be simultaneously wrong—and the reliance on the class-conditional noise assumption. Open questions include whether a consistency-rate threshold could replace the binary agree/disagree partition, and whether the framework's gains persist under more severe or correlated noise regimes.
