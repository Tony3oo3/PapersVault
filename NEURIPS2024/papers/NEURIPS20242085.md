## Problem

The fundamental cost of differential privacy in online learning was poorly understood: it was unclear whether the logarithmic overhead in mistake bounds observed in existing private algorithms was unavoidable for general hypothesis classes, and whether pure DP and approximate DP impose fundamentally different learnability constraints against adaptive adversaries. This paper resolves both questions by proving a universal Ω(log T) lower bound for any private online learner on nearly all hypothesis classes and constructing a class (point functions over ℕ) that is online learnable under approximate DP but not under pure DP against adaptive adversaries.

## Input / Output

The learner receives a hypothesis class H ⊆ {0,1}^X and a sequential stream of labeled examples (x_t, y_t) of length T, producing a prediction h_t ∈ {0,1}^X at each round before seeing the label. The adversary may be oblivious (all examples fixed in advance) or adaptive (each example chosen based on the learner's past predictions). Performance is measured by the expected number of mistakes in the realizable setting or expected regret in the agnostic setting, with the key distinction that non-private learners achieve a finite mistake bound of LD(H) independent of T, while the paper shows private learners must incur Ω(LD(H)·log T) mistakes.

## Method

For upper bounds, the paper reduces pure DP online learning against oblivious adversaries to DP-OPE by sampling a finite hypothesis class V from a probabilistic representation (parameterized by the representation dimension) and running a DP-OPE algorithm with each v ∈ V as an expert, yielding O(log²T·(RepDim(H)+log log T)²/ε) mistakes. For the impossibility against adaptive adversaries, the paper designs a multiplicative-update-based adversary for point functions that treats the learner's predictions as 'data points' in a dual online learning game, then applies the packing argument to show Ω(min(log d/ε, T)) mistakes are unavoidable. The general Ω(log T) lower bound constructs m = ⌈T/k⌉ sequences from a base stream by adaptively inserting error-inducing examples at positions where the algorithm predicts correctly with probability ≥ 1/3, then uses a 'smoothed binary search' (going to the more likely correct side with probability 3/4) to identify the sequence index with only O(1) independent copies of the algorithm, avoiding the log log T privacy blowup of standard binary search. The Ω(LD(H)·log T) bound is obtained by iterating this construction on non-overlapping subtrees of a shattered tree of depth LD(H).

## Results

The paper establishes a universal Ω(LD(H) log T / ε) lower bound on the number of mistakes for any (ε,δ)-differentially private online learner (Theorem 4.4), in stark contrast to the non-private setting where at most LD(H) mistakes suffice. For pure DP against oblivious adversaries, the paper provides an O(log²T · (RepDim(H) + log log T)² / ε) upper bound (Theorem 3.3), leaving a gap of log T · (log log T)². A key separation result shows that POINT_N is online learnable under approximate DP but provably not under pure DP against adaptive adversaries (Theorem 3.5, Corollary 3.6), with any pure DP algorithm forced to make Ω(T) mistakes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's lower bound of Ω(LD(H) log T / ε) (Theorem 4.4) generalizes and strengthens the Ω(log T) lower bound for point functions over [T] established by Cohen et al. [21] and Dmitriev et al. [22], extending it to all hypothesis classes containing non-complementary pairs. Against the non-private baseline, the separation is stark: the Standard Optimal Algorithm achieves at most LD(H) mistakes regardless of T [39], while any private learner's mistakes grow unboundedly as T → ∞. For the adaptive adversary setting, the paper's impossibility result for pure DP (Theorem 3.5) directly contrasts with Golowich and Livni [30], who showed the same class (Littlestone classes including POINT_N) is online learnable under approximate DP with O(log T) mistakes against oblivious adversaries. The paper's upper bound for pure DP (Theorem 3.3) improves upon the trivial O(T) bound from basic composition but remains a factor of log T · (log log T)² above the Ω(log T) lower bound, leaving the tight dependence on T as an open question.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the private online learning framework initiated by Golowich and Livni [30] (who gave approximate DP algorithms for Littlestone classes) and Dmitriev et al. [22] (who proved lower bounds for point functions under specific algorithmic assumptions). It leverages the probabilistic representation machinery of Beimel et al. [10] to reduce pure DP online learning to DP-OPE [7, 8] against oblivious adversaries, and departs from prior work by proving a universal Ω(LD(H) log T) lower bound that applies to all non-trivial hypothesis classes, generalizing the point-function-specific results of Cohen et al. [21] and the assumption-heavy bounds of Dmitriev et al. [22]. The key conceptual departure is showing that the oblivious-to-adaptive composition trick (which works under approximate DP via advanced composition) fundamentally fails under pure DP, yielding a clean separation between the two privacy notions in the adaptive setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a question raised by Sanyal and Ramponi [41] by proving that the logarithmic overhead in T is unavoidable for essentially all hypothesis classes under any meaningful DP guarantee, establishing a universal gap between private and non-private online learning. The separation between pure and approximate DP for adaptive adversaries (point functions over N are learnable under approximate DP but not pure DP) has practical implications: it shows that approximate DP is not merely a technical convenience but a necessary relaxation when dealing with adaptive data streams. Open questions include whether the O(log T) upper bound is tight for generic classes under pure DP, whether the lower bound extends to stochastic adversaries, and whether the gap between the Ω(LD(H) log T) lower bound and the O(log²T · (log log T)²) upper bound can be closed.
