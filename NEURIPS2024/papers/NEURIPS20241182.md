## Problem

The metric backbone of a weighted graph removes all edges not lying on any shortest path, retaining only 10–30% of edges in social networks. Intuition suggests this should dilute community structure: inter-community 'bridge' edges are more likely to be on shortest paths (and thus kept), while dense intra-community edges have many alternative paths (and thus removed). Yet prior empirical work shows the backbone preserves communities well. This paper provides the first formal proof explaining why this counterintuitive preservation occurs.

## Input / Output

The method takes a weighted distance graph (edge weights = costs/dissimilarities) and produces its metric backbone, a parameter-free sparsified subgraph containing only edges that appear on at least one all-pairs shortest path. For proximity graphs (e.g., social networks with interaction-frequency weights), a preprocessing step converts similarities to distances via weighted Jaccard neighborhood overlap. In the q-NN graph construction setting, the input is a cloud of points in a metric space, and the output is both the sparsified backbone and community assignments from downstream clustering algorithms (spectral, Leiden, Bayesian MCMC, Poisson learning).

## Method

The core theoretical contribution analyzes the metric backbone of weighted stochastic block models (wSBMs) using first-passage percolation. Proposition 1 characterizes shortest-path costs between vertices in specific community pairs via two independent FPP processes that meet at scale √(n log n). The key result (Theorem 1) shows that the backbone edge probability ratio p_ab^mb/p_cd^mb equals (1+o(1))·p_ab/p_cd for all community pairs, meaning the backbone thins edges approximately uniformly and thus preserves the community structure. The proof adapts Janson's coupling argument to extend from exponential to general cost distributions satisfying a regularity condition at zero. Theorem 2 then proves that spectral clustering on the backbone's weighted adjacency matrix recovers the true communities with loss O(1/(μ² log n)) whp, using Davis-Kahan perturbation bounds and a careful concentration argument on the backbone adjacency matrix via a threshold-graph decomposition.

## Results

Theoretically, the paper proves that in weighted SBMs, the metric backbone thins edges approximately uniformly: p_ab^mb / p_cd^mb = (1+o(1)) p_ab / p_cd, and spectral clustering on the backbone recovers communities with loss O(1/(μ² log n)) whp. Empirically, on Table 1, the approximate metric backbone of a √n/2-NN graph (roughly half the edges of a 10-NN graph) matches or slightly exceeds the ARI of the full 10-NN graph for both spectral clustering (e.g., MNIST: 0.563 vs 0.533) and Poisson learning (e.g., MNIST: 0.821 vs 0.814, FashionMNIST: 0.544 vs 0.526). Across all four real networks in Figure 1, the metric backbone and spectral sparsification achieve near-identical ARI for Bayesian MCMC, Leiden, and spectral clustering, while thresholding degrades performance due to disconnected components.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the Spielman-Srivastava spectral sparsification [37], tuned to retain the same number of edges as the metric backbone for a fair comparison. On all four real networks (High School, Primary School, DBLP, Amazon) and all three clustering algorithms (Bayesian MCMC, Leiden, spectral clustering), the metric backbone and spectral sparsification achieve essentially identical ARI, confirming that both preserve community structure well. The key practical advantage of the metric backbone is that it is parameter-free, whereas spectral sparsification requires tuning a hyperparameter to match the target edge count. In the q-NN graph construction setting (Figures 3–4), the metric backbone maintains stable ARI across all values of q, while spectral sparsification's performance degrades when the underlying graph's spectral properties become insufficient (e.g., large q on MNIST). Thresholding, a simpler baseline, consistently underperforms because it produces many small disconnected components. The metric backbone also produces more consistent edge sets across different q values (64k shared edges between G₃₀^mb and G₄₀^mb on MNIST vs. only 22k for spectral sparsification), making it a more robust preprocessing step.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends first-passage percolation (FPP) theory on random graphs—originally developed for complete graphs with i.i.d. weights (Janson 1999) and later generalized to inhomogeneous random graphs (Kolossváry & Komjáthy 2015)—to the setting of weighted stochastic block models with community-specific cost distributions. It builds on the metric backbone framework of Van Mieghem & Wang (2009) and McGeoch (1995), but departs from prior FPP work by analyzing shortest-path costs between vertices in *specific* community pairs rather than uniformly random vertex pairs, and by allowing heterogeneous cost distributions per community pair. The spectral recovery proof leverages the Davis-Kahan framework and the SBM spectral clustering consistency results of Lei & Rinaldo (2015), while the empirical section generalizes the contact-network experiments of Correia et al. (2023) to multiple clustering algorithms and network types, and introduces a new application to q-nearest-neighbor graph construction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a genuine conceptual paradox: despite the metric backbone preferentially retaining inter-community 'bridge' edges while removing intra-community edges (due to higher intra-community density providing shorter alternative paths), it provably preserves community structure by thinning edges approximately uniformly across all community pairs. This provides a parameter-free, computationally efficient sparsification tool that practitioners can apply without tuning hyperparameters, and the q-NN application demonstrates that it makes clustering robust to the number of neighbors—a practical benefit for graph construction pipelines. Limitations include the theoretical recovery guarantee (Theorem 2) requiring τ_max = τ_min, which restricts the class of SBMs covered, and the analysis is confined to the wSBM model; whether the results extend to more realistic community models (e.g., degree-corrected SBMs, overlapping communities) remains open. The approximate backbone (sampling 2 log n shortest-path trees) is shown to work empirically but lacks a formal guarantee.
