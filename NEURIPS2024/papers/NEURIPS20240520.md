## Problem

Chen et al. (ICLR 2023) showed that efficient sampling from an unknown distribution reduces to L²-accurate score estimation under minimal assumptions, but left open whether score estimation itself is computationally feasible. This paper establishes that L²-accurate score estimation is computationally hard under lattice-based cryptographic assumptions, even when sample complexity is polynomial, thereby proving a statistical-to-computational gap for score estimation in the absence of strong distributional assumptions.

## Input / Output

The hardness reduction takes oracle access to L²-accurate score estimates (with error O(1/√log d)) along the discretized forward OU process, plus poly(d) standard Gaussian samples, and produces a binary decision distinguishing Gaussian pancakes from the standard Gaussian with probability ≥ 1−δ. The positive (statistical) result takes n = poly(d, γ, 1/η, 1/δ) i.i.d. Gaussian pancakes samples and produces both an estimate û of the secret direction with ‖û−u‖² ≤ η² and, via substitution into the analytical score formula, L²-accurate score estimates with error O(max(1,1/σ⁸)·η²d).

## Method

The core reduction exploits the sampling-to-score estimation theorem of Chen et al. in a diagnostic way: it defines a test statistic Δ = max_k E_Q‖s_kh(x)+2πx‖² that measures how far the score estimates deviate from the standard Gaussian score −2πx. If the data distribution is Q, then Δ is small by definition; if it is a Gaussian pancakes distribution P_u with TV(P_u,Q)>1/2, then Lemma 3.3 forces Δ ≥ C₂/log d, yielding a clean threshold test. The proof verifies that Gaussian pancakes satisfy the required Lipschitz-score and finite-second-moment assumptions using Banaszczyk's transference theorems on lattice Gaussian mass, and handles concentration of the empirical Δ via a truncation argument with threshold M ≍ √d + 1/σ². For the statistical upper bound, the paper designs a projection-pursuit functional E(v) = E_{x∼P_u} g(⟨x,v⟩) that is monotone in |⟨v,u⟩|, enabling brute-force recovery of u with polynomial samples under the scaling γσ = O(1), which encompasses the cryptographically hard regime.

## Results

The main result (Theorem 3.1) shows that a polynomial-time algorithm with access to an L²-accurate score estimation oracle of error O(1/√log d) solves the Gaussian pancakes problem with probability at least 1−δ in poly(d)·log(1/δ) time, establishing a statistical-to-computational gap. Theorem 4.2 demonstrates that under the scaling γσ = O(1) (which includes the cryptographically hard regime), the secret direction u can be estimated to L²-error η using poly(d, γ, 1/η) samples via brute-force projection pursuit, confirming that the hardness is purely computational rather than statistical. The score estimation error bound of O(1/√log d) is the key quantitative threshold: any oracle achieving this accuracy (even in poly(d, 2^{1/ε²}) time) would break lattice-based cryptographic assumptions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or comparison tables. The 'comparison' is instead a reduction: the paper shows that efficient L²-accurate score estimation for Gaussian pancakes would imply an efficient distinguisher for the Gaussian pancakes problem, which is known to be as hard as worst-case lattice problems (Bruna et al., STOC 2021) and as hard as LWE (Gupte et al., FOCS 2022) in the regime γ ≥ 2√d and σ ≥ 1/poly(d). The paper contrasts the computational hardness of score estimation with the statistical achievability of the same task: under γσ = O(1), polynomially many samples suffice for L²-accurate score estimation via brute-force search over S^{d−1} (Theorem 4.2), but no polynomial-time algorithm is expected to achieve this. The reduction builds on Chen et al. (ICLR 2023), whose sampling-to-score estimation framework provides the technical backbone, but the paper's contribution is showing that the score estimation oracle in that framework cannot itself be implemented efficiently for Gaussian pancakes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper completes a conceptual circle in diffusion model theory: Chen et al. (ICLR 2023) showed that sampling from an unknown distribution reduces to L²-accurate score estimation under minimal assumptions, while this work proves the reverse direction—that score estimation itself is computationally hard for Gaussian pancakes distributions under lattice-based cryptographic assumptions (Bruna et al., STOC 2021; Gupte et al., FOCS 2022). The reduction mechanism directly applies Chen et al.'s Theorem 2 as a black box, using the score estimation oracle to construct a distinguisher for the Gaussian pancakes problem. The analysis of Gaussian pancakes properties (Lipschitz scores, TV/KL bounds) draws on Banaszczyk's transference theorems from lattice cryptography, and the polynomial sample complexity upper bound uses a projection pursuit estimator for the hidden direction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This establishes a formal statistical-to-computational gap for score estimation: even with polynomially many samples, no efficient algorithm can achieve L²-accurate score estimation for Gaussian pancakes unless lattice-based cryptography breaks—a result with direct implications for NIST post-quantum standardization. The key open question is identifying data assumptions that exclude hard Gaussian pancake instances while still capturing realistic distributions, which would delineate the boundary of when diffusion model training is computationally feasible. The paper also raises the intriguing question of whether white-box access to a trained diffusion model leaks privileged information (the score estimates) that cannot be extracted from samples alone, connecting to broader questions about what 'knowledge' a generative model encodes.
