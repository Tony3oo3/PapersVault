## Problem

Preference learning methods rely on tractable but oversimplified choice models (Bradley-Terry and its variants) that ignore well-documented context effects in human decision-making, such as preference reversals induced by dominated decoy options. State-of-the-art cognitive models that do capture these effects are computationally intractable, creating a gap between realistic human choice modeling and practical preference learning. This paper bridges that gap by making a computationally rational cognitive model of choice amenable to preference learning.

## Input / Output

The method operates on fixed-size sets of options (typically 2–3), each described by d numerical attributes, paired with the user's selected option. The noisy utility observations and ordinal attribute comparisons that drive the user's internal decision process are latent and never observed by the learning system; only the presented options and the chosen item are available. Outputs include the inferred utility weight vector w, choice model parameters θ (calculation noise, ordinal error rate, and per-attribute tolerances), and in application settings, concrete design recommendations selected by maximizing expected utility under the posterior.

## Method

The core contribution is the Computationally Rational Choice Surrogate (CRCS), which replaces two intractable integrals in the Howes et al. cognitive model with learned neural network surrogates: ū predicts expected option utilities from noisy observations, and q̂ predicts the choice probability distribution from the option set. Both networks are multi-task architectures conditioned on utility parameters w and choice model parameters θ via an embedding module, trained on samples generated from priors over options, utilities, and choice parameters. The LC-CRCS variant further integrates LCL's linear context-dependent weight adaptation mechanism (w + A·x_C) to capture cross-feature context effects that CRCS alone cannot model, making the full model differentiable and trainable end-to-end via gradient descent on the choice likelihood.

## Results

On four human choice datasets, LC-CRCS achieves the lowest negative log-likelihood (NLL) on Hotels (536 vs. 553 for LCL), District-Smart (3276 vs. 3305), and Dumbalska (99,147 vs. 100,683), with all differences significant at p < 0.01. On Car-Alt, LCL (7290) outperforms LC-CRCS (7345), attributed to insufficient training data for the CRCS surrogate. For utility function inference, CRCS achieves a Kendall's τ of 0.622 with independently collected rankings on District-Smart, substantially exceeding LCL (0.286) and Bradley-Terry (0.162) at p < 0.001. In the low-data elicitation setting, CRCS models significantly outperform LCL at all time steps (p < 0.01), and LC-CRCS further improves over CRCS (p < 0.05) even with minimal data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Bradley-Terry and Bower & Balzano, both CRCS and LC-CRCS achieve significantly lower NLL on all four datasets, with the largest gains on Dumbalska (LC-CRCS: 99,147 vs. BT: 103,669, a ~4.4% reduction). Against LCL, LC-CRCS wins on Hotels (536 vs. 553), District-Smart (3276 vs. 3305), and Dumbalska (99,147 vs. 100,683), but loses on Car-Alt (7345 vs. 7290), where the authors attribute the gap to insufficient option sets for training the CRCS surrogate. In the utility inference experiment (Kendall's τ on District-Smart), CRCS (0.622) and LC-CRCS (0.525) both significantly outperform LCL (0.286) and Bradley-Terry (0.162) at p < 0.001. In the active elicitation setting on Dumbalska, CRCS models achieve significantly higher expected likelihood of unseen choices than LCL at all time steps (p < 0.01), with the gap widening in low-data regimes where LCL has not yet learned the context effects built into CRCS. In the retrosynthesis case study, the CRCS-based planner produces top-k routes with significantly higher maximum true utility than Aizynthfinder (p < 1.61×10⁻⁵³ for all k), though the average number of solved routes is comparable (42.75 vs. 45.62).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper makes the computationally rational choice model of Howes et al. (2016) — which captures context effects like preference reversals through noisy utility observations and noisy ordinal attribute comparisons — tractable for preference learning by replacing its intractable integrals with learned neural network surrogates (CRCS). It then extends this surrogate by integrating the cross-feature weight-adaptation mechanism from the Linear Context Logit (LCL) model of Tomlinson and Benson (2021), yielding LC-CRCS. The work departs from the standard Bradley-Terry framework that dominates preference learning and RLHF by grounding the choice model in cognitive science, while retaining the practicality of a differentiable, gradient-based inference procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Preference learning underpins RLHF and AI alignment, yet the Bradley-Terry variants used in practice are known to miss well-documented context effects in human choice. By showing that a cognitively-grounded model improves both choice prediction and utility inference — especially in low-data elicitation settings where built-in cognitive priors matter most — the paper provides a principled path toward more accurate preference elicitation. Key limitations include the need for sufficient choice sets to train the surrogate (as seen with Car-Alt) and the restriction to fixed-size choice sets. Open questions include extending the model to variable-size option sets and integrating the reading/interpretation processes of human evaluators for LLM fine-tuning applications.
