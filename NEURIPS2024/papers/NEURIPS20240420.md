## Problem

Online fair division requires allocating indivisible goods immediately as they arrive, but prior work either assumes all player values are known at allocation time or does not optimize social welfare. This paper tackles the harder setting where player values are drawn from distributions with unknown means and are only observed for the player who receives an item, creating a bandit-learning problem coupled with fairness constraints (envy-freeness or proportionality in expectation) that depend on those unknown means and may be tight with zero slack.

## Input / Output

At each time step the algorithm observes the arriving item type k_t and the history of past (item type, allocated player, realized value) triples, but critically only sees the value of the player who received the item, not all players' values. The algorithm outputs a single fractional allocation matrix X ∈ R^{n×m} (rows sum to 1 per column) that is committed to for the remainder of the horizon after an initial T^{2/3}-step exploration phase, and the realized allocation is produced by sampling a player from the appropriate column of X at each step. The fairness guarantee is that X satisfies the EFE or PE linear constraints for the true mean matrix μ* with probability 1 − 1/T.

## Method

The algorithm is an explore-then-commit procedure: for the first T^{2/3} steps it allocates uniformly at random to build value estimates, then solves a semi-infinite LP that maximizes social welfare subject to fairness constraints holding for every μ in the confidence region μ̂ ± ε. The key theoretical insight (Property 2) is that for both envy-freeness and proportionality, there exists a near-optimal allocation X' with O(γ) welfare loss such that every constraint either has slack ≥ γ or all involved players are treated equally; combined with Lipschitz continuity of the constraint matrices in μ, this guarantees X' satisfies the constraints for all μ in the confidence region. Proving Property 2 for envy-freeness requires a novel combinatorial algorithm (Algorithm 3) that iteratively modifies allocations using 'envy-with-slack' directed graphs, equivalence classes of equally-treated players, and operations (remove-incoming-edge, cycle-shift, average-clique) that either merge equivalence classes or remove edges while preserving near-optimality. The resulting per-step regret is Õ(T^{-1/3}), yielding total regret Õ(T^{2/3}), which is shown tight by a matching lower bound.

## Results

The main result (Theorem 1) is that the Fair Explore-Then-Commit algorithm achieves $\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})$ regret while maintaining either envy-freeness in expectation (EFE) or proportionality in expectation (PE) with probability $1 - 1/T$. A matching lower bound (Theorem 2) of $\tilde{\Omega}(T^{2/3})$ regret is proven, establishing that the rate is tight up to logarithmic factors. The algorithm uses a warm-up phase of $T^{2/3}$ steps with uniform-at-random allocation, followed by a commit phase using a semi-infinite linear program that enforces fairness constraints over a confidence region around the estimated mean values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental baselines or empirical comparison tables. The key theoretical comparisons are: (1) The $\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})$ upper bound is matched by a $\tilde{\Omega}(T^{2/3})$ lower bound (Theorem 2), showing tightness. (2) Compared to Benadè et al. [5], who assume all player values are known at allocation time and achieve $o(1)$ realized envy, this paper's setting (unknown values) provably cannot achieve $o(\sqrt{T})$ realized envy (Theorem 3), but EFE algorithms achieve $\sqrt{T}\log T$ realized envy (Theorem 4). (3) Compared to linear bandits with a single safety constraint (Amani et al. [2]), which achieve $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})$ regret when the constraint has positive slack but only $\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})$ with zero slack, this paper handles multiple constraints that are often tight (zero slack) simultaneously, yet still achieves $\tilde{O}(T^{2/3})$ via structural properties of fairness constraints (Properties 1–4). (4) Compared to Yamada et al. [35], who incorporate fairness through Nash social welfare in the objective rather than as hard constraints, this paper enforces EFE/PE as explicit linear constraints while optimizing utilitarian social welfare.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of online fair division and multi-armed bandits, carving new ground by being the first to combine unknown player values (inducing a bandit learning problem) with fairness maintained through hard constraints (envy-freeness or proportionality in expectation). Prior online fair division work (Benadè et al.) assumes values are known at allocation time, eliminating the learning component, while Yamada et al. incorporate unknown values but handle fairness through the Nash social welfare objective rather than constraints. The paper also departs from the constrained bandits literature (Amani et al., Pacchiano et al.), which typically requires positive slack in the optimal solution to achieve O(√T) regret; here, fairness constraints are often tight, necessitating novel structural machinery (Properties 1–4) that exploits the specific algebraic form of envy-freeness and proportionality to still achieve T^{2/3} regret.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The tight T^{2/3} regret bound (matching upper and lower bounds) provides a clean characterization of the fundamental difficulty of learning under fairness constraints, and the combinatorial algorithm for envy-freeness (Lemma 1) is noted as potentially of independent interest to fair division researchers. Key open questions include whether O(√T) regret is achievable (blocked by the fact that fairness constraints are often tight and depend on every unknown mean), whether the exponential-in-n regret coefficient for envy-freeness is truly necessary, and whether the bandit-fair-division connection extends to other problems like online cake cutting. The practical motivation from the Food Drop program (10,000+ lbs of food redistributed monthly) grounds the theory in a real-world application, though the additive utility assumption may limit immediate deployment.
