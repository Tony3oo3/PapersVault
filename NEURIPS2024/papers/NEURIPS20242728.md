## Problem

There is no consensus on whether LLMs can produce reliable uncertainty estimates over the correctness of their open-ended generations without intervention. Prior work suggests prompting alone suffices, but this paper shows that black-box methods (perplexity, verbalized confidence, zero-shot classifiers) yield poorly calibrated uncertainties that do not improve with model scale in open-ended settings, leaving a practical gap for high-stakes decision-making applications.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242728/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a question and the LLM's greedily decoded answer (generated via few-shot prompting) and produces a calibrated P(correct) probability score. For the LoRA+Prompt variant, the fine-tuned model is prompted with the question, answer, and a two-choice correctness query (tokens 'i' and 'ii' for no/yes), and the probability is extracted from the softmax over these two target tokens. Correctness labels for training are generated by an auxiliary LLM (GPT-3.5 Turbo) that grades open-ended answers for semantic equivalence, tolerating rephrasings while penalizing verbose or incorrect responses.

## Method

The paper fine-tunes open-source LLMs (LLaMA-2 7B/13B, Mistral 7B) on a small dataset of ~1,000–20,000 graded correct/incorrect answer pairs using three parameterizations: a frozen-feature linear probe, LoRA adapters on the base model, and LoRA combined with a language prompt that frames correctness as a two-token multiple-choice question. A Jensen-Shannon Divergence regularization term between the fine-tuned and base model's output distributions prevents the calibration-tuned model from drifting too far from its original behavior, improving ECE from ~30% to ~11%. The key finding is that training through the model's features (LoRA) is necessary for open-ended generalization—frozen features alone suffice for multiple-choice but fail on open-ended generation—while the language prompt provides additional gains. The resulting uncertainty estimates generalize across subject matter, question format (MC↔OE), and even to the outputs of other models, suggesting they learn a general correctness-judgment mechanism rather than model-specific self-knowledge.

## Results

On open-ended MMLU, LoRA + Prompt fine-tuning reduces ECE from ~40–70% (zero-shot baselines) to ~10–20% and raises AUROC from ~50–65% to ~70–85% across LLaMA-2 7B/13B and Mistral 7B (base and instruct). As few as 1,000 graded examples suffice to outperform all black-box baselines, with diminishing returns beyond ~5,000. On the out-of-distribution LiveCodeBench coding task, Mistral-7B with LoRA + Prompt achieves ECE of 21.4% and AUROC of 85.1%, versus 70.2% ECE and 66.2% AUROC for the zero-shot classifier. Regularization via Jensen-Shannon Divergence further reduces mean ECE from 29.9% to 10.8% across six base models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Zero-Shot Classifier prompting baseline (Lin et al. [33]), LoRA + Prompt dramatically improves both ECE and AUROC on open-ended MMLU, with even the weakest fine-tuned model (LLaMA-2 7B) outperforming the strongest zero-shot model. The Verbalized confidence baseline (Tian et al. [51], Xiong et al. [60]) achieves moderate AUROC comparable to weaker fine-tuning methods but suffers from poor calibration (high ECE) even after temperature scaling, whereas LoRA + Prompt excels on both metrics. The sampling-based semantic clustering method (Kuhn et al. [30]) yields reasonable calibration but very weak discriminative performance (low AUROC), making it unsuitable for selective prediction; LoRA + Prompt dominates on AUROC. Max softmax probability (Plaut et al. [43]) is competitive only on multiple-choice MMLU and does not apply to open-ended generation, where LoRA + Prompt is the clear winner. Frozen-feature baselines (Probe on LLM features, sBERT [44], OpenAI embeddings) are insufficient for open-ended generalization, confirming that training through the model's features via LoRA is necessary for effective uncertainty estimation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the line of work that frames LLM uncertainty estimation as text completion over graded examples (Lin et al., Zhang et al.) and on prompting-based elicitation methods (Kadavath et al., Tian et al.), but departs sharply in its conclusions: it shows that black-box prompting and perplexity-based methods fail to produce calibrated uncertainties in open-ended generation and do not improve with model scale, whereas prior work on multiple-choice settings suggested otherwise. Methodologically, it extends the fine-tuning approach by adding JSD regularization to preserve base-model behavior and by demonstrating that training through the model's features via LoRA is necessary for open-ended generalization, in contrast to the frozen-feature probes of Azaria and Mitchell. It also introduces an open-ended variant of MMLU (MMLU-OE) as a new evaluation setting that exposes fundamentally different calibration dynamics than the multiple-choice format used in most prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, sample-efficient recipe (~1000 graded examples with LoRA) for making open-source LLMs produce calibrated P(correct) estimates, resolving an active debate about whether prompting alone suffices. The finding that a single fine-tuned model can estimate the uncertainties of other models (including closed-source ones) has broad practical implications for deployment, as it decouples uncertainty estimation from the generating model. Open questions remain: the current setup requires two separate models (one for answering, one for uncertainty), and the authors note that uncertainty-aware pre-training or alignment would be ideal but introduces a challenging online learning problem. The user study, while preliminary, is one of the first to test whether calibrated confidence actually changes human reliance behavior in collaborative settings, and it suggests the biggest gains come for lower-performing users.
