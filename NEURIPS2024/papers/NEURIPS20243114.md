## Problem

Comparing classifiers under multiple quality metrics is typically done via either weighted sums (requiring hard-to-justify weights) or Pareto-fronts (ignoring cardinal information and the distribution of data sets), leaving a gap between overly liberal and overly conservative analyses. Moreover, existing benchmarking methods rarely provide statistically valid tests that account for the uncertainty induced by treating a finite benchmark suite as a sample, nor do they quantify how robust conclusions are under deviations from i.i.d. sampling assumptions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a finite set of classifiers, a benchmark suite of s data sets (treated as an i.i.d. sample from a population), and n quality metrics Φ: C×D→[0,1]^n where the first z metrics are cardinal (differences interpretable) and the remaining n−z are purely ordinal (only ranking meaningful). It outputs the GSD-front (a subset of classifiers not strictly GSD-dominated by any competitor), a binary statistical test decision for whether a given classifier belongs to the GSD-front at significance level α, and a contamination-tolerance curve showing the maximum number k of data sets that can be arbitrarily redistributed before the test decision flips.

## Method

The paper embeds the range of the multidimensional quality metric into a preference system [A, R1*, R2*] where R1* is the component-wise ≥-relation (ordinal) and R2* encodes cardinal dominance of differences in the first z dimensions, then defines the GSD-front as the set of classifiers not strictly dominated under generalized stochastic dominance over all compatible utility representations. This front is provably a subset of the Pareto-front (Theorem 2) yet more discriminative, as it exploits both the distribution of performances and the cardinal structure of the metrics. For inference, the authors construct a consistent set-valued estimator (Theorem 1, requiring finite VC-dimension of the utility class) and two permutation tests — a static test rejecting H0 only if all pairwise auxiliary tests reject, and a dynamic test using Bonferroni-corrected levels to identify the maximal subset where the classifier is significantly in the GSD-front (Theorem 3). Robustness under non-i.i.d. sampling is handled via γ-contamination models from imprecise probabilities: the test statistic is evaluated under worst-case measures within the contamination set, yielding a p-value function F_C(k) that quantifies how many data sets can be contaminated before significance is lost. All test statistics are computed by solving linear programs, making the approach computationally tractable.

## Results

On the OpenML benchmark (80 binary classification datasets, 7 classifiers), the 0-empirical GSD-front identifies {RF, CART, kNN} as undominated, while the Pareto-front contains all 7 classifiers. SVM is statistically confirmed (α=0.05) to lie in the GSD-front of {SVM, kNN, xGBoost, RF, GLMNet}, with significance robust to contamination of up to 7 of 80 datasets. On PMLB (62 datasets), the newly proposed CRE classifier is not significantly in the GSD-front relative to five state-of-the-art classifiers (CART, RF, SVM, kNN, GLMNet) under a robust-accuracy metric, indicating no evidence of improvement over the state-of-the-art.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the GSD-front against three existing approaches on the OpenML benchmark. The Pareto-front contains all 7 classifiers, making it maximally conservative and uninformative for discrimination. First-order stochastic dominance (a special case of GSD with trivial cardinal structure) finds no classifier significantly larger than any other at the 5% level, showing that ignoring the cardinal nature of accuracy is too restrictive. The Demšar [24] test extended to multiple metrics (defining a 'marginal front' of classifiers not significantly worse on any single metric) also yields all 7 classifiers, again less discriminative than the GSD-front. The GSD-front, by leveraging both the distribution of the multidimensional quality metric and the ordinal/cardinal structure of the metrics, identifies only {RF, CART, kNN} as undominated — a strictly smaller and more informative set. On PMLB, the framework correctly fails to declare the new CRE classifier as significantly improving over state-of-the-art, demonstrating that the test does not produce false positives.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the generalized stochastic dominance (GSD) framework for classifier comparison introduced in Jansen et al. (JMLR 2023) by introducing the GSD-front as a discriminative set of undominated classifiers, along with consistent statistical estimators and permutation-based hypothesis tests. The preference-system formalism and the regularized permutation test for pairwise GSD comparisons are inherited from Jansen et al. (UAI 2023), while the robustification via γ-contamination models draws on the imprecise-probability and robust-statistics literature (Walley, Huber, Augustin). Conceptually, the GSD-front is positioned as a principled middle ground between the overly conservative Pareto-front (which ignores cardinal information and distributional structure) and the overly liberal weighted-sum approach (which requires hard-to-justify weights), filling a gap identified in the multicriteria benchmarking literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The GSD-front provides a statistically rigorous, assumption-light framework for multicriteria classifier benchmarking that simultaneously respects ordinal and cardinal metrics, accounts for sampling uncertainty, and quantifies robustness to non-i.i.d. contamination of the benchmark suite. This is practically important given the growing number of proposed classifiers and the recognized inadequacy of single-metric or purely Pareto-based comparisons. Key limitations include the restriction to classifiers (not regressors or other model types), the reliance on i.i.d. sampling for the base tests (mitigated but not eliminated by the contamination analysis), and the computational cost of solving large linear programs for the test statistics. Open questions include extension to regression-type analyses with dataset covariates, application to deep learning benchmark suites like DAWNBench, and whether the antisymmetry assumption on the GSD order can be relaxed.
