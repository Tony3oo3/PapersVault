## Problem

Continuous attractors are the standard theoretical framework for analog working memory, but they are structurally unstable: any infinitesimal perturbation to the recurrent dynamics destroys the continuum of fixed points. The field lacks a theoretical account of how much this fragility matters in practice—specifically, what the finite-time memory consequences are of the various bifurcations that arise, and whether the continuous attractor concept retains explanatory power for biological and artificial systems that can never be exactly at the fine-tuned parameter values.

## Input / Output

For the theoretical analysis, the input is an ODE vector field defining a continuous attractor plus a small perturbation (parameterized by ε), and the output is a persistent slow manifold with a quantified tangent flow speed, a linear memory-error bound (t‖φ‖∞), and a bifurcation topology classification (number and type of fixed points or limit cycles). For the empirical RNN experiments, inputs are angular velocity time series or target positions on a circle fed into trained networks (vanilla RNN, LSTM, GRU with ReLU/tanh/rectified-tanh activations, hidden sizes 64–256), and outputs include the decoded head direction or saccade target, the identified slow-manifold geometry (cubic spline fit to the invariant ring), the eigenvalue gap confirming normal hyperbolicity, and asymptotic generalization metrics (fixed-point count, memory capacity via conditional entropy).

## Method

The paper applies Fenichel's Persistent Manifold Theorem (via the special case due to Jones) to show that any small perturbation of a normally hyperbolic continuous attractor yields a diffeomorphic invariant slow manifold on which the residual flow is O(ε), establishing a universal 'ghost attractor' structure across all bifurcation types. A fast-slow decomposition (reparameterizing coordinates into tangent y and normal z components) makes the time-scale separation explicit: the normal flow is fast and attractive, while the tangent flow is slow with speed controlled by the bifurcation parameter. From this, the authors derive a worst-case memory error bound (Eq. 5) and a 'revival' proposition showing that the uniform norm of the tangent flow equals the minimum perturbation needed to restore an exact continuous attractor, thereby converting a geometric quantity into a practical distance metric. Empirically, they train 150+ RNNs across architectures and activation functions on two analog-memory tasks with injected state noise, then numerically extract the slow manifold by thresholding trajectory speed, fit a periodic cubic spline, identify fixed points via flow-direction flips, and verify normal hyperbolicity through the eigenvalue gap—revealing that all successful solutions share the slow-manifold motif regardless of their asymptotic topology (fixed-point ring vs. limit cycle), with generalization to longer timescales governed by the number and spacing of stable fixed points.

## Results

The paper establishes a universal error bound for approximate continuous attractors: the average memory deviation over time t is bounded by t‖φ‖∞, where ‖φ‖∞ is the uniform norm of the flow tangent to the slow manifold (Eq. 5). For the bounded line attractor, perturbations bifurcate to a single stable fixed point with probability 3/4 and to three fixed points (one saddle, two stable) with probability 1/4. Task-trained RNNs (10 per architecture, hidden sizes 64/128/256, trained for T₁=256 time steps) consistently exhibit a slow invariant ring manifold with a clear eigenvalue gap confirming normal hyperbolicity, and their asymptotic error scales inversely with the number of stable fixed points on the manifold.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper that does not benchmark against specific competing methods in the traditional sense. Instead, it validates its persistent manifold theory across five distinct ring attractor implementations (piecewise-linear Noorman et al., tanh bump attractor, low-rank network, EMPJ, and a simple ODE ring) and multiple RNN architectures (vanilla ReLU/tanh/rectified-tanh, LSTM, GRU). The theory's predictions are confirmed in all cases: perturbations of any ring attractor leave an invariant ring manifold intact, and the number of fixed points on that manifold determines asymptotic generalization. The key empirical validation is that RNNs trained with state noise (satisfying robustness conditions C3–C4) naturally converge to slow-manifold solutions whose memory error matches the theoretical bound t‖φ‖∞ at finite times and whose asymptotic error is predicted by fixed-point count and memory capacity (negative conditional entropy). No competing method is directly outperformed; rather, the paper unifies previously disparate observations (discrete attractors, limit cycles, slow tori) under a single theoretical framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges Fenichel's persistent manifold theory (1971) and Jones' geometric singular perturbation framework (1995) with the neuroscience literature on continuous attractor models of working memory (Seung 1996; Kim et al. 2017; Noorman et al. 2024). It addresses the long-standing 'fine-tuning problem' identified by Renart et al. (2003) and Chaudhuri & Fiete (2016) by showing that the 'ghost' slow manifold informally observed after bifurcation is precisely the invariant manifold guaranteed by Fenichel's theorem under normal hyperbolicity. The empirical validation draws on task-trained RNNs for angular velocity integration (Cueva et al. 2020) and memory-guided saccade tasks (Wimmer et al. 2014; Xie et al. 2022), extending the authors' own prior work on persistent learning signals (Park et al. 2023).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper resolves a fundamental tension in computational neuroscience: continuous attractors are structurally unstable yet appear to underpin robust biological memory. By providing a quantitative error bound (Eq. 5) linking the uniform norm of the slow flow to memory degradation, it gives experimentalists a concrete criterion for when an approximate continuous attractor is 'good enough' on behaviorally relevant timescales. The generalization analysis—showing that finite-time performance is governed by flow speed while asymptotic performance is governed by fixed-point topology—offers a principled way to interpret why trained networks sometimes fail beyond their training duration. Open questions include whether the continuous attractor can be directly recovered from neural recordings, whether the framework extends to higher-dimensional or non-compact attractors, and how to handle cases where the time-scale separation is too small for clean fast-slow decomposition.
