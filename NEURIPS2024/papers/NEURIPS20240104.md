## Problem

Classical RL algorithms struggle with goal-directed learning when goals change frequently, because the state×goal space explodes and experiences cannot be reused across objectives. Existing subgoal-generation methods either require predefined global environment knowledge (e.g., Dijkstra, A*) or depend on a mature Q-function to bootstrap subgoal quality, limiting their applicability in large-scale or non-stationary settings. The paper asks whether a neurally trained goal-reducer can generate nearly optimal subgoals from random exploration alone, without any prior distance or cost information.

## Input / Output

The goal-reducer accepts a concatenated representation of the current state s_t and the target goal g (which may be a 13×13 partial image in gridworld tasks, a 7-DoF coordinate vector in robot arm tasks, or a text-embedding vector in the treasure-hunting task) and outputs a subgoal representation s_g that lies between s_t and g in the state-transition graph. In integrated mode, the subgoal conditions a standard policy π(a|s,g) to produce an action; in standalone mode, the goal-reducer is applied recursively (up to K=3 steps, M=12 parallel subgoals) until a local policy's entropy drops below a threshold, at which point the local policy emits the final action.

## Method

The core contribution is a Loop-removal sampling strategy for training a VAE-based goal-reducer Φ(s,g)→s_g. During random exploration, trajectories are stored in a replay buffer; a filtration process (inspired by persistent homology) with radius ρ identifies and removes 'loops'—segments where the agent revisits a state within distance ρ—yielding loop-removed trajectories that approximate shortest paths. The VAE is trained on (s, g, s_g) triples sampled from these cleaned trajectories using a standard reconstruction + KL-divergence loss, with fixed random embeddings to avoid injecting prior structural knowledge. When integrated with DQL or SAC, the goal-reducer provides an additional KL-divergence regularization loss that pulls the policy toward actions optimal for the subgoal whenever the policy is more uncertain about the ultimate goal than about the subgoal. In standalone mode, the goal-reducer recursively reduces the goal until a local policy (trained only on one-step transitions) can act, eliminating the Bellman equation entirely and outperforming even the RL-integrated variant.

## Results

In the four-room navigation task, DRL+GR (goal-reducer-augmented DQL) converges significantly faster than plain DRL, and in the robot arm reach task, DRL+GR (goal-reducer-augmented SAC) similarly outperforms plain SAC. In a harder 19×19 maze, the standalone goal-reducer (GR) with only a local policy—no Bellman equation iteration—outperforms both GR+DRL and plain DRL, demonstrating that recursive goal reduction alone can solve navigation. In the fMRI treasure-hunting task, RSA reveals that vmPFC activity matches the goal representation (G) and goal-reducer (Φ) components, while bilateral putamen activity matches state representations (S and Int.S), with the putamen showing lower loading on goal and goal-reduction layers.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two standard RL baselines: plain Deep Q-learning (DQL, Mnih et al. 2015) for discrete action spaces and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC, Haarnoja et al. 2018) for continuous action spaces. In the four-room navigation task, DRL+GR consistently outperforms plain DRL in convergence speed across multiple maze sizes (13–21), with the gap narrowing but remaining positive as task difficulty increases. In the 7-DoF robot arm reach task, DRL+GR similarly beats plain SAC. Most notably, in the 19×19 maze, the standalone goal-reducer (GR) with only a local policy and no Bellman equation iteration surpasses both GR+DRL and plain DRL, indicating that recursive subgoal generation alone is sufficient and superior to value-function-based approaches in this setting. The fMRI comparison further shows that the goal-reducer's internal representations align with vmPFC (goal and goal-reduction) and putamen (state/local policy) activity patterns, supporting biological plausibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of goal-directed RL, classical pathfinding heuristics, and computational topology. It departs from prior subgoal-generation methods (GAN-based, Q-function-dependent, or HER-style trajectory sampling) by introducing loop-removal sampling, which borrows the filtration concept from persistent homology to identify and eliminate redundant revisits in random-walk trajectories, yielding subgoals without any value-function or global map knowledge. The 'divide and conquer' principle is inherited from Dijkstra/A* algorithms, but the paper's contribution is making this principle learnable in a neural, model-free setting via a VAE-based goal-reducer. On the neuroscience side, it extends the subgoal-navigation and cognitive-map literature by providing a trained neural network whose internal representations can be directly compared to fMRI activity via RSA.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most practically interesting result is that a standalone goal-reducer paired with a local policy (no Bellman iteration) outperforms both goal-reducer-augmented DQL and plain DQL on a 19×19 maze, suggesting that recursive subgoal decomposition may be sufficient for navigation without full value-function convergence. The fMRI correspondence with vmPFC and putamen offers a biologically grounded hypothesis for how humans decompose distant goals, though the loop-removal step lacks a clear neural mechanism, which the authors acknowledge as a key limitation. Open questions include whether the approach scales to high-dimensional continuous tasks beyond the 7-DOF robot arm, whether the filtration radius can be learned rather than grid-searched, and whether the fMRI findings generalize beyond the specific treasure-hunting paradigm.
