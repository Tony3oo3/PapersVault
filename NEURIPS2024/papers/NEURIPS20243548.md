## Problem

Learning rate warmup is a standard practice in deep learning, yet its underlying mechanism remains poorly understood, with competing explanations ranging from variance reduction to preventing large early updates. This paper asks: what is the actual mechanism by which warmup helps, and can it be made more efficient? The authors show that prior explanations are incomplete and that the dominant effect is to force the network into lower-sharpness regions of the loss landscape, enabling training at larger target learning rates.

## Input / Output

The study takes standard neural network training inputs (parameters, minibatch gradients, and dataset batches) across FCNs, WideResNets, and GPT-2-style Transformers on CIFAR-10/100, TinyImageNet, and WikiText-2/103. Beyond standard training outputs, the method produces sharpness trajectories (top Hessian eigenvalue) and, for Adam, preconditioned sharpness trajectories (top eigenvalue of P^{-1}∇²L) measured via LOBPCG in float64, which serve as the diagnostic quantities for characterizing warmup regimes. The proposed improvements (GI-Adam, η_init line search) output modified optimizer update rules and a principled initial learning rate, respectively.

## Method

The paper identifies two qualitatively distinct warmup regimes governed by the sign of the natural sharpness drift α: progressive sharpening (α>0, typical of flat initializations like μP) where warmup triggers a persistent catapult cycle, and sharpness reduction (α<0, typical of large initializations like SP) where warmup can piggyback on natural sharpness decrease. For Adam, the key quantity is the preconditioned sharpness λ^{P^{-1}H}, which starts at O(10^5)–O(10^6) due to near-zero gradient components in the initial preconditioner, causing instabilities that standard warmup mitigates. The authors propose GI-Adam, which initializes the second moment as v_0 = g_0², reducing initial preconditioned sharpness by ~√(1−β₂) (roughly two orders of magnitude) and eliminating the need for bias correction on the second moment. They further introduce a two-stage line search (exponential + binary) using only forward passes to estimate the critical learning rate η_c at initialization, setting η_init = η_c to save up to half the warmup duration or eliminate warmup entirely when η_trgt < η_c.

## Results

The paper's primary finding is mechanistic rather than a single benchmark number: warmup's dominant benefit is enabling training at higher target learning rates (η_trgt) by reducing sharpness (or pre-conditioned sharpness for Adam), with final performance determined primarily by η_trgt rather than warmup duration T_wrm. For Adam, the proposed GI-Adam (initializing the second moment with v₀ = g₀²) reduces the initial pre-conditioned sharpness by approximately two orders of magnitude (a factor of √(1−β₂)), consistently pushing the training failure boundary to higher η_trgt across CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet, WikiText-2, and WikiText-103. Setting η_init = η_c (estimated via a forward-pass-only line search) can save up to half the allocated warmup duration, and in some cases eliminate warmup entirely when η_trgt < η_c.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's main quantitative comparison is between the proposed GI-Adam and RAdam [23] (Liu et al., ICLR 2020), the prior art for addressing Adam's early-training instability. Across WRNs on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and TinyImageNet, as well as Pre-LN Transformers on WikiText-2 and WikiText-103, GI-Adam consistently performs on par with or better than RAdam in test accuracy/loss, while requiring only a one-line initialization change (v₀ = g₀²) versus RAdam's more complex variance-based correction. Both methods push the Adam failure boundary to higher target learning rates compared to standard Adam, but GI-Adam achieves this with a simpler mechanism. The paper also contrasts GI-Adam against standard Adam, showing that GI-Adam eliminates training failures (where certain layers output zero and gradients vanish) that standard Adam exhibits at moderate-to-high learning rates, and reduces the severity of early-training loss catapults that degrade performance even short of outright failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the loss-curvature perspective on training instabilities (Gilmer et al. [10]) and the self-stabilization model (Damian et al. [7]) to provide a unified mechanistic account of learning rate warmup across SGD, SGD-momentum, and Adam. It builds directly on the catapult mechanism (Lewkowycz et al. [22]) to explain how warmup reduces sharpness, and departs from the variance-reduction explanation for Adam's warmup benefit (Liu et al. [23]) by showing the key issue is high initial preconditioned sharpness rather than insufficient gradient statistics. The analysis is grounded in the parameterization-dependent sharpness dynamics identified by Kalra & Barkeshli [18, 19], distinguishing progressive-sharpening from sharpness-reduction regimes that determine whether warmup cooperates with or opposes natural training dynamics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides practitioners with a principled framework for understanding when and why warmup helps, yielding concrete improvements: GI-Adam (a one-line initialization change that mimics warmup's benefit) and a line-search-based initial learning rate selection that can eliminate warmup entirely in some cases. The identification of distinct warmup regimes based on initialization (μP vs. SP) offers actionable guidance for choosing parameterizations that reduce warmup dependence. Key limitations include experiments at relatively small scale (WRN-16-4, 4-block Transformers) and no exploration of Adam's β₁, β₂, ε sensitivity; the persistent catapult warmup strategy remains preliminary, and the question of how warmup affects the non-local stability constant c_max is left open.
