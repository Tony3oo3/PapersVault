## Problem

Two-photon holographic optogenetics enables precise, cellular-resolution photostimulation of neural populations, yet almost no algorithmic work exists for determining which stimulation patterns most efficiently reveal the underlying population dynamics. Traditional data-driven modeling is passive (protocols fixed before collection) and correlational, offering no causal interpretability and often oversampling some regions of activity space while missing others. This paper asks: how should one actively design causal circuit perturbations to minimize the data needed to identify low-rank neural population dynamics and the causal connectivity they imply?

## Input / Output

The method consumes (stimulus, response) trial pairs from two-photon calcium imaging at 20 Hz over 500–700 neurons in a 1 mm × 1 mm field of view; each trial is a 150 ms photostimulus (constrained to [0,1]^d with ‖u‖₁ ≤ 30, targeting 10–20 neurons) followed by a 600 ms response window. It produces three outputs: (i) a causal connectivity matrix H ∈ R^{d×d} mapping a single-timestep stimulus to the total population response, estimated via nuclear-norm-constrained regression; (ii) low-rank AR-k model parameters (A_s, B_s each decomposed as diagonal + rank-r term) capturing the full temporal dynamics; and (iii) a sequence of optimal photostimulation patterns selected online to accelerate estimation.

## Method

The core model is a low-rank AR-k system where each coupling matrix is parameterized as diagonal plus rank-r (A_s = D_{A_s} + U_{A_s}V_{A_s}^⊤), capturing per-neuron autocorrelation and direct stimulus response in the diagonal and inter-neuron coupling in the low-rank term. The key theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) is the first estimation-error bound for nuclear-norm regression under arbitrary, non-isotropic inputs, showing the error is governed by input energy projected onto the tangent space of the true matrix rather than by global properties like RIP. Building on this, Algorithm 1 alternates between fitting the current estimate and solving an A-optimal design subproblem (via Frank-Wolfe) that mixes two input distributions: one targeting the top-r right singular vectors of the current estimate (weighted heavily, by a factor of d₁−r) and one playing isotropically to identify the correct subspace. In practice the two distributions are mixed at equal rate to ensure robustness when the singular-vector estimate is inaccurate in early iterations. The procedure is validated both on a data-driven AR-k simulator (achieving 1.5–2× sample reduction over random and uniform baselines) and on real photostimulation data via adaptive trial selection, where it matches or improves upon random ordering in all cases.

## Results

On the data-driven simulator (Section 5.1), the active stimulation design (Algorithm 1) achieves a 1.5–2× reduction in the number of samples needed to reach a given relative error in the off-diagonal causal connectivity matrix H, compared to both Random and Uniform stimulation baselines, across all four mouse datasets and both rank settings (r=15, r=35). On real data (Section 5.2), active trial selection yields up to a 2× reduction in the number of observed trials required to match the prediction MSE of random trial selection on held-out test trials, with the worst case matching the baseline. The low-rank AR-k model (k=4, r=35) achieves near-identical roll-out prediction MSE and AUROC to the full-rank AR-k model, confirming that low-rank structure is sufficient for accurate dynamics modeling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its active learning procedure (Algorithm 1) against two baselines in the simulator experiment (Section 5.1): Random Stimulation (choosing γ=30 neurons uniformly at random per trial) and Uniform Stimulation (playing inputs from the isotropic A-optimal design λ^unif). The active method consistently outperforms both baselines, achieving 1.5–2× fewer samples to reach the same estimation error on Mouse 1 and both Mouse 3 field-of-view datasets; on Mouse 2 the gain is smaller but still present. In the real-data experiment (Section 5.2), the active method is compared against Random trial selection (uniformly sampling unobserved stimulus-response pairs). Active selection achieves up to 2× fewer trials to reach a given held-out MSE in the best splits, while in the worst split it matches the random baseline. The GRU network model (adapted from Pandarinath et al., 2018) is also compared as a dynamics model in Figure 2 but performs worse than the linear AR-k models on both MSE and AUROC, motivating the paper's focus on linear low-rank models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of active system identification, low-rank matrix recovery, and neural circuit perturbation. It extends the active system identification literature (Mehra 1974; Wagenmaker & Jamieson 2020; Mania et al. 2022) by incorporating low-rank structure into the estimation problem, and departs from classical low-rank matrix recovery (Candes & Recht 2012) by removing RIP/incoherence assumptions in favor of a precise characterization of estimation error under arbitrary, non-isotropic inputs (Theorem 1). The experimental platform builds on two-photon holographic optogenetics for causal circuit perturbation (Packer et al. 2015; Daie et al. 2021), but the algorithmic contribution—actively designing stimulation patterns to exploit low-rank structure—is, to the authors' knowledge, the first to demonstrate significant gains in this setting. The A-optimal experiment design step connects to classical optimal design theory (Pukelsheim 2006) solved via Frank-Wolfe.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides both a theoretical tool (the first non-isotropic nuclear-norm regression bound) and a practical protocol for reducing the number of photostimulation trials needed to identify neural population dynamics by up to 2×, which directly addresses a major bottleneck in optogenetic circuit-mapping experiments. The finding that a simple low-rank AR-k model matches or outperforms a GRU network for this data is practically useful, suggesting that the low-dimensional structure of motor cortex dynamics can be captured without the complexity of nonlinear models. Key limitations include the offline nature of the real-data experiments (restricted to 100 pre-defined stimulation patterns), the assumption of linear dynamics, and the uniform error metric across all neurons. Open questions include whether the approach scales to nonlinear dynamics, whether it can target specific dimensions of activity space, and whether the gains hold in truly online closed-loop settings.
