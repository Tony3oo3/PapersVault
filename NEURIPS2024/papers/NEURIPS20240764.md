## Problem

Standard LLM benchmarks like TRIVIAQA draw reference documents from publicly available sources (e.g., Wikipedia), making it impossible to rule out that models have memorized the content during pre-training. This contamination means high scores may reflect memorization rather than genuine reading comprehension, undermining the validity of model comparisons. REPLIQA addresses this gap by constructing a test set whose reference documents are entirely fictional and have never been exposed to the internet or any LLM training pipeline.

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs a ~1,000-word synthetic reference document (delivered as PDF with extracted raw text) with a question about its content. The expected output is either a short ground-truth answer directly extractable from a specific paragraph of the document, or the token UNANSWERABLE for the ~20% of questions whose answers are deliberately absent from the document. For the secondary topic-retrieval task, the model receives the document plus a fixed set of 17 candidate topic labels and must select the correct one. Each document carries exactly five QA pairs, and the long-answer field records the supporting paragraph.

## Method

REPLIQA is built through a multi-stage human annotation pipeline: 80–90 content creators write fictional documents across 17 topics using name generators and anonymization tools to avoid real-world references; documents are then automatically summarized, and question annotators write five specific questions from the summary (not the full text), which naturally yields ~20% unanswerable questions; answer annotators then read the full document and produce answers plus the supporting paragraph. To delay contamination, the 89,770 QA pairs are split into five subsets released bi-monthly from June 2024 to June 2025. The benchmark evaluates 18 state-of-the-art LLMs via the OpenRouter API in a context-conditional setting, measuring recall, F1, exact match, and unanswerable-detection accuracy, and contrasts performance on REPLIQA against TRIVIAQA to isolate the contribution of memorized knowledge versus in-context reading. A key finding is that models consistently perform worse on REPLIQA than TRIVIAQA, and that larger models are not necessarily better at extracting information from provided contexts.

## Results

Across 18 state-of-the-art LLMs, all models perform substantially better on TRIVIAQA (seen content) than on REPLIQA (unseen content). On REPLIQA₀, Mistral Large achieves the highest recall (72.29%) and GPT-4o the highest F1 (61.90), while on TRIVIAQA, GPT-4o leads with 86.80% recall and 83.43% F1. The recall gap between the two datasets ranges from ~14 points (Mistral Large: 86.79→72.29) to ~40 points (Command R+: 86.46→46.40). When reference documents are removed, TRIVIAQA recall barely drops (e.g., GPT-4o: 86.80→92.96%), whereas REPLIQA recall collapses (GPT-4o: 60.85→8.59%), confirming that models rely on memorized knowledge for seen content but must genuinely read for unseen content.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole external baseline is TRIVIAQA, a well-known reading-comprehension dataset whose reference documents are drawn from Wikipedia and the open web. Every one of the 18 evaluated LLMs scores significantly higher on TRIVIAQA than on REPLIQA₀, with recall gaps ranging from ~14 points (Mistral Large: 86.79% → 72.29%) to ~40 points (Command R+: 86.46% → 46.40%). A critical control experiment removes the reference document entirely: on TRIVIAQA, recall barely changes (GPT-4o even improves from 86.80% to 92.96%), while on REPLIQA₀ recall collapses to single digits (GPT-4o: 60.85% → 8.59%; Llama 3 70B: 56.39% → 16.80%). This asymmetry demonstrates that models answer TRIVIAQA questions from memorized pre-training knowledge rather than from reading the provided context, whereas REPLIQA forces genuine in-context reading. The comparison also reveals that larger models are not consistently better at reading: GPT-3.5 outperforms GPT-4o on REPLIQA₀ recall (58.98% vs. 60.85% is close, but GPT-3.5's F1 of 33.31 vs. GPT-4o's 61.90 shows GPT-4o is stronger overall), while Command R outperforms Command R+ (50.16% vs. 46.40% recall) and Claude Haiku outperforms Claude Sonnet on recall (65.80% vs. 66.54% is close), indicating that scale does not monotonically improve reading ability on unseen content.

## Relation to Prior Work

REPLIQA addresses a well-recognized gap in LLM evaluation: the inability to rule out data contamination when testing on publicly available benchmarks like TRIVIAQA or SQuAD. Rather than building on a specific prior method, it repurposes the standard reading-comprehension/QA task by ensuring all reference documents are synthetic and previously inaccessible, thereby isolating a model's actual reading ability from memorized knowledge. The evaluation protocol (F1, recall, EM metrics) follows Adlakha et al. [2023], and the staggered-release strategy for maintaining dataset integrity over time is a novel curation approach not seen in prior QA benchmarks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

REPLIQA provides the field with a practical tool to evaluate whether LLMs genuinely read provided context or simply recall training data, which is critical for retrieval-augmented generation deployments where proprietary documents are the source of truth. The finding that model scale does not reliably improve reading comprehension—and that smaller models sometimes outperform larger ones in context-conditional settings—challenges the default assumption that 'bigger is better' for RAG pipelines. Open questions include what architectural or training factors make models less memory-dependent, whether the synthetic-document approach generalizes to other languages or domains, and how long the staggered-release strategy can maintain dataset integrity as LLM training corpora grow.
