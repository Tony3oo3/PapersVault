## Problem

Existing action quality assessment (AQA) methods are designed for real-world, domain-specific actions (diving, gymnastics, workouts) and rely on normative features pre-trained on action recognition datasets, making them inapplicable to AI-generated videos that contain atypical actions with artifacts such as aberrant limb counts, irrational object shapes, and physically implausible motion. No dataset or benchmark exists for evaluating action quality in AI-generated videos from a human perception perspective, leaving it unclear how well current T2V models generate visually rational actions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241267/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs an AI-generated video clip (resolutions ranging from 256×256 to 2048×1536, 4–50 FPS, 1–4 s duration) with a text prompt describing the target action, generated by one of 18 T2V models. Rather than a single quality score, each video receives three continuous [0,100] ratings structured as a causal reasoning syllogism: subject quality (major premise), action completeness (minor premise), and action-scene interaction (conclusion), yielding 971,244 total ratings across 9,180 video-action pairs from 54 participants.

## Method

The core contribution is the GAIA dataset built around a novel 'action syllogism' evaluation framework that decomposes action quality into three causally ordered dimensions aligned with human reasoning: the action subject as major premise, action completeness as minor premise, and action-scene interaction as conclusion. 510 action prompts were assembled via GPT-4 from Kinetics-400 (whole-body), EgoGesture (hand), and valence-arousal (facial) sources, then fed to 18 T2V models (11 open-source lab studies and 7 commercial platforms) to produce 9,180 videos. Subjective evaluation employed a single-stimulus protocol with 54 participants, quality-controlled via pre-labeling eligibility tests, golden-video checks, and Krippendorff's α inter-annotator agreement verification. The paper then benchmarks 4 conventional AQA methods, 6 action-related metrics from VBench/EvalCrafter, 7 VQA methods, and 7 video-text alignment metrics against human ratings, revealing that all perform poorly (average SRCC of 0.454, 0.191, and 0.519 respectively), exposing a sizable gap between current automatic metrics and human action perception in AI-generated scenarios.

## Results

On the GAIA dataset (9,180 AI-generated videos, 971,244 ratings), all existing automatic evaluation methods perform poorly at action quality assessment. Traditional AQA methods achieve an average SRCC of only 0.454, action-related metrics from T2V benchmarks (VBench, EvalCrafter) achieve just 0.191, and VQA methods reach 0.519. The best-performing method overall is DOVER (a VQA model) with 0.5335 SRCC on the All-Combined dimension, while the best AQA method is TSA at 0.4861 SRCC. Even the strongest video-text alignment metric (InternLMScore) only reaches 0.4314 SRCC, confirming a sizable gap between current models and human action perception in AI-generated videos.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 17 existing methods across four categories on GAIA, and the central finding is that none achieves strong correlation with human action quality judgments. Among AQA methods, TSA (CVPR'22) performs best at 0.4861 SRCC (All-Combined), marginally ahead of ACTION-NET (0.4587) and CoRe (0.4437), all of which rely on Kinetics-pretrained I3D backbones ill-suited to AI-generated artifacts. Action-related metrics from VBench and EvalCrafter are the weakest category: Human Action (0.2831) and Action-Score (0.2600) are the best, while Flow-Score (0.1166) and Dynamic Degree (0.1162) are nearly uncorrelated with human ratings. VQA methods substantially outperform both AQA and action metrics, with DOVER leading at 0.5335 SRCC (All-Combined) and 0.6173 on Subject Quality, though all VQA methods still fall well below 0.65. Video-text alignment metrics (CLIPScore variants, BLIPScore, LLaVAAScore, InternLMScore) show moderate performance (0.36–0.43 SRCC), with InternLMScore (0.4314) being the best, suggesting that more powerful VLMs may eventually bridge the gap. Overall, the paper demonstrates that no existing method exceeds 0.54 SRCC, highlighting a critical gap in automatic action quality assessment for AI-generated videos.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of action quality assessment (AQA) and AI-generated video (AIGV) evaluation. Prior AQA work is confined to real-world, domain-specific actions (diving, gymnastics, skating) with coarse-grained expert/judge ratings, while existing T2V benchmarks (VBench, EvalCrafter) include only narrow action-related metrics adapted from real-world action recognition. GAIA departs from both lines by (1) targeting AI-generated videos with their characteristic artifacts (aberrant limbs, physically implausible motion), (2) introducing a causal-reasoning syllogism framework that decomposes action quality into subject quality, action completeness, and action-scene interaction, and (3) collecting large-scale ratings from a diverse participant pool rather than domain experts. The paper does not propose a new AQA algorithm but instead uses the dataset to systematically diagnose why existing AQA, action-metric, and VQA methods all fail in the AIGV setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The systematic demonstration that no existing AQA method (avg. SRCC 0.454), action-related metric (0.191), or VQA method (0.519) correlates well with human action-quality perception in AIGVs establishes a clear and urgent gap that the field has not previously quantified. The 971K-rating dataset spanning 18 T2V models and 510 action prompts provides a much-needed testbed for developing AIGV-specific AQA algorithms, and the causal-reasoning annotation scheme offers a principled decomposition that may guide future model design. Key limitations include the restricted subject types and styles, limited video resolutions/lengths due to model constraints, and the departure from conventional single-score annotation that may complicate direct comparison with prior AQA literature. Open questions include whether the syllogism-based decomposition generalizes to more complex multi-agent or long-form actions, and how to build automatic metrics that capture temporal coherence and scene interaction rather than just spatial fidelity.
