## Problem

Existing formal theorem-proving benchmarks (MINIF2F, FIMO) rely on high-school mathematics, target only one or two proof assistants, and contain problems trivially solvable by SMT solvers, while the growing role of LLMs in theorem-proving makes dataset contamination a critical concern. PUTNAMBENCH addresses these gaps by providing a fresh, multi-language benchmark of undergraduate-level competition problems that demands broad mathematical knowledge beyond what prior benchmarks cover.

## Input / Output

Each problem is presented as a formal theorem statement in Lean 4, Isabelle, or Coq, and the model must produce a complete formal proof verifiable by the respective proof assistant. A distinctive design choice is the 'factored solution' mechanism: for the ~60% of Putnam problems requiring a closed-form answer, the solution is defined as a separate constant outside the theorem statement, yielding two evaluation tasks—(1) identify the solution and prove it, or (2) prove the given solution correct. The benchmark contains 640 problems in Lean 4 and Isabelle and 412 in Coq (1,692 total formalizations), spanning algebra, analysis, number theory, geometry, and other undergraduate topics.

## Method

PUTNAMBENCH is a manually constructed benchmark of 1,692 formalizations of 640 Putnam competition problems, hand-crafted over several months by a team of two doctoral and five undergraduate students, with each formalization independently verified. The evaluation protocol tests seven approaches across the three languages: GPT-4 with in-context learning (pass@10), COPRA (agentic proof search with 60 queries and backtracking), ReProver (retrieval-augmented fine-tuned model), DSP (neurosymbolic pipeline where GPT-4 generates natural-language proof sketches filled by Sledgehammer), Sledgehammer (standalone SMT-based automation), Tactician (LSH-based tactic prediction), and CoqHammer (external constraint solver calls). The central finding is that collectively these methods solve only 6 out of 640 problems, with Sledgehammer dominating in Isabelle (3/640, all involving binary operations) and the single problem solved in both Lean 4 and Coq (Putnam 1988 B1) requiring only a single non-trivial witness choice followed by automated linear-arithmetic and ring tactics.

## Results

Across all three proof assistants, current neural and symbolic theorem-provers solve at most a handful of PUTNAMBENCH problems. In Lean 4 (640 problems), GPT-4 and COPRA each solve 1/640 (Putnam 1988 B1), while ReProver solves 0/640 with or without retrieval. In Isabelle (640 problems), DSP achieves the best result at 4/640, Sledgehammer solves 3/640, and GPT-4 solves 1/640. In Coq (412 problems), GPT-4 and COPRA each solve 1/412, while Tactician and CoqHammer solve 0/412. In total, only 6 distinct problems are proven across all languages and methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates seven methods across three proof assistants, and all perform very poorly, establishing PUTNAMBENCH as a hard open challenge. DSP (Jiang et al., 2022b) achieves the best single result at 4/640 in Isabelle, solving Putnam 2001 A1, 1971 B1, 1995 A1, and 1986 B1; two of these (1995 A1, 1986 B1) cannot be solved by Sledgehammer alone. Sledgehammer (Paulson & Blanchette, 2015) solves 3/640 in Isabelle, all involving algebraic properties of binary operations (magmas), demonstrating that SMT-based automation handles simple algebraic reasoning but fails on analysis or number-theoretic problems. GPT-4 in few-shot mode solves exactly one problem per language (Putnam 1988 B1 in Lean and Coq, Putnam 1986 B1 in Isabelle), and COPRA matches this in both Lean and Coq. ReProver (with and without retrieval), Tactician, and CoqHammer all solve zero problems, indicating that retrieval-augmented smaller models and Coq-specific automation tools are entirely unable to handle competition-level mathematics. The only problem solved in more than one language is Putnam 1988 B1 (Lean and Coq), which requires a single non-trivial witness choice followed by automated linear arithmetic.

## Relation to Prior Work

PUTNAMBENCH extends the line of formal theorem-proving benchmarks (MINIF2F, FIMO, PROOFNET) by shifting from high-school or textbook-level problems to undergraduate competition mathematics, and by being the first to simultaneously cover all three major proof assistants (Lean 4, Isabelle, Coq). It departs from prior benchmarks in two key ways: it uses Lean 4 rather than the now-legacy Lean 3, and it introduces a 'factored solution' mechanism that separates the task of finding a closed-form answer from proving its correctness, preserving the full difficulty of problems that require exhibiting a solution. The empirical evaluation draws on a broad set of existing methods—COPRA, DSP, Sledgehammer, CoqHammer, Tactician, ReProver, and raw GPT-4—positioning the benchmark as a common yardstick across the neurosymbolic, retrieval-augmented, and purely symbolic theorem-proving communities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-total failure of all evaluated methods (only 6 problems solved out of 640 in Lean/Isabelle, 1 out of 412 in Coq) establishes PUTNAMBENCH as a genuinely hard open challenge, with the authors identifying two root causes: the inability of current provers to synthesize new lemmas and orchestrate them into multi-step proofs, and the failure to leverage the deep mathematical content in libraries like Mathlib and MathComp. The multi-language design and factored-solution mechanism make the benchmark directly useful for tracking progress across the entire formal-mathematics ecosystem, and its alignment with the IMO Grand Challenge and the $10M AIMO Prize gives it strong incentive alignment. A key open question is whether the gap will close through better search, better library utilization, or fundamentally new architectures, and whether the Coq formalizations (which rely on a patchwork of repositories) will remain maintainable as those libraries evolve.
