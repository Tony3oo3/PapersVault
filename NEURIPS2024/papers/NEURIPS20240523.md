## Problem

Existing ICL theories typically assume structured training data that mirrors ICL tasks (e.g., x-y pairs for linear regression), yet LLMs are actually trained unsupervised on unstructured text like web content. This paper asks what structural elements within unstructured pre-training data are necessary for ICL to emerge, and characterizes scenarios where ICL provably fails regardless of model architecture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240523/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Models consume in-context prompts of the form c₁d₁…cₗdₗcₗ₊₁ (word pair sequences) or token sequences with repeating patterns (e.g., abacdc), and produce a single next-token prediction. Training data consists of unstructured sentences of fixed length S containing co-occurring word pairs, repeating token patterns, or inserted nuisance tokens. The paper evaluates both classical CBOW models and autoregressive transformers (1-layer and 5-layer) with learned, sinusoidal, or no positional embeddings, as well as LLaMA 2 on real country-capital data.

## Method

The paper provides a theoretical characterization of when ICL emerges from unstructured pre-training, using CBOW with squared loss as the analytical tool. For word analogy tasks, Theorem 1 proves that ICL arises purely from co-occurrence modeling without positional encoding or attention, provided the prompt length satisfies a bound depending on vocabulary size and sentence length. For logic reasoning tasks requiring pattern recognition and generalization to unseen tokens, Theorem 3 proves positional information is necessary, and Theorem 4 shows that blocked (consecutive) nuisance token structure is critical for ICL success. The paper further identifies two provable failure scenarios: Theorem 5 shows ICL fails when the in-context meta-pattern of repetition differs from all training patterns, and Theorem 6 shows ICL fails when co-occurring word pairs are restricted to fixed positions in training sentences. Empirical validation combines LLaMA 2 experiments on real country-capital data with extensive synthetic experiments across CBOW and autoregressive transformer configurations.

## Results

On LLaMA 2, ICL accuracy for country-capital completion is 0.58 (type A: capital ≠ largest city) vs. 0.96 (type B: capital = largest city), and 0.69 vs. 0.84 for US states, confirming that co-occurrence frequency drives ICL. For logic reasoning tasks (predicting x₁ from x₁x₂x₃x₁), a 1-layer transformer with learned or sinusoidal positional embeddings achieves 1.0 accuracy when all vocabulary tokens appear as first tokens in both train and test sets, but drops to 0.30 without positional embeddings; a 5-layer model recovers to 0.89 without positional embeddings. In the block-noisy single-pattern setting, learned positional embeddings yield 0.95–1.00 accuracy versus 0.01–0.55 for sinusoidal/RoPE, while the one-noisy scenario collapses to 0.00–0.10 across all configurations. In both identified failure scenarios (meta-pattern generalization and fixed-position co-occurrence), all architectures achieve 0.00–0.01 accuracy regardless of embedding dimension or positional encoding.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical paper, so comparisons are between model configurations rather than competing methods. The key comparison is between positional embedding strategies: learned and sinusoidal embeddings are equivalent for the 1-layer logic task (both achieve 1.0), while no positional embeddings drops accuracy to 0.30 (1-layer) or 0.89 (5-layer), confirming Theorem 3's necessity result. For the single-pattern task with block-noisy nuisance tokens, learned positional embeddings (0.95–1.00) substantially outperform sinusoidal (0.01–0.55) and RoPE (0.00–0.03), suggesting that learned embeddings better capture the blocked structure. In the dual-pattern task, 5-layer models with learned embeddings and d_E=100 reach 0.97–0.98 accuracy in the clean setting, while 1-layer models plateau at 0.33–0.51, consistent with the induction-head hypothesis requiring at least two layers. CBOW and transformer models are compared on a synthetic country-capital/IOC corpus: CBOW achieves 0.65–0.82 accuracy for country-capital and 0.15–0.79 for country-IOC, while the transformer achieves 0.00–0.34 and 0.77–1.00 respectively, showing the transformer excels when the training pattern (IOC always follows country) matches the ICL prompt structure. In both failure scenarios, no configuration (1-layer/5-layer, learned/sinusoidal, d_E=10/100) exceeds 0.01 accuracy, confirming the theoretical impossibility results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant ICL theory literature (gradient descent, Bayesian inference, Newton's method formulations) that assumes structured training data mirroring ICL tasks (e.g., x-y pairs for linear regression). Instead, it asks what enables ICL when training data is unstructured natural language, and connects ICL to the classical CBOW language model [36], showing that co-occurrence modeling alone suffices for word analogy ICL without attention or positional encoding. It also aligns with Chen et al. [13] on the role of parallel structures in pre-training data, but formalizes the necessity of positional information and blocked nuisance structure for logic-reasoning ICL tasks. The failure scenarios extend the data-diversity findings of Raventós et al. [42] and Yadlowsky et al. [63] with precise characterizations of when ICL provably breaks down.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides a more realistic theoretical lens on ICL by grounding the analysis in unstructured data rather than idealized task-mirroring corpora, and identifies concrete structural requirements (co-occurrence, positional information, blocked nuisance patterns) that must be present in pre-training data for ICL to emerge. The two provable failure scenarios—meta-pattern generalization and fixed-position co-occurrence—offer a principled explanation for why LLMs struggle with certain ICL tasks regardless of scale or architecture. Limitations include reliance on synthetic data, simplified model architectures, and the squared-loss-to-cross-entropy gap in CBOW experiments; open questions include whether these structural requirements hold in real LLMs trained on actual web corpora and how they interact with the many other factors (scale, data mixture, architecture depth) that influence ICL in practice.
