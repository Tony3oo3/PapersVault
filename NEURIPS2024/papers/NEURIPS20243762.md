## Problem

In cooperative MARL under centralized training with decentralized execution, agents decide simultaneously and cannot communicate actual actions before acting, so they may miscoordinate when multiple equally optimal joint actions exist. Existing communication methods only share observations or historical trajectories, leaving the circular dependency between agents' decisions unresolved. The paper asks whether treating agents asynchronously—assigning a dynamically determined priority of decision-making at each step—can enable explicit action coordination and escape suboptimal equilibria caused by relative overgeneralization.

## Input / Output

Each agent consumes its own observation (encoded to a hidden state), the hidden states of all other agents during the negotiation phase, and the actual actions of upper-level agents during the launching phase. The output is a joint action vector executed simultaneously in the environment, even though individual decisions are made sequentially. A learned world model additionally takes joint hidden states and predicted actions as input to produce H-step future trajectory predictions, which are scored by the critic to yield intention values used for priority selection.

## Method

SeqComm introduces a two-phase communication scheme built on MAPPO with parameter sharing across agents. In the negotiation phase, agents exchange hidden states, then each agent computes an intention value by using a learned world model to roll out H-step trajectories under the assumption that it is the first mover, averaging over F randomly sampled orderings of the remaining agents; the agent with the highest intention value is designated first mover, and the process recurses to fix the full priority order. In the launching phase, agents decide sequentially from highest to lowest priority, each conditioning its policy on the actual (not predicted) actions of all upper-level agents via an attention module that also handles variable agent counts. The paper proves that the joint policy improves monotonically and converges regardless of the priority order chosen at each step (Propositions 1–2), and derives a bound on the gap between model-based and true returns that quantifies when world-model errors make the selected order unreliable (Theorem 1). A local-communication variant restricts negotiation to nearby agents within a limited range, reducing overhead at the cost of coarser priority information.

## Results

SeqComm is evaluated on nine SMACv2 maps (protoss/terran/zerg at 5v5, 10v10, and 10v11) with reduced sight range (3 vs. default 9) and no ally perception, making coordination essential. The primary metric is win rate, reported as mean ± std over five random seeds. SeqComm (full communication) achieves the highest win rate across all nine maps, outperforming TarMAC, MAIC, and CommFormer, while the local-communication variant (range 3) also surpasses all baselines at the cost of slightly lower peak performance. Ablation studies confirm that the learned priority of decision-making (SeqComm) outperforms both random ordering and no-action-communication variants on every map.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MAPPO (the communication-free baseline built on the same PPO backbone), SeqComm achieves substantially higher win rates on all nine maps, confirming that explicit communication is critical under the high partial-observability and stochasticity settings of SMACv2. Against TarMAC (the strongest communication-based baseline, using two-round targeted communication), SeqComm outperforms it on every map, which the authors attribute to the gain from explicit action coordination in the launching phase rather than merely sharing observation-derived messages. In the appendix (Figure 8), SeqComm also surpasses MAIC and CommFormer on six maps (5v5 and 10v10 for all three races), with the full-communication version consistently ahead of the local-communication version. The ablation against a random-ordering baseline (Random) shows that SeqComm's learned priority determination is essential: Random converges to poor local optima and, surprisingly, performs worse than the no-action-communication variant (No action) in most tasks, validating that a bad decision-making order can actively impair coordination.

## Relation to Prior Work

SeqComm builds on MAPPO as its policy backbone but departs fundamentally from existing communication-based MARL methods (TarMAC, IC3Net, I2C) that focus on extracting valuable messages from partial observations. It also distinguishes itself from intention-sharing approaches (IS, Kim et al. 2021) that use world models to predict and communicate future trajectories, by treating agents asynchronously rather than synchronously, thereby resolving the circular dependency problem inherent in simultaneous decision-making. The conceptual lineage connects to Stackelberg equilibrium and coordination graph literature, but the key novelty is the two-phase communication scheme (negotiation via intention values, then launching via actual action transfer) that dynamically determines decision-making priority without requiring a fixed ordering.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical guarantee (monotonic improvement and convergence independent of priority order) that is rare in communication-based MARL, and the ablation showing that a random priority order can perform worse than no action communication at all is a useful cautionary result for practitioners. The local communication variant makes the approach more deployable in bandwidth-constrained settings. However, the method is currently instantiated only on MAPPO and evaluated solely on SMACv2, leaving open questions about scalability to larger agent counts, applicability to continuous action spaces, and sensitivity to world model quality in more complex or partially observable environments.
