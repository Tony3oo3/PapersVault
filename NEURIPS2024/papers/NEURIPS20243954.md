## Problem

Existing optimal tax design for congestion games assumes the designer has full knowledge of the underlying cost functions, which is unrealistic in practice. This paper tackles the first step of learning the optimal (marginal cost) tax with only equilibrium feedback — the designer can deploy a tax and observe the resulting Nash equilibrium load and cost, but cannot directly query the cost function. The core challenges are the exponentially large function space of taxes, the non-differentiability of the Nash equilibrium mapping, and the nonconvexity of the social cost objective with respect to tax.

## Input / Output

At each round, the designer selects a piece-wise linear tax function over the facility set F and observes the induced Nash equilibrium load y^t ∈ ℝ^F and the Nash cost vector c^t ∈ ℝ^F (the cost of each facility at its equilibrium load). The algorithm requires as hyperparameters the smoothness coefficient β of the cost functions and the target tolerance ε, and operates on a fixed grid of K = ⌈2β/ε⌉ points in [0,1]. The output is a piece-wise linear tax function τ_f(u) = τ̂_f(u) + εu for each facility f, where τ̂_f is a non-decreasing interpolant on the grid, achieving 6εF-optimality in social cost within O(F²β/ε) rounds.

## Method

The algorithm maintains a piece-wise linear approximation of the marginal cost tax τ*_f(u) = u·c_f'(u) on a uniform grid, adding an extra linear term εu to enforce a strongly convex potential function (ensuring unique, Lipschitz Nash equilibrium w.r.t. tax perturbation). At each round, facilities are partitioned into known (Nash load bracketed by two grid points with accurate tax estimates) and unknown. A dedicated exploratory subroutine (Algorithm 2) identifies a 'boundary' tax — one where the current equilibrium is on the edge of the feasible set — so that a small perturbation δ = εΔ²/8 in one facility's tax provably shifts the equilibrium by at most Δ, enabling a two-point gradient estimate of c_f' via the observed cost difference. The estimate is clipped to preserve monotonicity of the running tax approximation. Each round adds at most one new known grid point per facility, and the algorithm terminates when all facilities are fully characterized, yielding O(F²β/ε) total samples with no dependence on the (potentially exponential) number of actions.

## Results

The main theoretical guarantee (Theorem 1) establishes that Algorithm 1 outputs a 6εF-optimal tax within T ≤ 2Fβ/ε rounds, yielding O(F²β/ε) sample complexity with no dependence on the (potentially exponential) number of actions. Each round requires at most two tax realizations. In experiments on the nonlinear Pigou's example (one source, one target, two edges with costs c₀(x)=c and c₁(x)=x^p), the social welfare converges quickly to the optimal value across all tested parameter combinations (c ∈ {0.2, 0.6, 1}, p ∈ {2, 4}), and the learned piece-wise linear tax is accurate at the induced Nash equilibrium even though it does not uniformly converge to the marginal cost tax.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper presents the first algorithm for learning optimal tax in congestion games with equilibrium feedback, so no direct empirical baselines are compared. The authors explain why existing approaches are inapplicable: (1) naive enumeration of ε-approximations to the marginal cost tax requires O((1/ε)^(Fβ/ε)) samples, giving exponential dependence on F, β, and 1/ε; (2) MPEC/gradient-based methods (Li et al. 2020, Liu et al. 2022) require a differentiable and unique equilibrium, which fails here as y(τ) can be discontinuous (Lemma 5) and Ψ(y(τ)) is nonconvex in τ; (3) Stackelberg learning results (Bai et al. 2021, Zhong et al. 2021) assume finite actions or linear function approximation, which are vacuous for the exponentially large tax function space. The computational complexity for network congestion games is Õ(poly(V, E, m)), avoiding the exponential dependence on the number of paths A.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the classical optimal tax design literature (Nisan et al. 2007, Caragiannis et al. 2010, Paccagnan et al. 2021), which all assume the designer has full knowledge of the cost functions and can directly compute the marginal cost tax τ* = u·c_f'(u). It also differs from prior learning-in-congestion-games work (Krichene et al. 2015, Cui et al. 2022, Panageas et al. 2023), which is formulated from the player's perspective rather than the system designer's. The algorithm draws on nonparametric piecewise linear approximation (Takezawa 2005) to avoid parameterization assumptions, and on Stackelberg game / MPEC formulations conceptually, but sidesteps their requirements for differentiable equilibria, convexity, or finite action spaces by introducing an exploratory tax subroutine that pushes the Nash equilibrium to a 'boundary' where a small perturbation reveals information about unknown facilities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first result showing that the optimal tax in nonatomic congestion games can be learned with polynomial sample complexity O(F²β/ε) using only equilibrium feedback, with no dependence on the (potentially exponential) number of actions. The conjecture that this rate is tight, combined with the efficient network implementation via shortest-path algorithms, makes the result practically relevant for traffic toll design. Key open questions include extending to atomic congestion games, relaxing the Nash equilibrium assumption to no-regret dynamics, and removing the need for prior knowledge of the smoothness parameter β. The experimental validation is currently limited to the two-edge Pigou example, leaving scalability to larger networks untested.
