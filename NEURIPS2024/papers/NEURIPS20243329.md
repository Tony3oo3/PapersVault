## Problem

Most causal representation learning methods assume Markovian latent causal graphs (no unobserved confounding) and restricted data configurations—either interventions within a single domain or observational distributions from multiple domains. This paper addresses the more general non-Markovian setting where latent variables may be confounded, and where data arises from arbitrary combinations of interventions and domains, while also relaxing the disentanglement goal from full identifiability to a user-specified causal disentanglement map.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243329/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a latent selection diagram (LSD) encoding the shared latent causal graph plus S-nodes marking cross-domain mechanism shifts, a collection of intervention target sets specifying which variables were intervened on (and whether perfectly) in which domain, and the resulting observed distributions over the high-dimensional mixture X. It produces a Causal Disentanglement Map (CDM): a bipartite graph between true latent variables V and learned representations V̂, where the absence of an edge V_i → V̂_j certifies that V_j is identifiable with respect to V_i. The CDM can encode partial disentanglement (some edges remain) rather than requiring full separation of all latent factors.

## Method

The core technical contribution is a distribution-comparison framework built on the non-Markovian factorization (using c-components to handle confounding), which identifies invariant factors that cancel when comparing two distributions. From this, three graphical criteria are derived: Prop. 3 disentangles the ΔQ set (variables whose factors change) from canceled variables; Prop. 4 disentangles variables within a ΔQ set using conditional independence in the intervened subgraph, requiring a minimum number of distributions; and Prop. 5 handles the reverse direction by leveraging already-established disentanglements. The CRID algorithm iteratively applies these criteria over all combinations of perfect-intervention sets and baseline interventions, progressively removing edges from an initially fully-connected bipartite graph until convergence. The key distinction from prior Markovian work is that the ΔQ set is computed via c-components of the changed-variable set, so confounded variables propagate changes through their entire c-component rather than just along directed paths.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, establishing three graphical criteria (Props. 3–5) and the CRID algorithm for determining causal disentanglement in non-Markovian settings. Experimental validation uses synthetic data (chain, collider, and non-Markovian graphs) and MNIST, evaluating via Mean Correlation Coefficient (MCC) between learned representations and true latent variables. In all tested settings, MCC values are consistent with CRID's predictions: e.g., in the chain graph, MCC(V̂₃, V₁) is low relative to MCC(V̂₃, V₃), confirming V₃ is disentangled from V₁; in the non-Markovian graph, V₃ is fully disentangled from {V₁, V₂, V₄} using only two perfect interventions on V₃. No prior method achieves disentanglement in the non-Markovian (confounded) setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its identifiability guarantees against two key prior methods. Against Causal Component Analysis [21] (Wendong et al., 2023), the proposed method achieves disentanglement of V₃ from V₁ in the chain graph, whereas [21] concludes V₃ remains entangled with V₁ because V₁ ∈ Anc(V₃) — the non-Markovian relaxation and multi-domain data enable a strictly stronger result. Against Causal Representation Learning from Multiple Distributions [22] (Zhang et al., 2024), the proposed method disentangles V₁ from V₃ in the collider graph, while [22] (which assumes a Markovian setting) declares V₁ and V₃ non-disentangleable due to their adjacency in the Markov network. In both cases, the paper's method wins by exploiting the non-Markovian (confounded) structure and arbitrary combinations of interventions across domains, achieving disentanglement that prior Markovian-only methods provably cannot.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal representation learning lineage from ICA through Causal Component Analysis [21] and multi-distribution causal representation learning [22], building on the non-Markovian factorization of Bareinboim et al. [3] and the selection diagram formalism from transportability theory [33, 35, 36]. The key departures are threefold: (i) relaxing the Markovian assumption to allow unobserved confounding among latent variables, (ii) permitting arbitrary combinations of interventions and domain shifts rather than restricting to one or the other, and (iii) replacing the requirement for full disentanglement with a relaxed 'causal disentanglement map' that identifies which specific variable pairs are disentangleable. The CRID algorithm operationalizes these theoretical criteria into a practical decision procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to provide identifiability guarantees for causal representation learning in the presence of unobserved confounding, a pervasive challenge in empirical sciences where latent variables are rarely independent. The CDM output is practically useful: rather than a binary 'disentangled or not' answer, it tells practitioners exactly which variables can be separated given their available data and assumptions, directly informing experimental design. Key limitations include the requirement for known intervention targets, shared causal structure across domains, and invertible mixing; open questions include extending to unknown intervention targets, structural changes across domains, and scaling the CRID algorithm to larger graphs.
