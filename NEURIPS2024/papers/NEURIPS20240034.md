## Problem

Existing open-source singing datasets are constrained by low recording quality, limited language and singer diversity, and poor multi-task suitability—they lack controlled comparisons with phoneme-level annotations for multiple singing techniques, realistic music scores for real-world composition, and paired speech for speech-to-singing conversion. This scarcity of high-quality, multi-task singing data has become a major bottleneck for developing controllable and personalized singing systems across diverse languages and styles.

## Input / Output

Each of the 1,366 songs is structured as a triplet: a control group (natural singing), a technique group (densely applying one specific technique), and a paired speech recording, all sharing identical lyrics, rhythm, and key. Audio is captured at 48 kHz / 24-bit WAV; annotations are stored in TextGrid format with binary phoneme-level technique labels for six techniques, global style labels (singing method, emotion, pace, range), and realistic music scores in MusicXML with regular note durations. The four benchmarks consume these differently: SVS takes lyrics + music scores + technique sequence, recognition takes mel-spectrogram + pitch + phoneme boundaries, style transfer takes reference audio + target lyrics, and STS conversion takes paired speech + music scores.

## Method

The core contribution is a dataset construction pipeline built around a controlled comparison design: each song is recorded twice under identical conditions (natural vs. technique-intensive), enabling direct technique modeling and recognition. Realistic music scores are composed by music experts who refine auto-extracted MIDI (via RMVPE + ROSVOT) to enforce regular note durations, explicitly distinguishing them from fine-grained scores that fragment notes and impede real-world composition. Phoneme-level technique annotations are performed by music-trained annotators in Praat, marking binary presence/absence for six techniques per phoneme, with a multi-stage verification process including 25% random expert inspection per language. Four benchmarks are established using state-of-the-art systems (DiffSinger, RMSSinger, StyleSinger, AlignSTS) enhanced with phoneme-level technique embeddings, evaluated via FFE, MCD, cosine similarity, and MOS across both parallel and cross-lingual settings to validate dataset quality and task coverage.

## Results

Across four benchmark tasks on GTSinger, StyleSinger consistently achieves the best performance among all baselines. In technique-controllable SVS (parallel), StyleSinger attains FFE 0.25, MCD 3.27, MOS-Q 4.01, and MOS-C 4.15, outperforming DiffSinger (MOS-Q 3.81, MOS-C 3.83) and RMSSinger (MOS-Q 3.94, MOS-C 3.95). In technique recognition, breathy and falsetto achieve the highest F1 scores (0.99 and 0.96), while vibrato and glissando are the most challenging (F1 0.70). In STS conversion, StyleSinger (MOS-Q 3.83, MOS-S 3.88) surpasses AlignSTS (MOS-Q 3.68, MOS-S 3.73), and in style transfer, StyleSinger reaches MOS-S 4.01 (parallel) and 3.92 (cross-lingual). All methods remain notably below GT(vocoder) (MOS-Q 4.21, MOS-C 4.42), indicating substantial room for improvement.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StyleSinger [28] outperforms both DiffSinger [15] and RMSSinger [7] across all four benchmark tasks and all metrics. In technique-controllable SVS, StyleSinger improves MOS-C by 0.32 over DiffSinger (4.15 vs. 3.83) and by 0.20 over RMSSinger (4.15 vs. 3.95), with the largest gap in FFE (0.25 vs. 0.29 for DiffSinger). In style transfer, StyleSinger achieves Cos 0.93 vs. RMSSinger's 0.88 and MOS-S 4.01 vs. 3.79 (parallel), and maintains a 0.16 MOS-S advantage in cross-lingual settings (3.92 vs. 3.76). In STS conversion, StyleSinger beats AlignSTS [13] on every metric (FFE 0.28 vs. 0.35, MCD 3.38 vs. 3.52, Cos 0.92 vs. 0.85, MOS-Q 3.83 vs. 3.68, MOS-S 3.88 vs. 3.73), partly because StyleSinger uses realistic music scores while AlignSTS requires GT singing F0 as input. Notably, all learned methods still trail GT(vocoder) by 0.16–0.42 MOS points, confirming that current SVS models have not yet closed the gap to natural singing quality on this high-quality corpus.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions GTSinger as a comprehensive response to the fragmented landscape of existing singing datasets, each of which addresses only one or two limitations (e.g., VocalSet for controlled comparison, M4Singer for music scores, NHSS for paired speech). Methodologically, it builds on ROSVOT for both music score derivation and the technique recognition model, and leverages RMVPE for F0 extraction and the Montreal Forced Aligner for initial phoneme alignment. The benchmark experiments reuse and extend recent SVS systems (DiffSinger, RMSSinger, StyleSinger) by adding phoneme-level technique embeddings, rather than proposing a new synthesis architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GTSinger is the largest recorded singing corpus to date (80.59 hours, 9 languages, 20 singers, 6 techniques with controlled comparison) and the first to combine realistic music scores, global style labels, paired speech, and phoneme-level technique annotations in a single free-to-use dataset, making it a likely standard benchmark for the singing community. The persistent gap between the best model (StyleSinger, MOS-Q 4.01) and GT(vocoder) (MOS-Q 4.21) across all four tasks signals substantial headroom for future work in technique controllability and cross-lingual generalization. Open questions include whether the cross-lingual technique recognition drop (F1 dropping ~0.06–0.10) reflects a fundamental limitation of phoneme-level technique modeling or simply insufficient data per language, and whether realistic music scores can be fully exploited by next-generation pitch models.
