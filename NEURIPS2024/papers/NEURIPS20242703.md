## Problem

Existing earable-based sleep event detection systems are evaluated in controlled, single-sleeper settings and ignore the interference of co-sleepers (roommates, spouses) whose sounds are captured by the wearer's earbuds. No publicly available dataset captures this multi-sleeper, non-restrictive environment with fine-grained event labels, making it impossible to develop or evaluate wearer-aware sleep monitoring algorithms.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242703/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of synchronized dual-channel audio (feedback and feedforward microphones sampled at 24 kHz) and 6-axis IMU data (accelerometer + gyroscope at ~94 Hz) from modified Bose QC 20 earbuds, segmented into 5-second clips for classification and 10-second clips for detection. Outputs span three tasks: a binary wearer/non-wearer label, an 8-class sleep event label (bruxism, swallow, somniloquy, breathe, cough, snore, movement, plus a 'noise' class for non-wearer/background sounds), and per-event onset/offset timestamps. The dataset covers 420 hours (62 nights) from 24 participants in 12 co-sleeping pairs, with all non-somniloquy dialogue manually removed for privacy.

## Method

The core contribution is the DreamCatcher dataset itself: 12 pairs of participants slept in shared bedrooms for 6+ hours per night over 62 nights, with one member per pair wearing a portable PSG (Philips Alice PDx) for ground-truth reference. Annotation used a hierarchical Audacity-based workflow where at least three annotators simultaneously inspected each participant's binaural audio, IMU data, and the sleep partner's audio to attribute events to the correct source, with voting for disagreements. For benchmarking, the paper evaluates three tasks: (1) wearer event identification using hand-crafted inter-channel audio features (RMS-ED, Mel-FD, TDOA) and IMU features fed to traditional ML classifiers; (2) wearer-aware sleep sound event classification using SleepTSM, EarVAS, Wav2Vec2.0, BEATs, and CLAP; and (3) wearer-aware sleep sound event detection using SEDNet and ATST-SED. All models are evaluated with leave-one-user-out cross-validation to test generalization to unseen participants without calibration.

## Results

On the wearer-aware sleep sound event classification task, BEATs (feedback channel) achieves the highest accuracy at 90.73%, while Wav2Vec2.0 (feedback) attains the best macro-AUC of 91.60%. However, macro-F1 scores remain low across all models (best: 57.47% for BEATs feedback), reflecting severe class imbalance. For the wearer event identification task, Random Forest achieves 99.7% accuracy and 99.9% AUC using lightweight handcrafted features. For sound event detection, ATST-SED (feedback) reaches 24.73% macro-F1, substantially outperforming SEDNet (14.02% macro-F1).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BEATs dominates on accuracy (90.73% feedback) and macro-F1 (57.47%), substantially outperforming the purpose-built SleepTSM (73.01% acc, 35.61% F1) and EarVAS (78.07% acc, 36.76% F1), indicating that large pretrained audio models transfer well to sleep sound classification despite not being designed for this domain. Wav2Vec2.0 achieves the highest macro-AUC (91.60%) but lower accuracy than BEATs, suggesting it ranks classes well but struggles with hard decisions. CLAP in zero-shot mode performs poorly (37% accuracy), confirming that task-specific fine-tuning is essential. For event detection, ATST-SED (24.73% macro-F1) nearly doubles SEDNet's performance (14.02%), at the cost of 142× more FLOPs (44.16G vs 0.31G). Across all tasks, the feedback microphone consistently outperforms the feedforward channel, and multi-channel fusion (EarVAS dual-channel, SEDNet dual-channel) does not reliably improve over single-channel feedback, suggesting that the close proximity of the two earbud microphones limits spatial discrimination benefits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first open-source, multi-sleeper sleep event dataset collected on commodity earables, departing from prior earable-based sleep monitoring work (SleepTSM, EarSleep, Ren et al.) that evaluated exclusively in single-participant, controlled environments. Conceptually, it extends the subject-aware vocal activity sensing framework of EarSAVAS to the sleep domain, adding fine-grained sleep disorder events (bruxism, somniloquy, swallowing, etc.) and the critical non-wearer interference dimension. Rather than proposing a new architecture, the paper repurposes existing audio classification and detection models (Wav2Vec2.0, BEATs, CLAP, SEDNet, ATST-SED) as benchmarks to expose how poorly current methods handle the multi-sleeper setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DreamCatcher fills a critical gap: no prior open-source dataset captured the realistic challenge of distinguishing a wearer's sleep events from a sleep partner's sounds on earables, which is the dominant real-world deployment scenario. The benchmarks reveal that the joint wearer-identification-plus-classification task is substantially harder than either sub-task alone (macro-F1 drops to ~35–57% even when accuracy exceeds 70%), and that naive multi-channel fusion can degrade performance. Open questions include how to design architectures that natively handle the wearer/non-wearer distinction without a two-stage pipeline, how to generate realistic rare-event samples (cough, somniloquy) that respect sleep physiology, and whether emerging PPG-equipped earbuds can further disambiguate events.
