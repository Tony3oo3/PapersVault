## Problem

Convolutional deep kernel machines, the state-of-the-art kernel method for image classification, achieve 92.7% on CIFAR-10 but still lag behind neural networks (94–95%) with similar architectures. This gap is attributed to overfitting of representations: under the infinite-width limit, Gram matrices become deterministic point distributions, eliminating the stochastic regularising effect that finite-width models naturally enjoy. The paper asks whether targeted regularisation of these learned Gram matrices can close the generalisation gap.

## Input / Output

The model consumes 32×32 RGB images (CIFAR-10 or CIFAR-100) and outputs a categorical class prediction along with a full predictive distribution, enabling log-likelihood-based uncertainty quantification. Unusually, the model operates entirely on Gram matrices (pairwise dot products of features) rather than explicit feature vectors or weights, and uses a sparse set of inducing points (512–2048 per block) to make the O(P³) kernel computation tractable via minibatch training.

## Method

The paper introduces stochastic kernel regularisation (SKR), which replaces each learned inducing Gram matrix G_ii^ℓ during training with a sample from a Wishart distribution W(G_ii^ℓ/γ, γ) plus jitter, injecting noise analogous to dropout to prevent overfitting of representations. To enable this at scale, the authors develop a Taylor approximation of the KL-divergence terms in the DKM objective (replacing log-det and trace with a Frobenius-norm penalty on G⁻¹K − I), which dramatically improves numerical stability under TF32 (TensorFloat-32) arithmetic on modern GPUs, yielding ~5× speedup over prior FP64 implementations. The architecture is a ResNet20-inspired convolutional DKM with batch kernel normalisation, skip connections, and a normalised Gaussian base kernel, trained for 1200 epochs with Adam. The combination of SKR, the Taylor-approximated objective, and non-zero KL regularisation strength ν is necessary for stable training, as ablations show any single component is insufficient.

## Results

On CIFAR-10, the proposed convolutional deep kernel machine with stochastic kernel regularisation (SKR) achieves 94.52% test accuracy and -0.3611 test log-likelihood, matching the Adam-trained ResNet (94.55%) and substantially outperforming the previous kernel SOTA of 92.69% (Milsom et al., 2024). On CIFAR-100, the method reaches 75.31% test accuracy, improving over the prior CDKM (72.05%) and surpassing the AdamW-trained ResNet (74.13%), though still trailing the SGD-trained ResNet (79.42%). The DKM also provides markedly better uncertainty quantification than the Adam-trained neural network, with a test log-likelihood of -0.3611 versus -1.3003.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed CDKM with SKR outperforms the previous convolutional deep kernel machine (Milsom et al., 2024) by 1.83 percentage points on CIFAR-10 (94.52% vs. 92.69%) and by 3.26 points on CIFAR-100 (75.31% vs. 72.05%). It also surpasses the best fixed-kernel method, the Tuned Myrtle10 Kernel DACG of Adlam et al. (2023), by 3.32 points on CIFAR-10 (94.52% vs. 91.2%), and the NNGP-LAP-flip of Li et al. (2019) by 5.6 points (94.52% vs. 88.92%). Against the Adam-trained ResNet with the same architecture, the DKM is statistically indistinguishable on CIFAR-10 accuracy (94.52% vs. 94.55%, p = 0.76) but provides substantially better calibrated uncertainty (log-likelihood -0.36 vs. -1.30). The only comparison where the DKM is clearly outperformed is against the SGD-trained ResNet, which achieves 95.36% on CIFAR-10 (p = 0.0001) and 79.42% on CIFAR-100, consistent with the well-known generalisation advantage of SGD for ResNets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends the convolutional deep kernel machine of Milsom et al. (2024), which itself builds on the deep kernel process framework (Yang et al., 2023; Aitchison et al., 2021) and the NNGP theory of Lee et al. (2017). The key departure is the introduction of Stochastic Kernel Regularisation (SKR), which draws an explicit analogy to dropout in neural networks by sampling inducing Gram matrices from a Wishart distribution during training, thereby restoring the stochastic regularising effect lost in the infinite-width limit. The paper also departs from prior DKM implementations by replacing the exact KL-divergence objective with a second-order Taylor approximation and exploiting TF32 arithmetic, enabling roughly 5× faster training and significantly more epochs under a fixed compute budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By closing the CIFAR-10 gap from 92.7% to 94.5%—matching an Adam-trained ResNet—the paper provides strong empirical evidence that representation learning, not architectural inductive bias, is the primary reason NNGPs underperform finite neural networks. This has important implications for the theoretical understanding of deep learning, suggesting that kernel-based models with learned representations can in principle match neural networks on complex tasks. However, a gap to SGD-trained ResNets (95.36%) remains, and the method is still substantially slower per epoch (~260s vs ~45s), limiting practical adoption. Open questions include whether SKR and the Taylor-approximated objective scale to larger datasets (ImageNet), whether SGD-like optimisation can be stabilised for DKMs, and whether the approach generalises beyond image classification.
