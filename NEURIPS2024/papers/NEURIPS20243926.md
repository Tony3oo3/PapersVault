## Problem

Canonical distribution-shift methods require the entire target distribution to lie within the training support, but practical extrapolation involves only a handful of target samples that may be arbitrarily far outside that support. This paper asks: under what conditions can we reliably predict labels for a single out-of-support sample, and how should we design adaptation algorithms to achieve this?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243926/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled source distribution p(x_src, y_src) and as few as one out-of-support target sample x_tgt (no target distribution is needed). It produces a class label (or regression value) for x_tgt by first identifying the invariant latent variable c_tgt that lies within the source support C_src. The key constraint is that the target sample's out-of-support nature must arise solely from the changing variable s_tgt ∉ S_src, while c_tgt remains on-support.

## Method

The paper formulates extrapolation as a latent-variable identification problem under a minimal change principle: the latent space z = [c, s] is partitioned so that c (invariant, on-support) determines the label y, while s (changing, off-support) controls non-semantic attributes. Two identification theorems are established: for dense shifts (s affects all pixels), c must be discrete and s_tgt must be within a smoothness-bounded distance of S_src; for sparse shifts (s affects only a subset of dimensions), identification holds regardless of s_tgt's distance, provided mechanistic dependence (Jacobian rank subadditivity) holds on the c-exclusive dimensions. Practically, the theory motivates adding an entropy-minimization loss to MAE-TTT (as a surrogate for maximizing p(c_tgt)) and integrating LoRA-based low-rank updates with L1 sparsity constraints into TeSLA, both of which yield consistent improvements across CIFAR-10-C, CIFAR-100-C, and ImageNet-C.

## Results

On the SOTA TTA comparison (Table 2), adding sparsity constraints (SC) to TeSLA-s reduces average error from 12.1% to 11.7% on CIFAR10-C, from 37.3% to 37.0% on CIFAR100-C, and from 53.1% to 50.9% on ImageNet-C. For the generative adaptation experiment (Table 3), incorporating entropy minimization into MAE-TTT improves average accuracy on ImageNet-C from 45.92% to 47.77% across 15 corruption types. On synthetic data (Table 1), the proposed method achieves 0.69–0.78 accuracy under dense shifts and 0.70–0.76 under sparse shifts, consistently outperforming both the source-only baseline (0.45–0.59) and iMSDA (0.36–0.54).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sparsity-constrained variant (TeSLA-s+SC) outperforms all 8 baselines on all three benchmarks, achieving the lowest error rates: 11.7% on CIFAR10-C (vs. 12.1% for TeSLA-s, 14.1% for TENT, 13.9% for SHOT, 15.6% for BN), 37.0% on CIFAR100-C (vs. 37.3% for TeSLA-s, 39.0% for TENT), and 50.9% on ImageNet-C (vs. 53.1% for TeSLA-s, 57.4% for TENT). The improvement over the closest competitor TeSLA-s is modest but consistent (0.4–2.2 points), and the gap over earlier methods like TTT++ (15.8/44.4/59.3) and TTAC (13.4/41.7/58.7) is substantial. In the MAE-TTT experiment (Table 3), the entropy-minimization addition improves average ImageNet-C accuracy from 45.92% (TTT-MAE) to 47.77%, with the largest gains on elastic (50.7→69.0), frost (50.7→64.6), and brightness (69.1→73.8) corruptions. On synthetic data, the proposed method beats both the source-only baseline and iMSDA by 15–30 accuracy points across all shift distances, while iMSDA sometimes performs worse than the source-only model, confirming that domain-adaptation methods without target distribution access can be counterproductive for extrapolation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the latent-variable partition framework z=[c,s] from Kong et al. [18] and Li et al. [19], which required multiple source distributions with overlapping supports, to the far more challenging extrapolation setting with a single source distribution and as few as one out-of-support target sample. The identification proofs draw on discrete-subspace identification results from Kong et al. [38] and Jacobian-rank preservation lemmas from Brady et al. [39], adapting them to handle the absence of a full target distribution. On the practical side, the paper repurposes MAE-TTT [20] and TeSLA [21] as vehicles for its theoretical insights, adding entropy-minimization and sparsity-constraint terms respectively, rather than proposing an entirely new architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first principled characterization of when extrapolation beyond the training support is theoretically possible, revealing a clean trade-off: dense shifts (affecting all pixels) require bounded out-of-support distance and discrete class structure, while sparse shifts (affecting limited regions) permit extrapolation regardless of severity. This distinction has direct practical implications for test-time adaptation design, as validated by consistent (if modest) improvements over MAE-TTT and TeSLA. Key limitations include the potentially overly stringent global Jacobian norm condition in Theorem 4.2, the requirement for an explicit latent representation space, and the need for careful multi-loss re-weighting. Open questions include whether local smoothness conditions can relax the dense-shift bound and whether the framework extends to models without explicit autoencoding structure.
