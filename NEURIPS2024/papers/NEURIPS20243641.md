## Problem

Deep learning models tend to produce overconfident predictions, and during optimization, calibration error overfits earlier than classification accuracy, indicating distinct optimization objectives. Existing calibration strategies—post-processing mappings, Bayesian networks, or fixed training-time regularizers—either decouple calibration from the optimization process or use static hyperparameters that cannot adapt to the evolving confidence distribution. The paper addresses the gap of dynamically controlling the gradient decay rate in Softmax during training to maintain consistent calibration alongside accuracy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard RGB images at dataset-specific resolutions (32×32 for CIFAR-10/100, 64×64 for Tiny-ImageNet, variable for SVHN and 102 Flowers) and produces calibrated class probabilities where the predicted confidence reflects the true probability of correct classification, alongside the predicted class label. Unusually, the training loop also ingests a validation-set Relative Calibration Error (RCE) signal each epoch as a feedback input to the PID controller, making the loss function itself dynamic across epochs rather than fixed.

## Method

The core mechanism introduces a hyperparameter β into the Softmax denominator (J = −log e^{z_c} / (Σ_{j≠c} e^{z_j} + β·e^{z_c})), which controls how rapidly the gradient magnitude decays as instance-level probability p_c increases; smaller β yields slower decay and higher overall confidence. A PID controller dynamically adjusts β each epoch using the signed Relative Calibration Error (RCE = Σ |D_b|/N · (conf(D_b) − acc(D_b))) computed on the validation set as its feedback signal, with the update rule β_t = β_{t−1}·e^{−u(t)} where u(t) combines proportional, integral, and derivative terms of the RCE error. To counteract the gradient magnitude fluctuations induced by varying β, an adaptive learning rate schedule multiplies the base rate by the ratio of integrated gradient norms α(t−1)/α(t), where α(t) = (β_t ln β_t − β_t + 1)/(β_t − 1)². The PID gains (K_p=1, K_i=0.1, K_d=1) are chosen empirically, and the entire procedure is applied on top of standard SGD training with ResNet, VGG, or YOLOv3 backbones.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with ResNet18, the proposed PID-AGD method achieves 95.0% accuracy with ECE of 0.007 and MCE of 0.078, substantially outperforming the Softmax baseline (93.7% acc, ECE 0.041, MCE 0.281). On CIFAR-100 with ResNet35, it reaches 75.4% accuracy with ECE 0.011 and MCE 0.063, compared to the Softmax baseline of 73.8% accuracy, ECE 0.172, and MCE 0.351. The method consistently achieves the lowest ECE and MCE across all tested architectures (ResNet18, ResNet35, VGG16) and datasets while simultaneously matching or exceeding baseline accuracy, unlike DCA which improves calibration at the cost of 1.5–2.5% accuracy drop.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed PID-AGD method dominates all baselines on calibration metrics while maintaining or improving accuracy. Compared to the Softmax baseline, it reduces ECE by 83% (0.041→0.007) and MCE by 72% (0.281→0.078) on CIFAR-10/ResNet18, while simultaneously improving accuracy by 1.3%. Against Cosface and Center loss, which improve accuracy but leave calibration largely unchanged or worsened (e.g., Cosface MCE on CIFAR-100/ResNet18 is 0.501 vs. 0.344 for Softmax), the proposed method achieves both higher accuracy and dramatically lower calibration error. DCA (trainable calibration) improves ECE over Softmax but at a significant accuracy cost (e.g., 91.9% vs. 93.7% on CIFAR-10/ResNet18), whereas PID-AGD achieves lower ECE than DCA (0.007 vs. 0.020) while gaining 3.1% accuracy. In the post-hoc calibration comparison (Table 1), the proposed training-time method also outperforms Temperature Scaling, Vector Scaling, Histogram Binning, and TS-AvUC across ECE, MCE, and AdaECE on all tested datasets and architectures.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Zhang & Xie (2024), which introduced a fixed gradient decay coefficient β in the Softmax mapping to control calibration. The key departure is making β adaptive during training via a PID controller that monitors a newly defined Relative Calibration Error (RCE) on a validation set, rather than treating β as a static hyperparameter. Conceptually, it bridges the training-based calibration literature (label smoothing, focal loss, DCA) with classical feedback control, and adds an adaptive learning-rate compensation mechanism to counteract gradient-magnitude fluctuations induced by varying β. It contrasts with post-hoc methods (temperature scaling, histogram binning, TS-AvUC) by modifying the optimization process itself rather than applying a separate output-probability mapping after training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practical gap: calibration tends to overfit earlier than accuracy during training, and no existing training-time method dynamically adjusts the calibration-relevant hyperparameter in response to observed miscalibration. The substantial ECE reductions (e.g., 0.172→0.012 on CIFAR-100/ResNet35) while maintaining or improving accuracy are encouraging, but the approach lacks theoretical grounding for why β controls the confidence distribution, and the PID controller operates as a black box with a significant time lag. Open questions include joint optimization of the temperature τ and decay β, formalizing the control-system dynamics of neural network training, and whether the uniform-probability assumption underlying the gradient compensation holds in practice. The finding that Adam degrades accuracy by ~10 points under the dynamic loss is a useful caution for practitioners combining adaptive optimizers with time-varying objectives.
