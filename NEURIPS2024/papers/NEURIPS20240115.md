## Problem

No machine-learning-ready dataset has integrated geostationary satellite imagery with fine-grained, high-temporal-resolution solar radiation observations to support both area-based and point-based solar forecasting under consistent evaluation metrics. Existing resources are either limited to ultra-short-term sky images, restricted to a handful of European stations, designed for precipitation rather than solar radiation, or lack the 15-minute temporal resolution needed for short-term grid operations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240115/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For area-based forecasting, the model consumes 120×120-pixel images (600 km × 600 km at 5 km resolution) of physics-derived solar radiation at 15-minute intervals, using 12 frames (3 hours) to predict the next 12 frames. For point-based forecasting, the input is a 4-dimensional vector per timestep (three spectral bands from GOES-16/Himawari-8 plus solar zenith angle) concatenated with ground-measured solar radiation, with sequence lengths of 12 steps (3 h) for short-term or 96 steps (24 h) for day-ahead forecasting. The dataset spans 19 study areas across four continents with year-long sequences exceeding 35,000 timesteps, and includes auxiliary cloud masks and land cover types for scenario-based evaluation.

## Method

SolarCube's core contribution is the dataset pipeline: it aggregates GOES-16 and Himawari-8 imagery (VIS047, VIS086, IR1330 bands) to 5 km resolution, then generates surface solar radiation at 15-minute resolution using a radiative-transfer parameterization with Look-Up Tables that match satellite spectral values to atmospheric conditions, supplemented by MODIS albedo, elevation, and MERRA-2 water vapor inputs. The dataset is split geographically (14 training areas, 5 testing areas) rather than temporally to test spatial generalizability. For benchmarking, the authors evaluate ConvLSTM and video-transformer variants (Axial, Video-Swin, Divided-ST) on area-based tasks, and LSTM, LSTM-a, Transformer, and Informer on point-based tasks, all compared against physics-informed persistence baselines (clearness-index-based for short-term, previous-day for long-term). Performance is reported under Easy/Hard cloud-dynamics scenarios and across land cover types using relative metrics (rRMSE, rMBD) and Forecasting Skill relative to persistence.

## Results

For area-based short-term forecasting (3-hour input → 3-hour output at 15-min resolution), Divided-st achieves the best overall R² (0.792) and lowest rRMSE (22.32%), with a Forecasting Skill (FS) of 23.28% over the persistence baseline. For point-based short-term forecasting, Transformer leads with R² = 0.719 and rRMSE = 33.74% (FS = 11.68%). For point-based long-term (day-ahead) forecasting, LSTM-a achieves the best overall R² (0.672) and rRMSE (50.56%), with FS = 30.81%, while the persistence baseline degrades severely under Hard scenarios (rRMSE = 100.68%, R² = 0.192).

## Baselines & Comparisons

In area-based forecasting, Divided-st [52] outperforms ConvLSTM [24] by 2.1 percentage points in R² (0.792 vs. 0.771) and 1.5 points in rRMSE (22.32% vs. 23.84%), while Axial [50] and Video-swin [51] fall in between. Under Hard (dynamic cloud) scenarios, ConvLSTM [24] actually achieves the highest R² (0.595) and lowest rRMSE (21.94%), whereas Video-swin [51] shows the best FS (25.19%), indicating it benefits most from the persistence comparison in difficult conditions. In point-based short-term forecasting, Transformer [57] marginally leads all four deep models (R² = 0.719, rRMSE = 33.74%), with Informer [58] close behind (R² = 0.717); LSTM [55] and LSTM-a [56] trail by ~1 R² point. For point-based long-term (day-ahead) forecasting, LSTM-a [56] clearly dominates with R² = 0.672 and rRMSE = 50.56%, outperforming Informer [58] (R² = 0.621) and Transformer [57] (R² = 0.624) by 4–5 R² points, while plain LSTM [55] is the weakest deep model (R² = 0.608). The persistence baseline [53] is competitive in short-term area-based tasks (rRMSE = 29.09%) but collapses in long-term point-based Hard scenarios (rRMSE = 100.68%), highlighting the value of learned models for day-ahead forecasting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the radiative transfer parameterization framework of Liang et al. [39] and Li et al. [35] from hourly/daily resolution to 15-minute temporal resolution, generating the first satellite-derived solar radiation product at this cadence. It departs from existing ML-ready datasets (SEVIR for precipitation, sky-image datasets for ultra-short-term forecasting, Boussif et al. for coarse-resolution point-based tasks) by integrating geostationary satellite imagery (GOES-16, Himawari-8), physics-derived solar radiation, and ground measurements (BSRN, SURFRAD) into a unified benchmark spanning 19 study areas across four continents. For model evaluation, it adapts the EarthFormer architecture [27] for area-based video forecasting and standard time-series models (LSTM, Transformer, Informer) for point-based forecasting, positioning itself as a benchmark rather than a methodological contribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SolarCube fills a critical gap by providing the first ML-ready dataset that supports both point-based and area-based solar radiation forecasting at 15-minute resolution with consistent evaluation metrics, enabling fair comparison across model families and forecasting horizons. Key limitations include coverage of only 2018 data, absence of European study areas, and sparse ground stations in developing regions, which constrain temporal generalization and geographic representativeness. Open questions remain around leveraging infrared bands for nighttime continuity in area-based forecasting, exploiting sparse ground measurements to improve area-level predictions, and incorporating fairness-aware evaluation metrics for geographic equity in forecasting quality.
