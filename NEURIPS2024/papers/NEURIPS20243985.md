## Problem

A single task-level zero-shot CoT prompt applied uniformly across all instances is inherently limited: the task-level optimal prompt can actively degrade reasoning on certain instances while sub-optimal prompts succeed on them. The underlying mechanism explaining why a given prompt leads to correct versus incorrect reasoning for a specific question remains unclear, making it difficult to select the right prompt per instance.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243985/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text question and a fixed set of 9 candidate zero-shot CoT prompts (e.g., 'Let's think step by step.', 'Don't think. Just feel.') as input. For each question-prompt pair, it computes a synthesized saliency score S at the answer generation step (detected via regular expressions) by aggregating attention-gradient products across selected layers and heads. The output is either a single selected prompt (IAP-ss) or a majority-voted final answer from the top-k prompts ranked by S (IAP-mv, with k=3).

## Method

The paper first performs information-flow analysis using saliency scores (element-wise product of attention matrices and their gradients w.r.t. cross-entropy loss) to decompose zero-shot CoT reasoning into three components: question, prompt, and rationale. It discovers that good reasoning exhibits high question-to-prompt saliency in shallow layers (where the prompt absorbs question semantics) and high question-to-rationale and prompt-to-rationale saliency (where the rationale aggregates information from both sources), while bad reasoning lacks one or both flows. The most informative heads are the front heads at middle and end positions in shallow layers. Building on this, IAP computes a weighted synthesized score S = λ₁·I_qp + λ₂·I_qr + λ₃·I_pr over selected layers and heads for each candidate prompt. IAP-ss sequentially evaluates prompts and stops at the first one whose S exceeds a dataset-specific threshold; IAP-mv evaluates all prompts, retains the top-k by S, and takes the majority answer among them.

## Results

On GSM8k, IAP-mv improves LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct from 64.52% (best single prompt) to 66.34% and Qwen-14B-Chat from 60.50% to 62.81%. On SVAMP, IAP-mv achieves 77.33% (LLaMA-3) and 73.33% (Qwen), a 1.33% gain over the best task-level prompt. Across logic and commonsense tasks, IAP-mv yields larger margins: e.g., 42.40% on Tracking Shuffled Objects (LLaMA-3, +18% over best prompt) and 68.39% on CommonsenseQA (LLaMA-3, +3.44%). The paper reports a consistent 2%–4% accuracy enhancement over the optimal task-level prompt across all six benchmarks and four LLMs.

## Baselines & Comparisons

IAP-mv consistently outperforms both OPRO and Self-Discover across all overlapping tasks. On GSM8k, IAP-mv achieves 66.34% (LLaMA-3) and 62.81% (Qwen), beating OPRO (65.96% / 36.01%) and Self-Discover (8.50% / 56.33%); OPRO is particularly unstable, dropping to 36.01% on Qwen. On Causal Judgement, IAP-mv reaches 19.25% (LLaMA-3) and 29.95% (Qwen), surpassing OPRO (18.18% / 19.79%) and Self-Discover (10.70% / 11.23%). On Tracking Shuffled Objects, IAP-mv achieves 42.40% (LLaMA-3) and 25.60% (Qwen), well above OPRO (28.00% / 4.00%) and Self-Discover (36.00% / 24.00%). On CommonsenseQA and MMLU (where OPRO is not available), IAP-mv leads Self-Discover by 8.36% (LLaMA-3 CSQA) and 26.32% (Qwen MMLU). The simple AMV baseline is highly unstable—e.g., it drops to 1.44% on T-Obj (Qwen) and 28.22% on GSM8k (Qwen)—confirming that naive majority voting cannot distinguish good from bad reasoning, whereas IAP-mv's saliency-score-based selection reliably does.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends zero-shot CoT prompting (Kojima et al., 2022) by shifting the prompt-selection paradigm from task-level optimization (OPRO, Self-Discover, Plan-and-Solve) to instance-level adaptation, motivated by a mechanistic analysis of information flow within Transformer attention. It repurposes saliency score analysis—previously applied to in-context learning (Dai et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023)—to dissect how semantic information flows between question, prompt, and rationale during CoT reasoning, revealing that shallow-layer question-to-prompt aggregation is the critical differentiator between successful and failed reasoning. The key conceptual departure from prior prompt-optimization work is the argument that no single prompt is universally optimal across instances, and that the prompt-question interaction must be evaluated per-instance rather than per-task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first mechanistic account of what distinguishes good from bad zero-shot CoT reasoning at the attention level, offering interpretability insights that go beyond empirical prompt search. However, the practical gains are modest (2–4% over the best task-level prompt), the saliency computation adds significant overhead per instance, and the information-flow patterns are validated primarily on a handful of decoder-only LLMs, leaving open whether they generalize to other architectures or training regimes. The reliance on regular-expression-based answer-step detection also introduces fragility for models with irregular output formats, as acknowledged in the limitations. Future work could explore whether the identified saliency thresholds are stable across model scales and whether lighter-weight proxies for information flow could reduce the computational cost of instance-level prompt selection.
