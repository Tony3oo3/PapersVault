## Problem

Conventional sequential recommendation methods are grounded in deterministic information theory, assuming user behavior follows strict mechanical laws, yet real user interests are inherently random and fuzzy at any given moment. Existing diffusion-based SR methods (DiffuRec, DreamRec) operate in continuous space on candidate item embeddings rather than on the interaction sequence itself, introducing Gaussian noise that can push discrete data points away from meaningful states and causing information loss.

## Input / Output

The model takes a user's timestamp-ordered interaction sequence where each item is encoded as a semantic ID (a tuple of m discrete codes drawn from codebooks of size K, e.g., 32×256 for PQ or 6×256 for RQ-VAE, derived from BERT embeddings of item text descriptions) and produces the predicted next item as a semantic ID, which is mapped back to a concrete item (or the nearest item in embedding space if no exact match exists). The diffusion time step t is also fed to the model via sinusoidal embeddings added after the embedding layer.

## Method

DDSR applies discrete state space diffusion directly to the one-hot encoded semantic IDs of the interaction sequence, using structured transition matrices (uniform or importance-based Gaussian-kernel) to generate 'fuzzy sets' at each forward step, rather than adding continuous Gaussian noise to embeddings. A Transformer approximator is trained with cross-entropy loss to predict the next semantic ID given the fuzzy set at time t, and at inference the model starts from the historical sequence (not from pure noise) and iteratively applies the reverse discrete diffusion sampling formula to refine the prediction. The use of semantic IDs (via PQ or RQ-VAE) reduces the discrete state space from tens of thousands of item IDs to m×K nodes, enabling tractable transition matrices while injecting semantic information that addresses cold-start. Theoretically, the paper proves the diffused space is a completion of the original sample space, guaranteeing that models built on these fuzzy sets are solvable.

## Results

DDSR achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three datasets and all four metrics (Recall@10, NDCG@10, Recall@50, NDCG@50). On the Scientific dataset, DDSR reaches R@10=0.1207 and N@10=0.0663, improving +5.41% and +11.61% over the best baseline respectively. On Online Retail, the gains are most pronounced with +10.12% R@10 and +22.86% N@10 over the strongest competitor. All improvements are statistically significant (t-test p<0.05), with the largest relative gains observed on NDCG metrics, indicating better ranking quality rather than merely broader recall.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DDSR outperforms all eight baselines on every metric across all three datasets. Among conventional SR methods, SASRec (R@10=0.1049 on Scientific) and BERT4Rec (R@10=0.0473) are both substantially outperformed, with BERT4Rec being particularly weak. Among semantic-based methods, VQ-Rec is the strongest baseline (e.g., R@10=0.1532 on Online Retail), and DDSR improves upon it by +10.12% R@10 and +22.86% N@10 on that dataset; TIGER shows unstable performance, dropping to R@10=0.0745 on Online Retail. Among generative methods, DiffuRec (continuous diffusion) achieves R@10=0.1145 on Scientific but collapses to 0.0402 on Online Retail, while DreamRec (R@10=0.0845 on Scientific) is consistently weaker; DDSR's discrete diffusion approach avoids the information loss from continuous-space noise, maintaining stable gains. The ablation further confirms that removing diffusion (w/o diffusion row) degrades performance by ~7-10% on NDCG metrics, and replacing semantic IDs with random IDs causes a catastrophic drop (e.g., R@10 from 0.1207 to 0.0554 on Scientific), validating both design choices.

## Relation to Prior Work

DDSR draws on the discrete state space diffusion framework of D3PMs (Austin et al., 2021) and the uniform transition matrix design of Hoogeboom et al. (2021), but repurposes them for a fundamentally different target: rather than diffusing item embeddings toward noise (as DiffuRec and DreamRec do), it applies structured transitions to the user's interaction sequence itself, treating the sequence as the object to be 'fuzzified.' For the discrete representation, it builds on the semantic ID paradigm from VQ-Rec and TIGER (using PQ and RQ-VAE codebooks), but uses these IDs primarily to make the transition matrix computationally tractable rather than as the final output representation. The theoretical motivation is borrowed from fuzzy information processing (Tanaka et al., 1976), framing the diffused sequence as a completion of the incomplete observed sample space.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a coherent conceptual reframe—diffusing the 'cause' (interaction history) rather than the 'effect' (target item)—that provides a principled justification for why blurring input sequences can improve sequential recommendation, grounded in information diffusion theory. Practically, the combination of discrete diffusion with semantic IDs addresses two persistent SR challenges (data sparsity and cold-start) in a single framework. However, the theoretical proof in Appendix A is somewhat hand-wavy (relying on Lipschitz conditions and a 'maximum principle' without full rigor), and the computational overhead (m× complexity from semantic IDs plus iterative sampling) remains a significant barrier to deployment. Open questions include whether faster discrete diffusion samplers can close the efficiency gap, and whether the fuzzy-set interpretation generalizes beyond the specific transition matrices explored here.
