## Problem

Existing attribution techniques for CLIP (Grad-CAM, LRP, etc.) provide only high-level, entangled explanations that highlight the main object without revealing which fine-grained, distinctive features actually drive the zero-shot decision. The paper asks: what specific concepts do the vision and language encoders learn in common that cause image-text points to be closer or further apart in the joint embedding space, and can this shared knowledge be quantified?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241252/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image, a set of textual class names (e.g., 1,000 ImageNet labels), and a bank of 4,229 class-agnostic textual descriptors generated by an LLM (where the class name is absent, making descriptors transferable across classes). It produces: (1) multimodal concepts pairing grounded visual regions with natural-language descriptions, (2) a discrete mutual information score and its dynamics curve (AUC) between vision and language encoder concepts, and (3) the set of mutual concepts shared by both encoders for a given prediction. All concepts are mapped to integer indices, enabling exact discrete MI computation without continuous-space approximations.

## Method

The approach is entirely training-free and operates in three stages. First, prominent image patches are identified via Fiedler eigenvector decomposition of the patch affinity matrix (spectral graph clustering), then clustered with PCA or K-means into distinct visual concepts (object parts); each visual concept is encoded using visual prompt engineering (red circle + reverse-blur) and matched to a unique textual descriptor via CLIP similarity followed by Optimal Transport (Sinkhorn-Knopp) to enforce diversity. Second, textual concepts for the language encoder are retrieved as the top-u descriptors most similar to the predicted class in the language embedding space. Third, both concept sets are mapped to discrete integers, and MI is computed exactly via a contingency table; MI dynamics are measured by sequentially ablating vision concepts in order of importance and tracking the MI decay curve (reported as AUC). This discrete formulation avoids MI approximations, and the AUC serves as a proxy for the strength of shared knowledge, which the paper shows correlates linearly with zero-shot accuracy within each architecture family.

## Results

On the XAI evaluation metrics (Table 1), the proposed training-free multimodal concepts (PCA variant) achieve the best Deletion score (2.460), lowest Accuracy Drop (1.582), and highest Accuracy Increase (1.849), outperforming all three trained baselines on 3 of 4 metrics (losing only on Insertion to MM-CBM). When applied as additional descriptors for zero-shot classification (Table 2), the multimodal concepts yield gains of up to +3.75% top-1 accuracy (RN50×16: 68.47→72.22) and +2.35% for ViT-B/16 (67.93→70.28) over the Menon/Pratt baselines. Across 13 CLIP models (Table 3), the MI dynamics AUC shows a strong linear correlation with zero-shot accuracy (slope ≈11.24 for ViTs), with the best model ViT-B/16-dfn achieving AUC 4.62 and 76.24% top-1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against MM-CBM (Oikarinen, ICLR 2023), the proposed method wins on Deletion (2.460 vs 3.147), AccDrop (1.582 vs 2.634), and AccInc (1.849 vs 1.013) but loses on Insertion (3.168 vs 3.385). Against MM-ProtoSim (van Noord, ICCV 2023), the method wins on all four metrics: Deletion (2.460 vs 3.149), Insertion (3.168 vs 3.358), AccDrop (1.582 vs 2.665), and AccInc (1.849 vs 0.943). Against the Feature Maps baseline (formulated from Hernandez/Dani neuron annotation), the method wins on Deletion (2.460 vs 2.921), AccDrop (1.582 vs 2.283), and AccInc (1.849 vs 1.233) but slightly loses on Insertion (3.168 vs 3.114). For the zero-shot classification application, the method improves upon the Menon (ICLR 2023) and Pratt (ICCV 2023) descriptor-based baselines by +2.05% to +3.75% top-1 accuracy across all 8 CLIP architectures tested, with the largest gain on RN50×16 (+3.75%). All baselines require training on the full ImageNet training set, whereas the proposed method is entirely training-free.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of information theory and concept-based explainability for contrastive vision-language models. It builds on CLIP's dual-encoder architecture, visual prompt engineering (Shtedritski et al.) for region-specific encoding, and spectral graph decomposition (Melas-Kyriazi et al.) for patch separation, but departs from prior interpretability work in two key ways: (1) it rejects training-based explanation methods (NLEs, Concept Bottleneck Models) as unfaithful, and (2) it reframes the vision-language relationship as an information channel whose shared knowledge is quantified via mutual information over discrete textual concepts. Unlike modality-gap studies that investigate why image and text embeddings occupy separate regions, this work assumes the gap and analyzes the strength of shared encoding within it.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a training-free, human-readable lens into CLIP's zero-shot decisions and establishes a practical AUC-accuracy correlation (slope ≈ 11 for ViTs) that could inform model selection when accuracy alone is insufficient. The finding that general concepts (e.g., patterns, styles) are more strongly encoded than discriminative fine-grained ones (e.g., a siamang's body) offers insight into CLIP's training-data biases. Limitations include noisy visual concepts in some cases, restriction to zero-shot classification only, and the reliance on an LLM-generated descriptor bank whose quality, while ablated, remains a potential bottleneck. Extending the mutual-knowledge framework to non-contrastive models, retrieval, or segmentation tasks is left as future work.
