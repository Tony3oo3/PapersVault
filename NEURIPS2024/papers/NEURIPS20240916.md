## Problem

Existing VFI benchmarks contain short, low-diversity videos with limited variation in motion magnitudes, luminance, sharpness, and contrast, and are often sourced from specialized high-frame-rate sensors or professional equipment. No large-scale, general-purpose dataset exists that captures the full range of real-world video conditions (hand-held, action, drone, screen captures) at high resolution and frame rate, making it difficult to evaluate model generalizability across diverse domains.

## Input / Output

LAVIB provides 1-second video segments at 3840×2160 resolution and 60 fps, spatially cropped into 2×2 tubelets to fit GPU memory. For VFI evaluation, triplets (one preceding, one succeeding frame with a single interpolation target) or septuplets (two preceding, two succeeding frames with three interpolation targets) are constructed, yielding ~5.7M and ~2.4M groups respectively. Each video is annotated with four per-frame metrics—Average Flow Magnitude (AFM), Average Laplacian Variance (ALV), Average Root Mean Square contrast (ARMS), and Average Relevant Luminance (ARL)—which drive both the main balanced splits and the OOD challenge assignments.

## Method

The dataset is built through an automated pipeline: structured vocabulary queries retrieve 4K 60fps YouTube videos; 10-second clips are sampled at duration-dependent intervals; MViTv2-B embeddings remove near-duplicate clips; FlowFormer computes AFM to select the top-20% motion segments; and a 2×2 tubelet grid with extreme-metric retention (dropping the middle 20%) yields 283,484 clips from 17,204 source videos. Splits are balanced across all four metrics using DUPLEX farthest-point selection, while OOD challenges deliberately create imbalanced train/test distributions along each metric axis. Three VFI methods (RIFE, EMA-VFI, FLAVR) are benchmarked under single-frame, multi-frame (×2/×3/×4), multi-resolution, and OOD settings, evaluated with PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS, Watson-DFT, VSFA, and VFIPS.

## Results

On the LAVIB test set, FLAVR achieves the best performance across all seven evaluation metrics: PSNR 33.44, SSIM 0.981, LPIPS 2.934e-2, DISTS 4.430e-2, Watson-DFT 0.360, VSFA 0.626, and VFIPS 0.667. EMA-VFI follows closely with PSNR 33.14 and SSIM 0.978, while RIFE lags significantly behind with PSNR 27.88 and SSIM 0.871. Pre-training on LAVIB also improves downstream performance, yielding +1.23 PSNR on Xiph4K and +1.19 PSNR on X4K1KFPS compared to Vimeo-90K pre-training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLAVR [Kalluri et al., WACV 2023] outperforms EMA-VFI [Zhang et al., CVPR 2023] on all seven metrics, with the largest gaps in DISTS (4.430e-2 vs. 5.076e-2) and VFIPS (0.667 vs. 0.638), though PSNR and SSIM margins are modest (+0.30 and +0.003 respectively). Both FLAVR and EMA-VFI decisively outperform RIFE [Huang et al., ECCV 2022], which trails by −5.56 PSNR and −0.110 SSIM relative to FLAVR, with LPIPS nearly 5× worse (1.416e-1 vs. 2.934e-2). The paper attributes RIFE's underperformance to its limited model complexity being insufficient for the large variance in motion, sharpness, contrast, and luminance settings present in LAVIB. In OOD challenges, FLAVR consistently achieves the best scores, though the gap to EMA-VFI narrows in high-to-low motion settings where both models benefit from the simpler target domain. The benchmark also demonstrates that LAVIB pre-training transfers effectively to smaller domain-specific datasets, improving PSNR by +0.43 on Vimeo-90K, +1.23 on Xiph4K, and +1.19 on X4K1KFPS over Vimeo-90K pre-training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper does not build on a specific prior method but rather positions itself as a large-scale benchmark for the well-established VFI task, benchmarking existing methods (RIFE, EMA-VFI, FLAVR) under a new evaluation protocol. It departs from prior VFI datasets (Vimeo-90K, Xiph4K, X4K1KFPS, Inter4K) by introducing a metric-driven collection pipeline that explicitly balances videos across four quantitative dimensions (flow magnitude, sharpness, contrast, luminance), a strategy absent from earlier efforts that relied on fixed sensor types or standardized recording conditions. The OOD challenge splits, which deliberately create train/test imbalances along each metric axis, extend the evaluation paradigm beyond in-distribution accuracy to cross-domain generalizability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LAVIB's scale (283K clips, 77.6 hours of 4K 60fps video) and metric-driven diversity make it a practical pre-training resource that demonstrably improves downstream performance on smaller domain-specific benchmarks (Vimeo-90K, Xiph4K, X4K1KFPS), suggesting that data diversity matters as much as data volume for VFI generalization. The OOD challenges reveal asymmetric generalization gaps (e.g., low-to-high motion is harder than high-to-low), pointing to a fundamental sensitivity of current VFI architectures to training-domain motion statistics. Limitations include manually defined thresholds at each pipeline stage and a finite search vocabulary that constrains scene diversity, leaving open whether fully automated, metric-guided collection can scale further without human curation.
