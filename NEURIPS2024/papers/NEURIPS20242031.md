## Problem

Statistical prior models for neural network gradients have been largely overlooked due to their high dimensionality and complex interdependencies, making effective probability modeling difficult. This paper investigates whether pretrained large language models can serve as zero-shot gradient priors, using lossless gradient compression as the examination task since compression efficiency depends critically on accurate probability modeling.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242031/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes 32-bit floating-point gradients collected during training of various architectures (ConvNet, VGG-16, ResNet-18, ViT) on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and TinyImageNet. Gradients are serialized into grouped hexadecimal text (every 4 bytes grouped with a separator), tokenized, and fed to a frozen LLM with a 2048-token context window. The output is a losslessly compressed binary code produced by arithmetic coding, where the LLM's autoregressive token probabilities serve as the statistical model; any number within the final arithmetic coding interval represents the compressed data.

## Method

LM-GC integrates frozen pretrained LLMs (TinyLLAMA 1.1B, OpenLLAMA 3B, LLAMA 2 7B) with arithmetic coding for lossless gradient compression. The key serialization step converts each 32-bit float into eight 4-bit hexadecimal digits, then groups every four bytes (one float) with a separator (space, comma, or semicolon), achieving up to 38× token savings over plain representations. The grouped text is tokenized and passed to the LLM, which predicts each token's probability autoregressively; these probabilities act as P_AC in the arithmetic encoder, which repeatedly subdivides an interval [0,1) until the full stream is encoded. A critical finding is that serialization format dramatically affects performance: using unfamiliar symbols (ISO-8859-1) degrades compression by up to 70%, and omitting separators causes 40% degradation, because the LLM must recognize the structural correspondence between the text and the underlying float layout. The method outperforms PNG, FLAC, LZMA, GZIP, and FPZIP by 10–17.2% across architectures and datasets, and remains compatible with lossy techniques like quantization and sparsification.

## Results

LM-GC achieves compression rates of 39.38% on MNIST, 38.83% on CIFAR-10, and 71.90% on TinyImageNet (lower is better), outperforming the best traditional codec (FPZIP) by 8.8%, 5.9%, and 17.2% respectively. The improvement grows with dataset complexity, with TinyImageNet showing the largest gap. Across model sizes, compression improves from Tinyllama 1.1B (36.30% best) to LLAMA 2 7B (32.21% best) on CIFAR-10, indicating that larger LLMs better capture gradient structure. The method also shows compatibility with lossy techniques, consistently improving compression beyond plain quantization or sparsification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LM-GC outperforms all five traditional lossless codecs (PNG, FLAC, GZIP, LZMA, FPZIP) across every dataset and architecture tested. On CIFAR-10 with ConvNet, LM-GC (Tinyllama, Hs) achieves 38.83% compression rate versus FPZIP's 41.26% (best baseline), a 5.9% relative improvement. The gap widens substantially on TinyImageNet, where LM-GC reaches 71.90% compared to FPZIP's 86.88%, a 17.2% improvement, suggesting LLMs better capture complex gradient structures. On MNIST, the improvement is 8.8% over LZMA (43.19%). FLAC performs worst among baselines (52.37% on CIFAR-10, 107.36% on TinyImageNet where it actually expands data), while LZMA and FPZIP are the strongest traditional competitors. The paper also shows that serialization choice is critical: using ISO-8859-1 encoding or no separators degrades LM-GC performance to below baseline levels (e.g., 109.07% for LLAMA 2 with ISO), confirming that the gains depend on presenting data in LLM-friendly formats.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes pre-trained LLMs as zero-shot statistical priors for arithmetic coding, drawing on the broader 'language modeling is compression' line of work (Deletang et al., 2024) but applying it to a domain LLMs were never trained on: neural network gradients. It departs from the deep generative prior literature (Ulyanov et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2021), which requires training a model from scratch on target data, by instead leveraging off-the-shelf LLMs in a fully zero-shot fashion. The method also contrasts with the dominant gradient compression literature, which focuses almost exclusively on lossy techniques (quantization, sparsification), by demonstrating that lossless compression with an LLM-based prior can outperform general-purpose codecs (PNG, FLAC, LZMA, GZIP, FPZIP) that use adaptive statistical models not tailored to gradients.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that LLMs implicitly encode structural knowledge about numerical data distributions that transfers to gradient tensors, opening a path toward LLM-based gradient denoising, anomaly detection, and privacy-enhanced federated learning. However, the practical impact is currently limited by very poor throughput (~4 hours to compress 28 MB) and evaluation restricted to small models (≤7B LLMs) and modest architectures (ConvNet through ViT on 32×32 images). Key open questions include whether prompt engineering or few-shot adaptation can further improve gradient modeling, whether the approach scales to larger LLMs and deeper networks, and how to reduce the arithmetic coding bottleneck to make the method viable for real-time distributed training.
