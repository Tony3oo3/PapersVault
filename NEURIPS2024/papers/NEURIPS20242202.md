## Problem

Neural Causal Models (NCMs) offer a flexible optimization-based approach to partial identification of causal effects, but formal consistency guarantees existed only for discrete variables or linear SCMs. Two critical gaps remained: whether NCMs can approximate general SCMs with mixed continuous and categorical variables, and whether the empirical max/min optimization converges to correct identification bounds as sample size grows. Additionally, the role of network architecture and regularization in ensuring consistency was unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n observational samples from the true data-generating distribution, a causal graph (an ADMG encoding directed and bi-directed edges), and a treatment assignment T=t. It produces a partial identification interval [F_lower, F_upper] for a target causal quantity (e.g., ATE). The constraint matching observational distributions uses Sinkhorn distance between two empirical distributions: one from the n observed samples and another from m_n samples drawn from the NCM, with a radius α_n that accounts for both approximation error and sampling error. The NCM's implied distribution is only accessible through Monte Carlo sampling, making the constraint inherently stochastic.

## Method

The paper decomposes the approximation error between an SCM and its NCM surrogate into two components (Theorem 1): error from approximating structural functions via neural networks and error from approximating latent distributions via push-forward maps. For the latter, a three-part architecture is designed: (i) a wide or deep ReLU network approximates the distribution on each connected component of the support (mapped to a unit cube), (ii) a second network transforms from the unit cube back to the original support, and (iii) a Gumbel-Softmax layer stochastically mixes components with correct probabilities. A novel representation theorem (Proposition 1) shows that under a lower-bound density assumption, any distribution on a unit cube can be exactly simulated by pushing forward a uniform variable through a 1/d-Hölder continuous curve (a generalized Hilbert curve), enabling deep networks to achieve O(N^{-2/d}) approximation rates versus O(N^{-1/d}) for wide networks. Crucially, the paper constructs a counterexample (Proposition 2) proving that without Lipschitz regularization on the structural functions, the NCM approach is inconsistent even for identifiable models. The main consistency theorem (Theorem 4) then shows that with Lipschitz-regularized wide networks and carefully scheduled widths, depths, and temperature parameters, the estimator's liminf/limsup are contained in the correct partial identification interval with probability 1.

## Results

The paper is primarily theoretical, with a small empirical validation in Section 4.1 comparing the NCM approach against Autobounds on two IV settings with 5,000 samples. In the Binary IV setting, NCM produces an average bound of [-0.49, 0.05] versus Autobounds' [-0.45, -0.05], both covering the true ATE of -0.31. In the General IV setting (continuous variables, discretized for Autobounds), NCM achieves a much tighter bound of [2.49, 3.24] (width 0.75) compared to Autobounds' [1.40, 3.48] (width 2.08), with the true value being 3. The main contribution is the asymptotic consistency proof (Theorem 4) showing that the NCM-based estimator converges to the correct partial identification interval under Lipschitz regularization.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the NCM approach against Autobounds [Duarte et al., 2021], a polynomial-programming method for discrete causal inference. In the Binary IV setting, Autobounds produces a slightly tighter bound ([-0.45, -0.05]) than NCM ([-0.49, 0.05]), though both cover the true value of -0.31 and Autobounds is closer to the LP-optimal bound [-0.45, -0.04]. In the General IV setting with continuous variables, NCM substantially outperforms Autobounds: NCM's bound [2.49, 3.24] has width 0.75 versus Autobounds' [1.40, 3.48] with width 2.08, a roughly 2.8× improvement in tightness. This advantage is expected since Autobounds requires discretization of continuous variables (yielding ~2^14 variables in the optimization), while NCM handles continuous variables natively. Both methods successfully cover the true value in all experiments, confirming correctness, but NCM provides more informative bounds in the continuous setting.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Neural Causal Model (NCM) framework introduced by Xia et al. [51] and Balazadeh et al. [3], which connected Structural Causal Models to neural generative architectures for identification and partial identification. While prior work established approximation power for discrete SCMs and consistency only for linear models, this paper proves consistency for general SCMs with mixed continuous and categorical variables by developing new representation theorems for probability measures (Proposition 1, building on Hilbert curve constructions) and leveraging distribution-generation results from Perekrestenko et al. [43] and function approximation results from Yarotsky [53]. The key departure is the discovery that Lipschitz regularization is not merely a training convenience but a necessary condition for asymptotic consistency, demonstrated via a counterexample (Proposition 2) that no prior NCM work had identified.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This paper provides the first theoretical justification for using NCMs for partial identification in general (non-linear, mixed-variable) settings, giving practitioners confidence that the max/min optimization approach converges to valid bounds. The Lipschitz regularization requirement is a practically critical finding: without it, the method can produce arbitrarily wrong causal estimates even when the observational distribution is well-matched, which would be a silent failure mode in applied causal inference. The main open question is finite-sample guarantees, which the authors acknowledge require 'substantial further theoretical developments' in understanding the geometry of the constrained optimization; they take a first step only for the no-confounding case. The empirical validation is limited to two small IV examples, leaving open whether the theoretical rates are tight in practice.
