## Problem

Real-world data often exhibits only approximate symmetry (e.g., local topography breaks translation equivariance in climate data), yet existing approaches to relaxing equivariance constraints are restricted to CNN/MLP architectures and specific symmetry groups, and often require loss-function modifications. This paper develops a general, architecture- and symmetry-group-agnostic framework for constructing approximately equivariant models from any existing equivariant architecture.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243077/cropped_page7_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a context set of (location, value) pairs and target input locations as input, and outputs a parametric predictive distribution (Gaussian mean and variance) at each target location, representing a stochastic process prediction. Inputs can be 1-D, 2-D spatial, or 4-D spatio-temporal (longitude, latitude, time, elevation). The key architectural detail is that the decoder additionally receives B learnable fixed-input functions evaluated at spatial locations, which act as symmetry-breaking channels; these are zeroed out outside the training domain to recover strict equivariance for out-of-distribution generalisation.

## Method

The core theoretical result (Theorems 2–3) shows that under Hölder regularity and compactness, any non-equivariant operator between function spaces can be approximated by a G-equivariant operator augmented with a finite number of fixed additional inputs. Practically, this is realised by inserting B learnable fixed-input functions (parameterised by MLPs or Fourier expansions) into the decoder of existing equivariant NPs (ConvCNP, EquivCNP, TE-TNP, PT-TNP), where they are summed with the dataset embedding to break equivariance in a controlled number of degrees of freedom. During training, the fixed inputs are randomly zeroed with a fixed probability to force the model to learn a strong equivariant component; at test time outside the training domain, they are forcibly set to zero so predictions revert to the equivariant component. The number B directly controls the degree of equivariance breaking: B=0 recovers strict equivariance, while increasing B allows progressively more non-equivariant flexibility, with empirical results showing that a small number (1–4) of fixed inputs often suffices to capture symmetry-breaking features.

## Results

On the synthetic 1-D Gibbs-kernel GP task, the approximately equivariant TNP(Ṫ) matches the non-equivariant TNP in-distribution (−0.406 vs. −0.406 log-likelihood) while matching the equivariant TNP(T) out-of-distribution (−0.424 vs. −0.430), where the non-equivariant TNP collapses (−1.373). On the 2-D smoke plume task, TNP(Ṫ) achieves 4.715 log-likelihood, outperforming both the non-equivariant TNP (4.299) and equivariant TNP(T) (4.181). On the real-world ERA5 2-D spatial regression, PT-TNP(Ṫ) achieves 1.22 log-likelihood on Europe (vs. 1.06 for PT-TNP(T) and 1.14 for non-equivariant PT-TNP), and on the 4-D spatio-temporal task it reaches 1.21 on Europe and 0.76 on the US, consistently beating both equivariant and non-equivariant counterparts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares approximately equivariant variants against both their strictly equivariant and non-equivariant counterparts across three architecture families. Against the TE-TNP/PT-TNP baselines (Ashman et al., 2024), the proposed TNP(Ṫ) and PT-TNP(Ṫ) outperform the equivariant TNP(T)/PT-TNP(T) on all four evaluation settings (1-D ID, 1-D OOD, 2-D smoke, 2-D/4-D ERA5) while also matching or exceeding the non-equivariant TNP/PT-TNP in-distribution; notably, the non-equivariant PT-TNP completely fails on the US OOD test (log-likelihood < −10²⁶) while PT-TNP(Ṫ) achieves 0.76. Against the ConvCNP baseline (Gordon et al., 2019), ConvCNP(Ṫ) improves over ConvCNP(T) by 0.07–0.08 log-likelihood on the 1-D tasks and 0.19 on the 2-D smoke task. Against the RelaxedConvCNP of Wang et al. (2022a), the proposed ConvCNP(Ṫ) is competitive but slightly worse on the 1-D OOD setting (−0.412 vs. −0.405) while better on the 2-D smoke task (3.827 vs. 4.006 for RelaxedConvCNP, though the latter uses a different architecture). Against the EquivCNP baseline (Kawano et al., 2021), EquivCNP(Ẽ) consistently outperforms EquivCNP(E) by 0.07 log-likelihood on the 1-D tasks and 0.04 on the 2-D smoke task, confirming that the fixed-input approach generalises beyond translation to more general group equivariances.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the equivariant neural process lineage (ConvCNP, EquivCNP, TE-TNP) and the broader equivariant deep learning literature, but its core conceptual contribution is a theoretical result (Theorems 2–3) showing that any non-equivariant operator between function spaces can be approximated by an equivariant operator augmented with a finite set of fixed inputs. This framework unifies and generalizes prior approximate-equivariance methods (Wang et al. 2022a's relaxed convolutions, van der Ouderaa et al. 2022's non-stationary filters), which the paper shows are special cases, while extending applicability to arbitrary architectures (transformers, GNNs) and symmetry groups. The paper also draws an explicit connection to positional embeddings in transformers, reframing them as a mechanism for breaking permutation equivariance in a controlled way.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The architecture- and group-agnostic construction provides a practical recipe for introducing approximate equivariance into any existing equivariant NP with minimal modification (adding learnable fixed inputs to the decoder), which is immediately applicable to a wide range of spatio-temporal regression problems. The OOD strategy of zeroing out fixed inputs outside the training domain is a simple but effective mechanism for recovering equivariant generalisation, and the empirical finding that training with approximate equivariance regularises the equivariant component (improving OOD performance even when fixed inputs are zeroed) is a useful practical insight. Open questions include rigorously quantifying and controlling the degree of equivariance departure, scaling to larger and more complex problems, and understanding how the number of fixed inputs should be chosen in practice beyond the 'sufficiently high' heuristic.
