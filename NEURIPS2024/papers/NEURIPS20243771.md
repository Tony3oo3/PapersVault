## Problem

Observational data for CATE estimation suffers from unobserved confounding bias, while IV-based estimation requires nonzero compliance across all covariate values—yet in practice, some subgroups exhibit zero or near-zero compliance, making IV estimates either invalid or extremely high-variance. No existing method combines both data sources to simultaneously correct confounding bias and handle weak instruments, leaving a gap for reliable CATE estimation in settings where neither dataset alone suffices.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243771/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes two datasets: an observational set (X, A, Y) with unobserved confounding and an IV/intent-to-treat set (X, Z, A, Y) where the binary instrument Z may have heterogeneous or zero compliance across covariate regions. It also requires either a known feature mapping φ: X → R^d or a representation class Φ for the shared-representation variant. The output is an unbiased CATE function τ̂(x) defined over the full covariate space X—including regions where compliance is zero—along with the estimated bias function b̂(x) = τ(x) − τ^O(x) that quantifies the confounding correction.

## Method

The core contribution is a two-stage, compliance-weighted framework. Stage 1 estimates a biased CATE τ̂^O(x) from observational data using any standard learner (T-learner, random forest, neural network). Stage 2 constructs IV pseudo-outcomes of the form [YZ(1−π̂_Z) − Y(1−Z)π̂_Z] / [V̂_Z γ̂] and regresses them against τ̂^O(x) plus a bias model, weighting each sample by γ̂²(x)V̂_Z²(x) to down-weight high-variance observations from low-compliance regions. Two extrapolation strategies are provided: (1) parametric, where b(x) = θ^T φ(x) with known φ and θ is recovered via K-fold cross-fitted weighted OLS, and (2) shared representation, where τ(x) = h^T φ(x) and τ^O(x) = (h^O)^T φ(x) for a jointly learned φ, so the bias ν = h − h^O is estimated in the same representation space. K-fold cross-fitting ensures compliance and propensity weights are learned on data disjoint from the ERM step, and the resulting estimators are shown to be consistent with convergence rates governed by the slowest of the γ, π_Z, and τ^O (or φ) estimation rates.

## Results

In the high-dimensional simulation (d ∈ {5, 10, 20, 50}), the combined estimator τ̂(x) from Algorithm 1 achieves MSE of 0.40±0.07 (d=5), 1.25±0.20 (d=10), 4.05±0.68 (d=20), and 9.39±1.64 (d=50), substantially outperforming both the biased observational CATE τ̂^O(x) (MSE 1.40 to 37.1) and the high-variance IV CATE τ̂^E(x) (MSE 3.97 to 43.2). On the 401(k) SIPP 1991 dataset, the combined estimator τ̂(x) closely tracks the IV ground truth τ̂^E(x) (e.g., 9.83k vs. 10.0k at 8 years education, single) while the observational baseline τ̂^O(x) is upwardly biased (11.9k), confirming effective debiasing in a real-world setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its two-stage combined estimator against two baselines: (1) the observational CATE τ̂^O(x) estimated via the T-learner (Künzel et al., 2019) with Random Forest regressors, and (2) the IV CATE τ̂^E(x) computed via the ratio estimator in Equation 3 with compliance clipping at 0.1. The combined method τ̂(x) dominates both baselines on every metric and setting. Against the T-learner observational baseline, the combined method reduces MSE by 3.5× at d=5 (0.40 vs. 1.40) and by 4× at d=50 (9.39 vs. 37.1), eliminating the systematic upward confounding bias. Against the IV-only baseline, the combined method reduces MSE by roughly 10× at d=5 (0.40 vs. 3.97) and by 4.6× at d=50 (9.39 vs. 43.2), resolving the high variance from weak/zero compliance. On the 401(k) dataset, the combined estimator (9.83–10.2k) aligns closely with the IV ground truth (9.88–10.2k) while the T-learner baseline is consistently ~2k higher (11.8–11.9k), confirming debiasing in practice.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the two-stage framework of Kallus et al. (2018) and Hatt et al. (2022), which combine observational and randomized (RCT) data to debias CATE estimates, to the more challenging setting where the 'experimental' data is an IV study with heterogeneous and potentially zero compliance. It also builds on the compliance-weighting literature of Abadie et al. (2024) and Coussens & Spiess (2021), which reduce variance in weak-instrument ATE/LATE estimation, by generalizing the weighting scheme to the CATE setting and augmenting it with observational data to cover zero-compliance regions. The key conceptual departure is treating the IV study not as a source of unbiased point estimates everywhere, but as a source of bias information that can be extrapolated via parametric or shared-representation assumptions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a practical gap: in digital platforms and personalized medicine, researchers often have confounded observational data and an IV study with low or heterogeneous compliance, but no principled method to combine them for CATE estimation. The theoretical guarantees (consistency rates in Theorems 2–3) provide a clear roadmap for practitioners, and the two algorithmic variants (parametric extrapolation vs. shared representation) offer flexibility depending on available domain knowledge. However, the 401(k) application reveals that standard deviations across data splits are large enough to preclude statistical significance, and the method's performance in high-dimensional settings (Table 2) degrades substantially with dimension, leaving open questions about scalability and the practical cost of the realizability assumptions on the bias function.
