## Problem

Traditional adversarial attacks constrain perturbations via geometric Lp-norm bounds, which are increasingly ineffective against modern adversarial defenses and perform poorly in black-box transfer settings. The paper identifies that these geometric constraints implicitly encode semantic similarity as a Gaussian distribution centered at the original image, and asks whether replacing this implicit Gaussian with a data-driven probabilistic generative model can yield adversarial examples that are both more semantically faithful and harder to defend against.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244328/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an original RGB image, a target class label, and a surrogate (victim) classifier as input, and produces a single adversarial image that the classifier misassigns to the target class while preserving the original image's semantic content. For MNIST (28×28 grayscale), an EBM is trained per-image on semantic-preserving transformations (TPS deformation, scaling, rotation, cropping); for ImageNet (224×224 RGB), a pretrained diffusion model is fine-tuned on the single original image. The resulting adversarial examples involve substantial pixel-level modifications—far exceeding typical Lp-norm budgets—yet remain visually natural to human observers.

## Method

The core contribution is a probabilistic reformulation where the adversarial distribution is a product of a victim distribution p_vic (derived from the classifier's loss, e.g., CW or cross-entropy) and a distance distribution p_dis (modeled by a PGM rather than an explicit Lp norm). Two PGM-based constructions for p_dis are provided: (1) an EBM trained on a set of semantically-preserving transformations of the original image, sampled via Langevin Monte Carlo; and (2) a diffusion model fine-tuned on the original image, where the victim loss is injected at each denoising step via a Tweedie estimate of x_0, yielding a Gaussian with a shifted mean (Theorem 2). Theorem 1 establishes that this probabilistic framework is exactly equivalent to the classical box-constrained optimization when p_dis is a Gaussian, providing a principled bridge from geometric to semantic constraints. Practical techniques include rejection sampling to guarantee attack success and a two-stage refinement (auxiliary classifier scoring + EBM energy ranking) to select the most semantically faithful adversarial examples.

## Results

On MNIST, the proposed method achieves a 100% white-box attack success rate against adversarially trained MadryNet (vs. 25.3% for PGD, 30.2% for ProbCW, 29.4% for stAdv), 92.6% human annotation success, and 39.2% success against the Certified Defence (vs. 10.7% for PGD). On ImageNet, the method (c=20) achieves 91.3% white-box success against ResNet-50, 25.4% human annotation success (indicating near-imperceptibility), and a 59.6% average success rate across transferability and adversarial defence settings. The method ranks second overall in transferability/defence (behind NCF at 66.2%) but substantially outperforms all baselines in human imperceptibility.

## Baselines & Comparisons

On MNIST, the proposed method dominates all three baselines: it achieves 100% white-box success against adversarially trained MadryNet (vs. 25.3% PGD, 30.2% ProbCW, 29.4% stAdv), 61.4% transferability to non-adversarial MadryNet (vs. 15.1–17.4%), and 39.2% against Certified Defence (vs. 10.7–20.8%), while also scoring highest on human annotation (92.6%). On ImageNet, NCF [51] achieves the highest overall average (66.2%) and white-box success (94.2%), but at the cost of extreme visual detectability (only 2.6% human annotation success, meaning annotators almost always identify the manipulation). The proposed method (c=20) trails NCF by ~6.6 points in overall average but leads by 22.8 points in human annotation (25.4% vs. 2.6%), indicating far more natural-looking adversarial examples. Against cAdv [2], ACE [54], and ColorFool [35], the proposed method wins on human annotation (25.4% vs. 16.9%, 11.2%, 6.7%) and overall average (59.6% vs. 55.2%, 49.9%, 50.3%), while being competitive on white-box success (91.3% vs. 93.3%, 91.2%, 90.4%). The trade-off is clear: NCF maximizes attack success at the expense of imperceptibility, while the proposed method achieves a better balance of attack effectiveness and semantic preservation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes the classical adversarial example optimization problem (Szegedy et al., 2014; Carlini & Wagner, 2017) as sampling from a product of two distributions — a victim distribution derived from the classifier and a distance distribution encoding semantic similarity — drawing on Hinton's products-of-experts framework. It departs from prior unrestricted adversarial attacks (spatial transformations, color manipulation, texture addition) and from recent diffusion-based attack methods that ad-hoc combine attack gradients with generative gradients, by deriving the combination principled from the original optimization objective. The distance distribution is instantiated via energy-based models or diffusion models fine-tuned per image (leveraging DreamBooth-style single-image adaptation), replacing the implicit Gaussian semantics of L_2 constraints with data-driven semantic manifolds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The probabilistic lens provides a clean theoretical bridge between geometrically constrained and unrestricted adversarial attacks, showing that traditional L_p constraints are simply special cases (Gaussian distance distributions). The strong transferability gains (e.g., 61.4% vs. 15.1% on MadryNet-noAdv) and low human detectability suggest that semantic-preserving perturbations are a more robust attack axis than pixel-level noise. However, the per-image model training/fine-tuning requirement limits practical deployment, and the authors themselves frame the defense landscape as a rock-paper-scissors game where anticipating the attack form is key — leaving open how to build classifiers robust to this entire class of semantic-preserving attacks without degrading standard accuracy.
