## Problem

Prior parameter identification methods for linear causal models with latent variables either only recover edge coefficients between observed variables or require restrictive structural assumptions (e.g., latent variables cannot be effects of observed variables). This paper tackles the most general setting—where all variables, observed and latent, are flexibly related—and asks whether all edge coefficients, including those involving latent variables, can be identified from observational data given the causal structure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240968/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a known DAG structure G (specifying which of m+n variables are latent vs. observed and all directed edges) and i.i.d. samples of only the n observed variables, summarized by the sample covariance matrix. It outputs the full weighted adjacency matrix F (all edge coefficients among observed, latent, and mixed pairs) and the noise covariance Ω, subject to the constraint that latent noise variances equal 1. Only second-order statistics are used, so the Gaussian assumption is not strictly required for identifiability, though it is assumed for the likelihood formulation.

## Method

The paper first characterizes three fundamental indeterminacies: scaling of latent noise variance (resolved by fixing Ω_εL = I), group sign (flipping signs of all edges incident to a latent variable), and orthogonal transformation (arising when latent variables share identical parents and children). It then provides sufficient graphical conditions (Theorem 3) based on atomic covers—each atomic cover must contain at most one latent variable, and simple treks between latent and observed members must not pass through other latent variables—and proves one condition is necessary. For estimation, the method minimizes a Gaussian negative log-likelihood over F and Ω with the unit-variance constraint on latent noise, using a closed-form parameterization of Σ_X via block matrix inversion (Proposition 1). A key trick reparameterizes each entry of Σ_X using the simple trek rule, which automatically enforces the unit-variance constraint on all variables and yields edge coefficients bounded in [-1, 1] when observed variables are standardized. Optimization uses Adam with 30 random restarts, and the approach scales to ~300 variables within an hour.

## Results

On synthetic data (20 graphs, ~15 variables, 3 latent), the proposed Estimator-TR achieves 0.0003 MSE up to group sign indeterminacy at 10k samples in the GS case, while the standard Estimator achieves 0.0012. In the harder OT case (orthogonal transformation indeterminacy present), Estimator achieves 0.0182 MSE at 10k samples. On the real-world Big Five personality dataset (~20,000 samples, 50 indicators, 5 latent dimensions), the estimated edge coefficients align with established psychometric findings (e.g., Conscientiousness → Openness at +0.26, Agreeableness → Extraversion at +0.39). Under model misspecification, Estimator-TR maintains MSE of 0.001 at α=0.8 (mild nonlinearity) but degrades to 0.027 at α=0.3 (strong nonlinearity).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper explicitly states that no existing method can serve as a baseline because the setting is strictly more general than prior work: all variables (observed and latent) are allowed to be flexibly related, and edge coefficients involving latent variables are targeted, which neither the latent projection (ADMG) framework nor the proxy/instrumental variable framework can handle. The only internal comparison is between two variants of the proposed method: the standard likelihood Estimator (Eq. 4, constraining latent noise variance to identity) and the Estimator-TR (Eq. 5 + trek-rule parameterization, constraining latent variable variance to 1). Estimator-TR consistently outperforms the standard Estimator in the GS case (e.g., 0.0003 vs. 0.0012 MSE at 10k samples) due to the more interpretable parameterization, but performs slightly worse in the OT case (0.0351 vs. 0.0182 at 10k), likely because the trek-rule constraint is less flexible when orthogonal indeterminacy is present. The paper validates its identifiability theory by confirming that MSE decreases with sample size in the GS case (where parameters are theoretically identifiable) while remaining bounded in the OT case (where only identification up to orthogonal transformation is possible).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the atomic cover framework for structure identifiability of partially observed linear causal models (Dong et al., ICLR 2024) to the parameter identifiability problem, and leverages the simple trek rule (Sullivant et al., 2010) for a covariance parameterization trick. It departs from two prior lines of parameter identification work: (1) the latent projection/ADMG framework (halftrek criterion, G-criterion), which loses information by collapsing latent variables into bidirected edges and can only identify observed-observed coefficients, and (2) do-calculus/proxy-variable approaches, which similarly target only direct effects among observed variables. The key conceptual departure is working directly with the full DAG to identify all edge coefficients, including those involving latent variables, and systematically characterizing three types of fundamental indeterminacy (scaling, group sign, orthogonal transformation) that prior work did not jointly address.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide graphical sufficient conditions for full parameter identifiability in the most general partially observed linear causal model setting, with a provably necessary component (Corollary 2: no atomic cover may contain more than one latent variable). The three-type indeterminacy taxonomy gives practitioners a clear diagnostic: scaling is fixed by a unit-variance assumption, group sign is trivially resolvable with domain knowledge, and orthogonal transformation is the fundamental barrier. Open questions include deriving necessary-and-sufficient conditions (the current Theorem 3 is only sufficient), extending to non-Gaussian or non-linear settings beyond the robustness checks, and handling practical cases where the sufficient conditions fail (the paper suggests local sub-structure recovery but does not formalize this). For a DL/CV audience the direct relevance is limited, but the likelihood-based estimation with trek-rule parameterization is a clean technique for any latent-variable structural equation model.
