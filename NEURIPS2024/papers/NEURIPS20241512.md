## Problem

In cognitive diagnosis, many students interact with only a limited number of exercises, creating data sparsity that leads to both inaccurate proficiency estimates and unfair outcomes across demographic groups. Existing mitigations rely on more complex model architectures that sacrifice the interpretability required in high-stakes educational settings (e.g., GRE uses only IRT). This paper is the first to explicitly connect data sparsity to educational fairness and addresses it from a data-centric perspective rather than a model-centric one.

## Input / Output

The method consumes binary student-exercise response logs (correct/incorrect), exercise metadata (difficulty and discrimination parameters for IRT/MIRT, or knowledge-concept embeddings for NCDM), and a sensitive-attribute partition of students into two groups for fairness evaluation. Beyond the standard proficiency estimate θ_i and predicted response probability p_j(θ_i), the framework outputs group-fairness gaps (ΔRMSE, ΔMAE, ΔAUC, ΔACC) measuring the disparity in utility between the two sensitive-attribute groups. The augmented training data consists of 'fake students' generated by flipping exactly one response per original student, with the number of flips controlled by |C_i|.

## Method

CMCD generates augmented training samples by, for each student s_i, flipping one response at a time to create |C_i| fake students s_i', then enforcing two monotonicity-based constraints: if the original student answered correctly, their proficiency must be ≥ the fake student's (Hypothesis 1); if incorrectly, ≤ (Hypothesis 2). Violations of these partial-order constraints are penalized via indicator-gated squared regularization terms Ω_1 and Ω_2, weighted by a strength parameter c, and added to the standard cross-entropy loss. The framework is backbone-agnostic and plugs into any CD model (IRT, MIRT, NCDM) without architectural changes. Theoretical analysis proves that the MLE under the augmented loss is a consistent estimator of true proficiency and that the augmented loss is strongly convex in regions violating the monotonicity assumption, yielding faster convergence than the original loss.

## Results

On the Math dataset with the IRT backbone, CMCD achieves RMSE 0.394, MAE 0.300, AUC 0.842, and ACC 0.772, outperforming the Origin baseline (RMSE 0.409, MAE 0.325, AUC 0.821, ACC 0.751) with statistical significance (p < 0.001). On fairness, CMCD reduces the group disparity in MAE from 0.052 to 0.029 and in AUC from 0.027 to 0.009 under IRT. The ASSISTments dataset (Table 3) shows similar patterns: CMCD achieves AUC 0.745 (vs. 0.707 Origin) and ΔAUC 0.027 (vs. 0.028 Origin) under IRT, with the largest utility gains on traditional backbones (IRT, MIRT) over the neural-network-based NCDM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CMCD outperforms all six baselines on both utility and fairness across all three backbones (IRT, MIRT, NCDM) on the Math dataset. Against the counterfactual data augmentation baselines (CF-IRT, CF-MIRT, CF-NCDM adapted from Xiong et al. [44]), CMCD achieves substantially larger utility gains: e.g., under IRT, CMCD reaches AUC 0.842 vs. CF-IRT's 0.821 and CF-NCDM's 0.824, and reduces ΔMAE from 0.042–0.044 to 0.029. Against the fairness-aware baselines (CD+Reg/GF from Li et al. [23], CD+EO and CD+DP from Yao et al. [47]), CMCD simultaneously improves utility and fairness, whereas these baselines often sacrifice utility for fairness (e.g., CD+EO drops IRT AUC to 0.813 while only marginally reducing ΔMAE to 0.041). The largest relative improvements appear on the traditional IRT backbone (AUC +0.021 over Origin) compared to the neural-network NCDM backbone (AUC +0.002), consistent with the paper's argument that data sparsity affects simpler models more severely. On the ASSISTments dataset, CMCD again leads all baselines on utility (e.g., IRT AUC 0.745 vs. best baseline 0.713) and achieves the best or near-best fairness scores across all backbones.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the monotonicity assumption—a foundational interpretability principle in cognitive diagnosis (IRT, MIRT)—as a structural constraint for data augmentation, a technique previously applied to recommendation systems via counterfactual 'what-if' generation (Wang et al. 2021, Xiong et al. 2021). It departs from the dominant model-centric paradigm of designing increasingly complex architectures to handle sparsity, instead proposing a model-agnostic loss modification that can be plugged into any CD backbone. The paper also carves new ground by being the first to explicitly connect data sparsity to group fairness in educational diagnosis, showing that disadvantaged groups suffer disproportionately from sparse interaction logs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is significant: CD underpins high-stakes assessments (e.g., GRE) where both accuracy and fairness are non-negotiable, and the model-agnostic design means practitioners can adopt CMCD without replacing their existing IRT or neural CD pipelines. However, the fairness gains are modest on neural backbones (NCDM), and the evaluation is limited to two datasets with a single binary sensitive attribute, leaving open whether the approach generalizes to multi-valued attributes or larger-scale deployments. The authors themselves flag privacy as an unresolved concern, suggesting federated learning as a future direction, and the monotonicity assumption, while educationally grounded, may not hold in all real-world learning scenarios.
