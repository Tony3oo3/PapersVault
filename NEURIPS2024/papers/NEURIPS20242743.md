## Problem

While a growing body of work critiques data and benchmarking practices in ML, comparatively little attention has been paid to the data repositories where benchmark datasets are stored, documented, and shared. The paper asks how these repositories can be designed and leveraged to address well-known issues in ML benchmarking, from representational harms and construct validity to overemphasis on a few datasets and lack of reproducibility.

## Input / Output

The paper takes as its analytical subject the landscape of major ML benchmark data repositories (HuggingFace Datasets, Kaggle, OpenML, Papers with Code, TensorFlow Datasets, and the UCI ML Repository) alongside documented criticisms of current data and benchmarking practices. It produces a structured set of design considerations and best practices for benchmark repositories, organized along the dataset lifecycle from creation and development through documentation, sharing, and reuse for model evaluation.

## Method

The paper introduces the term 'benchmark data repository' and systematically maps five categories of criticisms of ML benchmarking to specific mechanisms by which repositories can serve as part of the solution. These span: recognizing datasets as scholarly contributions (via DOIs, citations, connection metadata, and ML-aware licenses); detecting and addressing dataset content issues (via contextual metadata, quality review, and standardized revision/deprecation processes); promoting data usability and reproducibility (via compositional, task, and benchmark metadata); encouraging holistic evaluation (via multi-metric leaderboards with uncertainty reporting); and diversifying benchmark datasets (via living datasets and improved task-driven discoverability). The recommendations are grounded in existing standards (FAIR principles, datasheets for datasets, Data Cards, Croissant), observed trends in the field, and the authors' experience as repository curators, positioning repositories as a third-party intervention point between dataset creators and users.

## Results

This is a position paper that does not present experimental results or quantitative benchmarks. Instead, it identifies and discusses design considerations for benchmark data repositories (e.g., HuggingFace Datasets, Kaggle, OpenML, Papers with Code, UCI ML Repository) to improve ML benchmarking practices, covering dataset citations, contextual metadata, quality review, reproducibility, holistic evaluation, and dataset diversity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare methods or present baselines. It is a survey/position paper that reviews existing benchmark repositories and proposes best practices for their design and use. The authors draw on examples from their experience as repository curators and cite prior work (e.g., surveys of dataset documentation, dataset retraction case studies, and leaderboard analyses) to motivate their recommendations, but no head-to-head method comparisons are conducted.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper synthesizes a growing body of criticisms of ML benchmarking practices—ranging from dataset quality issues (Paullada et al., Raji et al.) to reproducibility concerns (Raff, Pineau et al.) and overfitting to narrow benchmarks (Dehghani et al., Teney et al.)—and reframes them through the lens of repository design. It draws on established data-management standards (FAIR principles, datasheets for datasets, Data Cards, DOIs, the Montreal Data License) but is the first to systematically connect these standards to the specific role of benchmark data repositories as a third-party intervention point in the dataset lifecycle. The authors, being curators of the UCI ML Repository, ground their recommendations in practical repository experience rather than purely theoretical data-stewardship frameworks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first consolidated framework for how benchmark repositories can address well-known ML evaluation problems, offering actionable design considerations (persistent identifiers, contextual metadata, quality review, versioning, deprecation protocols, uncertainty reporting) that repository operators and dataset creators can adopt. A key open question the authors flag is incentivization: how to motivate dataset creators to provide thorough documentation, how to compensate reviewers, and how to hold repositories themselves accountable to best practices—challenges that depend on broader shifts in community norms. The long-term impact hinges on whether the ML community treats data curation as a scholarly contribution on par with model development, a norm that remains fragile.
