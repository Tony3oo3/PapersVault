## Problem

Predicting which quantum circuits a given quantum computer can reliably execute (quantum capability learning) is difficult because the number of possible Markovian errors grows exponentially with qubit count and errors combine in complex, hard-to-predict ways. Existing neural network approaches (CNNs, GNNs) achieve only modest accuracy on real devices because they fail to learn the underlying quantum physics governing how errors propagate and accumulate within a circuit.

## Input / Output

The network consumes a quantum circuit encoded as a 3D one-hot gate tensor I(c) ∈ {0,1}^{n×d(c)×n_ch}, a 2×n measurement matrix M(c), and two precomputed d×k matrices — a sign matrix S(c) and a permutation-indices matrix P(c) — that encode how each tracked error is transformed as it is pulled through the circuit's Clifford gates. The output is a single scalar: either the probability of successful trial PST(c) or the process fidelity F(c), selected by toggling between two parameter-free output heads. Circuits are restricted to Clifford (stabilizer) circuits, which enables the efficient Pauli-group-based error propagation used in the non-trainable post-processing stage.

## Method

The qpa-NN architecture splits into two sequential stages. The first is a GNN-like neural network N that predicts the rates of a polynomially-sized set of plausible local H and S error generators (k = O(n) for planar connectivity) at each circuit layer; it uses spatially localized sliding filters W_j that snip out only the gates within l connectivity-graph hops of each error's qubit support, encoding the physical principle that error rates depend primarily on nearby gates. The second stage is a non-trainable function f that converts these predicted per-layer error rates into a PST or F prediction: it uses the precomputed S(c) and P(c) matrices to propagate each error through the circuit (exploiting the fact that Clifford unitaries preserve the Pauli group), applies a first-order Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion to combine all layer error channels into a single effective error map, and then evaluates the closed-form low-error approximation (sum of S rates plus squared H rates over relevant Pauli operators). This design offloads the classically tractable but physically complex task of error combination to a known formula, letting the neural network focus solely on learning the error rates themselves — a key reason the architecture handles coherent errors (which add and cancel) far better than off-the-shelf CNNs.

## Results

On six 5-qubit IBM Q experimental datasets, qpa-NNs achieve an average 50.4% reduction in mean absolute error (MAE) over state-of-the-art CNNs (σ=16.7%), with per-dataset MAE ranging from 1.09% to 1.25% versus 1.55%–3.44% for CNNs. On 4-qubit simulations with purely coherent errors, qpa-NNs achieve a 52.4% MAE reduction (σ=3.0%) and a Pearson correlation of r=0.968 versus r=0.749 for CNNs. On a 100-qubit ring simulation, qpa-NNs achieve an MAE of 0.097%, demonstrating the first neural-network capability model for a 100+ qubit device.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the CNN architecture from Hothem et al. (2024b), which was the prior state of the art for quantum capability learning. qpa-NNs win decisively on all six 5-qubit experimental datasets, achieving an average 50.4% lower MAE than the original CNNs (Bayes factors K between 10³⁰ and 10³⁸³, far exceeding the K≥10² threshold for decisive evidence). To address the concern that CNNs were trained on a broader set of circuits (including low-PST ones), the authors fine-tuned each CNN on the same high-PST subset used for qpa-NN training; even then, qpa-NNs maintain a 32.2% average MAE advantage (σ=17.3%) and outperform fine-tuned CNNs on all six datasets. On 4-qubit coherent-error simulations, qpa-NNs achieve a 52.4% MAE reduction over hyperparameter-tuned CNNs, and additionally demonstrate moderate out-of-distribution generalization to random mirror circuits (MAE 0.72%, r=0.912), suggesting they learn physically meaningful error rates rather than circuit-specific patterns.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the quantum capability learning task formalized by Proctor et al. [2021a] and the CNN-based predictive models of Hothem et al. [2024b], which directly map circuit encodings to performance metrics. The key departure is a physics-informed architecture: rather than letting a neural network learn the full error-combination physics end-to-end, the qpa-NN predicts only local error rates (using GNN-inspired spatial filters over the device connectivity graph) and then delegates the error-combination step to a non-trainable function implementing a first-order BCH expansion over the Pauli group, grounded in the H/S error generator taxonomy of Blume-Kohout et al. [2022] and the low-error fidelity approximations of Madzik et al. [2022]. This hybrid design offloads the classically tractable but exponentially costly exact computation to known physics, while the learnable component focuses on the genuinely data-dependent part (which errors occur at what rate).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~50% MAE reduction over SOTA CNNs, backed by Bayes factors up to 10^383 across six experimental datasets and five simulated datasets, makes predictive capability models substantially more reliable for stakeholders choosing which circuits to run on near-term quantum hardware. The 100-qubit demonstration is the first capability model of any kind at that scale, suggesting the approach is viable for early fault-tolerant machines. Key limitations remain: the method is restricted to Clifford circuits (leveraging Pauli-group closure for efficient error propagation), assumes time-translation-invariant (Markovian) errors, and models only H and S error classes. Extending to non-Clifford circuits, non-Markovian noise, and additional error types (e.g., amplitude damping) are natural next steps that the authors outline but do not address.
