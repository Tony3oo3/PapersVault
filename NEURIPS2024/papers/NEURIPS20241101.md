## Problem

While autoregressive language models exhibit strong in-context learning, their out-of-distribution compositional generalization remains poorly understood, particularly whether the ability to maintain consistency with unviolated rules when one rule is broken (rule extrapolation) is specific to the Transformer architecture or more general. Additionally, no normative theory exists to explain why rule extrapolation should be considered rational OOD behavior, leaving the field without principled criteria for evaluating such generalization.

## Input / Output

Models are trained on sequences from six formal languages spanning the Chomsky hierarchy (regular, context-free, context-sensitive), each defined as the intersection of two rules. At evaluation time, the model receives an OOD prompt that violates rule R2 by construction (e.g., a sequence starting with 'b' in a language requiring 'a's first) and must produce a completion via next-token prediction. Success is measured by whether the completion satisfies the unviolated rule R1 (e.g., matching counts of a's and b's) and whether the completion portion still attempts to satisfy R2, with chance-level baselines computed per language to account for differing rule complexities.

## Method

The paper contributes two complementary components. Empirically, it trains and evaluates five architectures (Linear, LSTM, Transformer, Mamba, xLSTM) on all six formal languages, finding no single architecture dominates: Transformers excel on context-free and context-sensitive languages but fall below chance on regular languages requiring parity computation, while LSTMs and Mamba perform best there. Theoretically, the paper proposes a non-parametric prior p_R for OOD prompt completion that generalizes Solomonoff induction in two ways: it conditions on a pre-training dataset D of i.i.d. sequences (separating training from testing), and it models semimeasures as lists of conditionals rather than joint distributions, which is necessary because zero-probability prompts have multiple valid conditional factorizations. The prior weights each explanation by 2^{-K(p_i|)}, encoding Occam's razor via prefix Kolmogorov complexity, and the paper shows this normative algorithm predicts the observed training dynamics where Transformers first learn the simpler rule (R2: ordering) before identifying the full language (R1 ∩ R2) as its subset.

## Results

On the canonical context-free language L3 = {a^n b^n}, the Transformer achieves 65.7% OOD R1 accuracy (extrapolating the #a=#b rule when the ordering rule is violated), substantially outperforming the LSTM (37.6%), xLSTM (43.8%), and Mamba (29.6%). On the regular language L1 = {bα}, the picture reverses: the LSTM achieves 86.2% OOD R1 accuracy versus only 44.5% for the Transformer and 59.1% for Mamba. On the context-sensitive language L5 = {a^n b^n c^n}, the Transformer again leads with 18.7% OOD R1 accuracy (chance: 0.3%), followed by xLSTM (11.6%) and Mamba (9.9%). The headline finding is that no single architecture dominates: Transformers excel on context-free and context-sensitive grammars, while LSTMs and Mamba are superior on regular grammars that require parity computation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares five architectures (Linear, LSTM, Mamba, Transformer, xLSTM) across six formal languages spanning the Chomsky hierarchy. The Transformer dominates on context-free languages (L3: 65.7% vs. 37.6% LSTM, 29.6% Mamba on OOD R1; L4 Dyck: 98.0% vs. 54.3% LSTM) and context-sensitive languages (L5: 18.7% vs. 6.8% LSTM; L6 CS Dyck: 98.0% vs. 100% LSTM on OOD R1, with Transformer matching LSTM). The LSTM wins decisively on regular languages: on L1 it achieves 86.2% OOD R1 versus 44.5% for the Transformer and 59.1% for Mamba, and on L2 it reaches 100% OOD R1 while the Transformer collapses to 6.7%. Mamba tracks the LSTM on regular languages (59.1% on L1, 95.9% on L2) but falls behind on context-free tasks (29.6% on L3 vs. Transformer's 65.7%). The xLSTM consistently lands between the LSTM and Transformer across all languages, e.g., 43.8% on L3 OOD R1 (between LSTM's 37.6% and Transformer's 65.7%) and 67.9% on L1 (between LSTM's 86.2% and Transformer's 44.5%). The authors attribute the Transformer's weakness on regular languages to its known difficulty with parity computation, consistent with Zhou et al. (2023).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the compositional generalization literature by defining a new evaluation scenario—rule extrapolation—where OOD prompts violate one rule of a formal language's rule intersection, and the model is assessed on whether it still satisfies the remaining rule. It builds directly on Reizinger et al. (2024), who first observed better-than-chance rule extrapolation in Transformers on a^n b^n, by generalizing to six languages across the Chomsky hierarchy and five architectures (Linear, LSTM, Transformer, Mamba, xLSTM). The theoretical contribution adapts the Solomonoff prior (Solomonoff 2001; Li & Vitányi 1997) into a normative OOD completion algorithm by conditioning on a pre-training dataset and modelling semimeasures as lists of conditionals, thereby separating pre-training from testing in a way the original prior does not.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that no single architecture dominates rule extrapolation across language complexities: Transformers excel on context-free and context-sensitive grammars but fall below chance on regular languages requiring parity computation, while LSTMs and Mamba outperform them there. This challenges the implicit assumption that the best in-distribution architecture is also the best OOD generalizer. The normative theory, though uncomputable, provides a principled lens for interpreting training dynamics (simplicity-ordered rule acquisition) and suggests a direction for future practical algorithms. Limitations include the small-scale formal languages (far from natural language), a non-representative 14-participant human pilot, and the absence of exhaustive hyperparameter ablations, leaving open whether findings transfer to realistic sequence modeling tasks.
