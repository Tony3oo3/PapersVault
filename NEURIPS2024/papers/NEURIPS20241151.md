## Problem

Learning policies that simultaneously optimize multiple short-term and long-term rewards is critical in domains like healthcare, economics, and recommendation, yet the standard linear weighting approach used in prior work (Wu et al., ICML 2024) can only recover suboptimal policies when rewards are interrelated and fails entirely in non-convex regions of the objective space. This paper addresses the gap by formulating the problem as a multi-objective optimization (MOP) and proposing a method that guarantees Pareto optimality even under correlated objectives and missing long-term outcomes.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular observational data with individual covariates X, a binary treatment indicator A, fully observed short-term outcomes S, and long-term outcomes Y that may be partially missing (governed by an indicator R depending on X, A, and S). A set of K preference vectors in R^M_+ (where M = I + J is the total number of short- and long-term objectives) must be specified in advance to partition the objective space. The output is a set of K Pareto optimal policies, each a binary treatment rule π: X → {0,1}, providing distinct trade-offs among all objectives rather than a single scalarized solution.

## Method

The DPPL (Decomposition-based Pareto Policy Learning) method decomposes the MOP into K constrained subproblems, one per preference vector, where each subproblem restricts the search to a subregion Ω_k of the objective space defined by the smallest acute angle with u_k. Each subproblem is solved via a steepest descent procedure: a descent direction is computed by solving a quadratic program whose KKT dual yields Lagrange multipliers λ_m and β_k', and the policy parameters are updated iteratively until no direction simultaneously improves all objectives (Lemma 2 guarantees convergence to Pareto optimality within each subregion). A key theoretical contribution (Theorem 1) establishes an explicit mapping from each preference vector u_k to ε-constraint thresholds ε_m, giving decision-makers an intuitive interpretation: ε_m is the minimum acceptable level of the m-th reward while maximizing another, which guides practical selection of preference vectors without trial-and-error.

## Results

On both IHDP and JOBS (r=0.2, T=4), DPPL achieves higher ΔW (balanced welfare) than the linear weighting (LW) baseline for the majority of 10 preference vectors. On JOBS, DPPL's ΔW ranges from 152.4 to 160.7 versus LW's 143.1 to 155.2, with DPPL winning in 9 out of 10 preference vectors. DPPL also exhibits substantially lower variance across 50 runs: on JOBS, S-VAR is 54.5–61.5 for DPPL versus 56.5–88.4 for LW, and L-VAR is 81.4–99.3 versus 79.7–98.6, indicating more stable policy learning. Sensitivity analyses confirm DPPL's advantage persists across missing ratios r ∈ {0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.5} and time steps T ∈ {4, 6, 8, 10}.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the linear weighting (LW) method from Wu et al. (ICML 2024), which aggregates multiple rewards into a single weighted-sum objective. DPPL outperforms LW on the primary ΔW metric in 9 out of 10 preference vectors on JOBS (e.g., 159.4 vs. 155.2 at (1.00, 0.00); 156.9 vs. 143.1 at (0.00, 1.00)) and in 7 out of 10 on IHDP. The most consistent advantage is in variance: DPPL's S-VAR on JOBS is 54.5–61.5 versus LW's 56.5–88.4, reflecting the benefit of restricting each subproblem to a smaller subregion of the objective space. On individual reward metrics (S-REWARDS, L-REWARDS), the comparison is mixed—DPPL wins on short-term reward in most vectors but occasionally trails on long-term reward, consistent with the Pareto trade-off structure. The paper attributes LW's suboptimality to its inability to capture non-convex Pareto regions and correlated objectives, which DPPL's decomposition approach explicitly handles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Wu et al. (ICML 2024), which balanced a single short-term and a single long-term reward via linear weighting, to the multi-objective setting with I short-term and J long-term rewards. It departs from the linear weighting approach by adopting a decomposition-based multi-objective optimization framework (rooted in Pareto multi-task learning [40] and steepest-descent methods for multicriteria optimization [41]), and introduces a novel theoretical bridge to the ε-constraint method [39] that gives preference vectors an interpretable meaning. The causal identification and estimation machinery (potential outcomes, propensity scores, doubly robust estimators under missing long-term data) is inherited from the causal inference literature [11, 8, 27].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ε-constraint interpretation of preference vectors is a practically useful contribution: it lets decision-makers specify acceptable worst-case thresholds for secondary objectives rather than abstract weights, reducing the trial-and-error burden in multi-objective policy deployment. However, the experimental evidence is limited to two small benchmark datasets (IHDP, JOBS) with simulated long-term outcomes and binary treatments, and the performance gains over linear weighting, while consistent, are modest in absolute terms. Key open questions include extension to continuous treatments (e.g., dosage, price), scalability to many more objectives, and validation on real-world longitudinal data where long-term outcomes are genuinely observed.
