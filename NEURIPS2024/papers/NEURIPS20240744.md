## Problem

While DNN training exhibits diverse dynamical regimes (lazy/rich, oscillatory, low-dimensional), there is no quantitative, data-driven method to determine whether two training runs have equivalent (topologically conjugate) dynamics versus fundamentally different ones. Existing comparisons based on loss curves or parameter trajectories are too coarse and fail to respect the permutation symmetry inherent in DNN architectures, making it impossible to construct meaningful equivalence classes of training dynamics.

## Input / Output

The method consumes DNN weight trajectories sampled at each training iteration, using only the weights from the last hidden layer to the output layer as observables (to reduce dimensionality). Time-delayed copies of these weights (Takens embedding) are constructed, and when the weight count exceeds ~65,000 (as in Transformers), PCA projects the trajectory onto the top 10 principal components before time-delaying. The output is a set of Koopman eigenvalues (the spectrum) for each training run, pairwise Wasserstein distances between spectra, and a conjugacy/non-conjugacy verdict supported by a randomized shuffle significance test.

## Method

The framework leverages a theorem from Koopman operator theory stating that two discrete-time dynamical maps in the basin of a stable fixed point are topologically conjugate if and only if their Koopman eigenvalues are identical. In practice, the Koopman mode decomposition is approximated from data using DMD-RRR applied to time-delayed weight observables, yielding a finite set of eigenvalues that are invariant to permutation of hidden units. To handle the fact that numerical approximations will never yield exactly matching eigenvalues, the paper uses the Wasserstein distance between the two spectra as a similarity metric and introduces a randomized shuffle control (preserving the optimal assignment structure) to assess statistical significance of spectral differences. The approach is validated by recovering the known nonlinear conjugacy between online mirror descent and online gradient descent, then applied to identify non-conjugate dynamics in narrow vs. wide FCNs, architecture-specific transitions in CNN early training, and distinct stability properties in Transformers that do versus do not grok.

## Results

The Koopman-based framework correctly recovers the known topological conjugacy between Online Mirror Descent (OMD) and Online Gradient Descent (OGD): 25% of randomized eigenvalue shuffles produce Wasserstein distances ≥ the true distance (no statistical significance), while OMD/OGD vs. Bisection Method yields 0% (highly significant non-conjugacy). For single-hidden-layer FCNs on MNIST, narrow (h=5) and wide (h=40) networks have non-conjugate training dynamics (55% shuffle threshold), whereas intermediate width (h=10) aligns with the wide regime (81%). For CNNs, LeNet and ResNet-20 exhibit non-conjugate early-phase dynamics (0–4% shuffle threshold across the first four 100-iteration windows), and Transformers with constrained vs. unconstrained weight norms show distinct Koopman spectra, with the constrained case placing complex conjugate eigenvalues on the unit circle versus outside it for the unconstrained (grokking) case.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper validates its Koopman-spectral framework against the analytically proven conjugacy between OMD and OGD [24,25,26], successfully identifying the equivalence that is invisible from loss curves or raw parameter trajectories. The Bisection Method acts as a negative control and is correctly classified as non-conjugate to both OMD and OGD (0% of shuffles exceed the true Wasserstein distance, versus 25% for the true OMD–OGD pair). Across FCN widths (h = 5, 10, 40), the comparison reveals that the accuracy gain from h=10 to h=40 stems from increased model capacity rather than a change in training dynamics, since h=10 and h=40 exhibit conjugate Koopman spectra. For CNNs, LeNet (MNIST) and ResNet-20 (CIFAR-10) are compared at 100-iteration resolution: both show a similar coarse transition timeline consistent with Frankle et al. (2020), but fine-grained Koopman spectra are non-conjugate, indicating architecture-specific dynamical structure. For Transformers on modular addition, constrained-weight-norm training (no grokking) is compared against unconstrained training (grokking); the constrained case yields complex conjugate eigenvalues on the unit circle (stable oscillations) while the unconstrained case places them outside (unstable), providing a dynamical-systems signature that distinguishes the two regimes in the first 100 training steps.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of work: topological conjugacy theory from dynamical systems (which provides a precise definition of dynamical equivalence but has been intractable to compute for unknown systems) and data-driven Koopman operator methods (which can approximate nonlinear dynamics linearly but had not been used to identify conjugacies). Prior Koopman-based DNN work focused on optimization acceleration, pruning, and meta-learning, while prior training-dynamics studies (low-dimensional subspaces, lazy/rich regimes, early-phase transitions) relied on coarse-grained observables like loss and gradient magnitudes. The paper positions itself as the first to use Koopman spectra as a fingerprint for determining whether two DNN training processes are topologically conjugate, validated against the known OMD–OGD reparameterization conjugacy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a principled, permutation-invariant metric for comparing training dynamics that goes beyond loss curves, potentially enabling the construction of equivalence classes across architectures, initializations, and hyperparameter choices. However, the experiments are limited to small-scale settings (single-hidden-layer FCNs on MNIST, LeNet/ResNet-20, single-layer Transformers on modular addition), and the interpretation of Wasserstein distance magnitude remains heuristic. Open questions include whether the approach scales to large-scale training, how to handle continuous spectra, and whether the identified non-conjugacies (e.g., narrow vs. wide FCNs, grokking vs. non-grokking Transformers) translate into actionable training improvements.
