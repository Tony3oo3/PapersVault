## Problem

While large language models have improved dramatically since 2012, it remains unclear how much of this progress stems from algorithmic innovations (new architectures, better optimization, improved data usage) versus simply scaling up compute, parameters, and training data. Prior work quantified algorithmic progress in computer vision and reinforcement learning, but no comparable analysis existed for language model pre-training, leaving a critical gap for understanding the drivers of AI progress and informing policy.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a curated metadata dataset of ~231 language model evaluations (2012–2023) reporting token-level perplexity on WT103/WT2/PTB, parameter counts, training dataset sizes, and publication dates, sourced from ~200 papers plus 25 self-evaluated models. It produces statistical estimates of effective compute doubling times (with confidence intervals via bootstrapping), compute-equivalent gains for specific innovations (e.g., the transformer), and Shapley value decompositions attributing performance gains between parameter scaling, data scaling, and data efficiency across model pairs. A key constraint is that the model requires all four metadata fields to be present, and at most three models per paper are included to mitigate autocorrelation.

## Method

The core contribution is an augmented scaling law that extends the Chinchilla scaling law (L = E + A/N^α + B/D^β) by introducing effective parameters N_eff and effective data D_eff that grow exponentially with time, so that L = E + A/(N_eff)^α + B/(D_eff)^β. This encodes algorithmic progress as a constant exponential trend multiplying each error term, allowing the rate of progress to be estimated as a single parameter (α_year, β_year) per resource dimension. The authors fit ~90 model variants via leave-one-out cross-validation, selecting a benchmark-specific-constant model (model 7) with R²≈0.91, then derive closed-form doubling times for effective compute from the fitted parameters. A Shapley value decomposition in linear space attributes performance gains between compute scaling and algorithmic progress, and a sigmoid-gated variant of the model quantifies the transformer's compute-equivalent gain (median 9.6×) relative to non-transformer architectures.

## Results

The paper estimates that the compute required to reach a fixed language modeling performance threshold has halved every 6.1 months (median; 90% CI: 3.3–11.3 months) since 2012, with an aggregated estimate of 7.5 months (95% CI: 1.7–22.5 months) across model specifications. A Shapley value decomposition attributes 60–95% of performance gains between 2012 and 2023 to compute scaling, with only 5–40% from pre-training algorithmic progress. The transformer architecture alone yields a compute-equivalent gain of 9.6× (90% CI: 4.3×–34.5×), equivalent to roughly two years of algorithmic progress.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not propose a new model to benchmark against baselines; instead it performs a statistical decomposition of historical progress. The closest analog to a 'comparison' is the transformer vs. non-transformer analysis: the transformer lowers reducible loss by 5.4% (90% CI: 3.8%–6.9%) and achieves a 9.6× compute-equivalent gain over recurrent architectures (LSTMs, RNNs, state-space models). The paper's doubling-time estimates (6.1–7.5 months) are consistent with prior measurements in computer vision (Erdil & Besiroglu 2022: ~9 months) and deep RL sample efficiency (Dorner 2021: 5–18 months), placing language modeling at the faster end of the spectrum. The Shapley decomposition shows that compute scaling dominates over algorithmic progress in every model pair examined, with the relative contribution of algorithms declining over time (e.g., 43.8% for RNN→LSTM vs. 5.0% for Transformer→GPT-2). The authors note their estimates are consistent with, but slightly faster than, the 9-month halving found in the computer vision literature.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly adapts the augmented scaling law framework from Erdil and Besiroglu (2022) on algorithmic progress in computer vision to the language modeling domain, using the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022) as the base model and introducing effective data/parameter terms to capture algorithmic progress over time. It also draws on the compute-equivalent gain concept from Hernandez and Brown (2020) and the sample-efficiency measurement approach from Dorner (2021) in reinforcement learning. The key contribution is constructing a new dataset of 200+ language model evaluations on WikiText and Penn Treebank benchmarks spanning 2012–2023, enabling the first systematic statistical quantification of algorithmic progress specific to language model pre-training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first quantitative decomposition of language model progress into compute scaling versus algorithmic efficiency, finding that compute scaling has dominated recent gains (60–95% via Shapley values) despite the narrative around architectural breakthroughs. This has direct implications for AI forecasting and policy: if progress is primarily compute-driven, extrapolations based on compute budgets may be more reliable than those based on algorithmic innovation rates. Key open questions include whether the ~8-month halving rate will persist, how scale-dependence of algorithmic innovations affects extrapolation, and whether data quality improvements (conflated with data efficiency in the model) will shift the balance. The wide confidence intervals (2–22 months) and reliance on noisy benchmark perplexity data limit precision.
