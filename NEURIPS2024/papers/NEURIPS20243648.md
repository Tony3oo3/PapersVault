## Problem

Existing post-training backdoor defenses reduce attack success rates to near zero, creating a false sense of security that the backdoor threat has been eliminated. However, no prior work has investigated whether the backdoor truly persists within the defended model or whether it can be re-activated at inference time. This paper reveals that backdoors remain dormant in defense models and can be easily re-activated with tiny, well-designed perturbations to the original trigger.

## Input / Output

The method takes a defense model (a backdoored model that has undergone post-training purification), the original trigger ξ, and a small set of poisoned samples (as few as 2% or 1000 samples from the original poisoned data), and produces a single universal perturbation Δ_ξ constrained by an ℓ_p norm bound (ℓ_∞ ≤ 0.05 or ℓ_2 ≤ 2 for white-box/black-box; ℓ_2 ≤ 1 for transfer). In black-box settings, the adversary only has query access to the defense model's predicted scores; in transfer settings, the adversary simulates the defense process to create surrogate models without knowing the exact defense applied. The output perturbation is applied uniformly to all trigger images at inference time, re-activating the dormant backdoor to produce the target class prediction.

## Method

The paper first introduces the Backdoor Existence Coefficient (BEC), which uses TAC to identify backdoor-related neurons and CKA to measure activation similarity between the defense model and the original backdoored model across all layers, normalized by the clean model—revealing that BEC remains high even when ASR drops to near zero, confirming backdoors are dormant rather than eliminated. The re-activation attack is formulated as a constrained optimization problem: find the minimal universal perturbation Δ_ξ on the original trigger ξ such that the defense model classifies trigger-containing inputs to the target class, solved via PGD with a combined cross-entropy and margin loss in white-box settings. For black-box scenarios, they extend Square Attack into a Universal Square Attack that searches for a single generalizable perturbation across all poisoned samples rather than per-image perturbations. For transfer attacks, the adversary simulates multiple defense methods to create surrogate models and optimizes the perturbation across their ensemble. The approach is model-agnostic and extends naturally to multimodal contrastive learning (CLIP) by replacing the cross-entropy loss with the InfoNCE contrastive loss.

## Results

On CIFAR-10 with PreAct-ResNet18, the white-box re-activation attack (WBA) achieves an average ASR of 97.62% across six defenses, nearly matching the No Defense baseline (98.26%), while the black-box attack (BBA) reaches 71.64% average ASR—representing absolute improvements of 76.94% and 42.95% over defense models, respectively. The transfer attack (TA) further boosts ASRs to 99.16% (NC), 99.51% (NAD), and 97.44% (FT-SAM), surpassing even WBA in several cases. On CLIP (ImageNet-1K), WBA and TA achieve average ASRs of 92.08% and 70.19% against FT defense, and 80.46% and 70.19% against CleanCLIP, compared to only 56.84% and 26.84% for the defense models themselves.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's re-activation attack is evaluated against six post-training defense methods (Neural Cleanse, NAD, i-BAU, FT-SAM, SAU, FST) and two CLIP defenses (FT, CleanCLIP). WBA nearly restores the No Defense ASR across all defenses: e.g., 96.78% vs. 93.79% (No Defense) for BadNets+NC, and 99.93% vs. 99.76% for Blended+NC. BBA, while weaker, still achieves 71.64% average ASR versus 57.18% for the defense models, with the largest gaps against SAU (43.23% vs. 2.09%) and FST (55.27% vs. 2.40%). The transfer attack (TA) outperforms both WBA and BBA in several settings, reaching 99.51% ASR against NAD and 97.44% against FT-SAM—exceeding the No Defense baseline in some cases. On CLIP, WBA achieves 92.08% ASR against FT (vs. 56.84% defense) and 80.46% against CleanCLIP (vs. 26.84% defense), while TA reaches 70.19% for both. The paper also shows that the attack is more effective on defense models than on clean models (79.31% vs. 61.72% average WBA ASR), confirming that defense models retain a specific vulnerability absent in clean models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the backdoor attack-defense literature by shifting focus from the injection stage (where prior attacks like BadNets, WaNet, and Trojan operate) to the inference stage, where the adversary must re-activate a dormant backdoor in a defended model. Methodologically, it builds on Square Attack (Andriushchenko et al., ECCV 2020) by generalizing it from sample-specific to universal perturbation search, and repurposes the TAC neuron-identification metric (Zheng et al., ECCV 2022) to construct the novel Backdoor Existence Coefficient (BEC). Conceptually, it departs from the prevailing assumption in post-training defense literature (Neural Cleanse, NAD, i-BAU, FT-SAM, SAU, FST) that reducing ASR to near-zero equates to backdoor elimination, instead arguing that defenses merely suppress activation while preserving the underlying backdoor circuitry.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that BEC remains high across all tested defenses despite near-zero ASR challenges a core assumption in the backdoor defense community and provides a new metric (BEC) for assessing whether a defense truly removes backdoor structure. The re-activation attack is practical in white-box, black-box (10K queries), and transfer settings, and generalizes to multimodal CLIP models, making it a credible threat model for deployed systems. Key limitations include vulnerability to adaptive noise injection by defenders and the requirement that the adversary knows the original trigger and retains some poisoned samples. Open questions include designing defenses that simultaneously reduce both ASR and BEC, and developing attacks robust to query-noise defenses.
