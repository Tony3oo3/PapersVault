## Problem

Identifying which filters in a CNN are critical for class predictions is essential for structured pruning and selective class forgetting, but prior distributional approaches (e.g., TVSPrune) relied on unrealistic Gaussianity assumptions on class-conditional feature maps. This paper asks whether discriminative filters can be identified without any distributional assumptions, whether the framework extends beyond structured pruning to class unlearning, and whether better identification of discriminative components improves editing performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a pretrained CNN and estimates of class-conditional feature map distributions (computed from a small number of samples, e.g., 256 per forget class and 1024 per remain class), along with per-layer sparsity budgets. Crucially, it requires no access to the training data, loss function, or gradients—only distributional access to feature maps. The output is a set of binary pruning masks applied to convolutional filters, yielding either a sparsified model (DISCEDIT-SP) or a model with a target class effectively forgotten (DISCEDIT-U), which can optionally be fine-tuned for a small number of epochs.

## Method

DISCEDIT formalizes a filter's discriminative ability as the Bayes error rate of the best binary classifier trained on its class-conditional feature maps, which is equivalent to the Total Variation distance between those distributions. Since TV distance is intractable to compute, the paper derives a witness function-based lower bound (Theorem 1) that depends only on the first and second moments of a scalar-valued function f, requiring no assumptions on the underlying distributions. By choosing f as a linear projection of polynomial features, the bound reduces to monotonic functions of the Fisher discriminant and Minimax Probability Machine (Corollary 2), and a robust variant accounts for finite-sample estimation error (Theorem 3). DISCEDIT-SP retains filters whose minimum TV lower bound across all classes is high (i.e., discriminative for at least one class), while DISCEDIT-U prunes filters with high TV lower bound for a single target class, effectively removing the subnetwork responsible for that class's predictions.

## Results

On structured pruning (DISCEDIT-SP), the method achieves 76.1% sparsity on VGG19 (CIFAR-10) with only a 0.96% accuracy drop after fine-tuning, and 40.8% sparsity on ResNet50 (ImageNet) with a 2.58% drop. On class unlearning (DISCEDIT-U), the method reduces accuracy on the forget class by 82.5% (VGG16, CIFAR-10, no fine-tuning) while retaining 91.6% accuracy on the remain set after 1 epoch of fine-tuning. Across all pruning settings, DISCEDIT-SP consistently matches or outperforms TVSPrune, CHIP, and L1-norm baselines, with the largest margin over L1 (e.g., −1.21% vs −6.21% on ResNet56 at 60.7% sparsity).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DISCEDIT-SP outperforms all three structured pruning baselines (TVSPrune, CHIP, L1) across every CIFAR-10 and ImageNet setting in Table 2. The margin is most pronounced against L1-norm pruning, which suffers 4–5× larger accuracy drops (e.g., −6.21% vs −1.21% on ResNet56 at 60.7% sparsity). Against TVSPrune [39], the closest competitor, DISCEDIT-SP wins by 0.2–0.7% accuracy drop across all settings, with the advantage growing at higher sparsity (e.g., −0.96% vs −1.13% on VGG19 at 76.1%). Against CHIP [52], DISCEDIT-SP is consistently better on CIFAR-10 (e.g., −1.32% vs −1.62% on VGG16 at 75% sparsity) and slightly better on ImageNet (−2.58% vs −2.76% at 40.8% sparsity). For class unlearning (Table 1), DISCEDIT-U without fine-tuning achieves 82.5% forget-class accuracy drop on VGG16/CIFAR-10, substantially outperforming the GA baseline [23] (22.5% drop), though the GA baseline retains slightly higher remain-set accuracy (88.8% vs 82.5%); with 1 epoch of fine-tuning, DISCEDIT-U reaches 91.6% remain accuracy, surpassing the IU baseline (89.8%).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends TVSPrune (Murti et al., ICLR 2022), which identified discriminative filters for structured pruning under a Gaussianity assumption on class-conditional feature distributions. The key departure is the derivation of distribution-agnostic, witness-function-based lower bounds on the Total Variation distance (Theorems 1–3), which remove the Gaussian assumption and reveal previously unexplored connections between TV distance and classical discriminant classifiers (Fisher linear discriminant, Minimax Probability Machine). The framework is further extended beyond structured pruning to class-wise machine unlearning (DISCEDIT-U), a setting where prior discriminative-filter approaches had not been applied. Robustness to moment estimation errors is incorporated by adapting techniques from Kim et al. (2005) and Lanckriet et al. (2002).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By eliminating the Gaussianity assumption, the paper makes distributional pruning applicable to real networks where class-conditional feature maps are demonstrably non-Gaussian (validated via Shapiro-Wilk tests in Appendix E.3). The unified TV-distance framework provides a single theoretical lens for both pruning and unlearning, and the witness-function bounds are of independent interest for distributional statistics. However, the method's efficacy degrades sharply when the number of classes exceeds the network width (e.g., ResNet56 on CIFAR100), and fine-tuning on the remain set can inadvertently restore accuracy on the forget class—both open problems the authors flag for future work. Extending the approach to generative models and debiasing tasks is identified as a natural next step.
