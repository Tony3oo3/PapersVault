## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks suffer from three compounding biases—skewed query distributions that miss real-world comprehensiveness, grading bias from LLM judges, and progressive contamination—while the gold-standard Chatbot Arena is prohibitively expensive (~$2,936 per model), slow, and irreproducible. No prior benchmark simultaneously achieves high correlation with human preferences, efficient and reproducible execution, and dynamic data updating to resist contamination.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243115/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes ~2M text-in-text-out user queries detected from Common Crawl and a pool of 18+ ground-truth-based benchmarks spanning general and domain-specific topics, producing MixEval (4,000 queries, avg. 23 tokens/query, 95.15% English) and MixEval-Hard (1,000 queries, avg. 27.3 tokens/query). Each output sample pairs a real-world web query with a benchmark ground-truth answer, and the entire benchmark can be regenerated within one minute by re-sampling a different batch of web queries, yielding 85% unique benchmark queries and 99.71% unique web queries across versions. Scoring uses GPT-3.5-Turbo as a model parser to handle both free-form and multiple-choice responses across heterogeneous benchmark formats.

## Method

MixEval is a two-stage benchmark reconstruction pipeline. Stage 1 (web query detection) fine-tunes Vicuna 33B on GPT-4-labeled data to detect user queries in Common Crawl with >99% recall and >98% precision, followed by GPT-4 Turbo filtering of non-query sentences and modality classification to retain text-in-text-out queries. Stage 2 (benchmark mixture) maps each web query to its most similar benchmark query via dot-product similarity between normalized all-mpnet-base-v2 sentence embeddings, subject to a length constraint on the input field to prevent long-context dominance. MixEval-Hard applies rejection sampling with difficulty scores computed as the dot product of model accuracy weights and per-question error vectors, prioritizing harder questions while enforcing a cluster-distance threshold to preserve the original query distribution. The dynamic updating mechanism re-samples different web query batches from the same distribution, achieving 0.36 Std. score variance across five versions while maintaining significant data point turnover.

## Results

MixEval-Hard achieves a Spearman rank correlation of 0.96 with Chatbot Arena Elo (and 0.95 with Arena Elo En), while MixEval achieves 0.93 (and 0.91 with Arena Elo En) — the highest among all 27 benchmarks evaluated, exceeding the previous best (Arena-Hard at 0.86) by 10 percentage points. On the benchmark itself, Claude 3 Opus and GPT-4 Turbo lead with 88.1% and 88.8% on MixEval and 63.5% and 62.6% on MixEval-Hard, respectively. MixEval requires only 6% of the time and cost of MMLU to evaluate a single model, and its dynamic update pipeline produces versions with 0.36 Std. score variance (on a 0–100 scale) while maintaining an 85% unique benchmark query ratio across versions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central comparison is Spearman rank correlation with Chatbot Arena Elo across 27 benchmarks. MixEval-Hard (0.96) and MixEval (0.93) outperform all existing benchmarks: Arena-Hard (0.86, LLM-judged), ARC-c (0.85, ground-truth), MMLU (0.83), MT-Bench (0.80), BBH (0.78), and GSM8K (0.78) — a 7–10 point margin over the next-best. Among ground-truth-based benchmarks specifically, MixEval (0.93) surpasses ARC-c (0.85) by 8 points, making it the strongest non-human-preference benchmark. In terms of cost, evaluating one model on MixEval costs roughly 6% of MMLU's inference cost, versus ~$2,936 for a single model on Chatbot Arena (estimated at $0.05/vote on MTurk). The paper also shows that MixEval improves the Arena Elo correlation of every individual benchmark in its pool (e.g., BoolQ rises from 0.37 to 0.42, AGIEval from 0.50 to 0.73, SIQA from −0.17 to 0.40), and that sample-level mixture (MixEval) substantially outperforms both benchmark-level and uniform mixture strategies. On the model leaderboard (Table 3), proprietary models (Claude 3 Opus, GPT-4 Turbo, Gemini 1.5 Pro) consistently outperform open-source models, with LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct as the top 7B model at 75.0% on MixEval.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by repurposing existing ground-truth benchmarks through a distributional lens: rather than designing new evaluation questions, it mines real-world user queries from Common Crawl and uses embedding-based retrieval to re-select and re-weight samples from established benchmarks (MMLU, BoolQ, ARC, etc.) so their query distribution matches the wild. Conceptually, it bridges two previously separate evaluation paradigms—ground-truth-based benchmarks (efficient, impartial grading) and crowdsourced human-preference evaluation like Chatbot Arena (comprehensive, unbiased queries)—by importing the query distribution from the latter into the former. The concurrent work MAmmoTH2 also detects web queries but targets domain-specific instruction data, whereas MixEval targets general-purpose query distribution fidelity. The dynamic updating mechanism draws on contamination-mitigation ideas from prior work on rephrased samples but applies them to a mixture-based construction pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MixEval offers practitioners a 6%-cost, reproducible alternative to Chatbot Arena with 0.96 Spearman correlation to Arena Elo, which could meaningfully shift how the community conducts routine model evaluation. The finding that base models correlate 0.95 with their chat counterparts in human preference rankings is a notable empirical result suggesting pre-training dominates post-training in shaping user preferences, with implications for where model developers should invest. Limitations include text-in-text-out scope only, dependence on Common Crawl quality and the embedding-based matching (which can introduce annotation noise), and the fact that the correlation was measured against a single snapshot of the Arena leaderboard. Open questions include whether the correlation holds as the model landscape shifts, how the pipeline handles emerging multimodal benchmarks, and whether the 85% unique-query ratio across versions is sufficient to prevent contamination in the long term.
