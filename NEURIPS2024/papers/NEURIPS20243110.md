## Problem

Existing einsum benchmarks and random generators cover only a narrow slice of practical tensor expressions—typically few, dense, floating-point tensors with simple structures—while real-world einsum workloads span hyperedges, Hadamard products, traces, sparse intermediates, integer and complex data types, and hundreds of thousands of tensors. This mismatch means einsum libraries are tuned to problems that do not reflect the expressions they must actually execute, leaving critical performance pitfalls undetected.

## Input / Output

Each of the 168 benchmark problems is stored as a pickle file containing the einsum format string, NumPy input tensors, the sum of all output tensor values (for correctness verification), and two precomputed contraction paths in SSA format—one minimizing operation count and one minimizing peak intermediate tensor size—each annotated with log-operations, log-intermediate-size, and density metadata. Twelve additional metadata attributes per problem (tensor count, hyperedge count, independent components, dimension sizes, etc.) enable filtering by structural characteristics. The repository also ships generators and converters that produce new einsum expressions from quantum circuits, tree tensor networks, and other problem classes.

## Method

The benchmark is composed of 168 problems across seven categories (graphical models, tensor network language models, model counting, quantum computing, random, structural, and weighted model counting), deliberately spanning tensor counts from 26 to 579,972 and including hyperedges, Hadamard products, and traces that most existing generators omit. Contraction paths are computed using a graph partitioning strategy (kahypar) combined with a greedy algorithm incorporating multiple cost functions, providing both operation-minimizing and intermediate-size-minimizing paths. Five experiments then expose concrete performance pitfalls of current engines (opt_einsum, PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX, NumPy): path-computation bottlenecks from string generation and linear-format conversion, degraded performance on asymmetric tensor contractions, the advantage of sparse execution (SQLite outperforming dense PyTorch), inconsistent data-type support across backends, and a 1.44× speedup from hybrid CPU-GPU scheduling over either device alone.

## Results

On the hybrid CPU-GPU experiment (model counting problem mc_2020_082, 195,372 tensors), the hybrid policy achieves 3.28±0.01 s, a 1.44× speedup over CPU-only (4.71±0.03 s), while GPU-only is actually slower at 6.48±0.04 s (0.73×). On the sparse model counting problem mc_2021_036, single-threaded SQLite with sparse storage (34.2±0.04 s) outperforms multi-threaded PyTorch with dense storage (43.2±0.09 s). Across data types on mc_rw_blockmap_05_01.net (2,737 tensors), PyTorch achieves the best floating-point times (5.18 s for float32, 7.93 s for float64), while TensorFlow is roughly 8× slower on int32 (41.58 s vs. 5.18 s for PyTorch float32) and JAX struggles severely with int16 (220.18 s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

PyTorch is the strongest floating-point backend, completing float32 in 5.18 s and float64 in 7.93 s, but it silently upcasts int16/int32 to int64, making those types infeasible. TensorFlow shows the worst overall performance, taking 41.58 s on int32 where PyTorch's float32 finishes in 5.18 s (~8× gap), and it cannot handle float32 at all due to a rank-12 tensor limit. JAX is competitive on int32 (25.27 s) and float32 (19.36 s) but catastrophically slow on int16 (220.18 s) and truncates int64/float64/complex128 to smaller types. NumPy fails entirely on this problem due to its 32-distinct-index limit. On the sparse model counting problem, SQLite's sparse single-threaded execution (34.2 s) beats PyTorch's dense multi-threaded execution (43.2 s), demonstrating that sparsity-aware algorithms can outperform dense GPU/CPU libraries. The hybrid CPU-GPU policy (1.44× speedup) outperforms both CPU-only and GPU-only execution, a capability no current einsum library supports.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a response to the gap between the complexity of real-world einsum expressions and the narrow scope of existing benchmarks and random generators, which predominantly feature structurally simple, two-tensor expressions lacking hyperedges, Hadamard products, and traces. For computing the contraction paths bundled with each benchmark instance, it builds on the authors' own graph partitioning strategy [71] combined with greedy multi-cost-function approaches [10, 50]. The problem instances are drawn from diverse existing sources—model counting competitions, UAI 2022 graphical model benchmarks, quantum circuit datasets, and tensor network language model queries—unified under a single einsum format with rich metadata, rather than introducing a new problem formulation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark exposes concrete, actionable performance pitfalls in widely-used einsum backends (opt_einsum, PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX, NumPy): string-generation bottlenecks at scale, poor handling of asymmetric tensor shapes, complete absence of sparsity exploitation, broken integer data-type support, and the lack of hybrid CPU-GPU scheduling. These findings are particularly timely as einsum is increasingly adopted as a backend for quantum simulation, probabilistic inference, and novel ML architectures, where expressions grow in both tensor count and structural complexity. Open questions include whether the identified gaps persist in distributed settings, whether specialized hardware data types (e.g., TensorFloat-32) can be meaningfully benchmarked, and whether the benchmark's exclusion of trivially small expressions limits its utility for the dominant workload in current deep learning pipelines.
