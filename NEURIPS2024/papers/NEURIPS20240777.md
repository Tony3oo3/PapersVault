## Problem

Restless multi-armed bandits (RMABs) assume rewards decompose as a sum of per-arm contributions, but many real-world resource-allocation problems—food rescue trip completion, peer-review coverage, blood donation matching—have inherently non-separable global rewards where the value of pulling a set of arms cannot be written as a sum of individual values. This paper introduces the RMAB-G problem to handle such global rewards and develops both index-based and adaptive policies to solve it.

## Input / Output

The method takes a vector of arm states (binary engagement indicators), per-arm transition probability matrices, a global reward function (assumed monotone and submodular in the action vector), per-arm reward functions, a budget K limiting how many arms can be pulled per step, and a discount factor γ. It outputs a binary action vector selecting exactly K arms to pull, computed either by ranking pre-computed Whittle indices per arm-state or by adaptively searching over arm combinations. The global reward is consumed as a black-box set function over the pulled-arm subset, which is what makes the problem non-separable and prevents direct application of standard Whittle indices.

## Method

The paper develops two index-based policies that linearize the non-separable global reward into per-arm contributions: Linear-Whittle uses optimistic marginal estimates p_i(s_i) = max R_glob(s', e_i), while Shapley-Whittle uses pessimistic Shapley values averaged over arm subsets to better capture diminishing returns. Because these pre-computed indices ignore inter-arm interactions and can degrade to a 1/K approximation for highly non-linear rewards, the paper introduces two adaptive families: (1) Iterative policies that recompute indices at each selection step, conditioning on the set of arms already pulled to capture current-step marginal value, and (2) MCTS-based policies that search a tree of arm combinations using UCT for selection/expansion/backpropagation and Whittle-index-guided ε-greedy rollouts to estimate future value. The paper proves the problem is PSPACE-hard, establishes a 1−1/e inapproximability lower bound, and derives asymptotic approximation guarantees (β_linear, β_shapley) for the index-based policies under indexability, irreducibility, and uniform global attractor conditions.

## Results

On the real-world food rescue dataset, all proposed policies outperform all baselines by at least 5% in normalized reward. For the Notifications scenario (N=100, K=50), the best policies (Linear-Whittle, Shapley-Whittle) achieve ~1.93 normalized reward versus 0.975 for Vanilla Whittle and 1.547 for DQN. For Phone Calls (N=20, K=10), Iterative Shapley-Whittle achieves 1.294 versus 0.989 for Vanilla Whittle and 1.209 for DQN. On synthetic data, the best policy is within 3% of optimal for N=4, and for non-linear rewards (small θ_linear), Iterative and MCTS Shapley-Whittle outperform Linear-Whittle by 56%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Vanilla Whittle (Weber & Weiss, 1990), all proposed policies achieve substantially higher normalized reward: in the Notifications scenario, Linear-Whittle (1.932) and Shapley-Whittle (1.931) roughly double Vanilla Whittle's 0.975, while in Phone Calls the gap is smaller (1.288–1.294 vs. 0.989) due to the near-linear reward structure. Against vanilla MCTS (Kocsis & Szepesvári, 2006), the paper's MCTS-based policies (MCTS Linear-Whittle, MCTS Shapley-Whittle) outperform it by a wide margin (e.g., 1.293 vs. 1.070 in Phone Calls), demonstrating that incorporating Whittle indices into the rollout step is critical. Against DQN (Mnih et al., 2013), all proposed policies win: in Notifications, the best index-based policies achieve ~1.93 versus DQN's 1.547 (a ~25% gap), and in Phone Calls the gap narrows to ~7% (1.294 vs. 1.209). The paper notes that DQN performance degrades with increasing N (from 4% worse at N=4 to 9% worse at N=10 relative to the best policy), attributed to the exponential state space. Overall, Iterative and MCTS Shapley-Whittle consistently perform best among the proposed methods, particularly for non-linear reward functions where they outperform Linear-Whittle by up to 56%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the restless multi-armed bandit (RMAB) framework, which itself builds on Whittle's (1988) index policy and Weber & Weiss's (1990) proof of asymptotic optimality, by relaxing the separability assumption on rewards. The core departure is that standard RMABs decompose rewards as a sum over arms, while RMAB-G introduces a global non-separable (submodular) reward function that couples arm decisions. To handle this, the paper repurposes Shapley values (Shapley, 1953) to linearize the global reward into per-arm contributions, and combines the resulting indices with UCT-based MCTS (Kocsis & Szepesvári, 2006) for adaptive arm selection. The work sits at the intersection of index policies, submodular optimization, and sequential decision-making, but departs from prior submodular bandit work by focusing on learning optimal decisions under stochastic state transitions rather than learning an unknown reward structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The separability assumption in RMABs has been a silent barrier to applying the framework to many real-world resource allocation problems (food rescue, peer review, blood donation, emergency dispatch) where the objective is inherently a function of the set of arms pulled. By formalizing RMAB-G and proving PSPACE-hardness plus a 1−1/e inapproximability bound, the paper clarifies the computational landscape and motivates the need for the proposed index-based and adaptive policies. Key limitations include the absence of theoretical guarantees for the adaptive (iterative/MCTS) policies, reliance on submodular and monotonic reward assumptions, and validation on only one real-world domain (food rescue). Open questions include extending bounds to adaptive policies, handling non-submodular rewards, and validating in additional domains like peer review.
