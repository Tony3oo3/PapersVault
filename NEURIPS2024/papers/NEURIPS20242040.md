## Problem

State-of-the-art parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) methods such as LoRA and DoRA adapt models by modifying a small subset of weights, yet interpretability research has shown that hidden representations encode rich, linearly-structured semantic information. This paper asks whether directly intervening on representations—rather than updating weights—can yield a more parameter-efficient and equally effective finetuning paradigm.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242040/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a standard text prompt and operates on the frozen base LM's hidden representations at a small, fixed set of prefix and suffix token positions in selected transformer layers. For autoregressive LMs it produces generated text via standard language-modelling decoding; for encoder-only models it produces class labels through a lightweight classification head applied to the intervened CLS representation. Because interventions target only a constant number of positions (e.g., p prefix + s suffix tokens), the added inference cost does not scale with prompt length.

## Method

LoReFT defines an intervention function Φ(h) = h + Rᵀ(Wh + b − Rh) that edits a hidden representation h within the r-dimensional subspace spanned by the orthonormal rows of a learned projection matrix R ∈ ℝ^{r×d}, steering it toward the values predicted by a learned linear map Wh + b. This is a direct adaptation of the distributed interchange intervention (DII) from causal interpretability, where the counterfactual source is replaced by a learned projection of the current representation. DiReFT is an ablation that drops the orthogonality constraint on R and the difference operation, reducing to Φ(h) = h + W₂ᵀ(W₁h + b), which resembles LoRA applied to hidden representations rather than weights. A full ReFT is a set of such non-overlapping interventions, each specifying a function Φ, a set of token positions P, and a layer l, with independent parameters across interventions. The base LM remains entirely frozen; only the intervention parameters (R, W, b per intervention) and, for classification, a small head are trained, yielding 15×–65× fewer trainable parameters than LoRA while matching or exceeding its performance across commonsense reasoning, instruction-tuning, and GLUE.

## Results

On the eight-task commonsense reasoning benchmark, LoReFT achieves 80.2% average accuracy on LLaMA-7B and 86.6% on Llama-3 8B using only 0.031% and 0.026% trainable parameters respectively, outperforming DoRA (78.1% and 85.2%) by 2.1 and 1.4 points while using 27× fewer parameters. On instruction following (Alpaca-Eval v1.0), LoReFT reaches 85.60% win-rate with 0.0039% parameters, surpassing full finetuning (80.93%) and GPT-3.5 Turbo (86.30% within 0.7%). On GLUE with RoBERTa-large, LoReFT achieves 88.2% average with 0.014% parameters, matching full finetuning (88.6%) at 1/7000th the parameter budget. On arithmetic reasoning, LoReFT (42.6% avg on LLaMA-7B) trails LoRA (46.9%) and adapters, indicating a limitation for long chain-of-thought generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LoReFT consistently outperforms DoRA (the strongest weight-based PEFT) on commonsense reasoning across all four model sizes (e.g., 80.2% vs 78.1% on LLaMA-7B; 86.6% vs 85.2% on Llama-3 8B) while using 15–65× fewer trainable parameters. Against LoRA, LoReFT wins on commonsense reasoning, instruction following, and GLUE but loses on arithmetic reasoning (42.6% vs 46.9% on LLaMA-7B), where the longer chain-of-thought generations dilute the fixed-position intervention. On instruction following, LoReFT (85.60% win-rate) surpasses both LoRA (81.48%) and RED (81.69%) at the same 0.0039% parameter budget, and even beats full finetuning (80.93%). On GLUE with RoBERTa-large, LoReFT (88.2% avg) matches full finetuning (88.6%) and outperforms LoRA (88.1%), Adapter (88.0%), and RED (88.0%) at 0.014% parameters. Prefix-tuning, Series Adapters, and Parallel Adapters are all dominated by LoReFT on commonsense reasoning with 10–100× fewer parameters. DiReFT, the ablation without orthogonality constraints, trails LoReFT by 0.5–1.5 points on commonsense reasoning but remains competitive with LoRA at 25× fewer parameters, and underperforms more noticeably on GLUE with small models (83.2% vs 84.2% for LoReFT on RoBERTa-base).

## Relation to Prior Work

LoReFT is a direct algorithmic descendant of the Distributed Interchange Intervention (DII) formulation from Geiger et al. (2023) and Wu et al. (2023), replacing the counterfactual source representation with a learned linear projection to make the intervention trainable. DiReFT, its ablation, is essentially LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) applied to hidden representations rather than weight matrices, while the broader ReFT framework generalises prior representation-editing methods (RED, activation addition, RepE) under a unified intervention formalism. The key conceptual departure from all existing PEFTs is that ReFT leaves model weights entirely frozen and instead learns position- and layer-specific edits to a small subset of hidden representations, drawing on the interpretability literature's evidence that linear subspaces of activations encode task-relevant semantics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By achieving 15×–65× fewer trainable parameters than LoRA while matching or exceeding state-of-the-art PEFT performance on commonsense reasoning, instruction-following, and NLU, LoReFT offers a compelling efficiency–performance trade-off that could reshape how practitioners adapt large LMs, especially in memory-constrained or multi-task deployment settings. The generic ReFT framework (position, layer, intervention function) opens a design space beyond what weight-based PEFTs allow, and the authors flag open questions about why the method works, whether learned orthogonal subspaces compose without retraining, and how to automate the still-manual hyperparameter search. A notable limitation is weaker performance on chain-of-thought arithmetic reasoning, suggesting the intervention's effect diminishes over long generations, and the method has only been validated on LLaMA-family and RoBERTa models.
