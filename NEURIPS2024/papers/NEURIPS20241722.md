## Problem

Generative reconstruction methods for brain decoding produce increasingly high-fidelity images and text, but it is unclear how much of this fidelity comes from actually extracting signal from neural recordings versus relying on the powerful priors of the underlying generative models. No existing metric quantifies the fraction of brain signal a reconstruction method genuinely uses, making it impossible to distinguish genuine neuroscientific progress from improvements in generative model quality or evaluation metric exploitation.

## Input / Output

The method takes fMRI recordings (approximately 14,000 voxels in visual cortex for NSD-based vision methods, or ~90,000 voxels for whole-brain language decoding) and a target reconstruction pipeline as input. It produces a curve of reconstruction quality (DreamSim, CLIP, SSIM, pixel correlation, WER, BLEU, BERTScore) as a function of bottleneck dimension L, along with the effective dimensionality of the bottleneck representations, spatial maps of which brain regions the bottleneck weights emphasize, and decodability of specific visual features (object class, brightness, contrast, edge energy) from the bottleneck at each size. The bottleneck is constrained to be a linear mapping, preserving interpretability of which voxels contribute to the compressed representation.

## Method

BrainBits inserts a linear information bottleneck g_L between the raw fMRI signal and the reconstruction method's prediction head, compressing the brain data to an L-dimensional vector before mapping to the method's targets (e.g., VDVAE and CLIP latents for BrainDiffusers, VAE and BLIP latents for Takagi & Nishimoto, or GPT embeddings for Tang et al.). The bottleneck and the downstream mapping are jointly optimized on the original reconstruction objective for each value of L, sweeping from very small (1–5 dimensions) up to the full data dimensionality. To contextualize performance, the method also computes a method-specific random baseline by substituting synthetic N(0,1) data for the brain signal and a reconstruction ceiling by feeding ground-truth latents directly into the generative pipeline. The linear bottleneck design enables direct projection of learned weights back onto the brain volume to identify which regions are exploited, and principal component analysis of the bottleneck representations reveals the effective dimensionality actually used. Key findings show that 30–50 dimensions suffice for most vision reconstruction performance (a ~300× reduction from 14K voxels), that effective dimensionality is only ~16 for a 50-dim bottleneck, and that models consistently focus on early visual cortex periphery regardless of bottleneck size.

## Results

For BrainDiffuser, a 50-dimensional linear bottleneck (a ~300× reduction from ~14,000 visual-cortex voxels) retains 75% of DreamSim, 95% of CLIP cosine similarity, 100% of SSIM, and 89% of pixel correlation relative to the full method. The effective dimensionality of that 50-dim bottleneck is only ~16, compared to 2,257 for the raw fMRI input. For language reconstruction (Tang et al.), a 1,000-dim bottleneck recovers 50% of BERT score, 26% of BLEU, and 20% of METEOR, with an effective dimensionality of only 5–20. Random baselines are surprisingly high (e.g., BERT ≈ 0.79 for language), and reconstruction ceilings are lower than expected, narrowing the meaningful performance range (e.g., DreamSim spans only ~0.15–0.75 for BrainDiffuser).

## Baselines & Comparisons

BrainBits is an evaluation framework rather than a competing method, so it does not 'beat' baselines; instead it interrogates three existing reconstruction methods (BrainDiffusers, Takagi & Nishimoto 2023, and Tang et al. 2023) to quantify how much neural signal each actually uses. All three methods show the same qualitative pattern: reconstruction performance saturates rapidly as bottleneck size increases, with the vast majority of fidelity achieved by 30–50 dimensions for vision and ~1,000 for language. Vision methods substantially outperform the language method on all metrics, and the language method remains much closer to its (already high) random baseline, indicating it exploits far less of the available ~90,000-voxel whole-brain signal. Across all methods, the bottleneck mappings concentrate weight on the periphery of early visual cortex and do not meaningfully expand to higher-level regions even as bottleneck size grows, suggesting that current generative priors, not richer brain decoding, drive most of the apparent reconstruction quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a critical diagnostic framework for the rapidly growing field of fMRI-to-stimulus reconstruction using generative models. It builds directly on three recent reconstruction methods—BrainDiffusers (Ozcelik & VanRullen, 2023), Takagi & Nishimoto (2023), and Tang et al. (2023)—by inserting a linear information bottleneck into each pipeline to quantify how much neural signal is actually consumed. Conceptually, it departs from the prior work's implicit assumption that higher reconstruction fidelity reflects better brain modeling, instead drawing on information-theoretic bottleneck ideas to separate the contribution of generative priors from the contribution of decoded neural signal. The paper also engages with the broader evaluation literature (CLIP, DreamSim, BERTScore, WER) to argue that none of these metrics account for the strong priors embedded in modern generative models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 30–50 dimensions out of ~14,000 voxels capture most reconstruction performance challenges a core assumption in the brain-decoding community and motivates a shift in how results are reported and interpreted. The proposed three-part reporting standard (method-specific random baseline, reconstruction ceiling, and bottleneck-size curve) provides a concrete path toward more honest evaluation, but the framework is currently limited to linear bottlenecks, fMRI, and three methods, leaving open whether non-linear or vector-quantized bottlenecks would alter the picture. A key open question is how to design reconstruction methods that genuinely leverage more of the neural signal rather than relying on generative priors, and whether the field's current metrics can be calibrated to distinguish prior-driven from signal-driven performance.
