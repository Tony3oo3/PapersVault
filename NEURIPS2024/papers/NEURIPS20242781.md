## Problem

Existing medical LLM benchmarks are organized by question format (multiple-choice, QA) or NLP task type (NER, summarization) rather than by the actual clinical workflow, and many lack data from real-world clinical practice. This makes it impossible to assess whether LLMs can genuinely support a patient through the full arc of a hospital visit—from deciding which department to see, through diagnosis and treatment, to post-discharge self-care.

## Input / Output

The benchmark is entirely in Chinese and comprises 12 datasets (5 newly proposed, 7 rebuilt from MedDG, IMCS21, CMB, CMExam, and PsyQA). Classification tasks (department recommendation, examination/disease/treatment/medication prediction) take a clinical vignette with candidate options and require a single correct answer; generation tasks (pre-consultation summary, doctor response generation, dialogue summarization, and four QA tasks) take multi-turn dialogues or free-form patient questions and produce free-text responses. Each QA instance is additionally annotated with key medical entities (disease names, drug names, symptom terms) extracted via GPT-4 and manually verified, enabling entity-level recall scoring alongside standard BLEU-4.

## Method

The core contribution is the benchmark architecture itself: the patient clinical journey is segmented into four stages—planning (department recommendation, pre-consultation dialogue summary, hospital reception QA), access (doctor response generation, patient-doctor dialogue summarization), delivery (examination, disease, treatment, and medication prediction), and ongoing care (drug QA, insurance QA, mental health QA)—with 12 tasks mapped across these stages. Ten LLMs spanning public (ChatGLM3, QWen 7B–72B), private (ChatGPT, GPT-4), and medical-specialized (HuatuoGPT2 7B/34B, DISC-MedLLM) categories are evaluated in both zero-shot and one-shot settings. Beyond accuracy and BLEU-4, the paper introduces entity-recall metrics and a GPT-4-as-judge protocol scoring accuracy, helpfulness, and linguistic quality on a 1–100 scale. A key empirical finding is that no single model dominates all four stages, and the combined GPT-Judge + Entity score achieves 91.1% pairwise ranking alignment with human evaluation, substantially outperforming BLEU-4 (35.5%) or entity recall alone (73.3%).

## Results

On the MedJourney benchmark (12 tasks across 4 clinical journey stages), no single LLM dominates all tasks. In zero-shot evaluation, HuatuoGPT2-34B leads the Delivery stage with the highest accuracy on Examination Prediction (0.757), Disease Prediction (0.766), Treatment Prediction (0.662), and Medication Prediction (0.777). GPT-4 achieves the best entity recall across most QA tasks (e.g., 0.603 on PCDS, 0.341 on IQA), and in human evaluation GPT-4 ranks first with an average score of 80/100, followed by QWen-32B (76) and QWen-7B/HuatuoGPT2-34B (75). The combined GPT-Judge + Entity metric achieves 91.1% alignment with human ratings, outperforming BLEU-4 (35.5%) and entity recall alone (73.3%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates three categories of LLMs: public (ChatGLM3, QWen-7B/14B/32B/72B), private (ChatGPT, GPT-4), and medical-specialized (HuatuoGPT2-7B/34B, DISC-MedLLM). HuatuoGPT2-34B clearly outperforms all other models on the four Delivery-stage prediction tasks (EP, DP, TP, MP), suggesting targeted medical optimization, but lags behind GPT-4 and QWen-72B on Planning and Ongoing Care QA tasks. GPT-4 achieves the highest entity recall on 5 of 7 QA tasks (e.g., 0.603 on PCDS vs. 0.524 for QWen-32B) and the top human-evaluation average (80/100), yet its BLEU-4 scores are often lower than QWen-72B or ChatGPT, indicating it generates more semantically complete but less n-gram-overlapping responses. QWen-72B and QWen-32B are the strongest general-purpose models, ranking top-3 in both LLM-judge and human evaluation. DISC-MedLLM consistently performs worst across nearly all tasks (e.g., 0.269 EP accuracy vs. 0.757 for HuatuoGPT2-34B), and one-shot prompting further degrades its Delivery-stage performance (EP drops to 0.065). Notably, one-shot examples help general models on summarization (PDDS BLEU-4 improves from ~9 to ~15 for QWen-72B) but hurt medical-specialized models on prediction tasks, where the example acts as noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior medical LLM benchmarks (CBLUE, PromptCBLUE, CMB, CMExam, MultiMedQA, MedBench) that organize tasks by question format (multiple-choice, QA) or NLP task type (NER, classification, summarization). Instead, MedJourney reorganizes 12 tasks along the four stages of a patient's clinical journey—planning, access, delivery, and ongoing care—drawing on existing datasets (MedDG, IMCS21, CMB, CMExam, PsyQA) and introducing five new doctor-annotated datasets. The conceptual lineage traces to patient-journey frameworks from healthcare operations literature (Sehrawat 2023, Donaldson et al. 2021, Gualandi et al. 2019), which the authors adapt into an LLM evaluation structure. No single prior benchmark covers the full journey, making this a repurposing of established tasks into a clinically coherent evaluation pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MedJourney fills a practical gap by enabling clinicians and developers to assess where LLMs succeed and fail across the full patient experience, rather than in isolated task silos. The finding that the combined GPT-Judge + Entity metric achieves 91.1% alignment with human ratings (vs. 35.5% for BLEU-4) offers a concrete, low-cost evaluation recipe for medical LLM deployment. Key limitations include the Chinese-only scope (excluding Llama and other English-centric models), the absence of statistical significance testing, and the relatively small size of several new datasets (e.g., 137 DQA pairs, 148 TP pairs). Open questions include whether the journey-based organization generalizes to other languages and healthcare systems, and whether the observed one-shot degradation in domain-specific models on delivery tasks reflects a fundamental tension between in-context learning and fine-tuned medical knowledge.
