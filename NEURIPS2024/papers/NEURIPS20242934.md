## Problem

Existing contamination detection methods for LLM benchmarks rely on detecting whether specific test samples appear in training data, making them vulnerable to evasion (e.g., training on rephrased samples) and unable to quantify how much contamination inflates measured performance. This paper addresses the gap by reframing contamination as a performance-based phenomenon—artificially inflated, non-generalizing benchmark scores—enabling detection that is robust to evasion and provides a quantitative estimate of the contamination effect.

## Input / Output

The method requires only black-box benchmark scores: the target model's score on the original benchmark D and on a reference benchmark D_ref (which can be a rephrased version, synthetic samples from the same distribution, or a different benchmark for the same task), plus scores of a diverse set of ~20 reference models on both benchmarks. It outputs a contamination magnitude estimate δ (in percentage points of inflated performance), a p-value for the null hypothesis of no contamination, and a 95% lower confidence bound on δ. The method is applied per benchmark and per contamination type (syntax-, sample-, or benchmark-specific), with multiple-testing correction via Benjamini-Hochberg when scanning many models.

## Method

CONSTAT redefines contamination as a performance gap rather than an information-flow event, then tests this gap statistically. It constructs a hardness correction function H by fitting a smoothing spline through the empirical CDF mapping between reference-model scores on D_ref and D, accounting for difficulty differences between benchmarks. The target model's expected performance on D is predicted by evaluating H at its D_ref score, and the contamination effect is δ = S_D(M) − H(S_D_ref(M)). Statistical significance is obtained by bootstrapping over both the reference model set and the benchmark samples, using pivotal intervals to correct for estimation uncertainty, yielding a p-value that quantifies the probability of observing the performance gap under the null hypothesis of no contamination. The approach distinguishes three contamination types by varying the reference benchmark: rephrased samples (syntax-specific), synthetic samples (sample-specific), and an alternative benchmark for the same task (benchmark-specific).

## Results

CONSTAT detects 89.1% of syntax-specifically contaminated models and 98.4% of sample-specifically contaminated models in a controlled finetuning experiment, outperforming all five baselines. The best baseline (Shi et al. [41]) achieves only 84.8% and 70.5% respectively, and the only baseline applicable without oracle access to uncontaminated samples (Shi [40]) detects just 21.7% and 16.4%. In real-world evaluation, CONSTAT identifies significant contamination in MISTRAL-7b-v0.1 (δ̂=8.25% on GSM8k, p=0.15%), LLAMA-3-70b (δ̂=6.61% on ARC, p=0.03%), YI-34b (δ̂≈6% on ARC and Hellaswag), and all top-3 Open LLM Leaderboard 7B models (δ̂>10% benchmark-specific contamination). CONSTAT also achieves r²=0.94 in predicting the true contamination effect magnitude.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CONSTAT outperforms all five baselines on both contamination types. Against the four baselines requiring oracle access to uncontaminated samples for threshold tuning (Carlini et al., Mireshghallah et al., Yeom et al., Shi et al.), CONSTAT leads by 4.3–13.0 percentage points on syntax-specific detection and by 27.9–32.8 percentage points on sample-specific detection. The gap is most dramatic for sample-specific contamination, where CONSTAT reaches 98.4% versus 70.5% for the best baseline (Shi et al. [41]). The only baseline applicable in a realistic black-box setting, Shi [40], performs far worse at 21.7% (syntax) and 16.4% (sample), making CONSTAT the only method that is both practical and highly effective. Notably, CONSTAT requires no prior knowledge of uncontaminated samples, no access to training data, and no metadata leakage, whereas the other methods either need threshold tuning on clean data or rely on perplexity-based signals that can be evaded by training on rephrased samples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs fundamentally from the information-flow definition of contamination (detecting whether specific benchmark samples appeared in training data) by proposing a performance-based definition: contamination is any artificially inflated, non-generalizing benchmark performance regardless of cause. Prior black-box detection methods (Shi et al., Mireshghallah et al., Oren et al.) rely on perplexity-based metrics or membership inference that require metadata leakage or are evadable via rephrased training data, and none can quantify the contamination's effect on scores. CONSTAT instead borrows statistical tools—empirical CDFs, smoothing splines, and bootstrap pivotal intervals—from classical nonparametric statistics and applies them to the novel problem of comparing a model's performance across two benchmarks relative to a reference model set, a construction not present in prior contamination literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: CONSTAT provides the first black-box method that both detects and quantifies contamination without requiring training data access or uncontaminated samples, and its findings that top-3 Open LLM Leaderboard models exhibit >10% benchmark-specific contamination and that MISTRAL-7b-v0.1, LLAMA-3-70b, and LLAMA-2-INSTRUCT-70b show sample-specific contamination directly challenge the reliability of widely-cited benchmark scores. Key limitations include dependence on an uncontaminated reference model set (mitigated by leave-one-out analysis) and potential distributional biases in GPT-4-generated synthetic benchmarks. Open questions include how the method behaves when the entire reference model pool is contaminated, whether it extends to proprietary models with limited API access, and how to handle tasks where no suitable reference benchmark exists.
