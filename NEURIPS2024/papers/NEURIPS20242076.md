## Problem

Deep neural networks overfit and fail to generalize when trained on small datasets, a critical issue in domains like medical imaging where data collection is costly. Existing evolutionary training methods such as Knowledge Evolution improve generalization through iterative weight reinitialization but rely on a fixed, randomly chosen mask that partitions parameters into 'fit' and 'reset' subsets for the entire training process, limiting the model's ability to adapt which connections matter as learning progresses.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242076/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes RGB images resized to 256×256 pixels from fine-grained classification datasets (ranging from ~1,000 to ~12,000 training samples) and outputs a single class label per image. During each generation, a 20% random subset of the training data is used solely for computing parameter importance via SNIP, while the full dataset drives the standard cross-entropy (optionally label-smoothed) classification loss. The network architecture (ResNet-18 or ResNet-50) and its parameter count remain fixed throughout all generations.

## Method

DNR extends the Knowledge Evolution iterative training paradigm by replacing its fixed random mask with a data-aware dynamic masking scheme inspired by selective neurogenesis. At the end of each generation, SNIP computes a connection sensitivity score for every parameter by measuring the loss change when each connection is infinitesimally removed, using a small data subset. A sparsity constraint (default 20%) selects the top-scoring parameters to retain as the 'fit' hypothesis, while the remaining 80% are randomly reinitialized to form the 'reset' hypothesis for the next generation. Because the mask is recomputed from the current model state and data at every generation, the set of retained connections evolves adaptively—early layers stabilize quickly while later layers continue to reshuffle as class-specific features emerge. This dynamic, data-driven selection of which parameters to preserve versus reinitialize is the key distinction from KE's static random partitioning, and it requires only ~20 seconds of additional computation per generation.

## Results

On five fine-grained small datasets (CUB-200, Aircraft, Stanford Dogs, Flower102, MIT67) using ResNet18 with label smoothing, DNR at 10 generations achieves a mean accuracy of 67.45%, outperforming the long baseline (65.89%), Knowledge Evolution (63.17%), LW (66.39%), and LLF (67.37%). On large datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Tiny-ImageNet) with ResNet50, Smth+DNR(f10) reaches a mean of 74.72%, surpassing Smth+KE(f10) at 73.33% and Smth+LB(f10) at 72.99%. DNR also demonstrates superior robustness: 27.7% mCA on CIFAR-10-C natural corruptions vs. 23.4% (LB) and 16% (KE), and higher balanced accuracy under power-law class imbalance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Knowledge Evolution (KE, Taha et al. 2021), DNR's direct predecessor, the improvement is substantial: Smth+DNR(f10) achieves 67.45% mean accuracy vs. Smth+KE(f10) at 63.17%, a 4.28-point gain, with the largest margin on Flower102 (+5.80 pts) and MIT67 (+4.52 pts). DNR also clearly outperforms the non-iterative long baseline (Smth+LB, 65.89%) by 1.56 points, confirming that the evolutionary paradigm with dynamic masking adds value beyond simply training longer. Compared to the architecture-specific reinitialization methods LW (66.39%) and LLF (67.37%), DNR edges ahead on mean accuracy (67.45%) and wins on 4 of 5 datasets, though LLF retains a narrow advantage on Aircraft (68.87% vs. 66.63%). Against the older iterative methods DSD (55.68%) and BAN (55.05%), DNR's advantage is overwhelming, exceeding them by over 11 points in mean accuracy. Notably, KE underperforms even the long baseline at equal compute cost (63.17% vs. 65.89%), which the authors attribute to its fixed random mask limiting adaptability, whereas DNR's data-aware dynamic masking resolves this limitation.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a direct extension of Knowledge Evolution (KE), which introduced an evolutionary training paradigm that splits network parameters into fixed 'fit' and 'reset' subsets via a random binary mask, reinitializing the reset subset each generation. The key departure is replacing KE's predetermined random mask with a data-aware dynamic mask computed via SNIP's connection sensitivity at the end of each generation, inspired by selective neurogenesis in the hippocampus. The paper also positions itself against architecture-specific reinitialization heuristics (LLF, LW) that assume memorization is confined to later layers, and against transfer learning, arguing that domain shift makes pre-trained weights unreliable in low-data, domain-specific settings like medical imaging.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical motivation—training generalizable models when data is scarce and expensive (medical imaging, autonomous driving)—is well-motivated, and the consistent 2–4 point gains over KE across five fine-grained datasets, plus robustness to corruptions, adversarial attacks, and class imbalance, make this a useful drop-in improvement for practitioners in the low-data regime. However, the gains are modest and the method's sensitivity to the importance-estimation criterion (SNIP outperforms FIM, weight magnitude, and random) remains an open fragility; the authors acknowledge that a more robust parameter-selection technique is needed. The finding that the 20%-sparse model retains near-dense accuracy hints at a broader efficiency story, but the paper does not explore this beyond a single ablation, leaving the theoretical underpinnings of why dynamic masking outperforms fixed masking largely unexplained.
