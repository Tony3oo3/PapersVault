## Problem

Predicting reference joint motion for below-knee limb-impaired individuals is severely hampered by the scarcity of locomotion data from such patients, making subject-specific model training impractical. Directly applying gait prediction models trained on able-bodied subjects fails due to inherent differences in gait patterns, while fine-tuning or transfer learning requires substantial data and computational resources. This paper asks whether a well-trained able-bodied motion model can be repurposed for impaired individuals through input-level reprogramming alone, without altering any pretrained model parameters.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a 20-step temporal history (K=20) of 6 features per timestep: angular velocities of the shank and thigh segments and knee joint angular positions on both the right and left sides, forming a 20×6 input matrix. The output is the angular position of the ankle joint on the impaired side. Because direct ground-truth ankle motion is unavailable for amputees, desired outputs are synthesized by averaging gait cycles from able-bodied subjects matched in anthropometry (±5 cm height, ±5 kg mass) and walking speed (±0.1 m/s) at the corresponding gait-cycle phase.

## Method

ReMAP comprises three components: a frozen foundation module (two time-convolutional layers with kernel size 5 and dropout 0.2, topped by task-specific two-layer MLP heads) pretrained on ~6.56M able-bodied samples; a retrieval-augmented template mapping module that computes a correction input X_corr for each impaired input via either nearest-neighbor search (matching a sequence of 2m+1 desired outputs and averaging over an ε-neighborhood with linear or exponential weighting) or network inversion (gradient-descent optimization of a random input to reproduce the target output); and a lightweight three-layer MLP refurbish module that learns to map impaired inputs to the correction templates. The refurbish module is trained under three strategies—correction-based (MSE to X_corr), target-based (MSE between frozen-model predictions and desired outputs), or a weighted hybrid of both—while the foundation module remains entirely frozen. The hybrid strategy, which balances input-space alignment with output-space accuracy, achieves the best performance in low-data regimes, outperforming direct mapping by ~16% and fine-tuning by ~13% at 10% training data.

## Results

At a 10% training data ratio, the proposed hybrid (inversion) strategy with task-specific prediction heads achieves R² = 0.87 ± 0.09 for ankle joint angle prediction in below-knee amputees, while hybrid (neighbor) achieves R² = 0.86 ± 0.07. These substantially outperform direct mapping (R² = 0.71) and fine-tuning (R² = 0.74) under the same low-data regime. Zero-shot cross-mapping (applying the frozen able-bodied model directly to impaired inputs) yields negative R² (−0.32 ± 0.48), confirming that the domain gap between able-bodied and impaired gait is too large for direct transfer without input reprogramming.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares ReMAP against three baselines: zero-shot cross-mapping, direct mapping (learning an input-to-output MLP in front of the frozen model), and fine-tuning the foundation model on impaired data. At the critical 10% training ratio, hybrid (inversion) with task-specific heads (R² = 0.87) outperforms direct mapping (R² = 0.71) by approximately 16% and fine-tuning (R² = 0.74) by approximately 13%. With task-shared heads, hybrid (neighbor) (R² = 0.84) improves over direct mapping (R² = 0.77) by ~7% and over fine-tuning (R² = 0.73) by ~11%. Zero-shot cross-mapping fails entirely (negative R²), demonstrating that the gait domain gap cannot be bridged without any adaptation. The pure correction-based strategies (neighbor search, network inversion alone) perform poorly at low data (R² = 0.19–0.39), but combining them with target-based loss in the hybrid formulation recovers most of the performance gap, highlighting the importance of the dual-objective training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural model reprogramming paradigm (Elsayed et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2021) from classification tasks to motion regression, specifically for predicting ankle joint kinematics in below-knee amputees. It integrates two complementary mechanisms for computing correction templates: gradient-based network inversion (Kindermann & Linden 1990) to avoid storing the full able-bodied training corpus, and retrieval-augmented nearest-neighbor search with ε-neighborhood weighting to handle temporal ambiguity in the output signal. The theoretical risk bound in Appendix A.2 is adapted from the Voice2series reprogramming framework, and the multi-task TCN foundation module follows standard time-series prediction architectures. The paper positions itself as the first application of model reprogramming to motion regression, departing from prior gait-prediction work that either trains explicit models on able-bodied data or attempts direct transfer to impaired subjects without input-level adaptation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The approach offers a data-efficient pathway to personalize prosthetic control for below-knee amputees, where each patient's data is inherently scarce and gait patterns are highly individualized. The key open question is whether the desired ankle trajectories—derived from anthropometrically-matched able-bodied subjects rather than directly measured—adequately represent the true reference motion for powered prosthesis control, a validation the authors acknowledge is needed. The method's generality (any frozen regression model + lightweight input mapper) suggests applicability to other data-scarce regression problems, but the current evidence is confined to a single impairment type and a small cohort. Broader validation across impairment severities, longer-term gait adaptation, and closed-loop prosthesis integration remain important next steps.
