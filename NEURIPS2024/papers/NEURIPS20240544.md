## Problem

As LLMs rapidly master existing benchmarks, static evaluation datasets suffer from overestimation due to benchmark leakage (test samples appearing in pre-training data) and become insufficient for differentiating model capabilities. Manually curating new, harder datasets is costly and time-consuming, and no prior work has automated the process of updating benchmarks to maintain reliable, timely evaluation.

## Input / Output

The framework takes seed test samples from existing benchmarks (MMLU, BIG-Bench) along with optional task descriptions; for the extending strategy, it additionally requires extracting a core entity or statement (e.g., a sports star's name, an algebraic concept, a physical law) from each seed. The mimicking strategy outputs new samples in the same format as the original (multiple-choice with four options, or free-response), while the extending strategy outputs free-form questions organized into four cognitive levels (Remember/Understand, Apply, Analysis, Evaluation), each paired with a reference answer. Generated samples are heuristically validated (model self-check or program check) to filter incorrect or duplicate entries before inclusion.

## Method

The paper proposes two complementary LLM-based generation strategies. The mimicking strategy prompts an LLM (GPT-3.5-turbo or GPT-4) to produce a similar but distinct sample from each seed, preserving stylistic and contextual essence to maintain comparable difficulty. The extending strategy, inspired by Bloom's taxonomy reorganized into four cognitive groups, first abstracts each original question into a core entity or statement, then prompts LLMs to generate new questions at each cognitive level, enabling difficulty control through both the cognitive-level distribution and the popularity of the seed entity. For evaluation, mimicked samples use the original task's exact-match metric, while extended samples are scored via an LLM-as-a-judge protocol (GPT-4) assessing accuracy, coherence, and factuality. The framework is validated for stability (four iterations with low standard deviation), effectiveness against leakage (fine-tuned models show reduced overestimation on updated datasets), and adaptability to different generator backbones (GPT-4 and Claude-3-Opus).

## Results

The mimicking strategy demonstrates high stability with standard deviations of only 0–3% across four independent dataset generations, and model performance on mimicked datasets closely matches the original (average difference of 5%, max 18%). For benchmark leakage mitigation, fine-tuning on leaked test data inflates scores dramatically on original datasets (e.g., Llama2-7b on Element jumps from 19.9% to 57.1%, +37.2%), but on mimicked datasets the same leakage causes minimal or negative change (19.9% → 0.9%, −2.2%). The extending strategy further alleviates overestimation on tasks where mimicking fails (Algos, Algebra), with leakage-finetuned models dropping from 42.2% to 34.0% on Algos and from 52.0% to 31.2% on Algebra. Difficulty control via Bloom's cognitive levels shows that Apply and Evaluation levels are significantly harder, and more popular seed entities yield easier questions, enabling fine-grained model differentiation (23.76% variance on Sports vs. 3.82% on the original).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing dataset-update methods but instead benchmarks its two strategies (mimicking and extending) against the original static benchmarks (MMLU, BIG-Bench) under controlled leakage simulations. On the original datasets, leakage fine-tuning inflates scores by 20–48 percentage points (e.g., Llama2-13b on Algos: 6.1% → 54.4%), confirming severe overestimation. On mimicked datasets, the same leakage causes far smaller or even negative deltas in most tasks (Sports: −6.8 to −7.6%; Element: −2.2 to −4.0%; Phys: −6.2 to −13.5%), demonstrating effective mitigation. However, mimicking fails on Algos and Algebra, where label-distribution memorization still inflates scores by 22–39 points; the extending strategy resolves this, dropping leakage-finetuned Llama2-7b from 42.2% to 34.0% on Algos and Llama2-7b from 52.0% to 31.2% on Algebra. The paper also validates backbone adaptability by showing Claude-3-Opus-generated extended samples produce consistent leakage-mitigation results (e.g., Llama3-8b on Algebra drops from 59.0% to 39.6% with LoRA fine-tuning). Human evaluation confirms high quality: 94.7–98.3% full-score rates on fluency and coherence, with 90.8% agreement between LLM-as-judge and human evaluators.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of data contamination detection (Dodge et al. 2021, Li 2023, Schaeffer 2023) and benchmark construction (MMLU, BIG-Bench, EvoWiki, AntiLeak-Bench), but departs from both by proposing *proactive automated updating* of existing benchmarks rather than passive detection or from-scratch construction. The extending strategy is directly grounded in Bloom's taxonomy (Krathwohl 2002), reorganized into four cognitive groups to enable difficulty control, while the evaluation pipeline borrows the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm (Zheng et al. 2023, Bai et al. 2023). Unlike prior contamination-mitigation work that relies on perplexity-based detection or follow-up questions, this framework regenerates unseen samples via prompt-engineered LLM generation, making it applicable even to closed-source models where pretraining data is inaccessible.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

As LLMs rapidly saturate public benchmarks, this paper provides a practical, low-cost pipeline for timely re-evaluation without the expense of manual curation, which is increasingly urgent as model capabilities advance. The finding that mimicking alone fails for tasks with structured label distributions (e.g., Algos with uniform 1–10 labels) while the extending strategy succeeds reveals a subtle but important failure mode for automated benchmark regeneration. Key limitations include reliance on GPT-4/Claude as generation backbones (introducing potential self-preference bias), testing on only 10 sub-tasks, and the LLM-as-judge evaluation introducing its own circularity. Open questions include whether the framework scales to all benchmark formats, how to incorporate external or domain-specific knowledge for updates, and whether the difficulty-control mechanism generalizes beyond the four cognitive levels tested.
