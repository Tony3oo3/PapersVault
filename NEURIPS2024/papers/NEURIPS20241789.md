## Problem

Existing definitions of LLM memorization are either too permissive (completion-based tests easily evaded by minor alignment or in-context unlearning), too restrictive (any string could be labeled memorized if the prompt contains the string itself), or impractical (counterfactual definitions require retraining). This paper identifies that the 'illusion of compliance'—where models are instructed not to reproduce certain content without actually removing it from weights—renders standard memorization tests unreliable for legal and regulatory monitoring of data usage.

## Input / Output

Given a target text string s (tokenized into sequence y) and a frozen LLM M, the method searches over discrete token sequences of varying length to find the shortest adversarial prompt x* such that greedy decoding of M(x*) exactly reproduces y. The output is the Adversarial Compression Ratio ACR = |y|/|x*|, a scalar quantifying how much the target is compressed by the model, plus a binary memorization label (ACR > τ, with τ configurable and set to 1 in experiments). The search is constrained to prompts strictly shorter than the target for a string to be deemed memorized, and the algorithm returns the specific minimal prompt found.

## Method

The core algorithm, MINIPROMPT, frames memorization detection as an adversarial prompt optimization problem: find the shortest prompt x that causes greedy decoding of M to produce the target string y exactly. It uses GCG (Greedy Coordinate Gradient) as the inner-loop discrete optimizer, which iteratively replaces individual tokens with top-k candidates selected via gradient information over the autoregressive next-token prediction loss. The outer loop searches over prompt length: starting at 5 tokens, it runs GCG for n steps; upon success it decreases prompt length by 1, and upon failure it increases by 5, with n scaling up 20% per length increase. The key insight is that this optimization-based definition is robust to the 'illusion of compliance'—in-context unlearning, alignment instructions, or minor weight perturbations that fool completion-based tests cannot prevent a sufficiently short adversarial prompt from eliciting memorized content, because the information remains encoded in the weights.

## Results

On Pythia-1.4B, famous quotes achieve an average ACR of 1.17 with 47% of samples memorized (ACR > 1), while paraphrased versions of the same quotes drop to ACR 0.68 and only 11% memorized, confirming the metric captures exact-match memorization. Random token sequences and post-training Associated Press articles (Nov 2023) are never compressible (ACR ≈ 0), validating the absence of false positives. For in-context unlearning on Llama-2-7B-chat, the average ACR across five famous quotes is 6.54 without the unlearning system prompt and 4.74 with it—both well above 1, indicating memorization persists. On the Harry Potter unlearning benchmark, 57% of target text is compressible before unlearning and 50% remains compressible after, demonstrating that the unlearning procedure fails to remove the information from the weights.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the completion-based Discoverable Memorization test (Carlini et al., 2023), the ACR metric reveals memorization that completion entirely misses: on the TOFU benchmark, after just 16 gradient-ascent unlearning steps, zero unlearned QA pairs can be completed exactly, yet a substantial fraction remains compressible with ACR > 1, showing completion is an overly conservative and easily evaded signal. Against In-Context Unlearning (Pawelczyk et al., 2023), the paper demonstrates that ICUL system prompts successfully block verbatim completion of famous quotes but reduce the average ACR only from 6.54 to 4.74—both values far exceed the τ = 1 threshold, proving the model retains the memorized content despite the apparent compliance. Against the Eldan & Russinovich (2023) Harry Potter unlearning method, ACR shows that 50% of Harry Potter-related text remains compressible after unlearning (versus 57% before), and adversarial prompts can still elicit correct answers, whereas the original paper's perplexity-based evaluation suggested successful forgetting. Overall, the ACR metric consistently outperforms all three baselines by detecting memorization that each baseline's evaluation criterion declares absent.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from three existing formalizations of LLM memorization—discoverable (completion-based, Carlini et al. 2023), extractable (existence of any prompt, Nasr et al. 2023), and counterfactual (retraining-based, Zhang et al. 2023)—arguing each is either too permissive, too restrictive, or computationally infeasible for legal/regulatory use. It repurposes adversarial prompt optimization techniques from the jailbreaking literature (GCG, Zou et al. 2023; random search, Andriushchenko 2023) toward a new objective: finding the shortest prompt that elicits a target string, then defining memorization via the input-to-output token compression ratio. The compression framing also draws on information-theoretic work (Delétang et al. 2023) and general-purpose compressors (SMAZ) as alternative baselines, while the unlearning experiments build directly on TOFU (Maini et al. 2024) and Eldan & Russinovich's Harry Potter unlearning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ACR provides a practical, adversarial, and computationally tractable tool for monitoring whether models have truly unlearned content or merely hidden it behind alignment layers—a distinction with direct legal implications for copyright and fair-use disputes. The finding that multiple unlearning methods (in-context unlearning, gradient ascent on TOFU, and the widely-cited Harry Potter unlearning) leave memorized content intact at high ACR values challenges the field's working assumption that these techniques effectively remove knowledge. Key limitations include evaluation only on models up to ~12B parameters, inability to scale to full training sets, and the subjective choice of compression threshold τ, all of which constrain near-term deployment as a legal instrument.
