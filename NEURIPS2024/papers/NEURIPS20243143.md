## Problem

Existing recommendation unlearning evaluations lack a unified framework and overlook deeper model properties such as fairness and robustness to varying unlearning sets. While completeness, efficiency, and utility are commonly measured, the impact of unlearning on group-level fairness and the sensitivity of results to which data points are targeted remain unexplored, leaving practitioners without a comprehensive picture of unlearning trade-offs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243143/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes a trained collaborative filtering model (WMF, BPR, or LightGCN), an unlearning set of user-item interactions selected via one of three graph-centrality-based strategies (core, edge, or random), and a held-out test set. It produces four structured scores: MIO accuracy (a 3-layer 64-16-4 neural network classifier approaching 0.5 for complete unlearning), NDCG@20 and HR@20 on the test set, wall-clock runtime in seconds for the unlearning stage, and two fairness metrics (A-IGF measuring the NDCG gap between top-5% active and remaining inactive users, and shardGF measuring variance across shards for exact methods). The unlearning ratio defaults to 5% of training data, with sensitivity explored at 10%, 15%, and 20%.

## Method

CURE4Rec is an evaluation benchmark rather than a new unlearning algorithm; its core contribution is a four-aspect, three-strategy evaluation protocol applied uniformly across existing methods (Retrain, SISA, RecEraser, UltraRE, SCIF). The unlearning set selection treats user-item interactions as a bipartite graph and defines node importance as I(x) = c(x) · (Σ c(y))/|N(x)|, where c(x) is the degree-based centrality, enabling principled selection of core (high-importance), edge (low-importance), and random data. Completeness is assessed via a Membership Inference Oracle trained on concatenated user embeddings and average item embeddings, while fairness is decomposed into active-inactive group fairness (A-IGF) and shard-level fairness (shardGF) specific to ensemble-based exact methods. All methods are evaluated under identical training, validation, and test splits on three datasets (ML-100K, ML-1M, ADM) with three recommendation models, ensuring a controlled comparison across the full evaluation matrix.

## Results

On ML-100K (WMF, random unlearning set), SCIF (the approximate unlearning method) achieves NDCG@20 of 0.2699 and HR@20 of 0.2617, substantially outperforming all exact unlearning methods: UltraRE (0.2354/0.2282), RecEraser (0.2208/0.2142), and SISA (0.2092/0.2049), while approaching the Retrain gold standard (0.2872/0.3353). In terms of unlearning efficiency, SCIF completes in ~325s versus 467–482s for EU methods and 4526s for full retraining. On unlearning completeness (MIO accuracy, lower is better), SCIF achieves 0.563 versus 0.538 for Retrain, indicating a residual 4.1% gap. For recommendation fairness (A-IGF, closer to Retrain's −0.0403 is better), SCIF (−0.0268) is closer to the gold standard than any EU method, which all worsen unfairness toward active users.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The benchmark evaluates four existing unlearning methods (SISA, RecEraser, UltraRE, SCIF) plus the Retrain gold standard across three datasets and three recommendation models. SCIF (approximate unlearning via influence functions) dominates in utility, efficiency, and fairness: it achieves NDCG@20 within ~6% of Retrain while running 14× faster than full retraining and 1.4× faster than the best EU method (SISA). Among EU methods, UltraRE consistently outperforms RecEraser, which in turn outperforms SISA on utility across all datasets and models, confirming the progressive improvements in the division-aggregation pipeline. However, all EU methods significantly degrade recommendation fairness (A-IGF), with RecEraser and UltraRE clustering active users into the same shards and exacerbating the active/inactive utility gap by 2–3× compared to SISA's random assignment. SCIF's main weakness is unlearning completeness: its MIO accuracy (0.563) remains ~4% above Retrain (0.538), and it performs worst on edge data (MIO 0.601–0.635), suggesting imprecise influence estimation for low-impact data points.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the recommendation unlearning literature (SISA, RecEraser, UltraRE for exact unlearning; SCIF for approximate unlearning) and adapts the Membership Inference Oracle evaluation protocol from Li et al. (2023b) and user-oriented group fairness metrics from Li et al. (2021). It departs from prior unlearning benchmarks in image classification, LLMs, and diffusion models by being the first tailored to recommender systems, and it extends the standard evaluation triad (completeness, utility, efficiency) with two new dimensions: recommendation fairness and robustness to varying unlearning sets (core, edge, random data). The graph-based node importance scoring for selecting unlearning sets draws on centrality concepts from network analysis but applies them specifically to the collaborative structure of user-item interaction graphs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that the division-aggregation design inherent to exact unlearning methods (SISA, RecEraser, UltraRE) has a previously unrecognized side effect: it systematically clusters active users into the same shards, exacerbating group-level unfairness, with RecEraser and UltraRE performing worst due to their non-random grouping. This creates a practical tension for deployment: methods that guarantee unlearning completeness may inadvertently introduce or worsen recommendation bias. Open questions include the trade-off between completeness and fairness (the paper explicitly notes this is not resolved), whether the fairness degradation persists at larger unlearning ratios, and whether the AU approach's weaker completeness guarantee is acceptable in regulated settings. The benchmark also highlights that SCIF's influence estimation is less accurate for edge data, suggesting that data-point-level heterogeneity in unlearnability remains an underexplored challenge.
