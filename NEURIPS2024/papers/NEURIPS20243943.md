## Problem

Modern LLM-based text embeddings are opaque black-box representations, making them unsuitable for scientific domains like language neuroscience where trustworthy interpretation of what drives predictions is the end goal. The paper asks whether interpretable embeddings can be obtained directly through LLM prompting, where each embedding dimension corresponds to a human-readable yes/no question, rather than learning opaque model weights.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243943/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language text (in the fMRI setting, a 10-word n-gram at each timepoint) and a fixed set of yes/no questions, producing a binary embedding vector where each dimension is the LLM's 0/1 answer to one question. For fMRI prediction, these per-timepoint embeddings are interpolated via Lanczos resampling and fed into a ridge regression model that outputs predicted voxel responses (100 PCA components). The binary nature of the embedding makes it compact and directly human-inspectable, and a distilled variant replaces the many LLM calls with a single RoBERTa forward pass through 674 classification heads.

## Method

QA-Emb constructs an interpretable embedding by prompting a pre-trained LLM (Mistral-7B and LLaMA-3 8B, averaged) with a curated set of yes/no questions about the input text, mapping each answer to a binary dimension. The 'training' problem reduces to selecting which questions to ask: GPT-4 is prompted with six task-specific instructions (leveraging neuroscience knowledge, brainstorming semantic properties, summarizing high-error examples) to generate 674 candidate questions, which are then pruned to 29 via multi-task Elastic Net. A ridge regression model is fit on the resulting binary embeddings to predict fMRI voxel responses, with temporal delays modeled via lagged feature duplicates. To address the computational cost of d separate LLM calls, the pipeline is distilled into a single RoBERTa model with 674 classification heads trained on LLaMA-3 8B answers, achieving near-identical fMRI prediction accuracy in one feedforward pass.

## Results

On the primary fMRI prediction task (3 subjects, ~100k voxels each), QA-Emb achieves a mean test correlation of 0.116, a 26% improvement over the interpretable Eng1000 baseline (0.097) and a 5% improvement over BERT (≈0.110), while trailing the best LLaMA-2 70B model by 7%. With only 29 selected questions (vs. Eng1000's 985 features), QA-Emb reaches a mean test correlation of 0.122, surpassing Eng1000's full 985-feature performance of 0.118. Distilling QA-Emb into a single RoBERTa model with 674 classification heads preserves performance (0.111–0.113 mean test correlation vs. 0.114 original).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the interpretable Eng1000 baseline (Huth et al., Nature 2016), QA-Emb wins decisively: a 26% average improvement in test correlation (0.114 vs. 0.097), and with only 29 questions it already exceeds Eng1000's full 985-feature model (0.122 vs. 0.118). Against BERT (Devlin et al., 2019), QA-Emb achieves a modest 5% improvement in fMRI test correlation, making it the first interpretable embedding to match or beat a black-box transformer on this task. Against the strongest LLaMA baselines (Touvron et al. 2023; Meta 2024), QA-Emb trails by approximately 7% (best LLaMA-2 70B layer 36 reaches ~0.125 average), confirming that opaque LLM representations still hold a raw accuracy edge. In the secondary information retrieval task (MSMarco), QA-Emb alone (MRR 0.45) underperforms BM-25 (Robertson et al., 2009; MRR 0.77), but combining BM-25 with QA-Emb yields a small but significant gain (MRR 0.80 vs. 0.77), suggesting the two capture complementary signal. Overall, QA-Emb's key advantage is interpretability at near-competitive accuracy: every feature is a human-readable yes/no question, enabling direct cortical mapping of semantic selectivity.

## Relation to Prior Work

QA-Emb repurposes the LLM-prompting paradigm (autoprompting, single-neuron explanation) for a different purpose: instead of optimizing a single prompt or explaining one neuron, it selects a *set* of yes/no questions whose binary answers form the embedding vector. This sits at the intersection of interpretable text representations (bag-of-words, BM-25, Eng1000 co-occurrence features) and modern LLM-based embeddings (Sentence-BERT, CoLBERT, LLaMA hidden states), carving out a middle ground where each dimension is human-readable yet the underlying LLM captures contextual nuance. In the neuroscience setting, it extends the fMRI encoding-model literature (Huth et al. 2016; Schrimpf et al. 2021; Antonello et al. 2024) by replacing opaque transformer features with a flexible, question-defined feature space that can be pruned, inspected, and adapted per task.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical contribution is that 29 natural-language questions outperform a 985-feature interpretable baseline (Eng1000) by 26% and slightly beat BERT, while remaining fully human-readable—this makes it a viable tool for neuroscientists to convert verbal hypotheses about semantic selectivity into quantitative, testable models. Two limitations remain open: (1) the computational cost of d LLM calls per embedding (partially addressed by distillation into a multi-head RoBERTa, which barely degrades performance), and (2) the faithfulness of LLMs on harder or more nuanced yes/no questions, where accuracy can drop to near-chance. Broader applicability to social science, medicine, and RAG systems is suggested but not yet demonstrated at scale, and the heuristic question-selection procedure (GPT-4 brainstorming + Elastic Net pruning) leaves room for more principled discrete optimization.
