## Problem

Optimizing continuous adversarial up-concave functions (encompassing DR-submodular and concave functions) in online settings has been studied only in fragmented special cases, with no unified framework connecting different feedback types, constraint geometries, or regret notions. In particular, no prior algorithms provided dynamic or adaptive regret guarantees for DR-submodular maximization, and existing results typically required stronger assumptions (smoothness, differentiability) than the underlying problem demands.

## Input / Output

The framework takes as input a base online linear/quadratic optimization algorithm (e.g., SO-OGD or Improved Ader), a function class proven to be upper-quadratizable with associated maps g and h, and a query algorithm for g. The base algorithm is consumed only through its stated regret bound and high-level properties (e.g., number of queries per round, whether it uses projection or separation oracle), not its internal structure. The output is a concrete algorithm with explicit static, dynamic, or adaptive α-regret bounds, where α is an approximation coefficient determined by the function class (e.g., (1−e^{−γ}) for monotone functions over origin-containing sets, (1−h)/4 for non-monotone functions).

## Method

The core mechanism is the OMBQ (Online Maximization By Quadratization) meta-algorithm, which wraps a base linear/quadratic optimizer: at each round it plays h(x_t) instead of x_t, queries a map g(f,x_t) via a query algorithm G, and feeds the resulting quadratic surrogate back to the base algorithm, so that the base algorithm's regret on quadratic functions directly bounds the α-regret on the original up-concave functions. Three function classes are shown to be upper-quadratizable—monotone up-concave over general convex sets (via a curvature-based inequality), monotone up-concave over origin-containing sets (via a boosting integral transform), and non-monotone up-concave over general sets (via a shifted boosting transform)—each yielding a specific g, h, and approximation coefficient. Four additional meta-algorithms (FOTZO, STB, FOTZO-2P, SFTT) convert between feedback types (first-order↔zeroth-order, full-information↔trivial query) using randomized smoothing and random-permutation blocking, requiring only that the base algorithm's query policy be independent of current-round observations. Applying SO-OGD as the base yields projection-free algorithms with adaptive regret across all 18 feedback/geometry combinations, while applying Improved Ader yields the first dynamic regret guarantees for online DR-submodular maximization.

## Results

The paper's framework (Corollary 7) achieves improved or matching static regret exponents across 18 online optimization settings. For projection-free algorithms, the method improves upon all prior SOTA results; when including projection-based baselines, it improves 12 cases and matches 5. Key improvements include reducing the regret exponent from 3/4 to 2/3 for monotone semi-bandit stochastic feedback over 0∈K, and from 4/5 to 3/4 for monotone full-info stochastic feedback over general convex sets. The paper also provides the first dynamic and adaptive regret guarantees for online DR-submodular maximization (Table 3), with dynamic regret of O(T^{1/2}(1+P_T)^{1/2}) and adaptive regret of O(T^{2/3}) or O(T^{3/4}) depending on the setting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against 10 prior methods across online (Table 1), offline (Table 2), and non-stationary (Table 3) settings. Against the closest projection-free competitor [35] (Pedramfar et al., ICLR 2024), the new framework strictly improves the regret exponent in 12 of 18 online cases (e.g., 3/4→2/3 for monotone semi-bandit, 4/5→3/4 for monotone full-info stochastic) and matches in the remaining 5. Against projection-based baselines [46] (Zhang et al., ICML 2022) and [48] (Zhang et al., 2024), the method matches the best known exponents (1/2) while requiring fewer assumptions (up-concavity instead of DR-submodularity, no smoothness). The only existing result that outperforms the paper's is [39] (Wan et al., ICML 2023) for monotone bandit deterministic feedback (exponent 3/4 vs. 4/5), but [39] uses a convex optimization subroutine per iteration and is not projection-free. For offline optimization (Table 2), the method improves complexity from O(1/ε³) to O(1/ε²) for monotone 0∈K stochastic first-order feedback, matching the best prior [19]. For non-stationary settings (Table 3), no prior results exist, making the paper's dynamic regret O(T^{1/2}(1+P_T)^{1/2}) and adaptive regret O(T^{2/3}) the first of their kind for DR-submodular maximization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the quadratization framework of Pedramfar & Aggarwal [34]—originally developed for online convex optimization—to a broader class of 'upper-quadratizable' functions that subsumes both concave and DR-submodular functions. It generalizes the boosting constructions of Zhang et al. [46, 48], which were tailored to specific DR-submodular settings (monotone over origin-containing sets, non-monotone over general sets), into a single meta-algorithmic template (OMBQ) that works uniformly across all three function-class/geometry combinations. The meta-algorithms for feedback conversion (FOTZO, STB, SFTT) are lifted from [34] and the random-permutation blocking idea from [45, 47, 35], but are made applicable to the α-regret setting and to up-concave functions rather than just convex ones.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides the first dynamic and adaptive regret guarantees for online DR-submodular (and more generally up-concave) maximization, filling a notable gap in the non-stationary optimization literature. By reducing up-concave optimization to linear/quadratic optimization via a single meta-algorithm, it means any future improvement in base linear-optimization algorithms (e.g., better projection-free methods) automatically transfers to the submodular setting without re-derivation. The paper requires only up-concavity (a weaker condition than DR-submodularity) and drops smoothness assumptions present in prior SOTA, broadening applicability. Open questions include identifying further function classes that are upper-quadratizable, tightening the approximation coefficients (e.g., the (1−h)/4 factor for non-monotone functions), and extending the framework to constrained or composite objectives.
