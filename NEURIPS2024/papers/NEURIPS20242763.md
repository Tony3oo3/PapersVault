## Problem

Return-conditioned supervised learning (RCSL) methods like Decision Transformer lack stitching ability, limiting them to mimicking the best trajectories in a dataset rather than recombining suboptimal segments into superior ones. Prior attempts to inject Q-function stitching via conditioning fail because Q-functions over-generalize on expert datasets (where RCSL already excels) while being most useful on suboptimal datasets (where RCSL struggles), and no prior work adaptively manages this complementary relationship.

## Input / Output

The policy network consumes a sub-trajectory of context length K (RTGs, states, and optionally subgoal states) and predicts the next action. The Q-aid term in the loss is weighted by the total trajectory return R(τ) rather than the per-step RTG, meaning the degree of Q-guidance depends on the overall quality of the trajectory the sub-trajectory belongs to. For goal-reaching tasks (QCS-G), a randomly sampled future state serves as an additional subgoal conditioning input alongside RTG.

## Method

QCS pre-trains a Q-function via IQL (expectile regression + in-sample TD bootstrapping) and then freezes it during policy training. The key insight, validated through Neural Tangent Kernel analysis, is that Q-functions trained on expert data become nearly flat over the action space (over-generalized), while Q-functions trained on suboptimal data retain discriminative power. The policy loss combines standard RCSL action-matching MSE with a Q-aid term Q_θ^IQL(s, π_φ(τ)) weighted by w(R(τ)) = λ(R* − R(τ)), a monotone-decreasing function of trajectory return, so that optimal trajectories rely purely on RCSL imitation while suboptimal trajectories receive progressively stronger Q-guided stitching. The base architecture is interchangeable (DT, DC, or MLP), and the Q-function is never updated during policy training, preserving its stitching signal without introducing actor-critic instability.

## Results

On the D4RL MuJoCo benchmark (9 datasets across medium, medium-replay, and medium-expert quality), QCS-R achieves an average normalized return of 90.3, outperforming the best value-based method (SQL, 83.1), the best RCSL method (DC, 82.2), and the best combined method (CGDT, 82.4). On AntMaze (6 goal-reaching datasets), QCS-G reaches 80.4 average, surpassing the best value-based baseline (SQL, 71.8) and the best combined method (POR, 76.2), with particularly large gains on the challenging large-play and large-diverse environments (70.0 and 77.3 vs. 53.2 and 52.3 for SQL). QCS also exceeds the maximum trajectory return in the dataset on most MuJoCo benchmarks, demonstrating effective trajectory stitching.

## Baselines & Comparisons

QCS-R outperforms all 12 baselines on the MuJoCo average (90.3 vs. 83.1 for SQL, 82.2 for DC, 82.4 for CGDT), with the largest margins on medium-replay datasets where stitching is most critical (e.g., walker2d-m-r: 94.1 vs. 81.2 for SQL, a 12.9-point gap). On AntMaze, QCS-G (80.4 avg) beats the strongest value-based method SQL (71.8) by 8.6 points and the best combined method POR (76.2) by 4.2 points, with the most dramatic improvement on large-diverse (77.3 vs. 52.3 for SQL, a 25-point gap). Against RCSL baselines, QCS dominates: DT (74.7 avg on MuJoCo) and RvS-R (71.7) are outperformed by 15+ points on average. Among combined methods that also attempt stitching, QCS surpasses QDT, EDT, CGDT, ACT, and POR on the MuJoCo average, though CGDT (82.4) and ACT (79.6) are the closest competitors. On Adroit, QCS-R (75.2 avg) outperforms DC (62.1) and IQL (54.4), confirming generalization to high-dimensional manipulation. The only notable exception is antmaze-umaze-diverse, where CQL-aided QCS (85.2) slightly outperforms IQL-aided QCS (82.5), suggesting the choice of Q-learning algorithm can matter for specific dataset characteristics.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the RCSL paradigm (Decision Transformer, Decision ConvFormer, RvS) for offline RL and on IQL for in-sample Q-function learning, but departs from prior stitching-augmented RCSL methods (QDT, ACT, CGDT, EDT) that incorporate Q-functions implicitly through conditioning (e.g., replacing RTG prompts with Q-values or advantages). QCS instead explicitly adds a Q-value term to the RCSL loss, weighted by a trajectory-return-dependent coefficient, and provides a Neural Tangent Kernel analysis explaining *why* Q-functions over-generalize on expert data—making them unreliable precisely when RCSL is most confident. This mechanistic diagnosis of Q-function failure modes is the key conceptual departure from prior work that simply observes stitching limitations without explaining the underlying generalization pathology.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QCS offers a principled, lightweight mechanism to combine the stability of supervised sequence modeling with the stitching capability of dynamic programming, consistently exceeding maximum dataset trajectory returns across MuJoCo, AntMaze, and Adroit benchmarks. The NTK-based explanation of Q-function over-generalization on expert data provides a reusable diagnostic for practitioners choosing between value-based and sequence-modeling approaches. Open questions include whether the linear trajectory-return weight is optimal or whether other dataset characteristics (state/action dimensionality, reward sparsity) should modulate the Q-aid, and whether more sophisticated per-state over-generalization detection could further improve performance. The method also carries the practical overhead of pre-training a Q-function, which may limit adoption in settings where that cost is prohibitive.
