## Problem

The proliferation of VLM benchmarks has created a fragmented evaluation landscape where researchers must implement each protocol separately, making systematic cross-model comparison difficult and leaving blind spots in model capabilities. The paper asks what actually drives VLM progress across diverse visual capabilities and whether scaling data or model size is sufficient for all task types, particularly reasoning and relational understanding.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242619/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework takes RGB images and text prompts (class labels wrapped in natural language templates, averaged across multiple prompt variants following Cherti et al.) and produces zero-shot class predictions via cosine similarity between image and text embeddings. For relation benchmarks, it contrasts correct versus incorrect captions against image representations rather than class labels. The full 53-benchmark suite requires approximately 6 million images and over 2 hours on an A100 GPU, while a distilled 7-benchmark subset runs in under 5 minutes on a single A100.

## Method

UniBench unifies 53 vision-language benchmarks into a single codebase, categorizing them into 7 types (object recognition, reasoning, relation, robustness, texture, non-natural images, corruption) and 17 finer-grained capabilities. By evaluating 59 openly available VLMs (ranging from 38M to 4.3B parameters, trained on 13M to 12.8B samples) under a consistent zero-shot CLIP-style protocol, the paper isolates the effects of scaling training data and model size while controlling for architecture and learning paradigm. The central finding is that scaling boosts most capabilities but offers negligible benefit for reasoning and relations (only 3.41% improvement with 11× model size growth), and even decades-old benchmarks like MNIST remain unsolved by state-of-the-art VLMs despite ample digit examples in training data. Where scale falls short, data quality (strict CLIP score filtering) and tailored learning objectives (e.g., NegCLIP's hard-negative contrastive loss) prove more effective levers, with NegCLIP at 86M parameters outperforming models 50× larger on relational tasks.

## Results

Across 59 VLMs evaluated on 53 benchmarks, EVA02 ViT-E/14 (4.3B params, 2B training samples) achieves the highest overall mean performance at 61.2%, while DataComp ViT-B/32 (86M params, 12.8B samples) is the worst at 12.1%. Scaling training data by 1000× or model size by 11× yields only a 3.41% improvement on relation and reasoning benchmarks, compared to 17.4% on object recognition and robustness. Strikingly, all 59 VLMs underperform a simple 2-layer MLP (99% accuracy) on MNIST digit recognition, and even top-5 accuracy barely reaches 90%, indicating fundamental limitations in numerical comprehension that scale does not resolve.

## Baselines & Comparisons

EVA02 ViT-E/14 (Fang et al., 2023) dominates most benchmark types (Corruption 74.3%, Non-Natural Images 74.6%, Robustness 72.8%, Overall 61.2%) but is not the best on every axis. CLIPA ViT-G/14 (Li et al., 2023) leads Object Recognition at 71.1% and also tops several fine-grained capabilities (Character Recognition 74.3%, Geographic Diversity 46.8%, Rendition 84.2%). NegCLIP (Yuksekgonul et al., 2023) is the standout for Relation tasks at 66.8% with only 86M parameters, outperforming the 4.3B-parameter EVA02 ViT-E/14 (50.5%) by ~20 points on relational understanding, demonstrating that tailored hard-negative learning objectives beat raw scale. MetaCLIP ViT-H/14 (Xu et al., 2023) leads Texture (72.5%) and Medical (68.6%), while OpenCLIP ViT-g/14 (Ilharco et al., 2021) is the best on Reasoning (19.0%)—though all models remain near chance on this category. DataComp ViT-B/32 (Gadre et al., 2023), despite being trained on 12.8B samples (the largest dataset in the study), is the worst performer on 6 of 7 benchmark types, underscoring that data quantity without quality filtering is insufficient. OpenCOCA ViT-L/14 (Yu et al., 2022) leads Counting at 53.1%, and LLaVA 1.5 (Liu et al., 2023) as an LLM-style control model scores 36–41% on object recognition and relation, well below the best CLIP-style models, confirming that CLIP-style evaluation captures visual understanding that text-generation benchmarks miss.

## Relation to Prior Work

UniBench carves new ground by unifying 53 previously fragmented CLIP-style VLM benchmarks into a single, extensible codebase with systematic categorization into 7 types and 17 capabilities. While prior evaluation efforts (Salin et al. 2023, Bitton et al. 2023) addressed VLM benchmarking, they focused on LLM-style text-generation evaluation, leaving CLIP-style zero-shot classification models without a comparable unified framework. The paper builds conceptually on the CLIP paradigm (Radford et al. 2021) and the proliferation of individual benchmarks (Winoground, CLEVR, CountBench, etc.) but departs from the incremental 'add one more benchmark' approach by providing a structured, apples-to-apples evaluation pipeline with a distilled 5-minute subset.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that scaling training data by 1000× or model size by 11× yields only ~3% improvement on reasoning and relation tasks (versus 17% on other tasks) challenges the dominant 'scale everything' narrative and redirects attention toward data quality and tailored learning objectives. The MNIST result—where 59 VLMs trained on up to 12.8B samples are outperformed by a 2-layer MLP—exposes a fundamental blind spot in how the community gauges VLM progress. Limitations include the restriction to zero-shot CLIP-style evaluation (excluding LLM-style generative models from the main analysis) and the fact that 18 of 53 benchmarks are poorly correlated with ImageNet, meaning no single benchmark can serve as a proxy. The 5-minute distilled evaluation set and open-source codebase make this framework likely to become a standard reference for VLM progress tracking.
