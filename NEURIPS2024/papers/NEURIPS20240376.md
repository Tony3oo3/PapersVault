## Problem

Existing road network representation learning methods rely predominantly on the First Law of Geography (spatial proximity), which fails to capture long-range relationships between roads that share similar geographic configurations but are geospatially distant. This paper is the first to integrate the Third Law of Geography—stating that similar geographic configurations imply similar target variable values—into road network representation learning, addressing the gap where semantically similar but distant roads receive dissimilar embeddings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240376/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a road network graph from OpenStreetMap (topology, road type, length), street view images (SVIs) from Google Maps, and geospatial node locations. SVIs are encoded into 256-dim vectors via a pre-trained CLIP model, matched to roads by geospatial location, and aggregated via average pooling when multiple images correspond to a single road; roads lacking SVIs receive the mean representation of all other roads. The output is a 512-dimensional embedding per road segment, computed at inference as the element-wise average of three SGC encoder outputs (topology, geographic configuration, and spatial proximity views).

## Method

Garner constructs three graph views: the original road topology G^[0], a geographic configuration kNN graph G^[1] built on CLIP-encoded SVI similarity (connecting distant roads with similar surroundings), and a spatial proximity graph G^[2] generated via graph diffusion. A Simple Graph Convolution (SGC) encoder serves as the backbone for all views, exploiting its low-pass filtering property to minimize representation differences among connected nodes, thereby mapping configuration similarity into embedding similarity. A novel spectral negative sampling strategy, derived from the sparsest cut formulation and approximated via spectral graph sparsification (replacing the O(n²) complete graph with a d-regular expander), ensures that roads with dissimilar configurations receive distinct representations. Training uses a dual contrastive objective—maximizing mutual information (via Jensen-Shannon divergence) between the topology view and each of the two augmented views—with shared SGC parameters that automatically balance the First and Third Law contributions without manual weight tuning.

## Results

On road function prediction, Garner achieves 81.40% Micro-F1 and 62.45% Macro-F1 on Singapore (vs. best baseline MVGRL at 66.61% and 30.67%), and 82.97% Micro-F1 and 47.22% Macro-F1 on NYC (vs. MVGRL at 78.23% and 17.39%), representing improvements of up to 22% in Micro-F1 and 171% in Macro-F1. On road traffic inference, Garner reduces MAE to 2.80 (Singapore) and 3.30 (NYC), outperforming the best baseline by up to 18.5%. On visual road retrieval, Garner achieves Recall@10 of 0.4600 (Singapore) and 0.5531 (NYC), while all baselines score below 0.0324—essentially random-guess level—demonstrating that geographic configuration information from street view images is uniquely captured by the proposed method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Garner outperforms all seven baselines across every downstream task and both datasets. The strongest baseline is MVGRL (graph contrastive learning with diffusion), which Garner surpasses by 14.8 points in Micro-F1 and 31.8 points in Macro-F1 on Singapore road function prediction, and by 4.7 and 29.8 points respectively on NYC. SARN, the most recent road-network-specific contrastive method, is second-best on Singapore but trails Garner by 14.9 points in Micro-F1. RFN and SRN2Vec (both GAT/random-walk based) fail entirely on NYC due to out-of-memory errors, highlighting scalability limitations of prior methods. On visual road retrieval, the gap is most dramatic: all baselines score below 0.0324 Recall@10 (near random), while Garner reaches 0.4600–0.5531, confirming that street-view-derived geographic configuration information is not captured by any topology- or distance-only baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends road network representation learning, which has been dominated by methods leveraging the First Law of Geography (spatial proximity) through random walks (Deepwalk, SRN2Vec) or GNN-based message passing (RFN, SARN). It departs from this lineage by introducing the Third Law of Geography (geographic configuration similarity) as a complementary principle, using street view image embeddings (via CLIP) as a new data source to capture spatial context. The technical framework builds on SGC as a low-pass-filtering encoder, the Jensen-Shannon divergence MI estimator from Deep Graph Infomax, and graph diffusion from MVGRL, while the novel spectral negative sampling draws on sparsest cut theory and spectral graph sparsification to enforce dissimilarity for roads with different configurations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to integrate the Third Law of Geography into road network representation learning, demonstrating that geographic configuration (captured via street view images) provides substantially more discriminative information than spatial proximity alone—particularly for tasks like road function prediction where nearby roads can serve very different purposes. The dual contrastive objective elegantly balances both geographic laws without manual weight tuning. Limitations include dependence on potentially outdated street view imagery, evaluation on only two cities, and no consideration of adversarial robustness. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to other geographic laws, whether alternative visual or sensor data sources could substitute for SVIs, and how the method performs in cities with sparse street view coverage.
