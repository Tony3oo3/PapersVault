## Problem

Existing LLM reasoning methods (CoT, ToT, Reflexion) focus on planning over information already provided, but real-world tasks like medical diagnosis and troubleshooting require the model to actively seek missing information by asking effective questions. No prior approach explicitly models an LLM's own uncertainty and uses it to guide question selection, leaving a gap in how LLMs reduce their ignorance in interactive, multi-turn settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240762/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At each interaction step, UoT consumes the conversation history (sequence of past questions and answers) and the current possibility set Ω_i (the subset of all candidate answers still consistent with observed responses), along with a task description prompt. It outputs a single next question to pose to the Answerer. In the primary experiments, answers are constrained to binary yes/no responses, which enables clean entropy-based uncertainty computation; the possibility set is refined at each step by an LLM that partitions Ω_i into affirmative and negative subsets. In the open-set variant, the model must also initialize and iteratively update the possibility set from scratch.

## Method

UoT is a plug-and-play, training-free algorithm with three components. First, it generates m candidate questions via an LLM prompt, then builds a multi-step simulation tree for each candidate: Answerer nodes branch into yes/no children (with the possibility set partitioned by an LLM), and Questioner nodes generate m new simulated questions, expanding to a fixed depth of 3. Second, it assigns an uncertainty-based reward at each Answerer node derived from information gain: R_u(v) = (−p^A log p^A − p^N log p^N) / (1 + λ⁻¹|p^A − p^N|), which is normalized to [0,1] and sharpened by hyperparameter λ to penalize questions that split the possibility set very unevenly. Third, a reward propagation scheme computes accumulated rewards (summed along root-to-leaf paths) and expected rewards (propagated bottom-up using conditional probabilities at Answerer nodes and uniform averaging at Questioner nodes); the candidate question whose root node has the highest expected reward is selected. This design captures both immediate and multi-step information gain, distinguishing it from single-step heuristics or self-evaluation rewards used in ToT.

## Results

Across 5 datasets (Common, Things, DX, MedDG, FloDial) and 5 LLMs, UoT achieves an average 38.1% improvement in Success Rate (SR) over Direct Prompting. On GPT-4 in the closed-set setting, UoT reaches 71.2% SR on Common, 97.0% on DX, 88.0% on MedDG, and 67.3% on FloDial, while also reducing Mean Conversation Length in Successful Cases (MSC) to as low as 2.0 on DX. The method also improves efficiency: UoT(CS) on GPT-4 achieves MSC of 10.8 on Common vs. 13.1 for DP(CS), and 7.8 on FloDial vs. 13.4 for DP(CS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

UoT outperforms all five baselines on every dataset in the GPT-4 closed-set setting. Against CoT, UoT gains are dramatic (e.g., 71.2% vs. 20.7% on Common; 97.0% vs. 33.7% on DX), indicating that explicit reasoning chains without uncertainty modeling are poorly suited to information-seeking. CoT-SC (self-consistency ensemble) is the strongest non-tree baseline at 55.1% on Common, but UoT still leads by 16.1 points; on average UoT beats CoT-SC by 33.8%. Reflexion, which uses self-evaluation to iteratively refine questions, reaches 67.6% on Common but drops to 28.6% on FloDial, whereas UoT maintains 67.3% there. Among tree-based methods, Original-ToT (28.8% on Common) and Adapted-ToT (42.6%) both underperform UoT substantially; the paper reports average gains of 28.3% and 12.4% over these respectively. Notably, even the Pruned UoT variant (which halves the simulation tree for efficiency) surpasses Adapted-ToT by 7.4% on average, and a t-test confirms all UoT-vs-DP differences are statistically significant (p < 0.05) across all five datasets for both GPT-4 and Llama-3.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of LLM reasoning work (CoT, ToT, Reflexion) that focuses on planning over *given* information, instead framing the problem as active information-seeking where the model must reduce its own uncertainty by asking questions. It draws on information-theoretic concepts (entropy, information gain) to define a principled reward for question quality, and on tree-based simulation (analogous to ToT's search structure) to evaluate candidate questions over multiple future steps. The closest prior work includes Noever et al.'s 20-Questions study, Deng et al.'s Rephrase-and-Respond, and clinical information-seeking frameworks like MEDIQ and MDAgents, but none explicitly model the LLM's uncertainty and use information gain as a selection criterion. UoT is positioned as the first method to combine uncertainty-aware simulation, information-gain-based rewards, and reward propagation into a unified plug-and-play framework.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 38.1% average success-rate improvement over direct prompting (and 12.4% over the strongest tree-search baseline, Adapted-ToT) across five LLMs demonstrates that explicitly modeling uncertainty is a meaningful lever for information-seeking, not just a prompt-engineering trick. The introduced benchmark (5 datasets across 20 Questions, medical diagnosis, and troubleshooting) fills a gap in standard LLM evaluations that assume all information is provided upfront. Key limitations include the reliance on binary yes/no answers, the assumption that answers fully eliminate possibilities (rather than probabilistically updating them), and the use of an LLM to partition the possibility set, which introduces its own errors. Open questions include extending to truly open-ended answers, handling overlapping or partially-informative responses, and scaling to larger possibility spaces where the current uniform-prior assumption breaks down.
