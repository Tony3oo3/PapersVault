## Problem

LLMs exhibit cultural bias toward Western perspectives because English corpora dominate training data, and low-resource cultures are severely underrepresented. Existing remedies—prompt engineering to elicit cultural viewpoints or culture-specific pre-training—either fail when the model lacks underlying cultural knowledge or demand large-scale data collection and substantial compute, making them impractical for ordinary researchers and low-resource settings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242692/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The training input consists of 50 seed QA pairs from the World Values Survey where the same question x is paired with culture-specific answers y (e.g., 'Strongly agree' for Arabic vs. 'Disagree' for English on a gender-leadership question); only the question is augmented while the answer label is preserved. At inference, the fine-tuned model receives a text sentence prefixed with a cultural system prompt (e.g., 'You are an Arabic chatbot that knows Arabic very well') and outputs either a short classification token (OFF/NOT_OFF, HS/NOT_HS, 1/0) for detection tasks or a direct cultural opinion for open-ended questions, avoiding the hedging and multi-perspective responses typical of base LLMs.

## Method

CultureLLM fine-tunes a base LLM (GPT-3.5 or Llama-2-70b-chat) on a small set of 50 WVS seeds plus ~500 semantically equivalent augmented samples generated by a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 (semantic template generation) uses GPT-4 to rephrase each seed question into structurally diverse variants, retains only those whose embedding cosine similarity to the original exceeds τ=0.8, then parses the survivors into templates with replaceable slots identified via NLTK part-of-speech tagging. Stage 2 (intact sentence generation) fills those slots with GPT-4-generated context-aware synonyms and applies the same semantic filter, yielding word-level diversity on top of the sentence-level diversity from stage 1. The key insight is that LLMs are sensitive to prompt style and format, so enriching the surface form of a fixed set of cultural opinions—without altering their semantic content—provides enough signal for fine-tuning to internalize culture-specific stances at minimal cost, inspired by Attitude-Behavior Consistency theory.

## Results

On 59 culture-related classification datasets (68,607 test samples across 9 cultures), CultureLLM fine-tuned on GPT-3.5 achieves an average macro F1 that surpasses GPT-3.5 by 8.1%, Gemini Pro by 9.5%, and RAG by 7.94%, while reaching performance comparable to or exceeding GPT-4 on several tasks. On open-ended generation (65 questions), CultureLLM wins against GPT-3.5 on 8 of 9 cultures (e.g., WinRate 0.615 for Korean, 0.569 for Portuguese). Fine-tuning Llama-2-70b-chat with CultureLLM yields a 2.17% average improvement over the base model, and no catastrophic forgetting is observed on BIG-Bench-Hard or GSM8K.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CultureLLM (specific, per-culture fine-tuning on GPT-3.5) outperforms GPT-3.5 by 8.1% and Gemini Pro by 9.5% in average macro F1 across all 59 tasks, and beats RAG (Wikipedia-based retrieval augmentation) by 7.94%. Against GPT-4, CultureLLM achieves comparable performance overall and even surpasses it on several individual tasks, particularly in high-resource languages. The unified CultureLLM-One model exceeds GPT-3.5 by more than 4% but remains inferior to culture-specific models, suggesting that a single model struggles when cultural data from different languages intertwine. In Appendix D.2, CultureLLM also outperforms three culturally-specific pre-trained baselines—SeaLLM, TaiwanLLM, and CultureBank—confirming that the lightweight fine-tuning approach is competitive with resource-intensive pre-training. On the open-ended generation task, CultureLLM wins against GPT-3.5 on 8 of 9 cultures (only Turkish shows a slight negative WinRate of −0.062), and on Llama-2-70b-chat the fine-tuned model improves over the base by 2.17% average F1.

## Relation to Prior Work

CultureLLM positions itself as a cost-effective middle ground between two existing paradigms for cultural alignment in LLMs: prompt engineering (Kovač et al., Wang et al.), which assumes LLMs already encode all cultural knowledge, and large-scale cultural pre-training (SeaLLM, TaiwanLLM, Typhoon, PersianLLaMA), which requires expensive data collection and compute. The method repurposes the broader data augmentation literature (MetaMath, self-play fine-tuning, instruction backtranslation) by introducing a two-stage semantic augmentation pipeline—template generation via GPT-4 rephrasing with cosine-similarity filtering, followed by context-aware synonym replacement—applied to just 50 seed questions from the World Values Survey. The theoretical grounding in Attitude-Behavior Consistency theory (Fazio & Zanna) provides a principled justification for using survey responses as ground-truth cultural opinions, distinguishing it from prior cultural datasets generated from English or Chinese corpora alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a handful of culturally-grounded seed samples, augmented semantically, can fine-tune a mid-tier LLM to match or exceed GPT-4 on cultural classification tasks across 9 languages, offering a practical pathway for researchers without large compute budgets to build culturally-aware models. The finding that English fine-tuning outperforms native-language fine-tuning (Section 5.1) is a useful practical insight for low-resource settings, though it also highlights the underlying data scarcity problem. Key limitations include reliance on the GPT-3.5 fine-tuning API for main results (limiting reproducibility), only sentence- and word-level diversity in augmentation, and evaluation restricted primarily to classification tasks rather than open-ended cultural reasoning. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes beyond WVS's 7 topic areas, whether it scales to the hundreds of cultures not covered, and whether the cultural alignment transfers to generative tasks beyond simple stance detection.
