## Problem

Existing concept drift adaptation methods are reason-agnostic: they select from a fixed set of pre-defined corrective actions (retrain, reuse old models, ensemble) without diagnosing why the model degraded, making adaptations essentially 'shots in the dark' that can cause real-world harm in high-stakes domains. This paper identifies this fundamental limitation and proposes self-healing machine learning (SHML), a paradigm where deployed models autonomously diagnose the root cause of performance degradation and take diagnosis-guided corrective actions.

## Input / Output

The system consumes a deployed black-box model f, streaming or batch data, and an extractor function that converts raw data into a textual information vector of summary statistics (per-column means, standard deviations, percentiles, and per-covariate model performance) computed before and after the detected shift. The LLM processes this textual representation to produce a probability distribution ζ over a finite space of degradation reasons, then generates textual adaptation actions (e.g., 'remove samples with Insulin < 0 and retrain') that are executed via an interpreter function. The final output is an updated model f^a selected by evaluating candidate actions on a backtesting window of post-shift data.

## Method

H-LLM is an agentic system that instantiates the four-stage SHML framework (monitoring, diagnosis, adaptation, testing) using an LLM (GPT-4) as the reasoning engine. Monitoring uses standard statistical drift detection (DDM) to trigger the healing pipeline. Diagnosis employs chain-of-thought prompting with self-reflection, where the LLM performs Monte Carlo sampling over k candidate reasons conditioned on the summary-statistics vector, producing an empirical diagnosis distribution ζ̂. Adaptation conditions a second LLM call on ζ̂ to generate m candidate actions via CoT-based MC sampling, covering generic fixes, corrupted-data removal, and subgroup-specific retraining. Testing evaluates each action on a backtesting window and implements the empirically best one. Theoretically, the paper formalizes SHML as an optimization over the action space, proves the optimal diagnosis has zero entropy under an independent-actions assumption, and establishes its existence via compactness arguments.

## Results

On the simulated diabetes prediction task with DGP changes and data corruption, H-LLM achieves 0.93 accuracy (k=2, τ=0.05) versus 0.71 for the best reason-agnostic baseline (Partially Updating), a 22-point improvement. As corruption severity increases (k=8, τ=0.05), H-LLM maintains 0.68 accuracy while the best baseline drops to 0.54. Across five real-world datasets (Airlines, Poker, Weather, Electricity, Forest Type), H-LLM consistently outperforms all baselines, with the largest gap on Poker (0.70 vs. 0.49) and Forest Type (0.73 vs. 0.52).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks H-LLM against four standard reason-agnostic drift adaptation methods drawn from the concept drift literature [1]: No retraining, Partially Updating, New model training, and Ensemble Method. H-LLM wins across all corruption settings in Table 4, with margins ranging from 17 points (k=8, τ=0.05: 0.68 vs. 0.54) to 22 points (k=2, τ=0.05: 0.93 vs. 0.71). The gap widens as corruption severity increases, confirming that diagnosis-guided adaptation (removing corrupted samples before retraining) is substantially more effective than blindly retraining on noisy data. In the cross-dataset study (Table 5), H-LLM again dominates on all five datasets, with the most dramatic gains on Poker (+21 points) and Forest Type (+21 points) where feature corruption is most impactful. The No retraining baseline is consistently the weakest, while New model training and Ensemble Method perform nearly identically, suggesting that without diagnosis, retraining on corrupted data is essentially equivalent to doing nothing useful.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established concept drift adaptation literature (retraining, model reuse, ensemble methods) by introducing a diagnosis step as a first-class component that conditions the adaptation policy, which prior 'reason-agnostic' methods lack. It draws conceptual inspiration from self-healing systems in software engineering [68–72] and repurposes LLMs as hypothesis proposers and agents to perform self-diagnosis and propose corrective actions. The theoretical contribution—formalizing diagnosis quality via entropy and proving the optimal diagnosis has zero entropy under an independent-actions assumption—is new and not present in prior drift-handling or causal-attribution work [36, 37].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SHML offers a principled framework for high-stakes domains (healthcare, finance) where misguided automatic retraining can cause real-world harm, and the finding that diagnosis-guided adaptation substantially outperforms standard methods on corrupted-data scenarios is practically relevant. However, the current instantiation (H-LLM) is limited to tabular data with logistic regression, simple corruption patterns, and subgroup-level diagnoses, leaving open whether the approach scales to complex, multi-modal, or gradual-shift settings. Key open questions include improving diagnosis calibration, expanding the action space beyond data removal, and validating the framework on real-world production systems where the DGP is unknown.
