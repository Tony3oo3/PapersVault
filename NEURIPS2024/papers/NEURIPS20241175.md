## Problem

Previous online bilateral trade work maximizes gain from trade (the sum of seller and buyer utilities), which can produce highly asymmetric splits and discourage market participation. This paper introduces fair gain from trade (FGFT), defined as the minimum of the two utilities, and asks: what are the tight regret rates when a platform must equalize trader profits rather than merely maximize total surplus, under various information and distributional assumptions?

## Input / Output

At each round the platform posts a single price P_t ∈ [0,1] to an incoming seller (valuation S_t) and buyer (valuation B_t), both in [0,1]. In the two-bit feedback model, the platform only observes whether the seller accepted (𝕀{S_t ≤ P_t}) and whether the buyer accepted (𝕀{P_t ≤ B_t}), which is insufficient to reconstruct the FGFT reward at the posted price. In the full-feedback variant, both S_t and B_t are revealed after each round, enabling direct computation of FGFT for counterfactual prices.

## Method

The central technical tool is the Convolution Lemma, which expresses FGFT(p,s,b) as an integral of indicator functions and, under independence of S and B, shows that the expected FGFT is the (incomplete) convolution of the seller CDF and the buyer co-CDF. Algorithm 1 (Convolution Pricing) exploits this by first spending K = ⌊T^{2/3}⌋ rounds posting prices on a uniform grid to collect noisy CDF samples, then computing a discrete convolution of the seller and buyer feedback sequences to uniformly estimate expected FGFT across all grid points, and finally committing to the best grid price. This avoids the need for pointwise CDF estimation and yields a Õ(T^{2/3}) regret upper bound, matching a lower bound constructed via a reduction to the revealing-action problem in partial monitoring. In the deterministic setting, a double binary search algorithm achieves the tight Θ(log T) rate by first locating the seller's valuation, then the buyer's, and committing to their midpoint.

## Results

The paper establishes tight regret bounds for the fair gain from trade (FGFT) objective across four settings. With two-bit feedback, the regret is Θ(ln T) in the deterministic case, Õ(T^{2/3}) in the stochastic case with independent seller/buyer valuations, and Ω(T) (linear, hence unlearnable) in the general stochastic case. With full feedback (true valuations revealed), the regret improves to O(√T) in the stochastic case and O(1) in the deterministic case. The T^{2/3} rate is shown to be optimal up to logarithmic factors via a lower bound (Theorem 3) that reduces the problem to a revealing-action partial monitoring game.

**Table 1: Summary of regret bounds for fair bilateral trade.**

| Feedback | Deterministic | Stochastic (i.i.d. + independent valuations) | Stochastic (i.i.d.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-bit feedback | ln T | T^{2/3} | T |
| Full feedback | 1 | √T | √T |

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines; comparisons are between problem variants and objective functions. The paper shows that any no-regret algorithm for the standard gain from trade (GFT) objective can suffer linear FGFT regret (Section 2), demonstrating that fairness and efficiency are fundamentally misaligned. The independence assumption between seller and buyer valuations is shown to be necessary: without it, even the stochastic setting is unlearnable (Ω(T) regret, Theorem 1), whereas with independence the rate drops to T^{2/3}. The two-bit feedback model is strictly harder than full feedback: the deterministic case degrades from O(1) to Θ(ln T), and the stochastic case from O(√T) to Õ(T^{2/3}). Notably, the FGFT objective is 1-Lipschitz, which removes the need for the bounded-density (smoothness) assumption required by prior GFT work [9], making the fair version technically simpler in some respects despite the harder feedback structure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the online bilateral trade literature (Cesa-Bianchi et al. [9,12]) by replacing the standard gain-from-trade (GFT) objective with a fairness criterion—the fair gain from trade (FGFT), defined as the minimum of seller and buyer utilities. The key technical tool, the Convolution Lemma, plays a role analogous to the Decomposition Lemma in [9], but the 1-Lipschitzness of FGFT (unlike the discontinuous GFT) eliminates the need for the bounded-density smoothness assumption required in prior work. The lower-bound construction for the T^{2/3} rate directly leverages partial monitoring techniques from Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi [16], and the paper positions FGFT as an online implementation of the egalitarian (max-min) rule from social choice theory, connecting to the broader fairness-in-online-learning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a complete characterization of regret regimes for fair bilateral trade, showing that the fairness objective fundamentally alters the learning landscape: GFT-optimal algorithms can suffer linear FGFT regret, and independence of seller/buyer valuations is a hard requirement (not a technical convenience) for sublinear regret. The Convolution Lemma is a reusable technical tool for recovering reward information from two-bit feedback in settings where the reward is a min of two components. Open questions include closing the logarithmic gap in the stochastic independent case, understanding whether the adversarial full-feedback case is truly harder than the stochastic one, and extending to multi-dimensional pricing or contextual settings.
