## Problem

Existing Neural ODEs and their variants (ANODE, SONODE) lack a unified framework that simultaneously offers high scalability through multiple subnetworks, a dedicated control mechanism for capturing multi-scale dynamics, and provable convergence guarantees. Many real physical systems (neural dynamics, reaction-diffusion, fluid flow) exhibit highly nonlinear behaviors that fall into 'quasi-classes' of NODEs, yet no prior work has addressed convergence for such scalable, flexible architectures with tractable verification conditions.

## Input / Output

The method consumes segmented time series of system states (e.g., 30-point 3D trajectories for Hindmarsh-Rose, 50×50 spatial grids over 25-second windows for Reaction-Diffusion, 15-point depth profiles for Shallow Water) along with an optional bounded control signal u(t) ∈ L∞ᵐ. It outputs continuous-time state trajectories integrated via forward Euler or Dopri5, producing predicted future states over a horizon beyond the observation window (e.g., 10 s for neurons, 50 s for chemical fields, 2.3 s for water waves). The control term g(u(t)) is itself learned by an auxiliary subnetwork (MLP or two-layer CNN), with its initial value matched to the state initial condition.

## Method

CSODEs extend the standard NODE form ẋ = f(x) to ẋ(t) = A₀x(t) + Σⱼ₌₁ᴹ Aⱼfⱼ(Wⱼx(t)) + g(u(t)), combining a linear drift term, M parallel nonlinear subnetworks with activation functions fⱼ, and a dedicated control term g(u(t)) modeled by a separate subnetwork. The key theoretical contribution is a convergence theorem (Theorem 1) proving global asymptotic stability of the unique bounded solution via a set of linear matrix inequalities (LMIs) that are tractable to verify, under mild assumptions: activations must be odd-symmetric (sf(s)>0 for s≠0), continuous and strictly increasing, and satisfy a relaxed quadratic bound weaker than global Lipschitz continuity. The CSODE-Adapt variant replaces the MLP-based control subnetwork with two convolutional layers to better capture spatial structure in PDE-formulated systems. Training is standard supervised learning with MSE loss, Adam optimizer, and matched parameter counts (±1%) across all compared architectures to isolate architectural effects.

## Results

On the three physical dynamical system tasks, CSODE-Adapt (with convolutional subnetworks) achieves the best performance across all metrics: on Hindmarsh-Rose, MSE=0.408, MAE=0.370, R²=0.887; on Reaction-Diffusion, MSE=5.635e-3, MAE=0.035, CD=0.837; and on Shallow Water Equations, MSE=0.031, MAE=0.15, CD=1.89. The base CSODE model also outperforms all NODE variants (NODE, ANODE, SONODE) on every task, e.g., reducing Hindmarsh-Rose MAE from 0.682 (NODE) to 0.470 and Shallow Water CD from 2.89 (NODE) to 2.27. On MNIST image classification, CSODE achieves 0.39% test error with identical parameter count (0.22M) and FLOPS (2.11B) as NODE, ANODE, and SONODE, confirming no computational overhead from the added subnetwork.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CSODE and CSODE-Adapt consistently outperform all NODE-based baselines (NODE, ANODE, SONODE) across all three physical system tasks. On Hindmarsh-Rose, CSODE-Adapt reduces MSE by 73.7% vs NODE (0.408 vs 1.551) and improves R² from 0.515 to 0.887; on Reaction-Diffusion, it halves the CD from 1.311 (NODE) to 0.837; on Shallow Water, it reduces CD from 2.89 to 1.89. Against traditional MLP and RNN baselines, the ODE-based models show dramatic improvements (e.g., RNN→CSODE-Adapt on Hindmarsh-Rose: MSE drops from 1.975 to 0.408). Against Neural CDE [12], CSODE-Adapt is competitive but Neural CDE retains an edge on irregular-observation tasks (CharacterTrajectories: 97.8–98.2% vs 96.5–97.8% accuracy), while CSODE-Adapt wins on PhysioNet Sepsis without observation intensity (AUC 0.871 vs 0.865) and on Reaction-Diffusion (MSE 6.8e-3 vs 7.1e-3). Against TLODE [27], the CSTLODE variant (CSODE with Transformer layers) achieves lower MSE on Hindmarsh-Rose (0.411 vs 0.731) and Reaction-Diffusion (4.341e-3 vs 7.346e-3), confirming the benefit of the control term g(·) even when the backbone is a Transformer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Neural ODE framework (Chen et al., 2018) by introducing a composite architecture with multiple subnetworks and an explicit control term g(u(t)), positioning itself between the state-augmentation philosophy of ANODEs and the second-order specialization of SONODEs. It differentiates from Neural CDEs (Kidger et al., 2020), which also incorporate control, by using a dedicated control subnetwork rather than path-valued data driving the vector field, and by providing LMI-based convergence guarantees that prior NODE stability analyses (e.g., Llorente-Vidrio et al., 2021) did not address for scalable multi-subnetwork compositions. The theoretical contribution draws on Lyapunov and input-to-state stability methods from control theory, applied to a class of NODEs that prior work had not characterized for convergence under increasing depth and width.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The LMI-based convergence guarantee is practically useful: it gives practitioners a tractable certificate that a trained CSODE will not diverge, which is valuable for safety-critical physical modeling. However, the paper acknowledges that CSODEs are more sensitive to learning rate selection, that multi-scale interference in PDEs is only approximately captured, and that the inductive biases may not be preferable in all application scenarios. Open questions include extending the framework to the more general form ẋ = f(x,u) with guaranteed stability, developing adaptive hyperparameter strategies for the more complex architecture, and exploring whether the convergence conditions can be enforced during training rather than verified post-hoc.
