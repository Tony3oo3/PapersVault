## Problem

Detecting unwanted visual content (OOD samples, hateful images) in large-scale training datasets traditionally requires either additional vision-language data, in-distribution images, or manual annotation of out-distribution data — all of which are costly or ethically problematic. The paper asks whether a detector for unwanted visual data can be trained using only textual data, eliminating the need for any visual data during training while still operating on images at inference time.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243979/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

At training time, the method consumes only textual data: in-distribution text (e.g., ImageNet class names combined with prompt templates) and a synthetic corpus generated by inserting words from a ~370k English word set into prompt templates like 'This is a photo of a {}.' At inference time, an RGB image is encoded by a frozen VLM (CLIP ViT-B/16) into the joint embedding space, and the output is a scalar probability p(x) computed as a softmax over cosine similarities between the image embedding and K task embeddings (in-distribution) versus N trainable embeddings (out-distribution, default N=10). The method requires no access to backbone parameters, making it compatible with black-box foundation models.

## Method

HFTT is grounded in a theoretical result (Theorem 1) proving that in a well-trained bimodal model like CLIP, a cosine-similarity classifier trained solely on text embeddings can successfully partition image embeddings of the same classes. The training objective is a modified cross-entropy loss where the standard regularization term −Σ L(u,−1) is replaced by +Σ L(u,+1), which forces the decision boundary to sit close to the in-distribution rather than far from it; a focal loss weighting (α_j = (1−p(x_j))^γ) further amplifies gradients from near-boundary samples. To emulate the full visual data distribution without any images, the method synthesizes textual data by inserting words from a large corpus into prompt templates, treating all synthesized texts as out-distribution candidates. Only N trainable embeddings (default 10) in the joint embedding space are updated via gradient descent while the entire VLM backbone remains frozen, resulting in a training cost of under 2 minutes on a single V100 GPU and negligible inference overhead.

## Results

On ImageNet-1K OOD detection (Table 1), HFTT achieves an average AUROC of 91.76% and FPR95 of 33.33%, outperforming all baselines including those that require in-distribution images (e.g., NPOS: 91.22% AUROC, KNN: 90.97% AUROC) and those that do not (e.g., MCM: 89.86% AUROC, Energy: 85.70% AUROC). On the hateful image detection task (Table 2), HFTT achieves 99.06% average AUROC and 1.83% FPR95 across five innocuous datasets, substantially surpassing the next-best method NegLabel (98.82% AUROC) and traditional OOD methods like Energy (97.10% AUROC) and CLIPN (92.88% AUROC). The method requires only ~2 minutes of training on a single V100 GPU with no visual data, and inference adds negligible cost (2 × batch_size × embedding_dim × N FLOPS).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On the primary ImageNet-1K OOD benchmark, HFTT (91.76% avg AUROC) outperforms all 8 baselines tested in Table 1, including the strongest image-based methods NPOS (91.22%) and KNN (90.97%), as well as the best image-free method MCM (89.86%). The gap is most pronounced against Mahalanobis (61.51%) and ZOC (81.79%), which are far inferior. On hateful image detection (Table 2), HFTT (99.06% AUROC) decisively beats CLIPN (92.88%) and Energy (97.10%), and narrowly edges out NegLabel (98.82%). However, in the appendix comparison on standard ImageNet OOD (Table 7), NegLabel (94.21%) and CLIPN (93.10%) actually surpass HFTT (91.76%), indicating HFTT's advantage is most pronounced on abstract-concept tasks rather than clean classification boundaries. Against Park et al.'s textual outlier methods (WTO/CTO/DTO in Table 8), HFTT's simple word+prompt synthesis matches or slightly exceeds their more complex pipelines (88.30% vs. 88.30%/83.29%/88.02% avg AUROC). In the medical image domain (Table 4), HFTT dramatically outperforms MCM (93.60–98.35% vs. 47.10–68.74% AUROC), and also beats NegLabel and CLIPN on PathVQA (97.05% vs. 94.11% and 84.64% AUROC).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the VLM-based OOD detection line (ZOC, MCM, CLIPN, NegLabel) but departs fundamentally by eliminating the need for any visual data during training. It adapts the focal loss mechanism from dense object detection [33] to shape the decision boundary near the in-distribution, and draws on textual outlier exposure [39] for motivation, though its rule-based prompt-and-word synthesis is simpler and cheaper. The theoretical contribution (Theorem 1, Corollary 1) provides a formal justification for why a classifier trained on one modality of a well-aligned bimodal model can transfer to the other, a result not previously established in the OOD detection literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

HFTT addresses a practical bottleneck in data curation for large-scale visual datasets: the inability to manually screen millions of images for unwanted content. By requiring only textual data and a frozen VLM, it sidesteps both the annotation cost and the ethical concerns of collecting sensitive images (e.g., hateful content). The limitation is that performance is bounded by the VLM's representational capacity, as evidenced by the large gap between MCM and HFTT in the medical domain where CLIP has limited domain knowledge. Open questions include whether the approach generalizes to non-CLIP architectures with weaker text-image alignment, and whether the low intrinsic dimensionality assumption (enabling N=10 embeddings) holds for more complex or open-ended detection tasks.
