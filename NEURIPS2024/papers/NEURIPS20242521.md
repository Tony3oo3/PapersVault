## Problem

While LLMs show strong performance on individual medical tasks, the optimal way to structure LLM collaboration for complex medical decision-making remains unresolved. Existing multi-agent frameworks employ static, pre-determined agent counts and interaction patterns that do not adapt to the varying complexity of medical queries, unlike real-world clinical workflows where a primary care physician may handle a simple case alone or escalate to a multi-disciplinary team for complex cases. This mismatch between fixed architectures and variable task difficulty leads to suboptimal performance and unnecessary computational cost.

## Input / Output

The framework accepts medical queries in three modalities—text-only (e.g., USMLE-style questions, PubMed abstracts, clinical vignettes), image+text (pathology slides, chest X-rays, MRI scans paired with questions), and video+text (medical procedure clips with questions)—and produces a single multiple-choice answer (A/B/C/D, Yes/No, or Yes/No/Maybe) or diagnostic conclusion. For low-complexity cases the system uses 3-shot prompting, while moderate and high-complexity cases use zero-shot prompting with multi-agent deliberation. Inference time scales with complexity: approximately 14.7 s (low), 95.5 s (moderate), and 226 s (high) per query on average.

## Method

MDAgents is a four-stage adaptive multi-agent framework that mirrors real-world clinical triage. First, a moderator LLM classifies each query into low, moderate, or high medical complexity. Second, a recruiter LLM assembles the appropriate team: a single primary care physician agent for low complexity, a multi-disciplinary team (MDT) of 2–5 specialist agents for moderate complexity, or a sequential integrated care team (ICT) of multiple specialist sub-teams for high complexity. Third, analysis proceeds via task-appropriate protocols—few-shot CoT for solo agents, iterative multi-round discussion with consensus checking and moderator feedback for MDTs, and phased report generation across sequential specialist teams for ICTs. Fourth, a decision-maker agent synthesizes all outputs (direct answer, conversation history, or accumulated reports) into the final response. The key insight is that a general-purpose LLM can implicitly classify medical complexity with ~80% accuracy, and that this adaptive routing—combined with moderator review and MedRAG retrieval—yields up to 4.2% accuracy gains over the best static solo or group baselines while using fewer API calls (9.3 vs. 20.3 for a 5-agent group).

## Results

MDAgents achieves the best accuracy on 7 out of 10 medical benchmarks, with headline results of 88.7% on MedQA, 93.1% on SymCat, 80.8% on MedBullets, and 77.9% on DDXPlus (all with GPT-4V). The framework shows the largest gains on text-only clinical reasoning tasks (e.g., +5.2% over best solo on DDXPlus, +9.5% over best multi-agent) and multi-modal tasks (e.g., +3.2% over best group on Path-VQA). The combination of moderator review and MedRAG yields an additional 11.8% average accuracy improvement over the base MDAgents pipeline. MDAgents uses only 9.3 API calls on average (N=3 agents), compared to 20.3 for the Group setting (MedAgents, N=5), demonstrating a favorable accuracy-efficiency trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MDAgents outperforms all solo baselines (Zero-shot, Few-shot, CoT, CoT-SC, ER, Medprompt) on 8 of 10 benchmarks, with the largest margins on DDXPlus (+5.2% over CoT) and MedBullets (+4.8% over CoT-SC). Against multi-agent group methods, MDAgents beats Reconcile by 7.4% on MedQA and 9.5% on DDXPlus, and outperforms MedAgents by 13.8% on PMC-VQA. However, MDAgents is outperformed by Reconcile on PubMedQA (75.0% vs. 79.7%), by Weighted Voting on MedVidQA (56.2% vs. 57.8%), and narrowly trails ER on JAMA (70.9% vs. 71.0%). The multi-model baselines AutoGen and DyLAN are consistently weaker, with MDAgents exceeding them by 15–25% on most benchmarks. Overall, the adaptive complexity-based routing provides the most consistent gains, particularly on tasks requiring multi-modal reasoning or complex clinical reasoning where static single-agent or fixed-group architectures underperform.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the multi-agent LLM collaboration literature (MedAgents, ReConcile, AutoGen, DyLAN, multi-agent debate) by introducing an adaptive routing mechanism inspired by real-world clinical triage: a moderator LLM classifies query complexity and dynamically assigns either a solo agent, a multi-disciplinary team (MDT), or an integrated care team (ICT). Unlike prior multi-agent frameworks that use a fixed number of agents and static interaction patterns, MDAgents varies both team composition and collaboration structure at inference time. The framework also builds on established medical prompting techniques (CoT, Self-Consistency, Ensemble Refinement, Medprompt) and integrates RAG (MedRAG) as an optional knowledge-enhancement layer, positioning itself as a training-free orchestration system over general-purpose LLMs rather than a new medical foundation model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that matching collaboration structure to task complexity yields consistent gains over both solo and fixed-group baselines across diverse medical modalities (text, image, video), with the combined moderator-review + RAG ablation showing an 11.8% average accuracy improvement. However, the evaluation uses only 50 samples per dataset and is restricted to multiple-choice formats, limiting generalizability to open-ended or interactive clinical settings. Key open questions include whether the framework scales to patient-centered, multi-turn diagnostic dialogues, whether medical-specific foundation models (Med-PaLM, Med-Gemini) would further improve agent communication, and whether the computational cost (~3× that of solo CoT) is justified in deployment. The finding that LLMs can self-classify medical complexity with ~80% accuracy is promising but validated on only 25 questions, leaving the robustness of the routing mechanism under distribution shift uncertain.
