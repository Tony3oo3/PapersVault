## Problem

Gaussian Splatting represents 3D scenes as collections of continuous Gaussian distributions, which inherently cannot render sharp discontinuities and boundaries accurately — the continuous support of each Gaussian inevitably 'spills over' edges, producing blurry boundaries in rendered images. This is a fundamental limitation of the Gaussian Splatting technique that no prior work has addressed, as all existing methods render complete, unmodified Gaussian projections.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243565/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of source RGB images with known camera poses of a 3D scene and a target viewpoint, and produces a novel-view rendered image at real-time speed (~0.008s per image on an RTX 3090). Each Gaussian in the scene representation carries an additional learnable attribute c_curve ∈ R^{4M×2} (2D case) or R^{4M×3} (3D case) storing control points for M cubic Bézier curves (M=3 in experiments), which are projected to the image plane and used to define per-Gaussian boundary masks during rendering.

## Method

DisC-GS introduces a 'pre-scissoring' step: before α-blending, each projected Gaussian is segmented along M cubic Bézier curves whose control points are stored as a new per-Gaussian attribute and projected to the image plane. The Bézier curves are converted to implicit polynomial form B_imp(x,y)=0 via algebraic implicitization (O(1) per curve), enabling a binary indicator function g(·) that zeros out Gaussian contributions on the 'scissored' side of each boundary. The modified α-blending multiplies each Gaussian's contribution by g_i(p), effectively clipping the Gaussian at the boundary. To preserve differentiability during training despite the discontinuous g(·), the paper proposes a Bézier-boundary gradient approximation strategy: for each control point coordinate, it solves a pair of cubic equations to find the value φ that would make the Bézier curve pass through the current pixel, then approximates the gradient via linear interpolation between the current coordinate and φ, handling three cases (no solution, single-side, both-sides) with small ε terms to prevent gradient explosion.

## Results

DisC-GS achieves state-of-the-art performance across all three datasets and all three metrics (PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS). On Tanks&Temples, it reaches 24.96 PSNR / 0.866 SSIM / 0.120 LPIPS, outperforming the strongest baseline Mip-Splatting (23.78 / 0.851 / 0.178) by +1.18 dB PSNR. On Mip-NeRF360, it scores 28.01 PSNR / 0.833 SSIM / 0.189 LPIPS, and on Deep Blending it achieves 30.42 PSNR / 0.907 SSIM / 0.199 LPIPS. Rendering speed remains competitive at 0.008 s/image, only marginally slower than vanilla 2D/3D Gaussian Splatting (0.007 s/image) and ~900× faster than Mip-NeRF360 (7.143 s/image).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DisC-GS consistently outperforms all nine baselines across every dataset and metric. Against the closest Gaussian-Splatting competitors, it gains +1.18 dB PSNR over Mip-Splatting (23.78→24.96) and +1.00 dB over FreGS (23.96→24.96) on Tanks&Temples, while reducing LPIPS from 0.178 to 0.120. On Mip-NeRF360, it beats Mip-Splatting by +0.22 dB PSNR and FreGS by +0.16 dB, and on Deep Blending it surpasses FreGS by +0.49 dB PSNR and reduces LPIPS from 0.240 to 0.199. Against NeRF-based methods (Mip-NeRF360, INGP), the gains are larger: e.g., +2.74 dB PSNR over Mip-NeRF360 on Tanks&Temples. The paper also shows that DisC-GS works on both 2D and 3D Gaussian Splatting backbones (Table 2), with the 2D variant yielding slightly better results (24.96 vs. 24.67 PSNR on Tanks&Temples), and that the improvement is most pronounced in boundary-rich image regions (MaskedSSIM 0.855 vs. 0.819 for the 2D-GS baseline in boundary-rich areas, Table 4).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Gaussian Splatting lineage (Kerbl et al. 2023 for 3D, Huang et al. 2024 for 2D) by identifying and addressing a fundamental representational limitation: continuous Gaussian distributions inherently 'spill over' sharp boundaries, producing blurry edges. The solution repurposes cubic Bezier curves—well-studied in lane detection and text spotting—as learnable boundary representations attached to each Gaussian, combined with a modified α-blending function that zeros out contributions on the 'scissored' side. The gradient approximation strategy draws on differentiable rendering ideas (Kato et al. 2018) to handle the non-differentiability introduced by the binary indicator function, making the whole pipeline end-to-end trainable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By enabling Gaussian Splatting to render sharp discontinuities while preserving real-time speed (0.008s vs 0.007s baseline), this work removes a visible quality gap that has persisted in the method since its introduction. The framework is general, applying to both 2D and 3D Gaussian Splatting with minimal modification, and the consistent gains across three datasets (up to ~1.8 dB PSNR, ~0.08 LPIPS) suggest broad applicability. Open questions include scalability to scenes with very dense boundary structures, whether the fixed number of Bezier curves per Gaussian (M=3) is sufficient for highly complex geometry, and how the approach interacts with dynamic or multi-object scenes. The authors acknowledge limitations in rendering large scenes, shared with the broader Gaussian Splatting family.
