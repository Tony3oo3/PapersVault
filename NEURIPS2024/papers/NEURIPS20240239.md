## Problem

Text-space Graph Foundation Models (GFMs) use LLMs to project diverse node features into a unified text space, but the field lacks a comprehensive benchmark with unified problem settings and sufficient dataset diversity to understand when and why these models achieve positive transfer. Existing work evaluates on small, citation-heavy datasets with inconsistent protocols, making it impossible to compare methods or identify the conditions under which text-space GFMs actually outperform task-specific baselines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240239/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes text-attributed graphs where heterogeneous node, edge, and label attributes (product titles, molecular descriptions, paper abstracts, categorical values) have been converted to natural language and encoded into fixed-dimensional embeddings via Sentence-BERT. Evaluation spans 20+ datasets across CS citation, e-commerce, molecular biology, and other domains, producing node class labels (accuracy), link predictions (Hits@100), and graph-level property predictions (AUC-ROC or AP) under four paradigms: co-training vs. pre-training crossed with task-specific vs. cross-task transfer.

## Method

The paper curates and preprocesses 20+ text-space datasets by converting raw attributes into text descriptions (using Gemini for label descriptions) and encoding them with Sentence-BERT, then defines four fine-grained GFM paradigms based on (scenario × task relationship). Under each paradigm, it systematically evaluates three GFM building blocks—graph SSL (DGI, GCC, BGRL, GraphMAE), foundational graph prompts (OneForAll, Prodigy), and LLM-with-graph-projector (LLaGA with Mistral-7B)—alongside link prediction-specific models (BUDDY, SEAL) and a simple SBERT feature-propagation baseline. Key empirical findings: (1) LLM embeddings mitigate severe negative transfer but performance gaps across datasets persist; (2) positive transfer from co-training requires both transferable structural patterns and task-appropriate inductive biases, with graph classification benefiting most while node classification shows little gain; (3) link prediction-specific models with structural embeddings (BUDDY) benefit substantially from co-training, whereas generic GFM backbones do not; (4) on homophilous graphs, simple SBERT embeddings with feature propagation outperform complex graph in-context learning methods, indicating that the LLM text space itself is the primary driver of in-context transferability.

## Results

Across 20+ text-space datasets and four GFM paradigms, the benchmark reveals that text-space GFMs do not consistently outperform task-specific GCN baselines. For node classification co-training, GCN trained from scratch achieves 79.47% average accuracy, surpassing all GFM methods (OFA CD: 78.24%, LLaGA CD: 78.01%, GraphMAE CD: 77.04%). For graph classification on molecular datasets, co-training clearly helps: OFA achieves 0.236 AP on PCBA and 82.5 AUC on TOX21, outperforming single-dataset training. For link prediction, BUDDY (90.41 Hits@100 single, 90.05 co-train) substantially outperforms all GFM methods (LLaGA best at 85.03 co-train). In the pre-training setting, a simple SBERT-based baseline without any pre-training (67.41% zero-shot on Cora) matches or exceeds all GFM pre-training methods (OFA: 30.42%, LLaGA: 18.25%), indicating LLM embeddings themselves are the key enabler for in-context learning.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks six GFM building blocks: graph SSL (DGI, GraphMAE, GCC, BGRL), foundational graph prompts (OFA/OneForAll, Prodigy), LLM-with-graph-projector (LLaGA), and link prediction-specific models (BUDDY, SEAL). For node classification, OFA and LLaGA (supervised methods) come closest to GCN from scratch (79.63% and 79.38% vs. 79.47% average), but co-training degrades performance for all methods; DGI shows the most potential for scaling with data (76.35% ST → 76.96% CD). For link prediction, BUDDY dominates all GFM methods by ~10+ Hits@100 points (90.41 vs. LLaGA's 85.03), and co-training further benefits BUDDY at scale (75.48% single → 86.18% domain co-train on large graphs), while GCN co-training shows no benefit. For graph classification, OFA clearly wins over GraphCL (e.g., TOX21: 82.5 vs. 73.74 AUC; BACE: 77.32 vs. 64.42), with the label-space unification being the key advantage. In the pre-training transfer setting, the simple SBERT baseline (no pre-training) dramatically outperforms all GFM methods on zero-shot node classification (67.41% on Cora vs. OFA's 30.42% and LLaGA's 18.25%), while GraphLLM (67.33%) is the only GFM method competitive with it. Prodigy, GPPT, and GPrompt all fail in in-context learning without semantic label embeddings, confirming that LLM text-space embeddings—not the graph prompt architecture—are the critical component for transfer.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself as a comprehensive benchmark for text-space Graph Foundation Models (GFMs), building on the GFM paradigm formalized by Mao et al. [11] and the specific text-space approaches of OneForAll [19], Prodigy [20], and LLaGA [25]. Rather than proposing a new architecture, it repurposes existing GFM building blocks (graph SSL, foundational graph prompts, LLM-with-projector) under a unified evaluation framework spanning four paradigms (co-training/pre-training × task-specific/cross-task). It also incorporates link prediction-specific methods (BUDDY, SEAL) and pure LLM baselines (GraphLLM, GraphText) that prior GFM works largely overlooked, and introduces 20+ text-space datasets across citation, e-commerce, and molecular domains to enable cross-domain evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that text-space GFMs do not universally outperform task-specific models trained from scratch, and that positive transfer critically depends on both transferable structural patterns and task-appropriate inductive biases in the backbone. The finding that a trivial SBERT-based baseline (no pre-training, just feature propagation + nearest-neighbor label matching) outperforms complex graph prompt methods in in-context learning challenges the assumption that elaborate GFM architectures are necessary when LLM embeddings are already informative. Open questions include how to design task-specific GFMs (especially for link prediction), how to achieve cross-task in-context learning, and whether data augmentation via LLM-generated explanations can close the remaining gap for node classification.
