## Problem

Existing fairness metrics for Social Influence Maximization (SIM) quantify fairness solely through expected (marginal) outreach per group, which can classify clearly unfair stochastic outcomes as fair—for example, a diffusion process where 50% of the time group 1 receives no information while group 2 receives all of it, and vice versa the other 50%, yields equal expected outreach yet is intuitively unfair. This paper identifies this limitation and asks how to capture the full joint distribution of group-wise outreach to obtain a stronger fairness guarantee.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an undirected social network graph with nodes partitioned into two demographic groups, a uniform edge propagation probability p for the Independent Cascade model, an initial seedset (from degree centrality or greedy selection), and a fairness-efficiency tradeoff parameter β ∈ [0,1]. It outputs an optimized seedset of fixed cardinality |S| that maximizes β-fairness, along with the joint outreach probability distribution γ over [0,1]² (discretized into 100×100 bins) estimated via R=1000 Monte Carlo cascade simulations. The β-fairness score is a single scalar in [0,1] combining both fairness (orthogonal distance from the diagonal) and efficiency (diagonal distance from the ideal point (1,1)).

## Method

The paper introduces mutual fairness, a metric defined as the optimal transport distance between the joint outreach distribution γ and the ideal distribution γ* = δ_(1,1), using a transportation cost that assigns zero cost to movement along the diagonal (efficiency) and Euclidean cost to orthogonal movement (fairness), yielding the closed-form FAIRNESS(γ) = E[1 − |x₁ − x₂|]. A β-fairness generalization weights the orthogonal and diagonal components of the transport cost to trade off fairness against efficiency. To optimize this non-convex objective over the combinatorial seedset space, the authors propose S3D (Stochastic Seedset Selection Descent), which iteratively proposes candidate seedsets by sampling stochastically reachable nodes from the current seedset via short-horizon Independent Cascade simulations, evaluates each candidate's β-fairness through Monte Carlo outreach estimation, and accepts or rejects moves via Metropolis sampling at a fixed temperature to escape local minima. The algorithm retains the best-scoring seedset across k iterations and is shown to improve fairness over label-blind baselines with only a minor (or sometimes zero) efficiency loss across seven real-world networks.

## Results

Across seven real-world social network datasets, S3D achieves the highest mutual fairness score in nearly all settings, typically exceeding the best label-blind baseline (bas_g) by 2–8 percentage points in fairness while sacrificing only a small amount of efficiency (or sometimes improving it). For example, on APS Physics, S3D_g reaches 0.945 fairness vs. 0.898 for bas_g (a 4.7-point gain) at a modest efficiency cost (0.25 vs. 0.318). On High School (p=0.5), S3D_d achieves 0.88 efficiency and 0.96 fairness, simultaneously beating bas_d on both metrics (0.83/0.94). The paper also reports a case (Fig. 6d) where S3D improves efficiency from 83.1% to 87.9% while increasing fairness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares S3D against two families of baselines: label-blind strategies (degree centrality bas_d and greedy bas_g from Kempe et al.) and label-aware fair heuristics (hrt_d and hrt_g from Stoica et al.). S3D consistently outperforms all four baselines on mutual fairness across every dataset tested. On APS Physics, S3D_g achieves 0.945 fairness vs. 0.898 for bas_g and 0.868 for hrt_g, while maintaining 0.25 efficiency (vs. 0.318 for bas_g and 0.17 for hrt_g). On Antelope Valley (AV_0), S3D_g reaches 0.951 fairness vs. 0.926 for bas_g, a 2.5-point gain at comparable efficiency (0.241 vs. 0.30). On High School at p=0.01, S3D_d achieves 0.99 fairness vs. 0.91 for bas_d (an 8-point improvement) with identical efficiency (0.08). The fair heuristics (hrt_d, hrt_g) generally sacrifice substantial efficiency (e.g., 0.105 vs. 0.173 on APS) for only modest fairness gains over label-blind baselines, whereas S3D achieves the best of both worlds with tighter error bars (±0.002 vs. ±0.005–0.006).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Social Influence Maximization (SIM) problem introduced by Kempe et al. (2003) and the subsequent line of fair-SIM work (Stoica et al. 2020, Junaid et al. 2023, Farnadi et al. 2020, Fish et al. 2020, Tsang et al. 2018), all of which quantify fairness through marginal expected outreach per group. The key conceptual departure is shifting from these marginal metrics to the joint probability distribution of outreach across groups, leveraging optimal transport theory—a tool previously applied to fairness in machine learning (Black et al. 2020, Chiappa et al. 2020, Si et al. 2021) but not to SIM. The S3D seed-selection algorithm repurposes Metropolis sampling/simulated annealing from combinatorial optimization to navigate the non-convex β-fairness landscape, distinguishing it from the greedy or linear-programming approaches used in prior fair-SIM work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a fundamental blind spot in how fairness is measured for stochastic diffusion processes: widely adopted metrics (equity, equality, max-min) can certify clearly unfair outcomes as fair when the unfairness is perfectly correlated across groups. This has practical implications for health campaigns, job advertisements, and public information dissemination where stochastic spread is the norm. However, the S3D algorithm is a random combinatorial search with no convergence guarantees, its performance depends heavily on initialization, and the empirical fairness gains over strong baselines are modest in many settings (particularly dense or highly heterophilic graphs). Open questions include whether the approach scales to many groups without discretization overhead, whether convergence can be guaranteed under structured temperature schedules, and whether the findings generalize beyond the Independent Cascade model to other diffusion mechanisms.
