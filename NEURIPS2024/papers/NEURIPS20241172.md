## Problem

Existing analytical mesh extraction methods for neural networks rely on the piecewise linearity of CPWA functions (e.g., ReLU networks), but modern neural implicit surface networks incorporate non-linear positional encodings such as trilinear interpolation (HashGrid, TensoRF) to mitigate spectral bias. This non-linearity breaks the CPWA assumption, rendering prior edge-subdivision and tropical-geometry-based extraction techniques inapplicable to the most widely used surface representations.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full parameter set of a trained piecewise trilinear SDF network — specifically the HashGrid corner features at multiple resolution levels and the ReLU MLP weights — rather than evaluating the network at sampled points. It produces an analytical 3D mesh consisting of vertices, edges, polygonal faces, and per-face normal vectors derived from vertex ordering. The mesh is inherently parsimonious: vertices are placed only at true hypersurface intersections, avoiding the redundant fragmented planars that sampling-based methods like marching cubes generate.

## Method

The paper formalizes piecewise trilinear networks (HashGrid + ReLU MLP) within tropical geometry and proves that under the eikonal constraint (‖∇f‖=1), any hypersurface within a trilinear region reduces to a plane (Theorem 4.5), restoring linear solvability. For curved edges formed by the intersection of two such hypersurfaces, the method approximates the intersection by substituting one hypersurface with a diagonal plane (x=z), reducing the problem to solving a quartic polynomial via companion-matrix eigenvalue decomposition (Theorem 4.7). The extraction proceeds via a curved edge subdivision algorithm that iterates over neurons and layers, splitting edges at computed intersection points using sign-vectors to track linear regions, then skeletonizes the resulting vertex set to retain only zero-set vertices and their connecting edges. Faces are assembled from coplanar edges sharing a common region, and normals are encoded implicitly through counter-clockwise vertex ordering determined by the Jacobian at each face centroid.

## Results

On the Stanford 3D Scanning benchmark (Small model), the proposed method achieves the highest chamfer efficiency (CE) across all six objects, reaching 25.6 on Bunny, 15.8 on Dragon, 15.5 on Happy Buddha, 13.9 on Armadillo, 47.8 on Drill Bit, and 23.6 on Lucy, compared to the best MC baseline CE of 19.0, 11.2, 13.9, 11.2, 19.2, and 24.9 respectively. The method uses far fewer vertices (e.g., 4,341 for Bunny vs. 21,825 for MC-128) while maintaining a competitive chamfer distance (900×10⁻⁶ vs. 393×10⁻⁶). Angular distance on Bunny is 3.57° with 4,490 vertices, compared to 2.47° for MC-128 (21,825 vertices) and 1.63° for MC-192 (49,569 vertices), confirming the parsimony-accuracy trade-off.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Marching Cubes (MC) at all tested resolutions (32–196), the proposed method consistently achieves the highest chamfer efficiency, meaning it delivers the best accuracy-per-vertex trade-off. For example, on Drill Bit the method reaches CE 47.8 versus MC-64's 19.2, and on Bunny it achieves CE 25.6 with only 4,341 vertices compared to MC-128's 21,825 vertices at CE 11.6. Against Marching Tetrahedra (MT), the method produces lower chamfer distance at a lower vertex count (Figure 3), as MT's six-tetrahedra subdivision generates more intermediate vertices without proportional accuracy gains. Against Neural Dual Contour (NDC), the method outperforms on both chamfer distance and qualitative surface fidelity (Figure 6); NDC exhibits a generalization issue in the zero-shot setting, producing unsmooth surfaces, while the proposed method preserves fine geometric details (e.g., nose boundaries) with consistent normals. The method does not match the absolute lowest CD achievable by very high-resolution MC (e.g., MC-196 on Bunny: 141 vs. 900), but this requires 49,569 vertices versus 4,341, and the CE metric confirms the proposed method's superior parsimony.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Berzins' edge subdivision method for CPWA (ReLU) networks to the piecewise trilinear setting introduced by HashGrid and TensoRF positional encodings. The key conceptual departure is that trilinear interpolation replaces the hyperplane boundaries of CPWA regions with curved hypersurfaces, which prior analytical extraction methods (Analytic Marching, edge subdivision) cannot handle. The authors bridge this gap by showing, via tropical geometry, that the eikonal constraint—standard in SDF training—forces these hypersurfaces to be planes within each trilinear region, thereby recovering the linear structure that edge subdivision requires. The formalization through tropical polynomials and sign-vectors is a novel framing of Berzins' algorithm, though the core subdivision logic is inherited.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first analytical mesh extraction method applicable to the trilinear positional encodings (HashGrid, TensoRF) that underpin virtually all modern neural surface learning systems (NeuS, Neuralangelo, BakedSDF), moving beyond the CPWA-only limitation of prior exact extraction. The eikonal-to-planarity theorem provides a clean theoretical justification for why the approximation works and quantifies when it breaks down. However, the method is constrained to trilinear encodings and requires the eikonal loss to be well-satisfied; generalization to other non-linear encodings (Fourier features, etc.) remains open. The computational overhead from intermediate vertex/edge counts during iterative subdivision limits real-time applicability, and the diagonal-plane approximation, while empirically small, introduces a controlled error that may matter for high-precision applications.
