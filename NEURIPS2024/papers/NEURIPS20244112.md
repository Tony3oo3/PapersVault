## Problem

Decision support systems based on prediction sets improve human accuracy by restricting agency, but this same restriction can cause counterfactual harm: an expert who correctly predicted a label on their own may fail when constrained to choose from the system's prediction set. No prior framework provides a way to guarantee, by design, that such a system causes harm less frequently than a user-specified threshold without first deploying it and potentially causing that harm.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244112/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes a calibration set of (X_i, Y_i, Ŷ_i) triples—where Ŷ_i is the expert's prediction made on their own (no system), Y_i is the ground-truth label, and X_i are image features from noisy ImageNet16H images (16 classes, phase-noise-corrupted)—along with a user-specified harm bound α ∈ [0,1]. It outputs a harm-controlling set of thresholds Λ(α) ⊆ [0,1], where each λ ∈ Λ(α) guarantees that the average counterfactual harm of the corresponding prediction-set system C_λ stays below α. The prediction sets themselves are constructed from the softmax outputs of fine-tuned CNNs (VGG19, DenseNet161, GoogleNet, ResNet152, AlexNet) via a threshold-based ranking rule, so the system is agnostic to the specific set-valued predictor used.

## Method

The paper formalizes counterfactual harm within a structural causal model (SCM) where the expert's prediction is a function of exogenous variables (individual characteristics U, data-generating process V) and the prediction set. Under a counterfactual monotonicity assumption (larger prediction sets containing the ground truth make the task no easier for the same expert), harm reduces to the observable indicator 1{ŷ=y ∧ y∉C_λ(x)}, making it fully identifiable from observational data. Under the weaker, experimentally verifiable interventional monotonicity assumption, harm is only partially identifiable but is bounded by two observable terms. The computational framework then applies conformal risk control: under counterfactual monotonicity, it directly controls the harm indicator via a single threshold search; under interventional monotonicity, it splits the upper bound into a nonincreasing and a nondecreasing term, applies conformal risk control to each separately, and intersects the resulting threshold ranges to recover a valid (though potentially conservative) harm-controlling set Λ′(α) ⊆ Λ(α).

## Results

On the ImageNet16H dataset (ω=110 stratum), human experts achieve 0.771 average accuracy on their own, while pre-trained classifiers range from 0.691 (AlexNet) to 0.846 (VGG19). The key finding is that for α=0.01, the decision support systems C_λ* offering the greatest average accuracy are NOT harm-controlling, revealing a fundamental trade-off between accuracy and counterfactual harm. The framework's harm-controlling sets Λ(α) are valid (they never include λ values with H(λ)>α) but are often conservative due to estimation error in the calibration set; using larger calibration sets (50% vs. 30%) reduces this conservatism. On the ImageNet16H-PS dataset, real human predictions using C_λ confirm the MNL-predicted trends, validating the simulation-based approach.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against competing harm-control methods; instead, it evaluates the proposed conformal risk control framework across five pre-trained classifiers (VGG19, DenseNet161, GoogleNet, ResNet152, AlexNet) and three noise levels (ω=80, 95, 110). The qualitative trade-off between accuracy and counterfactual harm is consistent across all classifiers and noise levels, with the trade-off being most pronounced at higher difficulty (ω=110) and minimal at lower difficulty (ω=80, 95). The MNL-based simulation of human predictions (used on ImageNet16H, which only contains on-own predictions) tracks the real human predictions from ImageNet16H-PS closely in trend, though with a small absolute gap in accuracy. The interventional monotonicity assumption is empirically verified on ImageNet16H-PS by showing that experts achieve higher success probability from smaller prediction sets (containing the ground truth) across difficulty strata and competence levels. Systems with higher empirical coverage construct larger prediction sets on average and cause less counterfactual harm, confirming the expected relationship between set size and harm.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the prediction-set-based decision support setting introduced by Straitouri et al. (ICML 2023, 2024) by adding a formal harm-control objective, and adapts conformal risk control (Angelopoulos et al., ICLR 2024) to a new loss function that incorporates the expert's own prediction. It departs from Richens et al. (NeurIPS 2022) on counterfactual harm in a key way: rather than comparing a factual prediction made under a deployed system against a counterfactual prediction made without it, this paper compares the expert's own factual prediction against the counterfactual prediction they would have made using the system, enabling harm estimation without deployment. The structural causal model formalization and the two monotonicity assumptions (counterfactual and interventional) are inherited from Straitouri et al. but are here leveraged to establish identifiability results for harm and to drive a conformal risk control procedure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that the very mechanism enabling human-AI complementarity in prediction-set systems—restricting expert agency—inevitably introduces counterfactual harm, and that the accuracy-optimal threshold is generally not harm-controlling. This trade-off has practical implications for deploying such systems in high-stakes domains (e.g., medical diagnosis), where 'first, do no harm' principles apply. Key limitations include reliance on a single benchmark domain (noisy natural images), i.i.d. sampling assumptions, fixed (non-data-dependent) thresholds, and uniform weighting of all errors. Open questions include extending the framework to distribution shifts, label noise, data-dependent thresholds, fairness across demographic groups, and validation in real-world application domains.
