## Problem

Exact safety verification of ReLU Neural Control Barrier Functions (NCBFs) requires enumerating and checking every piecewise-linear activation region intersecting the safety boundary, a cost that grows combinatorially with network depth and system dimensionality. Prior exact methods (e.g., [23]) still solve a nonlinear program per segment, rendering verification intractable for systems beyond three dimensions or small networks. The paper asks whether the synthesis and verification of ReLU NCBFs can be co-designed so that the network is trained to minimize the verification workload while the verifier itself exploits structural shortcuts.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the continuous-time control-affine dynamics (f, g), the state space X, an initial safe set I, a safety set C defined by h(x)≥0, input constraints U, and a nominal control policy π_nom. It outputs a verified ReLU NCBF b_θ(x) with a Boolean safety certificate, a safe control input u that minimally deviates from π_nom, and—when verification fails—a categorized safety counterexample (correctness, hyperplane, or hinge type) that is fed back into the training dataset. The counterexample-guided loop means the training set is not fixed a priori but grows adaptively with each verification failure.

## Method

SEEV co-designs a synthesis component and a verification component. On the synthesis side, a novel boundary regularizer L_B penalizes the dissimilarity of activation patterns (measured via symmetric difference of activation sets) among boundary samples, using a generalized sigmoid σ_k to make the otherwise non-differentiable activation indicator differentiable; this clusters boundary samples into N_B groups and minimizes intra-cluster activation variance, directly reducing the number of boundary hyperplanes and hinges that must be verified. On the verification side, a Neural Breadth-First Search (NBFS) enumerates all boundary activation sets from an initial set found by binary search along a line segment between safe and unsafe samples, using linear programs (BoundaryLP, USLP, HingeLP) to prune the search. Verification then proceeds hierarchically—correctness, then hyperplane, then hinge—applying tight sufficient conditions (e.g., sign-invariance of W̄(S)ᵀg(x), or W̄(S)ᵀf(x)>0 implying u=0 suffices) before resorting to full nonlinear programs solved by dReal. The two components are integrated by feeding verifier counterexamples back into the training set, closing a CEGIS-style loop.

## Results

The primary metric is exact verification runtime (in seconds). SEEV completes verification in 0.39–60.1 s across all 11 test configurations, compared to 16–631 s for the prior exact method [23] and >3 h (timeout) for dReal and Z3 on most cases. On the 8-dimensional hi-ord_8 system, SEEV finishes in 0.54–22.4 s while all three baselines exceed the 3-hour timeout. On the 6-dimensional Spacecraft Rendezvous case, SEEV runs in 9.8 s and 60.1 s versus 179 s and 298.7 s for Baseline [23], with dReal and Z3 being unable to apply (UTD) due to the implicit NCBF-based controller.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SEEV is compared against three baselines: (1) the prior exact ReLU-NCBF verification method of Zhang et al. [23], (2) dReal (SMT-based, from Fossil [21]), and (3) Z3 (SMT-based, from Fossil [21]). SEEV wins on every configuration where a baseline completes: it is 126× faster than Baseline [23] on Darboux (M=256: 2.5 s vs 315 s), 5× faster on OA (L=1, M=128: 20.6 s vs 207 s), and 18–5× faster on Spacecraft Rendezvous (9.8 s vs 179 s; 60.1 s vs 298.7 s). On the 8-dimensional hi-ord₈ system, SEEV finishes in under 23 s while Baseline [23], dReal, and Z3 all exceed the 3-hour timeout. dReal and Z3 are additionally inapplicable (UTD) on the Spacecraft Rendezvous case because the NCBF-based safe controller does not admit an explicit closed-form expression. Overall, SEEV achieves 1–3 orders of magnitude speedup over the prior exact method and is the only method that scales to 8-dimensional systems within practical time limits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Zhang et al. (NeurIPS 2024) [23], which established exact safety verification conditions for ReLU NCBFs by exploiting piecewise-linearity, but still required solving a nonlinear optimization along every activation region at the safety boundary. SEEV departs from this by co-designing the synthesis and verification stages: a novel boundary-regularizer (using a generalized sigmoid to smooth activation-pattern dissimilarity) reduces the number of hyperplanes to verify during training, while a Neural Breadth-First Search (NBFS) enumeration and hierarchical sufficient-condition checks reduce per-segment verification cost. The differentiable verification loss leverages Agrawal et al. (NeurIPS 2019) [45] for convex optimization layers, and the counterexample-guided training loop connects to the CEGIS lineage in formal synthesis [21, 38–41], though the counterexamples here come from an exact geometric verifier rather than an SMT solver.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NCBF verification is the critical bottleneck preventing deployment of neural-network-based safety guarantees in autonomous systems; SEEV's 100×+ speedups (e.g., 2.5 s vs. 315 s on Darboux, 22.4 s vs. >3 h on the 8-D hi-ord system) make exact verification tractable for networks that were previously infeasible. The regularizer's ability to reduce boundary hyperplanes by an order of magnitude while preserving safe-region coverage (ρ ≈ 1) suggests a general principle: shaping the learned certificate to be verification-friendly is as important as the verifier itself. Key limitations remain: the method is restricted to ReLU activations (non-ReLU NCBFs lack the piecewise-linear structure), and scalability to very high-dimensional systems is still open, as acknowledged in the paper's limitations section.
