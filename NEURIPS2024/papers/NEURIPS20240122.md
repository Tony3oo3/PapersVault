## Problem

Learning-augmented secretary algorithms that use per-candidate predictions can assign zero probability of acceptance to the true best candidate when predictions are biased, which the authors identify as a fairness failure. The paper asks whether one can simultaneously achieve the near-optimal smoothness guarantee (expected value approaching the optimum as prediction error shrinks) and a constant-probability fairness guarantee for the best candidate, regardless of how biased the predictions are.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240122/cropped_page9_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives n candidates whose predicted values are known in advance and whose true values are revealed only upon arrival in a uniformly random order; at each arrival the algorithm must irrevocably accept or reject the candidate. For the k-secretary extension, the algorithm may accept up to k candidates and the fairness guarantee is stated per-rank (each of the top-k true-value candidates is accepted with a specified probability). The prediction error is defined either additively (max absolute difference) or multiplicatively (max relative difference), and the algorithm's guarantees degrade smoothly with this error.

## Method

The core mechanism is a 'pegging' rule layered on top of Dynkin's classical stopping condition (accept a record value after time 1/2). When the candidate with the highest predicted value arrives but fails the fairness condition, the algorithm does not simply reject it; instead it 'pegs' the decision to a future candidate whose predicted value is sufficiently high relative to the current candidate's true value, guaranteeing that the eventual acceptance will be close to optimal (smoothness). For non-pegged candidates arriving after time 1/2 with a record value, the algorithm accepts only if the true value is not too far below the top predicted value, preventing smoothness violations. This yields an additive guarantee of u_A ≥ u_{i*} − 4ε with probability 1 and a fairness guarantee of P[A = i*] ≥ 1/16. The k-secretary extension generalizes pegging via three maintained sets (Hopefuls, Blaming, Pegged) that track which top-predicted candidates have been deferred and which future candidates they are responsible for, achieving smoothness with constant C = 4k and per-rank fairness guarantees.

## Results

ADDITIVE-PEGGING achieves smoothness with C=4 (i.e., E[u_A] ≥ u_{i*} − 4ε for all instances) and fairness with F=1/16 (probability of accepting the best candidate ≥ 1/16 under any predictions). MULTIPLICATIVE-PEGGING, using the multiplicative error of Fujii and Yoshida, guarantees u_A ≥ u_{i*}(1−4ε) with probability 1 and the same F=1/16 fairness. For the k-secretary extension, k-PEGGING achieves smoothness C=4k and per-candidate fairness F_ℓ = max{(1/3)^{k+5}, (1−(ℓ+13)/k)/256}, ensuring every top-k/2 candidate is accepted with probability at least 1/1024 for k≥52.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to LEARNED-DYNKIN [30], ADDITIVE-PEGGING matches the smoothness guarantee (approaching 1 as ε→0) while adding a fairness guarantee of 1/16 that LEARNED-DYNKIN completely lacks—the paper constructs an explicit instance where LEARNED-DYNKIN accepts the best candidate with probability 0. In experiments, ADDITIVE-PEGGING outperforms LEARNED-DYNKIN in competitive ratio and fairness across nearly all instance types and ε values, with the gap most pronounced on Unfair instances where predictions are systematically inverted. Compared to the classical DYNKIN algorithm [27], ADDITIVE-PEGGING sacrifices fairness (1/16 vs. 1/e ≈ 0.368) but gains a smoothness guarantee that approaches 1 as prediction error decreases, whereas DYNKIN is capped at 1/e regardless of prediction quality. In experiments, DYNKIN matches ADDITIVE-PEGGING on fairness when predictions are uninformative (ε large) but falls behind on competitive ratio when predictions are accurate (ε small). HIGHEST-PREDICTION, which always accepts the highest-predicted candidate, performs well on Uniform and Adversarial instances for small ε but degrades sharply on Almost-constant and Unfair instances, particularly in fairness where it approaches random (1/n) performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the learning-augmented secretary problem formulation of Fujii and Yoshida (2023), which provides per-candidate predictions and achieves a smoothness guarantee of max{Ω(1), 1-O(ε)}, and on Dynkin's classical 1/e stopping rule. The key departure is the 'pegging' mechanism: when the highest-predicted candidate arrives but fails the fairness condition, the algorithm rejects them only if a future candidate has a sufficiently high predicted value, thereby preserving smoothness without sacrificing fairness. This idea is fundamentally different from both LEARNED-DYNKIN (which switches modes based on prediction error) and VALUE-MAXIMIZATION SECRETARY (which uses a single maximum-value prediction), and the authors note it simplifies and unifies aspects of prior analyses. The extension to k-secretary introduces additional bookkeeping (Hopefuls, blaming set, pegged set) to handle the multi-acceptance budget.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper exposes a concrete and previously unacknowledged failure mode: the state-of-the-art learning-augmented secretary algorithm can assign zero probability to hiring the truly best candidate, which is a serious fairness concern in applications like hiring. The pegging framework provides a principled way to simultaneously achieve near-optimal expected value (approaching 1 as prediction error vanishes) and a constant-probability fairness guarantee, and generalizes to both additive and multiplicative error definitions. Open questions include the Pareto-optimal trade-off curve between smoothness constant C and fairness F (upper-bounded at F ≤ 1/3+o(1) by Choo and Ling), incorporating group-level fairness or bias in the value-revelation process, and whether tighter constants are achievable. The practical impact is currently limited to the secretary-problem abstraction, but the conceptual insight—that good expected-value guarantees do not imply individual fairness—generalizes to other online decision-making settings with predictions.
