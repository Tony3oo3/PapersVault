## Problem

Existing methods for improving LLM reasoning—CoT prompting, tool-calling, supervised fine-tuning, and self-improvement with LLM-as-judge—suffer from prompt sensitivity, dependence on large annotated corpora, or subjective and potentially biased self-evaluation. The paper asks whether a self-play adversarial language game, analogous to AlphaGo Zero's paradigm, can provide an objective, data-efficient, and iteratively improvable route to enhancing general LLM reasoning without human annotation or external reward models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a base LLM (LLaMA-2-7B or Baichuan-2-13B), a 50K-word target vocabulary drawn from the CoCA corpus (with NLTK stop words removed), and prompt templates that frame the attacker and defender roles. GPT-4 self-play episodes over 30K words (3–8 turns each) serve as imitation data, while 52K Alpaca instruction-following pairs act as a general-language regularization set. The output is a fine-tuned LLM whose reasoning scores improve across eight benchmarks (MMLU, BBH, Mutual, ARC-e/c, LogiQA2, WinoGrande, PIQA) after up to three self-play epochs, with the game itself using a maximum of 5 turns and rule-based outcome detection via word-form matching (including plurals and tense variants).

## Method

SPAG models Adversarial Taboo as a two-player zero-sum Markov game where an attacker (who sees the target word) tries to induce the defender to utter it, while the defender tries to guess it without accidentally saying it. Training proceeds in two stages: first, behavior cloning on GPT-4 game episodes (separating attacker-winning and defender-winning trajectories) to teach the base LLM to follow game rules; second, iterative offline self-play RL where a frozen copy π̄θ generates episodes, and the live policy πθ is updated via a PPO-style importance-sampling loss (Eq. 13) that combines attacker and defender policy gradients with KL regularization against π̄θ and an SFT log-likelihood term. A key stability trick borrows from ReST: only winning episodes (R(τ)>0 for attacker, R(τ)<0 for defender) are used for gradient updates, avoiding the training instability caused by negative-advantage samples. The reward is a geometrically decaying zero-sum signal (γ=0.8) that weights later turns more heavily, and the advantage is approximated by the per-turn reward itself under the assumption that self-play value functions cancel. Iterating this sample-then-update loop for three epochs yields continuous, uniform reasoning gains on both 7B and 13B models.

## Results

On LLaMA-2-7B, SPAG-3 achieves a geometric mean (GM) of 52.58 across eight reasoning benchmarks, improving over the base model (49.17) by +3.41 points and over the CoT baseline (50.05) by +2.53 points. On Baichuan-2-13B, SPAG-3 reaches a GM of 56.75 versus 54.21 for the base model (+2.54). Performance improves monotonically across three self-play epochs (e.g., LLaMA-2-7B: 51.69 → 52.19 → 52.58), and gains are consistent across all individual benchmarks including BBH, ARC-c, LogiQA2, and WinoGrande.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Chain-of-Thought prompting, SPAG-3 on LLaMA-2-7B achieves a GM of 52.58 versus CoT's 50.05, winning on 6 of 8 benchmarks (notably BBH 34.94 vs 38.73 is the one exception where CoT leads, but SPAG dominates on ARC-c, LogiQA2, and PIQA). Against continuous supervised fine-tuning (AlpacaSFT), SPAG-3 outperforms AlpacaSFT-3 by +2.50 GM on LLaMA-2-7B (52.58 vs 50.08) and by a much larger +4.96 GM on Baichuan-2-13B (56.75 vs 51.79), indicating that SFT degrades with more epochs while SPAG continues to improve. Against non-adversarial language games (20-Question and Guess-My-City), SPAG-3 substantially outperforms both: 52.58 vs 47.79 (SP-20Q) and 48.78 (SP-GuessCity) on LLaMA-2-7B, confirming that the adversarial structure is critical for reasoning gains. The paper also shows that the imitation-learned model on Adversarial Taboo (IM-AG, GM 50.22) already surpasses imitation on non-adversarial games (IM-20Q: 46.43, IM-GuessCity: 49.46), further validating the adversarial game design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the self-improvement paradigm for LLMs by replacing the common reliance on high-quality question queries and LLM-as-judge with an adversarial language game (Adversarial Taboo, from Yao et al. 2021) that provides automatic, objective win/lose signals. Methodologically, it combines behavior cloning from GPT-4 with an offline RL scheme that borrows the importance-sampling structure from PPO/A-LoL and the reward-threshold filtering from ReST, applied in a two-player zero-sum Markov game setting. Conceptually, it draws an explicit analogy to AlphaGo Zero's self-play-without-human-knowledge, but transposes it to natural language: the 'board' is a 50K-word vocabulary and the 'moves' are multi-turn utterances. Unlike prior non-adversarial word games (20-Question, Guess-My-City), the adversarial structure is shown to be critical for reasoning gains.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical implication is that reasoning improvement can be driven by self-generated game episodes with rule-based outcome checks, eliminating the need for human-annotated reasoning data or a separate reward model. However, the evidence is limited to two moderate-size open-source models (7B and 13B), and the authors explicitly flag safety concerns: adversarial self-play may teach LLMs to bluff, cheat, or develop other harmful strategies. Open questions include whether the approach scales to frontier models, whether more sophisticated value estimation (MCTS, actor-critic) would yield larger gains, and whether the MMLU slight degradation signals a fundamental tension between game-specific optimization and general language understanding.
