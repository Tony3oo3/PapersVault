## Problem

Eliciting a flexible high-dimensional belief density from an expert who can only answer preferential questions (comparisons or rankings of alternatives) is an open problem: existing elicitation methods assume simple parametric families, and while normalizing flows are flexible enough, they suffer from collapsing or diverging probability mass when trained on the limited, indirect information that preferential data provide. No prior work has learned normalizing flows from preferential comparisons or rankings.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241768/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a collection of choice sets C_k = {x_1, …, x_k} of candidate points drawn from an unknown external distribution λ (not from the belief density itself), together with the expert's preference indicating which candidate is most probable (k-wise comparison) or a full ordering (k-wise ranking). The output is a normalizing flow T_φ whose density approximates the expert's belief density p_*(x). Critically, the candidates are not samples from the target; the only signal about the belief comes from which candidate the expert selects, making the problem fundamentally different from standard density estimation.

## Method

The expert's choice is modeled as a Random Utility Model with exponential noise, U(x) = log p_*(x) + W(x) where W ~ Exp(s), yielding closed-form k-wise comparison and ranking likelihoods. The key theoretical insight is that the k-wise winner distribution converges (as k→∞) to a tempered and tilted version of the belief density, p(x) ∝ λ(x)·p_*(x)^s, which motivates a novel empirical functional prior p(f) ∝ p_unif(f)·∏_i exp(f_i) evaluated at winner points. Training maximizes a function-space MAP (FS-MAP) objective combining the preferential likelihood with this prior, which encourages the flow to allocate more probability mass to regions consistent with the RUM decision model, thereby resolving the collapsing and diverging mass failure modes. The approach is architecture-agnostic (RealNVP for 2D, Neural Spline Flow for higher dimensions) and requires only s=1 for a tractable closed-form prior, at the cost of some prior misspecification for small k.

## Results

Across all six evaluation scenarios (2D to 20D synthetic densities plus the 7D Abalone dataset), the proposed flow-based method consistently outperforms the factorized normal baseline on log-likelihood and Wasserstein distance. For example, on Twogaussians20D the flow achieves a log-likelihood of −0.08 versus −6.35 for the normal baseline, and a Wasserstein distance of 4.55 versus 11.07. On the 2D Onemoon2D task, the flow reaches a Wasserstein distance of 0.25 (±0.04) compared to 0.45 (±0.04) for the normal. The flow also wins on MMTV in 5 of 6 scenarios, with the sole exception being Abalone7D where the normal slightly edges out the flow (0.26 vs. 0.29).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole baseline is a factorized normal distribution (independent per-dimension Gaussians with learned means and log-stds), trained on the same preferential data with the same FS-MAP objective but without the flexible flow representation. This baseline is not a previously published method but was designed specifically to validate the added value of the flow. The flow wins decisively on log-likelihood across all six datasets, with the gap widening in higher dimensions (e.g., −0.08 vs. −6.35 on Twogaussians20D). On Wasserstein distance, the flow also dominates in every scenario, reducing the distance by roughly 40–60% relative to the normal baseline. On MMTV, the flow wins in 5 of 6 cases; the only exception is Abalone7D where the normal achieves 0.26 vs. the flow's 0.29, likely because the factorized structure happens to approximate the marginal distributions well even if it cannot capture inter-variable correlations. The paper also includes an ablation (Figure D.1) showing that removing the functional prior from the flow training causes the LLM elicitation experiment to produce severely diverged marginals (e.g., mean AveRooms of 125 vs. the true ~5.4), confirming that the prior is essential for stable learning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of three literatures: normalizing flows for flexible density representation, discrete choice theory (random utility models with Plackett-Luce and exponential noise) for modeling expert preferences, and function-space Bayesian inference for the learning objective. It departs from standard flow learning, which requires either samples from the target density or log-density evaluations, by formulating a new likelihood based on k-wise comparisons and rankings. The functional prior is derived from the k-wise winner distribution (building on Malmberg and Hossjer's random sup-measure framework), connecting the tempered-density interpretation to a regularizer that prevents collapsing and diverging probability mass. Prior elicitation work has been restricted to simple parametric or low-dimensional copula models; this is the first to learn a fully flexible flow from preferential data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first practical method for eliciting flexible multivariate belief densities from experts via the ecologically valid interface of comparisons and rankings, which is critical for Bayesian prior elicitation and reward modeling in high-dimensional settings. Key limitations include the restriction to exponential noise with s=1 (for analytic tractability), the assumption of largely unimodal beliefs, and validation only on synthetic data plus a single LLM proxy rather than real human experts. Open questions include extending to active candidate selection, other noise families, multimodal beliefs, and rigorous validation with human subjects before deployment in decision-critical applications.
