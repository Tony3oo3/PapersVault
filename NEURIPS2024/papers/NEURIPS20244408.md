## Problem

Real-time decision-making tasks with sparse environment rewards remain challenging for RL agents, and existing human-guided RL methods rely on discrete feedback (e.g., good/neutral/bad) that loses information, requires complex credit-assignment tuning, and cannot support continued policy improvement once human input stops. Additionally, prior studies validate on small samples (N<10) and simple low-dimensional tasks, leaving open whether these approaches scale to general populations and complex visual environments.

## Input / Output

The agent consumes RGB visual frames (stacked 3-frame inputs for navigation tasks) as observations and receives continuous-valued human feedback at every timestep via mouse hovering over a window, alongside sparse environment rewards (e.g., pins hit, treasure found). It outputs continuous actions (3D for bowling, 2D destination vectors for navigation) and, through the parallel feedback simulator, produces predicted feedback values for state-action pairs to substitute for the human trainer. The human feedback is grounded into per-step dense rewards that are simply added to environment rewards, rather than used as a value function as in prior work.

## Method

GUIDE replaces discrete human feedback with continuous per-step feedback obtained via mouse hovering, eliminating the need for credit-assignment windows and enabling constant model updates. The key architectural insight is converting human feedback into dense rewards (r_t = r_t^hf + r_t^env) rather than treating it as a state-action value function, which allows seamless integration with off-the-shelf RL algorithms (DDPG with a shared 3-layer CNN encoder and 3-layer MLP heads for actor, critic, and feedback model). Concurrently, a separate neural network H(s,a) is trained by regression to mimic the specific human trainer's feedback patterns, enabling a transition to automated guidance where the simulator replaces the human for continued policy improvement. The framework is validated on three CREW tasks (Bowling, Find Treasure, 1v1 Hide-and-Seek) with 50 human subjects, including cognitive assessments to quantify how individual differences affect guided agent performance.

## Results

On the challenging Find Treasure and 1v1 Hide-and-Seek tasks, GUIDE-DDPG achieves up to 30% higher success rate than its DDPG RL baseline and 50% higher than c-Deep TAMER given the same 10 minutes of human guidance time. Averaged across all 50 untrained human subjects, GUIDE surpasses c-Deep TAMER by up to 40 points in success rate on these navigation tasks. On the simpler Bowling task, all methods perform similarly with GUIDE holding only a slight advantage over DDPG. Notably, GUIDE-DDPG reaches the same performance level as its RL baselines within half the training time, and the learned feedback simulator enables continued improvement during the second 10-minute automated guidance phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DDPG (the primary RL baseline), GUIDE achieves a clear advantage on the two challenging navigation tasks (Find Treasure and 1v1 Hide-and-Seek), with up to 30% higher success rate, while performing comparably on the simpler Bowling task. Against c-Deep TAMER (the enhanced continuous-action version of Deep TAMER used as the human-guided RL baseline), GUIDE outperforms by 50% in success rate for the top-15 cognitive scorers and by up to 40 points for the full cohort of 50 subjects on the navigation tasks. SAC was initially considered as a baseline but was replaced by DDPG due to DDPG's stronger performance with multi-step returns and avoidance of entropy collapse. A heuristic dense-reward upper bound (exploration + distance reward) is also included, and GUIDE's performance approaches this upper bound more closely for subjects with higher cognitive test scores. The paper also demonstrates that GUIDE's robustness to individual differences scales with task complexity: on Bowling, c-Deep TAMER and GUIDE are comparable, but on the harder navigation tasks, a significantly larger percentage of human trainers reach performance milestones using GUIDE.

## Relation to Prior Work

GUIDE extends the TAMER/Deep TAMER and COACH/Deep COACH lineage of real-time human-guided RL by replacing discrete {positive, neutral, negative} feedback with continuous-valued feedback grounded as dense per-step rewards (interactive reward shaping) rather than as a learned value function. It departs from prior work in three key ways: (1) the continuous feedback interface eliminates credit-assignment hyperparameters and information loss from discretization, (2) the reward-grounding formulation allows seamless combination with sparse environment rewards and reuse of off-the-shelf RL algorithms (DDPG), and (3) a parallel human feedback simulator trained concurrently enables continued policy improvement after human input ceases. The visual CNN encoder and DDPG backbone follow standard practices from recent visual RL work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The N=50 human study with cognitive assessments is the largest in the human-guided RL literature and provides the first quantitative evidence that individual cognitive differences (mental rotation, spatial mapping, reflex speed) significantly correlate with agent learning outcomes, raising important questions about how to mitigate human variability in deployment. The human feedback simulator for autonomous continued training addresses a practical gap—most prior methods require sustained human attention—but its interpretability and robustness to distribution shift remain open. Limitations include evaluation on moderate-complexity tasks, a single RL backbone (DDPG), and the absence of mechanisms to correct or adapt to poor-quality human feedback, leaving scalability to highly complex, long-horizon environments as a key future direction.
