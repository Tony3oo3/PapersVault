## Problem

Neural networks for human motion understanding lack access to biologically grounded muscle activation data, relying instead on surface-level representations like RGB video or skeletal pose. Existing EMG/sEMG datasets are small (5–22 subjects), cover few muscles, and are expensive to collect, limiting the development of models that can generalize across diverse motions and subjects. This paper addresses the data scarcity by introducing MinT, a large-scale synthetic muscle activation dataset, and establishes a benchmark for learning muscle activation estimation from pose sequences.

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes pose motion sequences preprocessed into 263-dimensional descriptors (skeletal joint positions plus additional features) at 20 fps, segmented into 1.4-second clips of T=28 frames. The network outputs 402 per-frame muscle strand activation values in [0,1] (80 lower-body strands from the Lai et al. model and 322 upper thoracolumbar strands from the Bruno et al. model). The underlying MinT dataset provides 9.8 hours of simulated data at 50 fps across 227 subjects, with ground reaction forces and effective muscle forces also available alongside the activation signals.

## Method

The dataset is constructed by mapping AMASS mocap sequences through SMPL-H mesh vertices to virtual marker positions, then feeding them into two validated OpenSim biomechanical models (Lai et al. lower body and Bruno et al. thoracolumbar spine). Ground reaction forces are estimated via trajectory optimization (OpenSimAD/OpenCap), followed by inverse kinematics and static optimization to derive muscle activations, with 1.4-second segments processed in parallel using 50 collocation points per second and a 10⁻³ error tolerance. For the estimation benchmark, five sequence-to-sequence architectures are trained from scratch (300k iterations, batch 256): an adapted VQ-VAE (from T2M-GPT), a fully convolutional network, an LSTM, a Mamba2 state-space model, and a 16-layer Transformer. The Transformer achieves the best PCC and SMAPE across all motion categories, and a transfer-learning experiment fine-tuning only the first and last Transformer blocks on the real-world Muscles in Action dataset demonstrates that ~8% of trainable weights suffice to partially bridge the synthetic-to-real domain gap.

## Results

On the MinT dataset, the 16-layer Transformer achieves the best performance across all metrics for both body models. For the lower body model (all actions), it reaches RMSE 0.048, SMAPE 45.1%, and PCC 0.54, outperforming the next-best Mamba2 (RMSE 0.051, SMAPE 55.4%, PCC 0.49). For the upper body model, the Transformer scores RMSE 0.033, SMAPE 107.7%, and PCC 0.55, again leading all baselines. The PCC gap is the most pronounced: the Transformer improves over VQ-VAE by 0.14 (lower body) and 0.23 (upper body), while RMSE differences are marginal (≤0.01), highlighting the importance of scale-invariant metrics for this task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The Transformer (16 layers) dominates all four baselines on every metric for both body models. Against VQ-VAE (adapted from T2M-GPT [78]), the Transformer improves PCC by 0.14 on the lower body (0.54 vs 0.40) and 0.23 on the upper body (0.55 vs 0.32), while reducing SMAPE by 14.6 and 7.6 percentage points respectively. FConv [26] and LSTM [31] cluster closely together (PCC 0.48–0.49 lower body, 0.47–0.48 upper body), both trailing the Transformer by roughly 0.05–0.07 PCC. Mamba2 [14] is the strongest non-Transformer baseline, achieving PCC 0.49 (lower) and 0.50 (upper), but still lags the Transformer by 0.05 and 0.05 PCC and by 10.3 and 5.0 SMAPE points. The paper notes that RMSE differences are marginal across architectures (≤0.01), making PCC and SMAPE the more discriminative metrics for this task where many muscles are near-zero most of the time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges computer vision and biomechanics by combining the AMASS motion capture corpus (in SMPL format) with OpenSim's validated musculoskeletal models (Lai et al. for the lower body, Bruno et al. for the thoracolumbar region) to generate synthetic muscle activation data at unprecedented scale. It departs from prior EMG/sEMG datasets (KIMHu, MIA, MuscleMap) which are small, limited in muscle coverage, and require invasive recording, by instead simulating 402 continuous muscle strand activations from mocap kinematics via trajectory optimization and static optimization. For the benchmark, the authors adapt the VQ-VAE motion-to-motion architecture from T2M-GPT [78] by swapping its prediction head, and compare against standard sequence-to-sequence backbones (LSTM, FConv, Mamba2, Transformer), positioning the task within the broader sequence-to-sequence literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MinT is the first large-scale synthetic muscle activation dataset (9.8 hours, 227 subjects, 402 muscle strands), enabling neural network-based muscle activation estimation without the resource-intensive and invasive EMG recording that has bottlenecked this field. The key open question is the synthetic-to-real domain gap: whether models trained on OpenSim-simulated activations transfer to real sEMG signals, which the authors partially probe via fine-tuning on the MIA dataset but do not fully resolve. Additional limitations include restriction to foot-ground-contact motions (no object interaction forces), assumed negligible object mass for lifting/throwing, and potential category distribution shift from discarding non-converging simulations. The finding that PCC and SMAPE are far more informative than RMSE for this task—due to the sparsity of muscle activation—offers a useful methodological lesson for future muscle-related benchmarks.
