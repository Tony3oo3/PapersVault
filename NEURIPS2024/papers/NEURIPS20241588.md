## Problem

Existing fairness-aware recommendation methods address either item fairness or user fairness in isolation, often at the expense of the other stakeholder group or the platform's own revenue. In an online setting with data uncertainty, the platform cannot even verify whether its fairness constraints are satisfied (no constraint feedback is available), making the problem substantially harder than standard constrained optimization with bandit feedback. The paper asks: what constitutes a fair recommendation across all stakeholder groups, and how can a platform implement it in practice while learning user preferences?

## Input / Output

In the offline formulation, the platform takes as input the full problem instance (arrival probabilities p, purchase probabilities y, item revenues r), item outcome matrix L, user outcome matrix U, a social welfare function W, and tunable fairness parameters δ^I, δ^U ∈ [0,1], and outputs a recommendation probability vector x ∈ Δ_N^M (or assortment probabilities q_j(S) in the extended setting). In the online setting, the platform only observes the arriving user's type J_t and a single binary purchase decision z_t per round, with no access to constraint violations or counterfactual outcomes for unoffered items. The fairness parameters serve as interpretable handles: δ^I = 1 recovers the item-fair solution, δ^I = 0 removes the item constraint, and intermediate values interpolate between platform revenue maximization and full stakeholder fairness.

## Method

The framework Problem (FAIR) is a constrained optimization that maximizes platform revenue subject to each item receiving at least δ^I fraction of its within-group fair outcome (computed via SWF maximization, supporting maxmin, K-S, Nash, Hooker-Williams, etc.) and each user type receiving at least δ^U fraction of their best-item utility. The online algorithm FORM addresses two coupled challenges—data uncertainty and fairness interfering with exploration—via a relaxation-then-exploration technique: at each round it solves a relaxed version of FAIR (FAIR-RELAX) where fairness constraints are loosened by η_t = M log(T)·max{Γ_{y,t}, Γ_{p,t}} to absorb estimation error, then perturbs the solution with a decaying exploration magnitude ε_t to ensure all items receive sufficient exposure for unbiased estimation. The key technical novelty is that the platform cannot observe constraint violations (unlike online knapsack or safe RL settings), so the relaxation magnitude must be analytically calibrated to the convergence rates of the inverse-probability-weighted purchase estimator and the sample-mean arrival estimator. Under a local Lipschitzness assumption on the user outcome matrix and item-fair solution, FORM achieves O(MN^{1/3} T^{-1/3}) regret for both revenue and fairness, with each iteration dominated by solving two linear programs of size MN.

## Results

On Amazon review data (N=30 items, M=5 user types, T=2000 rounds, assortment size K=3), FORM's time-averaged revenue converges rapidly to the optimal FAIR-REV benchmark, confirming the theoretical O(MN^{1/3} T^{-1/3}) regret bound. FairRec and TFROM reduce platform revenue by approximately 38% relative to OPT-REV, while FORM with fairness parameters δ^I = δ^U = 0.2 keeps revenue loss within 10%. FORM ensures all items attain or surpass the specified fairness threshold (both at δ^I = 0.8 and δ^I = 0.2) while simultaneously maintaining high user outcomes, whereas greedy and max-utility baselines produce extremely skewed item allocations with some items receiving minimal or zero revenue.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares FORM against six baselines: greedy, max-utility, min-revenue, random, FairRec [55], and TFROM [71]. FairRec and TFROM, which focus solely on two-sided fairness without considering platform revenue, reduce platform revenue by approximately 38% compared to OPT-REV, whereas FORM with δ^I = δ^U = 0.2 limits revenue loss to within 10%. On item fairness, min-revenue achieves the highest item fairness (as expected, since it directly promotes maxmin revenue fairness) but at a severe cost to the platform; random, FairRec, and TFROM also achieve high item fairness but with notable discrepancies between maximum and minimum item outcomes. Greedy and max-utility produce extremely skewed item allocations, with some items receiving minimal or zero revenue. FORM consistently strikes a balance, ensuring all items nearly attain or surpass the specified fairness level while maintaining high platform revenue and good user outcomes, outperforming all baselines on the joint revenue-fairness trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of multi-stakeholder fairness in recommender systems and constrained optimization with bandit feedback. Prior fairness-in-recommendation work (FairRec, TFROM, balanced neighborhoods) addresses either item or user fairness in static settings with full instance knowledge, while prior online constrained optimization (knapsack bandits, online bidding) assumes access to constraint feedback. FORM departs from both lines by (i) formulating a flexible multi-sided fairness problem via social welfare functions that explicitly includes platform revenue, and (ii) solving it online without the ability to verify constraint satisfaction—a setting no prior bandit-with-constraints work handles. The relaxation-then-exploration technique is a novel algorithmic contribution that bridges the gap between learning user preferences and maintaining fairness guarantees simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework gives platforms a principled, tunable mechanism (via δ^I, δ^U) to balance revenue against multi-stakeholder fairness, with a clean 'price of fairness' characterization (Theorem C.1) that enables practical parameter tuning via A/B testing. The sublinear regret guarantees (O(MN^{1/3}T^{-1/3})) for both revenue and fairness in an online setting with uncertain constraints are the first of their kind for this problem class. Limitations include the requirement of knowing the user outcome matrix U(θ), the stochastic (non-adversarial) arrival model, and validation only on a single Amazon dataset with modest scale (N=30, M=5, T=2000). Open questions include long-term fairness dynamics as user/item attributes evolve, real-world deployment at scale, and whether the framework extends to adversarial or non-stationary settings.
