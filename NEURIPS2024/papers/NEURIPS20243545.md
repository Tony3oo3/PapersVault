## Problem

Standard strategic classification assumes agents have full knowledge of the deployed classifier, which is unrealistic when models are proprietary, complex, or obfuscated for privacy. This paper introduces a Bayesian setting where agents hold a shared prior over the hypothesis class and the learner can strategically release a truthful subset of classifiers to shape agent behavior, asking how much information to reveal to maximize expected accuracy.

## Input / Output

The learner's optimization takes the ground truth mapping f, data distribution D, cost function c, agents' prior π over the hypothesis class H, and the deployed classifier h, and outputs an optimal subset H* ⊆ H containing h that maximizes expected strategic accuracy. The agent's best response problem takes a single agent's feature vector x and the released subset H, and outputs a manipulation point BR(x, H) that maximizes the agent's quasi-linear utility (probability of positive classification under the posterior π|_H minus manipulation cost). The key structural constraint is that H must contain h, making the information release truthful but potentially incomplete.

## Method

The paper formulates a Stackelberg game where the learner commits to a subset H ⊆ H containing the deployed classifier h, and agents project their prior π onto H to form a posterior π|_H before best-responding. For the agent's best response, the paper shows exponential oracle complexity is required in general (even for ℓ_p costs in R²), but provides an O(n^d)-oracle-call algorithm for low-dimensional linear classifiers via arrangement partitioning, and a Õ(n/ε²)-oracle approximation for V-submodular cost functions. For the learner's optimization, the paper proves NP-hardness for arbitrary priors via reduction from subset sum, then gives an O(n³) algorithm for discrete uniform priors by characterizing the utility through a single real-valued threshold R_H and showing only polynomially many distinct values of R_H exist. A key counter-intuitive result is that partial information release can strictly dominate both full and no information release for accuracy, while for FPR minimization no-information-release is always optimal and for FNR minimization full-information-release is always optimal.

## Results

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical evaluation. The key quantitative results are: (1) an exponential lower bound of Ω(2ⁿ/√n) oracle calls required to compute an agent's best response even in ℝ² with ℓₚ-norm costs (Theorem 3.1); (2) an O(nᵈ) oracle-call algorithm for low-dimensional linear classifiers (Theorem 3.2); (3) an O(n³) algorithm for the learner's optimal information release under discrete uniform priors (Theorem 4.6); and (4) NP-hardness of the learner's optimization problem for arbitrary priors (Theorem D.3). The paper also demonstrates via constructed examples that partial information release can strictly improve learner utility over full information release (e.g., U(H*)=1 vs. U({h})=2/3 in Example 2.3, and U(H*)=1 vs. U({h})=0.55 in Example 2.4).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is theoretical and does not include empirical comparisons against baselines. Conceptually, it extends the full-information strategic classification framework of Hardt et al. (2016) by relaxing the assumption that agents know the deployed classifier, and generalizes the binary all-or-nothing information release studied by Ghalme et al. (2021) to arbitrary subsets of the hypothesis class. The paper shows that the learner's optimal strategy can be strictly better than both full-information-release (H={h}) and no-information-release (H=supp(π)), establishing an informational middle ground not captured by prior work. For FPR minimization, no-information-release dominates full-information-release (Theorem 4.7), while for FNR minimization, full-information-release is always optimal — results that contrast with the accuracy objective where partial release can be strictly superior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends strategic classification (Hardt et al., 2016) by replacing the full-information assumption with a Bayesian prior over the hypothesis class, and extends the all-or-nothing information release framework of Ghalme et al. (2021) to allow the learner to release an arbitrary subset of classifiers. The agent best-response problem is connected to submodular optimization via El Halabi and Jegelka (2020), and the information release mechanism is positioned as a special case of Bayesian persuasion (Kamenica and Gentzkow, 2011) with a fixed state of the world. The paper explicitly frames its contribution as occupying the 'intermediate' ground between full opacity and full transparency that prior works have not studied.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that releasing partial information can strictly improve the learner's accuracy over both full and no information release—challenges the binary framing of transparency in strategic ML and suggests practitioners should think more carefully about *what* to reveal. However, the results are entirely theoretical and restricted to simplified settings (1D threshold classifiers, low-dimensional linear classifiers), with no empirical validation. Key open questions include: how to handle heterogeneous priors across demographic groups (fairness implications), whether agents can reverse-engineer the deployed classifier from the learner's optimal H choice (information leakage, acknowledged in Appendix H), and how the model extends to dynamically updated classifiers.
