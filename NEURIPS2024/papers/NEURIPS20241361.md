## Problem

Training LLMs on synthetic positive (correct) data for math reasoning yields modest gains with slow scaling (D^{-0.05} to D^{-0.15}) and amplifies spurious correlations from incorrect intermediate steps, leading to flat or inverse scaling. It remains unclear how to properly leverage negative (incorrect) synthetic responses to address these failure modes and extract more utility from the same synthetic data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241361/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes math problems from GSM8K/MATH synthetic datasets, along with positive solution traces (verified correct answers from the SFT policy or capable models like GPT-4/Gemini 1.5 Pro) and negative solution traces (incorrect answers sampled from the SFT policy). Rather than contrasting entire responses, preference pairs are constructed at the level of individual reasoning steps: a shared prefix y_{1:i} from the SFT policy is paired with a positive continuation +y_{i+1} and a negative continuation -y_{i+1} that diverge at the critical step. The output is a finetuned LLM policy that produces step-by-step solution traces, with the training objective being the DPO loss over these per-step preference triplets.

## Method

The core contribution is per-step DPO, which constructs negative data to enable per-step credit assignment by identifying 'critical steps'—intermediate reasoning steps where the model must make precise choices to succeed. For each negative response, Monte Carlo Q-value estimates are computed for every step under a Best-of-K (K=5) policy, and the first step with the lowest Q-value (the 'first pit') is identified as the critical decision point. Preference pairs are then constructed at this step, contrasting the correct continuation against the flawed one, rather than naively pairing entire positive and negative responses. The paper proves (Theorem 6.1) that this per-step DPO objective is equivalent to advantage-weighted RL, where advantages are computed under the BoK(π_sft) value function, and connects the generalization benefits to distributionally robust optimization. This construction achieves an 8× improvement in sample efficiency over positive data alone, unlearns spurious correlations, and avoids the model collapse that occurs when training on over-trained SFT checkpoints.

## Results

On GSM8K and MATH, SFT on positive synthetic data from GPT-4/Gemini-1.5 Pro yields error rates scaling as ~D^{-0.15} and ~D^{-0.05} respectively. Rejection finetuning (RFT) with self-generated positive traces from the 7B SFT policy doubles the effective sample efficiency (2×), while per-step DPO with carefully constructed negative traces achieves an 8× effective data multiplier over SFT alone. Standard DPO with random positive-negative pairing flatlines or degrades as synthetic data scales, whereas per-step DPO continues to improve consistently on both benchmarks for both DeepSeek-Math-7B and Llama2-7B.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Per-step DPO substantially outperforms RFT [69] on both GSM8K and MATH: while RFT achieves a 2× effective data multiplier over SFT, per-step DPO extends this to 8×, and the gap widens as synthetic data scales. Against standard DPO [41] with random positive-negative pairing, per-step DPO shows a clear and consistent advantage: standard DPO flatlines or even degrades in performance as D_syn grows (Figure 5c), whereas per-step DPO maintains favorable scaling. The paper attributes standard DPO's failure to an incorrect induced advantage function that exacerbates spurious correlations. On the didactic star graph problem, per-step DPO from an under-trained or early-stopped SFT checkpoint sharply reduces test error (phase transition), while from an over-trained SFT checkpoint it leads to model collapse—matching SFT's test error—because the Monte-Carlo advantage estimator overestimates spurious steps. Overall, per-step DPO wins on all main comparisons, with the 8× efficiency gain being the headline result.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the synthetic data generation pipeline of Li et al. [29] and the rejection fine-tuning / STaR framework [69,70] for self-generated positive data, and on DPO [41] and per-step DPO (Self-Explore) [23] for incorporating negative data. Its key departure is a formal theoretical analysis: it proves that per-step DPO with carefully constructed negative pairs is equivalent to advantage-weighted RL [40] under a Best-of-K value function, and connects the resulting generalization gains to causal confusion in imitation learning [12] and distributionally robust optimization [43]. Prior work on synthetic data [29,31,32,66] focused almost exclusively on positive (correct) responses; this paper is among the first to systematically study how the *structure* of negative data (per-step credit assignment vs. random pairing) determines whether preference optimization scales or flatlines.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 8× sample-efficiency gain from per-step negative data over positive-only SFT is directly actionable for teams scaling synthetic data pipelines for math reasoning, and the finding that self-generated positives are 2× more efficient than GPT-4/Gemini solutions challenges the common distillation intuition that a stronger teacher always yields better training data. The theoretical equivalence to advantage-weighted RL provides a principled lens for designing future preference-optimization objectives beyond math. Open questions include whether the framework generalizes to longer-horizon or multi-step reasoning tasks where synthetic data itself may contain errors, whether the 8× gain persists at larger model scales (beyond 7B), and how transfer to novel problem types is affected by the spurious-correlation dynamics identified here.
