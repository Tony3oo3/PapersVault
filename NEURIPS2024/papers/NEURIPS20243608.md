## Problem

Existing chart understanding benchmarks (FigureQA, DVQA, ChartQA) rely on synthetic or stylistically homogeneous charts with template-based questions, causing open-source MLLMs to appear competitive with or superior to proprietary models. The paper demonstrates via simple stress tests that this apparent parity is illusory: minor modifications to questions or charts degrade open-source model accuracy by up to 34.5%, revealing that these benchmarks overestimate general chart understanding capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243608/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each evaluation sample pairs a real-world chart image (re-rendered as high-resolution JPEG with the longer side at 1024px, average size 996×702px) with a short-answer question. Descriptive questions (4 per chart, 19 templates across 5 categories) probe basic elements like titles, axis labels, and tick counts; reasoning questions (1 per chart) require visual-numeric synthesis. Notably, 25% of descriptive questions are intentionally unanswerable. Outputs are short text answers (average ~2.9 tokens) graded as binary correct/incorrect by GPT-4o against human-verified references, achieving 98.5% agreement with human graders.

## Method

CharXiv is constructed via a four-step chart curation pipeline: (1) SigLIP-based pre-filtering of arXiv figures (8 subjects, 2020–2023) using cosine similarity ≥0.65 to existing chart embeddings, (2) manual selection by graduate students enforcing visual diversity, (3) deduplication removing pairs with cosine similarity ≥0.95, and (4) quality filtering for clarity and labeling. Questions are hand-curated: descriptive questions follow 19 templates across information extraction, enumeration, pattern recognition, counting, and compositionality; reasoning questions are authored by annotators from GPT-4V-generated samples and constrained to four answer types (text-in-chart, text-in-general, number-in-chart, number-in-general) requiring only visual and numerical reasoning without domain knowledge. The benchmark comprises 2,323 charts (1,000 validation, 1,323 test) with over 10K questions, and is evaluated using GPT-4o as an LLM judge. The paper also introduces a stress-test protocol with modified-question and modified-chart subsets of MathVista to quantify robustness gaps between open-source and proprietary models.

## Results

On the CharXiv validation set (1,000 charts), GPT-4o achieves 47.1% accuracy on reasoning questions and 84.45% on descriptive questions, while the strongest open-source model, InternVL Chat V1.5, achieves only 29.2% and 58.5% respectively—a 17.9-point gap on reasoning. Human annotators score 80.5% on reasoning and 92.1% on descriptive questions, far exceeding all models. A text-only random baseline (GPT-4o without seeing charts) scores just 10.8% on reasoning and 19.85% on descriptive questions, confirming that models do extract meaningful visual information. In a stress test on 174 modified examples from DVQA/FigureQA/ChartQA, SPHINX V2's accuracy drops by 34.5% (from 63.2% to 28.7%) when questions are slightly rephrased, revealing poor generalization in open-source models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CharXiv is a benchmark paper, so the central comparison is across 24 MLLMs (13 open-source, 11 proprietary) plus human and random baselines. GPT-4o leads all proprietary models with 47.1% on reasoning and 84.45% on descriptive questions, outperforming GPT-4V (37.1%/79.92%) by 10 and 4.5 points respectively, and Claude 3 Sonnet (32.2%/73.65%) by 14.9 and 10.8 points. Among open-source models, InternVL Chat V1.5 (29.2%/58.50%) leads but trails GPT-4o by 17.9 points on reasoning and 25.95 points on descriptive questions—a gap far larger than what prior benchmarks (DVQA, FigureQA, ChartQA) suggest. All 24 models fall well short of the 80.5% human reasoning accuracy, with the largest gap (33.4 points) for GPT-4o. The stress test in §2.2 further reveals that open-source models trained on existing chart benchmarks (e.g., SPHINX V2, IDEFICS 2, LLaVA) degrade by 20–35% on minimally modified questions or charts, while proprietary models remain near the diagonal, indicating that prior benchmark scores overestimate open-source generalization. On compositional tasks like counting axis ticks, 20 of 24 models score below 10% (near the 5.36% random baseline), while humans achieve 92.86%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in chart understanding evaluation by departing from the synthetic, template-based paradigm of prior benchmarks (FigureQA, DVQA, PlotQA, ChartQA) and the limited-source charts of ChartQA and MMC. Rather than building on a specific method, it repurposes the evaluation framework of MathVista while explicitly critiquing its design choices, and introduces a four-step curation pipeline (SigLIP pre-filtering, human selection, deduplication, quality check) to source 2,323 diverse real-world charts from arXiv. The introduction of unanswerable questions and the explicit separation of descriptive from reasoning questions represent deliberate extensions beyond what prior chart QA benchmarks offered.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The stress test showing a 34.5% accuracy drop for SPHINX V2 on minimally modified questions, combined with the 17.9% reasoning gap between GPT-4o and the best open-source model (versus near-parity on existing benchmarks), fundamentally challenges how the field interprets chart understanding scores. The finding that all models lag far behind humans (80.5% vs. 47.1% for GPT-4o) and that compositional tasks like counting axis ticks are near-random for 20 of 24 models reveals specific, actionable weaknesses. Open questions include whether the GPT-4o-based grading introduces systematic bias, whether the arXiv chart distribution generalizes to other domains (finance, healthcare), and whether the unanswerable-question component can be scaled beyond the current 25% inclusion rate.
