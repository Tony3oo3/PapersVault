## Problem

Existing RLHF methods for MLLMs rely on binary (superior vs. inferior) preference comparisons, which are insufficient for mitigating hallucinations because the large quality gap between the two levels makes it hard for models to discern subtle differences, and the inferior response is underutilized. The paper identifies two practical barriers to adopting multi-level preferences: constructing K-level preference datasets requires K(K-1)/2 comparisons that are expensive and noisy (14% contradiction rate for human annotators, 12% for GPT-4V), and no effective learning objective exists for robustly optimizing over such complex preference structures.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a text prompt as input and produces a text response (caption or answer). During training, each sample in the preference dataset consists of an image, a prompt, and K ranked responses (K=4 optimal), where rankings are established automatically without human or AI annotators. The auto-check mechanism extracts noun chunks and sentences from each response, encodes them with the CLIP text encoder, and computes cosine similarity against the ground-truth response to verify or correct the initial ranking, using a threshold of 0.85 for the accuracy metric.

## Method

The AMP framework has three components. First, two human-free data generation strategies produce initial K-level preference datasets: Multi-size Expert Generation (MEG) leverages LLaVA models of increasing size (2B, 7B, 13B, 34B) to create responses of graded quality, while Incremental Generation (IG) fine-tunes a base MLLM on progressively larger subsets of the training data to yield responses of increasing capability. Second, an auto-check mechanism refines rankings by computing local (noun-chunk) and global (sentence) similarity scores via CLIP, selecting the response with highest accuracy (and score as tiebreaker) as the best. Third, the MDPO algorithm extends DPO to K-level preferences by summing DPO losses over all K(K-1)/2 pairwise comparisons, and introduces a penalty term (log π_θ(y_w|x)/π_ref(y_w|x)) applied exclusively to the best response to explicitly boost its generation probability, preventing the policy from simultaneously suppressing both superior and inferior responses during training.

## Results

On MMHal-Bench, AMP-MEG-13B achieves a score of 3.23 with a hallucination rate of 0.34, outperforming the best RLHF baseline FGAIF (3.09 / 0.36) and the strongest general MLLM LLaVA-V1.6-13B (3.04 / 0.43). On the newly introduced MRHal-Bench, AMP-MEG-13B reaches a cumulative/mean score of 4.21/4.21 with a hallucination rate of 0.15/0.11, substantially lower than all baselines (next best: LLaVA-V1.6-13B at 3.73/3.79, Hal. 0.30/0.25). On LLaVA-Bench, AMP-MEG-13B scores 94.4 (Conversation), 91.2 (Detail), and 95.6 (Complex), matching or exceeding all compared models. On POPE (Table 2), AMP-MEG-13B achieves an overall F1 of 87.2 and a 'Yes' rate of 49.8, surpassing FGAIF (83.4 / 48.3) and LLaVA-V1.6-13B (86.3 / 41.0).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AMP outperforms all 13 compared baselines on MMHal-Bench and MRHal-Bench. Against the strongest RLHF baseline FGAIF [13], AMP-MEG-13B improves MMHal-Bench score from 3.09 to 3.23 and reduces hallucination rate from 0.36 to 0.34; on MRHal-Bench the gap is larger (4.21 vs 3.77 cumulative score, 0.15 vs 0.30 cumulative hallucination rate). Compared to the best general MLLM LLaVA-V1.6-13B [3], AMP-MEG-13B gains +0.19 MMHal-Bench score and reduces hallucination from 0.43 to 0.34, while also improving MRHal-Bench from 3.73 to 4.21. Against PPO-based LLaVA-RLHF-13B [11], AMP achieves a substantially lower hallucination rate (0.34 vs 0.57 on MMHal-Bench; 0.15 vs 0.45 on MRHal-Bench), though LLaVA-RLHF retains an edge on LLaVA-Bench Conversation (93.8 vs 94.4 is actually a win for AMP) and Complex (111.4 vs 95.6). On POPE (Table 2), AMP-MEG-13B leads in overall F1 (87.2) and 'Yes' rate (49.8) over FGAIF (83.4 / 48.3) and LLaVA-V1.6-13B (86.3 / 41.0), confirming consistent gains across both hallucination-specific and general benchmarks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) from binary to multi-level preferences, introducing the MDPO algorithm with a penalty term to stabilize training. It departs from prior RLHF-based hallucination mitigation methods (LLaVA-RLHF, RLHF-V, POVID, SILKIE, FGAIF) that rely on human- or AI-annotated binary preference pairs, instead proposing a fully automated multi-level preference dataset generation pipeline (Multi-size Expert Generation and Incremental Generation) with a CLIP-based auto-check mechanism. The work also introduces MRHal-Bench, the first benchmark specifically targeting hallucinations in multi-round dialogues, extending evaluation beyond single-turn settings used by POPE and MMHal-Bench.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that narrowing gaps between adjacent preference levels can outperform simply increasing the quality of the superior response challenges the common assumption in RLHF that better 'winning' examples are always more informative. The human-free dataset generation pipeline addresses a practical bottleneck in scaling preference learning, and the 2.25% inconsistency rate (vs. 14.4% for human and 12% for GPT-4V annotations) suggests automated pipelines can surpass annotator quality for multi-level rankings. Open questions remain: the authors acknowledge that standard response quality caps performance, and that preference optimization inherently biases the model toward the training distribution, potentially degrading generalization. The optimal 4-level setting (with 5-level performing worse) hints at an unexplored trade-off between preference granularity and noise accumulation.
