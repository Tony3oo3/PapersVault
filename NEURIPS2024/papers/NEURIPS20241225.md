## Problem

When an agent in a repeated game knows that its opponent uses a mean-based learning algorithm (e.g., MWU, Replicator Dynamics, or Best Response), it can potentially exploit this knowledge to gain significantly more utility than the game's equilibrium value. However, no exact solution existed for the optimizer's optimal strategy in zero-sum games against these learners, and no computational hardness results were known for general-sum games. This paper addresses both gaps: providing a closed-form optimal strategy for zero-sum games and proving NP-hardness for general-sum games.

## Input / Output

The method takes two utility matrices A (optimizer, n×m) and B (learner, n×m), the learner's algorithm (Replicator Dynamics, MWU, or Best Response), a step size η > 0, a horizon T, and the number of actions n and m. In the continuous zero-sum case, the output is a single constant strategy x* ∈ Δ(A) computed by minimizing a convex log-sum-exp function, along with a closed-form reward expression involving logarithms of exponentials. In the discrete case, the output is a time-varying strategy sequence x(1),…,x(T) and the corresponding total reward. For the NP-hardness result, the input is restricted to pure actions and binary/integer utility entries, and the output is a decision (YES/NO) on whether reward k is achievable.

## Method

For continuous-time zero-sum games, the key insight is that the optimizer's cumulative reward integral telescopes into a difference of log-sum-exp terms that depends only on the time-average of the optimizer's strategy, not its temporal profile. This implies a constant strategy x* is optimal, found by minimizing the convex function f(x) = ln(Σᵢ exp(η(hᵢ(0) − T·eᵢᵀAᵀx))), solvable via Frank-Wolfe in O(1/(εη)) iterations. For discrete-time MWU, the paper proves the discrete reward always exceeds the continuous reward (via a monotonicity argument on the per-round reward function), bounds the gap by ηT/2, and shows that under a structural condition on the game (two best responses differing on the optimizer's support), the optimizer achieves T·Val(A) + Ω(ηT) by alternating between two half-strategies. For general-sum games, the NP-hardness proof reduces Hamiltonian Cycle to the optimizer's reward maximization: graph edges become optimizer actions, vertices become learner actions, and the learner's best-response dynamics are engineered (via carefully chosen utility values of −4, 1, 0.85, −0.1) to enforce that the optimizer must trace a simple path, with the 0.85 'in-vertex' actions preventing premature cycle closure.

## Results

In zero-sum continuous games against a Replicator Dynamics learner, the optimizer's optimal reward is achieved by a constant strategy x* and equals (1/η)[ln(Σᵢ e^{ηhᵢ(0)}) − ln(Σᵢ e^{η(hᵢ(0) − TeᵢᵀAᵀx*)})], bounded in [T·Val(A), T·Val(A) + log(m)/η]. In the discrete-time (MWU) setting, the gap between the best discrete and continuous optimizer is at most ηT/2, and there exist games (satisfying Condition 1) where the gap is Ω(ηT), yielding total utility T·Val(A) + Ω(ηT). For general-sum games against a Best-Response (Fictitious Play) learner, the paper proves NP-hardness: no FPTAS exists for distinguishing between achievable reward T and at most T−1, via a reduction from Hamiltonian Cycle.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or comparison tables. The key quantitative comparisons are against theoretical benchmarks: (1) the game value T·Val(A), which the optimizer always exceeds by at least 0 and at most log(m)/η in the continuous zero-sum case; (2) the Stackelberg value, which prior work [Deng et al. 24, Braverman et al. 9] showed can be exceeded by Ω(T) against mean-based learners in specific games — this paper provides the first general computational hardness result showing that finding the optimal strategy is NP-hard in general-sum games; and (3) the continuous-time optimal reward, which serves as a lower bound for the discrete-time (MWU) case, with the gap tightly bounded at ηT/2. The paper extends Deng et al. [24] by providing an exact closed-form solution for zero-sum games (where Deng et al. only posed a control problem without an efficient algorithm) and by establishing the first NP-hardness lower bound for the general-sum case.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work initiated by Braverman et al. [9] and Deng et al. [24] on exploiting known learning algorithms of opponents in repeated games. While Deng et al. showed that Ω(T) gains over the Stackelberg value are possible against mean-based learners and formulated the optimization as a control problem, they did not provide an efficient algorithm. This paper closes the gap by delivering an exact closed-form solution for zero-sum games (showing a constant strategy suffices) and the first NP-hardness result for general-sum games via a reduction from Hamiltonian Cycle, thereby providing a complete positive/negative dichotomy for the two fundamental game classes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a constant strategy is optimal in zero-sum games against Replicator Dynamics is both elegant and practically useful, as it reduces the optimization to a single convex (log-sum-exp) minimization. The NP-hardness for general-sum games is the first computational barrier in this area, clarifying that no FPTAS exists and motivating the search for approximation schemes or restricted game classes. Key open questions remain: whether the hardness extends to no-regret learners like MWU with small step size, whether one can optimize up to o(T) in general-sum games, and how the results extend to multi-agent settings or rank-1 utility structures.
