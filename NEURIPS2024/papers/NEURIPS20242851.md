## Problem

Existing OOD detection benchmarks rely on semantic labels to designate OOD samples, but marginal OOD samples (e.g., 'African bush elephant' vs. 'African elephant') are semantically nearly identical to ID data, making the binary OOD/ID determination a Sorites Paradox with no principled boundary. This labeling noise inflates or deflates reported performance depending on how many such ambiguous samples are included, and no prior benchmark quantifies *how much* a sample has shifted relative to the ID distribution.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes RGB images from ImageNet-21K (test) and ImageNet-1K (ID), extracting 768-dim CLIP image features that are decomposed into two 384-dim sub-vectors (semantic and covariate). Each test image is assigned to one of 8×8=64 subsets based on its 10th-nearest-neighbor distance in each subspace to the ID set. The Syn-IS complement provides 5,000 SDXL-Turbo-generated images per subset (320,000 total), with prompts pairing ImageNet-21K labels against 51 curated style templates. Evaluated OOD detectors (ResNet-50 trained on ImageNet-1K) output a scalar OOD score per image, scored by AUROC, FPR@95, and AUPR within each subset.

## Method

The core contribution is Language Aligned Image feature Decomposition (LAID), which learns an orthogonal matrix W ∈ ℝ^{l×l} that splits a CLIP feature into semantic and covariate halves. W is trained entirely in the CLIP *text* space via triplet loss: for each iteration a standard text, a semantic-shift text (different ImageNet-21K label, same style prompt), and a covariate-shift text (same label, different style prompt) are encoded, and the loss enforces that the semantic sub-vector is close for the semantic-shift pair while the covariate sub-vector is close for the covariate-shift pair, plus an orthogonality regularizer ‖WᵀW − I‖². Because CLIP aligns text and image embeddings, the same W is then applied to image features to obtain their semantic/covariate decomposition without any image-side training. Shift degrees are measured as the minimum cosine distance from a test image's decomposed feature to the entire ID set in each subspace, and ImageNet-21K is partitioned into 64 subsets (8 levels × 8 levels) using data-driven interval boundaries. The Syn-IS dataset is generated with SDXL-Turbo using 51 style templates × ImageNet-21K labels, with prompts pre-scored for shift level via CLIP text-to-image-feature distance, ensuring balanced 5,000-image subsets that cover covariate variations (e.g., papercut, constructivist, HDR) absent from real-photo datasets.

## Results

On the IS-OOD benchmark (ImageNet-21K subsets), most OOD detection methods gain approximately 40% AUROC improvement from the smallest to the largest semantic shift level, while showing minimal variation across covariate shift levels. KNN achieves the highest semantic sensitivity (6.64), indicating it benefits most from increasing semantic distance, while ODIN shows the lowest covariate sensitivity (0.46), making it the most robust to style/augmentation changes. GradNorm (semantic sensitivity 1.27) and RankFeat (0.79) are notable outliers with the lowest semantic sensitivity, suggesting they rely on different detection mechanisms. On the Syn-IS dataset, the covariate correlation flips to positive for most methods (e.g., MDS 0.99, ODIN 0.92), revealing that excessive style shifts are themselves detected as OOD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 8 OOD detection methods (MSP, ODIN, MDS, GradNorm, KNN, DICE, RankFeat, ASH) on the proposed IS-OOD benchmark rather than proposing a new detection method. KNN emerges as the most semantically sensitive method (sensitivity 6.64), meaning its AUROC improves most dramatically as semantic distance from ImageNet-1K increases, while ODIN is the most covariate-robust (sensitivity 0.46), barely affected by style or augmentation changes. GradNorm and RankFeat are clear outliers: their AUROC barely changes across semantic shift levels (sensitivities 1.27 and 0.79), and RankFeat even shows a positive covariate correlation (0.51) on ImageNet-21K, suggesting these methods detect OOD via low-level features that CLIP cannot capture. On the Syn-IS dataset, the covariate picture inverts: MDS (0.99), ODIN (0.92), KNN (0.92), and ASH (0.92) all show strong positive covariate correlation, indicating that extreme style shifts (e.g., papercut, constructivist) are themselves flagged as OOD. GradNorm's performance declines notably on Syn-IS, attributed to its reliance on uniform softmax outputs that are less common in single-object generated images.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on CLIP's text-image alignment property to propose LAID, a method that transfers a learned orthogonal decomposition matrix from the text feature space to the image feature space, enabling separate measurement of semantic and covariate shifts. It departs from prior OOD detection benchmarks (OpenOOD, NINCO, ImageNet-OOD) that rely on binary ID/OOD labels, which the authors argue creates a Sorites Paradox for marginal samples whose semantic content closely overlaps with the ID data. The benchmark also extends the covariate-shift analysis of Full-Spectrum OOD Detection [12] by quantifying covariate shifts at fine-grained levels and studying their interaction with semantic shifts, while the Syn-IS dataset leverages Stable Diffusion XL to overcome the limited style diversity in ImageNet-21K.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reframes OOD evaluation from a binary classification problem to a continuous shift-degree analysis, providing a more principled way to assess how sensitive detection methods are to semantic versus covariate changes. The finding that most methods' performance is dominated by semantic shift while being relatively robust to moderate covariate shifts gives practitioners clearer guidance on where OOD detection methods actually work. However, the reliance on CLIP's imperfect text-image alignment limits the precision of the shift measurements, and the evaluation is restricted to ResNet-50 on ImageNet, leaving open whether the insights generalize to other architectures and domains. The observation that extreme covariate shifts (e.g., papercut style) can themselves trigger OOD detection raises important questions about whether current methods are detecting semantic novelty or merely surface-level distributional anomalies.
