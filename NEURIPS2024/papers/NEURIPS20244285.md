## Problem

Standard differential privacy calibration first selects a noise scale to satisfy an (ε,δ) budget, then translates that budget into operational attack risk (e.g., membership inference accuracy or sensitivity/specificity). This two-step procedure yields overly conservative noise requirements because the (ε,δ)→risk mapping is loose, unnecessarily degrading model utility. The paper asks whether one can bypass the intermediate ε parameter entirely and calibrate noise directly to a target attack risk level.

## Input / Output

The method takes a parameterized DP mechanism (e.g., DP-SGD with fixed subsampling rate p and iteration count T, parameterized by noise scale σ), a target risk threshold (maximum attack advantage η*, or minimum FNR β* within an FPR region [0, α*]), and discrete privacy loss random variables (X, Y) obtained from a dominating pair via the Doroshenko et al. discretization. It outputs the minimal noise scale ω* satisfying the risk constraint, found via binary search, along with the full f-DP trade-off curve f(α) computed in ~500 ms per evaluation. The PLRVs are finitely supported over an evenly spaced grid with user-defined discretization Δ (default 10⁻⁴), and the composed PLRVs for T-step DP-SGD are obtained via FFT-based convolution.

## Method

The core technical contribution is Theorem 3.3, which shows that for a discrete-valued dominating pair (P,Q) with associated PLRVs (X,Y), the attack advantage is tightly bounded by Pr[Y>0] − Pr[X>0], and the trade-off curve at any FPR level α is given by β*(τ,γ) = Pr[Y≤τ] − γ·Pr[Y=τ], where τ is the (1−α)-quantile of X and γ is a coin-flip probability that handles the discrete mass at the threshold. This extends the tight privacy analysis of Doroshenko et al. (2022) from (ε,δ) profiles to full f-DP trade-off curves, enabling direct binary-search calibration to operational risk without the lossy (ε,δ)→risk conversion. The paper further identifies a pitfall of advantage calibration: it can increase attack sensitivity by up to ~30 percentage points in the privacy-critical low-FPR regime (Proposition 3.3), and proposes FPR/FNR calibration (Eq. 18) as a safer alternative that explicitly controls risk at a chosen low FPR α* ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}. Empirically, direct FPR/FNR calibration reduces the required noise scale by up to 2× compared to standard calibration, translating to an 18.3 p.p. accuracy gain for GPT-2 fine-tuning on SST-2 at the same privacy risk level.

## Results

Direct FPR/FNR calibration reduces the required DP-SGD noise scale by up to 2× compared to standard (ε,δ) calibration at the same operational risk level, translating to an 18.3 percentage-point gain in GPT-2 classification accuracy on SST-2 at the same attack sensitivity. For advantage calibration, the noise reduction is even larger (up to 3.5×), but comes with a pitfall: it can increase attack true-positive rate by ~30 p.p. in the low-FPR regime (α=0.1) relative to standard calibration. On CIFAR-10 with a CNN trained via DP-SGD, FPR/FNR calibration similarly improves test accuracy at matched privacy risk across all three FPR levels (α* ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its direct attack-risk calibration against the standard two-step procedure: first calibrate noise to a target (ε*, δ*)-DP guarantee, then convert (ε*, δ*) to operational risk using the Kairouz et al. (2015) advantage bound η ≤ (e^ε−1+2δ)/(e^ε+1) or the Dong et al. (2022) (ε,δ)→f-DP conversion formula. On the noise-scale metric, the proposed method wins decisively: advantage calibration reduces σ by up to 3.5×, and FPR/FNR calibration reduces σ by up to 2×, both at the same target risk level. On the utility metric (task accuracy), the method wins by 18.3 p.p. on SST-2 (GPT-2) and shows consistent gains on CIFAR-10. However, the paper identifies a critical caveat: advantage calibration loses to standard calibration on the low-FPR attack sensitivity metric, where it can inflate attack TPR by ~30 p.p. at α=0.1 (Proposition 3.3). The FPR/FNR calibration approach avoids this drawback entirely, making it the recommended method. Figure 4 further shows that the standard (ε,δ)→f conversion (via Dong et al.'s formula) drastically overestimates attack risk compared to the paper's direct PLRV-based trade-off curve estimation, explaining the source of the unnecessary noise.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the f-DP (Gaussian DP) framework of Dong et al. (2022) and the tight discrete PLRV accounting of Doroshenko et al. (2022), extending them from computing (ε,δ) privacy profiles to directly computing the full trade-off curve T(P,Q)(α) via the Neyman-Pearson lemma. It departs from the standard calibration pipeline—choose ε, calibrate noise to (ε,δ), then map to risk—which is used in prior work on communicating DP guarantees (Nanayakkara et al. 2023, Franzen et al. 2022, Wood et al. 2018) and in economic approaches to choosing ε (Abowd & Schmutte 2015, Hsu et al. 2014). The paper also contrasts with variants of DP that directly bound MIA advantage (Cherubin et al. 2024, Ghazi & Issa 2023, Mahloujifar et al. 2022), showing that calibrating to advantage alone has a hidden cost in the low-FPR regime.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is substantial: up to 2× noise reduction and an 18 p.p. accuracy gain for GPT-2 at the same operational risk level, making DP-SGD training meaningfully more useful without sacrificing privacy. The conceptual warning that advantage calibration can silently increase attack sensitivity in the privacy-critical low-FPR regime (Proposition 3.3) challenges a natural design choice and redirects practitioners toward FPR/FNR calibration. Open questions include how to choose target α* and β* in a way compatible with data-protection regulation, and whether the approach extends to pathological mechanisms with catastrophic failures (where T(0)<1). The methods also apply to privacy auditing and relaxed threat models, broadening their utility beyond calibration.
