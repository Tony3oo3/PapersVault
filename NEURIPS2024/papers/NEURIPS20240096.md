## Problem

Learning rate warmup is a widely used heuristic in GPT training, yet the specific reasons early updates are too large—and by which criteria—remain poorly understood. This paper asks: what makes early updates 'too large,' and can optimizer modifications that explicitly normalize update size based on these criteria reduce or eliminate the need for warmup?

## Input / Output

The modified optimizers (LionA, LionAR) consume mini-batch gradients and current model parameters at each step, producing updated parameters with explicitly controlled update magnitudes. A key unusual aspect is the online estimation of the gradient signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) from micro-batch accumulation, which feeds into a per-layer scaling factor for the relative representation change correction. The angular update control in LionAR additionally requires tracking per-neuron weight magnitudes and applying norm-preserving projections after each update, replacing standard weight decay.

## Method

The paper identifies three distinct reasons early updates are too large and proposes corresponding optimizer modifications: (1) removing the β₁ bias correction in AdamW (or introducing an inverse bias correction in Lion) to prevent artificially amplified initial ℓ₂-norms; (2) LionAR, which scales matrix weight updates proportionally to their current magnitude and projects them back to a fixed norm, thereby controlling angular updates and making the optimizer invariant to initialization scale; and (3) an RRC correction factor derived from a theoretical analysis of how gradient signal-to-noise ratio amplifies changes in internal neural representations, which acts as an automatic warmup. The key finding is that no single metric suffices: controlling ℓ₂-norm alone (LionA) or angular updates alone (LionAR) reduces but does not eliminate the warmup benefit for GPT2, while combining LionAR with high momentum (β=0.98), Nesterov updates, and the inverse bias correction fully eliminates the need for warmup in the tested settings.

## Results

On GPT2-124M (OpenWebText, batch size 480, 5000 iterations), AdamW without warmup shows a permanent validation loss gap compared to even 5% warmup, which the paper attributes to large initial ℓ₂-norm updates, angular updates, and high gradient signal-to-noise ratio. The proposed LionAR optimizer (controlling angular updates via magnitude-proportional updates) reduces but does not eliminate the warmup benefit. Combining LionAR with high momentum (β=0.98), Nesterov momentum, and an inverse bias correction fully eliminates the warmup advantage, matching or exceeding the best warmup configurations. On ResNet-20/CIFAR-10, scaling initial updates by 128× drops accuracy from 92.13% to 88.22% with standard ReLU, but only to 91.82% with Leaky ReLU, confirming that non-linearity damage (dead ReLUs) mediates the degradation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against AdamW (the primary baseline), LionA (which perfectly controls the ℓ₂ update norm) still shows a significant no-warmup penalty, indicating that ℓ₂-norm alone is insufficient to explain warmup's benefit. LionAR (which additionally controls angular updates via magnitude-proportional matrix updates) narrows the gap substantially but a residual warmup advantage remains, pointing to the role of gradient signal-to-noise ratio and representation changes. Against RAdamW, which was previously proposed to reduce warmup needs, the paper shows RAdamW outperforms AdamW without warmup but still cannot match longer warmup schedules (Appendix C.1), consistent with its built-in warmup being too short (~40 steps vs. 100+ needed). The paper's key finding is that no single optimizer modification fully replaces warmup for GPT2, but the combination of angular update control + high momentum + inverse bias correction does, whereas for Llama2-124M, LionAR alone suffices to eliminate the warmup benefit entirely.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the rotational optimizer framework of Kosson et al. [19], which showed that weight decay makes standard optimizers approximate relative optimizers and that controlling angular updates reduces warmup benefit. It extends this by combining angular update control with the Lion optimizer's fixed-magnitude updates (LionA/LionAR) and adds a new analytical lens: the Relative Representation Change (RRC), which links the gradient signal-to-noise ratio to how quickly internal network features shift. The paper departs from curvature-based explanations of warmup (Gilmer et al., Smith et al.) by arguing that for adaptive optimizers the relevant quantity is not local curvature but the alignment of early gradients (high SNR) amplifying representation changes, connecting warmup to the critical batch size literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, multi-layered mechanistic account of why warmup helps—moving beyond the vague 'stabilizes training' narrative to specific optimizer-level causes (bias correction inflating early updates, angular updates exceeding equilibrium, and high initial gradient SNR amplifying representation changes). The practical finding that combining angular update control with high momentum (β=0.98) and inverse bias correction can eliminate warmup for GPT2-124M and Llama2-124M is actionable, though the RRC-based automatic warmup still has acknowledged practical limitations (distorted decay phase, sensitivity to batch size exceeding critical batch size). Key open questions include whether these findings hold at much larger scales (billions of parameters), whether the RRC correction can be made robust enough for production use, and whether the high-momentum recipe generalizes across architectures and training regimes.
