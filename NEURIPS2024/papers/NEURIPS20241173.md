## Problem

Classical methods for solving the linear-quadratic regulator (LQR) problem rely on matrix factorization or inversion subroutines that incur at least O(n³) overhead in the state dimension, making large-scale optimal control intractable. While quantum algorithms exist for linear systems, the nonlinear Algebraic Riccati Equation at the core of LQR has resisted quantum acceleration, and no end-to-end quantum algorithm with provable speedup for linear control had been demonstrated.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes s-sparse system matrices A, B, Q, R accessed via quantum oracles (row/column index and entry-value queries), an initial stabilizing policy K₀ stored in a classical-write, quantum-read qRAM data structure, and hyperparameters (step size σ, robustness θ, iteration count N). It outputs an ε-approximate optimal feedback gain K satisfying ‖K − K*‖_F ≤ ε, with the guarantee holding in the regime m ≪ n where the control dimension is much smaller than the state dimension. The quantum-accessible data structure allows intermediate classical updates to K between quantum gradient evaluations without re-preparing the full matrix state.

## Method

The algorithm is a hybrid quantum-classical policy gradient loop: a quantum differentiable simulator computes robust gradient estimates of the LQR objective f(K), and a classical computer updates K via gradient descent. The quantum simulator is built on a novel quantum Lyapunov equation solver that exploits the integral representation X* = ∫₀^∞ e^{At}Ωe^{Aᵀt}dt, approximated by trapezoidal quadrature and assembled via linear combination of unitaries (LCU) over block-encoded matrix exponentials, achieving polylogarithmic cost in n. The matrix exponential of Hurwitz-stable systems is computed via a quantum linear system solver (QLSS) formulation, yielding a block-encoding of e^{At} in Õ(αρt·polylog(1/ε)) queries. The gradient ∇f(K) = 2(RK − BᵀP(K))X(K) is estimated by block-encoding P(K) and X(K) (both Lyapunov solutions) and combining them, then vectorizing and reading out via amplitude estimation. This yields an end-to-end complexity of Õ(nε^{-1.5}), a super-quadratic speedup over the Õ(n³) classical baseline.

## Results

The proposed quantum policy gradient algorithm achieves an end-to-end complexity of Õ(nε⁻¹·⁵) for computing an ε-approximate optimal feedback gain, compared to Õ(n³) for state-of-the-art classical methods (Schur, Newton-Kleinman), yielding a super-quadratic speedup in the state dimension n. In numerical experiments on a mass-spring-damper system (g=4), the method converges to the ground-truth ARE solution within 750 iterations, versus 2×10⁴ iterations for the classical model-free policy gradient of Mohammadi et al. [45]. When scaling the system size from g=4 to g=64, the proposed method runs nearly 3 orders of magnitude faster than the classical baseline on a classical simulator.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against three classical baselines: the Schur method [35], the Newton-Kleinman method [32], and the model-based policy gradient of Mohammadi et al. [45]. Against the Schur and Newton-Kleinman methods (both Õ(n³)), the proposed quantum algorithm achieves a super-quadratic speedup by reducing the dependence on n from cubic to linear (Õ(nε⁻¹·⁵)), which is the first provable end-to-end quantum advantage for LQR. Against the classical policy gradient [45], the quantum method wins decisively on both convergence speed and runtime: it requires only 750 iterations versus 2×10⁴ (≈27× fewer) to reach the same accuracy on the g=4 benchmark, and runs approximately 3 orders of magnitude faster when scaling to g=64. The advantage stems from the quantum differentiable simulator providing robust, analytically-derived gradient estimates (via the Lyapunov equation solver) rather than the noisy stochastic approximations used by the classical zeroth-order estimators in [45].

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the classical policy gradient method for LQR (Mohammadi et al. [45]) and quantum linear system solvers for differential equations (Krovi [33]), but departs from both in a key way: rather than directly solving the nonlinear Algebraic Riccati Equation (which resists quantum acceleration), it reframes LQR as a policy gradient problem where each gradient step only requires solving linear Lyapunov equations. The quantum Lyapunov solver is constructed via integral representation, block-encoding, and linear combination of unitaries, drawing on the QSVT framework [22] and differentiable quantum simulation ideas [37]. The hybrid quantum-classical architecture (quantum simulator for gradients, classical computer for policy updates) is positioned as a practical design for the early fault-tolerant era, distinguishing it from prior quantum RL work that relies on strong quantum access models without end-to-end cost analysis.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first end-to-end quantum algorithm for a fundamental optimal control problem with a provable super-quadratic speedup (Õ(nε⁻¹·⁵) vs. Õ(n³) for classical methods), and the quantum Lyapunov solver it introduces is a reusable subroutine with exponential speedup over classical approaches. However, the speedup is purely theoretical—no quantum hardware results are presented—and the algorithm requires the regime m ≪ n plus sparse-access quantum oracles, limiting near-term applicability. Open questions include extending the approach to nonlinear or non-quadratic control problems, handling special cases where the PL condition may fail, and reducing the overhead of the quantum-accessible data structure in practice.
