## Problem

The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) framework is the standard tool for analyzing overparameterized PINN training dynamics, but its key properties—deterministic kernel at initialization, constancy during training, and vanishing Hessian—have only been established for linear PDEs. This paper shows that all three properties fail in the nonlinear regime even in the infinite-width limit, leaving a theoretical gap in understanding why nonlinear PINNs train slowly and unreliably, and motivating a principled shift toward second-order optimization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a PDE specified by a nonlinear differential operator R acting on the derivative vector Φ[u] (up to order k), boundary/initial conditions g, and a set of collocation points sampled via Latin hypercube sampling in the domain Ω. The neural network is a fully-connected architecture (single hidden layer for theory; 5 hidden layers × 20 neurons with tanh activation for experiments) trained via a discrete loss combining PDE residuals and boundary violations. The output is the approximate PDE solution u_θ evaluated at test points, with performance measured by relative L² loss. A key structural detail is that the NTK decomposes as K = Λ_R · K_Φ · Λ_R^T, where K_Φ (the linear derivative kernel) converges to a deterministic limit but Λ_R (encoding ∇R evaluated at the network output) remains stochastic, making the full kernel a random matrix for nonlinear PDEs.

## Method

The paper's core theoretical contribution is a three-part decomposition of NTK behavior for nonlinear PDEs: (1) the kernel at initialization converges only in distribution (not in probability) to a random limit because Λ_R depends on the Gaussian-process limit of the network output; (2) the kernel is provably dynamic during training even as m→∞, since Λ_R(t) evolves toward ∇R(Φ[u*]) while K_Φ stays nearly constant; and (3) the Hessian of the residuals H_r has a non-vanishing spectral norm bounded below by a constant depending on ∇²R, preventing convergence to a linear regime. To exploit these insights, the paper employs the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm (a trust-region variant of Gauss-Newton with geodesic acceleration) as the second-order optimizer. The key convergence result (Theorem 4.2) shows that under the Gauss-Newton flow, the training dynamics are governed by a matrix UDU^T with eigenvalues 0 or 1 rather than the NTK eigenvalues, making the method nearly spectrally unbiased and decoupling convergence from the slow-decaying NTK spectrum that plagues first-order methods. Numerically, LM achieves ~10⁻⁵ relative L² loss on Burgers' equation in far fewer iterations than Adam (~10⁻³) and handles high-convection and Navier-Stokes cases where Adam fails even with curriculum training or causality training.

## Results

The paper's headline quantitative finding is that on Burgers' equation, LM achieves a relative L² loss of ~10⁻⁵ in a few thousand iterations, while Adam and L-BFGS plateau at ~10⁻³. On the convection equation, LM without any enhancement reaches high accuracy at convection coefficient β=100, whereas Adam with curriculum training only manages β up to 20. On the Poisson equation with high-frequency components, LM significantly outperforms Adam+RFF, and combining LM with RFF yields remarkable loss reduction from the very first iterations. On Navier-Stokes (2D cylinder wake, Re=100), LM with causality training produces a reasonable solution in relative L² loss over PDE time, while Adam struggles.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LM consistently outperforms Adam [18] across all five PDE test cases (Poisson, Wave, Convection, Burgers', Navier-Stokes), achieving 2 orders of magnitude lower L² loss on Burgers' (~10⁻⁵ vs ~10⁻³) and handling convection coefficients 5× higher without auxiliary enhancements. L-BFGS [24] occupies an intermediate position between Adam and LM, which the paper attributes to its Hessian approximation converging slowly due to the poorly conditioned Hessian in PINNs (Remark 4.3). On the Poisson equation, LM alone surpasses Adam combined with Random Fourier Features [39], and LM+RFF further accelerates convergence from the first iterations. For the convection equation, LM without curriculum training [19] achieves accuracy at β=100, a regime where Adam+CT fails (limited to β≤20 per [19]). On Navier-Stokes, both Adam and LM are augmented with causality training [41] for a fair comparison, and LM still yields a more accurate solution over PDE time.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the NTK-based analysis of PINNs from the linear PDE setting (Wang et al. 2022 [40], Liu et al. 2020 [22]) to the nonlinear regime, where the standard NTK assumptions (deterministic kernel at initialization, constancy during training, vanishing Hessian in infinite width) all fail. It builds on the general NTK framework of Jacot et al. [15] and the Gram matrix analysis of Du et al. [4,5], but identifies a fundamental structural difference: the nonlinear operator R introduces a stochastic, dynamic component (Λ_R) that prevents convergence to the linearized regime. The practical recommendation to use second-order methods (specifically Levenberg-Marquardt) connects to the broader quasi-Newton optimization literature [27] and to Müller & Zeinhofer [26], who independently advocated second-order approaches for PINNs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges a widely-used analytical tool (NTK) in the PINN community by showing it is fundamentally inapplicable to nonlinear PDEs even in the idealized infinite-width limit, which redirects how researchers should reason about training dynamics for the most practically important class of PDEs. The practical recommendation to adopt second-order methods like LM is simple and actionable, with demonstrated benefits across Poisson, wave, convection, Burgers, and Navier-Stokes equations. Key open questions include: whether the full-rankness of J(t) can be established theoretically for nonlinear PDEs, how second-order methods scale to very large networks (the paper acknowledges this as the major limitation), and whether the convergence guarantees of Theorem 4.2 extend to inexact quasi-Newton methods like L-BFGS in the poorly-conditioned PINN setting.
