## Problem

Influence-based greedy heuristics (e.g., ZAMinfluence) are the dominant approach for identifying the most influential subset of training samples, yet they lack provable guarantees and can fail even in simple linear regression. This paper identifies two fundamental failure modes: the influence function's inability to account for leverage scores (causing under-estimation for high-leverage samples), and the violation of the additivity assumption due to non-additive collective effects (amplification and cancellation). The paper further shows that an adaptive variant of these heuristics can provably address the cancellation failure.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a training set of n samples with d-dimensional features and real-valued labels, a differentiable target function φ (e.g., prediction on a held-out test point or log-odds for classification), and a budget k specifying the maximum subset size. It returns a subset S of at most k training indices whose removal maximizes the actual effect A_{-S} = φ(θ̂_{-S}) − φ(θ̂). For non-linear models where the optimal parameters are non-unique, the actual effect is approximated by averaging over an ensemble of 5 independently trained models, and influence scores are similarly averaged before selection.

## Method

The paper derives a closed-form expression for the group effect in OLS via the Woodbury matrix identity, revealing that the exact group effect involves the inverse (I_k − X_S N^{-1} X_S^T)^{-1}, whose Neumann series expansion corresponds one-to-one with the Taylor series of the upweighted objective. The first-order truncation (used by ZAMinfluence) ignores leverage scores entirely, while the diagonal approximation (LAGS) still misses cross-leverage interactions. The authors prove that amplification (super-additivity from high cross-leverage among similar samples) and cancellation (sub-additivity from opposing residuals) cause LAGS to fail in c-MISS and 2-MISS respectively. The adaptive greedy algorithm, which refits the model and recomputes individual effects after each selection, is shown to satisfy sign consistency and order preservation properties that guarantee correct 2-MISS solutions under cancellation. Experiments on Concrete Compressive Strength (regression), Waveform (logistic regression), and MNIST (2-layer MLP with EK-FAC approximation) confirm that adaptivity consistently outperforms the vanilla greedy baseline across all k values.

## Results

The paper evaluates the adaptive greedy algorithm against the vanilla greedy algorithm (LAGS for regression, ZAMinfluence for classification) on three datasets: Concrete Compressive Strength (linear regression), Waveform Database Generator (logistic regression), and MNIST (2-layer MLP). The adaptive greedy algorithm surpasses the vanilla counterpart across all scenarios, all subset sizes k, and both evaluation metrics (averaged actual effect $\overline{A_{-S}}$ and winning rate). As k increases, the averaged actual effect grows for both methods, but the gap between adaptive and vanilla greedy widens, particularly in the MLP setting where non-additive interactions are most pronounced.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between the adaptive greedy algorithm and the vanilla (one-pass) greedy algorithm, which uses ZAMinfluence [Broderick et al., 2020] as the scoring function for classification and LAGS (leverage-adjusted greedy selection) for regression. The adaptive greedy algorithm wins on all three datasets—Concrete Compressive Strength, Waveform Database Generator, and MNIST—across every value of k under both the averaged actual effect and winning rate metrics. The advantage is most visible at larger k values, where the non-additive structure of collective influence (amplification and cancellation) becomes more consequential. On the MLP/MNIST experiment, the adaptive greedy consistently outperforms the vanilla greedy across multiple random seeds (0, 22, 42, 62, 82), confirming robustness to training randomness. The paper also theoretically proves that the vanilla greedy (LAGS) provably fails in 1-MISS, 2-MISS, and c-MISS under specific data generation processes, while the adaptive greedy provably solves 2-MISS in the cancellation regime (Theorem 4.2).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the influence function framework of Koh and Liang (2017) and the ZAMinfluence algorithm of Broderick et al. (2020), which extend individual-sample influence to group-level subset selection. It also builds on the adaptive greedy heuristic of Kuschnig et al. (2021), which iteratively refits the model after each selection. The paper's key departure is providing the first systematic negative analysis of these dominant heuristics: it proves that influence-based greedy methods fail in MISS even in ordinary least squares, identifying two distinct failure modes (leverage-score underestimation and non-additive group effects via amplification/cancellation) that prior work had not formally characterized.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The results challenge the widespread reliance on additive influence scores for group-level data attribution, with direct implications for the Linear Datamodeling Score (LDS) benchmark that assumes additivity. The adaptive greedy algorithm offers a practical remedy for cancellation but provably fails under amplification, and the theoretical guarantees are restricted to 2-MISS in linear regression, leaving the general k-MISS case open. The paper highlights an inherent performance-efficiency trade-off: capturing higher-order interactions (e.g., via second-order approximations) improves accuracy but increases computational cost, a tension that will shape future algorithmic design in data attribution.
