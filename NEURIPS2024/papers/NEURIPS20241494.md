## Problem

Unsupervised learning (UL)-based solvers for combinatorial optimization, such as the PI-GNN solver, suffer from two practical failures: they are easily trapped at trivial local optima (e.g., the all-zero plateau p=0_N) on complex problems like MIS on random regular graphs with degree d>16, and they require fragile artificial rounding from continuous relaxed variables back to the discrete solution space. Prior work (Angelini & Ricci-Tersenghi 2023, Wang & Li 2023) demonstrated that PI-GNN cannot match even simple greedy algorithms on these hard instances without access to training datasets or greedy initializations, raising questions about the practical applicability of UL-based solvers.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected graph G(V,E) with randomly initialized H₀-dimensional node embedding vectors (H₀ = int(N^0.8)) as input and produces a discrete binary solution x ∈ {0,1}^N (e.g., an independent set indicator, cut partition, or matching assignment). A key property is that the CRA annealing process drives the soft solution to exact 0 or 1 values within 32-bit floating-point precision by the time early stopping triggers, making the final threshold-based projection (at 0.5) robust and effectively eliminating the need for any ad-hoc rounding heuristic. The GNN architecture is a two-layer GCN or GraphSAGE with a sigmoid output layer, trained per-instance without any labeled data or historical instances.

## Method

CRA introduces a penalty term γΦ(p) = γΣᵢ(1−(2pᵢ−1)^α) added to the standard relaxed CO loss, where γ is a scheduling parameter and α is an even integer controlling curvature. When γ < 0, the convex penalty smooths the non-convexity of the objective and eliminates the trivial stationary point p=0_N, enabling active exploration; when γ > 0, the concave penalty forces each relaxed variable toward 0 or 1, automatically rounding the solution. The annealing schedule linearly increases γ from a small negative initial value (e.g., γ(0)=−20) by a small rate ε=10⁻³ per epoch, with early stopping when Φ(θ,C)≈0, so the final solution is already discrete. Theorem 3.1 guarantees that as γ→+∞ the relaxed solution converges to the original discrete optimum, and as γ→−∞ the loss becomes convex with a unique minimizer at p=1_N/2. Applied to the PI-GNN solver (CRA-PI-GNN), this single penalty mechanism simultaneously resolves both the local-optima entrapment and the rounding ambiguity, outperforming PI-GNN, greedy algorithms (DGA, RGA), and SDP-based methods on MIS, MaxCut, and diverse bipartite matching benchmarks without any training dataset.

## Results

On the MIS problem over 10,000-node random regular graphs, CRA-PI-GNN (GraphSAGE) achieves an approximation ratio (ApR) of 0.963 on 20-RRG and 0.924 on 100-RRG, dramatically outperforming the original PI-GNN solver (0.745 and 0.000, respectively) and surpassing the degree-based greedy algorithm (DGA: 0.891 and 0.848). On the MaxCut Gset benchmark (7 instances, 800–10,000 nodes), CRA-PI-GNN achieves ApR values of 0.988–1.000, consistently exceeding PI-GNN (0.912–0.990), RUN-CSP (0.950–0.982), and the Goemans–Williamson SDP relaxation (0.77–1.000). CRA also accelerates convergence, reaching better solutions in fewer epochs than PI-GNN across all tested degrees (d = 3, 5, 20, 100).

## Baselines & Comparisons

CRA-PI-GNN decisively outperforms the PI-GNN solver [Schuetz et al., 2022a], its direct baseline: on 100-RRG MIS, PI-GNN (both GCV and SAGE) collapses to ApR 0.000 (trapped at the trivial local optimum p=0), while CRA(SAGE) recovers to 0.924. Against the degree-based greedy algorithm (DGA) [Angelini & Ricci-Tersenghi, 2019], CRA(SAGE) wins by 7.2 points on 20-RRG (0.963 vs 0.891) and 7.6 points on 100-RRG (0.924 vs 0.848), directly refuting the claim that UL-based solvers cannot match greedy heuristics on hard RRGs. CRA also surpasses the random greedy algorithm (RGA) by 18.7 points on 20-RRG and 26.1 points on 100-RRG. Among data-driven baselines, CRA(SAGE) (0.963) outperforms both EGN [Karalias & Loukas, 2020] (0.775) and META-EGN [Wang & Li, 2023] (0.887) on 20-RRG, and does so without any training dataset or historical instances. On MaxCut Gset instances, CRA-PI-GNN beats the Goemans–Williamson SDP relaxation [Goemans & Williamson, 1995] on 5 of 7 graphs (e.g., 0.994 vs 0.970 on G14) and RUN-CSP [Toenshoff et al., 2019] on all 7 (e.g., 0.998 vs 0.975 on G22). In the appendix comparison against sampling-based solvers iSCO [Sun et al., 2023] and DIMES [Qiu et al., 2022] on large ERG instances (9,000–11,000 nodes), CRA (ApR 0.963) is slightly below iSCO (0.990–1.008) but substantially above DIMES (0.841–0.873), with the gap attributed to CRA's per-instance optimization overhead on smaller graphs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the PI-GNN solver (Schuetz et al., 2022a), which trains a GNN to output continuous relaxed variables for a single CO instance by directly minimizing a differentiable objective. The key departure is the introduction of a curvature-controlled penalty term Φ(p) with an annealing schedule on γ, which simultaneously smooths the non-convex landscape (when γ<0) and enforces discreteness (when γ>0), addressing both the local-optima trap and the fragile post-hoc rounding that prior UL-based solvers require. Conceptually, this draws on simulated annealing and prior annealing strategies for multi-instance UL solvers (Sun et al., 2022; Sanokowski et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2023), but adapts them to the single-instance setting where no training dataset is available. The paper directly responds to criticisms from Angelini and Ricci-Tersenghi (2023) and Wang and Li (2023), who showed PI-GNN underperforms greedy algorithms on hard RRG instances.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By demonstrating that a simple penalty term with annealing can overcome the local-optima bottleneck that was previously considered a fundamental limitation of single-instance UL-based solvers, this work makes the PI-GNN paradigm more practical for hard, dense CO problems where greedy methods degrade. However, the approach introduces two additional hyperparameters (γ(0) and ε) whose sensitivity is acknowledged but not fully resolved, and the per-instance training cost (~N^1.4–N^1.7 scaling) limits its advantage over specialized solvers like iSCO on small instances. Open questions include whether the annealing strategy generalizes to non-graph CO problems beyond the bipartite matching test, whether the asymptotic guarantees of Theorem 3.1 hold in practice at finite γ, and whether the GNN architecture choice (GraphSAGE vs. GCN) interacts with the annealing schedule in ways that could be exploited for further gains.
