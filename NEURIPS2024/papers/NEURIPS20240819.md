## Problem

Open-source medical LLMs significantly lag behind proprietary models like GPT-4 and MedPaLM 2, and preference learning techniques (DPO, KTO, RLHF) remain under-explored in biomedicine due to the scarcity of high-quality, extensively annotated preference datasets. This paper addresses that gap from a data-centric perspective, asking whether purpose-built medical instruction and preference collections can close the open-source-to-proprietary performance gap while preserving general capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240819/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes ~410K medical instructions spanning three task types (multiple-choice exam questions, PubMed literature QA, and open-ended clinical/research prompts) plus ~190K general-domain instructions for SFT, and ~100K instructions each paired with eight model completions for preference annotation. For preference learning, each instruction yields one chosen and one rejected completion (binarized per the Zephyr protocol), producing ~1.8M pairs for reward modeling. The trained LMs output chain-of-thought medical answers, while the reward model outputs scalar scores used for Best-of-N re-ranking and iterative preference optimization.

## Method

The core contribution is a four-stage training pipeline on Llama-3 8B/70B: (1) SFT on a mixture of 410K medical and 190K general instructions (UltraMix), (2) offline preference optimization via DPO or KTO on ~100K binarized medical + 75K general preference pairs, (3) reward model training on UltraMedical preferences augmented with UltraFeedback/UltraSafety/UltraInteract, and (4) iterative online preference learning where the reward model annotates self-generated completions (temperature 0.8, 5 candidates per instruction) to produce on-policy chosen/rejected pairs for further DPO/KTO rounds. A key empirical finding is that mixing medical and general domain data (UltraMix) consistently outperforms medical-only training (UltraMed), demonstrating that specialized generalists require both domain expertise and broad instruction-following. The 70B model achieves 86.5 on MedQA-USMLE, the highest among open-source LLMs, while the 8B model outperforms MedPaLM 1, GPT-3.5, and Meditron-70B on average medical benchmark scores.

## Results

The UltraMedical 70B model (UltraMix Best Ens) achieves an average score of 86.49 across medical benchmarks (MedQA, MedMCQA, PubMedQA, and six MMLU medical subcategories), surpassing OpenBioLLM-70B (86.06) and Llama-3-70B-Instruct (82.66), and approaching GPT-4 with 0-shot CoT (85.36) and Med-PaLM 2 (85.46). The 8B model (UltraMix Best Ens) reaches 78.32 average, outperforming all other ~7B open-source medical models including OpenBioLLM-8B (72.48) and Llama-3-8B-Instruct (68.56). On MedQA-USMLE specifically, the 70B model scores 85.4, the highest among open-source LLMs. The UltraMedical reward model (8B) achieves 80.53 average on the Medical RewardBench and 85.53 on RewardBench, competitive with larger general-domain reward models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The UltraMedical 70B model (86.49 avg) outperforms all open-source baselines including OpenBioLLM-70B (86.06), Llama-3-70B-Instruct (82.66), Mixtral-8x22B (79.16), DeepSeek-v2-Chat (77.90), and Med42-70B (70.74), while matching or exceeding GPT-4 0-shot CoT (85.36) and Med-PaLM 2 (85.46) on average. The 8B model (78.32 avg) decisively beats all ~7B open-source medical models: OpenBioLLM-8B (72.48, +5.8), Llama-3-8B-Instruct (68.56, +9.8), Internist-7B (67.79, +10.5), and Meerkat-7B (63.94, +14.4). On MedQA specifically, the 70B model scores 85.4 vs. GPT-4 0-shot CoT at 85.8 (−0.4) and Med-PaLM 2 at 85.4 (tie), but GPT-4 with MedPrompt (90.2) remains substantially ahead. The UltraMedical 8B reward model achieves 80.53 on Medical RewardBench, outperforming PairRM-LLaMA3-8B (78.48) and UltraRM-13b (75.08), while on general RewardBench it scores 85.53, slightly below PairRM (87.13) but above Eurus-RM-7B (83.01). The paper demonstrates that mixing medical and general domain data (UltraMix) consistently improves over medical-only data (UltraMed) by 0.3–1.3 points on average, confirming the specialized-generalist hypothesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the preference learning pipeline (DPO, KTO) established for general-domain LLMs into the biomedical setting, where high-quality preference-annotated data has been largely absent. It builds on the EvolInstruct self-evolution strategy from WizardLM for instruction complexity, the Zephyr binarization protocol for constructing chosen/rejected pairs, and the iterative DPO/KTO framework from RLHF workflow literature, applying them systematically to medical instructions. The 'specialized generalist' framing departs from prior medical LLM work (MedPaLM, Meditron, OpenBioLLM) that focused primarily on SFT or continued pre-training, by explicitly integrating offline and online preference optimization with a domain-specific reward model to preserve general capabilities alongside medical expertise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The UltraMedical collection (410K instructions, 100K preference-annotated pairs, 1.8M reward pairs) is the first large-scale, preference-annotated biomedical dataset released to the community, enabling iterative preference learning and reward modeling in a domain where these techniques were previously infeasible due to data scarcity. The 8B model outperforming MedPaLM 1, GPT-3.5, and Meditron-70B on average medical benchmarks demonstrates that data-centric alignment can close the open-source/proprietary gap without massive compute. Key open questions remain: GPT-4 annotation bias in the medical domain, the limited number of iterative preference learning rounds (only one due to compute), the persistent trade-off between medical and general performance, and whether reward models can scale effectively for medical re-ranking of large candidate sets.
