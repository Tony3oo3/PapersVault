## Problem

Randomized controlled trials sample individuals who may differ systematically from the target population, undermining the external validity of policy evaluations. Existing methods like inverse probability of sampling weighting (IPSW) estimate only the expected loss and can introduce uncontrolled bias when the sampling model is miscalibrated, with no finite-sample guarantee of validity. This paper addresses the gap of certifying valid inferences about the full out-of-sample loss distribution—not just its mean—under any user-specified degree of sampling model miscalibration.

## Input / Output

The method consumes trial data (X, A, L) from an RCT, covariate-only samples X from the target population, a fitted sampling model p̂(S|X) (e.g., logistic or XGBoost), a policy π specifying p_π(A|X), and a miscalibration degree Γ ≥ 1. It produces limit curves mapping each miscoverage probability α to a loss bound ℓ_α^Γ, such that P(L_{n+1} ≤ ℓ_α^Γ | S=0) ≥ 1−α is certified for all α. The output is a full distributional bound rather than a point estimate, and the informativeness of the curve (the fraction of the target population for which a non-trivial bound below L_max is obtained) decreases as Γ increases.

## Method

The core construction bounds the true distribution-shift ratio between target and trial populations using Γ-scaled nominal selection odds, yielding lower and upper weight bounds W̲^Γ and W̄^Γ for each sample. Trial data is randomly split: D′ is used to compute a finite-sample upper bound w̄_β^Γ on the weight of the future sample (via order statistics), while D'' is used to build a weighted empirical CDF that uses W̲^Γ for losses below the candidate threshold and W̄^Γ for losses above it, plus the future sample's weight w̄_β^Γ in the denominator. The limit ℓ_{α,β}^Γ is the smallest ℓ where this weighted CDF reaches (1−α)/(1−β), and the final limit minimizes over β ∈ (0,α) to yield the tightest valid bound. The proof combines conformal prediction under covariate shift (Tibshirani et al., 2019) with a marginalization argument from Jin et al. (2023), showing that the Γ-bounded weights guarantee coverage 1−α for any odds miscalibration up to degree Γ, without distributional assumptions. A benchmarking procedure omits observed selection factors to calibrate credible Γ values, and a reliability diagram assesses model calibration when no unmeasured factors are present.

## Results

The proposed method certifies valid finite-sample limit curves (ℓ_α^Γ) on out-of-sample loss, with informativeness (fraction of the target population receiving a non-trivial bound) staying above 90% for miscalibration degrees Γ∈[1,2] across all synthetic populations. On the NHANES seafood consumption data, the method certifies a 90% probability that out-of-sample blood mercury levels fall below the 8 μg/L reference limit under a low-consumption policy, for Γ∈[1,2]. The IPSW-based quantile benchmark and the proposed method at Γ=1 both yield negative miscoverage gaps (i.e., invalid inferences), while increasing Γ to 2 produces positive miscoverage gaps (valid but conservative), confirming the need for the miscalibration margin.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against a single baseline: an IPSW-based quantile estimator (Eq. 14), adapted from Huang et al. (2021) to the trial-plus-observational setting. Across all four synthetic target populations (A–D), the IPSW benchmark produces negative miscoverage gaps, meaning it is invalid as a certified bound on the out-of-sample loss. The proposed method at Γ=1 (perfectly calibrated model assumption) is also invalid, but at Γ=2 it achieves positive miscoverage gaps, i.e., valid inferences, while retaining >90% informativeness. On the NHANES real-data experiment, the IPSW expected-loss estimate suggests the 'treat all' (high seafood) policy is preferable (V_IPSW^π₁ = 1.54 vs. V_IPSW^π₀ = 1.64), but the proposed limit curves reveal that tail losses under the 'treat none' policy are actually lower, illustrating the cautionary principle that the method is designed to enforce. The XGBoost-based sampling model yields slightly tighter (less conservative) certified limits than the logistic model, particularly for populations B, C, and D where the logistic model is misspecified.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the conformal prediction framework (Vovk et al., 2005; Tibshirani et al., 2019) to the specific problem of generalizing RCT-based policy evaluations to a target population, building directly on the authors' prior work on off-policy evaluation with out-of-sample guarantees (Ek et al., 2023). It departs from standard inverse probability of sampling weighting (IPSW), which estimates only the expected loss and is sensitive to model misspecification, by constructing limit curves that upper-bound the full out-of-sample loss distribution with finite-sample validity. The sensitivity analysis machinery from Jin et al. (2023) and the benchmarking approach from Huang (2024) are adapted to handle miscalibrated sampling models through a bounded odds-ratio parameter Γ, rather than assuming correct specification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

For safety-critical applications like clinical decision support, the ability to certify that a policy's worst-case loss stays below a threshold—with a quantified tolerance for model miscalibration—addresses a real gap left by point-estimate methods like IPSW that can silently produce invalid inferences. The benchmarking procedure for selecting credible Γ values from observed covariates makes the method practically usable without requiring knowledge of unmeasured selection factors. Key limitations include the independence assumption (ruling out clustered or epidemic data), the trade-off between informativeness and robustness as Γ increases, and the requirement for a well-performing sampling model to keep Γ small. Open questions include extension to continuous action spaces, dependent samples, and computational scaling to very large trial datasets.
