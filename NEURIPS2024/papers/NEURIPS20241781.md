## Problem

The relationship between counterfactual fairness (CF) and group fairness (GF) in image classification remained unexplored because true counterfactual images—where only the sensitive attribute is altered while all other attributes remain unchanged—are impossible to collect in practice. Previous theoretical results on tabular datasets suggested CF implies GF, but it was unclear whether this holds for images, where sensitive attributes (e.g., sex) manifest as visual characteristics that can be influenced by other latent attributes. This paper constructs the first real-image CF benchmarks and demonstrates that CF does not imply GF for image classifiers, identifying the root cause as a latent attribute correlated with but not caused by the sensitive attribute.

## Input / Output

The classifier takes a single RGB facial image as input and outputs a binary class label (e.g., 'blond hair' on CelebA, 'smiling' on LFW). For CF evaluation, each original test image is paired with a counterfactual image generated by InstructPix2Pix that alters sex-related visual characteristics (facial hair, jawline, brow ridge, etc.) while preserving identity and non-sensitive attributes; these pairs form the CelebA-CF (230 pairs) and LFW-CF (144 pairs) benchmarks. GF metrics (DEO) are computed on the original CelebA/LFW test sets, while CF metrics (CD) are computed on the CF benchmarks, enabling simultaneous evaluation of both notions.

## Method

The paper proposes Counterfactual Knowledge Distillation (CKD), which combines a knowledge distillation term with the counterfactual pairing (CP) regularization to train a student model that achieves both CF and GF. The key insight is that CF-aware training (e.g., CP) forces the model to rely more heavily on a latent attribute G (e.g., hair length) that is correlated with but not caused by the sensitive attribute A (sex), worsening group fairness; a vanilla-trained model is empirically less dependent on G and thus serves as a robust teacher. CKD's distillation loss aligns the student's representations of both original and counterfactual images to the averaged teacher representations of that pair, simultaneously enforcing CF-invariance (CP effect) and G-robustness (KD effect). The final objective is L_CE + μ·L_CKD + λ·L_CP, where μ and λ control the distillation and pairing strengths. The paper also introduces a theoretical result (Theorem 4.1) bounding DEO by the model's sensitivity to G, and constructs CelebA-CF and LFW-CF using InstructPix2Pix with human-annotated quality filtering and reliability verification.

## Results

On CelebA, CKD achieves the best DEO (13.23) among all methods while maintaining competitive CD (4.44), compared to CP's CD of 2.53 but DEO of 51.01. On LFW, CKD achieves the lowest DEO (1.88) with CD of 7.94, outperforming CP (DEO 8.74, CD 9.20) and all GF-aware baselines. On the synthetic CIFAR-10B (α=0.8), CKD achieves DEO of 7.30 with CD of 2.85, substantially outperforming CP (DEO 33.23) and all GF-aware methods (DEO 14.79–25.73). CKD consistently achieves the best DEO across all three datasets, demonstrating that reducing reliance on the latent attribute G enables simultaneous CF and GF.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CP (Russell et al. [36]) achieves the best CD across all datasets (2.05 on CIFAR-10B, 2.53 on CelebA, 9.20 on LFW) but severely worsens DEO (33.23, 51.01, 8.74 respectively), confirming the paper's central finding that CF does not imply GF for images. GF-aware methods (SS, RW, COV, MFD, LBC) improve DEO over Scratch (e.g., MFD reduces CelebA DEO from 47.10 to 19.00) but have minimal impact on CD (e.g., MFD's CelebA CD is 4.61 vs. Scratch's 10.26, a modest gain). Naive combinations like SS+CP achieve strong CD (3.14 on CIFAR-10B) and moderate DEO (9.08), but still fall short of CKD's DEO of 7.30. SenSeI [42] and LASSI [31] (individual fairness methods) show only marginal CD improvements over Scratch and fail to improve DEO. CKD outperforms all baselines on DEO across all three datasets (7.30 vs. 9.08 for SS+CP on CIFAR-10B; 13.23 vs. 19.00 for MFD on CelebA; 1.88 vs. 1.90 for MFD+CP on LFW) while maintaining competitive CD, validating the theoretical prediction that reducing sensitivity to the latent attribute G bridges CF and GF.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of counterfactual fairness (CF) and group fairness (GF) research, building directly on the theoretical results of Anthis & Veitch [1] and Rosenblatt & Witter [35], who showed CF implies GF under specific Structural Causal Model conditions validated on tabular data. It departs from prior work by demonstrating that these conditions break down for image classification due to latent attributes correlated with (but not caused by) the sensitive attribute, and by introducing the first real-world counterfactual image benchmarks (CelebA-CF, LFW-CF) using diffusion-model-based editing (InstructPix2Pix) rather than the lower-quality VAE/GAN approaches used in earlier counterfactual image generation work. The proposed CKD method combines the counterfactual pairing regularization of Russell et al. [36] with knowledge distillation [12] to transfer robustness from a vanilla-trained teacher, a combination not previously explored in the fairness literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that CF-aware training can actively worsen group fairness (DEO) in image classification challenges a working assumption in the fairness community and provides a concrete theoretical explanation (Theorem 4.1) for why tabular-data results do not transfer to visual domains. The CelebA-CF and LFW-CF benchmarks are practically important as the first real-world datasets enabling simultaneous CF and GF evaluation, though they are limited to facial images with 'sex' as the sensitive attribute and rely on generated (not true) counterfactuals. Open questions include whether the latent-attribute-G mechanism generalizes to other sensitive attributes (age, race) and non-facial domains, whether CKD's reliance on a vanilla teacher is sufficient in all settings, and whether the faithfulness assumption for image SCMs (discussed in Appendix F) holds broadly.
