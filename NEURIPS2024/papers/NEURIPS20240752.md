## Problem

Whether an uninformed player can overcome her informational disadvantage through repeated strategic interactions alone to achieve her Stackelberg value. Prior work gave positive answers but relied on at least one player being myopic (not strategically rational). This paper shows that when both players are equally rational, modeled as PNE of a meta-game over algorithms, information asymmetry persists and the uninformed player cannot always attain her benchmark.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240752/cropped_page7_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a Bayesian game defined by a distribution D over a finite game family G, signal precisions p1 > p2 (where each player's signal reveals the true game with probability pi and is otherwise an independent draw from D), and a time horizon T. Each player's algorithm is a sequence of mappings from her signal and observed history (own utility, and in the full-information setting both players' realized actions) to a mixed strategy per round. The output is a characterization of which Stackelberg value benchmarks are achievable at PNE of the meta-game in the asymptotic regime T→∞, yielding achievability or impossibility guarantees per player.

## Method

The paper models repeated interactions as a meta-game where players' actions are full algorithms (T-round strategy sequences) and analyzes PNE in this space. For the positive result, P1 plays her optimal Stackelberg strategy while P2 employs a no-swap-regret algorithm; the no-swap-regret property forces P2's strategies to converge to her best response, granting P1 her Stackelberg value at equilibrium. For the negative result, the authors construct a two-game family where P2's utilities are identical across games but P1's differ, then use correlated strategy profiles (CSPs) as a low-dimensional sufficient statistic to show P1 always has a profitable deviation to conceal which game is realized. The key mechanism is the intertwining of learning and acting: if P2's learned knowledge would let her extract Stackelberg value, P1 deviates to an algorithm that prevents P2 from learning in the first place. Game parameters scale inversely with γ = (1−p*)/(1+p*) to handle partial information asymmetry where P2's signal precision is bounded by p* < 1.

## Results

The paper establishes a fundamental separation in achievable Stackelberg benchmarks under information asymmetry. Theorem 3.1 shows that for every distribution D, the fully-informed player P1 can achieve StackVal_1(G) − o_T(1) at a PNE of the meta-game, with a convergence rate of O(T^{3/4}) (Remark B.1). Theorem 3.2 shows the converse: for any signal precision p2 < 1, there exists a distribution D where no PNE allows the partially-informed P2 to achieve StackVal_2(D) − Ω_T(1), meaning the gap persists even as T → ∞. Proposition 4.2 further shows that when neither player is fully informed (p1, p2 < 1), no clear separation holds — the less-informed player can sometimes outperform the more-informed one depending on the structure of D.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The central 'comparison' is between two information regimes: (1) full asymmetry (p1=1, p2<1), where a hard separation exists — P1 always achieves her Stackelberg value while P2 provably cannot; and (2) partial asymmetry (p1,p2<1), where no separation is guaranteed and the information advantage can shift mid-interaction. The paper positions its findings as a direct contrast to prior work (e.g., [31, 8, 5, 35]) which established that an uninformed player can achieve her Stackelberg value through repeated interactions, but only against myopically best-responding or no-regret opponents. The key distinction is that this paper requires both players to be equally rational (PNE of the meta-game), and under this stricter equilibrium condition, the informational disadvantage persists. The paper also contrasts interaction-based learning with independent learning via external signals (Proposition A.5), showing that independent learning is strictly more powerful: it guarantees Stackelberg value achievability for all D, while interaction-based learning does not.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant line of work on learning in Stackelberg games (Letchford et al., Balcan et al., Blum et al., Roth et al.) which established that an uninformed player can achieve her Stackelberg value through repeated interactions with a myopically best-responding opponent. The key conceptual shift is modeling both players as fully rational long-term optimizers via the meta-game PNE framework (building on Deng et al. [17], Haghtalab et al. [28], and Brown et al. [10]), and analyzing information asymmetry through explicit signal precision parameters. The proof of the impossibility result (Theorem 3.2) leverages the correlated strategy profile (CSP) abstraction from Arunachaleswaran et al. [3] as a dimensionality-reducing analytical tool, while the positive result (Theorem 3.1) directly extends the no-swap-regret equilibrium constructions from Deng et al. and Haghtalab et al.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the prevailing narrative in the learning-in-games literature that repeated strategic interactions are sufficient for an uninformed player to overcome her informational disadvantage, showing this positive result is an artifact of the myopia assumption on at least one player. The finding that a player can successfully learn the game (Observation A.4) yet still be unable to extract her Stackelberg value at equilibrium reveals a fundamental tension between learning and acting that is specific to strategic settings. Open questions include characterizing which algorithm classes can be supported in meta-game PNE, extending to computational-bounded players, and studying alternative signal generation models (e.g., costly or strategically designed signals). The results are purely information-theoretic and for two-player settings, leaving the multi-player and computational aspects unexplored.
