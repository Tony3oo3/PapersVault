## Problem

Standard machine learning models for DFT Hamiltonian prediction treat the Hamiltonian as a one-shot regression target, ignoring its inherent self-consistent iterative structure. Prior work that does incorporate self-consistency (e.g., via DFT-in-the-loop losses) requires expensive DFT computations during training, rendering it impractical for large or complex systems. This paper asks whether the self-consistency of the Hamiltonian can be captured purely through network architecture, eliminating DFT from the training pipeline entirely.

## Input / Output

The model consumes three inputs per molecule: the atomic structure (atomic numbers as node features and relative position vectors as edge features), an initial Hamiltonian matrix (deliberately set to the identity to preserve Hermiticity), and the overlap matrix (easily precomputed from the basis set). Through DEQ fixed-point iteration, it outputs a converged Hamiltonian matrix H* that satisfies H* = f(H*, Z, R, S). From this converged Hamiltonian, orbital energies and coefficients are derived by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem HC = SCε, and the predicted Hamiltonian can also serve as an initial guess to accelerate downstream DFT SCF convergence.

## Method

DEQHNet wraps the QHNet equivariant graph network inside a Deep Equilibrium Model (DEQ) layer, transforming it from a one-shot predictor H = f(Z,R,S) into an iterative solver H* = f(H*,Z,R,S). The Hamiltonian and overlap matrices are converted into equivariant node features via a diagonal reduction step that exploits the Wigner-Eckart theorem: on-site 3-index overlap integrals (proportional to Clebsch-Gordan coefficients) couple the angular momentum indices of matrix elements into order-l equivariant embeddings. These matrix-derived features are then fused with structural features (processed through TFN-style message passing with tensor products and attention) in a TransBlock module, and the combined representations are expanded back into Hamiltonian blocks via the inverse tensor product (tensor expansion). The DEQ mechanism iterates this solver until the Hamiltonian reaches a fixed point, with convergence typically achieved in 2–3 iterations. Training uses a standard supervised loss (Frobenius norm + L1 norm of the Hamiltonian error) with no DFT computations in the loop, and backpropagation through the fixed point is handled via implicit differentiation (solving a linear system for the vector-Jacobian product).

## Results

On the QH9 dataset, DEQHNet reduces the Hamiltonian MAE (all blocks) from 173.92 to 105.80 (×10⁻⁶ Eₕ) on QH9-dynamic-mol, nearly halving the error, and from 96.85 to 62.14 on QH9-dynamic-geo. Orbital coefficient fidelity (cosine similarity ψ) jumps from 79.15% to 99.80% on QH9-dynamic-mol and from 94.45% to 99.92% on QH9-dynamic-geo. On MD17, DEQHNet outperforms QHNet on three of four molecules (Ethanol: 18.73 vs 20.91; Malonaldehyde: 17.97 vs 21.52; Uracil: 15.07 vs 20.12 in ×10⁻⁶ Eₕ Hamiltonian MAE) but underperforms on Water (36.07 vs 10.79). Notably, lower Hamiltonian MAE does not translate to lower orbital energy MAE, which remains higher for DEQHNet across most settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against QHNet (the primary baseline and architectural backbone), DEQHNet consistently achieves lower Hamiltonian MAE across all four QH9 subsets, with the largest gain on QH9-dynamic-mol (105.80 vs 173.92, a 39% reduction) and QH9-dynamic-geo (62.14 vs 96.85, a 36% reduction). On MD17, DEQHNet beats QHNet on Ethanol, Malonaldehyde, and Uracil but loses on Water (36.07 vs 10.79), which the authors attribute to limited training data for that molecule. Compared to SchNOrb, PhiSNet, and DeepH on MD17, DEQHNet is competitive or superior on Hamiltonian MAE for the larger molecules (Uracil: 15.07 vs 10.73 for PhiSNet-ori; Malonaldehyde: 17.97 vs 12.32 for PhiSNet-ori), though PhiSNet retains an edge on the smallest molecule. A consistent trade-off emerges: DEQHNet's superior Hamiltonian accuracy does not yield better orbital energy predictions (ε MAE is higher in most cases), which the authors explain via a noise-injection experiment showing that Hamiltonian error and eigenvalue error are not monotonically linked at the error magnitudes observed.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repositions Hamiltonian prediction from a direct structure-to-matrix mapping (as in SchNOrb, PhiSNet, DeepH, QHNet) to a fixed-point solving problem by embedding Deep Equilibrium Models (DEQ) within an equivariant GNN backbone (QHNet). It departs from prior self-consistency-integrating frameworks (Zhang et al., Li et al., Hu et al., Mathiasen et al.) that embed DFT iterations into the training loss, instead capturing self-consistency architecturally through DEQ's iterative convergence without any DFT computation during training. The diagonal reduction method for constructing equivariant node features from Hamiltonian/overlap matrices is adapted from OrbNet-Equi's Wigner-Eckart-based approach, and the overall equivariant message-passing design follows QHNet and Equiformer.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The predictor-to-solver reframing offers a principled way to incorporate the self-consistent iterative nature of DFT without the computational overhead of DFT-in-the-loop training, making the approach more scalable to larger systems. However, the empirical picture is mixed: DEQHNet significantly outperforms QHNet on QH9 (nearly halving Hamiltonian MAE on the dynamic subset) but underperforms on MD17 water (36.07 vs 10.79 MAE), suggesting it requires more training data to match predictor-style models on small datasets. A notable open question is the disconnect between Hamiltonian MAE and orbital energy MAE, which the authors identify as a fundamental metric-design challenge. The paper also acknowledges that for small systems where DFT cost during training is acceptable, explicit self-consistency methods may still outperform the DEQ-based approach.
