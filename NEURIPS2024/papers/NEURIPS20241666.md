## Problem

Existing multivariate time series imputation methods primarily model trend and seasonal components but underestimate the residual term, which the paper shows is the dominant source of imputation error. Compounding this, deep learning architectures exhibit spectral bias and cannot generalize well to the high-frequency components that characterize the residual term, leaving a critical gap in accurate imputation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241666/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an incomplete multivariate time series X ∈ R^{D×L} (D attributes over L consecutive timestamps) and a binary mask matrix M ∈ {0,1}^{D×L} indicating observed vs. missing entries. From the observed values, it extracts two frequency-domain conditions via FFT/IFFT: a high-frequency condition (components above a cutoff threshold F) and a dominant-frequency condition (top-κ amplitude components). The output is the imputed values for all missing positions, generated by a T-step reverse diffusion process starting from pure Gaussian noise, conditioned on the time-domain observations and both frequency-domain conditions.

## Method

FGTI augments a DDPM-based diffusion imputation model with two frequency-domain condition filters and two cross-domain representation learning modules. The high-frequency filter applies FFT to each attribute, discards components below a cutoff F, and maps back via IFFT to produce a condition C^H that guides residual-term imputation; the dominant-frequency filter selects the top-κ amplitude components and maps them back to produce C^D for trend and seasonal imputation. A transformer encoder maps the concatenated frequency conditions into a latent representation C^F, which is then fused with the denoising network's intermediate hidden representations via two cross-attention modules: time-frequency representation learning (per-attribute, capturing temporal dependencies) and attribute-frequency representation learning (per-timestamp, capturing cross-attribute dependencies). The paper proves (Proposition 3.1) that adding C^H and C^D to the diffusion reverse process strictly reduces the conditional entropy of the imputation target, providing a theoretical justification for the improved imputation accuracy.

## Results

On the KDD dataset with 10% missing values, FGTI achieves RMSE 0.406 and MAE 0.149, outperforming the strongest diffusion-based baseline CSDI (RMSE 0.459, MAE 0.177) by 11.5% and 15.8% respectively. On Guangzhou (10% missing), FGTI reaches RMSE 0.230 and MAE 0.170, beating CSDI (RMSE 0.306, MAE 0.210) by 24.8% and 19.0%. On PhysioNet (10% missing), FGTI obtains RMSE 0.580 and MAE 0.286, surpassing CSDI (RMSE 0.619, MAE 0.310) by 6.3% and 7.7%. FGTI maintains its lead across all missing rates (10%–40%) and all three datasets, with the gap widening at higher missing rates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FGTI outperforms all 15 baselines on every dataset and missing rate. The closest competitors are the diffusion-based CSDI and PriSTI: on KDD (10% missing), FGTI beats CSDI by 0.053 RMSE (11.5%) and PriSTI by 0.066 RMSE (14.0%); on Guangzhou, the margins are 0.076 RMSE over CSDI (24.8%) and 0.012 over PriSTI (5.0%); on PhysioNet, FGTI leads CSDI by 0.039 RMSE (6.3%) and PriSTI by 0.072 (11.0%). Among non-diffusion baselines, TimesNet and LaST are the strongest deep-learning methods, but FGTI still surpasses them by 17–20% RMSE on KDD and 30–42% on PhysioNet. Matrix-factorization methods (BTMF, TIDER) and GAN-based GAIN are consistently the weakest, with GAIN performing worse than the simple Mean imputation on PhysioNet. The advantage of FGTI grows with missing rate: at 40% missing on KDD, FGTI achieves RMSE 0.478 versus CSDI's 0.569 (16.0% improvement), while non-diffusion baselines degrade more sharply.

## Relation to Prior Work

FGTI extends the conditional diffusion model framework for time series imputation established by CSDI (Tashiro et al., 2021) and the DDPM generative process (Ho et al., 2020) by introducing two frequency-domain conditions—high-frequency and dominant-frequency filters—extracted via FFT/IFFT from observed values. While prior frequency-domain time series work (FEDformer, FreTS, APDNet) uses spectral information for forecasting or general representation learning, FGTI is the first to explicitly decompose frequency conditions by their role in STL components (high-frequency → residual, dominant-frequency → trend/seasonal) and inject them into a generative imputation pipeline via cross-domain cross-attention. The classifier-free guidance mechanism (Ho & Salimans, 2022) is repurposed to handle the multi-condition setting (observed values + two frequency conditions) in the reverse diffusion process.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clear diagnostic (Figure 1) showing that the residual term dominates imputation error, and a practical remedy: injecting frequency-domain priors into diffusion-based imputation to counteract the known spectral bias of deep networks. The technique is modular and could be grafted onto other generative imputation backbones beyond diffusion. Limitations include evaluation on only three datasets, added computational overhead from FFT/IFFT and cross-attention modules, and an implicit assumption that residual components align with high-frequency content—a correspondence that may weaken for non-stationary or regime-shifting series. Open questions include whether the fixed cutoff frequency F and top-κ selection generalize across domains without per-dataset tuning, and whether the approach extends to very long sequences where FFT cost becomes prohibitive.
