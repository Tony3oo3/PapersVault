## Problem

Existing image fusion methods predominantly rely on static fusion rules (e.g., max, min, addition) that lack any correlation with source-specific reconstruction quality, while the few dynamic fusion approaches that exist (MoE, dynamic adapters) are heuristic and lack theoretical guarantees. The paper asks whether dynamic image fusion can be conducted with a provable reduction in generalization error, and answers affirmatively by deriving the first generalization error upper bound for image fusion that explicitly identifies the role of fusion weights.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240067/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts paired or multi-source images (visible+infrared, MRI+CT, MRI+PET, multi-exposure sets, multi-focus sets) at standard resolutions and produces a single fused image of the same spatial dimensions. Uniquely, the input is consumed in two passes at test time: each source is first fed individually through the frozen encoder-decoder to compute pixel-wise reconstruction losses, and then all sources are jointly encoded and fused using the derived dynamic weights. No additional training data, fine-tuning, or extra parameters are required beyond the pre-trained baseline model.

## Method

TTD decomposes the fused image into M uni-source components D(E^(m)(x^(m))) and proves (Theorem 3.1) that the generalization error upper bound is reduced when the fusion weight ω^(m) is negatively correlated with the corresponding uni-source reconstruction loss ℓ^(m). The key mechanism is a pixel-level Relative Dominability (RD) defined as ω^(m) = Softmax(e^(−ℓ^(m))), which assigns higher fusion weight to regions where a source reconstructs well (low loss) and lower weight where it fails, while the softmax normalization ensures weights sum to one. This is a purely test-time adaptation: the baseline network remains frozen, and the only computation added is the per-source reconstruction pass followed by weight computation. The method is plug-and-play and has been validated as an extension to gradient-based RD (using |∇ℓ| in place of ℓ) for finer-grained dominance estimation, and empirically improves IFCNN, CDDFuse, and PIAFusion across VIF, MIF, MEF, and MFF benchmarks.

## Results

On the LLVIP VIF dataset, PIAFusion+TTD achieves the best performance across most metrics: EN 7.42, SD 56.14, AG 5.90, EI 15.15, SF 20.29, SCD 1.64, and CE 7.65. On MSRS, PIAFusion+TTD leads in SD (48.26), AG (4.18), EI (11.18), SF (12.99), and CE (6.92), while CDDFuse+TTD achieves the best EN (6.64) and SCD (1.58). Applying TTD to IFCNN on MEFB yields SD 52.86, EI 15.94, AG 6.38, and SF 21.99, outperforming all task-specific MEF methods. On MFI-WHU (MFF), IFCNN+TTD achieves SD 54.22, EI 19.10, AG 7.70, and SF 23.51, surpassing all compared multi-focus fusion methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TTD consistently improves its host baselines across all four fusion tasks: IFCNN+TTD gains +1.24 SD and +1.22 SF over IFCNN on LLVIP; CDDFuse+TTD gains +2.98 SD and +1.91 SF; PIAFusion+TTD gains +4.02 SD and +0.70 SF. Among all compared methods, PIAFusion+TTD achieves the best or second-best on 6 out of 7 LLVIP metrics, outperforming the strongest static baseline CDDFuse (SD 50.90 vs. 56.14) and the dynamic method TC-MoA (SF 9.78 vs. 20.29). On MSRS, PIAFusion+TTD leads in SD (48.26 vs. CDDFuse's 43.38) and SF (12.99 vs. 11.57), while CDDFuse+TTD edges out in EN (6.64 vs. 6.62) and SCD (1.58 vs. 1.52). On MEFB, IFCNN+TTD surpasses all task-specific MEF methods (e.g., MEFOpt SF 19.27 vs. 21.99; DEM SF 18.85 vs. 21.99) and the general method PMGI (SF 18.60 vs. 21.99). On MFI-WHU, IFCNN+TTD outperforms the best task-specific MFF method DRPL (SF 23.66 vs. 23.51 is close, but EI 18.93 vs. 19.10 and AG 7.66 vs. 7.70 favor TTD). The training-free DDFM is consistently outperformed by all TTD-augmented methods, confirming that the theoretical dynamic weighting provides gains even without additional training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground in image fusion by applying generalization error analysis to the fusion problem, a perspective absent from both static methods (DenseFuse, U2Fusion, CDDFuse, PIAFusion) and prior dynamic approaches (TC-MoA, MoE-based fusion). While TC-MoA and MoE-based methods introduced dynamic fusion weights through heuristic routing or adapter mechanisms, they lacked any formal justification for why dynamic weighting should help. The authors repurpose the covariance decomposition from statistical learning theory to prove that a negative correlation between fusion weight and uni-source reconstruction loss provably tightens the generalization error bound, then instantiate this as a pixel-level Relative Dominability computed at test time without retraining.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The first theoretical guarantee that dynamic fusion outperforms static fusion in image fusion fills a notable gap in a field that has relied on empirical validation. The plug-and-play nature (no retraining, no extra parameters) makes it immediately applicable to any existing fusion baseline, which is practically valuable. However, the method roughly doubles inference time, its performance depends heavily on baseline quality, and the gradient-based extension requires empirical channel selection rather than an adaptive criterion. Open questions include whether the dynamic mechanism can be integrated into training objectives, whether more principled gradient channel selection is possible, and whether the theoretical bound translates to gains in high-stakes deployments like medical imaging.
