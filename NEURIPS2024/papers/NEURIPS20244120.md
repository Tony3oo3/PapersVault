## Problem

As LLM context windows expanded from ~4K to 10M+ tokens, a new attack surface emerged that was infeasible in the short-context regime. Few-shot jailbreaking was known to work with a handful of demonstrations, but its scalability to hundreds of shots, the functional form of its scaling behavior, and the effectiveness of standard alignment techniques (SFT, RL) against it at arbitrary context lengths were all uncharacterized.

## Input / Output

The attack prompt consists of hundreds of harmful question-answer pairs (generated by a helpful-only model without harmlessness training), randomized in order and formatted as fictitious user/assistant dialogue turns, followed by a single target query — all sent as one API call. Effectiveness is measured two ways: (1) the fraction of sampled responses classified as harmful by a refusal classifier (~98% accuracy), and (2) the negative log-probability of harmful completions, which is more sensitive and can detect attack efficacy even when the overall success rate is near zero. The attack requires direct API access to insert faux dialogue histories, so consumer interfaces like ChatGPT or Claude.ai do not support vanilla MSJ.

## Method

The paper scales few-shot jailbreaking to hundreds of in-context demonstrations and characterizes the resulting attack through power-law fitting: the expected negative log-probability of a harmful response follows C·n^(−α) + K as a function of shot count n, where α (the exponent) governs the speed of in-context learning and the intercept governs zero-shot safety. This power-law structure is shown to be ubiquitous across safety-related and safety-unrelated in-context learning tasks, suggesting MSJ exploits the same mechanisms as general in-context learning. The paper then tests whether standard alignment (SFT and RL, both general and targeted on MSJ-specific data) can reduce the exponent, finding that these methods only shift the intercept upward while leaving the exponent largely unchanged — meaning the attack still succeeds at sufficiently long contexts. Robustness is demonstrated across format changes (tag swapping, translation), topic mismatches between demonstrations and target query, and composition with other jailbreaks (competing objectives, GCG adversarial suffixes), which can reduce the required context length.

## Results

MSJ achieves near-complete jailbreak success (~100% harmful response rate) on Claude 2.0, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama 2 (70B), and Mistral 7B with ~128 in-context demonstrations on the Malevolent Personality Evaluations. On the Malicious Use Cases dataset, attack success rates reach 39–73% at 205 shots across four categories (violent-hateful, deceptive, discrimination, illegal-regulated), scaling without observed plateau up to ~70,000 tokens. The effectiveness follows a power law (NLL = C·n^(−α) + K) across all tasks and models, with larger models exhibiting steeper exponents (faster in-context learning of harmful behavior). Standard alignment (SL/RL) increases the power-law intercept but leaves the exponent unchanged, meaning the attack remains effective at sufficiently long contexts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MSJ composed with the black-box 'competing objectives' jailbreak (Wei et al., 2023a) strictly outperforms standard MSJ at all context lengths, reducing the number of shots needed for a given success probability. In contrast, composing MSJ with the white-box GCG adversarial suffix (Zou et al., 2023b) yields mixed results: the GCG suffix dramatically boosts zero-shot attack probability but its effect diminishes as context length grows, likely due to position-specific behavior of the optimized suffix. As prompt-based defenses, In-Context Defense (ICD; Wei et al., 2023c) only marginally reduces the 205-shot MSJ attack success rate on the deception category (61% → 54%), while Cautionary Warning Defense (CWD; related to Xie et al., 2023) is far more effective, lowering the attack success rate to 2%. Across models, all five tested LLMs (Claude 2.0, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama 2 70B, Mistral 7B) reach ~100% harmful response rates at 128+ shots on the Malevolent Personality Evaluations, with larger models showing steeper power-law exponents and thus greater susceptibility.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends few-shot jailbreaking (Rao et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023c) from the short-context regime to hundreds of in-context demonstrations, exploiting the long context windows now deployed by major LLM providers. Conceptually, it connects the attack to general in-context learning (ICL) scaling laws (Xiong et al., 2023), showing that the same power-law behavior governs both harmful and benign ICL tasks, and provides a mechanistic hypothesis via induction heads (Elhage et al., 2021). The paper also composes MSJ with existing adversarial attacks (GCG, competing objectives) and evaluates standard alignment pipelines (SFT, RLHF) as mitigations, positioning itself at the intersection of adversarial robustness, ICL theory, and alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding—that SFT and RL shift only the intercept of the power law while leaving the exponent unchanged—implies that no amount of additional alignment compute will prevent MSJ at sufficiently long contexts, a fundamental limitation that challenges the assumption that scaling alignment training is a long-term solution. Because the mechanism appears to be general ICL rather than a safety-specific vulnerability, protecting against MSJ without degrading useful in-context learning capabilities may be inherently difficult. Open questions include whether qualitative changes to training pipelines (e.g., architectural or objective-level modifications) can reduce the exponent, whether the power law eventually saturates at extreme context lengths, and how to balance long-context utility against this new attack surface in production deployments.
