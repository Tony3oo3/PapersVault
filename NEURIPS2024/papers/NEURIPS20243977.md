## Problem

No large-scale dataset with human preference judgments existed for evaluating creative tasks like humor generation, and it was unclear whether standard alignment methods (RLHF, DPO) transfer effectively to domains where the distribution of high-quality outputs is extremely sensitive to small perturbations. This paper tackles both gaps by releasing 250M+ crowd-sourced ratings from The New Yorker's caption contest and proposing a reliable group-based evaluation benchmark for humor quality.

## Input / Output

For multimodal models (LLaVA, GPT-4o-vision), the input is the raw cartoon image; for language-only models (Mistral-7B, GPT-4o text), the input is a structured text description comprising a canny description, an uncanny description, a location, and a list of entities (either human-written from Hessel et al. or GPT-4o-generated). The model produces 10 candidate captions per cartoon, which are then evaluated as a group against four reference groups of 10 human submissions at different ranking tiers (top 10, #200–209, #1000–1009, and contest median) using GPT-4-based group comparison prompts with 5-shot in-context examples.

## Method

The paper's core technical contribution is a group-based evaluation protocol that compares sets of 10 captions (rather than single pairs) using GPT-4-Turbo or GPT-4o-vision as judges, which substantially improves evaluation reliability over pairwise comparison (e.g., 77.5% vs. 66.5% ranking accuracy for top-10 vs. #1000 discrimination). For alignment experiments, they finetune Mistral-7B and LLaVA-7B via LoRA using four strategies: SFT on top-1000 captions, RLHF (PPO) with a reward model trained on preference pairs separated by ≥3 standard deviations in human ratings, DPO on the same preference pairs, and Best-of-N sampling with the RLHF reward model. A key finding is that DPO significantly outperforms RLHF on the Best-Pick metric (30.22% vs. 13.19% win rate against contestant median), which they attribute to RLHF's tendency to drift out-of-distribution where the reward signal becomes unreliable for humor's highly sensitive output distribution. They also demonstrate that even GPT-4o and Claude-3-Opus underperform top human contestants, with a former New Yorker editor preferring human captions over Claude's in 98.4% of cases.

## Results

On the New Yorker Caption Contest benchmark (91 held-out cartoons), Claude-3-Opus achieves the highest Overall Win Rate at 54.40% against Top-10 human captions and 88.46% against the Contestant Median, while GPT-4o reaches 44.51% and 86.81% respectively. Among open-source models, Mistral-7B with DPO finetuning performs best at 9.34% Overall Win Rate vs. Top-10 and 31.32% vs. Contestant Median, substantially outperforming the 0-shot Mistral baseline (4.95% and 25.82%). Even the strongest SOTA model (Claude-3-Opus) is preferred over Top-10 human captions only 34% of the time by crowd workers and just 1.6% of the time by a former New Yorker cartoon editor, revealing a significant gap between AI and expert human humor generation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks against two open-source baselines: LLaVA 7B (multimodal, [Liu et al., 2024]) and Mistral-7B Instruct (language-only, [Jiang et al., 2023]). LLaVA 7B achieves only 3.85% Overall Win Rate vs. Top-10 human captions and 13.19% vs. Contestant Median, making it the weakest model evaluated; surprisingly, the language-only Mistral-7B 0-shot outperforms it (4.95% and 25.82%), suggesting that multimodal integration currently hinders caption generation quality. Among finetuning strategies applied to Mistral-7B, DPO is the clear winner, improving Overall Win Rate vs. Top-10 from 4.95% (0-shot) to 9.34% and Best Pick Win Rate vs. Top-10 from 1.65% to 10.44%, while SFT actually degrades performance (3.85% Overall) and RLHF offers only marginal gains (8.79% Overall). Even the best open-source configuration (Mistral-7B DPO) trails GPT-4o by roughly 35 percentage points on Overall Win Rate vs. Top-10 (9.34% vs. 44.51%), underscoring a large gap between open-source and frontier models on creative humor generation. The paper further shows that GPT-4o Vision (42.31% Overall vs. Top-10) underperforms GPT-4o text-only (44.51%), and Claude-3-Opus (54.40%) leads all models but still loses to Top-10 human captions in 45.6% of head-to-head group comparisons.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the New Yorker Caption Contest dataset (previously used for bandit algorithm evaluation and small-scale humor analysis by Hessel et al. 2022, Shahaf et al. 2015) into a massive-scale preference resource (250M+ ratings across 365 contests) and repurposes it as a benchmark for LLM alignment. Conceptually, it departs from the standard RLHF/DPO literature—which has focused on well-defined preference dimensions like helpfulness and harmlessness—by applying these alignment paradigms to a highly subjective creative task where the reward landscape is extremely sensitive to small perturbations. The group-comparison evaluation methodology (comparing sets of 10 captions rather than pairwise) is a novel departure from the pairwise ranking task in Hessel et al. 2022, motivated by the finding that group comparisons substantially improve evaluator reliability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first large-scale human preference dataset for a creative generation task, and its release will enable systematic study of humor alignment that was previously infeasible. The finding that RLHF/PPO degrades performance while DPO improves it for humor generation challenges the assumption that standard alignment pipelines transfer to creative domains, and the MLLM-underperforms-LLM result raises fundamental questions about vision-language integration. Key open questions include whether better reward models can capture the subtleties of humor, whether DPO's advantage generalizes to other creative tasks, and how to balance humor with offensiveness. The 1.6% expert preference rate for Claude over top human captions underscores that a significant capability gap remains.
