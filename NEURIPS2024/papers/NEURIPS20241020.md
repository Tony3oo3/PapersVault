## Problem

Despite empirical success of Deep Equilibrium Models (DEQ) over feedforward neural networks (FNNs), a theoretical understanding of when and why DEQ is provably advantageous in expressivity and learning dynamics remains limited. Existing NTK-based analyses show DEQs are nearly equivalent to not-so-deep FNNs in certain regimes, leaving open whether DEQ has fundamental, quantifiable advantages. This paper initiates a systematic study of separation and bias to clarify when DEQ is theoretically preferred.

## Input / Output

The DEQ consumes a d-dimensional input vector x ∈ [0,1]^d (or binary x ∈ {±1}^d for OOD tasks) through an implicit layer that solves the fixed-point equation z = σ(Wz + Ux + b) rather than performing forward propagation, then outputs a scalar y = Az. The width m of the DEQ directly controls the number of linear regions (up to 2^m), and the key constraint is that the implicit layer's Lipschitz constant must be < 1 for well-posedness. For the steep-function separation, the target function g(x) = δx₁/(2(1+δ−x₁)) with δ = 2^{-d} has exponentially large derivatives near x₁ = 1, making it a natural stress test for approximation efficiency.

## Method

The paper establishes two expressivity separations and one learning-dynamics bias result. First, a width-m ReLU-DEQ can generate 2^m linear regions (proven tight via a sawtooth construction), while an FNN with depth O(m^α) for α ∈ (0,1) requires width exp(Ω(m^{1−α})) to match this. Second, for a steep function defined as the solution to a fixed-point equation, a DEQ with width O(ε^{-1}) and weights O(ε^{-1}) achieves O(ε) approximation in L^∞ norm, whereas an FNN with bounded depth needs exponentially large weights; the key technical tool is a new lemma (Lemma 3) showing that if two fixed-point mappings agree at frequent intervals, their fixed points are close even when the Lipschitz constant is near 1. Third, for a simplified diagonal linear DEQ f(w,x) = Σ xᵢ/(1−wᵢ), gradient flow converges to an interpolator that minimizes a non-standard objective Q(β) = Σ(1/(2βᵢ²) + βᵢ(0)^{-3}βᵢ), which penalizes small-magnitude parameters and produces 'dense' features, yielding provably smaller GOTU error than diagonal linear networks on unseen-domain generalization tasks.

## Results

The paper establishes two expressivity separations between DEQ and FNN. Theorem 1 shows a width-m DEQ computing a function with 2^m linear regions cannot be approximated to L1 error < 1/16 by any FNN of depth O(m^α) (α∈(0,1)) unless its width is exp(Ω(m^(1-α))). Theorem 2 shows a DEQ with width O(ε⁻¹) and weights O(ε⁻¹) approximates a steep function g(x) (with exponentially large derivative near x₁=1) to ε accuracy in L∞ norm, while an FNN of bounded depth and width ≤ 2^(d/2C) requires exponentially large weights (≥ 2^(d/2C)). For learning dynamics, Theorem 3 characterizes the implicit bias of a diagonal linear DEQ as minimizing Q(β)=Σ(1/(2βᵢ²)+βᵢ(0)⁻³βᵢ) subject to interpolation, and Proposition 2 shows DEQ achieves GOTU error strictly below 4f̂({k})², improving over the 4f̂({k})²+O(ε) bound for diagonal linear networks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary comparison is between DEQ and FNN (the baseline architecture). On sawtooth functions with 2^5 and 2^10 linear regions, DEQ achieves near-zero test loss while FNNs of comparable parameter count (various depth-width combinations) fail to match this accuracy, empirically confirming Theorem 1. On the steep function g(x) with δ=2⁻¹⁰ and δ=2⁻²⁰, DEQ consistently achieves lower test loss than FNNs with similar total parameter counts across all tested width-depth configurations (Figure 1b, 1e). On the OOD/GOTU task with boolean function f₁, DEQ's generalization error on the unseen domain falls below the theoretical threshold from Proposition 2, while the diagonal linear network baseline (FNN) incurs a GOTU error of 4f̂({k})²+O(ε). The feature heatmaps (Figure 2) confirm that DEQ produces 'denser' features (smaller ℓ∞ norm lower bound) compared to FNN, consistent with the theoretical dense-bias result in Theorem 4.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical depth-separation framework (Telgarsky's sawtooth constructions, Eldan-Shamir) to the DEQ-vs-FNN comparison, providing the first quantitative expressivity separation between these two architectures. For the bias analysis, it adapts the implicit-regularization techniques of Woodworth et al. (kernel vs. rich regimes) to a simplified diagonal linear DEQ, and leverages the GOTU (Generalization on the Unseen) framework of Abbe et al. to demonstrate a concrete OOD advantage. The paper departs from the NTK-based line of DEQ analysis (Gao, Ling, Kawaguchi), which shows DEQs are nearly equivalent to shallow FNNs in the overparameterized regime, by working outside that regime and identifying settings where DEQ provably outperforms FNNs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic theoretical study establishing when and why DEQs are more parameter-efficient than FNNs, providing practitioners with principled guidance for architecture selection. The conjecture that DEQs have an inductive bias toward high-frequency components is intriguing but remains unproven beyond the simplified diagonal linear model. Key limitations include the reliance on a highly simplified DEQ (diagonal, linear) for the bias analysis and the restriction to specific function classes for the separations; extending these results to general nonlinear DEQs, stochastic optimization, and real-world architectures remains open.
