## Problem

The effectiveness of fine-tuning a pretrained linear model on a small target dataset in the overparametrized regime (d > n) has been characterized only under Gaussian data assumptions, leaving open whether these results extend to realistic non-Gaussian distributions. Moreover, there is no precise, distribution-agnostic criterion for when fine-tuning improves upon the pretrained model versus when it degrades performance. This paper provides an exact, rigorous analysis of generalization and classification errors for fine-tuned linear models and proves that the results are universal, depending only on the first and second-order statistics of the target distribution.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained weight vector w₀ ∈ ℝᵈ (obtained from source-domain training) and a small target dataset of n samples (X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ, y ∈ ℝⁿ) with d/n → κ > 1. For regression, it outputs the generalization error e_p of the SGD fine-tuned solution as a closed-form function of the a priori error e_a, noise variance σ², the proportional constant κ, and the spectral distribution of the data covariance. For binary classification, it outputs the classification error as a function of the initialization quality (parameterized by the ratio t_η/t_*), the class covariance matrices Σ₁, Σ₂, and the mean cross-correlation r. A key structural detail is that for classification, w₀ is first renormalized via a scalar α to fix its magnitude before SGD fine-tuning, decoupling direction from scale.

## Method

The core contribution is a Gaussian universality theorem (Theorem 1) proven via the Lindeberg method, showing that for a broad class of block-regular data matrices satisfying moment conditions up to order 6 and a variance-vanishing condition, the test error of any strongly convex regularized linear model (including SGD's implicit ℓ₂ regularization and explicit ridge/elastic-net) is asymptotically identical to that of a matching Gaussian design matrix. This universality reduces the analysis to the Gaussian case, where the Stieltjes transform of the empirical spectral distribution yields exact closed-form expressions for the generalization and classification errors. The implicit regularization property of SGD (convergence to the minimum-Bregman-divergence interpolator) is the key mechanism linking the pretrained initialization w₀ to the fine-tuned solution, and the analysis extends to mixture distributions for classification by applying universality class-by-class. The results identify precise regimes: fine-tuning always helps when the noise-to-signal ratio is low (regression) or the class separation is strong (classification), but can be harmful when noise dominates or the number of target samples is too small relative to parameters.

## Results

The paper provides exact asymptotic expressions for the generalization error of SGD fine-tuning in linear regression (Theorem 2): e_p = [2−κ(1−t)]/[κ(1−t)−1] · σ² + t/[κ(1−t)−1] · e_a, with a universal lower bound e_p ≥ σ²/(κ−1) + (κ−1)/κ · e_a attained only for scalar covariance. For binary classification (Theorem 3, Corollary 2), the fine-tuned error is e_p = Q(·) where the argument depends on κ, ρ = d(1−r)/σ², and the a priori error e_a. The key practical finding is that fine-tuning always succeeds when ρ = ω(1) (low noise), may or may not help when ρ = Θ(1), and always fails when ρ = o(1) (high noise); for κ ≫ 1, transfer learning fails whenever ρ < 1 regardless of e_a.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines in the standard sense; the 'comparisons' are between the fine-tuned model and the pretrained (a priori) model, and between different data distribution assumptions. The paper extends Dar et al. [2021], which analyzed transfer learning for linear regression under Gaussian data, by proving the same generalization error expressions hold universally for any distribution satisfying moment conditions (up to 6th order) and a variance-vanishing condition on quadratic forms. Compared to Montanari and Saeed [2022] and Dandi et al. [2024], the universality result here applies to mixture distributions (essential for classification) and allows non-vanishing means, whereas prior work required the mean norm to be negligible relative to data norm. Relative to Hastie et al. [2022], the paper generalizes from a specific random-feature data model to arbitrary block-regular matrices and from the ℓ₂ mirror to any strongly convex (possibly non-differentiable) mirror function, including the elastic net. The numerical experiments (Section 5) validate the theory by showing that empirical errors for Normal, Bernoulli, and χ² data entries all collapse onto the same theoretical curve predicted by the Gaussian analysis, confirming the universality claim.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the transfer learning analysis of Dar et al. (2021) for linear regression by establishing a Gaussian universality theorem via the Lindeberg method, showing that the generalization error depends only on first and second moments of the data distribution rather than requiring Gaussianity. It builds on the implicit regularization framework of Azizan and Hassibi (2018) and Gunasekar et al. (2018), which characterize SGD/SMD solutions as minimum-norm interpolators, and leverages the Gaussian min-max theorem of Akhtiamov et al. (2024) to handle non-scalar covariance matrices in classification. The universality result departs from prior work (Montanari & Saeed, Panahi & Hassibi) by applying to constrained/implicit regularization problems with random polytope constraints, mixture distributions, and non-vanishing means—settings where previous universality theorems do not directly apply.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first precise, distribution-agnostic characterization of when fine-tuning a pretrained linear model helps or hurts, yielding actionable conditions (noise level vs. a priori error, overparametrization ratio) that practitioners can use to decide whether to fine-tune. The universality result significantly broadens the applicability of existing Gaussian-based analyses to realistic non-Gaussian data, reducing the need for distributional assumptions in theoretical ML. Key limitations include restriction to linear models in the overparametrized regime, and open questions remain about extending the framework to non-convex regularizers, deeper architectures, and the interplay with explicit regularization in noisy settings.
