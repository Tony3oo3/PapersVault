## Problem

Existing methods for predicting potential outcomes from observational data are largely limited to point estimates without uncertainty quantification, and they fail to properly address selection bias arising from non-randomized treatment assignment. While CATE estimators exist, they are not tailored for PO prediction and impose inductive biases (e.g., assuming CATE is simpler than POs) that hurt finite-sample PO performance. The full distributional information about POs—critical for reliable medical decision-making—remains unexplored.

## Input / Output

The method consumes tabular patient covariates X ∈ R^{d_X} and a binary treatment indicator a ∈ {0,1} as conditioning variables for a diffusion process over the scalar outcome y. Because only one of the two potential outcomes is observed per individual (the fundamental problem of causal inference), the model uses three causal masks (observational, targeted, conditional) to identify which PO the loss should be computed on. The output is the full conditional distribution p(Y(a)|X) for both a=0 and a=1, from which point estimates, predictive intervals, and arbitrary quantiles can be obtained via iterative denoising sampling.

## Method

DiffPO is a conditional denoising diffusion model with a U-Net backbone (4 MLP residual blocks, 128-dim diffusion embedding) that learns the distribution of potential outcomes by gradually denoising a Gaussian prior conditioned on (x, a). The key innovation is an orthogonal diffusion loss that applies inverse propensity weighting w_π̂(x,a) = a/π̂(x) + (1-a)/(1-π̂(x)) to the standard ε-prediction objective, ensuring Neyman-orthogonality with respect to the propensity score nuisance function. This makes the loss first-order insensitive to propensity score misspecification, providing robustness against selection bias in finite samples. Training is two-stage: a fully connected network first estimates the propensity score (then frozen), and the diffusion model is trained with the weighted loss over 100 diffusion steps. The simplified training objective reduces to minimizing the weighted squared error between the true noise ε and the predicted noise f_θ(√ᾱ_t·y_0 + √(1-ᾱ_t)·ε, t | x, a), with the weight applied per sample.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset for learning PO distributions, DiffPO achieves an in-sample Wasserstein distance of 0.043 (a=0) and 0.032 (a=1), outperforming the best baseline CFR (0.921 and 0.909) by roughly 20×. For point estimation (RMSE), DiffPO reaches 0.143 (a=0) and 0.162 (a=1) in-sample, versus 0.263 and 0.261 for CFR. On CATE estimation across ACIC 2016 (77 datasets) and ACIC 2018 (24 datasets), DiffPO achieves the highest percentage of best runs (26.73% in-sample on ACIC 2016; 36.86% out-of-sample on ACIC 2018), surpassing the next-best DR-learner (14.54% and 22.13% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DiffPO dominates all baselines across every evaluation metric. On the primary task of learning PO distributions (Wasserstein distance), DiffPO (0.043 in-sample, a=0) outperforms the strongest baseline CFR (0.921) by a factor of ~21×, and S-learner (1.007) by ~23×. For point estimation (RMSE), DiffPO (0.143, a=0) beats CFR (0.263) by ~46% and GANITE (0.378) by ~62%. On CATE estimation (ACIC 2016/2018), DiffPO achieves the highest % best runs (26.73% in / 36.86% out on ACIC 2018), exceeding DR-learner (14.54% / 22.13%) and RA-learner (15.71% / 15.01%), despite not being specifically designed for CATE. GANITE shows the highest variance across all metrics (±0.16–0.35), reflecting its unstable GAN-based training. The baselines S-learner and T-learner, which do not address selection bias, consistently perform worst, confirming the importance of the orthogonal diffusion loss.

## Relation to Prior Work

DiffPO adapts the conditional denoising diffusion framework from DDPM [26] and CSDI [59] to the causal inference setting, where the generative target is the conditional distribution of potential outcomes p(Y(a)|X) rather than an unconditional data distribution. It departs from the dominant CATE-estimation literature (S/T/DR/RA-learners, TARNet, CFR, GANITE, TEDVAE) by treating the full PO distribution as the primary target rather than a point estimate, and by introducing a novel orthogonal diffusion loss that incorporates inverse propensity weighting directly into the denoising objective. The orthogonal loss draws on Neyman-orthogonal learning theory [18, 60] to ensure first-order insensitivity to propensity score misspecification, a property previously applied to CATE pseudo-outcomes but not to generative/diffusion-based PO learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Distributional knowledge of potential outcomes is essential for reliable medical decision-making (e.g., distinguishing a small-but-certain benefit from a large-but-uncertain one), yet most state-of-the-art causal ML methods only provide point estimates. DiffPO's orthogonal diffusion loss offers a principled, theoretically grounded way to address selection bias within a generative framework, and its flexibility (POs, CATE, predictive intervals from a single model) reduces the need for task-specific estimators. Key limitations include the 100-step sampling cost, reliance on standard unconfoundedness/overlap assumptions, and evaluation primarily on synthetic or semi-synthetic data; open questions include scaling to continuous or multi-valued treatments and improving sampling efficiency via fast solvers or consistency models.
