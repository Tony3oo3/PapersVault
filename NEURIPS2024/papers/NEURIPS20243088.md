## Problem

Current LLM benchmarks evaluate accuracy on realizable tasks with a unique correct answer, making them blind to whether models can faithfully quantify outcome uncertainty. A model can achieve high accuracy while being wildly miscalibrated, and this failure mode is invisible in standard question-answering evaluations. The paper asks: can LLMs serve as calibrated risk scorers on naturally underspecified prediction tasks where the true outcome is inherently uncertain?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243088/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of natural language prompts constructed from US Census ACS PUMS survey responses, where demographic features (e.g., age, sex, occupation, race) are rendered as bulleted text sentences, followed by a binary classification query. Two prompting schemes are used: multiple-choice (extracting the risk score as P(A)/(P(A)+P(B)) from next-token probabilities, averaged over all choice orderings to mitigate position bias) and numeric (verbally querying for a probability between 0 and 1, with two forward passes selecting the highest-likelihood numeric token). Output is a continuous risk score in [0,1] evaluated against ground-truth binary labels from the census data, with calibration, Brier score, AUC, and accuracy reported per model.

## Method

The folktexts package systematically translates tabular prediction tasks (defined by numeric features X and binary labels Y from ACS PUMS) into natural language prompts and extracts risk scores from LLMs, bridging the gap between the ML type signature and the LLM text interface. Five pre-specified benchmark tasks (ACSIncome, ACSPublicCoverage, ACSMobility, ACSEmployment, ACSTravelTime) are constructed from 28 census features, deliberately underspecified to introduce natural aleatoric uncertainty that forces calibrated models to output low-confidence scores. The key experimental design compares base models against their instruction-tuned counterparts across 17 LLMs (2B–141B parameters) using both prompting schemes, revealing that instruction-tuning polarizes multiple-choice answer distributions toward 0 or 1 regardless of true data uncertainty, while numeric prompting substantially improves calibration for instruction-tuned models at a small cost in AUC. Supervised baselines (logistic regression, XGBoost) trained on 1.5M samples provide near-perfect calibration references, and group-conditional calibration analysis (signed calibration error across race subgroups) surfaces fairness-relevant miscalibration patterns.

## Results

On the ACSIncome task, LLMs achieve strong predictive signal (AUC 0.62–0.87) but are wildly miscalibrated (ECE 0.14–0.63 with multiple-choice prompting), while the XGBoost baseline achieves near-perfect calibration (ECE = 0.00) and the best AUC (0.90). The best LLM for AUC is GPT 4o (it) at 0.87, still below XGBoost's 0.90; the best LLM for calibration under multiple-choice prompting is Gemma 2B (ECE = 0.14), still far from XGBoost. Switching to numeric prompting substantially improves calibration for instruction-tuned models (e.g., GPT 4o mini ECE drops from 0.24 to 0.05) at a small cost in AUC (0.85 → 0.83). Instruction-tuning consistently worsens multiple-choice calibration (higher ECE) while improving AUC and accuracy, and polarizes score distributions regardless of true data uncertainty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares 17 zero-shot LLMs against two supervised baselines: logistic regression (LR) and XGBoost, both trained on 1.5M ACS samples. On AUC, most LLMs (≥8B parameters) surpass the LR baseline (0.79), with GPT 4o (it) reaching 0.87, but all fall short of XGBoost (0.90). On calibration, the gap is stark: XGBoost achieves ECE = 0.00 and LR achieves ECE = 0.03, while the best LLM under multiple-choice prompting (Gemma 2B) still has ECE = 0.14 and most LLMs range from 0.16 to 0.63. Under numeric prompting, GPT 4o mini (it) narrows the calibration gap to ECE = 0.05, approaching LR-level calibration, but at a small AUC cost (0.83 vs. 0.85). On accuracy, only the instruction-tuned Mistral models (7B, 8x7B, 8x22B) and GPT 4o (it) surpass the LR baseline (0.74), while XGBoost leads at 0.82. The central finding is that LLMs have strong ranking signal but fundamentally different miscalibration patterns from supervised models: base models overestimate uncertainty (under-confident scores), while instruction-tuned models underestimate it (over-confident, polarized scores).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the folktables tabular benchmarking paradigm [9] to the LLM setting, constructing natural-language prediction tasks from US Census (ACS PUMS) data to evaluate LLMs as risk scorers. It departs from prior LLM calibration work [34–43], which has focused on realizable tasks (sentiment, knowledge QA, math) where a unique correct answer exists, by deliberately studying calibration on unrealizable, underspecified tasks with inherent aleatoric uncertainty. The methodology draws on the LLM-as-classifier literature (Hegselmann et al. [19], Slack and Singh [21]) and the algorithmic fairness calibration tradition [5–8], but reframes the evaluation question from 'is the model accurate?' to 'does the model faithfully reflect population-level outcome uncertainty?'

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that instruction-tuning systematically polarizes multiple-choice answer distributions regardless of true data uncertainty reveals a fundamental limitation of the dominant LLM evaluation paradigm: accuracy-focused benchmarks on realizable tasks cannot detect this miscalibration. This has direct practical implications for any deployment of LLMs in consequential risk-assessment settings (hiring, lending, criminal justice), where over-confident scores from instruction-tuned models could produce systematically unfair outcomes. Open questions include whether the AUC cost of numeric prompting is fundamental or an artifact of discrete token-space generation, whether subgroup miscalibration patterns generalize beyond the ACSIncome task, and whether any prompting strategy can fully recover the calibration lost to instruction-tuning.
