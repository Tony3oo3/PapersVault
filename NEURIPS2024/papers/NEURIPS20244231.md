## Problem

Building analytics research is hampered by the scarcity of publicly available, comprehensive, real-world datasets covering multiple buildings. The only comparable dataset, LBNL59, captures a single building with only 11 ontology classes, making it insufficient for studying interoperability, domain adaptation, and transfer learning across diverse building systems. This paper introduces the BTS dataset (three Australian buildings, 240 unique Brick classes, 10,000+ timeseries over three years) and benchmarks it on tasks that expose these interoperability challenges.

## Input / Output

For the classification benchmark, input consists of timeseries chunks (2/4/8 weeks) from individual IoT points, preprocessed via symmetric log scaling and standard scaling, then either reduced to global statistical features (mean, std, skew, kurtosis, RMS, quartiles) for tree/linear models or aggregated into 4-hour slots for deep learning architectures. Output is a multi-label vector over 240 Brick Point sub-classes where super-classes are +1, sub-classes are 0, and unrelated classes are -1. For the zero-shot forecasting benchmark, input is a 12-step (2-hour) multivariate window of resampled 10-minute IoT data, and output is the next 12 steps, with the model trained on one building and evaluated on the other two without fine-tuning.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the dataset and its benchmark formulation rather than a novel architecture. The multi-label classification task reformulates single-label Brick ontology assignment into a hierarchical multi-label problem (super-class = 1, sub-class = 0, other = -1) trained with binary cross-entropy and positive-sample weighting, evaluated via a time-and-building partition that forces cross-building generalization. Baselines span naive predictors (Zero, Mode, Random Proportional), traditional ML (Logistic Regression, XGBoost), and timeseries deep learning models (Transformer with Optuna-tuned hyperparameters, Informer, DLinear, PatchTST) adapted from TSLib. The zero-shot forecasting benchmark trains each model on one building's 1-month data and tests on the other two, using MAE, SMAPE, and R². Results show all models barely outperform naive baselines (e.g., XGBoost F1=0.27 vs. Mode F1=0.13; PatchTST SMAPE≈29% vs. persistence≈45%), confirming the task remains largely unsolved and highlighting the dataset's value as a testbed for domain shift, long-tail, and multimodal (KG + timeseries) learning.

## Results

On the primary multi-label timeseries classification task, the best deep learning model (Transformer with hyperparameter tuning) achieves 0.3615 F1 and 0.3489 mAP, while XGBoost reaches 0.2697 F1 and 0.2627 mAP. Naive baselines (e.g., Mode) achieve high accuracy (0.8592) but very low F1 (0.1296) due to extreme class imbalance. On the secondary zero-shot forecasting task, PatchTST achieves the lowest SMAPE (28.9–29.6%) across cross-building transfers, outperforming DLinear (34.1–41.2%), Informer (39.7–52.0%), and iTransformer (30.1–39.5%). The authors emphasize that all models perform only marginally better than naive baselines, indicating the problem remains largely unsolved.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks four timeseries architectures (Informer, DLinear, PatchTST, iTransformer) plus XGBoost and a Transformer on the new BTS dataset. On multi-label classification, the HP-tuned Transformer leads with 0.3615 F1, followed by Informer (0.3162), PatchTST (0.2981), and DLinear (0.2499); XGBoost achieves 0.2697 F1 with the highest accuracy (0.8593) among non-naive methods. On zero-shot cross-building forecasting, PatchTST consistently achieves the lowest SMAPE (28.9–29.6%), beating iTransformer (30.1–39.5%), DLinear (34.1–41.2%), and Informer (39.7–52.0%). DLinear shows competitive MAE but with notably higher standard deviation (e.g., ±0.16–0.22 vs. PatchTST's ±0.06–0.08), indicating less stable cross-building generalization. Informer performs worst on both tasks, suggesting its architecture is least suited to the heterogeneous, long-tailed nature of building timeseries. Overall, no method substantially surpasses the naive Mode baseline on F1 (0.1296), underscoring that the ontology classification problem remains open.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first publicly available, comprehensive, multi-building timeseries dataset (BTS) standardized under the Brick schema, complementing LBNL59 which covers only a single building with 11 ontology classes. The multi-label timeseries classification benchmark for ontology mapping builds on prior attempts (Zodiac, PLASTER, Rana et al.) that relied on private data and did not release code, while the zero-shot cross-building forecasting task repurposes existing architectures (DLinear, PatchTST, Informer, iTransformer) in a novel deployment scenario where models must generalize across arbitrary ontology permutations. Conceptually, the work sits at the intersection of building analytics, knowledge-graph-augmented multimodal learning, and domain adaptation, but does not propose a new method—its contribution is the resource and benchmark itself.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset addresses a critical bottleneck in building analytics research: the absence of accessible, comprehensive, multi-building real-world data needed to develop interoperable models. The benchmark results reveal that the ontology classification task is essentially unsolved—deep learning models achieve only marginal gains over naive baselines (F1 ~0.36 vs 0.13 for Mode), and zero-shot cross-building forecasting shows substantial room for improvement, particularly given domain shift and long-tailed class distributions. Key limitations include geographic restriction to three Australian non-residential buildings, anonymization that removes operational context, and limited benchmark depth (no full hyperparameter search). Open questions include whether generative AI can synthesize additional building data, how LLMs can interface with Brick-schema knowledge graphs, and whether the domain adaptation challenges identified here generalize to residential or international building stocks.
