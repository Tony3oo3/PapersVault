## Problem

Adversarial Collaborative Filtering (ACF) has been empirically shown to improve both recommendation performance and robustness against poisoning attacks, yet no theoretical explanation exists for why adversarial training at the embedding level benefits CF (in contrast to CV, where adversarial training typically degrades clean performance). Additionally, existing ACF methods apply a uniform perturbation magnitude to all users, leaving potential gains on the table.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243825/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes implicit user-item interaction data (binary feedback) and operates on learned user and item embeddings of dimension d. Unlike input-level adversarial methods, perturbations are applied directly to the parameter embeddings during training. The output is a ranked list of items per user, evaluated via Recall@k, NDCG@k for performance and T-HR@k, T-NDCG@k for attack success ratio. The personalized perturbation coefficient c(u,t) is computed dynamically at each epoch from the current user embedding norm relative to the mean norm across all users.

## Method

PamaCF builds on the APR min-max framework but replaces the uniform perturbation bound ε with a per-user magnitude ε_u = ρ · c(u,t), where c(u,t) = sigmoid((‖u_(t)‖ − mean‖u_(t)‖) / mean‖u_(t)‖) maps each user's embedding scale into (0,1). The theoretical foundation shows (via Theorems 1–4 on a Gaussian recommender model) that ACF provably reduces recommendation error versus standard CF in both clean and α-poisoned settings, and that the error-reduction bounds increase with perturbation magnitude up to a per-user ceiling tied to ‖u‖. In practice, the adversarial perturbation for each user is taken along the gradient direction of the BPR loss with step length ρ·c(u,t), so users with larger embedding norms receive larger perturbations. Training proceeds in two phases: T_pre epochs of standard BPR pre-training followed by adversarial training with the personalized-magnitude loss L_PamaCF = L_BPR(Θ) + λ·L_BPR(Θ + Δ^PamaCF).

## Results

On the Gowalla dataset with MF backbone, PamaCF achieves 13.48% Recall@20 and 10.94% NDCG@20 on clean data, outperforming the best ACF baseline (APR: 13.06%/10.65%) by +2.10%/+2.76%. Across all three datasets and attack types, PamaCF increases average recommendation performance by 13.84% (Recall@20) and 22.04% (NDCG@20) over the backbone model, while reducing the average attack success ratio (T-HR@50) by 49.92% and T-NDCG@50 by 43.73% compared to the best baseline defense method.

## Baselines & Comparisons

PamaCF consistently outperforms all five baselines across all datasets and attack types. Against the closest ACF competitor APR, PamaCF gains +2.10% Recall@20 and +2.76% NDCG@20 on clean Gowalla, with larger margins under attacks (e.g., +3.23% NDCG@20 under DP Attack). SharpCF, which uses sharpness-aware minimization, is outperformed by PamaCF on every metric, with the largest gap in NDCG@20 (10.94 vs. 10.02 on clean Gowalla). Detection-based methods (GraphRfi, LLM4Dec) and the denoise-based StDenoise all degrade recommendation performance relative to the untrained MF backbone (e.g., GraphRfi drops Recall@20 from 11.35% to 10.43% on Gowalla), while PamaCF improves it substantially. On the MIND dataset where LLM4Dec is the only additional baseline, PamaCF still leads with 1.30% vs. 1.20% Recall@20 on clean data. For robustness, PamaCF reduces T-HR@50 by up to 72.10% and T-NDCG@50 by up to 60.15% compared to the best baseline under specific attack configurations on Gowalla.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Adversarial Personalized Ranking (APR) framework [15] and its successor SharpCF [20], which apply a uniform perturbation magnitude ε to all user and item embeddings during adversarial training. The key departure is a theoretical analysis (Theorems 1–4) showing that ACF provably reduces recommendation error in both clean and poisoned settings, and that the error-reduction bounds are tightened by using user-specific perturbation magnitudes tied to embedding norms. This contrasts with the CV adversarial training literature, where a robustness-accuracy trade-off is well established [26], yet no prior work had provided a theoretical account of why ACF in recommender systems improves both performance and robustness simultaneously.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper fills a notable gap by giving the first theoretical justification for ACF's dual benefit (performance + robustness), moving the field beyond purely empirical validation. The practical takeaway—scaling perturbation magnitude by user embedding norm via a simple sigmoid mapping—is easy to adopt and yields consistent gains across three datasets, four attack types, and three backbone models. Limitations include the reliance on a simplified Gaussian single-item model for the theory, the preliminary-experiment bridge to practical BPR-based CF, and the restriction to non-sequential CF; extending the analysis to sequential recommendation and relaxing the Gaussian assumption are natural next steps.
