## Problem

CLIP confuses textual and visual representations in text-visual images, misclassifying visual objects based on misleading text (e.g., calling a dog a 'cat' because the word 'cat' appears in the image) and vice versa. Existing defenses against typographic attacks require additional training (learnable projections, prefixes, or weight interpolation) and typically only extract visual features while ignoring textual ones. The paper addresses the gap of achieving zero-shot disentanglement of both textual and visual factors without any retraining.

## Input / Output

The framework takes a 224×224 RGB image and its horizontally flipped counterpart as dual inputs to CLIP's frozen image encoder (ViT-B/32 or RN50×4), producing two 512-dimensional feature vectors. From these, it generates a binary disentangling mask (512-dim) and two disentangled feature vectors: textual features X^t and visual features X^v. Downstream, either feature vector replaces the original image embedding for zero-shot classification with text prompts (e.g., 'a photo of {}' for image recognition or 'text of {}' for text recognition), yielding a class label or recognized text string.

## Method

The core insight is that CLIP's image features exhibit horizontal flip invariance for visual content (cosine similarity ≈ 0.98) but not for text (drops to ≈ 0.82), because flipping preserves object semantics while rendering text nonsensical. MirrorCLIP exploits this by encoding both original and flipped images, then computing a disentangling mask M = Sign(X ⊙ X^f) that identifies which latent dimensions encode text (sign flip) versus visual content (sign preserved). Rather than hard boolean masking, the method applies soft filters: the textual filter X^t = (X ⊙ M^t) + (X − X^f) ⊙ M^v retains textual signal in both mask regions while subtracting the flip-invariant visual component, and the visual filter X^v = X^f + X ⊙ M^v leverages the flipped features (where text semantics vanish) augmented by the original visual-mask region for robustness to already-flipped inputs. The entire pipeline is training-free and operates entirely in CLIP's frozen latent space.

## Results

On 10 synthetic typographic attack datasets, MirrorCLIP achieves 50.09% average image recognition accuracy, outperforming CLIP (34.60%), Materzynska et al. (37.44%), PAINT (49.93%), and Defense Prefix (44.20%). On 3 real-world typographic attack datasets, MirrorCLIP reaches 68.69% average accuracy, surpassing the best baseline Defense Prefix (64.52%) by +4.17%. For text recognition on typographic images, disentangled textual features boost accuracy from 39.32% (vanilla CLIP) to 73.95%. On clean datasets without text, MirrorCLIP maintains or slightly improves over CLIP (61.95% vs. 61.59% average), confirming no degradation on text-free images.

## Baselines & Comparisons

MirrorCLIP outperforms all four baselines on real-world typographic defense (68.69% avg vs. Defense Prefix 64.52%, Materzynska 63.61%, PAINT 55.00%, CLIP 47.42%), with the largest margin over PAINT (+13.69%). On synthetic typographic datasets, MirrorCLIP (50.09%) edges out PAINT (49.93%) by a narrow 0.16% margin while decisively beating Defense Prefix (44.20%) by +5.89% and Materzynska (37.44%) by +12.65%. Notably, MirrorCLIP is the only zero-shot method that surpasses all training-based baselines (Materzynska, PAINT, Defense Prefix all require additional training) without any parameter updates. On clean datasets, MirrorCLIP slightly improves over CLIP (61.95% vs. 61.59%) and all baselines, confirming that disentanglement does not harm performance on text-free images. For text recognition, MirrorCLIP's disentangled textual features achieve 73.95% accuracy versus 39.32% for vanilla CLIP, a +34.63% improvement that no baseline addresses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on CLIP's contrastive image-text embeddings and departs from prior typographic defense methods (Materzynska et al. 2022, PAINT 2022, Defense Prefix 2023) that all require training additional parameters (projection matrices, learnable prefixes, or weight interpolation) on specified data. The key conceptual departure is exploiting an intrinsic property of CLIP—horizontal flip invariance for visual objects but not for text—to generate disentangling masks in a fully zero-shot manner, drawing on the observation from Ramesh et al. (Stable UnCLIP) that different dimensions of CLIP embeddings encode distinct semantic information. Unlike prior disentanglement work that learns a fixed projection, MirrorCLIP computes a per-image mask by contrasting sign patterns before and after flipping, making it adaptive to each input.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The zero-shot nature of MirrorCLIP is practically significant: it improves typographic defense by +15.49% (synthetic) and +21.27% (real-world) without any retraining, and boosts text recognition from 39.32% to 73.95%, offering a lightweight plug-in for CLIP-based systems. However, the paper acknowledges that deep entanglement prevents full separation (visible in generation artifacts), and the method fails on special palindromes where flipped text retains its shape. Open questions include whether the mirror-effect disentanglement generalizes to other vision-language models beyond CLIP, whether it handles multi-line or stylized text robustly, and whether the soft filters can be further refined to improve generation-quality disentanglement.
