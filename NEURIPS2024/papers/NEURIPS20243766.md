## Problem

Existing multimodal benchmarks evaluate LVLMs using single prompt types (true/false or multiple-choice) and measure only accuracy, creating a disconnect between benchmark scores and real-world user experience where questions arrive in arbitrary formats. This paper identifies that LVLMs frequently produce contradictory answers to the same knowledge point when the prompt format changes, a consistency failure that no prior benchmark or study has systematically measured or analyzed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243766/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

ConBench consists of 1,000 images (filtered for resolution >224×224, absence from mainstream training sets like COCO, and ≥3 foreground objects) sourced from MME, SeedBench, MMBench, and MMMU. Each image is paired with four prompts revolving around one knowledge point: three discriminative questions (true/false, multiple-choice, and format-constrained VQA) plus one open-ended caption prompt. The discriminative metric ConScore[D] awards a point only when all three discriminative answers are simultaneously correct; the generative metric ConScore[C] uses GPT-4 to judge semantic consistency between the caption and each discriminative answer, and the TDR refinement pipeline additionally produces a revised caption after iterative self-verification.

## Method

ConBench constructs its discriminative prompts by first modifying original benchmark questions to prevent text-only inference, then using GPT-4 to generate two additional question types per case, with balanced answer distributions (50/50 for true/false, 25% per option for multiple-choice) to eliminate positional bias. The evaluation spans three hierarchical capability levels—Sensation (50%), Cognition (26%), and Knowledge (24%)—across 19 fine-grained categories. The key analytical finding is that accuracy monotonically decreases as the discriminative solution space expands (true/false → multiple-choice → VQA), while consistency between discriminative answers and the caption increases over the same progression, with Pearson correlations exceeding 0.85. Building on these insights, the Trigger-based Diagnostic Refinement (TDR) method identifies low-confidence words (probability < 0.85) in a generated caption, formulates true/false verification questions around them, and feeds the diagnostic responses back to the LVLM to produce a refined caption, improving ConScore[C] by 9.1–9.6 points on LLaVA-NeXT-34B and MiniGemini-34B without any additional training.

## Results

On ConBench, Qwen-VL-Max achieves the highest discriminative consistency score (ConScore[D] = 37.00%), leading GPT-4-Omni (35.70%) by 1.3 points. In the generative domain, GPT-4-Omni leads with ConScore[C] = 62.21%, the only model surpassing 60, ahead of Qwen-VL-Max (58.42%) by 3.8 points. The proposed Trigger-based Diagnostic Refinement (TDR) method improves LLaVA-NeXT-34B by 9.1 points (48.3→57.4) and MiniGemini-34B by 9.6 points (49.6→60.2) on ConScore[C] without any additional training. Across all 14 evaluated LVLMs, accuracy consistently decreases as the discriminative solution space expands (true/false → multiple-choice → VQA), and closed-source models exhibit a bias advantage of ~3.24 in consistency at equal accuracy levels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Qwen-VL-Max [3] leads in discriminative consistency (37.00%) but trails GPT-4-Omni [1] in generative consistency (58.42% vs. 62.21%), confirming that closed-source GPT models produce more internally coherent captions despite slightly lower discriminative accuracy. GPT-4V [1] ranks 6th in ConScore[D] (29.20%) but jumps to 3rd in ConScore[C] (55.63%), a +3 rank shift that illustrates the consistency bias advantage of closed-source models. Among open-source models, InternVL-v1.2P-40B [8] is the strongest (34.70% ConScore[D], 53.75% ConScore[C]), particularly excelling in Cognition (34.9% consistency), while InternVL-v1.5-26B [7] follows at 31.40%/50.96%. The LLaVA series [18; 19] underperforms: LLaVA-v1.5-7B [18] scores only 16.60% ConScore[D] (last place), and LLaVA-NeXT-34B [19] (27.70%) does not enter the top ten. MiniGemini-34B [15] shows the largest rank improvement from D to C (+4), suggesting its captions are relatively more consistent than its discriminative answers would predict. Gemini-Ultra-Vision [24] (33.10%/54.64%) outperforms Gemini-Pro-Vision [24] (25.00%/48.49%) by a wide margin, indicating model scale matters for consistency. The TDR method applied to LLaVA-NeXT-34B [19] and MiniGemini-34B [15] closes much of the gap to top closed-source models, with MiniGemini-34B+TDR reaching 60.2% ConScore[C], surpassing all open-source baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the LLM consistency literature (Li et al. 2023, who defined generator-validator consistency for text-only models) to the vision-language setting, introducing a solution-space-based formulation that compares answers across true/false, multiple-choice, and open-ended VQA prompts around the same knowledge point. It repurposes images from existing multimodal benchmarks (MME, SeedBench, MMBench, MMMU) but reframes them through a consistency lens rather than a pure accuracy lens, filling a gap the authors identify in prior evaluation. The Trigger-based Diagnostic Refinement method draws directly on the paper's own empirical finding that narrower solution spaces yield higher accuracy, closing the loop between diagnosis and intervention without any training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

ConBench provides the first dedicated consistency evaluation for LVLMs, offering a complementary signal to accuracy that better reflects real-world user experience where questions arrive in arbitrary formats. The consistency bias finding (closed-source models sit systematically above the accuracy-consistency regression line) gives a quantitative explanation for why commercial models feel more reliable despite sometimes lower benchmark accuracy. Open questions include whether the GPT-judged caption consistency metric is robust across model families, whether TDR generalizes beyond the two 34B models tested, and whether training-time consistency objectives could close the gap between open- and closed-source models.
