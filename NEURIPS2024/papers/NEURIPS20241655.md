## Problem

Audio watermarking methods for AI-generated speech perform well under clean conditions, but their robustness against real-world perturbations—ranging from common audio codecs to adversarial attacks—remains largely unexplored. No systematic benchmark exists to evaluate how well these methods resist watermark removal (making AI audio undetectable) or watermark forgery (falsely marking human audio as synthetic), nor whether robustness is fair across demographic groups.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241655/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark operates on 5-second, 16 kHz audio waveforms paired with randomly sampled 16-bit watermark bitstrings. Three watermarking methods (AudioSeal/AudioSeal-B, Timbre, WavMark) each embed a watermark into 20,000 samples per dataset, and the detector outputs a binary watermarked/unwatermarked decision. Robustness is quantified via FNR (for removal) and FPR (for forgery) under 15 perturbation types, with audio quality of perturbed samples measured by SNR and ViSQOL (score ≥ 3 considered acceptable).

## Method

AudioMarkBench is a systematic evaluation framework rather than a new watermarking architecture. It benchmarks three SOTA methods against 15 perturbations organized into three threat levels: 12 no-box perturbations (codecs like EnCodec/Opus/SoundStream/MP3, filters, noise, quantization, time stretch, echo, smoothing), 2 black-box adversarial attacks (HSJA and Square attack adapted from image classifiers to audio waveform/spectrogram domains with 10,000 iterations), and 1 white-box gradient-based optimization (cross-entropy or ReLU loss with SNR-constrained scaling). The benchmark introduces AudioMarkData, a 20,000-sample subset of Common Voice balanced across 25 languages, 2 biological sexes, and 4 age groups, enabling fairness analysis of robustness gaps. Key findings include that all methods are vulnerable to white-box attacks and certain no-box codecs (EnCodec, Opus, SoundStream) at quality-preserving levels, while AudioSeal is most robust and WavMark least robust overall.

## Results

Under no perturbations, all three watermarking methods (AudioSeal, Timbre, WavMark) achieve FPR < 0.01 and FNR < 0.01 with properly selected detection thresholds on both AudioMarkData and LibriSpeech. Under white-box watermark-removal perturbations at SNR=20 dB (ViSQOL ≈ 3.2–3.9), FNR reaches 1.0 for all methods on both datasets, and FPR also reaches 1.0 under white-box forgery. Under no-box EnCodeC perturbations (24 kHz bandwidth), WavMark and AudioSeal-B achieve FNR ≈ 1.0 while AudioSeal maintains lower FNR, and under black-box HSJA with 10,000 spectrogram-domain queries, WavMark achieves SNR/ViSQOL of 40/4.5 (high-quality removal) while AudioSeal maintains SNR/ViSQOL below 10/3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AudioSeal (and its variant AudioSeal-B) is consistently the most robust method across all perturbation settings: it maintains the lowest FNR under no-box EnCodeC/Opus/SoundStream perturbations, resists black-box HSJA longest (SNR/ViSQOL < 10/3 even after 10,000 iterations), and is the first method to reach FNR=0 under white-box attacks (at SNR=50). WavMark is the least robust: its watermarks are removed by Gaussian noise and MP3 compression without quality loss, it achieves FNR=1.0 under EnCodeC at ViSQOL ≥ 3, and under black-box HSJA it reaches SNR/ViSQOL of 40/4.5 after 10,000 iterations. Timbre falls between the two, showing moderate vulnerability to no-box codecs and black-box attacks. AudioSeal-B, which uses bitwise-accuracy detection instead of a global probability, is slightly less robust than AudioSeal under white-box attacks (FNR remains 1.0 at SNR=30 vs. 0.85 for AudioSeal) but comparable under no-box and black-box settings. A notable fairness finding is that female-voiced audios exhibit significantly higher FNR than male-voiced audios under Gaussian noise (p ≈ 2.4×10⁻⁶ for AudioSeal), indicating a robustness gap across biological sex groups.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on three state-of-the-art audio watermarking methods (AudioSeal, Timbre, WavMark) and adapts adversarial attack techniques originally designed for image classifiers (HSJA, Square attack, I-FGSM) to the audio watermarking detection setting. It departs from prior audio watermarking work, which primarily evaluates performance in clean or lightly perturbed conditions, by introducing the first systematic robustness benchmark spanning no-box, black-box, and white-box threat models. The new AudioMarkData dataset extends the Common Voice corpus with balanced sampling across 25 languages, biological sex, and age groups, enabling fairness analysis that prior watermarking evaluations have not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that everyday audio operations (EnCodec, Opus, SoundStream) can completely strip watermarks while preserving perceptual quality, undermining the practical deployability of current methods for disinformation detection. The identified fairness gaps—female-voiced audios being significantly less robust to removal than male-voiced audios, and language-dependent robustness differences—raise important equity concerns for real-world deployment. Open questions include whether watermarking methods can be made robust to the full spectrum of perturbations simultaneously, how to close the sex/language robustness gaps, and whether the 25-language, 4-age-group coverage of AudioMarkData is sufficient for generalizable conclusions.
