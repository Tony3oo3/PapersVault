## Problem

Estimating tight Lipschitz constant upper bounds for deep feedforward neural networks via the LipSDP framework requires solving a single large semidefinite program whose size scales with the total number of neurons across all layers, making it computationally intractable for networks beyond ~30 layers or moderate widths. No existing approach decomposes this monolithic verification problem into layer-sized sub-problems while preserving the tightness of the resulting bound.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240333/cropped_page5_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes the weight matrices {W_i} of an l-layer FNN along with the slope-restriction interval [α, β] of the activation function (the paper focuses on [0,1], covering ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, and ELU). It outputs a single scalar Lipschitz upper bound L = √(1/F), computed as the spectral norm of W_l^T W_l (M_{l-1})^{-1}. The key structural property is that each layer's sub-problem depends only on the previous layer's intermediate matrix M_{i-1} and the current and next weight matrices, so the entire computation proceeds sequentially with no global coupling.

## Method

The paper applies a sequential block Cholesky decomposition to the large block-tridiagonal LipSDP matrix, yielding an exact equivalence (Theorem 3) between the global positive-definiteness condition and a cascade of small matrix inequalities M_i > 0, each of size d_i × d_i (the width of layer i). ECLipsE then decides each diagonal multiplier Λ_i by solving a small SDP (via Schur complement) that maximizes a scalar c_i controlling how much of the next layer's weight geometry is 'absorbed' at the current stage, reducing complexity from O(n⁴m⁴) to O(nm⁴). ECLipsE-Fast further restricts Λ_i to a scalar multiple of identity and derives a closed-form solution λ_i = 2/σ_max(𝓕_i), eliminating all SDP solves and dropping complexity to O(nm³). The geometric intuition is that at each layer the algorithm contracts (or expands) an ellipsoid representing the feasible region to be tangent to the ellipsoid of the next layer's weight matrix, with ECLipsE-Fast approximating this by inscribing/circumscribing balls instead.

## Results

On randomly generated FNNs with 80 neurons per layer, ECLipsE-Fast achieves computation times of 0.051 s (20 layers) and 0.136 s (100 layers), compared to 125 s and >15 min for LipSDP-Layer respectively — a speedup of over 2,400× at 20 layers and >6,600× at 100 layers. ECLipsE runs in 28.5 s at 20 layers versus 303 s for LipSDP-Neuron (~10× faster). In terms of accuracy, the normalized Lipschitz estimates from ECLipsE (0.765) and ECLipsE-Fast (0.881) at 20 layers/80 neurons are nearly identical to LipSDP-Neuron (0.765) and LipSDP-Layer (0.878), while both outperform the trivial bound. On MNIST-trained 3-layer networks, ECLipsE-Fast completes in under 1 s versus ~10 s for LipSDP-Layer, with matching Lipschitz estimates.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against LipSDP-Neuron [14], ECLipsE produces virtually identical normalized Lipschitz estimates (e.g., 0.765 vs. 0.765 at 20 layers/80 neurons) while being ~10× faster (28.5 s vs. 303 s); LipSDP-Neuron fails entirely beyond 30 layers at 80 neurons. Against LipSDP-Layer [14], ECLipsE-Fast matches the Lipschitz estimate closely (0.881 vs. 0.878 at 20 layers) but is ~2,400× faster (0.052 s vs. 125 s), and remains functional at 100 layers where LipSDP-Layer exceeds the 15-min cutoff. Against CPLip [16], both ECLipsE and ECLipsE-Fast are faster at all network sizes beyond 10 layers, and CPLip fails beyond 20 layers; however, CPLip provides slightly tighter (lower) Lipschitz estimates on small networks (e.g., 0.403 vs. 0.737 at 2 layers/80 neurons). Against LipDiff [20], ECLipsE and ECLipsE-Fast produce valid upper bounds, whereas LipDiff returns inadmissible estimates (10–100× the trivial bound at moderate sizes, up to 10⁶× at 50 layers/40 neurons), making direct accuracy comparison meaningless; ECLipsE-Fast is also faster than LipDiff at all tested sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the LipSDP framework (Fazlyab et al., 2019), which formulates Lipschitz constant estimation as a large semidefinite program exploiting slope-restrictedness of activation functions. The key methodological departure is the application of a sequential block Cholesky decomposition—borrowed from cascade control synthesis (Agarwal et al., 2019)—to the LipSDP matrix inequality, yielding an exact decomposition into layer-wise sub-problems. Prior scalability strategies (network splitting in LipSDP, chordal sparsity, dissipativity-based layer-wise LMIs) either sacrifice tightness or require ad hoc partitioning; here the decomposition is structural and exact, with the cascade topology of the FNN naturally inducing the sub-problem boundaries. The two resulting algorithms (ECLipsE solving small SDPs per layer, ECLipsE-Fast using a closed-form spectral-norm relaxation) occupy distinct points on the accuracy–efficiency spectrum not previously available in the LipSDP family.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is a dramatic reduction in certification time for deep networks—thousands of times faster than LipSDP-Layer for ECLipsE-Fast—while maintaining Lipschitz bounds comparable to or tighter than state-of-the-art SDP methods, making online robustness certification feasible. A notable limitation is that ECLipsE (the more accurate variant) still struggles with very wide networks (failing beyond ~300 neurons per layer for 50-layer nets), whereas ECLipsE-Fast remains efficient even at width 1000. Open questions include extending the compositional approach to CNNs (currently requiring unrolling to fully-connected form), residual architectures with skip connections, and whether sparse matrix or autodiff-based acceleration can further close the gap for wide networks. The work is particularly relevant for safety-critical deployment where Lipschitz certificates must be computed repeatedly during training or adaptation.
