## Problem

Most VLM-based zero-shot methods (e.g., prompt learning) evaluate in a base-to-novel setting where test samples are pre-divided into seen or unseen classes, which is unrealistic for practical deployment. Generalized zero-shot learning (GZSL), where the model must classify over the union of seen and unseen classes without knowing the division, has not been addressed in the VLM context. Additionally, finetuning CLIP via prompt learning introduces a weak generalizability problem where learned prompts overfit to seen classes and degrade performance on unseen classes.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image and a ChatGPT-generated descriptive paragraph per class (rather than simple class names or attribute vectors) as input. Frozen CLIP ViT-B/32 encoders produce 512-dimensional visual and linguistic features, while a BERT encoder extracts 768-dimensional features for a 5,996-word static attribute vocabulary. At inference, the model performs nearest-neighbor search in the latent space over all seen and unseen class text features, outputting a single class label without any seen/unseen indicator.

## Method

TPR introduces two core designs on top of frozen CLIP. First, a dual-space feature alignment module encodes visual features via cross-attention and self-attention with a projected attribute reservoir (static vocabulary + learnable tokens), producing representations in both a latent space and an attribute space; linguistic features are encoded via cross-attention only to avoid overfitting. Contrastive losses are applied independently in both spaces. Second, a topology-preserving loss uses the Pearson correlation coefficient to constrain the pairwise cosine-similarity matrix of finetuned attribute-space class text features to match that of the original CLIP text embeddings, thereby preserving the semantic class-angle structure and inheriting CLIP's balanced generalization across seen and unseen classes. Only the projection matrices, attention layers, and learnable attribute tokens are trained; all CLIP weights remain frozen.

## Results

TPR achieves the best harmonic mean (H) on 11 out of 12 datasets, with particularly strong gains on fine-grained benchmarks: 32.53% H on CUB (vs. 29.74% for CLIP), 70.45% H on FLO (vs. 52.50% for CLIP), and 32.87% H on FGVC-Aircraft (vs. 13.84% for CLIP). On the coarse-grained AwA2 dataset, TPR reaches 81.63% H (S=87.10, U=76.81), surpassing the best prompt-learning baseline PromptSRC (76.82% H) by 4.81 points. The only dataset where TPR does not achieve the best H is SUN (47.80 vs. CLIP's 47.84), a near-tie. When paired with the stronger EVA-02 backbone, TPR further pushes SOTA, e.g., 86.39% H on AwA2 and 40.36% H on CUB.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TPR outperforms all nine baselines on 11 of 12 datasets in terms of harmonic mean, with the sole exception being SUN where CLIP edges out TPR by 0.04 points (47.84 vs. 47.80). Against the five prompt-learning methods (CoOp, CoCoOp, MaPLe, PromptSRC, ProGrad), TPR achieves an average absolute improvement of +11.94% on H, with the largest gaps on fine-grained datasets (e.g., +12.79% H on CUB over CoOp, +13.05% on FGVC-Aircraft over ProGrad). Against the three generative-based GZSL methods (CE, LSA, ZLAP), TPR gains an average +10.68% on H, most notably +19.69% on FGVC-Aircraft over CE and +9.69% on Food101 over ZLAP. A critical distinction is that prompt-learning baselines often degrade unseen-class accuracy relative to zero-shot CLIP (e.g., CoOp drops from 29.61% to 18.23% U on CUB), whereas TPR consistently improves unseen performance (26.87% U on CUB vs. CLIP's 29.61% is a slight drop, but TPR's seen accuracy jumps to 41.22%, yielding a much higher H). The topology-preserving objective is the key mechanism enabling this: it maintains CLIP's pairwise class-angle structure, preventing the seen-class bias that plagues other fine-tuning approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the prompt learning line of work (CoOp, CoCoOp, MaPLe, PromptSRC, ProGrad) by shifting evaluation from the base-to-novel setting to the more realistic generalized zero-shot learning (GZSL) setting, where the model must classify both seen and unseen classes simultaneously without knowing the split. It departs from prior VLM-based ZSL methods that rely on a single shared latent space by introducing a dual-space alignment (latent + attribute) built around a novel attribute reservoir combining static vocabulary from multiple attribute datasets with learnable tokens. The topology-preserving objective, which uses Pearson correlation to maintain pairwise class angles relative to CLIP's original embeddings, is a new regularization strategy not seen in prior prompt learning or GZSL work, and it specifically targets the weak generalization problem identified by CoCoOp.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By addressing GZSL under the VLM context—a setting the authors note no prior work has evaluated—this paper provides a more practical and challenging benchmark for zero-shot generalization that better reflects real deployment. The topology-preserving loss offers a principled, lightweight mechanism to inherit CLIP's generalization during fine-tuning, potentially applicable to other VLM adaptation scenarios beyond ZSL. Limitations include reliance on LLM-generated text descriptions that may not capture fine-grained visual nuances (e.g., CUB), and the method's performance on generic scene datasets (SUN) remains weaker than on object-centric benchmarks. Open questions include whether combining expert attribute annotations with LLM text could close the remaining gap, and whether the approach extends to video or few-shot settings.
