## Problem

Queuing network control is a fundamental problem in manufacturing, healthcare, and communications, yet prior RL studies evaluate policies on a small number of idealized (exponential, stationary) instances with no common simulation environment or benchmark suite. There is no unified framework that supports general non-exponential and non-stationary event times, diverse network topologies (parallel, tandem, re-entrant, criss-cross), and systematic comparison of RL methods against classical queuing policies across realistic problem instances.

## Input / Output

The controller observes only queue lengths and the current timestamp (o_k = (t_k, Q(t_k))), while the full state includes residual inter-arrival and service times, making the problem technically a POMDP. The action is a binary M×N assignment matrix mapping jobs to servers, constrained by a network topology matrix B and server-pool capacities. The primary evaluation metric is the long-run time-averaged total queue length, estimated over 100 trajectories per policy, with the simulation advancing by discrete events (arrivals or service completions) rather than fixed time steps.

## Method

QGym is an event-driven discrete-event simulation framework built on the OpenAI Gym interface that advances time by the next occurring event (minimum residual arrival or service time) rather than fixed timesteps, supporting arbitrary user-specified arrival and service time distributions. The key RL contribution is a work-conserving (WC) policy parameterization that masks action probabilities for empty queues via a differentiable re-normalization, which the authors show is decisive: vanilla PPO fails to stabilize in every tested setting, while PPO-WC outperforms all classical baselines in 77% of instances. Additional stabilizing modifications include a behavior-cloning warm-up that imitates a MaxWeight-style policy, and truncating the GAE horizon at regenerative points (when all queues are simultaneously empty). The framework also supports job-level tracking for parallel-server systems, server pools to avoid matrix inflation, and batch GPU-parallel simulation for scalable training.

## Results

The primary metric is the time-averaged total queue length (lower is better), evaluated over 100 trajectories per policy. PPO-WC achieves a 77% pairwise win rate over all traditional queuing baselines across 20 problem instances. In the Reentrant (Exponential) network with L=2, PPO-WC achieves 13.6±0.4, outperforming MaxWeight (14.8±0.4) and cμ-rule (19.0±0.4). Under hyper-exponential noise (L=2), PPO-WC achieves 29.9±0.7 versus cμ-rule's 31.69±1.3, yielding a ~21% relative reduction in holding costs over the best traditional baseline. However, on the large-scale Hospital network (497 servers), PPO-WC (2.3E+4) still underperforms cμ-rule (4.4E+2), highlighting the sample-inefficiency gap in larger systems.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the cμ-rule, PPO-WC wins in most small-to-medium networks (e.g., 13.6 vs. 19.0 on Reentrant L=2; 15.4 vs. 16.1 on Criss-Cross) but loses on the large Hospital network (2.3E+4 vs. 4.4E+2). Against MaxWeight, PPO-WC achieves clear wins on small networks (L=2: 13.6 vs. 14.8; L=3: 22.6 vs. 24.8) but MaxWeight becomes competitive or superior on larger reentrant networks (L=6: MW 49.2 vs. PPO-WC 48.5; L=10: MW 80.1 vs. PPO-WC 83.1). Against Maximum Pressure and Fluid Policy, PPO-WC consistently wins across all reentrant instances, with gaps widening as L increases (e.g., L=10: PPO-WC 83.1 vs. MP 100.5, FP 93.6). Vanilla PPO fails catastrophically in every setting (queue lengths of 10^3–10^5), confirming that without the work-conserving inductive bias, RL cannot stabilize the system. PPO-BC (behavior cloning warm-up) improves stability but still underperforms traditional baselines in most instances, showing that stabilization alone is insufficient without the structural constraint.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the PPO algorithm (Schulman et al., 2017) and the RL-for-queuing framework of Dai & Gluzman (2022), which established behavior cloning as a warm-up strategy and the training hyperparameter conventions. However, it departs from prior RL-for-queuing work in two key ways: (1) it replaces the discrete-time MDP formulation (which requires exponential inter-event times) with an event-driven simulation that supports arbitrary, non-stationary event distributions, and (2) it introduces a work-conserving inductive bias into the policy parameterization that prior methods lacked. The framework also bridges the gap between RL benchmarking (OpenAI Gym style) and industrial discrete-event simulation (Simio, AnyLogic), which previously existed in separate ecosystems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

QGym provides the first unified, open-source benchmark for comparing RL and classical queuing policies across diverse network topologies (parallel servers, reentrant, criss-cross, tandem) and realistic calibrations (hospital, input-queue switch), filling a gap where prior work evaluated algorithms on only a handful of instances. The finding that vanilla PPO fails to stabilize in every setting, while a simple work-conserving mask enables it to beat classical policies in 77% of instances, provides a clear design principle for future RL-for-queuing research. However, the paper also reveals that RL still underperforms on large-scale realistic networks (e.g., the 497-server hospital system), pointing to sample inefficiency as a major open challenge. The observation that RL gains are larger under hyper-exponential (high-variance) noise suggests a promising direction for targeting RL to settings where classical policies are weakest.
