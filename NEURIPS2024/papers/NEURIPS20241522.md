## Problem

Prior deep learning research on time-series forecasting has fragmented into two isolated streams—long-term point forecasting with customized architectures and short-term probabilistic forecasting with generative models—each evaluated only in its native regime. This narrow focus produces skewed methodological choices (e.g., universal adoption of NAR decoding and RevIN in one stream, mean scaling in the other) and leaves critical questions unanswered, such as whether long-term point-forecasting architectures generalize to distributional estimation or whether short-term probabilistic models survive extended horizons. The rapid emergence of time-series foundation models claiming arbitrary-horizon and probabilistic capabilities further underscores the need for a unified evaluation framework.

## Input / Output

ProbTS consumes multivariate time series with a fixed lookback window (96 steps for long-term scenarios, 24–30 for short-term) plus optional covariates, and produces both point forecasts and distributional forecasts (100 samples drawn from the model's estimated conditional distribution) across prediction horizons ranging from 24 to 720 steps. A distinguishing feature is that the benchmark quantifies three intrinsic data characteristics per scenario—trend strength F_T, seasonality strength F_S (via STL decomposition), and non-Gaussianity (via Jensen-Shannon divergence from a fitted Gaussian)—to contextualize why certain methodological choices succeed or fail on specific datasets. All models are scored with unified metrics (NMAE for point, CRPS for distributional) computed after de-normalization to the original scale, ensuring fair cross-stream comparison.

## Method

ProbTS is a benchmarking toolkit (built on PyTorch Lightning, PyTorchTS, GluonTS, and TSlib) that explicitly decomposes forecasting models into an encoder f_φ and a forecaster p_θ, then systematically varies three orthogonal methodological axes: (1) the distributional estimation approach (point head vs. predefined distribution heads vs. normalizing flows/diffusion), (2) the decoding scheme (autoregressive h_t = f_φ(x_{t-1}, c_t, h_{t-1}) vs. non-autoregressive h_{t+1:t+T} = f_φ(x_{t-L:t}, c_{t-L:t+T})), and (3) the normalization strategy (RevIN vs. mean scaling vs. none). The benchmark evaluates 10+ classical models from both research streams and 8 time-series foundation models (Lag-Llama, TimesFM, Timer, MOIRAI, Chronos, UniTS, ForecastPFN, TTM) in zero-shot or fully-supervised settings across 12 dataset-horizon combinations. Key empirical findings include: AR decoding suffers error accumulation over long horizons with strong trends (mitigated by RevIN but not by mean scaling), NAR probabilistic models like CSDI face memory and learning-efficiency bottlenecks at long horizons, and foundation models with predefined mixture or quantized distribution heads degrade notably on high-non-Gaussianity data compared to flow- or diffusion-based probabilistic models.

## Results

ProbTS benchmarks 18+ models across 12 datasets (short-term and long-term horizons) using NMAE (point) and CRPS (distributional) metrics. On short-term forecasting, CSDI achieves the best CRPS on 4 of 5 datasets (e.g., 0.008 on Exchange-S, 0.050 on Electricity-S, 0.146 on Traffic-S), while iTransformer achieves the best NMAE on Traffic-S (0.158). On long-term forecasting, probabilistic models like TimeGrad and GRU NVP show significant CRPS degradation (e.g., TimeGrad CRPS rises sharply with horizon on ETTm1-L), and AR-based foundation models (TimesFM, Timer) lose their short-horizon advantage over NAR models (MOIRAI) as prediction length increases from 24 to 720 steps. RevIN normalization substantially improves AR models in long-term settings (e.g., GRU NVP with RevIN outperforms PatchTST on ETTh1-L) but can hurt NAR probabilistic models like CSDI on Weather-L.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central finding is that model strengths are highly scenario-dependent: long-term point forecasting models (iTransformer, PatchTST, TimesNet, N-HiTS, DLinear) that dominate on long horizons lose their edge in short-term settings, where probabilistic models (CSDI, TimeGrad, GRU NVP/MAF, Trans MAF) achieve substantially lower CRPS—e.g., CSDI's CRPS of 0.050 on Electricity-S vs. PatchTST's 0.067, and 0.219 on Wikipedia-S vs. 0.257. Conversely, probabilistic models degrade sharply in long-term distributional forecasting, with TimeGrad and GRU NVP showing large CRPS increases as horizon extends, while NAR models like CSDI face memory and efficiency constraints. Among AR vs. NAR decoding, AR models (TimeGrad, TimesFM, Timer) excel at capturing strong seasonality (e.g., Traffic) and short horizons but suffer error accumulation over long horizons with strong trends; NAR models (CSDI, MOIRAI, iTransformer) avoid this but are less efficient for probabilistic long-term forecasting. RevIN normalization rescues AR models in long-term point forecasting (GRU NVP + RevIN beats PatchTST on ETTh1-L) but does not consistently help NAR probabilistic models. For foundation models, MOIRAI (NAR) maintains competitive NMAE across all horizons (e.g., 0.128 at 24, 0.515 at 720 on ETTm1-L), while AR-based TimesFM and Timer show widening gaps vs. MOIRAI as horizon grows, and all foundation models underperform CSDI on high non-Gaussianity datasets for distributional accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

ProbTS unifies two previously siloed research streams in deep time-series forecasting: long-term point forecasting (iTransformer, PatchTST, TimesNet, N-HiTS) and short-term probabilistic forecasting (TimeGrad, CSDI, GRU NVP/MAF). It builds on existing toolkits (PyTorchTS, GluonTS, TSlib) but introduces a unified evaluation framework that simultaneously assesses point and distributional accuracy across short and long horizons, explicitly dissecting methodological choices (AR vs. NAR decoding, normalization strategy, distributional head design) that prior work treated as independent. The paper further extends this analytical lens to recently emerged time-series foundation models (Lag-Llama, TimesFM, MOIRAI, Chronos, Timer, UniTS), which had not been systematically compared under a common protocol spanning both forecasting paradigms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies long-term distributional forecasting as a critical yet underexplored challenge, showing that existing probabilistic models degrade substantially as horizons extend regardless of decoding scheme. It reveals that RevIN normalization can dramatically improve AR-based models in long-term settings (even surpassing NAR models on some datasets) while simultaneously harming NAR probabilistic models, pointing to an unresolved normalization design problem. For foundation models, the finding that predefined distribution heads (mixture, Student-t, quantized bins) struggle with high non-Gaussianity suggests a ceiling on their distributional expressiveness. Open questions include: effective normalization for NAR probabilistic models, mitigating AR error accumulation over extended horizons, and whether more flexible distribution estimation (e.g., normalizing flows or diffusion) can close the gap for foundation models on complex distributions.
