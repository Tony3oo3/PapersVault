## Problem

Existing harmful content datasets are limited in scope (covering only narrow object categories like guns or knives), restricted to real-world images without synthesized or video content, and ignore contextual information, leading to false positives when harmless objects appear in benign settings. This restricts the generalizability of detection methods and leaves a gap in handling the growing volume of AI-generated harmful media.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243588/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The annotation framework consumes individual images (512×512 for SDXL-generated, variable for crawled) or 5 uniformly sampled frames from videos (trimmed to ≤4,000 frames before extraction) along with a text prompt containing the harmfulness definition from HarmBench and Safe Latent Diffusion. Each sample produces a binary harmful/unharmful decision, a free-text reason (e.g., 'Yes, adult content'), a 10-class category label, and the full two-round debate transcript from both debaters. The complete dataset of 10,000 images and 1,000 videos includes equal splits of harmful/unharmful and real/synthesized content, with 10,228,011 words of debate arguments stored alongside each annotation.

## Method

The core contribution is a multi-agent VQA annotation framework where three GPT-4V instances, orchestrated via AutoGen, play the roles of affirmative debater, negative debater, and judge in a two-round debate over whether a given image or video is harmful. In-context learning is applied iteratively: misclassified samples from a human-validated subset are selected (2–4 at a time) as context examples to recalibrate the annotator, improving TPR from 72.73% to 87.27% on images. The dataset is constructed by expanding 45 keywords into 4,938 prompts via Llama3, then generating images with SDXL-1.0 and videos with AnimateDiff-v3, AnimateDiff-SDXL, and HiGen, alongside real content crawled from Google Images, YouTube, and Pexels. Ten harmful categories are distilled by GPT-4V from all annotated reasons, and for downstream evaluation the paper applies soft prompt tuning on InstructBLIP to demonstrate the dataset's utility for training general-purpose harmfulness detectors.

## Results

On VHD11K, GPT-4V with a long prompt achieves the highest average harmfulness detection accuracy at 78.60% (images) and 79.60% (videos), while all other methods remain below 70% average accuracy, revealing a systematic inability of existing detectors to handle the full spectrum of harmful content. Among dedicated detectors, Hive AI leads at 67.55% (images) and 67.30% (videos), whereas NudeNet and Q16 perform near chance level (50.93% and 55.08% on images). Finetuning InstructBLIP with soft prompt tuning on VHD11K-Images improves average accuracy from 68.60% (pretrained) to 75.50% on the VHD11K test split and from 77.51% to 85.21% on the SMID test split, outperforming InstructBLIP finetuned on SMID (68.90% and 73.37% respectively).

## Baselines & Comparisons

All eight baselines exhibit a strong bias toward classifying samples as unharmful, with per-class accuracies on harmful images ranging from 0.60% (CogVLM, long prompt) to 81.44% (InstructBLIP, long prompt), while unharmful accuracies often exceed 90%. GPT-4V with a long prompt is the only method to exceed 75% average accuracy on both images (78.60%) and videos (79.60%), and also achieves the best multi-class accuracy (70.40% images, 70.70% videos). Among dedicated detectors, Hive AI (67.55% images, 67.30% videos) substantially outperforms Q16 (55.08%, 61.60%), HOD (59.31%, 56.50%), and NudeNet (50.93%, 50.80%), confirming that narrow object-based detectors fail on the broader harmful-concept spectrum. InstructBLIP with a long prompt collapses on videos (100% harmful / 0% unharmful), while CogVLM degrades to near-random performance with long prompts (50.29% images, 52.20% videos). When InstructBLIP is finetuned via soft prompt tuning, VHD11K-Images training yields 85.21% average accuracy on the SMID test set versus 73.37% for SMID-trained InstructBLIP, demonstrating that VHD11K provides a more effective training signal than the prior SMID dataset.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the harmful content detection line of work (Q16, HOD, NudeNet, Hive AI) from narrow, object-level detection to full-spectrum contextual harmfulness recognition, and from real images only to a multimodal setting including synthesized images and videos. The annotation framework draws on the multi-agent conversation paradigm of AutoGen and in-context learning, repurposing GPT-4V as a debating annotator rather than a single-shot classifier. The dataset itself generalizes SMID (the only prior comprehensive harmful-content dataset) by adding synthesized content, video modality, and a broader taxonomy of 10 harmful categories distilled via LLM from VLM debate arguments.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

VHD11K is the first publicly available harmful-content dataset to include both synthesized (AI-generated) and video content, addressing a critical gap as generative models make harmful content creation trivial. The multi-agent debate annotation framework is a reusable pipeline that could be adapted to other moderation tasks, and the 10.2M words of debate arguments provide a rich resource for understanding harmfulness reasoning. However, the annotation relies entirely on GPT-4V (a single model family), the 82.5% human-alignment rate leaves room for systematic errors, and no error bars are reported across the 8-method benchmark, limiting confidence in the comparative conclusions. Open questions include whether the debate framework's gains over single-shot VLM annotation persist with non-GPT-4V annotators and whether the 10-category taxonomy is stable across cultures and content domains.
