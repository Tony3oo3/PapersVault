## Problem

Feedback alignment avoids the biologically implausible weight transport problem by using fixed random feedback weights, but it requires extensive data training to align forward weights with those feedback weights and significantly underperforms backpropagation. The brain, by contrast, appears ready to learn with very limited experience, suggesting a mechanism for pre-aligning synaptic connections before sensory data are encountered. This paper asks whether pretraining with random noise—mimicking prenatal spontaneous neural activity—can close the performance gap between feedback alignment and backpropagation.

## Input / Output

The network is a multi-layer feedforward MLP (typically 2–4 layers, 100 hidden neurons) that takes flattened images (784-dim for MNIST/Fashion-MNIST, 3072-dim for CIFAR-10/100, 1024-dim for STL-10) and outputs a softmax probability distribution over d classes. During the pretraining phase, inputs are sampled from a Gaussian distribution N(0, I) and labels from a discrete uniform distribution U(0, d−1) with no input-label correlation; during subsequent data training, standard labeled images are used. The pretraining phase is task-agnostic and dataset-agnostic, requiring no access to real data.

## Method

The proposed method is a two-phase training procedure: (1) pretrain the network with random Gaussian noise inputs paired with random uniform labels using the feedback alignment algorithm (fixed random feedback weights B_l replacing transposed forward weights in the backward pass), and (2) train on actual task data. The key mechanism is that random noise training drives the alignment angle between forward weights W_l and feedback weights B_l toward zero, making the FA error signal approximate the true backpropagation gradient before any real data are seen. This pre-alignment is order-dependent—random training must precede data training, as data training alone cannot recover the alignment lost or never achieved. Additionally, random noise training reduces the effective rank of the weight matrices (measured via Shannon entropy of normalized singular values), acting as a pre-regularization that biases the network toward low-rank solutions and thereby improves generalization, including on out-of-distribution and meta-learning tasks.

## Results

On a two-layer feedforward network with feedback alignment, random noise pretraining (FA w/) achieves 97.76% on MNIST, 88.26% on Fashion-MNIST, 53.58% on CIFAR-10, 24.45% on CIFAR-100, and 41.01% on STL-10, narrowing the gap to backpropagation (BP) to within 0.06–1.71 percentage points. The improvement over vanilla feedback alignment (FA w/o) is largest on harder datasets: +4.81% on STL-10, +4.28% on CIFAR-100, and +3.04% on CIFAR-10. The paper also reports that random noise pretraining reduces generalization error, improves out-of-distribution accuracy (transformed MNIST, USPS), and lowers meta-loss across three tasks (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, Kuzushiji-MNIST).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Compared to backpropagation (BP), FA with random noise pretraining is nearly matched on MNIST (97.76% vs. 97.82%, −0.06 pp) and CIFAR-100 (24.45% vs. 24.55%, −0.10 pp), and trails by a small margin on CIFAR-10 (53.58% vs. 54.01%, −0.43 pp) and STL-10 (41.01% vs. 42.72%, −1.71 pp). Compared to vanilla feedback alignment without pretraining (FA w/o), the proposed method wins on every dataset, with the largest absolute gains on the harder benchmarks: +4.81 pp on STL-10, +4.28 pp on CIFAR-100, and +3.04 pp on CIFAR-10, while the gain on the easier MNIST is modest (+0.49 pp). The paper further shows that the convergence speed (AUC of test accuracy) of the randomly pretrained FA network is statistically indistinguishable from BP (P < 0.001 for the difference vs. FA w/o, but the gap to BP is not significant), and that out-of-distribution generalization (transformed MNIST, USPS) is significantly better than FA w/o (P < 0.001 in both cases).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Feedback Alignment (FA) [Lillicrap et al., 2016], which replaces the transposed forward weights in backpropagation with fixed random feedback matrices to avoid the weight transport problem. The key departure is the addition of a random-noise pretraining phase before data training, inspired by spontaneous neural activity in prenatal brains, rather than modifying the learning rule itself as in weight mirror [Akrout et al., 2019] or phaseless alignment learning [Max et al., 2024]. Unlike those approaches that incorporate noise as an ongoing mechanism during data training, here noise is used purely as a structural pre-conditioning step: the vanilla FA algorithm is run on Gaussian inputs with random labels, and only then does the network encounter real data. The paper also draws on developmental neuroscience literature on prenatal spontaneous activity to motivate why such a phase might be biologically natural.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a remarkably simple, task-agnostic pretraining recipe (train with random noise, then train with data) that narrows the performance gap between biologically plausible feedback alignment and backpropagation, with potential practical relevance for neuromorphic hardware where weight transport is infeasible. However, the evidence is confined to small feedforward networks on relatively low-dimensional image datasets (MNIST through CIFAR-100), and the authors explicitly note that scalability to CNNs and other architectures remains untested. Open questions include whether the pre-regularization (effective rank reduction) effect persists at scale, whether the method benefits standard backpropagation (hinted at but deferred to follow-up), and whether the order-dependence finding (noise must precede data) generalizes beyond two-layer networks. The connection to developmental neuroscience is suggestive but remains a computational analogy rather than a validated biological mechanism.
