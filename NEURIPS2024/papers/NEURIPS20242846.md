## Problem

End-to-end speech-to-speech translation (S2ST) systems struggle to match cascade pipelines due to the compounded complexity of jointly performing recognition, translation, and synthesis, compounded by severe scarcity of paired S2ST training data. Additionally, existing S2ST models lack the ability to preserve the source speaker's voice identity and to control the duration of generated speech (isochrony), both of which are critical for practical applications such as video dubbing.

## Input / Output

The system takes a source speech utterance (16 kHz waveform) as input and produces a translated speech waveform in the target language. During inference, up to 10 seconds of the source speech serves as a prompt for the joint translation model and up to 5 seconds for the NAR acoustic model; if the utterance is shorter, the entire clip is used. The output speech is constrained to match the source duration (isochrony) and to retain the source speaker's timbre, with the codec operating at 50 tokens/second across 16 RVQ layers.

## Method

TransVIP decomposes S2ST into two sequential generation stages under a single end-to-end inference: (1) an autoregressive joint encoder-decoder model, initialized from SeamlessM4T, that simultaneously outputs translated text tokens and first-layer codec tokens by maximizing the joint probability P(Y′,T|X) = P(Y′|X,T)·P(T|X) via beam search, and (2) a textless non-autoregressive transformer that fills in the remaining 15 RVQ layers conditioned on the first-layer codes and a cross-lingual speech prompt. The input is disentangled into three channels—semantic (frozen SeamlessM4T encoder), acoustic (a 6-layer transformer sum-pooled into a single embedding that replaces a separator token), and isochrony (position + reversed-position + VAD embeddings concatenated with semantic features via cross-attention)—allowing each to be learned independently while jointly conditioning generation. Training leverages multi-task learning over S2ST, ST, and ASR datasets to overcome paired-data scarcity, and the SASCodec (built on SpeechTokenizer + DAC) incorporates multilingual semantic distillation from an MMS encoder to make the first-layer codes amenable to language modeling. A novel Layer Beam Search decoding strategy, which samples from top-K candidates per token to manage the combinatorial RVQ vocabulary, improves NAR acoustic model output quality over greedy decoding.

## Results

On the CVSS-T Fr-En test set (300 utterances), TransVIP achieves a speech BLEU of 32.60, outperforming SeamlessExpressive (30.85) by 1.75 points and SeamlessM4T-Medium (28.95) by 3.65 points. For voice preservation, TransVIP attains speaker similarity (SIM) of 0.320 (Fr-En) and 0.395 (En-Fr), surpassing SeamlessExpressive (0.256 and 0.335) while baselines without voice conditioning score near zero. On isochrony control, TransVIP achieves SLC₀.₂ of 0.70 (Fr-En) and 0.81 (En-Fr), exceeding SeamlessExpressive by 0.20 and 0.23 respectively, and the best speech rate compliance (0.55 Fr-En, 0.45 En-Fr). In the En-Fr direction, TransVIP matches SeamlessExpressive on BLEU (27.28 vs 27.39) while leading on SIM, A.PCP, and all isochrony metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the textless S2UT XM model (SpeechMatrix, 1.2B), TransVIP dominates on every metric: BLEU improves from 15.45 to 32.60 (Fr-En), SIM from 0.035 to 0.320, and SLC₀.₂ from 0.52 to 0.70, confirming that an intermediate text representation is essential for reliable S2ST. Compared to SeamlessM4T-Medium (1.0B), TransVIP wins decisively on BLEU (+3.65 Fr-En, +6.22 En-Fr), SIM (near-zero for SeamlessM4T vs. 0.320/0.395 for TransVIP), and all isochrony metrics, while trailing only slightly on text BLEU due to smaller S2TT training data. Against the strongest baseline, SeamlessExpressive (1.7B), TransVIP (1.1B) wins on BLEU in Fr-En (32.60 vs 30.85), SIM in both directions, speech rate compliance, and SLC in both directions; it trails on A.PCP in Fr-En (2.49 vs 2.74) and naturalness in En-Fr (3.40 vs 3.57), though the naturalness gap narrows substantially in Fr-En (3.19 vs 2.91). For the SASCodec, it outperforms SpeechTokenizer on SIM (0.871 vs 0.847 at 4 kbps) and WER (1.34 vs 1.44), and matches or exceeds DAC on naturalness while maintaining competitive reconstruction fidelity across all bandwidths.

## Relation to Prior Work

TransVIP builds on the codec-language-model paradigm established by VALL-E (AR first-layer + NAR remaining RVQ layers) and extends it to speech-to-speech translation by introducing a consecutive generation framework that jointly optimizes text and coarse-grained codec tokens within a single autoregressive decoder, initialized from SeamlessM4T. It departs from prior S2ST systems (Translatotron, PolyVoice, AudioPaLM, VioLA) that either attempt direct speech-to-speech mapping or perform multiple separate autoregressive passes, by unifying translation and speech generation into one joint probability while using a separate textless non-autoregressive model for acoustic detail. The codec design (SASCodec) merges SpeechTokenizer's semantic distillation with DAC's factorized L2-normalized quantization, and the isochrony control via encoder-side positional/VAD embeddings differs from decoder-side length-control strategies used in prior dubbing work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

TransVIP demonstrates that a 1.1B-parameter system can match or exceed a 1.7B SeamlessExpressive model on S2ST while adding voice preservation and isochrony control—capabilities critical for practical video dubbing that most prior S2ST systems lack. The finding that a textless NAR acoustic model (relying solely on semantic distillation in the codec) matches or surpasses text-conditioned variants challenges the assumption that phonetic/textual inputs are necessary for intelligible acoustic generation, and opens the door to training on arbitrarily segmented unsupervised audio. Key limitations include evaluation on only the Fr-En pair with ~5k hours of data (vs. 50k+ in prior work), occasional intonation/emotion drift, and the absence of many-to-many multilingual coverage, leaving open whether the approach scales to broader language pairs and whether finer-grained prosodic control can be achieved.
