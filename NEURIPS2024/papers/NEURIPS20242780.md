## Problem

Standard imitation learning assumes causal consistency between the expert's and imitator's environments, but this breaks down when unobserved confounders contaminate expert demonstrations. Prior causal imitation methods require full point identification of system dynamics, which is often infeasible in practice. This paper addresses the partially identifiable setting where either the transition distribution or the reward function cannot be uniquely recovered from confounded demonstrations, and asks whether robust imitation is still achievable.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242780/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes expert demonstration trajectories (state-action pairs S_t, X_t) sampled from a confounded observational distribution, along with a parametric reward family R that contains the expert's nominal reward. The reward Y_t is generally unobserved to the learner. The output is a stationary policy π(x|s) over finite action and state spaces, guaranteed to achieve at least expert performance (E_π[R₁] ≥ E[R₁]) in the worst-case MDP instance compatible with the demonstration data and causal graph. Rewards are bounded in [0,1] and the horizon is infinite with a fixed discount factor γ.

## Method

The paper first proves an impossibility result: when both the transition T and reward R are non-identifiable due to unobserved confounders, no policy can be guaranteed to match expert performance across all compatible MDP instances (Thm. 1). For the two partially identifiable settings, the authors augment GAIL with partial identification bounds. CAIL-R (Alg. 1) handles identifiable T with non-identifiable R by weighting the discriminator's imitator signal with the propensity score P(x|s), reducing the minimax program to matching weighted occupancy measures P(x|s)ρ(s) vs. P(x|s)π(x|s)ρ_π(s). CAIL-T (Alg. 2) handles identifiable R with non-identifiable T by collecting imitator trajectories from the worst-case occupancy measure, obtained by iteratively solving linear programs that allocate transition probability mass to minimize the Bellman value subject to observational bounds on T. Both algorithms are implemented as adversarial training loops with a discriminator network and a policy network updated via forward RL.

## Results

In the fully confounded MDP_obs setting (1000 random instances), all imitators consistently fail to match expert performance, with prevalent negative performance gaps validating Theorem 1; in one detailed example, CAIL achieves a gap of E_π[R_t] − E[R_t] = −1.9019 yet still outperforms BC and GAIL. In the MDP_obs-Y (confounded reward) driving experiment, CAIL consistently outperforms both BC and GAIL across 100 trajectories in both convergence and final expected return. In the MDP_obs-S (confounded transition) medical treatment experiment on MIMIC-III, CAIL achieves the highest expected return among all strategies, while BC and GAIL fail to match expert performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares CAIL (both CAIL-R and CAIL-T variants) against two baselines: Behavioral Cloning (BC), which mimics the expert's nominal behavior policy P(X|S) via supervised learning, and GAIL (Ho & Ermon, 2016), which learns a policy by solving a standard min-max game. In the MDP_obs-Y driving scenario, CAIL consistently outperforms both BC and GAIL in final expected return, with the key difference being that CAIL optimizes the imitator using a weighted reward signal P(x|s)log(1−D(s,x)) from the discriminator rather than the unweighted GAIL objective. In the MDP_obs-S medical treatment scenario on MIMIC-III, CAIL-T achieves the best performance while both BC and GAIL fail to obtain a policy matching expert performance. In the fully confounded MDP_obs setting where no method can guarantee expert-level performance (per Theorem 1), CAIL still consistently outperforms BC and GAIL by effectively learning from the constructed worst-case MDP instances, though it does not reach expert performance (gap = −1.9019 in the reported example).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal imitation learning framework established by Zhang et al. (2020) and Kumor et al. (2021), which required point-identifiability of system dynamics from confounded demonstrations, to the partial identification regime where the transition distribution or reward function cannot be uniquely recovered. It builds directly on the GAIL adversarial framework (Ho & Ermon, 2016) and adapts bounding strategies from Manski (1990) and Zhang & Bareinboim (2019) to derive non-identifiability bounds for MDP components. The key conceptual departure is showing that prior causal imitation methods, which assume sufficient structural knowledge to recover exact dynamics, fail in a fundamental way when both transition and reward are confounded, and then providing minimax-based algorithms for the two intermediate cases where only one component is non-identifiable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The impossibility result (Theorem 1) establishes a clear boundary for what is achievable in causal imitation learning: when both transition and reward are confounded, no policy can guarantee expert performance, which has direct implications for deploying imitation learning in high-stakes domains like autonomous driving and medical treatment where unobserved confounders are common. The two proposed algorithms (CAIL-R and CAIL-T) offer practical worst-case guarantees for the partially identifiable settings, though the current bounds are relatively loose natural bounds and experiments are limited to small binary MDPs. Open questions include tightening the partial identification bounds, extending to continuous state/action spaces, and validating the approach on larger-scale real-world problems beyond the single MIMIC-III application.
