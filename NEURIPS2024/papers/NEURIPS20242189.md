## Problem

Performance on many AI safety benchmarks is strongly correlated with general model capabilities, meaning apparent safety progress may simply reflect scaling rather than targeted safety improvements. This conflation obscures which safety problems genuinely require dedicated research effort versus those that will be resolved by continued scaling. The paper asks: which safety benchmarks actually measure distinct safety properties, and how should the safety community allocate research effort accordingly?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242189/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a matrix of per-model scores on a suite of capabilities benchmarks (MMLU, MATH, GSM8K, BBH, etc.), normalized to zero mean and unit variance per column, along with scores on safety benchmarks and approximate training FLOPs (6 × params × tokens). It produces a single scalar capabilities score per model, Spearman correlation coefficients between that score and each safety benchmark, and a three-way classification of safety tasks: those whose scores improve with scale, those that improve only under specific training paradigms (e.g., instruct-tuning), and those uncorrelated with scale. All analyses are run separately for base and instruct/chat model classes to isolate the effect of safety training techniques.

## Method

The core technique is spectral analysis of the n×m model-by-benchmark score matrix. Rather than using the Pearson correlation matrix, the authors compute the Spearman correlation matrix for robustness to outliers in heterogeneous model sets, then extract its largest eigenvalue λ and associated unit eigenvector v; the components of v act as data-driven benchmark weights, and the projection Av yields each model's capabilities score, with λ/m representing the fraction of variance in normalized scores captured by this single component (≈71–72% empirically). They then compute Spearman correlations between each model's capabilities score and its performance on each safety benchmark, separately for base and instruct/chat model classes. This yields a 'capabilities correlation' coefficient per safety benchmark that quantifies how much of the benchmark's variance is explained by general capabilities. By comparing correlations across model classes, the method identifies whether safety techniques like RLHF or instruction tuning shift a benchmark's correlation from negative to positive, thereby distinguishing benchmarks that will be solved by scaling from those requiring novel algorithmic intervention.

## Results

The capabilities component (first principal component of 14 capabilities benchmarks) explains 72% of variance in base model scores and 71% in chat model scores, and is strongly correlated with training FLOPs (r=0.96 for both model classes). Machine ethics benchmarks show the highest positive capabilities correlations (ETHICS average: 0.80; STEER Rationality: 0.54), while malicious-use benchmarks show strong negative correlations for base models (WMDP Bio: −0.91; WMDP Chem: −0.88) that are partially mitigated by instruction tuning (e.g., CyberSecEval2 MITRE shifts from −0.25 to +0.55). Sycophancy worsens with scale (−0.73), and bias benchmarks like CrowS-Pairs show near-zero correlation (−0.32 base, +0.18 chat), indicating scaling alone does not resolve these issues.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare competing methods; instead, it benchmarks safety evaluations against a unified capabilities score derived via spectral analysis. The key comparison is between base and instruct/chat model classes: instruction tuning systematically weakens negative capabilities correlations on malicious-use tasks (e.g., HarmBench DR splits shift from ≈−0.45 to near-zero or positive), but WMDP remains strongly anti-correlated (≈−0.87) even after chat tuning, suggesting unlearning-based safety is not yet aligned with scaling. In adversarial robustness, static benchmarks (AdvGLUE: 0.68, AdvDemonstration: 0.75 for chat) are moderately correlated with capabilities, while dynamic/jailbreak robustness (HarmBench) is anti-correlated. For bias, BBQ Disambiguated shows a strong positive correlation (bias decreases with scale), whereas CrowS-Pairs and BBQ Ambiguous show near-zero correlation, meaning scaling does not resolve these biases. The paper's central finding is that safety research should prioritize benchmarks with low capabilities correlation (e.g., sycophancy at −0.73, WMDP at −0.87) as these will not be solved by scaling alone, while high-correlation benchmarks (e.g., ETHICS at 0.80, TruthfulQA MC1 at 0.83) will improve naturally with model scale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the observational scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., Ruan et al.) and the psychometric factor-analysis approach of Ilić [37] by applying spectral analysis of benchmark scores specifically to safety benchmarks, shifting the perspective from 'how does model performance improve with scale' to 'how do safety benchmarks saturate with scale.' It builds on the ongoing debate between McKenzie et al. and Wei et al. about inverse scaling in safety tasks, but provides a systematic, quantitative meta-analysis across dozens of benchmarks and model classes rather than isolated case studies. The use of Spearman correlation and the three-category classification (improves with scale, improves with tuning, uncorrelated) are methodological contributions that go beyond prior single-benchmark analyses.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled framework for the AI safety community to allocate research effort toward problems that will not be solved by scaling alone, directly addressing the 'Bitter Lesson' argument applied to safety. Its central finding—that many widely-used safety benchmarks (ETHICS, TruthfulQA MC1, some bias measures) are largely measuring general capabilities rather than safety-specific properties—challenges the assumption that improving these benchmarks constitutes safety progress. Key limitations include dependence on the specific model set used (correlations may shift as new architectures emerge), the snapshot nature of the analysis, and the fact that high capabilities correlation does not guarantee a safety property is fully achieved. Open questions include how to design benchmarks that genuinely isolate safety-specific properties and how to develop training methods that increase the capabilities correlation for currently uncorrelated safety metrics.
