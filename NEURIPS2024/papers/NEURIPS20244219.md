## Problem

Sequential data is ubiquitous in machine learning, yet identifying and quantifying evolving patterns within such data has relied entirely on human expertise with no standardized measure. This gap prevents principled decisions about whether sequential models are appropriate, what temporal order (number of past observations) to use, and which features are sufficient to reveal temporal dependencies. The paper addresses this by introducing a quantitative metric grounded in mutual information that unifies these three questions.

## Input / Output

The method accepts either (a) a sequence of D-dimensional data points with known temporal correspondence (e.g., a single tracked time series or video stream) or (b) independent sets of samples collected at each of T timestamps without tracking the same object across time (e.g., medical records from different patients at different ages). For case (a), it outputs a scalar EVORATE value estimating I(Z_{t-k+1}^t; Z_{t+1}) for a chosen window k; for case (b), it additionally outputs the optimal transport plan π* approximating the joint distribution between consecutive timestamps. The EVORATE scalar can then be used to select the optimal autoregressive order, rank features by informativeness, or serve as a go/no-go criterion for deploying sequential models.

## Method

EVORATE replaces the trainable neural critic in the MINE mutual information estimator with an autoregressive function f and a squared-error metric, so that m(x,y) = -||f(g(x_1),...,g(x_k)) - g(y)||²₂, where g is a learned encoder. This design respects temporal dependencies that standard MI estimators ignore, and Proposition 1 shows the expected MLE loss decomposes into a model-related KL term plus a data-related term H(Z_{t+1}) - I(Z_{t+1}; Z_{t-k+1}^t), establishing MI as the fundamental limit on prediction performance. For the no-correspondence setting, EVORATE_W uses the Wasserstein distance to compute an optimal transport plan π* between consecutive timestamp distributions, which serves as a proxy joint distribution for MI estimation; the squared-error cost in the OT objective simultaneously drives the transport plan toward the true joint distribution (Lemma 1) and the autoregressive model toward the ground-truth transition function. The encoder g is trained separately via an autoencoder reconstruction loss to prevent representation collapse, and the overall computational cost is O(B²d + B³) per iteration, dominated by the Sinkhorn-based OT computation.

## Results

On the Evolving Domain Generalization (EDG) classification tasks, the proposed EDG algorithm (built on EVORATE_W insights) achieves 70.3% average accuracy across RMNIST, Rotated Gaussian, and PowerSupply, outperforming the second-best baseline DRAIN (58.6%) by 11.7 percentage points. As a metric for evolving patterns, EVORATE consistently correlates with prediction performance across five time-series datasets (e.g., EVORATE=4.67 for Player Trajectory with RMSE=1.16, vs. EVORATE=1.58 for M4-Monthly with sMAPE=11.93), whereas ForeCA shows near-constant values (0.35–0.49) that fail to discriminate between datasets. On M4-Weekly order selection, EVORATE identifies k=90 as the optimal trade-off (sMAPE=6.28, EVORATE=2.55), matching the best achievable performance while using 3× less computation than k=270.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's EDG algorithm (leveraging EVORATE_W) outperforms all six baselines on the EDG classification benchmark, achieving 70.3% average accuracy versus DRAIN (58.6%), Mixup (57.0%), DIVA (56.7%), CORAL (56.2%), LSSAE (55.4%), and IRM (52.4%). The largest margin is on Rotated Gaussian (91.2% vs. 61.0% for DRAIN), where strong evolving patterns (EVORATE_W=1.58) allow the sequential model to exploit temporal structure. On PowerSupply, all methods cluster tightly (70.8–71.3%), consistent with its low EVORATE_W (0.46) indicating weak evolving patterns. As a metric, EVORATE outperforms ForeCA [24] across all five time-series datasets: ForeCA yields nearly identical values (0.33–0.50) regardless of dataset difficulty, while EVORATE ranges from 1.58 to 4.67 and correctly ranks datasets by prediction error. In the order-selection experiment on M4-Weekly, EVORATE identifies k=90 as optimal (matching k=270's sMAPE of 6.28 with 3× less computation), while ForeCA incorrectly suggests shorter sequences are more forecastable. The squared-error critic in EVORATE also outperforms both joint and separable neural-net critics from MINE [8] and CPC [37] in MI estimation accuracy on sequential data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes mutual information estimation (MINE, CLUB) for sequential data by replacing the standard trainable neural-network critic with an autoregressive squared-error function, arguing that temporal dependency makes standard critics biased. It further extends the framework to the correspondence-free setting by leveraging optimal transport (Wasserstein distance, Sinkhorn) to construct a proxy joint distribution between consecutive timestamps. The only prior work on pattern estimation for sequential data is ForeCA (forecastability via spectral entropy), which the authors contrast as limited to cyclic patterns and computationally intractable for high-dimensional data. The EDG algorithm in Section 6.5 applies the EVORATE_W insight to evolving domain generalization, building on LSSAE and DRAIN.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

EVORATE provides a principled, quantitative criterion for three practical decisions that previously relied on expert intuition: whether a sequential model is warranted, what autoregressive order to use, and which features to retain. The OT-based EVORATE_W extends applicability to the common real-world scenario where individual samples are not tracked across timestamps (e.g., medical data across ages, traffic camera frames). Limitations include the O(B³) cost of Sinkhorn-based OT, the absence of NLP/LLM experiments due to compute constraints, and the Gaussian assumption in the theoretical guarantees (Assumption 1). Open questions include whether the squared-error critic advantage persists in very high-dimensional or non-Gaussian regimes, and how the method scales to multi-modal sequential data.
