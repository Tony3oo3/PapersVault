## Problem

Existing privacy risk assessments for LLM fine-tuning have been confined to encoder-based architectures, a narrow set of adaptation methods (typically full fine-tuning or top-layer tuning), and limited attack strategies, leaving no comprehensive benchmark to answer how different adaptation techniques influence privacy risk. This paper addresses that gap by asking: to what extent, and in what ways, do different LLM adaptation methods affect membership leakage of sensitive domain data?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240308/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes a query set of 1000 member and 1000 non-member text samples (reduced to 10 per class for in-context learning due to memory constraints) drawn from three sensitive-domain datasets (Sujet Finance, CorpClimate, Synthetic-Text-to-SQL), a target adapted LLM, and optionally a reference pre-trained model for calibration-based attacks. Outputs include binary membership predictions per sample, aggregate AUC-ROC and FPR@0.1%/1%TPR scores per attack method, and post-adaptation model accuracy as a utility metric. Seven attack methods are evaluated, six requiring only black-box access to output probabilities and one (Gradient-Norm) requiring white-box parameter access.

## Method

PrivAuditor is a unified empirical benchmark that systematically crosses five LLM architectures (T5-Large, LLaMA-7B, OPT-6.7B, BLOOM-7B, GPT-J-6B), eight adaptation techniques spanning full fine-tuning, parameter-efficient methods (LoRA, Adapter-H, Prefix-tuning, P-tuning, Prompt-tuning, Top2Head), and in-context learning, and seven membership inference attacks (Likelihood, Likelihood-ref, Zlib Entropy, Neighborhood, Min-K, Min-K++, Gradient-Norm). The key finding is a monotonic relationship between the fraction of trainable parameters and membership leakage AUC-ROC: full fine-tuning and in-context learning yield the highest attack success (AUC-ROC 0.82–0.92), while prompt-based methods with fewer trainable parameters remain closer to chance (0.55–0.65). Beyond parameter count, the benchmark reveals that adaptation techniques differ in their attack surfaces—methods modifying deeper layers (LoRA, full fine-tuning) embed more memorized information than input-representation methods (Prompt-tuning, P-tuning)—and that larger model capacity, more training iterations, and less domain data all increase vulnerability, while greater data volume shifts the model toward generalization and reduces leakage.

## Results

Across five LLM architectures (T5-Large, LLaMA-7B, OPT-6.7B, BLOOM-7B, GPT-J-6B) and three sensitive-domain datasets, Likelihood-ref achieves the highest black-box AUC-ROC, reaching 0.70–0.75 on CorpClimate and Sujet Finance but only 0.57–0.62 on the structurally homogeneous SQL dataset. Full fine-tuning yields the most severe membership leakage (AUC-ROC 0.81–0.88 on T5-Large, 0.79–0.88 on LLaMA-7B), while parameter-efficient methods like LoRA reduce this to 0.59–0.74 and 0.58–0.74 respectively, at comparable downstream accuracy (≈0.64–0.67). In-context learning, despite no parameter updates, still leaks at AUC-ROC 0.88–0.92, though with substantially lower task accuracy (0.46–0.53).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among adaptation techniques, full fine-tuning is the most vulnerable to all six black-box attacks (AUC-ROC 0.81–0.88), while LoRA [38] reduces leakage to 0.59–0.74 with only a ~0.01 accuracy drop, making it the best privacy–utility trade-off. Prompt-tuning [40] and P-tuning [41] achieve the lowest AUC-ROC among parameter-updating methods (0.54–0.63 and 0.58–0.69 respectively) but at slightly lower accuracy than LoRA. Adapter-H [35] and Prefix-tuning [42] fall in between, with AUC-ROC of 0.57–0.69 and 0.57–0.65. In-context learning [43] is surprisingly the second-most vulnerable (AUC-ROC 0.88–0.92) despite zero parameter updates, though its task accuracy is substantially lower (0.46–0.53 vs. 0.64–0.67). Among attack methods, Likelihood-ref [12] consistently outperforms Likelihood [27] by 0.08–0.15 AUC-ROC across all models and datasets; Gradient-Norm [11] (white-box) achieves the highest scores (0.54–0.73) but requires parameter access; Min-K [21] and Min-K++ [31] perform near chance (AUC-ROC ≈ 0.50–0.63), and Neighborhood [29] offers modest gains over raw Likelihood (0.52–0.67).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by providing the first systematic benchmark for privacy leakage in LLM *adaptation* (as opposed to pre-training), unifying seven attack methods (likelihood-based, reference-calibrated, zlib-entropy, neighborhood, Min-K/Min-K++, gradient-norm) across five model architectures and seven adaptation techniques. Prior privacy studies on LLMs either focused on pre-training data extraction (Carlini et al. 2021, Shi et al. 2023) or examined only a narrow subset of fine-tuning methods (typically full fine-tuning or top-layer tuning) on encoder-based models. The paper repurposes established MIA formulations from the broader ML privacy literature into a standardized evaluation pipeline specifically tailored to the 'pre-train-then-adapt' paradigm that dominates current LLM deployment practice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark provides practitioners with a standardized auditing tool (PrivAuditor) to quantify privacy risk before deploying domain-adapted LLMs in sensitive settings (finance, climate policy, structured data), directly addressing GDPR-style compliance needs. Key open questions remain: how differentially private adaptation methods trade off utility against the measured leakage, whether co-designing privacy-preserving mechanisms with parameter-efficient techniques can close the gap, and whether the observed trends (more parameters → more leakage, more data → less leakage) hold for next-generation models. The finding that even in-context learning (no parameter updates) yields high attack AUC (~0.88–0.92) challenges the assumption that parameter-free adaptation is inherently privacy-safe, though this is partly tautological since examples are literally in the prompt.
