## Problem

While prior work has demonstrated that language model representations can linearly predict human brain responses to language, the specific domains where LMs systematically fail to capture human processing remained uncharacterized. This paper addresses that gap by asking: what elements of human neural responses to narrative text are not explained by LM embeddings, and can targeted fine-tuning close those gaps?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244381/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The encoding pipeline takes each word with its 19-word preceding context as input to GPT-2 XL (layer 7) or Llama-2 (layer 3), producing a fixed-dimensional LM embedding that is linearly mapped via ridge regression to 306 MEG channels at 25ms temporal resolution. For hypothesis discovery, the system consumes pairs of sentences (top-100 least-predicted vs. top-100 well-predicted by MSE) and outputs ranked natural-language hypotheses validated by a fine-tuned FLAN-T5-XXL verifier. The final fine-tuning stage takes GPT-2 XL and produces domain-specific models trained on Social IQa (33.4k multiple-choice items) or PiQA (16.1k items), evaluated on their ability to predict MEG for annotated social/emotional or physical words.

## Method

The core contribution is a bottom-up, data-driven pipeline that first computes per-word MSE between actual and ridge-regression-predicted MEG responses, then automatically proposes and validates hypotheses about what distinguishes poorly-predicted from well-predicted sentences using a GPT-3 proposer and FLAN-T5-XXL verifier (adapted from Zhong et al., 2023). This reveals two dominant divergence domains: social/emotional intelligence and physical commonsense. To test whether these gaps stem from insufficient knowledge representations, the authors fine-tune GPT-2 XL on Social IQa and PiQA using a multiple-choice-as-language-modeling formulation (concatenating each answer option to the question and applying cross-entropy over summed token logits). Fine-tuned models show significantly improved Pearson correlation with MEG during language-processing time windows (50–300ms), with the improvement concentrated specifically on words annotated as belonging to the corresponding domain, while general language-modeling perplexity remains unchanged.

## Results

Using GPT-2 XL (layer 7) and Llama-2 (layer 3) to predict MEG responses, the paper identifies two dominant domains where LMs diverge from human brain processing: social/emotional intelligence and physical commonsense. Fine-tuning GPT-2 XL on Social IQa improves MEG prediction across most channels in the 50–300 ms post-word-onset window, while fine-tuning on PiQA improves prediction in the 50–275 ms window. Critically, the MSE reduction is category-specific: social words show significant improvement 200–275 ms post-onset on the social model, and physical words show significant improvement 150–225 ms on the physical model. A control experiment confirms that fine-tuning does not improve general language-modeling ability (base loss 0.088 vs. social 0.115 vs. physical 0.100 on held-out Harry Potter chapters), ruling out the explanation that better LM performance drives the brain-alignment gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against competing neural-encoding methods; instead, its comparisons are internal. (1) GPT-2 XL vs. Llama-2 7B: both models show similar spatio-temporal prediction patterns, but Llama-2's top divergent hypotheses focus almost exclusively on physical objects and events, with a notable absence of social/emotional themes, suggesting Llama-2 may handle social context better. (2) Base GPT-2 XL vs. fine-tuned variants: the social-fine-tuned model outperforms the base model on the majority of MEG channels during 50–300 ms, and the physical-fine-tuned model outperforms the base on almost all channels during 50–275 ms; outside these windows, the fine-tuned models are actually worse, indicating the gains are specific to linguistic comprehension. (3) Category-specificity control: the social model does not significantly improve on physical words, and the physical model shows only a marginal (non-significant) boost on social words—attributed to incidental social content in the PiQA dataset. (4) LM-ability control: 3-fold cross-validation on remaining Harry Potter chapters shows the fine-tuned models have comparable or slightly worse language-modeling loss than the base model, confirming the brain-alignment improvement is not a byproduct of better next-word prediction.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the neural encoding modeling literature (Wehbe et al., 2014; Jain & Huth, 2018; Toneva & Wehbe, 2019) that has focused on demonstrating alignment between LM representations and human brain responses. Rather than measuring how well LMs predict brain activity, it inverts the question to identify what LMs *miss*, adapting the proposer/verifier framework of Zhong et al. (2023) from text corpus comparison to the novel setting of divergent-vs-convergent sentence sets defined by prediction error. It also builds on Aw & Toneva (2023), who showed that task-specific fine-tuning (summarization) improves brain alignment, by testing whether fine-tuning on the *specifically identified* knowledge gaps (social/emotional, physical commonsense) yields targeted improvements.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, data-driven account of *where* and *why* LMs diverge from human language processing, moving beyond the general observation that alignment is imperfect. The finding that targeted fine-tuning on Social IQa and PiQA selectively improves prediction for annotated social and physical words (with time-selective effects confined to language processing windows) offers a principled path toward more brain-aligned models. Key limitations include reliance on narrative-only stimuli, a single participant for the Moth dataset, and the possibility that additional undetected divergences exist in non-narrative text. Open questions include whether these two domains are truly the primary gaps or merely the most detectable ones, and whether the approach generalizes to other modalities, languages, or model scales.
