## Problem

Existing theoretical analyses of perceptron learning dynamics rely on either student-teacher frameworks (restricted to linearly separable mappings) or linearized output assumptions, leaving the role of the nonlinearity and the input-data distribution in shaping learning curves uncharacterized. In particular, whether and how learning speed and forgetting differ between supervised and reinforcement learning rules in a genuinely nonlinear perceptron performing binary classification had not been investigated.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244333/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The perceptron receives an input vector x ∈ R^N drawn from two multivariate Gaussian distributions with means ±μ and covariance Σ (or, for the MNIST validation, raw 28×28 pixel values or Gabor-filtered 1440-dimensional features of digits 0 and 1) and outputs a binary label ŷ = ±1. The theoretical framework produces closed-form flow equations for the mean and covariance of the weight distribution p(w,t), from which learning curves, alignment dynamics, and exponential forgetting curves across sequential tasks are predicted without simulation.

## Method

The paper derives a Fokker-Planck-type probability flow equation for the weight distribution by Taylor-expanding the stochastic update rule in the small-learning-rate limit, then computes the first two moments (mean and covariance) of p(w,t) to obtain closed-form ODEs. For supervised learning (regularized SGD on binary cross-entropy, i.e., online logistic regression) and reinforcement learning (REINFORCE policy gradient with reward baseline), the noise-averaged update ⟨f_i⟩_L is evaluated analytically using a shifted error-function sigmoid, yielding expressions involving the standard normal CDF and Owen's T function. The key mechanistic insight is that the noise-averaged gradient decomposes into a signal term pushing weights along the decoding direction μ and a regularization-like term that depends on the input covariance structure, causing noise parallel to μ to slow learning while noise orthogonal to μ accelerates it for SL (with an ambiguous effect for RL). The framework is validated by matching predicted learning curves against SGD training on Gabor-filtered MNIST and by quantifying how input noise and learning rule modulate the exponential forgetting rate in a continual-learning setup.

## Results

The paper derives closed-form flow equations for weight dynamics in a nonlinear perceptron under both supervised learning (logistic regression) and reinforcement learning (REINFORCE). The key quantitative finding is that for SL, the logarithmic derivative of alignment scales as g_iso + σ²h_iso² + O(σ⁴), meaning higher isotropic input noise accelerates learning, while for RL the noise effect is indeterminate. On MNIST (digits 0 vs. 1, Gabor-filtered inputs, λ=1), the theoretically predicted learning curve closely matches empirical test accuracy, and the alignment of w with μ continues to evolve non-trivially even after the task is learned. In continual learning (N=500, λ=10, η=10⁻²), forgetting decays exponentially with the number of tasks, with higher input noise producing faster forgetting, while the decay constant is largely independent of whether SL or RL is used.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is primarily a theoretical paper, so it does not benchmark against competing methods in the traditional sense. Instead, it compares two learning rules (SL vs. RL) under identical task conditions and validates its analytical flow equations against direct numerical simulation and real MNIST data. The central comparison is between SL and RL dynamics: for isotropic noise, SL shows a clear speedup with increasing σ (Fig. 3A), while RL shows an ambiguous, parameter-dependent trend. For anisotropic noise, both SL and RL agree that shifting variance into the decoding direction slows learning while shifting it orthogonal speeds it up (Fig. 3C), contrasting with Saxe et al. [2019] where variance along the task dimension helped in a two-layer setting. The theory-vs-empirical comparison on MNIST (Fig. 5B) shows close agreement between the Gaussian-approximation prediction and actual hold-out test accuracy, confirming the approach generalizes beyond strictly Gaussian inputs. In the continual-learning setting (Fig. 6), the forgetting rate is insensitive to the choice of learning rule but sensitive to input noise level, suggesting a trade-off between learning speed and retention.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the stochastic-process framework of Yaida (2018) and Murray & Escola (2020) to the nonlinear perceptron, departing from both the student-teacher framework (which is restricted to linearly separable tasks) and the linearized-perceptron approach (which approximates the output as linear). Unlike other SDE-based analyses of SGD (He et al. 2019, Li et al. 2019, 2021) that add Gaussian noise to the mean update, this work derives the noise statistics directly from the stochastic update rule, enabling analysis of weight covariance dynamics. The comparison of supervised and reinforcement learning rules builds on insights from the student-teacher literature (Werfel et al. 2003, Züge et al. 2023, Patel et al. 2023) but generalizes them to non-linearly-separable classification with structured input noise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a concrete path toward analyzing learning dynamics in more complex multilayer architectures, and the qualitative differences between SL and RL under varying noise structures offer a potential route for designing experiments to distinguish learning rules in biological neural circuits. Key limitations include the Gaussian input assumption (which may fail for late-stage training on complex data, as noted by Refinetti et al. 2023), the neglect of higher-order terms in the learning rate, and the restriction to a single-layer perceptron. Open questions include how these dynamics scale to deeper networks, how non-Gaussian input structure affects the flow equations, and whether the observed trade-off between learning speed and forgetting rate persists in more realistic continual-learning settings.
