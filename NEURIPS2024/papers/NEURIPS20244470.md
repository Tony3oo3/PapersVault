## Problem

Existing computational models of decision confidence are restricted to scenarios where all choices carry equal reward, making perception confidence and decision confidence inseparable. In real-world decisions, actions yield different potential rewards, and this value asymmetry should modulate confidence independently of perceptual certainty, yet no generalizable normative framework captures this interaction. The paper addresses this gap by formally defining decision confidence as the probability of making the optimal decision in a general POMDP setting with heterogeneous rewards, asymmetric priors, and perceptual uncertainty.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a perceptual observation (e.g., Gabor filter orientation), a prior probability distribution over hidden states, and reward/payoff values for each action, with the belief state represented as a two-parameter Gaussian (mean and variance) due to the simplicity of the observation model. It outputs a continuous confidence score computed as the product of exp(E[r(s,a)]) over the action trajectory, which is then binarized into low/high confidence reports using a threshold fit via maximum likelihood with a Bernoulli likelihood. In the value-based decision-making experiment, the input is replaced by subjective food ratings serving as expected reward values, and the output is mapped to a continuous sliding-scale confidence report.

## Method

The core contribution defines decision confidence as the probability of optimality over a trajectory in a POMDP, where per-step optimality is set to p(o_t|b_t,a_t) = exp(r(b_t,a_t)), converting the additive reward sum into a multiplicative probability product. This 'soft optimality' definition is shown to be equivalent to the planning-as-inference framework: minimizing the KL-divergence between an approximate trajectory distribution parameterized by a policy π(a|b) and the true optimal trajectory distribution yields an objective that jointly maximizes expected total reward and the information entropy of the policy (maximum entropy RL / soft Q-learning on the belief state). The model is validated on two psychophysics experiments by fitting only two free parameters (internal and external observation noise) via gradient descent and grid search, then predicting confidence on held-out asymmetric trials. It is compared against perception confidence (posterior probability of the chosen state), observation confidence (belief without prior), and an expected value ratio heuristic using AIC, with soft optimality outperforming all baselines for a majority of subjects in both experiments.

## Results

On the perceptual decision-making task (10 subjects, Locke et al. 2020), the Soft Optimality model achieved the lowest AIC for 5 out of 10 subjects, outperforming the Perception (Bayesian confidence hypothesis), Observation, and Expected Value Ratio baselines. On the value-based decision-making task (33 subjects, Brus et al. 2021), Soft Optimality achieved a mean AIC of 206.65 versus 282.11 for the Expected Value Ratio heuristic, and was the best-fitting model for 21 of 33 subjects. The AIC advantage was largest precisely for the subjects where Soft Optimality was the best model, indicating the improvement is not a statistical artifact.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares Soft Optimality against three baselines: (1) the Perception model (posterior probability of the chosen direction, i.e., the Bayesian confidence hypothesis from Khalvati et al. 2021), (2) the Observation model (belief without prior), and (3) the Expected Value Ratio heuristic (ratio of expected rewards of actions). On the perceptual decision-making task, Soft Optimality achieved the lowest AIC for 5 of 10 subjects, beating the Perception model in those cases; notably, subjects were explicitly instructed to report *perception* confidence, yet half still reported *decision* confidence, which Soft Optimality captures. On the value-based decision-making task (no perceptual ambiguity), Soft Optimality outperformed the Expected Value Ratio with a mean AIC of 206.65 vs. 282.11 and won for 21 of 33 subjects. The AIC gap was substantially larger for subjects where Soft Optimality won (e.g., Subject 6: 118.28 vs. 333.47; Subject 10: 224.71 vs. 799.15), confirming the advantage is not a marginal statistical artifact. Adding a choice-bias parameter to the prior did not change which subjects Soft Optimality best explained, reinforcing the robustness of the result.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the planning-as-inference framework (Toussaint & Storkey 2006; Botvinick & Toussaint 2012) and its equivalence to maximum-entropy reinforcement learning (Ziebart et al. 2008; Levine 2018) from modeling *choices* to modeling *confidence evaluation*—the self-assessment of whether a decision was optimal. It departs from the Bayesian confidence hypothesis (Khalvati et al. 2021; Pouget et al. 2016), which defines confidence as the posterior probability of the correct choice and is only well-defined when all options carry equal reward. By grounding confidence in the probability of optimality under a POMDP with asymmetric rewards, the paper unifies perception, prior, and value into a single normative account that prior descriptive/statistical models (e.g., positive confidence–value correlations) could not capture systematically.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework offers a generalizable, parameter-light normative model of decision confidence that extends beyond the symmetric-reward setting where the Bayesian confidence hypothesis applies, and it is validated across two distinct experimental paradigms (perceptual and value-based). The behavioral finding that roughly half of subjects reported decision confidence despite explicit instructions to report perception confidence suggests the self-evaluation mechanism is partially autonomous, a point with implications for how confidence is measured in future experiments. Open questions include whether the model generalizes to multi-step trajectories, more than two choices, and whether the exponential (soft-optimality) form is truly preferred over other monotonic reward-to-probability mappings in richer task structures.
