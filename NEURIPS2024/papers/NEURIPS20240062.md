## Problem

Task-optimized RNNs used to model neural dynamics have fixed recurrent weight matrices, so inputs can only perturb network state through static input weights. Decades of neuroscience show that synaptic strengths are continuously modulated by neuromodulatory chemicals, yet this structured flexibility is absent from standard RNN models. The paper asks whether incorporating neuromodulatory gain scaling into low-rank RNNs can improve both task performance and generalization while preserving the interpretability of low-dimensional dynamics.

## Input / Output

The NM-RNN receives task-specific time-series inputs (timing cues for Measure-Wait-Go, sinusoidal angle encodings plus fixation signals for multitask tasks, and integer sequences with a query index for the Element Finder Task) along with a one-hot contextual task identity fed exclusively to the neuromodulatory subnetwork. The output-generating subnetwork produces the task response (a linear ramp, a reproduced or inverted angle, or a scalar element value), while the neuromodulatory subnetwork emits a K-dimensional signal s(t) ∈ [0,1]^K that dynamically rescales each rank-1 factor of the recurrent weight matrix. The neuromodulatory subnetwork evolves on a deliberately slower timescale (τ_z ≫ τ_x) to reflect the relatively slow dynamics of biological neuromodulators.

## Method

The NM-RNN couples two subnetworks: a small full-rank neuromodulatory RNN (dimension M) and a larger low-rank output-generating RNN (dimension N, rank K). The neuromodulatory subnetwork produces a K-dimensional signal s(t) = σ(A_z z(t) + b_z) that linearly scales each rank-1 component ℓ_k r_k^T of the recurrent weight matrix W_x, so that W_x(z(t)) = Σ_k s_k(z(t)) ℓ_k r_k^T. This gain scaling lets the network dynamically up- or down-weight different dynamical modes throughout a trial, providing structured flexibility absent in fixed-weight RNNs. Theoretically, when W_x is symmetric and the nonlinearity is removed, the resulting decoupled dynamics show that s(t) controls the decay rate of each mode, making it functionally analogous to the LSTM forget gate; the paper proves that under suitable assumptions the NM-RNN's linearized dynamics are exactly reproducible by an LSTM cell-state update. Empirically, parameter-matched NM-RNNs outperform low-rank RNNs on timing extrapolation, multitask retraining (where only the neuromodulatory subnetwork is retrained for a novel task), and long-dependency sequence retrieval, while vanilla RNNs and LSTMs serve as baselines.

## Results

On the Measure-Wait-Go timing task, the NM-RNN (N=100, M=5, K=3) achieves the lowest median L2 loss on extrapolated intervals among all four model types (LR-RNN, NM-RNN, vanilla RNN, LSTM), while the LR-RNN fails to reproduce shorter and longer extrapolated intervals. On the four-task multitask benchmark (DelayPro, DelayAnti, MemoryPro, MemoryAnti), the NM-RNN (N=100, M=20, K=3) matches LSTM and vanilla RNN performance in percent correct and considerably outperforms the parameter-matched LR-RNN, even when only the neuromodulatory subnetwork is retrained on the novel MemoryAnti task. On the Element Finder Task (T=25), NM-RNNs (N=18, M=5, K=8) achieve LSTM-level MSE loss, while both low-rank and full-rank RNNs largely fail to solve the task.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The NM-RNN is compared against three baselines: low-rank RNNs (LR-RNNs), LSTMs, and full-rank vanilla RNNs, all parameter-matched. Against LR-RNNs, the NM-RNN consistently wins: it achieves lower L2 loss on extrapolated MWG intervals (where LR-RNNs fail on both shorter and longer intervals), higher percent correct on the multitask benchmark, and solves the EFT while LR-RNNs fail. Against LSTMs, the NM-RNN matches performance on the multitask task and the EFT, and achieves slightly lower L2 loss on extrapolated MWG intervals. Against vanilla (full-rank) RNNs, the NM-RNN matches on MWG training accuracy and multitask performance, but clearly outperforms on the EFT where vanilla RNNs fail. Notably, the NM-RNN's advantage over LR-RNNs is not due to more parameters: the comparison LR-RNN's input weight matrix actually contains more parameters than the entire neuromodulatory subnetwork, since it must process all inputs (context, sensory, and fixation) directly.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of low-rank RNNs (Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic, 2018), hypernetworks (Ha et al., 2017), and computational neuromodulation models dating back to Abbott (1990) and Marder's circuit-level work. It departs from prior neuromodulation-in-RNN work (Tsuda et al., 2021) by using learned, time-varying modulatory signals rather than constant, pre-specified ones, and from hypernetwork approaches by restricting the generated weight matrix to low-rank form for dynamical interpretability. A key conceptual departure is the formal demonstration that the NM-RNN's low-rank dynamics reduce to the LSTM cell-state update equation under symmetry and orthonormality assumptions, providing a biologically-grounded derivation of a mechanism previously introduced purely as an engineering solution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, biologically-motivated explanation for why gated architectures like LSTMs work: synaptic gain scaling on low-rank recurrent factors naturally produces forget-gate-like dynamics. This bridges two literatures (computational neuroscience and sequence-modeling ML) that have developed largely independently. Open questions include whether the mechanism scales to networks of thousands of neurons, how neuromodulation interacts with the learning process itself (the paper only analyzes post-training dynamics), and whether the low-rank structure and gating behavior are observable in actual neural recordings. The small network sizes (~100 neurons) and absence of comparison to biological data limit the strength of the biological claims.
