## Problem

Push-Relabel is the most widely used max-flow algorithm in practice, yet no theoretical guarantees existed for warm-starting it with a predicted flow that is not a pre-flow or cut-saturating. Prior learning-augmented results only covered augmenting-path algorithms like Ford-Fulkerson, leaving the challenge of bringing rigorous warm-start analysis to non-augmenting-path algorithms open. Additionally, the gap relabeling heuristic, long employed in practice to speed up Push-Relabel, had no rigorous theoretical justification for its effectiveness.

## Input / Output

The algorithm takes a directed network G=(V,E) with integral capacities and a predicted pseudo-flow f̂ that satisfies capacity constraints but may violate flow conservation (having both excess and deficit nodes) and need not saturate any cut. The error η is defined as the smallest value such that f̂ is η-far from being cut-saturating and the total excess plus deficit is at most η. The output is a maximum flow and minimum cut, with running time O(η·n²). In the empirical setting, inputs are N×N grayscale images (N up to 480) with fixed object/background seed sets, and the output is the segmentation boundary obtained as the min-cut of the corresponding graph.

## Method

The core insight is that the algorithm maintains a saturated s-t cut whose t-side is monotonically decreasing (nodes only move from t-side to s-side, never the reverse), a property shared with the gap relabeling heuristic. The warm-start procedure has three phases: (1) augment the predicted pseudo-flow by a small s-t flow in the residual graph to obtain a cut-saturating pseudo-flow; (2) solve an auxiliary max-flow problem on the t-side subgraph to route excess to deficit nodes or t, and if not all excess is resolved, the auxiliary min-cut defines a new cut that moves the remaining excess nodes to the s-side; (3) mirror this process on the s-side to push all deficit nodes to the t-side. Once all excess is on the s-side and all deficit on the t-side, the maintained cut is a min-cut, and a final Push-Relabel pass routes excess back to s and deficit from t to produce the max-flow. The gap relabeling heuristic is used throughout, and the paper provides its first theoretical analysis via a potential function argument tied to the monotonically decreasing cut.

## Results

On image segmentation graphs (N×N pixels, N up to 480), warm-start Push-Relabel achieves up to 56% speedup over cold-start Push-Relabel at 480×480 (e.g., SHOE: 364.42s vs 756.77s; DOG: 363.41s vs 834.63s). Cold-start Push-Relabel is already 15–20× faster than cold-start Ford-Fulkerson at 120×120 (e.g., BIRDHOUSE: 5.42s vs 109.06s), and Ford-Fulkerson becomes infeasible (>1 hour) at 240×240 and above. The warm-start advantage grows with image size: at 30×30, warm-start is marginally slower than cold-start, but by 480×480 the gap is substantial across all four image groups.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against cold-start Ford-Fulkerson [FF56], cold-start Push-Relabel is 15–20× faster at 120×120 (e.g., 5.42s vs 109.06s on BIRDHOUSE) and Ford-Fulkerson exceeds the 1-hour timeout at 240×240 and above, making Push-Relabel the only feasible baseline at large sizes. Against warm-start Ford-Fulkerson [DMVW23], cold-start Push-Relabel is still 5–7× faster at 240×240 (e.g., 60.67s vs 400.19s on BIRDHOUSE), and warm-start Push-Relabel is the only method that completes at 480×480 where warm-start Ford-Fulkerson also times out. Against cold-start Push-Relabel [GT88], warm-start Push-Relabel is slightly slower at 30×30 (e.g., 0.06s vs 0.05s on BIRDHOUSE) but pulls ahead at 120×120 and shows its largest gains at 480×480, where it achieves 17–56% speedups (SHOE: 364.42s vs 756.77s; DOG: 363.41s vs 834.63s). The paper notes that despite the theoretical bound being O(η·n²) for Push-Relabel versus O(η·m) for Ford-Fulkerson, both warm-start methods scale similarly in practice, and the practical speedup of Push-Relabel over its worst-case bound is consistent with known subquadratic behavior.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the learning-augmented algorithms framework from Davies et al. (DMVW23) and Polak & Zub (PZ22), who provided the first theoretical guarantees for warm-starting Ford-Fulkerson/Edmonds-Karp with predicted flows, to the Push-Relabel algorithm — the de facto state-of-the-art practical max-flow method. The key departure is that prior work improved upon sub-optimal augmenting-path algorithms, whereas this paper provides the first rigorous analysis of warm-starting Push-Relabel with an arbitrary pseudo-flow (not necessarily feasible or cut-saturating). A notable conceptual contribution is the first theoretical justification for the gap relabeling heuristic, which has been used in practice for decades without a formal running-time guarantee; the paper shows both the heuristic and the new algorithm maintain a cut whose t-side is monotonically decreasing, which directly yields the O(η·n²) bound.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a gap explicitly identified in DMVW23 by providing rigorous warm-start guarantees for the algorithm that practitioners actually use, and it explains why the gap relabeling heuristic works — a long-standing open question in the max-flow literature. The O(η·n²) bound is a meaningful improvement over the O(m·n²) cold-start, though it is asymptotically worse than the O(mη) warm-start Edmonds-Karp bound; the authors argue this reflects the community's incomplete understanding of why Push-Relabel outperforms its worst-case bound in practice, connecting to the broader beyond-worst-case analysis program. Open directions include extending the monotonic-cut property to more general network classes, applying the framework to min-cost flow or multi-commodity flow, and developing rigorous guarantees for other empirically successful heuristics (e.g., global relabeling) through the predictions lens.
