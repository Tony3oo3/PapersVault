## Problem

State-of-the-art vision language models exhibit striking failures on basic multi-object reasoning tasks (counting, visual search, scene description, visual analogy) despite excelling at complex image description and generation. The paper asks whether these failures follow the same patterns as human binding errors—driven by representational interference from shared feature representations rather than simply by object count—and whether the binding problem from cognitive science provides a unifying explanation.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243603/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Multimodal language models receive 2D sprite images or 3D Blender-rendered scenes (4–50 objects for search, 1–20 for counting, 8–15 for scene description) and produce structured outputs: bracketed true/false for detection, bracketed integers for counts, JSON arrays of {shape, color} objects for scene descriptions, or bracketed integers for target pair selection. Text-to-image models receive natural-language prompts specifying exact object identities and counts (1–10 instances) and produce photorealistic images that are scored via human evaluation on Prolific. The visual analogy task uniquely manipulates input format: the 'unified' condition presents all six objects in a single image, while the 'decomposed' condition splits them across three separate images to reduce binding interference.

## Method

The paper is an empirical evaluation study rather than a new architecture. Its core technical contribution is a novel scene description benchmark that quantifies representational interference via 'feature triplets'—sets of three objects where one pair shares a feature and another pair shares a different feature (e.g., {green X, green triangle, yellow triangle} creates an opportunity for the illusory conjunction 'yellow X'). Across four task families (disjunctive/conjunctive visual search, numerosity estimation with controlled feature entropy, scene description, and relational match-to-sample analogy), the authors show that VLM performance is predicted by the probability of feature interference (triplet count, feature entropy) rather than object count alone, mirroring human capacity limits. A key diagnostic manipulation in the analogy task decomposes a single multi-object image into separate per-pair images, and the resulting performance improvement across all models and sub-tasks (analogy, relation decoding, full feature decoding, single feature decoding) isolates multi-object binding as the bottleneck rather than abstract relational reasoning. The theoretical framing argues that the very presence of binding errors implies VLMs have learned compositional (rather than conjunctive) feature representations, and that solving the binding problem will require mechanisms analogous to serial attention or slot-based object-centric representations.

## Results

In disjunctive (popout) visual search, all four multimodal language models (GPT-4v, GPT-4o, Gemini Ultra 1.5, Claude Sonnet 3.5) achieved 100% accuracy regardless of distractor count (4–50), while in conjunctive search accuracy dropped from ~90% at 5 objects to substantially lower values as object count increased. On numerosity estimation, all VLMs (both multimodal language and text-to-image models) showed human-like subitizing limits: near-perfect accuracy for 1–5 objects with a sharp drop at 6+, with performance modulated by feature entropy (highest in high-entropy/low-interference conditions). In the visual analogy task, decomposing the stimulus into separate images significantly improved performance across all models and sub-tasks (e.g., GPT-4v analogy accuracy rose from 91% unified to 99% decomposed; Gemini Ultra 1.5 from 56% to 60%), while Claude Sonnet 3.5 achieved 100% in both conditions.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper evaluates nine VLMs (five multimodal language models and four text-to-image models) rather than proposing a new method, so the 'baselines' are the models themselves compared under controlled task conditions. Claude Sonnet 3.5 outperforms all other models on the visual analogy task, achieving 100% accuracy in both unified and decomposed conditions across all sub-tasks, while Gemini Ultra 1.5 shows the weakest performance (56% on analogy in the unified condition). GPT-4o and GPT-4v perform well on the full analogy task (99% and 91% unified, respectively) but show notable drops on relation decoding and single feature decoding in the unified condition, suggesting binding-related interference. The critical comparison is within-model: decomposing the stimulus into separate images improves accuracy across all models and all sub-tasks (except where performance is already at ceiling), with the largest gains seen in GPT-4v (e.g., +11 points on relation decoding, +15 on full feature decoding) and GPT-4o (e.g., +10 on relation decoding). LLaVA 1.5, the only open-source model tested, performed substantially worse than all proprietary models on every task, underscoring a gap between open and closed VLMs on multi-object reasoning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the binding problem framework from cognitive science and neuroscience (Treisman & Gelade's Feature Integration Theory, illusory conjunctions, subitizing limits) to provide a unified explanation for diverse failures in state-of-the-art VLMs. Rather than building on a prior ML method, it bridges two literatures: the well-studied human capacity constraints in multi-object visual processing and the recent empirical observations that VLMs struggle with counting, relational generation, and visual analogy. The normative formalization of the binding problem by Frankland, Webb & Cohen (2021) and the object-centric learning literature (slot attention, MONET) inform the discussion of potential solutions, while prior VLM evaluation work (Rahmanzadehgervi et al., Rane et al., Mitchell et al., Yiu et al.) provides the empirical motivation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a single theoretical lens (representational interference from compositional coding) that unifies previously disparate VLM failure modes across counting, visual search, scene description, and analogy, and introduces feature triplets as a quantitative predictor of binding errors. The counterintuitive implication that binding failures actually evidence the presence of compositional representations (a strength for generalization) reframes the problem: the challenge is not to eliminate compositionality but to add serial or slot-based binding mechanisms. Key open questions include whether slot-based or sequential attention methods can be scaled to current VLM architectures without sacrificing generalization, and whether the observed capacity limits are fundamental to transformer-based processing or artifacts of specific training regimes. Limitations include reliance on proprietary models (no access to internal representations), a relatively narrow task set, and the absence of open-source model validation beyond LLaVA-1.5.
