## Problem

Existing data valuation methods assign value to discrete datasets but do not formalize the value of the underlying sampling distribution, nor do they provide a theoretically grounded policy for comparing distributions from small preview samples. In data marketplaces where buyers evaluate vendors via limited samples, this gap is critical: no prior method offers a principled way to conclude that one vendor's distribution is more valuable than another's, especially when vendor data is heterogeneous.

## Input / Output

The method takes sample datasets D_i from n vendors (each drawn i.i.d. from distribution P_i, with potentially different sizes m_i) and produces a valuation score ν̂(D_i) = -d̂(D_i, D_U) for each, where D_U is a uniform mixture constructed by subsampling each D_i to a common minimum size. The output is not just a scalar score but an actionable comparison policy: a criterion margin Δ'_{Υ,ν} that, when exceeded by the difference in scores, certifies that one distribution is more valuable than another by a user-specified margin ε_Υ with a stated confidence level (1-2δ'). Notably, no ground-truth reference dataset is required.

## Method

The paper defines distribution value as the negative MMD to a reference, Υ(P) = -d(P, P*), and assumes each vendor distribution follows a Huber contamination model P_i = (1-ε_i)P* + ε_i Q_i. Under this model, MMD yields a clean linear characterization: Υ(P) = -ε·d(P*, Q), making the effect of heterogeneity on value explicit and interpretable. Since P* is unknown, the paper replaces it with a convex mixture P_ω of vendor distributions, deriving an error bound |Υ(P) - Υ̂(P)| ≤ ε_ω·d(Q_ω, P*) via the MMD triangle inequality (Proposition 2). The uniform mixture P_U is then selected as the specific reference through a game-theoretic argument: in a two-player zero-sum game where the row player picks a vendor and the column player adversarily permutes vendor order, the uniform strategy is worst-case optimal (Proposition 3), proven via strong duality of the associated linear programs. The final comparison policy (Theorem 1) combines the MMD estimator's uniform convergence with the Huber-closed property to give a finite-sample criterion margin that a buyer can check to conclude P ≻ P' + ε_Υ with probability ≥ 1-2δ'.

## Results

The paper evaluates data distribution valuation via Pearson correlation ρ(ν, ζ) between sample-based values and expected test performance. On classification (CIFAR-10 vs. CIFAR-100), the conditional variant (Ours cond.) achieves ρ = 0.989 with a validation set, while the base MMD method (Ours) achieves ρ = 0.763, outperforming CS (0.889), MMD² (0.764), LAVA (−0.907), and DAVINZ (−0.437). Without a validation set (ρ(ν̂, ζ)), Ours achieves 0.564 on CIFAR-10 and 0.461 on TON, beating all baselines except MMD² (0.563, 0.480) which is nearly tied. On regression (CaliH vs. KingH), Ours achieves ρ = 0.896 (with D_val) and 0.767 (without), outperforming DAVINZ (0.852 / 0.048) and decisively beating IG (−0.907) and VV (−0.603).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The MMD-based method (Ours) is essentially tied with MMD² across all settings (e.g., 0.763 vs. 0.764 on CIFAR-10, 0.584 vs. 0.526 on TON), which is expected since their values differ only by a square and the evaluation focuses on ranking. Against CS (Class-wise Shapley), Ours wins on TON (0.584 vs. 0.451) and decisively without a validation set (0.564 vs. −0.874 on CIFAR-10), while CS edges ahead on CIFAR-10 with D_val (0.889 vs. 0.763) by exploiting label information. LAVA and DAVINZ, both gradient-based, perform poorly under the Huber model (negative correlations on CIFAR-10: −0.907 and −0.437), because gradients from out-of-distribution samples are uninformative about true data value. On regression, Ours outperforms DAVINZ without D_val by a large margin (0.767 vs. 0.048 on CaliH; 0.769 vs. 0.227 on Census15), while reference-free baselines IG and VV produce strongly negative correlations (−0.907, −0.603), confirming that removing the reference entirely fails under heterogeneity. Overall, the paper reports that MMD outperforms four leading valuation metrics on 3 out of 4 classification settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant data valuation literature (Data Shapley, LAVA, DAVINZ, CS-Shapley) that assigns value to a discrete dataset, by formalizing the value of the underlying sampling distribution and providing a comparison policy between distributions from their samples. It builds on the MMD framework from Gretton et al. [26] and the Huber contamination model from robust statistics [30], but is the first to combine them for distribution-level valuation with finite-sample guarantees. The closest prior work, Tay et al. [60], uses MMD² for computational convenience in a synthetic-data reward setting but does not address distribution comparison or provide theoretical guarantees for the approximation error of a constructed reference. The game-theoretic justification for the uniform mixture as reference draws on zero-sum game theory but is applied here in a novel context where the payoff matrix is unknown.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work fills a practical gap in data marketplaces where buyers must compare vendor distributions from limited preview samples without access to a ground-truth reference, providing the first theoretically grounded comparison policy with explicit error bounds. The clean characterization that MMD value under Huber is linear in the outlier fraction (Eq. 2) is a useful structural result that may inform future robust valuation methods. Key limitations include the restriction of theory to the Huber model (though MMD is empirically effective under non-Huber settings), and the open question of whether the framework extends to more general heterogeneity models or can incorporate incentive compatibility and truthfulness guarantees for data vendors.
