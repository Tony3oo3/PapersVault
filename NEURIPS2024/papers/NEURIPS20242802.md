## Problem

Existing Federated Prototype Learning methods assign a single averaged prototype per class per client, which fails to capture the rich feature variance in 'hard' domains (e.g., SVHN) where samples are scattered, while being sufficient for 'easy' domains (e.g., MNIST) with tight clustering. This creates systematic cross-domain performance gaps where harder domains consistently underperform, a disparity that prior FedPL work has not addressed.

## Input / Output

Each client receives image samples from a single heterogeneous domain (e.g., one client on MNIST, another on SVHN) and processes them through a shared ResNet-10 backbone to produce 512-dimensional feature vectors. The output is a standard M-class prediction from a linear classifier, but the training signal is driven by clustered prototypes (multiple per class) rather than a single mean vector, with the number of prototypes adapting to the local domain's feature sparsity.

## Method

FedPLVM introduces two mechanisms to mitigate cross-domain representation variance. First, a dual-level prototype clustering strategy: at the client level, FINCH (a parameter-free clustering algorithm using cosine similarity) partitions same-class feature vectors into multiple local clustered prototypes, capturing intra-domain variance; at the server level, a second clustering pass aggregates all local prototypes into a smaller set of global clustered prototypes, reducing communication overhead and limiting privacy leakage. Second, an α-sparsity prototype loss modifies the standard InfoNCE-style contrastive objective by raising the cosine similarity to a fractional power α∈(0,1), which—due to the concavity of the power function—disproportionately amplifies inter-class distances relative to intra-class ones, pushing feature distributions apart more aggressively. A correction term (L_corr) then pulls the average intra-class similarity back toward 1 to counterbalance the α-induced intra-class spread, and the total local loss combines this with a standard cross-entropy term weighted by λ.

## Results

On Digit-5, FedPLVM achieves 69.25% average test accuracy, outperforming the strongest baseline FPL (66.88%) by 2.37% and FedAvg (59.37%) by 9.88%. On Office-10, it reaches 60.65% average accuracy (+7.55% over FedAvg), and on DomainNet it attains 42.55% (+7.03% over FedAvg). Notably, gains are largest on harder domains: SVHN improves by 5.3% over FPL on Digit-5, while the easier MNIST domain improves by only 0.58%, confirming the method's goal of reducing cross-domain performance disparity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedPLVM outperforms all six baselines across all three datasets. Against the closest competitor FPL (CVPR 2023), it gains +2.37% on Digit-5, +2.10% on Office-10, and +2.24% on DomainNet, with the largest per-domain margin on SVHN (+5.30% on Digit-5) and Quickdraw (+4.93% on DomainNet). FedFA (arXiv 2023) is the second-strongest baseline, trailing FedPLVM by 2.51% on Digit-5 and 1.66% on Office-10. FedPCL (NeurIPS 2022) and FedProto (AAAI 2022) are consistently 3–4% below FedPLVM in average accuracy. Standard FL baselines FedAvg and FedProx lag by roughly 10% and 10.1% respectively on Digit-5, highlighting the benefit of prototype-based methods in heterogeneous-domain settings. The paper also shows that under unbalanced client distributions (Sec. F), FedPLVM still surpasses FPL by 2.94% on SVHN, and under label non-IID (Dirichlet α=0.5) it leads FPL by 1.55% on Digit-5 and 1.54% on Office-10.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Federated Prototype Learning (FedPL) framework established by FedProto (Tan et al., AAAI 2022), which decomposes the model into a feature extractor and classifier and uses class-mean prototypes for alignment. It most directly builds on FPL (Huang et al., CVPR 2023), which introduced global-level prototype clustering to handle domain shift, but departs from it in two key ways: (1) adding a local-level clustering step to capture intra-domain feature variance that averaging discards, and (2) replacing the standard InfoNCE-style contrastive loss with an α-sparsity prototype loss that asymmetrically amplifies inter-class separation. The paper explicitly positions its contribution as addressing 'unequal learning challenges across domains' (a property of the data itself) rather than FPL's focus on imbalanced client counts per domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies a concrete and under-addressed failure mode in FedPL: single averaged prototypes are insufficient for 'hard' domains with high feature variance, leading to systematic performance gaps that persist even when client distributions are balanced. The dual-level clustering + α-sparsity loss combination is a lightweight, parameter-free (FINCH) solution that improves hard-domain accuracy by 3–5% without increasing communication cost, making it practically deployable. However, the evaluation is limited to small-scale classification benchmarks (Digit-5, Office-10, DomainNet) with a ResNet-10 backbone, and the authors acknowledge that noisy labels would distort prototype clustering—a significant real-world concern. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger backbones and datasets, how it interacts with label noise, and whether the α-sparsity mechanism generalizes to non-prototype-based FL methods.
