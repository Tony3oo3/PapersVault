## Problem

Algorithmic contract design has focused almost exclusively on the one-shot setting, where the principal offers a single contract and the agent best-responds. In practice, principal-agent relationships are repeated, and agents respond to repeated interactions through no-regret learning rather than complex dynamic strategies. This paper initiates the study of optimal dynamic contract design against a mean-based no-regret learning agent, asking what contract sequence a principal should offer over T rounds to maximize her revenue.

## Input / Output

The method takes a contract problem (action costs, outcome probability distributions, principal rewards) and a known time horizon T, assuming the agent applies a mean-based no-regret algorithm (e.g., multiplicative weights, EXP3, follow the perturbed leader). The output is a free-fall dynamic contract: a single contract parameter α (or a base contract p scaled by α) offered for λT rounds, followed by the zero contract for the remaining (1−λ)T rounds. Both α and λ are computable in polynomial time by enumerating pairs of indifference points, and the result holds even for oblivious (non-adaptive) principals.

## Method

The core result (Theorem 3.1) proves that among all dynamic linear contracts, a free-fall contract is always optimal, via a sequence of rewriting rules that transform any dynamic contract into free-fall form without decreasing the principal's utility. The proof proceeds in a continuous-time analogue (reducing the discrete problem via Theorem 2.4, adapted from Deng et al. 2019), where trajectories are sequences of (contract, duration, action) segments constrained by mean-based best-response conditions. Three key rewriting lemmas drive the transformation: merging consecutive segments that induce the same action, shifting boundary segments to favor the higher-reward action, and showing the principal should abruptly switch to the zero contract rather than gradually slowing the agent's descent through action regions. The result generalizes to arbitrary contracts with single-dimensional scaling (Theorem D.1), and the paper further shows that in a positive-measure subset of contract games, the optimal dynamic contract makes both players strictly better off than the best static contract (Theorem 3.2), with welfare improvements of Θ(n) and agent utility improvements of Θ(log n).

## Results

In the canonical 3-action success/failure example (Figure 1), the optimal free-fall dynamic contract yields the principal a revenue of (5/12)T, a 5/4 multiplicative improvement over the optimal static contract's (1/3)T. More broadly, Theorem 3.2 establishes that in a positive-measure subset of contract games, the optimal dynamic contract improves total welfare by a Θ(n) factor and the agent's utility by a Θ(log n) factor over the best static contract. For the unknown-horizon setting, Theorems 4.2–4.3 show that for any error tolerance ε > 0, there exists a minimum time-uncertainty ratio γ = exp(Ω(1/ε)) beyond which no dynamic contract can maintain a (1+ε) advantage over the static benchmark, while for any γ ≥ 1 some nonzero advantage ε = Ω(1/poly(γ)) remains achievable.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the optimal static contract (repeating the best one-shot contract every round). The paper's central finding is that free-fall dynamic contracts strictly outperform this baseline in the mean-based learning setting: the illustrative example achieves a 5/4 revenue gain, and Theorem 3.2 shows unbounded (Θ(n)) welfare gains in a positive-measure family of instances. In contrast, against a no-swap-regret agent, the best static contract is provably also the best dynamic contract (Observation I.2), so no dynamic advantage exists. The paper also contrasts mean-based learning with no-swap-regret learning: committing to the weaker mean-based algorithm can paradoxically benefit the agent (higher utility under the optimal dynamic contract) because mean-based dynamics approach the larger CCE set, whereas no-swap-regret dynamics are confined to the smaller CE set. No empirical baselines or competing algorithmic methods are compared, as the contribution is entirely theoretical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the framework of optimizing against no-regret learners—established for auctions by Braverman et al. [15] and for general repeated games by Deng et al. [27]—to the principal-agent contract setting, where the key structural difference is that surplus is split between players rather than fully extracted by the designer. It also departs from the economics literature on repeated contracts, which typically assumes fully rational strategic play and yields overly complex optimal contracts, by modeling the agent as a no-regret (specifically mean-based) learner. The continuous-time reduction technique from [27] is directly adapted, and the mean-based learning definition is taken from [15], but the contract-specific structure (linear contracts, indifference points, moral hazard) creates a fundamentally different optimization landscape.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean, poly-time computable characterization of the optimal dynamic contract (free-fall structure) and reveals that dynamic contracting can be a Pareto improvement over static contracting—both players benefit—contrasting sharply with the auction setting where the learner is fully exploited. The unknown-time-horizon result identifies a fundamental limitation: even limited uncertainty about T sharply degrades the principal's dynamic advantage, a gap absent from all prior work on optimizing against learners. Key open questions include the optimal dynamic contract without single-dimensional scaling (where free-fall may not be optimal and computability is open), contracts against agents with hidden types, and the two-learner setting.
