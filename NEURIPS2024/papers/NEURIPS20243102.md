## Problem

Despite substantial attention to fair dataset collection practices, there remains a significant gap in understanding the practical challenges and trade-offs that dataset curators actually encounter throughout the curation lifecycle. Existing scholarship has focused on theoretical guidelines and implicit challenges identified in the fairness literature, but lacks grounded empirical evidence from practitioners engaged in fair dataset work on the ground.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243102/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The study takes 30 semi-structured interviews (45–60 minutes each) with ML dataset curators from academia and industry who have experience curating fair vision, language, or multi-modal datasets. The output is a two-axis taxonomy: challenges are organized along the dataset lifecycle (requirements, design, implementation, evaluation, maintenance) and a social ecological model of the broader fairness landscape (individual, discipline, organization, regulatory, socio-political levels), with each challenge mapped to one or more of three fairness dimensions—composition, process, and release.

## Method

The authors conducted 30 semi-structured interviews between November 2023 and March 2024, recruiting participants through purposive sampling of public dataset authors, social media outreach, and snowball sampling. Thematic analysis followed an inductive approach: four authors independently coded two interviews to build and refine a shared codebook, then the remaining interviews were equally distributed for coding across the team. The analysis surfaced three dimensions of fairness (composition, process, release) that participants considered during curation, and mapped identified challenges to both lifecycle-specific phases and a social ecological model of the broader fairness landscape. The approach deliberately avoided prescribing a specific definition of fairness, empowering participants to articulate their own, which revealed the contextual and contested nature of fairness definitions in practice.

## Results

This is a qualitative study with no quantitative metrics. The authors conducted 30 semi-structured interviews (45–60 min each, Nov 2023–Mar 2024) with ML dataset curators and produced a taxonomy of challenges organized across five dataset lifecycle phases (Requirements, Design, Implementation, Evaluation, Maintenance) and five levels of a broader fairness landscape (Individual, Discipline, Organization, Regulatory, Socio-Political). Three dimensions of fairness—composition, process, and release—cut across all phases. The taxonomy identifies specific challenges such as scoping trade-offs, taxonomy design under data scarcity, vendor opacity, annotator diversity gaps, gold-standard paradigm limitations, spurious correlations, and power differentials.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a qualitative, interview-based study and does not compare methods against quantitative baselines. It builds on Hutchinson et al.'s [78] dataset lifecycle framework and provides empirical grounding for prior theoretical and guideline-focused work (e.g., [3, 42, 55, 78–80, 91, 93, 111]) that identified implicit challenges in the fairness literature. The authors position their contribution as shifting from prescriptive guidelines to grounded, practitioner-reported challenges and trade-offs, filling a gap they identify between existing fairness scholarship and actual curation practice. No head-to-head method comparisons or benchmark evaluations are presented.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Hutchinson et al.'s [78] dataset lifecycle framework to organize challenges, but departs from the predominantly theoretical and guideline-focused scholarship (datasheets, data cards, design principles) by grounding its taxonomy in 30 semi-structured interviews with actual dataset curators. It provides empirical support for prior work that identified implicit challenges in the fairness literature [3, 42, 55, 78–80, 91, 93, 111], while extending the analysis beyond the dataset artifact itself to encompass a social ecological model [21] spanning individual, disciplinary, organizational, regulatory, and socio-political levels. The paper explicitly positions itself as shifting from prescriptive recommendations to descriptive understanding of what curators actually experience.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The taxonomy fills a recognized gap between fairness guidelines and the practical realities of dataset curation, offering stakeholders a structured vocabulary for the trade-offs curators navigate (e.g., fairness vs. scale, diverse annotation vs. gold-standard paradigms, legal compliance vs. data availability). A key open question is whether the findings generalize beyond the predominantly North American and European sample, and how to operationalize the recommended systemic changes (funding for maintenance, codified data worker protections, accessible legal resources) at institutional scale. The paper also raises the unresolved tension of whether fairness is even measurable or stable enough to serve as a curation target, leaving the field without a consensus operationalization.
