## Problem

Behavior cloning (BC) was believed to incur quadratic dependence on horizon H in offline imitation learning, motivating online algorithms like Dagger that achieve linear dependence under recoverability conditions. This paper asks whether the offline–online gap is fundamental or an artifact of the specific loss function and analysis used. The authors show the gap is smaller than previously thought: with the right loss and analysis, offline IL matches online IL in the realizable setting, and online IL cannot improve further without additional structural assumptions on the policy class.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n reward-free expert trajectories of length H (state-action sequences from an MDP) and outputs a learned imitator policy parameterized by a neural network (depth-2 MLP for Walker2d, CNN for Beamrider, GPT-2 small for Dyck language generation). Rewards are normalized so cumulative reward lies in [0, R], with R = O(1) for sparse and R = H for dense settings. For the language task, the input is a Dyck-3 prefix and the output is a completed bracket sequence, scored by a binary validity reward.

## Method

The core contribution is a new analysis of behavior cloning with the logarithmic loss (LogLossBC), which minimizes the negative log-likelihood of expert actions. The key insight is that log loss implicitly performs maximum likelihood estimation over trajectory distributions, yielding convergence in squared Hellinger distance D_H²(P^π̂, P^π*) at rate O(log|Π|/n). A novel trajectory-level distance ρ(π‖π′)—which is symmetric when the expert is deterministic—enables translating this supervised error into rollout regret as J(π*)−J(π̂) ≤ 4R·D_H²(P^π̂, P^π*), achieving horizon-independent sample complexity without error amplification. For stochastic experts, a variance-dependent bound with σ²_π* controls the slow 1/√n rate, and a signed recoverability parameter  μ̃ can restore linear-in-H scaling. Lower bounds confirm that without parameter sharing, online IL (LogLossDagger) can still improve over offline IL, but with parameter sharing—as in transformers—the two are equivalent.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 2.1 + Corollary 2.1) shows that LogLossBC achieves regret O(R·log(|Π|)/n) for deterministic experts, which is horizon-independent under sparse rewards (R=O(1)) and linear in H under dense rewards (R=H) when the policy class uses parameter sharing (log|Π|=O(1)). This improves upon the classical O(H²·log(|Π|)/n) bound for indicator-loss BC by a factor of O(H). Empirically, on Walker2d-v4 and BeamRiderNoFrameskip-v4 (H varied from 50 to 500), the expected regret of LogLossBC is largely independent of horizon, consistent with theory; on BeamRider, regret actually improves slightly with longer horizons. On the Dyck_3 language task, regret does increase with H, but this is attributed to growing policy class complexity (measured via log-product-norm) rather than a fundamental horizon penalty.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary empirical baseline is the original MSE-based behavior cloning of Pomerleau [63]. On Walker2d-v4 and the Car environment, LogLossBC and MSE-BC achieve comparable regret across all horizons and trajectory counts, with no clear winner; the authors note that under the Gaussian policy parameterization used for Walker2d, the two losses are closely related up to state-dependent heteroskedasticity. Theoretically, LogLossBC improves upon indicator-loss BC (the standard offline baseline) by an O(H) factor in the dense-reward regime, going from quadratic to linear horizon dependence for parameter-sharing classes. Compared to the online Dagger algorithm [72], LogLossBC matches or improves upon the O(μH·log|Π|/n) bound for parameter-sharing classes (where μ=Ω(1)), while Dagger retains an advantage only for non-stationary product classes with no parameter sharing. The paper also shows via a lower bound (Theorem 2.2) that no online algorithm can improve upon LogLossBC's O(H/n) rate for dense rewards with parameter-sharing classes, even with known dynamics and recoverability μ=1.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper re-analyzes the standard log-loss behavior cloning objective (already ubiquitous in practice and equivalent to next-token prediction in language modeling) through a novel Hellinger-distance-based trajectory-level argument, showing it achieves horizon-independent sample complexity for general policy classes with parameter sharing. It extends Rajaraman et al.'s (2021) trajectory-level distance analysis from linearly parameterized policies to arbitrary finite classes, and subsumes the classical Dagger/Aggrevate online IL framework by showing that for parameter-sharing policies, online interaction provides no additional sample complexity benefit. The work departs from the Ross & Bagnell (2010) paradigm that attributed quadratic horizon dependence to error amplification inherent to BC, instead showing the issue was a suboptimal loss function (indicator/MSE) rather than a fundamental limitation of offline learning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result resolves a long-standing theoretical question by showing the offline/online IL gap is smaller than previously believed: for the parameter-sharing policies used in virtually all practical IL and language modeling, offline LogLossBC is already minimax-optimal and online interaction cannot help. This has direct implications for autoregressive language modeling, suggesting that exposure bias may stem from optimization or architecture challenges rather than a fundamental limitation of next-token prediction. Key limitations include the realizable/finite-class setting, and open questions include whether Hellinger distance is the right proxy for real-world performance, how misspecification and control-theoretic stability constraints interact with these guarantees, and whether the results extend to infinite-dimensional function classes like deep networks.
