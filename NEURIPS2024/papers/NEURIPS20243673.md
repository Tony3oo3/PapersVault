## Problem

The G-Bispectrum is the lowest-degree complete G-invariant, making it the ideal computational primitive for G-invariant layers in group-equivariant CNNs. However, its O(|G|²) space and time complexity (and O(|G|³) for the equivalent G-TC) has prevented practical adoption in deep learning, where even O(|G|) operations become prohibitive for large groups. The paper asks whether the inherent redundancies in the full G-Bispectrum can be exploited to achieve complete invariance at O(|G|) cost.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243673/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on real-valued signals Θ: G → ℝ defined over a finite group G (e.g., C₈ for rotations, D₈ for rotations plus reflections), typically obtained as feature maps from a G-convolutional layer applied to 28×28 grayscale handwritten digit or letter images. It produces O(|G|) selected bispectral coefficients (the selective G-Bispectrum) that are provably sufficient to invert and recover the original signal up to group action. In the classification pipeline, these coefficients are fed to a secondary MLP that outputs a class label.

## Method

The core contribution is Algorithm 1, which selects a minimal set of O(|G|) bispectral coefficient pairs (ρ₁, ρ₂) by iteratively choosing pairs whose tensor product ρ₁ ⊗ ρ₂ generates irreps not yet covered, starting from the trivial representation. The paper proves this selective G-Bispectrum is invertible (hence complete) for all finite commutative groups, all dihedral groups Dₙ, and the octahedral/full octahedral groups, reducing space complexity from O(|G|²) to O(|G|) and time complexity from O(|G|²) to O(|G| log |G|) when an FFT on G is available. The selective G-Bispectrum is integrated as a G-invariant pooling layer in G-CNNs, sitting between the G-convolution and a secondary MLP. Experiments on SO(2)/O(2)-MNIST and EMNIST show it matches G-TC accuracy while being significantly faster, and substantially outperforms Max G-pooling especially when the number of convolutional filters is small.

## Results

On SO(2)/O(2)-MNIST with C8/D8 groups, the selective G-Bispectrum achieves 95% accuracy (24 filters, C8) and 93% accuracy (4 filters, D8), closely matching G-TC (96%) while being significantly faster. On EMNIST, it reaches 77% (C8) and 74% (D8) versus 80% and 79% for G-TC. Training time scales linearly with group size when an FFT is available (e.g., 247 s vs. 1465 s for G-TC on C_{2^7}), and with only 2 convolutional filters the selective G-Bispectrum maintains 96% accuracy on SO(2)-MNIST compared to 63% for Max G-pooling.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against G-TC pooling (Sanborn & Miolane, NeurIPS 2023), the selective G-Bispectrum achieves nearly identical accuracy (95% vs. 96% on SO(2)-MNIST; 77% vs. 80% on SO(2)-EMNIST) while reducing time complexity from O(|G|³) to O(|G| log |G|) and space from O(|G|²) to O(|G|), yielding a ~6× speedup on C_{2^7}. Against Max G-pooling (Cohen & Welling, ICML 2016), the selective G-Bispectrum wins decisively when the number of filters is small: with only 2 filters it reaches 96% on SO(2)-MNIST versus 63% for Max G-pooling, and with 4 filters on O(2)-MNIST it scores 93% versus 78%. With many filters (24), Max G-pooling catches up to near-parity (96% vs. 95%), suggesting the selective G-Bispectrum's advantage is most pronounced under tight parameter budgets. Against Avg G-pooling, the selective G-Bispectrum dominates across all settings (e.g., 95% vs. 74% on SO(2)-MNIST, 77% vs. 40% on SO(2)-EMNIST). The paper also demonstrates that the selective G-Bispectrum is robust to adversarial attacks in the same sense as G-TC: optimizing an input to match a target selective G-Bispectrum recovers only the original signal up to group action, whereas Max G-pooling produces noisy, non-invariant outputs.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Kakarala's foundational work on the G-Bispectrum as a complete G-invariant (originally proven for cyclic groups) by showing that only O(|G|) bispectral coefficients are needed for inversion across all commutative groups, all dihedral groups, and the octahedral groups. It positions itself as a computational bridge between the expensive G-TC invariant layer of Sanborn & Miolane (O(|G|³) time) and the lossy Max G-pooling of Cohen & Welling, offering a complete invariant at O(|G|log|G|) time with FFT. The selective G-Bispectrum is integrated into the G-CNN architecture of Cohen & Welling and the bispectral network framework of Sanborn et al., but the key departure is the algorithmic selection of a minimal complete subset of coefficients via the Kronecker table structure.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The O(|G|) space complexity result removes the primary barrier to using complete G-invariant layers in G-CNNs for larger symmetry groups (e.g., fine-grained rotation discretizations or 3D octahedral symmetries), which were previously intractable. However, the empirical validation is limited to small groups (C₈, D₈) on MNIST/EMNIST, and the paper itself notes that Max G-pooling recovers much of the accuracy gap when the number of convolutional filters is increased, suggesting the practical advantage is most relevant for parameter-constrained settings. Open questions include whether the completeness guarantee holds for arbitrary finite groups (the algorithm is general but proofs are only for specific families), whether the approach scales to continuous or infinite groups, and whether the accuracy gap versus G-TC persists on harder datasets like CIFAR or ImageNet with larger groups.
