## Problem

Generating k autocomplete drafts from a language model requires k separate autoregressive inference passes, making multi-suggestion features (e.g., GitHub Copilot, Gmail Smart Compose) computationally expensive. No existing decoding method can produce multiple diverse drafts at the cost of a single forward pass through the model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243773/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prefix of m tokens and produces k short completion drafts (typically 10 tokens each, suited to autocomplete). It operates on any pre-trained autoregressive LM without weight modification. A pre-built n-gram frequency table (n=2 to 6, ~14 GB from RedPajama) is consulted at each step for per-draft smoothing, and can be swapped for domain-specific corpora (code, healthcare, etc.) at inference time. Generation length is practically capped around 10 tokens due to progressive loss of embedding linearity.

## Method

At each autoregressive timestep, Superposed Decoding constructs a single superposed embedding as a probability-weighted linear combination of the most recent token embeddings across all k active drafts, and feeds it as one input to the LM instead of k separate inputs. The resulting next-token distribution is then combined with a draft-specific n-gram interpolation (exponential mean over n=2–6 n-gram models) to produce a unique per-draft distribution, from which k² candidate extensions are formed and the top-k are retained. The key insight is that LLM representations are approximately linear, so a weighted superposition of token embeddings yields a next-token distribution that closely approximates the average of individual draft distributions. This requires no retraining or architectural changes and is complementary to other decoding strategies, enabling hybrid approaches (e.g., Nucleus Sampling followed by SPD) that expand semantic coverage at no additional compute cost.

## Results

On OpenWebText (15-token prefix, 10-token generation, k=3 drafts), Superposed Decoding achieves a best-draft average perplexity of 4.63, 5% lower than Nucleus Sampling (5.17), while requiring only a single inference pass. In wall-clock latency, SPD is 2.44× faster than Nucleus Sampling for k=3 and 3.54× faster for k=8. On fact-based benchmarks, SPD outperforms Nucleus Sampling and Beam Search at P@1, and three drafts yield coverage gains of up to 2.72% on TriviaQA and 1.69% on Natural Questions. In a compute-normalized human evaluation (3 SPD drafts vs. 1 Nucleus draft), users prefer SPD 63.6% of the time.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Nucleus Sampling [18], Superposed Decoding wins on every major axis: best-draft perplexity is 5% lower (4.63 vs. 5.17), latency is 2.44× lower for k=3 drafts, P@1 factuality is higher on both TriviaQA and Natural Questions, and human preference favors SPD 63.6% of the time in a compute-normalized 3v1 setting (and still 51.4% in a 1v1 setting). Against Top-k Sampling [12], the paper does not run a dedicated head-to-head table, but Top-k is grouped with other standard methods in the latency comparison (Figure 6) where SPD is uniformly faster for k≥2. SPD also outperforms Beam Search and Greedy Decoding on P@1 factuality, and in the inference-time scaling experiment (Table 2), combining Nucleus Sampling with SPD (NS_SPD3) reaches 79.95% coverage on TriviaQA at 100 compute units versus 76.56% for vanilla Nucleus Sampling alone, a 3.4-point gain.

## Relation to Prior Work

Superposed Decoding repurposes the documented linearity of token representations in large language models (Jiang et al. 2024; Park et al. 2023) in a novel way: instead of running k separate autoregressive passes for k drafts, it feeds a probability-weighted superposition of k token embeddings as a single input to the LM. This departs from standard decoding methods (Greedy, Top-k, Beam Search, Nucleus Sampling) that scale linearly in inference passes, and from multi-token prediction or Medusa, which achieve parallelism but require retraining or fine-tuning. The method also borrows n-gram interpolation (Khandelwal et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2024) as a lightweight coherence filter, positioning SPD as a training-free, plug-in decoding algorithm complementary to existing efficiency techniques like speculative decoding and quantization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SPD offers a practical 2.44×+ speedup for multi-draft generation (k≥3) without any model modification, making it immediately deployable in production autocomplete and drafting systems where compute is a barrier. Its complementarity with Nucleus Sampling for inference-time scaling (Table 2) suggests a simple recipe for improving coverage at scale. Key limitations remain: linearity degrades after ~10 timesteps, capping generation length; drafts are syntactically but not semantically diverse; and coherence depends on the quality of external n-gram models. Open questions include whether embedding orthogonality can restore semantic diversity, whether resets can extend useful generation length, and whether the approach generalizes to longer-form or domain-specific settings without heavy n-gram storage.
