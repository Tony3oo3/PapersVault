## Problem

Feedforward networks account for over 60% of parameters and FLOPs in modern LLMs, yet structured linear approximations (low-rank, block-diagonal) for FFNs have not been thoroughly explored at scale for pre-training from scratch. While prior work studied these approximations in compression or fine-tuning contexts, their behavior during full pre-training—including scaling properties, practical inference efficiency, and optimization challenges—remains largely uncharacterized for recent transformer-based LLM architectures.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240153/cropped_page0_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The models consume text token sequences (max length 1024) from the RefinedWeb corpus and produce next-token predictions for language modeling. The structured FFN modules replace the two dense linear projections (with GeLU activation) inside each transformer block, reducing parameters to 63% or 32% of the original FFN size while preserving the same input/output dimensions. A secondary vision experiment uses CIFAR-10 RGB images (32×32) with 5-layer MLPs and ViTs to probe whether block-diagonal structures serve as a useful inductive bias for locality-sensitive tasks.

## Method

The paper introduces three structured linear parameterizations for FFN layers—LowRank (factorized U^r V^r with bottleneck R), BlockShuffle (two block-diagonal matrices interleaved with a shuffle permutation), and BlockDense (block-diagonal followed by a dense or low-rank projection)—and trains transformers from 110M to 1.3B parameters using them from initialization. To address the parallelism-bound latency problem during online decoding (small batch, sequence length 1), a pre-merge technique combines the structured matrices into a single dense layer, dynamically selecting the faster parametrization based on batch size. To counter the poor training dynamics (loss spikes, slow convergence) caused by symmetries in the deep linear factorization, the paper proposes self-guided training: a dense residual branch W is added with a cosine-decaying coefficient α, initialized as W₀ = U₀V₀, so that the well-conditioned dense gradient guides feature specialization before the structured matrices take over. A stochastic variant (Eq. 2) reduces the extra compute to ~25% of original FFN FLOPs. Scaling experiments show steeper loss-vs-FLOPs curves and that wide-and-structured networks (GQA attention + LowRank FFN) achieve lower perplexity with fewer parameters and higher throughput than dense or GQA baselines at matched training FLOPs.

## Results

Under the same training FLOPs, the Wide and Structured networks (using LowRank FFN + reduced attention heads) achieve lower perplexity with substantially fewer parameters: at the -m scale, 219M params reach PPL 17.89 vs. 18.29 for the dense 335M Transformer; at the -l scale, 464M params reach PPL 14.27 vs. 14.29 for the dense 729M Transformer. Throughput improves by 8% and 17% over GQA at the -m and -l scales respectively. With self-guided training, the perplexity gap of LowRank (32% FFN params) on the 1.3B model shrinks from ~1.1 to 0.4, while retaining a 1.35× training speed-up.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is the GQA (Generalized Multi-Query Attention) Transformer [Ainslie et al., 2023], which is the current standard for efficient attention in LLMs. The Wide and Structured networks outperform GQA on both perplexity and throughput at both scales tested: at -m, PPL 17.89 vs. 18.23 (GQA) with 8% higher throughput; at -l, PPL 14.27 vs. 14.40 (GQA) with 17% higher throughput. Against the standard dense Transformer, the structured models match or beat perplexity while using 34–36% fewer parameters (219M vs. 335M at -m; 464M vs. 729M at -l). The paper also compares three structured parametrizations (LowRank, BlockDense, BlockShuffle) against each other, finding that LowRank and BlockDense consistently outperform BlockShuffle by 0.4–0.8 PPL at the -m and -s scales, suggesting block-diagonal-only structures are suboptimal for FFN layers in language models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the structured-matrix literature (Monarch decomposition from Dao et al., block-diagonal patterns from ShuffleNet, spectral initialization from Khodak et al.) from its prior contexts—compression of pre-trained weights, LoRA-style fine-tuning, and ResNet/CNN experiments—into the train-from-scratch pretraining of modern Transformer LLMs at up to 1.3B parameters. It departs from prior work in three key ways: treating structured FFNs as an architectural choice from initialization rather than a post-hoc approximation, scaling to a regime where training-FLOPs and throughput comparisons become meaningful, and introducing self-guided training to address the optimization pathologies (loss spikes, slow convergence) that arise from the symmetry of deep linear factorizations. The pre-merge technique for online decoding is a practical addition not present in prior structured-matrix studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Since FFNs account for over 60% of LLM parameters and FLOPs, demonstrating that structured alternatives can match or beat dense models at equal training compute (e.g., 464M vs. 729M parameters with comparable perplexity and 17% higher throughput) offers a concrete path to cheaper pretraining and faster inference. The steeper scaling curves suggest the advantage may grow with scale, but the study is capped at 1.3B parameters and does not explore optimal scaling laws for structured matrices, leaving open whether the trend persists at the 70B+ scale of current frontier models. The self-guided training technique is broadly applicable to any factorized linear layer, and the observation that block-diagonal structures benefit vision (locality) but underperform in NLP highlights task-dependent inductive biases that warrant further investigation.
