## Problem

Deep-learning decoders achieve state-of-the-art performance in brain-machine interfaces but their black-box nature raises safety concerns for physical control applications, where explainable linear models like the Kalman filter remain the norm. This paper asks whether the performance gap between these two regimes can be closed without sacrificing interpretability, by asking: can we have the best of both worlds?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244256/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The decoders consume 50ms non-overlapping bins of spiking band power (300–1000 Hz) from intracortical microelectrode arrays; KalmanNet uses only ~20–30 'active' channels while the other decoders use ~60–70 morphologically consistent channels. Outputs are 4-dimensional kinematic states (index and middle-ring-small finger positions and velocities), with tcFNN predicting velocity only. During online trials, the predicted velocity is integrated with a 2% position-stabilization term to drive a virtual hand visualization in real time.

## Method

KalmanNet retains the linear state-space structure of the Kalman filter (dynamics matrix A and observation matrix C) but replaces the closed-form Kalman gain with a set of three GRUs plus seven linear+ReLU layers that implicitly model the noise covariances, allowing the gain to vary nonlinearly over time. The network is fed four KF-derived features (observation, innovation, forward-evolution, and forward-update differences) and trained with a scaled MSE loss over 60-bin (3 s) sequences. The key mechanistic finding is that KalmanNet learns a non-linear trust system: it up-weights neural observations during high-velocity movement initiation and shifts trust to the dynamical model (which encodes exponential velocity decay) during stopping. The authors validate this mechanism by constructing a heteroscedastic Kalman filter (HKF) whose process noise scales linearly with ground-truth velocity, which matches KalmanNet's offline velocity performance while remaining fully within the explainable linear-KF framework.

## Results

Offline (n=13 days, two monkeys), KalmanNet achieves velocity correlation of 0.65 and velocity MSE of 3.3×10⁻⁷, statistically indistinguishable from the LSTM (p=0.64, B₀₁=3.3 for correlation; p=0.72, B₀₁=3.4 for MSE) while significantly outperforming the KF (p<10⁻⁷) and tcFNN (p<0.001). Online (n=5 days, Monkey N), KalmanNet achieves a 97% success rate versus 90% for LSTM, with smoother paths (p<10⁻⁷), though at the cost of lower throughput (p<10⁻¹⁶) and longer trial times (p<10⁻⁵). Under 100× out-of-distribution noise injection, KalmanNet suffers a ~4300× MSE increase while the LSTM shows only ~2×, and on task-context generalization the simple KF degrades least (7.98% MSE increase) versus 44.45% for KalmanNet and 50.34% for LSTM.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the **KF** (Kalman [34]), KalmanNet significantly outperforms on all offline velocity metrics (correlation p<10⁻⁷, MSE p<0.01) and all online metrics (success rate, throughput, trial time, path efficiency), confirming that the RNN-augmented Kalman gain computation adds substantial value over the fixed-gain linear filter. Against **tcFNN** (Willsey et al. [16], non-ReFIT), KalmanNet significantly beats it on offline velocity correlation (p<0.001) and outperforms it on every online metric, though the offline velocity MSE difference is not significant (p=0.29, B₀₁=2.1). Against **LSTM** (Costello et al. [17]), KalmanNet is statistically indistinguishable offline on both velocity correlation (p=0.64, B₀₁=3.3) and MSE (p=0.72, B₀₁=3.4), but online it trades lower throughput and longer trial times for a higher success rate (97% vs 90%) and smoother paths (p<10⁻⁷). The LSTM, however, is far more robust to large out-of-distribution noise (~2× MSE increase vs ~4300× for KalmanNet at 100× noise magnitude) and generalizes worst across task contexts (50.34% MSE increase vs 7.98% for the KF), highlighting a fundamental trade-off between peak performance and robustness that KalmanNet does not fully resolve.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts KalmanNet (Revach et al., 2022), originally designed for general tracking with partially known dynamics, to the BMI decoding setting, where it replaces the standard Kalman filter's fixed-gain computation with GRU-based networks that learn a time-varying trust between dynamics and neural observations. It positions itself against the established BMI decoder lineage—KF, tcFNN (Willsey et al., 2022), and LSTM (Costello et al., 2023)—and introduces a heteroscedastic KF (HKF) that replicates KalmanNet's key mechanism (velocity-dependent process noise) within a purely linear, explainable framework. The work also connects to the broader model-based deep learning literature (Shlezinger et al., 2023), framing the KF's state-space structure as an inductive bias/regularizer for the embedded RNNs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that explainability and state-of-the-art decoding performance need not be mutually exclusive in BMI, with the HKF showing that a simple linear modification (velocity-scaled process noise) can match the deep KalmanNet offline. However, two important caveats temper this optimism: KalmanNet exhibited the worst robustness to out-of-distribution noise (up to ~1000× MSE increase), while the 'black-box' LSTM was the most robust, and the simple KF generalized best to altered task contexts (8% vs. 34–50% MSE increase for neural models). Open questions include whether outlier-robust KF variants or hypernetwork-based adaptation can close KalmanNet's noise-robustness gap, and whether the trust-modulation insight transfers to other BMI applications such as speech neuroprostheses.
