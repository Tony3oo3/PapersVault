## Problem

LLM serving systems default to FCFS scheduling because output generation length is assumed unknowable before execution, causing severe Head-Of-Line blocking where long requests delay all shorter ones. The paper argues that exact length prediction is unnecessary—only the relative ordering of generation lengths within a batch is needed to approximate SJF/SRTF scheduling, and this ordering can be learned cheaply with a small auxiliary model.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241881/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The ranking predictor takes a natural-language prompt (truncated to 2,048 tokens) and outputs a single scalar score via a linear head on the last hidden state of a small OPT model (125M or 350M parameters). At serving time, the scheduler consumes these scores to sort the request queue by (priority, score) at each iteration, producing a running batch subject to memory/batch-size constraints. Training labels are derived by bucketing ground-truth generation lengths in increments of 10 tokens and ranking the bucketed values within each training batch, making the labels robust to sampling randomness.

## Method

The core insight is that Kendall's Tau correlation between a predicted schedule and the oracle SJF/SRTF schedule strongly predicts end-to-end latency, and that a listwise ranking loss (ListMLE) directly optimizes this correlation better than classification or regression losses. A small OPT model is trained with ListMLE on 10k prompts (batch size 32, 5 epochs, Adam at lr=2e-5) to assign ranking scores, with the linear head mapping hidden states to a single float. The scheduler (Algorithm 1) operates at the iteration level: it batch-runs the predictor on new arrivals, sorts all queued requests by (priority, score), fills the running batch, and applies a starvation-prevention mechanism that promotes long-waiting requests with a finite PriorityQuantum after a StarvationThreshold. This design is compatible with continuous batching and PagedAttention, adds less than 2% overhead from the predictor, and is implemented in ~500 lines of vLLM code.

## Results

On chatbot serving with a burst of 2K requests, the ranking scheduler achieves up to 2.0× lower mean latency and 2.8× lower P90 latency compared to the Perception Only (PO) baseline, and up to 6.9× lower mean latency vs. FCFS at 64 req/s. For synthetic data generation, the method reduces the time to generate 1K samples by 2.4×–6.5× (e.g., 30.48 s vs. 197.38 s for Llama-3-8B on LMSYS-Chat-1M) and increases 5-minute throughput by up to 3.2× (4,434 vs. 1,348 samples). The ranking predictor (OPT-125M/350M) adds less than 2% overhead to the serving pipeline, and the starvation-prevention mechanism reduces max_waiting_time by up to 3.4× with <10% latency overhead in most cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Perception Only (PO, Zheng et al. 2023), the ranking method wins decisively on end-to-end latency: up to 2.0× lower mean and 2.8× lower P90 latency in burst serving, despite PO achieving a slightly higher Kendall's Tau on LMSYS-Chat-1M (0.67 vs. 0.62). The key advantage is that PO must run the full LLM to generate 15 tokens per request before scheduling, incurring massive HOL blocking, whereas the lightweight OPT predictor adds <2% overhead. Against the S3-style classification baseline (Jin et al. 2024), the ranking method achieves substantially higher Kendall's Tau (0.54 vs. 0.24 on ShareGPT; 0.62 vs. 0.17 on LMSYS-Chat-1M) and lower latency in all settings. In synthetic data generation, the classification method actually underperforms FCFS (e.g., 421.92 s vs. 343.29 s for 1K samples on Llama-3-8B/ShareGPT) due to preprocessing overhead and poor identification of short requests, while the ranking method achieves 143.18 s. The ranking method also outperforms MLFQ and FCFS across all configurations, with the largest gains on LMSYS-Chat-1M where output-length variance is higher.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the line of LLM generation-length prediction work (S3, TetriServe, DynamoLLM, Magnus) that formulates the problem as classification or regression of exact token counts, arguing instead that only the relative ordering of lengths is needed for SJF/SRTF-style scheduling. It builds on the vLLM/Orca serving infrastructure (PagedAttention, continuous batching) and the scheduling-theory insight that SJF minimizes average latency, but replaces the standard FCFS or MLFQ policies with a learning-to-rank approach (ListMLE loss, Kendall's Tau evaluation) trained on a small OPT backbone. The key conceptual shift is from 'predict the length' to 'predict the rank,' which the authors show is both more robust to distribution shifts and simpler to deploy (~500 LoC).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for production LLM serving: a 2.8× p90 latency reduction in chatbot serving and 6.5× throughput gain in synthetic data generation, with <2% predictor overhead, make this an attractive drop-in improvement for existing vLLM deployments. However, the paper acknowledges that Kendall's Tau does not perfectly capture end-to-end scheduling quality (e.g., shuffling within the shortest 70% vs. longest 70% yields identical Tau but very different latency), and the scheduler has not yet been validated against newer optimizations like chunk-prefill or prefill-decode disaggregation. Open questions include how the approach interacts with multi-turn conversations, adaptive sampling parameters, and whether the ranking predictor's generalization across distribution shifts is sufficient without frequent retraining.
