## Problem

While large transformer models trained via next-token prediction have revolutionized language and vision, it remains unclear whether this generative modeling paradigm can be applied to real-world robotic control. In particular, humanoid locomotion has been tackled primarily through reinforcement learning or model-based control, but the potential of autoregressive sequence modeling over sensorimotor data—and its ability to leverage incomplete data sources like human videos without motor commands—has not been explored.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sliding window of 16 past observation-action pairs, where each observation is a vector of joint positions, IMU readings, and a velocity command, and each action is a joint position command vector. At inference, the model autoregressively predicts the next joint position command, which is executed on the robot; the predicted observation is discarded in favor of the ground-truth sensor reading. A key design choice is modality-aligned prediction: given an observation token, the model predicts the next observation, and given an action token, it predicts the next action, rather than predicting the next token in a single interleaved sequence.

## Method

The paper trains a 4-layer causal transformer (192 hidden dim, 4 heads) to model the joint distribution of sensorimotor sequences via negative log-likelihood under a Gaussian assumption, rather than learning a conditional action policy. The critical mechanism for handling heterogeneous data is the use of learnable mask tokens: when actions are unavailable (as in MoCap or YouTube video data), action slots are replaced with a shared [M] token and the corresponding prediction loss is ignored, allowing the model to learn from observation-only trajectories. Training data is drawn from four sources—complete trajectories from an RL policy, action-free trajectories from a model-based controller, retargeted human MoCap, and retargeted YouTube video poses—enabling the model to benefit from both labeled and unlabeled data. At deployment, the model operates zero-shot on the Digit humanoid, executing predicted joint position commands while conditioning on real sensor feedback.

## Results

The model achieves zero-shot real-world deployment on the Digit humanoid robot across diverse San Francisco surfaces (walkways, concrete, asphalt, tiled plazas, dirt roads) using only 27 hours of walking data. In simulation, the model's position tracking error consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art RL policy [29] across all commanded yaw velocities in [-0.4, 0.4] rad/s, with near-perfect tracking for straight-line walking. Prediction error on held-out validation data correlates strongly with tracking error (Pearson r = 0.87 across 14 models), confirming that next-token prediction loss is a reliable proxy for locomotion performance. Scaling studies show monotonic improvements with dataset size, context length, and model size (1M → 8M parameters).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole quantitative baseline is the RL-trained policy from Radosavovic et al. (Science Robotics, 2024) [29], which was previously the state-of-the-art for real-world humanoid locomotion. The proposed sequence-modeling approach wins on position tracking error at every tested yaw command (0.00, ±0.05, ±0.10, ±0.20, ±0.30, ±0.40 rad/s), with the gap widening at higher turning rates (Figure 6, left; N=245 trajectories). Notably, the proposed model was trained on a dataset that includes trajectories generated by this same RL policy, so the comparison is not entirely independent. The paper also shows that jointly training with action-free trajectories (model-based controller, MoCap, YouTube) consistently improves tracking error over training on complete trajectories alone, a benefit the RL baseline does not exploit. The authors acknowledge that their approach still lags behind the RL policy in robustness and behind model-based MPC controllers overall.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the autoregressive next-token prediction paradigm from large language models to humanoid locomotion, applying a causal transformer to sensorimotor sequences rather than text. It builds directly on the authors' own prior RL-based humanoid locomotion work [29], reusing the same transformer architecture but replacing the RL training objective with sequence modeling. The mask-token mechanism for handling incomplete trajectories (e.g., human videos without motor commands) draws a conceptual parallel to BERT's masked language modeling, while the broader framing connects to sensorimotor pre-training for manipulation [28] and the general trend of generative pre-training across modalities.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The demonstration that pure next-token prediction can match or slightly exceed an RL-trained policy in trajectory tracking, and that a model trained on only 27 hours of walking data can deploy zero-shot in a real city, suggests a fundamentally different and potentially more scalable path to real-world robot control. The finding that incomplete data (videos without actions) consistently improves performance opens a route to leveraging the vast pool of human video on the internet, though the reliability of the computer vision pipeline (PHALP + IK retargeting) remains a significant practical bottleneck. Key open questions include whether this approach can close the robustness gap with RL at scale, whether the favorable scaling trends in data size, context length, and model size will persist, and whether the paradigm extends beyond locomotion to manipulation or more complex whole-body tasks.
