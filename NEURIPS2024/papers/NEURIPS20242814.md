## Problem

A seller with N homogeneous data points must learn a revenue-optimal pricing curve over T rounds without knowing the buyer type distribution, facing two compounding challenges: (1) existing discretization schemes for the offline pricing problem have exponential dependence on the approximation parameter ε, which is incompatible with online learning where ε must vanish as T→∞; and (2) the seller receives asymmetric feedback—the buyer reveals her type only if she makes a purchase—placing the problem in an intermediate regime between bandit and full-information settings that prior work has not addressed for multi-item (multi-parameter) markets, especially in the adversarial setting.

## Input / Output

The seller is given N homogeneous data points, the m known valuation curves v_i: [N]→[0,1] (monotone non-decreasing, v_i(0)=0), an approximation parameter ε, and a time horizon T. Each round, the seller must output an anonymous pricing curve p_t: [N]→[0,1] (chosen before observing the buyer's type); the arriving buyer of type i_t then purchases n_{i_t,p_t} points maximizing v_{i_t}(n)−p_t(n) subject to non-negative utility, and reveals her type only if n_{i_t,p_t}>0. The pricing curve is constrained to be non-decreasing and the buyer breaks ties by choosing the largest n.

## Method

The paper develops three discretization schemes that exploit the m-step structure of optimal prices (Lemma 3.1: any price curve can be replaced by one with at most m jumps without revenue loss), yielding Õ((N/ε)^m) size under monotonicity alone, Õ((L/ε²)^m) under L/N-Lipschitz smoothness (eliminating N dependence), and Õ((J/ε³)^m) under diminishing returns (polylog N dependence). For the stochastic setting, the algorithm maintains UCBs on the type distribution q (via Hoeffding's inequality on purchase-conditional observations) rather than directly on each price curve, then composes these into revenue UCBs—avoiding the exponential-in-m regret that naive per-price UCBs would incur. For the adversarial setting, the algorithm adapts FTPL with a novel reward design: when no purchase occurs, each price curve p is rewarded with the sum of revenues over all types that would NOT purchase at p_t, providing a tight upper bound near the chosen price while encouraging exploration of prices that could have elicited a purchase. This yields Õ(m√T) regret (stochastic) and Õ(m^{3/2}√T) regret (adversarial), both polynomial in m.

## Results

The paper achieves Õ(m√T) regret in the stochastic setting and Õ(m^{3/2}√T) regret in the adversarial setting, with computational complexity depending on the chosen discretization scheme. Under monotonicity alone, the discretization has size Õ((N/ε)^m); under smoothness, it reduces to Õ((L/ε²)^m) with no dependence on N; and under diminishing returns, it becomes Õ((J/ε³)^m) with only polylogarithmic dependence on N. These bounds are achieved via UCB (stochastic) and FTPL (adversarial) algorithms adapted to handle asymmetric feedback where buyer types are revealed only upon purchase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against two prior discretization-based pricing methods: Hartline and Koltun [25] and Chawla et al. [14]. On discretization size, the proposed methods dominate both baselines: under monotonicity, Algorithm 1 achieves Õ((N/ε)^m) versus Hartline & Koltun's exponential Õ(2^N · ε^{-N}) and Chawla et al.'s super-polynomial N^{O(ε^{-2} log ε^{-1})}. Under smoothness or diminishing returns, the dependence on N is eliminated or reduced to polylogarithmic, which is critical since N (number of data points) is typically very large in practice. On adversarial regret, the proposed FTPL-based algorithm achieves Õ(m^{3/2}√T), improving over Hartline & Koltun's Õ(m√(TN)) (which has an extra √N factor) and Chawla et al.'s Õ(mT^{3/4}) (which has worse T-dependence). In the stochastic setting, all methods achieve Õ(m√T) regret, so the advantage of the proposed discretization is purely computational (smaller search space per iteration).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian Unit-demand Pricing Problem (BUPP) from the offline setting to an online learning framework with asymmetric feedback, building on discretization schemes from Hartline and Koltun [25] and Chawla et al. [14] for offline revenue maximization. The online learning algorithms adapt classical UCB and FTPL methods, but require novel constructions to handle the intermediate feedback regime between bandit and full-information settings, where buyer types are revealed only upon purchase. The key structural insight—that any pricing curve can be replaced by an m-step function without revenue loss (Lemma 3.1)—reduces the search space and enables discretization schemes with polynomial (rather than exponential) dependence on the approximation parameter, leveraging data-specific properties like smoothness and diminishing returns that prior work did not exploit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first online learning framework for multi-parameter data pricing with asymmetric feedback, addressing a gap where prior work handled only single-item markets or only the stochastic setting. The practical relevance is to emerging data marketplaces (e.g., materials science platforms, advertising data exchanges) where sellers must dynamically set price curves without knowing buyer type distributions. Key limitations include the assumption that the seller knows all valuation curves a priori, the restriction to homogeneous data, and computational complexity that remains exponential in the number of types m. An important open direction, noted by the authors, is relaxing the assumption that valuation curves are known, which would make the problem substantially harder but more realistic.
