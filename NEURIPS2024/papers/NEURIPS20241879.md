## Problem

Existing graph-based active learning methods either rely solely on network topology (graph signal sampling) or use GNN-based spatial-domain criteria that lack well-defined complexity measures and theoretical guarantees. Moreover, most approaches operate online, requiring iterative annotator feedback, and fail to account for noise in both node covariates and labels. This paper addresses the need for an offline, noise-robust active learning strategy that jointly leverages network structure and node covariates with provable generalization guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method takes an undirected weighted graph (adjacency matrix A), a node covariate matrix X ∈ R^{n×p}, and a query budget B, then outputs a set of B queried nodes along with node-level predictions for all unlabeled nodes. A key design choice is the spectral dimension d = min{p, B}, which controls the frequency band of the graph signal space and is fixed before any labeling occurs. The output also includes per-node weights from the biased sampling procedure, which are used in a weighted least-squares regression to recover the graph signal.

## Method

The method defines a function space H_ω(X,A) = Proj_{L_ω} Span(X) that projects node covariates onto the low-frequency graph spectral subspace, jointly encoding network topology and covariate information. Node selection uses a two-stage biased sampling strategy: first, randomized spectral sparsification (via a potential function on the weighted covariance matrix) samples m ≪ n candidate nodes to ensure representativeness (lower-bounded eigenvalues of the design matrix); second, within the candidate set, the node maximizing the increase in bandwidth frequency ω(S) is selected to ensure informativeness. The informativeness criterion is computed as the gradient of a regularized eigenvalue problem involving the graph Laplacian power and a projection operator onto the complement of the queried set. The theoretical analysis establishes a generalization error bound that reveals a trade-off: a larger spectral dimension d reduces approximation bias (capturing less-smooth signal components) but increases variance by making it harder to control the condition number of the design matrix.

## Results

On the five real-world networks with varying homophily (Table 1), the proposed method achieves the best Micro-F1 on Cora (86.5% at 140 labeled nodes) and Texas (80.4% at 45 nodes, a 4.3-point gain over IGP), while remaining competitive on PubMed, Citeseer, and Chameleon. On the large-scale Ogbn-Arxiv network (n=169,343), the method achieves up to 4.8% Macro-F1 improvement over the best baseline (GPT) at 320 labeled nodes (34.3% vs 29.5%). On Co-Physics, the proposed method reaches 89.2% Macro-F1 at 40 labeled nodes, outperforming GPT (88.9%). The method also demonstrates strong noise robustness in synthetic experiments, significantly outperforming D-optimal, RIM, GPT, and SPA across all noise levels (σ² from 0.5 to 1.0) on small-world, community, and scale-free topologies.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method wins or ties on most settings: it matches GPT on Cora (86.5% at 140 nodes) and clearly outperforms IGP on Texas (80.4% vs 76.1% at 45 nodes) and Chameleon at 100 nodes (37.2% vs 33.4%). On large-scale Ogbn-Arxiv, it surpasses GPT by up to 4.8% Macro-F1 at 320 labeled nodes (34.3% vs 29.5%) and leads on Co-Physics (89.2% vs 88.9% Macro-F1 at 40 nodes). However, the method does not dominate everywhere: RIM outperforms it on Chameleon at 75 labeled nodes (42.8% vs 35.7%), and IGP edges ahead on PubMed at 60 nodes (83.5% vs 82.3%) and Citeseer at 120 nodes (75.8% vs 75.5%). In synthetic regression experiments, the proposed method significantly outperforms all four offline baselines (D-optimal, RIM, GPT, SPA) across all noise levels and network topologies, with the gap widening as noise increases, confirming its noise-robustness advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate research threads: graph signal recovery theory (Gadde et al., KDD 2014), which defines signal complexity via bandwidth frequency in the graph spectral domain, and randomized spectral sparsification (Lee & Sun, SIAM J. Computing 2018), which provides biased sampling guarantees for maintaining eigenvalue bounds of covariance matrices. It departs from GNN-based active learning methods (AGE, IGP, GPT, SPA) that operate in the spatial domain and require online annotator interaction, and from classical optimal experimental design (D-optimal) that ignores network topology. The key conceptual move is defining a function space at the intersection of the graph spectral domain and the covariate span, then using spectral sparsification to simultaneously control representativeness (generalization under noise) while maximizing informativeness (bandwidth frequency) in an offline, budget-constrained setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides one of the first generalization error bounds for offline active learning on graphs that explicitly accounts for noise in both node labels and covariates, revealing a formal trade-off between informativeness (capturing high-frequency signal components) and representativeness (controlling condition number of the design matrix) governed by the spectral dimension d. Limitations include reliance on a linear (SGC-equivalent) model, the need for a priori knowledge of homophily vs. heterophily to choose the signal subspace, and scalability constraints for very large graphs (though ~2s per node at n=169K is reasonable). Open questions include extending the theory to nonlinear GNNs, developing adaptive subspace selection that handles both homophily and heterophily without prior knowledge, and transitioning to an online setting that iteratively incorporates response information.
