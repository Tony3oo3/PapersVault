## Problem

While Transformers are known to be Turing-complete in expressivity, it remains unclear what targets they can actually learn from scratch via gradient descent. This paper identifies a 'global reasoning barrier': tasks where the label depends on a large, non-local subset of input tokens (e.g., long syllogism chains, cycle connectivity) cannot be efficiently weakly learned by regular Transformers, and it formalizes this with a new measure called globality degree. The paper further asks whether scratchpad techniques can break this barrier and, if so, how to design scratchpads that also generalize out-of-distribution.

## Input / Output

For the cycle task, input is a semicolon-separated sequence of directed edges (e.g., 'a>x; n>y; q>a;...') plus two query vertices, and output is a binary connectivity label. For parity and addition, inputs are bit or digit sequences embedded in a fixed ambient dimension with random spaces or random tokens as positional markers (e.g., '_01_10_0__1_' for parity, 'fs$46+ih$98' for addition), producing a single parity bit or a full digit sequence respectively. The inductive scratchpad appends a structured sequence of states separated by a special <STATE> token (#) after a <START> delimiter, terminated by <EOS>, where each state encodes the current algorithmic state (e.g., current DFS node, cumulative parity, or shifted operands).

## Method

The paper introduces the globality degree, defined as the minimum number of input tokens (beyond the token histogram) needed to achieve inverse-polynomial mutual information with the label, and proves (Theorem 1) that a T-regular Transformer trained by population gradient descent fails to weakly learn a cycle-task variant with globality Ω(n). It then shows that agnostic scratchpads (unsupervised intermediate tokens) cannot break this barrier, while educated scratchpads with low autoregressive globality can. The key practical contribution is the inductive scratchpad: by applying attention masking and position reindexing so that each new state is generated as if the input were only the question plus the immediately preceding state (Q<START>s[i]#), the model learns a single induction function g rather than the full state sequence. This decouples the model from the number of reasoning steps, enabling length generalization (e.g., 10→18 digits for addition, 30→55 bits for parity) and OOD generalization on the cycle task, in contrast to fully educated scratchpads that overfit to the training-time step count.

## Results

On the cycle task, a 10M-parameter GPT-2-style Transformer fails to learn for n≥7 within 100k iterations, demonstrating the globality barrier. With an inductive scratchpad, the paper achieves length generalization of up to 6× on arithmetic: the random-space method generalizes addition from 10 to 18 digits, and the shift method from 4 to 26 digits. For parity, the inductive scratchpad generalizes from 30 to 50–55 bits. The DFS scratchpad reduces the cycle task's globality from n to 3, enabling efficient learning at scale.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's inductive scratchpad outperforms most baselines on addition length generalization: the random-space method (10→18 digits) beats Shen et al. [17] (10→12) and Jelassi et al. [36] (5→15), while the shift method (4→26) surpasses Kazemnejad et al. [34] (8→9) and Jelassi et al. [36]. The strongest baseline, Zhou et al. [37] (40→65 digits), uses FIRE relative positional embeddings, randomized position encodings, reversed output, and index hints, and still outperforms the paper's methods in absolute generalization range; however, the paper notes its methods use significantly smaller models and more natural input formatting. On parity, the inductive scratchpad (30→55 bits) outperforms Anil et al. [11] (8→20 bits, using 128B pre-trained models) and Kazemnejad et al. [34] (8→12 bits), and matches or slightly exceeds RASP-L [35] (30→50 bits) while requiring only random spaces in the input rather than special index-hint tokens. The paper emphasizes that its models are often remarkably smaller than those used by competing methods, making the per-parameter efficiency of the inductive scratchpad a key advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the expressivity-focused line of work on Transformers (Merrill & Sabharwal's TC⁰/TC¹ characterizations, RASP/RASP-L) by shifting the question from 'what can a Transformer compute?' to 'what can a Transformer learn from scratch via gradient descent?' It introduces the globality degree as a distribution-level measure that is simpler and more targeted than prior learning-complexity measures (statistical dimension, cross-predictability, noise sensitivity, NTK alignment), which typically require orbit arguments over classes of distributions or i.i.d. assumptions. On the practical side, it extends the scratchpad/chain-of-thought paradigm (Nye et al., Anil et al., Shen et al.) by distinguishing agnostic, educated, and inductive scratchpads, showing that only the inductive variant—enforced via attention masking and position reindexing—avoids overfitting to the number of reasoning steps.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The globality degree provides a concrete, computable criterion for predicting when a regular Transformer will fail to learn a reasoning task, which is valuable for both theory (separating expressivity from learnability) and practice (diagnosing why a model struggles before scaling up). The negative results—that agnostic scratchpads cannot break the barrier and that fully educated scratchpads overfit to step count—set important boundaries for the widely-used chain-of-thought approach. Open questions include extending the measure to strong learning (beyond weak learning), understanding whether pre-trained models can acquire inductive scratchpad behavior on new tasks, and whether the cycle-task benchmark generalizes to more natural reasoning distributions. The inductive scratchpad is lightweight (two special tokens + attention masking) and could be integrated into existing LLM pipelines, but its current validation is limited to synthetic tasks (parity, addition, cycle).
