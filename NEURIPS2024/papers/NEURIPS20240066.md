## Problem

Text-to-image diffusion models generate high-quality images from text prompts, but the mechanisms by which individual tokens and their interactions shape the output remain opaque. Prior interpretability work (e.g., MI and CMI maps from Kong et al.) provides only coarse, per-token information without decomposing how tokens contribute redundantly, uniquely, or synergistically, limiting the ability to diagnose generation failures, detect demographic biases, or understand semantic confusions such as homonym and co-hyponym errors.

## Input / Output

The method takes a text prompt containing at least two phrases of interest and a pre-trained Stable Diffusion 2.1 model, operating entirely in the model's latent space rather than pixel space. Phrase probabilities p(y) are computed via BERT's masked-token prediction, while conditional image probabilities are derived from the diffusion model's denoising MMSE; the integral over the infinite SNR range is evaluated using truncated logistic-based importance sampling with 50 samples. Resulting latent-space information maps are bilinearly interpolated to image resolution for visualization, and image-level scalar PID values are obtained by averaging the MSE term over all pixels.

## Method

DiffusionPID decomposes the mutual information between two text phrases and a pixel into redundancy (R), uniqueness (U), and synergy (S) terms by exploiting the identity that MI in a diffusion model equals the difference in denoising MMSE between the conditional and unconditional denoisers (Eq. 3, derived via the orthogonality principle). Redundancy is computed as the difference between the minimum positive and minimum negative information components across the two phrases, with phrase probabilities obtained from BERT; uniqueness and synergy are then derived algebraically from MI and redundancy. The authors further introduce Conditional PID (CPID), which conditions all PID terms on the remaining prompt context, yielding more accurate per-phrase attribution in multi-concept prompts. This fine-grained decomposition produces pixel-level heatmaps that reveal, for instance, which image regions encode shared (redundant) versus novel (synergistic) information between two words, enabling applications from bias auditing to prompt intervention.

## Results

On the gender bias task (188 occupations × 2 genders), the model shows strong stereotypical associations: male redundancy is 0.871 for 'Doctor' vs. 0.090 for female, and 0.605 vs. 0.038 for 'Plumber', while female redundancy dominates for 'Babysitter' (0.531 vs. 0.240). Average redundancy across all occupations is 0.286 for males vs. 0.194 for females, indicating a systematic bias against generating females. On the ethnic bias task (188 occupations × 4 ethnicities), the Black group has the lowest average redundancy (0.133) compared to Asian (0.233), Caucasian (0.255), and Hispanic (0.236), revealing a strong bias against generating Black individuals. Qualitatively, PID synergy maps correctly identify contextual disambiguation for homonyms (e.g., 'bat' + 'baseball' → sports context), redundancy maps localize shared semantics for synonyms ('cube'/'cuboid') and co-hyponyms ('cat'/'elephant'), and uniqueness maps isolate distinctive features (e.g., toothbrush bristles).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares DiffusionPID against three baselines: MI and CMI (from Kong et al., 2023) and DAAM cross-attention maps (from Tang et al., 2022). For synonym and co-hyponym identification, PID redundancy maps clearly highlight the shared object region (e.g., the 'cube'/'cuboid' overlap), while MI, CMI, and DAAM intersection maps are described as 'very sparse' and 'fail to find the region of interest.' For prompt intervention, PID correctly identifies redundant terms (low uniqueness, high redundancy) whose removal leaves the image nearly unchanged, whereas MI, CMI, and DAAM maps are 'highly activated for these redundant terms, incorrectly suggesting that removing this term should have significantly altered the image.' For homonym disambiguation, PID synergy maps reveal whether the model successfully extracts new information from word combinations (high synergy = success) or fails (low synergy = failure), a distinction that MI/CMI/DAAM cannot provide since they only measure individual token contribution. Overall, PID's decomposition into R, U, and S provides strictly more diagnostic information than the single-scalar MI/CMI or attention-based DAAM maps across all evaluated tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mutual information and conditional mutual information framework for diffusion models established by Kong et al. [59] to a full Partial Information Decomposition (PID), decomposing the information between text tokens and generated pixels into redundancy, uniqueness, and synergy terms following the pointwise PID formulation of Finn & Lizier [60] and the foundational multivariate PID of Williams & Beer [4]. The key departure from prior interpretability work (DAAM cross-attention maps, MI/CMI pixel maps) is the explicit modeling of *interactions* between tokens rather than their individual contributions, and the introduction of Conditional PID (CPID) to account for the contextual influence of the remaining prompt. This positions the work as a fine-grained information-theoretic complement to attention-based attribution methods, which the paper shows to be insufficient for diagnosing redundancy and synergy effects.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DiffusionPID provides a principled, pixel-level diagnostic tool that can quantify gender and ethnic biases, explain homonym disambiguation failures, identify co-hyponym confusion, and enable practical prompt intervention by removing redundant terms — all tasks where prior methods (MI, CMI, DAAM) produce sparse or misleading maps. Key limitations include the restriction to two phrases at a time, the requirement of white-box access to the diffusion model (ruling out closed-source systems), and evaluation on a single model (Stable Diffusion 2.1). Open questions include extending PID to more than two input variables, applying the framework to non-diffusion generative models, and developing proxy methods for closed-source systems.
