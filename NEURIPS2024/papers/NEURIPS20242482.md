## Problem

LLMs frequently produce incorrect, stereotypical, or nonsensical responses to mundane everyday cultural questions, particularly for underrepresented cultures and low-resource languages, because such knowledge is rarely documented in the formal online sources (e.g., Wikipedia) used for pretraining. No existing benchmark captures this type of everyday cultural commonsense across multiple languages and cultures simultaneously, making it impossible to directly compare LLMs' cultural knowledge across regions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242482/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark presents 500 question templates per country/region across six socio-cultural categories (food, sports, family, education, holidays, work-life), localized into 13 languages. For short-answer evaluation, the LLM receives a question in the local language or English (with an optional persona prompt instructing it to act as a person from the target country) and must generate a concise answer that is matched against up to five human-annotated responses using lemmatizers, stemmers, and accent normalization. For multiple-choice evaluation (English only), the LLM selects one correct answer from four options, where distractors are drawn from other countries' annotations to the same question, enabling cross-cultural discrimination.

## Method

BLEND is constructed through a four-stage pipeline: (1) native annotators from each of 16 countries/regions generate 10–15 culturally relevant questions per category, which are filtered to remove duplicates and country-specific proper nouns, then templated with 'your country' replaced by the target region name; (2) questions are translated into local languages by native speakers; (3) five annotators per question (recruited via Prolific or directly for underrepresented regions) provide short answers, with invalid or 'I don't know' responses replaced until five valid answers are collected; (4) 1–2 reviewer annotators per country aggregate variant answers and assign final vote counts. Evaluation uses two prompting strategies (direct question and persona-based) averaged per country, with SAQ scored by exact-match against annotated answers and MCQ scored by selecting the highest-voted annotation as the correct option. The key design choice is using identical question templates across all 16 countries/regions, enabling direct cross-cultural comparison and revealing that LLMs perform significantly better for high-resource cultures (e.g., 79.22% for US in English) than underrepresented ones (e.g., 12.18% for Ethiopia in Amharic).

## Results

On BLEND's short-answer questions (SAQ) in local languages, the average performance across all 16 evaluated LLMs drops from 79.22% for US culture to 12.18% for Ethiopian culture, a 67-point gap. GPT-4, the best-performing model, shows a maximum 57.34% performance difference across countries in the SAQ format, and a 31.63% disparity between South Korea and North Korea (same language, different culture). In the multiple-choice format (MCQ), overall scores are higher than SAQ, with a Pearson correlation of 0.93 between average SAQ and MCQ performance across countries, and 0.98 between English SAQ and MCQ, confirming strong alignment between the two evaluation formats.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 16 LLMs on BLEND rather than comparing a single proposed method against baselines. GPT-4 [24] is the best-performing model overall, achieving the highest scores across most countries in both SAQ and MCQ formats. Qwen1.5-72B [5] outperforms all other models on Chinese cultural questions, and HyperCLOVA-X [34] exceeds even GPT-4 on North Korean cultural questions, demonstrating that region-specific models benefit from richer local training data. For mid-to-high-resource languages (e.g., Spanish, Chinese), LLMs perform better when prompted in the local language, whereas for low-resource languages (e.g., Amharic, Sundanese, Azerbaijani), English prompting yields higher scores. Gemini [30], Aya [4], and SEA-LM [23] are among the other evaluated models, all showing the same pattern of strong performance on well-represented Western cultures and significantly degraded performance on underrepresented regions such as Ethiopia, West Java, and Assam.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from prior cultural NLP benchmarks (CLIcK, HAE-RAE Bench, GeoMLAMA, CultureAtlas) that are limited to a single language, a single culture, or rely on scraped online sources like Wikipedia and social media. BLEND carves new ground by being the first hand-crafted, multilingual benchmark that uses the same 500 question templates across 13 languages and 16 countries/regions, enabling direct cross-cultural comparison of LLM performance. It also distinguishes itself from social-media-derived datasets (StereoKG, CAMEL, CultureBank) by targeting mundane everyday knowledge that is not documented in formal online sources, recruiting native-speaker annotators who have lived in their target regions for over half their lives.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a stark 57.34% performance gap (GPT-4, US vs. Ethiopia) that quantifies the cultural blind spots of current LLMs, providing a concrete target for future training data curation. A key open question it surfaces is why low-resource-language cultures perform better when prompted in English than in their local language, suggesting that linguistic capability, not cultural knowledge, is the bottleneck. Limitations include the small annotator pool (5 per question), English-only MCQ format, and difficulty evaluating long-form responses against short annotated answers, leaving the evaluation of cultural adaptiveness in natural conversational contexts unresolved.
