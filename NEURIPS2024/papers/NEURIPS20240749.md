## Problem

Existing LLM bias benchmarks rely on human-annotated examples targeting overt stigmatization, leaving a critical gap: no systematic framework assesses whether models' internal representations of disease prevalence across demographic subgroups align with actual epidemiological data. This is especially concerning for healthcare applications where biased prevalence assumptions could propagate disparities, yet no prior work bridged demographic bias evaluation with real-world knowledge grounding and multi-language assessment.

## Input / Output

The framework consumes three input streams: (1) 10 prompt template variants per disease-demographic pair (e.g., '[Disease] patients are usually [Demographic Group] in America') in English, Chinese, French, and Spanish, fed to LLMs to extract raw logits; (2) ThePile 825 GB corpus scanned with 50–250 token windows to count co-occurrences of 89 disease keywords against 6 race/ethnicity and 3 gender subgroup terms; and (3) age-adjusted CDC/NHIS prevalence rates (per 10,000) for 15 diseases with data spanning at least 5 of 6 race/ethnicity subgroups. Outputs include per-template logits averaged into a unified score, demographic subgroup rank orderings per disease, corpus co-occurrence counts, Kendall's tau correlations between model rankings and both corpus and real-world rankings, and drift scores (δ) quantifying how alignment shifts rankings relative to base models.

## Method

Cross-Care is a benchmark framework that triangulates three ranking signals—corpus co-occurrence frequencies, model logit-derived demographic rankings, and real-world prevalence rankings—using Kendall's tau to quantify alignment or misalignment. Co-occurrence extraction uses a hierarchical keyword dictionary (89 diseases from PubMed MeSH/SNOMED CT, 9 demographic terms from HolisticBias) scanned over ThePile with multithreaded 250-token windows, validated against the Infini-gram API. Model evaluation spans a controlled group (Pythia and Mamba trained exclusively on ThePile) and an in-the-wild group (Llama2/3, Mistral, Qwen at 7B–72B scales with RLHF, SFT, DPO, and biomedical continued pre-training), extracting logits from 10 template variants per disease-demographic-language combination. A key design choice is the drift metric δ, which measures how much each alignment strategy perturbs the base model's demographic rank ordering, revealing that alignment methods minimally correct prevalence misalignment and sometimes worsen it. Multi-language evaluation with native-speaker-verified translations exposes that models hold different demographic representations across languages, with pretraining data mixture (not alignment) as the dominant determinant.

## Results

Across all evaluated models (Pythia, Mamba, Llama2, Llama3, Mistral, Qwen1.5) and all four languages (English, Chinese, Spanish, French), no model's disease-logit rankings achieved Kendall's τ > 0.35 against real-world CDC/NHIS prevalence (Min = −0.73, Max = 0.33, Median = −0.05, Avg = −0.06, Var = 0.03), indicating essentially no grounding in actual epidemiological data. In contrast, model logits showed consistently stronger correlation with ThePile co-occurrence counts than with real-world prevalence, especially for gender subgroups. Among 89 diseases, White was the top-ranked race/ethnicity subgroup in ThePile for 87/89 diseases, while in real-world data Indigenous was often the top-ranked subgroup; Pacific Islander was consistently the bottom-ranked subgroup in both ThePile and model logits, but Indigenous was the second-bottom in ThePile (86/89 diseases) while models ranked Hispanic second-bottom.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a benchmark/evaluation paper rather than a method-vs-baseline comparison, so the key contrasts are between three reference points: ThePile co-occurrence counts, real-world CDC/NHIS prevalence, and model logits. Model logits track ThePile co-occurrence far more closely than real-world prevalence (Kendall's τ near zero vs. real data, but consistently positive vs. co-occurrence), confirming that pretraining data distributions—not epidemiological knowledge—drive model representations. Among alignment strategies tested on Llama2-70B (base, RLHF/chat, SFT/Tulu2, DPO/Tulu2-dpo, Biomed/Meditron), none improved alignment with real-world prevalence; in fact, RLHF and DPO increased preference for female over male and decreased preference for the Black subgroup in English, while SFT and DPO preserved the base model's rank-ordering (δ ≥ 0.8). Cross-language analysis revealed that the same model produces different demographic preferences depending on the prompt language (e.g., Llama2 prefers Black in English/Spanish but White in Chinese/French templates), and alignment methods only shifted preferences within the language of the preference data, suggesting pretraining data composition is the dominant factor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the co-occurrence analysis methodology from Hansen et al. [62] ('Seeds of Stereotypes') by adding verified medical keywords, multithreaded processing, and real-world prevalence grounding. It departs from the dominant paradigm in LLM bias evaluation (CrowS-Pairs, BBQ, StereoSet, ILPS), which relies on human-annotated stereotype examples and measures overt stigmatization, by instead using CDC/NHIS epidemiological statistics as ground truth to assess whether models' internal disease-demographic associations reflect actual population-level prevalence. The authors position Cross-Care as the first framework to jointly evaluate demographic bias, real-world knowledge grounding, and multilingual consistency in a single benchmark.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that no tested model (across six architectures, three sizes, five alignment strategies, and four languages) achieved meaningful correlation (τ > 0.35) with real-world disease prevalence, and that alignment methods sometimes worsened rather than corrected the discrepancy, has direct implications for the safety of deploying LLMs in clinical settings. The cross-lingual inconsistencies (e.g., Qwen favoring Asian subgroups in Chinese/English but Black subgroups in Spanish/French) suggest that pretraining data composition, not alignment, is the primary determinant of these biases, pointing toward data curation as the more effective intervention. Key limitations include the restriction to 15 diseases with reliable prevalence data, the absence of NER-based disambiguation, and template sensitivity, all of which constrain generalizability and motivate the authors' proposed future work on RAG-based prevalence correction and real-world-data-aware synthetic training.
