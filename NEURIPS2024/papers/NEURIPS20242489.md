## Problem

While transformers have achieved remarkable empirical success, a theoretical characterization of which parameter scaling regimes (depth, width, pause tokens) are necessary and sufficient for solving different classes of algorithmic reasoning tasks has been largely missing. Prior results either assumed unrealistic polynomial-depth scaling or provided only one-directional circuit-complexity reductions that do not clarify when tasks like graph connectivity become expressible. This paper bridges the gap between worst-case computational complexity classes (L, NL, NC) and the representational capabilities of bounded-size transformers operating in realistic sublinear-width regimes.

## Input / Output

Graphs are encoded as tokenized sequences where each vertex and each edge occupies a single token position, followed by a task-specific query embedding (e.g., source and sink node identifiers for connectivity or shortest path). The theoretical model assumes the embedding dimension m grows sublinearly with sequence length N (m = O(N^ε) for small ε), and optionally appends N' blank pause tokens that extend the computational tape without encoding input information. Outputs range from binary indicators (connectivity, cycle check, edge existence) to integer-valued answers (node/edge counts, node degree, shortest path length, triangle count), all produced from the first N positions of the final transformer layer.

## Method

The paper introduces a representational hierarchy that partitions graph reasoning tasks into three families—retrieval, parallelizable, and search—based on their equivalence classes under O(1)-round reductions in the Massively Parallel Computation (MPC) model. The central technical tool (Theorem 8) proves that any R-round MPC protocol with sublinear local memory can be simulated by a transformer of depth O(R) and sublinear embedding dimension, improving upon prior results that required local memory below n^{1/4}. This yields tight bounds: retrieval tasks (node count, edge existence) are solvable by single-layer transformers with m = O(log N); parallelizable tasks (connectivity, cycle check, bipartiteness) require and are solvable by logarithmic-depth transformers with m = O(N^ε); and search tasks (shortest path, diameter) require logarithmic depth with wider embeddings m = O(N^{1/2+ε}). Empirically, the authors train 60M-parameter transformers from scratch and fine-tune T5-11B on the GraphQA benchmark, demonstrating that transformers outperform GNNs (GCN, MPNN, GIN) on global-reasoning tasks while GNNs retain an inductive-bias advantage on local tasks in low-sample regimes.

## Results

On the GraphQA benchmark, the 60M-parameter transformer trained on 100K samples achieves near-perfect accuracy on retrieval tasks (100% node count, 100% edge count, 96.1% edge existence) and strong performance on parallelizable tasks (98.0% connectivity, 98.0% cycle check) and search tasks (97.2% shortest path). The fine-tuned T5-11B model reaches 98.4% connectivity and 92.8% shortest path with only 1K training samples. In contrast, the best LLM prompting strategy (COT) achieves at most 58.0% cycle check and 45.2% connectivity, while GNN baselines (MPNN) reach 94.4% connectivity but only 72.6% shortest path even with 100K samples.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against GNN baselines, the 60M transformer with 100K samples decisively outperforms MPNN (94.4%), GIN (94.0%), and GCN (83.8%) on connectivity (98.0%) and shortest path (97.2% vs. MPNN's 72.6%), confirming the theoretical prediction that transformers excel at global reasoning tasks. However, MPNN outperforms the 60M transformer on the local task of node degree (99.8% vs. 91.7% at 100K samples), demonstrating GNNs' favorable inductive bias for neighborhood-level structure in low-sample regimes. On cycle check, MPNN (100.0%) slightly edges out the 60M transformer (98.0%), though the gap narrows with more data. Against LLM prompting strategies, trained transformers dominate across all eight tasks: the 60M transformer with 100K samples exceeds the best prompting method (COT) by 42.8 points on connectivity (98.0% vs. 45.2%), 40.0 points on cycle check (98.0% vs. 58.0%), and 58.6 points on shortest path (97.2% vs. 38.6%), despite the LLMs having orders of magnitude more parameters. The fine-tuned 11B transformer further extends these margins, achieving 98.4% connectivity and 92.8% shortest path with only 1K training examples.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes the logarithmic-depth connectivity result of Sanford et al. [71]—which connected transformers to the massively parallel computation (MPC) model—to two broader equivalence classes of graph reasoning tasks (parallelizable/L-complete and search/NL-complete) established by Nanongkai and Scquizzato [60]. It improves the MPC-to-transformer simulation to handle larger local memory regimes (δ > 1/4), enabling positive results for all of L and NL. The empirical evaluation builds on the GraphQA benchmark of Fatemi et al. [24] and contrasts transformers against GNN baselines whose expressivity limits are characterized by the Weisfeiler-Lehman connection [84] and the CONGEST model [51].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first work to provide a tight, multi-regime characterization of which transformer parameter scalings (depth, width, pause tokens) are necessary and sufficient for distinct classes of graph reasoning tasks, bridging the gap between circuit complexity classes and practical model architectures. Key open questions include whether the hierarchy extends beyond graph tasks to other algorithmic problems, whether bounded-size MLPs (rather than arbitrary functions) alter the bounds, and whether search tasks can be solved in the LogDepthPause regime. The assumption of unbounded MLPs and the expressivity-learnability gap remain important caveats, and broader scaling-law experiments would strengthen the empirical connection to theory.
