## Problem

No Free Lunch (NFL) analysis in adversarial (maximin) optimisation is a long-standing open problem: prior work demonstrated 'free lunch' for the maximin and 'Maximisation Over All Test Cases' solution concepts, but whether NFL holds when Nash Equilibrium is the solution concept remained unresolved. Additionally, no formal black-box complexity framework existed to characterise the query complexity of finding Nash Equilibria in adversarial settings.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives a payoff matrix g: X × Y → R over finite strategy spaces X and Y (with |X| = |Y| = n for bimatrix games), and is restricted to querying individual entries g(x,y) without access to the full matrix or any structural properties (no convexity, continuity, or differentiability assumed). The output is the unique pure strategy Nash Equilibrium (x*, y*), and the cost is measured as the number of unique matrix entries queried before the algorithm first hits the row or column containing the NE (or both, for the full NE). The problem class is required to be closed under permutation (c.u.p.), meaning permuting rows and columns of any payoff function in the class yields another function in the class.

## Method

The paper proves an NFL theorem for pure strategy Nash Equilibrium in two-player zero-sum games with unique NE by induction on search space size, using two game-theoretic lemmas (from Maiti et al.) to show that row/column permutations preserve the unique NE and that sub-problem classes remain c.u.p., enabling the isomorphism argument central to the original NFL proof. For black-box complexity, the authors introduce an unrestricted black-box model (Algorithm 1) where algorithms adaptively query payoff entries based on full query history, then combine Yao's minimax principle with the NFL theorem to show that the worst-case expected query complexity of any randomised algorithm equals that of a simple diagonal-querying strategy, yielding a (n+1)/2 lower bound for n×n bimatrix games. Applied to binary voting games, this framework distinguishes polynomial-solvable instances (DIAGONAL, Θ(n) queries) from exponentially hard ones (PLATEAU, e^Ω(n) queries), demonstrating that the choice of solution concept and problem structure fundamentally determines whether efficient black-box adversarial optimisation is possible.

## Results

The paper establishes several key theoretical bounds. Theorem 4.2 proves that no randomised black-box algorithm can solve an n×n bimatrix game with a unique pure Nash Equilibrium in fewer than (n+1)/2 queries in the worst case, improving the previous best bound by Maiti et al. [27] by a multiplicative factor of 4. For binary voting games (search space {0,1}^n × {0,1}^n), Theorem 4.3 shows the black-box complexity is e^Ω(n) (exponential), while Theorem 4.4 shows the structured DIAGONAL_n class has complexity Θ(n) (linear), and Theorem 4.5 shows PLATEAU_n retains exponential complexity e^Ω(n). The general lower bound (Theorem 4.1) is ⌈log_k|X×Y|⌉ − 1 for payoff functions taking k distinct values.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no empirical baselines or experimental comparisons. The main theoretical comparison is with Maiti et al. [27], whose query-efficient algorithm for finding unique NE in two-player zero-sum matrix games achieved a lower bound of approximately n/8; this paper's Theorem 4.2 improves that to (n+1)/2, a factor-of-4 sharpening. The paper also contrasts with Wolpert and Macready [46] and Service and Tauritz [39], who demonstrated 'free lunch' (i.e., some algorithms outperform others on average) for the maximin and 'maximisation over all test cases' solution concepts respectively. This paper's Theorem 3.1 shows the opposite—No Free Lunch—holds when Nash Equilibrium is the solution concept, resolving a long-standing open question. The DIAGONAL_n result (Θ(n)) also provides a concrete benchmark that is polynomially solvable, in contrast to the exponential hardness of PLATEAU_n and general binary voting games.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the No Free Lunch (NFL) theorem lineage from Wolpert and Macready [45, 46] and Droste et al. [11] into the adversarial (maximin) optimisation setting, specifically for the Pure Strategy Nash Equilibrium solution concept. While Wolpert and Macready [46] and Service and Tauritz [39] previously demonstrated that free lunches exist for the maximin and 'Maximisation Over All Test Cases' solution concepts respectively, this paper proves the opposite (NFL) for NE, showing that the choice of solution concept is the critical determinant. The black-box complexity framework is adapted from the single-objective setting [13, 8, 10, 43] and combined with Yao's Principle to yield a linear lower bound for bimatrix games, sharpening the prior logarithmic-type bound from Maiti et al. [27].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This resolves a long-standing open problem in adversarial optimisation theory by showing that the 'free lunch' phenomenon is not universal across solution concepts, which has direct implications for the design and evaluation of co-evolutionary and adversarial search algorithms (e.g., for GAN training, cybersecurity, spatial games). The introduction of black-box complexity to adversarial optimisation provides the first general lower-bound framework for this setting, distinguishing polynomially-solvable problem classes (DIAGONAL) from exponentially-hard ones (PLATEAU). Key limitations include the restriction to finite discrete search spaces, two-player zero-sum games, and unique pure NE; extending to mixed strategies, multiple equilibria, or continuous domains remains open.
