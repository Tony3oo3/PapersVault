## Problem

Designing informative interventions for causal structure learning typically requires iterative likelihood-based inference of the causal graph at each step, which is slow and intractable in domains like single-cell gene expression where the data likelihood is unavailable. Existing intervention design methods are either restricted to likelihood-based settings or are non-amortized, meaning they cannot generalize across different experimental environments without re-optimization. This paper asks whether a single trained policy can amortize the entire intervention design loop, producing adaptive, informative interventions via a single forward pass without any intermediate causal graph estimation.

## Input / Output

The policy takes as input the interventional history h_t ∈ R^((n₀+t)×d×2), formed by concatenating all observed data samples with one-hot indicators of which variables were intervened on at each step; intervention values are not separately encoded for do-interventions since they are already reflected in the data. The output is the next intervention I_t ∈ R^(d×2), where the first column is a binary mask (thresholded at 0) selecting which variables to intervene on and the second column gives the continuous intervention value or shift amount. In the SERGIO gene-expression setting, the output reduces to d dimensions since interventions are binary knockouts or knockdowns with no free value parameter.

## Method

CAASL parameterizes the intervention design policy as a transformer with alternating self-attention over the sample axis and the variable axis, encoding permutation equivariance to variable ordering and permutation invariance to history ordering; after L layers, max-pooling over samples yields a d×l embedding passed through an MLP that outputs Gaussian-Tanh logits for the intervention. The policy is trained with REDQ (an ensemble variant of Soft Actor-Critic) on a simulator of the design environment, using a telescoping reward defined as the step-wise improvement in the expected number of correct adjacency-matrix entries predicted by the pretrained AVICI amortized causal posterior. This reward is likelihood-free and connects to a Barber-Agakov lower bound on multi-step expected information gain, but replaces the log-posterior with a simpler accuracy metric that proved more effective in practice. At test time, deploying the policy requires only a single forward pass per step—no intermediate Bayesian inference or graph search—enabling real-time, amortized intervention design that generalizes zero-shot to distribution shifts in graph priors, noise distributions, intervention types, and data dimensionality.

## Results

On the synthetic linear SCM environment (d=10, budget T=10, n₀=50 observational samples), CAASL achieves cumulative returns of approximately 76, compared to ~72 for the random intervention baseline and lower values for likelihood-based baselines DiffCBED and SS Finite. On the SERGIO single-cell gene expression simulator (d=10, 74% dropout, 10X Chromium noise), CAASL surpasses the random baseline after just 5 interventional acquisitions. Zero-shot OOD generalization is demonstrated across graph prior shifts (Erdős-Rényi → Scale-Free), mechanism changes, noise distribution changes (Gaussian → Gumbel, homoskedastic → heteroskedastic), intervention type changes (do → shift, knockout → knockdown), and dimensionality increases (d=10 → d=30), with CAASL consistently outperforming random and likelihood-based baselines in most settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CAASL is compared against four baselines: Random interventions, Observational data collection, DiffCBED [Tigas et al., ICML 2023], and SS Finite [Sussex et al., NeurIPS 2021]. On the synthetic in-distribution setting, CAASL achieves ~76 returns versus ~72 for Random, and clearly outperforms both DiffCBED and SS Finite, which require explicit likelihood evaluation and intermediate causal graph inference (1000 posterior inferences for 100 environments × budget 10). Across all zero-shot OOD settings (graph prior, mechanism, noise, intervention type, dimensionality), CAASL maintains its advantage over Random and the likelihood-based baselines. The one notable exception is the SERGIO environment with increasing dimensionality (d > 10), where the Random baseline becomes competitive with CAASL, which the authors attribute to the high missingness rate (~74%) reducing the relevance of the encoded design-space symmetries. DiffCBED and SS Finite are omitted from OOD and SERGIO comparisons where the data likelihood is intractable, further highlighting CAASL's likelihood-free advantage.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the standard infer-design-experiment loop in active causal structure learning by amortizing the entire design step into a single transformer policy trained with RL (SAC/REDQ). It builds directly on AVICI (Lorch et al., NeurIPS 2022) as a pretrained, likelihood-free reward model, and connects its telescoping reward to the Barber-Agakov lower bound on multi-step expected information gain from sequential Bayesian experimental design (Foster et al., 2021; Blau et al., 2022). Unlike prior likelihood-based design methods (Tigas et al., 2022, 2023; Sussex et al., 2021) that require intermediate posterior inference at each step, CAASL performs only a forward pass of the policy at test time, and unlike prior RL-for-causal-discovery work (Zhu et al., 2019; Sauter et al., 2023) that uses RL for graph search, it uses RL for intervention design with a fixed amortized inference model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical significance lies in enabling lab-in-the-loop intervention design for single-cell gene regulatory networks, where data likelihoods are intractable and high missingness (74–85%) rules out most existing design algorithms. The zero-shot generalization to higher dimensions (d=30 from d=10 training) and to unseen intervention types (shift from do) suggests a single trained policy could serve across experimental conditions, reducing the need for retraining. However, the random baseline remains highly competitive in the SERGIO setting, and the authors note no theoretical guarantees for OOD performance, leaving open whether the encoded symmetries are sufficient under extreme missingness. Future work on scaling to larger gene networks, incorporating real offline data, and providing formal generalization bounds would strengthen the practical case.
