## Problem

Existing diffusion-based time series generators faithfully reproduce the overall data distribution but systematically fail to preserve the distribution of extreme values (block maxima), because the linear noise schedule attenuates the high-frequency components that encode abrupt peaks far faster than low-frequency trends. This gap undermines critical applications such as risk management, scenario planning, and disaster preparedness where accurate tail modeling is essential.

## Input / Output

During training, the model consumes univariate time series of length T (standardized to zero mean, unit variance) and computes block maxima by partitioning each series into disjoint windows. At generation time, a single block maxima value is sampled from a pre-fitted GEV distribution and fed as a conditioning signal to the denoiser; the model then produces a full univariate time series of length T whose maximum is guided toward the specified value. The encoder is a 3-layer transformer with fully connected layers, and the entire pipeline operates in the univariate setting.

## Method

FIDE introduces three coupled modifications to standard DDPM. First, a high-frequency inflation step multiplies the top-κ Fourier coefficients of each training sample by a weight γ>1 before the forward diffusion process, counteracting the rapid dissipation of high-frequency energy (formally shown in Theorem 2 to preserve the high/low frequency ratio by a factor of γ). Second, the reverse denoising process is made conditional on the block maxima value y0, extending the variational lower bound from the marginal p(x0) to the joint p(x0, y0) (Theorem 3). Third, a GEV negative log-likelihood term is added to the standard noise-prediction loss, penalizing the model when the estimated denoised sample's maximum deviates from the fitted GEV distribution. The combined objective L_FIDE = E‖ε̃_n − ε̄_n‖² − λ·log L_GEV(μ,σ,ξ, ỹ0) is optimized end-to-end with a 3-layer transformer denoiser, and sampling proceeds by iteratively denoising from Gaussian noise while conditioning on a GEV-sampled block maxima.

## Results

On the primary task of preserving block maxima distribution, FIDE achieves the best JS Divergence, KL Divergence, and CRPS across all five datasets (AR1, Stock, Energy, Temperature, ECG). For example, on the Temperature dataset, FIDE reduces JS Divergence to 0.0007 (vs. 0.0082 for DDPM and 0.0077 for Diffusion-TS) and CRPS to 0.0517 (vs. 0.1516 for DDPM). On the Predictive Score metric (forecasting block maxima using an LSTM trained on generated data), FIDE achieves the best result on 3 of 5 datasets and is second-best on the remaining two. For the secondary task of preserving the overall (all-values) distribution, FIDE remains competitive with DDPM and Diffusion-TS, confirming that its emphasis on extremes does not significantly degrade general distribution fidelity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FIDE decisively outperforms all nine baselines on the three distribution-fidelity metrics (JS Divergence, KL Divergence, CRPS) for block maxima across all five datasets. The most dramatic gains are over GAN-based methods (TimeGAN, cGAN) and VAE-based methods (beta-VAE, c-beta-VAE, TimeVAE), which show 10–50× worse JS/KL divergence on block maxima. Among diffusion-based baselines, FIDE improves over DDPM by roughly 2–3× on JS Divergence (e.g., 0.0007 vs. 0.0082 on Temperature) and over 2× on CRPS (0.0517 vs. 0.1516 on Temperature), and similarly over Diffusion-TS. On the Predictive Score metric, FIDE is slightly behind RealNVP and Fourier-Flows on Temperature (0.6694 vs. 0.7871) and ECG (0.9040 vs. 0.9655), but leads on AR1, Stock, and Energy. For the all-values distribution (Table 2), FIDE is within 1–5% of DDPM and Diffusion-TS on KL Divergence and CRPS, confirming that its extreme-value focus does not substantially compromise general distribution modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

FIDE extends the DDPM framework (Ho et al., 2020) to the time series domain by introducing three targeted modifications: a frequency-domain high-frequency inflation strategy, conditional generation on block maxima, and a GEV-distribution-based regularization loss. Prior diffusion models for time series (CSDI, Diffusion-TS) were designed primarily for imputation or general generation without explicit attention to tail/extreme-value fidelity, while GAN- and flow-based time series generators (TimeGAN, Fourier-Flows, RealNVP) have known difficulties capturing heavy-tailed distributions. The paper's conceptual departure is identifying the frequency-domain mechanism by which standard diffusion noise schedules disproportionately attenuate the high-frequency components that encode abrupt block maxima, and addressing it through a principled inflation factor rather than ad-hoc data augmentation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Accurate modeling of extreme values in time series is critical for risk management, disaster preparedness, and scenario planning in healthcare, finance, and climate domains, yet the paper demonstrates that even strong diffusion baselines systematically under-represent block maxima distributions. The clean theoretical characterization (Theorem 1) of why this happens—linearly increasing noise variance causes high-frequency power to decay as δ ≪ 1 relative to low-frequency power—provides a reusable diagnostic for any diffusion-based time series generator. Limitations include restriction to univariate series and a single extreme-value statistic (block maxima); open questions include extension to multivariate extremes, peaks-over-threshold statistics, and whether the FFT/IFFT overhead at each training step scales acceptably for very long sequences.
