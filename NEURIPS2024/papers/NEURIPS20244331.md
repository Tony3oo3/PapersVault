## Problem

Existing causal transportability methods provide identification criteria for policy effects across domains but lack efficient finite-sample estimators when data must be combined from multiple experimental studies collected under different policies and structural assumptions. This paper bridges the gap between identification and estimation by developing graphical criteria and multiply-robust estimators for evaluating stochastic/conditional policies (dynamic treatment regimes) from heterogeneous multi-domain experimental data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244331/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes m source-domain experimental datasets P_{π_i}^i(V) (each collected under a potentially different behavior policy π_i), the target-domain covariate distribution P^0(C), and a set of causal diagrams G_{π_i}^i together with selection diagrams G_{π_0}^{Δ_{0,i}} encoding which mechanisms may differ across domains. The target policy π_0 is specified as a sequence of conditional distributions over actions. The output is a scalar estimate of E_{P_{π_0}^0}[Y] with an asymptotic normal distribution, enabling construction of confidence intervals; the estimator achieves n^{-1/2} convergence when nuisance parameters converge at the slower n^{-1/4} rate.

## Method

The core contribution is a DML-based estimator (Defs. 5, 7) that decomposes the target policy effect into a telescoping sum of influence-function-like terms φ^i, each estimable from a different source dataset, plus a target-domain term. Identification is guaranteed by d-separation criteria in selection diagrams (Defs. 4, 6) that ensure domain transfer and adjustment conditions hold, yielding an adjustment formula (Eqs. 1, 8) expressing the policy effect as a product of conditional expectations from different source distributions weighted by the target policy. The estimator uses L-fold cross-fitting: nuisance parameters (regression functions μ and density ratios ω) are estimated on training folds and evaluated on held-out folds. Density ratios are estimated via a classification-based reduction (assigning domain labels and using off-the-shelf classifiers). The key theoretical property is multiply robustness: the error decomposes into a sum of R_i terms (each converging to Normal(0, ρ_i²)) plus products of nuisance errors, so the estimator remains consistent if either the regression or the density-ratio component is correctly specified at each stage, and achieves the parametric n^{-1/2} rate under n^{-1/4} nuisance convergence.

## Results

The paper evaluates three estimators—OM (outcome model), PW (probability weighting), and the proposed DML (double machine learning)—by measuring absolute error (AE) of the policy effect estimate across sample sizes N ∈ {2500, 5000, 10000, 20000}. In synthetic simulations (Figs. 3a, 3b), DML consistently achieves the lowest AE and fastest convergence, corroborating the theoretical n^{-1/2} rate guarantee when nuisances converge at n^{-1/4}. On the ACTG 175 trial (Figs. 3c, 3d), DML and OM converge faster than PW under noisy nuisance estimation, though DML does not uniformly dominate OM at every sample size. On Project STAR (Fig. 4), all three estimators improve with sample size, with DML again showing the steepest convergence curve.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed DML estimator against two component baselines: OM (a purely regression-based estimator using nuisance μ) and PW (a purely density-ratio-based estimator using nuisance ω). In synthetic simulations for both two-domain and multi-domain settings, DML clearly outperforms both OM and PW across all sample sizes, achieving the lowest absolute error and fastest convergence, consistent with its doubly-robust theoretical guarantee. On the ACTG 175 real-world data, under noisy nuisance estimation, both DML and OM converge faster than PW, but DML does not consistently beat OM at all sample sizes because its error is a combination of OM and PW errors—when PW has high error, DML's error is also elevated. On Project STAR, DML again shows the fastest convergence relative to both OM and PW, though the gap narrows at larger sample sizes. Overall, DML wins in the synthetic and Project STAR settings, ties or slightly trails OM in parts of the ACTG 175 analysis, and uniformly outperforms PW across all experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of the causal transportability literature (Bareinboim & Pearl; Lee et al.; Correa & Bareinboim) and the double/debiased machine learning estimation framework (Chernozhukov et al.). It directly extends the authors' prior line of work on combining multiple experimental datasets (Jung et al., ICML 2023; NeurIPS 2024) by introducing policy evaluation (dynamic treatment regimes) as the target estimand and by handling structural discrepancies across source domains via selection diagrams. The identification criteria (Theorems 1, 4) generalize the adjustment-based transportability conditions to the multi-policy, multi-domain setting, while the multiply-robust DML estimators (Theorems 3, 6) adapt the cross-fitting and sample-splitting machinery to the resulting multi-dataset adjustment formulas.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper bridges a recognized gap between identification (knowing that a policy effect is expressible from available data) and estimation (computing it efficiently from finite samples) in the multi-domain transportability setting, which is directly relevant to meta-analysis of clinical trials and policy evaluation in education. Key limitations include the reliance on correctly specified causal and selection diagrams (requiring domain knowledge), the computational burden of estimating high-dimensional density ratios, and the fact that convergence guarantees are stated in the infinite-sample limit without finite-sample uncertainty quantification. Open questions include whether the graphical criteria can be relaxed (e.g., to partial identification), how the estimators behave under model misspecification of the causal graph, and whether the approach scales to very large numbers of source domains or high-dimensional covariates.
