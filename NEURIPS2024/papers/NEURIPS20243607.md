## Problem

Traditional subword tokenizers (BPE, Unigram, BytePiece) are static, frequency-driven, and decoupled from the LLM's learning dynamics, meaning they cannot adapt to the model's evolving understanding during training. End-to-end learnable tokenizers address this coupling but introduce heavy computational overhead and require stringent data quality. The paper asks whether a lightweight, iterative refinement procedure can produce a tokenizer that is both computationally efficient and tightly aligned with a specific LLM's performance.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a large training corpus (~16B tokens from The Pile) and an initial oversized vocabulary of 150K tokens (generated by Unigram or BytePiece), then iteratively prunes it down to a target size (50K or 30K) over 5 epochs. The output is a model-specific optimized vocabulary that, when applied to raw text, produces token sequences for the LLM. Notably, the resulting vocabulary is not transferable across model sizes without performance loss, and the pruning decisions depend on a randomly initialized LLM's cross-entropy predictions rather than corpus statistics alone.

## Method

ADAT (Adaptive Tokenizer) extends the Unigram pruning framework by replacing the purely frequency-based token loss with a combined score that incorporates an LLM's cross-entropy prediction error for each token. At each of 5 iterative epochs, a randomly initialized Pythia model is trained on 0.3B tokens, then run in inference on 0.1B tokens to compute per-token cross-entropy loss; this LLM-driven loss is combined with the Unigram frequency loss via the balance function a/(λ·log(b+1)), and tokens with the lowest combined score are pruned. Two stabilizing mechanisms are added: a Viterbi-based randomized tokenization ensures uniform exposure of all vocabulary tokens during training, and a momentum term (β-weighted previous-iteration loss) smooths pruning decisions across epochs. The key insight is that tokens which are infrequent yet easy for the LLM to predict can be safely removed, while frequent tokens that the LLM struggles to predict should be retained, yielding a vocabulary that is simultaneously compact and performance-optimal for the target model.

## Results

On the Pythia-70M model with a 50K vocabulary, ADAT initialized with Unigram achieves an average benchmark accuracy of 44.51%, surpassing the Unigram baseline (42.22%) by +2.29 points and BPE (41.91%) by +2.60 points. The perplexity on PG19 drops dramatically from 16.52 (Unigram) to 6.97 (ADAT+Unigram), a reduction of 9.55 points. When initialized with BytePiece, ADAT still improves average accuracy from 37.21% to 39.91% (+2.70) and reduces PPL from 71.5 to 67.19. Across model scales (70M, 160M, 410M), ADAT consistently outperforms Unigram by 2.0–2.4 points in average accuracy.

## Baselines & Comparisons

ADAT is compared against three baselines: BPE, BytePiece, and Unigram. Against Unigram (the strongest baseline), ADAT+Unigram wins on 7 of 9 downstream benchmarks (ARC-E, Boolq, Lambda, LogiQA, SciQ, SST-2, Winogrande) but slightly trails on ARC-C (18.46 vs. 19.54) and PIQA (59.93 vs. 60.50), yielding a net +2.29 average improvement. The PPL advantage is the most striking: 6.97 vs. 16.52, nearly a 2.4× reduction. Against BPE, ADAT+Unigram achieves +2.60 average accuracy and a PPL of 6.97 vs. 22.31. Against BytePiece, ADAT+BytePiece improves average accuracy by +2.70 (39.91 vs. 37.21) and reduces PPL from 71.5 to 67.19, demonstrating that ADAT can rescue a weaker initial vocabulary. Notably, the 70M ADAT model (44.51) outperforms the 160M Unigram model (44.04), effectively bridging a 2.3× parameter gap through vocabulary optimization alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

ADAT extends the Unigram vocabulary-pruning framework (Kudo, 2018) by replacing its purely frequency-based loss with a composite criterion that also incorporates per-token cross-entropy from a small LLM trained on the current vocabulary. This positions the method as a middle ground between static frequency-based tokenizers (BPE, BytePiece, Unigram) that are decoupled from model training, and end-to-end learnable tokenizers (MANTa, Neural) that introduce gradient-based modules and heavy data-quality requirements. The iterative train-infer-prune loop with Viterbi-based random sampling and loss momentum is a practical simplification that avoids the architectural modifications required by prior learnable-tokenizer approaches while still coupling vocabulary selection to model performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method offers a computationally cheap (2 GPU hours vs. 48–510 for full training) and architecturally agnostic way to improve tokenizer-model alignment, with the potential to be dropped into any subword-tokenization pipeline. A key limitation is that each model size yields a different vocabulary, which complicates knowledge distillation, speculative decoding, and multi-model deployments that assume a shared token space. Open questions include whether the large PPL gains (partly attributable to ADAT's lower compression rate) persist at much larger scales (10B+ parameters), whether the approach transfers to non-English or code-centric corpora, and whether the cross-model vocabulary mismatch can be mitigated without sacrificing the per-model optimization benefit.
