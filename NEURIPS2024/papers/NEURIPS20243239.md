## Problem

Research on autonomous racing at the physical limits of vehicle handling has been constrained by the high cost of real-vehicle testing and the limited physics fidelity of existing open-source simulators (e.g., CARLA, F1Tenth, DeepRacer), none of which are designed for high-speed sports car racing. No publicly available platform combines realistic vehicle dynamics, a Gym/ROS2 interface for both RL and classical control, and a large-scale human driving dataset, making it difficult to benchmark and compare algorithms in a reproducible, accessible setting.

## Input / Output

The agent receives a 125-dimensional partially observable state vector at 25 Hz, comprising telemetry from the last three time steps (linear/angular velocities, accelerations, slip angles, force feedback, look-ahead curvature, range-finder distances to track edges) plus the absolute control values from the two preceding steps and a 2D distance to a track reference path. The output is a 3-dimensional continuous action (throttle, brake, steering) normalized to [-1, 1], applied as deltas to the current control values rather than absolute commands, which the authors found critical for reducing oscillation. The simulation is strictly real-time (300 Hz physics on Windows), and actions are injected via a vJoy virtual joystick device.

## Method

The paper builds a high-fidelity racing simulation platform on Assetto Corsa, exposing a Gym-compliant and ROS2 interface through a custom plugin that reads shared-memory telemetry at the physics rate and writes controls via UDP/vJoy, with a track-recovery feature for RL stability. For benchmarking, they train and evaluate four method families: Soft Actor-Critic (with n=3 step returns, which they identify as essential for convergence), TD-MPC2 (a model-based RL world model with receding-horizon MPC at inference), IQL (offline RL), and a classical single-track MPC with a Pacejka tire model. The key empirical contributions are a 2.3M-step human driving dataset (927 laps from 15 drivers spanning professional to beginner) and 64M steps of SAC replay data across 4 tracks and 3 cars, which they use to show that (i) human demonstrations substantially improve sample efficiency and final lap times, (ii) pretraining TD-MPC2 on multiple source tracks enables few-shot transfer to unseen tracks with far fewer crashes, and (iii) bootstrapping with human data allows the agent to complete laps without any reference path, whereas SAC from scratch fails entirely in that setting.

## Results

On the F317 car, SAC fD (SAC initialized with human demonstrations) achieves the best lap times across most tracks, slightly outperforming the best human driver on Monza (104.08s vs. 104.31s) and Red Bull Ring (83.51s vs. 83.53s for SAC from scratch). With the GT3 car, human experts remain slightly faster than SAC fD (e.g., 108.38s vs. 108.96s on Barcelona). The classical MPC controller achieves the best single result on Indianapolis/F317 (59.41s), while the built-in AI is consistently the slowest and fails to complete a lap on Monza/F317. Both SAC and TD-MPC2 outperform most human drivers after only ~6 hours of training, with SAC requiring approximately 4 days to match the best human lap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SAC fD (SAC with human demonstrations) is the strongest RL method overall, beating the best human driver on F317/Monza (104.08s vs. 104.31s) and matching or exceeding SAC from scratch on every F317 track. TD-MPC2 fD is consistently 0.5–1.8s slower than SAC fD on F317 tracks, while TD-MPC2 ft (pretrained on other tracks) shows the clearest benefit in few-shot transfer, completing full laps on unseen tracks after only a few episodes versus hundreds for SAC fD. The classical MPC controller wins on Indianapolis/F317 (59.41s) but is 2–3s slower on technical tracks like Barcelona and Monza, reflecting its reliance on hand-tuned per-track trajectories. IQL (offline RL) is competitive but trails SAC fD by 0.5–1.5s on F317 and fails entirely on GT3/Monza. The built-in Assetto Corsa AI is the weakest baseline, losing to all other methods and failing to finish a lap on the most challenging track (MNZ/F317).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of accessible driving simulators (CARLA, F1Tenth, DeepRacer) and professional-grade racing platforms (Unity/Matlab/Simulink-based systems used by Formula teams), arguing that neither extreme serves the research community well for high-speed racing. It builds directly on the SAC-based racing work of Wurman et al. (2022) in Gran Turismo, but addresses the critical gap of public availability by leveraging Assetto Corsa's plug-in interface. The algorithmic baselines (SAC, TD-MPC2, IQL, MPC) are all established methods; the contribution is the benchmark environment, the large-scale human dataset, and the systematic evaluation protocol rather than new algorithms.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first open-source, high-fidelity racing simulation platform with a comprehensive human driving dataset (927 laps across 4 tracks and 3 cars) and RL replay data (64M steps), making autonomous racing research accessible without the cost and complexity of professional simulators. The finding that SAC with human demonstrations matches a professional e-sports driver on the F317 but is outperformed by experts on the GT3 highlights that vehicle dynamics remain a key bottleneck. Open questions include whether image-based observations (rather than telemetry) can close the remaining gap, whether manual gear shifting unlocks further performance, and whether the few-shot transfer results generalize to truly unseen track geometries rather than just different layouts of similar difficulty.
