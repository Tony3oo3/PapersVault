## Problem

Standard Adam's timestep parameter assumes a stationary optimization objective, but RL is inherently nonstationary—target network updates and bootstrapped value estimates cause abrupt changes in gradient magnitude that Adam's bias correction fails to handle, leading to oversized updates and degraded performance. Prior approaches either layer RL-specific implementation tricks on top of the standard toolchain or reset the entire optimizer state, discarding valuable momentum information. This paper asks whether a minimal, principled modification to Adam can address nonstationarity without sacrificing accumulated optimization history.

## Input / Output

The method operates on per-parameter gradients from the RL objective (PPO clipped surrogate plus TD loss, or DQN TD loss) and the running Adam state (first moment m, second moment v, and timestep t). The key operational detail is that t is reset to 0 at each boundary of a 'stationary period'—after a target network update in DQN or at the start of each new rollout batch in PPO—while m and v are retained. The output is the standard Adam parameter update, but with bias correction terms (1−β₁^t) and (1−β₂^t) recomputed from the local rather than global timestep, making it a one-line code change to any existing Adam implementation.

## Method

Adam-Rel resets Adam's timestep counter t to 0 at the start of each new supervised learning sub-problem (triggered by target network updates or new rollout batches) while preserving the momentum estimates m and v. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.1) shows that under a sudden k-fold increase in gradient magnitude, standard Adam's update size peaks at approximately (1−β₁)/√(1−β₂) ≈ √10 for default hyperparameters, whereas Adam-Rel's update size remains bounded and converges to 1 as k→∞. The mechanism works because the bias correction terms re-account for the effective sparsity of momentum estimates relative to the new gradient scale, without discarding the directional information accumulated in m and v. In the absence of large gradient magnitude changes, Adam-Rel reduces to a form of learning rate annealing over short horizons (e.g., 16 PPO epochs) or warmup over longer horizons (e.g., ~1000 DQN steps), providing a second stabilizing benefit beyond nonstationarity correction.

## Results

On Atari-10 with DQN, Adam-Rel achieves 65.7% human-normalized performance, substantially outperforming Adam (28.8%) and Adam-MR (23.5%). On Atari-57 with PPO, Adam-Rel outperforms Adam in 33 of 55 games and achieves 338% vs. 220% human-normalized performance at the 75th percentile of the performance profile. On Craftax-1B with PPO, Adam-Rel outperforms Adam on all reported metrics, with the largest gains on the hardest achievements (collecting diamonds, eating a plant), while Adam-MR achieves dramatically poorer performance across all metrics.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against standard Adam, Adam-Rel consistently wins: on Atari-10 DQN it more than doubles human-normalized performance (65.7% vs. 28.8%), on Atari-57 PPO it outperforms in 33/55 games and lifts the 75th-percentile score from 220% to 338%, and on Craftax-1B it improves all metrics including the hardest achievements. Against Adam-MR (full optimizer reset from Asadi et al.), Adam-Rel dramatically outperforms, particularly in on-policy RL where Adam-MR's performance collapses—on Craftax-1B Adam-MR is far worse than Adam itself, and on Atari-10 DQN it scores only 23.5% vs. Adam-Rel's 65.7%. This result contradicts the gradient contamination hypothesis, showing that retaining momentum across target changes (with only the timestep reset) is essential. Against Adam with β₁=β₂ (Dohare et al.), Adam-Rel outperforms on all metrics, while the β₁=β₂ variant shows no significant improvement over default Adam, indicating that equalizing decay coefficients is insufficient to address nonstationarity.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Adam optimizer (Kingma & Ba, 2014) and the well-studied problem of nonstationarity in RL, where the optimization objective shifts as the agent's policy or target network updates. It departs from the 'gradient contamination hypothesis' (Bengio et al., 2021; Asadi et al., 2023), which argued that stale momentum estimates are harmful and should be fully discarded at target changes, by showing that retaining momentum while resetting only the timestep parameter (Adam-Rel) is both theoretically justified and empirically superior. The method also subsumes learning rate annealing as a special case when no large gradient jumps occur, connecting it to established stationary-optimization techniques like warmup schedules (Liu et al., 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Adam-Rel is a one-line modification that consistently improves performance across both on-policy (PPO on Craftax-1B, Atari-57) and off-policy (DQN on Atari-10) settings, making it a low-cost, high-impact addition to the RL toolchain. The finding that full optimizer resets (Adam-MR) can actually *hurt* performance relative to standard Adam challenges the prevailing gradient contamination narrative and suggests that prior work may have overcorrected. Open questions remain around continuous nonstationarity (e.g., Polyak averaging), applicability to other nonstationary domains (RLHF, continual learning), and a deeper understanding of how gradient magnitudes evolve in practice versus the step-function model assumed in the theory.
