## Problem

Existing trajectory prediction methods assume fixed-length, sufficiently long historical observations, but real-world scenarios (e.g., a vehicle suddenly appearing around a corner) demand immediate predictions from arbitrarily few observed points. Complicating matters, longer trajectories do not always outperform shorter ones because additional history can introduce interference, so a one-directional 'long-to-short' knowledge transfer is insufficient.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240903/cropped_page1_idx3.webp)

## Input / Output

The method accepts a 2D position sequence of arbitrary length T > 1 (any number of observed time steps) and outputs K predicted future trajectories, each of F future steps. During training, M random masks are applied to the same observed trajectory to generate M sub-trajectories of different lengths, all fed through a single shared encoder-decoder backbone (e.g., HiVT or QCNet). At inference, no masking or distillation is performed; any-length input is simply passed through the trained model directly.

## Method

LaKD introduces a dynamic, bidirectional knowledge distillation mechanism where a single shared encoder processes all trajectory lengths, and the direction of knowledge transfer is determined at each training step by comparing prediction performance (minimum L2 distance to ground truth) across the M masked variants. The latent features of the 'good' trajectory are distilled into those of 'bad' trajectories via KL divergence, enabling long trajectories to filter interference and short trajectories to capture richer temporal context. To prevent knowledge collision caused by the shared encoder acting as both teacher and student, a dynamic soft-masking strategy computes per-neuron importance scores from gradients, accumulates them across iterations via element-wise maximum, and scales gradient updates inversely to importance—protecting critical units from disruptive updates while prioritizing less important ones. A decay coefficient tied to the regression loss gradually activates the masking as training progresses, and the entire framework is plug-and-play, compatible with any existing trajectory prediction backbone.

## Results

LaKD achieves the best performance across all three datasets (Argoverse 1, nuScenes, Argoverse 2) with both HiVT and QCNet backbones. On Argoverse 1 with QCNet (K=1), LaKD reaches minADE 0.9982, minFDE 2.0718, and MR 0.3439, outperforming the strongest baseline FLN (1.0631/2.2083/0.3579). On Argoverse 2 with QCNet (K=1), LaKD achieves minADE 1.6574 and MR 0.5753, again leading all baselines. The method is notably robust to observation length, with averaged metrics across all trajectory lengths showing consistent gains over fixed-length baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LaKD consistently outperforms all four baselines (Orig, RM, DTO, FLN) across all three datasets and both backbones (HiVT and QCNet). Against the most closely related method FLN [54], LaKD achieves the largest relative gains on Argoverse 1 (e.g., QCNet K=1 minADE: 0.9982 vs. 1.0631, a 6.1% reduction) and Argoverse 2 (QCNet K=1 minADE: 1.6574 vs. 1.6940). Compared to DTO [38], which distills only from long to short trajectories, LaKD's bidirectional dynamic distillation yields clear improvements (e.g., Argoverse 2 QCNet K=1: 1.6574 vs. 1.7713 minADE). Against the original fixed-length baselines (Orig), LaKD demonstrates that handling variable-length observations is essential: on Argoverse 1 with QCNet, minADE drops from 1.1656 to 0.9982. On nuScenes, where the performance gap between methods is narrower, LaKD still leads (HiVT K=1 minADE: 3.4296 vs. 3.5640 for FLN), and notably outperforms RM which actually degrades performance on nuScenes (3.6580 vs. 3.5973 for Orig), highlighting the importance of the knowledge distillation mechanism over simple random masking.

## Relation to Prior Work

LaKD departs from the traditional teacher-student knowledge distillation paradigm by using a single shared encoder as both teacher and student, with the direction of knowledge transfer determined dynamically by prediction performance rather than model size. It builds on the neuron-importance concept from Michel et al. [37] to design its dynamic soft-masking mechanism, which protects critical units during gradient updates. Conceptually, it extends the trajectory prediction task from fixed-length observations (HiVT, QCNet) and instantaneous two-frame prediction (DTO) to truly arbitrary-length observations, while contrasting with FLN [54] by rejecting the assumption that longer trajectories always contain more useful information and instead enabling bidirectional knowledge exchange.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework addresses a practical gap in autonomous driving where observation time is insufficient (e.g., a vehicle suddenly appearing around a corner), and its plug-and-play design means it can be integrated with any existing trajectory prediction backbone without architectural modification. A key open question acknowledged by the authors is how to determine 'good' versus 'bad' trajectories beyond the current heuristic of minimum distance to ground truth; more principled strategies such as reinforcement learning are suggested but left unexplored. The absence of error bars or multiple runs limits confidence in the reported margins, and the method's reliance on a single shared encoder introduces the knowledge-collision problem that the soft-masking mechanism only partially mitigates.
