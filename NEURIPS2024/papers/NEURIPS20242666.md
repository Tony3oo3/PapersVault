## Problem

Invariance-based distributional robustness methods (e.g., anchor regression, DRIG) provably minimize the robust risk only when training distributions are heterogeneous enough to fully identify it. In practice, this identifiability condition is rarely met, yet prior work has treated non-identifiability as a binary failure rather than studying the achievable robustness in the partially identifiable regime. This paper formalizes that regime and asks: what is the best worst-case performance any model can guarantee when the robust risk itself is only set-identified from training data?

## Input / Output

The method consumes multi-environment training data (X_e, Y_e) generated under additive shifts A_e, a PSD test shift upper bound M_test decomposed into seen (range ⊂ S) and unseen (range ⊂ S⊥) components, and a user-specified norm bound C on the true causal parameter β⋆. It outputs a linear predictor β_Θeq^rob that minimizes the worst-case robust risk, along with an estimated minimax quantity quantifying the irreducible robustness floor. The empirical estimator (Algorithm 1) requires only the reference environment data, estimated shift subspaces, and the identified causal parameter projection β^S, making it computable from standard multi-environment datasets without access to the true noise covariance.

## Method

The paper introduces the worst-case robust risk — a supremum of the standard robust risk over all observationally equivalent model parameters consistent with training data — and its minimax counterpart, which is algorithm-independent and well-defined even when the robust risk is not point-identified. In the linear additive-shift setting, they derive a closed-form expression (Theorem 3.1) showing the minimax quantity decomposes into a non-identifiability penalty γ′(C_ker + ‖Rᵀβ‖₂)² plus the identifiable robust risk, and that for large unseen shifts the optimal predictor is orthogonal to the non-identified shift directions (i.e., it 'abstains' from predicting there). The empirical estimator minimizes a three-term loss: reference-environment squared loss, an invariance penalty aligning β with the identified causal parameter β^S along seen directions, and a non-identifiability penalty pushing β toward zero along unseen directions. They further show that existing methods (anchor regression, DRIG, OLS) achieve the minimax rate only in the limit of vanishing unseen shifts and become increasingly suboptimal as the proportion of new test directions grows, a gap confirmed on synthetic and Perturb-seq single-cell data.

## Results

On synthetic Gaussian data, in the identifiable setting the worst-case robust predictor and anchor regression both achieve asymptotically constant robust risk as shift strength γ grows, while OLS error increases linearly. In the partially identifiable setting (varying γ′ for unseen shift directions), all methods exhibit linearly increasing test error, but the worst-case robust predictor has a substantially smaller slope and closely matches the analytic theoretical lower bound. On real-world Perturb-seq single-cell gene expression data (d=622 genes, 28 prediction problems), the worst-case robust predictor maintains relatively stable MSE as perturbation strength s increases, while anchor regression, DRIG, ICP, and OLS all show significantly increasing MSE, with the gap widening as the proportion of unseen test shift directions grows from 0% to 100%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed worst-case robust predictor against anchor regression [41], DRIG [45], ICP [35], and pooled OLS on both synthetic and real-world data. At 0% unseen shift directions (fully identifiable setting), anchor regression and DRIG are optimal, as expected since they protect against shifts observed during training. However, as soon as any unseen directions are present, anchor and DRIG become inferior to OLS/ERM, and the gap to the worst-case robust predictor grows monotonically with the proportion of unseen shifts. ICP shows similar degradation to anchor and DRIG in the partially identifiable regime. On the Perturb-seq real-world experiments, the worst-case robust predictor achieves the lowest MSE across all perturbation strengths s for every proportion of unseen shifts (33%, 67%, 100%), with its test loss remaining relatively stable while all baselines increase sharply with s. Theoretically, Corollary 3.2 shows that anchor regression is optimal only in the limit of vanishing unseen shifts (γ′→0) but deviates significantly from the minimax lower bound for γ′≥γ′_th, confirming the empirical observations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the finite robustness framework of anchor regression (Rothenhausler et al., 2021) and DRIG (Shen et al., 2023) by addressing the case where test shifts include directions not observed during training, making the robust risk only partially identifiable. Prior invariance-based methods implicitly assumed that identifiability conditions were satisfied (i.e., test shifts lie within the span of training shifts), but the authors formalize the more general partially identifiable setting and introduce the worst-case robust risk—a minimax quantity that remains well-defined even when the standard robust risk is not computable from training data. The framework draws on partial identification concepts from econometrics and is conceptually related to concurrent work on partial transportability (Jalaldoust et al., 2024), though it operates in a more agnostic multi-environment setting without requiring a priori causal graph knowledge.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first formal characterization of the best achievable robustness when identifiability conditions fail, showing that invariance-based methods (anchor regression, DRIG, ICP) degrade to OLS-like performance as the proportion of unseen test directions grows—a finding confirmed on both synthetic and real-world Perturb-seq data. The main limitation is the reliance on a linear additive-shift setting for explicit computation of the worst-case robust risk, leaving open the extension to non-linear relationships, classification, and more general shift models. A practical implication is that benchmarking robustness methods on partially identifiable settings (rather than only fully identifiable ones) is essential for assessing their true effectiveness, and the derived worst-case robust predictor offers a principled way to 'abstain' from prediction in non-identified directions.
