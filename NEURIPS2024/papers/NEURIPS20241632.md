## Problem

Existing 3D Gaussian Splatting SLAM systems (GS-SLAM, SplaTAM, Gaussian Splatting SLAM) assume static environments and suffer severe tracking degradation or outright failure when dynamic objects appear. No prior method combines the real-time rendering and high-fidelity reconstruction advantages of 3D Gaussians with robust pose estimation in the presence of moving objects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241632/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system consumes a sequence of RGB-D frames (I_i ∈ R³, D_i ∈ R) and produces per-frame camera poses in SE(3) along with a persistent 3D Gaussian ellipsoid map that can be rendered to novel-view color and depth images at up to 300 FPS at 1080p. Keyframe insertion is governed by optical-flow distance to ensure sufficient overlap, and a OneFormer semantic segmentation network (163 ms/frame) provides dynamic-object priors. The full pipeline runs at ~2 FPS on an RTX 3090 Ti with ~9 GB memory on the BONN dataset.

## Method

DG-SLAM builds a two-thread SLAM system (tracking frontend + mapping backend) over a 3D Gaussian ellipsoid map. For motion masking, it fuses spatio-temporal depth-warp residuals across a sliding window of keyframes with OneFormer semantic masks, producing a precise dynamic-region mask that suppresses both semantic omissions (e.g., balloons, boxes) and depth-warp edge noise. Camera tracking uses a hybrid coarse-to-fine strategy: DROID-VO provides an initial pose and dense bundle adjustment refines it with motion-mask-weighted reprojection residuals, after which a fine stage minimizes photometric and depth losses from Gaussian splatting rendering, gated by an accumulated-opacity reliability mask to exclude under-optimized regions. The mapping thread optimizes Gaussian parameters with RGB, SSIM, and depth losses masked by the motion mask, and employs a two-stage adaptive point-add/prune strategy (uniform sampling plus under-fitting-pixel insertion; opacity/scale/axis-ratio pruning plus multi-view observation re-checking) to maintain geometric integrity.

## Results

On the TUM RGB-D dataset, DG-SLAM achieves an average ATE of 2.2 cm across six sequences (three highly dynamic, two slightly dynamic, one static), outperforming the closest competitor DROID-VO (3.3 cm) and GS-SLAM (15.5 cm). On the more challenging BONN dynamic dataset, DG-SLAM achieves 5.5 cm average ATE, substantially beating DROID-VO (15.4 cm), Rodyn-SLAM (12.3 cm), and GS-SLAM (33.1 cm). For static scene reconstruction on BONN, DG-SLAM achieves 8.06 cm accuracy and 43.67% completion ratio (≤5 cm), surpassing Co-SLAM (18.06 cm / 16.27%) and ESLAM (40.94 cm / 34.93%). On the static ScanNet dataset, DG-SLAM attains 8.6 cm average ATE with only 20 tracking and 40 mapping iterations, competitive with Co-SLAM (8.8 cm) while using far fewer iterations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DG-SLAM outperforms all 15 compared baselines on both the TUM and BONN dynamic datasets. The closest competitor is DROID-VO (3.3 cm avg ATE on TUM vs. DG-SLAM's 2.2 cm), which DG-SLAM surpasses by 1.1 cm on average and by a wider margin on BONN (15.4 vs. 5.5 cm). Among Gaussian-splatting-based SLAM methods, DG-SLAM substantially outperforms GS-SLAM (15.5 vs. 2.2 cm on TUM; 33.1 vs. 5.5 cm on BONN) and SplaTAM (74.4 vs. 2.2 cm on TUM; 57.4 vs. 5.5 cm on BONN), which both suffer severe tracking failures on dynamic sequences. Against NeRF-based methods (NICE-SLAM, Co-SLAM, ESLAM, Vox-Fusion, iMAP), DG-SLAM achieves 10–40× lower ATE on dynamic sequences, where these methods frequently fail entirely (marked 'X'). The prior dynamic SLAM system Rodyn-SLAM (5.3 cm on TUM, 12.3 cm on BONN) is also clearly outperformed. On the static ScanNet benchmark, DG-SLAM (8.6 cm) is competitive with Co-SLAM (8.8 cm) and outperforms NICE-SLAM (10.7 cm), SplaTAM (11.9 cm), and GS-SLAM (15.1 cm), confirming that the hybrid tracking strategy does not sacrifice static-scene performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

DG-SLAM sits at the intersection of two recent SLAM research threads: (1) the shift from neural implicit representations (NICE-SLAM, Co-SLAM, ESLAM) to explicit 3D Gaussian splatting for scene maps (GS-SLAM, SplaTAM, Gaussian Splatting SLAM), and (2) the long-standing problem of robust tracking in dynamic scenes (ReFusion, Rodyn-SLAM). It departs from prior Gaussian SLAM methods by being the first to handle dynamic objects, and from prior dynamic SLAM methods by replacing neural implicit maps with differentiable Gaussian splatting rendering. The hybrid pose optimization borrows the DROID-SLAM factor graph and VO for coarse initialization, then refines via photometric alignment against the Gaussian map — a two-stage strategy not present in either lineage alone.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates that 3D Gaussian splatting can serve as a viable, real-time scene representation for dynamic SLAM, closing a gap where prior Gaussian SLAM systems failed entirely in the presence of moving objects. The significant performance margins over both NeRF-based and Gaussian-based baselines on dynamic benchmarks suggest the explicit, differentiable rendering pipeline is well-suited for online pose refinement. Key limitations remain: the system is constrained to small-scale indoor scenes (no loop closure), and its robustness is partially dependent on the quality of an external semantic segmentation network (OneFormer), which introduces a 163 ms per-frame overhead and a domain gap in unseen environments. Open questions include whether the adaptive Gaussian management strategy scales to large-scale or outdoor scenes, and whether motion detection can be made self-contained without semantic priors.
