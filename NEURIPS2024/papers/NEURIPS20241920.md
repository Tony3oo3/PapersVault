## Problem

While empirical evidence shows that SGD produces simpler, better-generalizing solutions than GD, the mechanism by which stochasticity induces structural simplification (e.g., rank deficiency) in overparameterized networks remains poorly understood. Prior work on diagonal linear networks revealed sparsity effects, but the analogous phenomenon for general two-layer linear networks with matrix-valued parameters—where the relevant structure is the singular value spectrum rather than individual coordinates—had not been characterized. This paper asks: how does stochasticity drive the parameter matrix toward lower rank, and what are the precise forces governing this process?

## Input / Output

The analysis takes a two-layer linear network with weights W1 ∈ R^{p×l} and W2 ∈ R^{l×k} (width l ≥ p+k for full-rank initialization) trained on vector regression data (X ∈ R^{n×p}, Y ∈ R^{n×k}) under square loss. The key output is a closed-form deterministic expression for the time evolution of det(Θ^TΘ) under both GF and SGF, plus a full SDE for the eigenvalues of W^T W that decomposes into a repulsive drift term and a multiplicative (geometric Brownian) noise term. Notably, despite the stochastic dynamics of the parameters, the determinant evolution under SGF is entirely deterministic, depending only on the noise intensity δ, step size η, and data covariance tr(X^TX).

## Method

The paper introduces a block-matrix parametrization Θ = [W1^T | W2] that reveals the multiplicative structure dΘ = ΘJ dt of gradient flow, enabling a clean proof that det(Θ^TΘ) is conserved under GF (zero trace of J) but decays exponentially under SGF via an Itô correction term −2ηδk·tr(X^TX)·det(Θ^TΘ) dt. To uncover the mechanism, the authors derive the SDE governing the eigenvalues of M = W^TW using tools from matrix-valued stochastic processes (Bru, 1989), revealing two competing forces: a repulsive drift term (s_i c_j² + s_j c_i²)/(s_i − s_j) that pushes eigenvalues apart, and a geometric Brownian motion term 2√(s_i c_i²) dε_i that multiplicatively pulls them toward zero. In the large-noise limit with l=2, they prove that s_0^{−α} is a super-martingale while s_1^{−α} is a sub-martingale for α=(p−3)/2, establishing a clear separation where one singular value grows in expectation while the other shrinks. The results are generalized to arbitrary losses (via time-varying noise covariance) and discrete gradient algorithms (via a determinant contraction lemma), and validated experimentally on linear and ReLU networks for both regression and classification.

## Results

The paper establishes a sharp dichotomy: under gradient flow (GF), the determinant of the parameter matrix ΘᵀΘ is exactly preserved (Theorem 4.1), while under stochastic gradient flow (SGF) with label noise intensity δ, it decays deterministically as det(Θ(t)ᵀΘ(t)) = det(Θ₀ᵀΘ₀)·exp{−2ηδk·tr(XᵀX)·t} (Theorem 4.2). The derived eigenvalue SDE (Theorem 5.1) reveals two competing forces: a repulsive interaction between eigenvalues that pushes them apart, and a geometric Brownian motion component that multiplicatively pulls singular values toward zero. Experiments on synthetic regression (n=1000, p=5, k=2, l=10, Gaussian initialization) confirm that with δ=2, the smallest l−k singular values of W₁ collapse toward zero under SGF while remaining stable under GF, and this effect extends to classification with logistic loss and 2-layer ReLU networks under SGD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The central comparison is between deterministic gradient flow (GF) and stochastic gradient flow (SGF) on two-layer linear networks. GF preserves the rank of the parameter matrix throughout training (det(ΘᵀΘ) remains constant), meaning the network retains all l hidden directions regardless of initialization. In contrast, SGF provably drives the determinant to zero at a rate proportional to ηδk·tr(XᵀX), effectively eliminating l−k redundant singular values and yielding a rank-k solution. The eigenvalue SDE analysis shows this is not merely a uniform shrinkage: the repulsive force between eigenvalues (term (sᵢcⱼ²+sⱼcᵢ²)/(sᵢ−sⱼ)) actively separates them, while the geometric Brownian motion (√(sᵢcᵢ²)·dεᵢ) pulls the smaller eigenvalues toward zero. In the large-noise limit with l=2, Theorem 5.2 shows s₀^{−α} is a super-martingale while s₁^{−α} is a sub-martingale for α=(p−3)/2, confirming a clear separation where one eigenvalue grows in expectation and the other diminishes. Experiments with SGD (rather than label noise) at larger stepsizes (η=0.1 for regression, η=0.5 for classification) reproduce the rank-deficiency effect, while smaller stepsizes (η=0.005) behave more like GF, confirming that the stochasticity strength controls the degree of implicit regularization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the deterministic eigenvalue dynamics of linear networks (Saxe et al. 2014, Arora et al. 2019, Varre et al. 2023) to the stochastic setting by deriving an SDE for singular values under label-noise gradient flow, leveraging the matrix-valued stochastic process framework of Bru (1989). It builds on the label-noise GD framework of Blanc et al. (2020) and Damian et al. (2021) as a proxy for SGD, and on Li et al. (2019) for continuous-time SDE modeling of discrete SGD. The key departure from prior work on diagonal linear networks (Pesme et al. 2021, Pillaud-Vivien et al. 2022) is the treatment of full non-diagonal parameter matrices, where a repulsive force between eigenvalues emerges as the mechanism for rank deficiency — a phenomenon absent in the diagonal case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean mechanistic explanation for the empirically observed rank-reducing effect of SGD: a repulsive force between eigenvalues combined with geometric Brownian motion pulling them toward zero. This identifies a specific regularization mechanism unique to stochastic training that has no deterministic counterpart. However, the authors acknowledge that the determinant analysis only guarantees at least one singular value vanishes, and a full characterization of the eigenvalue SDE solution (including collision avoidance) remains open. The results are supported by experiments extending beyond linear networks to ReLU architectures and beyond regression to classification, suggesting broader applicability, but the theoretical treatment is confined to two-layer linear networks with specific assumptions.
