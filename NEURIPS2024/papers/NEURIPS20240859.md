## Problem

Despite the success of diffusion models in image generation, their semantic spaces remain poorly understood, making precise, disentangled, and localized image editing without additional training a difficult task. Existing editing methods either require extra training, are limited to global (non-localized) control, rely on heuristics without mathematical grounding, or depend on text supervision via CLIP. This paper identifies and theoretically justifies the local linearity and low-rank structure of the posterior mean predictor (PMP) in diffusion models, enabling a principled, training-free, and supervision-free approach to localized editing.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (e.g., 64×64 or 256×256), a binary region-of-interest mask Ω specifying which pixels to edit, and a pretrained diffusion model (unconditional DDPM/U-ViT or T2I models like Stable Diffusion, DeepFloyd IF, or Latent Consistency Models). For T-LOCO Edit, an optional text editing prompt (e.g., 'with glasses') is additionally provided. The output is a single edited image where only the masked region changes semantically, along with the identified editing direction vector v_p in the noise space x_t. Editing is performed at a single timestep t ∈ [0.5, 0.7] in the noise (or latent) space, with masking applied in image space after decoding for latent-space models.

## Method

LOCO Edit exploits two empirically observed and theoretically proven properties of the PMP f_{θ,t}(x_t): (1) it is locally linear for t ∈ [0.2, 0.7], and (2) its Jacobian J_{θ,t}(x_t) is low-rank (rank ratio < 0.1). The pipeline: (i) DDIM-inverts the input image x_0 to obtain x_t at the chosen timestep; (ii) computes the top-r SVD of the masked Jacobian ∇_{x_t} P_Ω(f_{θ,t}(x_t)) via the Generalized Power Method to extract candidate editing directions from the right singular vectors; (iii) projects the selected direction onto the nullspace of the outside-ROI Jacobian ∇_{x_t} P_{Ω^C}(f_{θ,t}(x_t)) to suppress unwanted changes outside the mask; (iv) perturbs x_t along the normalized direction v_p by a scalar λ and DDIM-samples to produce the edited image. The theoretical analysis assumes data lies on a union of K orthogonal low-rank Gaussian subspaces and proves that the posterior mean's Jacobian has rank bounded by the intrinsic dimension, exhibits local linearity with error O(λ·α_t/(1−α_t)·O(λ)), and that its top singular vectors converge to the data subspace basis as t→1. For T2I models, the text-supervised variant computes the editing direction as J_{θ,t}^e(x_t)^T · d where d is the difference in PMP outputs between the original and editing prompts, then applies the same masking and nullspace projection.

## Results

LOCO Edit achieves the highest Local Edit Success Rate (0.80) and Transfer Success Rate (0.91) among all compared methods, while requiring only a single image and 79 seconds of learning time. It is the only method that simultaneously supports one-step editing, localized editing, no additional supervision, and theoretical grounding. On image consistency metrics, LOCO Edit reports LPIPS of 0.08 and SSIM of 0.71, slightly behind BlendedDiffusion (LPIPS 0.03, SSIM 0.94) but with far superior transferability and mask-accurate editing.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LOCO Edit substantially outperforms all baselines on Local Edit Success Rate (0.80 vs. 0.55 for BlendedDiffusion, 0.47 for Asyrp, 0.37 for ∂ε_t/∂x_t, and 0.32 for Pullback/NoiseCLR), demonstrating its superior ability to edit only the masked region while preserving the rest. On Transfer Success Rate, LOCO Edit (0.91) far exceeds NoiseCLR (0.66), Asyrp (0.58), ∂ε_t/∂x_t (0.24), and Pullback (0.14), while BlendedDiffusion cannot transfer at all. LOCO Edit is the only method combining one-step editing, localization, no supervision, and theoretical grounding; BlendedDiffusion achieves the best LPIPS (0.03) and SSIM (0.94) but fails to edit within masks in many cases and requires per-image optimization. In terms of efficiency, LOCO Edit needs only 1 image and 79 s of learning versus 100 images and 1 day for NoiseCLR or 475 s for Asyrp, while matching the 2 s transfer edit time of ∂ε_t/∂x_t.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the nullspace projection technique from GAN editing (Zhu et al., NeurIPS 2021) and the DDIM sampling/inversion framework (Song et al., ICLR 2021), but departs from prior diffusion-model editing work in a key way: instead of discovering global semantic directions in the h-space or noise space (Kwon et al. 2023, Park et al. 2023), it identifies that the posterior mean predictor (PMP) is locally linear with a low-rank Jacobian, enabling localized editing via SVD of the PMP's Jacobian. Unlike CLIP-guided methods (BlendedDiffusion, DiffEdit) that require text supervision and iterative optimization, or contrastive-learning approaches (NoiseCLR) that need 100 images and a day of training, LOCO Edit achieves localized editing in a single step from a single image with no supervision. The theoretical analysis under a mixture-of-low-rank-Gaussians assumption provides the first principled justification for why the Jacobian's singular vectors correspond to semantic editing directions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LOCO Edit is the first method to simultaneously achieve single-step, training-free, unsupervised, and localized editing in diffusion models, with editing directions that are linear, transferable, and composable—properties not previously demonstrated in the diffusion editing literature. The theoretical framework (Theorem 1) connects the low-rank structure to the intrinsic dimensionality of the data distribution, offering a geometric interpretation that could inform future work on representation learning in diffusion models. Open questions include extending the theory to T2I models with text conditioning, flow-matching objectives, and transformer architectures, as well as exploring coarse-to-fine editing across multiple timesteps and 3D applications. The gap between the mixture-of-Gaussians assumption and real data distributions remains a limitation, and the method's performance on very fine-grained edits (e.g., subtle color changes) is not thoroughly characterized.
