## Problem

Existing approaches to inductive reasoning with LLMs use batched hypothesis generation and refinement, which cannot support the online cycle of experimentation and belief updating that characterizes human active learning. Additionally, formal-language hypothesis spaces are often insufficiently expressive to capture the full range of human concepts, and it remains unclear whether humans reason with deterministic or fuzzy (probabilistic) rules when the ground truth is deterministic. This paper asks how to build a tractable, online probabilistic inference model over natural-language hypotheses that both outperforms recent LLM-based baselines and matches human behavior.

## Input / Output

The method consumes text-serialized scene descriptions (Zendo: blocks with color, size, orientation, groundedness, and touching attributes; ActiveACRE: objects with color, material, and shape) and binary rule-following feedback over 7 active-learning rounds. It produces a weighted pool of natural-language hypothesis strings (particles) with approximate posterior probabilities, binary predictions on held-out test scenes, and a single experiment proposal per round selected by expected information gain. Each hypothesis is optionally equipped with noise parameters (ε, δ) that convert a hard rule into a fuzzy one, with Gaussian priors truncated to [0.5, 1].

## Method

The core algorithm, LLM-SMC-S, is a variant of Sequential Monte Carlo Samplers in which the forward kernel is defined by an LLM that selectively revises only the lowest-likelihood particles while leaving high-likelihood ones unchanged, concentrating computation on improving bad hypotheses. The LLM serves as a proposal distribution for a Monte Carlo estimator, and a reverse kernel (uniform up to maximum string length) enables proper importance weighting, with correctness guaranteed by a proper-weighting proposition. For active learning, the LLM proposes candidate experiments that support and falsify each tracked hypothesis, and the best experiment is selected by maximizing expected KL-divergence information gain under the approximate posterior. A key empirical finding is that assuming fuzzy (probabilistic) rules with noise parameters, combined with online inference, matches human accuracy (R²=0.57 on fine-grained metrics) far better than hard deterministic rules (R²=0.10), even though hard rules achieve higher raw task accuracy.

## Results

On Zendo, the proposed Online, Hard model achieves 6.55±0.13/8 predictive posterior accuracy, outperforming all baselines including Batch w/ Refinement (6.18±0.14) and human participants (5.26/8). On ActiveACRE, Online, Hard achieves 0.92±0.03 predictive posterior, 0.87±0.04 ROC AUC, 0.98±0.01 F1, and 0.35±0.11 task solving rate—the last metric showing a 2.3× improvement over the best batch baseline (0.15). The Online, Fuzzy variant (5.35±0.09 on Zendo) closely matches human accuracy (5.26) and explains 57% of variance in fine-grained human accuracy patterns (R²=0.57), whereas hard rules drop to R²=0.10.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the ReAct-style Direct LLM baseline [31], the proposed Online, Hard model improves Zendo accuracy from 4.60 to 6.55 (+1.95) and ActiveACRE task solving from 0.00 to 0.35, while also substantially improving ROC AUC (0.60→0.87). Against the batched hypothesis-refinement approach of Qiu et al. [9] and Wang et al. [10], Online, Hard gains 0.37 on Zendo (6.55 vs 6.18) and more than doubles task solving on ActiveACRE (0.35 vs 0.15), demonstrating that online sequential inference is more effective than batched refinement for recovering the true rule. The Online, Hard model surpasses human performance on Zendo (6.55 vs 5.26), but the Online, Fuzzy variant (5.35) closely matches humans and provides a significantly better fit to human behavioral patterns (R²=0.57 vs R²=0.10 for hard rules; log-likelihood −1479 vs −5210 for the full human dataset). The paper also shows that replacing the information-gain criterion with LLM-only or random experiment selection degrades Zendo accuracy from 6.55 to 4.72 and 5.84 respectively, confirming that probabilistic experiment design is critical even when the LLM proposes candidate experiments.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper generalizes Sequential Monte Carlo Samplers (SMC-S) by using an LLM as the proposal distribution, enabling tractable online inference over natural-language hypotheses. It departs from the Bayesian cognitive science tradition (Bramley et al., Tenenbaum et al.) that models concept learning over handcrafted formal grammars, instead using natural language for expressivity and leveraging LLMs for approximate inference. The paper explicitly positions recent LLM inductive reasoning work (Wang et al., Qiu et al., Ellis) as 'batch inference' and contrasts it with the proposed online, sequential approach, while drawing on active learning literature for the information-gain-based experiment selection criterion.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key cognitive science finding—that humans appear to reason with fuzzy, probabilistic rules rather than deterministic ones, and that this is necessary to match human behavior even though hard rules yield higher accuracy—challenges the standard assumption in Bayesian cognitive models that learners converge on the true deterministic rule. The bounded-rationality result (inverted U-curve in human-model fit as a function of compute budget) provides a quantitative link between computational cost and humanlike performance. Open questions include whether the approach scales to richer hypothesis spaces involving unseen objects (abductive reasoning), whether it captures paradigm shifts rather than just 'normal science,' and whether the LLM's role as proposal distribution introduces biases that confound the cognitive modeling claims.
