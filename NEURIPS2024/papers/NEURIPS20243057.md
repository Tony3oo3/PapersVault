## Problem

Existing AQA datasets are restricted to mono-modal video from one-shot elite competitions, lacking pose data, fine-grained step-wise scoring, and progressive difficulty levels, which confines models to a narrow 'one-shot examination' setting. This prevents generalization to learners at varying skill levels and obscures the scoring process behind a single aggregate number. LucidAction addresses these gaps by introducing the first multi-view, multi-modal AQA dataset organized according to curriculum learning principles with detailed penalty-based annotations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243057/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes target-exemplar pairs of multi-view RGB video (8 synchronized views at 4K/60fps, 103 frames extracted at interval 5 and stacked as 20 clips of 8 frames) and/or 2D/3D pose sequences, with the exemplar's ground-truth score provided as a conditioning signal. The output is a relative quality score (predicted offset added to the exemplar's score) plus optional binary predictions for up to 5 penalty items per action, each carrying a severity level from {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0} that decomposes the final score into interpretable execution errors.

## Method

The dataset is structured in three tiers—8 gymnastics events, 4 curriculum levels per event, and 259 representative actions—captured via an 8-camera MoCap system (DJI Osmo Action 3 at 4K/60fps) with 3D poses reconstructed from multi-view RTMPose 2D estimates and annotated by professional gymnasts using a standardized penalty rubric. For benchmarking, the paper employs a contrastive regression framework (TPT as primary baseline) with a four-module pipeline: backbone (I3D for video, ST-GCN for pose) → temporal action decoder → pair encoder for target-exemplar interaction → score regressor. Ablations show that multi-view channel concatenation after the pair encoder yields the largest single-view gain (+6.6% ρ), dual-stream RGB+3D pose improves ρ by 6.4%, curriculum-ordered training consistently outperforms mixed-level training at equal data volume, and auxiliary penalty classification heads boost ρ by up to 5.7% and reduce R-ℓ₂ by 20%.

## Results

On the proposed LucidAction benchmark, the best contrastive regression baseline TPT achieves a Spearman's rank correlation (ρ) of 0.701 and relative L2 distance (R-ℓ2) of 0.624, substantially outperforming the direct regression baseline USDL (ρ=0.540, R-ℓ2=0.708) by 30% in ρ and 12% in R-ℓ2. Across the three established AQA datasets (AQA-7, MTL-AQA, FineDiving), TPT also leads all baselines, reaching ρ=0.872 on AQA-7, ρ=0.960 on MTL-AQA, and ρ=0.945 on FineDiving. The consistent gap between contrastive regression methods and direct regression across all four datasets confirms that pairwise comparison is critical for capturing subtle execution quality differences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates four baselines: USDL (direct regression), CoRe (group-aware contrastive regression), TSA (procedure-aware temporal segmentation), and TPT (temporal parsing transformer). TPT dominates all other methods on every dataset and both metrics, with the largest margin on LucidAction where it beats TSA by 5.8% in ρ (0.701 vs. 0.643) and by 0.066 in R-ℓ2 (0.624 vs. 0.690). CoRe, the earliest contrastive regression approach, trails TPT by 7.6% in ρ on LucidAction (0.625 vs. 0.701), showing that temporal parsing and pair-encoding design matter significantly. USDL, the only non-contrastive method, is the weakest on all four datasets, with a particularly large gap on LucidAction (ρ=0.540 vs. 0.701 for TPT), confirming that absolute score regression struggles with the multi-level, multi-event complexity of the new benchmark. The consistent ordering USDL < CoRe < TSA < TPT across all datasets suggests a clear progression from direct scoring to increasingly sophisticated pairwise temporal modeling.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the AQA dataset landscape by departing from the dominant paradigm of single-view, web-sourced, elite-competition footage (MIT Dive&Skate, AQA-7, FineDiving, etc.) toward a controlled multi-view MoCap setup with curriculum-structured difficulty levels. Conceptually, it repurposes the curriculum learning principle from Bengio et al. into data organization for AQA, introducing a three-tier hierarchy (Sport Event → Curriculum Level → Action) that no prior AQA dataset has attempted. Methodologically, it does not propose a new AQA architecture but instead benchmarks existing contrastive regression approaches (TPT, CoRe, TSA) and a direct regression baseline (USDL) to characterize the dataset's difficulty and to validate the utility of its multi-modal and fine-grained annotation design.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LucidAction addresses a critical bottleneck in AQA research: the absence of progressive, multi-level, multi-modal data that mirrors how humans actually learn and are assessed in sports. The penalty-based annotation scheme (up to 5 specific error items per action with graded severity) offers interpretability that prior datasets' single scalar scores lack, and the guaranteed availability of perfect exemplars per action category enables cleaner contrastive regression training. Key limitations include the controlled MoCap environment (limited generalizability to real-world lighting/backgrounds), gymnastics-specific scope, and residual subjectivity in expert scoring. Open questions include whether curriculum-structured training transfers to other sports or domains, and whether the multi-view advantage persists under realistic single-view deployment.
