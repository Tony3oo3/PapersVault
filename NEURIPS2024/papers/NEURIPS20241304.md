## Problem

Despite SSMs like Mamba achieving competitive language modeling performance, there was no fine-grained theoretical characterization of their expressive capacity relative to transformers and traditional RNNs. Existing results only placed SSMs within TC⁰ circuit complexity, leaving open which specific computational tasks (state tracking, counting, hierarchical structure) SSMs can or cannot handle, and how their strengths and limitations differ from those of attention-based architectures.

## Input / Output

The SSM consumes sequences of discrete symbols over a finite alphabet Σ, encoded as token embeddings e(σ) ∈ ℝᵈ, processed through L stacked layers of elementwise linear state updates. For predictive modeling, the output at each position is the set of possible next symbols (an element of 2^|Σ|), while for recognition it is a binary membership label. Critically, all results are stated at arbitrary input lengths with finite precision (bounded fractional bits), and the SSM must use a single fixed architecture per language rather than scaling with sequence length.

## Method

The paper defines a general SSM layer as h_t = A(x_t) ∘ h_{t-1} + B(x_t) followed by a Mix-Norm-Mix transformation, then characterizes expressiveness through three formal language families: finite-state automata, counters, and stacks. The key positive result (Theorem 4) shows that SSMs with nonnegative gates can predictively model exactly the star-free regular languages, proven by translating the Krohn-Rhodes cascade product decomposition of set-reset automata into stacked SSM layers. The key negative result (Theorem 2) proves that nonnegative gates prevent recognition of PARITY (and hence all non-star-free regular languages) because activations on repeated inputs converge, making modular counting impossible. For counter languages and bounded-depth Dyck languages, SSMs implement unbounded counting via A≡1 with input-dependent B, and model hierarchical structure with O(h log K) dimensions without simulating a stack. All positive results are verified empirically on Mamba, confirming learnability and length generalization on Flip Flop, 27 formal languages, and Dyck(K,h).

## Results

On the FlipFlop task, a one-layer Mamba model converges to 0 test error in both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings after ~1400 training steps, whereas transformers in Liu et al. (2023a) continued making occasional mistakes after 10K steps. On the 27-language test suite, Mamba achieves perfect accuracy on all 11 star-free languages with full length generalization (in-distribution and out-of-distribution), but performs poorly on non-star-free languages (e.g., PARITY), consistent with Theorem 4. On counter languages (Theorem 5), Mamba reaches perfect in-distribution accuracy but shows weaker out-of-distribution length generalization compared to transformers. On Dyck(K=8, h=10), a two-layer Mamba achieves essentially perfect predictive modeling on the out-of-distribution test set (string lengths 700–1400), matching the strong performance reported for transformers and LSTMs by Yao et al. (2021b).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the transformer results of Bhattamishra et al. (2020) on 27 formal languages, Mamba clearly wins on star-free languages, achieving perfect accuracy with full length generalization where transformers show mixed results and often fail to generalize even on star-free tasks. On non-star-free languages (e.g., PARITY), Mamba loses to transformers, as predicted by Theorem 2 (NONNEGATIVE gate constraint). On counter languages, Mamba matches transformers on in-distribution accuracy but lags in out-of-distribution length generalization, likely because the SSM construction uses unbounded state values. On the FlipFlop task, Mamba decisively outperforms the transformer baseline from Liu et al. (2023a), reaching 0 error in ~1400 steps versus persistent errors at 10K steps. On Dyck(K,h), Mamba with 2 layers matches the strong performance previously reported for both transformers and LSTMs by Yao et al. (2021b), confirming that stack-free SSM constructions can achieve the same optimal memory scaling (O(h log K) units).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the well-established program of characterizing neural sequence model expressiveness through formal languages—previously applied to transformers (Bhattamishra et al. 2020, Hahn 2020, Liu et al. 2023b) and RNNs (Weiss et al. 2018, Hewitt et al. 2020)—to the emerging class of state space models. It builds on the Krohn-Rhodes decomposition theorem from automata theory to translate cascade products of set-reset automata into stacked SSM layers, and on Mamba (Gu and Dao 2023) as the concrete SSM instantiation. Unlike prior coarse-grained SSM results (Merrill et al. 2024 showing TC0 containment; Jelassi et al. 2024 showing copying limitations), this work provides fine-grained separations within TC0, identifying exactly which regular languages SSMs can and cannot model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first decidable characterization of the regular languages that modern SSMs (e.g., Mamba) can model—exactly the star-free languages—offering a concrete theoretical tool for predicting SSM capabilities and failures in language modeling. The finding that the nonnegative gate, a design choice motivated by optimization stability, provably limits expressiveness (ruling out PARITY and all modular counting) has direct implications for future SSM architecture design, potentially motivating complex-valued or sign-symmetric gates. Open questions include whether SSM-based LLMs actually learn the stack-free hierarchical constructions identified here, whether hybrid SSM-attention architectures are necessary to cover both star-free state tracking and modular counting, and whether these formal-language results transfer to realistic language modeling tasks.
