## Problem

Kernel regression methods (Nadaraya-Watson and Kernel Ridge Regression) suffer from high computational costs: NW requires O(n) time per test point and KRR requires O(n³) training time. Existing coreset approaches either rely on projection-based compression whose accuracy degrades with the target error rate, or require super-linear preprocessing. The paper asks whether distribution compression via kernel thinning can accelerate both training and inference of kernel regression while matching or exceeding the statistical efficiency of i.i.d. subsampling.

## Input / Output

The method consumes n i.i.d. pairs (x_i, y_i) with x_i ∈ X ⊂ R^d, a base reproducing kernel k over X, and a hyperparameter (bandwidth h for NW or regularization λ' for KRR). It outputs a coreset S_out ⊂ S_in of exactly √n points (a hard subset, not a weighted set) and a regression estimate f̂(x) evaluated at any query point. The key structural detail is that the thinning operates on the joint space X×Y using a meta-kernel that couples the base kernel with the response values, so the coreset selection is informed by both features and labels simultaneously.

## Method

The core contribution is constructing two meta-kernels defined over X×Y so that all function averages needed by the regression estimator lie in a single RKHS, enabling kernel thinning to control their approximation error simultaneously. For NW, the meta-kernel is k_NW((x₁,y₁),(x₂,y₂)) = k(x₁,x₂) + k(x₁,x₂)·y₁y₂, ensuring both the numerator (weighted sum of y·k) and denominator (sum of k) are in H(k_NW). For KRR, the meta-kernel is k_RR((x₁,y₁),(x₂,y₂)) = k²(x₁,x₂) + k(x₁,x₂)·y₁y₂, ensuring the loss components f² and f·y are in H(k_RR). KT-COMPRESS++ is then run with the meta-kernel to produce a √n-point coreset in O(n log³n) time, after which the standard NW or KRR closed-form solution is applied to the coreset. This yields O(√n) inference time, O(n log³n) training for KT-NW and O(n^{3/2}) for KT-KRR, with MSE rates strictly better than i.i.d. subsampling (n^{-β/(β+d)} for NW vs. n^{-β/(2β+d)} for ST-NW).

## Results

On the California Housing dataset (d=8, N=20,000), KT-KRR achieves a test MSE of 0.5580% with a training time of 0.0153s and prediction time of 0.0083s, compared to Full-KRR's 0.4137% MSE at 11.1s training. On the large-scale SUSY dataset (d=18, N=5,000,000), KT-KRR achieves 22.00% test classification error with 1.79s training time, outperforming ST-KRR (22.71%) while remaining competitive with RPCholesky preconditioning (19.99% at 3.46s) and FALKON (19.99% at 5.06s). The theoretical guarantees show KT-NW achieves MSE rate n^{-β/(β+d)} with O(n log³n) training and O(√n) inference, while KT-KRR achieves near-minimax O(m log n / n) rate in Õ(n^{3/2}) time for finite-rank kernels.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against RPCHOLESKY (Chen et al., 2022), KT-KRR trades accuracy for speed: on California Housing, RPCHOLESKY achieves 0.3503% MSE versus KT-KRR's 0.5580%, but KT-KRR trains 21× faster (0.0153s vs 0.3237s) due to its O(n log³n) compression versus RPCHOLESKY's O(n²) pivot selection. On the SUSY dataset, KT-KRR (22.00% error, 1.79s) loses to both RPCholesky preconditioning (Díaz et al., 2023; 19.99%, 3.46s) and FALKON (Rudi et al., 2017; 19.99%, 5.06s) in test error, but trains roughly 2× faster than RPCholesky and 3× faster than FALKON. KT-KRR consistently outperforms the i.i.d. subsampling baseline (ST-KRR) on both datasets: 0.5580% vs 0.5736% MSE on California Housing and 22.00% vs 22.71% error on SUSY, confirming the theoretical advantage of kernel-aware thinning over uniform subsampling. The paper notes that RPCHOLESKY, while providing good low-rank kernel matrix approximation, is not designed to preserve function averages, explaining its accuracy advantage over KT-KRR in some settings.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the kernel thinning (KT) framework of Dwivedi and Mackey [10] and its near-linear-time variant KT-COMPRESS++ [23]—originally designed for unsupervised tasks like Monte Carlo integration and hypothesis testing—to the supervised regression setting. The key conceptual departure from prior coreset methods for regression (Boutsidis et al., Zheng and Phillips, Phillips) is that those rely on projection-based compression (Johnson-Lindenstrauss style) whose accuracy degrades with the target error rate, whereas KT provides worst-case distributional approximation guarantees. The paper also contrasts with Nyström subsampling and RPCholesky approaches that exploit kernel matrix structure for low-rank approximation, noting that RPCholesky is not designed to preserve function averages, which explains its different error profile.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically grounded recipe for accelerating kernel methods in supervised learning: construct a meta-kernel that encodes both the input geometry and the response dependence, then apply KT-COMPRESS++ to obtain a coreset of size √n. This yields a quadratic speedup for KRR (from O(n³) to Õ(n^{3/2})) while matching near-minimax statistical rates, and a strictly better MSE rate than i.i.d. subsampling for NW at the same O(√n) query time. Open questions include obtaining sharper error rates for infinite-dimensional kernels (where KT-KRR does not provably beat standard thinning), combining the framework with learned/parameterized kernels (e.g., Recursive Feature Machines), and whether the meta-kernel construction generalizes beyond NW and KRR to other kernel-based supervised estimators.
