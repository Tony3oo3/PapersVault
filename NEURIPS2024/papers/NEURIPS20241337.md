## Problem

Implicit neural representations (INRs) are trained on a single signal, so their learned features are finely tuned to that one signal's morphology and cannot transfer to represent a second, even similar, signal. Unlike CNNs and Transformers, INRs lack a transfer learning mechanism, limiting their ability to encode data-distribution priors for tasks like inverse imaging. The paper asks whether the initial layers of an INR can be made transferable across a distribution of similar signals.

## Input / Output

The method takes 2D pixel coordinates in [-1, 1] (or 3D coordinates for occupancy) as input to a 6-layer SIREN MLP with sinusoidal activations, producing RGB pixel values (or occupancy values) as output. For the main experiments, images are 178×178; high-resolution Kodak images go up to 512×768. At test time, the trained shared encoder weights initialize a new INR whose decoder is randomly initialized, and the full network is then fine-tuned on the target signal. For inverse problems (super-resolution, denoising), the input is a corrupted or downsampled image and the output is the recovered latent image.

## Method

STRAINER splits a SIREN INR into a shared encoder (first K layers, typically 5 of 6) and signal-specific decoders (remaining layers). During training, the encoder is shared across N INRs (N=1 or 10) each fitting a different image from the same domain, with the total L2 loss summed over all N signals; the encoder learns a common input-space partition attuned to low-frequency structure shared across the domain. At test time, the trained encoder weights initialize a new INR for an unseen signal, paired with a randomly initialized decoder, which is then optimized as a standard INR. The key insight, grounded in SplineCAM partition analysis, is that initial INR layers capture coarse, less-partitioned geometry (shared across similar signals) while deeper layers encode fine, image-specific detail—making the initial layers the natural locus for transferability. This requires only 1–10 training images and ~5000 gradient steps (~25 s on an A100), versus 14,000+ images and ~1 day for transformer-based meta-learning baselines.

## Results

On in-domain image fitting, STRAINER-10 (shared encoder trained on 10 images) achieves 57.80±3.46 PSNR on CelebA-HQ, 58.06±3.75 on AFHQ, and 62.80±3.17 on OASIS-MRI, representing a ≈10–13 dB gain over vanilla SIREN with random initialization (44.91/45.11/53.03 PSNR). STRAINER-10 also outperforms Meta-learned 5K on CelebA-HQ (+4.7 dB) and AFHQ (+4.8 dB), though it trails on OASIS-MRI (62.80 vs. 67.02 PSNR). Training the shared encoder requires only 24.54 seconds on an A100 GPU, compared to 23.8 minutes for Meta-learned 5K and ~1 day for TransINR/IPC.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla SIREN (random initialization), STRAINER-10 wins decisively by ~13 dB on CelebA-HQ (57.80 vs. 44.91 PSNR) and ~13 dB on AFHQ (58.06 vs. 45.11), confirming that shared encoder features provide a far stronger starting point than random weights. Compared to SIREN fine-tuned (initialized from another same-domain image), STRAINER-10 still leads by ~7 dB on CelebA-HQ and ~5 dB on AFHQ, showing that explicit layer-sharing captures more transferable structure than naive weight reuse. Against Meta-learned 5K (MAML-based), STRAINER-10 wins on CelebA-HQ (+4.7 dB) and AFHQ (+4.8 dB) but loses on OASIS-MRI (62.80 vs. 67.02 PSNR), suggesting meta-learning retains an edge on single-channel medical data. Against the transformer-based TransINR and IPC, STRAINER-10 outperforms both on all three in-domain datasets (e.g., 57.80 vs. 51.86 for TransINR and 49.72 for IPC on CelebA-HQ) while requiring orders of magnitude fewer training images (10 vs. 14,000) and dramatically less compute (24.5 s vs. ~1 day). In inverse problems, STRAINER-10 recovers super-resolved images ~3× faster than SIREN (1102 vs. 3329 iterations) at comparable PSNR, and achieves denoising in 76 vs. 203 iterations.

## Relation to Prior Work

STRAINER builds directly on the SIREN architecture (Sitzmann et al., NeurIPS 2020) and departs from the dominant meta-learning paradigm for INR initialization (MAML-based Meta-learned 5K, Transformer-hypernetwork approaches like TransINR and IPC) by proposing a far simpler mechanism: explicitly sharing initial encoder layers across multiple INRs being fit to different signals simultaneously. The motivation draws on SplineCAM's geometric analysis of INR partitioning, which reveals that early layers capture coarse, domain-general structure while deeper layers encode signal-specific detail. This positions STRAINER as an architectural alternative to hypernetwork-based initialization, requiring only 1–10 training images versus 14,000 for Transformer-based baselines, and is conceptually closer to the shared-trunk design in Siamese SIREN but applied across independent signals rather than within a single signal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

STRAINER provides a data-efficient (10 images), computationally cheap (20× faster than Meta-learned 5K), and architecturally simple way to encode distributional priors into INR initialization, which could make INRs more practical for low-resource settings like patient-specific medical imaging or real-time applications. The surprising out-of-domain transfer (face-trained encoder improving cat and MRI reconstruction) suggests the learned partition geometry captures broadly useful spatial structure, though the paper acknowledges it remains unclear what features are actually transferred and how they compare to CNN/Transformer transfer learning. Open questions include whether the approach extends to video, 3D radiance fields, or other modalities beyond the occupancy fitting demo, and whether the occasional PSNR instability during test-time optimization can be systematically addressed.
