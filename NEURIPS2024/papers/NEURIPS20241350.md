## Problem

Deep learning-based molecular generative methods, including GFlowNets, suffer from poor sample efficiency because the reward oracle (e.g., docking simulations) is computationally expensive while the chemical space is combinatorially large. The PMO benchmark revealed that classical genetic algorithms, which encode domain-specific chemical knowledge in their operators, often outperform recent deep learning methods under limited oracle budgets. The paper addresses how to distill the domain knowledge embedded in genetic algorithms into a deep generative policy while retaining the amortized, proportional-to-reward sampling capability of GFlowNets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a scalar-valued score function (oracle) — ranging from ML-based property predictors (GSK3β, JNK3) to expensive docking scores (QuickVina 2) — and a GRU-based RNN policy pretrained on ZINC 250K SMILES. It produces molecules as SMILES strings (or SELFIES), with the number of oracle calls strictly limited (10K in PMO, 100–1000 steps for SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor design). A key design detail is that the genetic search operates on molecular graphs (ring-aware crossover, seven mutation types via SMARTS patterns) while the generative policy operates on 1D SMILES sequences, creating a representation gap that the off-policy GFlowNet training bridges.

## Method

Genetic GFN alternates between two generation strategies — SMILES sampling from the current policy and graph-based genetic search (Graph GA crossover + mutation) — and fine-tunes the policy via trajectory balance loss with a rank-based reweighted replay buffer. The genetic search serves as a domain-specialized off-policy exploration that produces samples with large SMILES-space distances from the current policy, enabling the GFlowNet to escape local modes that MLE-based imitation (as in GEGL) cannot. A KL-divergence penalty against the pretrained policy regularizes generation toward the compact valid-molecule space, and the rank-based reweighting (Eq. 4) biases replay toward high-reward trajectories. The inverse temperature β and weight-shifting factor k provide controllable score-diversity trade-offs, and the GFlowNet policy periodically resets the GA population by sampling diverse individuals proportional to rewards, mitigating premature convergence.

## Results

On the Practical Molecular Optimization (PMO) benchmark with 10K oracle calls, Genetic GFN achieves a total AUC Top-10 of 16.213 across 23 score functions, attaining the highest score in 14 out of 23 oracles. It outperforms the strongest GA baseline Mol GA (15.686) by 0.527 and the RL baseline SMILES REINVENT (15.185) by 1.028, with statistically significant margins (p < 0.01). In the SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor design task, Genetic GFN reaches Top-100 average scores of 0.891 (PLPro) and 0.873 (RdRp) with only 100 training steps—10× fewer than competing methods—while also achieving higher diversity than Mol GA and REINVENT.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Genetic GFN outperforms all 14 baselines in total AUC Top-10 on the PMO benchmark, winning 14 of 23 individual oracles. Against the strongest GA baseline Mol GA (15.686), it gains +0.527 total, with the largest per-oracle margins on #3 (+0.173), #19 (+0.163), and #23 (+0.038), while Mol GA wins on #7, #10, #13, #15, #17, and #19. Against SMILES REINVENT (15.185), the gap is +1.028, with particularly large advantages on #3 (+0.085), #19 (+0.164), and #21 (+0.053). The fragment-based GFlowNet baselines (Fragment GFN: 9.929, Fragment GFN-AL: 9.917) are substantially outperformed by 6.3 points, highlighting the advantage of SMILES-based string generation over fragment-based graph generation for sample-efficient optimization. Among GFlowNet variants (Table 3a), Genetic GFN (16.213) beats LS-GFN (15.230) by ~1.0 AUC Top-10, attributed to the domain-specialized genetic search providing larger effective search distances than local search. In the SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor design (Table 5), Genetic GFN at 100 steps (0.891/0.873) surpasses MolRL-MGPT at 1000 steps (0.772/0.854) by 0.119/0.019, demonstrating 10× sample efficiency. In multi-objective optimization (Table 4), Genetic GFN achieves hypervolumes of 0.718 (GSK3β+JNK3) and 0.642 (GSK3β+JNK3+QED+SA), outperforming HN-GFN (0.669/0.416) and all other baselines.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the GEGL framework (Ahn et al., 2020), which distills genetic algorithm samples into a deep generative policy via maximum likelihood estimation, by replacing MLE with GFlowNet trajectory balance training. The off-policy nature of GFlowNets (Bengio et al., 2023; Malkin et al., 2022) is the key enabler: it allows the policy to learn a reward-proportional distribution from GA-generated samples that are off-distribution, rather than merely imitating them. The paper also directly responds to the PMO benchmark finding (Gao et al., 2022) that classical genetic algorithms outperform deep learning methods under sample-efficient settings, by showing that a GA-guided GFlowNet can surpass both pure GA and pure deep learning baselines. The RNN-based SMILES generation architecture follows REINVENT (Olivecrona et al., 2017), and the genetic operations are adopted from Graph GA (Jensen, 2019).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 10× reduction in reward calls for SARS-CoV-2 inhibitor design (100 vs. 1000 steps) has direct practical implications for drug discovery, where docking evaluations are the dominant computational bottleneck. The paper demonstrates that the score-diversity trade-off is controllable via inverse temperature and rank-based sampling parameters, which is important for generating viable candidate sets rather than a single overfitted molecule. A key limitation is the assumption that effective domain-specific GA operators exist; extending this to domains without well-designed genetic operators (e.g., combinatorial optimization) remains open. The KL-divergence penalty, while included, was not statistically significant in ablations (p=0.136), suggesting the core contribution is the GA-GFlowNet synergy rather than the regularization.
