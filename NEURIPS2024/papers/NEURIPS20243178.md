## Problem

Existing LLM-based tabular data synthesis methods (GReaT, TabuLa, REaLTabFormer) rely on continued pre-training, which forces the model to memorize original data and creates a vulnerability where an attacker knowing one or two feature values can reconstruct entire records. No prior method leverages LLMs for tabular synthesis while structurally minimizing this leakage risk, and existing privacy metrics (DCR, NRS) measure only row-to-row distance without capturing the generator's own memorization behavior.

## Input / Output

The method consumes an original tabular dataset (mixed numerical and categorical columns) and, at inference, takes k randomly resampled rows (default k=5) formatted as JSON dictionaries with randomly permuted feature order as the prompt. The output is a single synthetic row in the same JSON format, which is then parsed back into a dataframe via pattern matching. A key design constraint is that the prompt rows at generation time must differ from those used during fine-tuning to prevent verbatim reproduction.

## Method

HARMONIC replaces continued pre-training with instruction fine-tuning on kNN-constructed datasets: for each row, its k nearest neighbors (default k=5) are concatenated as input and the target row as output, with a filtering step discarding sets where more than half the neighbors disagree on the label. Feature order is randomly permuted within each JSON row to eliminate pseudo-positional memorization, and the loss is computed only on the output tokens so the model learns inter-row structural relationships rather than memorizing input rows. At generation, temperature sampling (T=0.7) over the fine-tuned LLaMA-2-7b-chat (via LoRA) produces diverse synthetic rows. The framework also introduces two evaluation metrics: DLT, which measures the generator's perplexity gap between original and synthetic data to quantify leakage, and LLE, which fine-tunes a downstream LLM classifier on synthetic data and evaluates weighted F1 on the original test set.

## Results

On the privacy metrics, HARMONIC achieves the best DCR on 3 of 4 classification datasets (GM: 8.08, AD: 2.47, BU: 2.52) and the best DLT (closest to zero) on 3 of 4 datasets (GM: −0.16, DI: −0.37, BU: −0.34), substantially outperforming LLM-based baselines RTF (DLT as low as −163.71) and GReaT. On effectiveness, HARMONIC achieves comparable MLE and LLE scores to state-of-the-art methods, with an average performance drop of less than 5% relative to the original training data. On the regression Abalone dataset, HARMONIC also maintains strong privacy (DCR 0.18, DLT −0.38) while achieving competitive LLE (0.22) and MLE (0.24) scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against non-LLM baselines (SMOTE, TVAE, CTAB-GAN, TabDDPM, TABSYN), HARMONIC achieves the highest DCR on GM (8.08 vs. 2.21–5.36), AD (2.47 vs. 0.16–0.86), and BU (2.52 vs. 0.15–1.38), though TABSYN and GReaT outperform it on DI DCR (1.37/1.36 vs. 0.44). Against the LLM-based baselines, HARMONIC's DLT is dramatically better than RTF across all datasets (e.g., −0.16 vs. −22.04 on GM; −0.98 vs. −163.71 on AD), confirming that continued pre-training causes severe data memorization. Compared to GReaT, HARMONIC wins on DCR for GM (8.08 vs. 5.84) and AD (2.47 vs. 1.51) and on DLT for GM (−0.16 vs. −2.14), DI (−0.37 vs. −0.44), and BU (−0.34 vs. −2.22), though GReaT edges out HARMONIC on AD DLT (−0.67 vs. −0.98) and BU DCR (8.30 vs. 2.52). Against PrivBayes (differential privacy) on the Diabetes dataset, HARMONIC trades a lower DCR (0.44 vs. 0.82) for substantially better utility (LLE 0.75 vs. 0.20) and distributional fidelity (CS 0.95 vs. 0.88). On the regression Abalone task, HARMONIC outperforms both RTF and GReaT on DLT (−0.38 vs. −68.99 and −0.71) and DCR (0.18 vs. 0.11 and 0.15), while maintaining competitive effectiveness.

## Relation to Prior Work

HARMONIC departs from prior LLM-based tabular synthesis methods (GReaT, TabuLa, REaLTabFormer) that rely on continued pre-training over row-by-row text conversions, instead using instruction fine-tuning with kNN-constructed neighbor groups to teach the LLM inter-row relationships without memorizing individual records. The kNN algorithm (Cover & Hart, 1967) is repurposed here not as a classifier but as a data-structuring tool for building the fine-tuning corpus. On the evaluation side, the paper extends beyond traditional distance-based privacy metrics (DCR, NRS) and MLE-based effectiveness benchmarks by introducing DLT (perplexity-based generator leakage) and LLE (downstream LLM task performance), addressing gaps the authors identify in how LLM-specific leakage and utility are measured.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DLT metric exposes a stark privacy gap: continued-pre-training LLM generators (RTF) show DLT values as low as -163.71, while HARMONIC's fine-tuning approach stays near -1, suggesting pre-training-based synthesis may be far more vulnerable to data extraction attacks than previously quantified. The LLE metric highlights that synthetic data effective for weak classifiers (MLE) may not transfer well to LLM-based downstream tasks, a practical concern as LLMs increasingly replace traditional ML in tabular applications. Open questions include whether the approach extends to regression and high-cardinality settings, whether it composes with differential privacy for stronger guarantees, and whether the kNN-based instruction construction generalizes to larger or relational tables.
