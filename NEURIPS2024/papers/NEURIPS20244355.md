## Problem

Fairness metrics in FairML are routinely evaluated on real-world data plagued by measurement biases—proxy label noise, selection bias, and extra-classificatory policy effects—that can render parity assessments vacuous. No unified framework existed to quantify how sensitive a given fairness metric evaluation is to these biases, particularly in the oblivious setting where auditors lack access to model covariates. The paper demonstrates that even minor, realistic violations of underlying assumptions can widen sensitivity bounds to the point where the original fairness evaluation becomes uninformative.

## Input / Output

The framework operates in the oblivious setting, consuming only the protected attribute A, true outcome Y, and prediction Ŷ (all discrete), along with a user-specified causal DAG encoding the bias structure, a scalar sensitivity parameter (e.g., P(Y_P ≠ Y) for proxy bias or P(S=0) for selection bias), and optional domain-specific probabilistic constraints. It outputs lower and upper bounds on the target fairness metric (e.g., FPR parity, equalized odds, demographic parity) under the specified degree of bias, solved via branch-and-bound optimization. A key constraint is that the metric must be expressible as a polynomial in factual and counterfactual probabilities, and all variables must be discrete for the function-response representation to apply.

## Method

The core contribution is a unified causal sensitivity analysis framework that adapts the autobounds discrete causal inference machinery to the FairML oblivious setting. Each measurement bias is encoded as a directed acyclic graph over (A, Y, Ŷ) plus bias-specific variables; unobserved covariates X are marginalized out via latent projection, preserving causal structure over observed variables with a bounded number of latent nodes. The partial identification problem—bounding a query Q(C) over the set of SCMs consistent with the observed distribution and sensitivity constraint—is then converted into a polynomial optimization over the probability simplex Δ^k using the function response framework, where each SCM corresponds to a point in the simplex. This is solved via a branch-and-bound solver that guarantees valid bounds even without full convergence. The framework's key flexibility is that multiple biases can be combined simultaneously (yielding non-linear sensitivity), and arbitrary domain-specific assumptions can be encoded as additional polynomial constraints, enabling practitioners to compare how different assumption sets affect the informativeness of a fairness evaluation.

## Results

Across 14 canonical FairML benchmark datasets, 100% exhibit at least one of the three measurement biases (proxy, selection, ECP), 92% exhibit two, and 61% exhibit all three simultaneously. The cross-dataset sensitivity analysis (Fig. 5) shows that even small bias levels (e.g., 5% proxy label error) can render the upper bound of equalized odds at its maximum possible value, making the parity evaluation vacuous. Demographic parity is the most robust metric to all three biases, while positive/negative predictive parity are the most fragile, with equalized odds intermediate due to its max-of-FPR/FNR construction. The automated computational bounds exactly match the algebraically derived bounds of Fogliato et al. [27] under identical assumptions, validating the framework.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against the algebraic sensitivity bounds of Fogliato et al. [27] for proxy label bias on the COMPAS dataset. The automated causal sensitivity analysis framework exactly reproduces Fogliato et al.'s bounds for FPR, FNR, and PPV under the assumption P(Y_P=1|Y=0)=0, confirming correctness. The key extension is flexibility: by switching to P(Y_P=0|Y=1)=0 or by dropping the X→Y_P edge in the causal graph, the framework yields qualitatively different (and tighter) bounds without requiring new algebraic derivations. Specifically, dropping the X→Y_P edge fully identifies FNR (Proposition 2) under the first assumption and FPR (Proposition 3) under the second, for all levels of proxy bias. The paper does not claim to 'beat' Fogliato et al. on a performance metric; rather, it subsumes their results as a special case while demonstrating that the choice of causal assumptions materially changes the informativeness of fairness evaluations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies and extends the line of FairML sensitivity analysis that previously addressed individual biases in isolation: Fogliato et al. derived algebraic bounds for proxy label bias, Kallus and Zhou studied selection bias effects on equalized odds, and Coston et al. formalized counterfactual risk assessments for extra-classificatory policies. The key methodological departure is leveraging the autobounds framework of Duarte et al. [21] and the function response framework of Balke and Pearl [4] to convert partial identification problems into tractable polynomial optimization over the probability simplex, enabling a single computational pipeline to handle any combination of biases and metrics in the oblivious setting. Unlike prior algebraic approaches that require hand-derived bounds per bias-metric pair, this framework allows practitioners to encode arbitrary domain-specific probabilistic assumptions and immediately obtain valid bounds via a branch-and-bound solver.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The empirical finding that 100% of realistic FairML benchmark datasets exhibit at least one measurement bias (and 61% exhibit all three simultaneously) reframes measurement bias from an edge case to a central concern in fairness auditing. The demonstration that even 5% proxy label noise or 5% selection can render parity evaluations vacuously wide challenges the practical utility of standard fairness certifications and motivates the need for sensitivity analysis as a routine part of ML auditing. Key limitations include restriction to the discrete, oblivious setting (no access to covariates), inability to model interference between individuals, and the fact that the causal graphs used are context-specific and may not generalize. Open questions include extending the framework to continuous variables, handling more general (non-binary) ECPs, and developing training procedures that are robust to the identified biases rather than merely diagnosing them.
