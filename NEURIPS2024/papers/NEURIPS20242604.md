## Problem

Existing work on algorithmic monoculture has been limited to small markets with only two decision-makers and has not incorporated the two-sided competition and fixed capacity constraints that characterize real domains like hiring and college admissions. This paper asks how monoculture (all firms use the same evaluation algorithm) versus polyculture (firms use independent algorithms) affects stable matching outcomes when many applicants and firms jointly interact through a market mechanism.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242604/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a continuum of applicants with true values drawn from a probability measure η, a noise distribution D, m homogeneous firms with total capacity S < 1, and uniformly random applicant preference orderings. The critical structural distinction is that under monoculture each applicant receives a single shared noisy estimate v+X across all firms, while under polyculture each firm independently draws v+X_f. The output is a stable matching characterized by a single shared cutoff P (identical across all firms by symmetry), from which per-applicant matching probabilities, firm welfare (average true value of matched applicants), and applicant welfare (average rank of the matched firm) are derived.

## Method

The paper builds on the Azevedo-Leshno continuum model of stable matching, where stable matchings are in one-to-one correspondence with market-clearing cutoffs, and instantiates monoculture and polyculture by specifying how applicant estimated values are drawn. The core analytical insight is that the monoculture/polyculture distinction reduces to comparing a single noisy draw X against the maximum order statistic X^(m) of m i.i.d. draws, since an applicant matches under monoculture iff v+X exceeds the shared cutoff and under polyculture iff v+max(X_1,...,X_m) exceeds it. For maximum-concentrating noise (lighter-than-exponential tails), this yields a 'wisdom of the crowds' result: as m→∞, polyculture matching probability converges to a step function at the capacity threshold v_S, while monoculture matching probability remains constant and suboptimal. The model is further extended to differential application access (varying numbers of applications per applicant), where monoculture is shown to be more robust, and all theoretical predictions are validated computationally using logistic regression models trained on the ACSIncome dataset.

## Results

In ML-based experiments on ACSIncome (Texas 2018, 13,593 applicants, 5 firms × capacity 1000, 45 logistic regression models), polyculture outperforms monoculture in accuracy for 47/50 random subsets of 5 models, while monoculture produces better average applicant rank (closer to top choice) for 50/50 subsets. On California 2018 data (19,567 applicants), the polyculture accuracy advantage is weaker (19/50 subsets beat all monoculture markets; 44/50 beat the monoculture average), but monoculture still dominates on applicant rank for 50/50 subsets. For differential application access, the average absolute change in market accuracy is 1.54% under polyculture vs. 0.056% under monoculture (Texas), and 0.58% vs. 0.061% (California), confirming monoculture's greater robustness.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between monoculture (all firms use the same evaluation algorithm) and polyculture (firms use independent, idiosyncratic evaluations). On firm welfare (accuracy of matched applicants), polyculture consistently wins: in the Texas experiment, 47/50 polyculture markets are more accurate than every monoculture market in the same subset, and in California, 44/50 polyculture markets outperform the average monoculture market. On applicant welfare (average rank of matched firm), monoculture wins decisively: 50/50 subsets show monoculture producing better (lower) average applicant rank, with matched applicants almost always receiving their top choice under monoculture. On robustness to differential application access, monoculture is substantially more stable (0.056% vs. 1.54% average accuracy change in Texas; 0.061% vs. 0.58% in California). The theoretical results (Theorems 1–3) explain these patterns: polyculture's 'wisdom of the crowds' effect concentrates matches on highest-value applicants as the number of firms grows, while monoculture's shared score ensures all matched applicants get their top choice but leaves high-value applicants at risk of a single bad noise draw.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the algorithmic monoculture analysis of Kleinberg and Raghavan (2021) from two decision-makers to a large two-sided matching market, using the Azevedo-Leshno continuum framework to obtain a tractable cutoff characterization of stable matchings. It also draws on the school choice tie-breaking literature (single vs. multiple lottery numbers), where increased correlation in decision-maker preferences is known to increase top-choice matches, and on Castera et al.'s analysis of statistical discrimination in stable matching. The key departure from prior monoculture work is the incorporation of market-level competition and two-sided preferences, which prior ML-community analyses (Kleinberg-Raghavan, Bommasani et al.) did not model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reconciles a tension between the monoculture literature (which emphasizes harms) and the matching markets literature (which finds correlation in evaluation benefits applicants), showing that both can be true simultaneously: monoculture hurts firm welfare but helps applicant welfare on average. This reframing has direct implications for policy debates around shared hiring algorithms and college admissions systems. Key limitations include the homogeneous-firm assumption and the maximum-concentrating noise requirement (long-tailed noise reverses Theorem 1, as shown in follow-up work), and the model does not address biases across demographic groups or search frictions. Open questions include extending to heterogeneous firms, endogenous algorithm adoption, and settings where noise is correlated across applicants.
