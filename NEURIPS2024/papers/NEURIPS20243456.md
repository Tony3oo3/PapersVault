## Problem

RLHF-based safety alignment of LLMs relies on human preference data that is costly to collect, difficult to specify precisely (leading to over-refusal or undesirable response styles), and expensive to update as safety guidelines evolve. Existing AI-feedback methods like Constitutional AI use general principles that leave too much discretion to the model, while human-feedback rule-based methods like Sparrow still require large-scale human annotation. The paper addresses how to achieve fine-grained, composable control over safety behavior with minimal human data and direct incorporation of behavioral rules into the RL reward signal.

## Input / Output

The RBR takes a prompt-completion pair and computes a set of proposition probabilities (e.g., 'contains apology', 'is judgmental', 'contains disallowed content') via a grader LLM using few-shot classification prompts that output only 'yes' or 'no' tokens, from which logit-based probabilities are extracted. These probabilities serve as features for a linear reward function whose output is added to the helpful-only RM score to form the total PPO reward. The final trained model produces safety-aligned text completions that balance refusing unsafe requests (with correct style) while complying with safe and boundary-case prompts.

## Method

RBRs decompose a content and behavior policy into binary propositions (e.g., 'refuses', 'judgmental', 'complies') and rules specifying desired/undesired combinations for each response type (hard refusal, soft refusal, comply). A grader LLM scores each proposition via few-shot classification prompts, and the resulting probabilities are fed into a linear model whose weights are fit on synthetically generated comparison data using a hinge loss that enforces the policy-defined ranking of completions. The fitted RBR is added directly to the helpful-only RM score during PPO training, bypassing the distillation of rules into RM preference data. Only 518 human-labeled completions are needed to tune the classification prompts (Gold set), while all weight-fitting data is synthetically generated. This direct incorporation avoids the information loss and imbalance issues that arise when distilling behavioral rules into a reward model trained on comparison data.

## Results

On the internal safety evaluation (human-labeled), RBR-PPO achieves an F1 score of 97.1% between Not-Unsafe (97.27%) and Not-Overrefuse (97.01%), compared to 91.7% for the Human-PPO baseline and 95.8% for the Helpful-PPO baseline. On the automated internal evaluation, RBR-PPO reaches 94.4% F1 (93.95% Not-Unsafe, 94.95% Not-Overrefuse). On XSTest overrefusal, RBR-PPO matches the Helpful baseline at 99.5% while the Human baseline drops to 95.5%. Capability benchmarks (MMLU, HellaSwag, GPQA, Lambada) show no meaningful degradation across all three PPO variants.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RBR-PPO outperforms the Human-PPO baseline (trained with human-annotated safety preference data) on F1 by 5.4 points (97.1 vs 91.7) in human evaluation, primarily because Human-PPO over-refuses on 15.3% of safe Comply prompts (Not-Overref 84.70%) while RBR-PPO over-refuses on only 3.0% (97.01%). Against the Helpful-PPO baseline, RBR-PPO improves F1 by 1.3 points (97.1 vs 95.8) by substantially increasing safety (Not-Unsafe 97.27% vs 93.64%) while keeping over-refusals nearly identical (97.01% vs 98.13%). On XSTest, RBR-PPO matches Helpful-PPO at 99.5% overrefusal rate while Human-PPO drops to 95.5%, confirming that RBRs avoid the over-cautiousness problem of human-labeled safety data. On WildChat safety, RBR-PPO achieves 96.0% Not-Unsafe, substantially above Helpful-PPO (69.3%) but slightly below Human-PPO (99.8%), reflecting a deliberate trade-off toward fewer over-refusals. Capability scores remain statistically equivalent across all three methods (e.g., MMLU: 74.4–75.9%), indicating no capability regression from safety training.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RLHF pipeline (SFT → RM → PPO) by introducing Rule-Based Rewards (RBRs) that use LLM-graded binary propositions as direct reward features during PPO training, rather than distilling safety rules into a reward model via synthetic or human comparison data. It is most closely related to Sparrow (Glaese et al.), which also uses explicit rules, but departs by replacing human-annotated data with an LLM grader and by fitting a lightweight linear model to ensure correct ranking of completions. In contrast to Constitutional AI and RLAIF, which generate synthetic preference data to train a separate RM, RBRs skip the RM distillation step entirely and incorporate rule-based signals directly into the total reward, avoiding information loss from that intermediate step.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant for production safety alignment: RBRs achieve a higher F1 (97.1 vs. 91.7) than a human-feedback baseline while requiring only 518 human-labeled examples for prompt tuning, and they can be updated by modifying rules without recollecting data. A key open limitation is that the method requires behaviors to be decomposable into clear, binary propositions, which may not hold for harder alignment tasks beyond safety. The finding that distilling safety rules into RM comparison data can cause severe over-refusals (an 'always-refuse' model) due to class imbalance is a practical warning for teams using standard RLHF for safety, and the paper's correction of the human baseline to fix this issue somewhat complicates the head-to-head comparison.
