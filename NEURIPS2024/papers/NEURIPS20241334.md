## Problem

Despite widespread empirical evidence that transformer LLMs follow power-law scaling in model size and data size, a rigorous theoretical explanation for why these scaling laws exist has been missing. Existing approximation and estimation theories for feedforward networks predict convergence rates depending on the ambient data dimension D, which are far too slow to match observed practice. The gap arises because real-world data (including text) concentrates on low-dimensional manifolds, but no prior theory connected transformer scaling laws to this intrinsic geometric structure.

## Input / Output

Theoretically, the input is a point x ∈ M ⊂ R^D on a d-dimensional compact Riemannian manifold, which is embedded into a token sequence where each coordinate x^i occupies a separate token column in a d_embd × l embedding matrix. The output is a scalar f(x) decoded from the first element of the last token column. In the empirical LLM setting, the input is a natural language token sequence processed by a 125M-parameter GPT-style transformer, and the output is a next-token cross-entropy loss used to fit scaling exponents in log-log space.

## Method

The paper establishes two complementary theories. For approximation, it constructs a shallow transformer with only O(log(d)) depth (independent of target accuracy ε) that approximates β-Hölder functions on M by decomposing f via a partition of unity into local tangent-space pieces, then using a novel Interaction Lemma to implement sparse pairwise token multiplications in parallel across O(d·ε^{-d/β}) attention heads. For statistical estimation, it computes the covering number of the resulting transformer class to bound the generalization error of the empirical risk minimizer, yielding the scaling law E‖T̂_n − f‖² ≤ Õ(D·d²·n^{-2β/(2β+d)}). The key architectural insight is that transformers can approximate low-dimensional functions with logarithmic depth in d (versus O(log(1/ε)) for ReLU networks), and the resulting scaling exponents α_D = 2β/(2β+d) and α_N = 2β/d depend only on intrinsic dimension d, which the paper validates empirically by estimating d via MLE on token embeddings and matching predicted exponents to observed LLM scaling curves within ±0.02.

## Results

The paper establishes that the data scaling exponent is α_D = 2β/(2β+d) and the model scaling exponent is α_N = 2β/d, where d is the intrinsic dimension of the data manifold and β is the Hölder regularity. For β=1 (Lipschitz), pretraining 125M-parameter LLMs on OpenWebText, The Stack-SQL, and Tiny Stories yields predicted α_D values that match observed exponents within ±0.02 absolute error. For model scaling, the ID-free prediction α_N = α_D/(1−α_D) recovers GPT-2's observed α̂_N = 0.076 as 0.106 and Chinchilla's α̂_N = 0.34 as 0.33, with the latter being remarkably close.

**Table 1: ID-free estimation of scaling exponents for GPT-2 and Chinchilla.**

| Model | Observed α̂_N | Predicted α_D | Observed α̂_D | Predicted α_N |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-2 | 0.076 | 0.070 | 0.095 | 0.106 |
| Chinchilla | 0.34 | 0.25 | 0.28 | 0.33 |

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper validates its theoretical scaling exponents against three empirical scaling-law references. Against GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019), the ID-free prediction overestimates α_N (0.106 vs. observed 0.076) and underestimates α_D (0.070 vs. observed 0.095), with absolute errors of ~0.03. Against Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022), the prediction is much tighter: α_N = 0.33 vs. observed 0.34 (error 0.01) and α_D = 0.25 vs. observed 0.28 (error 0.03). Against the Pythia suite (Biderman et al., 2023), the predicted α_N systematically under-estimates the empirical α̂_N, which the authors attribute to possible under-training of the largest models and intrinsic entropy of the data distribution. Overall, the theory matches data scaling exponents most accurately (±0.02 on the three pretraining datasets) and model scaling exponents less precisely, with Chinchilla being the closest match.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the manifold-based approximation and estimation theory for deep networks (Chen et al. 2019, 2022; Nakada and Imaizumi 2020) from feedforward ReLU and convolutional residual networks to transformers, and provides the first rigorous theoretical justification for the empirical connection between intrinsic data dimension and neural scaling laws observed by Sharma and Kaplan (2022). The approximation construction adapts Yarotsky's (2016) piecewise-constant strategy but replaces the O(log(1/ε)) ReLU depth with an O(log(d)) transformer depth via a novel Interaction Lemma for sparse pairwise token operations. The classification extension draws on the Audibert–Tsybakov (2007) excess-risk decomposition under a margin condition to bridge the regression theory to the next-token prediction setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper closes a long-standing gap by showing *why* transformer scaling laws depend on intrinsic dimension, with the exponent α_D = 2β/(2β+d) now derived from first principles rather than fitted empirically. The O(log(d)) depth result provides a clean theoretical explanation for why wide, shallow transformers outperform deep ReLU networks on low-dimensional data, aligning with practical observations from Kaplan et al. (2020). Key limitations include the simplifying Lipschitz assumption (β=1), the regression-to-classification bridge remaining partially heuristic, and the absence of a cross-entropy loss bound; the computational scaling exponent α_C is left entirely open. The ID-free prediction formula α_D = α_N/(α_N+1) offers a practical tool for estimating one exponent from the other without needing to estimate intrinsic dimension directly.
