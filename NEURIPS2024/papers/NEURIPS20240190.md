## Problem

Existing 3D Gaussian Splatting pruning methods reduce the number of Gaussian primitives but fail to proportionally accelerate rendering because they overlook the fragment count (the number of pixels each Gaussian projects onto). Profiling reveals that the rasterization stage is the dominant bottleneck and its cost scales with total fragments, not Gaussian count; prior pruning actually increases fragments per Gaussian, negating most of the expected speedup.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240190/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained 3D Gaussian Splatting model (containing millions of anisotropic Gaussian primitives with learned opacity, rotation, scale, and spherical harmonic coefficients) and a target camera viewpoint, and produces a rendered novel view image at 1080P (1920×1080) resolution. It operates as a post-training fine-tuning step requiring only 5,000 epochs with a batch size of 1 (or 4 for dynamic scenes), after which the learned per-Gaussian truncation thresholds are applied at inference time with no additional computational overhead beyond the standard rasterization pipeline.

## Method

The core contribution is fragment pruning: learning a per-Gaussian truncation threshold T_i that controls how many pixels (fragments) each 2D Gaussian projects onto during rasterization, rather than removing entire Gaussian primitives. The key technical challenge is that the truncation uses a non-differentiable indicator function; the authors approximate it with a sigmoid function Sigmoid((E_i(x) − T_i)/t) to enable a fully differentiable fine-tuning pipeline. During fine-tuning, only the truncation thresholds T_i and opacities o_i are trainable while all geometric parameters (rotation, scale) and SH coefficients are frozen to prevent overfitting. After fine-tuning, the sigmoid approximation is discarded and the hard indicator is used directly for rendering, incurring zero extra inference cost. The approach is orthogonal to existing Gaussian primitive pruning (e.g., Mini-Splatting) and can be stacked on top of it for compounding speedups.

## Results

On the Jetson Orin NX edge GPU at 1080p, applying fragment pruning to vanilla 3DGS increases FPS by +17, +20, and +26 on Mip-NeRF 360, Tanks&Temples, and Deep Blending respectively, while improving PSNR by +0.04 to +0.05. When combined with Mini-Splatting (SOTA Gaussian primitive pruning), the method achieves 54, 60, and 60 FPS on the three datasets (capped at 60), representing a 1.71× speedup over Mini-Splatting alone on Tanks&Temples. On the dynamic Plenoptic Video dataset, fragment pruning applied to 4DGS yields +8 FPS (14→22) with a marginal PSNR gain of +0.03.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against vanilla 3DGS [16], fragment pruning more than doubles FPS (e.g., 20→37 on Mip-NeRF 360, 16→36 on Tanks&Temples) while simultaneously improving PSNR by 0.04–0.05, showing that the learned truncation thresholds remove redundant fragments that degrade quality. Compared to Mini-Splatting [18] (SOTA Gaussian primitive pruning), the combined Mini-Splatting + Ours achieves 54–60 FPS versus 35–39 FPS for Mini-Splatting alone, with PSNR gains of +0.16 on Tanks&Temples (23.34 vs 23.18) and +0.06 on Deep Blending (30.04 vs 29.98), while Mip-NeRF 360 PSNR is nearly identical (27.38 vs 27.34). Against NeRF-based baselines (Mip-NeRF 360 [6], INGP-Big [13], Plenoxels [9]), the method achieves substantially higher FPS (54–60 vs. no real-time capability) and competitive or superior PSNR on Tanks&Temples and Deep Blending, though Mip-NeRF 360 PSNR (27.38) remains slightly below the Mip-NeRF 360 baseline (27.69). On dynamic scenes (Plenoptic Video), 4DGS + Ours [28] reaches 22 FPS with 32.04 PSNR, outperforming 4DGS alone (14 FPS, 32.01 PSNR) and all prior dynamic methods (HexPlane 0.56 FPS, HyperReel 2.00 FPS, MixVoxels-L 16.7 FPS) in speed while matching or exceeding their quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) by Kerbl et al. by introducing a new pruning granularity: rather than removing entire Gaussian primitives as prior compression methods (Compact 3D Gaussian, LightGaussian, Mini-Splatting) do, it prunes at the fragment (pixel) level within each Gaussian by learning a per-Gaussian truncation threshold. The key conceptual departure is the profiling insight that the rasterization stage's cost is governed by total fragment count, not Gaussian count, and that prior primitive-level pruning actually *increases* fragments-per-Gaussian (from ~10 to ~65), undermining proportional speedups. The method is explicitly orthogonal to and composable with existing Gaussian pruning, using a differentiable sigmoid approximation of the truncation indicator to enable end-to-end fine-tuning of thresholds.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is enabling 60 FPS rendering on edge GPUs (Jetson Orin NX) for AR/VR applications, which was previously unachievable with standard 3DGS (35 FPS) or even Mini-Splatting (35 FPS). The fragment-count bottleneck insight is broadly applicable to any rasterization-based 3D representation and could inform future compression strategies. Limitations include the 16–21% additional fine-tuning overhead and the fact that the technique is validated primarily on a single edge device at 1080p. Open questions include whether threshold learning can be integrated into pre-training to eliminate the fine-tuning cost, and whether the approach generalizes to other downstream tasks like 3D content generation or text-to-3D.
