## Problem

Deep learning for rapid SAR-based flood mapping is hindered by the absence of large, expert-annotated datasets: existing resources rely on noisy automatic or semi-automatic CEMS labels, cover limited geographic regions, omit pre-event imagery needed to distinguish permanent water from flood, and do not provide Single Look Complex (SLC) data for phase exploitation. Kuro Siwo addresses this by delivering a globally diverse, manually photointerpreted multi-temporal SAR dataset spanning 43 flood events across six continents, paired with the first unified benchmark (BlackBench) for this task.

## Input / Output

Each sample is a triplet of 224×224 Sentinel-1 tiles (two pre-event + one post-event) at 10 m resolution in both VV and VH polarizations, available as radiometrically calibrated GRD backscatter or minimally processed complex-valued SLC (phase + amplitude), optionally concatenated with SRTM DEM and derived slope channels. The model outputs a per-pixel 3-class mask (no water / permanent water / flood) and a derived binary water mask; the SLC variant preserves the full complex signal so downstream users can design their own preprocessing and exploit interferometric coherence.

## Method

The dataset is constructed by a team of five SAR experts who manually photointerpret GRD imagery for 43 events (2015–2022), correcting or replacing CEMS shapefiles to eliminate thresholding artifacts and inter-annotator bias; 67,490 labeled triplets are stored alongside 533,847 unlabeled triplets to support self-supervised pretraining. BlackBench evaluates 17 model configurations—U-Net, DeepLabv3, and UPerNet with ResNet/Swin/ConvNeXt backbones, change-detection architectures (FC-EF, SNUNet-CD, ChangeFormer), ConvLSTM, and a MAE-pretrained ViT (FloodViT) with sinusoidal temporal embeddings—under varying time-series lengths (2 vs. 3 captions) and elevation inputs. Key empirical findings: using all available pre-event captions consistently outperforms single-caption inputs, DEM/slope channels yield only marginal gains, and the SSL-pretrained FloodViT matches supervised baselines despite training only a small convolutional decoder, while permanent-water detection remains the weakest class due to boundary ambiguity near overflowing rivers.

## Results

On the Kuro Siwo test set (10 unseen flood events across 6 continents), the best-performing model UNet-ResNet50 (3 temporal captions, no elevation) achieves 80.12% F1 for flood, 78.24% F1 for permanent water, and 83.85% F1 for combined water detection, with a mean IoU of 76.20%. The SSL-pretrained FloodViT (MAE + ViT encoder) reaches 78.70% F1-flood and 83.21% F1-water with only a small trainable decoder, matching most supervised baselines. Temporal context (3 vs. 2 captions) consistently improves performance across all semantic segmentation architectures, while adding DEM or slope information yields only marginal gains.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UNet-ResNet50 (Ronneberger et al.) is the top performer overall, achieving 80.12% F1-flood and 76.20% mIoU, edging out UPerNet-ConvnextS (79.30% F1-flood) and UPerNet-SwinB (79.28% F1-flood) by roughly 0.8–0.9 points. DeepLabv3 variants (Chen et al.) are competitive but consistently trail UNet by 1–1.5% F1-flood, with DeepLab-ResNet50 reaching 78.70%. Among change-detection architectures, FC-EF-Conc (Daudt et al.) at 75.06% F1-flood and 71.17% mIoU substantially outperforms FC-EF-Diff (74.27% F1-flood, 53.52% mIoU), whose poor permanent-water score (10.93%) drags its overall mIoU down. SNUNet-CD (Fang et al.) and Changeformer (Bandara et al.) land in the 74–77% F1-flood range, below all semantic segmentation models. ConvLSTM (Shi et al.) achieves 77.53% F1-flood, the best among recurrent/change-detection baselines but still ~2.6 points below UNet-ResNet50. Notably, the SSL-pretrained FloodViT (He et al. MAE + ViT) matches or exceeds all change-detection methods (78.70% F1-flood, 83.21% F1-water) despite training only a small convolutional decoder, suggesting the unlabeled Kuro Siwo component is a strong foundation for future flood-mapping research.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of SAR-based flood mapping datasets (UNOSAT, Sen1Floods11, Ombria, MMFlood, S1GFloods) by departing from their reliance on CEMS or semi-automatic annotations in favor of expert manual photointerpretation at unprecedented scale (43 events, 6 continents). It also breaks from prior work by providing both GRD and SLC Sentinel-1 products, the first flood mapping dataset to include the complex-valued SLC signal, and by adding a large unlabeled component to enable self-supervised pretraining. The benchmark (BlackBench) unifies semantic segmentation architectures (U-Net, DeepLabv3, UPerNet with ResNet/Swin/ConvNeXt backbones), change detection models (FC-EF, SNUNet-CD, ChangeFormer), and a MAE-based ViT under a common spatially-disjoint evaluation protocol, whereas prior datasets typically evaluated on random splits or a single unseen event.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Kuro Siwo addresses the critical bottleneck of large, high-quality annotated SAR data for flood mapping, and the SLC component opens an underexplored avenue for exploiting phase information (e.g., interferometric coherence) in flood detection. The finding that CEMS annotations exhibit only ~50% IoU with expert photointerpretation underscores a practical quality gap that has likely depressed reported performance in prior CEMS-based datasets. Key open questions include whether multispectral (Sentinel-2) fusion can complement SAR under cloud-free conditions, whether a dynamic permanent-water layer would help disambiguate flood from standing water, and how to extend from rapid mapping to longer-term flood forecasting. Limitations include the European bias in event distribution and C-band penetration constraints in dense vegetation.
