## Problem

Global epistasis — a monotonic nonlinearity transforming a sparse latent fitness function — produces observed fitness functions that are dense in the epistatic (Graph Fourier) domain, making them inefficient to learn from limited data with standard MSE loss. The paper asks whether a contrastive (ranking) loss can bypass this barrier by directly recovering the sparse latent ranking function without needing to model the nonlinearity.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242961/cropped_page4_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes protein sequences (binary or multi-alphabet, typically with 3+ mutations) and their observed scalar fitness values as input, and produces a scalar fitness prediction per sequence that induces a ranking. A key property is that the Bradley-Terry loss consumes only the pairwise ordering of observed fitness values, not their magnitudes, making training invariant to any monotonic transformation of the labels. Sequences are encoded as one-hot or binary vectors over mutated positions, and the model is a standard CNN or fully-connected network.

## Method

The core contribution is training a fitness model by minimizing the Bradley-Terry contrastive loss (Eq. 3), which sums log(1+exp(−(f_θ(x_i)−f_θ(x_j)))) over all pairs where y_i > y_j, rather than the standard MSE loss. The theoretical justification is a fitness-epistasis uncertainty principle (derived from the discrete entropic uncertainty principle of Dembo et al.) showing that a fitness function cannot be simultaneously concentrated in both the fitness and epistatic domains; monotonic nonlinearities concentrate the fitness domain, forcing epistatic density that inflates the data requirements for MSE-based estimation per compressed-sensing scaling laws. Because the BT loss is invariant to monotonic relabeling, it effectively learns the sparse latent ranking function directly, avoiding the dense epistatic representation. The paper validates this through complete-data recovery experiments, incomplete-data simulations showing BT loss robustness to epistatic density, and empirical benchmarks on FLIP and ProteinGym where BT-trained CNNs outperform MSE-trained CNNs on nearly all tasks.

## Results

On the FLIP benchmark (15 tasks across GB1, AAV, and Thermostability datasets), the Bradley-Terry (BT) loss outperforms the standard MSE loss on 14 of 15 tasks in Spearman correlation, with the largest gains on multi-mutation splits: GB1 3-vs-rest (0.880 vs 0.814), AAV 1-vs-rest (0.485 vs 0.335), and Thermostability mixed (0.453 vs 0.349). BT loss also improves top-10% recall in most settings (e.g., AAV des-mut: 0.387 vs 0.318; GB1 3-vs-rest: 0.664 vs 0.539). On ProteinGym datasets with many multi-mutants, BT again edges out MSE (CAPSD_AAV2S: 0.920 vs 0.912; GFP_AEQVI: 0.873 vs 0.867 Spearman). In simulations, BT loss maintains near-perfect Spearman correlation even when the epistatic-domain entropy approaches its maximum, while MSE performance degrades sharply with increasing epistatic density.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole baseline is the FLIP benchmark's CNN model trained with standard MSE loss (Dallago et al., 2021). The BT loss wins on 14 of 15 FLIP tasks in Spearman correlation, with the only exception being GB1 1-vs-rest (0.091 vs 0.133), a task with very low absolute performance for both methods. The largest relative gains occur on splits where the test set contains many multi-mutations relative to the training set: AAV 1-vs-rest improves by 0.150 Spearman (0.485 vs 0.335), GB1 3-vs-rest by 0.066 (0.880 vs 0.814), and Thermostability mixed by 0.104 (0.453 vs 0.349). For top-10% recall (a practical protein-engineering metric), BT also leads in most cases, with notable gains on AAV des-mut (0.387 vs 0.318) and GB1 3-vs-rest (0.664 vs 0.539). On the two ProteinGym datasets with many multi-mutants, BT again outperforms MSE by small but consistent margins (0.008 and 0.006 Spearman). The paper attributes these gains to global epistasis making observed fitness functions dense in the epistatic domain, which impedes MSE-based learning under the compressed-sensing scaling regime, while the BT loss—being invariant to monotonic transformations—recovers the underlying sparse ranking function more data-efficiently.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of global epistasis modeling (Otwinowski et al., 2018), which posits that observed fitness is a monotonic nonlinearity applied to a sparse latent fitness function, and the Graph Fourier / epistatic sparsity framework (Brookes et al., 2022) that characterizes how many measurements are needed to learn such functions. The key departure is reframing the modeling problem: rather than jointly fitting parametric forms for both the latent function and the nonlinearity (as in spline or tanh-based global epistasis models), the paper treats the latent function as a ranking function to be extracted via the Bradley-Terry contrastive loss, leaving the nonlinearity entirely non-parametric. The theoretical backbone is the discrete entropic uncertainty principle (Dembo et al., 1991), which the authors specialize to the Graph Fourier transform to derive a fitness-epistasis uncertainty principle linking concentration in the fitness domain to density in the epistatic domain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is simple and broadly applicable: replacing MSE with the Bradley-Terry loss in any fitness-prediction pipeline consistently improves Spearman correlation and top-10% recall across the FLIP and ProteinGym benchmarks, with no case of degradation. The uncertainty principle provides a clean theoretical explanation for why MSE struggles under global epistasis (dense epistatic representations require more data per compressed-sensing scaling laws), but the authors are candid that the analogy to neural network training is not rigorously proven, and the near-perfect recovery of the specific sparse latent function from complete data remains unexplained. Open questions include characterizing exactly when the BT loss recovers the sparse latent function versus an arbitrary ranking, extending the analysis to realistic sequencing-count noise, and establishing formal guarantees for contrastive losses in the presence of dense epistatic representations.
