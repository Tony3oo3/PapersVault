## Problem

When test samples lack class labels, it is difficult to quantify whether a hypothesis learned on labeled training data remains applicable to unlabeled test data. Existing distribution discrepancy methods either require symmetric label access across both datasets, ignore the specific hypothesis being evaluated, or rely on model confidence scores that become unreliable under distribution shift. This paper addresses the gap of measuring hypothesis-specific distribution discrepancy using only labeled training samples and unlabeled test samples.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a labeled training set, an unlabeled test set, and a pre-trained classifier hypothesis (e.g., ResNet-18, ViT-B/16). The density ratio is built as a lightweight adapter on top of the frozen classifier backbone, so the input representation is the penultimate feature of the hypothesis rather than raw pixels. The output is a scalar discrepancy score per sample pair, which is then aggregated into AUROC/AUPR over 100,000 positive (same-distribution) and negative (cross-distribution) pairings drawn from subsets of M=1000 samples each.

## Method

I-Div decomposes the joint distribution ratio Q(x,y)/P(x,y) into a density ratio r(x) and a likelihood ratio v(x,y), then estimates the discrepancy as E_P[|r(x)v(x,y)−1|·L(h(x),y)] using only training samples via importance sampling. The density ratio is hypothesis-oriented: it reuses the classifier's backbone ψ and appends a learnable adapter (two fully connected layers with GELU activation followed by a Softplus layer) to produce strictly positive outputs, trained by minimizing a KL-divergence-based objective with normalization constraints over the merged training+test inputs. The likelihood ratio is adaptive rather than directly estimated: it is derived from the learned density ratio via a proximal algorithm that minimizes the generalization error upper bound (involving Rademacher complexity and Talagrand's contraction), yielding a closed-form expression that reduces to the covariate-shift assumption v=1 at the extremes of the regularization parameter. Theoretical guarantees include a convergence rate for the density ratio estimator (Theorem 3.1) and a generalization bound for the discrepancy estimator (Theorem 3.2), both scaling as O(1/√N).

## Results

I-Div is evaluated via AUROC/AUPR for distinguishing positive (same-distribution) from negative (cross-distribution) sample pairs. On CIFAR-10 with one class held out (Table 1), I-Div achieves a perfect 100% AUROC and AUPR across all 10 target classes. The key differentiator appears in Table 3: for semantically similar test sets (CIFAR-10.1, ACC 73.4%; STL-10, ACC 63.0%), I-Div yields low AUROC values of 43.4 and 37.2 respectively, correctly indicating transferable knowledge, while for fully dissimilar sets (RGI, SVHN, DTD) it reaches 100.0. On ImageNet→OOD (Table 4), I-Div produces intermediate AUROC (62.6–72.4) for partially overlapping datasets like OIDv4 and Caltech256, versus 100.0 for specialized sets like Flowers102 and DTD.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All five baselines (MSP, NNBD, MMD-D, R-Div, H-Div) produce near-100% AUROC across virtually all test datasets, failing to reflect the degree of semantic overlap between training and test distributions. I-Div is the only method that yields a graded response: it drops to 43.4 AUROC on CIFAR-10.1 and 37.2 on STL-10 (both sharing CIFAR-10's 10-class taxonomy), correctly signaling that the hypothesis remains applicable, while MSP and R-Div still report 100.0. MMD-D shows the most variation among baselines (90.2–100.0 AUROC) but still over-discriminates relative to I-Div on the similar-dataset cases. On domain adaptation (PACS, Office-Home), I-Div reports 39.7–51.4 AUROC, reflecting partial transferability, whereas MSP and R-Div again saturate at 100.0. On corrupted and adversarial data, I-Div's AUROC tracks classification accuracy decline (corrupted) or stays low (adversarial, matching human perception), while R-Div achieves 100% separation at any noise level, indicating it measures raw input shift rather than hypothesis applicability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the density ratio estimation literature (KLIEP, LSIF, NNBD) by making the ratio hypothesis-oriented: rather than estimating a generic input density ratio, it conditions the ratio on a specific trained hypothesis via a lightweight adapter on the backbone features. It also builds on the authors' own R-Div and H-Div lines of work, which measure distribution discrepancy through expected risk differences, but departs from them by incorporating importance sampling to handle the asymmetric setting where training data is labeled and test data is unlabeled. The adaptive likelihood ratio, derived from a proximal optimization that trades off the generalization error bound against the normalization constraint, is a novel component not present in prior density-ratio or divergence-based methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

I-Div provides a principled, theoretically grounded metric for assessing whether a trained model's knowledge transfers to unlabeled test data, which is a practical bottleneck in OOD detection, domain adaptation, and model deployment. The key limitation is that the likelihood ratio cannot be precisely estimated without test labels, so the method relies on an adaptive approximation that converges to the covariate-shift assumption (v=1) at the extremes of the regularization parameter. Open questions include whether the likelihood ratio can be bypassed entirely, whether the hypothesis-oriented density ratio generalizes to multi-task or multi-hypothesis settings, and how the method behaves under severe distribution shifts where the adapter's capacity may be insufficient.
