## Problem

While prior work has established that deep vision models exhibit a simplicity bias—favoring easier-to-extract features like texture over shape—there has been no comprehensive quantitative framework for measuring the complexity of the individual features these models actually learn. This paper asks four concrete questions: what simple versus complex features look like, when they emerge during training, where they flow through the network architecture, and how their complexity relates to their importance in driving predictions.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a single ImageNet-trained ResNet50, extracting 10,000 overcomplete features (5× the 2048 penultimate-layer neurons) via Non-Negative Matrix Factorization on the full 1.2M training set. Complexity scores are computed over 20,000 validation images by measuring V-information between each feature and linear probes at every residual block output. The outputs are per-feature complexity scores K(z,x) in [0,1], importance scores Γ(z_i) via gradient-input at the penultimate layer, and temporal decoding scores Λ tracking when each feature becomes linearly decodable across 90 training epochs.

## Method

The core contribution is a V-information-based complexity metric K(z,x) = 1 − (1/n)Σ_ℓ I_V(f_ℓ(x)→z), where the predictive family V is restricted to linear probes with Gaussian posteriors, yielding a closed-form expression proportional to Var(z)·R². Features are extracted using the Craft overcomplete dictionary (NMF on ReLU activations), producing 10,000 directions in the penultimate layer that overcome superposition. The paper then applies this metric along four axes: (1) qualitative visualization of a simple-to-complex spectrum (color/line detectors → structured object parts), (2) CKA-based analysis showing simple features are 'teleported' via residual connections while complex features are incrementally constructed through main-branch interactions, (3) temporal tracking revealing complex features emerge later in training, and (4) a complexity-importance analysis showing the model progressively simplifies its most important features over training—a 'sedimentation' process analogous to Levin's universal search, where important features become accessible at earlier layers via shorter computational paths.

## Results

The paper trains a ResNet50 on ImageNet (90 epochs, 78.9% validation accuracy) and extracts 10,000 features via an overcomplete NMF dictionary on the penultimate layer. Key quantitative findings include: a correlation coefficient of approximately 0.5 between feature complexity (K) and time-to-decode (Λ), confirming that complex features emerge later in training; CKA analysis showing that simple features (bottom 1% complexity) are carried almost entirely by residual connections beyond block 3, while complex features (top 1%) require progressive construction through both main and residual branches; and a 'sedimentation' effect where the average complexity of the top-1% most important features decreases over training while the global average complexity increases. Simplicity bias is absent at epoch 1 (features uniformly distributed in complexity-importance space) but clearly emerges by epoch 90.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is an empirical analysis paper rather than a method comparison study, so there are no direct baseline comparisons. The paper's contributions are framed as answers to four questions about feature complexity in a single ResNet50 model. The key empirical observations that serve as the main findings are: (1) a spectrum of simple-to-complex features exists within the model, with simple features resembling color/low-frequency detectors and complex features resembling structured objects (whiskers, insect legs, ears); (2) residual connections act as a 'teleportation' mechanism for simple features, while the main branch incrementally constructs complex features; (3) complex features are learned later in training, with a ~0.5 correlation between complexity and decoding time; and (4) the model exhibits a Levin-machine-like behavior where important features are progressively simplified (moved to earlier layers) during training, a process the authors term 'sedimentation.' The paper also shows that complex features are less robust to Gaussian noise perturbations and less redundant in their encoding, and that removing the most complex features causes noticeable performance drops despite their individually low importance (the 'support features' hypothesis).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on V-information (Xu et al., ICLR 2019) to introduce a computationally-grounded complexity metric for individual neural network features, and on the Craft overcomplete dictionary extraction method (Fel et al., CVPR 2023) to obtain 10,000 features from a ResNet50 penultimate layer. It positions itself at the intersection of the simplicity-bias literature (Shah et al., Hermann et al.) and the explainability/feature-visualization tradition, but departs from prior work by providing the first large-scale quantitative framework that scores each feature's complexity via cumulative V-information across layers, rather than relying on qualitative descriptions or proxy measures like non-linearity propagation or redundancy-based metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a practical, closed-form complexity metric that can be applied to any feature in any model, enabling systematic study of the simple-to-complex feature spectrum in large vision models. The 'sedimentation' finding—that important features become decodable at earlier layers over training, analogous to Levin's universal search—offers a new mechanistic perspective on why simplicity bias emerges, though it is demonstrated only on a single ResNet50/ImageNet setup. Key limitations include the assumption that each layer provides an optimal linearly-decodable representation (which the authors acknowledge is violated early in training), the single-architecture scope, and the moderate correlation (~0.5) between complexity and time-to-decode. Open questions include whether the sedimentation process generalizes to transformers and other architectures, and how the 'support feature' role of complex features interacts with robustness and generalization.
