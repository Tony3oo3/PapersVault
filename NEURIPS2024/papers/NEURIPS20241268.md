## Problem

Fairness methods for bias mitigation operate under subtly different problem settings—stage of intervention, sensitive feature composition, fairness notion, and output distribution—making direct benchmarking misleading. Prior benchmarks typically search for a single 'best method per dataset' without aligning these desiderata, and commonly report a fairness-accuracy trade-off that may be an artifact of evaluating on biased labels. The paper asks: how can fairness methods be properly compared when their applicability constraints and optimization targets differ?

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes tabular datasets (folktables ACS tasks with 620K–3.2M samples and the 856-sample SchoolPerformance dual-label dataset) where each sample has a feature vector, sensitive attribute labels (sex, age, race, etc.), and a binary output label. The Evaluator produces parity-based fairness violation scores (max deviation from group-mean statistic) alongside accuracy or AUROC, reported per sensitive-feature format (binary, intersectional, parallel), per fairness notion (7 total), and per output type (hard binary or soft score). The dual-label SchoolPerformance dataset is unusual: models are trained on human-annotated biased labels but evaluated on the original unbiased outcome labels, enabling separation of true accuracy impact from biased-label artifacts.

## Method

ABCFair is a PyTorch-idiomatic benchmarking pipeline built around three configurable components: a Dataset class that adapts sensitive features to binary, categorical (intersectional), or parallel formats and optionally includes them in model inputs; an abstract FairnessMethod base class whose subclasses implement preprocessing (data repair, label flipping, prevalence sampling, learning fair representations), inprocessing (fairret norm/KLproj regularization, LAFTR adversarial training, prejudice remover, exponentiated gradient), or postprocessing (error parity thresholding); and an Evaluator that computes group-wise statistics and measures fairness violation as the maximum relative deviation from the overall mean. The key design insight is that comparability requires explicitly matching the four desiderata (intervention stage, sensitive feature composition, fairness definition, output distribution) before comparing methods, rather than assuming a single canonical setup. The benchmark is validated on 10 methods, 7 fairness notions, 3 sensitive feature formats, and 2 output types, revealing that preprocessing methods struggle to achieve low violations, that intersectional sensitivity inflates violations, and that on unbiased labels the expected fairness-accuracy trade-off reverses into a synergy.

## Results

On the ACSPublicCoverage dataset (soft demographic parity, binary sensitive features), the naive model achieves AUROC = 81.2% with a fairness violation of 0.046. At the strictest threshold k=0.01, Fairret KLproj, Fairret Norm, and Prejudice Remover all maintain 81.2% AUROC with no performance loss, while LAFTR drops to 65.4%, Exponentiated Gradient to 70.5%, and Error Parity to 69.5%. On the dual-label SchoolPerformance dataset, evaluating on unbiased labels reveals an accuracy-fairness synergy (higher fairness → higher accuracy), inverting the traditional trade-off observed with biased labels. Preprocessing methods (Data Repairer, Label Flipping, Prevalence Sampling) frequently fail to satisfy the lowest fairness violation thresholds even at maximum strength.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Fairret KLproj and Fairret Norm (both inprocessing, [7]) dominate the comparison: they achieve 81.2% AUROC at the strictest k=0.01 threshold with zero performance loss, matching the naive model while satisfying the fairness constraint. Prejudice Remover [33] also matches the naive AUROC at k=0.01 but degrades slightly at intersectional/parallel formats (79.2% at k=0.20). LAFTR [39] and Exponentiated Gradient [2] suffer large drops (65.4% and 70.5% respectively at k=0.01 binary), indicating their optimization targets are misaligned with soft-score demographic parity. Error Parity [13], the sole postprocessing method, consistently underperforms on soft scores (69.5% at k=0.01) but recovers to 78.8–78.9% accuracy when evaluated on hard predictions (Table 6), confirming its design for binary outputs. Preprocessing methods (Data Repairer and Prevalence Sampling from Aequitas [45], Label Flipping [17], Learning Fair Repr. [51]) struggle most: Data Repairer and Prevalence Sampling fail to meet k=0.01 entirely, and Learning Fair Repr. incurs a catastrophic 18-point AUROC drop (63.0%) at all thresholds. The paper's key finding is that methods appearing best on the traditional biased-label trade-off (e.g., LAFTR, Exponentiated Gradient) perform worst when accuracy is measured on unbiased labels, inverting the ranking.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on a long line of fairness benchmarks (Cardoso et al. 2019, Friedler et al. 2019, Biswas and Rajan 2020, Fairea 2021, Islam et al. 2021, FFB 2023, Cruz and Hardt 2024) that typically search for the best method per dataset without accounting for the fact that methods operate under different desiderata—stage of intervention, sensitive feature format, fairness notion, and output distribution. ABCFair departs from this by formalizing these four comparability challenges and designing an adaptable pipeline (Dataset, FairnessMethod, Evaluator) that can be configured to match a specific real-world use case. It also introduces the use of a dual-label dataset (Lenders and Calders 2023) in a benchmarking context for the first time, enabling evaluation on both biased and unbiased labels to challenge the fairness-accuracy trade-off narrative.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The adaptable benchmarking framework addresses a genuine practical gap: practitioners cannot simply pick the 'best' fairness method from a leaderboard because methods are only comparable under matching desiderata. The dual-label finding—that methods performing best on the traditional trade-off actually perform worst on unbiased labels—is conceptually important but rests on a single small dataset (856 samples), limiting its generalizability. Key open questions include whether the framework scales to more method types (causal fairness, multicalibration), whether in-group fairness should be incorporated, and whether more dual-label datasets can be created to robustly validate the synergy finding. The paper itself acknowledges these limitations and the need for broader validation.
