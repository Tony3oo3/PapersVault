## Problem

Existing VQA benchmarks for vision-language models are vulnerable to 'blind' solutions that exploit language biases and imbalanced answer distributions, allowing models to answer correctly without processing images. This paper identifies that VLMs still struggle with natural adversarial samples—image-question pairs that humans answer easily but models fail—and proposes a benchmark that reliably measures genuine visio-linguistic capability by structurally preventing such shortcut solutions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240542/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test sample is a balanced group of two images and two questions whose answers alternate (e.g., Yes for image A / No for image B on question 1, reversed on question 2), so a model predicting the same answer regardless of input cannot exceed chance. The model receives one image and one question at a time and outputs a yes/no or two-option multiple-choice answer; performance is reported at four granularities—Acc (per pair), Q-Acc (both images correct for a question), I-Acc (both questions correct for an image), and G-Acc (all four pairs correct in a group). The benchmark spans 10,000 samples drawn from Flickr30K, DOCCI (long captions >100 words), and XM3600 (Chinese and Hindi).

## Method

The benchmark is collected via a semi-automated pipeline: (1) discriminative VLMs (CLIP, BLIP-2, LongCLIP, NLLB-CLIP) are used to find confounding image-text pairs where the model incorrectly matches an image with another image's caption; (2) GPT-4 is prompted to generate questions that yield different answers for the two images; (3) two human annotators verify each generated QA pair, retaining only samples where both agree. The vision-centric design—pairing each question with two images that produce different answers—enforces reliance on visual input and eliminates the language-prior shortcuts that plague prior benchmarks like MME and MMStar. For fine-grained analysis, each sample is tagged with 1–8 skills from a 27-skill taxonomy covering objects, attributes, spatial/action/part relations, and reasoning (counting, logic, comparison, differentiation, world knowledge). A debiasing evaluation protocol adjusts the VQAScore likelihood threshold τ per sample to force different predictions across paired images, revealing that models' true ranking ability far exceeds their raw accuracy once answer biases are removed.

## Results

On NaturalBench (7,600 English VQA samples), human annotators achieve 92.1% group accuracy (G-Acc), while the best model, GPT-4o, reaches only 39.6% G-Acc — a 52.5-point gap. The best open-source model, Qwen2-VL-72B, scores 36.9% G-Acc (55.2 points below humans). Most of the 53 evaluated VLMs perform only 5–20% above random chance (6.3% G-Acc), and a blind GPT-3.5 finetuned on QA data alone cannot exceed random chance, confirming NaturalBench's vision-centric design prevents language-prior shortcuts that plague prior benchmarks like MME and MMStar.

## Baselines & Comparisons

All 53 evaluated VLMs fall substantially short of human performance (92.1% G-Acc), with the gap ranging from 52.5 points (GPT-4o, 39.6% G-Acc) to over 85 points for weaker models like BLIP-2 (7.7% G-Acc). Among open-source models, Qwen2-VL-72B leads at 36.9% G-Acc, followed by Molmo-72B (29.3%) and Llama3.2-Vision-8B (28.8%), all still 55–63 points below humans. A critical finding is that a blind GPT-3.5 finetuned on QA data alone cannot exceed random chance (6.3%) on NaturalBench, whereas on prior benchmarks (MME, MMMU, MMStar, ScienceQA, AI2D, MM-Bench) the same blind model often matches or surpasses LLaVA-1.5 finetuned with images — demonstrating that NaturalBench's balanced two-image protocol effectively eliminates language-prior shortcuts. Debiased evaluation (adjusting the answer-likelihood threshold τ) yields 35–40% absolute G-Acc gains across models (e.g., GPT-4o rises from 39.6% to 75.6%), revealing that much of the performance gap stems from answer biases (e.g., repeating 'Yes' or 'B') rather than genuine visual reasoning failures. On the NaturalBench-Retrieval subset, even the strongest SigLIP-SOViT model achieves only 42.1% group score, confirming that the confounding image pairs are genuinely challenging for discriminative VLMs as well.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the concept of natural adversarial examples from image classification (Hendrycks et al.) to the VQA setting, but departs sharply from prior adversarial VQA benchmarks (Adversarial VQA, Human-Adversarial VQA) by not targeting any specific model and not perturbing inputs. Its evaluation protocol builds on the balanced-answer idea from VQAv2 and Winoground, but automates the most labor-intensive steps (finding confusing image pairs via CLIP/BLIP-2 mismatches, generating questions via ChatGPT) to make dynamic benchmark curation feasible. The debiasing analysis builds on the VQAScore likelihood formulation for deterministic evaluation, repurposing the threshold parameter as a debiasing knob.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

NaturalBench is likely to become a standard stress-test for VLMs given its scale (10,000 samples), balanced protocol that blocks blind shortcuts, and demonstrated extensibility to new data sources and languages. The finding that a single threshold adjustment (debiasing) recovers 35–40% of lost accuracy suggests current VLMs largely encode the correct visual evidence but are systematically biased in answer selection — a practically actionable insight for model developers. Open questions include whether the benchmark will remain challenging as models improve, how to debias without oracle knowledge of image-question pairings, and whether the semi-automated pipeline can scale to truly dynamic, continuously-updated evaluation.
