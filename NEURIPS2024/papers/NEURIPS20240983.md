## Problem

Causal machine learning theory predicts that models trained on causal features should generalize better to new domains because causal mechanisms are invariant to environmental changes. However, this prediction has not been systematically tested on realistic tabular datasets with natural domain shifts. This paper empirically evaluates whether causal feature selection actually improves out-of-domain generalization, finding that it does not in any of the 16 tasks studied.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240983/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on 16 tabular binary-classification tasks (from ACS, BRFSS, MIMIC-III, TableShift, and others) where domains are defined by natural groupings such as race, U.S. Census region, education level, or insurance type. For each task, features are manually classified into four sets—causal, arguably causal, anti-causal, and all—based on domain expertise from social and biomedical research, with the arguably causal set serving as a superset that captures epistemic uncertainty. The output is a Pareto-frontier analysis of in-domain vs. out-of-domain accuracy and shift gap vs. out-of-domain accuracy, reported with 95% Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals across 42K+ trained models.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is a systematic empirical evaluation framework that tests whether causal feature selection improves domain generalization. For each of 16 tasks, they train 12–23 machine learning methods (XGBoost, LightGBM, SAINT, TabTransformer, NODE, FT-Transformer, IRM, REx, IB-IRM, CausIRL, AND-Mask, DRO, Group DRO, DANN, Deep CORAL, Domain MixUp, MMD) on each feature set with 50 HyperOpt trials, then compute Pareto-frontiers of in-domain/out-of-domain accuracy and shift gap/out-of-domain accuracy. They also apply causal discovery algorithms (PC, ICP, FCI) to check whether data-driven causal parent identification improves upon expert-knowledge selections, and conduct extensive robustness tests by removing or adding individual features. The key finding is that all features Pareto-dominate both causal selections in in-domain and out-of-domain accuracy across all 16 tasks, causal ML methods perform within the range of standard models on causal features, and adding anti-causal features paradoxically improves out-of-domain performance.

## Results

Across all 16 tabular prediction tasks (health, employment, education, social benefits, politics), predictors trained on all available features Pareto-dominate both conservative causal and inclusive arguably-causal feature selections in both in-domain and out-of-domain accuracy (16/16 tasks). In 7/16 tasks, causal-feature predictors essentially degenerate to majority-label prediction, and in 7/16 tasks the absolute shift gap (in-domain minus out-of-domain accuracy) is significantly smaller for all features than for causal features. Adding anti-causal features (caused by the target) to the causal set improves out-of-domain accuracy in all 5 tasks where such features exist. A total of 42K models were trained for main results and 468K for robustness checks, confirming findings are stable under single-feature misclassification.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Causal ML methods (IRM, REx, IB-IRM, CausIRL, AND-Mask) are evaluated on 8 tasks with ≥2 training domains; none outperforms standard models (XGBoost, LightGBM, etc.) trained on the arguably-causal feature set (8/8 tasks), and their performance typically interpolates between the conservative causal and arguably-causal selections. Causal discovery algorithms (PC, ICP, FCI) rarely identify causal parents (ICP: 1/6, PC: 4/14, FCI: 1/14 tasks), and when they do, their selected features are always Pareto-dominated by the expert-defined arguably-causal set. Domain generalization baselines (DRO, Group DRO, DANN, Deep CORAL, Domain MixUp, MMD) and tabular deep-learning models (SAINT, TabTransformer, NODE, FT-Transformer) are included in the Pareto-frontier analysis but none beats the all-features standard model. The one notable exception across all comparisons is the ASSISTments task, where removing the 'tested skill' feature (a clearly causal variable) from random subsets slightly improves out-of-domain accuracy, an unexplained anomaly the authors flag for future work.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the TableShift benchmark (Gardner et al., 2023) for tabular domain generalization and the causal machine learning literature on invariant risk minimization (IRM, REx, IB-IRM, CausIRL, AND-Mask). While prior empirical work (Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz, 2020; Gardner et al., 2023) showed that domain generalization algorithms do not systematically outperform empirical risk minimization, this paper shifts the question from algorithmic design to feature selection: it asks whether restricting to causally relevant features, as justified by the invariance of causal mechanisms (Peters et al., 2016; Meinshausen, 2018), improves out-of-domain performance. The paper also evaluates classic causal discovery algorithms (PC, ICP, FCI) as an alternative to expert-knowledge-based feature selection, positioning itself as a direct empirical stress-test of the theoretical guarantees that underpin the causal ML approach to domain generalization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The uniform failure of causal feature selection to outperform all-features models across 16 diverse tasks (health, employment, education, politics) is a strong empirical challenge to the optimism in the causal ML community about domain generalization. The paper clarifies that the theoretical guarantees (minimax optimality under do-interventions) require assumptions—complete causal parent sets, interventions on features only—that are unlikely to hold in real tabular data, shifting the burden of proof to proponents of causal methods to identify settings where they do succeed. The counterintuitive finding that adding anti-causal features improves out-of-domain performance, and the puzzling ASSISTments exception where removing a clearly causal variable helps, open concrete questions for future theoretical work. The practical takeaway for practitioners is straightforward: on the tabular datasets studied, training the best model on all available features remains the best strategy for domain generalization.
