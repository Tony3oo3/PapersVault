## Problem

In mixed-motive multi-agent cooperation, individual and collective objectives are imperfectly aligned, yet existing approaches either rely on hand-crafted reward modifications (reputation, norms, contracts) or lack theoretical grounding for convergence. No prior work provides a principled gradient-based mechanism that simultaneously respects individual interests while steering learning toward stable collective optima, with formal guarantees on the learning dynamics.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-agent individual losses, a collective loss (e.g., sum of individual rewards), the current policy parameters, and a scalar alignment magnitude λ. It outputs a single adjusted gradient vector ξ̃ that replaces the standard gradient in any CTDE optimizer (Adam). Crucially, the full Hessian is never materialized; instead, Hessian-vector products H_c^T ξ_c are computed via Pearlmutter's trick at O(n) cost per weight, and the sign of λ is determined on-the-fly from inner products of the collective gradient, individual gradient, and ∇H_c to ensure correct attraction/repulsion behavior near fixed points.

## Method

The paper first formalizes mixed-motive games as differentiable games (DMG), then proposes Altruistic Gradient Adjustment (AgA), defined as ξ̃ = ξ_c + λ(ξ + H_c^T ξ_c), where ξ_c is the collective gradient, ξ is the individual gradient, and H_c is the collective Hessian. The key insight is that the adjustment term ξ + H_c^T ξ_c encodes both the individual interest (ξ) and the local curvature of the collective landscape (H_c^T ξ_c), so that a single gradient step simultaneously advances collective welfare and individual utility. Corollary 4.3 proves that choosing sign(λ) = sign(⟨ξ_c, ∇H_c⟩(⟨ξ, ∇H_c⟩ + ‖∇H_c‖²)) guarantees the adjusted gradient is pulled toward stable fixed points (θ(ξ̃, ∇H_c) ≤ θ(ξ_c, ∇H_c)) and pushed away from unstable ones, using an infinitesimal alignment argument. AgA is plug-and-play within any CTDE framework (IPPO, MAPPO) and is validated on a two-player public goods game, sequential social dilemmas (Cleanup, Harvest), and a new large-scale StarCraft II environment (Selfish-MMM2) with 10 heterogeneous agents and action space 18^10.

## Results

On the two-player public goods game, AgA achieves a social welfare of 2.903 (vs. 2.814 for Simul-Co and 2.684 for SL) and the highest equality metric of 0.960, with both players receiving nearly identical rewards (r₁=1.443, r₂=1.459). In the Cleanup sequential social dilemma, AgA records an average social welfare of 105.15, a ~56% increase over the runner-up SL method, while in Harvest it improves SW by 33.55 over SVO. On the large-scale Selfish-MMM2 environment (10 heterogeneous agents, action space 18¹⁰), AgA attains a 92.64% win rate against the built-in bot, exceeding the next-best SL method by 23.61 percentage points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AgA consistently outperforms all four baselines across every environment and metric. Against CGA (Mescheder et al., 2017) and SGA (Balduzzi et al., 2018), which are gradient-adjustment methods targeting stable fixed points of individual objectives, AgA achieves substantially higher social welfare (2.903 vs. 2.306/2.312 in the public goods game) and equality (0.960 vs. 0.929/0.924), because these methods converge to unstable or local-optimal points that neglect collective goals. Against SVO (McKee et al., 2020), which uses a shared-reward compensation mechanism, AgA improves SW by 0.739 in the public goods game and by 33.55 in Harvest, while also achieving higher equality. Against SL (Roesch et al., 2024), the strongest baseline that incorporates social welfare into individual rewards, AgA still leads by 0.219 SW in the public goods game, by ~56% SW in Cleanup, and by 23.61 percentage points in win rate on Selfish-MMM2. Notably, AgA also achieves the tightest reward distribution (lowest Gini coefficient) across all settings, indicating superior fairness alongside collective performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the differentiable game framework (Balduzzi et al., 2018; Letcher et al., 2019) and gradient adjustment techniques (CGA from Mescheder et al., 2017; SGA from Balduzzi et al., 2018) to the mixed-motive cooperation setting. While prior gradient adjustment methods target stable fixed points of individual objectives in zero-sum or general-sum games, and most mixed-motive MARL work operates at the reward level (Prosocial, Inequity Aversion, SVO, Selfishness Level), AgA is the first to formulate mixed-motive games as differentiable games and align individual and collective objectives through a gradient-level adjustment that simultaneously incorporates both the collective gradient and a Hessian-vector product term. The sign-alignment mechanism in Corollary 4.3 provides a principled criterion absent from prior gradient adjustment methods.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first theoretical guarantee (Corollary 4.3) that a gradient adjustment can pull toward stable fixed points of the collective objective while respecting individual interests, filling a gap where prior mixed-motive MARL methods lacked convergence analysis. The Selfish-MMM2 benchmark (10 heterogeneous agents, action space 18^10) addresses a real gap in large-scale mixed-motive evaluation, surpassing the 10-player PD testbed used in D3C. However, the 2–3× per-step computational overhead from Hessian-vector products limits practical adoption, and the authors acknowledge that tested environments remain somewhat removed from real-world mixed-motive scenarios. Open questions include whether the sign-alignment criterion generalizes beyond the neighborhood-of-fixed-points assumption and how AgA interacts with non-stationary opponent learning in truly open-ended settings.
