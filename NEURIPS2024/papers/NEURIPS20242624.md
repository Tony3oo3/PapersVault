## Problem

The straightforward zero-shot OOD detection pipeline with pre-trained VLMs selects OOD labels from a semantic pool (e.g., WordNet) and uses CLIP to classify images into ID/OOD groups, but simply expanding the pool with larger lexicons fails to improve performance. The paper identifies two root causes: uncommon words reduce the expected activation probability of OOD labels, and (near-)synonyms create high mutual dependence that violates the independence assumption underlying the theoretical performance model. The paper establishes a theoretical framework showing that performance enhancement requires simultaneously increasing pool size and expected OOD label activation probability while maintaining low mutual dependence among label activations.

## Input / Output

The method takes RGB test images and a set of natural-language ID class names as input, producing a binary ID/OOD classification decision in a zero-shot setting where no ID images are available for fine-tuning. The OOD score is computed as the total positive count across all selected OOD labels (i.e., the number of OOD labels whose image-text similarity exceeds an implicit threshold), and the method introduces less than 1% additional forward latency over the NegLabel baseline. Prompt ensembles are applied differently to the original semantic pool labels (e.g., 'a photo of the nice [label]') versus the CSP labels (e.g., 'a nice photo of [label]').

## Method

The core contribution is the Conjugated Semantic Pool (CSP), which constructs OOD label candidates by combining adjectives from a lexicon with broad superclass names (e.g., 'white creature', 'valuable item', 'communal place'), each serving as a cluster center for samples sharing similar visual properties across different categories. This contrasts with the original semantic pool of unmodified specific class names (e.g., 'cat', 'wallet'), which serve as cluster centers for samples from the same category but with varying properties. The theoretical analysis models OOD label activations as independent Bernoulli random variables and derives via the Lyapunov CLT that the optimal FPR is monotonically decreasing in both the semantic pool size M and the expected activation probability q2, while requiring low mutual dependence among activations. The CSP satisfies all three requirements: it enlarges M, increases q2 (since property clusters span many OOD categories, yielding higher expected softmax scores as validated empirically), and maintains low mutual dependence (since property cluster centers are distributed differently from category cluster centers in feature space, with only 3.94% of original labels finding similar counterparts in the CSP). The method is a post-hoc detector that inherits all hyperparameters, the NegMining algorithm, and the OOD score from NegLabel, with the sole modification being the expanded semantic pool.

## Results

On the ImageNet-1k OOD detection benchmark (CLIP ViT-B/16), the proposed CSP method achieves an average AUROC of 95.76% and FPR95 of 17.51% across four OOD datasets (iNaturalist, SUN, Places, Textures), outperforming the primary baseline NegLabel by 1.55% in AUROC and 7.89% in FPR95. The largest gains appear on Textures (FPR95: 25.52% vs. 43.56%) and SUN (FPR95: 13.66% vs. 20.53%), while iNaturalist shows the smallest improvement (FPR95: 1.54% vs. 1.91%). The method also consistently outperforms NegLabel across all CLIP architectures tested (ResNet50 through ViT-L/14-336px) and on hard OOD tasks such as WaterBirds-vs-Placesbg (FPR95: 12.07% vs. 29.16%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed CSP method outperforms all 15 baselines across all four OOD datasets on the ImageNet-1k benchmark. Against the primary baseline NegLabel [29], CSP achieves a 7.89% FPR95 reduction (17.51% vs. 25.40%) and 1.55% AUROC gain (95.76% vs. 94.21%), with the largest per-dataset FPR95 improvements on Textures (−18.04%) and SUN (−6.87%). Compared to other VLM-based methods, CSP surpasses CLIPN [64] by 13.59% FPR95 and LSN [43] by 12.71% FPR95, and outperforms MCM [40] by 26.42% FPR95. On hard OOD tasks (Table 2), CSP consistently beats NegLabel, with the most dramatic gain on WaterBirds-vs-Placesbg (FPR95: 12.07% vs. 29.16%, a 17.09% reduction). The method also generalizes across CLIP architectures (ResNet50 to ViT-L/14-336px), maintaining a 5–10% FPR95 advantage over NegLabel in every configuration.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on NegLabel (Jiang et al., ICLR 2023), which established the pipeline of selecting OOD labels from WordNet and using CLIP for zero-shot OOD detection. The key conceptual departure is theoretical: NegLabel assumed a constant similarity gap between OOD labels and OOD versus ID images, whereas this paper models the activation of each OOD label as a Bernoulli variable and shows via Lyapunov CLT that the gap dynamically shrinks as the selection ratio increases, producing an inverted-V performance curve. The paper also positions itself against the broader VLM-based OOD detection literature (ZOC, MCM, NPOS, CLIPN, LSN) and traditional image-only methods, but its core contribution is a new expansion strategy—the conjugated semantic pool (CSP) of adjective+superclass combinations—that moves beyond the paradigm of selecting labels from larger lexicons.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical framework provides practitioners with a principled understanding of when adding more OOD labels helps versus hurts, and the finding that simple lexicon expansion fails (due to uncommon words reducing activation probability and synonyms violating independence) is a useful negative result that redirects future work. The CSP approach is a lightweight, post-hoc modification (<1% latency) that achieves a substantial 7.89% FPR95 improvement over NegLabel across multiple CLIP architectures and OOD benchmarks. However, the method's effectiveness depends on OOD samples exhibiting diverse visual properties (it underperforms on iNaturalist where plant images are visually homogeneous), and the paper does not explore modifying the ID label set, leaving open whether jointly optimizing both ID and OOD label selections could yield further gains.
