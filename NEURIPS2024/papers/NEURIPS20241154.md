## Problem

Despite the widespread adoption of preference-based learning (RLHF/DPO) for aligning language models, the field lacks a systematic understanding of which components—preference data, learning algorithm, reward model, and policy training prompts—drive downstream performance. Prior comparisons of PPO and DPO are limited to narrow domains or synthetic settings, and the relative importance of each component has not been disentangled under a unified evaluation protocol.

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes text prompts and produces text responses. Preference data consists of prompt–(chosen, rejected) response triples with scalar rankings; the reward model maps a prompt–response pair to a single scalar score; the policy model generates free-form text conditioned on a prompt. Evaluation spans 11 benchmarks across six capability categories (factuality, reasoning, coding, truthfulness, safety, instruction following), using both exact-match metrics (GSM8k, MMLU, HumanEval+) and LLM-as-a-judge metrics (AlpacaEval, TruthfulQA).

## Method

Starting from TULU 2 13B (a Llama 2 SFT checkpoint), the paper ablates four axes of preference-based learning under a fixed evaluation suite. (1) Preference data: 14 public datasets spanning web-scraped, human-annotated, and synthetic sources are compared via DPO, revealing that synthetic data with per-aspect GPT-4 annotations (UltraFeedback fine-grained) outperforms all others. (2) Algorithm: PPO and DPO are trained on identical data, showing PPO yields consistent gains in reasoning, coding, and safety while DPO degrades truthfulness. (3) Reward model: 13B and 70B reward models trained on single-dataset vs. multi-dataset mixtures are evaluated both directly (RewardBench, Best-of-N) and downstream via PPO, finding that larger RMs boost GSM by up to 5% but have marginal effects elsewhere. (4) Policy training prompts: domain-targeted prompts (e.g., GSM train set) improve math performance when paired with strong reward models, but remixed generalist prompts do not improve overall scores. The recommended recipe combines UltraFeedback fine-grained data, PPO with a 70B reward model, and optionally domain-specific prompts.

## Results

The best model (TULU 2+PPO 13B trained with a 70B UltraFeedback reward model) achieves 62.8 average across 11 benchmarks, outperforming the SFT baseline (56.8) by 6.0 points and the best DPO model (61.0) by 1.8 points. PPO provides the largest gains in reasoning (+1.3 over DPO), coding (+2.9), and safety (+2.3), while DPO retains a 2.5-point edge in truthfulness. On GSM8k specifically, the 70B reward model with targeted math prompts pushes accuracy from 46.0% (SFT) to 62.0%, a 16-point improvement. The Llama 3 8B variant with PPO and a 70B reward model reaches 65.8 average, the highest score in the study.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), PPO wins on average by 0.7 points across all datasets, with the largest margins in coding (+2.9), reasoning (+1.3), and safety (+2.3), but loses 2.5 points on truthfulness; the best PPO model (62.8) beats the best DPO model (61.0) by 1.8 points overall. Against Llama 2 Chat 13B (Touvron et al., 2023), the PPO model improves the average from 57.7 to 62.8 (+5.1), with particularly large gains in reasoning (24.7→53.9) and instruction following (51.2→55.8). Against Vicuna 1.5 13B (Zheng et al., 2023), the PPO model leads by 7.2 points (62.8 vs. 55.6), driven by superior reasoning (53.9 vs. 39.3) and truthfulness (72.3 vs. 62.8). Against Nous Hermes 13B (Teknium, 2023), the PPO model leads by 11.5 points (62.8 vs. 51.3), though Nous Hermes matches or exceeds PPO on coding (47.7 vs. 47.3) and truthfulness (80.5 vs. 72.3). Against the TULU 2 SFT baseline (Ivison et al., 2023), preference training adds 6.0 points on average, with the largest single-category gain in instruction following (+11.6) and truthfulness (+15.7), while factuality remains essentially unchanged (55.4→55.4).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the RLHF pipeline (Christiano et al., Ouyang et al.) and the DPO formulation (Rafailov et al.), using TULU 2 as the base model suite. While concurrent work (Xu et al. 2024, Tajwar et al. 2024) has compared DPO and PPO, those studies are limited to constrained domains, ground-truth rewards, or small synthetic settings. This paper departs from prior comparisons by conducting a controlled, multi-variable ablation across 14 preference datasets, two algorithm families, four reward model configurations, and varied policy training prompts, all under a unified 11-benchmark evaluation suite. It also extends the reward model literature (RewardBench, Lambert et al.) by explicitly measuring downstream policy performance rather than only direct RM accuracy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a concrete, empirically grounded recipe for preference-based learning (synthetic per-aspect data + PPO + large reward model) that practitioners can directly adopt. The finding that substantial reward model improvements (13B→70B, larger data mixtures) yield only marginal downstream gains—except in math—challenges the assumption that better reward models should proportionally improve policies, pointing to an open question about how to better leverage RM quality in PPO. Limitations include reliance on a single model suite (TULU 2), two base architectures (Llama 2/3), no multilingual evaluation, and the significant compute cost of PPO relative to DPO that is not explicitly quantified. Future work on iterative reward model updates, overoptimization control, and transferring findings to other base models remains important.
