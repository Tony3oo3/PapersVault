## Problem

Logical Credal Networks (LCNs) support representing imprecise probabilistic knowledge with cycles and probability bounds on arbitrary formulae, but prior work addressed only marginal inference. Abductive reasoning tasks—finding the most probable explanation of observed evidence (MAP) or partial explanation (Marginal MAP)—were entirely unexplored for LCNs. The challenge is compounded by the fact that, unlike graphical models, a single truth assignment in an LCN may correspond to multiple probability distributions, requiring new formal task definitions and inference algorithms.

## Input / Output

The method takes an LCN (a set of propositions with probability-labeled sentences specifying marginal and conditional probability bounds), a set of evidence propositions with known truth values, and a set of MAP propositions to explain. It produces a complete or partial truth assignment (the MAP assignment) that maximizes the lower (maximin) or upper (maximax) posterior probability given the evidence. A key distinction from graphical models is that evaluating any candidate assignment requires solving a non-linear program over 2^n interpretation variables, making each evaluation NP-hard rather than linear-time.

## Method

The paper formally defines maximin and maximax MAP/MMAP tasks for LCNs, then proposes three tiers of algorithms. For exact inference, it combines depth-first search, limited discrepancy search, and simulated annealing with exact MAP assignment evaluation via non-linear programming (solving the full marginal inference program at each leaf). For approximate inference, it extends the ARIEL message-passing scheme to directly extract MAP assignments from converged factor-to-proposition messages (AMAP), and introduces an augmented-LCN trick that encodes a candidate assignment as auxiliary propositions with deterministic conditional constraints, enabling cheap approximate evaluation during LDS or SA search (ALDS, ASA). The augmented-LCN approach replaces the expensive exact NLP solve with a single approximate marginal inference call, allowing the search-based methods to scale from ~8 propositions (exact) to 50–100+ propositions (approximate).

## Results

On 10 realistic LCN instances (Toy through Hepatitis), AMAP (message-passing) solves all instances in 0.85–8.22 seconds, while exact search methods (DFS, LDS, SA) exceed the 2-hour time limit on 5 of 10 instances (Credit, Suicide, Tank, Alarm, Hepatitis). Approximate search methods ALDS(3) and ASA solve all instances in 134–282 seconds. On random LCNs with n=70 propositions, AMAP completes in 79–132 seconds across polytree/DAG/random topologies, whereas exact methods cannot solve any instance within the time limit. On factuality LCNs with 100 atoms, AMAP solves all 10 instances in 1807 seconds while ALDS and ASA cannot complete.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three families of inference algorithms: exact search (DFS, LDS, SA), approximate message-passing (AMAP), and approximate search (ALDS, ASA). AMAP is the fastest by a wide margin, solving all realistic LCN instances in under 9 seconds, but it only guarantees a local optimum due to its independence assumption. ALDS and ASA, initialized from AMAP's solution, consistently find better (higher-scoring) MAP assignments on n=10 random LCNs (Figure 2), at a cost of roughly 20–30× the runtime of AMAP. Exact methods (DFS, LDS, SA) are sound and complete but are limited to problems with ≤8 propositions due to the NP-hard non-linear program required for each MAP assignment evaluation; they fail entirely on instances with ≥10 propositions within the 2-hour budget. The key practical takeaway is a two-stage pipeline: use AMAP for a fast initial solution, then refine with ALDS or ASA if the time budget permits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Logical Credal Network (LCN) framework from Marinescu et al. (NeurIPS 2022), which previously supported only marginal inference, to abductive reasoning via MAP and Marginal MAP tasks. The key conceptual departure from classical MAP/MMAP in probabilistic graphical models is that an LCN encodes a *set* of distributions over interpretations rather than a single distribution, necessitating the novel maximin/maximax formulations (maximizing lower or upper probability bounds). The approximate inference component builds directly on the ARIEL message-passing scheme (Marinescu et al., IJCAI 2023) and the credal marginal MAP work (Marinescu et al., NeurIPS 2023), adapting them from marginal to abductive settings. Classical search strategies (DFS, LDS, simulated annealing) from the graphical model literature are repurposed with LCN-specific exact or approximate assignment evaluations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work opens the door to using LCNs for explanation and diagnosis tasks in domains with imprecise or bounded knowledge, and the application to LLM factuality scoring (via FActScore-style atomic decomposition) is a timely and practically motivated use case. However, the practical impact is currently limited: exact methods handle only ≤8 propositions, and the approximate methods, while scaling to hundreds of variables, rely on an independence assumption that yields only local optima. Open questions include developing tighter bounding schemes for branch-and-bound search, understanding when the AMAP independence assumption is violated in practice, and validating the factuality application on real (non-random) LLM outputs with real knowledge bases.
