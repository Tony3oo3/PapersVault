## Problem

Language models finetuned on simple textual demonstrations of novel facts (e.g., 'The capital of Andoria is Copperton') fail to generalize that knowledge beyond straightforward question answering to reasoning tasks like multi-hop or indirect QA. The paper identifies the root cause: under causal language modeling, models preferentially learn word co-occurrence statistics as a shortcut rather than encoding true factual associations, and this shortcut representation does not support flexible reasoning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained transformer language model (LLaMA 3 8B/70B, Gemma 7B) and a text corpus expressing factual knowledge in either narrative form (explicit entity co-occurrence) or referencing form (implicit association via ad-hoc intermediate attributes like colors). The output is a finetuned model evaluated on five task types: simple QA, multiple choice, reverse QA, indirect commonsense reasoning, and 2-hop reasoning, all in a 5-shot closed-book format. The synthetic Country-city-animals dataset contains 40 novel facts (20 capital-city + 20 city-animal) with randomly generated entity names to ensure complete novelty to any pretrained model.

## Method

The paper first characterizes two distinct knowledge representations in transformers via layer-wise ablation of the finetuning parameter delta: co-occurrence statistics are parameterized across middle layers and only support simple QA, while true factual associations reside in the lower 1/3 of layers and support all reasoning tasks. Based on this, two strategies are proposed. First, training on 'referencing' text where the tail entity is linked to the head only through a temporary ad-hoc attribute (e.g., 'Paris is colored green; the capital of France is colored green') removes direct word co-occurrence and forces the model to learn the underlying factual association. Second, 'active forgetting' finetunes the model normally on narrative text, then resets the upper 2/3 layer parameters back to their pretrained values (clearing the co-occurrence shortcut), and finetunes again so the lower layers can continue learning factual associations with a non-zero loss signal. Both strategies are validated on synthetic data and real-world benchmarks (MQuAKE-T, 2WikiMultiHopQA), showing significant improvements in multi-hop and indirect reasoning accuracy.

## Results

On the synthetic Country-city-animals dataset, finetuning on Referencing (implicit association) text dramatically outperforms Narrative (explicit co-occurrence) text on reasoning tasks: LLaMA 3 8B achieves 92.5% on 2-hop reasoning vs. 38.8% for Narrative, and 97.5% on Reverse QA vs. 52.5%. On the real-world MQuAKE-T dataset, Referencing training yields 74.6% multi-hop QA accuracy vs. 61.3% for the original narrative form (LLaMA 3 8B). The active forgetting method further improves generalization from narrative text, boosting 2-hop reasoning from 38.8% to 51.2% on synthetic data and multi-hop QA from 61.3% to 66.2% on MQuAKE-T.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three training strategies: plain finetuning on narrative text (which learns co-occurrence statistics), finetuning on referencing/implicit-association text (which learns factual associations), and active forgetting (resetting upper-layer parameters mid-training to unblock factual association learning). Referencing text consistently and substantially outperforms narrative text across all reasoning tasks and all three model architectures (LLaMA 3 8B, LLaMA 3 70B, Gemma 7B), with the largest gap on 2-hop reasoning (e.g., 92.5% vs. 38.8% for LLaMA 3 8B). On MQuAKE-T, Referencing training improves multi-hop QA by 13.3 points over the original narrative form (74.6% vs. 61.3%, LLaMA 3 8B). Active forgetting improves over plain finetuning on all datasets (e.g., 51.2% vs. 38.8% on synthetic 2-hop; 66.2% vs. 61.3% on MQuAKE-T multi-hop), while simply restricting training to the lower 1/3 layers does not help and sometimes slightly hurts, confirming that co-occurrence statistics will be re-learned in lower layers if they are the only tunable parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on co-occurrence bias in language models (Kang & Choi 2023; Ju et al. 2024) by providing a mechanistic account: it shows that co-occurrence statistics and true factual associations are not just different in quality but are parameterized in distinct layers of the transformer (middle vs. lower 1/3). It draws on the shortcut learning literature to explain why the model preferentially learns co-occurrence, and on knowledge editing work (Meng et al. 2022, 2023) that independently identified lower-layer parameterization of factual associations. The active forgetting strategy repurposes parameter-reset techniques from language plasticity (Chen et al. 2023) and transfer learning (Li et al. 2020) for the specific goal of unblocking factual association learning during finetuning.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a concrete explanation for a widely observed but poorly understood failure mode: why finetuning on narrative text yields knowledge that passes simple QA but fails at multi-hop and indirect reasoning. The two proposed remedies—training on implicit-association text and active forgetting via upper-layer parameter reset—are simple and immediately actionable for practitioners doing continual pretraining or knowledge injection. Open questions include whether the layer-wise separation holds during pretraining from scratch, how to automatically generate implicit-association rewrites at scale, and whether the findings generalize beyond the relatively simple triplet facts studied here.
