## Problem

Modern RL algorithms require tuning ever-growing numbers of hyperparameters, and small changes can cause drastic performance shifts that vary across environments. Existing evaluation approaches—sensitivity curves, performance percentiles, and cross-environment benchmarks—do not quantify what proportion of an algorithm's reported performance gain is attributable to per-environment hyperparameter tuning versus inherent algorithmic quality. The community lacks a scalable, standardized methodology to characterize and compare this sensitivity across algorithms.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of RL algorithms with defined hyperparameter grids, a target distribution of environments, and performance data (AUC) from many stochastic runs per algorithm-environment-hyperparameter-seed combination (200 seeds per configuration in the experiments). Performance is [5,95]-percentile normalized per environment to enable cross-environment comparison, and divergent runs (>10% divergence rate) are filtered out. The outputs are a scalar sensitivity score per algorithm, a scalar effective dimensionality per algorithm, and a 2D performance-sensitivity plane that positions each algorithm relative to a chosen reference point in one of five qualitatively distinct regions.

## Method

The paper defines two complementary metrics. The hyperparameter sensitivity Φ(ω) is the difference between the per-environment tuned score (average over environments of the best normalized score achievable by tuning hyperparameters independently per environment) and the cross-environment tuned score (best single hyperparameter setting's average normalized score across all environments), using [5,95]-percentile-normalized AUC. The effective hyperparameter dimensionality d(ω) measures the minimum number of hyperparameters that must be tuned per environment to retain at least 95% of the per-environment tuned performance, computed by finding the largest subset of hyperparameters that can be frozen at the cross-environment optimal value while meeting the threshold. A performance-sensitivity plane visualizes algorithms in 2D (sensitivity vs. per-environment tuned score) relative to a reference algorithm, partitioning the space into five regions that encode trade-offs between performance gains and sensitivity costs. The methodology is validated on six PPO normalization variants (advantage per-minibatch zero-mean, advantage percentile scaling, advantage lower-bounded percentile scaling, value target symlog, observation zero-mean, observation symlog) via a 4.3M-run grid search over four hyperparameters spanning five orders of magnitude across five Brax MuJoCo domains, revealing that normalization variants which improve tuned performance also increase sensitivity.

## Results

Across 4.3 million runs (13 trillion environment steps) over 625 hyperparameter settings in 5 Brax MuJoCo environments (Ant, HalfCheetah, Hopper, Swimmer, Walker2d), no normalization variant simultaneously improved performance and reduced hyperparameter sensitivity. All advantage normalization variants increased per-environment tuned performance but also increased sensitivity: per-minibatch zero-mean normalization fell in Region 2 (performance gain exceeds sensitivity increase), while both percentile scaling variants fell in Region 4 (sensitivity increase exceeds performance gain). Value target symlog degraded performance and may have slightly increased sensitivity (Region 5). For effective hyperparameter dimensionality, some variants (e.g., PPO, observation symlog) reach 95% of peak performance after tuning only 3 of 4 hyperparameters, while advantage percentile scaling requires tuning all 4.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper uses unnormalized PPO as the reference point and compares six normalization variants on the performance-sensitivity plane. Advantage per-minibatch zero-mean normalization is the only variant that lands in Region 2, meaning its performance gain (e.g., +57.8 on HalfCheetah, +7.7 on Swimmer) outweighs its sensitivity increase. Both advantage percentile scaling and lower-bounded percentile scaling fall in Region 4: they improve performance (e.g., +35.4 and +20.3 on Walker2d respectively) but at a greater cost in sensitivity, indicating enhanced reliance on per-environment tuning. Value target symlog is the only variant in Region 5, degrading performance substantially on Ant (8.57 vs. 38.95) and HalfCheetah (1731 vs. 2312) while possibly increasing sensitivity. Observation normalization variants (zero-mean and symlog) show negligible performance changes and may slightly reduce sensitivity, though confidence intervals are too wide to confirm. On effective hyperparameter dimensionality, advantage percentile scaling shifts the 95%-of-peak threshold to require tuning all 4 hyperparameters, whereas PPO and observation symlog reach that threshold with only 3 tuned hyperparameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in the evaluation of RL algorithms by introducing two quantitative metrics—hyperparameter sensitivity (Φ) and effective hyperparameter dimensionality (d)—along with a performance-sensitivity plane for graphical analysis. It departs from prior approaches that addressed related but narrower questions: sensitivity curves (Sutton & Barto) handle only single hyperparameters in single environments, performance percentiles (Jordan et al., 2020) assume random sampling rather than practitioner-style tuning, and the Cross-environment Hyperparameter Benchmark (Patterson et al., 2024) focuses on finding one universally good setting rather than quantifying the gap between per-environment and cross-environment tuning. The experimental study builds on PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), the PureJaxRL implementation (Lu et al., 2022), and DreamerV3 normalization tricks (Hafner et al., 2023; Sullivan et al., 2023), but the core contribution is the evaluation methodology itself rather than any algorithmic modification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that normalization variants which improve PPO's tuned performance simultaneously increase its hyperparameter sensitivity challenges the widely held assumption that normalization makes RL algorithms easier to tune, suggesting some reported performance gains may be artifacts of increased tuning effort rather than genuine algorithmic improvements. The methodology provides practitioners with a concrete, reproducible framework for evaluating algorithms along the sensitivity dimension beyond benchmark scores, which is increasingly important as hyperparameter counts continue to grow. Key limitations include dependence on the chosen environment distribution, score normalization method, and sweep granularity; the study covers only 5 MuJoCo environments and 4 hyperparameters of PPO. Open questions include whether the methodology generalizes to larger algorithm families, whether AutoRL methods reduce sensitivity of their base algorithms, and how the metrics behave under different environment distributions.
