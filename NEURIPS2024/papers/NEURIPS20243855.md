## Problem

Video MAE pre-training has been confined to short clips (16–32 frames) because the dense self-attention decoder that reconstructs all masked tokens scales quadratically with sequence length, quickly exhausting GPU/TPU memory. This short context prevents the encoder from learning long-range temporal dynamics needed for complex actions. The paper asks whether decoder masking can make 128-frame MAE pre-training feasible, and whether the resulting long-context encoder genuinely outperforms short-context baselines.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243855/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

During pre-training, the model consumes 128-frame video clips partitioned into 16×16×2 spatio-temporal tubelets (yielding 8×14×14 tokens at 112×112 spatial resolution) and produces reconstructed FSQ-MagViT quantized tokens (18-bit codebook) for only the top-15% salient decoder-visible positions plus a 5% random fraction. The adaptive tokenizer itself is trained on 16-frame clips at 128×128 and then frozen; for longer videos it is applied via a sliding 16-frame window with stride 16. During fine-tuning, the same encoder outputs action, verb, or noun class logits evaluated on a single temporal crop.

## Method

The core contribution is an adaptive decoder masking strategy built on a jointly trained FSQ-MagViT tokenizer and a CNN-based token scorer with a differentiable top-k selection layer. The token scorer embeds the video with two strided CNN layers and computes pairwise Euclidean distances between corresponding spatial locations in adjacent frames, using these distances as a learned saliency signal; the top-k tokens (k=768 in the tokenizer, 15% budget in MAE) are designated for decoding while the rest are zeroed out. Reconstruction targets are the quantized FSQ-MagViT tokens rather than raw RGB pixels, which the authors show improves downstream accuracy by ~3 points over pixel targets. The adaptive tokenizer is trained end-to-end (pixel + GAN + perceptual losses) on Kinetics-600 and then frozen, decoupling token-importance learning from MAE gradients to avoid biasing selection toward easy-to-reconstruct tokens. With only 15% of tokens decoded, the quadratic decoder cost drops enough to fit 128-frame sequences in memory, enabling full-model (not partial-freeze) MAE pre-training at long context and yielding consistent gains over 32-frame pre-training on EPIC-Kitchens-100 and Diving48.

## Results

On EPIC-Kitchens-100, LVMAE (ViT-L) with unlabeled Kinetics-710 pre-training achieves 75.5% top-1 Verb accuracy, surpassing the previous SOTA (Avion, 73.0%) by 2.5 points, and 50.9% top-1 Action accuracy. On Diving48, LVMAE (ViT-L) reaches 94.9% top-1 accuracy, improving over the prior best (MC-ViT-L, 91.0%) by 3.9 points while using only ~1M unlabeled videos versus 27M video-text pairs. The ViT-B variant without any extra pre-training data still achieves 47.3% Action and 73.1% Verb on EK100, and 87.8% on Diving48. On FineGym288, LVMAE (92.8%) edges out the prior SOTA TCT (92.6%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

On EPIC-Kitchens-100 Verb classification, LVMAE (ViT-L) with unlabeled K710 pre-training achieves 75.5%, outperforming Avion (73.0%) by 2.5 points, TAdaFormer-L (71.7%) by 3.8 points, and LaViLa (72.0%) by 3.5 points, all while using no labeled video-text pairs during pre-training. On Noun classification, LVMAE (59.6–61.8%) trails Avion (65.4%) and TAdaFormer-L (64.1%), which the authors attribute to the prevalence of noun categories in large-scale labeled pre-training data. On Diving48, LVMAE (ViT-L) achieves 94.9%, beating MC-ViT-L (91.0%) by 3.9 points, Video-FocalNet-B (90.8%) by 4.1 points, and AIM ViT-L/14 (90.6%) by 4.3 points, despite using ~1M unlabeled videos versus 27M video-text pairs for MC-ViT. On Something-Something-V2, LVMAE (71.0% at 64 frames) exceeds both VideoMAE V1 and V2 for the Base model despite fewer pre-training epochs and single-crop evaluation. On FineGym288, LVMAE (92.8%) marginally surpasses TCT (92.6%) without using explicit temporal cues. The adaptive decoder masking strategy consistently outperforms Random, Uniform, Flow, and EVEREST baselines at both 32 and 128 frames across all evaluated datasets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the dual-masking framework of VideoMAE V2 by replacing its content-agnostic decoder masking (random or uniform) with a learned, content-adaptive token importance scheme. The importance module is built on MAGVIT's 3D-CNN tokenizer augmented with Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ) and a differentiable top-k selection layer, trained end-to-end to jointly learn tokenization and token priority. Unlike concurrent work EVEREST, which learns token saliency during MAE pre-training itself (risking bias toward easy tokens), this paper decouples the importance learning from the MAE objective, and unlike motion-guided approaches (MGMAE, MGM), it does not rely on explicit optical flow or codec motion vectors. The encoder masking remains standard randomized tube masking from VideoMAE, keeping the architectural core simple.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first clear evidence that long-video (128-frame) MAE pre-training yields consistently better encoders than short-video (32-frame) pre-training, even when the short model is fine-tuned on long videos—suggesting the pre-training context length itself shapes the learned representations in ways that fine-tuning cannot recover. The adaptive decoder masking technique is a practical, drop-in improvement that any video MAE pipeline could adopt to reduce memory by ~4x while maintaining or improving accuracy. Limitations include restriction to moderate model sizes (ViT-B/L) and datasets, no code release, and the 128-frame context, while a significant leap from 16-32 frames, still falls short of truly long-form video understanding. Open questions include whether the approach scales to hour-long videos (potentially combined with memory modules), whether the decoupled importance learning remains optimal at larger scales, and whether the FSQ-MagViT token targets generalize beyond action classification.
