## Problem

Open-ended value evaluation of LLM-generated responses faces two unresolved challenges: prompt-based large LLM evaluators struggle to adapt to less common or evolving value systems (e.g., Schwartz Basic Values, Moral Foundation Theory), while fine-tuned small LLM evaluators overfit to training distributions and lose robustness to textual perturbations and out-of-domain scenarios. No existing framework simultaneously achieves adaptability to arbitrary value definitions with minimal annotation and generalizability across diverse expressions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241876/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a provocative scenario prompt paired with an LLM-generated response (open-ended text with no ground-truth reference) and a value dimension drawn from one of three value systems (14 social risk categories, 10 Schwartz basic values, or 5 moral foundations). It outputs a discrete label indicating whether the response adheres to, opposes, or is unrelated to the specified value; the social risk system uses a binary formulation (carry risk / not carry risk) while the other two use three classes. The framework requires fewer than 100 human-labeled samples per value type for calibration.

## Method

CLAVE connects a large proprietary LLM (GPT-4) and a small fine-tuned LLM (e.g., Mistral-7B via LoRA) through an intermediate representation of generalized value concepts. In a training phase, the large LLM extracts value concepts from clustered batches of labeled samples (K-Means on text embeddings), which are then deduplicated via hierarchical clustering on concept embeddings to form a compact concept set per value dimension. At inference, the large LLM extracts concepts from the new text and maps them to the pre-built concept set via embedding similarity retrieval (threshold θ=0.7), and the small LLM—fine-tuned with negative-log-likelihood loss on the concept-augmented inputs—predicts the final label. This design lets the large model handle scenario diversity and expression variation (generalizability) while the small model aligns with human value definitions (adaptability), achieving a superior balance compared to either model alone.

## Results

On the ValEval benchmark (13k+ samples across three value systems), CLAVE-Mistral (GPT-4 + Mistral-7B) achieves the best or near-best accuracy across all nine evaluation splits. On Social Risks, it reaches 88.36% (Original), 83.99% (Perturbed), and 88.65% (Generalized); on Schwartz Values, 75.26%, 75.05%, and 82.45%; and on Moral Foundations, 67.56%, 51.25%, and 35.25%. The key advantage is robustness: on the Perturbed and Generalized splits, CLAVE-Mistral outperforms fine-tuned Mistral-7B by 7.5–35 percentage points and fine-tuned GPT-3.5-Turbo by 2–15 points, while requiring only a single A100 GPU and ~$50 in API costs versus $90–$106 for fine-tuning larger models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CLAVE-Mistral's primary advantage over fine-tuned small LLMs (Mistral-7b, Llama2-7b, Phi-3) is robustness: on the Perturbed split it gains 7.5 pp over Mistral-7b on Social Risks (83.99 vs 76.50) and 4.2 pp on Schwartz (75.05 vs 70.89), while on the Generalized (OOD) split the gap widens dramatically to 35.1 pp on Social Risks (88.65 vs 53.51) and 6.3 pp on Schwartz (82.45 vs 76.19). Against fine-tuned large LLMs (GPT-3.5-Turbo, Llama2-70b), CLAVE-Mistral matches or exceeds them on Perturbed and Generalized splits (e.g., 88.65 vs 74.05 on Social Risks Generalized; 82.45 vs 83.31 on Schwartz Generalized) at a fraction of the cost ($50 vs $90–$106). Prompt-based evaluators (GPT-4 with various prompting strategies) remain competitive on Social Risks Original (84–91%) but collapse on less common value systems: Schwartz Original drops to 40–56% and Moral Foundation Original to 25–56%, whereas CLAVE-Mistral maintains 75.26% and 67.56% respectively. Value-specific evaluators (Kaleido-xl, Value Fulcra, DeNEVIL) are highly domain-bound and perform poorly outside their training distribution (e.g., Kaleido-xl scores only 38.54% on Social Risks). Overall, CLAVE-Mistral achieves the best balance of adaptability (matching fine-tuned models on Original splits) and generalizability (substantially outperforming them on Perturbed and OOD splits) across all three value systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of LLM-as-evaluator research (prompt-based methods like G-Eval, FairEval, ChatEval, and tuning-based methods like Auto-J, Kaleidoscope, Value Fulcra, DeNEVIL) and the emerging literature on combining large and small LLMs (distillation, cascading, routing). It departs from prior combination strategies by arguing that in value evaluation specifically, large and small models are *complementary* rather than hierarchically ordered: large closed-source models excel at generalizability across diverse expressions and scenarios, while small fine-tuned models excel at adaptability to specific human value definitions. The novel architectural bridge is the 'value concept' — a highly generalized intermediate representation extracted by the large model and used as input to the small model, decoupling robust text comprehension from value-specific judgment. The ValEval benchmark extends existing datasets (BeaverTails, Value Fulcra, DeNEVIL, Moral Stories, Do-not-Answer) by adding perturbation and OOD splits for systematic robustness evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CLAVE provides a practical, cost-effective framework (single A100 + GPT-4 API, ~$50 per value system) that outperforms fine-tuned large LLMs (GPT-3.5-Turbo, Llama-2-70B) while requiring <100 annotated samples per value type, making value evaluation accessible for less popular or evolving value systems. ValEval standardizes evaluation across three major value systems with explicit robustness testing, filling a gap where prior benchmarks lacked perturbation and OOD splits. Open questions include: whether the static concept set can be dynamically updated as new scenarios emerge, whether the framework extends to multilingual settings, and whether the transparency of value concepts can be leveraged for explainable AI auditing. The paper acknowledges limitations in English-only coverage, three value systems, and the fixed large/small model pairing.
