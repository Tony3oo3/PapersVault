## Problem

Existing semantic similarity benchmarks (MRPC, QQP, STS) evaluate semantic equivalence without explicitly controlling for lexical influence, making it unclear whether models truly encode meaning or merely exploit surface-form overlap. The prior SUGARCREPE dataset only provides a two-caption (one positive, one negative) format, which cannot disentangle whether a model's success stems from recognizing semantic equivalence or from detecting lexical dissimilarity. This paper asks: can VLMs and ULMs reliably identify semantic equivalence when the surface lexical composition changes, and can they detect semantic differences when lexical composition is held nearly constant?

## Input / Output

Each sample pairs an MS-COCO image with a triplet of captions: P1 (original positive), P2 (a semantically equivalent but lexically distinct paraphrase of P1), and N (a hard negative that is lexically similar to P1 but semantically different). Two evaluation modes are supported: Image-to-Text (ITT), where the model scores each caption against the image embedding, and Text-to-Text (TOT), where the model scores each caption against the other positive caption's embedding. A sample is a hit only if both P1 and P2 outscore N, yielding a binary per-sample score aggregated into accuracy.

## Method

The dataset is constructed by extending SUGARCREPE's 4757 image-caption pairs with a second positive caption P2 generated via an instruction-tuned Mistral 7B model conditioned on a composite meta-prompt combining role-play, explicit rules, and few-shot demonstrations. An iterative generation loop rejects duplicates and superfluous outputs, and a separate validator LLM agent (same model, different prompt) checks semantic consistency of P2 against P1, triggering regeneration on failure. Two human experts then correct residual errors (hallucinations, spatial inversions, meaning shifts) and validate the full triplet. The five hard-negative categories (swap/replace of objects, attributes, and relations) are inherited from SUGARCREPE, and the design guarantees that P1–P2 pairs have low lexical/syntactic similarity (SLS ≈ 0.2–0.4) while P1–N pairs have high similarity (SLS > 0.75), creating a controlled 3-way semantic (in)equivalence probe. Evaluation uses cosine similarity between CLIP-style or ULM text/image embeddings, with no fine-tuning required for the primary benchmark.

## Results

On SUGARCREPE++, the best VLM (FLAVA) achieves 54.4% ITT accuracy on Swap Object, 59.2% on Swap Attribute, 89.6% on Replace Object, 73.4% on Replace Attribute, and 60.1% on Replace Relation, compared to human performance of 96.7–100%. The CLIP baseline scores 45.2%, 45.2%, 86.8%, 65.6%, and 56.3% respectively. Among ULMs, the best (Angle-Llama-7b-nli-v2) reaches only 38.0% on Swap Object and 45.8% on Swap Attribute in the text-only setting, versus 96.7% and 93.3% for humans. GPT-4o with a 3-way prompt achieves 67.6% (Swap Object) to 96.3% (Replace Object), approaching human levels on replace-type tasks but still falling 29–32 points short on swap-type tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FLAVA, trained with multiple pre-training objectives, outperforms the CLIP baseline by 9.2 points on Swap Object ITT (54.4% vs. 45.2%) and 13.9 points on Replace Relation ITT (60.1% vs. 56.3%), and is the best VLM on 4 of 5 subsets. Models trained with contrastive loss alone (CLIP, ALIGN, ALIP) consistently underperform multi-objective models (FLAVA, BLIP, ALBEF), with ALBEF scoring as low as 28.9% on Swap Object ITT. Among ULMs, Angle-Llama-7b-nli-v2 (62.3× BERT params) achieves the best average TOT accuracy (69.0%), still trailing human performance (96.4%) by ~27 points, and all ULMs score below 50% on Swap Object. Compositionality-enhancing methods NegCLIP and CLIP-SVLC improve over CLIP on most subsets (e.g., NegCLIP: 55.3% vs. 45.2% on Swap Object ITT), while BLIP-SGVL and CyCLIP degrade, confirming that text encoder strength is the key bottleneck. Notably, ALBEF and XVLM rank above CLIP on SUGARCREPE but drop below it on SUGARCREPE++, revealing that compositionality benchmarks do not capture lexical sensitivity. GPT-4o with a 3-way prompt (Prompt-3) reaches 96.3% on Replace Object and 93.3% on Replace Attribute—near human levels—but only 67.6% on Swap Object, a 32-point gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends SUGARCREPE (Hsieh et al., NeurIPS 2023), which posed a 2-way caption selection task to test VLM compositionality, by adding a second semantically-equivalent but lexically-different positive caption to create a 3-way semantic (in)equivalence problem. It also draws on the broader semantic text similarity literature (MRPC, QQP, STS) and compositionality benchmarks (Winoground, CREPE, VL-Checklist), but departs from them by explicitly controlling for lexical variation to isolate whether models encode semantics beyond surface form. The generation pipeline leverages instruction-tuned Mistral 7B with iterative meta-prompting and LLM-as-validator, building on recent LLM-agent approaches for data curation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a substantial and consistent gap (10–50%) between VLM/ULM performance and human performance, particularly on swap-object and replace-relation subsets, confirming that current models conflate lexical overlap with semantic equivalence. A key open question the paper surfaces is whether compositionality (as measured by SUGARCREPE or Winoground) is sufficient for robust semantic understanding, since models that excel on SUGARCREPE can degrade sharply on SUGARCREPE++. The identification of the text encoder as the primary bottleneck, combined with the finding that even GPT-4o struggles on swap tasks, points toward targeted improvements in text representation learning rather than simply scaling vision encoders.
