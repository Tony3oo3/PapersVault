## Problem

Existing LLM-generated text detection benchmarks (TuringBench, MGTBench, MULTITuDE, MAGE, M4) evaluate detectors under idealized conditions, overlooking critical real-world factors such as diverse prompt usages, human post-editing (word substitutions, paraphrasing), adversarial writing noises (spelling errors), and mixed human-LLM content. This paper asks: how do state-of-the-art detectors actually perform when confronted with these practical challenges, and which factors most undermine their robustness and generalization?

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes text samples spanning four domains (academic abstracts, news articles, creative stories, social media reviews) generated by four LLMs (GPT-3.5-turbo, PaLM-2-bison, Claude-instant, Llama-2-70b), each subjected to five attack conditions (direct, prompt, paraphrase, perturbation, data mixing) and length-augmented into 20-word intervals up to 360 words. Detectors produce a binary human/LLM-generated prediction, scored via AUROC (for threshold-free zero-shot evaluation) and F1 (balancing precision and recall), with detailed per-domain, per-LLM, and per-attack breakdowns across four structured tasks.

## Method

DetectRL constructs a comprehensive benchmark by (1) collecting 2,800 human-written samples per domain from pre-ChatGPT sources to avoid contamination, (2) generating matched LLM texts via domain-specific prompts (title→abstract, summary→article, prompt→story, first-sentence→review), and (3) applying a taxonomy of attacks: prompt-based (few-shot, ICO/SICO), paraphrase-based (DIPPER, Google Translate back-translation, LLM polishing), perturbation-based (DeepWordBug character-level, TextFooler word-level, TextBugger sentence-level via TextAttack), and data mixing (multi-LLM sentence splicing, LLM-centered 75/25 substitution). A sentence-level splitting augmentation creates 20-word length intervals to stress-test length sensitivity. Four evaluation tasks are defined: in-domain robustness (multi-domain/LLM/attack), cross-distribution generalization (train on one, test on another), varying text length (training-time and test-time), and real-world human writing (attacks applied to human texts to simulate post-editing). Fifteen detectors—eleven zero-shot (DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars, DNA-GPT, Revise-Detect, LRR, NPR, Log-Rank, Rank, Log-Likelihood, Entropy) and four supervised (RoBERTa/XLM-RoBERTa base/large)—are benchmarked under identical protocols, revealing that supervised classifiers maintain >97% AUROC while zero-shot methods drop to ~39% under perturbation attacks.

## Results

On the DetectRL benchmark, supervised detectors (RoBERTa/XLM-RoBERTa classifiers) dominate, with Rob-Base achieving the highest overall average score of 93.02 and maintaining >99% AUROC across multi-domain and multi-LLM settings. Among zero-shot methods, Binoculars leads with 79.61% average, but all zero-shot detectors suffer severe degradation under adversarial perturbation attacks (average AUROC drops to ~39.24%) and in the multi-LLM setting (average AUROC of only 58.61%). Notably, DetectGPT and Entropy—previously considered SOTA—achieve the lowest scores (29.05% and 28.01% average respectively), performing near or below random chance in several sub-settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Supervised RoBERTa/XLM-RoBERTa classifiers overwhelmingly outperform all nine zero-shot baselines, with Rob-Base achieving 93.02% average vs. the best zero-shot method Binoculars at 79.61%—a 13.4-point gap. Among zero-shot methods, Binoculars [Hans et al., ICML 2024] is the clear leader, outperforming Revise-Detect. [Zhu et al., EMNLP 2023] by 15.5 points and Log-Rank [Gehrmann et al., ACL 2019] by 17.1 points on average. DetectGPT [Mitchell et al., ICML 2023] and Entropy [Lavergne et al., 2008] perform worst, scoring 29.05% and 28.01% respectively—near random—despite being previously considered SOTA. Fast-DetectGPT [Bao et al., 2023] (55.33%) and DNA-GPT [Yang et al., ICLR 2024] (60.70%) also underperform relative to simpler baselines like Log-Rank (62.48%) and LRR (62.46%). The gap between supervised and zero-shot detectors widens dramatically under perturbation attacks (97.22% vs. 39.24% average AUROC) and in the multi-LLM setting (98.09% vs. 58.61%), where Claude-generated text is particularly challenging for all zero-shot methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself against a lineage of LLM-generated text detection benchmarks (TuringBench, MGTBench, MULTITuDE, MAGE, M4) that evaluate detectors under idealized conditions—matching prompts, clean generation, and no adversarial manipulation. DetectRL departs from this paradigm by introducing a realistic adversarial evaluation framework that incorporates prompt attacks (Few-Shot, SICO/ICO), paraphrase attacks (DIPPER, back-translation, LLM polishing), perturbation attacks (DeepWordBug, TextFooler, TextBugger via TextAttack), and data mixing, all applied to texts from four high-risk domains. On the detector side, it benchmarks both zero-shot methods (DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars, DNA-GPT, Revise-Detect, Log-Rank, LRR, NPR) and supervised classifiers (RoBERTa, XLM-RoBERTa), but its core contribution is the benchmark construction methodology rather than a new detection algorithm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that the field's most celebrated zero-shot detectors (DetectGPT, NPR, Fast-DetectGPT, DNA-GPT) are effectively non-functional in realistic deployment settings, with average scores below 60% and some below 30%, while supervised classifiers maintain >90% performance. This finding has immediate practical implications: organizations deploying detection systems cannot rely on the zero-shot methods that dominate recent literature. The open-source framework also enables the community to rapidly extend the benchmark as new LLMs and attack methods emerge, addressing the cat-and-mouse dynamic. Key open questions include whether zero-shot detection can ever be made robust to adversarial perturbations, how detectors should adapt to the rapid proliferation of new LLMs with distinct statistical signatures, and whether the supervised approach's dependence on training data from known LLMs limits its long-term viability.
