## Problem

Three mainstream graph contrastive learning frameworks (InfoNCE-based, DGI-like, and BGRL-like) achieve comparable performance through fundamentally different mechanisms, yet their shared underlying factor has remained unidentified. This paper reveals that all three implicitly exploit a common mechanism—representation scattering—but fail to fully leverage it: negative-sample-based methods incur high computational cost and introduce false-negative bias, while bootstrapping methods rely on Batch Normalization as an indirect, unguided form of scattering. Additionally, none of these frameworks account for the interconnected topology of graphs when performing scattering.

## Input / Output

SGRL takes a graph's node feature matrix X ∈ R^{N×D} and adjacency matrix A ∈ R^{N×N} as input, requiring no data augmentation or manually designed positive/negative sample pairs. It outputs dense node representations H ∈ R^{N×K} from a single-layer GCN encoder, which are then used for downstream node classification (via a linear logistic regression probe trained on 10% of labels) and node clustering (via K-means on the learned embeddings). The method operates entirely in self-supervised mode, with no label information needed during representation learning.

## Method

SGRL introduces two complementary mechanisms operating on separate encoders. The Representation Scattering Mechanism (RSM) uses a target encoder to produce node representations, projects them onto a unit hypersphere via L2 normalization, computes the mean as a scattered center c, and minimizes a center-away loss that directly pushes each representation away from c—eliminating the need for negative samples entirely. The Topology-based Constraint Mechanism (TCM) uses an online encoder to generate topologically aggregated representations by summing original embeddings with k-order neighbor aggregations (Â^k H_online + H_online), then a predictor maps these to align with the target encoder's scattered representations. The adversarial tension between scattering (RSM) and aggregation (TCM) is balanced via Exponential Moving Average updates of the target encoder from the online encoder at each epoch, enabling adaptive scattering that preserves local topology while achieving global representation diversity.

## Results

SGRL achieves the highest F1-score across all five benchmark datasets for node classification: 79.40% (WikiCS), 90.23% (Computers), 93.95% (Photo), 94.15% (Co.CS), and 96.23% (Co.Physics). On average, SGRL outperforms the three mainstream GCL baselines—BGRL, GRACE, and DGI—by 1.23%, 2.14%, and 3.26% respectively. For node clustering, SGRL achieves the best NMI on 3 out of 5 datasets (Computers: 0.5380, Co-CS: 0.7961, Co-Physics: 0.7232) and best Homogeneity on 2 out of 5, while remaining competitive on the rest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SGRL outperforms all 12 baselines on every dataset where a comparison is possible. Against the three mainstream GCL frameworks, SGRL beats BGRL (the strongest baseline) by 1.23% average F1, GRACE by 2.14%, and DGI by 3.26%. Among advanced methods, SGRL surpasses ProGCL (the best negative-sampling method) by up to 0.48% on Co.CS and avoids the OOM issues that affect ProGCL, GCA, and GRACE on Co.Physics. SGRL also outperforms augmentation-free methods AFGRL (by up to 0.88% on Co.CS) and iGCL (by up to 1.46% on Co.Physics), as well as the supervised GCN baseline on all five datasets, demonstrating that the self-supervised scattering mechanism captures more discriminative structure than even label-guided training. On clustering, SGRL achieves the best NMI on Computers (0.5380 vs. BGRL's 0.5364), Co-CS (0.7961 vs. BGRL's 0.7732), and Co-Physics (0.7232 vs. DGI's 0.6540), while DGI retains a slight edge on WikiCS NMI (0.4312 vs. 0.4188).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the BGRL bootstrapping architecture (online/target encoders with EMA and a predictor) but reinterprets it through a new theoretical lens. Its central conceptual departure is unifying three major GCL paradigms—DGI (JS divergence between original and shuffled graphs), GRACE/InfoNCE (negative-sample-based uniformity), and BGRL (Batch Normalization as implicit scattering)—under a single mechanism called 'representation scattering' (center-away + uniformity on a subspace). Rather than proposing yet another augmentation or negative-sampling strategy, SGRL replaces the indirect scattering mechanisms of prior work with an explicit center-away loss on a hypersphere (RSM) and a topology-aware constraint (TCM), thereby eliminating the need for data augmentation entirely.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unification of three seemingly disparate GCL frameworks under representation scattering provides a cleaner theoretical foundation for the field and suggests that future GCL design should explicitly optimize scattering rather than relying on indirect proxies (negative samples, BN, shuffling). However, the practical gains are modest (1–3% F1 over strong baselines), the method is validated only on five homogeneous citation/co-purchase graphs with a single-layer GCN encoder, and the authors themselves flag heterogeneous graphs and the RSM–TCM balancing strategy as open problems. Whether the scattering principle generalizes beyond GCL to other self-supervised graph tasks remains to be shown.
