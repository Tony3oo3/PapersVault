## Problem

Transformer-based LLMs make next-token predictions that depend on their context in ways that remain poorly understood. While it is known that LLMs exploit training data statistics, the precise functional form of this dependence—how context tokens are selectively used, dropped, or aggregated—has not been characterized. This paper asks whether LLM predictions can be approximated by simple N-gram-based rules derived from training data counts, and what this reveals about learning dynamics, overfitting, and the structure of model predictions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a token context C (up to 7 tokens) and pre-computed N-gram count tables from the training corpus (tabulated via distributed map-reduce over sliding windows). For each context, it produces the optimal rule from a ruleset: a regular expression over the context (specifying which tokens are kept, dropped, or marginalized), the count of training-data N-grams matching that expression, and the variational or L∞ distance between the rule's predictive distribution and the LLM's next-token distribution. The ruleset parameter M controls maximum context length, and three ruleset families (suffix, subgram, all) vary which token operations are permitted.

## Method

The core mechanism defines N-gram rules via regular expressions: given a context C and a pattern σ over {+, −, *}, each token is mapped to itself (+), the empty string (−), or a wildcard (*), producing a fuzzy-match pattern whose matching N-gram counts in training data yield a next-token distribution R_σ(t|C). Three rulesets are constructed by restricting σ: suffix (all +), subgram (+ and −), and all (+, −, *), with M bounding the pattern length. The optimal rule for a context is the one minimizing variational distance to the LLM's prediction, and the paper tracks this optimal rule distance across training steps, model runs, and context counts. Key empirical findings include an approximation criterion (low model variance across runs implies low optimal rule distance), a curriculum learning signature (predictions shift from simple to complex rules over training), and a holdout-free overfitting detector (monitoring short-context training loss). On TinyStories, the full ruleset R_7^all achieves 79% top-1 agreement with LLM predictions, and on Wikipedia the corresponding figure is 68%.

## Results

On TinyStories (160M model, 100 validation stories), the full ruleset R_7^all achieves 78.0% top-1 accuracy between the per-prediction optimal N-gram rule and the LLM's next-token distribution, compared to 56.7% for the stupid-backoff baseline. Using the L∞ distance for rule selection yields a slightly higher 78.9%. On Wikipedia (1.4B model, 10 holdout chunks), the corresponding figures are 65.0% (variational distance) and 67.7% (L∞ distance) for R_7^all versus 43.9% for backoff. Notably, the 78% rule-approximation accuracy on TinyStories exceeds the model's own 69.6% top-1 accuracy against ground truth, indicating that LLM predictions are often well-described by simple N-gram statistics of the training data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's sole explicit baseline is 'stupid backoff' (Brants et al. [4]), a single N-gram rule that performs standard backoff over M tokens of context. The proposed rulesets consistently and substantially outperform this baseline, with the gap widening as context length M increases: at M=7 on TinyStories, R_7^all achieves 78.0% top-1 accuracy versus 56.7% for backoff (a 21.3-point margin), while R_7^subgram reaches 71.1% and R_7^suffix reaches 65.6%. The same pattern holds on Wikipedia, where R_7^all scores 65.0% (variational) or 67.7% (L∞) against 43.9% for backoff. Among the proposed rulesets themselves, allowing wildcard (marginalized) tokens in the rule context (R^all) provides the largest gains over pure subgram or suffix rules, particularly at larger M, confirming that the ability to drop or marginalize context tokens is critical for matching LLM predictions on novel holdout contexts.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of N-gram-based analyses of LLMs (infini-gram [19], in-context learning N-gram studies [1, 23], synthetic Markov chain analyses [9]) by introducing a richer rule formalism where each context token can be kept, discarded, or marginalized, yielding a combinatorial family of rules rather than a single longest-match or vanilla N-gram model. Unlike neuron-level studies [28] or representational analyses [27], it treats the transformer as a black box and works entirely from training-data statistics on natural language. The paper also connects to the broader interpretability literature on rule extraction [15, 21] and learning trajectories [7, 8], but is the first to systematically quantify how well structured N-gram rules approximate transformer next-token distributions and to derive practical criteria (overfitting detection, model-variance threshold) from this approximation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that 79% of TinyStories and 68% of Wikipedia next-token predictions are matched by simple N-gram rules provides a concrete, quantifiable lower bound on the 'statistical' component of LLM behavior, which is directly relevant to understanding brittleness, dataset bias, and memorization. The holdout-free overfitting criterion (monitoring short-context train loss) is a practically useful technique if it generalizes to larger models and datasets, though this remains untested. Key limitations include the restriction to small models (≤1.4B) and relatively simple datasets, the purely descriptive (not explanatory) nature of the rules, and the open question of whether an analogous framework using distributional categories rather than individual tokens could capture higher-level reasoning.
