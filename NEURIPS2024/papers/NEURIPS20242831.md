## Problem

DNN-based encoding models of the ventral visual cortex have been evaluated almost exclusively on random in-distribution splits, leaving it unknown whether they can predict neural responses when test images differ from training images even in low-level attributes like contrast or hue. This paper asks whether such distribution shifts degrade neural predictivity, whether model rankings are conserved across shifts, and whether the magnitude of the shift can be quantified to predict the performance drop.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242831/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes 8,233 unique natural images (83 ms presentations, 300+ semantic categories) and corresponding multiunit spike-count firing rates recorded from 640 electrodes across V2, V4, CIT, and AIT in seven macaques over 109 sessions. A linear ridge-regression model maps pre-trained DNN feature vectors (extracted from the pre-final or intermediate layers of 8 architectures) to trial-averaged neuronal responses, producing ceiling-normalized squared Pearson correlations (r²_pred/r²_cons) as the predictivity metric. The key evaluation outputs are OOD/InD performance ratios per attribute and hold-out strategy, and the Closest Cosine Distance (D_CCD) computed from ResNet18 features to quantify the train-test distribution shift.

## Method

The paper introduces MacaqueITBench, a large-scale benchmark of 300,000+ image-response pairs, and constructs 15 attribute-based OOD splits per session by holding out images in the top, bottom, or middle quartiles along five image-computable attributes (hue, contrast, saturation, intensity, temperature). For each split, a ridge-regression linear model is fit on training images to predict neural responses, and performance is compared against a random 25% in-distribution hold-out. To explain the OOD performance drop, the authors propose the Closest Cosine Distance (D_CCD)—the mean over test images of the minimum cosine distance to any training image in a pre-trained ResNet18 feature space—which correlates with OOD predictivity (Spearman ρ = −0.49 for distance-based splits, ρ = −0.45 across all attribute-based splits) and outperforms MMD and covariate-shift classifiers. The framework is validated across 8 DNN architectures (ResNet, ViT, CLIP, DinoV2, BiT, Noisy Student, and billion-scale semi-supervised models) and 4 domain-generalization algorithms (ERM, IRM, GroupDRO, DeepCORAL), all of which fail to generalize under OOD shifts.

## Results

Across all 8 DNN architectures and 5 OOD attributes (hue, saturation, intensity, temperature, contrast), the OOD/InD neural predictivity ratio drops well below 1.0, ranging from a best-case of 0.66 (CLIP on high-temperature) to a worst-case of 0.20 (ViT on high-saturation). The Closest Cosine Distance (D_CCD) computed from ResNet18 features strongly predicts the OOD performance drop, with Spearman correlations of ρ = −0.49 (distance-based splits) and ρ = −0.45 (attribute-based splits), outperforming both MMD and Covariate-Shift metrics. Critically, the in-distribution BrainScore ranking does not predict OOD ranking (all Spearman rank correlations p > 0.05), demonstrating that model superiority on standard benchmarks is not conserved under distribution shifts.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 8 pre-trained DNNs (ResNet18, ResNet18_swsl, ResNext101, Noisy Student, ViT, BiT, DinoV2, CLIP) and 4 domain-generalization-trained models (ERM, IRM, GroupDRO, DeepCORAL) as feature extractors for linear neural-encoding models. No model generalizes well: all OOD/InD ratios are substantially below 1.0, and even specialized domain-generalization architectures (IRM, GroupDRO, DeepCORAL) fail to close the gap. CLIP achieves the highest OOD ratios on temperature (0.66) and saturation (0.49), while ViT is worst on saturation (0.20) and intensity (0.18). The key finding is that BrainScore ranking (where ViT > ResNext101 > CLIP > ResNet18 > BiT) is completely uncorrelated with OOD ranking (where CLIP > ResNet18 > BiT > ResNext101 ≈ ViT), with all Spearman correlations non-significant (p > 0.05). The proposed D_CCD metric outperforms MMD and Covariate-Shift as a predictor of OOD performance, providing a practical tool for anticipating generalization failure.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two well-developed but previously disconnected literatures: DNN-based neural encoding models of the ventral visual cortex (Yamins & DiCarlo, BrainScore) and the OOD generalization literature in computer vision (domain generalization methods like IRM, GroupDRO, DeepCORAL). It builds on theoretical work by Canatar et al. (2021) and Patil et al. (2024) that links generalization performance to the magnitude of distribution shift, applying this framework to biological neural encoding for the first time. The paper departs from prior neural encoding evaluations—which have relied on random IID splits within homogeneous image sets—by systematically constructing OOD splits along low-level image attributes (hue, contrast, saturation, intensity, temperature) and showing that even these simple shifts dramatically impair model performance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that DNN-based models of the visual cortex retain as little as 20% of their in-distribution performance under simple attribute shifts, and that model rankings are not conserved across shifts, fundamentally challenges how the field evaluates and compares neural encoding models. The Closest Cosine Distance metric offers a practical, image-computable tool for predicting generalization performance without requiring new neural recordings. Key open questions include whether fine-tuning DNNs on neural data could improve OOD generalization, and how performance changes when the pre-trained DNN itself encounters OOD images (e.g., ImageNet-C corruptions). The data-limited regime of neuroscience—where collecting responses to billions of images is infeasible—makes OOD generalization not just an academic concern but a practical bottleneck for the field.
