## Problem

Transition path sampling (TPS) in molecular dynamics requires conditioning a reference stochastic process to reach a rare terminal state, a problem definitively solved by Doob's h-transform. However, naively estimating the h-transform demands simulating enough forward trajectories to capture rare-event probabilities, making it infeasible for high-dimensional molecular systems. Existing alternatives—importance sampling, MCMC shooting, and score-matching methods—all require expensive trajectory simulation during training, limiting their efficiency when the target event is rare.

## Input / Output

The method takes a known reference drift (derived from a potential energy function U) and diffusion matrix G_t, along with fixed initial and terminal points A and B, and produces full transition path trajectories connecting them. During training, the method samples directly from the variational Gaussian (mixture) marginals without simulating the underlying SDE; at test time, trajectories are generated by integrating the learned drift via an SDE solver. The method supports both first-order (overdamped Langevin) and second-order (Langevin with velocities) dynamics, operating in either Cartesian or internal coordinates for molecular systems.

## Method

The paper formulates Doob's h-transform as a Lagrangian action minimization over path distributions (Theorem 1), shown to be equivalent to a Schrödinger Bridge problem with Dirac delta endpoint measures. The key computational contribution is a Gaussian (mixture) path parameterization where a neural network outputs mean perturbations and per-dimension variances, with boundary conditions enforced by design through coefficients that vanish at t=0 and t=T. Training is entirely simulation-free: samples are drawn from the variational marginals via the reparameterization trick, the Fokker-Planck-consistent drift u_{t|0,T} is computed analytically from the Gaussian parameters (Proposition 3), and the control drift v_{t|0,T} is obtained in closed form from u and the known reference drift. This eliminates the need for trajectory simulation, inner minimization over v, and rejection of paths that miss the target, reducing potential energy evaluations by orders of magnitude compared to MCMC shooting baselines.

## Results

On the Müller-Brown potential, the proposed method requires only 1.28M potential energy evaluations to sample 1,000 transition paths, compared to 1.03B for fixed-length two-way shooting MCMC (~800× fewer), while achieving comparable log-likelihood (858.50 vs. 866.56) and a slightly higher maximum log-likelihood (909.74 vs. 907.15). On alanine dipeptide with exact (point-mass) state definitions, MCMC is intractable (>10B evaluations), whereas the proposed method with internal coordinates and 5 Gaussian mixtures completes in 51.2M evaluations, achieving a minimum transition-state energy of −15.95. For the Chignolin protein (414 degrees of freedom), the method samples a feasible folding transition within 25.6M energy evaluations over a 1 ps trajectory.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is MCMC-based two-way shooting (with uniform point selection), which provides theoretical guarantees and serves as a proxy for ground truth. On the Müller-Brown potential, the proposed method achieves comparable path quality (log-likelihood 858.50 vs. 866.56; max log-likelihood 909.74 vs. 907.15) while requiring roughly 800× fewer potential energy evaluations (1.28M vs. 1.03B). The max energy of sampled paths is slightly higher (−14.81 vs. −17.80), indicating marginally less optimal transition states on average, but the best (minimum) transition-state energy is comparable (−40.56 vs. −40.21). On alanine dipeptide, the advantage is decisive: fixed-length MCMC with exact point-mass state definitions is computationally intractable (>10B evaluations for even a single trajectory), while the proposed method with internal coordinates and Gaussian mixtures completes in 38–51M evaluations. Variable-length MCMC with relaxed (CV-based) state definitions uses 187.54M evaluations, still ~4× more than the proposed method's 51.2M, and requires prior-informed collective variables that are unavailable for larger systems.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies three previously separate threads—Doob's h-transform for conditioning Brownian motion, Schrödinger Bridge theory (KL minimization over path measures), and flow-matching-style Gaussian path parameterizations—into a single variational framework for transition path sampling. While the connection between TPS and Doob's h-transform has been explored (Das et al., 2019, 2021, 2022; Singh and Limmer, 2023), prior methods all require trajectory simulation during training (MCMC shooting, importance sampling, or score matching). The key departure here is a simulation-free training objective where the h-transform is recovered as the minimizer of a Lagrangian action over Gaussian (mixture) marginals, with boundary conditions enforced analytically by the parameterization rather than through rejection or importance weighting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~1000× reduction in potential energy evaluations (1.28M vs. 1.03B on Müller-Brown) makes TPS tractable for systems where MCMC shooting is infeasible, as demonstrated on alanine dipeptide with exact (point-mass) boundary conditions where fixed-length MCMC cannot complete even a single trajectory. The simulation-free design and the clean theoretical equivalence to a Schrödinger Bridge problem suggest the variational formulation could generalize to other rare-event conditioning tasks beyond molecular dynamics. Key limitations include the restriction to point-mass (Gaussian-noise) boundary conditions rather than arbitrary sets, fixed path length, and the need for a known reference drift; extending to variable-length paths and set-valued endpoints is identified as future work.
