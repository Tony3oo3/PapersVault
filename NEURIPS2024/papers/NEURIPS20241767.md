## Problem

Shuffling (sampling-without-replacement) strategies are standard in practical deep learning but have received limited theoretical treatment for minimax problems, especially in nonconvex settings with nonsmooth regularizers. The core difficulty is that the bilevel reformulation of minimax problems introduces a 'hyper-gradient' whose evaluation requires full finite-sum computations, making it unclear how to construct valid shuffling estimators. This paper asks whether shuffling gradient methods can be efficiently developed for both nonconvex-linear and nonconvex-strongly-concave minimax problems with general nonsmooth convex terms.

## Input / Output

The algorithms consume a finite-sum minimax objective L(w,u) = f(w) + (1/n)Σ H_i(w,u) − h(u) where f and h are proper, closed, convex (possibly nonsmooth), and H_i are smooth in (w,u). In the nonconvex-linear setting, H_i(w,u) = ⟨F_i(w), Ku⟩ for a given matrix K, and the method additionally requires a smoothing parameter γ > 0 when h is not strongly convex. The output is an ε-approximate KKT point (ŵ, û) of the original minimax problem, constructed from an ε-stationary point of the bilevel upper-level problem via a proximal step and the (approximate) lower-level maximizer. Oracle complexity is O(nε^{-3}) evaluations under strong convexity of h, degrading to O(nε^{-7/2}) without it, with a √n improvement under random permutations.

## Method

The paper adopts a bilevel optimization decomposition: the lower level solves max_u H(w,u) − h(u) to define Φ_0(w), and the upper level minimizes f(w) + Φ_0(w). For the nonconvex-linear setting, the problem is reformulated as a compositional minimization involving the Fenchel conjugate h*, which is smoothed via a strongly convex perturbation b(u) when h is merely convex. The key technical novelty is a new shuffling estimator for the hyper-gradient ∇Φ_γ that uses two independent permutations—one to build a running approximation of the inner function value F(w) (updated incrementally within each epoch) and another for the Jacobian—so that each inner iteration only requires one F_i and one ∇F_i evaluation rather than the full sum. For the nonconvex-strongly-concave setting, Algorithm 2 alternates between approximating the lower-level maximizer u_0*(w) (via either a full gradient ascent step or a shuffling gradient ascent epoch) and performing a shuffling proximal gradient descent epoch on the upper level, yielding semi-shuffling and full-shuffling variants with O(nε^{-3}) or O(nε^{-4}) oracle complexity depending on the strong convexity structure.

## Results

The paper's primary contributions are theoretical oracle complexity bounds: Algorithm 1 (nonconvex-linear setting) achieves O(nε⁻³) evaluations with strong convexity of h and O(nε⁻⁷/²) without it, while Algorithm 2 (nonconvex-strongly-concave setting) achieves O(nε⁻³) evaluations for both ∇_w H_i and ∇_u H_i in the semi-shuffling variant, and O(nε⁻³) for ∇_w H_i with O(nε⁻⁴) for ∇_u H_i in the full-shuffling variant (reducible to O(nε⁻³) under additional assumptions). With random shuffling, all complexities improve by a factor of √n. Empirically, on the w8a and rcv1 LIBSVM datasets (k_b=32 blocks, 200 epochs), both SGM variants show convergence curves comparable to SGD and Prox-Linear, though no specific numerical accuracy values are reported in the text.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its two SGM variants against SGD (biased stochastic gradient estimation, Driggs et al. [10]) and Prox-Linear (stochastic extragradient with random reshuffling, Emmanouilidis et al. [11]) on the w8a and rcv1 datasets. The paper reports that both SGM variants achieve 'comparable performance' with SGD and Prox-Linear, as shown in convergence plots (Figure 1) after 200 epochs. No method is declared a clear winner; the experiments serve as supportive evidence that shuffling strategies can be incorporated into minimax algorithms without sacrificing convergence quality. The comparison is qualitative (visual convergence curves) rather than quantitative, and the paper's main contribution lies in the theoretical oracle complexity matching state-of-the-art nonconvex optimization rates, which the baselines do not directly address in the same bilevel/hyper-gradient framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the shuffling gradient method lineage (Nguyen et al. [30], Safran & Shamir [32]) to the minimax setting by introducing a novel shuffling estimator for the hyper-gradient ∇Φ₀, which arises from a bilevel optimization decomposition (Dempe [9]). It departs from prior shuffling-for-minimax work (Das et al. [8], Cho & Yun [3], Emmanouilidis et al. [11]) in three key ways: it uses a sequential bilevel (leader-follower) perspective rather than a simultaneous Nash-equilibrium update, it handles nonsmooth composite terms f and h, and it addresses two distinct problem structures (nonconvex-linear and nonconvex-strongly-concave) that are not special cases of each other. The smoothing technique for the Fenchel conjugate in the NL setting and the two-level inexactness in the NC setting are new algorithmic ingredients not present in the cited prior work.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This provides the first theoretical justification for incorporating shuffling strategies into nonconvex minimax algorithms with nonsmooth regularizers, matching the state-of-the-art O(nε⁻³) oracle complexity seen in nonconvex optimization. The empirical validation is limited (two LIBSVM datasets, 200 epochs, comparable rather than superior performance to SGD), so the practical advantage of shuffling over i.i.d. sampling in minimax remains an open empirical question. Open directions include extending the analysis to PL conditions (as in [3]), handling the fully nonconvex-nonconcave case, and investigating whether the √n improvement from random permutations materializes in large-scale deep learning applications where shuffling is already standard practice.
