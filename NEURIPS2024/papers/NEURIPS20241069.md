## Problem

Visual representations for visuomotor control are typically pretrained on large out-of-domain datasets that may not transfer to different robot embodiments, or trained end-to-end via behavior cloning. In-domain self-supervised methods exist but rely on bag-of-frames objectives (contrastive augmentations, masked autoencoding) that discard the sequential causal structure inherent in demonstration data. This paper asks whether explicitly modeling dynamics over observation sequences can yield better in-domain representations from limited demonstrations without augmentations, contrastive sampling, or ground-truth actions.

## Input / Output

DynaMo consumes sequences of h consecutive RGB frames (h=2 for Franka Kitchen, h=5 for other environments) at 224×224 (or 128×128 for xArm Kitchen) from one to four camera views, and produces a frozen ResNet18 embedding per frame that is concatenated across views for downstream policy training. Critically, no action labels are required during pretraining—only the raw observation sequence is used. The learned encoder is then frozen and paired with various policy heads (VQ-BeT, Diffusion Policy, MLP, kNN) for imitation learning.

## Method

DynaMo jointly trains three components end-to-end: a ResNet18 image encoder, a causally masked transformer inverse dynamics model that infers a low-dimensional latent transition z_t (dim z ≪ dim s) between consecutive frame embeddings, and a causally masked transformer forward dynamics model that predicts the next-frame embedding from the current embedding and latent transition. The training objective is a cosine-similarity loss on one-step forward predictions, with the target embedding obtained via stop-gradient (SimSiam-style) or a momentum encoder, plus a covariance regularization term (λ=0.04) to prevent representation collapse. The key insight is that modeling the unimodal latent transition between frames forces the encoder to capture task-relevant fine-grained features (e.g., block positions) rather than fixating on visually dominant but less informative features (e.g., the robot arm), all without any data augmentations, negative pairs, or access to ground-truth actions.

## Results

On four simulated benchmarks, DynaMo achieves 3.64/4 on Franka Kitchen, 0.65/2 on Block Pushing, 0.66/1 on Push-T, and 0.93/1 on LIBERO Goal, representing an overall 39% improvement over prior state-of-the-art pretrained and self-supervised representations. The largest gains appear on the harder closed-loop tasks: Block Pushing (0.65 vs. 0.52 for the best SSL baseline RPT) and Push-T (0.66 vs. 0.57 for MoCo-v3). On real robots, DynaMo outperforms the best baseline by 43% on the single-task Allegro hand (e.g., 9/10 on Microwave vs. 5/10 for ImageNet) and by 20% on the multi-task xArm Kitchen environment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DynaMo outperforms all five pretrained representations (R3M, VC-1, MVP, ImageNet, Random) on Block Pushing and Push-T, and matches or exceeds them on Franka Kitchen and LIBERO Goal. Among self-supervised baselines, DynaMo wins on Block Pushing (0.65) over the closest competitor RPT (0.52) and on Push-T (0.66) over MoCo-v3 (0.57), while BYOL narrowly edges it on Franka Kitchen (3.75 vs. 3.64). DynaMo substantially outperforms MAE (0.07 on Push-T, 0.00 on Block Pushing) and TCN-SV (0.07 on Push-T) across nearly all tasks. On LIBERO Goal, DynaMo (0.93) matches ImageNet pretraining and surpasses all SSL methods, with the next-best being TCN-SV at 0.76. The paper also shows DynaMo's advantage generalizes across policy classes (VQ-BeT, Diffusion Policy, MLP, kNN) on Push-T, where it leads every baseline in every policy class except Diffusion Policy where it ties ImageNet at 0.73.

## Relation to Prior Work

DynaMo departs from the dominant paradigm of in-domain self-supervised pretraining for robot manipulation, which relies on contrastive learning with augmentations (BYOL, MoCo), masked autoencoding (MAE, MVP, VC-1), or temporal contrast (TCN) — all of which treat demonstration videos as a bag of frames or use symmetric temporal objectives. Instead, it repurposes the classical control-theoretic decomposition into inverse and forward dynamics models (well-studied in neuroscience for motor control) as a self-supervised visual pretraining objective, jointly learning an encoder, a latent inverse dynamics model, and a forward dynamics model without ground-truth actions. The method also builds on SimSiam's stop-gradient trick and VICReg's covariance regularization to prevent representation collapse, but the core novelty is the dynamics-prediction loss over latent transitions rather than pixel-space reconstruction or contrastive alignment.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that in-domain self-supervised pretraining can be highly effective for visuo-motor control when the objective explicitly exploits sequential causal structure, achieving a 39% average improvement over prior SOTA representations across six simulated and real environments. This is practically important for robotics settings where collecting large demonstration datasets is expensive and out-of-domain pretraining may not transfer across embodiments. Open questions include whether the approach scales to larger unlabeled robot datasets, whether it generalizes beyond manipulation to locomotion or deformable-object tasks, and whether the dynamics objective can be combined with other self-supervised signals. The authors note the absence of error bars due to compute constraints, and the evaluation is limited to lab-based manipulation tasks.
