## Problem

Discovering Lyapunov functions that certify global stability of dynamical systems is a 130-year-old open problem with no known general solution. Existing algorithmic tools (e.g., SOSTOOLS) are restricted to small polynomial systems whose Lyapunov functions are sums of squares, and no method exists for non-polynomial systems. A further obstacle for applying language models is that, for open problems, training data cannot be generated by sampling problems and solving them—no generic solver exists to produce the target solutions.

## Input / Output

The model takes a system of n ordinary differential equations (2–5 equations, polynomial or non-polynomial) as input and produces a single scalar Lyapunov function V(x) as output. Both are represented as symbolic expression trees enumerated in Polish (pre-order) notation, with operators as internal nodes and variables/constants as leaves; integer constants are tokenized in base 1000 and real constants in scientific notation. Predicted functions must pass external verification—SOS solvers for polynomial systems, SMT/dReal solvers for non-polynomial—to confirm the Lyapunov conditions (positive definiteness, properness, and non-increasing along trajectories) hold.

## Method

The core innovation is a backward generation procedure that constructs stable dynamical systems from randomly sampled Lyapunov functions, guaranteeing correctness by design while obscuring the generative pattern through algebraic expansion and simplification. Concretely, V is built as a sum of a positive-definite proper component and a non-negative cross-term; the vector field f is then assembled as a negative gradient flow scaled by random non-negative functions plus a random component orthogonal to ∇V, ensuring ∇V·f ≤ 0 by construction. Sequence-to-sequence transformers (8 layers, 10 heads, 640-dim embeddings) are trained on 1 million such pairs, and adding just 300 forward-generated examples to the backward training set boosts out-of-distribution accuracy from 35% to 89% on barrier functions. An expert iteration loop further fine-tunes the model on its own verified predictions, yielding roughly 15% relative improvement on newly generated systems.

## Results

On held-out test sets, the BPoly model achieves 99% in-domain accuracy (100% with beam size 50), and the best mixture model (PolyMixture: BPoly + 300 FBarr examples) reaches 84% on the FSOSTOOLS benchmark and 89% on FBarr. On the 'into the wild' discovery task, PolyMixture finds Lyapunov functions for 11.8% of 3-equation polynomial systems and 10.1% of 5-equation systems, while the NonPolyMixture model discovers Lyapunov functions for 12.7% of non-polynomial systems (where no algorithm is known). Human performance on the same FSOSTOOLS problems was 9.33%, far below the model's 84%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's transformer models dominate all four baselines across every test set. On the FSOSTOOLS benchmark (polynomial systems solvable by SOSTOOLS), PolyMixture achieves 84% versus 46% for LyzNet (the best AI baseline), 32% for Fossil 2, 30% for ANLC, and 15% for SOSTOOL's own findlyap. On the 'into the wild' discovery task (Table 6), the gap widens dramatically: PolyMixture finds Lyapunov functions for 11.8% of 3-equation polynomial systems versus 4.3% (LyzNet), 0.9% (Fossil 2), 0.6% (ANLC), and 1.1% (findlyap); on 5-equation systems it reaches 10.1% versus 2.1% for LyzNet. For non-polynomial systems, where no algorithmic method exists, NonPolyMixture discovers Lyapunov functions for 12.7% of systems, outperforming LyzNet (3.5%), Fossil 2 (1.0%), and ANLC (0.6%). The paper also notes that transformer inference is far faster: 13.9s per system (beam 50) versus an average of 935s for findlyap with a 2400s timeout.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on using sequence-to-sequence transformers for symbolic mathematics (Lample & Charton 2019 for tokenization, and subsequent work on symbolic integration, Gröbner bases, and theorem proving) to the specific problem of discovering Lyapunov functions for dynamical systems. It departs from prior neural Lyapunov methods (Fossil 2, ANLC, LyzNet), which train feed-forward networks to approximate local or semi-global Lyapunov functions and verify with SMT solvers, by instead training a transformer to output explicit symbolic expressions for global Lyapunov functions. The key methodological departure is the backward generation procedure—constructing stable systems from randomly sampled Lyapunov functions—which sidesteps the fundamental difficulty that no general algorithm exists for generating (system, Lyapunov function) training pairs for open problems.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work demonstrates that generative models can discover explicit, verifiable solutions to a 130-year-old open problem in mathematics, including for non-polynomial systems where no algorithm is known, positioning transformers as potential tools for mathematical research rather than just pattern matching. The backward generation recipe is broadly applicable to other open problems where solutions can be sampled but problems cannot be easily generated. Key limitations include the small system size (≤5 equations), the lack of a reliable benchmark for non-polynomial systems (no way to determine if a random system is stable), and the black-box nature of the model's 'reasoning.' Open questions include whether scaling to larger models and systems will maintain or improve the discovery rate, and whether the expert iteration loop can be iterated to progressively expand the class of solvable systems.
