## Problem

Task graphs encode partial orderings over key-steps in procedural activities and are interpretable, human-readable representations useful for downstream tasks like mistake detection. However, prior methods to construct task graphs relied on hand-crafted procedures—inductive logic programming, rule-based parsing, or co-occurrence counting—rather than a principled learning framework, preventing their integration into end-to-end neural architectures. This paper asks whether task graphs can be learned directly from observed action sequences via differentiable optimization.

## Input / Output

The Direct Optimization (DO) model takes as input a set of weakly supervised key-step action sequences (no graph annotations at training time) and directly outputs a binarized (n+2)×(n+2) adjacency matrix representing the learned DAG, where n is the number of key-steps plus start/end placeholders. The Task Graph Transformer (TGT) instead consumes D-dimensional text or video embeddings of key-steps (extracted via a pre-trained EgoVLPv2 model) and produces the same adjacency matrix through a learned mapping. For the downstream mistake detection task, the system consumes a stream of observed actions and flags the current action as a mistake if its pre-conditions in the predicted graph are not yet satisfied.

## Method

The core contribution is the Task Graph Maximum Likelihood (TGML) loss, which models the probability of observing a key-step sequence given a graph as a product of per-step conditional probabilities, where each probability is the ratio of the sum of edge weights from observed key-steps to the current key-step over the sum over all unobserved key-steps. Minimizing the negative log-likelihood yields a contrastive objective: the first log-term pushes up weights of edges from the current key-step to all previously observed key-steps, while the second (weighted by β) pushes down weights of edges from yet-unobserved key-steps to observed ones. The DO model uses this loss to perform gradient descent directly on the adjacency matrix (its only parameters), with row-wise softmax normalization and DAG-constraining masks. The TGT model wraps the same loss around a transformer encoder that processes key-step embeddings (with a distinctiveness cross-entropy to prevent representation collapse) and a relation head that concatenates all (n+2)² node-pair vectors and maps them through a relation transformer and MLP to produce the score matrix, enabling the framework to be plugged into complex neural architectures.

## Results

On CaptainCook4D task graph generation, the Direct Optimization (DO) method achieves 87.8 F1 (86.4 Precision, 89.7 Recall), outperforming the best prior method MSG² (71.1 F1) by +16.7 points. The feature-based TGT-text model achieves 80.8 F1, also surpassing all competitors. On online mistake detection, DO achieves 75.9 average F1 on Assembly101-O (+19.8 over PREGO) and 58.3 on EPIC-Tent-O (+7.5 over PREGO), while TGT-text achieves the best result on EPIC-Tent-O at 64.1 average F1. TGT trained on video embeddings also exhibits emerging video understanding abilities, reaching 77.3% on pairwise ordering and 74.3% on future prediction versus 50% random baseline.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against five baselines spanning inductive logic programming, statistical counting, LLM reasoning, and implicit forecasting. MSGI [39] (ILP-based) performs worst at 12.8 F1, indicating that purely logical inference from sequences is insufficient. The GPT-4 LLM baseline [1] achieves 55.0 F1 by generating graphs from key-step descriptions alone, showing that language priors capture some but not most ordering constraints. Count-Based [3] (60.6 F1) improves by leveraging co-occurrence statistics from matched video segments but still misses complex dependencies. MSG² [20] (71.1 F1) is the strongest prior method, combining ILP with heuristics, yet remains 16.7 F1 points below the proposed DO method. On online mistake detection, PREGO [13] (39.4 avg F1 on Assembly101-O, 32.1 on EPIC-Tent-O) relies on an implicit forecasting module and is substantially outperformed by the explicit graph-based DO approach (75.9 and 58.3 respectively), with the gap widening when ground-truth action sequences are used. Notably, TGT-text outperforms DO on EPIC-Tent-O (64.1 vs 58.3), suggesting that semantic key-step names benefit from the transformer's feature-based processing, while DO's purely symbolic optimization excels on Assembly101-O where key-step names are less distinctive.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the established paradigm of task graph construction, which has relied on hand-crafted mining procedures (MSGI, MSG²), co-occurrence counting, or LLM-based generation from textual descriptions. The key conceptual shift is framing task graph learning as a maximum likelihood estimation problem over key-step sequences, yielding a differentiable TGML loss that enables direct gradient-based optimization of the adjacency matrix. The Task Graph Transformer (TGT) further extends this by wrapping the loss in a transformer encoder and relation head that predicts graphs from text or video embeddings, building on EgoVLPv2 for feature extraction. For downstream mistake detection, the paper contrasts its explicit graph-based approach against PREGO's implicit forecasting-based representation, showing the former's advantage.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The differentiable formulation is practically significant because it allows task graphs to be integrated into end-to-end neural pipelines rather than serving as a separate, hand-engineered preprocessing step. The substantial improvements in online mistake detection (+19.8% on Assembly101-O) demonstrate that explicit, learned graph representations outperform implicit ones for procedural error detection. Key limitations remain: the approach requires labeled key-step sequences, does not model optional or repeatable steps, and is sensitive to noisy action detection. Open questions include learning task graphs without sequence labels, handling proficiency errors alongside procedural ones, and scaling to open-vocabulary procedures.
