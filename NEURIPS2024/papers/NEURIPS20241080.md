## Problem

Existing argument mining datasets such as DebateSum (240K examples) are limited to pre-season summer camp evidence, excluding the rich argumentative structures of regular-season competitive debates. No prior dataset captures the full spectrum of high school and college debate arguments across multiple formats (Policy, Lincoln-Douglas, Public Forum) at scale, leaving a significant gap for training and evaluating LLMs on argumentative summarization and mining tasks.

## Input / Output

The primary input is a full-text debate evidence document (a 'card') extracted from .docx files, preserving formatting cues like underlining, bolding, and highlighting that encode hierarchical extractive summaries. For summarization, the model produces a short biased abstractive summary (the 'tag') matching the debater's own rhetorical framing; for classification tasks, it outputs one of four argument categories (Topicality, Disadvantages, Advantages, Counterplans) or a binary stance label (aff/neg). The hierarchical metadata (pocket → hat → block → tag) provides natural multi-level ground-truth summaries at different granularities.

## Method

The paper's core contribution is the OpenDebateEvidence dataset (3.5M documents, 45 metadata columns) sourced from the OpenCaseList project, processed through a custom .docx-to-XML parsing pipeline that extracts formatting-based hierarchical structure and performs neural semantic deduplication via gte-base-en-v1.5 embeddings (removing 77.4% near-duplicates). For evaluation, the authors fine-tune LLaMA3-8B/70B and Mistral-7B using three parameter-efficient techniques—LoRA (rank 8), ReFT (rank 4), and Orthogonalization—on the debate evidence, then assess performance via ROUGE F1, perplexity, and GPT-4o-as-judge scoring on OpenDebateEvidence, DebateSum, BillSum, and xSum. A key finding is that the hierarchical biased metadata (pocket/hat/tag) serves as multi-level supervision, and that fine-tuning on OpenDebateEvidence transfers to unrelated argumentative datasets like BillSum, with LoRA on LLaMA3-70B achieving the strongest results across all benchmarks.

## Results

On OpenDebateEvidence (10,000 sampled documents), the LoRA-fine-tuned LLaMA3-70B achieves the best ROUGE F1 scores: R-1 37.2%, R-2 15.8%, R-L 33.4%, with perplexity of 27.3 and GPT-4o judge scores of 8.2 (output quality) and 8.1 (support quality). The LLaMA3-8B LoRA model trained on 1M examples from OpenDebateEvidence reaches R-1 32.2%, R-2 9.9%, R-L 27.4% with the lowest perplexity (21.8), outperforming base Google Gemini (R-1 32.5%) and Anthropic Claude (R-1 31.2%). On BillSum, LLaMA3-70B LoRA achieves R-1 54.6%, R-2 30.5%, R-L 50.0%, and on DebateSum it reaches R-1 39.4%, R-2 18.5%, R-L 35.1%, consistently leading across all three datasets.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three model architectures (Mistral-7B, LLaMA3-8B, LLaMA3-70B) across four configurations (Base, LoRA, ReFT, Orthogonalization), plus base Google Gemini and Anthropic Claude as external references. LoRA consistently outperforms ReFT and Orthogonalization across all models and datasets: on OpenDebateEvidence, LLaMA3-70B LoRA beats ReFT by 1.3 points on R-1 (37.2 vs. 35.9) and Orthogonalization by 3.0 points (37.2 vs. 34.2). The LLaMA3-8B LoRA model trained on 1M OpenDebateEvidence examples (R-1 32.2%) surpasses both base Gemini (32.5%) and base Claude (31.2%) on R-2 (9.9 vs. 13.5/12.9) and R-L (27.4 vs. 29.4/28.1), though Gemini and Claude edge it slightly on R-1. On BillSum, LLaMA3-70B LoRA (R-1 54.6%) outperforms base Gemini (48.7%) by 5.9 points and base Claude (47.3%) by 7.3 points. On DebateSum, the same pattern holds with LLaMA3-70B LoRA (R-1 39.4%) beating Gemini (35.5%) by 3.9 points. Notably, Gemini and Claude achieve higher GPT-4o judge scores (8.5–8.7 output quality) than the fine-tuned LLaMA3-70B LoRA (8.2), suggesting the proprietary models produce more polished prose even when their ROUGE scores are lower.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper is a direct, large-scale successor to DebateSum (Roush & Balaji, 2020), expanding it from 240K pre-season examples to 3.5M documents spanning the full debate season across Policy, Lincoln-Douglas, and Public Forum formats. Conceptually, it repurposes standard parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques (LoRA, ReFT, Orthogonalization) for the domain-specific task of argumentative abstractive summarization, leveraging the unique hierarchical metadata (hat/pocket/tag) as multi-level ground-truth summaries. It positions itself against IBM's Project Debater and other argument mining datasets (ArgAnalysis35K, IAM) by emphasizing real-world competitive debate evidence with human-annotated rhetorical structure rather than synthetic or parliamentary debate data.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset's 25× scale over prior work and its rich hierarchical metadata (biased abstractive tags, extractive underlines, spoken highlights) provide a uniquely structured resource for training models on multi-granularity argument summarization, with demonstrated transfer benefits to legislative summarization (BillSum). Key limitations include a strong left-wing philosophical bias in the evidence corpus, underrepresentation of Public Forum debate, and potential noise from rare cases of academic dishonesty. Open questions include whether the dataset's cultural and ideological biases propagate into trained models, how well findings generalize beyond American competitive debate, and whether the hierarchical summary structure can be exploited for more structured generation tasks.
