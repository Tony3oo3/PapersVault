## Problem

Lipschitz continuity provides certified robustness guarantees in continuous domains like vision, but fundamentally breaks down in discrete or non-metrizable domains such as natural language: no natural input distance metric exists, any imposed metric is task-dependent, and key architectures like Transformers are provably non-Lipschitz. This paper seeks a domain-independent robustness certification that works uniformly across modalities without requiring norm or distributional assumptions on the input space.

## Input / Output

The method operates on a trained neural network and a data distribution D. It decomposes the network into intermediate metric spaces (hidden layers) and, for each layer k, estimates the k-volatility via Monte-Carlo sampling from D, yielding a per-layer scalar score. For regularization, the standard supervised loss is augmented with a λ-weighted k-volatility term computed on the current minibatch. For test-time certification, given a reference set A and a perturbation radius δ in the representation space, the algorithm outputs a probabilistic upper bound on the probability of loss change exceeding η.

## Method

The core contribution is knowledge continuity, a statistical (rather than analytical) robustness property defined over a network's hidden representation spaces. A network's function is decomposed into a sequence of measurable maps through intermediate metric spaces (metric decomposition); k-volatility at a data point is the expected absolute loss change divided by the distance in the k-th hidden layer, and knowledge continuity requires this expectation to be bounded by ε across the data distribution. This yields probabilistic certified robustness bounds (Theorem 4.1) that depend only on the loss function and the learned metric spaces, independent of input modality, norms, or distribution. Crucially, the paper proves that knowledge continuity does not compromise universal approximation (Propositions 4.3–4.4) and that in continuous settings it generalizes Lipschitz continuity, recovering its tight certification bounds (Propositions 4.6–4.8). Practically, k-volatility is estimated via a zeroth-order Monte-Carlo algorithm, and a Beta-distributed layer-sampling strategy focuses regularization on the most vulnerable hidden layers, outperforming adversarial training baselines (TextFooler, ALUM) by up to 2× in training speed while improving robustness.

## Results

On IMDB sentiment classification, KCReg (knowledge continuity regularization) achieves 75.1–89.3% accuracy under TextFooler attacks and 84.9–91.3% under BERT-ATTACK across BERT, GPT-2, and T5, substantially outperforming both TF-trained and ALUM baselines. On ANLI natural language understanding, KCReg yields modest gains of 1–4 points over ALUM on some reasoning splits (e.g., 48.2% vs 44.5% on ANLI_R1 with T5) while remaining competitive on others. Knowledge continuity also serves as a predictive diagnostic: regressing average k-volatility against TextFooler attack success rate explains 35% of variance (R²=0.35), rising to R²=0.48 when combined with model size and family. Training speed is up to 2× faster than TextFooler and 3× faster than ALUM since KCReg requires only zeroth-order Monte-Carlo computations rather than inner-optimization steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

KCReg dominates both baselines on IMDB under both TextFooler and BERT-ATTACK across all three architectures. Against TextFooler-trained models, KCReg gains 5.9–23.3 points on IMDB_TF (e.g., 87.8% vs 64.5% for GPT-2) and 22.4–30.7 points on IMDB_BA (e.g., 90.6% vs 51.3% for GPT-2). Against ALUM, the margins are even larger on IMDB_BA, where ALUM degrades severely (27.5% for GPT-2) while KCReg reaches 90.6%. On ANLI, the picture is more nuanced: KCReg wins by 1–4 points on ANLI_R1 and R2 for GPT-2 and T5 (e.g., 48.2% vs 44.5% on T5 ANLI_R1), but trails ALUM by ~1 point on BERT ANLI_R3 (45.3% vs 46.3%) and T5 ANLI_R3 (44.3% vs 44.4%). TextFooler training is not applicable to ANLI (marked X in the table), so only ALUM serves as a direct ANLI baseline.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper introduces 'knowledge continuity' as a domain-independent generalization of Lipschitz continuity for certified robustness, shifting the focus from bounding output change with respect to input-space distance to bounding loss change with respect to hidden-representation-space distance. It builds directly on the Lipschitz continuity literature (spectral normalization, Parseval networks, Lipschitz-margin training) and incorporates an auxiliary certification result from Zhang et al. [89] to recover input-space guarantees in continuous settings. The framework also distills empirical intuitions from virtual adversarial training methods for language (ALUM [43], Yoo & Qi [85]) but provides the theoretical certification guarantees those methods lack. Unlike randomized smoothing approaches for language [34, 74], knowledge continuity yields bounds independent of perturbation type and domain modality.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key contribution is showing that certified robustness can be formulated for discrete and non-metrizable domains (e.g., natural language) without the fundamental obstacles that prevent Lipschitz continuity from applying there, while simultaneously proving that this robustness constraint does not sacrifice universal approximation—a property Lipschitz continuity does not preserve. The practical applications (layer-wise vulnerability localization, Monte-Carlo-based regularization, test-time certification) offer actionable tools for practitioners. Open questions include whether the expressiveness guarantees hold under bounded Hausdorff dimension of the metric decomposition (Conjecture 4.5, stated negatively), and whether the probabilistic (rather than deterministic) certification bounds are sufficient for safety-critical deployment. The empirical gains over ALUM and TextFooler baselines are moderate, and the framework's practical utility depends on careful choice of metric decompositions and Monte-Carlo estimation quality.
