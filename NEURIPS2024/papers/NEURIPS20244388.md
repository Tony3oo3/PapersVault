## Problem

Despite the widespread use of the Elo rating system in sports analytics, it has lacked rigorous theoretical guarantees on how quickly its ratings converge to true player skills. Prior work established convergence in distribution to a stationary distribution but provided no explicit rates. This paper fills that gap by deriving convergence rates for time-averaged Elo ratings under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model, showing they are competitive with state-of-the-art offline estimators while operating in the more challenging online setting.

## Input / Output

The method operates online: at each step a pair of players is sampled according to a distribution q over edges of a comparison graph G, their match outcome is observed, and ratings are updated via a zero-sum rule with step-size η, followed by orthogonal projection onto the bounded zero-sum polytope [-M,M]^n ∩ {x: Σx_k = 0}. The output is the time-averaged rating vector over a window of t steps (after a burn-in of T steps), which approximates the true BTL parameters ρ with high probability. The convergence rate is governed by the spectral gap λ_q of the match-up distribution, and the paper also produces optimal match-up distributions (via SDP) that maximise this gap for a given graph topology.

## Method

The paper interprets Elo as stochastic gradient descent on the BTL log-likelihood with fixed step-size, equivalently as a non-reversible Markov chain on R^n. The key technical tool is establishing positive Ricci curvature (L2 contraction) of the chain, yielding a step-by-step coupling with exponential contraction rate κ ∝ η·e^{-2M}·λ_q. Since the chain is non-reversible and does not converge in total variation (its equilibrium is continuous while finite-time states are discrete), the authors introduce a noisy version with additive uniform noise to enable exact coalescence, then transfer concentration bounds from the noisy chain back to the original via a deterministic error bound. For tournament design, they show that optimising the match-up distribution q to maximise λ_q is equivalent to the fastest-mixing Markov chain problem (formulable as an SDP), and extend this to a parallel setting via the Birkhoff-von-Neumann decomposition of substochastic matrices into matchings of the graph.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 2.5) shows that time-averaged Elo ratings satisfy (1/n)‖A^{t,T} − ρ‖₂² ≲ e^{4M}(log n)²/(λ_q² t) with high probability, matching the state-of-the-art offline MLE rate up to a log factor while operating in the more challenging online setting. For tournament design (Corollary 3.1), choosing the optimal match-up distribution derived from the fastest-mixing Markov chain yields (1/n)‖A^{t,T} − ρ‖₂² ≲ n(diam G)²(log n)²/t whp. Experimentally, on a dumbbell graph with two cliques of 20 vertices connected by a single edge, the optimal sequential distribution requires only Õ(n) total matches for convergence versus Õ(n³/k) for the uniform distribution — a dramatic improvement of over 10× in total games played.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is against offline BTL estimation methods. Elo's rate of e^{4M}(log n)²/(λ_q² t) matches Hajek et al. [20] up to a log factor and improves the constant in front of M (e^{4M} vs e^{8M}). Against Shah et al. [37], whose rate is ne^{8M}log n/(λ_q t), Elo is comparable or better when q corresponds to an expander graph (where n/λ_q ≍ 1), but Shah et al. can be better for certain sparse topologies. Against Li et al. [27], Elo avoids the requirement of uniform edge sampling and the δ (degree-ratio) factor, though Li et al. additionally provide ℓ_∞ bounds. In the tournament design experiments on dumbbell graphs, the optimal sequential distribution (derived from the fastest-mixing continuous-time chain) dramatically outperforms the uniform distribution: for k=1 bottleneck edge, the spectral gap improves from λ_q n ≍ k/n² to λ_q n ≍ 1, reducing required matches from Õ(n³/k) to Õ(n). The parallel (discrete-time) version achieves similar per-round convergence but requires more total games, and provides no real improvement over the optimal sequential version when the graph already has a continuous-time chain with order-1 spectral gap.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Aldous's foundational work [6,7] on Elo as a Markov process—which established convergence in distribution to a unique stationary distribution—by deriving explicit convergence rates for time-averaged ratings under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model. It departs from the dominant BTL estimation literature [20, 37, 27], which operates in an offline setting (computing ratings after all matches are observed), by analyzing Elo's inherently online, sequential update rule. The tournament design section introduces a formal connection to the fastest-mixing Markov chain problem [39, 10, 32], which had not previously been made in the sequential setting, and leverages curvature-based concentration inequalities [22, 35] to handle the non-reversible, continuous-state nature of the Elo chain.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a simple, interpretable, online system like Elo achieves convergence rates competitive with sophisticated offline MLE methods is practically significant for sports analytics and any domain relying on dynamic skill estimation. The tournament design framework—linking optimal match scheduling to the fastest-mixing Markov chain problem—provides a principled, SDP-solvable approach to graph design that can dramatically reduce the number of matches needed (e.g., Õ(n) vs Õ(n³/k) for dumbbell graphs). Key open questions include: rigorously bounding the maximum Elo rating to remove the artificial projection step, characterizing the stationary distribution's shape for ℓ∞ bounds, and extending the analysis to non-stationary settings where player skills drift over time.
