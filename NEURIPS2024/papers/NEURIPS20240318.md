## Problem

Generating realistic synthetic causal benchmark datasets with an exactly specified marginal causal effect (e.g., ATE) remains difficult: existing generative methods enforce causal constraints via soft optimisation, requiring post-hoc verification and risking constraint violation. No prior generative model simultaneously learns flexible data representations and exactly parameterises the marginal causal effect, the degree of unobserved confounding, and the propensity score.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240318/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes observational tabular data with a continuous or binary outcome Y, a binary treatment T, and mixed continuous/discrete pretreatment covariates Z. The user additionally specifies a target causal margin (any valid CDF, including Gaussian, logistic, or probit for binary outcomes) and a correlation parameter ρ controlling unobserved confounding. The output is a trained generative model (marginal flows, Frugal Flow, propensity flow) that produces synthetic datasets exactly satisfying the specified ATE and confounding level, as well as inferred ATE estimates when used in an inference setting.

## Method

Frugal Flows construct a likelihood-based joint distribution in three sequentially trained components: univariate neural spline flows (NSFs) for each pretreatment covariate margin, a Frugal Flow that jointly learns the causal marginal CDF F_{Y|do(T)}^{-1} (a univariate NSF) and the copula c(v_Z | v_{Y|do(T)}) (a multivariate autoregressive NSF), and a propensity flow for T|Z. The key architectural insight is placing the causal margin as the first dimension in the autoregressive copula flow, which constrains its marginal to be uniform and thereby forces the univariate flow to target the marginal causal CDF rather than the conditional p_{Y|T}. This joint estimation is essential because estimating the causal margin separately from the copula would recover the conditional rather than the marginal effect. For discrete covariates, a dequantised empirical CDF (Ruschendorf transform) replaces the NSF marginal flow. During simulation, the user can swap in any valid CDF for the causal margin, introduce unobserved confounding by sampling (V_{Y|do(T)}, V_{T|Z}) from a Gaussian copula with correlation ρ, and freely customise the propensity score, all without retraining the model.

## Results

On simulated data with known ATE (N=25,000, 25 runs), Frugal Flows achieve the lowest bias in ATE estimation across all three data-generating models (M1–M3), e.g. recovering ATE=1.00±0.09 on M3 (10 covariates) versus 1.13±0.06 for outcome regression and 0.87±0.15 for causal normalising flows. For ATE=5, FF estimates 5.00±0.24 (M1) and 5.18±0.30 (M3), while CNF underestimates at 4.23±0.20 and 4.73±0.28 respectively. In the benchmarking experiments on Lalonde and e401(k) real-world data, FFs generate synthetic samples with exactly the user-specified ATE of 1000, and 10 causal inference methods correctly recover this value on unconfounded data, while all methods exhibit expected bias when unobserved confounding is injected via a Gaussian copula.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against outcome regression (OR), Frugal Flows consistently outperform: OR estimates the conditional rather than marginal effect, producing systematic upward bias (e.g. 1.28 vs. true 1.0 on M₁, 1.46 vs. 1.0 on M₂) that FFs avoid by directly parameterising the causal margin. Against propensity score matching (Stuart et al., 2011), FFs show substantially lower variance (±0.09–0.30 vs. ±0.47–1.06) and less bias, particularly on the 10-covariate model M₃ where matching estimates 0.90±0.48 versus FF's 1.00±0.09. Against causal normalising flows (Javaloy et al., 2024), FFs achieve tighter confidence intervals and less underestimation of the ATE (e.g. 5.00±0.24 vs. 4.23±0.20 on M₁ with ATE=5), though CNF is competitive on M₂. In the benchmarking realism comparison (Appendix D.3.6), FFs produce synthetic data whose covariate correlation matrices more closely match the original e401(k) data than Credence (Parikh et al., 2022), and unlike Credence, modifying the causal constraint in FFs does not alter the learned covariate joint distribution or propensity score.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two prior threads: the frugal parameterisation of Evans and Didelez (2024), which explicitly parameterises the marginal causal effect and builds the rest of the model around it using parametric copulae, and the copula-flow architecture of Kamthe et al. (2021), which uses neural spline flows to learn flexible dependency structures. The key departure is that Frugal Flows learn the causal margin flow and the copula flow jointly (rather than sequentially as in Kamthe et al.), which is necessary to target the marginal causal effect rather than the conditional effect. For benchmarking, the paper positions itself against soft-constraint optimisation methods (Parikh et al. 2022, RealCause, Credence) that require post-hoc verification of causal constraints, and against causal normalising flows (Javaloy et al. 2024) for inference.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Frugal Flows provide the first likelihood-based generative model that exactly parameterises the marginal causal effect (ATE, risk ratios, odds ratios) while simultaneously learning flexible data representations, eliminating the need for post-hoc constraint verification in synthetic benchmark generation. The ability to simulate binary outcomes from logistic/probit margins is practically important because logistic regression is not collapsible, making this infeasible for fully conditional simulation methods. Open questions include scalability to smaller datasets (FFs require large N for accurate inference), the impact of dequantisation on discrete/categorical data structure, and whether alternative copula architectures could reduce the hyperparameter tuning burden. The method is primarily a validation tool rather than a recommended inference method for real-world treatment effect estimation.
