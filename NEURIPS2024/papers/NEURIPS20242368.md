## Problem

Existing drag-based image editing methods rely on n-step iterative latent optimization—70–80 iterations for motion-based approaches like DragDiffusion and ~50 gradient steps for gradient-based ones like DragonDiffusion—making each edit take 20–50 seconds and limiting real-time practical use. The core challenge is achieving precise spatial manipulation of image content in latent space without the slow, multi-step optimization loop.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242368/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes an RGB image (encoded via a VAE into a 4-channel latent at 1/8 spatial resolution), a set of k drag instructions each specified as a handle-to-target vector, and a user-defined mask region delineating the area to be edited. The output is a single edited RGB image decoded from the optimized latent. Notably, no LoRA training or per-image preparation is required, and the entire pipeline (inversion + one-step warpage + sampling) completes in ~3 seconds on an RTX 3090.

## Method

FastDrag replaces iterative latent optimization with a one-step warpage operation: a Latent Warpage Function (LWF) treats drag instructions as external forces on a stretched material, computing per-pixel warpage vectors whose magnitudes decay inversely with distance from the handle point and whose directions align with the drag vector, then relocating each masked pixel to its new position in a single pass. Null regions created by this relocation are filled via Bilateral Nearest Neighbor Interpolation (BNNI), which blends the four nearest non-null neighbors in horizontal and vertical directions with distance-weighted coefficients. A consistency-preserving strategy injects key and value pairs from the self-attention layers captured during diffusion inversion into the sampling pass, anchoring unedited regions to the original image. The U-Net is further equipped with LCM-distilled weights to reduce both inversion and sampling to 10 steps, yielding a total editing time of 3.12 s per point—nearly 7× faster than the prior fastest method (DiffEditor) and 28× faster than DragDiffusion.

## Results

On DragBench (205 images, 349 drag pairs), FastDrag achieves an editing time of 3.12 s per point on an RTX 3090, making it ~700% faster than the previous fastest method (DiffEditor, 21.68 s) and ~2800% faster than DragDiffusion (21.54 s). In terms of drag precision (MD↓), FastDrag scores 32.23, competitive with DragDiffusion (33.70) and DiffEditor (28.46), though GoodDrag achieves the lowest MD (22.96). For image fidelity (1-LPIPS↑), FastDrag reaches 0.86, on par with DragDiffusion (0.89) and DiffEditor (0.89). Notably, FastDrag requires no LoRA preparation step, unlike all motion-based baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FastDrag dramatically outperforms all five baselines on editing speed: 3.12 s vs. 21.68 s (DiffEditor), 21.54 s (DragDiffusion), 20.41 s (DragNoise), 45.83 s (GoodDrag), and 52.63 s (FreeDrag), while also eliminating the ~1 min LoRA preparation required by all motion-based methods. On drag precision (MD), FastDrag (32.23) is competitive with DragDiffusion (33.70) and DragNoise (33.41) but trails GoodDrag (22.96) and DiffEditor (28.46), indicating a modest quality trade-off for the speed gain. On image fidelity (1-LPIPS), FastDrag (0.86) matches GoodDrag (0.86) and is close to DragDiffusion and DiffEditor (both 0.89), while substantially outperforming DragNoise (0.63) and FreeDrag (0.70). Even without LCM (FastDrag†, 5.66 s), the method remains 3.8× faster than DiffEditor, confirming that the one-step warpage optimization—not the LCM acceleration—is the primary source of speedup. Overall, FastDrag trades a small amount of drag precision (MD) for an order-of-magnitude speed improvement, positioning it as the fastest drag-based editing method with competitive quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

FastDrag builds directly on the latent diffusion model (LDM) framework and the diffusion inversion/sampling pipeline established by DragDiffusion, while incorporating LCM-distilled U-Net weights for few-step inference and the self-attention key-value replacement technique from MasaCtrl/DragonDiffusion for consistency preservation. Its key departure from prior drag-based editing methods (DragDiffusion, FreeDrag, DragNoise, GoodDrag, DiffEditor) is replacing n-step iterative latent optimization—whether motion-based or gradient-based—with a single-step geometric warpage operation that simulates stretched-material deformation. This one-step latent relocation, combined with bilateral nearest-neighbor interpolation to fill null regions, constitutes the core methodological novelty, positioning FastDrag as an efficiency-focused alternative within the established drag-editing paradigm.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~7× speedup over the fastest prior method (DiffEditor) and ~28× over DragDiffusion, achieved without any LoRA training preparation, makes interactive drag-based editing practically viable for real-time or near-real-time applications. However, the quality trade-off is notable: FastDrag's MD (32.23) is substantially worse than GoodDrag (22.96), suggesting that one-step geometric warpage cannot fully replicate the fine-grained semantic adjustments of iterative optimization. Open questions include whether the LWF generalizes to more complex multi-object deformations, whether the approach extends to video or 3D editing, and whether the quality gap can be closed without reintroducing iterative steps.
