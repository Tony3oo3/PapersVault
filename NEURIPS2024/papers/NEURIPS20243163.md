## Problem

DANE achieves the best-known deterministic communication complexity for non-accelerated distributed optimization under second-order dissimilarity, but its local subproblems must be solved with accuracy that tightens across iterations, incurring a logarithmic overhead in local computation. FEDRED mitigates this only for gradient-descent local solvers, and no existing accelerated federated algorithm uniformly dominates DANE in communication complexity. The paper addresses both gaps: reducing local computation cost while preserving communication efficiency, and delivering an accelerated variant with the best-known complexity among all distributed convex methods.

## Input / Output

The algorithm receives n distributed local functions f_i (each μ-convex), an initial point x^0 = v^0, a client participation rate s ∈ [n], and the strong-convexity constant μ. At each round the server broadcasts the current prox-center v^r and a drift-correction gradient to a uniformly sampled subset of s clients; each client runs an arbitrary (possibly stochastic) local solver on its subproblem and returns its local model and gradient. The output is a weighted-average iterate x̄^R (or the raw iterate x^R for the accelerated variant) after R communication rounds, with deterministic or in-expectation suboptimality guarantees that are continuous in μ.

## Method

S-DANE replaces the standard proximal-point subproblem centered at x^r with one centered at an auxiliary sequence v^r (the 'stabilized prox-center'), and updates v^r via an extra-gradient step using the aggregated local gradients. This stabilization, borrowed from the Solodov–Svaiter hybrid projection-proximal-point method, relaxes the inexact-solution condition from O(δ²/r²) to O(δ²), eliminating the logarithmic local-computation overhead: a constant number of O(√(L/δ)) gradient steps per round suffices. The accelerated variant ACC-S-DANE layers Monteiro–Svaiter acceleration (sequences A_r, B_r, a_{r+1}) on top, improving the communication rate from Õ(δ/μ) to Õ(√(δ/μ)) for strongly convex problems and from O(δD²/ε) to O(√(δD²/ε)) for convex ones. Adaptive versions employ a line-search on the regularization parameter λ, dynamically doubling or halving it each round so that the similarity constant δ need not be known a priori, while preserving the same complexity up to a logarithmic factor.

## Results

S-DANE achieves the same deterministic communication complexity as DANE (O(δD²/ε) for general convex, O(δ/μ log(D²/ε)) for strongly convex) but reduces local gradient queries from O(√(L/δ) log(LD²/ε)) to O(√(L/δ)), eliminating the logarithmic overhead. Acc-S-DANE further improves communication to O(√(δD²/ε)) and O(√(δ/μ) log(D²/ε)) respectively, matching the best-known rates among all accelerated distributed methods while depending on the smaller constant δ rather than δ_max. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, S-DANE reaches 90% test accuracy within 50 communication rounds, while all compared baselines remain below 90% after 80 epochs. On the polyhedron feasibility problem (n=10, d=1000, m=10⁵), both S-DANE and Acc-S-DANE consistently outperform DANE, SCAFFOLD, SCAFFNEW, FEDPROX, and ACC-EXTRAGRADIENT in convergence speed.

## Baselines & Comparisons

S-DANE matches DANE [57] exactly in communication complexity (O(δD²/ε) convex, O(δ/μ log(D²/ε)) strongly convex) but strictly improves local computation by removing the logarithmic factor in the number of local gradient queries (O(√(L/δ)) vs. O(√(L/δ) log(LD²/ε))). Acc-S-DANE achieves the best-known communication complexity among all existing distributed convex optimization methods, improving over DANE from O(δ/μ) to O(√(δ/μ)) in the strongly convex regime, and depends on the smaller constant δ rather than δ_max used by Acc-Extragradient [33] and Acc-SONATA [61]. In the polyhedron feasibility experiments (n=10, d=1000), both S-DANE and Acc-S-DANE converge faster than DANE-GD, SCAFFOLD, SCAFFNEW, FEDPROX-GD, and ACC-EXTRAGRADIENT across all tested settings. On CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, S-DANE reaches 90% accuracy in 50 communication rounds while DANE, SCAFFOLD, SCAFFNEW, and FEDPROX all remain below 90% after 80 epochs. The adaptive line-search variants further outperform all fixed-λ baselines on the ijcnn logistic regression task for both i.i.d. (α=2) and non-i.i.d. (α=0.2) data splits, demonstrating practical advantage from exploiting local second-order dissimilarity without prior knowledge of δ.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on DANE (Shamir et al., 2014), the seminal distributed proximal-point algorithm that achieves the best-known communication complexity for non-accelerated federated methods by exploiting second-order dissimilarity. The key conceptual departure is importing the stabilized prox-center idea from the hybrid projection-proximal point method (Solodov & Svaiter, 1999) into the distributed setting, which relaxes the accuracy condition for local subproblems and eliminates the logarithmic overhead in local computation that plagues DANE and its FEDRED variant. For acceleration, the paper applies the Monteiro-Svaiter framework rather than the more common Güler/CATALYST approach, yielding a distributed accelerated method whose complexity depends on the smaller constant δ rather than δ_max used by ACC-EXTRAGRADIENT and AccSVRS.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

S-DANE and Acc-S-DANE are the first federated algorithms to simultaneously achieve the best-known communication complexity (among all methods, including accelerated ones) and near-optimal local computation efficiency, resolving a long-standing trade-off in the field. The adaptive line-search variants provide the first provably efficient parameter-free algorithms that exploit local second-order similarity without prior knowledge of δ, which is practically important since this constant is typically unknown. Open questions include extending the analysis to non-convex objectives, weakening the assumption from per-function strong convexity to only aggregate strong convexity, and exploring whether the stabilized prox-center idea transfers to other distributed frameworks beyond proximal-point methods.
