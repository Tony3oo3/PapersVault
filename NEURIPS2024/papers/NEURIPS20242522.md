## Problem

Formally verifying the global performance of specific trained neural networks is intractable with existing methods: PAC-Bayes bounds characterize training procedures rather than individual models, and model-checking or abstract-interpretation approaches verify only local robustness with poor asymptotic scaling. The core difficulty is that neural network expressivity makes it hard to compress global behavior into proofs that are both compact and faithful to the model's actual learned mechanisms, especially when relying solely on architecture or training-procedure knowledge.

## Input / Output

The method consumes the full weight matrices of a trained one-layer attention-only transformer (positional embeddings, token embeddings, Q/K/V/O matrices, and unembed matrix) along with a specification of the input distribution (uniform over all d_vocab^n_ctx sequences). It produces a formal proof certificate consisting of a computational trace C(M) that evaluates to a numeric accuracy lower bound b, paired with a non-computational argument Q establishing that C(M') ≤ expected accuracy for all models M'. Proof length is quantified by the FLOPs of the computational trace, and bound tightness by the ratio b/s̄. The approach is validated across 151 random seeds and four values of K (4, 5, 10, 20).

## Method

The paper reverse-engineers trained transformers via standard mechanistic interpretability (SVD of circuit matrices, zero-ablation, qualitative heatmap inspection) to decompose model computation into three circuits: the QK circuit (attention scoring), the OV circuit (token copying), and the direct path (skip connection), each further split into position-independent and position-dependent components. Proofs are then constructed by performing 'pessimal ablation'—setting unexplained components to worst-case values that minimize performance—over a convex relaxation of the input space, so that checking a small set of boundary sequences suffices to bound accuracy on all sequences. Three tiers of proof are developed: brute-force (O(v^{k+1}kd), treats model as black box), cubic (O(v³k²), uses circuit decomposition and convexity to reduce to pure sequences), and sub-cubic (O(v²k² + v²d), additionally exploits low-rank structure via SVD, a 'mean+diff trick' for sums of unequal-variance terms, and a 'max row-diff trick' to avoid full matrix multiplications). Across 102 proof strategies, the authors introduce 'unexplained dimensionality' as a quantitative metric for mechanistic understanding, finding that more compact proofs both require and provide more interpretability, while faithfulness of the interpretation modulates the proof-length-versus-bound-tightness tradeoff.

## Results

On Max-of-4 (n_ctx=4, d_vocab=64, d_model=32), the cubic proof achieves a 95.31±0.87% accuracy lower bound using 2^25 FLOPs, compared to the brute-force baseline's exact 99.92±0.15% bound at 2^47 FLOPs — a ~2^22 reduction in compute. Sub-cubic proofs (e.g., low-rank QK SVD) further reduce cost to 2^21–2^22 FLOPs but yield weaker bounds of 61–70%. Across 151 seeds and four values of K (4, 5, 10, 20), the cubic proof consistently recovers 88–95% of true accuracy in under two minutes, while brute force on Max-of-20 would require ~2^148 FLOPs (~2^70× the cost of training GPT-3).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead it compares 102 internally constructed proof strategies of varying compactness. The brute-force proof (treating the model as a black box) is the tightest baseline, achieving the exact accuracy of 99.92% but requiring 2^47 FLOPs and 2^30 unexplained dimensions. The cubic proof, which uses only the three-path decomposition (QK, OV, direct) and pessimization over sequence ordering, recovers 95.3% of the true accuracy at 2^25 FLOPs — a 2^22× reduction — while reducing unexplained dimensionality to 2^14. Sub-cubic proofs that additionally exploit the rank-1 structure of EQKE and the near-identity structure of EVOU via SVD reduce FLOPs further to ~2^21 but sacrifice bound tightness, dropping to 61–70% accuracy bounds. The key trade-off is that more mechanistic understanding (lower unexplained dimensionality) enables shorter proofs but, due to imperfect faithfulness of the interpretation, yields looser bounds — a relationship the paper quantifies via the unexplained dimensionality metric and confirms via the SVD singular-value ratio analysis (Figure 4).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the circuit decomposition framework from Elhage et al. [13] (QK circuit, OV circuit, direct path) and the ablation-based faithfulness assessment from Chan et al. [6] and Wang et al. [54] into a formal proof methodology, where unexplained model components are 'pessimally ablated' to derive worst-case performance bounds. It departs from prior formal verification work (model checking, abstract interpretation, SMT-based approaches) that verifies local robustness properties derived from architecture alone, instead reverse-engineering specific trained weights to prove global performance guarantees. The PAC-Bayes and uniform convergence literature provides generalization bounds over training procedures, whereas this paper targets bounds on particular trained models, bridging the gap between mechanistic interpretability (which identifies circuits empirically) and formal verification (which proves properties rigorously).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes a concrete prototype for using mechanistic interpretability as a proof-compaction tool, introducing 'unexplained dimensionality' as a quantitative metric linking interpretability depth to proof length. The identification of compounding structureless errors as a fundamental barrier—where worst-case composition of small per-component errors yields vacuous bounds—is a key open problem that likely generalizes beyond this toy setting. However, the entire study is conducted on 1-layer attention-only transformers solving Max-of-4, so scalability to deeper architectures, MLPs, or realistic tasks remains entirely untested. The authors themselves frame this as a feasibility study, and the practical impact will depend on whether mechanistic interpretability can scale to produce sufficiently faithful decompositions of large models.
