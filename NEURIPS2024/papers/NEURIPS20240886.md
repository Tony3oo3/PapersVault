## Problem

Subject-independent seizure detection from intracranial EEG (iEEG) is severely limited by large domain shifts across subjects (varying electrode placements, brain anatomy) and across time intervals within the same subject, causing normal and seizure signals to become nearly indistinguishable in the raw domain. Existing approaches either rely on non-invasive EEG methods that fail on the more complex iEEG signals, employ adversarial domain adaptation that suffers from negative transfer, or use elaborate pre-training pipelines that sacrifice efficiency. The paper identifies a universal, domain-consistent seizure pattern—seizure events consistently exhibit higher average spectral amplitude than adjacent normal events—and asks how to exploit this pattern to build an efficient, generalizable detector.

## Input / Output

For each detection segment (a single-channel iEEG window of ℓ timestamps, e.g., 250 points at 250 Hz), the model also consumes 2N adjacent contextual segments (N on each side, each of length ℓ) and K channel-level reference patterns obtained by K-Means clustering over the entire channel's frequency-domain representations. All segments are transformed to the frequency domain via DFT before comparison. The output is a binary seizure/normal label per segment, produced by a linear classifier on a CNN-encoded representation of the difference matrix; the difference matrix itself (a (2N+2K+1)×(2N+2K+1)×d tensor) is also retained as an interpretable artifact that visualizes seizure evolution.

## Method

DMNet implements self-comparison through two complementary references: a contextual reference (2N adjacent frequency-domain segments capturing local temporal dependencies across the seizure phase) and a channel-level reference (K K-Means centroids of the channel's full recording, capturing global channel-specific patterns and providing a stable normal background). These references are concatenated with the target segment's DFT representation into an augmented sequence, on which a fully differencing operation computes all pairwise differences, followed by signed min-max normalization to remove scale variations across subjects and channels. The resulting difference matrix encodes the relative spectral deviation of the target from both local and global baselines, directly capturing the universal 'higher-amplitude-during-seizure' pattern in a subject-invariant form. A lightweight CNN encoder (base filter count as low as 8) processes the 2D difference matrix to produce a compact representation for a linear classifier, yielding a model with only ~19% of the parameters of the smallest baseline while achieving the best F2-score across all three datasets.

## Results

DMNet achieves F2-scores of 55.93% (Clinical), 81.54% (MAYO), and 75.15% (FNUSA), outperforming the best prior iEEG-based method PPi by 4.81, 10.52, and 3.56 percentage points respectively. On the MAYO dataset, DMNet reaches 90.06% recall, substantially exceeding all baselines. The model also maintains high efficiency with only 19.4% of the parameters of the smallest competing model and the fastest inference time (45.5 s per subject file).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DMNet outperforms all 16 baselines on F2-score across all three primary datasets. Against the strongest iEEG-based competitor PPi (NeurIPS 2023), DMNet gains +4.81 F2 on Clinical (55.93 vs. 51.12), +10.52 on MAYO (81.54 vs. 71.02), and +3.56 on FNUSA (75.15 vs. 71.59). The EEG-based methods (Abou-Abbas, Zhao, Dissanayake) and generic domain-generalization methods (SelfReg, GroupDRO, CORAL, CDANN, VREx, etc.) trail substantially, with the best DG baseline (SelfReg) reaching only 48.63 F2 on Clinical and 32.12 on MAYO. On MAYO, DMNet's recall of 90.06% far exceeds the next-best (CDANN at 79.55%), indicating superior sensitivity to seizure events. On the broader TUSZ generalization test (179 unseen subjects), DMNet again leads with 65.86 F2 versus PPi's 56.09, confirming robustness beyond the primary evaluation sets.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm in subject-independent iEEG seizure detection, which has relied on adversarial domain adaptation (SICR, SEEGNet) or multi-stage pre-training strategies (PPi) to learn subject-invariant representations. Instead, it introduces a self-comparison mechanism grounded in the empirical observation that seizure events consistently exhibit higher spectral amplitude than adjacent normal segments, encoding this via a fully-differenced matrix between the target segment and two constructed references (contextual and channel-level). The method repurposes a classical signal-processing idea (differencing) within a CNN encoder, positioning itself as a domain generalization problem but solving it through explicit structural comparison rather than learned invariance.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: DMNet is deployed in a real clinical auxiliary diagnosis system, achieves the best F2-scores across three datasets while using only 19.4% of the smallest competing model's parameters and the fastest inference time, making it viable for real-time clinical use. The self-comparison mechanism offers a potentially transferable principle for other time-series domain generalization tasks where a local reference signal is available. However, the authors acknowledge the lack of theoretical grounding and the limited parameter count as constraints on broader generalizability, and the clinical dataset contains only 8 subjects, leaving open whether the approach scales to more heterogeneous populations.
