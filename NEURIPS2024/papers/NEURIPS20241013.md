## Problem

While learning progress (LP) has been shown to guide human goal selection, it only captures manifest changes in observable performance and provides no signal when performance remains flat despite genuine progress toward a solution. This paper asks whether humans also rely on a latent form of learning progress (LLP)—progress inferred from knowledge of the environment's structure and one's own action history, even in the absence of performance improvements—and whether LLP can be distinguished from and added to standard LP in explaining autotelic goal selection.

## Input / Output

The model consumes per-trial behavioral data from a hierarchical potion-making task: binary success/failure feedback, the set of unique action sequences attempted per goal, and the goal's total action-space size (12 for 2-action goals, 24 for 4-action goals). LLP is computed as 1 minus the fraction of possible action sequences tested, dropping to zero upon first success. The model outputs a softmax probability over the six available goals per trial, updated goal values via a shared delta-rule learning rate α, and per-factor weights β_f; models are compared via protected exceedance probability (PXP) and average responsibility (R) from hierarchical Bayesian inference.

## Method

The paper introduces a novel hierarchical reinforcement learning paradigm in which participants (N=175) freely select among six goals (potions) of varying difficulty and hierarchical structure, then choose ordered ingredient sequences to achieve them. Goal selection is modeled as a multi-armed bandit with a softmax over weighted goal values, where each factor (performance, LP, LLP, hierarchy) is updated via a delta rule with a shared learning rate. The key innovation is the LLP factor, defined as the remaining unexplored fraction of a goal's action space, which updates on every trial regardless of outcome—unlike LP, which requires observable performance change. The experimental design deliberately decorrelates LP from LLP through deterministic feedback, a limited action space enabling elimination-based reasoning, sparse early rewards, and hierarchical relationships among a subset of goals. Hierarchical Bayesian inference is used to fit and compare six model variants, revealing that a performance + LLP model (PXP=1, R=0.63) substantially outperforms a performance + LP model (R=0.12), with negative β_LLP indicating a preference for goals close to being solved.

## Results

The winning model (Performance + LLP) achieved a protected exceedance probability (PXP) of 1 and average responsibility (R) of 0.63, substantially outperforming all alternatives. The Performance + LP model (the closest standard baseline) achieved only R = 0.12, while Performance-only (R = 0.00) and Performance + Hierarchy (R = 0.04) were marginally represented. Behaviorally, hierarchical goals were learned with significantly fewer attempts (G3: M = 16.66 vs. G4: M = 22.1, p = 0.009) and higher performance (G3: M = 0.37 vs. G4: 0.29, p = 0.004), yet were selected less often (G3: M = 0.18 vs. G4: M = 0.21, p = 0.002), consistent with LLP-driven avoidance of near-solved goals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between computational models of goal selection that differ in which intrinsic motivation signal they incorporate. The LLP-based model (Performance + LLP, R = 0.63) decisively outperforms the standard Learning Progress model (Performance + LP, R = 0.12), indicating that humans track latent progress (e.g., number of action sequences eliminated) rather than relying solely on observed performance changes. The Performance + LLP + Hierarchy model (R = 0.22) captures a secondary subset of participants but does not surpass the simpler LLP model, suggesting hierarchy influences goal selection primarily indirectly through its effect on learning speed and thus LLP. Performance-only (R = 0.00) and Performance + Hierarchy (R = 0.04) models are essentially non-informative, ruling out the possibility that goal selection is driven by raw success rates or structural preferences alone. Across all four behavioral clusters identified via K-means, LLP remained a central component of goal selection, though the magnitude of β_LLP varied (cluster 2 efficient learners: M = −3.17 ± 0.18 vs. other clusters: M = −2.46 ± 0.16, p = 0.001).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on learning-progress-driven goal selection in humans (Ten et al. 2021; Poli et al. 2022), which showed that manifest LP and performance jointly guide activity choice. The key conceptual departure is introducing latent learning progress (LLP) — a model-based signal tracking the shrinking of the solution space through action elimination — as distinct from and potentially more informative than externally observable LP. For the learning component in simulations, the paper adopts the options framework (Sutton et al. 1999) to enable agents to exploit hierarchical structure, and uses hierarchical Bayesian inference for model comparison, following standard practices in computational cognitive modeling.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that LLP, rather than manifest LP, best explains human goal selection in a hierarchical task suggests that humans track progress through internal models even when performance is flat, with direct implications for designing autotelic AI agents that can detect progress before receiving the first reward. However, the LLP formalization is task-specific (discrete action sequences with deterministic feedback), the sample is homogeneous (undergraduate students), and goals are drawn from a predefined menu rather than self-generated. Open questions include whether LLP generalizes to continuous or stochastic environments, how it interacts with other intrinsic motivation signals (novelty, prediction error), and how individual differences in hierarchical reasoning moderate its role.
