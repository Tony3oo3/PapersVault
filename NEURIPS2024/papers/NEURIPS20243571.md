## Problem

Existing LLM benchmarks primarily test models on 'clean' and logically structured tasks, leaving unexplored whether LLMs can understand cunning texts containing misleading premises, intentional ambiguity, and word games that are trivially handled by humans. Since LLMs are trained on curated corpora, they may systematically fall into traps set by fallacious reasoning or linguistic tricks, yet no dedicated benchmark measures this fallacy understanding ability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243571/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The benchmark takes Chinese cunning texts (sourced from the Ruozhiba forum) as input. Task 1 appends four candidate explanations (one correct, three confusingly wrong) and expects a single letter selection; Task 2 expects a label from 8 predefined cunning types (e.g., Reasoning Error, Word Game, Paradox); Task 3 expects a free-form explanation generated by the model, which is scored 1–10 by GPT-4 or 1–5 by human annotators (scaled ×2 for comparability). The dataset contains 834 samples with an imbalanced type distribution (Reasoning Error 53.4%, Word Game 28.7%, some types as low as 0.6%), motivating the use of F-1 for classification.

## Method

FLUB is constructed by crawling 9,927 posts from the Ruozhiba forum, manually filtering and rewriting them into 834 clean cunning-text samples, then annotating each with a cunning type (via 3-annotator voting + senior review, Fleiss' Kappa > 0.767), a correct explanation, and three LLM-plausible wrong candidates. Three tasks of increasing difficulty evaluate fallacy understanding: Answer Selection (accuracy), Cunning Type Classification (macro F-1), and Fallacy Explanation (GPT-4-as-judge score). The paper further probes prompting strategies—Chain-of-Thought and in-context learning (0/1/2/5-shot)—across 13 LLMs (ERNIE-Bot, GPT, ChatGLM3, Qwen, Yi, Baichuan2). A key finding is that CoT provides no stable gain because these tasks demand linguistic intuition and cultural knowledge rather than step-by-step logical decomposition, and that larger models do not always outperform smaller ones on the classification task due to degraded sensitivity to internet slang.

## Results

On FLUB, GPT-4-Turbo achieves the best overall performance (geometric mean of 22.00 with CoT), followed by ERNIE-Bot-4.0 (20.28) and Qwen-72B-Chat (18.13). All models perform poorly on Cunning Type Classification, with F-1 scores below 15.0, and some (e.g., ERNIE-Bot-3.5-Turbo at 1.99, Baichuan2-7B-Chat at 1.02) fall below the random baseline of 7.90. Humans achieve 93.35% Selection Accuracy and 63.69% Classification F-1, revealing a substantial gap between human and LLM fallacy understanding. Chain-of-Thought prompting yields inconsistent gains, helping some models (e.g., GPT-4-Turbo: 20.60→22.00) while hurting others (e.g., ERNIE-Bot-3.5: 15.13→13.86).

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4-Turbo leads all models on every task: 82.73% Selection Accuracy, 13.97 Classification F-1, and 9.21 GPT-4 Explanation Score (all with CoT), outperforming the second-best ERNIE-Bot-4.0 (71.34 / 14.42 / 8.11) and Qwen-72B-Chat (61.51 / 12.26 / 7.90). Among open-source models, Qwen-72B-Chat is the strongest (overall 18.13 with CoT), followed by Yi-34B-Chat (16.17) and Qwen-14B-Chat (14.10); smaller 6–7B models (ChatGLM3-6B, Qwen-7B, Yi-6B, Baichuan2-7B) cluster around 9–12 overall. Notably, ERNIE-Bot-3.5 (15.13 w/o CoT) outperforms GPT-3.5-Turbo (9.91) on overall score, and CoT actually degrades ERNIE-Bot-3.5's performance (15.13→13.86) while improving GPT-3.5-Turbo (9.91→12.74). All LLMs fall far short of human performance (93.35% Selection, 63.69% F-1), with even the best model (GPT-4-Turbo) achieving only 82.73% Selection and 13.97 F-1. The paper also shows that in-context learning with 5-shot demonstrations generally improves performance over zero-shot, whereas 1-shot can sometimes hurt, and that CoT benefits are concentrated in models above 10B parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing a fallacy-understanding evaluation axis that sits at the intersection of commonsense reasoning benchmarks (CommonsenseQA, PIQA, HellaSWAG) and humor/word-game NLP, but targets a distinct gap: texts that are intentionally misleading, ambiguous, or built on faulty premises. Rather than extending a specific prior method, it repurposes the standard LLM evaluation pipeline (multiple-choice selection, classification, open-ended generation) on a new corpus of real-world 'cunning texts' sourced from the Chinese forum Ruozhiba. The automatic evaluation design draws on MT-Bench's GPT-4-as-judge paradigm, and the prompting experiments follow the established CoT and in-context learning literature, but the core contribution is the dataset and the three-task formulation around fallacy comprehension.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

FLUB exposes a substantial and persistent gap between human and LLM performance on fallacy understanding (humans score 93.35% on selection vs. 79.38% for the best LLM; classification F-1 stays below 15 for all models), suggesting that current LLMs remain vulnerable to the kind of misleading reasoning encountered in everyday discourse. The finding that Chain-of-Thought prompting provides no consistent benefit—and sometimes degrades performance—on these intuition-heavy, non-logical tasks challenges the broad assumption that CoT is a universal improvement. Key limitations include the Chinese-only scope, reliance on GPT-4 as an automatic judge, and the relatively small dataset (834 samples), leaving open whether the findings generalize to multilingual settings and whether targeted training on fallacy-rich data can close the human-LLM gap.
