## Problem

Existing procedural activity datasets contain only videos of individuals performing step-by-step tasks correctly, lacking the error examples and annotations needed to train AI systems that can detect deviations from procedures. This gap is particularly acute for cooking activities in real kitchen environments, where ingredients continuously change shape and color, making error detection harder than in assembly or lab settings with static objects.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244306/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The dataset provides synchronized egocentric RGB video (GoPro 4K at 30 fps), depth frames (HoloLens2 at 360p, 30 fps in articulated hand tracking mode), audio, IMU sensor streams, and head/hand pose tracking across 384 recordings (94.5 hours) of 24 recipes. For supervised baselines, videos are split into 1-second sub-segments and features are extracted via pre-trained backbones; for zero-shot VLM baselines, engineered text prompts (single-step or error-category-specific) are paired with video clips. Outputs include binary error/normal labels per recipe step, temporal step boundaries with class labels for multi-step localization, and ordered key-step sequences for procedure learning.

## Method

The paper introduces CaptainCook4D, an egocentric 4D dataset with 5.3K step annotations, 10K fine-grained action annotations, and 7 error categories (preparation, measurement, technique, timing, temperature, missing steps, ordering), collected from 8 participants in 10 real kitchens using three error-induction strategies (impromptu, disordered steps, scripted errors). For supervised error recognition, three architectural variants are proposed: V1 (MLP on per-sub-segment features), V2 (Transformer over all sub-segments within a step to capture long-term temporal cues), and V3 (unified multimodal Transformer with cross-attention over video, audio, depth, and text features from ImageBind). For zero-shot error recognition, a prompt-ensembling strategy (V2) generates seven error-category-specific query prompts via Llama3 and combines VLM predictions with an OR operation, substantially outperforming single-prompt queries. Multi-step localization uses an ActionFormer head on pre-trained features evaluated across six data splits, and procedure learning employs self-supervised embedder networks trained with Cycleback Regression and Contrastive-Inverse Difference Moment losses combined with a Pro-Cut Module for key-step segmentation.

## Results

On the CaptainCook4D benchmark, the best supervised error recognition model (Omnivore + V2 transformer) achieves 69.96% accuracy and 55.39% F1 on the step split, while the best zero-shot VLM (TimeChat with prompt-ensembling V2) reaches only 46.1% F1, highlighting the difficulty of error recognition in egocentric cooking videos. For Multi-Step Localization, the Omnivore-based ActionFormer model with the Person split (P) achieves the highest mAP of 48.16% at IoU=0.1 and 41.16% at IoU=0.5. In Procedure Learning, the EgoProceL-based model (M2) achieves an average precision of 15.78% and IOU of 5.82% across 24 recipes, substantially above the random baseline (7.61% P, 2.22% IOU) but well below scores on existing procedure learning datasets, reflecting the challenge of longer step durations.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For Procedure Learning, the EgoProceL-based model (M2, using Cycleback + Contrastive-Inverse Difference Moment loss) achieves an average precision of 15.78% and IOU of 5.82% across 24 recipes, marginally outperforming the Cycleback-only model (M1, Dwibedi et al. 2019) at 15.62% P and 5.78% IOU, while both substantially exceed the random baseline (7.61% P, 2.22% IOU). For Zero-Shot Error Recognition, TimeChat with the prompt-ensembling variant (V2) achieves the best F1 of 46.1%, outperforming Video-LLaVa V2 (41.8% F1) by 4.3 points; however, both VLMs struggle significantly with the single-prompt variant (V1), where TimeChat drops to 2.26% F1 and Video-LLaVa to 6.7% F1, demonstrating that error-category-specific prompts are critical. In Supervised Error Recognition, the Omnivore-based V2 transformer (55.39% F1) outperforms the multimodal ImageBind V3 model with all modalities (V,A,D,T) at 49.84% F1 on the step split, suggesting that strong video features alone can rival multimodal fusion for this task. Across all tasks, the paper demonstrates that current methods remain far from solving error recognition in egocentric procedural videos, with the best F1 scores staying below 56%.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the procedural error recognition task introduced by Assembly101 (which framed mistake detection as multi-class classification over assembly videos) to a fundamentally different domain: cooking, where objects continuously change shape and color rather than remaining static. It builds on EgoProceL's self-supervised procedure learning setup (Pro-Cut Module, Cycleback Regression loss) and ActionFormer for multi-step localization, while departing from prior error datasets (HoloAssist, IndustReal, ATA) that are confined to lab environments with constant-shape objects. The 4D egocentric data collection via HoloLens2 and GoPro, combined with real kitchen settings, positions this work as a multimodal generalization of both the assembly-error and egocentric-cooking dataset lineages.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The dataset fills a critical gap: no prior resource combines cooking (dynamic objects), real environments, 4D sensing, and both intentional/unintentional errors in an egocentric setting, making it a natural testbed for transferring error-detection methods to sensitive domains like medicine and chemistry. The consistently poor baseline performance across all three tasks (error recognition F1 ≤ 50, MSL mAP ≤ 48, procedure learning IOU ≤ 7) signals that current methods are far from adequate, opening substantial room for future work. Key limitations include the small participant pool (8 people, 10 kitchens), the combinatorial error space being only partially sampled, and the geographic constraint on data collection, all of which bound the dataset's generalizability.
