## Problem

Prior work on model refusal and noncompliance has focused almost exclusively on safety-related queries (offensive content, dangerous information, privacy violations), leaving a large gap in understanding when models should *not* comply with requests that are incomplete, unsupported, indeterminate, or anthropomorphizing. The paper asks two questions: (RQ1) how well do state-of-the-art models handle these broader noncompliance scenarios, and (RQ2) how can we train models to exhibit appropriate noncompliance without triggering over-refusal or degrading general capabilities?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241572/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

For evaluation, the model receives a single user text prompt (optionally preceded by a system prompt instructing noncompliance behavior) and produces a free-form text response; a GPT-3.5 judge then classifies the response as compliant or noncompliant per subcategory-specific rubrics, yielding a compliance rate per category. For training, the input is a prompt–response pair from the 11,477-instance COCONOT training set (or 927 preference pairs from COCONOT-PREF), and the output is a fine-tuned LoRA adapter or DPO-updated model weights. The contrast set (379 benign prompts) is used to measure over-refusal, where higher compliance is desirable.

## Method

The paper introduces a five-category taxonomy of contextual noncompliance (incomplete, indeterminate, unsupported, humanizing, safety) with fine-grained subcategories and prescribes expected model behaviors ranging from direct refusal to clarification questions to disclaimers. COCONOT is constructed via a Self-Instruct-style iterative generation loop using GPT-4, with automatic deduplication via sentence transformers and manual verification of the 1,000-prompt evaluation split; a contrastive set is built by transforming noncompliance prompts into benign counterparts. For training, the authors compare four strategies on Llama-2 7B / Tulu-2 7B: (1) SFT from scratch on Tulu2Mix + COCONOT, (2) continued full-parameter SFT on COCONOT, (3) continued LoRA finetuning on COCONOT, and (4) DPO preference tuning on COCONOT-PREF (compliant responses preferred over noncompliant ones). The key finding is that LoRA finetuning achieves the best trade-off—significantly reducing compliance on noncompliance prompts while preserving general capabilities (MMLU, AlpacaEval) and contrast-set compliance—whereas full SFT causes catastrophic forgetting and SFT-from-scratch induces over-refusal; DPO on contrast data then further reduces over-refusals without sacrificing noncompliance gains.

## Results

On the COCONOT benchmark (1,000 prompts), GPT-4 incorrectly complies with 29.8% of incomplete requests and 14.1% of indeterminate requests, while Llama-3-70B reaches 29.9% compliance on unsupported requests. Among training strategies, LoRA finetuning of Tulu-2 7B on COCONOT achieves the best balance: 17.8% incomplete compliance, 14.2% unsupported compliance, and 90.8% contrast compliance, while maintaining MMLU (50.0) and AlpacaEval (74.2) close to the Tulu-2 baseline. Adding DPO on contrastive preference data (COCONOT-PREF) further improves contrast compliance to 93.1% with minimal loss in noncompliance. The merged LoRA adapter (Tulu-2-no-refusal + Tulu-2) achieves the strongest overall profile, outperforming GPT-4 on COCONOT noncompliance (e.g., 9.1% safety vs. 11.4%) while retaining general capabilities.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four training strategies against two baselines (Tulu-2 7B and Tulu-2-No-Refusal 7B) plus GPT-4 as a reference. Full-parameter continued SFT on COCONOT dramatically improves noncompliance (e.g., incomplete compliance drops from 25.8% to 1.3%) but causes catastrophic forgetting, collapsing AlpacaEval from 73.9 to 18.7 and XSTEST benign compliance to 26.3. LoRA finetuning avoids this collapse, maintaining AlpacaEval at 74.2 and MMLU at 50.0 while still reducing incomplete compliance from 25.8% to 17.8% and unsupported from 21.0% to 14.2%. The merged LoRA adapter (Tulu-2-no-refusal + Tulu-2) achieves the best noncompliance profile, beating GPT-4 on safety (9.1% vs. 11.4%) and humanizing (4.9% vs. 6.1%) compliance while matching its general capabilities. DPO on contrastive preference data (COCONOT-PREF) recovers overrefusal, lifting contrast compliance from 90.8% to 93.1% on Tulu-2-7B with only a ~2.6% increase in incomplete compliance. SFT from scratch (T2M+CoCoNot) achieves the lowest noncompliance rates (e.g., 0.0% indeterminate) but suffers the worst overrefusal (74.9% contrast), confirming that parameter-efficient methods strike a better trade-off.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies previously independent research threads—safety refusal, unanswerable/ambiguous question detection, model abstention, and content moderation—under a single taxonomy of 'contextual noncompliance.' It draws on Self-Instruct-style data generation, SituatedQA for underspecified queries, and XSTEST-style contrastive sets for overrefusal measurement, but the key conceptual contribution is the unified taxonomy and the argument that noncompliance should be evaluated as a multi-faceted capability rather than a safety-only concern. For training, it builds on LoRA for parameter-efficient adaptation and DPO for preference optimization, combining them in a pipeline (LoRA SFT + DPO on contrast data) that has not been previously explored for noncompliance induction.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The COCONOT benchmark and taxonomy provide a practical, reusable resource for evaluating and improving the full spectrum of appropriate noncompliance in chat models, going well beyond the safety-only framing that dominates current evaluation. The finding that even GPT-4 complies with ~30% of incomplete/unsupported requests highlights a significant and underappreciated gap in model behavior that affects user trust and experience. Open questions include whether the training strategies are robust to jailbreaking, whether models can leverage their own epistemic awareness more effectively, and whether LoRA-based continued finetuning generalizes as a solution to catastrophic forgetting more broadly. The paper also acknowledges limitations: synthetic data generation introduces noise, the taxonomy may not cover all subcategory scope, and US-centric legality definitions may introduce bias.
