## Problem

Optimistic verifiable training schemes, where a third-party auditor replicates training to detect misbehavior, are undermined by GPU hardware nondeterminism: even with identical data, seeds, and software, different GPU architectures produce divergent model weights due to floating-point accumulation order differences. This makes exact weight comparison impossible, rendering existing proof-of-learning protocols forgeable and non-robust, while proof-based (ZK) alternatives are too slow to scale beyond trivial models.

## Input / Output

The trainer consumes a dataset D, model architecture with training specifications (epochs, loss, target precision b_m), and a shared pseudo-random seed R; the auditor additionally receives the trainer's rounding log file F (packed at 1.6 bits per entry, encoding per-element rounding direction as 0/1/2) and the Merkle tree root. The trainer outputs a model in target precision, a Merkle tree root (SHA-256 hashes of weights at checkpoint interval k), and the rounding log; the auditor outputs a matching or mismatching Merkle root, and upon mismatch, an interactive binary search pinpoints the exact training step of divergence.

## Method

The key insight is that GPU nondeterminism introduces errors only in the lower floating-point bits, so training at a higher precision (FP64) than the target (FP32) and rounding after each intermediate layer confines errors below the rounding boundary. However, values can straddle the boundary, causing the trainer and auditor to round differently; to fix this, the trainer logs the rounding direction (up, down, or ignore) for each layer output using an adaptive per-layer threshold τ that suppresses logging when the value is close enough to the rounded value that both GPUs would agree. The auditor reads the log and applies a rev correction to force identical rounding. Model weights at each checkpoint are hashed (SHA-256) and stored in a Merkle tree, enabling the verification game's binary-search dispute protocol to identify the exact divergent step with O(log n) communication. Efficient radix-3-to-radix-2 packing and zip compression reduce the rounding log storage by ~77% over naive encoding, and the adaptive threshold further improves compressibility by increasing the fraction of 'ignore' entries.

## Results

The method achieves exact training replication (zero weight divergence) at FP32 precision across all pairs of three NVIDIA GPU architectures (A40, Titan XP, RTX 2080 Ti) for both full training of ResNet-50 (90.7% test accuracy on CIFAR-10) and fine-tuning of GPT-2 (perplexity 4.22 on Shakespeare). With efficient radix-3 encoding, rounding logs require only 105 MB for ResNet-50 and 22 MB for GPT-2 (further reducible to 96 MB and 18 MB with zip compression), a 77% reduction over naive logging. Training time overhead is modest: 1.2–1.4× for the trainer and 1.3–1.7× for the auditor relative to standard training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Garg et al. [2023] (ZK proof for logistic regression), the proposed method reduces storage from 350 MB to 106 KB and time from 72 s to 7 s for one training pass. Against Abbaszadeh et al. [2024] (ZK proof for VGG-11), the method is 220× faster per training iteration (6 s vs. 22 min) while using comparable proof/checkpoint size (1.2 MB vs. 1.36 MB). Against Jia et al. [2021] (proof-of-learning), the method requires over 140× less storage for GPT-2 (18 MB total vs. 2.78 GB of saved weights) and eliminates the statistical error inherent in weight-comparison protocols. Against Gupta et al. [2023] (MPC-based secure GPT-2 inference adapted to training), the method avoids an estimated 10,000× data overhead (18 MB vs. 189 GB) and 40× time overhead (24.5 s vs. 983 s). In all four comparisons, the proposed optimistic verification scheme wins decisively on both storage and time, at the cost of requiring a trusted third-party auditor.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the interactive verification game from Teutsch and Reitwießner (2019), originally designed for blockchain consensus, to the ML training setting, using Merkle trees of model weight hashes and binary search to locate the exact step of divergence between trainer and auditor. It departs from prior proof-of-learning protocols (Jia et al., 2021) that simply compare model weights at checkpoints and are known to be forgeable due to GPU nondeterminism, and from proof-based ZK systems (Garg et al., 2023; Abbaszadeh et al., 2024) that are computationally intractable for real-world models. The key conceptual departure is introducing a mechanism to *eliminate* cross-GPU nondeterminism at its source—by training in higher precision, logging rounding decisions near boundaries, and applying an adaptive per-layer threshold—rather than tolerating or statistically bounding the resulting weight divergence.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work makes optimistic verifiable training practically viable for models with tens to hundreds of millions of parameters across heterogeneous GPU hardware, a setting where prior methods either fail (proof-of-learning) or are intractable (ZK proofs). A striking open question is the enormous gap between logged rounding decisions and those actually needed by the auditor (only 2e-6% to 9e-6% are used), suggesting that a predictive mechanism for when divergence will occur could reduce storage by orders of magnitude. Limitations include the 1-of-n auditor honesty assumption, the added 1.2–1.7× training latency, and the fact that the scheme does not yet extend to distributed training or closed-source base models, both of which are increasingly common in practice.
