## Problem

Most federated learning methods assume model homogeneity (identical architectures) and task homogeneity (identical objectives) across clients, making them unable to handle real-world scenarios where clients differ in hardware, data distribution, and learning goals simultaneously. Existing approaches to model heterogeneity rely on knowledge distillation requiring public datasets or prototype matching that fails for related-but-inconsistent tasks, while task heterogeneity is almost entirely unaddressed in federated settings. No prior method provides a unified, flexible mechanism to couple heterogeneous client models across all three heterogeneity dimensions.

## Input / Output

Each client contributes private data in diverse formats: 561-dimensional sensor vectors (Human Activity), 28×28 grayscale digits (MNIST), 32×32 RGB images (CIFAR-10/100, PACS, Adience Faces), and clients may run different tasks on the same images (e.g., 2-class gender vs. 8-class age on Adience Faces). The output is a personalized class label per client, with the key constraint that clients can have different model architectures (varying convolutional filter counts, layer depths, and fully-connected dimensions) and different output cardinalities. The only information uploaded to the server is a flexibly chosen subset of local model parameters (the 'global shared layers'), whose identity depends on the heterogeneity type.

## Method

FedSAK decomposes each client model into a feature extractor f_i and prediction head h_i, then flexibly designates a subset w_i ⊆ θ_i as the global shared layers: the full model under data heterogeneity, the prediction head under model heterogeneity, or the feature extractor under task heterogeneity. On the server, shared layers from all M clients are stacked into a p-way tensor W^l per layer, and a Tucker-based tensor trace norm (sum of matrix trace norms over all mode-unfoldings) is applied as a regularizer to induce a low-rank structure that couples client parameters through shared low-dimensional subspaces. The server computes the subgradient of the trace norm via SVD of each mode-unfolding and broadcasts updated shared layers back to clients, who then perform local SGD on their full heterogeneous models. This design avoids the need for public datasets, prototype matching, or identical architectures, while the tensor trace norm provides a principled mechanism for knowledge transfer that adapts to the degree of inter-client correlation.

## Results

On the data heterogeneity benchmark (Table 1), FedSAK achieves the highest average test accuracy across 12 settings on 4 datasets, e.g., 98.46% on HumA (10,6), 98.85% on MNIST (100,2), 84.49% on CIFAR-10 (20,3), and 39.16% on CIFAR-100 (20,30), outperforming the best baselines (FedProto, Ditto, pFedMe) by 0.7–13.2 percentage points. Under model heterogeneity (Table 2), FedSAK leads all settings, surpassing FedGH by up to 5.98% on PACS (M=4). On the task heterogeneity benchmark (Table 3, Adience Faces), FedSAK achieves 76.95% total accuracy at M=21:1, improving over Local by 6.58% and over FedAvg-c by 1.24%. On ResNet18/CIFAR-10 (Table 5), FedSAK reaches 77.69% accuracy with the lowest time (7,118 s) and memory (0.918 GB) among all 14 methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

FedSAK outperforms all 13 baselines on the majority of data-heterogeneity settings in Table 1, with the largest margins over conventional FL methods (FedAvg, FedProx) and moderate gains over personalized methods (Ditto, pFedMe) and heterogeneous FL methods (FedProto, FedGH). The only setting where FedSAK trails a baseline is CIFAR-100 (20,50), where Ditto achieves 32.97% vs. FedSAK's 31.47%. Under model heterogeneity (Table 2), FedSAK consistently beats FedMD, FedProto, and FedGH across all CIFAR-10 and PACS configurations, with the largest gap of 5.98% on PACS (M=4) over FedGH. On task heterogeneity (Table 3), FedSAK surpasses FedAvg-c (the only comparable baseline) by 1.2–3.9% in total accuracy across all four client-ratio settings, and notably avoids the bias toward majority-task clients that FedAvg-c exhibits at M=15 (1:2 ratio). On the ResNet18/CIFAR-10 experiment (Table 5), FedSAK's 77.69% accuracy is slightly below GPFL (78.59%) and Ditto (77.98%), but it achieves the lowest wall-clock time (7,118 s vs. 18,749 s for GPFL) and the smallest memory footprint (0.918 GB vs. 1.83 GB), demonstrating its communication-efficiency advantage on larger networks.

## Relation to Prior Work

FedSAK builds on the Federated Multi-Task Learning (FMTL) framework of Smith et al. (NeurIPS 2017) and the Tucker-based tensor trace norm regularization of Zhang et al. (KDD 2021), repurposing the latter from centralized multi-task learning to a federated server-side coupling mechanism. It departs from prior heterogeneous FL methods (FedMD, FedProto, FedGH) that rely on knowledge distillation or prototype matching requiring public datasets, instead using a low-rank structural constraint that needs no external data. The key conceptual departure is the flexible definition of shared layers (w_i ⊆ θ_i), which unifies data, model, and task heterogeneity under a single optimization objective rather than treating each as a separate problem.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work addresses a practical gap where most FL methods assume model homogeneity, yet real deployments face heterogeneous hardware, architectures, and even different prediction tasks across clients. The tensor trace norm coupling is elegant and requires no public datasets, but its O(min_k d_k² · ∏_{i≠k} d_i) computational complexity on the server becomes a bottleneck for very large models, and the low-rank assumption may not hold when client tasks are truly unrelated. Open questions include scalability to dynamic client participation, adaptive selection of which layers to share, and whether the approach extends to non-classification tasks like segmentation or generation.
