## Problem

Existing dataset copyright protection methods for text-to-image models—adversarial perturbations (Glaze), backdoor-based verification, and digital watermarking—are all vulnerable to adversarial purification, introduce security risks, or are easily removable during diffusion fine-tuning. No prior approach can detect unauthorized style or content mimicry at the representation level, where the watermark is intrinsic to the protected data's semantic structure rather than an embedded digital signal that can be stripped away.

## Input / Output

The method takes a generated (mimic) image and an identifier z (a CLIP text embedding, e.g., of the string 'swz') as input, and produces 128-bit watermark bits along with a watermark distribution score (t@k@100%wd) for copyright attribution. Unlike traditional embed-then-extract pipelines, verification uses a watermark extractor with K predefined mapping relationships and the identifier z to probe the disentangled style domain, enabling one-sample verification without requiring the original protected image. For multi-party scenarios, the output extends to a per-defender watermark distribution across T style domain encoders to attribute hybrid or partial infringements.

## Method

The core mechanism disentangles each protected unit into a style representation via a ResNet encoder regularized by a VAE, with an identifier z injected as a key bias that shifts the style domain S_z to a marginal distribution. A UNet decoder (2m+1 activation layers) is modulated by sub-vectors of the style code with layer-wise guidance dropout to foster specialization. Training employs dynamic contrastive learning with two regularization terms: a self-generalization loss (ψ₁) that pulls central samples of the contraction domain toward the anchor while pushing boundary samples away, and a mutual-exclusivity loss (ψ₂) that maximizes separation between the contraction domain and a dynamic historical negative queue, both using momentum-updated encoder copies. The watermark is implicitly quantized from the mutual exclusivity of contraction domains, so no invisible bits are embedded in the image itself. Verification computes the probability P_z(x|φ≈D) of a mimic sample falling within the protected contraction domain, and for multi-party cases compares watermark distributions across T encoders to attribute ownership.

## Results

On the primary comparison (Table 2), the proposed z-Watermarking method achieves 99.83% average watermark accuracy (Avg acc) and 97.7% t@k@100%wd (proportion of samples with 100% bit accuracy), dramatically outperforming all digital watermarking baselines which range from 55.37% to 66.50% Avg acc and ≤0.1% to 7.9% t@k@100%wd. Across five datasets (CelebA, CUB, Dreambooth, Artists, AIs), the method maintains Avg acc above 99.5% and t@k@100%wd above 96% under standard mimicry conditions, while non-mimic models show only ~50% Avg acc. In the generalization study across 7 black-box models/APIs (including DALL-E-3, Imagen2, PixArt-α), the method achieves 98.60% average Avg acc and 94.29% average t@k@100%wd with 100% True Positive detection on most models.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against four digital watermarking baselines (DCT-DWT-SVD, RivaGan, SSL, Trustmark) and one additional method (RoSteALS) on the task of detecting unauthorized dataset usage in AI-generated images. The proposed z-Watermarking method decisively outperforms all baselines on both metrics: it achieves 99.83% Avg acc versus 55.37–66.50% for baselines (a gap of 33–44 percentage points), and 97.7% t@k@100%wd versus ≤0.1–7.9% (a gap of up to 97.6 percentage points). DCT-DWT-SVD (57.76% Avg acc, ≤0.1% t@k@100%wd) and RivaGan (61.34%, ≤0.1%) are the weakest, essentially failing to extract complete watermarks from diffusion-generated images. SSL (64.39%, ≤0.1%) and Trustmark (55.37%, 6.6%) show marginally better partial extraction but still cannot reliably attribute ownership. RoSteALS (66.50%, 7.9%) is the strongest baseline but remains far below the proposed method, indicating that traditional invisible watermarks are fundamentally vulnerable to removal or dilution during diffusion model fine-tuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant paradigm of dataset copyright protection—embedding explicit information (adversarial perturbations à la Glaze, backdoors à la DIAGNOSIS, or digital watermarks à la Luo et al.) into protected data—by instead treating the disentangled style domain of protected units as an implicit, self-contained watermark. Conceptually, it repurposes disentangled representation learning (style-content separation via VAE latent regularization, ResNet style encoders, and contrastive domain delineation) for a forensic purpose: the contraction domain around each protected unit's style anchor, shaped by an identifier z and dynamic contrastive learning, becomes the copyright boundary itself. The method draws on IP customization techniques (CLIP-based identifier embedding, momentum-updated encoders) but applies them to a verification rather than generation task, and it explicitly positions against the vulnerability of embedded watermarks to diffusion-based purification attacks.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The one-sample verification capability and the watermark distribution framework for hybrid/partial infringements address practical gaps in current AI copyright enforcement, where defenders often encounter only a single suspicious image from a black-box API. However, the method's reliance on a pre-trained style domain encoder and its sensitivity to geometric transformations (rotation drops t@k@100%wd from 97.9% to 14.8%) raise open questions about real-world robustness. The paper also acknowledges vulnerability to targeted style-domain manipulations (e.g., Glaze-style adversarial optimization), and the 'extremely unlikely probability' argument in Eq. 7, while mathematically framed, lacks a formal security proof. Broader implications include whether this implicit watermarking paradigm can extend to multi-modal (video, audio) copyright protection and whether the style domain can be standardized as a legal evidence format.
