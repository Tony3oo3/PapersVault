## Problem

Graph-based nearest neighbor search methods (HNSW, DiskANN, NSG) rely on constructing navigable graphs where greedy routing succeeds from any node to any target, but the minimum sparsity of such graphs in high dimensions was largely unknown. While low-dimensional constructions (Delaunay, sparse neighborhood graphs) achieve O(1) or 2^{O(d)} average degree, these degrade to O(n) when d = Ω(log n), leaving a gap between the trivial complete graph and what might be achievable. This paper establishes the first tight upper and lower bounds on navigable graph sparsity for high-dimensional point sets.

## Input / Output

The method takes a set of n distinct points in an arbitrary domain X and a distance function D (not required to be a metric or symmetric for the upper bound) and produces a directed graph with bounded average degree. The upper bound construction works universally for any distance function, while the lower bound is specific to Euclidean distance over random ±1 vectors in {−1,1}^d. The output graph must satisfy the navigability property: greedy routing from any starting node to any target node in the dataset always terminates at the target, and the upper-bound construction additionally guarantees termination in at most 2 steps.

## Method

The upper bound (Theorem 1) constructs a navigable graph with average degree O(√(n log n)) by taking the union of two edge sets: (1) for each node i, edges from its m nearest neighbors back to i itself, and (2) random edges (or a greedy set-cover in the deterministic variant) ensuring every node beyond the m-th nearest neighbor has an edge into i's near-neighborhood. Balancing m = √(n log n) yields the bound, and the construction runs in O(n²(T + log n)) time. The lower bound (Theorem 2) shows that for random ±1 vectors in {−1,1}^d with d = Ω(log n), any navigable graph requires average degree Ω(n^{1/2−δ}). The proof defines fixed-radius near-neighborhoods O_j and shows via sharp binomial CDF anti-concentration bounds (Ahle/Cramér) that these neighborhoods have size Θ(√n) but pairwise intersections of only O(log n) or O(n^{c√(log n/d)}), forcing Ω(n^{3/2}) total edges since each neighborhood requires |O_j|−1 internal edges that cannot be shared across neighborhoods.

## Results

The paper establishes nearly tight upper and lower bounds on the sparsity of navigable graphs for high-dimensional nearest neighbor search. The upper bound (Theorem 1) shows that for any point set of size n in any dimension under any distance function, a directed navigable graph with average degree at most $2\sqrt{n\ln n}$ can be efficiently constructed, with greedy routing succeeding in at most 2 steps. The lower bound (Theorem 2) shows that for random $\pm 1$ vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $d \geq c\log n / \delta^2$, any navigable graph requires average degree $\Omega(n^{1/2-\delta})$, making the upper bound essentially tight up to a $\log n$ factor. A more general lower bound (Theorem 4) gives $\Omega(n^{3/2-\epsilon})$ total edges, and a worst-case maximum degree lower bound (Theorem 13) shows that some point sets in $O(\log n)$ dimensions force maximum out-degree $n-1$.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental baselines or dataset comparisons. The paper positions its bounds relative to prior theoretical constructions: the Delaunay graph is navigable but has average degree $O(n)$ in dimension $d \geq 3$, and the sparse neighborhood graphs of Arya and Mount (1993) achieve average degree $2^{O(d)}$, which is no better than the complete graph when $d = \Omega(\log n)$. The paper's upper bound of $O(\sqrt{n\log n})$ average degree improves upon both of these in the high-dimensional regime ($d = \Omega(\log n)$), while the lower bound of $\Omega(n^{1/2-\delta})$ shows that this improvement is essentially the best possible. The gap between the upper and lower bounds is a factor of $O(\sqrt{\log n})$, and the authors note that closing this gap is an open problem. The maximum degree lower bound (Theorem 13) shows that no construction can avoid worst-case degree $n-1$, in contrast to the average degree results.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the theoretical study of navigable graphs—originating from Kleinberg's small-world analysis—to the high-dimensional nearest neighbor search setting, where prior results (Delaunay graphs, sparse neighborhood graphs of Arya and Mount) only provide useful sparsity bounds for low dimensions (d = O(1) or d = O(log n) with exponential-in-d constants). The upper bound construction repurposes ideas already present in practical graph-based ANN methods (HNSW, DiskANN, NSG) that combine k-nearest-neighbor edges with random long-range edges, but provides the first formal proof that this simple union yields a navigable graph with O(√(n log n)) average degree for any distance function. The lower bound departs from prior work by introducing sharp anti-concentration bounds for binomial random variables to show that near-neighborhoods of random high-dimensional points have negligible overlap, a probabilistic argument not previously applied to graph navigability.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

These near-tight bounds (matching up to a log factor) provide the first theoretical justification for why graph-based ANN methods like HNSW and DiskANN require roughly √n edges per node in high dimensions, and why no fundamentally sparser navigable graph exists for random high-dimensional point sets. Key open questions remain: whether beam search or other relaxed search strategies can reduce the required sparsity, whether the maximum-degree lower bound of n−1 (shown via a JL-embedded star construction) can be circumvented, and whether end-to-end α-approximation guarantees can be proven for greedy routing on these graphs. The gap between the O(√(n log n)) upper bound and the Ω(√n) lower bound, while small, leaves room for potential improvements in the log factor.
