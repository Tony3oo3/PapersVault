## Problem

Multi-agent debate between LLMs has shown empirical promise for improving answer quality, but lacks a theoretical understanding of its internal dynamics. Debate is susceptible to echo chamber effects and shared misconceptions that cause convergence to incorrect answers, yet no principled framework existed to formally explain these failure modes or to guide the design of effective interventions.

## Input / Output

The method takes a natural language question (yes-no, multiple-choice, arithmetic, or open-ended) and iteratively processes responses from n LLMs over T rounds, with each model observing the pruned/modified response set from the previous round. Between rounds, sentence embeddings (ADA-2) serve as tractable proxies for latent concept distributions, enabling KL-divergence-based selection and modification of responses. Final correctness is evaluated via regex matching on extracted answers (BoolQ, MMLU, MathQ) or a GPT-4 judge scoring full responses (TruthfulQA).

## Method

The paper formalizes multi-agent debate through a latent concept space, proving that each model's generation probability decomposes into a 'generation without other agents' term and a 'skew' term caused by other agents' responses (Lemma 4.2), establishing debate as a special case of in-context learning. From this framework, they derive that identical models or similar responses lead to static debate dynamics (Theorems 5.1–5.2) and that shared misconceptions monotonically decrease expected correctness (Theorem 5.4). Three interventions are then designed and theoretically justified: diversity pruning (selecting k responses maximizing pairwise KL to counter echo chambers), quality pruning (selecting k responses minimizing KL to the task), and misconception refutation (using an LLM to identify and correct errors in each response). In practice, the interventions are applied sequentially—quality pruning, then diversity pruning, then misconception refutation—using sentence embedding distances as proxies for the intractable concept-space KL divergences.

## Results

Across four benchmarks (BoolQ, MMLU, TruthfulQA, MathQ) and three model families (GPT-3.5, Llama-2/3, Mistral), the combined interventions (quality pruning + diversity pruning + misconception refutation) consistently match or exceed the Society of Minds (SoM) baseline. Headline improvements include: MMLU accuracy rising from .74 to .79 (6×GPT-3.5), .70 to .75 (6×Llama-3), and .47 to .52 (6×Llama-2); MathQ accuracy rising from .88 to .93 (6×GPT-3.5) and .33 to .48 (6×Llama-3); and TruthfulQA rising from .63 to .69 (6×GPT-3.5). The largest relative gains appear on weaker models (Llama-2) and on MathQ, where debate without interventions provides minimal benefit.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is Society of Minds (SoM) from Du et al. [2023], the standard iterative multi-agent debate procedure where all agents see all previous responses each round. The proposed method (combining quality pruning, diversity pruning, and misconception refutation) wins or ties SoM in 22 out of 24 benchmark-model configurations in Table 1. The most substantial wins occur on MMLU with weaker models (Llama-2: .47→.52, a 5-point gain; Llama-3: .70→.75) and on MathQ (Llama-3: .33→.48, a 15-point gain; GPT-3.5: .88→.93). The method ties SoM on a few configurations (e.g., 6×Llama-3 on BoolQ at .78, 3×Llama-3+3×Mistral on BoolQ at .80) and loses marginally on one (6×Mistral on MathQ: .19 vs .18). Ablation results (Table 3) confirm that individual interventions can underperform SoM when applied in isolation, but the full combination is consistently superior, indicating the interventions are complementary by design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the iterative multi-agent debate procedure introduced by Du et al. (2023, Society of Minds) and the Bayesian latent-concept framework for in-context learning from Xie et al. (2021). While prior debate work (Chan et al. 2023, Liang et al. 2023, Khan et al. 2024) has explored variants of the debate loop empirically, this paper is the first to provide a formal theoretical treatment of debate dynamics using the latent concept space, deriving provable results about echo-chamber convergence and shared-misconception failure modes. The three proposed interventions (diversity pruning, quality pruning, misconception refutation) are grounded directly in the theoretical objective rather than being heuristic modifications, distinguishing them from prior ad-hoc debate variants.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The theoretical framework reveals that multi-agent debate is fundamentally susceptible to convergence on majority opinions, especially when models share training data and thus share misconceptions — a limitation that adding more agents cannot fix. The practical interventions offer a principled way to mitigate these issues, with consistent (if modest) gains across four benchmarks and three model families. Key limitations include the reliance on sentence embeddings as a proxy for the intractable latent concept space (which degrades performance on arithmetic tasks) and increased inference cost from misconception refutation. Open questions include whether the framework extends to settings with truly heterogeneous model capabilities, whether the latent concept space can be made more directly accessible, and whether the interventions generalize to domains where embedding similarity is a poor proxy for conceptual distance.
