## Problem

Estimating amino acid substitution rate matrices from multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) traditionally requires full phylogenetic tree reconstruction, an NP-hard step that limits scalability to MSAs with only ~1,000 sequences. This computational bottleneck prevents fitting rich site-specific evolutionary models to the hundreds of thousands of sequences now available, and leaves open whether principled probabilistic models of protein evolution can compete with large protein language models on variant effect prediction.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244138/cropped_page4_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes MSAs (up to ~454K sequences of length 364 in the largest benchmark) and produces either a single global 20×20 (or 21×21 with gaps) transition-rate matrix under the LG model, or a separate 21×21 rate matrix for every site in the alignment under the SiteRM model. For variant effect prediction, given a wild-type sequence and a mutant sequence, the output is a scalar score computed as the sum of per-site log-likelihoods under the site-specific rate matrices at a fixed evolutionary time t=1 (one expected mutation per site). The method operates on raw aligned sequences with gaps treated as a distinct state, and requires no pre-computed trees.

## Method

FastCherries replaces expensive full tree reconstruction with a near-linear-time divide-and-conquer pairing algorithm that groups sequences into disjoint pairs minimizing total Hamming distance, then estimates site-rates and quantized branch lengths via coordinate ascent on CherryML's composite likelihood over those pairs. The key insight is that CherryML's rate matrix estimation only depends on cherries (leaf pairs) and their distances, not on the full tree topology, so the tree can be bypassed entirely. For the SiteRM model, CherryML's rate matrix estimation is applied independently at each site, with regularization that mixes empirical transition counts with pseudocounts from the LG prior (λ=0.5) to prevent overfitting of the 400-parameter per-site matrices. For variant effect prediction, the score is log p(x_mut | x_wt, t=1) = Σᵢ log(exp(Qᵢᶠ)[x_wt,ᵢ, x_mut,ᵢ]), which conditions the evolutionary model on the wild-type — a conceptual distinction from standard log-likelihood-ratio scoring that the authors argue explains why this independent-sites model outperforms epistatic protein language models like ESM-1v on ProteinGym benchmarks.

## Results

On the ProteinGym DMS substitutions benchmark (2.4M variants, 217 assays), SiteRM achieves Spearman 0.423, AUC 0.734, MCC 0.330, NDCG 0.776, and Recall@10 0.207, outperforming the large protein language model ESM-1v ensemble (Spearman 0.407, AUC 0.723) and the epistatic Potts-model-based EVmutation (Spearman 0.395, AUC 0.716) on all metrics except Recall@10. On the human clinical substitutions benchmark (63k variants, 2,525 proteins), SiteRM achieves a mean AUC of 0.911, exceeding ESM-1b (0.892) by ~0.02 AUC and falling less than 0.01 AUC below the state-of-the-art TranceptEVE (0.920). For the LG rate matrix estimation task, CherryML with FastCherries is 10–100× faster end-to-end than CherryML with FastTree while incurring only ~50% relative statistical efficiency loss and a small ~2% asymptotic bias in median relative error.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the large protein language model ESM-1v ensemble, SiteRM wins on Spearman (0.423 vs 0.407), AUC (0.734 vs 0.723), MCC (0.330 vs 0.320), and NDCG (0.776 vs 0.749), but loses narrowly on Recall@10 (0.207 vs 0.211). Against the epistatic Potts-model-based EVmutation, SiteRM wins on Spearman (0.423 vs 0.395), AUC (0.734 vs 0.716), and MCC (0.330 vs 0.305), ties on NDCG (0.776 vs 0.777), but loses on Recall@10 (0.207 vs 0.222). Against DeepSequence ensemble, SiteRM wins on Spearman, AUC, and MCC while tying on NDCG and losing on Recall@10 (0.207 vs 0.226). Against ESM-IF1, SiteRM wins on Spearman (0.423 vs 0.422), AUC (0.734 vs 0.730), and NDCG (0.776 vs 0.748), but loses marginally on MCC (0.330 vs 0.331) and Recall@10 (0.207 vs 0.223). On the clinical benchmark, SiteRM (AUC 0.911) outperforms ESM-1b (0.892) by ~0.02 AUC but trails the top methods TranceptEVE (0.920) and GEMME (0.919) by less than 0.01 AUC. For the LG rate matrix estimation, CherryML+FastCherries is 10–100× faster than CherryML+FastTree with comparable held-out likelihood, at the cost of ~50% statistical efficiency and a ~2% asymptotic bias.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends CherryML (Prillo et al., 2023), which replaced full MSA likelihood with a composite likelihood over tree cherries to enable scalable rate matrix estimation, by eliminating the remaining computational bottleneck: full phylogenetic tree reconstruction. The key methodological departure is FastCherries, a near-linear time divide-and-conquer algorithm that estimates only the cherry pairs and branch lengths needed by CherryML's composite likelihood, bypassing NP-hard tree inference entirely. The SiteRM model then generalizes the LG model (Le & Gascuel, 2008) from a single global rate matrix to site-specific rate matrices, regularized toward the LG prior. For variant effect prediction, the paper departs from the dominant paradigm of scoring via sequence likelihoods p(x) or masked conditional probabilities (as in ESM, EVmutation, DeepSequence) by instead conditioning a continuous-time Markov evolutionary model on the wild-type sequence, framing traditional scoring as the t→∞ special case.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The near-linear scalability of FastCherries (processing 450K sequences in ~1000 seconds on one core) removes the practical barrier to applying phylogenetic models to the largest available MSAs, enabling site-specific rate matrix estimation at scale for the first time. The finding that this independent-sites evolutionary model is competitive with or outperforms large protein language models on ProteinGym challenges the assumption that modeling residue-residue epistasis is necessary for accurate variant effect prediction, suggesting that principled probabilistic treatment of the evolutionary process and conditioning on the wild-type may be more important than architectural complexity. Open questions include whether the time-reversibility assumption limits accuracy in some settings, whether the small asymptotic bias (~2% median relative error) matters for downstream tree reconstruction, and whether the fast cherry generation can be leveraged to train more complex epistatic evolutionary models that combine the best of both approaches.
