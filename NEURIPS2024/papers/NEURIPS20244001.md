## Problem

Safety-tuned LLMs can still divulge harmful content, but prior work has not established whether refusal behavior depends on the model's inferred evaluation of the user (user persona) or revealed the internal mechanics of how such persona effects operate. This paper asks: does manipulating user persona via activation steering bypass safety filters more effectively than direct refusal manipulation, and what layer-specific safeguards and representational changes underlie this effect?

## Input / Output

The method consumes adversarial text queries from SneakyAdvBench (a 100-item holdout of rewritten AdvBench attacks designed to be less obviously harmful) and persona steering vectors constructed via CAA from ~100 first-person identity statements per persona. The steering vector is injected into hidden representations at a specific layer (typically layer 13 of Llama 2 13B chat) during forward computation, with a positive or negative multiplier. Outputs include binary safety classifications from an LLM auto-rater, per-attack and aggregated response rates, layerwise cosine similarity profiles between steering vectors, and open-ended interpretive text generated via Patchscopes to reveal how persona vectors reshape the model's reading of adversarial queries.

## Method

The paper constructs persona steering vectors using contrastive activation addition (CAA): ~100 first-person identity statements per persona are converted to yes/no questions, and the difference in hidden representations between 'Yes' and 'No' answer tokens is averaged to form a direction in activation space. At inference, this vector is added (or subtracted) to all token positions at a target layer, and the model continues forward computation. The key finding is that layer 13 is most effective because input contextualization is largely complete but next-token prediction has not yet dominated. To probe mechanics, the authors formulate early decoding as a Patchscope that shortcuts early-layer representations to the final layer, revealing that harmful content persists in earlier layers even when the model's surface output is a refusal. Open-ended Patchscopes then demonstrate that pro-social persona vectors cause the model to form more charitable interpretations of adversarial queries (e.g., reading a misinformation request as a 'thought experiment'), while anti-social vectors with negative multipliers have the opposite effect. Finally, cosine similarity between persona vectors and the refusal vector at layer 13 predicts downstream response-rate changes a priori.

## Results

On SneakyAdvBench-eval (100 adversarial queries) with Llama 2 13B chat, the baseline response rate is 39%. Manipulating user persona via CAA- (negative steering vector at layer 13) is the most effective intervention: the selfish persona CAA- achieves a 53% response rate (+14 pp over baseline), followed by close-minded CAA- (48%) and unlawful CAA- (48%). In contrast, directly steering the refusal vector (refusal CAA-) only boosts the response rate to 42% (+3 pp). Early decoding from layers >13 recovers harmful content in 56% of cases even when the model's final-layer output is safe, and combining CAA- with early decoding pushes the selfish persona to 79%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three intervention strategies—prompt prefix (PP), activation steering (CAA+/CAA-), and early decoding (ED)—against a Baseline Prompting condition (39% response rate). Persona-based CAA- substantially outperforms direct refusal manipulation: selfish CAA- reaches 53% versus refusal CAA- at 42%, a 11 pp gap, confirming that manipulating the model's evaluation of the user is more effective than directly targeting refusal behavior. Activation steering also outperforms natural language prompting for persona induction: CAA- effects are larger in magnitude and more symmetric across pro-social/anti-social pairs, whereas PP effects are smaller and asymmetric (only 'curious' PP significantly increases responsiveness). CAA interventions selectively increase responsiveness to adversarial queries while leaving non-adversarial queries largely unaffected, whereas PP induces refusal across the board. Early decoding amplifies all interventions, with CAA- ED for selfish reaching 79%, but the paper notes that persona steering's effect is highly localized to layer 13 (only 4% more effective when aggregated across all layers), unlike early decoding which is attack-specific across layers.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the representation-engineering and activation-steering line of work (Rimsky et al. 2023, Zou et al. 2023a) by shifting the intervention target from the model's own system persona or direct refusal behavior to the model's inferred *user* persona, a dimension largely unexplored in prior jailbreaking literature. It unifies early decoding (Din et al. 2023, Schwartz et al. 2020) and open-ended self-interpretation (Chen et al. 2024, Ghandeharioun et al. 2024) under the Patchscope framework to probe where in the computation safety decisions are made. The work departs from prior system-persona studies (Gupta et al. 2023, Aher et al. 2023) by showing that the *user's* perceived attributes, rather than the model's own role, are the more potent lever for modulating refusal.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that safety-tuned models retain harmful content in early layers while gating it in later layers reframes safety as a distributed, layer-specific property rather than a global capability removal, with direct implications for how safety training should be designed and audited. The demonstration that indirect persona manipulation outperforms direct refusal control, and that geometric properties of steering vectors predict downstream safety effects a priori, offers both a new attack surface for red-teaming and a potential tool for pre-deployment safety assessment. Key limitations include reliance on a single primary model (Llama 2 13B) with only preliminary Gemma 7B validation, moderate inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff's α ≈ 0.38–0.42), and a focus on refusal behavior rather than broader capability effects, leaving open whether these mechanics generalize across architectures and whether persona-induced charitable reinterpretation can be systematically mitigated.
