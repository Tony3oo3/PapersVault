## Problem

Existing benchmarks for large multimodal agents (LMAs) evaluate capabilities in isolation—single tasks, static scenarios, or controlled lab settings—failing to assess the compositional skills (perception, interaction, reasoning, decision-making) that practical tasks demand simultaneously. No prior benchmark provides a dynamic, multi-agent environment where an agent must perceive multimodal content, role-play to gather incomplete information, perform multi-step reasoning, and make goal-directed decisions all within a single coherent task.

## Input / Output

The benchmark consumes 50 curated murder mystery scripts (each with character scripts exceeding 5,000 words, text-rich and media-rich image clues, and a truth manual) plus over 3,000 annotated multiple-choice and open-ended questions organized by game phase. In arena mode, agents receive their role script, public image clues, and real-time dialogue from other agents, producing a binary win/loss outcome per match. In CoE mode, the same multimodal inputs are decomposed into 8 scored metrics (LSU, TIU, MIU, RP, SPC, ITD, MMR, CMD), with perception and cognition scored as accuracy over question sets and role-play/decision-making scored on 0–100 scales derived from dialogue transcripts and key-character interrogation counts.

## Method

WhodunitBench introduces a two-mode evaluation framework rooted in scripted murder mystery games. The arena mode pits LMAs against each other (or a naive baseline agent) in faction-based pairwise matches across 50 scripts, using win rate as the sole metric; scripts are curated by domain experts for scientific integrity, reasoning complexity, and logical coherence. The Chain of Evaluation (CoE) mode decomposes the game into its natural stages—Perception, Role-play Interaction, and Cognition—and maps each to specific question types: 1,911 multiple-choice perception questions (text-rich images, media-rich images, long scripts), annotated key-clue statements and core-role identifications for interaction scoring, and 1,308 multi-step reasoning questions built from human-constructed reasoning chains (1–4+ hops) with GPT-4-generated distractors. The key design insight is that the incomplete-information structure of murder mystery games forces agents to actively seek clues through role-played dialogue rather than relying on a static prompt, thereby evaluating interaction and reasoning jointly in a manner that mirrors real-world task completion.

## Results

In the arena-style evaluation, GPT-4V achieves the highest average win rate of 24.2% as a non-murderer suspect, followed by Claude (19.2%), Gemini (16.9%), Yi-Vision (11.1%), and Qwen-VL-Plus (10.6%). In the Chain of Evaluation (CoE), GPT-4V (Direct) attains the highest overall average score of 45.84, with Gemini (Direct) at 41.81 and Claude (Direct) at 40.19, while Yi-Vision and Qwen-VL-Plus score only 21.57 and 22.45 respectively. Notably, even the strongest agent (GPT-4V) achieves a maximum win rate of only 25% against the naive agent, and role-play interaction scores (RP) remain below 8/10 across all models, indicating a significant gap in dynamic multi-agent collaboration.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V outperforms all other LMAs across nearly every CoE metric, achieving the highest scores in perception (LSU 93.60, TIU 79.29, MIU 68.09), multi-modal reasoning (MMR 57.12), and case murder detail (CMD 25.18) under Direct prompting. Gemini and Claude form a strong second tier (avg 41.81 and 40.19), both substantially ahead of Yi-Vision (21.57) and Qwen-VL-Plus (22.45), which struggle particularly on long-script understanding and multi-step reasoning. In the arena, GPT-4V wins 25.0% of matches against the naive agent and 29.5% against Gemini, while weaker models like Qwen-VL-Plus win only 9.1% against the naive agent. Interestingly, CoT prompting does not consistently help: it improves MMR for all models but degrades TIU for GPT-4V (79.29→51.88) and Claude (67.39→35.31), and reduces overall CoE averages for Yi-Vision, Gemini, Claude, and GPT-4V. The paper also notes that stronger models paradoxically perform worse as the murderer (Gemini is most frequently identified), suggesting over-communication as a liability in deception tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of game-based agent evaluation (AvalonBench, DDD, Werewolf-style games) by introducing a multimodal, dynamic murder mystery environment that simultaneously demands perception, role-playing interaction, multi-step reasoning, and goal-directed decision-making under incomplete information. Unlike prior benchmarks that isolate a single capability in static settings (GAIA, VisualWebArena, MCOT) or focus on text-only social deduction (AvalonBench, DDD), WhodunitBench is the first to combine all four capability dimensions in a single online competitive arena, complemented by a granular Chain of Evaluation (CoE) with over 3,000 annotated questions. The work also repurposes the murder mystery game genre—previously suggested only for text-based multiple-choice testing [26]—into a full multimodal simulation with image clues, long scripts, and multi-agent dialogue.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that even state-of-the-art LMAs (GPT-4V, Claude-Opus) achieve at most 25% win rate against a deliberately naive agent, and their role-play interaction scores barely exceed 20/100, exposing a significant gap between static perception benchmarks and dynamic, compositional task performance. The finding that CoT reasoning sometimes degrades performance in incomplete-information games and that stronger models are paradoxically worse at deception (Gemini is most easily identified as the murderer) challenges assumptions about scaling and reasoning strategies in interactive settings. Open questions include how to disentangle interaction quality from reasoning quality in evaluation, whether the findings generalize beyond murder mystery scripts, and how to reduce the substantial API cost (≈20M tokens per full run) that limits reproducibility.
