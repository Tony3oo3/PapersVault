## Problem

Existing MoE upcycling methods like BTX only reuse the FFN parameters from specialized dense models to initialize MoE experts, discarding the domain-specific knowledge embedded in their attention modules. Moreover, BTX averages attention parameters across all specialized models, which can degrade performance by blending divergent specializations. This paper asks whether both FFN and attention parameters can be fully leveraged during upcycling to improve MoE quality without increasing compute.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241791/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes a pre-trained dense seed model (with a parallel attention transformer architecture) plus N domain-specialized dense expert models, each continued pre-trained on 100B tokens of domain-specific data (code, math, law, plus general text). It produces a sparse MoE model where every transformer block contains both a soft-routed Mixture-of-Attention layer and a top-1-routed MoE FFN layer, with non-expert parameters (embeddings, layer norms) initialized by uniform averaging across the dense models and routers randomly initialized.

## Method

BAM (Branch-Attend-Mix) upcycles both FFN and attention parameters into expert layers via a three-phase process: branching a seed model into N copies, continued pre-training each on a specialized domain, and initializing the mixture model's experts directly from the corresponding dense models. The key architectural innovation is a soft-routed Mixture-of-Attention layer where every token is assigned to all attention experts (rather than top-k), which ablations show is critical for surpassing the BTX baseline. Two variants are offered: BAM (Expert KV) includes per-expert Q, K, V, and output projections for maximum specialization, while BAM (Shared KV) shares K/V projections across experts for reduced memory and faster inference. To offset the extra compute from soft-routing all attention experts, BAM adopts a parallel attention transformer architecture that computes attention and FFN experts concurrently, partially masking the overhead under the more expensive FFN computation.

## Results

On the large-scale 2B-parameter experiments, BAM (ExpertKV) with data-matching achieves an average perplexity of 3.19 across four domains (pretrain, code, law, math), compared to 3.27 for the BTX baseline. On the small-scale 590M-parameter experiments, the improvement is more pronounced: BAM (ExpertKV) data-matching reaches 9.70 average perplexity versus 10.72 for BTX. On downstream tasks (large-scale), BAM (ExpertKV) data-matching achieves 34.02% average score across math, code, law, knowledge, reasoning, and MMLU benchmarks, outperforming BTX's 32.71%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is BTX (Branch-Train-Mix), which upcycles only FFN parameters from specialized dense models into an MoE while averaging attention parameters. BAM consistently outperforms BTX across all four evaluation domains (pretrain, code, law, math) under both data-matching and compute-matching regimes at both the 590M and 2B scales. The ExpertKV variant (which includes full Q, K, V, and output projections in each attention expert) yields the largest gains: 9.5% average perplexity reduction at small scale (9.70 vs. 10.72) and 2.4% at large scale (3.19 vs. 3.27). The SharedKV variant, which trades some performance for inference efficiency, still beats BTX in every domain. On downstream tasks, BAM (ExpertKV) data-matching improves the average score from 32.71% to 34.02%, with the largest gains in law (85.47% vs. 81.85%) and code (12.83% vs. 10.05%). Ablations confirm that even when BTX is given more FFN experts to match BAM's total or active parameter count, BAM retains its advantage, indicating the gains come from upcycling attention experts rather than simply adding parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

BAM extends the Branch-Train-Mix (BTX) upcycling paradigm, which initializes MoE FFN experts from specialized dense models but averages away their attention parameters. It also builds on the Mixture of Attention (MoA) framework, which introduces attention experts with top-k routing and shared KV projections. BAM's key departure is twofold: it upcycles attention parameters into dedicated attention experts (rather than averaging them) and replaces top-k routing with soft routing for those attention experts, while adopting a parallel attention transformer architecture to offset the additional compute.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that specialized attention parameters carry domain-specific knowledge that is lost under uniform averaging provides a clear rationale for extending upcycling beyond FFN layers, and the ablation showing soft routing is essential (top-1/top-2 attention routing fails to beat BTX) is a useful design constraint for future MoA-based systems. However, the improvements are modest (roughly 1–10% relative perplexity gains), experiments are limited to 590M–2B parameter scales, inference latency is higher than BTX due to per-expert KV caches, and no code is released. Open questions include whether the approach scales to 7B+ models, whether the parallel attention architecture is strictly necessary, and how to further close the inference-efficiency gap.
