## Problem

Existing scalable causal estimators (back-door, sequential back-door, g-formula) cover only a narrow subset of identification scenarios, while estimators for broader classes like the front-door adjustment or Tian's adjustment require explicit summation over high-dimensional variables, making them computationally intractable. No prior framework simultaneously achieves broad coverage of sum-product causal estimands and polynomial-time scalability with doubly robust guarantees.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a structural causal model (causal graph), L-fold-split sample sets drawn from up to K distinct distributions (e.g., observational and interventional), and a target estimand expressed as a UCA functional. It produces a point estimate of the causal effect via the DML-UCA estimator along with finite-sample confidence bounds derived from partial influence functions. A key structural requirement is that the target estimand must be expressible as an expectation over a product of conditional distributions (not a quotient), and bifurcated variables—those simultaneously fixed in some conditionals and marginalized in others—must be identifiable from the graph.

## Method

The paper defines the Unified Covariate Adjustment (UCA) class, a sum-product functional that generalizes SBD, front-door, Tian's adjustment, Verma's equation, counterfactual directed effects, and transportability into a single algebraic form involving ordered conditional distributions and known policy measures. For estimation, DML-UCA introduces two nuisance families—regression parameters (μ) computed via nested conditional expectations and ratio parameters (π) computed as density ratios—combined in a cross-fitted DML estimator. A key technical device is 'empirical bifurcation,' where independent copies of bifurcated variables are generated by permuting sample copies, enabling nested regression to correctly handle variables that are fixed in one conditional and summed in another. The estimator achieves polynomial time O(K·n_max + T(m, n_max, K)) relative to sample size and variable count, and a finite-sample guarantee via partial influence functions (PIFs) for the multi-distribution setting establishes that DML-UCA converges at rate n^{-1/2} when nuisances converge at n^{-1/4}, with doubly robust error decomposition.

## Results

DML-UCA achieves polynomial time complexity O(K·n_max + T(m, n_max, K)) for the full UCA class (including FD, Tian's adjustment, and Verma's equation), compared to exponential O(n·2^m + T(m,n)) for existing FD and Tian's estimators (Fulcher et al., 2019; Bhattacharya et al., 2022). In running-time experiments, existing estimators hit the 300-second timeout as the dimension of summed variables increases (e.g., |Z| up to 100 for FD), while DML-UCA scales smoothly. In AAE (average absolute error) experiments with nuisance convergence capped at n^{-1/4}, DML-UCA outperforms all baselines across FD, Verma's equation, and Jung's equation at sample sizes n ∈ {2500, 5000, 10000, 20000}, demonstrating faster convergence consistent with its doubly robust n^{-1/2} rate.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DML-UCA is compared against five baselines across three estimand scenarios (FD, Verma's equation, and Jung's equation). Against Fulcher et al. (2019) for the front-door adjustment, DML-UCA wins decisively on scalability: the Fulcher estimator's running time grows exponentially with the dimension of the mediator Z (hitting the 300s timeout at |Z|≈30–50), while DML-UCA remains polynomial. On AAE, DML-UCA also converges faster than Fulcher et al. at all sample sizes tested. Against Jung et al. (2021a) for Verma's equation and Jung's equation, the same pattern holds: the Jung et al. estimator times out at moderate dimensions of summed variables (|B| or |R| up to 100), while DML-UCA scales linearly. On AAE, DML-UCA achieves lower error than Jung et al. (2021a) across all sample sizes for both Verma's and Jung's equations. Guo et al. (2023) and Bhattacharya et al. (2022) share the same exponential complexity class as Fulcher et al. for FD and Tian's adjustment respectively (Table 2), so DML-UCA's polynomial advantage applies to them as well. For the broader gID setting, DML-UCA's general complexity O(K·n_max + T(m, n_max, K)) improves upon DML-gID's O(K·n_max·2^{2m} + T(m, n_max, K)) by removing the exponential 2^{2m} factor, though this comparison is theoretical (Table 2) rather than empirical.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the sequential back-door adjustment (SBD) / g-formula estimation framework (Robins, 1986; Pearl & Robins, 1995) to a broader class of sum-product causal functionals, unifying front-door adjustment, Tian's adjustment, Verma's equation, counterfactual directed effects, transportability, and off-policy evaluation under a single 'Unified Covariate Adjustment' (UCA) class. The estimation methodology builds directly on the double/debiased machine learning (DML) framework of Chernozhukov et al. (2018), adapted to multi-distribution settings via partial influence functions (Pires & Branco, 2002). The key departure from prior work is the introduction of 'empirical bifurcation'—permuting copies of variables that play dual roles (fixed in one conditional, summed in another)—which enables polynomial-time estimation for functionals where prior estimators (Fulcher et al., 2019; Bhattacharya et al., 2022) required exponential-time summation over high-dimensional mediators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The UCA framework provides a principled, unified lens for a wide spectrum of causal estimands that were previously handled by ad hoc estimators with incompatible complexity profiles, and the DML-UCA estimator is the first to achieve both broad coverage and polynomial-time scalability simultaneously. A key open question is the boundary of UCA expressiveness: the paper explicitly identifies non-UCA functionals (e.g., the 'napkin' estimand, certain gID scenarios under partial observability) where the framework does not apply, leaving a gap for quotients of sum-products. The finite-sample guarantees via partial influence functions are a novel technical contribution, but the practical utility depends on the quality of density ratio estimation in high-dimensional continuous settings, which remains an active challenge.
