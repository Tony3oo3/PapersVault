## Problem

Signed Graph Neural Networks (SGNNs) suffer from two persistent issues: real-world signed graph datasets are extremely sparse, and unbalanced triangles prevent SGNNs from learning proper node representations. Existing graph data augmentation methods cannot be directly applied because signed graphs lack node features and labels, and the paper proves both theoretically (via a novel generalization error bound) and empirically that random DropEdge actually worsens link sign prediction performance rather than improving it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243715/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes only the signed graph adjacency matrix (entries in {+1, −1, 0}) and the training edge set with their signs—no node features or labels are required, which is critical since real-world signed graph datasets typically provide only structural information. It produces two outputs: an augmented training edge set (with edges added, removed, or sign-flipped based on balance-theory constraints) and a per-edge difficulty score derived from local triangle balance. These are then fed into a curriculum-learning training loop where the SGNN backbone progressively encounters easier-to-harder edges, ultimately producing link sign predictions (positive/negative) for held-out edges.

## Method

SGA is a three-stage plug-in framework for SGNNs. First, a pre-trained SGCN encoder projects nodes into an embedding space, and a multinomial logistic regression classifier estimates the probability of positive/negative edges between all node pairs; edges are added or removed based on four probability thresholds. Second, a balance-theory-guided filter retains only those candidate additions that do not introduce new unbalanced triangles (verified by checking all common neighbors), while deletions are applied unconditionally. Third, each training edge is assigned a difficulty score computed from its local balance degree (the ratio of balanced to unbalanced triangles containing that edge), and a linear pacing function progressively exposes the model to harder edges across training epochs. The key theoretical contribution is a generalization error bound for SGNNs showing that the gap is inversely proportional to the number of training edges, which explains why random edge dropping (DropEdge) is counterproductive for edge-level tasks and motivates the structure-aware augmentation strategy.

## Results

SGA improves link sign prediction across all six real-world signed graph datasets and five backbone models. The largest gains are for SGCN: up to 26.2% F1-binary improvement on Slashdot (67.3→85.0), 14.8% AUC on Wiki-RfA (65.8→75.6), and 12.9% AUC on Epinions (68.6→77.4). Unsigned GNNs also benefit substantially, with GAT gaining 37.3% F1-binary on Bitcoin-alpha (63.3→86.9). GS-GNN, which does not rely on balance theory, shows only marginal or slightly negative changes, confirming SGA's mechanism targets unbalanced-triangle sensitivity.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SGA is evaluated as a plug-in on top of five backbone models: two unsigned GNNs (GCN, GAT) and three signed GNNs (SGCN, SiGAT, GS-GNN). SGCN benefits the most, with consistent AUC gains of 3–15% across all six datasets and a standout 26.2% F1-binary jump on Slashdot, because SGCN's balance-theory-based message passing is most sensitive to unbalanced triangles. SiGAT also improves but more modestly (1–2.7% AUC), as its attention mechanism partially mitigates triangle imbalance. GS-GNN, which models latent groups rather than strict balance, shows near-zero or slightly negative F1 changes (e.g., −0.3% F1-binary on Slashdot), confirming SGA's gains are specifically tied to reducing unbalanced-triangle harm. Among unsigned GNNs, GAT sees the largest relative improvement (up to 37.3% F1-binary on Bitcoin-alpha), while GCN shows moderate gains (up to 13.7% F1-binary on Slashdot). Overall, SGA wins or ties on the vast majority of dataset–model–metric combinations, with the only notable losses being a 2% AUC drop for GCN on Bitcoin-otc and a 3.2% F1-binary drop for GAT on Wiki-RfA.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of signed graph neural networks (SGCN, SiGAT, GS-GNN) and graph data augmentation, but deliberately departs from the standard augmentation toolkit (DropEdge, node perturbation, subgraph sampling) by demonstrating—both theoretically and empirically—that random structural perturbation is ineffective or harmful for edge-level tasks on signed graphs. It builds on structural balance theory to guide which candidate edges to add or remove, and adapts curriculum learning principles from CLNode [36] to introduce edge difficulty scores as a training signal. The generalization bound proof follows the analytical framework of Zhou et al. [64] for GCNs, extended here to the signed-graph setting with a three-way (positive/negative/absent) edge prediction classifier.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SGA provides a practical, model-agnostic plug-in that substantially improves link sign prediction across five backbone architectures and six real-world datasets, addressing two persistent issues (sparsity and unbalanced triangles) that have limited SGNN performance. The first generalization error bound for SGNNs offers a theoretical foundation for understanding why naive edge dropout fails in this setting. However, the method's reliance on balance theory limits its applicability to signed graphs that deviate from this structural assumption (as GS-GNN's smaller gains suggest), and the approach has only been validated on link sign prediction, leaving open questions about transfer to signed graph clustering, node classification, or dynamic signed networks.
