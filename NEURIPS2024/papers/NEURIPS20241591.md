## Problem

Existing dynamic data pruning methods for GNNs, such as InfoBatch, achieve training speedups but become significantly more vulnerable to class imbalance and noisy/biased data, suffering 5–6% performance drops in biased scenarios. Unlike CV and NLP where separate solutions for balancing and robustness already exist, graph learning lacks a unified pruning paradigm that simultaneously preserves efficiency, class balance, and noise robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241591/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

GDeR takes a graph dataset, a GNN encoder (GCN, PNA, or GraphGPS), and a fixed pruning ratio s% as input, and at each epoch produces a dynamically selected subset of (1−s)% of the full dataset for the next epoch's training, alongside the updated GNN model. The subset size is constant across epochs, but its composition changes: a scheduler function (inverse power decay) controls how many samples are drawn from the currently retained set versus the previously pruned set, ensuring coverage of the full dataset over training.

## Method

GDeR projects GNN graph embeddings onto a unit hypersphere via a learned projector, then models the embedding distribution with von Mises-Fisher distributions centered at K trainable prototypes per class. Two regularization losses—compactness (pulling embeddings toward their own-class prototypes) and separation (pushing inter-class prototypes apart)—shape the hypersphere into well-separated clusters. For each epoch, GDeR computes three per-sample scores: an outlier-risk score via prototype-based Mahalanobis distance, a familiarity score from the ratio of intra-class to inter-class prototype distances, and a balancing score reflecting minority-cluster membership. The final sampling probability (Eq. 11) is the sigmoid of familiarity divided by the product of sigmoid outlier-risk and sigmoid balancing scores, so under-learned, low-risk, minority samples are preferentially retained. For unsupervised pre-training without ground-truth labels, GDeR replaces the class-based losses with a ProtNCE contrastive loss to avoid error accumulation from pseudo-labels.

## Results

On OGBG-MOLHIV (ROC-AUC) with PNA, GDeR achieves 76.4% at 50% remaining data, matching the full-dataset baseline of 76.3%, while the best dynamic baseline InfoBatch reaches only 76.3% and the best static method EL2N-2 reaches 75.0%. At the aggressive 20% remaining ratio, GDeR retains 75.8% ROC-AUC versus 74.1% for InfoBatch and 73.0% for EL2N-2. On OGBG-MOLPCBA (AP), GDeR achieves 28.0% at 50% remaining (full dataset: 28.1%) and 24.8% at 20% remaining, outperforming InfoBatch (27.3% and 24.1%) and all static baselines. GDeR also delivers a 2.0× lossless training speedup on OGBG-MOLHIV and a 2.81× speedup on ZINC pre-training with only 30% of the data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GDeR consistently outperforms the strongest dynamic baseline InfoBatch across all pruning ratios and both OGB datasets: on OGBG-MOLHIV at 20% remaining, GDeR achieves 75.8% ROC-AUC vs. InfoBatch's 74.1% (a 1.7% gap), and on OGBG-MOLPCBA at 20% remaining, GDeR reaches 24.8% AP vs. 24.1% for InfoBatch. Against the best static methods (EL2N-2, Glister, DP), GDeR leads by 1.5–2.8% ROC-AUC on OGBG-MOLHIV at 20% remaining and by 0.5–1.2% AP on OGBG-MOLPCBA at the same ratio. At 50% and 70% remaining, GDeR matches or slightly exceeds the full-dataset performance (76.4% vs. 76.3% on MOLHIV; 28.5% vs. 28.1% on MOLPCBA at 70%), while InfoBatch only ties the full dataset at 50% and above. In imbalanced scenarios (1:9 ratio on MUTAG/DHFR with GCN), GDeR improves F1-macro by 0.3–4.3% over no-pruning baselines, whereas InfoBatch and other dynamic methods fail to beat the unpruned model. Under 10% Gaussian noise perturbation on MUTAG, GDeR at 30% pruning outperforms InfoBatch by 2.0–6.1% accuracy and matches or exceeds dedicated robustness plugins (DropEdge, GRAND) with 3.6–7.8% accuracy gains at 5% and 30% noise ratios.

## Relation to Prior Work

GDeR repurposes prototype learning and hyperspherical (vMF) embedding techniques—originally developed for few-shot classification and contrastive representation learning—into a dynamic data-pruning mechanism for GNNs. It departs from existing graph pruning work (which is largely absent) and from dynamic pruning methods like InfoBatch [23] and ε-greedy/UCB [58] by replacing loss-magnitude-based selection with a prototype-cluster-aware sampling distribution that simultaneously encodes familiarity, outlier risk, and class balance. The 'Graph Training Debugging' framing positions data pruning not merely as an efficiency tool but as a diagnostic process that corrects distributional pathologies (imbalance, noise) during training, a perspective not present in prior pruning literature.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

GDeR provides the first unified graph-level pruning method that addresses efficiency, class imbalance, and noise robustness in a single plug-in, which is practically valuable for on-device and resource-constrained GNN deployment. The 2.81× lossless speedup on ZINC pre-training and consistent gains across GCN/PNA/GraphGPS backbones suggest broad applicability within the graph domain. However, the authors acknowledge that extension to CV and NLP remains unexplored, and the reliance on a fixed number of prototypes per class (K=2) may limit scalability to very high-class-count or open-set settings. An open question is whether the prototype-based hypersphere formulation generalizes to node-level or edge-level pruning, where the notion of a 'graph sample' and its class label is less natural.
