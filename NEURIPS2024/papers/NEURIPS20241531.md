## Problem

Neural approaches to propositional satisfiability typically require millions of training samples and produce unsatisfiability predictions without easily verifiable certificates, limiting their reliability and integration with symbolic methods. This paper asks whether learning to generate resolution proofs (rather than merely predicting satisfiability) can yield better representations with far fewer training examples while providing sound, efficiently checkable certificates for both SAT and UNSAT verdicts.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241531/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a CNF formula as a set of clauses over Boolean variables and operates on a dynamic formula graph that grows as new clauses are derived through resolution. It produces two types of output in parallel: a satisfying truth assignment (decoded from literal embeddings via a sigmoid MLP) or a resolution proof of unsatisfiability (a sequence of clause-pair resolutions terminating in the empty clause). Both tracks share a common formula embedding and the model terminates when either track succeeds or a timeout is reached, with the winning track's certificate guaranteeing soundness.

## Method

NeuRes combines a message-passing GNN formula embedder (identical to NeuroSAT's) with three attention-based clause-pair selection mechanisms (Casc-Attn, Full-Attn, Anch-Attn) and a truth assignment decoder, operating in two parallel tracks on a shared dynamic representation. The key architectural choice is dynamic embeddings: after each resolution step, a full message-passing round updates all clause embeddings to reflect the new clause's influence, which is decisively better than static embeddings. Training is certificate-driven via teacher-forcing on solver-generated resolution proofs and satisfying assignments, with time-discounted losses that weight later (easier) steps more heavily. A bootstrapped expert-iteration procedure then pre-rolls model proofs and replaces teacher proofs whenever the model's proof is shorter, progressively reducing the training dataset by ~32% and improving both success rate and proof optimality without additional guidance.

## Results

On SR(40) test problems, NeuRes achieves 98.2% total proven accuracy (96.8% SAT, 99.6% UNSAT) and 91.65% total predicted accuracy, while NeuroSAT achieves only 70% SAT proven accuracy (no UNSAT proofs) and 85% total predicted accuracy. NeuRes was trained on just 16K formulas (8K sat + 8K unsat) compared to NeuroSAT's millions of training samples. With top-3 fan-out, NeuRes further improves to 99.9% proven accuracy while reducing proof length to 0.57× the teacher proof length, and the bootstrapped training procedure reduces the training dataset's total proof steps by ~32%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against NeuroSAT [43], NeuRes wins decisively on every comparable metric: 98.2% total proven accuracy vs. 70% (SAT-only, no UNSAT proofs provided), and 91.65% total predicted accuracy vs. 85%. This advantage is achieved with orders of magnitude less training data (16K formulas vs. millions). NeuRes also provides easily verifiable resolution certificates for UNSAT verdicts, which NeuroSAT cannot produce. Against the BooleForce solver [8] (used as the teacher for generating ground-truth proofs), NeuRes is slower in wall-clock time (28.7–45.15 ms vs. 4.5 ms average on SR(40)), but the paper positions this comparison as a runtime reference rather than a competitive benchmark, noting that the gap between neural and industrial solvers remains large. The key differentiator is that NeuRes produces certificates and can shorten teacher proofs by ~32% through expert iteration, capabilities the traditional solver does not offer in this framework.

## Relation to Prior Work

NeuRes builds directly on NeuroSAT's message-passing GNN formula embedder and graph construction, but replaces the classification MLP head with attention-based clause-pair selection for autoregressive resolution proof generation and a parallel truth-assignment decoder. Conceptually, it departs from the NeuroSAT line of work by shifting the training signal from a single-bit satisfiability label to full certificate sequences (resolution proofs or satisfying assignments), aligning it more closely with the formal proof generation literature (tactic prediction, whole-proof search) than with prior neural SAT solvers. The expert-iteration bootstrapping mechanism, where the model progressively replaces longer teacher proofs with its own shorter ones, draws on self-improving training paradigms from theorem proving and combinatorial optimization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The central finding that certificate-driven training yields substantially better representations with orders-of-magnitude less data (16K vs. millions of samples) challenges the assumption that neural SAT solvers require massive datasets, and suggests that structured, verifiable training signals may be more informative than shallow classification labels. The bootstrapping result—where the model shortens its teacher's proofs by ~32% without additional guidance—opens a path toward neural proof optimization that could complement traditional solvers whose certificates are expensive to verify. However, the practical impact is currently limited: NeuRes runs ~10× slower than BooleForce, is evaluated only on the SR distribution, and the authors acknowledge it cannot outperform industrial solvers as a standalone tool. Open questions include whether the certificate-driven training signal generalizes beyond the SR distribution, whether the approach scales to larger formulas, and how it integrates into hybrid solver pipelines.
