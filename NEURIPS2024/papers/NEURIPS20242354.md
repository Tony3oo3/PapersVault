## Problem

Existing contrastive dimension reduction (CDR) methods such as CPCA, PCPCA, CLVM, CVAE, and CVI all assume that unique foreground information exists and require the number of unique dimensions as a user-specified tuning parameter. Two fundamental questions remain unaddressed: (1) whether contrastive information actually exists in the foreground group, and (2) how many dimensions are unique to the foreground relative to the background. This paper tackles both gaps by introducing a formal hypothesis test and a consistent estimator for the contrastive dimension.

## Input / Output

The method requires foreground data X (n_x samples in R^p), background data Y (n_y samples in R^p), and externally estimated intrinsic dimensions d_x and d_y (e.g., via method-of-moments), with the constraint n_x > d_x and n_y > d_y. Beyond the binary p-value and integer contrastive dimension estimate, the method outputs the full set of singular values (cosines of principal angles) between the estimated subspaces, providing a continuous measure of how strongly each direction is shared versus unique. The hypothesis test is only applicable when d_x ≤ d_y; when d_x > d_y, the contrastive dimension is automatically positive and only the CDE is reported.

## Method

The core technical contribution rests on the principal angles between the foreground subspace V_x and background subspace V_y, with the contrastive subspace defined as Proj_{V_y^⊥}(V_x) and its dimension d_xy equal to the count of nonzero principal angles plus max(d_x − d_y, 0). For the hypothesis test, a contrastive bootstrap resamples the foreground from itself and the background from the pooled data (enforcing V_x ⊂ V_y under H0), then compares the observed largest principal angle against the bootstrap null distribution. For the CDE, V_x and V_y are estimated via the top d_x and d_y eigenvectors of the sample covariance matrices, and the singular values of Ũ_x^T Ũ_y (which equal cosines of principal angles) are counted below a threshold 1−ε to yield the integer estimate. Theoretical guarantees include consistency of the CDE under distinct-eigenvalue assumptions (Theorem 1) and a finite-sample error bound of O(n_x^{-1/2} + n_y^{-1/2}) on the estimated singular values under sub-Gaussianity, derived via Davis-Kahan perturbation and matrix concentration inequalities (Theorem 2).

## Results

In simulations, the CDE correctly recovers the ground-truth contrastive dimension in all four settings (d̂_xy = 0 when d_xy = 0, d̂_xy = 6 when d_xy = 6, and d̂_xy = 1 for both semi-synthetic image experiments), with the hypothesis test producing conservative p-values (0.814 under H₀, 0.029 under H₁). On nine real datasets, the method yields p-values ranging from 0.000 (mHealth, Perturb-Seq) to 0.161 (Pathogen) and contrastive dimension estimates from 1 (mHealth, CelebA:Glasses) to 28 (Perturb-Seq). As a downstream validation, using the CDE estimate of d_xy = 5 for CPCA on corrupted MNIST achieves 94.5% classification accuracy, plateauing by dimension 5.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare the CDE or hypothesis test against competing estimators of contrastive dimension, as the authors note no such estimators exist in the literature. Instead, validation is performed against known ground truth in simulations (perfect recovery of d_xy in all four settings) and against prior literature observations on real data. For example, the CDE estimate of d̂_xy = 4 for Mouse Protein aligns with the d_xy ≥ 2 reported in Abid et al. (2018) and Severson et al. (2019); d̂_xy = 1 for mHealth matches the single-direction separation shown in Abid et al. (2018); and d̂_xy = 17 for BMMC is consistent with the 3rd and 4th PCPCA directions separating pre/post-transplant groups in Li et al. (2020). The hypothesis test is acknowledged to be conservative (empirical Type I error well below nominal α in Simulation 1), and the authors note that for the Pathogen dataset, the large p-value (0.161) aligns with Weinberger et al. (2023) finding that only a deep learning method (CVI) succeeds where linear methods fail, suggesting the linear framework's limitations on nonlinear data.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the contrastive dimension reduction (CDR) framework established by CPCA, PCPCA, CLVM, CVAE, and CVI, which all assume the existence of foreground-unique structure and require the reduced dimension as a user-specified input. The authors build on the principal-angle geometry between subspaces (Ye and Lim, 2016) and the Davis-Kahan perturbation theorem to formalize two diagnostic questions that prior CDR methods left open: whether contrastive information exists at all, and how many dimensions it spans. The contrastive dimension estimator (CDE) is, to the authors' knowledge, the first such estimator in the literature, and the contrastive bootstrap hypothesis test is a new resampling scheme tailored to the null hypothesis V_x ⊂ V_y.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

By providing a principled hypothesis test and a consistent dimension estimator, this work gives CDR practitioners a concrete decision procedure for when to apply contrastive methods and what reduced dimension to use, removing a previously ad hoc tuning step. The finite-sample error bound (Theorem 2) gives practitioners a quantitative handle on estimation uncertainty. Key open questions include extending the framework to nonlinear manifolds (where the linear subspace assumption breaks down, as seen in the Pathogen and CelebA results), developing a more powerful likelihood-ratio test to reduce the conservatism of the bootstrap test, and understanding robustness to misspecification of the intrinsic dimension inputs d_x and d_y.
