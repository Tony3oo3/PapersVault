## Problem

Existing lip reading datasets and benchmarks are exclusively monolingual, making them inadequate for the growing population of bilingual and multilingual users who naturally code-switch between languages. While code-switching has been extensively studied in speech recognition, the visual (lip-reading) counterpart remains entirely unexplored, leaving no public dataset or evaluation protocol for this task.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240967/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The input is a 3-second lip region video clip at 1920×1080 resolution and 30 FPS, cropped via SPIGA facial landmark detection with a 5-frame center-point averaging filter to stabilize the lip ROI. The output is a text transcription that may contain Chinese characters, English words, or a mixture of both within a single sample. Evaluation is decomposed into three metrics: CER for Chinese segments, WER for English segments, and MER as a holistic measure that scores characters and words as their respective basic units.

## Method

The paper introduces the CSLR benchmark (85,560 clips, 62 speakers, 71.3 hours) with a 1:1:1 ratio of Chinese, English, and code-switching samples, and systematically evaluates lip reading backbones (ShuffleNet front-end with BiGRU, MSTCN, or Conformer back-end) under both monolingual and code-switching training. The key empirical contribution is adapting four code-switching strategies from speech recognition—Vanilla CTC, Bi-Encoder CTC, Language Aware Training (LAT), and Language-Routing Mixture-of-Experts (LR-MoE)—to the visual domain, all trained with CTC loss. The Conformer backbone consistently outperforms RNN and TCN alternatives, and LR-MoE achieves the best code-switching accuracy (MER 37.54%) by routing features through language-specific experts supervised by a language-identification loss. Qualitative error analysis reveals that visually similar lip movements across languages (e.g., /nu:/ vs. /wu/) are the primary source of cross-lingual confusion.

## Results

On the CSLR benchmark, the best-performing code-switching method (LR-MoE CTC with ShuffleNet+Conformer) achieves CER 33.01%, WER 45.37%, and MER 37.54% on the code-switching test set. The vanilla single-stream CTC baseline performs worst at CER 35.32%, WER 49.05%, and MER 40.35%, confirming the difficulty of code-switching lip reading. Among back-end architectures, Conformer outperforms BiGRU and MSTCN by 1.6–3.1% in MER under code-switching settings, while monolingual evaluation shows Conformer reaching CER 30.95% (CN) and WER 44.66% (EN).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper adapts two families of code-switching speech recognition methods to lip reading: the Bi-Encoder and Language Aware Training (LAT) strategies from Song et al. [38], and the Language-Routing Mixture-of-Experts (LR-MoE) from Tan et al. [36]. LR-MoE CTC [36] achieves the best performance across all three metrics, improving over Bi-Encoder+LAT [38] by 0.53% in CER, 0.71% in WER, and 0.59% in MER. The Bi-Encoder [38] already provides a substantial gain over the vanilla single-stream CTC baseline (2.51% MER improvement), confirming that separating language-specific encoders helps. Adding LAT on top of Bi-Encoder [38] yields only a marginal 0.27% MER improvement, suggesting diminishing returns from additional monolingual supervision. Overall, the gap between the best and worst method is 2.81% in MER, indicating that while code-switching strategies help, the task remains far from solved with current approaches.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves out a new intersection between two established research threads: sentence-level lip reading (LRS2, CMLR) and code-switching speech recognition (ASRU2019, Tan et al. 2024, Song et al. 2022). While code-switching has been extensively studied in the audio domain—evolving from multi-encoder architectures to single-encoder mixture-of-experts fusion—the authors explicitly note that lip reading research has been confined to monolingual settings due to the absence of cross-linguistic video data. The paper transfers the Bi-Encoder, Language Aware Training, and Language-Routing MoE strategies from speech recognition into the visual speech domain, using standard lip reading backbones (ShuffleNet + Conformer/BiGRU/MSTCN) as the front-end and sequence model.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

CSLR is the first publicly available code-switching lip reading benchmark, filling a clear gap for the growing population of bilingual and multilingual users in real-world communication. The qualitative finding that visually similar lip movements across languages (e.g., 'new' vs. '午', '口味' vs. 'coffee') cause systematic cross-language misrecognition highlights a fundamental modality limitation that audio-based code-switching does not face, pointing toward the need for language-specific lip movement feature decoupling. Key open questions include how to model the transition dynamics between languages in a single video, whether audio-visual fusion can resolve the visual ambiguities, and whether the 1:1:1 monolingual/code-switching ratio is optimal for training. The privacy limitation (non-anonymizable facial video) and the modest speaker count (62) constrain generalizability.
