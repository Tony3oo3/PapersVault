## Problem

Reinforcement learning for critical single-life systems (e.g., patient glucose regulation, chemical reactor control) requires safety from the very first trial, yet existing safe RL methods either cannot guarantee safety during training or impose prohibitive computational overhead for action verification. The paper addresses how to combine a model-based safety regularizer with a model-free RL agent so that the system is safe from deployment while still converging to the performance of unconstrained model-free RL.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a state observation vector (e.g., blood glucose level and its derivative, chemical concentrations and temperatures, or cart position and pole angle) together with a pre-built estimated environment model f̃ that encodes sub-optimal dynamics. It produces a single combined control action a_β(s) = β(s)·a_reg(s) + (1−β(s))·a_rl(s), where a_reg is the deterministic MPC output and a_rl is a sample from a multivariate Gaussian SAC policy. The focus module β_ψ(s) is a small neural network ([128,32] hidden layers, shifted-tanh outputs in (0,1)) that maps each state to its combination weight, and the MPC action a_reg is cached in the replay buffer so the constrained optimization is solved only once per state.

## Method

RL-AR runs two parallel agents: a constrained MPC safety regularizer that hard-codes safety constraints g(s_k)≥0 over a prediction horizon using the estimated model f̃, and a Soft Actor-Critic off-policy RL agent that learns from actual environment interactions. A learned focus module β_ψ(s) combines their actions via a state-dependent weighted sum, initialized to β≥1−ε for all states so the safe regularizer dominates early training. The key theoretical insight (Lemma 1) is that this combination is equivalent to a regularized optimization with state-dependent regularization parameter λ=β(s)/(1−β(s)), which bounds the deviation of the combined policy from the safe regularizer (Theorem 1) while guaranteeing unbiased convergence to the optimal RL policy as β(s)→0 for well-exploited states (Theorem 3). The focus module is updated by gradient ascent on the clipped double-Q value of the combined action, and the state-dependent (rather than scalar) weight ensures monotonic performance improvement in the tabular case (Theorem 2).

## Results

RL-AR achieves zero failures across all four safety-critical environments (Glucose, BiGlucose, CSTR, Cart Pole) during the first 100 training episodes, while the strongest baseline SAC fails 19.0, 59.4, 99.2, and 93.6 episodes respectively. After convergence, RL-AR matches or exceeds SAC's normalized return in three of four environments and is competitive in Cart Pole, while significantly outperforming static MPC in regulation speed and steady-state error. The method tolerates parameter discrepancies exceeding 60% between the estimated and actual environment models before safety is compromised, and operates at an average decision time of 0.037 s per step.

## Baselines & Comparisons

RL-AR is the only method (alongside static MPC) that achieves zero training failures across all four environments, while MPC does not adapt its policy and is significantly outperformed in converged return. Compared to SAC (the model-free RL baseline that disregards safety), RL-AR eliminates all training failures (e.g., 99.2→0 in CSTR, 93.6→0 in Cart Pole) while matching or exceeding SAC's normalized return after convergence. Against the safe-RL baselines CPO and SEditor, RL-AR is dramatically safer (e.g., 100→0 failures in CSTR for CPO, 97.2→0 for SEditor) and achieves higher returns, since CPO and SEditor rely on biased cost estimates from the estimated model and still fail in the actual environment even after pretraining. RPL is relatively safe (3.6–7.8 failures) by leveraging the safe initial MPC policy, but its un-regularized residual action leads to less stable combined actions and lower converged returns than RL-AR. The state-dependent focus module is key: ablation in Appendix D.1 shows that replacing it with a scalar weight produces noticeably less steady return improvements, and the choice of SAC (over TD3) as the RL agent accelerates convergence via entropy regularization.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the control regularization framework of Cheng et al. (2019b), which combined a deterministic safety policy with a stochastic RL policy via a fixed scalar weight, by introducing a state-dependent 'focus module' that learns when to trust the safety regularizer versus the RL agent. It departs from prior safe RL methods (CPO, SEditor) that require observing failures to estimate safety costs, and from verification-based approaches (CBF, reachability) that incur high computational overhead, by hard-coding safety constraints into an MPC regularizer whose forecasting ability prevents unsafe states before they occur. Unlike Gros & Zanon (2019) or Zanon et al. (2020), which use RL to tune MPC parameters, RL-AR uses the model-free RL agent to converge to the optimal policy while the MPC serves purely as a safety enforcer rather than a knowledge source.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RL-AR addresses the 'single-life' control problem where no failure is tolerated during training—a critical requirement in medical (glucose regulation), industrial (CSTR), and other safety-critical applications where classic model-based control is preferred but limited by model accuracy. The key limitation is the assumption of a reasonably accurate estimated model; the paper quantifies that failures emerge only when model parameters deviate by more than ~60%, and future work on online model updating or robust regularizers would extend applicability. The 0.037s per-step decision time makes the approach practical for real-time deployment, and the theoretical guarantees (monotonic improvement, unbiased convergence) provide a principled foundation that distinguishes it from heuristic safe RL methods.
