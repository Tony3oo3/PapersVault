## Problem

No existing 3D human motion dataset includes data from blind or low-vision pedestrians, leaving a critical gap in the ability of motion prediction and generation models to handle the distinctive, subtle movement patterns of people who navigate using mobility aids (white canes, guide dogs) in real-world urban settings. This paper asks whether state-of-the-art text-to-motion and motion prediction models, trained exclusively on sighted participants, can generalize to this underrepresented population, and finds they cannot.

## Input / Output

For text-to-motion generation, the model receives natural language descriptions at two granularities (high-level ~26 words summarizing intent and aid usage; low-level ~44 words specifying step counts and precise cane/dog positioning) and produces a full 3D motion sequence in SMPL joint representation with an additional joint tracking the mobility aid. For motion-conditioned prediction, the model takes 0.5 seconds of past motion history plus a text embedding (encoded via LLaMA2) and predicts the next 9.5 seconds of 3D poses across 24 joints. The underlying motion data is captured via 18 Xsens IMU sensors (body + aid) at outdoor urban intersections, curbs, and staircases, synchronized with third-person and egocentric video.

## Method

The core contribution is the BlindWays benchmark: 1,029 motion clips (~0.6M poses) from 11 blind/low-vision participants across 8 engineered urban routes, annotated with 2,058 paired high- and low-level text descriptions by 15 annotators (3 motion experts, 12 novices) using a custom Tkinter video annotation interface. The paper then systematically benchmarks text-to-motion models (HumanML3D, MotionGPT) and stochastic motion prediction models (CVAE, DLow, MDN) under three training regimes: from scratch on BlindWays, pre-trained on Motion-X then fine-tuned on BlindWays, and pre-trained on Motion-X only. A key empirical finding is that pre-training on the large general-purpose Motion-X dataset is detrimental for blind motion—particularly for arm and mobility-aid joints—due to domain shift, with FDE degrading by up to 16.5% on the aid joint compared to training from scratch on BlindWays. Per-keypoint analysis reveals that while Motion-X pre-training helps head-joint generalization, it introduces harmful bias for the nuanced cane-sweeping and guide-dog-coordination motions that define blind navigation.

## Results

On the text-to-motion task (Table 2), models trained exclusively on Motion-X produce highly unrealistic blind motions, with FID scores of 11.203 (HumanML3D) and 15.002 (MotionGPT), far from the real-data FID of 0.257. Training directly on BlindWays dramatically improves realism (FID 3.340 for HumanML3D, 5.101 for MotionGPT) and text-motion alignment (R Top1 0.060 vs. 0.041). For motion-conditioned prediction (Table 4), MDN achieves the best overall quality with ADE 0.45, FDE 0.56, and NDMS 0.28, while MotionGPT performs poorly (ADE 3.01, FDE 4.76), confirming that generative pre-trained transformers struggle in history-conditioned settings.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks five baselines: HumanML3D [15] (autoencoder + text-to-motion), MotionGPT [28] (generative pre-trained transformer), CVAE [34], DLow [73], and MDN [33] (stochastic CVAE variants for motion prediction). In text-to-motion, HumanML3D trained on BlindWays alone outperforms MotionGPT on FID (3.340 vs. 5.101) and R Top1 (0.060 vs. 0.054), while MotionGPT achieves higher MModality (3.993 vs. 1.896). Critically, pre-training on Motion-X followed by fine-tuning on BlindWays degrades FID substantially (8.612 for HumanML3D, 10.313 for MotionGPT) compared to training on BlindWays from scratch, revealing a domain-shift penalty. In per-keypoint analysis (Table 3), BlindWays-trained models show 8% lower FDE on arm joints and 16.5% lower FDE on mobility-aid joints versus Motion-X-trained models, though Motion-X pre-training helps head-joint accuracy. For motion-conditioned prediction, MDN [33] dominates on quality metrics (ADE 0.45, FDE 0.56, NDMS 0.28) over CVAE (ADE 0.47) and DLow (FDE 0.59), while MotionGPT is an order of magnitude worse (ADE 3.01), confirming its unsuitability for history-conditioned tasks.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first 3D motion dataset featuring blind and low-vision pedestrians with mobility aids (white canes, guide dogs) navigating real-world urban environments, a domain entirely absent from existing benchmarks like Human3.6M, AMASS, HumanML3D, and Motion-X. Rather than proposing a new architecture, it repurposes established text-to-motion models (HumanML3D, MotionGPT) and motion-prediction baselines (CVAE, DLow, MDN) to probe their generalization limits on this underrepresented population. The IMU-based (Xsens) capture pipeline with two-level text annotations (high-level intent, low-level step-by-step dynamics) extends the annotation paradigm of Motion-X and HumanML3D to a safety-critical, outdoor, multi-actor setting where vision-based keypoint estimation is unreliable.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

BlindWays exposes a critical accessibility gap: state-of-the-art motion models trained on sighted, lab-based, or simplified-scene data produce highly unrealistic blind motions (FID 11–15 vs. 0.26 for real data), with direct safety implications for autonomous vehicles that must predict disabled pedestrians. The finding that pre-training on the large, diverse Motion-X dataset can be actively detrimental for arm and mobility-aid joints (negative transfer) challenges the common assumption that more diverse pre-training always helps. However, the small participant pool (N=11, ~2.8 hours) and the $6,500 Xsens suit limit scalability, and the paper leaves open whether lower-cost IMU or vision-based capture can replicate these findings at scale, as well as how models should be designed to handle the inherently stochastic and context-dependent nature of blind navigation.
