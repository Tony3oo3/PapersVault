## Problem

Deep neural networks lack the precise probabilistic semantics of probabilistic graphical models: while a sigmoid DNN can be viewed as a Bayesian network, standard gradient descent training is inconsistent with the joint distribution that BBN semantics define. This paper asks whether there exists an alternative PGM structure in which the DNN's forward pass and training gradient are exactly correct, thereby providing a rigorous probabilistic interpretation of DNNs.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240150/cropped_page4_idx2.webp)

## Input / Output

The method operates on a trained all-sigmoid DNN with binary input vectors and binary (or categorical) output labels. The HMC fine-tuning algorithm treats hidden-layer activations as continuous variables in (0,1) sampled from a normal distribution whose variance is p(1−p)/L, where L is a hyperparameter controlling the approximation degree: small L behaves like Gibbs sampling over binary variables, while large L recovers the DNN forward pass. The output is a set of fine-tuned weights and calibrated output probabilities, obtained by averaging 1000 MCMC samples per test point.

## Method

The paper constructs an infinite-width tree-structured Markov network (equivalently, a CRF) from any DNN in two steps: first, shared parent nodes are unrolled into separate copies so the undirected structure becomes a tree/forest; second, L copies of every non-output node are created with edge weights divided by L, causing the number of copies to grow exponentially with depth. The key theorem proves that as L→∞, variable elimination in this PGM yields exactly the sigmoid activation values of the DNN forward pass, and the CRF's log-likelihood gradient matches the cross-entropy gradient of the network. Building on this, the authors derive a contrastive-divergence algorithm using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo over the continuous hidden variables (justified by the central limit theorem applied to the L Bernoulli copies), which can fine-tune a pre-trained DNN to improve calibration while remaining computationally feasible on the original network size rather than the infinite PGM.

## Results

On synthetic BN/MN datasets, HMC fine-tuning with L=10 achieves the lowest mean absolute error (MAE) in most settings, e.g., 5.300×10⁻³ vs. 6.593×10⁻³ for DNN on BN(0.3) with 100 training epochs, and 23.53×10⁻³ vs. 34.44×10⁻³ with 1000 epochs. On the Covertype dataset, HMC (L=10) reduces expected calibration error (ECE) from 4.207×10⁻² (DNN, 100 epochs) to 2.352×10⁻² for label 1, and from 6.634×10⁻² to 5.233×10⁻² for label 2 with 1000 epochs. HMC (L=10) runs approximately 4× faster than Gibbs sampling (~630s vs. ~2700s for 20 fine-tuning epochs), while HMC (L=1000) closely matches DNN performance, confirming the theoretical prediction that larger L recovers the DNN forward pass.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares three fine-tuning strategies against the DNN (SGD-trained) baseline: Gibbs sampling and HMC with L ∈ {10, 100, 1000}. HMC (L=10) consistently achieves the best calibration (lowest MAE) on synthetic datasets with small-to-moderate weights (≤1), outperforming both DNN and Gibbs by statistically significant margins (p < 0.0001). For larger weights (≥3), HMC (L=100 or 1000) matches or slightly beats DNN, while HMC (L=10) degrades toward Gibbs-like behavior. On Covertype, HMC (L=10) improves ECE over DNN in 3 of 4 settings (e.g., 2.352 vs. 4.207 for label 1 at 100 epochs), whereas Gibbs sampling performs worse than DNN in 3 of 4 settings, suggesting it is too far from the DNN's operating point. Computationally, HMC (L=10) is roughly 4× faster than Gibbs (~630s vs. ~2700s for 20 fine-tuning epochs), making it a practical middle ground between the DNN forward pass and full Gibbs sampling in the direct PGM.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the established line of work connecting DNNs to probabilistic graphical models (VAEs, RNNs/HMMs, CNNs/GPs) by providing an exact, general construction: any sigmoid DNN corresponds to an infinite-width tree-structured Markov network in which forward propagation equals exact variable-elimination inference. It departs from prior DNN-as-GP work (Dutordoir et al., Sun et al.) by making the PGM correspondence explicit for all sigmoid architectures rather than specific cases, and from Bayesian neural networks by treating the network itself as defining a joint distribution over its nodes rather than a distribution over weights. The proposed HMC fine-tuning algorithm builds on contrastive divergence (Carreira-Perpinan & Hinton) and Neal's Bayesian HMC, but is the first to theoretically justify HMC over hidden variables (rather than weights) in a DNN, motivated by the central-limit-theorem argument that L copies of a Bernoulli node approximate a Gaussian.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result provides precise probabilistic semantics for DNNs as joint distributions over all variables, enabling direct import of PGM algorithms (MCMC, belief propagation, contrastive divergence) for calibration, interpretation, and reverse inference (output-to-input reasoning). However, the practical impact is currently limited: the theorem is proven only for sigmoid activations, the PGM is infinite (making direct use impossible), and empirical validation is restricted to small synthetic networks and 1000-point Covertype subsets. Open questions include formal results for non-sigmoid activations via modified normalization, theoretical guarantees linking HMC in the DNN to Gibbs in the large treewidth-1 PGM, and whether the approach scales to deeper, wider networks or more complex datasets.
