## Problem

MLLM-based robot policies (e.g., RT-2, RoboFlamingo) require activating billions of parameters at every timestep, imposing prohibitive GPU memory, power, and latency demands on resource-constrained robotic platforms. The paper identifies that the bulk of timesteps during manipulation are relatively 'easy' situations where a much smaller model suffices, yet existing approaches always run the full MLLM, wasting computation on redundant layers.

## Input / Output

At each timestep the system consumes a single gripper-camera RGB image and a natural language instruction, and produces a 7-DoF end-effector action: six continuous values for position and orientation plus one discrete gripper open/close value. The action head is a lightweight 4-layer LSTM with a 12-timestep history window followed by two small MLPs (pose and gripper), so temporal aggregation and action decoding add negligible cost relative to the MLLM backbone. The key design constraint is that the activated LLM size varies per timestep, so the action head must be robust to features from any exit depth.

## Method

DeeR partitions the LLM layers of OpenFlamingo into N consecutive groups, each yielding an intermediate hidden state that is max-pooled across the token dimension into a compact representation for action prediction. At inference, an action-consistency criterion compares the L2 norm of predicted actions from two adjacent exits against a per-exit threshold η_i, terminating computation at the first exit where predictions stabilize (η_N = ∞ guarantees termination). Training bridges the train-inference gap via two random exit-sampling strategies—uniform per-timestep and segment-based (a single exit shared across a random sub-window)—plus auxiliary action heads at every exit to ensure intermediate features are prediction-ready. Thresholds are determined by solving a constrained optimization over average FLOPs, peak FLOPs, and GPU memory, either analytically from a demonstration dataset (assuming an exponential exit distribution) or via Bayesian Optimization with online environment interaction.

## Results

On the CALVIN LH-MTLC benchmark, DeeR achieves an average successful length of 2.83 (D→D), 4.13 (ABCD→D), and 2.82 (ABC→D) while consuming only 8.6, 10.0, and 12.5 GFLOPs per action respectively — a 5.2–6.5× reduction in LLM FLOPs compared to RoboFlamingo++ (31.2 GFLOPs). With online threshold optimization (DeeR w. online), performance further improves to 2.92, 4.13, and 2.90 at 8.5, 9.7, and 9.5 GFLOPs. In real inference on an Nvidia V100, DeeR reduces LLM inference time by 68.1% (17.5 ms vs. 55 ms) at comparable task success (4.08 vs. 4.07 avg. length on ABCD→D).

## Baselines & Comparisons

DeeR outperforms its direct baseline RoboFlamingo++ across all three CALVIN settings (e.g., 2.83 vs. 2.71 on D→D, 4.13 vs. 4.07 on ABCD→D, 2.82 vs. 2.59 on ABC→D) while using roughly 3–4× fewer LLM FLOPs. Against the strongest SOTA baseline GR-1 (which uses additional proprioceptive input), DeeR is slightly behind on ABCD→D (4.13 vs. 4.21) and ABC→D (2.82 vs. 3.06), but GR-1 does not report D→D results. In the zero-shot generalization setting (ABC→D), DeeR substantially surpasses all traditional imitation-learning baselines: HULC (0.67), RT-1 (0.9), SPIL (1.71), and SuSIE (2.69). The online-optimized variant (DeeR w. online) further narrows the gap to GR-1 on ABC→D (2.90 vs. 3.06) and matches it on ABCD→D (4.13 vs. 4.21) at only ~9.5 GFLOPs versus GR-1's unreported (but presumably full-model) cost. In real-time inference, DeeR achieves a 68.1% reduction in LLM latency (17.5 ms vs. 55 ms) at essentially identical task success, confirming that the FLOP savings translate to practical speedup.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on RoboFlamingo (Li et al., ICLR 2024), which demonstrated that pretrained MLLMs (specifically Flamingo) can be fine-tuned into effective low-level robotic policies, and on the broader early-exiting literature in CV, NLP, and multimodal settings. The key conceptual departure is adapting dynamic early-exit to sequential robotic decision-making: prior early-exit methods rely on confidence or entropy of SoftMax outputs, which are unavailable for continuous action prediction, so the authors introduce an action-consistency criterion and integrate temporal information (LSTM) on top of the multi-exit architecture. Additionally, the threshold-setting problem is framed as a budgeted optimization (solved via Bayesian Optimization or dataset-based heuristics), a formulation not explored in prior early-exit work for vision or NLP.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 5.2–6.5× reduction in LLM FLOPs and 2–6× reduction in GPU memory without performance loss is practically significant for deploying MLLM-based robots on resource-constrained platforms, potentially democratizing access to foundation-model-powered robotic control. However, the evaluation is confined to the simulated CALVIN benchmark with a single robot embodiment and a 7-DoF end-effector, leaving open whether the 'easy situations dominate' observation generalizes to more complex, long-horizon, or real-world manipulation. The paper also acknowledges that only LLM efficiency is addressed; the vision encoder's computational cost remains unoptimized, and real-robot validation is future work.
