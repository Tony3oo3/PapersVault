## Problem

NeRF-based inverse rendering methods struggle to recover high-frequency environment lighting while simultaneously estimating accurate material properties. Existing approaches either rely on low-frequency lighting representations (spherical Gaussians, low-resolution environment maps) that cannot capture natural illumination details, or apply the split sum approximation without physically meaningful supervision of the pre-integrated lighting network. Additionally, the split sum approximation inherently ignores self-occlusions, causing shadows to be incorrectly absorbed into albedo predictions and degrading material estimation.

## Input / Output

The method consumes 100 posed RGB images of a single object (with camera poses estimated via Colmap for real-world captures and foreground segmentation masks for CO3D) and produces a triangular mesh extracted via marching cubes from a learned SDF, per-vertex material attributes (albedo, metalness, roughness), and a single scene-specific MLP that encodes pre-integrated illumination as a function of reflection direction and roughness. Unlike methods that output explicit environment maps, the illumination is stored implicitly in the MLP and can be queried at arbitrary resolution; the base environment map is recovered by querying the MLP at zero roughness (perfect specular). Relighting is performed by rendering the extracted mesh with Blender's physically-based shader using the predicted material maps and a novel environment map.

## Method

SplitNeRF embeds the split sum approximation into a NeuS-style surface rendering pipeline, decomposing outgoing radiance into a pre-integrated illumination term g(ω_r, ρ) and a precomputed BRDF integral. The illumination is modeled as a single MLP with positional encoding on direction and roughness inputs, supervised by a novel Monte Carlo regularization loss (L_D) that compares the MLP's output against Monte Carlo estimates of the actual hemisphere integral computed using the predicted environment map itself (extracted by querying g(ω, 0)), ensuring the network learns a physically consistent representation across all roughness levels. To address the split sum's blindness to geometry, the authors derive per-point occlusion factors for both diffuse and specular components and supervise a separate occlusion MLP with Monte Carlo visibility estimates sampled from the predicted SDF geometry, weighted by volume rendering weights to focus on surface points. A soft L2 penalty on predicted metalness discourages the model from explaining radiance through over-predicted metallic surfaces. The spatial network uses progressive hash grid encoding (16 levels, 2^{19} hashmap) to predict SDF, material properties, and occlusion factors, while the illumination network is a 5-layer MLP with softplus output; the full system trains in approximately one hour on a single A100 GPU.

## Results

On the NeRFactor dataset, SplitNeRF achieves the best relighting PSNR of 27.31 dB (vs. 24.06 for the next-best NVDiffRecMC), best relighting SSIM of 0.941, and best relighting LPIPS of 0.061, while also attaining the lowest normals MAE of 17.52° and competitive albedo PSNR of 25.29 dB. On the extended Blender dataset, the method achieves the best relighting PSNR (22.73 dB), SSIM (0.906), and LPIPS (0.106), and on Shiny Blender it achieves the best relighting PSNR (24.96 dB). All results are obtained in approximately 0.81 hours on a single A100 GPU, compared to 0.98–18.38 hours for baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SplitNeRF outperforms all six baselines on relighting PSNR across all three synthetic datasets: 27.31 dB on NeRFactor (vs. 24.06 for NVDiffRecMC, 23.78 for TensoIR), 22.73 dB on Blender (vs. 22.58 for TensoIR), and 24.96 dB on Shiny Blender (vs. 24.60 for NVDiffRecMC). It also achieves the best normals MAE on every dataset (17.52° on NeRFactor, 16.18° on Blender, 9.07° on Shiny Blender), indicating superior geometry recovery. On albedo reconstruction, SplitNeRF edges out TensoIR on PSNR (25.29 vs. 25.21) and SSIM (0.924 vs. 0.929 is slightly lower for Ours), but TensoIR retains a lead on LPIPS (0.087 vs. 0.108). On Shiny Blender, NVDiffRecMC slightly outperforms SplitNeRF on SSIM (0.911 vs. 0.904) and NMF on LPIPS (0.136 vs. 0.144), though SplitNeRF leads on PSNR. In terms of efficiency, SplitNeRF trains in 0.81 hours on a single A100, making it 1.2× faster than NVDiffRec (0.98 hr), 3.7× faster than NVDiffRecMC (2.95 hr), and 22.7× faster than NeRO (18.38 hr).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on NeuS's SDF-based surface rendering pipeline and incorporates the split sum approximation from real-time physically-based rendering (Karis & Games, 2013) into a NeRF-style inverse rendering framework. It departs from prior pre-integrated lighting representations—autoencoder-based illumination networks (Neural-PIL), learnable per-roughness images (NVDiffRec), and integrated spherical harmonic encodings (NeRO)—by using a single scene-specific MLP supervised with a novel Monte Carlo regularization that enforces physical consistency. The occlusion factor, supervised via Monte Carlo visibility sampling, addresses a known blind spot in the split sum approximation that prior inverse rendering methods have not explicitly handled.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The ~1-hour training time on a single A100 (versus 2–20+ hours for baselines) makes high-quality relightable object digitization practically accessible, lowering the barrier for large-scale scene reconstruction. The Monte Carlo regularization for the illumination MLP is a clean, generalizable technique that could be adopted by other inverse rendering pipelines. Key open questions remain: the far-field illumination assumption limits applicability to near-field lighting, indirect illumination and subsurface scattering are unmodeled, and the low MC sample count for occlusion still leaves shadow artifacts in albedo for small, bright light sources.
