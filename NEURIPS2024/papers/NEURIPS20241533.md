## Problem

Current Representational Similarity Matrices (RSMs) for vision models compute similarity between semantic concept vectors only at matching spatial locations, coupling semantic content with spatial alignment. This causes largely identical but spatially shifted representations (e.g., translated or rotated images) to be scored as dissimilar, even when the network has learned spatial invariances. The paper argues that representational similarity should be invariant to arbitrary spatial permutations of semantic features, not merely to translations as in prior work.

## Input / Output

The method consumes intermediate feature maps from CNN layers (N×C×W×H) or ViT token sequences (N×D×T), unifying spatial dimensions into a joint extent S to form representations Z ∈ R^{N×C×S}. It produces an N×N semantic RSM where each entry K_ij is the kernel similarity between z_i and the optimally permuted z_j, making it a drop-in replacement for standard RSMs in downstream tasks such as CKA computation, image retrieval, or correlation with classifier output probabilities. The spatial extent S can be as large as 64×64=4096 for early CNN layers, which drives the computational cost of the matching step.

## Method

For each pair of representations z_i and z_j, the method constructs an S×S affinity matrix A_ij of inner products between all semantic concept vectors, then solves a bipartite assignment problem (via the Hungarian/Jonker-Volgenant algorithm) to find the permutation matrix P_ij that maximizes ⟨z_i, P_ij z_j⟩. The resulting semantic RSM entry K_ij = k(z_i, P_ij z_j) is invariant to any spatial permutation and, depending on the kernel, to orthogonal transformations along the channel dimension. To address the O(N²)×O(S³) complexity, three approximation algorithms are proposed: Greedy breadth-first matching, TopK-Greedy (optimal matching for highest-norm vectors plus greedy for the rest), and Batch-Optimal (optimal matching within norm-sorted batches of size b). The Batch-Optimal approximation with b=512 achieves under 8% error relative to the optimal solution while reducing runtime by 36× at spatial extent 4096, making it practical for most experimental settings.

## Results

On the EgoObjects retrieval task, permutation-invariant (PI) similarity dramatically improves F1@1 over standard spatio-semantic similarity, e.g. CLIPSeg with Cosine Similarity jumps from 0.280 to 0.835, and SAM ViT/B32 from 0.614 to 0.804. On the output-similarity prediction task (Table 1), PI consistently yields more negative Pearson correlations with Jensen-Shannon Divergence, indicating better alignment with classifier behavior (e.g. ResNet18 Cosine Sim: −0.276 → −0.326; ViT-L/32 Cosine Sim: −0.138 → −0.188). The Batch-Optimal approximation (b=512) achieves 96.8% of optimal matching quality at s=4096 while running 36× faster than the optimal Jonker-Volgenant solver.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary comparison is between standard spatio-semantic RSMs (which assume fixed spatial correspondence) and the proposed permutation-invariant semantic RSMs. On EgoObjects retrieval, PI wins across all five feature extractors (CLIP, CLIPSeg, DinoV2-Giant, SAM, BiT-50) and all three kernels, with the largest gains for models with high spatial extent: CLIPSeg Cosine Sim improves by +0.555 F1@1 and SAM RBF by +0.310. On the CityScapes retrieval benchmark, PI consistently improves Cosine Sim and RBF retrieval (e.g. CLIPSeg Cosine Sim 0.662→0.694) while Inner Product shows mixed results. On the output-similarity prediction task, PI achieves more negative Pearson correlations in 18 out of 21 architecture-kernel combinations, with the exception of ConvNeXtV2-Base where the standard RSM slightly outperforms PI for Cosine Sim and RBF kernels. The RBF kernel is noted as generally unsuitable for predicting output probability similarity (sometimes yielding positive correlations), while Cosine Similarity and Inner Product are consistently negative.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the RSM/CKA framework introduced by Kriegeskorte et al. and formalized for neural networks by Kornblith et al., which has become the dominant tool for comparing representational similarity across models and layers. It directly builds on Williams et al.'s identification of spatial-alignment sensitivity in RSMs and their proposed translation invariance, but generalizes this to full spatial permutation invariance by recasting the similarity computation as a bipartite set-matching problem (Hungarian algorithm). The key conceptual departure is arguing that the similarity measure should be invariant to all spatial configurations of semantic features, not merely to the specific equivariances of the underlying architecture.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that standard spatio-semantic RSMs underestimate similarity by 30–40% in early layers, and that this systematically biases CKA-based model comparisons, has direct implications for the large body of work relying on CKA for architecture comparison, transfer learning analysis, and interpretability. The dramatic retrieval improvements for dense-prediction models (e.g., CLIPSeg F1@1 jumping from 0.28 to 0.84) suggest the coupling issue is not merely theoretical but practically consequential. However, the O(S³) per-pair computational cost limits applicability to high-resolution early layers, and the overall correlations with classifier output probabilities remain low, leaving open whether better kernels or additional invariances are needed to fully capture predictive similarity.
