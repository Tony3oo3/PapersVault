## Problem

Humans segment moving objects from random dot stimuli using the Gestalt principle of common fate in a zero-shot manner, yet 40 state-of-the-art deep optical flow models fail to generalize to these appearance-agnostic patterns, often performing near chance level. This paper asks whether a neuroscience-inspired motion energy model, originally designed to explain neural tuning in cortical area MT, can bridge this gap and match human segmentation capability.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20244356/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline consumes a 9-frame video window (either a textured video or a random dot stimulus with 500 dots and 8-frame lifetime) and produces a binary per-pixel foreground mask at input resolution. The motion estimator outputs multi-scale representations at 5 resolutions (obtained by repeated blur-and-downsample by 2), which are fed into a shared-parameter recurrent segmentation network. All motion estimators are frozen; only the downstream segmentation network is trained from scratch per model using binary cross-entropy on ground-truth masks from Kubric-rendered synthetic videos.

## Method

The core contribution is implementing the Simoncelli & Heeger (1998) motion energy model as a fixed-weight CNN that operates directly in pixel space via oriented spatio-temporal Gabor filters, squaring nonlinearities, Gaussian pooling, and an intersection-of-constraints stage to resolve ambiguities—contrasting with optical flow networks that match deep features between two frames. This motion energy CNN is paired with a coarse-to-fine segmentation network whose shared-parameter recurrent architecture aggregates motion features from coarsest to finest scale before predicting a binary segmentation. The motion energy model has orders of magnitude fewer parameters than the optical flow baselines yet achieves 60% IoU on random dot stimuli, outperforming the best optical flow model (GMFlow at 38.1%) by over 20 percentage points and matching human performance in a psychophysical shape-identification task, while all 40 optical flow variants fall short of human capability.

## Results

On the i.i.d. test set, the motion energy model achieves 75.9% IoU (F-Score 0.845), trailing the best optical flow models (FlowFormer++ at 90.8% IoU, GMA(+P) at 89.5% IoU) but matching earlier deep learning flow estimators. On zero-shot random dot stimuli, the motion energy model achieves 60.0% IoU (F-Score 0.718), outperforming the best optical flow model (GMFlow at 38.1% IoU) by over 20 percentage points. Strikingly, the top-performing models on original videos (FlowFormer++, GMA) generalize worst to random dots, dropping below 10% IoU. In the human comparison, the motion energy model is the only model to match human performance on the shape identification task, while all optical flow models fall short.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The motion energy model decisively outperforms all 40 evaluated optical flow models on zero-shot random dot segmentation. FlowFormer++ (Shi et al., CVPR 2023) achieves the best original-video performance (90.8% IoU) but generalizes worst to random dots (9.2% IoU), as does GMA (Jiang et al., ICCV 2021) at 7.7% IoU. GMFlow (Xu et al., CVPR 2022) is the strongest optical flow baseline on random dots at 38.1% IoU, still trailing the motion energy model by 22 percentage points. Older architectures like FlowNet 2.0 (Ilg et al., CVPR 2017) and PWC-Net (Sun et al., CVPR 2018) generalize better than their Transformer-based successors (37.7% and 34.7% IoU respectively) but remain far below the motion energy model. RAFT (Teed & Deng, ECCV 2020) achieves only 13.2% IoU on random dots despite 88.6% on original videos. In the supplemental comparison, the state-of-the-art OCLR motion segmentation model (Xie et al., NeurIPS 2022) scores 2.6% IoU on random dots versus 60.0% for the motion energy model, confirming that the generalization gap is a property of the motion estimator rather than the segmentation network.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the Simoncelli & Heeger (1998) motion energy model—a spatio-temporal filtering architecture originally designed to explain tuning properties of individual neurons in cortical area MT—as a practical motion estimation component in a computer vision segmentation pipeline. It departs fundamentally from the dominant deep optical flow paradigm (FlowNet → PWC-Net → RAFT → GMA → GMFlow → FlowFormer++), which estimates motion by matching learned deep features between two frames, in favor of direct pixel-space spatio-temporal filtering with a post-processing ambiguity-resolution stage. The paper is the first to study motion energy models for moving object segmentation and explicitly positions the work at the intersection of Gestalt psychology (common fate), computational neuroscience, and computer vision, bridging a gap that prior work in each community had not addressed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper reveals a fundamental and counterintuitive gap: the most accurate deep optical flow models on natural videos (FlowFormer++, GMA) generalize worst to random dot stimuli (<10% IoU), while a 40-year-old neuroscience model with orders of magnitude fewer parameters matches human performance in a controlled psychophysics study. This challenges the implicit assumption that architectural sophistication and training on large datasets yield better generalization, and suggests that the entanglement of motion estimation with appearance in DNNs is a core limitation. Open questions include how to integrate motion energy principles with state-of-the-art optical flow architectures, whether human generalizable motion perception is innate or learned, and how to extend the motion energy model to match SOTA performance on natural scenes while preserving its generalization properties.
