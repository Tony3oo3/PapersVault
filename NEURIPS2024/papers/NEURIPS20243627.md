## Problem

RLHF datasets are widely used to align LLMs with human preferences, yet no systematic technique exists to audit which specific human values are actually embedded within them. Researchers often assume these datasets serve societal interests without empirical evidence, while public concerns about censorship and value misalignment grow. This paper addresses the gap by introducing a method to classify and quantify the human value distribution in RLHF preference data.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243627/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes concatenated human-prompt and assistant-response text pairs (max 128 tokens) from RLHF datasets as input and produces a single dominant human value label from a 7-class taxonomy (Information Seeking, Wisdom/Knowledge, Duty/Accountability, Civility/Tolerance, Empathy/Helpfulness, Well-being/Peace, Justice/Human & Animal Rights). At the dataset level, the output is an aggregate value distribution profile showing the percentage of preferences assigned to each value category, enabling cross-dataset comparison of value orientation.

## Method

The approach is a two-phase pipeline. Phase 1 constructs a hierarchical human values taxonomy through an integrated literature review of 120 articles from nine ethics and philosophy journals, organized into seven top-level categories via hypernym-hyponym grouping and ethical paradigm alignment. Five interdisciplinary annotators then qualitatively label 6,501 sampled preferences from Anthropic/hh-rlhf using this taxonomy as a codebook, achieving Krippendorff's Alpha of 0.85. Phase 2 trains a RoBERTaForSequenceClassification model on these annotations (80/20 split, 8 epochs, batch size 64, cross-entropy loss with class weights for imbalance, dropout regularization, early stopping patience 2) to scale classification to the full datasets (338,704 + 19,578 + 52,002 preferences). The key design choice is grounding the ML classifier in a philosophically derived taxonomy rather than data-driven clustering, ensuring the output categories carry interpretable ethical meaning.

## Results

The RoBERTa-based classifier achieved 84% accuracy on a 500-sample human evaluation, with per-class F1 scores ranging from 0.629 (Empathy & Helpfulness) to 0.883 (Justice & Human/Animal Rights). Qualitative annotation of 6,501 ground-truth preferences (Krippendorff's Alpha = 0.85) revealed Information Seeking (36.96%) and Wisdom/Knowledge (30.75%) as the dominant values, while Justice & Human/Animal Rights (3.12%) and Well-being & Peace (5.94%) were the least represented. Across all three audited datasets, Wisdom/Knowledge was the most prevalent value (78.17% in OpenAI WebGPT, 66.56% in Alpaca GPT-4, ~33.8% in Anthropic hh-rlhf), and Justice & Human/Animal Rights was the least (0.04%–1.76%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces a novel auditing technique and does not benchmark against prior value-classification methods; no direct method baselines are reported. The primary comparison is cross-dataset: the authors audit three widely used RLHF datasets (Anthropic/hh-rlhf, OpenAI WebGPT Comparisons, Alpaca GPT-4-LLM) to reveal how their embedded value profiles differ. All three datasets are heavily skewed toward information-utility values (Wisdom/Knowledge and Information Seeking), with Justice & Human/Animal Rights severely underrepresented everywhere (0.04% in WebGPT, 0.17% in Alpaca GPT-4, 1.76% in Anthropic). The OpenAI WebGPT dataset is the most extreme, with Wisdom/Knowledge dominating at 78.17% and Information Seeking at only 5.67%, whereas Anthropic hh-rlhf shows a more balanced (though still information-heavy) split between Information Seeking (~31.8%) and Wisdom/Knowledge (~33.8%). The authors also note that the classifier's F1 drops to 0.629–0.649 for the underrepresented prosocial values (Empathy & Helpfulness, Well-being & Peace), consistent with the limited training signal for those classes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground at the intersection of value-embedding research (Solaiman & Dennison, Sorensen et al.) and dataset auditing (Hirota et al., Birhane et al.), but explicitly positions the specific task of classifying human values within RLHF preference datasets as unaddressed in prior literature. The closest prior work—Birhane et al. annotating values in ML research papers and Obi & Gray examining values in practitioner judgment—neither targets RLHF datasets nor provides a computational classifier. The authors also deliberately depart from the Value Sensitive Design framework and Schwartz's Theory, constructing an AI-specific hierarchical taxonomy from philosophy, axiology, and STS literature to support both qualitative annotation and transformer-based multi-class classification.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that Justice & Human/Animal Rights appears in only 0.04% of WebGPT preferences and 0.17% of Alpaca GPT-4 preferences raises concrete concerns for deploying LLMs in legal, medical, or civic contexts where such values are critical. The practical contribution—a RoBERTa classifier with 84% human-evaluated accuracy plus released ground-truth and classification datasets—gives researchers a reproducible tool for auditing value distributions before training reward models. Key limitations include the Western-centric taxonomy, the single-dominant-value-per-preference constraint, and the absence of sub-value granularity, leaving open questions about cross-cultural applicability and whether value-aware curation can meaningfully shift model behavior.
