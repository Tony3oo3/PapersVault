## Problem

The dominant two-stage approach (Winsorized Mean Estimator) for user-level DP mean estimation assumes balanced users and relies on clipping, which introduces severe bias under heavy-tailed distributions and makes sensitivity scale with the largest user's sample size. This paper addresses both limitations simultaneously by replacing the clipping-based estimator with a Huber loss minimizer whose connecting points adapt to per-user sample sizes.

## Input / Output

The method takes a user-level dataset of n users, where user i contributes m_i samples in R^d (with m_i assumed public), along with privacy parameters (ε, δ) and a prior bound R on the population mean norm. It outputs a single vector in R^d satisfying user-level (ε,δ)-DP. The key structural detail is that the Huber loss connecting points T_i and weights w_i are set adaptively from the public m_i values before observing any sample values, so no additional privacy budget is consumed by parameter selection.

## Method

The estimator computes user-wise means y_i, then finds the minimizer of a weighted sum of Huber losses φ_i(s, y_i) with per-user connecting points T_i, solved via an iterative Weiszfeld-type algorithm in O(nd/ξ) time. For imbalanced users, weights are w_i = (m_i ∧ m_c)/Σ(m_j ∧ m_c) and thresholds scale as T_i ∝ 1/√(m_i ∧ m_c), so larger users get smaller thresholds (less bias) but capped weights (controlled sensitivity). The final output clips the Huber minimizer to B_d(0, R) and adds Gaussian noise whose scale is determined by a smooth sensitivity constructed from a three-case analysis of the dataset (no outliers, few outliers, many outliers), yielding a valid β-smooth sensitivity that guarantees (ε,δ)-DP. This avoids the hard clipping of the two-stage method, replacing it with a soft penalty that reduces bias for heavy-tailed data while keeping sensitivity bounded.

## Results

The paper presents results as MSE convergence curves (Figures 1–2) rather than tabular numbers. Theoretically, for bounded support with balanced users, HLM achieves MSE ≲ R²/(mn) + dR²/(mn²ε²)·ln(mnd)·ln(1/δ) (Theorem 2), matching the two-stage rate up to a log factor. For heavy-tailed distributions (p-th moment bounded), HLM achieves MSE ≲ 1/(mn) + [d·ln(nd)/(mn²ε²) + (d/(m²n²ε²))^(1−1/p)·ln²(nd)]·ln(1/δ) (Theorem 3), which is strictly better than the WME bound Õ((d/(n²ε²))(1/m + m^(4/p−2)·n^(6/p))). For imbalanced users, the MSE scales as R²/N + dR²γ/(Nnε²)·ln²(Nnd)·ln(1/δ) (Theorem 5), where γ measures imbalance degree and remains bounded under Assumption 3.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is the Winsorized Mean Estimator (WME) from Levy et al. [34], the standard two-stage approach for user-level DP. On balanced users with bounded distributions (Uniform, Gaussian), HLM and WME achieve nearly identical MSE, confirming that the Huber approach does not sacrifice performance in the simplest setting. However, on heavy-tailed distributions (Lomax with a=4, and real IPUMS income/salary data), HLM shows a significantly faster MSE convergence rate than WME, with the gap widening in higher dimensions (d=3). For imbalanced users, the comparison is stark: as the imbalance parameter γ increases from 1 to 3, WME's MSE grows substantially across all tested distributions (Uniform, Gaussian, Exponential, IPUMS), while HLM's MSE remains nearly flat, demonstrating that the adaptive selection of weights w_i and connecting points T_i effectively neutralizes the sensitivity inflation caused by users with large local sample sizes.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper departs from the dominant two-stage Winsorized Mean Estimator (WME) of Levy et al. [34], which first identifies a concentration interval and then clips user-wise averages into it. Instead, it replaces the clipping operation with Huber loss minimization, a standard tool from robust statistics [47], and calibrates the added Gaussian noise via the smooth sensitivity framework [48]. The key conceptual departure is that Huber loss penalizes large deviations linearly rather than hard-clipping them, which the authors show reduces bias for heavy-tailed distributions and allows adaptive per-user thresholds for imbalanced sample sizes. The paper also positions itself as the first to simultaneously address robustness to model poisoning and user-level DP, extending a unification previously achieved only at the item level [52–55].

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical relevance is significant for federated learning and recommendation systems where users naturally have heterogeneous sample sizes and gradient distributions are heavy-tailed. The method's linear-time implementation via a modified Weiszfeld algorithm makes it deployable. However, two limitations remain open: a lower bound on the number of users n is required (which the authors acknowledge cannot be entirely removed), and the analysis assumes local sample sizes m_i are public information. Extending the approach to higher-level learning problems (e.g., private stochastic optimization under user-level DP) and to settings where m_i are also private are natural next steps.
