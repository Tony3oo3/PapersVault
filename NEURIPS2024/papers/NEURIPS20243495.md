## Problem

In emergent communication, good task performance is often taken as evidence that agents have developed meaningful protocols, yet prior work showed discrimination-trained agents can generalize to random noise, suggesting their messages may encode no recognizable input properties. This paper identifies semantic consistency—inputs mapped to the same message should be more similar than random pairs—as a goal-agnostic prerequisite for meaningful communication, and asks whether the two dominant EC objectives (reconstruction and discrimination) actually guarantee it.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243495/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The sender receives a single input image (Shapes: 3×64×64 with random shape, color, position; MNIST: 1×28×28 digits) and produces a discrete message of 4 symbols drawn from a vocabulary of 10, enforced via Gumbel-Softmax. In the reconstruction task, the receiver outputs a full image in the input space; in the discrimination task, the receiver additionally receives d−1 independently sampled distractor images and outputs a position index. The discrete bottleneck forces a many-to-one mapping, so each message represents an equivalence class of inputs, which is the unit of analysis for semantic consistency.

## Method

The core contribution is a theoretical analysis: the authors prove that under an unrestricted receiver, the reconstruction objective reduces to minimizing within-cluster variance (equivalent to k-means), guaranteeing semantic consistency, while the discrimination objective depends only on the uniformity of message probabilities and imposes no constraint on which inputs share a message—uniformly random assignments can be globally optimal. They further introduce spatial meaningfulness, a stricter property accounting for distances between messages, and show reconstruction satisfies it under mild simplicity and non-degeneracy conditions on the receiver, while discrimination does not. Empirically, agents use a two-stage training procedure: a continuous CNN autoencoder is pretrained and frozen, then a GRU-based message generator (sender) and message embedder (receiver) are trained with the discrete EC objective (MSE for reconstruction, infoNCE/cross-entropy for discrimination) over 200/100 epochs on Shapes and MNIST respectively.

## Results

On the Shapes dataset, the reconstruction objective achieves 31.64% discrimination accuracy with a TopSim of 0.34 and a trained message variance of 1334.38 (vs. 2554.77 for the random baseline), while the discrimination objective achieves 61.96% discrimination accuracy but only 0.09 TopSim and 2280.24 message variance (vs. 2793.65 random). On MNIST, reconstruction reaches 88.00% discrimination accuracy with 0.365 TopSim and 371.63 message variance (vs. 1029.57 random), outperforming discrimination's 78.76% accuracy, 0.360 TopSim, and 1226.14 message variance (vs. 1784.59 random). The key finding is that reconstruction induces significantly more semantically consistent protocols, as measured by the improvement of trained message variance over the random baseline (1220.39 vs. 513.41 on Shapes), confirming the theoretical prediction that distance-based objectives guarantee semantic consistency while probability-based objectives do not.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares two EC objectives: the reconstruction game (distance-based, following Lin et al. [34]) and the discrimination game (probability-based, following Lazaridou et al. [28]). Discrimination clearly wins on raw task performance, achieving 61.96% discrimination accuracy on Shapes versus 31.64% for reconstruction, and 78.76% versus 88.00% on MNIST (where reconstruction actually edges ahead). However, reconstruction dominates on the paper's proposed meaningfulness metrics: it achieves a TopSim of 0.34 versus 0.09 on Shapes (a 3.8× gap), and a much larger improvement in message variance over the random baseline (1220.39 vs. 513.41 on Shapes; 657.94 vs. 558.45 on MNIST). The supervised discrimination variant (MNIST) partially closes the gap, reaching 87.10% accuracy and 0.269 TopSim, but still lags reconstruction on message variance improvement. The paper's theoretical contribution explains this asymmetry: under mild assumptions, every optimal reconstruction protocol is provably semantically consistent (Theorem 5.2), while optimal discrimination protocols can be entirely arbitrary in how they group inputs (Theorem 5.5), meaning task success in discrimination does not guarantee meaningful communication.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new theoretical ground within the well-established Lewis game framework for emergent communication, introducing formal definitions of 'semantic consistency' and 'spatial meaningfulness' as goal-agnostic prerequisites for meaningful protocols. It builds conceptually on Rita et al. (2022), who decomposed the discrimination objective into co-adaptation loss and mutual information, and on Guo et al. (2021), who empirically connected MI to pairwise input distances within equivalence classes. The paper departs from prior work by proving that the discrimination objective's optimal solutions are agnostic to input geometry (even random mappings can be globally optimal), while reconstruction's optimal solutions are provably equivalent to k-means clustering, thereby providing a theoretical explanation for Bouchacourt & Baroni's (2018) empirical finding that discrimination-trained agents generalize to noise.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The result that a globally optimal discrimination protocol can be semantically arbitrary challenges the field's implicit assumption that good task performance implies meaningful communication, and provides a clear theoretical rationale for preferring distance-based (reconstruction) objectives when the goal is to induce natural-language-like protocols. Open questions include the observed gap between theory and practice in channel utilization (discrimination agents use fewer unique messages than the uniform-optimal prediction suggests), the validity of Euclidean distance as a proxy for semantic similarity, and whether the results extend to more complex discrimination variants (multi-target, multi-modal, different-view candidates). The spatial meaningfulness framework also opens a path toward formally connecting EC protocol structure to compositionality and ease-of-teaching properties.
