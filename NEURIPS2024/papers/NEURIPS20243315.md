## Problem

Sampling from unnormalized, multi-modal target distributions remains difficult: local MCMC kernels (MALA, HMC) mix slowly across modes, while variational methods using continuous normalizing flows (CNFs) have been limited by expensive maximum-likelihood training that requires solving neural ODEs repeatedly. Flow matching (FM) offers a simulation-free training objective for CNFs, but it has only been applied to generative modeling with access to target samples, leaving open how to train CNFs adaptively during MCMC when only the unnormalized log-density is available.

## Input / Output

The method takes an unnormalized target log-density π(x) (evaluated pointwise with its gradient), a base distribution π₀ (typically a standard Gaussian), a reference distribution p₀, and initial flow-network parameters θ₀. It produces trained CNF parameters θ_K that define a time-indexed vector field v_t^θ and a probability path from p₀ to π, from which samples can be generated by integrating the neural ODE. The algorithm operates on N parallel particles (e.g., 128–1024 chains), and the vector field is parameterized as an MLP with Fourier feature augmentation for the time coordinate, augmented with a learned reweighting of the target log-gradient.

## Method

Markovian Flow Matching (MFM) is an adaptive MCMC scheme that alternates between a local MALA kernel and a non-local flow-informed random-walk kernel, where the latter maps the current state to reference space via the CNF, proposes a Gaussian step, and maps back to target space. The CNF is trained on-the-fly by minimizing the conditional flow-matching (CFM) objective using the current ensemble of MCMC samples as a dynamic proxy for the target, eliminating the need for true target samples. An adaptive tempering mechanism, driven by an effective-sample-size criterion, interpolates between a simple base density and the target to enable discovery of multiple modes without prior knowledge of their locations. Under mild regularity assumptions (Robbins-Monro step sizes, geometric ergodicity of the Markov kernel, and Lipschitz continuity of the objective gradient), the flow-network parameters converge almost surely to a local minimum of the CFM objective.

## Results

On the 4-mode Gaussian mixture, MFM (k_Q=10) achieves an MMD of 8.13e-4, competitive with FAB (2.69e-4) and DDS (1.76e-4) while running ~9x faster than DDS (117s vs 114s) and ~7x faster than FAB (117s vs 101s). On the 64-dimensional Field system, MFM achieves a KSD U-statistic of 2.61 in 52.3s, outperforming DDS (15.2, 2400s) and NF-MCMC (548, 2000s) by orders of magnitude in both quality and speed, though FAB achieves a lower KSD (0.14) at 74x the runtime (3880s). On the 1600-dimensional Log-Gaussian Cox process, MFM achieves a KSD U-statistic of 1.13e-1 in 117s, outperforming NF-MCMC (11.8) and FAB (1.55e-1) while being 28x faster than DDS (3260s) and 9x faster than FAB (1040s).

## Baselines & Comparisons

MFM clearly dominates NF-MCMC across all four experiments: on the Field system, NF-MCMC's KSD U-statistic (548) is ~200x worse than MFM's (2.61), and on the LGCP it is ~100x worse (11.8 vs 1.13e-1), likely due to non-convergence within the feasible iteration budget. Against DDS, MFM trades a small quality gap for a large speed advantage: DDS achieves a slightly better KSD on the LGCP (7.59e-2 vs 1.13e-1) and a better MMD on the 4-mode mixture (1.76e-4 vs 8.13e-4), but at 20–28x the runtime; conversely, MFM is far superior on the 16-mode mixture (MMD 2.87e-3 vs 1.02e-1) where DDS collapses to nearby modes. FAB achieves the best KSD on the Field system (0.14 vs 2.61) and the best MMD on both synthetic mixtures, but at 74x (Field) and 9x (LGCP) the computational cost of MFM, and it fails to capture both modes in the bimodal Field target. AT-SMC is the fastest method by a wide margin (4–6s) and achieves the best KSD on the LGCP (1.39e-2), but its sample quality degrades sharply on the multimodal 4-mode mixture (MMD 3.95e-2 vs MFM's 8.13e-4) and the Field system, where it struggles to explore both global minima without the flow-informed global proposals that MFM provides.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes the simulation-free flow matching objective (Lipman et al., ICLR 2023) from generative modeling into probabilistic inference, embedding a continuous normalizing flow within an adaptive MCMC loop. It extends the adaptive MCMC-with-NFs framework of Gabrié et al. (PNAS 2022) and the local-global kernel design of Samsonov et al. (NeurIPS 2022) by replacing discrete normalizing flows with CNFs trained via conditional flow matching, eliminating the need for target samples or importance-weighted objectives. The convergence analysis closely mirrors the Markovian score climbing framework of Naesseth et al. (NeurIPS 2020), while the adaptive tempering schedule is borrowed from sequential Monte Carlo (Del Moral et al., 2006). The key departure from prior work is the first integration of CNFs into an adaptive MCMC algorithm, leveraging the fact that FM requires only the log-target gradient rather than normalized target samples.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MFM provides a practical, computationally efficient (10–25× faster than competing flow-based methods in high-dimensional settings) approach to sampling from complex multi-modal unnormalized densities, with the adaptive tempering mechanism enabling discovery of modes not known a priori. However, the convergence guarantee is only to a local optimum of the FM objective, and the empirical finding that DDS outperforms MFM on the 4-mode example despite identical architecture suggests the FM objective itself may be a bottleneck. Open questions include whether tailored CNF architectures (exploiting model-specific structure beyond the log-posterior gradient) can close this gap, and whether non-asymptotic convergence rates can be established. The method's reliance on a standard Gaussian base measure (rather than an informed one) makes it more broadly applicable but also more challenging to tune.
