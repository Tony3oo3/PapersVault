## Problem

Aligned LLMs remain vulnerable to diverse jailbreak attacks (GCG, AutoDAN, PAIR, TAP, Base64, LRL) that bypass safety guardrails, yet existing unsupervised defenses either fail to detect multiple attack types simultaneously or reject a large fraction of benign queries, rendering them impractical. No prior method achieves both high detection rates across heterogeneous jailbreak strategies and a low false positive rate on legitimate user queries without requiring additional trained models.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single text user query and requires white-box access to the protected LLM's word embedding layer for perturbation. It outputs a binary decision: either a canned refusal message (query rejected) or the LLM's normally generated response (query passed). Each detection requires N×(P+1) total LLM forward passes (110 with default N=P=10), though early exit after N=10 queries is possible when the first-stage filter triggers, reducing overhead to ~1.3× the original inference time.

## Method

The key insight is that the refusal loss landscape (the probability the LLM refuses to answer) has distinct geometry for malicious versus benign queries: malicious queries occupy regions with smaller loss values and steeper gradients (larger gradient norms). Gradient Cuff exploits this via a two-step procedure: (1) sampling-based rejection, where N independent LLM responses are generated and the empirical refusal rate f_θ(x) = 1 − (1/N)ΣY_i is computed, rejecting if f_θ(x) < 0.5; (2) gradient norm rejection, where the gradient of the refusal loss is approximated via zeroth-order estimation by adding P random Gaussian perturbations to the mean-pooled sentence embedding and computing directional derivatives of f_θ, rejecting if ‖g_θ(x)‖ exceeds a threshold t calibrated on a benign validation set to a target FPR σ. A probabilistic guarantee (Theorem 1) bounds the gradient approximation error as O(1/√P) with confidence 1−O(1/N+1/P), confirming that increasing N and P tightens the estimate. The approach is training-free, model-agnostic (tested on LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, Vicuna-7B-V1.5, Gemma, and Qwen2), and complementary to prompt-engineering defenses like Self-Reminder.

## Results

On LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, Gradient Cuff achieves an average refusal rate (TPR) of 0.883±0.013 across 6 jailbreak attacks with a false positive rate (FPR) of only 0.022±0.015 on benign queries. On Vicuna-7B-V1.5, it achieves TPR 0.743±0.018 at FPR 0.034±0.018, reducing the average attack success rate from 76.7% to 25.7%. On the MMLU utility benchmark, Gradient Cuff preserves model performance (0.378 on LLaMA-2-7B-Chat, 0.462 on Vicuna-7B-V1.5), comparable to PPL (0.411, 0.487) and far better than SmoothLLM (0.271, 0.303).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Gradient Cuff outperforms PPL by a large margin on both models: 0.883 vs 0.732 average TPR on LLaMA-2-7B-Chat and 0.743 vs 0.401 on Vicuna-7B-V1.5, while maintaining a comparable or lower FPR. Against SmoothLLM, Gradient Cuff achieves substantially higher TPR (0.883 vs 0.763 on LLaMA-2; 0.743 vs 0.413 on Vicuna) at a similar FPR, and also preserves MMLU utility far better (0.462 vs 0.303 on Vicuna). Erase-Check achieves the highest TPR (0.905 on Vicuna) but is impractical due to an FPR of 0.862–0.988, rejecting nearly all benign queries. Against supervised methods, Gradient Cuff matches or exceeds LLaMA-Guard (0.883 vs 0.760 on LLaMA-2; 0.743 vs 0.773 on Vicuna) and trades a small TPR gap to Safe-Decoding (0.883 vs 0.955 on LLaMA-2) for a dramatically lower FPR (0.022 vs 0.080). When combined with Self-Reminder, Gradient Cuff boosts the malicious refusal rate by 12.2% on LLaMA-2 and 35.0% on Vicuna, demonstrating strong complementarity with prompt-engineering defenses.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the loss landscape visualization framework of Li et al. (NeurIPS 2018) and zeroth-order gradient estimation techniques from black-box optimization (Berahas et al., Liu et al.), repurposing them for LLM safety. It departs from existing jailbreak defenses—PPL (perplexity-based), SmoothLLM (input perturbation), Erase-Check (sub-sentence checking), and Self-Reminder (prompt engineering)—by instead exploiting intrinsic properties of a newly formalized 'refusal loss' function, specifically its functional value and gradient norm in the embedding space. The approach is training-free and model-agnostic within white-box access, positioning it as a complementary signal to prompt-engineering and supervised guard models rather than a replacement for alignment training.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Gradient Cuff provides a practical, training-free defense that achieves strong detection across six heterogeneous jailbreak attack families while maintaining low false-positive rates, addressing a gap where prior methods either miss fluent attacks (PPL) or over-reject benign queries (Erase-Check). Key limitations include the white-box requirement (access to embedding layers), the ~110× inference overhead per query, and significant performance degradation under adaptive attacks (e.g., Adaptive-PAIR drops refusal rate from 0.694 to 0.356 on Vicuna). Open questions include whether the refusal loss landscape properties persist in larger or closed-source models, whether the keyword-based jailbreak indicator can be replaced by more robust semantic assessment, and how to reduce the query budget without sacrificing detection quality.
