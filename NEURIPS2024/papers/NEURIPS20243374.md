## Problem

No publicly available benchmark, dataset, or deep learning model exists for localizing sounds emitted by small animals (e.g., rodents) in the small, reverberant laboratory environments required for naturalistic social neuroscience experiments. Existing SSL benchmarks target human-audible frequencies in large rooms, while rodent vocalizations span ~100–100,000 Hz and occur in arenas under one square meter with hard, reflective walls. Classical beamforming methods like MUSE, currently used by neuroscientists, degrade substantially in these reverberant conditions.

## Input / Output

The model consumes raw multi-channel audio waveforms sampled at 125 or 250 kHz from microphone arrays of 4, 8, or 24 channels arranged in different geometries across three environments. It outputs the parameters of a 2D Gaussian distribution (mean and covariance) over the arena floor, from which a 95% confidence set is derived; the target resolution is ≤1 cm to attribute vocalizations to individual animals. The benchmark dataset comprises 767,295 annotated sound events from speakers, robots, earbud-mounted animals, and freely behaving rodents, with ground truth 2D positions extracted from synchronized overhead video.

## Method

The core model is a 1D convolutional network that processes raw multi-channel waveforms directly (no spectrogram or other featurization) and regresses the mean and covariance of a 2D Gaussian over the environment. Training uses a negative log-likelihood loss—a proper scoring rule—that jointly optimizes point-estimate accuracy and uncertainty calibration, encouraging the 95% upper level set of the Gaussian to function as a valid 95% confidence set. When the network is overconfident, a post-hoc temperature scaling procedure (following Guo et al.) rescales the covariance to improve calibration, measured via ECE and MCE. The benchmark evaluates this DNN against the MUSE delay-and-sum beamforming baseline on both localization error (mean, median, %<1 cm) and calibration quality across five single-source datasets, and extends to a vocalization-attribution task on dyadic interaction data where the model must determine whether zero, one, or two animals fall within its confidence set.

## Results

On the controlled Speaker-4M-E1 and Edison-4M-E1 datasets, the 1D convolutional DNN achieves sub-centimeter (<1 cm) localization error on 80.6% and 66.0% of test samples, respectively, compared to only 5.9% and 3.1% for the MUSE beamforming baseline. On harder datasets with uncontrolled 3D variation (SoloGerbil-4M-E1, Hexapod-8M-E2, MouseEarbud-24M-E3), DNN sub-centimeter rates drop to 5.2–8.7%, yet still exceed MUSE (0.3–3.3%) by 2–3×. For calibration, the DNN achieves ECE of 0.03–0.22 (0.05–0.11 after temperature scaling) versus MUSE's 0.12–0.25, indicating more reliable confidence intervals.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole baseline is MUSE, a delay-and-sum beamforming algorithm (Neunuebel et al. 2015; Warren et al. 2018) that is currently the standard tool in rodent neuroethology labs. The DNN outperforms MUSE on every one of the five Task 1 datasets, with the largest margins on the controlled playback conditions: 80.6% vs. 5.9% sub-centimeter accuracy on Speaker-4M-E1 (a ~14× improvement) and 66.0% vs. 3.1% on Edison-4M-E1 (~21×). On the more challenging real-animal datasets the gap narrows but remains consistent (e.g., 8.7% vs. 3.3% on MouseEarbud-24M-E3). For uncertainty calibration, the DNN also beats MUSE on both ECE and MCE across all datasets; MUSE is systematically over-confident (its 95% confidence sets contain the true source far less than 95% of the time), while the DNN's intervals are closer to nominal after temperature scaling. No comparison is made against other deep-learning SSL methods (e.g., spectrogram-based CNNs or transformer architectures), so the DNN's advantage over MUSE should be read as a proof-of-concept rather than a state-of-the-art claim.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper repurposes standard deep learning SSL approaches (1D CNNs on raw multi-channel waveforms) and classical beamforming (MUSE) for a domain where no prior benchmark existed: localizing rodent vocalizations, including ultrasonic calls, in small reverberant laboratory arenas. It explicitly departs from existing SSL benchmarks (L3DAS, LOCATA, STARSS23) along two axes—frequency range (extending into ultrasonic) and environment scale (sub-square-meter reverberant cages vs. human-scale rooms). The acoustic simulation component draws on the image source method from the audio ML community, and the calibration analysis follows Guo et al.'s temperature scaling framework, but the core contribution is the dataset and benchmark rather than a new method.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first large-scale (767K events) public dataset enabling systematic evaluation of SSL in the bioacoustic domain, bridging two communities (neuroscience and acoustic ML) that have had limited overlap. Key open questions remain: DNNs fail to generalize across frequency ranges and call types, simulated data barely exceeds chance on real data, and the practical goal of attributing vocalizations to individuals in social groups (Task 2) still leaves 65–72% of calls unresolvable. Future work on 3D localization, multimodal (audio+video) attribution, and more robust acoustic simulations are identified as necessary next steps.
