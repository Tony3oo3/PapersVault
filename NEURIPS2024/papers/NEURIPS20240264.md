## Problem

GNNs consistently perform better for high-degree nodes than low-degree nodes in node classification, yet prior hypotheses for this degree bias are often not rigorously validated and can be contradictory. No prior work provides a comprehensive theoretical analysis that explicitly links a node's degree to its test and training misclassification probability in the semi-supervised setting.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240264/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The analysis operates on an undirected graph with fixed adjacency matrix A and node features X sampled from class-specific distributions, trained semi-supervisedly on a labeled subset S. The theoretical output is a lower bound on the squared inverse coefficient of variation R_{i,c'} (which upper-bounds misclassification probability) expressed in terms of node-specific quantities: l-hop collision probabilities and l-hop prediction homogeneity. Empirically, the paper reports per-node test loss, representation variance, and training loss curves stratified by degree across 8 real-world networks.

## Method

The paper linearizes message-passing GNNs into jumping-knowledge-network form for three graph filters (RW: D^{-1}A, SYM: D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}, ATT: attention-based) and applies Cantelli's inequality to bound misclassification probability via the squared inverse coefficient of variation R_{i,c'}. For test-time bias, it proves R_{i,c'} is larger for high-degree nodes because (a) their random-walk collision probability is lower (more diverse neighborhoods) and (b) their l-hop prediction homogeneity is more negative (more homophilic neighborhoods), with the SYM filter additionally scaling representations by sqrt(degree) to push low-degree nodes closer to decision boundaries. For training-time bias, it proves via Lipschitz continuity of cross-entropy that SYM's per-step loss change for node i is bounded by a factor of sqrt(D_ii), meaning low-degree nodes' losses adjust more slowly, while RW does not exhibit this degree-dependent learning-rate disparity. All theoretical results are validated on 8 datasets, and the paper proposes a four-criterion roadmap for alleviating degree bias targeting collision probability, prediction homogeneity, representational distributional gaps, and training-rate disparities.

## Results

The paper proves that high-degree test nodes have a lower probability of misclassification (Theorem 1: P(misclass) ≤ 1/(1+R_{i,c'})), and that this gap is driven by lower collision probability (more diverse neighborhoods) and higher prediction homogeneity for high-degree nodes (Theorems 2–3). Empirically, degree bias (lower test loss for high-degree nodes) is consistently observed across RW, SYM, and ATT GNNs on all 6 homophilic datasets (CoraML, CiteSeer, CS, Physics, Amazon Photo, Amazon Computers) but is absent on the 2 heterophilic datasets (chameleon, squirrel), confirming the theoretical prediction that heterophily neutralizes the homogeneity advantage. During training, SYM adjusts loss on low-degree nodes more slowly than on high-degree nodes (Theorem 4: |Δloss| scales with √D_ii), yet all three GNN variants ultimately reach their maximum possible training accuracy (MAJ_WL), which is often close to 100%, indicating expressive power is not a significant limiting factor.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a theoretical analysis paper rather than a method comparison, so it does not benchmark a proposed method against named baselines. Instead, it compares three graph filter variants—RW (random walk), SYM (symmetric), and ATT (attention)—to dissect the mechanisms behind degree bias. RW and SYM exhibit opposite variance patterns: RW representations of low-degree nodes have higher variance (spreading them closer to decision boundaries), while SYM representations of low-degree nodes have lower variance but are scaled closer to the decision boundary in expectation. SYM additionally shows a training-time disparity where low-degree node loss decreases more slowly than high-degree node loss, a phenomenon not observed to the same degree for RW. ATT behaves similarly to RW in most respects since attention matrices are also row-stochastic. Across all three filters, the inverse collision probability is positively correlated with node degree, and test loss is negatively correlated with degree on homophilic graphs but uncorrelated on heterophilic graphs (chameleon, squirrel).

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the general message-passing GNN framework [Di Giovanni et al., TMLR 2023] and the linearized Jumping Knowledge Network formulation [Xu et al., ICML 2018] to provide the first comprehensive theoretical analysis that explicitly bounds a node's misclassification probability as a function of its degree. It departs from prior theoretical analyses of degree bias that either rely on overly strong graph models (e.g., Contextual Stochastic Block Models) or posit insufficient GNN expressive power, instead using Cantelli's inequality, collision probability, and prediction homogeneity to derive filter-specific bounds. The paper positions itself as a unifying distillation of 38 prior degree-bias papers whose hypotheses it finds often unvalidated and sometimes contradictory (e.g., H5 vs. H10).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, theoretically-grounded roadmap for alleviating degree bias (maximizing inverse collision probability, increasing prediction homogeneity, minimizing representational distributional gaps, reducing training-rate disparities) that goes beyond ad-hoc augmentation strategies in prior work. A key limitation is the reliance on linearized GNNs and the semi-supervised node-classification setting; extending the analysis to non-linear architectures, inductive learning, and heterogeneous/directed graphs remains open. The finding that WL-test expressiveness does not significantly limit GNN accuracy on real-world networks challenges a common assumption in the GNN literature and redirects attention toward data- and filter-level factors as the primary drivers of degree bias.
