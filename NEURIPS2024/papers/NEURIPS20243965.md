## Problem

In individualized reinforcement learning, individual-specific factors (e.g., learning styles, genetic makeup) causally influence state-transition dynamics but remain unobserved in practice. Unlike prior work on time-varying latent factors, these factors are time-invariant, making identifiability from observed trajectories fundamentally harder. No prior work provides theoretical guarantees for recovering such time-invariant latent factors from state-action data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes per-individual state-action trajectories (sequences of d_s-dimensional states and d_a-dimensional actions over T timesteps), where in the text-based PersuasionForGood setting, utterances are first embedded via BERT into 768-dimensional vectors before sequential encoding. The output is a discrete latent group assignment κ̂_m per individual (quantized to one of G embedding vectors) plus an individualized policy π_κ* parameterized by κ. The latent factor is time-invariant—identical across all timesteps for a given individual—and the policy treats κ as an augmented input alongside the state.

## Method

The framework has two stages. First, a modified VQ-VAE estimates latent factors: a sequential encoder (LSTM or Conv1D) maps each full trajectory to a continuous representation z_m, a vector quantization layer discretizes z_m to the nearest of G embedding vectors (yielding κ̂_m), and a conditional decoder reconstructs s_t given (s_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, κ̂_m), trained via an ELBO combining reconstruction, quantization, and commitment losses. Second, the estimated κ is augmented into the RL policy input (e.g., Q-learning), and for new individuals the policy is initialized from the group-specific policy and fine-tuned on new interactions. The key theoretical contribution establishes identifiability under two settings: for finite κ, a group determinacy assumption (≥2G−1 individuals per group) enables identification via finite mixture model theory; for infinite κ, a post-nonlinear temporal model with rank-deficiency conditions on covariance submatrices determines both existence and cardinality of latent factors.

## Results

On the synthetic latent estimation task (Case 1, finite categorical κ with cardinality 4), the proposed method achieves a Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) of 0.942 (±4.0e-05) after adding the noise estimator, compared to 0.646 for the base quantized encoder alone. On the PersuasionForGood corpus (1017 dialogues, 32 personality traits), canonical correlation analysis confirms effective latent recovery when latent dimensions are appropriately tuned. In all three policy learning environments (Pendulum, HeartPole, Half Cheetah), the proposed method outperforms all baselines in both jumpstart (initial performance boost) and accumulative reward over the full training process.

## Baselines & Comparisons

For latent estimation, the Disentangled Sequential Autoencoder [85] captures the static part of the latent space early in training but fails to achieve full identifiability over time because it cannot model individualized transition processes, resulting in worse PCC than the proposed method. The population-level component baseline fails entirely to identify individual-specific factors since it overlooks underlying group differences. For policy learning, the proposed method outperforms SAC [24], DDPG [56], and Aligned Latent Models [20] in the Pendulum environment on both jumpstart and accumulative reward; it similarly beats D3QN [78] and Rainbow DQN [28] in HeartPole. A key finding is that cross-individual transfer (using population knowledge without individual-specific grouping) can actually degrade performance compared to the proposed group-aware approach, while random group embedding still improves over no grouping, confirming that the estimated latent group structure is what drives the gains.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the VAE architecture with a vector quantization layer for discrete latent estimation, and grounds its identifiability theory in two distinct theoretical pillars: finite mixture model identifiability from grouped samples (Vandermeulen & Scott) for the finite-latent case, and the post-nonlinear causal model (Zhang & Hyvarinen) for the infinite-latent case. It departs from prior RL-with-latent-variables work (POMDPs, factored MDPs, contextual MDPs, latent MDPs) by targeting time-invariant, individual-specific latent factors rather than time-varying ones, and by providing the first formal identifiability guarantees for this setting. The key conceptual departure is that time-invariant latents cannot supply the temporal variability that existing identifiability methods exploit, so the authors instead leverage group-structure information from the population.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper establishes that latent individual-specific factors influencing state transitions are identifiable from observed trajectories alone, which is a prerequisite for principled individualized RL policy design. This opens a path toward personalized decision-making in healthcare, education, and other domains where individual heterogeneity is driven by unobserved factors. Key limitations include the assumption of time-invariance (time-varying latents remain unaddressed), the absence of a formal nonparametric proof for continuous latent factors, and the lack of modeling for instantaneous causal dependencies within a state. Privacy and robustness concerns for real-world deployment are acknowledged but left for future work.
