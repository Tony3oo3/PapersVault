## Problem

Traditional clinical trials pre-define subgroups and follow fixed allocation rules, making them inefficient at discovering the best-performing subgroups post hoc. While some adaptive designs can identify a single best subgroup, they fail to handle the common scenario where multiple subgroups exhibit similar (tied) treatment effects, and none provide a principled way to dynamically identify and merge the best set of subgroups during an ongoing experiment.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242707/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes sequentially enrolled participants across T stages, each with a scalar covariate X_it, binary treatment assignment D_it, and continuous outcome Y_it, where the covariate space is pre-partitioned into m subgroups. At each stage it produces updated treatment allocation probabilities per subgroup, a dynamically identified tie set of best subgroups (merged into a single unit), and at the final stage a point estimate with a two-sided confidence interval for the merged best-subgroup treatment effect. The tie set is defined via a sqrt(N)-local neighborhood, so subgroups whose true effects differ by o(N^{-1/2}) are treated as equivalent and merged.

## Method

The design operates in three coordinated steps at each stage. First, it solves a minimax optimization (in epigraph form) that maximizes the worst-case separation between the current best-subgroup set and every other subgroup, subject to a resource constraint on total treatment budget and feasibility bounds on allocation probabilities. Second, it performs dynamic subgroup identification via bootstrap resampling: B bootstrap replicates of the treatment-effect estimates are generated, and subgroups whose bootstrap estimates fall within a data-adaptive interval (width N_t^{-delta} * V_hat^{delta}, delta=0.25) around the top subgroup are flagged as tied and merged. Third, the interval hyperparameters (c_L, c_R) are selected by minimizing a bootstrap-calibrated loss that measures how well the empirical coverage of the merged estimate matches a target Bernoulli distribution. The paper proves that the identified tie set converges to the true tie set, the allocation converges to the oracle optimum, and the merged treatment-effect estimator is asymptotically normal and semiparametrically efficient.

## Results

On the synthetic PBC dataset (5 subgroups, T=15 stages, n_t=400 per stage), the proposed dynamic subgroup identification CARA design achieves a standard deviation of 40.94 for the estimated best-subgroup treatment effect, roughly half that of all three baselines (~80–81). The √N-scaled bias is 29.98, and the 95% confidence interval is (9.28, 11.35), the narrowest among all methods. The correct selection probability of the best tie set (Subgroups 2 and 4, true merged ATE = 10.70) increases monotonically with stages and approaches 1 after 15 stages, outperforming complete randomization, ε-greedy, and UCB1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares its proposed dynamic subgroup identification CARA design against three baselines: complete randomization (fixed e=0.5), the ε-greedy multi-armed bandit algorithm (ε=0.1, from Sutton & Barto [37]), and the UCB1 algorithm (from Auer et al. [4]). The proposed method dominates on all reported metrics: it achieves a standard deviation of 40.94 versus 80.29 (CR), 81.19 (ε-greedy), and 80.85 (UCB1), representing roughly a 2× reduction in estimation variance. Its √N-scaled bias of 29.98 is comparable to ε-greedy (28.84) and substantially lower than CR (34.55) and UCB1 (34.82). The 95% CI width is 2.07 for the proposed method versus 4.06–4.11 for all baselines. On correct selection probability (Figure 1), the proposed design rises to near 1 by Stage 15, while the three baselines plateau at lower values. The ε-greedy baseline is the closest competitor on bias but still suffers from roughly double the variance, and none of the MAB baselines incorporate the tie-set merging mechanism that the proposed design uses to identify multiple best subgroups simultaneously.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the CARA (covariate-adjusted response-adaptive randomization) design framework, which itself builds on response-adaptive randomization and covariate-adaptive allocation literature, by integrating dynamic subgroup identification directly into the adaptive allocation loop. It departs from prior CARA work (Hu et al. 2015, Zhu & Zhu 2023) that assumes subgroups are pre-specified, and from post-hoc subgroup identification methods (causal trees, clustering) that rely on untestable causal assumptions in observational settings. The closest prior work is the Bayesian adaptive design of Guo et al. (2017), but this paper operates in a frequentist, model-free framework and extends the objective from identifying a single best subgroup to identifying the best *set* of subgroups with tied effects, using large-deviation-theory-based minimax optimization and bootstrap-based tie detection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first frequentist, model-free adaptive design that simultaneously learns treatment allocation and dynamically identifies the best set of subgroups (handling ties), with full theoretical guarantees including consistency, convergence to the oracle design, and semiparametric efficiency of the resulting estimator. This is practically important for precision medicine trials where multiple subgroups may share the top treatment effect (as in the FORTE trial) and where treatment costs constrain the total number of patients who can be treated. Key limitations include the assumption of no delayed outcomes, the focus on costly-treatment scenarios with a global resource constraint, and the open question of how to incorporate early stopping for subgroups with adverse effects. The empirical validation, while including two real-data-motivated synthetic studies, remains limited in scope (single outcome type, fixed number of subgroups).
