## Problem

Video transformers are slow to train because standard spatiotemporal patch tokenization produces a fixed, large number of tokens regardless of video content, even though many patches repeat over time. Existing token-reduction methods either incur significant overhead that negates speedups, require dataset-specific tuning, or are not content-aware (removing the same number of tokens from a static lecture as from high-action footage). The paper seeks a simple, content-aware tokenization that reduces tokens proportionally to redundancy without per-dataset tuning.

## Input / Output

The method takes a video clip of shape C×T×H×W, splits it into non-overlapping spatiotemporal patches of size C×D_x×D_y×D_t, and produces a variable number of tokens (always ≤ the standard count) that are fed to a ViT. The output is a class label for action recognition, computed as the mean of all output tokens projected to N_C dimensions. Because the token count is content-dependent, training uses example packing with a block-diagonal attention mask so that tokens from different videos in a batch attend only to each other.

## Method

RLT identifies temporally consecutive patches at the same spatial location whose L1 difference (between the last frame-crop of the earlier patch and the first frame-crop of the later patch) falls below a threshold τ, and removes the redundant patches, retaining only the first patch in each run. A learnable length encoding φ_L of size (T, d_embed) is added to the standard factorized positional encoding to communicate each token's run-length to the transformer. Because RLT operates on raw patches before the embedding MLP, it requires no gradient propagation through pruning, no padding, and remains fully compatible with Flash Attention and other hardware-optimized kernels. The only tunable hyperparameter is τ (typically 0.1), which is dataset-agnostic and controls the speed-accuracy tradeoff. During training, variable-length inputs are handled via example packing with block-diagonal attention masks, and the method can be stacked with random masking for additional speedup.

## Results

On Kinetics-400, RLT matches or exceeds baseline ViT accuracy (80.6% vs 80.5% for ViT-B, 84.6% vs 84.8% for ViT-L) while reducing GFLOPS from 180 to 120 (ViT-B) and 598 to 405 (ViT-L), yielding 1.67× and 1.62× throughput speedups respectively. During fine-tuning, RLT reduces wall-clock training time by 40% (14.4h → 10.2h for ViT-B, 21.6h → 15.4h for ViT-L) while matching baseline accuracy. At 30 FPS, the speedup grows to 2.1×, and on long-video datasets (COIN, Breakfast) RLT reduces total token count by up to 80%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against Token Merging (ToMe) [5], RLT consistently wins on both accuracy and throughput: on Kinetics-400 ViT-B, RLT achieves 80.6% vs ToMe's 80.4% at 52.6 clips/s vs 34.4 clips/s, because ToMe's proportional attention mechanism is incompatible with Flash Attention. Against random masking [27], RLT matches the speedup (1.67× vs 1.68×) but improves accuracy by 0.5% on K400 ViT-B (80.6% vs 80.1%) and 0.5% on SSv2 (69.8% vs 69.3%), demonstrating that content-aware token selection matters. Against STA [36], RLT substantially outperforms on accuracy (80.6% vs 80.4% for ViT-B; 84.6% vs 80.4% for ViT-L on K400) while also being faster, since STA requires a fixed number of tokens to remove per video rather than adapting to content. During training, RLT achieves the same 1.4× speedup as random masking but matches baseline accuracy (80.1% vs 79.2% on K400 ViT-B), whereas random masking requires additional epochs to close the gap. RLT's advantage grows with model size and FPS: at ViT-H the accuracy gap over ToMe widens to 0.2% with a 1.45× vs 1.27× speedup, and at 30 FPS RLT delivers 2.1× training speedup versus 1.4× at 7.5 FPS.

## Relation to Prior Work

RLT draws its core idea from inter-frame prediction in video codecs (H.264/H.265) and classical run-length encoding, applying them to the tokenization stage of video transformers. It departs from prior token-reduction methods (Token Merging, DynamicViT, A-ViT, STA) by operating on raw spatiotemporal patches before embedding rather than on embedded tokens within the network, eliminating the need for padding, model architecture changes, or learned pruning modules. The factorized positional encoding and example-packing strategy are adapted from NaViT [10], and the pre-trained VideoMAE checkpoints provide the base models. Unlike fixed-ratio methods (ToMe, random masking), RLT is content-aware: the number of tokens removed varies per video based on actual temporal redundancy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

RLT offers a practical, drop-in speedup (30–40% in training, ~35% throughput at inference) that is compatible with hardware-optimized kernels like Flash Attention, a property that learned pruning and Token Merging lack. The ability to train at 30 FPS with >100% speedup and reduce tokens by up to 80% on long static videos opens a path toward scaling video transformers to longer, higher-FPS inputs without proportional compute increases. Key limitations remain: RLT cannot handle global camera motion (few tokens are pruned), is inapplicable to dense tasks requiring one-to-one token correspondence, and relies on a single heuristic threshold τ that, while dataset-agnostic, still requires a one-time tuning decision. Whether RLT can be extended to video generation or tracking, and how it interacts with learned temporal attention patterns at scale, remain open.
