## Problem

Previous models of sequence-generating circuits (e.g., in zebra finch HVC) relied on guessed plasticity rules and addressed either the organization or the maintenance of sequential dynamics, but not both under ongoing biological noise such as synaptic turnover. No prior work had discovered—rather than imposed—local plasticity rules capable of self-organizing sparse sequences while simultaneously self-sustaining them through stochastic structural perturbations, nor had the role of inhibitory-to-excitatory plasticity in this context been explored.

## Input / Output

The method takes a randomly connected network of 25 excitatory and 8 inhibitory threshold-linear neurons (no I→I connections, consistent with HVC anatomy) and produces a set of coefficients {c_k} and time constants {τ_k} defining a local plasticity rule. Each network is driven by 400 activations of 110 ms, where a fixed excitatory kick at t=10 ms is followed by 65 ms of partially shared Poisson input, mimicking thalamic drive from nucleus Uva. The loss function evaluates a linear decoder's ability to read out elapsed time from the final 50 activations, penalized by total network activity and total synaptic change, so the output rule is optimized for temporal fidelity, sparsity, and metabolic economy simultaneously.

## Method

The paper employs meta-learning via CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy) to search over a parameterized space of plasticity rules, where each rule is a weighted sum of basis functions F_k(x_i, x_j, w_ij, τ_k) built from filtered pre- and postsynaptic activities and synapse size, with per-term time constants. The outer evolutionary loop samples candidate {c_k, τ_k} vectors, simulates 10 randomly initialized networks under each candidate for 400 activations, and evaluates the composite loss; the inner loop is the network simulation itself. A key design choice is an L1-like penalty on the quantity of synaptic change evoked by each basis term (rather than on coefficient magnitude), enabling fair comparison across terms with different activity/weight dependencies. Dropout analysis—zeroing individual coefficients and measuring loss impact on 100 test networks—reveals that the critical terms reduce to a temporally asymmetric Hebbian pair (x̃_i·x_j and x_i·x̃_j) plus a postsynaptic activity bound (w_ij·x̃_j·x_j), constituting a generalization of Oja's rule. When synaptic turnover is introduced, a constant potentiation term and a synapse-size-independent activity bound emerge, providing homeostatic compensation; when plasticity is extended to E→I and I→E synapses, the learned rules jointly adjust excitation and recurrent inhibition to restore a targeted neuron's firing envelope (duration, peak amplitude, and timing) after perturbation, outperforming E→E-only rules under high turnover rates.

## Results

Meta-learning discovers a 3-term temporally asymmetric generalization of Oja's rule (Eq. 4) that organizes random E→E connectivity into feedforward sequence generators, with a decoder loss significantly below no-plasticity baselines. When synaptic turnover is introduced, the learned rule expands to 4 terms (Eq. 6) incorporating a constant potentiation term and a synapse-size-independent activity bound. In the key comparison (Fig. 4d), both meta-learned rules outperform the Fiete et al. [11] rule under synaptic turnover (p=2.5×10⁻⁸, Cohen's d=−0.45; p=7.2×10⁻⁷, Cohen's d=−0.44), while differences without turnover are not significant after Bonferroni correction (p=0.016, p=0.029). Adding plasticity on E→I and I→E synapses further improves time decoding, particularly at high turnover rates (Fig. 5c).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's primary baseline is the Fiete et al. [11] rule, which uses multiplicative asymmetric Hebbian learning combined with a single-synapse bound and bounds on total afferent/efferent synaptic strength per neuron. Under synaptic turnover (p_ST=0.00072, ~90% synapse survival per activation), both meta-learned rules (with and without turnover during training) significantly outperform Fiete et al. on the time-decoding loss (p=2.5×10⁻⁸ and p=7.2×10⁻⁷, respectively; Cohen's d≈−0.45), indicating the meta-learned rules better maintain sequential dynamics when synapses are stochastically added and removed. In the absence of perturbation, the meta-learned rules show a small but non-significant advantage after 4-fold Bonferroni correction (p=0.016 and p=0.029, Cohen's d≈−0.30), suggesting the key benefit of the discovered rules is robustness to biological noise rather than peak performance in clean conditions. The advantage is attributed to the meta-learned rules' lack of hard bounds on individual synapse size or total synaptic strength, which permits flexible compensatory rescaling after perturbations. A secondary comparison (Fig. 5c) shows that rules learned on all three synapse types (E→E, E→I, I→E) outperform E→E-only rules, with the gap widening at higher turnover rates, confirming that inhibitory plasticity contributes additional stability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the meta-learning framework for discovering plasticity rules (Bengio et al. 1991; Confavreux et al. 2020) to the specific problem of self-organizing and maintaining sequence-generating dynamics in a zebra finch HVC-inspired circuit. Prior sequence-formation models (Fiete et al. 2010; Jun & Jin 2007; Zheng & Triesch 2014) imposed guessed plasticity rules with hard synaptic bounds and focused on either organization or maintenance in isolation; here, the rules are discovered via CMA-ES optimization and are explicitly tested under biological noise (synaptic turnover). The paper further departs from prior HVC modeling by incorporating plasticity on inhibitory synapses (E→I and I→E), motivated by recent experimental evidence of unsupervised recovery involving both excitatory and inhibitory synaptic changes (Wang et al. 2024).

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a principled, feedback-free framework for discovering local plasticity rules that both organize and maintain a specific computational motif, showing that flexible rescaling without hard bounds outperforms rigid-constraint models under biologically realistic perturbation. The mechanistic finding that E→I and I→E plasticity create a joint attractor in the space of postsynaptic response amplitude and duration offers a concrete account of how inhibitory plasticity stabilizes timing representations. Key limitations include the computational cost of CMA-ES (24–72 hours per training run), seed-dependent solutions, dense rule representations that resist closed-form interpretation, and a basis set that excludes feedback-modulated plasticity. Open questions include whether the paradigm generalizes to other canonical motifs (line attractors, limit cycles) and how discovered self-organized rules interact with subsequent feedback-driven learning.
