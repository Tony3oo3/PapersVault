## Problem

Existing fine-grained VLM benchmarks primarily evaluate the ability to distinguish between similar captions given an image, while the complementary task of distinguishing between visually similar images given a caption remains underexplored. The few benchmarks offering visual hard-negatives either allow multiple aspects to change simultaneously (limiting diagnostic precision) or rely on simplistic synthetic scenes (limiting ecological validity). VisMin addresses both gaps by presenting image pairs that differ in exactly one controlled aspect—object, attribute, count, or spatial relation—within complex, natural COCO-style scenes.

## Input / Output

The evaluation task is a binary image-text matching problem in two symmetric settings: (1) given two images and one caption, select the matching image; (2) given one image and two captions, select the matching caption. Images are either natural COCO photos (for object/attribute edits) or synthetically generated 512×512 scenes (for counting/spatial relation edits); captions are human-written (COCO, VSR) or LLM-generated free-form descriptions. For foundational VLMs (CLIP, SigLIP), scoring uses cosine similarity between image and text embeddings; for MLLMs, the task is reformulated as a binary-choice VQA prompt (e.g., 'Does this image depict: C1 or C2?').

## Method

The pipeline synthesizes minimal-change image-text pairs in three stages: (i) an LLM (Mistral 47B) generates edit instructions and edited captions, then diffusion models execute the visual edits—SDXL inpainting with Grounding-DINO masks for object/attribute changes, LMD for source scene synthesis, GLIGEN layout-diffusion for spatial repositioning, and Lama inpainting for object removal in counting edits; (ii) a VQA-based automatic filter (LLaVA 7B answering LLM-generated questions) removes ~75% of inconsistent samples; (iii) a rigorous 4-step human verification on AMT (naturalness, visual edit fidelity, edit instruction minimality, textual edit fidelity) retains only high-quality examples. For fine-tuning, the 64K training samples are used to augment CLIP's contrastive batches with hard-negative image-text pairs (no architecture or loss changes), and to instruction-tune Idefics2-8B via QLoRa (4-bit, r=64, α=16) on 65K reformulated binary-choice samples. The key design choice is using layout-based diffusion models rather than end-to-end diffusion, which provides precise control over object positions and counts to guarantee that changes are truly minimal.

## Results

On the VisMin benchmark, GPT-4o achieves the highest average score of 73.93%, while the best open-source foundational VLM (SigLip ViT-L/16) reaches 56.39% and the best MLLM (Idefics2) reaches 55.99%. All models score below random chance (25%) on spatial relation understanding, with CLIP (ViT-L/14) at only 9.16% text score and 4.66% image score. Fine-tuning CLIP on VisMin data (VisMin-CLIP) improves the average from 46.42% to 63.74%, and fine-tuning Idefics2 (VisMin-Idefics2) improves it from 55.99% to 86.43%, approaching GPT-4o's performance. On 10 out-of-distribution benchmarks, VisMin-CLIP achieves the best score in 11 out of 18 tasks, with notable gains on CountBench (49.84% vs. 33.65% baseline) and Winoground text score (32.59% vs. 19.26% baseline).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among foundational VLMs, SigLip (ViT-L/16) leads with 56.39% AVG, slightly ahead of BLIP (56.36%), while CLIP (ViT-L/14) trails at 46.42%. Surprisingly, MLLMs underperform foundational VLMs on object and attribute understanding: Idefics2 (55.99%) and LLaVA-1.6 (38.28%) score lower than SigLip and BLIP, primarily due to very low image scores (e.g., LLaVA's image score drops to 32.8% on objects vs. 93.0% text score). GPT-4o dominates all categories with 73.93% AVG, the only model to exceed 50% on spatial relations (89.07% text, 50.48% image). After fine-tuning, VisMin-CLIP (63.74% AVG) outperforms NegCLIP (48.96%), CounterCurate-CLIP (55.54%), and SPEC-CLIP (50.55%) on object, attribute, and count categories, though it still lags on spatial relations. VisMin-Idefics2 (86.43% AVG) achieves near-GPT-4o performance, with dramatic spatial relation gains (from 18.6% to 83.0% text score). On out-of-distribution benchmarks (Table 4), VisMin-CLIP wins 11/18 tasks, outperforming NegCLIP by 9% on CountBench and 13.3% on Winoground text score, while using only 65K training samples versus 637K for SPEC-CLIP.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the line of work on fine-grained VLM evaluation (Winoground, MMVP, EQBEN, SPEC) by introducing a controlled minimal-change formulation where only one aspect (object, attribute, count, or spatial relation) differs between image pairs, while maintaining complex natural COCO scenes. It departs from prior visual hard-negative benchmarks that either allow multi-aspect changes (Winoground, MMVP) or restrict to synthetic/graphic-engine scenes (SPEC, EQBEN), and from caption-only hard-negative approaches (ARO, Sugarcrepe, NegCLIP). The data pipeline repurposes LLMs (Mistral 47B) for edit instruction generation and a combination of diffusion models (SDXL inpainting, GLIGEN, LMD, Lama) for image synthesis, building on the diffusion-based hard-negative generation paradigm of DEMON and SPEC but with more precise layout control and a rigorous four-step human verification process.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals that all evaluated VLMs—both foundational and MLLMs—score below random chance on spatial relation understanding, and that MLLMs paradoxically underperform foundational VLMs on object/attribute discrimination, pointing to a critical gap in current model training. The 64K-sample minimal-change training dataset provides a practical, model-agnostic resource that improves fine-grained understanding across 10+ out-of-distribution benchmarks without architectural changes, and also enhances general image-text alignment (COCO retrieval). Open questions include whether the spatial-relation deficit can be addressed through targeted training data, whether the diffusion-model artifacts in the dataset limit ceiling performance, and whether the binary-choice evaluation format generalizes to open-ended compositional reasoning.
