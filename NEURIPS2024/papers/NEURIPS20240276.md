## Problem

Existing attacks on invisible image watermarks are either destructive (causing significant quality loss) or constructive (failing against resilient watermarks), leaving open the fundamental question of whether pixel-level invisible watermarks bounded in ℓ₂-distance can be robust against any quality-preserving attack. This paper asks whether there is an inherent trade-off between watermark invisibility and resilience, and whether a single attack can provably remove all such watermarks while maintaining image quality.

## Input / Output

The method takes a single watermarked RGB image x_w as input and produces a reconstructed watermark-free image x̂ that approximates the original unwatermarked image x₀. The attack is parameterized by a noise level σ (or equivalently a diffusion time step t*) that controls the trade-off between watermark removal strength and output fidelity; higher noise removes more watermark signal but degrades image quality. The method is agnostic to the specific watermarking scheme, requiring no knowledge of the watermarking algorithm, secret key, or detection procedure.

## Method

The proposed regeneration attack follows a two-stage pipeline: it first maps the watermarked image to an embedding via φ, adds i.i.d. Gaussian noise N(0, σ²I_d) to destruct the watermark, then reconstructs the image via a regeneration function A, yielding x̂ = A(φ(x_w) + N(0, σ²I_d)). Three instantiations are provided: identity embedding with classical denoisers (BM3D, TV-denoising), VAE encode-decode (where the stochastic sampling inherently adds noise), and latent diffusion models (encode to latent, forward-diffuse to time t*, then reverse-diffuse to reconstruct). The key theoretical result (Theorem 4.3) proves that for any Δ-invisible watermark under ℓ₂-distance, the attack achieves f-Certified-Watermark-Free, meaning no detection algorithm can simultaneously maintain low false positive and false negative rates after the attack; the proof leverages the Gaussian differential privacy tradeoff function framework. A complementary utility theorem (Theorem 4.4) guarantees that if the generative model denoises a noisy unwatermarked image well, it also denoises the noisy watermarked image well, with only a slightly larger failure probability.

## Results

Across four pixel-level watermarking schemes (DwtDctSvd, RivaGAN, SSL, StegaStamp) on MS-COCO and SDP-generated images, the proposed regeneration attacks (Diffusion, VAE-Bmshj2018, VAE-Cheng2020) achieve over 99% watermark removal (TPR@FPR=0.01 < 0.01) for DwtDctSvd, RivaGAN, and SSL while maintaining PSNR above 30 dB. For the most robust pixel-level scheme RivaGAN, the diffusion-based attack removes 98% of watermarks with PSNR > 30. In contrast, StegaStamp (which introduces larger perturbations, PSNR ≈ 28.5) requires substantially higher noise levels for effective removal, and the semantic Tree-Ring watermark remains fully detectable (TPR@FPR=0.01 ≈ 1.0) under all tested attacks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares the proposed regeneration attacks (Diffusion, VAE-Bmshj2018, VAE-Cheng2020) against five baseline attack categories: brightness/contrast adjustment, JPEG compression, Gaussian noise, and Gaussian blur. On the quality-detectability tradeoff (Figures 4 and 7), the regeneration attacks consistently dominate all baselines, occupying the lower-right region (lower TPR@FPR=0.01 and higher PSNR/SSIM) for DwtDctSvd, RivaGAN, and SSL watermarks. Destructive attacks like JPEG compression and brightness change do remove watermarks but at the cost of significant quality degradation (lower PSNR/SSIM), while constructive attacks like Gaussian blur fail to remove resilient watermarks like RivaGAN. The diffusion-based regeneration attack achieves the best image quality among all methods (visually sharper than VAE outputs), while VAE attacks yield slightly higher PSNR/SSIM scores but introduce blurring. StegaStamp is the hardest to attack due to its larger perturbation magnitude (PSNR ≈ 28.5 vs. ~40 for others), and only the diffusion attack with high noise steps (≥100) achieves effective removal. The semantic Tree-Ring watermark is robust against all attacks including the proposed regeneration attacks, with TPR@FPR=0.01 remaining at or above 0.994 across all tested methods.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously separate categories of watermark-removal attacks—destructive (noise, compression) and constructive (denoising, blurring)—into a single 'regeneration attack' framework that first corrupts an image's embedding and then reconstructs it via a generative model. The theoretical guarantee (f-Certified-Watermark-Free) is derived from the Gaussian differential privacy tradeoff function framework of Dong et al. (2022), establishing a formal connection between watermark removal and privacy-preserving mechanisms. Unlike prior empirical attacks (DiffWA, PGD-based surrogate attacks, adaptive key-replication attacks) that target specific watermarking schemes, this work provides a scheme-agnostic impossibility result for all ℓ₂-bounded invisible watermarks, leveraging the same diffusion and VAE architectures that underpin modern image generation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The provable removal guarantee fundamentally undermines the industry's current strategy of embedding invisible pixel-level watermarks in AI-generated images (as pledged by Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI), suggesting a necessary shift toward semantic-preserving watermarks like Tree-Ring. The irony that generative models both create the need for provenance tracking and enable watermark removal raises open questions about whether any watermarking scheme can simultaneously be invisible, robust, and provably undetectable. A key limitation is that the theoretical guarantee applies specifically to ℓ₂-bounded perturbations; watermarks that alter content semantically (while remaining visually similar) escape the guarantee, but at the cost of visible distortion. Future work should explore whether tighter semantic watermarks can close the gap between invisibility and robustness.
