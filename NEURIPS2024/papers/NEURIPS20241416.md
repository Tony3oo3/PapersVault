## Problem

Existing neural impulse response field methods (e.g., NAF, INRAS) can approximate general energy decay trends but fail to capture detailed waveform characteristics, particularly phase information and time-of-arrival accuracy, because they lack physical constraints that enforce multi-view consistency. The absence of wave propagation principles in the modeling leads to overfitting on training poses and poor generalization to novel emitter-listener configurations.

## Input / Output

The model takes an emitter pose (position p_e ∈ R³, direction ω_e ∈ R³) and a listener position p_l ∈ R³ as conditioning inputs, then queries a 6-layer MLP (with hash-grid encoding) at N_θ×N_φ×N_r = 80×40×64 points distributed over a sphere and along each ray to produce a density σ and a discrete signal sequence s[n]. The rendered output is a full impulse response h[n] (0.1s or 0.32s at 16–24 KHz) in both time and frequency domains, and at inference time the spherical integration can be swapped to per-ear HRTF-weighted rendering to produce binaural audio without retraining.

## Method

AVR adapts NeRF-style volume rendering to acoustics by introducing two key modifications: (1) a frequency-domain rendering formulation where arbitrary propagation delays u/v are handled as phase shifts e^{-j2πfu/v} rather than fractional time shifts, avoiding the interpolation problem of discrete time-domain sampling; and (2) spherical integration that casts rays uniformly over the full sphere (stratified azimuth, uniform elevation) and composes directional signals h_ω(t) with a listener gain pattern G(ω) to recover the omnidirectional impulse response. The network F_Θ outputs a volume density σ and a re-transmitted signal s(t) at each query point, and the rendering equation (Eq. 4) accumulates signals along each ray with alpha composition, a 1/(tv) energy decay factor, and per-point time delays. Training uses a composite loss combining spectral (real/imaginary), amplitude, phase (cos/sin encoding), time-domain L1, multi-resolution STFT, and energy terms, with frequency-domain supervision emphasized to exploit lower local variability. The companion AcoustiX simulator, built on the Sionna ray-tracing engine, generates physically accurate IRs with correct time-of-flight and phase relationships by applying frequency-dependent reflection/scattering coefficients per octave band.

## Results

On the MeshRIR real-world dataset (0.1s IR), AVR achieves a phase error of 0.85, amplitude error of 0.54, envelope error of 1.15%, T60 error of 3.9%, C50 error of 0.92 dB, and EDT error of 35.1 ms — substantially outperforming all baselines. Notably, AVR is the only method that beats the random phase error floor of 1.62, meaning all other learning-based methods (NAF, INRAS) fail to learn valid phase information. On simulated datasets (2D Room, iGibson Avonia, iGibson Montreal), AVR similarly leads on amplitude, envelope, C50, and EDT metrics, with phase errors of 1.04–1.53 versus 1.61–1.70 for all other methods.

## Baselines & Comparisons

AVR outperforms NAF [29] on every metric for MeshRIR: phase error drops from 1.61 to 0.85 (47% reduction), amplitude from 0.64 to 0.54, envelope from 1.59% to 1.15%, C50 from 1.25 to 0.92 dB, and EDT from 39.0 to 35.1 ms. Against INRAS [44], AVR achieves a 47% phase error reduction (1.61→0.85), 30% amplitude reduction (0.77→0.54), and 38% envelope reduction (1.85→1.15%), though INRAS retains a slight T60 advantage (3.4% vs 3.9%). Traditional audio-encoding baselines (AAC, Opus) are dominated across all metrics, with AVR achieving roughly 40–50% lower phase and amplitude errors. On the longer 0.32s RAF-Furnished dataset, AVR also beats AV-NeRF [27] on all six metrics (e.g., phase 1.59 vs 1.62, amplitude 0.69 vs 0.93, EDT 19.8 vs 25.9 ms). The critical finding is that NAF, INRAS, and AV-NeRF all produce phase errors at or above the random baseline of 1.62, indicating they fail to learn meaningful phase structure, whereas AVR's physics-based frequency-domain rendering enables valid phase synthesis.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the volume rendering framework from NeRF (Mildenhall et al., 2021) to acoustic impulse response synthesis, introducing frequency-domain rendering with spherical integration to handle the unique challenges of time-series signals and omnidirectional microphone capture. It departs from prior neural implicit acoustic field methods (NAF, INRAS, AV-NeRF) by embedding wave propagation physics—specifically time delay and energy decay—directly into the rendering equation rather than relying on the network to implicitly learn these relationships. The companion simulator AcoustiX builds on the Sionna ray tracing engine (Hoydis et al., 2022), modifying it with acoustic propagation equations to fix phase and time-of-flight inaccuracies present in existing simulators like SoundSpaces 2.0.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that all prior learning-based methods produce phase errors at chance level (1.62) reveals a fundamental limitation in the field: without explicit physical constraints, networks cannot learn valid phase relationships in impulse responses, which are critical for spatial audio perception. This positions physics-informed rendering as a necessary (not merely beneficial) component for high-fidelity acoustic synthesis. Key limitations include per-scene training requirements, ~10× slower inference than direct network baselines, and evaluation on relatively small datasets (single rooms, one office). Open questions include whether multi-modal inputs (visual geometry, sparse acoustic samples) can enable zero-shot generalization to novel scenes, and whether efficient sampling strategies from the NeRF literature can close the inference gap.
