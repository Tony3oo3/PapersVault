## Problem

Prior shape-recognition benchmarks (Cue Conflict, Stylized-ImageNet) rely on single-object images with uniform textures produced by legacy style transfer, lacking coherent naturalistic scenes and often losing shape information entirely. This paper asks whether current VLMs can recognize abstract shapes when they are represented by arrangements of diverse visual elements within complex, naturalistic scenes — a fundamentally harder perceptual task that better mirrors how humans perceive shape invariance across transformations.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242807/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each test sample is a generated RGB image in which a binary shape conditioning image (e.g., a dog silhouette) is embedded as an arrangement of scene elements (e.g., buildings collectively forming a dog shape in a city scene), paired with a text prompt querying for the shape class, scene type, or both. For zero-shot and ICL evaluations, models produce free-text responses scored by whether the correct class name appears in the output (a recall metric rather than exact-match accuracy); for domain generalization, CLIP produces a discrete class prediction among all shape classes using the prompt template 'A photo of {class_name}.' The three dataset splits vary in shape type — 16 object classes (IN), 21 brand logos (LOGO), and 6 hypernym categories over 456 emoji icons (ICON) — each rendered across 11 scene domains.

## Method

The dataset is generated by conditioning ControlNet (QRCode Monster variant) on binary shape images while using Stable Diffusion (RealisticVision V5.1) with single-word scene-type prompts, producing images where scene elements collectively form the target shape at controlled illusion-strength levels validated by human annotation (>95% recognizability). The paper then evaluates VLMs under three paradigms: (1) zero-shot prompting of generative VLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini, LLaVA, InstructBLIP, CogVLM, Qwen-VL, MoE variants) with closed-set shape/scene identification; (2) few-shot in-context learning with four constrained demonstration-selection tasks (ICL1–ICL4) that systematically vary whether the context includes the test shape type, scene type, both, or neither; and (3) domain generalization by fine-tuning CLIP (ResNet-50 backbone) on five source scene domains and testing on six unseen target domains, comparing ERM, MixUp, RegMixUp, GroupDRO, VREx, and DPLCLIP. The central finding is that while humans easily identify the embedded shapes, VLMs consistently default to describing scene elements rather than the abstract shape, with zero-shot shape recall typically 10–30% and ICL providing minimal improvement, whereas full-parameter fine-tuning of CLIP with MixUp/RegMixUp achieves ~72% cross-domain shape accuracy.

## Results

On IllusionBench, zero-shot shape recall for most VLMs ranges between 10–30%, in stark contrast to Stylized-ImageNet where all 14 evaluated models exceed 30%. Human annotators achieve 95.6–97.2% shape recognition accuracy, highlighting a large gap. In the domain generalization setting (IllusionBench-IN), full-parameter fine-tuning with RegMixUp achieves 73.00% average test accuracy and MixUp achieves 71.79%, far surpassing linear probing (ERM: 22.36%) and prompt learning (DPLCLIP: 13.62%). Across all paradigms, VLMs consistently exhibit higher scene recall than shape recall, confirming a persistent bias toward texture/scene features over abstract shape.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the prior shape-recognition benchmark Stylized-ImageNet (Geirhos et al., 2019), IllusionBench reveals substantially lower VLM shape recall (10–30% vs. >30%), demonstrating that the difficulty gap is not merely an artifact of the older dataset's limitations. Among domain generalization methods, RegMixUp (Pinto et al., 2022b) achieves the best average accuracy at 73.00%, narrowly beating MixUp (Yan et al., 2020) at 71.79%. Both substantially outperform GroupDRO (Sagawa et al., 2019) and VREx (Krueger et al., 2021), which fall below 71.79%. DPLCLIP (Zhang et al., 2023b), a prompt-learning approach, performs worst at 13.62%, even below simple ERM linear probing (22.36%), indicating that CLIP's embedding space does not linearly encode the abstract shape information. Overall, the paper's key finding is that no current VLM or DG method achieves human-level shape recognition, with the best full fine-tuning result (73%) still far below the ~96% human accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on ControlNet-conditioned diffusion models (Zhang et al., ICCV 2023) for dataset generation and explicitly departs from the Cue Conflict and Stylized-ImageNet benchmarks (Geirhos et al., ICLR 2019), which it critiques for lacking naturalistic scenes, losing shape information through legacy style transfer, and containing only single objects with uniform textures. Conceptually, it extends the shape-perception evaluation lineage from early CNN classifiers to modern vision-language models, introducing a new formulation where shapes are represented by arrangements of visual elements within coherent scenes rather than by style-transferred single objects. The evaluation spans three paradigms—zero-shot generative VLMs, in-context learning, and contrastive CLIP fine-tuning for domain generalization—broadening the scope beyond prior single-paradigm shape bias studies.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a striking and persistent gap: human annotators identify the hidden shapes with 95–97% accuracy, while even GPT-4o and Gemini achieve only 10–30% shape recall, and in-context learning fails to close this gap. This suggests that the texture/scene bias identified in early CNNs has not been resolved in state-of-the-art VLMs, raising open questions about whether scaling or instruction tuning alone can produce genuinely shape-invariant visual representations. The finding that CLIP can be fine-tuned to 71–73% cross-domain accuracy with MixUp/RegMixUp offers a constructive path forward, but the zero-shot and ICL failures highlight that current VLMs lack the abstract compositional shape perception that underpins human visual robustness. The paper also raises practical concerns about content moderation, since the same generative techniques that create these illusions could be used to bypass visual filters.
