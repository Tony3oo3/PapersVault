## Problem

Learning macroscopic dynamics (e.g., temperature, free energy) from microscopic simulations requires computing forces on all microscopic coordinates, which is computationally prohibitive for large systems (tens of thousands of atoms or grid points). Existing closure-modeling and dynamics-identification methods all assume access to full force vectors, leaving open the question of whether accurate macroscopic dynamics can be learned when only a small random subset of microscopic forces is available.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241552/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes microscopic configurations x ∈ R^N (particle positions and velocities for molecular dynamics, or PDE grid values) sampled from a trajectory distribution, together with forces computed only on a random subset of n·p coordinates (where p ≪ 1, e.g., 1/1024 for a 51,200-atom system). It produces a low-dimensional latent dynamics model g_θ(z) that predicts the time evolution of user-specified macroscopic observables (e.g., instantaneous temperature, spatial-average population, free energy) in a latent space of dimension d ≪ N. The sparsity assumption ensures each individual force depends on only O(M) neighbors, so the cost of computing the partial subset is independent of total system size.

## Method

The approach has two stages: (1) an autoencoder learns a closure mapping φ(x) = (z*, ẑ) with a condition-number penalty on φ'(x)φ'(x)^T to keep the Jacobian well-conditioned, and (2) a neural network g_θ(z) is trained to approximate the latent dynamics. The key technical contribution is a new loss L_{x,p} that maps the standard macroscopic-space loss L_z = ||φ'(x)f(x) − g_θ(z)||² back to microscopic space via the Moore-Penrose inverse: L_x = ||f(x) − (φ'(x))^† g_θ(z)||², then replaces the full force vector with an unbiased stochastic estimate using only the partial forces on the sampled subset, scaled by 1/p. Theorem 1 proves that minimizing L_x is equivalent to minimizing L_z up to multiplicative constants given by the eigenvalue bounds of φ'(x)φ'(x)^T (justifying the condition-number constraint), and Theorem 2 (via Rademacher complexity) establishes that the minimizer of L_{x,p} converges almost surely to the optimal expected risk as the number of configurations grows. This allows training with forces on as few as 50 atoms out of 51,200 while maintaining accuracy comparable to full-force training.

## Results

The paper reports mean relative error on test trajectories as the primary metric. Across all four systems, the proposed partial-force loss L_{x,p} consistently outperforms the full-force loss L_z: on the Predator-Prey system (N=100, p=1/5), L_{x,p} achieves 1.34×10⁻³ vs. 3.19×10⁻³ for L_z; on the Allen-Cahn system (N=40,000, p=1/25), 3.98×10⁻³ vs. 6.93×10⁻³; and on the small Lennard-Jones system (N=4,800, p=1/16), 1.17×10⁻³ vs. 4.45×10⁻³. Notably, on the large Lennard-Jones system (N=307,200, p=1/1024), L_{x,p} achieves 4.96×10⁻³ using only 50 atoms' forces per configuration, while L_z is infeasible. The method also demonstrates that smaller p values (e.g., 1/5) require fewer training data to reach a target error tolerance of 3×10⁻³ compared to larger p or full forces.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against external methods; instead, it compares its proposed partial-force loss L_{x,p} against the standard full-force loss L_z (which requires forces on all microscopic coordinates) as the primary baseline. L_{x,p} wins on every system tested: it reduces test error by roughly 2–4× on the Predator-Prey, Allen-Cahn, and small Lennard-Jones systems. On the large Lennard-Jones system (51,200 atoms), L_z is entirely infeasible because computing forces on all 307,200 coordinates is prohibitively expensive, while L_{x,p} still achieves ~5×10⁻³ error using only 50 atoms' forces per configuration. The paper further shows that among different p values, smaller p (1/5, 1/4) actually requires fewer total force computations to reach the same error tolerance as larger p or full forces, suggesting that partial forces provide more diverse configuration coverage per unit of compute. The method is also validated across three latent model architectures (MLP, OnsagerNet, GFINNs), with L_{x,p} outperforming L_z in all cases.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the closure modeling pipeline established by Fu et al. (2023) and Chen et al. (2024), which combine autoencoder-based dimension reduction with neural network dynamics identification in latent space. The key departure is in the training procedure: rather than requiring forces on all microscopic coordinates (as in prior closure modeling and reduced-order modeling work), the authors introduce a loss L_{x,p} that maps the macroscopic loss back to microscopic coordinates and uses partial forces as an unbiased stochastic estimator. This is conceptually distinct from the equation-free framework, which relies on local macroscopic-microscopic coupling, and from partial-observation learning, which reconstructs unobserved states rather than unobserved forces.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The method addresses a fundamental computational bottleneck: force evaluation on all microscopic coordinates scales poorly (e.g., DFT is limited to a few thousand atoms), yet macroscopic observables like temperature or free energy depend on the full system. By requiring forces on only a fixed subset (e.g., 50 atoms out of 51,200), the approach enables macroscopic dynamics learning at scales previously infeasible. Key limitations include the sparsity assumption (ruling out mean-field systems like McKean-Vlasov), the need for a trajectory distribution that may be impractical for very large systems, and the deterministic dynamics assumption. Open directions include combining with active learning for configuration selection, extending to stochastic systems, and adopting permutation-invariant encoders for particle systems.
