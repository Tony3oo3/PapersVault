## Problem

Classical learning rate tuners (linesearch, quadratically greedy rules) were designed for full-batch convex optimization yet empirically underperform constant learning rates on deep learning benchmarks. The paper identifies the missing piece: these tuners disrupt the edge-of-stability (EOS) feedback mechanism that naturally stabilizes Hessian sharpness during training, creating a snowball effect of ever-increasing sharpness and ever-decreasing learning rates. The central question is how to design tuners that exploit curvature stabilization rather than greedily minimizing the objective at each step.

## Input / Output

CDAT consumes the current model parameters w_t, the gradient ∇f(w_t), the update direction u_t from any base optimizer (GD, RMSProp, Adam, etc.), and the second directional derivative u_t^T ∇²f(w_t) u_t computed via double forward-mode autodiff (costing ~3× the objective evaluation in both time and memory). It outputs a scalar learning rate η_t = σ·n_t/d_t where n_t = max{-∇f(w_t)^T u_t, 0} and d_t = |u_t^T ∇²f(w_t) u_t| + ε. The hyperparameter σ interpolates between greedy (σ=1) and on-edge (σ=2) behavior, and optional exponential moving averages can smooth n_t and d_t for stochastic or adaptive-optimizer settings.

## Method

CDAT generalizes the quadratically greedy rule by replacing the factor 1 in the numerator with a tunable scaling σ, so that σ=2 selects the largest stepsize for which the quadratic approximation of the loss does not exceed the current loss (the 'on-edge' condition). The key insight is that targeting the EOS (η·λ_max ≈ 2) preserves the nonlinear negative feedback between the largest Hessian eigenvalue and its eigendirection projection, preventing the progressive sharpening that causes classical tuners to stall. The paper extends the Damian et al. (2023) two-variable model (x_t, λ_t) with a dynamic learning rate variable y_t = η_t λ_t − 2, showing analytically that undershooting the EOS (y < 0) eliminates the stabilizing feedback and drives runaway sharpening, while CDAT at σ=2 maintains y near zero. In the mini-batch regime, stochasticity attenuates progressive sharpening and shifts the optimal σ below 2 proportionally to batch size, explaining why linesearches appear effective at moderate batch sizes despite failing in the full-batch setting.

## Results

In the full batch regime, classical learning rate tuners (linesearch and quadratically greedy) qualitatively underperform fine-tuned constant learning rates across ResNet34/CIFAR-10, MLP Mixer/CIFAR-10, NanoLM/Tiny Shakespeare, and ViT/ImageNet, with learning rates decreasing by 3+ orders of magnitude while sharpness keeps increasing. CDAT with σ=2 (on-edge) outperforms both the quadratically greedy rule (σ=1) and fine-tuned constant learning rates in all full batch settings, and targeting slightly above the edge (σ=2.0625) provides further gains on most examples. In the stochastic regime, CDAT's benefits are subdued: the optimal scaling factor σ decreases with batch size (e.g., σ≈1.8 at batch 256 vs. σ≈2 at full batch), and EMA smoothing of the curvature estimates is essential for stable performance.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the Armijo linesearch and the quadratically greedy rule (Schaul et al.), CDAT (σ=2) clearly wins in the full batch regime: both classical tuners undershoot the edge of stability, triggering a snowball of decreasing learning rates and increasing sharpness that stalls training, whereas CDAT maintains the optimizer at or slightly above the EOS and naturally induces a warm-up-like learning rate increase. Against Polyak stepsizes, CDAT σ=2 loses in the full batch regime, but CDAT with a slightly aggressive scale (σ=2.06) partially recovers the gap, suggesting Polyak's larger steps are captured by a modest shift in the on-edge scaling. Against hyper-gradient descent, CDAT wins: hyper-gradient descent performs only on par with constant learning rates and exhibits progressive sharpening similar to the linesearch. Against prefixed warm-up schedules (linear/cosine decay), CDAT is competitive but does not fully match the best tuned schedules in the full batch regime (Fig. 19), indicating that the feedback loop exploited by CDAT may miss additional nonlinear effects. In the stochastic regime, the linesearch and quadratically greedy rule can perform on par with or better than constant learning rates at medium batch sizes, a success the paper attributes to attenuated progressive sharpening and larger effective learning rates from mini-batch noise rather than to the original convex-optimization rationale.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the edge-of-stability (EOS) framework of Cohen et al. (2021) and the self-stabilization model of Damian et al. (2023) by introducing a dynamic learning rate variable into the curvature dynamics analysis, revealing a closed-loop feedback mechanism that explains why classical tuners fail. The proposed CDAT rule is a direct generalization of the quadratically greedy stepsize of Schaul et al. (2013), differing only by a factor of 2 in the numerator, but reinterpreting the objective from minimizing instantaneous loss to targeting the EOS. The paper departs from the classical optimization tradition (Armijo linesearch, Polyak stepsizes) by arguing that their core design principle—greedy loss decrease—is actively harmful in deep learning because it suppresses the nonlinear stabilization that makes constant-learning-rate training effective.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that theoretically sound classical tuners (linesearch, quadratically greedy) systematically underperform constant learning rates in the full-batch regime challenges a foundational assumption in adaptive optimization and provides a concrete diagnostic (undershooting the EOS) for understanding their failure. CDAT's ability to naturally induce warm-up-like behavior suggests a path toward schedule-free training, though its benefits are subdued in the stochastic regime where most practical training occurs. Key open questions include developing more complete models of curvature dynamics that capture higher-order stabilization, understanding the correct Hessian statistic to track in the stochastic regime, and whether control-theoretic feedback designs can yield tuners that match or exceed prefixed schedules.
