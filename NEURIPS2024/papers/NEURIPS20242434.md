## Problem

Existing semantic importance methods (concept activation vectors, concept bottleneck models, gradient-based explanations) identify which concepts matter for a model's predictions but lack rigorous statistical guarantees such as false positive rate or false discovery rate control, making it impossible to distinguish genuinely important concepts from noise. Moreover, methods like post-hoc concept bottleneck models explain a trained surrogate linear classifier rather than the original nonlinear predictor, and none provide a principled, adaptive ranking of concepts by importance. This paper formalizes semantic importance at three levels of conditional independence and introduces valid sequential tests that address both gaps simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The method takes a fixed black-box model (an encoder f mapping images to d-dimensional embeddings and a classifier g producing per-class scores), a small user-specified set of m semantic concepts (typically m ≈ 20, represented as unit vectors in the embedding space via a vision-language model's text encoder), and either a dataset of images (for global tests) or a single image with its embedding (for local tests). It outputs, for each concept, a rejection time (how many sequential steps until the null of independence is rejected), a binary importance decision at a user-chosen significance level α with FDR control across all m concepts, and a natural importance rank induced by the ordering of rejection times. No surrogate model is trained; the original classifier g is probed directly.

## Method

The paper defines three null hypotheses of conditional independence between model predictions Ŷ and concept projections Z_j (marginal, global conditional, and local conditional), then tests each using sequential kernelized independence testing (SKIT) with testing-by-betting. The test statistic is the plug-in estimate of the MMD witness function between the joint and null distributions, and the payoff at each step is κ_t = tanh(ρ_t(D) − ρ_t(D̃)), which is provably a fair game under the null. The betting fraction v_t is chosen via the Online Newton Step strategy, making the wealth process adaptive to problem hardness—harder nulls take longer to reject, naturally inducing an importance rank. For conditional tests (C-SKIT, X-SKIT), the null distribution is constructed by sampling from P_{Z_j|Z_{-j}} or P_{H|Z_C=z_C} using nonparametric estimators, avoiding the need for parametric generative models. A greedy FDR post-processor (Algorithm A.2) adjusts rejection thresholds across all m concepts to guarantee false discovery rate control at level α.

## Results

On AwA2, SKIT achieves the best importance F1 score (0.65) and rank agreement across models (0.50), while C-SKIT achieves 99.50% accuracy on the original model. On Imagenette, C-SKIT retains 98.99% accuracy of the original classifier (vs. 95.85% for PCBM) and achieves rank agreement of 0.54, with X-SKIT reaching 0.59. On CUB, X-SKIT achieves a rank agreement of 0.82 across models and an F1 score of 0.93 against ground-truth per-image semantic annotations, validating local conditional importance detection.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The sole external baseline is Post-hoc Concept Bottleneck Models (PCBM) [Yuksekgonul et al., 2022], which trains a sparse linear layer on top of concept projections. On AwA2, SKIT outperforms PCBM on both importance F1 (0.65 vs. 0.53) and cross-model rank agreement (0.50 vs. 0.36), and C-SKIT also beats PCBM on F1 (0.57 vs. 0.53) and rank agreement (0.46 vs. 0.36). On Imagenette, C-SKIT preserves 98.99% of the original model's accuracy while PCBM degrades to 95.85%, and C-SKIT achieves higher rank agreement (0.54 vs. 0.45). X-SKIT is the first method to provide local semantic explanations with statistical guarantees, so no direct baseline comparison is possible on CUB; however, its 0.93 F1 against ground-truth annotations demonstrates strong alignment. A key qualitative distinction is that PCBM explains a surrogate linear model rather than the original nonlinear predictor, whereas SKIT/C-SKIT/X-SKIT provide Type I error and FDR control directly on the deployed model.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of two research threads: (1) sequential kernelized independence testing (SKIT) and testing-by-betting frameworks (Podkopaev et al., Shekhar & Ramdas, Shafer), which provide valid, adaptive hypothesis tests via wealth processes and e-values, and (2) semantic concept-based interpretability (T-CAV, CBMs, PCBMs, XRT), which identifies interpretable features for model predictions. The key departure is that prior semantic importance methods either train surrogate linear models (PCBM, CBM) or use gradient heuristics (T-CAV) without statistical guarantees, while prior conditional independence tests (CRT, XRT) operate in raw input space rather than concept space. This work bridges both by formalizing semantic importance as conditional independence hypotheses and testing them with sequential kernelized procedures (C-SKIT, X-SKIT), coupled with an FDR post-processor from Wang & Ramdas.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first framework to provide Type I error and FDR control for semantic concept importance, enabling transparent communication of uncertainty in explanations—a critical gap for high-stakes deployments (e.g., medical imaging). The sequential testing approach naturally induces an importance ranking without arbitrary thresholds, and the method operates directly on the original (potentially nonlinear) model rather than a surrogate. Key limitations include the need for samplers from conditional distributions (P_{Z_j|Z_{-j}}, P_{H|Z_C=z_C}), reliance on characteristic kernels (RBF assumed), and no guarantee that user-specified concepts are well-represented in the model's feature space. Open questions include statistical guarantees on rejection times, extension to non-Euclidean data modalities, and whether the transferability of importance ranks across VL models observed empirically holds more broadly.
