## Problem

Despite the empirical success of multi-modal contrastive learning (e.g., CLIP) over single-modal approaches, the fundamental mechanism behind this advantage has remained theoretically unexplored. Prior work analyzed single-modal and multi-modal contrastive learning in isolation, but no unified framework existed to compare their optimization trajectories and downstream generalization under the same data model and training procedure.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242591/cropped_page8_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The data model generates each sample as a concatenation of a task-relevant signal vector (yμ, where y is Rademacher) and a task-irrelevant Gaussian noise vector (ξ ~ N(0, σ²I)), with the two modalities sharing the same label but having independent noise. For single-modal learning, the positive pair is formed by augmenting the noise with additional Gaussian corruption while preserving the signal; for multi-modal learning, the positive pair is the corresponding sample from the second modality. The output is a learned embedding from a single-layer ReLU network, evaluated by a linear probe on an OOD test distribution where the test signal ν is only weakly aligned with the training signal μ.

## Method

The paper analyzes a single-layer ReLU network trained with the InfoMax contrastive loss (with stop-gradient) under gradient descent, decomposing each weight vector into a signal-learning coefficient γ and per-sample noise-memorization coefficients ρ. A two-stage trajectory analysis reveals that in single-modal learning, noise memorization grows to Ω(1) while signal learning remains at Õ(1/√n), yielding non-vanishing OOD test error; in multi-modal learning, the cross-modality contrastive objective couples the two networks so that the higher-SNR second modality (‖μ̃‖ = C_μ‖μ‖ with C_μ ≥ 2.66) amplifies signal learning to Ω(1) while suppressing noise memorization to Õ(1/√n), yielding vanishing test error. The critical distinguishing factor is the signal-to-noise ratio: augmentation in single-modal learning preserves the original SNR and thus cannot improve feature quality, whereas the second modality in multi-modal learning provides an independent, higher-quality signal channel that cooperatively boosts the target modality's feature learning.

## Results

On ColoredMNIST under distribution shift (reversed color-label correlations), multi-modal contrastive learning achieves 82.13% OOD test accuracy versus only 12.68% for single-modal (SimCLR-style) contrastive learning, while both attain comparable training accuracy (~88%). In synthetic experiments, multi-modal learning converges to nearly 1.0 OOD test accuracy while single-modal stagnates around 0.5 (chance level). Theoretically, the paper proves that single-modal contrastive learning yields a non-vanishing downstream test error of Θ(1), whereas multi-modal contrastive learning achieves a vanishing test error of o(1), with the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) identified as the critical factor driving this gap.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper's central comparison is between single-modal contrastive learning (implemented via SimCLR-style augmentations on image data alone) and multi-modal contrastive learning (jointly encoding image and text/caption modalities). Multi-modal learning decisively outperforms single-modal on OOD test accuracy by a margin of ~70 percentage points (82.13% vs. 12.68%) on ColoredMNIST, while training accuracy is essentially matched (87.77% vs. 88.43%). The theoretical analysis attributes this gap to the cooperation between modalities: the higher-SNR second modality (text) boosts signal learning in the target modality (image), whereas single-modal learning, lacking this cross-modal signal, predominantly memorizes noise and fails to generalize. In the synthetic setting, the gap is even more stark, with multi-modal reaching near-perfect OOD accuracy while single-modal remains at chance level, confirming that data augmentation alone cannot substitute for the quality boost provided by a second, higher-SNR modality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the feature learning analysis of Wen & Li (ICML 2021) [56] for single-modal contrastive learning into a unified framework that simultaneously covers multi-modal contrastive learning. While prior theoretical work has studied single-modal [56, 50, 49] and multi-modal [38, 44, 12] contrastive learning separately, no prior work provided a side-by-side trajectory-based optimization comparison under a shared data model. The key departure from [56] is the removal of the adjustable bias term in the activation function and the introduction of a second modality whose signal quality (controlled by C_μ) drives the generalization gap.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first systematic theoretical comparison showing *why* multi-modal contrastive learning generalizes better: the second modality's higher SNR cooperatively boosts signal learning while suppressing noise memorization, whereas single-modal augmentation preserves the same SNR and thus the network memorizes noise, yielding Θ(1) downstream error. The analysis is limited to a linear signal-noise data model and single-layer ReLU networks, so extending to non-linear data, deeper architectures, and realistic multi-view augmentations remains open. The practical implication—that data quality (SNR) of the auxiliary modality is the critical lever—aligns with empirical findings on CLIP data curation but now has a formal optimization-theoretic grounding.
