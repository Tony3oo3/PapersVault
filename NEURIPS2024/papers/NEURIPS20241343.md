## Problem

Existing causal representation learning methods require perfect single-node interventions on every latent variable and at least d environments to recover the full latent SCM. This paper asks whether one can identify *which* latent nodes have shifted their causal mechanisms under far more general interventions (edge additions/removals, reversals, multi-node changes) and with fewer than d environments, without recovering the full SCM or the mixing function.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241343/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes K ≥ 2 datasets of observed variables X^(k) ∈ R^p, each generated as a linear mixing of d latent factors under a potentially different linear SCM, plus a detection threshold α. The number of latent factors d is estimated from the rank of the sample covariance matrix rather than specified a priori. The output is a collection of sets S^(k,k') for each environment pair, listing the indices of latent nodes whose causal mechanisms differ between those two environments. Notably, no environment needs to be designated as 'observational'—any can serve as reference.

## Method

The key insight is that each row of the unmixing matrix M^(k) = B^(k)H encodes the causal mechanism of one latent node, so a node is shifted iff its row changes (up to sign) across environments. The algorithm applies ICA to each environment to obtain the unmixing matrix up to permutation and sign, then resolves the permutation ambiguity by sorting noise components via a test function ψ (e.g., P(|y|≤1)), leaving only sign ambiguity. A node Z_i is certified non-shifted between environments k and k' if and only if the sorted rows satisfy M̃_i^(k) = ±M̃_i^(k'); a lemma shows the negative-sign case is impossible due to the positive diagonal of B, so equality suffices. In the finite-sample setting, a test statistic L_i^{k,k'} based on the L1 norm of element-wise absolute differences between rows is compared against α to flag shifted nodes, yielding a consistent detector as n → ∞.

## Results

On synthetic data (n=10⁶), the method achieves perfect F1 scores of 1.000 for d≤10 across all graph types (ER2, ER4, SF2, SF4), with performance degrading to F1≈0.53–0.69 at d=40 due to increased estimation difficulty. On the OpenPsychometrics Five Factor dataset, the method correctly identifies Neuroticism and Extraversion as shifted across gender (L-scores 0.638 and 0.633, exceeding the α=0.5 threshold), while detecting no shifts between US and UK (all L-scores ≤0.302), consistent with existing psychological literature. In the fully observed setting (G=I), the method outperforms the DCI baseline in most configurations, achieving F1=1.000 where DCI achieves 0.60–0.92.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares against DCI (Wang et al., NeurIPS 2018), the only existing method for direct mechanism shift detection in linear SEMs, in the fully observed setting where G=I. iLCS outperforms DCI in 11 out of 14 configurations, with the largest margins on small graphs: F1 of 1.000 vs. 0.73 (SF2, d=5) and 1.000 vs. 0.60 (ER2, d=5). DCI achieves a higher F1 in one case (ER4, d=10: 0.84 vs. 0.78), and the two methods tie on SF4 at d=5 (both F1=0.78). Notably, iLCS achieves perfect F1=1.000 in four configurations where DCI falls short (ER2 d=10, SF2 d=5, SF2 d=10, ER4 d=15), while DCI achieves perfect scores in only one (ER4 d=15). The comparison is limited to the fully observed setting since DCI does not handle latent variables, which is the primary setting of this paper.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of linear causal representation learning (CRL) and direct mechanism shift estimation. It builds on the ICA-based identifiability framework of Seigal et al. (2022), which required d perfect single-node interventions to recover the full latent SCM, and on the direct mechanism shift literature (Wang et al. 2018, Ghoshal et al. 2019) which operates only on observable variables. The key departure is twofold: it relaxes the intervention requirements from d perfect single-node interventions to fewer, arbitrarily general interventions (soft/hard, multi-node, edge additions/removals/reversals), and it relaxes the recovery target from the full SCM to merely the set of shifted nodes, bypassing estimation of both the mixing matrix G and the SCM B.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The requirement of d perfect single-node interventions is a major practical bottleneck in CRL, and this paper shows that identifying shifted nodes—a task directly relevant to root cause analysis and domain adaptation—does not require full SCM recovery. The constructive identifiability result (row-wise comparison of the unscrambled unmixing matrix) provides a clean, checkable criterion. Limitations include the restriction to linear mixing and specific noise assumptions (non-Gaussian, pairwise distinct), and the need for a test function ψ for consistent ordering. Extending to nonlinear mixing and relaxing noise assumptions are identified as key future directions.
