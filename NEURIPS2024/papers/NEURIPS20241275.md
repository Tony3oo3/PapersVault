## Problem

While primate visual systems transform complex pixel dynamics into straighter temporal trajectories that enable prediction via linear extrapolation, the utility of straightening as a primary self-supervised learning objective for artificial networks remains unexplored. Most existing SSL methods optimize for invariance to augmentations, which necessarily discards temporally-varying features such as object location, size, and orientation, limiting both predictive capability and robustness.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241275/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes temporally structured image sequences of at least three frames with smooth geometric transformations (translation, rescaling, rotation) and linear photometric shifts (brightness, contrast, saturation, hue) applied across frames. For MNIST experiments, inputs are 64×64 patches over 20 frames; for CIFAR-10, 32×32 images over 3 frames; for EgoGesture, single-channel depth frames (6 frames per clip). The output is a fixed-dimensional neural embedding per frame that supports one-step prediction via linear extrapolation (z_{t+2} = 2z_{t+1} − z_t), and on which linear probes can decode object identity, location, size, and orientation.

## Method

The core contribution is a parameter-free straightness loss that maximizes the average cosine similarity between successive difference vectors of three temporally adjacent embedding points, combined with VICReg-style variance and covariance regularization to prevent representational collapse. The architecture is a standard feedforward CNN (7-layer convnet for MNIST, ResNet-18 with a 3-layer projector for CIFAR-10), with the straightness loss applied at the output layer and optionally at an earlier block. Unlike invariance-based objectives that collapse all frames of a sequence to a single point, straightening preserves all structured temporal variation while making trajectories linear, which geometrically yields parallel within-class trajectories and orthogonal across-class trajectories, enabling class separation via low-dimensional subspaces. The straightening loss can also be added as a lightweight regularizer to existing SSL objectives (Barlow Twins, SimCLR, W-MSE, DINO), systematically improving adversarial and noise robustness without altering the original training setup.

## Results

On sequential CIFAR-10 with ResNet-18, the straightening objective produces representations that are substantially more robust to both Gaussian noise (Fig. 4D) and PGD adversarial attacks (Fig. 4E) than the invariance (VICReg) objective, with negligible degradation in clean-image recognition accuracy. On the CIFAR-C corruption benchmark (highest severity), straightening outperforms invariance on most corruption types, particularly in the noise category, while invariance only wins on corruptions (brightness, saturation, contrast, fog) that were explicitly included as training augmentations. When used as a regularizer, straightening systematically improves adversarial robustness for all four tested SSL objectives (Barlow Twins, SimCLR, W-MSE, DINO) without degrading their clean performance (Fig. 5B).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against VICReg (invariance, [3]), straightening wins decisively on robustness: it maintains higher classification accuracy across all Gaussian noise levels (Fig. 4D) and all PGD attack budgets (Fig. 4E), and outperforms on most CIFAR-C corruptions (Fig. 4F). VICReg only surpasses straightening on corruptions that were part of its training augmentations (brightness, saturation, contrast, fog). Against the four SSL methods used in the regularizer experiments (Barlow Twins [35], SimCLR [7], W-MSE [14], DINO [6]), none of the original objectives produce straightened representations (Fig. 5A, gray), but adding the straightening loss as a regularizer improves both straightness and adversarial robustness for every method tested (Fig. 5B, blue), even though each was already heavily tuned to its optimal hyperparameters. The paper does not report a single headline accuracy number; instead, the key finding is a consistent robustness advantage (noise + adversarial) across all comparisons, with straightening also preserving dynamic features (location, size, orientation) that invariance-based methods discard by design.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper operationalizes a biological finding from Henaff et al. (2019, 2021) that primate visual cortex straightens temporal trajectories of natural video, turning it into a parameter-free SSL loss (cosine similarity of consecutive difference vectors) combined with VICReg-style whitening regularization. It departs from invariance-based SSL (SimCLR, DINO, Barlow Twins, W-MSE) by preserving rather than discarding temporal structure, and from prior linear-prediction approaches (Oord et al. 2018, Henaff 2020) by being parameter-free and context-adaptive. Crucially, it provides the converse of a recent result (Toosi & Issa 2023; Harrington et al. 2022) showing that robustified networks produce straightened representations: here, explicitly training for straightness yields robustness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The bidirectional straightness–robustness relationship offers a computationally cheap alternative to adversarial training: adding a simple straightening regularizer to any SSL objective that operates on ≥3-frame sequences improves adversarial and noise robustness across Barlow Twins, SimCLR, W-MSE, and DINO without degrading clean accuracy. Key limitations include reliance on synthetic temporally-augmented sequences rather than natural video, a single prediction horizon, and evaluation primarily on CIFAR-10-scale data. Open questions include whether hierarchical multi-timescale straightening can capture the full temporal structure of natural video, and whether the robustness gains persist at ImageNet scale or with transformer backbones.
