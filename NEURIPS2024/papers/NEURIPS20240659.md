## Problem

Existing human-chess-move prediction models (Maia) train completely independent architectures for each skill level, producing volatile and incoherent predictions across the skill spectrum—e.g., a player might be predicted to play well at one rating, make a blunder at the next, then play well again. This incoherence makes them poor teaching tools and AI partners, since real human improvement is smooth and gradual. The paper asks whether a single unified model can coherently capture human decision-making across the full range of chess skill.

## Input / Output

The model takes a single current chess position (18-channel 8×8 tensor, no multi-board history), plus categorical skill-level embeddings for both the active player and the opponent. It produces three outputs: a move probability distribution over all legal moves (policy head), a set of auxiliary binary labels describing the move's properties (piece moved, piece captured, origin/destination square, check delivery), and a continuous game-outcome estimate (value head). The Markovian single-position input is a deliberate departure from prior work that used six-position histories, enabling use in puzzle and training contexts where no game history exists.

## Method

Maia-2 encodes the chess position with a 12-block ResNet backbone, then applies channel-wise patching (flattening each of 8 feature-map channels into a 64-dim vector, treating channels as the sequence length rather than spatial patches). The core novelty is a skill-aware multi-head self-attention module where concatenated active/opponent skill embeddings are injected additively into the query vectors, allowing attention to adaptively reweight position features based on the players' skill levels. Two sequential skill-aware attention blocks progressively fuse skill and position before feeding into the three prediction heads. Training is fully supervised with balanced cross-entropy (policy), binary cross-entropy (auxiliary), and regression (value) losses, using a data-balancing strategy that oversamples cross-skill matchups. The entire model has 23.3M parameters, compared to Maia-1's 92M across nine independent models, yet achieves ~2 percentage points higher move-prediction accuracy and dramatically improved coherence (27% monotonic positions vs. 1%).

## Results

On the Maia Testset, Maia-2 achieves 53.25% average top-1 move prediction accuracy, surpassing the best Maia-1 model (Maia 1500 at 51.39%) by nearly 2 full percentage points and Leela (44.53%) by ~9 points. Maia-2 also reduces average move prediction perplexity from a previous record of 4.67 bits (Maia 1900) to 4.07 bits. On coherence, Maia-2 treats 27% of positions monotonically (probability of correct move increases with skill) versus only 1% for Maia-1, and 22% transitionally versus 17% for Maia-1.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Maia-2 outperforms all three baseline families across every skill group. Against Stockfish (best depth 15, 40.43% avg), Maia-2 gains ~13 percentage points, confirming that traditional engines optimized for optimal play are poor predictors of human moves. Against Leela (best config 1500, 44.53% avg), the gap is ~9 points, again highlighting the need for human-behavior-specific modeling. Against the original Maia suite (best single model Maia 1500 at 51.39% avg), Maia-2 gains ~1.9 points on average, with the largest margin on Advanced players (+1.54 pp over Maia 1500). Notably, Maia-2 achieves this with only 23.3M parameters versus Maia-1's 92M (9 independent 10.3M models), and without requiring 6-board history as input. On the Cross-skill Testset, Maia-2 consistently outperforms both Maia 1100 and Maia 1900 across nearly all active/opponent skill combinations, demonstrating that its unified architecture generalizes better to unseen skill pairings than the independent per-level models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on Maia (McIlroy-Young et al., KDD 2020), which trained nine independent models—each targeting a specific skill level—to predict human chess moves from Lichess data. The key departure is replacing these separate models with a single unified architecture that introduces skill-aware attention with channel-wise patching to dynamically fuse player skill encodings with ResNet-encoded board positions. The position encoding backbone follows the AlphaZero/Leela ResNet design, and the concept-probing analysis draws on McGrath et al.'s linear probe methodology for AlphaZero. Unlike prior work, Maia-2 also explicitly models opponent skill level and uses only the current position rather than a six-board history, trading a small amount of historical context for training efficiency and deployment flexibility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The coherence results—27% of positions treated monotonically versus 1% for Maia—provide the first quantitative evidence that a single neural model can capture the smooth, progressive nature of human skill development in chess, which is essential for the model to serve as a credible teaching aid or relatable AI partner. The linear probe analysis further validates that the model learns skill-dependent concepts (board evaluation) that vary with skill while keeping skill-independent concepts (basic rules) constant, aligning with human cognitive theory. However, the paper explicitly leaves the development of actual teaching tools and AI partners out of scope, and the strongest players are grouped into a single bucket, leaving the upper tail of skill unmodeled. Open questions include whether the skill-aware attention mechanism generalizes to other domains with continuous skill spectra, and whether incorporating search (as in prior KL-regularized work) would further improve both accuracy and coherence.
