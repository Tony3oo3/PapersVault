## Problem

The Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure is the standard tool for controlling the false discovery rate in large-scale multiple testing, yet its adversarial robustness—resistance to strategic perturbation of a small number of p-values before the procedure is executed—has never been formally studied. This paper asks: under what conditions can an omniscient adversary break BH's FDR guarantee by perturbing as few as one p-value, and how large is the resulting FDR inflation?

## Input / Output

The adversary receives a realized collection of N p-values (with known null/alternative labels), a control level q ∈ (0,1), and a corruption budget c ≥ 1, and must produce a perturbed collection p′ with ‖p − p′‖₀ ≤ c that maximizes the false detection proportion FDP[BH_q; p′]. The output is the perturbed p-value vector, the resulting BH rejection decisions, and the adversarial FDR adjustment Δ_c = E[c/k̃_{+c}; B_{N+1}^0 ≥ c], which quantifies the expected increase in FDR over the uncorrupted case. The adversary is assumed omniscient (knows H₀, H₁, and q), though modifications for an oblivious adversary are discussed.

## Method

The paper reframes BH as a 'balls into bins' process: p-values are assigned to N+1 ordered bins, and BH's rejection count k̃ becomes a stopping time identifying the largest prefix of bins containing exactly i balls. The INCREASE-c algorithm exploits this structure by moving c null p-values from the top bin (N+1) into bin k̃_{+c}, expanding the rejection region and injecting false positives. For c=1, a provably optimal algorithm MOVE-1 is derived by characterizing the feasible set of adjusted rejection counts via two combinatorial sets L (increases) and R (decreases). The theoretical analysis combines a martingale/optional-stopping argument (Theorem 3.1) with the generalized ballot theorem of Takács to obtain exact probabilities under uniform alternatives, and then uses Pinsker's inequality on KL divergences between bin-assignment distributions to extend bounds to general sub-uniform alternatives (Theorem 4.5), yielding non-asymptotic lower bounds on Δ_c as a function of q, N, N₀, and the alternative distribution.

## Results

On synthetic data (N=1000, q=0.10, π₀=0.90), INCREASE-c with μ₁=0 (alternatives indistinguishable from nulls) drives post-corruption FDR to 0.99 even with a single perturbation (c=1), versus the nominal 0.09 control level. As signal strength increases to μ₁=2, the same c=1 budget yields only 0.14 FDR, requiring c=500 to reach 0.89. On the real Credit Card Fraud Detection dataset (284,807 transactions), INCREASE-10 pushes the average FDP from 0.09 to 0.23 while expanding the rejection count from 56.39 to 72.85 alleged frauds. The simple INCREASE-1 algorithm achieves nearly identical FDR adjustment to the provably optimal MOVE-1 (e.g., 0.751 vs 0.775 at μ₁=1), though at a larger perturbation distance (‖z−z′‖₁ of 2.297 vs 0.492).

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not benchmark against competing multiple-testing procedures; instead, it demonstrates the vulnerability of BH itself by comparing pre- and post-perturbation FDR. The key internal comparison is between the simple INCREASE-c algorithm and the provably optimal MOVE-1 (for c=1): INCREASE-1 achieves 0.751 average FDP at μ₁=1 versus MOVE-1's 0.775, a gap of only 0.024, while requiring a perturbation distance roughly 4.7× larger (‖z−z′‖₁ = 2.297 vs 0.492). The paper also contrasts two regimes: when alternatives are highly sub-uniform (μ₁=2), BH is relatively robust (FDR stays at 0.14 for c=1), but when alternatives are barely distinguishable from nulls (μ₁=0), BH is catastrophically vulnerable (FDR jumps to 0.99 for c=1). The theoretical lower bound L₁ from Theorem 4.5 tracks the simulated Δ₁ closely for small q (≤0.10), confirming that stricter control levels paradoxically increase adversarial vulnerability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the adversarial robustness literature from single and sequential hypothesis testing (Jin & Lai 2021; Cao et al. 2022) to the multiple-testing setting, specifically targeting the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure's FDR control. It builds directly on the BH framework (Benjamini & Hochberg 1995) and its application to conformal p-value-based outlier detection (Bates et al. 2023), reframing BH's rejection count as a balls-into-bins process connected to the generalized ballot problem (Takács 1962). A key departure from prior work is the explicit distinction between distributional robustness (Su 2018) and adversarial robustness: the paper demonstrates that BH's well-known distributional robustness to arbitrary alternative distributions does not translate into resistance to test-time p-value perturbation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that a single p-value perturbation can push FDR from its guaranteed level to near 1.0 when alternatives are close to the null has direct implications for safety-critical applications of BH (fraud detection, OOD detection, anomaly detection) where false positives carry real costs. The paper notes that BH is one member of the broader family of step-up procedures, suggesting the vulnerability likely extends to other such methods, yet no defensive mechanisms are proposed. Open questions include: whether the omniscient adversary model is realistic in practice, whether lightweight defenses (e.g., robust stopping times, perturbation detection) can restore FDR guarantees, and whether the results extend to step-down or hybrid procedures.
