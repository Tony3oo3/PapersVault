## Problem

Existing large-scale freeway traffic datasets (e.g., PeMS) are designed for forecasting, not anomaly detection, and lack lane-level granularity, anomaly labels, and any accounting for the inherent delay between when a crash occurs and when it is officially reported. This paper introduces the first lane-level freeway dataset purpose-built for anomaly detection and defines a new evaluation metric—Reduction in Reporting Delay—that measures how much sooner a model detects an incident relative to its official report.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240496/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Input consists of 30-second radar sensor readings (speed, occupancy, volume) from 49 sensors × 4 lanes = 196 nodes along 18 miles of I-24, organized into a spatiotemporal graph where nodes at adjacent milemarkers and across a sliding time window are connected with typed relational edges. Output is a per-node anomaly flag determined by whether the squared reconstruction error exceeds a per-node threshold, plus the reconstructed node features. The evaluation is unusual in that ground-truth crash timestamps carry an unknown reporting delay (up to 30 minutes assumed), so the primary metric rewards detecting anomalies *before* the official report time rather than merely matching a binary label.

## Method

The paper benchmarks six unsupervised autoencoder architectures for node-level graph anomaly detection: MLP AE, Transformer AE, GCN AE, GCN-LSTM AE, STG-GAT AE, and STG-RGCN AE. All are trained to minimize MSE reconstruction of current-graph node features on anomaly-free data, then flag anomalies at test time when reconstruction error exceeds a threshold tuned to a target false-positive rate. The key architectural insight is that a relational spatiotemporal graph—connecting nodes across both space (adjacent milemarkers, all lanes) and time (k previous snapshots) with edge-type annotations—allows GNN message passing to capture how anomalies propagate through the network. The GCN AE (spatial only) achieved the lowest average reporting delay (−10.2 min) and highest AUC (0.70), while the STG-GAT AE achieved the best miss rate (0% at 10% FPR) and lowest reconstruction error, suggesting spatial structure is critical for reconstruction quality while temporal-relational structure helps catch harder-to-detect crashes.

## Results

On the FT-AED dataset (5-day validation, 5% FPR threshold), the GCN Autoencoder achieves the best average Reduction in Reporting Delay of −10.20 ± 5.98 minutes with the highest AUC (0.70), detecting crashes over 10 minutes before official reporting. The STG-GAT Autoencoder achieves the lowest reconstruction MSE (0.0089) and, at a 10% FPR threshold, detects all 12 validation crashes (0% miss) with a mean delay of −13.33 ± 2.56 minutes. Models without spatial information (Transformer, MLP) perform worst, with the Transformer actually detecting anomalies *after* reporting (+2.42 min) and missing 41.67% of crashes.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the six autoencoder baselines, the GCN AE (spatial only, no temporal) wins on Reporting Delay (−10.20 min) and AUC (0.70), while the STG-GAT AE wins on reconstruction quality (MSE 0.0089) and, at higher FPR tolerances (10%), achieves 0% miss rate with the lowest delay (−13.33 min). The GCN-LSTM AE is worst across all metrics, suggesting that encoding temporal dynamics in the latent space via recurrence is inferior to injecting them directly into the graph structure. The Transformer and MLP, which ignore spatial relationships, miss 41.67% of crashes and have the highest reconstruction errors, confirming that spatial graph structure is critical. The rule-based California algorithm [23] (Appendix A.8) technically detects 100% of crashes (0% miss, −15 min delay) but at a 99% false positive rate, making it impractical for real-world deployment; in contrast, the GNN autoencoders achieve comparable or better detection delays at 5–10% FPR.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground by introducing the first large-scale lane-level freeway traffic dataset (FT-AED) designed specifically for anomaly detection, departing from existing sensor-based datasets (PeMS, METR-LA, PEMS-BAY) that target traffic forecasting at 5-minute granularity without anomaly labels. Conceptually, it repurposes the well-established autoencoder reconstruction-error paradigm for anomaly detection and applies it to a spatiotemporal graph of lane-level radar sensor readings, but the core contribution is the dataset and the new 'reduction in reporting delay' metric that explicitly accounts for the practical challenge of delayed crash reporting. The benchmarking of GNN autoencoders (GCN, GAT, RGCN) follows standard unsupervised anomaly detection practice, with the novelty lying in the problem formulation and data rather than the architectures.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The 30-second lane-level granularity and explicit anomaly labels (42 crashes + 19 manually identified anomalies) address a real gap: prior datasets either lack anomaly labels or aggregate at 5-minute intervals too coarse for rapid incident response. Key open questions remain: validation is not performed at the lane level (crashes affect downstream traffic), AUC is acknowledged as potentially misleading for this task, and speed reconstructions lose wave-like dynamics that could mask subtle anomalies. The single-month, single-corridor scope limits generalizability, and the small crash count (42) constrains statistical confidence in the model rankings. Future work should extend the dataset temporally and geographically, develop lane-level validation protocols, and benchmark a broader set of anomaly detection methods beyond reconstruction-based autoencoders.
