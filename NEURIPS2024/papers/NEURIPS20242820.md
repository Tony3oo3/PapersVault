## Problem

Transformer-based temporal point process models achieve state-of-the-art flexibility but rely on the computationally intensive thinning algorithm for inference, which is slow, sensitive to the intensity function, and empirically degrades time-prediction accuracy. Previous intensity-free alternatives that avoid thinning (e.g., IFTPP) use simpler RNN architectures and underperform Transformer-based methods. The paper asks whether one can retain the modeling power of self-attention while eliminating the thinning bottleneck entirely.

## Input / Output

The model consumes a sequence of marked events (timestamp, event type) forming the history H_t, with future events masked to prevent leakage. For next-event prediction it outputs a scalar predicted time (the mean of a log-normal mixture conditioned on the previous mark) and a predicted event type (argmax over the Transformer-based CPMF evaluated at the true next time). For long-horizon prediction it autoregressively generates P events, using each predicted time as a proxy for the true time when computing the next type, and the entire procedure is fully parallelizable across a batch of sequences.

## Method

DTPP decomposes the MTPP log-likelihood into two independently optimizable components: a conditional PMF for marks parameterized by a continuous-time Transformer (same architecture as A-NHP, with sinusoidal temporal embeddings and multi-layer self-attention over the full history), and a conditional PDF for inter-event times modeled as a mixture of M log-normals whose parameters depend only on the most recently observed mark (a first-order Markov property). Because the two sub-models share no parameters, they are trained separately by maximizing their respective log-likelihood terms, avoiding the Monte Carlo integral approximation required by CIF-based methods. At inference, the predicted time is the closed-form mean of the appropriate log-normal mixture and the predicted type is the argmax of the Transformer CPMF, so no thinning or random sampling is needed. The Markov assumption on times acts as a strong regularizer that reduces overfitting, while the Transformer retains full-history modeling capacity for the mark distribution, and the decomposition guarantees that no modeling power is lost relative to a joint CIF parameterization.

## Results

On next-event prediction, DTPP achieves the best RMSE and error rate across all four real-world datasets in Table 1: 0.12 RMSE / 59.06% error on Amazon, 0.08 / 7.12% on Taxi, 0.05 / 40.12% on Taobao, and 1.07 / 50.41% on StackOverflow-V1. The largest margin is on Amazon where DTPP's RMSE of 0.12 is 3.2× lower than the best CIF-based baseline (A-NHP at 0.42) and 3.2× lower than the best CPDF-based baseline (VI-DPP at 0.38). On long-horizon prediction (P=20 events), DTPP outperforms HYPRO on average OTD across all three datasets (e.g., 15.00 vs 18.81 on Taxi) while being 8,130× faster on Taxi, 524× faster on Taobao, and 2,768× faster on StackOverflow-V2, using only 400K parameters versus HYPRO's 850K.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DTPP outperforms all six baselines on every reported metric across all datasets. Among CIF-based methods, A-NHP [41] is the strongest baseline (best RMSE on 3 of 4 datasets), yet DTPP still achieves 3.2× lower RMSE on Amazon (0.12 vs 0.42) and 1.1× lower on StackOverflow-V1 (1.07 vs 1.18). Among CPDF-based methods, VI-DPP [30] is the closest competitor on time prediction (e.g., 0.07 vs 0.05 RMSE on Taobao), but DTPP wins on error rate across all datasets. IFTPP [35] is consistently outperformed by DTPP on both metrics, confirming the benefit of the Transformer architecture over the RNN used in IFTPP. For long-horizon prediction, DTPP beats HYPRO [40] on average OTD across all three datasets (e.g., 15.00 vs 18.81 on Taxi with 32 proposals) and on RMSE* for Taxi and Taobao, while being 524–8,130× faster in inference time. The paper also notes that DTPP is the only method significantly outperformed by A-NHP on the 1-D Hawkes synthetic datasets, where the log-normal mixture is a misspecified model for the univariate intensity.

## Relation to Prior Work

DTPP sits at the intersection of two lines of temporal point process research: Transformer-based CIF models (THP, SAHP, A-NHP) that capture complex history dependencies but require the computationally expensive thinning algorithm for inference, and intensity-free CPDF-based models (IFTPP, VI-DPP) that decompose the likelihood into separate time and mark distributions but use simpler architectures (RNN or parametric). The paper combines the self-attention architecture of A-NHP with the decomposable likelihood framework of VI-DPP and the log-normal mixture for inter-event times from IFTPP, while introducing a Markov property (dependence only on the most recent mark) for the time distribution. This is a deliberate departure from CIF-based modeling, motivated by the desire to eliminate thinning at inference time while retaining the expressive power of attention over full event history for the mark distribution.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The most important contribution is the empirical demonstration that the thinning algorithm does not merely slow down inference but actively degrades predictive accuracy of neural point processes, a finding consistent across five real-world datasets and multiple CIF-based baselines. The 8,130× speedup in long-horizon prediction (Taxi) makes previously impractical inference feasible, and the Markov property on inter-event times acts as an effective regularizer that reduces overfitting. Key limitations include the data-hungry Transformer component (unsuitable for data-scarce regimes) and the fact that the CIF-vs-CPDF comparison is somewhat confounded by architectural differences. Open questions raised include whether CIF-based methods can be modified to match CPDF-based accuracy, and whether globally normalized models (analogous to HYPRO) can be developed for the CPDF framework.
