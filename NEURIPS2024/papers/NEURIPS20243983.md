## Problem

Existing jailbreak benchmarks systematically overstate attack effectiveness because their forbidden prompts are often vague, unanswerable, or not actually forbidden, and their evaluators define success merely as non-refusal rather than measuring whether the response provides genuinely useful harmful information. This paper identifies a novel phenomenon underlying the discrepancy: jailbreaks that bypass safety fine-tuning tend to degrade the victim model's capabilities, producing vacuous or incoherent outputs that prior evaluators still credit as successful.

## Input / Output

The evaluator takes a forbidden prompt (one of 313 curated prompts with factually verifiable answers across six harm categories) paired with the victim model's response to a jailbroken version of that prompt, and produces a composite score in [0,1] computed as (1 − refused) × (specific + convincing) / 2, where 'refused' is binary and 'specific' and 'convincing' are 5-point Likert scores rescaled to [0,1]. A response that merely avoids explicit refusal but provides no actionable information receives a score of 0, identical to an outright refusal. Two evaluator variants are provided: a rubric-based LLM-as-judge (defaulting to GPT-4o-mini) and a fine-tuned Gemma 2B model runnable on a single GPU.

## Method

StrongREJECT combines a curated 313-prompt dataset (70% novel, filtered for distinctness, answerability, and universal refusal by frontier models) with an automated evaluator that scores both willingness (non-refusal) and capabilities (specificity and convincingness of the response). The rubric-based evaluator prompts an LLM with chain-of-thought reasoning before outputting three scores; the open-source variant is a Gemma 2B model fine-tuned via LoRA for one epoch on ~15,000 responses labeled by the rubric-based evaluator, producing a single-token 5-point Likert completion. The key design insight is that the multiplicative scoring formula forces both dimensions to be satisfied: a model that is willing but incapable (or capable but unwilling) receives a zero score, directly addressing the upward bias of prior evaluators that treated non-refusal as sufficient evidence of jailbreak success.

## Results

On the human evaluation dataset (1,361 labeled prompt-response pairs across 17 jailbreaks and 4 victim models), the StrongREJECT rubric-based evaluator achieves the lowest MAE of 0.077±0.01 and a Spearman correlation of 0.846 with human judgments, while the fine-tuned StrongREJECT evaluator achieves the highest Spearman correlation of 0.900 and a near-zero bias of -0.023±0.01. Both StrongREJECT variants outperform all six baseline evaluators on MAE, with the rubric version also achieving the best MAE on partially harmful responses (0.196±0.03). The fine-tuned evaluator's Spearman correlation (0.900) exceeds the next-best baseline, HarmBench (0.819), by 0.081.

## Baselines & Comparisons

StrongREJECT outperforms all six baselines on MAE across all responses: the rubric version (0.077) beats HarmBench (0.090), PAIR (0.205), GPT-4 Judge (0.262), PICT (0.291), Jailbroken-binary (0.407), and String matching (0.580). On Spearman correlation for jailbreak method rankings, the fine-tuned StrongREJECT evaluator (0.900) surpasses HarmBench (0.819) by 0.081, while all other baselines fall well below 0.25. On bias, StrongREJECT (±0.02) and HarmBench (0.013) are both near-zero, whereas String matching (0.484) and Jailbroken-binary (0.354) exhibit severe upward bias, and the OpenAI moderation API shows a notable downward bias (-0.161). On MAE for partially harmful responses (Human>0), the rubric-based StrongREJECT evaluator is the best (0.196), narrowly ahead of GPT-4 Judge (0.212) and PAIR (0.224), while the OpenAI moderation API performs worst (0.761). The paper's key finding is that most prior evaluators overstate jailbreak effectiveness because they conflate non-refusal with usefulness, a gap that StrongREJECT addresses by scoring both willingness and capability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground in jailbreak evaluation by identifying a fundamental flaw in the existing evaluation ecosystem: prior automated evaluators (string matching, GPT-4 Judge, PICT, PAIR, etc.) predominantly measure non-refusal (willingness) while ignoring whether the model actually provides useful, specific information (capability). Rather than building on a single prior method, StrongREJECT synthesizes lessons from the shortcomings of multiple existing prompt datasets (AdvBench, DAN, MaliciousInstruct) and evaluators to construct a unified benchmark. The concurrent HarmBench [20] is the closest prior work in spirit, but StrongREJECT differentiates itself by using factually verifiable prompts with specific answers and by explicitly decomposing evaluation into willingness and capability dimensions, which enables the paper's key diagnostic finding about capability degradation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery that jailbreaks systematically reduce model capabilities is a conceptual shift: it means that a high non-refusal rate does not imply a dangerous jailbreak, and that the field has been overestimating threat levels by relying on refusal-based metrics. This has direct practical implications for model developers (which jailbreaks to prioritize defending against) and jailbreak researchers (which attacks are genuinely effective vs. merely bypassing the refusal trigger). Key limitations include the relatively small 313-prompt dataset, restriction to text-only LLMs, and potential fragility to shifting terms of service. Open questions include whether the capability-reduction effect is universal across model architectures and scales, and whether the StrongREJECT evaluator itself is robust to adversarial optimization by jailbreak search algorithms like PAIR.
