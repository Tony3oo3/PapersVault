## Problem

CLIP-style visual-language alignment produces opaque latent features where individual word-level contributions cannot be inspected, and its visual encoder struggles with patch-level semantics. Lexical representations—vectors where each dimension scores similarity to a specific vocabulary word—are inherently interpretable and sparse, but learning them effectively has required complex multi-stage training, large-scale data, and additional regularization (e.g., bag-of-words penalties) that limit model capacity. The paper asks whether a unified, interpretable lexical representation can be learned for both modalities with a simple single-stage contrastive objective and modest multi-modal data.

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB image (flattened into patches and processed by a frozen DINOv2 base model) and a text caption (fed to Llama 2 7B via an in-context prompt that asks the model to identify important words). Both modalities are mapped to a shared 17,149-dimensional sparse lexical vector (non-negative, ℓ₂-normalized) where each dimension corresponds to a vocabulary token; sparsity is enforced by value thresholding at 1/√V, yielding an average of ~1,081 active dimensions. For retrieval, dot-product similarity between image and text lexical vectors produces the cross-modal ranking.

## Method

LexVLA pairs a frozen DINOv2 visual backbone (with a small trainable adapter of one self-attention layer and two MLPs) with a LoRA-fine-tuned Llama 2 text encoder that uses an in-context prompt to perform lexical prediction—identifying important words in a caption—rather than producing a single caption embedding. Both modalities share a unified vocabulary but use distinct codebooks: the text codebook (Llama 2's output embedding matrix) is frozen to preserve pre-trained semantics, while the image codebook is initialized from the text codebook and fine-tuned to adapt to visual features. Training uses the standard InfoNCE contrastive objective augmented with a proposed overuse penalty, a weighted variant of the FLOPs loss that penalizes tokens whose average activation across the batch is disproportionately high, thereby suppressing false activation of semantically irrelevant tokens (e.g., '@' or 'contemporary') that the unweighted FLOPs loss encourages as a shortcut. Only ~109M parameters are trainable (70M image codebook, 17M visual projector, 21M LoRA adapter), and the entire model is trained for 12 epochs on 9.2M CC-12M pairs with 8 A100 GPUs.

## Results

On zero-shot cross-modal retrieval, LexVLA trained on only 12M CC-12M pairs achieves MSCOCO I2T R@1 of 55.4 and T2I R@1 of 39.8, and Flickr30k I2T R@1 of 83.9 and T2I R@1 of 67.8. These numbers surpass CLIP trained on 0.4B pairs (MSCOCO I2T R@1 52.4, T2I R@1 33.1) and CLIP trained on 1.1B pairs (MSCOCO I2T R@1 53.4, T2I R@1 36.2) by a substantial margin. On the PatchDis interpretability metric, LexVLA achieves 36.3 mIoU on MSCOCO 2017, far exceeding CLIP (5.3) and VDR (12.6), demonstrating strong patch-level visual interpretability from global supervision alone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LexVLA (12M) decisively outperforms all 15M-data baselines: it beats DeCLIP by +30.1 R@1 on MSCOCO I2T, SLIP by +27.7, VDR by +24.5, and ProtoCLIP by +25.2, confirming that the lexical representation with DINOv2 and Llama 2 backbones is far more effective than prior methods at the same data scale. Against CLIP trained on 0.4B pairs, LexVLA gains +3.0 R@1 on MSCOCO I2T and +6.7 on T2I, and +2.1 / +5.7 on Flickr30k I2T / T2I, respectively. Against CLIP at 1.1B scale, the margins narrow to +2.0 (MSCOCO I2T) and +3.8 (MSCOCO T2I) but remain consistent. The closest competitor is STAIR (1.1B data): LexVLA trails by 2.3 R@1 on MSCOCO I2T and 1.6 on T2I, but wins on Flickr30k by +2.7 (I2T) and +1.2 (T2I), achieving comparable overall performance with roughly 90% less training data. On the PatchDis interpretability metric, LexVLA (36.3 mIoU) substantially outperforms CLIP (5.3, near random) and VDR (12.6), validating that the unified lexical representation provides genuine patch-level semantic grounding.

## Relation to Prior Work

LexVLA extends the CLIP-style contrastive alignment paradigm by replacing the dense latent feature space with a sparse, word-indexed lexical representation, and by substituting CLIP's ViT/BERT encoders with DINOv2 (for local-inclined visual features) and Llama 2 (for in-context lexical prediction). The sparsity regularization builds directly on the FLOPs loss of Paria et al. (ICLR 2020), which the authors adapt into a weighted overuse penalty to prevent false activation of meaningless tokens. Unlike prior lexical VLA methods (VDR, STAIR, LexLIP) that require multi-stage training, large-scale data, or bag-of-words constraints, LexVLA achieves alignment in a single fine-tuning stage on 12M pairs, leveraging the inductive biases of the frozen single-modal backbones rather than learning everything from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The key practical takeaway is that strong single-modal pre-trained models (DINOv2, Llama 2) can be aligned for cross-modal retrieval with roughly 100× less multi-modal data than CLIP-scale training, while simultaneously yielding interpretable, patch-level representations that CLIP-style latent alignment cannot provide. The overuse penalty is a simple, general-purpose fix for a known failure mode of FLOPs-based sparsity regularization that could transfer to other sparse-representation settings. Open questions include whether the sub-word vocabulary inherited from Llama 2's tokenizer limits fine-grained word-level interpretability, whether the approach scales to larger LLMs or vision backbones, and whether the data-efficiency gap narrows when competitors are given equal architectural freedom.
