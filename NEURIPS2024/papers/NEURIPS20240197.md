## Problem

Existing neural divide-and-conquer methods for large-scale combinatorial optimization rely on problem-specific heuristics in either the dividing or conquering stage, limiting generalizability across CO problem types. Moreover, all prior methods adopt separate training schemes that ignore the interdependencies between dividing and conquering policies, and they fail to account for error connections introduced at sub-problem boundaries by sub-optimal dividing, leading to biased reward signals and convergence to local optima.

## Input / Output

UDC takes a CO instance (node coordinates for VRPs, a cost matrix for ATSP, a graph for MIS, or item values/weights for KP) and first constructs a sparse KNN graph (K=100) as input to the dividing GNN. The dividing stage outputs a full-length initial solution, which is then segmented into fixed-length (n-node) sub-problems; the conquering stage produces improved sub-solutions that are merged back. The final output is a feasible solution (tour, route, or subset) after r iterative conquering stages, with sub-problem coordinates normalized to a unit square and capacity/prize constraints proportionally dispatched to ensure merged-solution legality.

## Method

UDC employs a 12-layer Anisotropic GNN (AGNN, d=64) with a T-revisit heatmap regeneration mechanism as the dividing policy, generating initial solutions in O(TKN) time, and uses problem-specific constructive solvers (ICAM, POMO, MatNet, DPN, or AGNN) as the conquering policy on fixed-length sub-problems. The key training innovation is the Divide-Conquer-Reunion (DCR) procedure: after the first conquering pass, a Reunion step rolls the decomposition point by n/2 and re-solves the shifted sub-problems, explicitly repairing error connections at sub-problem boundaries caused by sub-optimal dividing. Both policies are trained simultaneously via REINFORCE with per-policy baselines (α initial solutions for dividing, β sub-solutions for conquering), eliminating the need for pre-training and avoiding the antagonism inherent in separate training schemes. The framework is conquering-policy-agnostic and requires no heuristic design, enabling direct application to 10 diverse CO problems under a single unified training protocol.

## Results

On TSP, UDC-x50 (α=50) achieves gaps of 1.58%, 1.78%, and 2.49% to LKH3 on 500-, 1,000-, and 2,000-node instances respectively, outperforming all other neural divide-and-conquer methods (GLOP: 2.19%/3.12%/3.89%, H-TSP: 6.22%/6.91%/7.49%). On CVRP, UDC-x250 (α=50) achieves the best overall results, beating even LKH3 on CVRP1,000 (43.00 vs 46.40) and CVRP2,000 (60.01 vs 64.90), with gaps of 3.01%, best, and best on 500/1,000/2,000-node instances. On PCTSP and SPCTSP, UDC-x250 achieves gaps as low as 0% (beating OR-Tools and GLOP), and on OP it reaches 2.98% gap at 500 nodes vs 10.38% for Sym-NCO.

## Baselines & Comparisons

UDC clearly outperforms all existing neural divide-and-conquer methods (GLOP, H-TSP, TAM, L2D, SO) across all tested problems and scales, with the largest margins on CVRP (e.g., UDC-x250 achieves 1.55% gap vs GLOP's 5.01% on CVRP2000) and TSP2000 (2.49% vs GLOP's 3.89%). Against single-stage RL-based solvers, UDC beats ICAM on TSP2000 (2.49% vs 5.93%) and all CVRP scales (e.g., 1.55% vs 2.21% on CVRP2000), but ICAM retains a slight edge on TSP500 (0.77% vs 1.58%) and TSP1000 (1.58% vs 1.78%). UDC-x250 surpasses the classical LKH3 solver on CVRP1000 (43.00 vs 46.40) and CVRP2000 (60.01 vs 64.90), making it the best overall method on those benchmarks. Against sub-path solvers BQ and LEHD, UDC achieves better gaps on TSP1000/2000 and all CVRP scales while being significantly faster (e.g., 23s vs 3m for TSP2000 greedy). On PCTSP and SPCTSP, UDC-x250 achieves the best results (0% gap on PCTSP500/1000/2000, 0% on SPCTSP500/1000/2000), outperforming GLOP (9.03%/8.08%/8.84%) and OR-Tools (9.38%/12.66%). UDC also dominates POMO by large margins (e.g., 2.49% vs 56.85% gap on TSP2000) and ELG on larger scales (2.49% vs 11.37% on TSP2000).

## Relation to Prior Work

UDC builds on the heatmap-based solver lineage (AGNN from Bresson & Laurent 2018, DIFUSCO from Sun & Yang 2023) for its dividing policy and on constructive RL solvers (ICAM, POMO) for its conquering policy, but departs fundamentally from prior neural divide-and-conquer methods (GLOP, TAM, H-TSP, L2D, RBG) in two ways: it uses learning-based policies in both stages without any problem-specific heuristics, and it introduces the DCR training method that explicitly accounts for error connections at sub-problem boundaries. The key conceptual departure is the insight that separate training schemes (pre-train conquering, then train dividing) create an antagonism where the dividing policy adapts to a sub-optimal conquering policy, and that a 'Reunion' step repairing boundary connections provides a more faithful reward signal for the dividing policy.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The DCR training method addresses a structural weakness in all prior neural divide-and-conquer approaches: the interdependency between dividing and conquering policies was previously ignored, leading to sub-optimal convergence. UDC's demonstrated applicability across 10 diverse CO problems (TSP, CVRP, KP, MIS, ATSP, OP, PCTSP, SPCTSP, OVRP, min-max mTSP) without problem-specific heuristics makes it a practical general-purpose framework. However, the framework is limited to problems with decomposable aggregate objectives, feasibility guaranteed by masks, and non-unique sub-solutions, excluding problems like TSPTW or dense-graph MIS. Open questions include whether better loss functions (beyond REINFORCE) could further improve training efficiency and whether the framework can be extended to the excluded problem classes.
