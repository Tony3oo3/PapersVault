## Problem

As LMs are deployed as agents that access sensitive user data (calendars, emails, notes) and execute communication tasks, they may unintentionally leak private information in violation of contextual privacy norms. Prior evaluation approaches rely on probing questions or training-time extraction attacks, but a growing body of evidence shows a disconnect between LMs' ability to answer privacy questions and their actual behavior in agentic settings. No existing framework systematically evaluates LM privacy norm awareness through realistic, action-based agent trajectories.

## Input / Output

Each data point consists of a privacy-sensitive seed (a 5-tuple: data type, data subject, data sender, data recipient, transmission principle), an extended vignette (a 5-sentence scenario), and an executable agent trajectory (a sequence of action-observation pairs from a sandbox with tools like Gmail, Slack, Notion, Facebook, and Zoom). The final action is stripped from the trajectory; the evaluated LM must generate it. Outputs are two-fold: (1) binary probing answers on privacy appropriateness at seed, vignette, and trajectory levels, and (2) a final agent action scored for leakage via an LM-based few-shot classifier that checks whether extracted sensitive information items are inferable from the action, yielding a leakage rate (LR) and a helpfulness-conditioned adjusted leakage rate (LR_h).

## Method

PrivacyLens is a procedural data construction and multi-level evaluation framework. It starts with 493 privacy-sensitive seeds collected from U.S. regulations (HIPAA, FERPA, GLBA, etc.), privacy research on vulnerable groups, and Prolific crowdsourcing, all structured via a Contextual Integrity 5-tuple. A template-based GPT-4 pipeline extends each seed into a vignette, and a ToolEmu-based sandbox with a GPT-4 emulator generates executable agent trajectories, with a Surgery Kit module iteratively refining outputs against unit tests (e.g., removing explicit sensitivity keywords, eliminating placeholders). Evaluation operates at two levels: QA probing (zero-shot accuracy on whether a data flow is acceptable) and action-based evaluation where LMs (using ReAct) generate the final action and are scored for information leakage. The framework is dynamically extensible—each seed can be mapped to multiple vignettes and trajectories by injecting contextual conditions (reciprocal disclosure, legitimate need, close relationships, etc.), enabling red-teaming and contamination mitigation.

## Results

On the PrivacyLens dataset (493 trajectories), GPT-4 leaks sensitive information in 25.68% of cases and Llama-3-70B in 38.69% of cases, even with privacy-enhancing prompt engineering. A key finding is the large discrepancy between probing accuracy and action-based evaluation: GPT-4 and Claude-3-Sonnet answer nearly all trajectory-level probing questions correctly yet still leak information in 27.23% and 38.83% of cases, respectively. Weaker models show a dramatic drop in probing accuracy when moving from seed-level to trajectory-level questions (e.g., Mistral-7B drops from 94.32% to 63.29%; Llama-3-8B from 88.84% to 31.44%). Prompt engineering (privacy-enhancing vs. basic) does not significantly reduce leakage rates in the action-based evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper does not compare against a single competing method but rather evaluates 9 LMs (4 closed-source: ChatGPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude-3-Haiku, Claude-3-Sonnet; 5 open-source: Mistral-7B, Mixtral-8x7B, zephyr-7b, Llama-3-8B, Llama-3-70B) under two evaluation paradigms (QA probing vs. agent action) and two prompt settings (Basic vs. Privacy-Enhancing). The central comparison is between probing accuracy and action-based leakage: strong models (GPT-4, Claude-3-Sonnet) achieve near-perfect probing accuracy yet still leak in 25–39% of action-based cases, revealing a significant知行 gap. Scaling does not help: Llama-3-70B (38.69% LR) performs worse than Llama-3-8B on action-based evaluation despite better probing scores, and Claude-3-Sonnet (38.83%) is worse than Claude-3-Haiku on leakage. Privacy-enhancing prompts improve probing but do not significantly reduce action-based leakage. On the extensibility experiments (Table 3, 50 trajectories from 10 seeds), GPT-4 and ChatGPT-3.5 achieve the lowest leakage (14.00% LR), while Mixtral-8x7B is worst (30.00% LR). On the ConfAIde and CultureBank subsets (Table 5), GPT-4 achieves the lowest leakage (9.38% and 12.50% respectively), while Claude-3-Sonnet and Mixtral-8x7B are among the worst (21.88–33.33% and 12.50–37.50%).

## Relation to Prior Work

PrivacyLens builds on the Contextual Integrity theory (Nissenbaum, 2004) to define a structured 5-tuple seed schema, and leverages ToolEmu (Ruan et al., ICLR 2024) as the sandbox infrastructure for constructing executable agent trajectories. It departs from the closest prior work, ConfAIde (Mireshghallah et al., ICLR 2024), which evaluates contextual privacy via probing questions on a single application with only 20 test cases, by shifting to action-based evaluation in a multi-tool agent setting with 493 diverse scenarios. The probing question format is adapted from Shvartzshnaider et al. (HCOMP 2016), originally designed to survey human privacy expectations, repurposed here as a baseline for LM evaluation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework reveals that even state-of-the-art models like GPT-4 leak sensitive information in over 25% of agent actions despite near-perfect probing accuracy, and that neither scaling nor privacy-enhancing prompts close this gap—highlighting a fundamental disconnect between privacy reasoning and privacy-aware action. The extensible, procedurally-constructed dataset (493 seeds → vignettes → trajectories) enables dynamic red-teaming and can be instantiated with other seed collections (demonstrated on ConfAIde and CultureBank subsets). Key limitations include U.S.-only privacy norms, reliance on GPT-4 for trajectory collection (potentially biasing evaluation of other models), and restriction to LM-mediated communication scenarios; open questions include how to actually improve privacy norm awareness in agents and how to extend evaluation to cross-cultural and non-communication settings.
