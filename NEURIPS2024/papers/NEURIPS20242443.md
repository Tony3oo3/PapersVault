## Problem

While extensive guidance exists for selecting visual foundation models (VFMs) in 2D image tasks, the optimal encoder choice for complex 3D scene understanding remains unclear because 3D scenarios introduce additional requirements—geometric awareness, multi-view consistency, and instance-level discrimination—that are absent in 2D benchmarks. No prior work has systematically compared image, video, and 3D foundation models across the full spectrum of 3D scene understanding capabilities.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242443/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The framework consumes multi-view posed images, video clips (16–25 frames), and 3D point clouds, with text descriptions for language-conditioned tasks. For image and video encoders, per-pixel features are projected into 3D via camera intrinsics/extrinsics and aggregated over visible views using mean pooling, yielding a unified point-cloud feature embedding. Downstream outputs are task-specific: free-form VQA answer text (via a frozen LLM), per-object grounding scores, per-point semantic class probabilities (20 classes on ScanNet), and a 6-DoF homography (R, t) for partial scene registration.

## Method

Lexicon3D introduces a unified probing architecture that freezes all seven evaluated VFMs (DINOv2, LSeg, CLIP, StableDiffusion, V-JEPA, StableVideoDiffusion, Swin3D) and trains only shallow task-specific heads: a Q-Former module bridging to a frozen LLM for VQA, a CLIP-text-fused attention head for grounding, a single linear layer for segmentation, and a REGTR-style transformer cross-encoder for registration. The key design choice is the multi-view 3D projection module that converts 2D pixel features into point-cloud embeddings, enabling direct comparison of image, video, and 3D encoders under identical downstream conditions. The study further ablates diffusion noise levels and feature layers, video downsampling strategies (keyframe vs. clip sampling), and mixture-of-vision-expert (MoVE) feature concatenation, revealing that DINOv2 leads overall, video models excel at instance discrimination, diffusion models dominate geometric registration, and language-pretrained encoders underperform on language tasks contrary to intuition.

## Results

Across four 3D scene understanding tasks, DINOv2 achieves the best overall performance, scoring 73.2 CIDEr on ScanQA and 129.1 CIDEr on SQA3D for vision-language reasoning, and 62.8 mIoU for semantic segmentation on ScanNet. StableVideoDiffusion leads in visual grounding (54.7% overall accuracy on ScanRefer) and partial scene registration (96.8% RR@0.05m, 0.06m RTE), while StableDiffusion also excels in registration (91.7% RR@0.05m). Notably, CLIP—despite its language-pretrained design—achieves only 3.4 mIoU on segmentation and underperforms DINOv2 on VQA, challenging the assumption that language-aligned encoders are optimal for vision-language tasks.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DINOv2 outperforms the 3D-LLM reference [36] on both ScanQA (73.2 vs. 69.4 CIDEr) and SQA3D (129.1 vs. 124.5 CIDEr), establishing it as the strongest general-purpose encoder. StableVideoDiffusion [12] surpasses the Multi3DRefer [108] reference on ScanRefer grounding (54.7% vs. 54.3% overall), with its advantage concentrated in the 'multiple' category (46.5% vs. 46.1%), reflecting video models' instance-discrimination strength. DINOv2 dramatically outperforms the GrowSP [110] reference on ScanNet segmentation (62.8 vs. 31.6 mIoU), while CLIP [68] collapses to 3.4 mIoU, revealing that language-pretrained features are poorly suited for fine-grained pixel-level semantics. In registration, StableVideoDiffusion (0.06m RTE) and StableDiffusion (0.09m RTE) far exceed DINOv2 (0.14m) and Swin3D (0.23m), showing generative pretraining imparts strong geometric correspondence. In the supplementary comparison, Uni3D [116] underperforms Swin3D [97] on scene-level tasks (2.7 vs. 35.2 mIoU) due to its object-centric pretraining, while SAM [43] matches LSeg on grounding but lags significantly on semantic segmentation (30.9 vs. 47.5 mIoU), confirming that instance-aware and semantic-aware encoders serve complementary roles.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the 2D probing paradigm (Banani et al. [6], Ranzinger et al. [70]) to complex 3D scene understanding, introducing a unified framework that projects image/video features into 3D point clouds via multi-view reprojection (following 3D-LLM [36]) and evaluates them with frozen linear or shallow heads. It builds on REGTR [99] for the registration task and Multi3DRefer [108] for grounding, but departs from prior work by being the first to systematically compare image, video, and 3D foundation models across four complementary 3D tasks under identical probing conditions. The paper also introduces a novel partial scene registration task to isolate geometric correspondence capability from semantic matching.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive benchmark showing that encoder choice for 3D scene understanding is task-dependent and that the default assumption of using language-pretrained encoders (CLIP, LSeg) for vision-language tasks is not well-founded. The findings provide practical guidance for practitioners building 3D scene understanding systems, and the MoVE (mixture-of-vision-expert) strategy offers a simple, effective recipe for combining complementary encoders. Key limitations include evaluation restricted to indoor scenes, frozen-encoder probing only (no finetuning), non-identical model capacities, and absence of error bars, leaving open questions about outdoor generalization, the effect of task-specific finetuning, and whether conclusions hold with larger-scale 3D pretraining data.
