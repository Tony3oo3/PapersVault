## Problem

Deep RL agents are prone to goal misalignment, where they optimize proxy objectives (e.g., tracking the enemy paddle in Pong) rather than the true task goal, and their black-box nature prevents domain experts from detecting or correcting such failures. Existing concept bottleneck models (CBMs) only represent single-object properties, lacking the relational reasoning between objects that RL tasks fundamentally require.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242134/cropped_page1_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

SCoBots consume a stack of n game frames, which are first converted into object-centric state descriptions via a pretrained extractor (OCAtari), yielding per-object properties: class, (x,y) position, previous position, height/width, dominant RGB, and orientation. The output is a discrete action selected by a decision tree policy, accompanied by fully inspectable intermediate representations at two levels: object property vectors (Ω_t) and relational concept vectors (Γ_t) computed from a user-defined set of transparent functions (e.g., directed distances, speed, velocity, plan intersection).

## Method

SCoBots decompose the classic deep policy s→a into three successive, inspectable bottleneck stages: (1) an object extractor ω maps raw frames to object property sets, (2) a relation extractor μ_F applies a predefined set of transparent relational functions F (distances, speeds, velocities, etc.) to object pairs to produce relational concepts, and (3) an action selector ρ maps relational concepts to actions. The action selector is initially trained as a small ReLU network via PPO, then distilled into an interpretable decision tree using VIPER, trading some flexibility for full transparency. Two human-guidance mechanisms exploit the inspectable structure: concept pruning (removing irrelevant objects, properties, relations, or actions to eliminate spurious correlations) and object-centric reward shaping (crafting expert reward signals directly from relational concepts to address reward sparsity, credit assignment, and misalignment). This architecture preserves the standard MDP formulation while enabling multi-level inspection and intervention from single-object properties through relational concepts to the final action decision.

## Results

On 9 Atari games, guided SCoBots achieve human-normalized scores on par with or exceeding deep CNN PPO agents on 7 out of 9 games, with particularly large margins on Kangaroo (4050 vs 790), Seaquest (2411 vs 837), and Skiing (−6530 vs −23004). SCoBots slightly underperform the deep baseline only on Boxing (69.0 vs 90.3) and Freeway (32.9 vs 33.6). The guided SCoBots also outperform the original PPO-v4 scores from Schulman et al. on 5 of 9 games (Asterix, Bowling, Pong, Seaquest, Tennis), while remaining competitive on the rest.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the deep CNN PPO baseline (Mnih et al. 2015 architecture, PPO-v5), guided SCoBots outperform on 7 of 9 games, with the largest margins on Kangaroo (4050 vs 790, ~5×), Seaquest (2411 vs 837, ~2.9×), and Skiing (−6530 vs −23004, ~3.5× closer to human). SCoBots lose only on Boxing (69.0 vs 90.3) and are marginally behind on Freeway (32.9 vs 33.6). Against the original PPO-v4 scores reported by Schulman et al. 2017, SCoBots are competitive but generally below: they exceed the original on Asterix (5043 vs 4532), Bowling (137 vs 40), Pong (17.5 vs 20.7 is actually below), Seaquest (2411 vs 1204), and Tennis (0.0 vs −14.8), but fall well short on Kangaroo (4050 vs 9929) and Skiing (−6530 vs −13901). The unguided SCoBots (no concept pruning or expert reward) perform comparably to the deep baseline on most games, confirming that the interpretability constraints do not inherently sacrifice performance.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends Concept Bottleneck Models (Koh et al., ICML 2020) from supervised image classification to reinforcement learning, introducing successive bottleneck layers that extract not only single-object properties but also relational concepts between objects. It builds on the interactive concept-revision paradigm of Stammer et al. (CVPR 2021) and the VIPER decision-tree extraction algorithm (Bastani et al., NeurIPS 2018) to produce inherently interpretable policies. The key departure from prior CBM work is the explicit incorporation of relational reasoning (distances, velocities, trajectories between objects) and the decomposition of the policy into independently optimizable, human-inspectable stages, positioning SCoBots as a counterpoint to post-hoc XAI methods like importance maps that the authors argue lack faithfulness.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The discovery of a previously unknown goal misalignment in Pong—where agents track the enemy paddle (which correlates >99% with the ball) rather than the ball itself—challenges the reliability of post-hoc importance-map explanations and highlights a concrete failure mode in even the simplest RL benchmarks. SCoBots provide a practical, extensible framework for domain experts to inspect, prune, and reward-shape agent behavior at the concept level, addressing reward sparsity, credit assignment, and misalignment with minimal intervention. Limitations include reliance on the quasi-perfect OCAtari object extractor, restriction to Atari-style environments with few objects, and the need for manually specified relational functions; open questions include scaling to maze/platform environments, integrating attention or language models for concept generation, and whether task rewards can bootstrap object-centric representations end-to-end.
