## Problem

While CLIP-ViT models can be decomposed into per-component contributions (attention heads, MLPs, tokens) and interpreted via text through CLIP's shared image-text space, this approach does not extend to other ViT architectures like DeiT, DINO, Swin, or MaxViT. These models lack a corresponding text encoder, employ diverse attention mechanisms (windowed, grid, block), and apply various linear transformations (pooling, patch merging, downsampling) between blocks, making both decomposition and interpretation non-trivial.

## Input / Output

The framework takes an RGB image processed through any ViT model and a set of text feature instantiations (e.g., 'spotted pattern', 'forest') as input. It produces per-component contribution vectors (one per attention head and MLP in the last few layers) mapped into CLIP image space, continuous importance scores for each component-feature pair, and downstream artifacts including retrieved images ranked by feature relevance and token-level contribution heatmaps. The decomposition is restricted to direct contributions from the last 10–20 layers depending on model depth, with all earlier layers accumulated into a single residual vector.

## Method

The framework has three core components. First, REPDECOMPOSE automatically decomposes the final representation into per-component contributions by recursively traversing the model's computational graph in reverse, exploiting the linearity of transformers to distribute linear operations (LayerNorm, projections, concatenations, reductions) over individual contribution vectors until hitting non-linear nodes. Second, COMPALIGN trains a separate linear map for each component to project its contributions into CLIP image space, minimizing cosine distance to the CLIP representation while applying an orthogonality regularizer (‖fᵢᵀfᵢ − I‖_F) that, per Theorem 1, forces each map to be a scalar multiple of an orthogonal transformation, preserving relative feature importance within and across components. Third, a scoring function assigns each component-feature pair a continuous importance score by computing the correlation between the component's CLIP-aligned contribution and the full representation, both projected onto the subspace spanned by CLIP text embeddings of feature instantiations, enabling ranking of components by feature relevance without rigid one-to-one role assignment.

## Results

On zero-shot segmentation (Table 5), the proposed Decompose method outperforms both Chefer et al. and GradCam across all four tested models, achieving e.g. 0.772 pixAcc / 0.529 mIoU / 0.831 mAP on DeiT vs. 0.731/0.479/0.787 (Chefer) and 0.653/0.463/0.713 (GradCam). On the Waterbirds spurious correlation task, ablating location-associated components improves worst-group accuracy substantially, e.g. CLIP rises from 0.507 to 0.744 and DeiT from 0.733 to 0.815, while average group accuracy also increases. The COMPALIGN alignment method achieves a 0.185 match rate and 0.17 cosine distance to ImageNet-pretrained ground-truth descriptions, compared to 0.08/0.23 for the single-map baseline. Spearman rank correlations between the proposed scoring function and a CLIP-based ground-truth proxy range from 0.53 to 0.85 across models and features.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against the single-map alignment baseline (Moayeri et al. [19], 'One map only'), COMPALIGN improves the TEXTSPAN description match rate from 0.08 to 0.185 and reduces cosine distance from 0.23 to 0.17, confirming that per-component orthogonal maps preserve feature roles better than a shared linear projection. In zero-shot segmentation (Table 5), Decompose outperforms Chefer et al. [8] on all three metrics for both DeiT (0.772 vs 0.731 pixAcc) and DINO (0.758 vs 0.731 pixAcc), and substantially outperforms GradCam [28] across all four models — e.g. on MaxViT, Decompose achieves 0.716 pixAcc / 0.424 mIoU versus GradCam's 0.473 / 0.171. On the Waterbirds spurious correlation task, the component-ablation intervention (not a direct baseline comparison but a before/after) yields the largest gains for CLIP (worst-group 0.507→0.744) and DINO (0.800→0.911), demonstrating that the scoring function correctly identifies location-biased components for removal. Chefer et al.'s method does not support MaxViT or SWIN, limiting that comparison to two models.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper directly extends Gandelsman et al.'s (ICLR 2024) decomposition of CLIP-ViT's final representation into per-head, per-token, per-MLP contributions, generalizing it from the CLIP-specific architecture to arbitrary ViT variants (DeiT, DINO, DINOv2, Swin, MaxViT) that lack a shared text encoder and employ diverse attention mechanisms. The COMPALIGN alignment procedure builds on Moayeri et al.'s cross-model linear alignment but extends it from mapping only the final representation to mapping each individual component contribution separately, with an orthogonality regularizer derived from a norm-preservation theorem. The scoring function for component-feature attribution departs from the rigid one-component-one-label paradigm of TEXTSPAN by introducing a continuous importance score, motivated by the observation that features are often distributed across multiple components.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The framework provides a practical, architecture-agnostic tool (REPDECOMPOSE + COMPALIGN) for interpreting ViT internals via text, enabling applications like feature-specific image retrieval, token-level visualization, and zero-shot spurious correlation mitigation across six diverse architectures. A notable finding is that larger models (MaxViT, Swin) encode features diffusely across many components rather than localizing them, challenging the assumption that individual attention heads or MLPs have clean, single-feature roles. Key limitations include restricting analysis to direct contributions from the last few layers (no higher-order interaction terms), not decomposing convolutional blocks, and relying on CLIP as the interpretation anchor, which may not capture all relevant semantic directions. Open questions include whether specific subspaces within component contributions correspond to well-defined properties, and how indirect (higher-order) contributions shape the final representation.
