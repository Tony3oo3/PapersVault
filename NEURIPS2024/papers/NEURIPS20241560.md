## Problem

Despite the rapid adoption of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), existing trustworthiness benchmarks for MLLMs are fragmented, typically covering only one or two aspects (e.g., safety or robustness) and focusing exclusively on multimodal risks in images while neglecting how the visual modality alters the base LLM's behavior in text-only scenarios. No unified, comprehensive evaluation framework exists that systematically assesses MLLM trustworthiness across all critical dimensions and accounts for cross-modal interactions.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241560/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each task consumes an RGB image paired with a text prompt (or a text-only prompt for cross-modal impact studies, where images are deliberately semantically relevant or irrelevant). Outputs are scored via a mix of objective metrics (accuracy, Pearson correlation, chi-square p-value) and subjective metrics (toxicity scores from Moderation/Perspective APIs, GPT-4-based scoring with 0.91 human correlation, refuse-to-answer rates via classifier models). The final output is a per-model ranking across 5 aspects and 10 sub-aspects, plus per-task scores such as Attack Success Rate, Cure Rate, and Deterioration Rate derived from base accuracy measurements.

## Method

MultiTrust organizes trustworthiness into a two-level taxonomy of 5 primary aspects (truthfulness, safety, robustness, fairness, privacy) and 10 sub-aspects, instantiated as 32 tasks spanning generative and discriminative formats. The key methodological innovation is a dual evaluation strategy: (1) multimodal risks, where novel image-based scenarios (e.g., visual illusions, adversarial patches, NSFW images, typographic jailbreaks) test capabilities unique to the vision modality, and (2) cross-modal impacts, where standard text-only tasks (e.g., sentiment analysis, PII queries, stereotype classification) are re-evaluated when paired with semantically relevant or irrelevant images to measure how the visual input destabilizes the base LLM's aligned behavior. The benchmark curates 20 adapted datasets and 8 novel datasets (synthesized via Stable Diffusion, GPT-4V, and manual collection), evaluates 21 MLLMs (4 proprietary, 17 open-source) selected for architectural and training-paradigm diversity, and provides a modularized toolbox with unified model interfaces separating data, inference, and evaluation for extensibility.

## Results

Across 32 tasks and 21 MLLMs, GPT-4V ranks #1 overall and Claude3 #2, with a correlation coefficient of 0.60 between general capabilities (MMBench/MME) and trustworthiness. Proprietary models refuse 69.5% of NSFW image descriptions on average while most open-source models reject none; under untargeted adversarial attacks, most models' image captioning accuracy drops below 20% from over 90%. For privacy awareness, the average accuracy is 72.30% for basic detection but drops to 55.33% for complex reasoning, with only GPT-4V and Gemini-Pro exceeding 70% on the latter. Notably, pairing text-only privacy queries with relevant images reduces LLaVA-NeXT's refusal-to-answer rate from 51% to 1.5%, demonstrating cross-modal degradation of safety alignment.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GPT-4V dominates across nearly all sub-aspects (ranked #1 in 8 of 10), while Claude3 excels specifically in jailbreaking resistance (rank #1) and privacy leakage (rank #1), reflecting their distinct safety guardrail designs. Among open-source models, Qwen-VL-Plus (overall #3) and LLaVA-NeXT (#5) lead, but both trail proprietary models by 2–4 positions on average, with the gap widening sharply on safety (jailbreaking: #4 vs #1–2) and privacy leakage. A critical finding is that MiniGPT-4-L2 (frozen Llama-2 base) outperforms mPLUG-Owl2 (fine-tuned Llama-2 base) on typographic jailbreaking (79.5% vs 14.5% RtA) and NSFW refusal (34% vs 0%), directly demonstrating that multimodal fine-tuning catastrophically degrades the base LLM's safety alignment. For adversarial robustness, CogVLM and InternVL-Chat (both with large custom vision encoders) achieve substantially lower attack success rates than LLaVA-NeXT and InternLM-XC2, suggesting that encoder architecture suppresses transferability of adversarial perturbations. The cross-modal analysis reveals that LLaVA-NeXT's privacy refusal rate collapses from 51% (text-only) to 1.5% (with relevant images), while MiniGPT-4-L2 retains 24% refusal under the same condition, indicating that frozen LLMs preserve more of their original safety behavior in multimodal inference.

## Relation to Prior Work

MultiTrust adapts the trustworthiness aspect taxonomy established for LLMs by TrustLLM and DecodingTrust (truthfulness, safety, robustness, fairness, privacy) to the multimodal setting, while departing from prior MLLM benchmarks (Unicorn, RTVLM, MM-SafetyBench, etc.) that typically cover only one or two aspects and focus exclusively on threats within the image modality. Its key conceptual extension is the dual evaluation strategy that, beyond multimodal risks, measures cross-modal impacts—how semantically relevant or irrelevant images alter base LLM behavior on text-only tasks—a dimension absent from all prior MLLM trustworthiness work. Individual task designs draw on established techniques (FigStep for typographic jailbreaking, AdvGLUE for adversarial text, COCO-O for OOD generalization), but the unified 32-task, 5-aspect framework with a modularized toolbox is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first comprehensive, unified benchmark for MLLM trustworthiness, and its central finding—that multimodality amplifies internal risks from base LLMs (irrelevant images destabilize text-only performance, relevant images exacerbate biases and privacy leakage, and multimodal fine-tuning can catastrophically undo safety alignment)—challenges the assumption that a well-aligned LLM base guarantees a trustworthy MLLM. Open questions include how to preserve alignment during multimodal training, how to ensure cross-modal inference stability, and whether existing techniques (RLHF, SFT with GPT-4V data) can provide all-round guarantees. Limitations include the exclusion of machine ethics, restricted analysis of closed-source model internals, and the risk of dataset misuse.
