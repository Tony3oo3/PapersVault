## Problem

Learning causal structures among discrete latent variables remains largely unsolved: existing identifiability results either assume linear relationships, restrict latent structures to trees or pyramids, or require binary latent variables. No general criterion exists for identifying flexible discrete latent structures with non-linear (categorical) measurement models, leaving a gap for the most common real-world scenario of discrete survey and assessment data.

## Input / Output

The method consumes observed discrete data as joint probability contingency tables over subsets of observed variables, requiring the dimension of each observed variable's support to exceed that of any latent variable's support (the Sufficient Observation Assumption). It outputs two components: (1) a set of causal clusters partitioning observed variables by their shared latent parent (the measurement model, fully identifiable), and (2) a partial DAG over the latent variables representing the structure model, identified up to a Markov equivalence class. The latent support dimension r is estimated from the rank of 2-way contingency tables before structure learning begins.

## Method

The core contribution is a graphical criterion for tensor rank: the rank of the n-way probability tensor for an observed variable set equals the support size of the minimal conditional set that d-separates all variables in that set (Theorem 3.3). This links tensor rank directly to d-separation in the causal graph, enabling structural inference from rank tests alone. The algorithm proceeds in two phases: first, causal clusters are identified by checking whether 3-way tensor rank differs from r (Rule 1: different parents) and whether adding a 4th variable preserves rank r (Rule 2: same parent), with overlapping clusters merged; second, the PC algorithm is adapted (PC-TENSOR-RANK) so that conditional independence tests between latent variables are replaced by tensor rank tests—specifically, L_i ⊥ L_j | L_p iff the rank of the joint tensor over their children equals r^|L_p|. Practical rank estimation uses the CR statistic for 2-way tables and non-negative CP decomposition with a chi-square goodness-of-fit test for higher-order tensors.

On the measurement model task (Table 2), the method achieves 0.00–0.08 latent omission at 50k samples, compared to 0.20 (BayPy) and 0.50 (LTM) on the most complex structure SM3. ## Baselines & Comparisons

The proposed method decisively outperforms all three baselines on structure model learning. BPC (Building Pure Cluster, Silva et al. 2006) is designed for linear latent variable models and fails catastrophically on discrete data, achieving 1.00 edge omission (i.e., missing every true edge) across all structure types and sample sizes. BayPy (Gu & Dunson 2023) similarly achieves 1.00 edge omission on most structures because it assumes a pyramid (hierarchical) latent structure and binary latent variables, making it inapplicable to general discrete latent structures. LTM (Choi et al. 2011) performs moderately (0.10–0.50 edge omission) but is limited to tree-structured latent graphs and cannot handle the general DAG structures tested here. The proposed method achieves near-perfect recovery (0.00–0.05 edge omission) at 50k samples across all five structure types, including the complex 4-latent-variable SM3 structure where LTM still misses half the edges (0.50). On the measurement model task, the proposed method also leads, achieving 0.00–0.08 latent omission at 50k versus 0.20 (BayPy) and 0.50 (LTM) on SM3, while BPC remains at 0.83–0.90.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the causal cluster identification strategy from Silva et al. (2006) and the PC algorithm framework (Spirtes et al., 2000) to the discrete latent variable setting, replacing linear-algebraic second-order statistics and standard CI tests with tensor rank conditions on contingency tables. It departs from prior discrete latent variable work (Gu and Dunson, 2023; Wang et al., 2017) that required binary latent variables, tree/pyramid structures, or prespecified architectures, by establishing a general graphical criterion linking tensor rank to d-separation in arbitrary discrete DAGs. The approach also contrasts with Kivva et al. (2021), which recovers latent structure via mixture oracles, by offering a simpler algebraic test that operates directly on observed contingency tables.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides the first general identifiability result for discrete latent variable causal structures without linearity or binary assumptions, opening a path for causal discovery in settings like psychological and educational assessments where data are naturally categorical. Key limitations include the restriction to pure-children (3PLSM) structures, the requirement that observed variable support exceeds latent support, and the inability to scale to high-dimensional data. Open questions include extending to impure structures (edges among observed variables), handling heterogeneous latent state spaces efficiently, and validating on real-world datasets beyond simulations.
