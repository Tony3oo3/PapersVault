## Problem

Existing Video-LLM benchmarks evaluate models only through video-level multiple-choice QA on short, single-event clips, leaving fine-grained event-level understanding and time-sensitive localization entirely untested. As a result, state-of-the-art models that score well on benchmarks like MVBench or EgoSchema still fail at practical tasks such as grounding events of interest within long, multi-event videos, largely due to short context lengths, improper timestamp representations, and lack of multi-event training data.

## Input / Output

The model consumes video frames sampled at 1 FPS at 224×224 resolution along with a text instruction specifying the task and query. For Image-LLM baselines, 8 uniformly sampled frames are used with an appended duration hint; for Video-LLMs, each model's default frame count is preserved. Outputs are structured text responses whose format is task-dependent: MCQ letter choices for referring tasks, explicit start-end timestamp pairs in seconds for grounding tasks (e.g., 'The event happens in 10.2-12.8 seconds'), timestamped event descriptions for dense captioning, and combined MCQ answer plus supporting temporal boundary for grounded QA. The benchmark spans 12 tasks, 7,289 samples, and 7,002 videos with an average duration of 129.3 s (max 795 s), covering 8 domains.

## Method

E.T. Chat reformulates timestamp prediction as an embedding matching problem rather than discrete token generation, exploiting the Transformer's natural strength in selective copying over numerical calculation. A Q-Former-based frame compressor merges each frame's patch embeddings into a single text-conditioned token, which is concatenated with text tokens and fed into a Phi-3-Mini-3.8B LLM. A special <vid> token, when generated during inference, triggers a matching step: two MLPs project the <vid> hidden state (from the second-to-last LLM layer) and all frame-token hidden states (from the final layer) into a shared alignment space, and the timestamp is recovered by cosine-similarity argmax over frames. Two key modifications improve temporal continuity: bi-directional self-attention is enabled among video tokens (breaking the causal mask that would otherwise block inter-frame information flow), and the binary matching label is replaced with a smoothed 1/(α|t−t_gt|) distribution. The model is fine-tuned with LoRA adapters on the LLM and the frame compressor's attention layers, using the purpose-built E.T. Instruct 164K dataset (163,880 multi-event samples from 14 source datasets) with a joint language-modeling plus matching loss.

## Results

On E.T. Bench (7,289 samples, 12 tasks, 7,002 videos), E.T. Chat (3.8B) achieves the best open-source performance on most tasks: 38.6 F1 on [TVG], 30.8 F1 on [TAL], 62.5 F1 on [VHD], and 44.6 Acc on [RAR], substantially outperforming prior Video-LLMs like TimeChat (26.2 TVG F1) and LITA (22.2 TVG F1). Among commercial MLLMs, Gemini-1.5-Pro leads on [RAR] (61.1 Acc) and [TEM] (32.1 Rec), while GPT-4o achieves the highest [DVC] F1 (46.9). E.T. Chat narrows the gap to commercial models, reaching comparable [VHD] (60.9 vs. 60.8 for Gemini-1.5-Flash) and [DVC] F1 (39.8 vs. 46.9 for GPT-4o) on the 470-sample subset, while still trailing on [RAR] and [TEM].

## Baselines & Comparisons

E.T. Chat (3.8B) outperforms all open-source Video-LLMs on [TVG] (38.6 vs. 26.2 for TimeChat, 22.2 for LITA), [TAL] (30.8 vs. 18.2 for VTimeLLM, 18.0 for LITA), and [VHD] (62.5 vs. 48.2 for VTG-LLM, 40.5 for TimeChat), with gains of 12–22 points over the strongest prior open-source baselines. On [DVC] F1, LITA (39.7) and VTG-LLM (40.2) slightly edge out E.T. Chat (38.4), while on [TEM] Recall, TimeChat (18.0) marginally leads E.T. Chat (16.5). Against commercial MLLMs on the 470-sample subset, E.T. Chat† achieves competitive [VHD] (60.9 vs. 60.8 Gemini-1.5-Flash) and [DVC] F1 (39.8 vs. 46.9 GPT-4o), but trails Gemini-1.5-Pro on [RAR] (33.3 vs. 61.1) and [TEM] (14.8 vs. 32.1). Image-LLMs (e.g., Qwen-VL-Chat) perform comparably to Video-LLMs on short-video referring tasks but collapse on grounding (TVG F1: 16.2 vs. 38.6), confirming the importance of temporal modeling. Notably, Video-LLaMA-2 and Video-LLaVA fail to produce valid timestamp outputs on most grounding tasks (F1 ≈ 0), attributed to overfitting on instruction-tuning data lacking timestamp annotations.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the LLaMA-VID [51] frame compression architecture (Q-Former + context aggregator + projector) and the broader Video-LLM paradigm of visual encoder + projector + LLM. It departs from existing Video-LLM benchmarks (SEED-Bench, MVBench, EgoSchema, Video-Bench) that evaluate video-level understanding via MCQ, by introducing a unified 12-task taxonomy for event-level and time-sensitive understanding with open-ended outputs. The key architectural departure is reformulating timestamp prediction as an embedding matching problem rather than discrete token generation, motivated by the known weakness of Transformers in numerical computation. The instruction-tuning dataset E.T. Instruct 164K extends prior datasets like TimeIT [82] and the 723K Corpus [51] by focusing on multi-event, longer-duration videos with fine-grained temporal annotations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The benchmark reveals a critical gap: SOTA Video-LLMs that score well on existing video-level MCQ benchmarks perform near-randomly on event-level grounding tasks (F1 < 10% for several models), exposing that current training pipelines (short videos, coarse annotations, discrete token prediction) are fundamentally misaligned with real-world temporal understanding needs. The embedding matching approach for timestamps offers a practical architectural alternative, though absolute performance remains low (best grounding F1 ~38-43%), indicating the task is still far from solved. Key limitations include potential data leakage from using val/test splits of existing datasets, low spatial resolution (1 token per frame), and reliance on repurposed rather than self-collected annotations. Open questions include whether the embedding matching paradigm generalizes to higher spatial resolutions and whether the benchmark's rule-based evaluation adequately captures open-ended response quality.
