## Problem

Traditional active learning assumes that every instance selected by the algorithm will be labeled by a human, but in high-stakes settings (fraud investigation, medical biopsy approval), human experts may reject labeling requests due to regulation, domain knowledge, or algorithmic aversion. This means the most informative samples to the current ML classifier may never be labeled, wasting the human expert's examination budget. The paper formalizes this as the Active Learning with Instance Rejection (ALIR) problem and asks how to select labeling requests that maximize model improvement given both examination and labeling costs under unknown, heterogeneous human discretion behavior.

## Input / Output

The algorithm iteratively consumes an unlabeled pool, a growing labeled dataset D (only accepted instances), a human decision dataset L (all examined instances with accept/reject labels), and a total budget B that accounts for per-instance examination cost c_e and labeling cost c_l. At each step it outputs a single instance for the human to examine and potentially label, then updates both the ML classifier (on D) and the Bayesian human discretion model (on L). Performance is evaluated only on the subpopulation where the human has positive probability of labeling, since instances always rejected are excluded from the test set.

## Method

SEL-BALD extends the BALD mutual-information acquisition function by weighting it with the human acceptance probability e(x), yielding e-BALD (Theorem A.1 shows I(y;θ|x,D) = e(x)·I(y;θ|x,D,a=1)). Since e(x) is unknown, the paper introduces Joint-BALD, which adds a second information-gain term measuring epistemic uncertainty about the human discretion model itself, so the algorithm simultaneously exploits informative samples for the classifier and explores to learn e(x). Two practical variants control exploration: Joint-BALD-UCB uses an empirical quantile (hyperparameter β) of the MC-dropout posterior over e(x) as an upper confidence bound, while Joint-BALD-TS samples a single posterior draw per iteration (Thompson Sampling). Both the predictive model and the human discretion model are 3-layer Bayesian neural networks with MC dropout (T=40 samples), and the full loop re-trains both models after every accepted or rejected instance until the budget is exhausted.

## Results

On the synthetic two-circle dataset (budget 450), Joint-BALD-UCB achieves 0.99 accuracy and Joint-BALD-TS achieves 0.98, compared to 0.78 for RANDOM and 0.55 for Naive-BALD. Joint-BALD-UCB and Joint-BALD-TS also balance the examination/labeling budget more effectively (~50% each) versus Naive-BALD which spends 95% of budget on examination with only 5% labeling. Across real-world datasets (Give-Me-some-Credit, MNIST, Fashion MNIST, CIFAR-10, Adult, Mushroom), Joint-BALD-UCB and Joint-BALD-TS consistently outperform both RANDOM and Naive-BALD in both accuracy and number of samples labeled, with the gap widening as budget increases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper compares four baselines: RANDOM (uniform random selection), Naive-BALD (standard BALD ignoring human rejection), e-BALD (BALD weighted by estimated label probability), and Joint-BALD (joint information gain without exploration bonus). Naive-BALD performs worst (0.55 accuracy on synthetic data) because it repeatedly selects high-information instances that humans will reject, wasting 95% of budget on examination. RANDOM achieves 0.78 accuracy but still wastes 91% of budget on examination. e-BALD improves labeling efficiency (61% labeling) but relies on a noisy early estimate of the human discretion model, leading to greedy exploitation of the wrong region and only 0.76 accuracy. Joint-BALD-UCB and Joint-BALD-TS, which add exploration via UCB or Thompson Sampling over the human discretion model, achieve near-perfect accuracy (0.99 and 0.98) by balancing exploration of the human behavior model with exploitation of informative samples. On all six real-world datasets, the UCB and TS variants consistently dominate both RANDOM and Naive-BALD, with the advantage growing at larger budgets where the initial human behavior estimate becomes less reliable.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the Bayesian Active Learning with Disagreement (BALD) framework [Houlsby et al., 2011; Gal et al., 2017] to the Active Learning with Instance Rejection (ALIR) setting, where a human decision maker may reject labeling requests. It departs from the selective labels literature [Lakkaraju et al., 2017; De-Arteaga et al., 2018], which focuses on evaluating model performance under selective observation, by instead addressing how to *acquire* informative labels when human discretion is heterogeneous and unknown. The exploration mechanisms (UCB [Auer, 2002] and Thompson Sampling [Thompson, 1933]) are borrowed from the bandit literature and applied here to the human discretion model, distinguishing this work from prior abstention-feedback active learning [Fang et al., 2012; Yan et al., 2015; Robinson et al., 2024] that lack information-theoretic guarantees in the deep Bayesian setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a practically important gap: in high-stakes domains like fraud investigation and medical diagnosis, active learning algorithms that ignore human rejection behavior waste most of their budget on examination rather than labeling, as demonstrated by the dramatic accuracy gap (0.55 vs. 0.99) between Naive-BALD and Joint-BALD-UCB. Key limitations include the assumption of a single homogeneous human decision maker, the risk of model misspecification for the human discretion function, and unaddressed fairness implications in prioritization. Open questions include extending to multiple heterogeneous human decision makers, integrating semi-supervised learning, and studying fairness constraints in ALIR.
