## Problem

A growing body of work applies pretrained large language models (GPT-2, LLaMA) to time series forecasting, hypothesizing that sequential modeling capabilities transfer from text to time series. However, no prior study has systematically ablated the LLM component to determine whether it actually contributes to forecasting performance or whether the surrounding encoding and projection layers are sufficient on their own.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241921/cropped_page3_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

Each method consumes a multivariate time series window (e.g., 96 timesteps across 7–862 channels) that is first processed via instance normalization and patching into token sequences, then projected to match the LLM's embedding dimension. The model outputs a point forecast over a prediction horizon (96/192/336/720 steps for ETT, Traffic, Electricity, Weather; 24/36/48/60 for Illness), evaluated by MAE and MSE. The ablation variants retain the same input encoding and output projection, differing only in what sits between them.

## Method

The paper proposes three ablation variants applied uniformly to three popular LLM-based forecasters (Time-LLM, CALF, OneFitsAll): (i) w/o LLM removes the language model entirely, passing encoded tokens directly to the output projection; (ii) LLM2Attn replaces the LLM with a single randomly-initialized multi-head attention layer; (iii) LLM2Trsf replaces it with a single randomly-initialized transformer block. Beyond these structural ablations, the authors test whether pretraining matters by randomly re-initializing LLM weights, whether positional/sequential information is used by shuffling input timesteps, and whether LLMs help in few-shot (10% data) settings. They further identify that a minimal encoder combining patching with one layer of attention (PAttn) plus a linear projection matches or exceeds LLM-based performance, suggesting the encoding—not the language model—drives forecasting quality.

## Results

Across 13 datasets and two metrics (MAE and MSE), the LLM-free ablations outperform the full LLM-based methods in 26/26 cases for Time-LLM, 22/26 for CALF, and 19/26 for OneFitsAll. The 'w/o LLM' ablation (simply removing the language model) achieves the best MAE on 12/13 datasets for Time-LLM (e.g., 0.419 vs. 0.432 on ETTh1) and 11/13 for OneFitsAll, while using only 0.55M–9.38M parameters versus 6,652M–91M for the full LLM versions. Training time drops by up to three orders of magnitude (e.g., Time-LLM takes 3,003 min on Weather vs. 1.91 min for w/o LLM), and inference time is 2.3–28.2× faster for ablations. In few-shot settings (10% data), LLaMA-based Time-LLM ties w/o LLM (8 wins each), while GPT-2-based CALF is outperformed by ablations in 12/14 cases.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper ablates three popular LLM-based forecasters—Time-LLM (LLaMA-7B, 6,652M params), CALF (GPT-2, 180M params), and OneFitsAll (GPT-2, 91M params)—by removing or replacing the LLM with a single attention layer or transformer block. For Time-LLM, all three ablations outperform the full model on every dataset for both MAE and MSE (26/26 cases), with 'w/o LLM' achieving the best MAE on 12/13 datasets using only 0.55M parameters. For CALF, ablations win in 22/26 cases; 'LLM2Trsf' is the strongest ablation (11 wins) while the original CALF wins only 4. For OneFitsAll, ablations win in 19/26 cases, with 'w/o LLM' winning 11/13 datasets. Notably, in few-shot (10% data) settings, LLaMA-based Time-LLM ties w/o LLM (8 wins each), and GPT-2-based CALF is outperformed by ablations in 12/14 cases, indicating no few-shot advantage from pretraining. The computational cost difference is dramatic: Time-LLM requires 3,003 minutes to train on Weather vs. 1.91 minutes for w/o LLM, a ~1,570× reduction, while maintaining or improving accuracy.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper carves new ground as a systematic empirical critique of the rapidly growing body of LLM-based time series forecasting work (OneFitsAll, Time-LLM, CALF), rather than building on a specific method lineage. It positions itself alongside the parallel literature on simple, efficient time series models (DLinear, FITS) that have already shown transformer-free architectures can be competitive, but extends that argument to the LLM setting by isolating the LLM component through three ablation strategies. Conceptually, it departs from the working assumption in the LLM-time-series literature that pretrained language models encode transferable sequential dependencies applicable to numerical time series.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The findings challenge a significant and well-funded research trend (1,200+ GitHub stars across the three ablated methods) by showing that the LLM component is largely dispensable for standard forecasting, with simpler models achieving comparable or better accuracy at up to three orders of magnitude lower compute. The paper explicitly acknowledges limitations—evaluation is restricted to forecasting on uniform-interval series and three specific architectures—leaving open whether LLMs might prove essential for non-uniform series, classification, question-answering, or multimodal time-series-language tasks. The practical implication is that practitioners should default to lightweight encoders (patching + attention) unless a task genuinely requires textual reasoning, and the conceptual implication is that the field should redirect LLM-time-series research toward tasks where language understanding is actually the bottleneck.
