## Problem

Despite growing use of GNNs for power flow, optimal power flow, and cascading failure prediction, no publicly available graph dataset tailored for GNN training and benchmarking exists for electrical power grid applications. The OGB taxonomy reveals a complete absence of graph-level datasets in the society domain, and no real-world power grid dataset provides ground-truth explanations for evaluating GNN explainability methods.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243516/cropped_page3_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

Each graph instance represents a single loading condition (sampled at 15-minute resolution over one year, yielding 34,944 graphs per grid for node-level tasks) with node features (bus-type-dependent quantities like P_g, P_d, Q_g, Q_d, V, θ, N_loads, N_gen) and edge features (branch conductance/susceptance for PF/OPF; active/reactive power flow, line reactance, and line rating for cascading failure). For graph-level cascading failure tasks, the input graph has branches removed per an outage list, and outputs include a binary stability label, a 4-class cascading failure category (combining DNS>0/0 with cascading/no-cascading), a continuous DNS value in MW, and a Boolean explainability mask over edges marking which branches tripped during the cascade.

## Method

The dataset is constructed by running MATPOWER for AC power flow and optimal power flow simulations, and the Cascades AC physics-based model (validated against WECC historical blackout data) for cascading failure simulations across four real-world grids (IEEE24, IEEE39, IEEE118, UK). Benchmarking employs four GNN architectures (GCN, GAT, GINE, Transformer) with grid search over 1–3 message-passing layers and 8/16/32 hidden dimensions, trained for 50 epochs with Adam and learning-rate scheduling. For explainability, the paper evaluates 10+ methods spanning gradient-based (Saliency, Integrated Gradient, Grad-CAM), perturbation-based (Occlusion, GNNExplainer, PGM-Explainer, SubgraphX), and generative (GSAT, D4Explainer, RCExplainer) explainers using both human-centric balanced accuracy against ground-truth cascading edges and model-centric fidelity+ scores, following the GraphFramEx systematic evaluation framework with top-k sparse edge masks. The key empirical findings are that Transformer with 3 MPLs consistently outperforms other architectures, regression tasks remain challenging (R² ≤ 0.43), and GSAT is the only method that simultaneously achieves high accuracy and faithfulness, bridging the gap between physics-based ground-truth explanations and model-centric reasoning.

## Results

For node-level power flow and optimal power flow tasks, the Transformer (3 MPL layers, 32 hidden units) achieves the lowest MSE across all four grids, ranging from 2.02e-05 (UK PF) to 8.99e-05 (IEEE24 OPF). For graph-level cascading failure classification, balanced accuracy reaches 0.9982 (IEEE118 binary) and 0.9962 (IEEE118 multi-class), with the best models being Transformer or GINE depending on the grid. Graph-level regression (predicting demand not served in MW) is notably weaker, with R² peaking at only 0.43 on IEEE24 and falling below 0.26 on other grids. For explainability, GSAT achieves a balanced accuracy of 0.9 on IEEE24 and ~0.8 on UK, significantly outperforming all other methods, while gradient-based methods (IntegratedGrad, Saliency) yield the highest faithfulness scores.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Among the four GNN architectures, TransformerConv consistently outperforms GCNConv, GATConv, and GINEConv on both node-level and graph-level tasks, with GCN showing the weakest performance due to its inability to leverage edge features critical for power flow modeling. GINEConv is the second-best architecture, particularly strong on IEEE118 and UK graph-level classification (0.9982 and 0.9951 balanced accuracy respectively). For explainability, GSAT is the only method that simultaneously achieves high human-centric accuracy (0.9 on IEEE24, ~0.8 on UK) and high model-centric faithfulness, bridging the gap between physics-based ground-truth explanations and model predictions. Gradient-based methods (Saliency, IntegratedGrad) produce the most faithful explanations on the three IEEE grids but fail to identify any actual cascading edges, while Occlusion and Grad-CAM are most faithful on the UK dataset. On IEEE39 and IEEE118, no explainability method (including the Truth baseline) successfully identifies cascading edges within the top-k explanatory set, attributed to the extremely low proportion of Category A instances (<1%) and the high ratio of total edges to cascading edges (0.03 for IEEE118). The Random and Inverse baselines confirm the evaluation is meaningful, with Random scoring near chance and Inverse at 0.5 accuracy as expected.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper positions itself at the intersection of GNN-based power system analysis and graph explainability benchmarking. Prior work has applied GNNs to power flow [5,6], optimal power flow [7,8], and cascading failure prediction [9-12], but relied on synthetic grids or ad-hoc IEEE test cases without providing comprehensive, GNN-ready datasets. PowerGraph departs from existing power grid datasets (EGS, PSML, Simbench) by being explicitly structured for graph neural network training with node/edge features, and fills a gap in the OGB taxonomy by providing the first graph-level dataset in the society domain. For the explainability component, the paper adopts the GraphFramEx systematic evaluation framework [37] and benchmarks against a broad set of non-generative and generative explainers, but is the first to do so on a real-world power grid dataset with physics-based ground-truth explanations.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

PowerGraph provides a much-needed standardized benchmark for the GNN-in-power-systems community, enabling fair comparison of architectures and explainability methods on consistent data. A key open question surfaced by the results is the fundamental misalignment between physics-based ground-truth explanations (cascading edges) and what the model actually relies on for its predictions — the Truth baseline is not faithful, and only GSAT bridges both perspectives. Regression performance remains weak (R² peaks at 0.43), highlighting that GNNs are not yet reliable surrogates for physics-based DNS estimation. Future work on temporal graph representations of cascading stages and scaling to larger synthetic grids (e.g., [49]) are identified as natural next steps.
