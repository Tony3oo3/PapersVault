## Problem

While in-context learning in transformers has been studied in supervised settings such as linear regression, the mechanisms by which transformers learn to extract statistical patterns from sequential context remain poorly understood. This paper introduces a controlled Markov chain sequence modeling task to study how in-context learning of n-gram statistics emerges during training, revealing multi-phase learning dynamics and the formation of statistical induction heads that compute accurate next-token probabilities from in-context bigram counts.

## Input / Output

Each training example is a token sequence of length T (typically 100) generated by sampling a transition matrix from a flat Dirichlet prior over k states (primarily k=2 or 3) and then drawing tokens from the resulting Markov chain, initialized at its stationary distribution. The model must output a probability distribution over the next token by computing in-context bigram statistics (empirical counts of token pairs), rather than relying on training-data statistics. The task is inherently non-deterministic: even with full knowledge of the transition matrix, optimal prediction requires a Bayesian posterior over the next state given the observed context.

## Method

The paper trains two-layer attention-only transformers with causal masking and relative positional encodings, showing they form statistical induction heads: the first layer copies the previous token at each position, while the second layer attends to all positions whose preceding token matches the current token, thereby computing empirical bigram counts. To enable theoretical analysis, the authors define a minimal linear model parameterized by a k×k matrix W (mimicking second-layer attention) and a vector v (mimicking relative positional embeddings), and prove via population gradient descent that the second layer aligns first (learning a mixture of identity and uniform attention), which then provides the gradient signal for the first layer to learn the correct one-step-back positional offset. Empirically, training exhibits a clear phase transition from a suboptimal unigram strategy to the correct bigram strategy, and the authors demonstrate that the presence of the simpler unigram solution acts as a distractor that measurably delays convergence to the optimal bigram solution.

## Results

Two-layer attention-only transformers trained on ICL-MC converge near the Bayes-optimal (bigram) solution, but learning proceeds through distinct phases: an initial rapid drop from uniform predictions to the unigram strategy, a long plateau, and a sudden phase transition to the bigram strategy. The KL-divergence between the model's output and the unigram strategy drops first, then the KL-divergence to the bigram strategy drops sharply while the unigram KL increases, confirming the phase transition. Removing the unigram signal from the training distribution (using doubly stochastic transition matrices) accelerates convergence to the bigram solution, even when evaluated on the full distribution. The minimal model analysis (Proposition 3.1) proves that two population gradient descent steps suffice: the first step (with step size O(1/T²)) aligns the second layer weights W to a mixture of identity and uniform attention, and the second step (with step size Ω(T)) learns the positional encoding v₁ = Ω(η₂ log T) that enables bigram counting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper is a mechanistic study rather than a method comparison, so it does not benchmark against competing architectures or algorithms. Instead, it compares model configurations and data distributions to isolate the factors driving the observed learning dynamics. A single-layer transformer fails to undergo the phase transition or converge beyond the unigram strategy even when trained 8× longer, establishing that two layers are necessary for bigram learning. Adding an MLP layer to the two-layer attention-only transformer produces qualitatively identical training dynamics (Figure 10), confirming that attention alone suffices. Training on a doubly stochastic distribution (where unigram and uniform strategies coincide) yields faster convergence than training on a mixture that includes unigram-only examples, demonstrating that the simpler unigram solution acts as a distractor that delays bigram learning. The minimal model (Eq. 4) reproduces the same two-phase training dynamics as the full transformer, validating the theoretical analysis. Results extend to k=8 states (requiring longer sequences of length 200) and to trigram (n=3) learning, where the model exhibits four stages corresponding to uniform, unigram, bigram, and trigram strategies.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Bayesian interpretation of in-context learning (Xie et al., ICLR 2022) to define unigram and bigram strategies as posterior distributions, and extends the induction head literature (Elhage et al., Olsson et al.) by showing that transformers learn 'statistical induction heads' that compute empirical bigram counts rather than simply copying the token following the most recent occurrence. It departs from Bietti et al. (2023), who study a related Markov chain task but with a single shared chain and deterministic trigger tokens, by using a fresh transition matrix per sequence so that statistical induction heads are necessary and sufficient. The phase-transition and simplicity-bias threads (Power et al., Chen et al., Kalimeris et al.) provide the conceptual vocabulary, but this paper is the first to show that a simpler in-context solution can actively delay convergence to the correct one in a controlled setting.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a minimal, analytically tractable setting in which the multi-stage dynamics of ICL—uniform → unigram → bigram—can be precisely characterized, offering mechanistic explanations (layer alignment, gradient signal structure) that are difficult to extract from large-scale language models. The finding that the unigram solution acts as a distractor rather than a stepping stone reframes simplicity bias as potentially harmful, with implications for data curriculum design and training strategies. Key limitations include the synthetic nature of the task, small state spaces (k ≤ 8), and reliance on a simplified linear model for theory; open questions include whether the same dynamics appear in natural language, how they scale to higher-order n-grams, and whether targeted data mixtures can eliminate the unigram plateau without sacrificing generalization.
