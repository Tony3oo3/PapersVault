## Problem

Existing causal reasoning benchmarks for LLMs fall into two disconnected camps: open-ended causal commonsense reasoning (CCR) datasets grounded in real-world events but lacking formal causal-theory validation, and symbolic causal-inference-engine benchmarks with rigorous theoretical backing but no real-world grounding. This gap makes it difficult to rigorously test whether LLMs genuinely understand causal mechanisms in everyday activities or merely memorize surface patterns. COLD bridges this gap by constructing a closed, real-world-grounded causal system that supports exhaustive, theory-driven evaluation.

## Input / Output

The primary evaluation input is a causal query triplet (a premise event plus two candidate events) framed as a multiple-choice question asking which choice is the more plausible cause or effect; the model must output a single choice token (A or B). For the deeper ATE-based analysis, the input is an event trajectory (a sequence of events up to E₁) combined with an intervention prompt specifying do(E₁) or do(¬E₁), and the output is a causal strength estimate Δ computed as the difference in predicted probabilities. Across five daily activities, the framework generates ~9.5 million instance-level samples from 4,392 skeleton triplets, enabling exhaustive evaluation that approaches the mini-Turing test in scale.

## Method

COLD builds on the DeScript crowdsourced script corpus to create observational DAGs (G_o) for five daily activities, then manually constructs causal graphs (G_c) that encode which events cause which others. Using d-separation on G_c, the framework identifies independent event clusters and generates causal query triplets where the d-separated node becomes the incorrect choice, with temporal order from G_o determining cause vs. effect labels. The closed-activity design naturally satisfies SUTVA (each event has exactly two versions: occurring or not), enabling well-defined interventions. For evaluation, the paper uses two complementary schemes: (1) prompt-based MCQA where LLMs select the more plausible cause/effect, and (2) Average Treatment Effect estimation via the backdoor criterion, where language models serve as probability estimators p̂(E₂|E₁, z=t) over trajectories z, and Δ_M = p_M(E₂|do(E₁)) − p_M(E₂|do(¬E₁)) quantifies causal strength. The backdoor adjustment consistently improves over naive temporal-ordering baselines, with gemma-7b achieving the highest ATE accuracy (72.80% on cake, 77.80% on shopping).

## Results

On the COLD benchmark (10K sampled causal query triplets per activity), performance varies dramatically across LLMs: smaller models (gpt-neo-125M, gpt-neo-2.7B, gpt-j-6B) perform at or below chance (~50%), while stronger models like phi-2 (85.14% on cake, 71.74% on bus) and gemma-7b (81.47% on cake, 70.29% on bus) achieve the highest success rates. Even the best LLMs fall well short of the 92.20% human performance recorded in the validation study. In the ATE-based evaluation (Table 4), applying backdoor adjustments consistently improves causal strength estimation over the temporal-only scheme by 5–15 percentage points across models, with gemma-7b achieving the highest backdoor-adjusted accuracy (72.80% on cake, 77.80% on shopping).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates 10 open-weight LLMs as the primary comparison axis rather than comparing a single method against baselines. Three models (gpt-neo-125M, gpt-neo-2.7B, gpt-j-6B) perform at exactly chance level (~50%), indicating no causal reasoning ability on this benchmark. gpt-neo-1.3B performs below chance (42–46%), suggesting systematic bias. The strongest performers are phi-2 (71.74–85.14%), gemma-7b (70.29–81.47%), and Meta-Llama-3-8B (67.39–80.79%), all of which substantially outperform the chance-level models by 20–35 percentage points. However, even the best LLM (phi-2 at 85.14% on cake) trails the 92.20% human average by roughly 7 points, and performance drops notably on less common activities like bus riding and tree planting. In the ATE-based analysis (Table 4), backdoor-adjusted estimates (Δ_M) consistently outperform the temporal-only scheme by 5–15 points for most models, with gemma-7b showing the largest gains (e.g., 46.80→72.80 on cake), confirming that explicit causal adjustment improves over relying on temporal ordering alone.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper bridges two previously separate lines of causal reasoning research in NLP: (1) real-world grounded causal commonsense reasoning (CCR) benchmarks like COPA and EventCausality, which capture commonsense but lack formal causal-theory backing, and (2) symbolic causal inference engine approaches (Jin et al. 2023, 2024) that are theoretically rigorous but disconnected from real-world events. It builds on script knowledge (DeScript corpus, Wanzare et al. 2016) and the DAG construction from ScriptWorld (Joshi et al. 2023) to create observational graphs, then manually constructs causal graphs over these events. The evaluation protocol follows the MCQA prompting strategy of Robinson and Wingate (2023), and the exhaustive query generation is inspired by Pearl and Mackenzie's (2018) mini-Turing test concept.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

COLD provides the first framework that simultaneously offers real-world grounding, a formal causal graph, SUTVA adherence, and an exhaustive (~9.5M) set of causal queries, enabling rigorous evaluation that resists memorization. The finding that even the strongest open-weight LLMs (phi-2, gemma-7b) achieve only 70–85% on trivial daily activities, while humans score 92%, underscores a persistent gap in LLM causal understanding. Key limitations include coverage of only 5 activities, restriction to simple pairwise causal triplets, and use of unconditional d-separation. Open questions include whether causal reasoning can be acquired through language modeling on the generated trajectories, and whether the framework extends to more complex causal structures (confounders, long causal chains, conditional independence).
