## Problem

Maximizing mutual information between states and skill descriptors is the standard objective for unsupervised skill discovery, but the paper demonstrates it is ambiguous for exploration: two skill sets can achieve identical MI while one covers the state space far more effectively. The gap is that MI maximization does not explicitly encourage state coverage, so skills may converge to compact, well-separated regions rather than exhaustively exploring the environment.

## Input / Output

The policy network takes a state vector and a continuous skill descriptor z ∈ R^20 as input and outputs an action distribution; a separate C-Learning classifier takes (s₁, a, s₂, z) tuples to estimate the SSR ratio m(s₁,s₂,z) = p(s₂|s₁,a,z)/p(s₂). The algorithm produces a set of n=6 evolving skill policies whose joint state occupancy covers the environment, along with per-skill SSR estimates and a per-skill uncertainty map u_t(s,z) that identifies the next target state for each skill. Skills are dynamic across epochs, continuously shifting toward under-visited regions rather than converging to a static partition.

## Method

LEADS derives a new objective G(θ) by starting from the Jensen lower bound on MI expressed through the SSR, then (i) adding a denominator 1+Σ_{z'} m(s₁,s₂,z') to penalize skills that overlap in state space, and (ii) replacing the natural sampling distribution p(s|z) with a Dirac distribution δ(s|z) placed on the state maximizing an uncertainty measure u_t(s,z). This measure combines an exploration term (log-ratio of the current skill's SSR at s against the cumulative historical SSR of all skills, equivalent to a per-state KL novelty score) and a repulsion term (sum of log-ratios of the current skill's SSR against each other skill's SSR, both from the initial state and from each skill's previous target state). The algorithm proceeds in epochs: roll out episodes per skill, train the SSR via on-policy C-Learning, compute u_t to select target states, then perform SGD on θ to maximize G(θ)+λ_h·H(θ) while updating the SSR off-policy. This decouples the exploration planning (via SSR and uncertainty) from the policy update, allowing skills to continuously migrate toward unexplored regions.

## Results

LEADS achieves the highest state-space coverage on 5 out of 6 evaluation tasks, measured as the percentage of discretized state cells visited. Headline numbers: 100.0% on Easy Maze, 96.0% on U Maze, 90.8% on Hard Maze, 89.7% on Fetch-Reach, 87.4% on Finger, and 85.4% on Fetch-Slide. The largest margins over the next-best baseline appear on U Maze (+16.2 pp over CSD/LSD) and Fetch-Slide (+22.4 pp over CSD). The sole exception is the Finger environment, where CSD achieves 96.2% versus LEADS's 87.4%.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LEADS outperforms all seven baselines on five of six tasks (Easy, U Maze, Hard, Fetch-Reach, Fetch-Slide), with the largest gaps over RND (up to +68 pp on Fetch-Reach) and SMM (up to +63 pp on Fetch-Reach). Against the closest skill-based competitors, LEADS leads CSD by +16.2 pp on U Maze and +22.4 pp on Fetch-Slide, and leads LSD by +20.8 pp on Hard Maze. The only task where LEADS is not best is Finger, where CSD reaches 96.2% versus LEADS's 87.4% (p = 0.071, not statistically significant). DIAYN, the original mutual-information-maximization baseline, is consistently outperformed by LEADS across all six environments, confirming the paper's argument that standard MI maximization is insufficient for exploration. Among pure-exploration methods (RND, NGU), LEADS dominates on every task, with NGU being the strongest non-skill baseline (76.4% on Finger) yet still trailing LEADS by 11 pp on that task.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the mutual information maximization framework for unsupervised skill discovery (DIAYN, VIC) by arguing that MI is an ambiguous objective for exploration: two skill sets with identical MI can have very different state coverage. It builds directly on C-Learning for successor state representation (SSR) estimation and the SSR formalism of Blier et al., repurposing the SSR not just as a density estimator but as the backbone of a novel uncertainty measure that simultaneously promotes exploration (visiting under-covered states) and inter-skill repulsion. The key departure from prior MI-based methods (DIAYN, SMM, LSD, CSD, METRA) is that LEADS skills are dynamic—evolving across epochs to chase unexplored regions—rather than converging to a static set, and the objective explicitly incorporates an exploration bonus within the MI-maximization framing rather than treating exploration and diversity as separate concerns.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

LEADS provides a principled, reward-free mechanism for exhaustive state-space coverage via skill diversity, outperforming both skill-based and pure-exploration baselines across maze and robotic control tasks. The main limitation is its dependence on reliable SSR estimation: C-Learning fails in high-dimensional MuJoCo environments like HalfCheetah, suggesting that LEADS's practical reach is bounded by the quality of available SSR estimators. An open question is whether newer contrastive SSR methods (e.g., Eysenbach et al. 2022, Zheng et al. 2023) can extend LEADS to harder continuous-control domains. The dynamic-skill paradigm also raises the question of how to automatically adapt the number of skills, which the authors leave as future work.
