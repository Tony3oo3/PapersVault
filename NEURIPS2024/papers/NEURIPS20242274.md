## Problem

While knowledge distillation (teacher→student) is a well-established paradigm for improving LLMs, the reverse direction—whether the act of teaching can improve the teacher model itself—remains unexplored. Inspired by the Feynman learning method in human education, this paper asks whether LLMs can 'learn by teaching' (LbT) to enhance their own reasoning, potentially enabling continuous self-improvement without relying on human-annotated data or stronger teacher models.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242274/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a text problem (math word problem, LeetCode coding problem, or classification instance) along with a teacher LLM and one or more student LLMs. The teacher generates multiple rationale-answer (TR-TA) pairs; each pair is then used as an in-context learning exemplar for students to solve similar exam problems (EPs), and student accuracy on those EPs constitutes the LbT score. M1 outputs a single selected answer, M2 outputs a DPO-fine-tuned teacher model, and M3 outputs a refined set of positive and negative ICL exemplars that improve both student and teacher performance.

## Method

The paper instantiates three LbT levels as three methods. M1 (L1, observing feedback) implements a search-based output generation pipeline where each teacher-generated rationale is scored by its ability to teach students via ICL to correctly solve similar problems; the best answer is selected via MAX or SUM aggregation of LbT scores, outperforming self-consistency by 3.3–18.2% on MATH. M2 (L2, learning from feedback) uses these LbT scores to construct preference pairs and fine-tune the teacher with DPO, showing that LbT scores capture rationale quality beyond binary correctness (e.g., penalizing verbose or non-generalizable reasoning). M3 (L3, iterative learning) prompts the teacher to reflect on student failure cases and iteratively refine ICL exemplar sets, demonstrating that feedback from multiple diverse (and weaker) students accelerates improvement over self-teaching. The core mechanism across all methods is replacing self-evaluation or correctness matching with student-performance-based scoring, which provides automatic, fine-grained quality signals for rationales.

## Results

On 181 MATH test problems with 256 TR-TA pairs, M1 (SUM) improves GPT-4o's accuracy from 87.84% (greedy) to 96.69% (with GPT-4o mini as student), a +4.98% gain over self-consistency (91.71%). For weaker models, gains are larger: LLaMA3-8B self-teaching improves from 45.85% to 82.87% (+18.23% over SC), and Mistral-7B teaching LLaMA3-8B improves from 19.88% to 53.59% (+12.71% over SC). For M2 (DPO fine-tuning), LLaMA3-8B achieves 32.2% on 500 MATH problems vs. 30.4% for correctness-based DPO and 29.0% original. For M3 (iterative prompt optimization), using multiple diverse students (LLaMA3-70B+8B+Mistral-7B) as feedback sources yields 67.09% F1 on Liar after 5 iterations, outperforming self-teaching (63.96%).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is Self-Consistency (SC) [Wang et al., ICLR 2023], which samples multiple rationales and selects the majority answer. M1 (SUM) outperforms SC in all 11 teacher-student configurations tested, with improvements ranging from +3.31% (LLaMA3-70B→Mistral-7B) to +18.23% (LLaMA3-8B self-teaching). Notably, M1 also outperforms SC with far fewer samples: using only 24 TR-TA pairs, M1 still beats SC with 256 pairs by 0.17%–8.29% (Table A6). The second baseline is self-evaluation scoring [Xie et al., NeurIPS 2024], where the model rates its own rationales. On 70 MATH problems with 16 TR-TA pairs, M1 (SUM) consistently outperforms self-evaluation: e.g., GPT-4 self-teaching achieves 72.86% vs. 68.57% for self-eval, and GPT-3.5→Mistral-7B achieves 57.14% vs. 52.86%. For M2, the baseline is correctness-guided DPO (using only binary correct/incorrect labels); M2 with LbT scores achieves 32.2% vs. 30.4%, showing that LbT provides finer-grained preference signals than binary correctness.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper inverts the dominant learning-from-teacher (LfT) paradigm in LLMs—where knowledge distillation and synthetic-data generation transfer knowledge from strong to weak models—by proposing learning-by-teaching (LbT), where a teacher model improves itself by observing how student models respond to its rationales. Methodologically, M1 plugs an LbT-based rationale scorer into the well-established search-based output generation pipeline (self-consistency, tree of thoughts), M2 feeds LbT scores into DPO for preference-based fine-tuning, and M3 adapts iterative prompt optimization by replacing self-evaluation with multi-student feedback. The conceptual lineage draws from the Feynman learning method in human education and from prior work on weak-to-strong generalization, but the specific mechanism of using in-context-learning student performance on similar problems as a rationale quality signal is novel.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper opens a promising direction for continuous model improvement without relying on human-annotated data or access to stronger teacher models, which is a key bottleneck for frontier LLMs. The weak-to-strong generalization finding—that GPT-4o improves from 87.84% to 96.69% on MATH by teaching GPT-4o mini—suggests LbT could help superhuman models self-improve. However, significant limitations remain: the method requires carefully selected similar exam problems (EPs), incurs substantial additional inference cost, and the study is explicitly preliminary with limited task coverage. Open questions include whether LbT-based scoring generalizes to open-ended tasks without functional variants, whether the TP-EP similarity requirement can be relaxed, and whether the approach scales to more complex multi-step reasoning.
