## Problem

Training generative models for combinatorial problems like the Job Shop Scheduling Problem typically requires either expensive optimal solutions from exact solvers (supervised learning) or careful formulation of a Markov Decision Process with reward design (reinforcement learning), both of which are costly and fragile. Self-supervised and semi-supervised paradigms, which learn from unlabeled data, have seen almost no application in the JSP or broader combinatorial optimization literature, leaving a gap in how to train high-quality constructive models without external annotations.

## Input / Output

The model takes a JSP instance encoded as a disjunctive graph where each operation vertex carries 15 hand-crafted features (processing time, job/machine quartiles, relative position within job and machine). The encoder processes the full graph in a single non-autoregressive forward pass, while the decoder operates autoregressively: at each of |O| steps it receives an 11-dimensional context vector tracking the partial solution state (completion times, machine loads, quartile positions) and outputs a probability distribution over jobs. The output is a complete operation schedule; multiple solutions are sampled in parallel by stochastic decoding, and the makespan of each is computed to select the best.

## Method

The architecture is a Pointer-Network-inspired encoder-decoder: the encoder stacks two Graph Attention Network layers over the disjunctive graph to produce operation embeddings, and the decoder combines a Memory Network (Multi-Head Attention over job context vectors) with a Classifier Network (FNN over concatenated operation embedding and job state) to score each candidate job. The key training contribution is SLIM (Self-Labeling Improvement Method): at each training step, β solutions are sampled in parallel from the current model, the one with minimum makespan is selected as a pseudo-label, and the model is updated via cross-entropy on that solution's decision sequence. This eliminates the need for optimal solutions, MDP formulation, or reward engineering, requiring only the ability to generate multiple solutions and evaluate them by the problem objective. Unlike the Cross-Entropy Method, SLIM trains a single global model across many instances using gradient descent rather than per-instance parameter optimization, and unlike RL it avoids partial-reward noise by conditioning on the full solution quality.

## Results

On Taillard's benchmark, the proposed Graph Model (GM) trained with SLIM achieves an average Percentage Gap (PG) of 13.4% in greedy mode and 7.8% when sampling 512 solutions (GM₅₁₂), outperforming all constructive baselines. On Demirkol's benchmark, GM₅₁₂ achieves 13.1% PG, again the best constructive result. On extremely large instances (200×20 to 500×40), GM₅₁₂ produces better average makespans than CP-Sat (e.g., 21794.5 vs 21848.5 on 200×20) in 31 seconds vs. 3600 seconds.

## Baselines & Comparisons

GM₅₁₂ (7.8% avg PG on Taillard) outperforms all greedy and randomized constructive baselines: PDRs (14.5–17.2% randomized), INSA (14.8%), SBH (14.8%), L2D (20.5%), and CL (10.5%). Against non-constructive approaches, GM₅₁₂ roughly matches the RL-enhanced metaheuristic L2S₅ₖ (7.4%) while requiring only seconds vs. minutes of computation, and clearly outperforms NLS_A (11.3%), L2S₅₀₀ (12.2%), and MIP/Gurobi (8.4% on Taillard, 14.6% on Demirkol). However, CP-Sat remains the strongest overall (2.0% on Taillard, 3.5% on Demirkol), though on extremely large instances (≥200×20) GM₅₁₂ actually produces better makespans than CP-Sat in a fraction of the time. On the TSP generalization test, SLIM trains the Attention Model to match POMO20 (trained for hundreds of epochs) after just one epoch (100k steps), demonstrating cross-problem applicability.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the Pointer Network encoder-decoder architecture and GAT-based graph encoding for JSP, but departs from the dominant RL training paradigm (PPO-based methods like L2D and CL) by introducing SLIM, a self-labeling strategy that samples multiple solutions and trains via cross-entropy on the best one. Conceptually, it draws on pseudo-labeling from semi-supervised learning and shares the sample-and-select structure of the Cross-Entropy Method, but differs by training a single global model across instances rather than optimizing per-instance mixture models. The authors explicitly position SLIM as simpler than RL (no MDP formulation, no reward design) and as avoiding the degenerate solutions that plague other self-labeling approaches by jointly using probabilistic generation and objective-based selection.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

SLIM offers a practical simplification for training generative models on combinatorial problems: it eliminates the need for expensive MIP-generated optimal solutions and the complexity of RL reward design, while matching or exceeding state-of-the-art learning-based JSP solvers. The demonstration on TSP (matching POMO20 after one epoch) suggests broad applicability beyond scheduling. Key limitations include the inefficiency of using only one sampled solution per instance for gradient updates and the memory cost of large β, pointing toward hybridization with RL or smarter sampling strategies as natural next steps. Open questions include whether SLIM scales to more complex scheduling variants (Flexible JSP, Dynamic JSP) and whether the observed advantage over PPO generalizes beyond the specific training regime tested.
