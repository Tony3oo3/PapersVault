## Problem

Bayesian two-step change point detection for Hawkes processes requires inferring the posterior of model parameters, but the point process likelihood is non-conjugate to any standard prior, forcing reliance on MCMC or variational inference methods that lack analytical expressions. This computational bottleneck impedes the timeliness required for online change point detection, where parameters must be re-estimated at every new event.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243813/cropped_page14_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a sequential stream of event timestamps, a set of B scaled shifted beta density basis functions with bounded support [0, T_φ], and a Gaussian prior covariance matrix K. At each new timestamp t_{m+1}, it operates on the sub-sequence since the most recent change point t_{τ_m:m} to produce posterior samples of the parameter vector w and intensity upperbound λ̄, then generates a predictive confidence interval for the next event time. A change point is flagged when the observed t_{m+1} falls outside this interval, and the algorithm resets its estimation window accordingly.

## Method

CoBay-CPD models the Hawkes process with a sigmoid link function λ*(t) = λ̄·σ(h(t)) to capture both excitatory and inhibitory effects, where h(t) = w^T Φ(t) is a linear combination of basis functions. The key insight is a two-part data augmentation: Pólya-Gamma latent variables linearize the sigmoid in the product term, and a marked Poisson process linearizes the exponential integral term, rendering the augmented likelihood conditionally conjugate to the Gaussian prior on w. This yields a fully analytical Gibbs sampler with closed-form conditionals—Pólya-Gamma for ω, a modified marked Poisson process for Π, a Gamma for λ̄, and a Gaussian for w—eliminating the need for MCMC or variational approximations. The two-step CPD then alternates between this Gibbs sampler (estimation) and the Ogata thinning algorithm (prediction), checking whether the observed next timestamp falls within a confidence interval of the predictive distribution to decide if a change point has occurred.

## Results

On the synthetic dataset, CoBay-CPD achieves the lowest FNR (0.13 vs. 0.33–0.50 for baselines), lowest FPR (0.46% vs. 0.60–0.76%), lowest MSE (0.05), and fastest runtime (4.62 min vs. 5.50–23.09 min). On the WannaCry real-world dataset, CoBay-CPD reduces FNR to 0.21 (vs. 0.34–0.54) while cutting runtime to 6.24 min (vs. 9.72–29.76 min). On NYC Vehicle Collisions, CoBay-CPD achieves FNR=0.13, FPR=0.89%, MSE=0.01, and RT=8.70 min, outperforming all baselines on every metric simultaneously.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CoBay-CPD is compared against three baselines: SMCPD (BCPD + sequential Monte Carlo), SVCPD (BCPD + Stein variational inference), and SVCPD+Inhibition (SVCPD extended to a nonlinear Hawkes process with inhibitory effects). CoBay-CPD wins decisively on FNR across all three datasets (e.g., 0.21 vs. 0.34–0.54 on WannaCry; 0.13 vs. 0.22–0.58 on NYC), indicating far fewer missed change points. It also achieves the lowest MSE and fastest runtime on every dataset, with runtime roughly 2× faster than SMCPD and 3–7× faster than SVCPD+Inhibition. On FPR, CoBay-CPD is competitive but not always the lowest: on WannaCry, SVCPD and SVCPD+Inhibition report slightly lower FPR (0.00–0.01 vs. 0.05), reflecting their tendency to detect fewer change points overall (which inflates FNR). On NYC, CoBay-CPD achieves the best FPR (0.89%) while also having the best FNR, demonstrating a superior precision-recall balance. The key advantage stems from the conjugate Gibbs sampler providing exact posterior samples (vs. variational approximations in SVCPD) combined with the nonlinear Hawkes model capturing both excitation and inhibition.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper combines two previously separate threads: the Bayesian two-step estimation-prediction CPD framework for Hawkes processes (Detommaso et al., 2019) and the Pólya-Gamma / marked-Poisson-process data augmentation for sigmoid-linked nonlinear Hawkes processes (Zhou et al., 2022, building on Donner & Opper, 2018). The key departure is restricting the augmentation to the post-change-point interval [t_{τ_m}, t_m] rather than the full observation window, which makes the augmented likelihood conditionally conjugate to a Gaussian prior and yields a fully analytical Gibbs sampler. This replaces the non-conjugate Stein variational inference or MCMC samplers used in prior Bayesian CPD methods, eliminating the need for approximate posterior characterization.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The main practical impact is enabling timely (near-real-time) change point detection in Hawkes processes, since the analytical Gibbs sampler avoids the iterative convergence overhead of MCMC or the approximation error of variational methods. A key limitation acknowledged by the authors is that the method is restricted to univariate Hawkes processes; extending to multivariate settings where change points need not align across dimensions remains open. The approach also depends on the sigmoid link function for conjugacy, which constrains the class of nonlinear Hawkes models to which it applies. For practitioners monitoring dynamic event streams (cybersecurity, traffic, finance), the efficiency gain (4.6 min vs. 23 min on synthetic data) makes online deployment feasible where prior methods were not.
