## Problem

While large language models have demonstrated growing ability to perceive and act within 3D environments, generating camera trajectories conditioned on natural language instructions remains unexplored. Existing trajectory control methods in video generation either require user-provided trajectories or are limited to basic motion types, and none address the full pipeline of interpreting compositional language, placing trajectories in specific 3D scenes, and producing visually coherent rendered video.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241725/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The system accepts a natural language camera operation instruction (e.g., 'Do a dolly zoom focusing on the chandelier') along with a pre-trained 3D radiance field (NeRF or 3DGS) and the set of input images used to reconstruct that scene. It outputs a full M-frame camera trajectory parameterized by per-frame rotation (in S²×S² space), translation, intrinsic matrix, and a global duration scalar for velocity, which is then used to render a photorealistic video via the radiance field. The trajectory is composed from multiple atomic segments that are affinely transformed to pass through scene-specific anchor points.

## Method

CineGPT is a GPT-based autoregressive model that first quantizes camera trajectories into discrete tokens via a VQ-VAE trajectory tokenizer (codebook of 256 vectors, dimension 256, temporal downsampling rate 4), then uses a 24-layer cross-modal transformer decoder to generate trajectory tokens autoregressively from text tokens, trained on ~1000 manually constructed text-trajectory pairs in Blender. The Anchor Determinator addresses scene grounding by using CLIP cosine similarity to select the best-matching input image as an initial anchor, followed by gradient-based refinement of camera parameters to maximize CLIP similarity between the rendered image and the text prompt (converging in 100–1000 steps). A GPT-4 LLM agent orchestrates the full pipeline: it decomposes the user's compositional query into sub-tasks, calls CineGPT and the Anchor Determinator (potentially multiple times) to obtain atomic trajectories and anchor points, then composes the final trajectory by applying affine transformations to align segment endpoints with anchors and with each other.

## Results

On the full ChatCam configuration (GPT-4 agent + Anchor Determinator), the method achieves a Translation MSE of 5.3 and Rotation MSE of 2.9, substantially outperforming both baselines. In the user study, ChatCam scores 84.9% on visual quality preference and 67.9% on text-alignment preference, compared to 5.7%/3.8% for SA3D and 9.4%/28.3% for LERF. Ablations show that removing the Anchor Determinator degrades Translation MSE from 5.3 to 16.2 and Rotation MSE from 2.9 to 8.5, while swapping GPT-4 for LLaMA-2 or GPT-3.5 yields modest increases in trajectory error (6.4/7.3 Translation MSE).

## Baselines & Comparisons

ChatCam outperforms both baselines by a wide margin on every reported metric. Against SA3D [12], which uses SAM embeddings for 3D grounding, ChatCam reduces Translation MSE from 19.5 to 5.3 (3.7× improvement) and Rotation MSE from 6.3 to 2.9, while user-study scores jump from 5.7% to 84.9% (visual quality) and 3.8% to 67.9% (alignment). Against LERF [37], which uses CLIP embeddings, the Translation MSE drops from 17.7 to 5.3 and Rotation MSE from 4.9 to 2.9; visual quality preference rises from 9.4% to 84.9% and alignment from 28.3% to 67.9%. The baselines, lacking explicit trajectory generation, simply interpolate keyframe poses and frequently place the camera inside objects, producing artifacts and poor text alignment. The ablation without the Anchor Determinator (Translation MSE 16.2, Rotation MSE 8.5) shows that scene-grounded anchoring is critical for competitive performance, bringing the system close to baseline-level accuracy when omitted.

## Relation to Prior Work

ChatCam draws on the VQ-VAE framework for discretizing continuous camera trajectories into tokens and borrows the embedding/commitment loss formulation from T2M-GPT for training the trajectory tokenizer. Conceptually, it departs from prior trajectory-control work (TC4D, Direct-a-Video, MotionCtrl, CameraCtrl) that requires user-supplied trajectories or is limited to basic motion types, and from 3D visual grounding methods (LERF, SA3D) that learn explicit 3D feature fields, by instead using CLIP-based image matching plus gradient-based refinement as a lightweight anchor mechanism. The key positioning is that no prior work generates camera trajectories from natural language descriptions; existing approaches either predict motion sequences autoregressively or accept trajectories as input to video diffusion models.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This is the first system to close the loop from a natural-language camera instruction to a rendered video on a radiance field, lowering the technical barrier for creators in film, broadcasting, and VR. The main limitations are the small training corpus (~1000 manually created trajectories), restriction to static scenes, and dependence on a powerful LLM (GPT-4) for planning, which constrains scalability and deployment. Open questions include extending to dynamic 4D scenes, handling longer/more compositional instructions, and whether the VQ-VAE tokenizer can scale to richer trajectory vocabularies without the current 1000-sample bottleneck.
