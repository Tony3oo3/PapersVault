## Problem

Goal-directedness is a critical dimension of AI safety risk, yet no formal, computable measure existed to quantify how goal-directed a system's behaviour is with respect to a given utility function or set of target variables. Existing approaches either provide only a binary agentic/non-agentic distinction (Kenton et al., 2023) or focus on inferring which utility a system optimises (IRL) rather than measuring the degree to which it does so. This paper introduces Maximum Entropy Goal-Directedness (MEG), a continuous, philosophically grounded measure that operationalises Dennett's intentional stance as predictive usefulness of a goal hypothesis.

## Input / Output

The method requires a causal model (typically an MDP or CID), a policy π (or a set of observed trajectories from which π is estimated), and either a specific utility function U or a parametric class of utility functions U^Θ. It requires black-box access to environment dynamics (the ability to roll out policies and measure outcomes) but does not require an explicit causal graph or knowledge of hidden confounders. The output is a non-negative scalar MEG score representing the log-likelihood ratio of the observed policy under the best-matching maximum-entropy goal-directed policy versus a uniform random policy, bounded above by the sum of log domain sizes across all decision variables.

## Method

MEG constructs, for each attainable expected utility level u, the unique maximum-entropy policy π_{U,u}^{maxent} that achieves exactly that utility while being as random as possible elsewhere, then measures the cross-entropy of the observed policy under the best-matching such policy (minus the uniform baseline). In MDPs, each maximum-entropy policy takes the form of a soft-optimal (Boltzmann) policy parameterised by a rationality parameter β, computed via soft value iteration; finding the optimal β reduces to a one-dimensional concave maximisation solved by gradient ascent. The key insight over standard MCE IRL is that by constructing a separate maximum-entropy policy for each expected-utility constraint (rather than a single policy with n linear feature constraints), the method achieves translation and scale invariance and accommodates arbitrary nonlinear utility functions. For unknown-utility settings, the algorithm jointly optimises over utility parameters θ (e.g., neural network weights) and β, providing a lower bound on MEG when the utility class is non-convex.

## Results

In the policy-optimality experiment on CliffWorld, known-utility MEG decreases monotonically from 2.4 (ε=0.1) to 0.008 (ε=0.9) as ε-greedy policies become less optimal, while unknown-utility MEG drops from 26.1 to 0.091 over the same range. In the task-difficulty experiment, known-utility MEG for optimal policies falls from 37.8 (k=1 goal square) to 16.8 (k=3), reflecting that easier goals provide less evidence for deliberate optimisation. Unknown-utility MEG, by contrast, remains high (32–35) across all goal-region lengths because the optimal policy also performs well on a narrower utility function targeting only the nearest goal squares.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This paper introduces a new metric (MEG) rather than competing with existing methods, so there are no external baselines. The key internal comparison is between known-utility MEG (measured against the environment reward) and unknown-utility MEG (measured against a learned MLP utility class). The two metrics diverge sharply on the task-difficulty experiment: known-utility MEG drops by ~55% as the goal region grows from 1 to 3 squares, while unknown-utility MEG stays within a narrow 32–35 band. This validates the paper's central claim that unknown-utility MEG captures goal-directedness relative to the *hardest* utility function in the hypothesis class, not just the environment reward. The ε-greedy experiment confirms the expected monotonic relationship: both metrics approach zero as the policy approaches uniform random, consistent with the theoretical lower bound (Proposition 3.2). The paper also contrasts MEG qualitatively with Kenton et al.'s 'Discovering Agents' algorithm, noting that MEG avoids the arbitrariness of mechanism-variable labelling and provides a continuous rather than binary measure of agency.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts Ziebart's maximum causal entropy (MCE) framework from inverse reinforcement learning, which seeks to *find* a utility function explaining observed behavior, into a tool for *measuring* how well a given utility function explains behavior. It departs from MCE IRL in two key ways: it handles arbitrary (nonlinear) utility functions rather than only linear feature-weighted ones, and it constructs a family of maximum entropy policies parameterized by expected utility rather than a single policy with multiple linear constraints. The philosophical grounding in Dennett's intentional stance connects it to Kenton et al.'s 'Discovering Agents' (binary agent detection in causal models) and Orseau et al.'s Bayesian IRL approach (posterior over agent vs. device), but MEG provides a continuous, scale-invariant scalar where those prior works offer only binary or probabilistic distinctions.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

MEG offers a principled, computable metric for AI safety evaluations that can quantify how goal-directed a system is with respect to specific variables, potentially informing governance and dangerous-capability assessments. The pseudo-terminal goals theorem (Theorem 4.1) provides a useful theoretical guarantee that goal-directedness toward downstream targets implies goal-directedness toward mediating variables, reducing the need to look arbitrarily far into the future. However, significant limitations remain: MEG requires active environment access (not just observational data), may be computationally intractable in high-dimensional settings, is sensitive to the choice of variables measured, and does not handle distributional shifts. The experiments are small-scale (CliffWorld MDPs), and the authors explicitly flag scaling to real-world neural network agents and applying MEG to mechanistic interpretability as important future directions.
