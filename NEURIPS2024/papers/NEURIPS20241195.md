## Problem

While the hippocampus is known to support both spatial navigation and episodic memory, it remains unclear how place cells—spatially selective firing fields—emerge from a network whose primary function is pattern completion of sensory experiences. Prior computational models either imposed explicit sparsity constraints or failed to reproduce the full phenomenology of place cells, including remapping, reversion to prior representations in familiar rooms, and orthogonal coding across multiple environments.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241195/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The network receives 1-second episodic memory segments of high-dimensional experience vectors (EVs) sampled from weakly spatially modulated (WSM) Gaussian random fields as an agent traverses simulated rooms. Each EV is randomly masked (0–20% occlusion) and corrupted with Gaussian noise to simulate partial observation. The RAE outputs reconstructed, noiseless EVs, while the key emergent output is the hidden-layer firing rate map projected onto 2D (or 3D) room geometry, quantified by Spatial Information Content (SIC) and pairwise Pearson correlations across rooms.

## Method

The paper models CA3 as a continuous-time recurrent autoencoder (RAE) with input projections W^in, recurrent connections W^rc, and output projections W^out, where hidden units use ReLU nonlinearity. Training minimizes an MSE reconstruction loss on partially occluded EVs plus an MSE penalty on total hidden-layer firing rates, without explicit sparsity constraints. The central insight is that because WSM signals make sensory experience temporally continuous during traversal, the pattern-completion objective drives activation boundaries to rotate and segment the experience manifold (EM), producing localized, place-like firing fields. Recurrent connections enable remapping by repositioning the relative geometry of EMs in hidden-layer space—allowing neurons to be selectively inhibited or reactivated across rooms—while input projections remain largely stable, supporting reversion to prior place fields upon re-entry into familiar environments. The framework reproduces remapping, orthogonal representations across 20 rooms, representational drift, and multi-field place cells in larger enclosures.

## Results

In a 1m×1m room, ~10% of 1000 hidden units are active (mean firing > 0.1 Hz), and ~80% of those active units achieve Spatial Information Content (SIC) > 5, which is 20× the SIC of the WSM inputs (0.29 ± 0.01). For orthogonality across 20 rooms over 30 cycles, the mean Pearson correlation between different rooms is 0.164 ± 0.029 while same-room correlation is 0.710 ± 0.097 (a gap of ~0.55). Reversion to prior place fields upon re-entry to a familiar room improves from r = 0.35 in early cycles to r = 0.92 by cycle 30, and same-room population correlations exceed 0.8 after the third cycle.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary quantitative comparison is with the experimental data of Alme et al. [21] (PNAS 2014), who recorded 342 neurons across 11 rooms. The RAE model (1000 units, 20 rooms, 30 cycles) reproduces the key orthogonality signature: the gap between same-room and different-room population correlations is ~0.55 in the model versus ~0.57 in the experiment, and the absolute correlation values are of similar magnitude (model: 0.164 vs. 0.710; experiment: 0.08 vs. 0.65). The authors note that exact numerical agreement is not expected due to differences in room count, unit count, and environment specifics. Beyond this single direct comparison, the paper does not benchmark against other computational models (e.g., Benna & Fusi [39], Raju et al. [40], Levenstein et al. [41]) with head-to-head quantitative metrics; instead it positions its framework as qualitatively reproducing a broader set of hippocampal phenomenology (remapping, reversion, drift, multi-field cells) that those prior models do not jointly capture.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the line of work connecting hippocampal pattern completion to spatial representation, including Benna and Fusi's autoencoder model with sparsity constraints [39], HMM-based sequence learning [40], and RNNs trained for predictive coding [41]. It departs from these by showing that place fields emerge from a recurrent autoencoder performing pattern completion on temporally continuous sensory inputs without any explicit sparsity constraint, and by providing a geometric mechanism (activation boundaries segmenting experience manifolds) that simultaneously explains place field formation, remapping, and reversion. The paper positions itself as a unifying 'sensory as spatial' framework that reconciles the episodic-memory and spatial-navigation views of CA3, which prior works treated as separate or only loosely connected.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper offers a parsimonious mechanistic account of place cell emergence that makes several sharp, testable predictions (e.g., blocking recurrent connections should abolish reversion but not place field formation; rapidly varying sensory context should disrupt place fields; the dimensionality of temporally smooth experience sets place field dimensionality in both physical and abstract spaces). Key limitations include the absence of direct statistical comparison with rodent hippocampal recordings and the assumption that sensory inputs are well-modeled by smooth Gaussian random fields. Open questions include what specific sensory signals compose the experience vectors in vivo, how the framework extends to more complex behavioral contexts, and whether the attractor landscape properties observed here match the dynamics of biological CA3.
