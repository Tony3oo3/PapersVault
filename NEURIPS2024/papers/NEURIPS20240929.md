## Problem

While prediction accuracy for protein variant effects has seen substantial progress, reliable uncertainty estimates are rarely reported despite being essential for practical protein engineering tasks like Bayesian optimization. This paper asks whether state-of-the-art supervised variant effect prediction can be achieved within a Gaussian process framework (which provides closed-form posterior uncertainty) and then investigates the actual calibration quality of those uncertainty estimates.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240929/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a protein variant sequence (defined as substitutions relative to a fixed wild-type reference) and the 3D structure of the wild-type protein (typically AlphaFold-predicted). It consumes three pretrained-model outputs: ESM-2 mean-pooled sequence embeddings (1280-d), ProteinMPNN structure-conditioned amino acid distributions per site, and ESM-2 zero-shot log-likelihood-ratio scores. The output is a predicted variant effect score with a full posterior distribution (mean and standard deviation), enabling per-instance uncertainty quantification. The model is restricted to substitutions on a fixed structure and cannot handle insertions or deletions.

## Method

Kermut is a Gaussian process regression model whose covariance is a weighted sum of two kernels: a structure kernel and a sequence kernel. The structure kernel is a product of three components operating on single-mutant sites — a Hellinger kernel comparing ProteinMPNN inverse-folding amino acid distributions at each site, an exponential kernel on the log-probability of the specific mutated residue, and an exponential kernel on the Euclidean distance between α-carbons — and is extended to multi-mutants by summing over all pairs of mutation sites (a set kernel). The sequence kernel is a squared exponential kernel on mean-pooled ESM-2 embeddings, capturing epistatic and global sequence effects. A zero-shot mean function (ESM-2 log-likelihood ratio) is added to the GP mean, and all hyperparameters are optimized via marginal likelihood. This composite design lets the model leverage local structural similarity for unseen sites while the sequence kernel captures higher-order interactions, achieving state-of-the-art Spearman correlation on ProteinGym while running orders of magnitude faster than deep learning baselines.

## Results

On the ProteinGym substitution benchmark (217 DMS assays), Kermut achieves state-of-the-art performance with an average Spearman correlation of 0.662 and average MSE of 0.589, outperforming the strongest baseline ProteinNPT (0.613 Spearman, 0.683 MSE). The largest gains appear in the challenging contiguous (0.610 vs. 0.547) and modulo (0.633 vs. 0.564) split schemes, where the structure kernel provides critical signal for unseen sites. Kermut also runs orders of magnitude faster, completing a 5-fold CV loop in ~10 minutes versus >400 hours for ProteinNPT on the same hardware.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Kermut outperforms all five baseline categories across every split scheme and both metrics (Spearman and MSE). Against the strongest baseline, ProteinNPT (a non-parametric transformer), Kermut gains +0.049 average Spearman (0.662 vs. 0.613) and reduces MSE by 0.094 (0.589 vs. 0.683), with the largest margin in the contiguous split (+0.063 Spearman). Against embedding-based models (Tranception, MSAT, ESM-1v), Kermut leads by 0.09–0.12 average Spearman, with the structure kernel driving particularly large gains in binding and stability tasks (0.630 and 0.824 Spearman vs. 0.536 and 0.772 for ProteinNPT). Against one-hot-encoding baselines (TranceptEVE, MSAT, DeepSequence, ESM-1v), the gap is even wider at 0.18–0.44 average Spearman. Notably, Kermut achieves these gains while training and evaluating ~40× faster than ProteinNPT (10 min vs. >400 h for four datasets across three split schemes), and provides built-in, well-calibrated uncertainty estimates without requiring Monte Carlo dropout or other post-hoc UQ techniques.

## Relation to Prior Work

Kermut extends the Gaussian process regression framework (Rasmussen & Williams) to protein variant effect prediction by introducing a composite kernel that fuses a structure kernel (Hellinger distance on ProteinMPNN inverse-folding distributions, mutation-probability kernel, and inter-residue distance kernel) with a sequence kernel (squared-exponential on ESM-2 embeddings). The multi-mutant generalization via pairwise summation is motivated by Ding et al.'s finding that linear models suffice for mutation effects, while the structural featurization draws on the inverse-folding literature. The approach is conceptually closest to xGPR (GPs on PLM embeddings) and mGPfusion (structure-aware GPs), but uniquely combines pretrained sequence and structure models within a single GP and adds a zero-shot mean function from ESM-2 log-likelihood ratios.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that a carefully designed GP can match or exceed state-of-the-art deep learning methods (ProteinNPT, Tranception) on the ProteinGym benchmark while providing principled posterior uncertainty estimates at a fraction of the computational cost (~10 minutes vs. ~400 hours). The calibration analysis reveals a nuanced picture: overall confidence-interval calibration is good, but instance-specific error-based calibration remains challenging, an open problem the authors explicitly flag for the community. Key limitations include O(N³) scaling, inability to handle insertions/deletions, and the fixed-structure assumption that may degrade for multi-mutant variants, leaving room for approximate GP methods and explicit epistasis modeling as future directions.
