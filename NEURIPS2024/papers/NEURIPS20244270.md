## Problem

In standard Bayesian persuasion, the revelation principle restricts the sender's strategy space to direct-revelation schemes, enabling efficient computation of optimal signaling schemes. This paper shows that when the receiver may choose any approximately optimal action (δ-best response) rather than the exact best response, the revelation principle fails catastrophically—direct-revelation schemes can be suboptimal by a factor of 2—and the general problem of computing the optimal robust signaling scheme is NP-hard. The central question is whether principled, efficient algorithms exist despite the absence of this structural characterization.

## Input / Output

The method takes a finite state space Ω with prior μ₀, a finite action space A, sender and receiver utility functions s(ω,a) and r(ω,a) normalized to [0,1], and a robustness parameter δ > 0. It produces an optimal (or ε-approximate) robust signaling scheme φ*: Ω → Δ(Σ) that maps each state to a distribution over signals, where each signal is indexed by a pair (A, ã) encoding the δ-best-response set and the best-response action. The signal space can be as large as n·2^{n-1} in the exact formulation, and the robust utility is defined as the worst-case sender utility over all δ-BR receiver strategies.

## Method

The paper develops three algorithmic tools. First, an LP formulation where variables φ(ω, A, ã) represent conditional signal probabilities and constraints enforce signal semantics (best response and δ-BR set membership) via Bayes' rule, yielding a polynomial-time algorithm when the action space is constant. Second, for small state spaces, the paper uses arrangements of hyperplanes in the (m−1)-dimensional simplex to bound the number of feasible signal tuples by n^{O(m)}, then performs DFS on a symmetric-difference graph over these tuples (checking feasibility via LPs) to enumerate them before solving the restricted LP. Third, a QPTAS for the general problem partitions the simplex into cells centered at k-uniform distributions (via Althöfer's covering), defines signals by cell center plus best-response and worst-case actions, and solves a quasi-polynomial-size LP that approximates the sender's utility while avoiding discontinuities in the receiver's response. NP-hardness is established via a reduction from a balanced subset-sum variant, where the utility structure forces the optimal signal to correspond to a zero-sum subset of exactly n/2 elements.

## Results

The paper establishes three main algorithmic results for robust Bayesian persuasion under approximate best response. (1) An exact polynomial-time algorithm via LP of size O(2^n · m · n) when the action space n is constant or logarithmic. (2) An exact algorithm running in n^{O(m)} time when the state space m is constant, leveraging arrangements-of-hyperplanes bounds and a DFS over feasible signal tuples. (3) A quasi-polynomial-time approximation scheme (QPTAS) achieving additive error ε in time O(n² · m^{⌈12.5·log(2n)/ε²⌉}) for the general problem. On the negative side, exact computation is NP-hard (via reduction from a balanced subset-sum variant), and direct-revelation signaling schemes are suboptimal by at least a factor of 2 or an additive gap of 1/2, demonstrating the failure of the revelation principle.

## Baselines & Comparisons

This is a purely theoretical paper with no experimental baselines or empirical comparisons. The key conceptual comparisons are: (1) Against the classical revelation principle — the paper proves it fails in the robust setting, with direct-revelation schemes achieving at most half the optimal robust utility (Proposition 3.1). (2) Against the standard Bayesian persuasion LP [Dughmi and Xu, 2016] — the robust LP requires fundamentally different semantics because multiple actions can be δ-optimal responses to a single signal, making the signal space exponential in n. (3) Against robust Stackelberg equilibria [Gan et al., 2023] — the paper notes that while both settings lack a revelation principle, the complexity results do not transfer directly: Bayesian persuasion's compact representation and the need to construct the signal space make the problem structurally different, and the small-state-space algorithm here achieves poly(n^{O(m)}) complexity versus exponential-in-n for the Stackelberg approach.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the classical Bayesian persuasion framework of Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011) and its computational treatment by Dughmi and Xu (2016) to a robustness model where the receiver may choose any δ-optimal action rather than the exact best response. The key departure from prior work is that the revelation principle—which underpins the standard LP formulation and all existing efficient algorithms—provably fails in this model, with direct-revelation schemes being suboptimal by a factor of 2. The algorithmic toolkit draws on combinatorial geometry (arrangements of hyperplanes for bounding feasible signal subsets) and the Althöfer (1994) covering result for the QPTAS, while the NP-hardness reduction from a balanced subset-sum variant introduces a novel use of the robustness parameter to encode combinatorial constraints.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The failure of the revelation principle in the robustness model is a significant conceptual result: it means that the entire 'characterize-and-solve' paradigm that has made Bayesian persuasion tractable in standard settings no longer applies, forcing fundamentally new algorithmic approaches. The QPTAS and the small-state-space algorithm provide the first efficient (or quasi-efficient) solutions for this robust variant, opening the door to practical robust information design. Open questions include whether the quasi-polynomial gap can be closed to polynomial time, whether the techniques extend to multi-receiver or sequential settings, and how the results interact with other robustness notions (e.g., uncertain priors, quantal response) studied in parallel literature.
