## Problem

Existing classification-with-rejection methods either require retraining a model from scratch with a modified loss function or rely on confidence scores from calibrated probability estimates. This paper asks whether rejection can be formulated as a distributional comparison problem—finding an 'idealized' data distribution where a pretrained model performs best and using the density ratio between that distribution and the actual data distribution as a rejection criterion—enabling post-hoc rejection without retraining.

## Input / Output

The method takes a pretrained and calibrated classifier h (e.g., ResNet-18 for images, MLP for tabular data), a pointwise loss function (log-loss in practice), and a divergence parameter α ≥ 1. It produces a scalar density ratio ρ(x) = dQ/dP(x) for each input, which is thresholded at τ ∈ (0,1] to yield either a class prediction (when ρ(x) > τ) or a rejection token (when ρ(x) ≤ τ). The loss L'(x) is estimated by sampling Y ~ h(x) and computing E[ℓ(Y, h(x))], effectively using the calibrated softmax output as a proxy for the true posterior P(Y|X=x).

## Method

The core mechanism defines an idealized distribution Q by solving a GVI-style optimization: argmin_Q L(Q) + λ·D_φ(P||Q), where L(Q) is the expected model loss under Q and D_φ is a φ-divergence regularizer preventing Q from collapsing to a point mass. The optimal density ratio ρ = dQ/dP admits a closed form via Lagrange multipliers (Theorem 3.3): for KL-divergence (α=1), ρ(x) = (1/Z)·exp(−L'(x)/λ) is a Gibbs/exponential-tilt of P; for α-divergences with α>1, ρ(x) = [(α−1)/2 · (b − L'(x)/λ)]^{2/(α−1)}_+ with a normalization constant b found via bisection. Rejection is then simply r_τ(x) = [ρ(x) ≤ τ], rejecting inputs where the idealized distribution assigns lower mass than the data distribution. The framework recovers Chow's rule when using KL-divergence with a Bayes-optimal classifier, and the idealized distributions are shown to be equivalent to the adversarial distributions in DRO (Corollary 3.4), unifying three previously separate perspectives.

## Results

At a target of 80% coverage, the KL-divergence density ratio rejector achieves 99.93% accuracy on clean HAR, 99.16% on clean Gas Drift, 99.93% on clean MNIST, 97.22% on clean CIFAR-10, 96.55% on clean OrganMNIST, and 97.08% on clean OctMNIST. Under 25% label noise, the (α=3)-divergence rejector reaches 92.50% on CIFAR-10 and 96.29% on OrganMNIST, while the KL variant achieves 98.56% on HAR and 99.89% on MNIST. The two divergence variants (KL and α=3) produce nearly identical accuracy, differing only in the coverage region their τ threshold covers, consistent with the theoretical local equivalence of φ-divergences.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against PredRej (post-hoc predictor-rejector [48]), the density ratio rejectors win on every dataset in both clean and noisy settings, with the largest gaps on CIFAR-10 (97.22 vs 91.40 clean; 92.25 vs 85.84 noisy) and OrganMNIST (96.55 vs 93.79 clean). Against CSS (cost-sensitive classification [14]), the density ratio methods dominate on clean CIFAR-10 (+1.77), clean OrganMNIST (+2.06), and noisy CIFAR-10 (+2.67), but CSS edges out on noisy Gas Drift (98.71 vs 97.30) and noisy OrganMNIST (96.74 vs 95.86). Against DEFER (cross-entropy surrogate [49]), the density ratio rejectors win on most datasets, though DEFER achieves the best noisy Gas Drift result (99.02 vs 97.30) at slightly lower coverage (77%). Against GCE (generalized cross-entropy [13]), the comparison is closest: GCE wins on noisy HAR (98.85 vs 98.56) and noisy MNIST (99.95 vs 99.89), while the density ratio methods win on clean CIFAR-10 (+2.97), clean OrganMNIST (+2.87), and noisy CIFAR-10 (+0.28). Overall, the density ratio rejectors are competitive or superior across the board, with the notable caveat that CSS/DEFER/GCE require training from scratch while the density ratio approach is purely post-hoc on a pretrained calibrated classifier.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper reframes classification with rejection from the standard loss-augmentation perspective (Chow's rule, cost-sensitive classification, surrogate losses) to a distributional one: finding an 'idealized distribution' where a pretrained model performs best, then rejecting via the density ratio between this distribution and the data distribution. The idealized distribution is formally defined through a Generalized Variational Inference (GVI) objective with φ-divergence regularization, and the paper shows a direct structural equivalence to the adversarial distributions in Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO). The KL-divergence special case recovers Chow's rule, while the α-divergence family (including χ²) yields closed-form density ratio rejectors that are post-hoc applicable to any calibrated classifier, unlike prior methods (DEFER, CSS, GCE) that require retraining from scratch.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The unified GVI/DRO/rejection framework provides closed-form idealized distributions that are directly reusable for DRO adversarial distribution construction, giving the theory utility beyond rejection. Practically, the post-hoc density ratio rejector avoids the costly retraining required by most competing baselines and offers a single threshold τ for continuous accuracy-coverage trade-off tuning. Key limitations include the reliance on calibrated P(Y|X) estimates (via temperature scaling), the failure of the gradient-based density ratio learning approach (Appendix O), and the observation that different α-divergences produce nearly identical rejectors in practice due to local equivalence of φ-divergences. Open questions include extending to regression (where estimating conditional variance is harder), exploring IPM-based idealized distributions, and developing practical gradient-based methods for the density ratio objective.
