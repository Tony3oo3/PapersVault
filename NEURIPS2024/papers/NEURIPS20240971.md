## Problem

Existing model editing methods are evaluated primarily on reliability, generalization, and locality, but the impact of sequential editing on the general capabilities of language models (world knowledge, reasoning, safety, etc.) remains largely unexplored. This paper asks: how do sequential model editing operations affect the fundamental abilities of LLMs as the number of edits scales from dozens to thousands?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240971/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The evaluation pipeline takes a base or instruction-tuned LLM (Llama2-7B, Mistral-7B, GPT2-XL, or Pythia 160M–12B) and a sequence of knowledge tuples drawn from COUNTERFACT or ZsRE, applies them sequentially via one of six editing methods (ROME, MEMIT, PMET, MEND, GRACE, KN), and produces per-benchmark scores across seven tasks (MMLU, BBH, GSM8K, CommonsenseQA, TriviaQA, TruthfulQA, ToxiGen) at edit counts ranging from 1 to 10,000. The sequential protocol means each edit is applied to the already-edited model, so accumulated damage is captured rather than resetting between edits.

## Method

This is a comprehensive empirical evaluation study rather than a new editing algorithm. The authors apply six editing methods—spanning locate-then-edit (ROME, MEMIT, PMET, KN), meta-learning (MEND), and adapter-based (GRACE)—sequentially to eight models of varying scale and instruction-tuning status, using the EasyEdit framework for editing and vLLM-accelerated inference for evaluation. They structure the analysis around five research questions: (1) how edit count degrades performance, (2) whether instruction tuning confers robustness, (3) whether larger models resist editing better, (4) whether different capability dimensions degrade uniformly, and (5) whether safety alignment survives editing. A key empirical discovery is the 'muting effect': after ~10,000 sequential edits, the model's intrinsic knowledge structure is so disrupted that it outputs empty strings or random characters for any input. The study also quantifies editing efficiency (e.g., MEMIT on Llama2-7B for 10K edits requires ~120 hours on a single RTX A6000) and deployment incompatibilities introduced by architecture-modifying methods like GRACE and SERAC.

## Results

On Llama2-7B (base), the unedited model achieves MMLU 0.4587, GSM8K 0.1440, BBH 0.4000, and CSQA 0.5921. After 100 sequential edits, PMET and MEND retain near-original performance (MMLU ≈ 0.458, GSM8K ≈ 0.15), while ROME collapses to MMLU 0.0007 and MEMIT drops to MMLU 0.4428 with GSM8K falling to 0.0986. KN is catastrophic, dropping all benchmarks to ~0 after just 10 edits. At 10,000 edits, all methods trigger a 'muting effect' where the model outputs empty strings for any input.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper evaluates six editing methods (ROME, MEMIT, PMET, MEND, GRACE, KN) as baselines against the unedited model's general capabilities. PMET and MEND are the clear winners: both preserve MMLU, GSM8K, BBH, and CSQA scores within ~1–2% of the unedited baseline even after 1,000 sequential edits on Llama2-7B. MEMIT is the second-most robust, maintaining performance through ~50 edits but degrading sharply at 100 edits (GSM8K drops from 0.1509 to 0.0986) and collapsing entirely by 500 edits. ROME is fragile: it holds up through 10 edits but suffers a 41% MMLU drop at 50 edits (0.4587→0.2701) and is effectively destroyed by 100 edits. GRACE shows moderate degradation at 10 edits (MMLU 0.4272 vs. 0.4587) and is completely destroyed by 100 edits. KN is the worst performer, reducing all benchmark scores to near-zero after only 10 edits, triggering the 'muting effect' immediately. On Mistral-7B, the same ordering holds, though PMET and MEND are not available for that model, leaving MEMIT as the only method that survives 50 edits.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the model editing evaluation paradigm, which has traditionally focused on three dimensions—reliability, generalization, and locality—by introducing a comprehensive assessment of general capabilities (world knowledge, reasoning, arithmetic, reading comprehension, safety) after sequential editing. It builds on the locate-then-edit lineage (ROME, MEMIT, PMET), meta-learning approaches (MEND), retrieval-based methods (SERAC), and adapter-based methods (GRACE), while also connecting to prior work identifying pitfalls such as knowledge distortion and catastrophic forgetting. The key departure is shifting the evaluation lens from whether a specific edit was successful to whether the model's broader competence survives, and formalizing the 'muting effect' observed at extreme edit counts.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that even dozens of edits can compromise safety alignment, and that the muting effect (complete loss of coherent output) emerges at ~10k edits, provides critical practical guardrails for anyone considering deploying edited models in production. The observation that instruction-tuned models and larger models are more robust, and that PMET/MEND scale far better than ROME/KN, offers actionable guidance for method selection. Open questions remain: whether any editing method can truly scale to thousands of edits without degradation, how to preserve safety alignment during editing, and whether the uniform degradation across capability types reflects a fundamental limitation of parameter-level editing or an artifact of the specific benchmarks used.
