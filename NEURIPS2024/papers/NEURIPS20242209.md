## Problem

Standard instruction tuning computes loss only on the completion tokens, which can cause rapid overfitting—particularly when training data pairs long instructions with short outputs or when only a small number of examples are available under the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis. This paper asks whether simply extending the loss to cover the instruction tokens as well can serve as an effective, hyperparameter-free regularizer to mitigate this overfitting.

## Input / Output

The method consumes standard instruction-completion pairs where the instruction (length m) may contain special prompt template tokens (e.g., <|user|>, <|assistant|>) and the completion (length n) is the target response. It produces a fine-tuned language model (evaluated on LLaMA-2-7B, LLaMA-2-13B, and OPT-6.7B) assessed across 21 benchmarks: 18 traditional NLP tasks in six categories plus three LLM-judged open-ended generation benchmarks (MT-Bench, AlpacaEval 1.0, AlpacaEval 2.0). The critical implementation detail is a binary loss mask that includes all instruction and completion tokens while zeroing out static template tokens.

## Method

Instruction Modelling (IM) replaces the standard instruction tuning loss (negative log-likelihood over completions only) with a loss computed over both instruction and completion tokens, excluding prompt template tokens via an indicator function—no new hyperparameters or architectural changes are introduced. This forces the model to also predict instruction tokens given prior context, acting as an implicit regularizer that constrains memorization of training examples. The paper identifies two conditions where IM is most effective: (1) high instruction-to-output length ratios (e.g., Less MMLU Chat ≈ 26:1, Science Literature ≈ 25:1), and (2) low-resource settings with few training examples, consistent with the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis. Mechanistic analysis confirms the overfitting-reduction hypothesis: IM exhibits higher training loss but lower test loss, generates outputs with lower BLEU similarity to training examples, and suffers less instruction tuning tax on NLP tasks across epochs. IM can be further combined with NEFTune for additional gains on AlpacaEval win rates.

## Results

Across 7 instruction tuning datasets, IM consistently improves mean NLP task performance over standard Instruction Tuning (IT) by 0.49–2.46 points (e.g., 48.95 vs. 46.58 on Alpagasus Dolly 3k; 48.00 vs. 45.54 on Alpagasus Dolly 9k). On AlpacaEval 1.0, IM achieves the largest relative gains on datasets with long instructions and short outputs: Less MMLU Chat (4.42 → 9.78, +121%) and Less Tydiqa (5.12 → 10.10, +97%), while gains are more modest on LIMA (33.06 → 32.94, −0.12). IM also improves AlpacaEval 2.0 in 6 of 7 datasets (e.g., 2.46 → 3.29 on Alpagasus Alpaca 5k; 1.20 → 1.93 on Less MMLU Chat). Combining IM with NEFTune yields further AlpacaEval 1.0 gains of up to +13.31 points (Less Tydiqa: 10.10 → 23.41).

## Baselines & Comparisons

The primary baseline is NEFTUNE (Jain et al., ICLR 2024), which adds noise to token embeddings during instruction tuning. IM outperforms NEFTUNE on mean NLP task performance across all 7 datasets, with the largest margins on Alpagasus Dolly 3k (+1.88) and Alpagasus Dolly 9k (+2.01). On AlpacaEval 1.0, IM wins on 5 of 7 datasets (e.g., +4.34 on Less BBH ICL, +3.56 on Less MMLU Chat, +1.75 on Less Tydiqa), while NEFTUNE narrowly edges out IM on Alpagasus Alpaca 5k (21.37 vs. 19.52) and Alpagasus Dolly 9k (31.61 vs. 30.77). On AlpacaEval 2.0, IM achieves the highest score in 5 of 7 datasets, with the largest gain on Less BBH ICL (3.56 vs. 2.87). The paper also shows that combining IM with NEFTUNE (IM+NEFTUNE) further boosts AlpacaEval 1.0 by up to +13.31 points (Less Tydiqa) and +12.55 points (Alpagasus Alpaca 5k), though it can slightly reduce NLP task scores in some settings (e.g., −0.53 on Less BBH ICL). Both methods substantially outperform the untrained LLAMA-2-7B-BASE (mean NLP 49.32, AlpacaEval 1.0 = 0.01), confirming that instruction tuning is essential for open-ended generation quality.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the standard instruction tuning paradigm (established by InstructGPT/Ouyang et al., where loss is computed exclusively on completion tokens) by simply including instruction tokens in the loss computation, which the authors term Instruction Modelling (IM). It is positioned alongside regularization-based approaches to instruction tuning such as NEFTune (embedding noise) and KL divergence penalties, but departs from them by requiring no additional hyperparameters or auxiliary objectives. The work also connects to the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis (LIMA) and data-selection literature (Alpagasus, LESS), showing that the benefit of IM is most pronounced precisely in the low-data, high-quality-regime these works target. A closely related prior observation by Huerta-Enochian (2024) noted that instruction loss weighting matters for short-completion data; this paper generalizes that insight into a broader, hyperparameter-free guideline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical takeaway is that a one-line change to the loss mask can meaningfully reduce overfitting during instruction tuning, particularly in low-resource settings where the SAH applies—offering practitioners a free regularization lever without architectural changes or extra compute. The finding that KL divergence, the standard regularization tool in alignment, fails to preserve instruction-following ability while IM does, is a useful negative result that redirects attention toward loss-design choices. Open questions include whether IM composes well with RLHF/DPO pipelines, whether the benefit persists at much larger model scales (70B+), and whether the instruction/output length ratio effect holds for non-English or multimodal instruction tuning.
