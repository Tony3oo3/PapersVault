## Problem

Existing scaling laws for LLMs (Kaplan, Chinchilla) treat vocabulary size as a fixed hyperparameter, ignoring its impact on compute allocation. This oversight has led to arbitrary and often suboptimal vocabulary choices across models (e.g., Llama2-7B uses 32K tokens while Gemma-7B uses 256K despite similar parameter counts). The paper asks: given a compute budget, what is the optimal vocabulary size, and how should it scale with model size?
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243625/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes three inputs: non-vocabulary parameters N_nv, a FLOPs budget C, and the amount of training data measured in characters H (rather than tokens, to remain vocabulary-agnostic). It outputs the optimal vocabulary size V, the corresponding vocabulary parameters N_v = V·d, and the full optimal allocation (N_nv, N_v, H). A key design choice is the use of a unigram-normalized loss L_u instead of standard cross-entropy, which removes the vocabulary-size bias in loss values and enables fair comparison across models with different V. The tokens-to-characters compression ratio is modeled by a quadratic function f(V) = a·log²(V) + b·log(V) + c fitted across 25 BPE tokenizers.

## Method

The paper proposes three complementary approaches to predict the compute-optimal vocabulary size. Approach 1 (IsoFLOPs fitting) trains 6 model families (N_nv from 33M to 1.13B) with vocabulary sizes from 4K to 96K at matched FLOPs budgets, then fits power laws yielding N_nv = 0.08·C^0.50, N_v = 0.20·C^0.42, and H = 6.42·C^0.50. Approach 2 (derivative-based) inverts the problem by minimizing FLOPs with respect to V for a fixed loss target, solving ∂C/∂V = 0 numerically, and then scaling the result via N_v ∝ N_nv^γ with γ ≈ 0.83. Approach 3 (parametric loss fit) extends the Chinchilla risk decomposition to L_u = −E + A₁/N_nv^α₁ + A₂/N_v^α₂ + B/D^β, fitted on all experimental points (not just compute-optimal ones), enabling prediction of locally optimal V even under overtraining or undertraining. All three approaches converge on the finding that vocabulary parameters should scale sub-linearly with non-vocabulary parameters (γ < 1), and that most existing LLMs under-allocate vocabulary parameters.

## Results

The paper predicts that optimal vocabulary parameters scale as N_v^opt ∝ N_nv^γ with γ≈0.83–0.84, meaning vocabulary should grow slower than non-vocabulary parameters. Empirically, on 3B-parameter models (N_nv=2.87B), using the predicted optimal vocabulary consistently improves average zero-shot accuracy across 7 tasks: from 50.3% to 51.6% at 2.3e21 FLOPs (overtraining), from 47.9% to 48.5% at 1.2e21 FLOPs (compute-optimal), and from 43.2% to 43.9% at 2.8e20 FLOPs (undertraining). The most striking single-task gain is on ARC-Challenge, where increasing vocabulary from 32K to 43K at 2.3e21 FLOPs improves accuracy from 29.1% to 32.0%. The paper further predicts that Llama2-70B should have used a vocabulary of at least 216K (7× its actual 32K) for compute-optimal training.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not compare against other scaling-law methods but instead validates its predicted optimal vocabulary sizes against the conventional V=32K baseline (used by Llama2) across three practical training regimes. In all three settings—undertraining (2.8e20 FLOPs), compute-optimal training (1.2e21 FLOPs), and overtraining (2.3e21 FLOPs)—the model with the predicted optimal vocabulary outperforms the V=32K baseline on average accuracy (43.9 vs 43.2, 48.5 vs 47.9, and 51.6 vs 50.3, respectively). The gains are most pronounced on reasoning-heavy tasks: ARC-Challenge improves by 2.9 points (29.1→32.0) in the overtraining setting, and HellaSwag improves by 1.1 points (53.0→54.1). In the undertraining regime, the optimal vocabulary is actually smaller than 32K (24K), confirming that the method adapts to data scarcity. The paper also notes that among existing LLMs, only StarCoder2-3B, OLMo-7B, InternLM2-20B, and Gemma2-27B have vocabulary sizes close to the predicted optimal, while most others (including Llama2-70B at 32K vs. predicted 216K+) significantly under-allocate vocabulary parameters.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the scaling laws framework established by Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. (Chinchilla) by introducing vocabulary parameters as a third scaling dimension alongside non-vocabulary parameters and training data. It adopts the unigram-normalized loss from Roh et al. to enable fair comparison across different vocabulary sizes, and borrows the FLOPs-minimization-for-fixed-loss formulation from Sardana & Frankle for its derivative-based approach. The key departure from prior work is treating vocabulary size as a variable to be optimized rather than a fixed hyperparameter, which all previous scaling law formulations implicitly assumed.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The finding that most mainstream LLMs (Llama2, Falcon, Qwen2, etc.) use substantially undersized vocabularies—Llama2-70B predicted to need 216K vs. its actual 32K—provides concrete, actionable guidance for model architects and suggests that a simple tokenizer change could yield meaningful downstream gains without additional compute. However, empirical validation is limited to 3B parameters, and predictions for 70B+ models are extrapolations from power-law fits. Open questions include whether the findings generalize to multilingual models, non-transformer architectures, and byte-level tokenization, and whether the community will adopt larger vocabularies given the increased inference memory cost.
