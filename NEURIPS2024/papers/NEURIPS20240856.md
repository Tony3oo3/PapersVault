## Problem

Predictive ML on relational databases (RDBs) lags behind other domains largely because no established public benchmarks exist for multi-table RDB data. Existing evaluations either flatten RDBs into single tables (losing relational structure) or evaluate GNNs on pre-processed graph datasets that impose arbitrary structural and feature constraints, making fair head-to-head comparisons between tabular and graph-based approaches impossible.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20240856/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The pipeline takes a dynamic RDB (a set of linked tables with PK/FK relationships, up to ~2B rows) and a target cell specification {state s, table k, row i, column j}, along with a chosen graph extraction strategy (Row2Node or Row2N/E) and sampling parameters. The output is a predicted value for the target cell, evaluated via AUC (binary classification), RMSE (regression), accuracy (multi-class), or MRR (foreign-key/ranking prediction). Critically, all predictions must respect temporal constraints: only information available at RDB state s may be used, preventing label leakage from future events.

## Method

4DBInfer operationalizes a 4D evaluation space spanning datasets, tasks, graph extractors, and predictive baselines. RDBs are converted to heterogeneous graphs via two strategies: Row2Node (each row becomes a node, FK-PK pairs become directed edges) and Row2N/E (rows from tables with multiple FKs become typed edges with edge features). A graph sampling operator Φ (implemented via GraphBolt for billion-node scale) extracts subgraphs around each target node, which are then fed into either late-fusion pipelines (Deep Feature Synthesis for automated cross-table aggregation, followed by tabular models: MLP, DeepFM, FT-Transformer, XGBoost, AutoGluon) or early-fusion GNN architectures (R-GCN, R-GAT, HGT, R-PNA) that produce trainable node embeddings from the first layer. The key design principle is that all baselines share identical featurization and data transformation steps, ensuring comparisons are not confounded by preprocessing choices that favor one model family over another.

## Results

Across 8 RDB datasets and 14 predictive tasks, GNN-based early-fusion models (R2N and R2N/E) achieve the best median performance rank, with R-PNA and R-GAT frequently topping individual benchmarks. On foreign-key (FK) prediction tasks, GNNs show the largest margins: R-PNA/R2N/E reaches 0.3758 MRR on Diginetica Purchase and R-GAT/R2N/E reaches 0.3055 MRR on Amazon Purchase, versus 0.0749 and 0.1191 for the best DFS-based models respectively. DFS-based late-fusion models remain competitive on relationship-attribute tasks (e.g., DFS/MLP achieves 0.7627 AUC on Diginetica CTR, nearly matching R-PNA/R2N's 0.7635), while simple Join baselines are consistently outperformed by both DFS and GNN pipelines across all datasets. Single-table models are either inapplicable (N/A) or perform at chance level (e.g., 0.5000 AUC), confirming that cross-table information is essential.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper benchmarks 8 base predictors across 5 pipeline categories (Single, Join, DFS, R2N-GNN, R2N/E-GNN) on 14 tasks. Among tabular baselines, XGBoost and AutoGluon generally outperform DeepFM and FT-Transformer within the same pipeline category, but all tabular models are surpassed by GNN-based approaches on most tasks. Among GNNs, R-PNA and R-GAT are the strongest performers overall, with R-PNA excelling on entity-attribute tasks (e.g., 0.7645 AUC on Amazon Churn, 0.8896 on StackExchange Churn) and R-GAT leading on foreign-key prediction (e.g., 0.3055 MRR on Amazon Purchase, 0.7940 MRR on MAG Citation). HGT is competitive but slightly trails R-PNA/R-GAT on most benchmarks. The key finding is that no single baseline dominates all tasks: R2N and R2N/E graph extractions each produce top-ranked models on different datasets, and DFS-based late fusion remains a strong (sometimes near-best) competitor, particularly on relationship-attribute tasks where the gap to GNNs narrows to <0.01 AUC.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies two previously siloed research threads—tabular prediction (via Deep Feature Synthesis/propositionalization [41, 47]) and graph neural network prediction (via heterogeneous GNNs like R-GCN [57], R-GAT [6], HGT [37])—under a single 4D evaluation framework for relational databases. It builds directly on the Row2Node graph extraction paradigm [15] while introducing a complementary Row2N/E variant, and extends PNA [13] to handle heterogeneous graphs with edge features. The key departure from prior RDB benchmarks (RDBench [79], RelBench [23], CRLR [54]) is scale (up to 2B rows), task diversity, and the explicit inclusion of multiple graph extraction strategies as a first-class evaluation axis rather than a fixed preprocessing choice.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The absence of a neutral, large-scale RDB benchmark has forced the community into either single-table tabular evaluations or graph evaluations on pre-processed structures that may mask or introduce inductive biases unrelated to the original data. By providing a unified 4D toolbox with 8 diverse datasets spanning e-commerce, social networks, and academic domains, this work enables the first systematic head-to-head comparisons that can actually inform practitioners on which modeling paradigm (tabular, GNN, or hybrid) is appropriate for their specific RDB. The central open question the paper surfaces is that no single approach dominates across all dataset-task-extractor-predictor combinations, suggesting that future high-performing solutions may lie at the boundary between tabular and graph ML, and that the right choice is highly context-dependent.
