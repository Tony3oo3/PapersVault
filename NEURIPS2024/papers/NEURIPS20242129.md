## Problem

Long-term time series forecasting (LTSF) models have escalated to millions of parameters to capture complex temporal and spatial dependencies, yet yield only marginal accuracy gains over simple linear baselines. Patching-based methods destroy temporal/spatial identities and require high-dimensional latent spaces to compensate, while existing decomposition methods struggle on datasets with intricate correlations (e.g., Traffic, Electricity). No prior method achieves both parsimony (minimal parameters) and full capability (SOTA accuracy) simultaneously.

## Input / Output

The model consumes multivariate time series of shape (N variates × T_in steps), where T_in is 168 for hourly datasets (ECL, Traffic, ETTh1, ETTh2) and 432 or 384 for 10-minute/15-minute datasets (ETTm1, ETTm2, Weather). It produces predictions of shape (N × T_out) for horizons T_out ∈ {3, 24, 96, 192, 336}. The hidden dimension d is set to only 8, and the spatial component is disabled for volatile datasets lacking cross-variate correlations, reducing the model to ~5K parameters versus ~25K for regular datasets.

## Method

SSCNN sequentially decomposes each series into four structured components—long-term (trend), seasonal (cyclic), short-term (local irregularity), and spatial (cross-variate regularity)—using attention-based normalization (T-AttnNorm and S-AttnNorm) with learned selection maps that weight which time steps or variates contribute to each component. Each component is then extrapolated to future horizons via a separate mapping matrix (E*), with the long-term component using uniform averaging, the seasonal component using cycle-level softmax attention, the short-term component using a small regression window, and the spatial component using zero-padding. The eight resulting component/residual streams are fused through a polynomial regression layer capturing both additive and multiplicative interactions. Theoretically, decomposition explicitly isolates components into disjoint subspaces (eliminating cross-component interference entirely), whereas patching only implicitly attenuates it; this yields a parameter count of O(d_step² + T_in·T_out/c² + T_in²/c² + d_step·T_out) versus O(d_patch·T_in·T_out + d_patch²) for patching methods, with d_step=8 versus d_patch=64–256.

## Results

SSCNN achieves the best MSE and MAE across all seven benchmark datasets (Weather, Traffic, Electricity, ETTh1, ETTh2, ETTm1, ETTm2) with prediction lengths of 3–336 steps. On the most challenging datasets, it records MSE 0.118 / MAE 0.212 on Electricity and MSE 0.338 / MAE 0.235 on Traffic, outperforming the next-best baselines by 2–4%. Remarkably, SSCNN uses only ~25,000 parameters (ECL/Traffic) or ~5,000 parameters (other datasets), representing over 99% fewer parameters than PatchTST, iTransformer, and TimesNet, and 87% fewer than DLinear for long-horizon forecasting.

## Baselines & Comparisons

SSCNN wins on every dataset and every prediction length against all eight baselines. Against the strongest Transformer-based competitors, it improves average MSE by 8.8% over iTransformer, 4.4% over PatchTST, and 8.3% over Crossformer. On the complex, highly regular datasets (Electricity and Traffic), the gains are most pronounced: SSCNN beats iTransformer by 2% and PatchTST by 4% on ECL, and by 2% and 3% respectively on Traffic. Against the decomposition-based SCNN (its closest architectural relative), the selection mechanism yields an additional ~8% improvement, confirming its value for fine-grained dependency modeling. Against DLinear, SSCNN matches or slightly exceeds accuracy while using 87% fewer parameters at long horizons (T_out=336). The only trade-off is computational cost: SSCNN's FLOPs are higher than iTransformer's because it processes the full sequence at each layer rather than compressing via fully-connected layers, though the authors note this is amenable to parallel acceleration.

## Relation to Prior Work

SSCNN extends the decomposition-based forecasting line initiated by SCNN (Deng et al., 2024) by introducing a selection mechanism (T-AttnNorm and S-AttnNorm) that distinguishes fine-grained dependencies across individual time steps, addressing SCNN's weakness on complex datasets like Traffic and Electricity. Conceptually, the paper departs from the dominant patching paradigm (PatchTST, iTransformer, Crossformer) by providing the first formal analysis showing that decomposition explicitly isolates components into disjoint subspaces while patching only implicitly attenuates their interactions through orthogonality. It also positions itself against the field's trend of inflating parameter counts toward LLM-scale models, arguing that domain-structured decomposition can achieve superior accuracy with orders-of-magnitude fewer parameters.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper challenges the prevailing 'bigger is better' assumption in LTSF by demonstrating that a ~25K-parameter model can match or exceed million-parameter Transformers across seven standard benchmarks, with the strongest gains on datasets with complex regular patterns. The analytical framework comparing decomposition and patching from a representation-space perspective provides a principled rationale for why structured decomposition is inherently more parameter-efficient, potentially guiding future architecture design. Key limitations include higher FLOPs than compression-based baselines (iTransformer) and the need to manually configure cycle length c and short-term span δ; the authors identify automating architecture selection and reducing computational cost as important future directions.
