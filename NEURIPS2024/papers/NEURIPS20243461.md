## Problem

In causal bandits with an unknown causal graph containing latent confounders, the optimal intervention is not restricted to the parents of the reward node, making learning only the parent set insufficient. While full causal structure discovery is unnecessary for regret minimization, no prior work characterizes exactly which latent confounders must be detected to correctly identify all possibly optimal arms (POMISs), nor provides a sample-efficient algorithm with theoretical guarantees for learning causal graphs with confounders.

## Input / Output

The algorithm consumes interventional data samples obtained from do-operations on subsets of discrete observed variables (each with domain [K]) and a binary reward variable Y. It produces a learned semi-Markovian causal graph (directed edges plus bidirected edges for latent confounders) restricted to the ancestors of Y, a set of POMISs (each a special subset of An(Y)), and ultimately the optimal hard intervention arm. The key constraint is that the algorithm requires access to interventional data from specific do(W=w) and do(X_i=x_i, W=w) configurations, which are collected during the discovery phase and reused for confounder detection.

## Method

The paper proposes a two-phase algorithm. Phase 1 learns the induced observable subgraph on An(Y) via a randomized procedure that repeatedly computes transitive closures under randomly sampled interventions and accumulates edges through transitive reduction, then detects only the necessary and sufficient subset of latent confounders (characterized by Theorem 3.1: confounders between Y and An(Y), plus confounders between specific ancestor pairs determined iteratively via MUCT) to construct the POMIS set. Phase 2 applies standard UCB over the reduced POMIS action space. The key theoretical contribution is proving that the number of interventional samples needed to identify all POMISs scales polynomially in n, and establishing a sublinear cumulative regret bound. The confounder detection uses an interventional do-see test comparing P(x_j|do(x_i),do(W=w)) against P(x_j|x_i,do(W=w)), with sample complexity governed by gap assumptions (ε, γ) and a lower-bound assumption (η) on conditional probabilities.

## Results

The main theoretical result (Theorem 5.1) establishes that Algorithm 4 achieves sublinear cumulative regret R_T bounded by a term that is polynomial in the number of nodes n (for the discovery phase) plus a standard UCB regret term over the POMIS set. The number of interventional samples required to learn the true POMIS set scales as 8αd_max·log(|An(Y)|)·(KA|An(Y)| + max(B,C)), which is polynomial in n. In simulations (Figures 2–4), Algorithm 4 consistently uses fewer interventional samples than full graph discovery (learning all latent confounders), with the advantage diminishing as the latent confounder density ρ_L increases. The number of arms in the POMIS set remains small for randomly generated graphs, making the UCB phase converge quickly relative to the discovery phase.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not include a formal head-to-head comparison table against prior causal bandit methods (e.g., [7], [9], [10]), as those assume causal sufficiency (no latent confounders) or known MECs, making direct comparison inapplicable. The primary empirical comparison is between Algorithm 4 (partial discovery targeting only necessary and sufficient confounders per Theorem 3.1) and a full-discovery baseline that learns all latent confounders among An(Y). In Figure 2, Algorithm 4 requires fewer interventional samples across all tested densities (ρ, ρ_L ∈ {0.2, 0.4, 0.6}), with the gap narrowing as ρ_L increases toward 0.6. In Figure 4 (cumulative regret at T ~ 10^8, ρ = ρ_L = 0.3, n ∈ {10, 15, 20}), Algorithm 4 achieves lower cumulative regret than full discovery in all three graph sizes, confirming that selective confounder testing reduces the discovery-phase cost. The paper also contrasts its approach against the naive baseline of running UCB over all (K+1)^n possible arms, showing that discovery reduces the action space to the (typically much smaller) POMIS set before bandit learning begins.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on Lee & Bareinboim's (2018) POMIS framework—which characterizes possibly optimal interventions via UC-territories and interventional borders—and on Kocaoglu et al.'s (2017) randomized experimental design for learning causal graphs with latent variables. It departs from prior causal bandit work (Lu et al. 2021, Konobeev et al. 2023, De Kroon et al. 2022) that either assumes a known causal graph or causal sufficiency (no latent confounders), by handling unknown graphs with latent confounders and no parametric assumptions. The key conceptual departure is a partial-discovery result: Theorem 3.1 characterizes exactly which subset of latent confounders must be detected for correct POMIS identification, showing full causal discovery is unnecessary.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first sample-efficient, theoretically grounded algorithm for causal bandits with unknown graphs containing latent confounders, with polynomial sample complexity in the number of nodes. The partial-discovery characterization is practically significant because it can reduce the number of confounder tests from O(n²) to O(n) in favorable graph structures. Limitations include strong assumptions (faithfulness, constant gap conditions ε and γ, lower-bounded conditional probabilities η), the worst-case exponential growth of the POMIS set, and empirical validation only on small randomly generated chordal graphs (n ≤ 20). Open questions include whether the characterization tightens under additional structural assumptions, whether the approach extends to continuous variables or stochastic interventions, and how the method performs when the gap constants are small in practice.
