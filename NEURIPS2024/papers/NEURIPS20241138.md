## Problem

Existing canonical-based video editing methods consolidate video content into a single representative image but focus exclusively on reconstruction fidelity, producing distorted or warped canonical images that downstream image editors (e.g., ControlNet) cannot recognize or edit meaningfully. This limits their utility for precise, localized video editing tasks where a natural-looking canonical image is essential for the editing operation to succeed.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20241138/cropped_page1_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method takes an RGB video (up to 100 frames) and an optional text prompt as input, and produces one or more natural canonical images along with the corresponding edited video sequence. For complex scenes, the video is segmented into k canonical images (typically k=3) with overlap windows between segments; frames within overlap regions are reconstructed via linear interpolation between adjacent canonical images. The canonical image is a standard-resolution RGB image that encodes all spatial and temporal content of the video, and editing is performed directly on this image before warping back to all frames.

## Method

NaRCan introduces a hybrid deformation field that decomposes pixel displacement into a trainable homography matrix (capturing global rigid motion) and a residual deformation MLP (capturing local non-rigid warping), replacing the monolithic MLP displacement prediction of prior methods like CoDeF. The key innovation is integrating a LoRA-fine-tuned latent diffusion model as a regularizer during training: a scene-specific token is added to the diffusion model, which is fine-tuned on the input frames, and its denoising output provides a diffusion loss that constrains the canonical image to appear natural rather than merely reconstructive. A hierarchical noise-and-update scheduling strategy progressively reduces noise intensity (0.4→0.3→0.2) and target-image generation frequency (every 10→100→2000 iterations) across 12,000 training steps, accelerating training by 14× (4.8 h → 20 min on a single RTX 4090). For overly complex scenes, Separated NaRCan splits the video into k segments with dedicated residual MLPs and overlap windows, using the grid trick (concatenating canonical images into a 2×2 grid) with ControlNet to ensure cross-segment style consistency.

## Results

On the BalanceCC benchmark (50 videos), NaRCan achieves the best temporal consistency across all three metrics: short-term warping error of 0.0065, long-term warping error of 0.0484, and interpolation error of 8.365. The method also accelerates training by 14× (from 4.8 hours to 20 minutes) via its noise and diffusion prior update scheduling. In a user study with 36 participants, NaRCan achieves the highest preference ratio across temporal consistency, text-prompt alignment, and overall quality compared to all baselines.

## Baselines & Comparisons

NaRCan outperforms all three quantitative baselines on every temporal consistency metric. Against CoDeF [45], the closest canonical-based competitor, NaRCan reduces long-term warping error by 38% (0.0484 vs. 0.0785) and interpolation error by 4.1% (8.365 vs. 8.721), while also improving short-term warping error (0.0065 vs. 0.0085). Against Hashing-nvd [5], NaRCan achieves a 7% reduction in short-term warping error and a 9% reduction in interpolation error. Against MeDM [13], NaRCan improves long-term warping error by 17% (0.0484 vs. 0.0583) and interpolation error by 16% (8.365 vs. 9.941). CCEdit [15] is compared only qualitatively in Figure 5, where it fails to correctly identify background objects and exhibits temporal inconsistency. In the user study, NaRCan is preferred over all baselines on all three subjective criteria (temporal consistency, prompt alignment, overall quality).

## Relation to Prior Work

NaRCan extends the canonical-based video editing lineage initiated by CoDeF [45] by replacing its pure MLP deformation field with a hybrid architecture combining a trainable homography matrix for global motion and a residual deformation MLP for local non-rigid changes. The key conceptual departure from prior canonical methods (CoDeF, Hashing-nvd) is the introduction of a LoRA fine-tuned latent diffusion model as a regularizer that enforces naturalness on the canonical image, addressing the observation that reconstruction-accurate canonical images can be visually distorted and thus unusable by downstream tools like ControlNet. The Separated NaRCan variant borrows the grid trick from RAVE [24] to maintain style consistency across multiple canonical images, and the noise/diffusion-prior update scheduling is a novel training acceleration technique specific to this pipeline.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper identifies and addresses a practical gap in canonical-based video editing: canonical images optimized purely for reconstruction fidelity can be so distorted that standard image-editing tools fail to recognize scene content, rendering the entire pipeline useless for tasks like prompt-based editing. The 14× training speedup from the hierarchical noise scheduling (4.8 hours → 20 minutes) makes the approach more accessible, and the modular design (deformation field + diffusion prior + separation) allows practitioners to tune quality vs. speed trade-offs. However, the approach still requires per-scene LoRA fine-tuning (~30 minutes) and can fail under extreme scene changes, leaving open questions about scalability to longer videos, reducing the fine-tuning overhead, and whether the diffusion prior can be replaced by lighter-weight naturalness constraints.
