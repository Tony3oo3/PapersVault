## Problem

Standard vision models (CNNs, ViTs) maintain a fixed correspondence between their token structure and image pixel locations, meaning each token is inherently tied to a specific spatio-temporal position. This 'on-the-grid' entanglement is particularly problematic for video, where scene content moves across the image plane due to ego-motion or object motion, causing the representation to change even though the underlying scene elements remain the same. The paper asks whether a self-supervised video model can learn representations that bind to scene elements rather than pixel locations, enabling consistent tracking of content through time.

## Input / Output

MooG takes sequences of RGB video frames (128×128 or 256×256) and processes them recurrently, maintaining a persistent set of 1024 off-the-grid latent tokens (512-dimensional) whose number is independent of input resolution. During training, the model is unrolled over 8-frame clips and trained to predict the next frame via L2 pixel loss. For downstream readouts, the model additionally accepts query points or bounding boxes from the first frame (for tracking tasks) and produces point tracks, depth maps, or box predictions through cross-attention decoders that read from both predicted and corrected latent states.

## Method

MooG is a recurrent transformer architecture with three components that decouple representation structure from image structure via cross-attention: a predictor (3-layer self-attention transformer) that forecasts the next latent state from the previous corrected state, a corrector (2-layer cross-attention transformer) that updates the prediction by attending to a CNN-encoded feature grid of the current frame, and a decoder (6-layer cross-attention transformer) that reconstructs pixels using spatial coordinates as queries attending to the predicted state. The key design choice is that the frame is decoded only from the predicted state (not the corrected state), preventing the problem from collapsing to auto-encoding and forcing tokens to genuinely anticipate scene content. The latent state is initialized with random Gaussian noise (σ=10⁻⁴) to break token symmetry, and the model is trained with a simple L2 next-frame prediction loss on sub-sampled pixels. For downstream tasks, the paper attaches either grid-based readout decoders (cross-attention with spatial coordinates for dense predictions like depth) or recurrent query-based readouts (per-target latent states updated by cross-attending into MooG states) for tracking tasks like points and boxes.

## Results

In the frozen-representation setting (Table 1), MooG (~35M params) achieves 0.839 AJ on MOVi-E point tracking, 0.0359 AbsRel on depth, and 0.793 IoU on box tracking, substantially outperforming DINOv2-B (0.544 AJ, 0.0370 AbsRel, 0.738 IoU) and VideoMAE v2-B (0.681 AJ, 0.0458 AbsRel, 0.736 IoU) with fewer parameters. In the end-to-end setting (Table 2), MooG reaches 0.886 AJ on MOVi-E points and 0.778 AJ on DAVIS points, while rivaling TAPIR (0.823 vs. 0.824 AJ) on DAVIS-8 point tracking and outperforming TAP-Net (0.687 AJ) by a wide margin. For monocular depth on Waymo, MooG achieves 0.094 AbsRel, beating DPT with ViT-22b (0.154 AbsRel) by a large margin despite using a much smaller backbone.

## Baselines & Comparisons

Against DINOv1-B and DINOv2-B, MooG wins decisively in the frozen setting on point tracking (0.839 vs. 0.518/0.544 AJ on MOVi-E; 0.687 vs. 0.409/0.402 AJ on DAVIS) and box tracking (0.793 vs. 0.724/0.738 IoU), while DINOv2 slightly edges MooG on MOVi-E depth (0.0370 vs. 0.0359 AbsRel). Against VideoMAE v2, MooG outperforms the S and B variants across all tasks in the frozen setting, and is competitive with the 1B-parameter G variant (0.839 vs. 0.822 AJ on MOVi-E points; 0.0359 vs. 0.0311 AbsRel on depth), despite using ~30x fewer parameters. Against the on-the-grid baselines (Grid, Grid Rec.), MooG consistently wins in the frozen setting (e.g., 0.839 vs. 0.769/0.778 AJ on MOVi-E points), though the gap narrows in the end-to-end setting where Grid Rec. slightly surpasses MooG on MOVi-E points (0.902 vs. 0.886 AJ). Against TAP-Net, MooG wins by a large margin on both DAVIS-8 (0.824 vs. 0.687 AJ) and DAVIS-full (0.510 vs. 0.392 AJ). Against TAPIR, MooG ties on DAVIS-8 (0.824 vs. 0.823 AJ) but trails on DAVIS-full (0.510 vs. 0.580 AJ), reflecting TAPIR's specialized cost-volume design for long sequences. Against DPT (ViT-L/16, ViT-E/14, ViT-22b) on Waymo depth, MooG achieves 0.094 AbsRel, outperforming all three by 0.06–0.07 AbsRel despite using a far smaller backbone.

## Relation to Prior Work

MooG builds on the cross-attention-based latent token paradigm established by Perceiver/PerceiverIO and object-centric methods like Slot Attention, but departs from both by (1) not assuming a one-to-one token-to-object mapping and (2) processing video recurrently so tokens persistently track scene elements across arbitrary-length sequences. It contrasts with the dominant grid-based self-supervised video learners (DINO, VideoMAE v2) by explicitly decoupling the representation structure from the pixel grid, and with Scene Representation Transformers by adding recurrence to encourage temporal binding. The key conceptual departure is that a simple next-frame prediction loss—without any explicit tracking or object supervision—is sufficient to induce scene-grounded, motion-tracking latent tokens.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper demonstrates that off-the-grid representations learned with a trivial L2 pixel loss outperform strong grid-based self-supervised baselines (DINO, VideoMAE v2) on point tracking, depth estimation, and object tracking, particularly in the frozen-representation setting, suggesting that architectural inductive bias (decoupling tokens from the image grid) can substitute for scale or task-specific supervision. However, the model remains deterministic with a short 8-frame prediction horizon, struggles with semantic downstream tasks, and the gap over grid-based baselines narrows substantially in the end-to-end setting. Open questions include whether introducing stochasticity and longer horizons would yield richer higher-level features, how the approach scales in model size and pretraining data, and how tokens behave when scene elements appear or disappear.
