## Problem

Existing approaches to detecting strategic manipulation of ML models either assume known and identical feature-manipulation costs across agents (strategic classification) or treat gamed inputs as distributional outliers (anomaly detection). In practice, manipulation costs are unknown and heterogeneous across agents, and gamed attributes need not be outlier-like. This paper addresses the problem of ranking agents by their gaming propensity without ground-truth fraud labels and without assuming uniform manipulation costs.

## Input / Output

The method consumes a tabular observational dataset of the form {(x_i, d_i, p_i)}, where x_i are covariates (e.g., demographics, diagnosis codes), d_i ∈ {0,1} are binary decisions (e.g., whether a diagnosis was reported), and p_i is an agent indicator (e.g., insurance plan or U.S. state). The output is an ordinal ranking of all agents by their gaming deterrence parameter λ_p, constructed from pairwise causal effect estimates rather than point estimates, since λ_p is provably only partially identifiable. Rankings are evaluated via top-k sensitivity and discounted cumulative gain against ground-truth rankings (synthetic) or domain-relevant state-level statistics (Medicare case study).

## Method

The core insight is to recast gaming detection as a causal effect estimation problem in the Neyman-Rubin potential outcomes framework, where each agent is a 'treatment' and the decision d_i is the 'outcome.' The paper proves that while the gaming parameter λ_p is only partially identifiable (Proposition 1), a ranking of agents by λ_p is fully identifiable (Theorem 1): under shared reward/cost functions and standard causal assumptions (conditional exchangeability, consistency, positivity), the sign of the pairwise average treatment effect τ(p,p′) = E[d_i(p)|x_i] − E[d_i(p′)|x_i] determines the ordering of λ_p. In practice, standard causal effect estimators (S+IPW, DragonNet, R-learner, PSM) are trained on the observational data to estimate τ for all agent pairs, and the resulting pairwise comparisons are aggregated into a global ranking. All models are neural networks with one-hot encoded agent indicators, trained via a 7:3 train-test split with early stopping on a 20% validation subset.

## Results

On synthetic data with 20 agents and high confounding (mean range 0.9), the best causal estimator (S+IPW) achieves 0.860 ± 0.135 top-5 sensitivity at 7 audits, compared to 0.520 ± 0.215 for KNN outlier detection and 56.1 ± 5.11 vs. 42.3 ± 10.8 DCG. In terms of area under the sensitivity curve (AUSC), S+IPW improves from 0.614 ± 0.096 (no confounding) to 0.837 ± 0.067 (maximum confounding), while the payout-only baseline degrades to 0.299 ± 0.067 (worse than random at 0.473 ± 0.101). In the U.S. Medicare case study, the S+IPW gaming ranking correlates most strongly with for-profit provider prevalence (Spearman ρ = 0.294, p = 0.036 for % for-profit OP PT/SP providers; ρ = 0.272, p = 0.053 for for-profit-to-non-profit hospital ratio).

## Baselines & Comparisons

Causal effect estimators (S+IPW, DragonNet, R-learner) consistently outperform all non-causal baselines at moderate-to-high confounding. S+IPW is the strongest causal method, achieving 0.860 sensitivity at 7 audits vs. 0.520 for KNN [17] and 42.3 DCG for KNN vs. 56.1 for S+IPW at mean range 0.9. Anomaly detection methods (KNN [17], ECOD [22], DIF [23]) maintain roughly constant performance across confounding levels (slightly above random) but are outperformed by causal methods by 10–30% in AUSC at high confounding. The payout-only naive baseline degrades below random ranking under sufficient confounding (AUSC 0.299 vs. 0.473 at R_μ = 1.0), confirming that raw decision rates are misleading when populations differ. Among causal estimators, S+IPW uniquely improves with increasing confounding (0.614 → 0.837 AUSC), while DragonNet [45] and R-learner [39] degrade slightly (0.755 → 0.696 and 0.728 → 0.635 respectively), likely due to overlap challenges. PSM [43] underperforms other causal methods due to difficulties with multi-treatment confounding control, and S/T-learners [44] perform similarly to DragonNet.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends strategic classification (Hardt et al., 2016), which models gaming as utility maximization but assumes known or identical feature-manipulation costs across agents, by introducing a heterogeneous gaming deterrence parameter λ_p that scales each agent's perceived cost. It departs from the anomaly-detection paradigm for fraud detection (which assumes gamed inputs are distributional outliers) by instead making behavioral assumptions grounded in utility maximization. The key conceptual move is recasting multi-agent gaming detection as a causal effect estimation problem under the Neyman-Rubin potential outcomes framework, where each agent is a 'treatment' and the observed decision rate is the outcome—enabling counterfactual comparisons that prior strategic-classification work (e.g., Dong et al., 2018, which also allows heterogeneous costs) does not exploit.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper provides a clean theoretical separation: the gaming parameter λ_p is only partially identifiable (lower-bounded), but a full ranking of agents by λ_p is identifiable via causal inference—a result that makes the problem tractable without requiring knowledge of ground-truth decision rates. The practical implication is that audit resources in settings like Medicare upcoding can be targeted more efficiently, with the synthetic experiments showing causal estimators (especially S+IPW) outperforming anomaly detectors and payout-only baselines under realistic confounding. Key limitations include the assumption that all deviation from ground truth is due to gaming (ignoring quality differences), the strong rational-actor assumption, and the fact that the Medicare case study is exploratory with mostly non-significant correlations. Open questions include formalizing when IPW estimation errors preserve the sign of treatment effects, handling bounded rationality more rigorously, and extending to non-binary or multi-dimensional decisions.
