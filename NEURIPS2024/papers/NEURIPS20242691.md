## Problem

Bounding the convergence of general state-space Markov chains to stationarity in Wasserstein distance is a fundamental but notoriously difficult problem. Existing analytical tools (drift & minorization, drift & contraction, and the recent contractive drift condition) are pen-and-paper methods that only yield closed-form bounds for highly structured, stylized chains, leaving realistic systems without computable convergence guarantees. No general-purpose computational algorithm existed to bridge this gap.

## Input / Output

The method takes as input a locally Lipschitz random mapping f defining the Markov chain transition, a positive function U bounded away from zero (specifying the drift magnitude), and a compact state space X ⊂ R^d. It produces an explicit Wasserstein convergence bound of the form W(X_n, X_∞) ≤ Cr^n with computable constants C and r, or a polynomial O(1/n^{m-1}) bound obtained by iteratively solving a sequence of CDEs. The intermediate output is the CDE solution V, a Lipschitz continuous Lyapunov function on X, whose sup and inf values directly determine the exponential rate r = 1 − inf U / sup V.

## Method

The core insight is that the contractive drift condition (CD), an inequality KV ≤ V − U, is an equality by nature: if the inequality has a solution, the corresponding equation KV = V − U (the Contractive Drift Equation, CDE) also has one. Solving the CDE via a neural network is more natural than PINN-style PDE solving because only Lipschitz continuity is required (neural networks are inherently Lipschitz) and SGD simultaneously handles both the integrated residual and the integral operator K. Crucially, the equality form admits a simple unbiased gradient estimator using two independent copies of the random mapping (f₁ and f₋₁), avoiding the biased conditional stochastic optimization that would arise with the inequality form. The DCDC algorithm trains a neural network via SGD on this unbiased estimator, then converts the learned solution into a rigorous convergence bound by verifying the CD on a finite grid with high probability (Theorem 5 provides the sample complexity). For polynomial rates, DCDC iteratively solves a sequence of CDEs (KV₁ = V₁ − V₀, KV₂ = V₂ − V₁, …), each yielding a tighter bound.

## Results

DCDC generates explicit Wasserstein convergence bounds for four realistic Markov chains. For mini-batch SGD on logistic regression (d=2, m=100, α=0.1, β=10), it recovers an exponential rate of 1−1.07×10⁻³ with pre-multiplier C=8.1, decomposing the rate into a regularization contribution (1×10⁻³) and an intrinsic data-driven contribution (7×10⁻⁵). For a tandem fluid network (c=1, r₁=1.1, r₂=1.0), it obtains W(Xₙ,X∞)≤5.67(1−0.017)ⁿ. For a 3-dimensional autoregressive process with largest eigenvalue λ=0.850, DCDC recovers the exact rate by producing V≡0.668, yielding KV=0.850V=λV. All experiments complete within 10 minutes on an M2 MacBook Air with 8GB RAM using 1M Adam steps.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DCDC is the first general-purpose sample-based algorithm for bounding Markov chain convergence, so no direct computational baselines exist for comparison. The paper positions itself against two categories of prior work: (1) analytical drift-and-minorization (D&M) and drift-and-contraction (D&C) methods, which produce overly conservative TV bounds and cannot handle realistic unstructured chains; and (2) the contractive drift (CD) framework of Qu et al. (2023), which yields parametrically sharp bounds but only for stylized, structured models amenable to closed-form analysis. The autoregressive process example serves as an internal validation: DCDC recovers the exact pen-and-paper rate λ=0.850 (via V≡0.668), confirming that the neural-network solver can match analytical results when they are available. For the mini-batch SGD and tandem network examples, DCDC discovers structure (wedge-shaped Lyapunov functions, boundary-removal planes) that mirrors techniques used in the analytical CD literature, demonstrating that the learned solutions are not merely numerical artifacts but capture the same geometric insights that closed-form methods exploit.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the Contractive Drift Condition (CD) framework of Qu et al. (2023), which replaced the traditional two-region drift-and-minorization/contraction (D&M/D&C) conditions with a single condition enforced on the entire state space to yield explicit Wasserstein convergence bounds. The key conceptual move is observing that the CD inequality can be replaced by an equality (the Contractive Drift Equation, CDE) without losing the convergence guarantee, which transforms the problem into one amenable to physics-informed neural network (PINN) solvers. The PINN lineage (Sirignano & Spiliopoulos 2018; Raissi et al. 2019) provides the training machinery, but the adaptation to integral equations rather than PDEs—exploiting the fact that SGD already handles the integral in the CDE—avoids the biased-gradient problem of conditional stochastic optimization that would arise with the inequality form. The paper also positions itself relative to the growing literature on learned Lyapunov functions for deterministic systems (Liu et al. 2023; Dawson et al. 2023), extending the certificate-learning paradigm to random dynamical systems with quantified convergence rates.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

DCDC is the first general-purpose computational method for bounding Markov chain convergence in Wasserstein distance, moving the field from the 'pen-and-paper age' to a regime where realistic, less-structured chains (e.g., stochastic processing networks, constant-step-size SGD on finite datasets) can be analyzed without closed-form tractability. The discovery that the network independently recovers analytically-derived geometric structures—wedge-shaped Lyapunov functions and the boundary-removal technique—suggests the method captures genuine dynamical structure rather than merely fitting residuals. Key limitations include the restriction to compact state spaces (a stitching strategy for non-compact spaces is sketched but not formalized) and the absence of sample-complexity lower bounds, both flagged for future work. Broader implications include potential use of the CD condition as a policy regularizer in reinforcement learning and control of ergodic systems.
