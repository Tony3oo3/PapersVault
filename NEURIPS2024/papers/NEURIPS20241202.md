## Problem

Current instruction-guided image editing models fail on action-centric edits (e.g., 'make the dog jump in the air') and reasoning-centric edits (e.g., 'move the mug to the right of the table') because existing training datasets are either noisy—containing unmentioned visual changes—or entirely lack such examples. The root cause is a data quality gap: high-quality 'truly minimal' edit pairs, where only the prompt-described change occurs, are scarce for non-object/attribute edits.

## Input / Output

The model takes a source RGB image (256×256) and a natural-language editing instruction as input, and produces a single edited RGB image as output. The critical constraint is that the output should contain one (or at most two) semantic change matching the prompt while preserving all other aspects of the source image exactly. Training data consists of triplets (source image, prompt, target image) where the target is a truly minimal modification of the source.

## Method

The paper introduces AURORA, a 289K-example dataset assembled from four carefully curated sub-datasets: MagicBrush (object/attribute edits via human inpainting), Action-Genome-Edit (11K video frame pairs with tightly controlled human-annotated captions), Something-Something-Edit (119K first/last frame pairs from pre-defined action templates), and Kubric-Edit (150K simulation-engine pairs covering spatial, rotation, count, and attribute changes). The model is the InstructPix2Pix latent diffusion architecture, first pretrained on InstructPix2Pix then MagicBrush, then finetuned on the full AURORA mix with equal sampling across all four sources (upsampling smaller sets by 15×). The key design principle is 'truly minimal change': every training pair must contain only the prompt-described visual difference, with no camera movement, no extraneous changes, and no quality degradation. The paper also contributes AURORA-BENCH (400 expert-curated examples across 8 tasks including OOD transfer) and DiscEdit, a discriminative metric that tests whether a model correctly distinguishes prompts requiring a change from those requiring none.

## Results

On AURORA-BENCH, the AURORA model achieves an overall human-judged edit success score of 41.3 (scale 0–100), outperforming the strongest baseline MagicBrush (32.6) by 8.7 points. The largest gains are on action-centric and reasoning-centric tasks: AURORA scores 35.6 on Action-Genome (vs. 16.3 for MagicBrush), 27.3 on WhatsUp spatial reasoning (vs. 3.0), and 59.6 on Kubric (vs. 9.3). On the DiscEdit discriminative metric, AURORA achieves 0.565–0.592 accuracy on most tasks (above the 0.5 random baseline), while MagicBrush falls below random (0.371–0.477).

## Baselines & Comparisons

AURORA outperforms all four baselines on the overall AURORA-BENCH score. Against MagicBrush (the strongest baseline, overall 32.6), AURORA (41.3) wins decisively on action tasks (Action-Genome: 35.6 vs. 16.3; Something-Something: 31.8 vs. 17.0) and reasoning tasks (WhatsUp: 27.3 vs. 3.0; Kubric: 59.6 vs. 9.3; CLEVR: 46.1 vs. 22.0), while remaining competitive on object/attribute edits (60.5 vs. 61.7) but trailing on global edits (Emu-Global: 33.0 vs. 42.3). Against InstructPix2Pix (20.5), AURORA leads by 20.8 points overall, with particularly large margins on reasoning (Kubric: 59.6 vs. 5.7) and action (Action-Genome: 35.6 vs. 13.3). Against MGIE (22.5), AURORA leads by 18.8 points, again dominated by action and reasoning gaps. Against GenHowTo (10.8), AURORA leads by 30.5 points across nearly every task, confirming the paper's hypothesis that GenHowTo's noisy training data leads to over-editing and poor instruction adherence.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds directly on the InstructPix2Pix architecture and training recipe, fine-tuning it on a newly curated dataset (AURORA) that combines MagicBrush (object/attribute edits), video-frame pairs from Action Genome and Something-Something (action edits), and Kubric simulation pairs (spatial/reasoning edits). It departs from prior editing datasets (InstructPix2Pix, HQ-Edit, GenHowTo) by enforcing a 'truly minimal change' criterion — each source-target pair contains only the single semantic change described by the prompt — and by explicitly covering action-centric and reasoning-centric edits that prior datasets largely ignored. The proposed DiscEdit metric draws on the line of work repurposing diffusion models as zero-shot discriminators (Krojer et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023), adapting that idea to evaluate whether an editing model understands when a prompt requires no change.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper's most impactful finding is methodological: standard visual-similarity metrics (CLIP-I, DINO, L1/L2) used to evaluate editing models essentially reward copying the source image, with a trivial 'naive copy' baseline outperforming all editing models — undermining a common evaluation practice in the field. The AURORA dataset (289K examples) and AURORA-BENCH (400 expert-curated pairs across 8 tasks) provide much-needed resources for action and reasoning-centric editing, and the Sim2Real transfer from Kubric to real-world spatial reasoning (WhatsUp) is encouraging. However, absolute performance remains low (41.3/100 overall), the model still struggles with complex real-world action edits, and the authors openly question whether one-step image editing is the right paradigm for action-centric tasks versus full video generation. The work positions instruction-guided editing as a stepping stone toward generative world simulators, but the gap to robust world modeling remains large.
