## Problem

Synthetic data generated by deep generative models (DGMs) such as CTGAN and TVAE introduce substantial bias and imprecision into downstream statistical analyses, causing standard errors to shrink slower than the canonical 1/√n rate and confidence intervals to under-cover the true parameter. Existing correction methods (e.g., the √(1+m/n) SE inflation) were derived for parametric generators and do not address the data-adaptive bias inherent to DGMs, leaving no generator-agnostic strategy for valid inference from a single DGM-based synthetic dataset.

## Input / Output

The method requires three inputs: a trained DGM (CTGAN or TVAE), the original dataset it was trained on (needed to compute the bias correction), and a synthetic dataset sampled from the DGM. The output is a post-processed (shifted) synthetic dataset tailored to a specific target parameter, from which standard estimators yield valid standard errors and confidence intervals. Crucially, the debiasing is estimand-specific: the same DGM must be separately debiased for each parameter of interest, and the procedure requires conditional sampling from the DGM (e.g., generating A and Y at fixed X levels) for regression-type targets.

## Method

The paper derives a two-step von Mises expansion of θ(P̃_m) − θ(P) into eight terms, identifying two bias terms that break root-n consistency: one arising from data-adaptive estimation on the synthetic data (removed by using EIC-based debiased estimators for the analysis) and one arising from the DGM's slow convergence to the true distribution (removed by shifting the synthetic samples). For the population mean, the shift is simply the difference between the original sample mean and the DGM's estimated mean (computed from a large DGM-generated sample). For a linear regression coefficient, the shift adds b·{Ã_i − Ê_{P̂_n}(A|X̃_i)} to each synthetic outcome, where b is the bias computed by evaluating the EIC on the original data against the DGM's conditional predictions. No retraining or fine-tuning of the DGM is required; the entire correction is a post-processing step. The resulting estimator has variance (1/m + 1/n)·E{φ(O,P)²}, generalizing the parametric √(1+m/n) correction, and converges at root-n rates when m→∞, making synthetic-data analysis asymptotically equivalent to original-data analysis.

## Results

The debiasing strategy restores root-n convergence of the standard error for both the population mean and the linear regression coefficient: the empirical SE convergence exponent increases from 0.01–0.31 (default synthetic data) to 0.43–0.50 (debiased synthetic data), matching the theoretical target of 0.5. For the population mean, debiasing achieves nominal 95% CI coverage across all sample sizes (n = 50 to 5000) and both DGMs (CTGAN, TVAE). In the International Stroke Trial case study (n = m = 500), default synthetic data yields 7 out of 15 false-positive significance conclusions, while debiased synthetic data reduces this to 0 out of 15, matching the behaviour of the original data.

## Baselines & Comparisons

The paper does not benchmark against external competing debiasing methods; instead, the primary comparison is between default (un-debiased) synthetic data and the proposed debiased synthetic data, evaluated on the same DGMs (CTGAN and TVAE) and the same downstream estimators. Debiasing consistently wins on all three metrics—bias, SE convergence rate, and CI coverage—across both DGMs and both estimators. For the population mean, debiasing achieves nominal coverage for all four model configurations (CTGAN/TVAE × SDV/Synthcity hyperparameters), while default synthetic data shows progressively worsening coverage as n grows. For the regression coefficient, TVAE (Synthcity) achieves nominal coverage at all sample sizes after debiasing, while CTGAN remains slightly anti-conservative, likely due to residual overfitting bias from the absence of sample splitting. In the Adult Census Income case study with m = 10^6, debiased synthetic data CIs are visually indistinguishable in length and position from those obtained on the original data, confirming asymptotic equivalence, whereas default synthetic data CIs are noticeably narrower and can exclude the true parameter.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper adapts the debiased/targeted machine learning framework (van der Laan and Rose, 2011; Chernozhukov et al., 2018) to a fundamentally different setting: rather than using ML in the analysis step, ML (specifically DGMs like CTGAN and TVAE) is used in the *generation* of synthetic data, and the debiasing is applied as a post-processing shift to the synthetic samples. It builds on the correction factor √(1+m/n) from Raab et al. (2016) for parametric synthetic data generators and the EIC-based estimator for regression coefficients from Vansteelandt and Dukes (2022), but extends both to the DGM setting where the generator's slow convergence introduces additional bias. The paper positions itself as the first generator-agnostic solution for valid inference from DGM-based synthetic data, departing from prior approaches (Raghunathan et al., 2003; Raisa et al., 2023) that are restricted to parametric or Bayesian generators.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

This work provides the first formal theoretical guarantees (root-n consistency, valid variance estimation) for statistical inference from DGM-generated synthetic data, addressing a gap that has limited the practical adoption of synthetic data in rigorous statistical analysis. The practical appeal is strong: debiasing requires only a post-processing shift of synthetic samples (no retraining), making it generator-agnostic and easy to implement. Key limitations include the requirement to know the target parameter in advance, the need for access to original training data, and the fact that coverage does not always reach nominal levels (particularly for CTGAN regression coefficients), likely due to residual overfitting bias from the absence of sample splitting. Open questions include extension to high-dimensional settings, simultaneous debiasing for multiple parameters, incorporation of differential privacy constraints, and whether efficient sample splitting (e.g., k-fold cross-fitting) can further improve coverage without incurring excessive finite-sample bias from smaller training sets.
