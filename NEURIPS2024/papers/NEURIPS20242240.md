## Problem

Current text-to-video diffusion models produce largely static outputs with minimal motion dynamics, failing to capture the complex movements described in text prompts. This stems from two root causes: (1) standard text encoders like CLIP are biased toward encoding nouns and spatial content while under-representing verbs and actions, and (2) text conditioning is applied only through spatial cross-attention on a per-frame basis, ignoring the temporal dimension where motion actually resides.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20242240/cropped_page2_idx1.webp)

## Input / Output

The model takes a single free-form text prompt as input and generates a 16-frame video at 256×256 resolution, encoded in a 32×32×16 latent space via a VQGAN with 8× compression. During inference, only the text prompt is required; optical flow supervision is used exclusively during training. The motion encoder is a CLIP ViT-H/14 (354M parameters, 77-token sequence, 1024-dim embeddings) initialized from pre-trained CLIP weights and fine-tuned with motion-specific objectives.

## Method

DEMO decomposes both text encoding and text conditioning into separate content and motion pathways. For encoding, it retains the original CLIP as a content encoder and introduces a motion encoder (initialized from CLIP) fine-tuned with a text-motion loss that constrains the [eos]-token cross-attention maps to mimic the temporal dynamics (optical flow) of ground-truth videos, plus a regularization loss preserving CLIP's original contrastive alignment. For conditioning, it keeps the original spatial cross-attention as content conditioning and adds a new temporal transformer (motion conditioning module) that injects motion embeddings along the temporal axis, trained under a video-motion loss that penalizes the L2 distance between consecutive-frame differences of the predicted clean latent and the ground-truth video latent. The full training objective combines the standard diffusion loss with weighted text-motion, regularization, and video-motion terms, all applied on top of the ModelScopeT2V base model fine-tuned on WebVid-10M.

## Results

On MSR-VTT (zero-shot), DEMO achieves FID 11.77, FVD 422, and CLIPSIM 0.2965, improving over the ModelScopeT2V baseline (FID 14.89, FVD 557, CLIPSIM 0.2941). On EvalCrafter, DEMO substantially boosts motion quality metrics: Flow Score from 2.51 to 4.89, Motion AC-Score from 44 to 58, and Action Score from 75.88 to 78.22. On VBench, Motion Dynamics improves from 62.50 to 68.90, though Temporal Flickering drops slightly from 96.02 to 94.63. On WebVid-10M validation, DEMO reaches FID 9.86, FVD 351, and CLIPSIM 0.3083.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DEMO outperforms its direct baseline ModelScopeT2V across all benchmarks on FID, FVD, and motion-specific metrics (e.g., FVD 422 vs 557 on MSR-VTT; Flow Score 4.89 vs 2.51 on EvalCrafter; Motion Dynamics 68.90 vs 62.50 on VBench), and also beats the fine-tuned ModelScopeT2V variant on every metric. Against MagicVideo, DEMO achieves a substantially lower FVD (422 vs 1290 on MSR-VTT; 547 vs 655 on UCF-101). However, DEMO trails several baselines on UCF-101 FVD: SVD (242), PyoCo (355), Make-A-Video (367), and Show-1 (394) all report lower FVD than DEMO's 547, and PyoCo also achieves a lower FID (9.73–10.21 vs 11.77) on MSR-VTT. On CLIPSIM, Make-A-Video (0.3049) and Show-1 (0.3072) slightly exceed DEMO (0.2965) on MSR-VTT, and VideoFactory (0.3005) also edges ahead. In the human evaluation, DEMO is preferred over ModelScopeT2V (74% motion quality), LaVie (62%), and VideoCrafter2 (52%) on motion quality, but loses on visual quality to LaVie (46%) and VideoCrafter2 (42%), which the authors attribute to their use of higher-quality training datasets (Vimeo-25M, JourneyDB) versus WebVid-10M.

## Relation to Prior Work

DEMO builds directly on ModelScopeT2V, a latent video diffusion model with a 3D U-Net backbone, and extends it by decomposing the single CLIP text encoder into separate content and motion encoders, paired with a novel temporal transformer for motion conditioning. This departs from prior T2V work that relies on spatial-only cross-attention for text conditioning, and contrasts with approaches that inject motion via external control signals (depth maps, poses, reference videos, bounding boxes) by deriving motion purely from textual descriptions. The pilot study demonstrating CLIP's differential sensitivity to content versus motion parts-of-speech provides a concrete diagnostic motivation for the decomposition, building on known observations about VLMs' bias toward nouns over verbs.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper addresses a persistent and practically important limitation in T2V generation—producing videos with realistic, complex motion—without requiring external references or pre-trained motion models, making the approach broadly applicable. The decomposition strategy (content vs. motion in both encoding and conditioning) plus the text-motion and video-motion supervision signals offer a reusable recipe that could be integrated into other latent video diffusion architectures, as demonstrated by the ZeroScope experiments. Key open questions remain: the model cannot generate sequential motions (e.g., 'a man talks, then a mixer is shown'), and increased motion dynamics come at a slight cost to temporal smoothness and flickering, suggesting a fundamental tension between motion richness and temporal coherence that the field has not yet resolved.
