limitations: CGGM requires additional lightweight per-modality classifiers during training, which incur extra computational resources (8–24 MB additional GPU memory) and forward passes, though these are discarded at inference. surprise_factor: The finding that a non-dominant modality (audio in CMU-MOSI) can have a *negative* cosine similarity with the fusion gradient—meaning it actively opposes the multimodal optimization direction—is a somewhat striking diagnostic, though it follows logically from the modality-competition literature. ## Problem

Multimodal models suffer from a 'greedy learner' effect where training converges on a single dominant modality, leaving other modalities under-optimized. Existing gradient modulation strategies are restricted to specific loss functions (cross-entropy), optimizers (SGD), and two-modality settings, and they only adjust gradient magnitude while ignoring gradient direction, which can point the fusion module toward the dominant modality and away from under-utilized ones.

## Input / Output

The method operates on late-fusion architectures where modality-specific encoders (Transformer encoders for UPMC-Food 101, CMU-MOSI, IEMOCAP; DeepLab v3+ for BraTS 2021) produce per-modality representations that are concatenated and fed into a shared fusion module. Inputs range from pre-extracted BERT/ViT features (food image + recipe text), extracted audio/text/video features (sentiment and emotion datasets), to raw 3D MRI volumes randomly sliced into 2D patches (BraTS 2021 with flair, t1, t2, t1ce). Outputs are task-specific: 101-class food labels, continuous sentiment scores, 4-class emotion labels, or 3-class nested tumor segmentation masks. Lightweight per-modality classifiers (1–2 MSA layers + FC, or a light decoder for segmentation) are attached during training to compute balancing signals but are discarded at inference.

## Method

CGGM introduces two coupled modulation mechanisms guided by auxiliary modality-specific classifiers. First, a magnitude modulation computes a per-modality balancing term B from the relative improvement rate (Δε) of each classifier's evaluation metric between consecutive iterations; the encoder gradient for modality m_i is scaled by B, which is small when that modality is improving fast and large when it is lagging, thereby slowing the dominant modality and accelerating under-optimized ones. Second, a direction modulation uses the classifier gradients as cheap proxies for true unimodal gradients (validated via t-SNE) and adds a cosine-similarity loss that pushes the fusion-module gradient toward the B-weighted average of all unimodal gradient directions, preventing the fusion from optimizing exclusively along the dominant modality. The total training objective is L_task + λ·L_gm, and the approach is agnostic to loss function, optimizer, and number of modalities, requiring only the addition of small classifiers that are removed at test time.

## Results

On UPMC-Food 101 (classification), CGGM achieves 92.94% accuracy and 92.90% F1, surpassing the previous best QMF (92.87%/92.85%). On CMU-MOSI (regression), CGGM reaches 82.84% binary accuracy, 33.73% 7-class accuracy, and 0.915 MAE. On IEMOCAP (classification), CGGM attains 75.38% accuracy and 74.97% F1, a substantial margin over the next-best ReconBoost (73.14%/72.71%). On BraTS 2021 (segmentation), CGGM achieves an average Dice of 73.94 (WT 76.94, TC 72.75, ET 72.14), outperforming ReconBoost (71.80) by over 2 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

CGGM consistently outperforms all compared baselines across all four datasets. On IEMOCAP, the margin is largest: CGGM (75.38% Acc) beats ReconBoost (73.14%) by 2.24 points and AGM (72.35%) by 3.03 points, indicating that gradient-direction modulation provides a substantial boost over magnitude-only methods. On UPMC-Food 101, CGGM (92.94% Acc) narrowly edges out QMF (92.87%) and clearly surpasses ReconBoost (92.52%) and PMR (92.01%). On BraTS 2021, CGGM achieves 73.94 average Dice, exceeding ReconBoost (71.80) by 2.14 points and QMF (71.61) by 2.33 points, demonstrating strong gains in the 4-modality segmentation setting. On CMU-MOSI, CGGM leads UMT (81.78% Acc-2) by 1.06 points and MSLR (81.22%) by 1.62 points. Notably, several baselines (AGM, QMF, ReconBoost) are not applicable to CMU-MOSI due to their reliance on cross-entropy loss or two-modality assumptions, whereas CGGM handles all task types and modality counts without modification.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper extends the family of gradient modulation methods for balanced multimodal learning (Wu et al. 2022, Peng et al. 2022, Li et al. 2023, Fan et al. 2023, Hua et al. 2024) by addressing two key limitations: prior methods only modulate gradient magnitude while ignoring direction, and they are restricted to specific loss functions (cross-entropy), optimizers (SGD), and two-modality settings. CGGM introduces lightweight per-modality classifiers that serve a dual purpose—measuring relative modality utilization for magnitude scaling and providing proxy unimodal gradient directions for a cosine-similarity-based direction loss. The approach is deliberately task-agnostic, applying the same modulation logic to classification, regression, and segmentation without architectural changes.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The gradient direction modulation is a genuinely new angle on the modality-competition problem: the observation that a non-dominant modality can optimize in the *opposite* direction to the fusion module (cosine similarity < 0) and that enforcing alignment with a weighted average of unimodal directions helps is a useful diagnostic and remedy. The method's generality (no restrictions on loss, optimizer, or number of modalities) makes it a practical drop-in for diverse multimodal pipelines. However, the required auxiliary classifiers add training-time compute and memory, and the paper acknowledges this as an open limitation; whether the direction loss can be approximated without explicit per-modality forward passes remains an open question.
