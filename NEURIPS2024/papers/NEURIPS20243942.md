## Problem

Existing implicit neural inverse rendering methods fail to accurately decouple environment lighting, shadows, and PBR materials in high-illumination scenes, causing shadow and specular reflection artifacts to be baked into albedo and roughness. This limits the quality of relighting and downstream material editing, particularly when strong direct lighting produces hard shadows and subtle specular highlights.
![Teaser figure](../imgs/NEURIPS20243942/cropped_page2_idx0.webp)

## Input / Output

The method consumes a set of multi-view RGB images with known camera poses and first reconstructs scene geometry via NeuS (200k iterations, batch size 1024, single V100 GPU, ~5 hours for BRDF stage). It outputs a full PBR decomposition: spatially-varying albedo and roughness maps (via an encoder-decoder network with sparsity-regularized latent codes), a 128-lobe spherical Gaussian environment map, surface normals, a visibility field, and a 24-lobe indirect illumination field. The estimated materials support arbitrary relighting and de-shadowing by re-evaluating the rendering equation under new lighting conditions.

## Method

RobIR employs a two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 trains a NeuS SDF and uses an octree tracer (inspired by PlenOctree) to extract smooth normals, a visibility field, and indirect illumination modeled as 24 spherical Gaussians supervised by NeuS radiance. Stage 2 fixes these priors and jointly optimizes 128 direct SGs, albedo, roughness, and a scene-specific ACES tone-mapping scale γ∈(0,1] under the rendering equation constraint, where the ACES curve (with learnable γ) maps the unbounded PBR color [0,+∞) to [0,1] to preserve contrast in extreme lighting. After 100 epochs, a regularized visibility estimation (RVE) module—an MLP distilled from the learned visibility field—directly predicts per-SG visibility with a KL-based edge loss that sparsifies residuals at shadow boundaries, eliminating stubborn shadow artifacts at object edges. The BRDF uses a simplified Disney model (F₀=0.02, isotropic specular) approximated entirely via closed-form SG inner products.

## Results

On the synthetic high-illumination benchmark, RobIR achieves an albedo PSNR of 25.09 (SSIM 0.9303, LPIPS 0.0972), substantially outperforming the next-best baseline TensoIR (20.52 PSNR) by over 4.5 dB. For environment map estimation, RobIR reaches 16.32 PSNR and 0.1215 MAE, compared to InvRender's 13.47 PSNR and 0.1624 MAE. On relighting, RobIR scores 24.65 PSNR (SSIM 0.9118), and on roughness it achieves the lowest MAE of 0.045, nearly halving the error of the best prior method (0.066 for TensoIR).

## Baselines & Comparisons

RobIR outperforms all seven baselines across every reported metric. Against the closest neural-field competitor TensoIR [17], RobIR gains +4.57 dB albedo PSNR (25.09 vs. 20.52) and reduces roughness MAE from 0.066 to 0.045, while TensoIR's environment map is severely degraded (5.19 PSNR vs. 16.32). InvRender [56], the prior SOTA for indirect-illumination-aware inverse rendering, trails RobIR by 5.97 dB in albedo PSNR and 3.85 dB in environment-map PSNR. The 3D-Gaussian-based methods Relightable-GS [11] and GS-IR [24] show the weakest albedo quality (17.63 and 14.88 PSNR respectively) and the highest roughness errors (0.104 and 0.142 MAE), confirming that the Gaussian-splatting representation struggles with shadow decoupling under high illumination. NVDiffrec [34] and NVDiffrecMC [14] (compared in the supplement) both bake shadows and specular highlights into albedo, yielding LPIPS values roughly 2× higher than RobIR. NeRO [26] is included in the qualitative figures (Figs. 4–6) but not in the quantitative table; visually it also exhibits shadow baking in albedo and roughness under strong lighting.

## Relation to Prior Work

RobIR builds directly on the implicit inverse rendering lineage from NeRFactor (visibility-aware factorization), PhySG (spherical Gaussian approximation of the rendering equation), and InvRender (indirect illumination modeling), all of which operate on SDF-based geometry from NeuS. Its key departures are twofold: (1) introducing a scene-scaled ACES tone mapping curve to keep PBR color in a wider dynamic range before loss computation, and (2) a Regularized Visibility Estimation (RVE) stage that distills the learned visibility field into per-SG visibility predictions, replacing the sampling-based visibility that leaves stubborn edge shadows. The octree tracer (inspired by PlenOctree) replaces sphere tracing for both efficiency and precision, but the conceptual novelty lies in the tone-mapping and visibility distillation rather than the geometry representation.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The paper tackles a practically important failure mode—shadow and specular baking into albedo/roughness under strong illumination—that has limited the usability of neural inverse rendering outputs for relighting and downstream asset creation. The ACES tone mapping trick is a creative repurposing of a film-industry HDR tool, and the RVE distillation is a clean, low-cost fix for a persistent visibility artifact. However, the method inherits NeuS's inability to handle translucent or thin geometry, cannot model anisotropic (metallic) materials due to the isotropic SG assumption, and does not address dynamic lighting. Open questions include whether the scene-specific γ can be predicted analytically rather than learned, and how the framework extends to multi-object or open-world scenes.
