## Problem

Selecting training data for task-specific finetuning of foundation models is critical but challenging: existing similarity-based retrieval methods do not guarantee distribution alignment between selected data and the target task, they overlook near-duplicates prevalent in web-crawled repositories (14–52% of documents), and they struggle to scale to repositories with billions of examples. TSDS addresses all three gaps simultaneously through a principled optimization formulation.

## Input / Output

The method takes a small set of M representative query examples from the target task and a large candidate repository of N examples, both encoded into a shared metric space (e.g., 32,768-dim gradient embeddings for instruction tuning or 512-dim Universal Sentence Encoder embeddings for continued pretraining) with an l₂ distance function. Rather than outputting a fixed subset, it produces a categorical probability distribution over all N candidates, from which B examples are sampled with replacement each epoch during finetuning. The framework is metric-space agnostic, supporting both model-agnostic semantic embeddings and model-specific features like gradients.

## Method

TSDS formulates data selection as a regularized optimal transport problem (Problem RT) that transports probability mass from query examples to candidates, balancing a transport cost (distribution alignment) against a diversity regularizer via a hyperparameter α. The key theoretical contribution is a closed-form solution: the optimal transport assigns each query's probability mass evenly to its K-nearest neighbors, where K is determined by the α/C trade-off, reducing the problem to nearest-neighbor search. To handle near-duplicates, the authors replace the uniform regularizer with a KDE-based one using the Epanechnikov kernel, which weights each candidate by the inverse of its local density estimate so clustered duplicates receive proportionally less probability mass. The resulting KNN-KDE algorithm uses approximate nearest-neighbor search (HNSW via Faiss) for scalability, running in O((M+N) log N) time for the retrieval step, and requires only 28 hours of one-time preprocessing for a 150M-example corpus with under 1 hour per task-specific selection.

## Results

On task-specific instruction tuning with a 1% selection ratio, TSDS (KNN-KDE) achieves 55.4 F1 on TydiQA, 47.9 on MMLU, and 42.0 on BBH with LLaMA-2-7B, and 63.6, 60.5, and 56.3 with Mistral-7B, consistently outperforming the state-of-the-art LESS baseline by up to 6 points (e.g., 63.6 vs. 59.0 on TydiQA/Mistral-7B). On domain-specific continued pretraining, TSDS outperforms DSIR by up to 3 F1 points (e.g., 76.7 vs. 74.8 on ChemProt with 1K annotated data) and shows an average improvement of 1.92 points over DSIR at the 1K annotated data setting. The method is robust to near-duplicates, maintaining consistent performance when 1% of candidates are duplicated up to 1,000 times, while baselines drop by more than 2 points.

## Baselines & Comparisons

TSDS is compared against two state-of-the-art baselines: LESS (Xia et al., 2024) for instruction tuning and DSIR (Xie et al., 2023) for domain-specific continued pretraining. On instruction tuning, TSDS (KNN-KDE) consistently outperforms LESS across TydiQA and BBH at all selection ratios (0.5%, 1%, 5%) for both LLaMA-2-7B and Mistral-7B, with the largest gap being 4.6 points on TydiQA/Mistral-7B at 1% (63.6 vs. 59.0). On MMLU, TSDS is comparable to or slightly below LESS, likely because Mistral-7B is already well-tuned for multiple-choice questions. On continued pretraining, TSDS outperforms DSIR on 8 out of 10 task-size combinations, with the largest margin of 1.9 F1 points on ChemProt (1K) (76.7 vs. 74.8) and 1.1 points on IMDB (1K) (89.8 vs. 87.7); the two exceptions (ChemProt 3K and AGNews 1K) show comparable performance. TSDS also substantially outperforms random selection in all settings, confirming that distribution alignment and diversity-aware selection are critical for effective finetuning.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper unifies ideas from optimal transport, kernel density estimation, and nearest-neighbor search into a single framework for task-specific data selection, departing from prior approaches that either retrieve top-k similar examples (similarity-based methods, LESS) or match distributions in a limited feature space (DSIR's n-gram importance resampling). The key conceptual departure is formulating selection as a regularized transport problem whose closed-form solution reduces to a KNN operation, and augmenting the diversity regularizer with KDE to handle near-duplicates—a problem prior selection methods largely overlooked. The framework is model-agnostic in its metric-space formulation, though the instruction-tuning experiments adopt LESS's gradient-based encoding to capture task-specific utility.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical finding that a 1% selection ratio can outperform training on the full repository (especially for Mistral-7B, where full-data finetuning degrades performance) underscores that data selection is not merely an efficiency trick but a quality lever for task-specific adaptation. The robustness to 1000× near-duplicate injection addresses a real-world pain point in web-crawled corpora that prior selection methods fail to handle. Open questions include whether the reliance on hand-crafted representative examples (which may encode biases) can be alleviated, whether Sinkhorn-based OT variants would improve scalability further, and whether the framework extends naturally to multimodal or non-textual data selection.
