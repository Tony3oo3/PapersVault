## Problem

Existing E2E-AD evaluation relies on open-loop log-replay (e.g., nuScenes) that suffers from distribution shift and causal confusion, or on closed-loop testing over long fixed CARLA routes where the exponential-decay driving score yields high variance and obscures per-skill differences. Moreover, prior methods each collect their own training data, making algorithm-level fair comparison infeasible. Bench2Drive addresses both gaps by providing a standardized large-scale training set and a granular, closed-loop multi-ability evaluation protocol.

## Input / Output

E2E-AD models consume 6 surround cameras (900×1600, JPEG quality 20), a 64-channel LiDAR (85 m range, 600k pts/s), 5 radars, IMU/GNSS, HD-Map, and target waypoints sampled at 10 Hz, and produce either low-level control signals (steering, throttle, brake) or future trajectory waypoints. The benchmark scores these outputs with four metrics—driving score, success rate, driving efficiency (average speed percentage at 20 checkpoints per route), and comfortness (ratio of smooth 20-frame segments using nuPlan-style acceleration/jerk thresholds)—reported per scenario and aggregated into five skill groups (Merging, Overtaking, Emergency Brake, Give Way, Traffic Sign).

## Method

Bench2Drive is a closed-loop benchmark centered on a 2-million-frame, 13,638-clip expert dataset collected by the Think2Drive world-model RL agent across 44 interactive scenarios, 23 weathers, and 12 CARLA towns, with full annotations (3D bounding boxes, depth, semantic/instance segmentation, HD-Map, and expert value/feature estimates). The key design choice is replacing multi-kilometer evaluation routes with 220 short routes (~150 m each, 5 per scenario) so that the infraction penalty's cumulative exponential decay does not dominate the score, enabling isolated per-skill assessment with reduced variance. Beyond the standard driving score, the benchmark introduces driving efficiency (average speed percentage checked every 5 % of route length) and comfortness (segmented smoothness over 20-frame windows using empirically derived acceleration and jerk bounds) to capture trajectory quality. The dataset is partitioned into mini (10 clips), base (1,000 clips), and full (10,000 clips) subsets to match different compute budgets, and all baselines (UniAD, VAD, TCP, ThinkTwice, DriveAdapter, AD-MLP) are trained on the identical base subset to ensure fair algorithm-level comparison.

## Results

On Bench2Drive's 220-route closed-loop evaluation (base training set of 1,000 clips), DriveAdapter* achieves the highest Driving Score (64.22) and Success Rate (33.08%), followed by ThinkTwice* (DS=62.44, SR=31.23%) and TCP-traj* (DS=59.90, SR=30.00%). In the multi-ability assessment, DriveAdapter* leads with a mean ability score of 42.08%, while AD-MLP (which uses only ego state without sensors) scores a mere 0.87% mean ability and 0% success rate. Notably, open-loop L2 error poorly predicts closed-loop performance: UniAD-Base achieves a lower L2 (0.73) than VAD (0.91) yet scores worse in closed-loop (DS 45.81 vs. 42.35, SR 16.36% vs. 15.00%), confirming the paper's central argument that open-loop metrics are insufficient for E2E-AD evaluation.

## Baselines & Comparisons

DriveAdapter* [27] outperforms all other baselines on Driving Score (64.22 vs. 62.44 for ThinkTwice* and 59.90 for TCP-traj*) and mean multi-ability score (42.08% vs. 37.17% and 34.22%), benefiting from its decoupled perception-planning architecture with adapter modules and expert feature distillation. ThinkTwice* [30] is competitive on Driving Score but lags on Overtaking (18.42% vs. 26.38%) and Emergency Brake (35.82% vs. 48.76%). TCP-traj* [5] achieves the best Emergency Brake score (51.67%) and Traffic Sign score (46.28%) among distillation-based methods, but its Comfortness (18.08%) is far below UniAD (43–47%) and VAD (46.01%), indicating aggressive driving. VAD [22] and UniAD [4], which lack expert distillation, cluster in the mid-range (DS 42–46) and are clearly outperformed by distillation-based methods by 15–20 DS points, confirming the paper's finding that expert features provide critical guidance. AD-MLP [23], which uses only ego state without raw sensors, fails catastrophically in closed-loop (0% success rate, DS=18.05) despite being a reasonable open-loop baseline, highlighting the gap between open-loop fitting and actual driving competence. Across all methods, interactive skills (Merging, Overtaking, Emergency Brake) remain the weakest, with even the best method (DriveAdapter*) scoring below 50% on these abilities.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper builds on the CARLA simulation ecosystem and the Think2Drive reinforcement-learning expert model for data collection, but departs fundamentally from prior closed-loop benchmarks (CARLA Leaderboard V1/V2, Longest6) by replacing long, high-variance routes with 220 short (~150 m) scenario-specific routes that enable granular multi-ability assessment. It also addresses a key gap in the E2E-AD literature: prior methods each collect their own training data, making algorithm-level comparison infeasible; Bench2Drive provides a standardized 2-million-frame official training set. Conceptually, it extends the closed-loop evaluation paradigm from a single aggregate driving score to a disentangled 5-skill (Merging, Overtaking, Emergency Brake, Give Way, Traffic Sign) evaluation with additional efficiency and comfortness metrics.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

Bench2Drive fills a critical gap by providing the first closed-loop, multi-ability, standardized-data benchmark for E2E-AD, enabling fair algorithm-level comparisons that were previously impossible. The empirical finding that open-loop L2 error fails to predict closed-loop performance in a behaviorally diverse dataset (AD-MLP fails here but works in nuScenes) strengthens the case for closed-loop evaluation as the primary metric. A key open question is the simulation-reality gap: CARLA's cartoon-style rendering limits transferability, and the authors note that generative models (diffusion-based world models) may eventually bridge this. All tested methods score poorly on interactive skills (Merging, Overtaking, Emergency Brake), suggesting that current imitation-learning paradigms are insufficient for reasoning about multi-agent interaction.
