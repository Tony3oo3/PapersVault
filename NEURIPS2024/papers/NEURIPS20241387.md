## Problem

Instruction-following agents in grounded environments face a tension between sample efficiency and deployment cost: LLMs can provide world knowledge for policy improvement, but requiring online LLM calls at inference time is impractical and expensive. Prior approaches either rely on costly expert demonstrations or use LLMs as online action predictors, which degrades sharply in environments with large action spaces. The paper asks whether LLM world knowledge can be distilled into a small, offline-trained feedback model that identifies productive behaviour for imitation learning without any online LLM access.

## Input / Output

The LFM takes a triple of (language instruction, verbalized observation, action) and outputs a binary productivity label indicating whether the action advances the task. Verbalized observations are generated by a benchmark-specific procedure: for Touchdown, CLIP embeddings align noun-phrases from instructions to 300×300 image patches to populate egocentric scene templates; for ALFWorld and ScienceWorld, raw state information populates language templates. The downstream policy consumes (instruction, verbalized observation) and selects from environment-provided plausible actions by minimum token-level perplexity, with the most recent 20 steps included in the observation context.

## Method

The core mechanism is a two-stage distillation-and-imitation pipeline. First, a base policy is rolled out to collect trajectories, and GPT-4 is prompted in batch over 20-step windows to identify which taken actions were productive for the instruction (a critique task rather than a generation task). This LLM feedback is parsed into step-level yes/no labels to train a small 770M FLAN-T5 feedback model via cross-entropy. Second, in iterative rounds, the current policy is rolled out, the LFM filters for actions it predicts as productive, and the policy is updated by imitation learning on the accumulated desirable-behaviour dataset. The key insight is that critiquing whether a taken action is productive is substantially easier than generating the correct action from scratch, making the approach robust to large action spaces (e.g., ScienceWorld's ~1.9k actions) where direct LLM action prediction (ACTPRED) fails. The LFM generalizes to unseen test environments, enabling one round of adaptation without new demonstrations or LLM calls.

## Results

On three instruction-following benchmarks, LFM improves task-completion rate over the behaviour cloning (BC) baseline on all environments: ALFWorld (64.1% vs. 62.6%), ScienceWorld (47.1% vs. 45.8%), and Touchdown (59.7% vs. 57.5%). With one round of adaptation to unseen test environments (LFMA), gains are substantially larger: 74.6% on ALFWorld, 49.3% on ScienceWorld, and 62.8% on Touchdown. All methods are limited to 100k GPT-4 output tokens, making the comparison controlled for LLM usage cost.

## Baselines & Comparisons

LFM outperforms the behaviour cloning baseline on all three benchmarks, with the largest gain on ALFWorld (+1.5%) and Touchdown (+2.2%). Against ACTPRED (using GPT-4 to directly predict actions for imitation learning), LFM wins on all benchmarks, with the most pronounced advantage on ScienceWorld (47.1% vs. 39.0%), where the large action space (~1.9k actions) makes direct action prediction particularly error-prone. LFM also outperforms DAGGER (Ross et al., AISTATS 2011) across all benchmarks (e.g., 64.1% vs. 55.2% on ALFWorld, 47.1% vs. 22.5% on ScienceWorld), confirming that batched feedback identification is more effective than batched corrective action prediction. Finally, LFM surpasses the previous state-of-the-art on all three benchmarks: Micheli & Fleuret (57.6%) on ALFWorld, Lin et al. (45.8%) on ScienceWorld, and Schumann & Riezler (29.3%) on Touchdown, with LFMA adaptation extending these margins to 74.6%, 49.3%, and 62.8% respectively.

## Relation to Prior Work

This paper sits at the intersection of language-grounded instruction following (ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, Touchdown) and LLM-based agent design, but departs from the dominant paradigm of using LLMs as online expert policies (RT-2, ReAct, WebGPT) by instead distilling LLM world knowledge into a small, deployable Language Feedback Model. Conceptually, it repurposes the RLHF/RLAIF idea of learning from LLM feedback, but applies it to imitation learning in grounded environments rather than text alignment, and replaces corrective-action methods like DAGGER with a critique-based signal. The verbalization step (converting visual observations to language via CLIP alignment) follows recent work (ALFWorld, VELMA) that enables language models to reason over grounded observations, but the feedback-model architecture and the batched offline-to-online distillation pipeline are new.

## Why It Matters / Open Questions

The practical impact is significant: once the small LFM is trained, policy improvement requires no further LLM calls, making deployment cost-effective and feasible in settings where online LLM access is impractical. The generalization result—3.5–12% task-completion gains on unseen environments with a single adaptation round and no new demonstrations—suggests that LLM world knowledge, once distilled into a feedback signal, transfers across environments in ways that direct action prediction does not. Key limitations include dependence on faithful verbalization (which is itself an open problem for rich visual scenes), LLM hallucination in grounded settings, and the still-substantial GPU requirements for the 770M FLAN-T5 policy. Open questions include whether the approach extends to real-world robotics, whether the feedback signal can be repurposed as a dense reward for RL, and whether smaller policy models can close the 10% gap observed when dropping from 770M to 250M parameters.
